Silk Fibroin‐Modified Titanium Implants for Bone Tissue Engineering: Structural Basis, Functionalization and Composite Strategies
Authors: Jiajun Liu, Yan Wang, Xin Li, Guang Li, Zexin Zheng, Lu Zhang, Xiaotian Feng, Tiancheng Ma, Yujing Chen, Yanxin Li, Yibo Liu, Benchao Dong, Peichuan Yang, Yan Li, Mengran Qin, Jianxiong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBone scaffolds represent a common surgical approach for repairing bone defects, and the clinical demand for effective graft substitutes remains high due to the limited availability of autologous bone. Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are widely used as load‐bearing implants; however, their inherent biological inertness hinders early osteogenesis and delays osseointegration. With advances in bone tissue engineering, biofunctionalized 3D‐printed porous Ti scaffolds have emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these limitations. Among the various biomaterials investigated, silk fibroin (SF) has attracted considerable interest owing to its excellent biocompatibility, controllable biodegradability, tunable mechanical properties, and intrinsic capacity for drug loading. The integration of SF onto Ti scaffolds through coatings, hydrogels, or composite structures mitigates the biological inertness of Ti and confers additional functionalities, such as controlled drug release, osteogenic stimulation, and immunomodulation. This review summarizes the structural characteristics, drug‐delivery behavior, and degradation mechanisms of SF, and highlights current evidence on its osteogenic and anti‐inflammatory effects both in vitro and in vivo when combined with porous Ti scaffolds. Furthermore, we discuss emerging multifunctional strategies, including composite coatings incorporating hydroxyapatite, metal‐ion doping, and SF‐based hydrogel systems. Overall, this review provides comprehensive insights into SF‐enhanced Ti scaffolds for next‐generation bone repair.Keywords: biomaterials, bone defects, bone tissue engineering, scaffolds, silk fibroin

Introduction

Bone scaffold is a key surgical technique for repairing and reconstructing bone defects, with over two million procedures performed each year globally [1]. However, artificial bone scaffold materials—such as ceramics, metals, polymers, and other alternatives—have become extensively used in clinical practice due to the scarcity of autologous bone [2, 3]. Metallic materials, including tantalum, Ti, and their alloys, exhibit high mechanical strength and are suitable for load‐bearing bone defect repair, making them preferred candidates for addressing large segmental bone loss [4, 5, 6, 7].

In recent years, with advances in bone tissue engineering, 3D‐printed porous Ti scaffolds have been widely applied in bone repair and regeneration. Among various design strategies, triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS) have emerged as a promising architecture for porous scaffolds. TPMS are special surfaces characterized by periodic repetition in three dimensions and zero mean curvature, exhibiting highly symmetrical crystal‐like structures. They have been widely used in multiple fields, particularly in structural biomaterials and bone tissue engineering fabrication. TPMS scaffolds typically include three structures—gyroid, diamond, and primitive [8, 9]. Their uniformly distributed porosity facilitates an optimal balance between yield strength and elastic modulus, thereby minimizing the risk of stress shielding from excessive stiffness or mechanical failure from insufficient support. Such balanced mechanics mimic natural cancellous bone and endow TPMS scaffolds with substantial potential in bone repair and regeneration.

Despite these advantages, standalone porous bone scaffolds face several challenges, including host immune rejection, uncertain therapeutic performance, and limited osteogenic capability. Preclinical study of porous Ti implants has shown that although bone ingrowth occurs within the scaffolds, its penetration depth is limited and predominantly confined to the region near the implant–bone interface [10]. Therefore, under pathological conditions such as bone defects, porous Ti scaffolds without biological modification are expected to exhibit reduced osteointegration performance, thereby limiting their further application. Consequently, the ability to regulate bone metabolism is a key requirement for future bone repair implants.

Nevertheless, due to the complex pore architecture of porous Ti scaffolds, traditional surface modification techniques cannot form uniform coatings on their surfaces, particularly in the inner core regions. Moreover, the irregular geometries of bone implants further complicate the modification process. One approach focuses on functionalizing porous Ti by embedding cell‐laden gelatin methacrylate hydrogels [11]. Another strategy applies coatings of gelatin, BMP‐2, and vancomycin onto Ti substrates to enhance bioactivity and antibacterial performance [12]. The above approaches offer new insights into designing localized drug‐delivery systems for porous Ti scaffolds. However, drug release mediated through the biodegradation of hydrogels for bone defect treatment may lead to burst‐release behavior, thereby compromising their functional performance. Furthermore, osteointegration is a long‐term process that requires precise and sustained drug release within the implant to ensure lasting therapeutic effects [13, 14]. Therefore, developing controlled‐release drug‐delivery systems for porous Ti scaffolds remains a critical area of research.

Recent advances in nanomaterials have created new opportunities for designing controlled‐release drug‐delivery systems [15, 16, 17, 18]. In previous work, a Ti functional scaffold was developed in which SF was used to load icariin combined with metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), achieving controlled release of an anti‐osteoporosis agent [19]. This provides important inspiration for this area. As a biocompatible material, SF contains backbone segments with different isoelectric points; thus, its charge state and molecular conformation shift with pH changes. This property enables SF to modulate the pH‐responsive release of zeolitic imidazolate framework‐8 (ZIF‐8) [20]. Additionally, SF‐based hydrogels—characterized by excellent biocompatibility, biomimetic extracellular‐matrix‐like architecture, and high water content—have been widely applied in bone tissue engineering [21], particularly in conditions such as osteoarthritis [21], bone defects [22], and osteoporosis [23].

Overall, 3D‐printed intelligent bone scaffolds have attracted considerable attention in bone tissue engineering [24, 25]. Achieving intelligent drug loading and release of bone scaffolds remains a major challenge in this area. The emergence of SF offers a unique solution to this critical issue. SF exhibits cell‐mimetic properties, along with excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, facilitating osteoblast adhesion and proliferation [26, 27, 28]. Therefore, this review aims to present a comprehensive overview of the intrinsic structural characteristics of SF and their mechanistic implications for drug delivery. It will also examine how SF adheres to three‐dimensional porous Ti scaffolds and contributes to the spatiotemporal controlled release of drugs in bone scaffolds.

Methods

A literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase from database inception to March 2026. The search strategy combined medical subject headings terms and free‐text keywords, including but not limited to “silk fibroin,” “scaffold,” “coating,” “drug delivery,” “drug release,” “titanium,” “hydrogel,” and “bone regeneration.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to refine the search.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) original research articles involving silk fibroin–based biomaterials (e.g., scaffolds, coatings, or hydrogels); (2) studies investigating drug delivery functions or biological effects related to bone regeneration and osseointegration; (3) studies conducted at the in vitro, in vivo, or clinical level. Studies were excluded if they were conference abstracts or if the full text was unavailable.

To ensure the reliability of the included evidence, the methodological quality of the selected studies was carefully considered during data extraction and synthesis, with particular attention to experimental design, sample size, control settings, and outcome measures.

Characteristics of Silk Fibroin

Silk fibroin, a natural protein‐based biopolymer derived from domesticated silkworms, features a highly ordered molecular structure and tunable physicochemical properties, rendering it highly suitable for biomedical applications. Its unique composition—consisting of heavy and light chains assembled through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions—promotes the formation of dynamic secondary structures such as α‐helices and β‐sheets, which critically determine its mechanical strength, structural stability, and biological performance. These conformational characteristics endow SF with excellent biocompatibility, favorable osteoconductivity, and controllable biodegradability. Furthermore, SF exhibits pH‐responsive behavior, enabling environment‐dependent modulation of drug release, and degrades in a predictable manner influenced by its crystalline content and susceptibility to enzymatic degradation. Collectively, these attributes position SF as a versatile platform for coating Ti scaffolds, functioning not only as a structural support but also as a stimuli‐responsive carrier for controlled drug delivery and enhanced osseointegration.

Structure and Functional Features of Silk Fibroin

Silk, extracted from the domesticated silkworm, is primarily composed of two proteins: SF (∼70–75 wt.%) and sericin (∼25–30 wt.%) [29]. SF is a natural protein‐based biopolymer consisting of 18 amino acids, mainly with glycine (45%), alanine (29%), and serine (12%) (Figure 1B) [30, 31]. Protein chemistry and DNA sequence analysis have revealed that SF comprises three subunits in a 6:6:1 ratio—namely, a ∼390 kDa heavy chain (H‐chain), a ∼26 kDa light chain (L‐chain), and the glycoprotein P25 (Figure 1A) [32, 33]. The heavy and light chains are connected via disulfide bonds, while the P25 is linked to both chains through non‐covalent bonds [34]. These subunits collectively assemble through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions to form a reverse β‐sheet structure. This β‐sheet structure is primarily stabilized by repetitive hexapeptide domains (GAGAGX, where X can be alanine, serine, tyrosine, or valine) and tetrapeptide domains (GAAS) [29]. The crystalline, hydrophobic β‐sheet domains prevent the penetration of water and proteases, thereby facilitating the gradual biodegradation of SF within biological environments [27, 32].

Silk I and Silk II represent the two major conformational states of SF (Figure 1C) [35], and their structural differences mainly lie in the relative content of α‐helices and β‐sheets. Silk I refers to the natural conformation of SF present in the silkworm gland just before spinning [36]. It is a hydrated and metastable structure, primarily composed of α‐helices and lacking extensive β‐sheet formation [31, 37]. This is because hydrogen bonds in Silk I are formed within individual molecular chains rather than between adjacent chains. Such intramolecular hydrogen bonding not only stabilizes the Silk I structure but also restricts the stacking of interchain β‐sheets [38].

Silk II is formed during the spinning process of the silkworm and is naturally derived from Silk I [36, 39]. Beyond this biological transformation, the conversion from Silk I to Silk II can be artificially induced through various external stimuli, including solvent treatment, mechanical stretching, ionic interactions, and pH modulation [31, 40, 41, 42]. Silk II is characterized by densely packed, antiparallel β‐sheet crystals and lacks α‐helical structures [43, 44]. These structural features endow Silk II with enhanced stability and hydrophobicity.

The unique structure of SF imparts excellent biocompatibility and outstanding mechanical properties, enabling its wide application in diverse biomedical fields, including coatings for Ti scaffolds [45], drug delivery systems [46], and synthetic polymers [47].

pH‐Sensitive Behavior of Silk Fibroin

The pH‐responsiveness of SF is closely associated with its intrinsic secondary structure, particularly the dynamic balance between β‐sheet and α‐helix. The secondary structure of SF varies under different pH conditions. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis of SF particles produced at different pH values showed that the secondary structure changed from a silk II structure (mainly β‐sheet) at pH 4 to a silk I structure (mainly α‐helices) at pH 9 [48]. Importantly, the drug release behavior of SF is influenced by its secondary structure, with different conformations showing varied release profiles.

For instance, recent work demonstrates the development of structure‐controlled, drug‐loaded SF coatings with tailored secondary structures on Ti plates [49]. Notably, the α‐structured SF coating effectively suppressed initial burst release. Under neutral pH conditions, this α‐structured coating released only 35.4% of Ag+ within the first 24 h, compared to 72.5% for β‐structured coatings, and sustained release for more than 30 days. In a rabbit femoral defect model, α‐structured SF coating enhanced new bone formation and improved integration between newly formed bone and surrounding tissue, suggesting rapid and durable osseointegration. These findings indicate that under acidic conditions, SF preferentially adopts a β‐sheet structure, which facilitates burst drug release, whereas in neutral or alkaline environments, it stabilizes into an α‐helical conformation, enabling sustained and controlled release.

Furthermore, it has been reported that SF coating released high concentrations of Cu2+ and Ag+ in acidic environments (pH 5.0) [20]. achieving 99.99% killing of planktonic bacteria and complete eradication of biofilm‐embedded bacteria, thereby preventing biofilm formation. In contrast, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the lower ion release profile promoted alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, collagen secretion, and calcium deposition, which indicated potentiated osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

Biodegradability of Silk Fibroin

Biodegradable materials are those that can gradually break down in the body and are gradually absorbed or excreted, thereby reducing long‐term foreign body reactions and increasing suitable biocompatibility [50, 51]. The degradation rate of SF is closely related to the relative proportions of Silk I and Silk II structures. Specifically, a higher silk II content enhances β‐sheet formation, which enhances structural stability and slows degradation compared to α‐helices formation. This tunable degradation behavior makes SF particularly advantageous for sustained drug delivery applications [27, 52].

As a protein‐based biomaterial, the degradation of SF is primarily influenced by pH and proteolytic enzymes, which preferentially degrade non‐crystalline regions through enzymes such as α‐chymotrypsin, proteinase XIV, and collagenase IA [27, 53]. Owing to the variable isoelectric points (pI) along its backbone, SF exhibits pH‐responsive release of metal ions. When the pH falls below the pI of SF, the material surface becomes positively charged, leading to increased electrostatic repulsion. Consequently, positively charged metal ions such as Zn2 +, Cu2 +, and Ag+ are released more rapidly. This responsive mechanism facilitates high‐concentration ion release in acidic microenvironments, such as those produced by bacterial metabolism, which lowers local pH, to achieve effective antibacterial action, while allowing low‐level ion release under physiological conditions, supporting osteogenic activity [20, 54].

Methods for Coating Silk Fibroin on Ti Porous Scaffold

Currently, Ti porous scaffolds alone face several challenges, including potential host immune rejection, limited clinical validation, and insufficient osteogenic capacity [55]. Preclinical trials of porous Ti scaffold implants have shown that although bone ingrowth occurs within the scaffold, its penetration is limited and largely confined to the bone‐implant interface [10]. Therefore, unmodified porous Ti scaffolds lacking biofunctionalization are likely to exhibit suboptimal osseointegration, particularly under pathological conditions such as femoral head necrosis, thereby compromising therapeutic efficacy.

To address these limitations, various biofunctionalization strategies have been explored to enhance the performance of porous Ti scaffolds, particularly through the incorporation of bioactive coatings and drug‐delivery systems [11, 12]. However, achieving uniform and controllable modification within complex porous architectures remains a significant challenge, especially for long‐term therapeutic applications requiring sustained drug release [13, 14].

Common methods for applying SF coatings include dip coating [19, 56, 57], electrodeposition, electrospinning, chemical conjugation, and plasma‐assisted deposition [58, 59, 60]. Techniques such as dip coating and spin coating allow precise control over coating thickness by adjusting parameters including SF concentration, immersion speed, and solution volume [57]. However, dip coating and drop casting typically produce coatings ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers in thickness [61, 62], while spin coating is limited to flat substrates with minimal surface roughness [63]. As such, these techniques are more suitable for non‐porous implants, such as Ti rods, and are less effective for achieving uniform coatings on porous scaffolds. For porous Ti scaffolds, freeze‐drying is the primary method [19, 64]. It offers promising potential for the application of SF coatings, enabling the uniform deposition of SF onto complex porous Ti scaffolds. In this process, the scaffold is immersed in an SF solution, followed by freeze‐drying to induce a water‐insoluble coating on the surface [19]. When SF was in its solution state or in the freeze‐dried state, it was predominantly dominated by silk I conformation, which is suitable for sustained drug release [65].

Therefore, surface modification of Ti implants with SF coating holds considerable potential, as it improves the bioactivity of Ti and enhances osseointegration, leading to optimal adhesion to surrounding tissue [56, 66, 67, 68]. This approach provides a solution to the intrinsic bioinertness of porous Ti implants, offering a viable pathway toward more effective bone regeneration therapies.

TPMS Architecture's Impact on SF Coating

TPMS may also play an important role in the performance of SF‐functionalized coating systems. In many ways, the Gyroid, Diamond, and Primitive surfaces are the most important and commonly observed TPMS structures [69]. Sea urchins [70], butterfly wing scales [71], and beetle exoskeletons [72] are examples of natural systems where these structures have been found. TPMS designs have been widely used in bone scaffolds due to their trabecular bone‐mimicking porous morphology, which is characterized by continuous surfaces and the absence of abrupt stress concentrations [73, 74].

Since the TPMS architecture forms a continuous void/solid interface, TPMS‐based porous scaffolds exhibit greater advantages for bone ingrowth than solid implant rods [75]. The void space in this structure acts as a network of interconnected pore channels that promote bone ingrowth and cell adhesion [76]. Besides, mechanical interlock theory suggests that the complicated porous topology of TPMS scaffolds may promote the infiltration and attachment of SF coating within the void space [77]. The combination of TPMS scaffolds with SF hydrogels may maximize the filling of interconnected void spaces, particularly when photocurable hydrogels are used. By means of photocrosslinking technology, SF hydrogels can firmly adhere within the void space of the TPMS scaffolds [58]. In addition, such hydrogels can serve as drug delivery carriers, enabling the biofunctionalization of Ti scaffolds and potentially enhancing in vivo osseointegration. A gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)/silk fibroin methacryloyl (SilMA)‐based nanocomposite hydrogel incorporating mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) has been developed for the dual release of stromal cell‐derived factor‐1α (SDF‐1α) and osteoprotegerin (OPG), aiming to modulate osteoimmune responses and enhance bone regeneration [78]. Thus, such structures may help to integrate SF coatings with the TPMS scaffold, minimizing coating detachment, increasing coating stability, and improving coating uniformity.

In addition, the interconnected pore network and high specific surface area of TPMS scaffolds may further influence the release kinetics of SF drug‐loaded coatings or hydrogels. Previous studies have shown that drug diffusion behavior and sustained release can be regulated by adjusting the thickness, crystallinity, and structural order of SF coatings [77, 79, 80]. As a result, TPMS architectures may provide a larger interfacial area for the attachment of SF hydrogel coatings and molecular adsorption, thereby promoting more uniform drug distribution and controlled release. Furthermore, the interconnected void spaces of TPMS scaffolds may improve fluid permeability and nutrient transport in the bone microenvironment, which may further influence local drug diffusion and coating degradation behavior.

Collectively, the unique interconnected architecture and high surface area of TPMS scaffolds may provide additional structural advantages for SF‐based coating systems in terms of coating adhesion, drug loading, and sustained drug release, highlighting the promising potential of such scaffolds in multifunctional applications for bone tissue engineering.

Drug Release Mechanisms

Drug release mechanisms can be categorized into six major types based on material properties and control strategies. Diffusion‐controlled systems, including reservoir and matrix designs, where release follows Fick's law or the Higuchi model [81]. Biodegradation or erosion‐controlled systems, governed by bulk or surface erosion, with kinetics predicted by models [82, 83]. Swelling‐controlled systems, where water‐induced polymer relaxation regulates release [84, 85]. Osmosis‐driven systems rely on osmotic pressure gradients for zero‐order release [86]. Ion‐exchange systems utilize electrostatic interactions for controlled release and Prodrug approaches, where chemical conjugation enables enzyme‐ or hydrolysis‐mediated drug liberation [87]. These fundamental strategies form the basis for the design of advanced stimuli‐responsive systems, including those incorporating SF as a functional material. By leveraging SF's unique physicochemical properties, such as pH sensitivity, β‐sheet stability, and tunable hydrophilicity, researchers have achieved controlled and sustained release for diverse therapeutic applications [88].

A representative example of such innovation is the development of human serum albumin–SF (HSA–SF) nanocapsules with pH‐responsive drug release properties [89]. This pH‐dependent behavior is influenced by SF concentration, suggesting that controlled drug delivery can be achieved by tuning the pH conditions. In vitro drug release experiments, HSA‐SF nanocapsules containing 50% SF exhibited significantly enhanced pH‐responsive release for all tested drugs at acidic conditions (pH 4.5–5.4), whereas HSA nanocapsules without SF showed no pH‐dependent release. These results confirm the inherent pH sensitivity of SF. Furthermore, confocal laser scanning microscopy and small‐angle X‐ray scattering analyses indicated that, during drug release at different pH levels, nanocapsules exhibited increased internal porosity and decreased particle density, consistent with a diffusion‐controlled release process. Thus, diffusion control may represent a key mechanism underlying the pH‐dependent sustained drug release of SF.

Building upon this concept, SF has also been integrated into Ti implant systems for sustained drug delivery. Using a layer‐by‐layer self‐assembly strategy, wogonin‐encapsulated SF nanoparticles have been integrated into Ti implants, endowing them with the capacity to modulate macrophage polarization [90]. Mechanistically, in this system, the stable secondary β‐sheet structure of SF was destroyed in an acidic environment, resulting in the degradation of SF nanoparticles to stably release the drug in 7 days, which may sustainably activate paracrine signaling to promote osteoblast differentiation (Figure 2A–C). Similarly, Ti surfaces have been functionalized by immobilizing SF nanoparticles loaded with the peptide LL‐37 (Figure 2D) [91]. LL‐37 exhibited a loading efficiency of up to 40.3%, and the release of peptide LL‐37 in the in vitro assay solution could last for 168 h (Figure 2E). The underlying mechanism relies on the cationic properties of LL‐37, whereby electrostatic attraction with negatively charged SF nanoparticles enables efficient and stable peptide loading.

In summary, these studies highlight how SF serves as a versatile platform capable of incorporating multiple release mechanisms—diffusion, pH sensitivity, structural degradation, and electrostatic binding—to achieve controlled and sustained therapeutic delivery in both nanoscale and implantable systems.

Biological Performance of SF‐Coated Ti Scaffold

The incorporation of SF as a surface modification strategy for porous Ti scaffolds has garnered increasing interest due to its remarkable biocompatibility, tunable secondary structures, and versatile functionalization capacity. As a naturally derived protein‐based biopolymer, SF not only mimics cellular components but also supports osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. Its ability to serve as a bioactive interface and drug delivery platform further enhances its value in bone tissue engineering. This section highlights the biological performance of SF‐coated Ti scaffolds, encompassing their effects on osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and macrophages in vitro, as well as their osteointegration capacity in vivo. By modulating cell behavior and immune responses, SF coatings hold great potential for improving osseointegration, particularly under challenging pathological conditions.

In Vitro Performance

Cellular Response of Osteoblasts

SF has demonstrated the capacity to modulate key osteoblastic functions—including viability, adhesion, migration, and differentiation—thereby establishing its broad applicability in bone tissue engineering. For example, a composite scaffold has been developed by integrating an SF sponge with functionally graded Ti [64]. Statistical analysis revealed significantly enhanced osteoblast area and density on the composite scaffold compared to the unmodified porous Ti scaffold, highlighting the beneficial role of SF in promoting biocompatibility. Additionally, an SF‐based encapsulation strategy has been developed to regulate osteoblast differentiation towards osteocytes [92]. The SF encapsulation induced morphological changes from osteoblast‐like to osteocyte‐like shapes and upregulated osteogenic markers, including ALP, type I collagen (Col I), and osteocalcin (OCN), as well as calcium deposition. This approach accelerated cell calcification without exogenous cytokines, indicating that SF‐based encapsulation provides a conducive microenvironment for osteogenic differentiation. Similarly, a biofunctionalized Ti coating has been developed using genetically modified SF (TiBP‐SF) [93]. TiBP‐SF exhibits reversible binding to Ti substrates and enhanced osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3‐E1 cells without additional cytokines, demonstrating that TiBP‐SF provides an osseointegration‐promoting microenvironment for biomedical applications. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SF coatings can directly promote osteoblast maturation and differentiation by providing a bioactive and osteoinductive microenvironment.

Cellular Response of Osteoclasts

Bone regeneration is a dynamic, multistage process requiring the orchestrated interplay of cellular recruitment, immune modulation, and balanced osteoblast‐osteoclast activity [94, 95, 96]. While SF itself lacks intrinsic inhibitory effects on osteoclast activity, its excellent biocompatibility and tunable structure make it an ideal carrier for loading and delivering anti‐resorptive agents [97, 98, 99]. When formulated into hydrogels or scaffolds, SF‐based systems can achieve sustained and localized release of therapeutic molecules, thereby indirectly contributing to osteoclast suppression and promoting bone regeneration.

For example, a combination of SilMA with GelMA, incorporating multiple bioactive factors, has been employed to construct a nanocomposite hydrogel‐based drug delivery system [78]. The nanocomposite hydrogel system restored the balance of osteoblast–osteoclast crosstalk, as evidenced by co‐culture with RAW264.7, and suppressed osteoclast formation by downregulating tartrate‐resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) expression through OPG‐mediated receptor activator of nuclear factor‐κ B ligand (RANKL) inhibition. This indicates that the SF hydrogel possesses controlled drug release capabilities and that combining SF‐based systems with osteoclast‐inhibitory bioactive factors can synergistically enhance osteogenic potential. Building upon this strategy, a dual‐responsive micro/nanosphere delivery system based on regenerated silk fibroin (RSF) has been developed for osteoarthritis therapy [100]. The phenylboronic acid‐modified RSF nanoparticles loaded with isoliquiritin significantly inhibited osteoclast differentiation from bone marrow‐derived macrophages in vitro.

These findings highlight RSF‐based drug delivery platforms as promising tools for modulating osteoclastogenesis and treating early‐stage osteoarthritis.

Cellular Response of the Macrophage Cell

Implantation of biomaterials inevitably triggers an inflammatory response [101, 102]. After implantation, biomaterial surfaces rapidly adsorb blood and interstitial proteins, thereby triggering coagulation cascades and immune responses that initiate inflammation [103]. Danger signals from damaged tissues and adsorbed proteins activate granulocytes and monocytes, marking the onset of acute inflammation. This neutrophil‐driven response gradually transitions to macrophage‐dominated chronic inflammation, which is mediated by cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐13, and MCP‐1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1) [103]. Previous studies have shown that macrophage‐secreted cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐10, and IL‐13 promote bone formation, whereas proinflammatory factors, including TNF, IL‐1, and IL‐6, enhance bone resorption [104]. Eventually, fibrotic tissue forms and replaces the original tissue during the repair process. Collectively, these findings indicate that macrophages play a crucial role in immune regulation during biomaterial‐mediated tissue repair.

The implantation of porous Ti scaffolds requires consideration not only of their osteogenic capacity but also of the macrophage‐mediated immune response they elicit. In this context, in addition to directly promoting osteoblast differentiation and inhibiting osteoclast activity at the implant interface, optimizing macrophage‐mediated immune responses represents an effective strategy to enhance osseointegration, particularly under osteoporotic conditions. For example, a composite system integrating a 3D‐printed porous Ti scaffold, icariin@Mg‐MOF‐74, and SF has been developed to achieve the sustained co‐release of icariin and Mg2 + (Figure 2I) [19]. In this system, macrophages exist in two distinct functional phenotypes: the pro‐inflammatory M1 type and the anti‐inflammatory M2 type [105]. This study demonstrated that the SF‐wrapped scaffold promoted macrophage polarization from the pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype through inhibition of the Notch1 signaling pathway (Figure 2J,K). Compared with control groups, the scaffold significantly upregulated osteogenic markers such as OCN and ALP, while simultaneously downregulating osteoclast‐related markers including Acp5, c‐Fos, Oscar, and Sema (Figure 2L,M), thereby creating a pro‐regenerative osteoimmune microenvironment.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that SF‐functionalized Ti scaffolds can enhance osseointegration not only through direct osteogenic stimulation but also by modulating macrophage polarization and the local immune microenvironment.

In Vivo Applications

Unmodified porous Ti implants exhibit suboptimal osseointegration performance in vivo. Compared to natural bone tissue, porous Ti implants exhibit inferior biological adhesion and are primarily secured within the bony matrix through mechanical interlocking rather than biological bonding [106], rendering them more susceptible to damage [107]. The integration process between porous Ti implants and bone tissue is relatively slow, typically requiring more than 3–6 months, which is often associated with a prolonged recovery period. Furthermore, the presence of a native oxide layer on the Ti surface impedes direct interfacial interactions between the implant and surrounding bone tissue [108], thereby impairing new bone formation. This limitation in osseointegration is primarily attributed to the biological inertness of the Ti surface, which restricts effective implant–bone bonding [109], and may ultimately lead to severe complications, including implant loosening, failure, and secondary bone defects [110, 111].

Accordingly, promoting effective osseointegration of porous Ti implants remains a persistent challenge. In addition, prolonged exposure of porous Ti implants to human body fluids may trigger undesirable biochemical reactions at the implant–bone interface. Such reactions can induce electrochemical corrosion, ultimately compromising the structural integrity and lifespan of the Ti implant [112, 113]. Surface modification represents a practical and efficient approach to overcoming these limitations of porous Ti implants. This approach preserves the intrinsic mechanical advantages of Ti while imparting additional interfacial functionalities, thereby enhancing antibacterial performance, reinforcing osseointegration, and improving resistance to electrochemical corrosion [106]. Notably, SF‐based coatings may emerge as a promising strategy for regulating and enhancing osseointegration in porous Ti scaffolds. SF can function as a pH‐sensitive drug delivery platform, and its integration with porous Ti scaffolds enables precise and sustained therapeutic release, thereby further enhancing osseointegration.

To evaluate the potential of SF‐modified Ti scaffolds, it is essential to investigate their performance in vivo, particularly with respect to bone integration and tissue response. Accordingly, several studies have focused on assessing the long‐term stability and osteogenic outcomes of these functionalized implants. Structure‐controlled drug‐loaded SF coatings with distinct secondary conformations have been coated on Ti substrates (Figure 2F) [114]. Notably, the α‐structured SF coating demonstrated an ability to suppress the initial burst release. In a rabbit femoral defect model, the α‐structured SF coating significantly enhanced new bone formation and bone–implant bonding, indicating rapid and durable osseointegration (Figure 2G). Under neutral pH conditions, the α‐structured coating released only 35.4% of Ag+ within the first 24 h, compared with 72.5% for β‐structured coatings, and maintained sustained release for more than 30 days (Figure 2H).

Composite Coatings and Multifunctional Strategies

Hydroxyapatite for Osteoconductivity

The incorporation of bioactive Hydroxyapatite (HA) into the SF scaffold can elicit favorable cellular responses under both static and dynamic in vitro culture conditions. HA/SF composite scaffolds promote more robust cell growth than pure SF scaffolds and exhibit enhanced mechanical strength comparable to that of cancellous bone (Figure 3A) [115].

SF can bind to the substrate in a network‐like architecture, thereby increasing interfacial binding strength. Following the incorporation of SF, the adhesion between the HA coating and the Ti substrate was further reinforced. Accordingly, Ti/SF/HA composite scaffolds exhibit higher binding strength than Ti/HA counterparts, reaching 15.31 ± 1.5 versus 10.18 ± 0.91 MPa, respectively (Figure 3B) [66]. Moreover, incorporation of HA into the SF coating can enhance osteogenic activity. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the HA‐modified group exhibited higher expression of OCN compared to the pure SF coating group (Figure 3C).

Akt phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are critical regulatory pathways involved in diabetes‐induced impairment of osseointegration. SF/HA composite coatings significantly ameliorated diabetes‐associated impairment of Ti osseointegration [56]. This improvement was attributed to the coating's ability to suppress diabetes‐induced ROS overproduction, restore redox homeostasis in osteoblasts, and thereby mitigate the attenuation of Akt phosphorylation. Compared to the Ti substrate, the SF/HA coating group significantly reactivated the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, as evidenced by increased Akt expression levels in the Western blot assay (Figure 3F). Furthermore, histomorphometric analysis revealed that the SF/HA coating group exhibited the highest percentage of newly formed bone (24.3 ± 3.1%), which was 3.1‐fold greater than that of the Ti group (p < 0.05) (Figure 3D,E).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the incorporation of hydroxyapatite into SF‐modified Ti scaffolds exerts synergistic effects on both interfacial mechanics and biological performance.

Metal Ions

Metal Ion‐Mediated Osteoimmunomodulation

Zinc (Zn2 +) plays a critical role in maintaining physiological homeostasis, immune functions, and oxidative stress balance [116, 117]. The activity of immune cells can be modulated by zinc in vitro and in vivo. Zn2 + primarily triggers the activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby enhancing osteoblast proliferation and differentiation and upregulating osteogenic gene expression, ultimately promoting bone formation (Figure 4A) [118].

Similarly, Magnesium ions (Mg2 +) play dual roles in promoting both osteogenesis and angiogenesis [119]. In this study, a hydrogel was fabricated using photocurable SilMA and subsequently chelated with Mg2 +. qRT‐PCR, immunofluorescence staining, and Western blot analyses revealed that the expression of osteogenic markers (Runx2, ALP, OPN, COL‐1, and OCN) in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) was significantly upregulated in the group treated with 10 mm MgCl2. Mg2 + and bisphosphonates co‐released from the hydrogel synergistically promote osteogenesis [119]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Mg2 + promotes osteogenesis through anti‐inflammatory immunoregulatory mechanisms [19, 120, 121]. A Ti plate coated with tannic acid (TA) and Mg2 + not only stimulates osteogenesis but also endows the implant surface with favorable osteoimmunomodulatory properties [122]. TA endowed the coating with potent ROS‐scavenging capacity, thereby contributing to a more favorable oxidative environment for tissue repair. Meanwhile, Mg2 + played a key role in regulating macrophage behavior by suppressing M1‐type polarization and promoting a shift toward the anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype, ultimately reducing local inflammation and establishing an enhanced osteoimmune microenvironment (Figure 4F).

Overall, the incorporation of Zn2 + and Mg2 + into SF‐based systems enables simultaneous regulation of osteogenesis and immune responses.

Metal Ion‐Mediated Infection Control

Postoperative infection associated with orthopedic implants represents one of the leading causes of implant failure, imposing substantial health and economic burdens on affected patients. In recent years, the incidence of implant‐related infection has risen in parallel with the growing number of joint replacement and spinal fusion procedures performed worldwide. Among the prophylactic strategies currently available, local application of vancomycin powder has been established as a cornerstone approach, demonstrating superior efficacy compared with intravenous administration [123].

However, conventional local antibiotic therapy is increasingly challenged by the emergence of drug‐resistant pathogens. Pathogenic organisms like Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) can use the toxin–antitoxin regulatory system once a biofilm has formed on the implant surface to move toward a chronic infection phenotype [124]. This results in significantly increased antibiotic tolerance and makes standard treatments ineffective, a clinical situation that frequently calls for revision surgery as a last resort [125]. Concurrently, the conventional local drug delivery approach using bone cement as a carrier is hampered by a pronounced burst‐release effect, which not only compromises sustained antibiotic efficacy but may also further accelerate the selection of antibiotic‐resistant strains [126]. These compounding limitations have directed research attention toward non‐antibiotic strategies centered on metal ions. In this context, SF has emerged as an ideal scaffold coating for metal ion–based antimicrobial systems, owing to its intrinsic capacity to reduce and stabilize metal nanoparticles [127].

Among metal ion–loaded SF systems for anti‐infection applications, silver represents the most extensively investigated platform. The antibacterial mechanisms of Ag ion nanoparticles involve multiple parallel pathways and are partly governed by the electrical conductivity of the underlying substrate. Electron transfer between silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and the Ti substrate induces ROS generation, leading to oxidative stress, membrane disruption, and bacterial death. Although ROS production was also observed in osteoblasts, cell viability was not significantly affected [128].

Building on this mechanistic foundation, one study developed an AgNPs/SF biomimetic coating on Ti surfaces, which enabled sustained Ag+ release for 28 days, enhanced antibacterial activity against S. aureus, and simultaneously promoted osteoblast proliferation and mineralization, demonstrating dual anti‐infective and osteogenic effects [129]. Researchers have also used SF as a stabilizing and reducing agent for the in situ synthesis of AgNPs within SF scaffolds in relation to the composite architecture of SF and AgNPs. At low AgNP concentrations, a corresponding SF/AgNP system demonstrated good antimicrobial activity and biosafety by exhibiting robust antibacterial activity against Gram‐negative bacteria and antibiotic‐resistant strains without affecting the osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs [130]. Most recently, optimization of the AgNPs doping ratio in electrospun PCL‐SF nanofibrous scaffolds demonstrated that 0.1% w/v AgNPs effectively inhibited both Escherichia coli and S. aureus while simultaneously enhancing osteoblast proliferation and ALP activity, highlighting its potential for infected bone defect repair [131].

Among more sophisticated multi‐metal ion synergistic systems, the combined deployment of copper and silver has attracted particular attention. In one representative study, copper oxide microspheres (µCuO) and AgNPs were co‐assembled onto the surface of porous polyetheretherketone (PEEK) implants via an SF interlayer, exploiting the pH‐responsive characteristics of the infectious microenvironment to achieve on‐demand ion release. Under the acidic conditions prevailing at infection sites (pH 5.0), a marked burst of Cu2 + and Ag+ release was triggered, achieving 99.99% killing of planktonic bacteria and complete eradication of established biofilms. Conversely, at physiological pH (7.4), the lower level of ion leaching was sufficient to promote osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis [20].

Therefore, compared with conventional systemic antibiotic injection strategies, surface coating modification of metallic implants may represent a promising anti‐infection approach with a lower risk of inducing severe antimicrobial resistance. In particular, the integration of bioactive coatings such as SF with metallic nanoparticles has emerged as a highly promising strategy for implant‐associated infection prevention.

SF Hydrogels for Porous Scaffold

SF‐based hydrogels have garnered significant interest as composite coatings for the functionalization of bone scaffolds due to their excellent biocompatibility, biomimetic architecture, high water content, and inherent flexibility [21]. A representative example is a trifunctional SF‐based hydrogel (SF‐ZIF@NA) that selectively inhibits osteoclast fusion while preserving precursor osteoclasts (pOCs), thereby facilitating osteoporotic bone repair [23]. The hydrogel enzymatically removes sialic acid through neuraminidase (NA) encapsulated within ZIF‐8 nanoparticles (Figure 4C). Moreover, the pH‐responsive release of Zn2 + ions further enhanced osteogenic activity. In osteoporotic rat models, the hydrogel significantly increased bone density and vascularization, achieving near‐complete defect repair within eight weeks (Figure 4D). Besides, incorporating hydroxyapatite into SF hydrogels can raise the compressive strength up to 4.5 MPa, partially addressing the mechanical requirements for large bone defect repair [22].

Importantly, SilMA, a photocrosslinkable derivative of SF, provides a facile and effective strategy for the fabrication of SF‐based hydrogels. An ultraviolet (UV)‐light‐assisted dip‐coating strategy has been employed to deposit SilMA onto porous Ti implants (Figure 4B) [58]. Building on this approach, the authors showed that SilMA generated uniform and mechanically stable coatings with controllable thicknesses (87–156 µm), and 30% concentration of SilMA achieved a compressive strength of 930.83 kPa and a shear adhesion of 1.6 MPa after 7 days (Figure 4E). Crucially, the UV‐based process avoided complex pretreatment steps, suggesting a scalable and cost‐efficient route for Ti implant coating.

This photocuring strategy is particularly suitable for TPMS scaffolds, as the SilMA hydrogels can easily penetrate the interconnected porous structure. After UV irradiation, a uniform SF hydrogel coating is formed on the scaffold surface without affecting the mechanical properties. By combining the mechanical strength of TPMS Ti scaffolds with the bioactive and drug‐delivery properties of SF hydrogels, this approach provides a promising strategy for multifunctional bone implants.

Limitation

Despite the promising advances in SF‐functionalized porous Ti scaffolds, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, evidence from large‐animal studies and clinical validation is still lacking, and the majority of current research is still restricted to in vitro trials and small‐animal models. Specifically, composite drug‐loaded coatings on TPMS Ti scaffolds have not been thoroughly assessed for long‐term biosafety, degradation behavior, mechanical stability, and prolonged therapeutic performance. Thus, long‐term implantation studies using multifunctional coated TPMS Ti scaffolds represent an important gap in this field.

Second, there is considerable variation in current research on scaffold architecture, coating techniques, drug‐loading systems, and experimental models. This makes it challenging to compare studies directly and restricts the development of standard design principles for SF‐based composite scaffolds.

Lastly, even though SF‐based composite coating systems have demonstrated possible biological activity in bone tissue engineering applications, it is still unclear what specific molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways underlie these biological effects. Further mechanistic studies are required to better support the clinical translation of SF‐functionalized porous Ti scaffolds.

Conclusions

SF has emerged as a highly versatile biopolymer for enhancing the performance of porous Ti scaffolds. Its tunable secondary structures, excellent biocompatibility, and pH‐responsive degradation enable SF to function as both a biomimetic coating and a controllable drug‐delivery matrix. When applied to Ti implants, SF promotes osteoblast adhesion and differentiation, supports a favorable osteoimmune environment, and enables sustained release of therapeutic agents, thereby improving osseointegration under both healthy and pathological conditions.

Composite strategies further expand SF's functionality: incorporation of hydroxyapatite enhances osteoconductivity, metal ions modulate immune responses, and hydrogel or MOF‐based systems provide precise control over drug kinetics. These synergistic effects highlight SF's capacity to transform inert Ti into a biologically active, multifunctional implant surface.

Looking forward, integrating drug‐loaded SF hydrogels with 3D‐printed porous Ti scaffolds represents a significant step toward next‐generation bone implants that unite mechanical stability with intelligent therapeutic function.
