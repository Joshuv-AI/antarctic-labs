Identifying urban low-altitude traffic corridors: A computational fluid dynamics simulation approach

Zhipeng Liu, Zhizhou Wu, Jizhao Wang, Bin Pan, Wenxin Jiang

ABSTRACT
With the rapid expansion of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) applications in urban logistics, inspection, and emergency services, structured low-altitude networks are increasingly needed to support strategic airspace management. This paper proposes a computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-inspired method for identifying urban low-altitude traffic corridors (ULATCs). Rather than predicting atmospheric wind or actual UAV traffic dynamics, the method uses PhiFlow to construct synthetic incompressible velocity fields constrained by buildings and restricted areas. Multidirectional streamline tracing and geometric post-processing are then used to extract smooth, obstacle-avoiding candidate corridor centerlines. In a 5×5 km2 case study in Shenzhen, China, the method identified 10 principal corridors with a mean individual length of 4,904 m and a unique network length of 40.271 km. Compared with the Voronoi–A* and Risk-GNG baseline methods, CFD-FHVCT achieved a 500-m coverage ratio of 93.55% with the shortest unique network length and the highest length-weighted straightness. Path-planning results for six origin–destination pairs further showed that the corridor-constrained paths had 42.3% lower total curvature, despite a 14.6% increase in path length, while mean flight time decreased by 2.2%. These results indicate that the identified network can support smoother trajectories with lower maneuvering demand. Overall, CFD-FHVCT provides a feasible computational framework for strategic urban low-altitude corridor identification and network design, with the synthetic velocity field used to guide corridor extraction rather than to predict physical wind or actual UAV traffic dynamics.

1. Introduction With the rapid advancement of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) technology, its applications have expanded substantially across commercial delivery, logistics transportation, and public service sectors [ 1 – 3 ]. For example, by March 2024, China’s food delivery platform Meituan had completed more than 250,000 urban drone delivery orders, reducing average delivery times by over 50%. As demand for low-altitude missions and operations continues to increase, large-scale UAV operations place growing demands on safe operation, reliable communication, and efficient management within limited urban airspace [ 4 – 6 ]. The management of low-altitude airspace remains at an exploratory stage, with existing studies primarily focusing on four categories of airspace structures: free-flight [ 7 , 8 ], hierarchical structures based on heading and altitude [ 8 , 9 ], urban geography-based zoning [ 10 , 11 ], and corridor-based structures [ 12 ], as illustrated in Fig 1 . Among these approaches, corridor-based airspace design establishes a structured urban low-altitude airspace network that provides UAVs with predefined flight routes. This design reduces potential conflicts and management complexity while enabling the concentrated deployment of communication and sensing infrastructure along critical flight paths. By avoiding extensive infrastructure deployment and the associated increase in costs, corridor-based airspace management is widely regarded as a promising solution for low-altitude airspace control [ 13 , 14 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0357628.g001 Fig 1 Author-generated schematic of corridor-based airspace. Building geometry is shown for illustration; no proprietary map or satellite basemap is used. Urban environments are highly complex, with substantial variations in building density, height, and openness. In urban wind studies, CFD has been used to examine how building configurations shape connected low-resistance passages and ventilation corridors [ 15 , 16 ]. This study draws only a mathematical and topological analogy from that work: an obstacle-constrained vector field is used to reveal continuous planar passages and to guide smooth centerline extraction. The simulated field is synthetic and is not intended to reproduce atmospheric wind conditions, UAV demand generation, or real traffic-flow dynamics. Accordingly, the identified streamlines are interpreted as candidate spatial structures for strategic corridor planning rather than as predictions of physical UAV trajectories. Section 3 introduces the proposed corridor-identification and path-evaluation methods. Section 4 presents the flow-field, corridor-network, baseline-comparison, sensitivity, and path-planning results. Section 5 discusses interpretation, applicability, and limitations, and Section 6 summarizes the main conclusions. 2. Related work Structured drone route networks have been deployed in several Chinese cities, including Shenzhen and Hangzhou, to support commercial drone delivery services [ 17 ]. Zhai et al. introduced the Global Three-Dimensional Split-Tessellation Theory (GeoSOT-3D) to discretize continuous airspace into regular three-dimensional grid cells [ 18 ]. Drawing inspiration from terrestrial highway classification systems, they further designed a hierarchical route network composed of four distinct grid scales. Slama et al. proposed a low-risk urban corridor generation approach by performing risk-minimized path planning for large-scale origin–destination (OD) tasks, clustering path points, and extracting high-density, low-risk frequent passage zones [ 19 ]. Stuive and Gzara projected a three-dimensional road network into the air to construct a drone air traffic network and evaluated its performance using a constrained system optimization (CSO)–based traffic assignment model [ 20 ]. However, the resulting aerial network primarily focused on airspace above roadways, thereby failing to fully exploit available airspace resources such as parks and areas above low-rise buildings. He et al. developed a corridor-based route network planning method based on priority decoupling strategies and spatial cost functions, and subsequently proposed a network optimization approach incorporating congestion pricing [ 21 , 22 ]. However, this approach relies heavily on predefined OD pair topologies, which may necessitate global network replanning as the number of urban OD points increases. Research integrating CFD with transportation has been extensively explored [ 23 , 24 ]. Early pioneering work by Lighthill and Whitham [ 25 ] drew an analogy between traffic flow and fluid flow. Li applied the conservation laws of fluid dynamics to traffic flow modeling and optimization [ 26 ]. Dong et al. further analyzed the similarities between traffic flow and fluid flow [ 27 ]. They constructed a two-dimensional fluid domain representing a traffic roundabout and employed the Fluent simulation platform to analyze flow smoothness, thereby identifying the underlying causes of roundabout traffic congestion. Based on these analyses, they optimized the traffic roundabout by adjusting vehicle entry angles and regulating flow velocity. In urban wind environment research, CFD is widely employed for wind field simulation and the identification of urban ventilation corridors. Liu et al. applied CFD to simulate the urban ventilation environment of Shanghai, China, and identified 14 primary ventilation corridors using the Lowest Cost Path (LCP) method [ 16 ]. Their results demonstrated that urban building coverage and floor area ratio significantly affect wind fields, leading to variations in wind speed and direction. Lau et al. combined CFD with urban roughness modeling to simulate the urban ventilation environment in Taiwan City [ 15 ]. They further employed the A* search algorithm to identify urban ventilation corridors. Existing studies have investigated the application of CFD to low-altitude airspace corridor design. By solving Laplace partial differential equations to derive streamlines, these studies generate flight corridors that bypass buildings, enabling drones to naturally avoid urban obstacles [ 12 , 28 ]. However, these methods primarily emphasize the geometric feasibility of streamlines, with limited assessment of the linear smoothness of the resulting corridors. Moreover, naturally occurring streamlines within flow fields often exhibit substantial interlacing, which increases corridor structural complexity and complicates low-altitude airspace traffic management. To address these limitations, this paper integrates a CFD-inspired synthetic-field formulation with urban drone corridor-network planning. The proposed method uses the resulting obstacle-constrained field to identify continuous candidate passages with comparatively simple geometry under the adopted two-dimensional representation. It therefore provides a technical framework for strategic candidate-network extraction, rather than for automatic operational deployment. 3. Methodology 3.1. Theoretical framework and model formulation This study uses CFD as a spatial structural analysis tool to identify potential ULATCs. The governing equations generate a smooth synthetic vector field whose geometry is constrained by the planar obstacle topology. Streamlines extracted from this field provide candidate obstacle-avoiding centerlines for a structured aerial network. The continuum representation is therefore a computational device for passage extraction; it does not assert that UAV traffic behaves physically as an incompressible fluid. Given the computational overhead associated with three-dimensional simulation in high-density urban domains, this research adopts a two-dimensional formulation to screen the dominant planar connectivity of a candidate corridor network over a 5 × 5 km 2 study area. This representation preserves connectivity only under the adopted rasterized obstacle mask; it does not establish equivalence with the full three-dimensional airspace. The output should therefore be treated as a strategic-level candidate backbone that requires subsequent three-dimensional obstacle and operational safety assessment before implementation. 3.2. Urban environment and airspace modeling To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed methodology, the Futian District in Shenzhen, China, was selected as the empirical study site. As a global pioneer in the low-altitude economy, Shenzhen hosts intensive drone operations from major logistics providers such as Meituan and Fengyi. However, the current fragmented route planning paradigm necessitates a unified corridor network to serve as a standardized airspace base map and mitigate coordination complexities. In alignment with the “National Airspace Basic Classification Method” (2023) by the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) [ 29 ], this study focuses on Class W airspace (below 120 m AGL), which constitutes the primary operational domain for low-altitude logistics UAVs. The study area encompasses a 5 × 5 km 2 zone within the central urban district, incorporating eight designated no-fly zones above major parks and government facilities, as illustrated in Fig 2 . Despite the high density of commercial structures, approximately 41% of buildings in this region are lower than 30 m. Based on China’s logistics route specifications, which stipulate operational altitudes between 40 m and 120 m, the strategic screening considers corridors in the 90–120 m layer above zones where mapped buildings are lower than 30 m. Structures exceeding this threshold are treated as rigid obstacles in the planar mask. Under these static building-height assumptions, the 30 m threshold provides a nominal vertical separation of at least 60 m from the lower boundary of the selected layer; it is not an operational safety guarantee. 10.1371/journal.pone.0357628.g002 Fig 2 Study area and modeled constraints. China’s “Specifications for Designating Urban Scenario Light and Small Unmanned Aerial System Logistics Routes” stipulate that designated flight routes should be above 40 m in altitude, with a maximum height not exceeding 120 m. The establishment of ULATCs is typically planned within an altitude range of 90–120 m. Accordingly, this study models buildings higher than 30 m and designated prohibited airspace as obstacles. The available input does not comprehensively represent thin or temporary vertical objects such as cranes, tower cranes, antennas, masts, power lines, and construction equipment. These objects must be incorporated through higher-resolution three-dimensional surveys and operational constraint layers before any candidate corridor is used in practice. This study primarily uses the 3D-GloBFP dataset to obtain urban building outlines and height data [ 30 ]. The building-height estimation model in this dataset achieves an R 2 ranging from 0.66 to 0.96, with a root mean square error (RMSE) between 1.9 and 14.6 m. These data are used for strategic-level screening, but their uncertainty and spatial resolution do not replace detailed obstacle surveys or site-specific safety assessment. Buildings near the 30 m classification threshold and locally tall or narrow objects require particular verification. For environmental modeling, a 50 m × 50 m two-dimensional grid is used for discretization. Navigable and non-navigable regions are generated through obstacle mapping. The X and Y axes are expressed in UTM Zone 50N coordinates (meters), maintaining consistency with the GIS inputs and subsequent spatial calculations. Cells containing mapped buildings higher than 30 m are designated as non-navigable obstacles. Urban environment processing and modeling. Based on the urban binary obstacle mask constructed in Section 3.2 , the mask is converted into a geometry representation compatible with PhiFlow. A flood-fill algorithm is employed to detect the presence of enclosed regions within the computational domain [ 32 ]. If enclosed regions are identified, they are filled to prevent numerical instability during pressure field computation. 3.3.2. Multi-directional steady-state fluid injection. To comprehensively identify the urban traffic corridor network, steady-state flow is injected from the east, west, south, and north boundaries of the simulation domain. These four simulations are conducted independently to capture traffic corridors under different dominant flow directions. 3.3.3. Computational fluid dynamics numerical solution. The core of the simulation is based on PhiFlow’s numerical solution of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, as given in Eq (1) : ∂ 𝐯 ∂ t + ( 𝐯 · ∇ ) 𝐯 = − 1 ρ ∇ p + ν ∇ 2 𝐯 + 𝐟 (1) Where v denotes the velocity field, ρ is the fluid density, p is the pressure, ν is the kinematic viscosity, and f denotes the external body force. Advection field update: In fluid simulation, advection describes the process by which physical quantities, such as density and velocity, are transported and updated by the fluid motion. The governing equation for this process is given in Eq (2) . ∂ ϕ ∂ t + 𝐮 · ∇ ϕ = 0 (2) Where ϕ represents the physical quantity being advected (primarily fluid velocity and density in this paper), t is time, 𝐮 is the velocity vec t or field of the fluid, and ∇ ϕ is the gradient of the physical quantity ϕ . The term 𝐮 · ∇ ϕ represents the convective component, describing the transport of ϕ along the direction of the velocity field 𝐮 . In this study, the MacCormack algorithm is employed to compute the advected fluid density field [ 33 ]. The MacCormack method is a second-order accurate predictor–corrector finite-difference scheme. This method effectively reduces numerical dissipation and preserves sharp density field boundaries by alternately applying forward and backward spatial differencing. Compared with more complex high-order schemes, such as ENO and WENO, the MacCormack scheme provides a favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. The semi-Lagrangian algorithm is employed to compute the advected fluid velocity field [ 34 ]. As a streamline-based advection scheme, the semi-Lagrangian method performs interpolation and reconstruction of physical quantities by tracing streamlines backward along the velocity field. Owing to its unconditional stability, this method maintains numerical stability even with large time steps, without a significant increase in computational cost. Compared with the CFL constraints of traditional Eulerian methods, the semi-Lagrangian approach provides a stable numerical representation of the obstacle-constrained synthetic velocity patterns used in this study. Addition of directional driving force: A directional driving force is applied according to the density field to orient the synthetic flow toward a prescribed target boundary. Regions with higher density receive larger velocity increments. This numerical construction promotes continuous directional passages in the obstacle-constrained field; it does not model UAV acceleration, pilot behavior, or traffic demand. Incompressible projection: At each time step, a predicted velocity field is first computed through advection and the application of directional forces. The resulting velocity field may not satisfy the incompressibility constraint, that is, its divergence may be non-zero. To enforce incompressibility, a corrective pressure is obtained by solving a pressure Poisson equation, and the resulting pressure gradient is used to update the velocity field. This numerical operation produces a smooth divergence-free field around the modeled obstacles; it is used as a geometric passage indicator and does not emulate an onboard UAV obstacle-avoidance controller. After the prescribed time-step iterations, the resulting field reveals regions with relatively high synthetic velocity magnitude and consistent direction under each imposed boundary condition. The four directional fields are stored for subsequent streamline tracing. Their magnitudes are interpreted only on a relative, within-simulation basis and are not calibrated atmospheric wind speeds. 3.4. Corridor identification Traditional path-finding algorithms typically rely on predefined OD pairs; however, such pairs may not be available during strategic corridor-network screening. The proposed velocity-field-based method instead traces relatively high-magnitude, directionally consistent regions in the synthetic fields described in Section 3.3 . It therefore identifies candidate passages without requiring predefined OD pairs. Streamline point update: In the streamline tracing process, the fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) method is employed for kinematic integration to accurately simulate particle trajectories [ 35 ]. A streamline is defined as a trajectory that is everywhere tangent to the local velocity vector. The RK4 scheme enhances integration accuracy by evaluating the velocity field at multiple intermediate points, enabling streamline points to be updated more precisely along the local flow direction. Corridor extraction and optimization. The streamline tracing algorithm produces a large number of streamlines, many of which are redundant due to insufficient length or low straightness. This study adopts a corridor-based segmented path-planning framework that uses a pre-identified corridor network to generate obstacle-screened routes from origin to destination. The A* algorithm is employed for path planning. The core principle of the A* algorithm lies in evaluating the total cost of each node and iteratively expanding the node with the lowest estimated cost to identify the least-cost path. The cost function of the A* algorithm is defined in Eq (9) . f ( n ) = g ( n ) + h ( n ) (9) where f ( n ) is the total estimated cost from the start node to the end node, passing through node n , g ( n ) is the actual movement cost from the start node to the current node n , and h ( n ) is the estimated heuristic cost from the current node n to the end node. Each flight mission is divided into the following three stages: Access stage (origin-to-corridor): The UAV departs from its origin and plans the shortest feasible path to connect to the nearest accessible low-altitude corridor node. Obstacle avoidance with respect to surrounding buildings is explicitly considered at this stage. Traversal stage (corridor network): After entering the corridor network, the UAV travels along predefined corridors until reaching the corridor node closest to the target destination. Path planning at this stage relies on the pre-constructed corridor network map. Egress stage (corridor-to-destination): The UAV exits the corridor network at the selected exit node and plans the shortest path to the final destination. Similar to the first stage, obstacle avoidance with respect to buildings is taken into account. This study considers UAV flight in a two-dimensional space, simulating a scenario in which the UAV ascends vertically from the origin to a designated flight altitude, cruises at this altitude to a position above the destination, and then descends vertically. The corridor-based segmented path-planning method developed under this framework is compared with the free-flight mode using path length, average flight velocity, path curvature, and total flight time to quantify differences in route geometry and kinematic performance. The UAV velocity update accounts for the platform’s maximum velocity and acceleration constraints at each time step; when negotiating curved segments, the UAV is additionally subject to a turn-related speed constraint. These modeled constraints do not constitute a collision-risk or operational-safety assessment. 4. Results 4.1. CFD simulation results Fig 8 presents the synthetic velocity fields for the Futian case. Discussion The spatial distribution of the extracted corridors indicates that the CFD-inspired formulation tends to preserve continuous open urban spaces, including arterial-road spaces, parks, and areas with relatively low building density. This correspondence is consistent with the intended role of the synthetic velocity field, which is used to identify planar continuity around obstacles rather than to represent atmospheric wind or urban UAV traffic. The comparison with Voronoi–A* and Risk-GNG also highlights different network-design priorities. Voronoi–A* may be more suitable when spatial coverage is the primary objective, whereas Risk-GNG favors the avoidance of higher-exposure areas. CFD-FHVCT, in comparison, yields a more compact network while maintaining relatively smooth trajectories. These results highlight the different priorities of the three methods in terms of spatial coverage, network compactness, and geometric directness in the tested area. The path-planning results indicate that the main benefit of the identified network lies in reducing route curvature and maneuvering demand, while no consistent advantage in route length or flight time was observed across the six OD pairs. The reported standard deviations summarize variation among the six OD pairs rather than uncertainty from repeated experiments. The sensitivity analysis showed that grid resolution had the strongest influence on the extracted network, as coarser obstacle discretization could eliminate narrow passages. Other tested parameters mainly affected local branches and the spatial extent of the network, while the locations and overall configuration of the main corridors remained largely unchanged. The present study evaluates a two-dimensional screening framework in a single 5 × 5 km 2 urban case. The resulting evidence is therefore case-specific, and broader applicability should be examined in larger and geographically diverse urban areas. The current formulation focuses on static planar obstacle topology, while operational applications would also require high-resolution three-dimensional representation of thin or temporary obstacles, together with weather, dynamic airspace restrictions, corridor capacity, traffic demand, and multi-UAV conflict management. These factors provide natural directions for extending the framework toward three-dimensional, time-dependent, and capacity-aware operational planning. Accordingly, the identified corridors are best regarded as a strategic spatial backbone for subsequent operational assessment and should be verified against site-specific three-dimensional environmental information and current airspace constraints before practical deployment. 6. Conclusion This study proposed a CFD-inspired and origin–destination-independent method for extracting candidate urban low-altitude corridor centerlines from synthetic incompressible velocity fields constrained by a two-dimensional obstacle topology. In the Futian case study, the method identified 10 principal corridors with a mean individual length of 4,904 m, a mean straightness of 0.913, and a unique network length of 40.271 km, with the resulting network mainly distributed along continuous open urban spaces. Compared with the Voronoi–A* and Risk-GNG baseline methods, CFD-FHVCT produced the shortest unique network and the highest length-weighted straightness, with a 500-m coverage ratio of 93.55%. Across the six OD pairs, the main path-planning benefit was reduced trajectory curvature and maneuvering demand rather than consistent reductions in route length or flight time. Overall, the results demonstrate the feasibility of CFD-FHVCT as a computational framework for strategic urban low-altitude corridor identification and candidate-network design. The identified network provides a spatial backbone for subsequent operational assessment, and future work can extend the framework toward three-dimensional and operationally constrained urban airspace applications.
