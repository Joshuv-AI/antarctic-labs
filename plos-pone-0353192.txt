Uncertainty-aware bilevel energy storage planning for active distribution networks: A fixed-topology DistFlow-based MISOCP framework with voltage-security assessment

Zhenhua Miao, Ziyan Li, Yongjie Wang

ABSTRACT
Historical renewable-load variability, voltage-security risk, and investment-operation coupling can materially change the assessed value of distribution-level energy storage. This study develops an uncertainty-aware bilevel energy storage system (ESS) planning framework in which the upper level selects a discrete siting-sizing-duration plan and the lower level solves a fixed-topology, multi-scenario mixed-integer second-order cone program (MISOCP) in which the network physics are represented by a DistFlow-SOCP formulation. An ESS-aware topology robustness check is further introduced to examine whether ESS-induced topology changes affect the planning conclusions obtained under the common-topology comparison. Complete daily load, photovoltaic, and wind observations from Denmark in 2016 and 2017 are represented as joint 72-dimensional trajectories. Input-risk-stratified historical-day reduction is examined for K = 3, 5, 10, and 20, with K = 10 used for the principal 33-bus study and K = 20 used as a higher-fidelity scenario-set check. Exhaustive evaluation of 211 alternatives identifies a 1.00 MW/ 2.00 MWh ESS at bus 24. Under K10 and the baseline fixed topology, the proposed design reduces daily TOTEX from 73,151.02 to 51,826.24 CNY/day (29.2%) relative to No ESS, raises the minimum voltage from 0.94138 to 0.95405 p.u., and reduces cumulative undervoltage exposure from 0.013528 to 0.001740 p.u.·h. Cross-year and seven-day chronological tests retain the economic and security pattern. On the released 240-bus feeder, the reference design improves voltage exposure, loading, and losses at a small incremental economic cost. These results show that historical tail conditions, common-topology attribution, and physical security metrics are important for auditable ESS planning.

1. Introduction High penetrations of photovoltaic (PV) generation, wind power, flexible demand, and converter-interfaced resources are changing the operating conditions of distribution feeders. Variability in demand and renewable availability can create sustained low-voltage states, reverse power flow, and costly corrective dispatch. Energy storage systems (ESSs) can shift energy, buffer renewable fluctuations, and support voltage, but their value depends on how uncertainty, network physics, and investment cost are represented [ 1 – 6 ]. Prior work has developed stochastic, robust, and bilevel formulations for storage planning and active distribution network operation [ 7 – 15 ]. Network-constrained methods based on branch-flow or second-order-cone models improve physical fidelity while retaining computational tractability [ 16 – 18 ]. However, three issues remain important for a transparent planning comparison. First, generated or average-day inputs can suppress the historical tail conditions that carry the voltage-security signal. Second, allowing topology to vary among storage strategies creates a moving baseline. Third, monetary penalties alone do not establish physical security improvement. This study addresses these issues through a historical-data-driven, fixed-topology bilevel framework. The upper level evaluates a complete discrete ESS siting-sizing-duration domain, whereas the lower level solves a fixed-topology multi-scenario MISOCP with DistFlow-SOCP network physics on one common radial topology. Daily-equivalent total expenditure (TOTEX) combines operating and annualized investment costs. Voltage performance is reported independently through the minimum voltage, undervoltage hours (UVH), maximum depth, cumulative undervoltage exposure (UVE), and mean voltage margin. The main contributions are: (i) joint 72-dimensional historical load-PV-wind scenarios with input-risk stratification and exact worst-day-retention audits and K = 3, 5, 10, and 20 fidelity checks; (ii) exhaustive evaluation of the 211-alternative 33-bus design domain, with PSO, GA, and DE used only as search benchmarks; (iii) deterministic, cross-year, and seven-day chronological robustness checks under a common topology; and (iv) engineering-scale case study on a released 240-bus feeder, including location comparisons and a separate severe-stress experiment. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The Materials and methods section presents the systems, historical scenarios, fixed-topology lower-level model, and discrete upper-level design domain. The Results section reports the scenario, ablation, planning, robustness, 240-bus, and sensitivity results. The Discussion and Conclusions sections discuss the implications, limitations, and conclusions. Materials and methods 2.1. System description and framework overview The framework is demonstrated on a modified IEEE 33-bus active distribution network and evaluated on a released 240-bus feeder. Both systems use a common fixed-topology protocol: a reference radial topology is identified or reconstructed once and is then held unchanged across the compared ESS strategies. Fig 1 summarizes the historical-data-driven planning workflow. 10.1371/journal.pone.0353192.g001 Fig 1 Overview of the uncertainty-aware bilevel ESS planning framework with fixed-topology DistFlow-SOCP network physics and MISOCP operational evaluation. The workflow proceeds in four stages. First, uncertainty-aware scenario generation constructs representative operating conditions over the 24 h horizon. Second, a baseline no-ESS reconfiguration stage identifies the fixed reference topology. Third, the fixed-topology lower-level MISOCP evaluates multi-scenario feeder operation, combining DistFlow-SOCP network physics with exact PWL DoD degradation and other device constraints. Finally, the upper-level optimizer determines ESS siting and sizing by minimizing TOTEX through the lower-level operating consequences. This sequence links uncertainty representation, fixed-topology operation, and bilevel ESS planning without changing the comparison topology. 2.1.1. Test system, baseline feeder, and ADN construction. A one-time no-ESS topology-identification step is first performed over the 24 h horizon, and the resulting radial topology is used as the fixed reference topology for all subsequent ablation and ESS planning cases. Lower-level operational model Given the common feeder configuration, deterministic baseline inputs, and the fixed reference topology Z f i x established in the System description and framework overview subsection , the lower-level model determines the multi-period dispatch of the ADN under a reduced set of representative uncertainty scenarios [ 21 , 22 ]. The model is formulated over a 24 h horizon and explicitly captures the coupling among network physics, heterogeneous controllable resources, and scenario-dependent renewable/load realizations. The feeder network physics are represented by a fixed-topology DistFlow-SOCP formulation, while the network-state and standard device-dispatch variables are continuous. The exact PWL DoD degradation representation introduces auxiliary binary segment-selection variables; consequently, the complete lower-level operational problem is a mixed-integer second-order cone program (MISOCP). These auxiliary binary variables arise from the degradation model rather than feeder reconfiguration, and the feeder topology remains fixed throughout all ESS candidate evaluations. Let t ∈ T = { 1 , … , T } denote the hourly scheduling interval with T = 2 4 , k ∈ K = { 1 , … , K } denote representative scenarios with probability π k satisfying ∑ k ∈ K π k = 1 , i ∈ N denote buses, and ( i , j ) ∈ L denote branches. On this basis, the lower-level problem minimizes the expected operating expenditure while enforcing uncertainty-aware operational constraints on the common fixed feeder structure. 2.2.1. Objective function and cost/emission/risk accounting. This subsection formulates the scenario-based operating objective of the active distribution network (ADN) over a 24-h horizon. Under renewable-generation and demand uncertainties, the lower-level dispatch is designed to minimize the probability-weighted expected operating expenditure (OPEX), while explicitly monetizing carbon emissions and embedding security-oriented soft constraints to preserve mathematical feasibility under stressed realizations [ 22 – 25 ]. For each representative scenario k ∈ K with probability π k , where ∑ k ∈ K π k = 1 , the dispatch yields a scenario-wise operating cost J k . The lower-level objective is therefore expressed as m i n OPEX = ∑ k ∈ K π k J k (1) J k = C k grid + C k mt + C k deg + C k co 2 + C k volt + C k shed (2) In Equation (2) , C k grid , C k mt , and C k deg denote the upstream electricity procurement cost, microturbine (MT) operating cost, and ESS degradation cost, respectively [ 26 , 27 ]; C k co 2 is the monetized carbon-emission cost; and C k volt and C k shed are penalty terms associated with voltage-limit violations and involuntary load shedding [ 17 , 28 ]. All monetary terms are measured in Chinese Yuan (CNY). Power variables are expressed in MW, and with Δ t = 1 h, all energy-related quantities are consistently evaluated in MWh. This unified monetary formulation is intentionally adopted so that routine operating costs, environmental externalities, and security-driven feasibility costs can be assessed within a common economic framework and subsequently interfaced with the upper-level TOTEX-based planning problem [ 18 , 23 , 25 ]. Economic operating costs are first quantified through electricity procurement under a time-of-use (TOU) tariff. This term provides the main market signal for peak shaving and valley filling, allowing operational flexibility to be valued economically [ 23 , 29 ]. The OPEX defined in the Objective function and cost/emission/risk accounting subsection is minimized subject to a set of constraints that jointly enforce feeder physics, device-level feasibility, inter-temporal energy coupling, and security-oriented operating limits [ 30 , 31 ]. In the proposed framework, these constraints are organized according to the physical logic of ADN operation rather than by device type alone. Specifically, the feasible set is constructed in five layers: topology treatment, network power-flow equations, unified nodal injections and device output limits, flexible-demand and ESS dynamics, and finally security-preserving soft constraints. Such an organization is adopted to make explicit how uncertainty scenarios are translated into physically admissible dispatch decisions on the common fixed feeder structure Z f i x . As established in the System description and framework overview subsection , feeder reconfiguration is performed only once in the baseline no-ESS stage to identify the reference topology, after which all subsequent multi-scenario dispatch evaluations are carried out under fixed topology. Accordingly, the topology-related constraints below should be interpreted as a one-time identification step followed by fixed-topology operation. For this one-time identification step, a standard single-commodity-flow (SCF) formulation is adopted [ 16 , 30 , 32 ], with binary branch-status variables z i j ∈ { 0 , 1 } and fictitious flow variables f i j . Bilevel ESS planning model and solution methodology In this study, bilevel denotes a nested planning-operation structure rather than a general-purpose bilevel optimization algorithm. The upper layer selects discrete ESS planning alternatives, while the lower layer evaluates the corresponding operational responses under the prescribed network constraints. For the reported 33-bus case, the finite planning domain is solved through exhaustive enumeration of candidate ESS configurations, and the optimal solution is identified according to the defined numerical criteria. Under the fixed reference topology Z f i x established in the System description and framework overview subsection , the upper-level planning problem optimizes ESS siting and sizing decisions for the ADN. Discussion 4.1. Main methodological contribution and evidence boundary The methodological contribution is not a new standalone optimizer or a new conic relaxation. It is an auditable attribution protocol that combines historical joint uncertainty, input-risk stratification with exact worst-day-retention auditing, fixed-topology attribution, discrete ESS siting-sizing-duration decisions, a fixed-topology MISOCP evaluator with DistFlow-SOCP network physics, daily-equivalent TOTEX, and penalty-independent voltage-security metrics. This structure separates storage effects from changes in scenario representation or feeder topology. The complete 33-bus domain supports the planning result within the declared discrete design space, whereas the released 240-bus feeder supports executability and a cost-security trade-off at the tested engineering scale. The evidence does not establish a universal optimizer advantage, a universal storage size, field performance, or scalability to arbitrary network sizes. 4.2. How uncertainty changes the assessed ESS value Historical uncertainty changes storage value because stressed load-renewable combinations contribute disproportionately to voltage exposure, load-shedding backstops, and security-related costs. The deterministic and K10 results therefore differ in more than average procurement cost. K20 selects the same bus, power, energy, and duration and retains the same minimum-voltage floor, but its longer violation duration and larger cumulative exposure show that a higher-fidelity representation can be more conservative without changing the selected plan. Cross-year and seven-day tests further separate this conclusion from one representative-day calculation. 4.3. Voltage-security interpretation beyond monetary penalties Voltage-security claims are based on the joint interpretation of V min , UVH, D max , cumulative UVE, and MVM, not on monetary penalties alone. V min identifies the voltage floor; UVH and UVE describe violation duration and accumulated exposure; D max identifies the deepest excursion; and MVM summarizes the remaining margin. Voltage and shedding penalties keep rare stressed cases solvable but do not substitute for these physical indicators. The results do not imply that ESS replaces an on-load tap changer, SVC, capacitor bank, or inverter-based reactive-power control. Those alternatives were not subjected to a common investment comparison. The narrower conclusion is that, within the stated ADN resource set and fixed topology, the retained ESS improves the reported economy-security trade-off. 4.4. Fixed topology, ESS duration, topology sensitivity, and temporal scope A fixed topology is defensible when switching states are prescribed by protection, maintenance, regulatory, or operating policy, or when the scientific objective is attribution to storage on a common network. If topology is an active decision, joint reconfiguration and ESS planning could change the preferred location, economic value, and security improvement. The present study reports a focused topology-sensitivity check; joint switching-storage planning remains outside its scope. The 33-bus design domain uses discrete 2, 3, and 4 h duration classes, while the 240-bus screen uses bounded 2 h and 4 h families. Relaxing this grid could widen the CAPEX-OPEX frontier: longer durations may favor multi-hour energy shifting, whereas shorter durations may remain attractive for local voltage or overload support. A continuous-duration optimum was not solved. The principal planning model is a 24 h representative-day model with a cyclic terminal-SOC condition. Annual Year A and Year B evaluations test repeated daily economics and seasonal variation, and the seven-day test adds interday SOC coupling. These checks do not constitute full-year chronological co-optimization or seasonal energy-storage planning. 4.5. Scalability, generalizability, limitations, and future work For the fixed-topology MISOCP evaluator with DistFlow-SOCP network physics, network-state variables and constraints grow approximately with branches × time periods × scenarios before presolve, with additional binary-variable growth from the exact PWL degradation segments. The tested 240-bus instance contained 139,000 rows, 259,848 columns, 693,648 nonzeros, and 83,136 second-order-cone/quadratic constraints. Reported per-case K8 multi-scenario evaluation runtimes were 14.04–31.10 s across the listed 240-bus cases; the maximum SOCP-to-AC voltage mismatch was 2.13 × 10 −8 p.u., and the maximum physical residual was 9.99 × 10 −7 . Accordingly, computational burden can increase with the number of scenarios, ESS candidates, and degradation segments, although the feeder topology remains fixed across candidate evaluations. These figures demonstrate tested-scale executability, not a proof for networks with thousands of buses or arbitrary feeder types. The evidence is further bounded by balanced positive-sequence models, one national historical data package, finite candidate sets, and daily-equivalent economics. The DoD-dependent PWL model improves cycling-cost accounting but does not represent temperature, rate effects, or detailed electrochemistry. Future work should examine unbalanced multiphase feeders, feeder-specific forecasts, longer chronological horizons, continuous or richer duration choices, detailed degradation states, and joint topology-storage planning. Each extension should retain the same audit principle: compare plans on explicitly controlled network, uncertainty, and economic definitions. 5. Conclusions This study developed a historical-data-driven bilevel ESS planning framework with complete discrete upper-level evaluation and a fixed-topology DistFlow-based MISOCP operational model. Joint Year A load-PV-wind trajectories were reduced with input-risk-stratified historical-day selection, K10 was used for the principal 33-bus assessment, and K20 also selected the selected bus 24, 1.00 MW/2.00 MWh, 2 h design. Relative to No ESS, the proposed 33-bus design reduced K10 TOTEX from 73,151.02 to 51,826.24 CNY/day, raised the minimum voltage from 0.94138 to 0.95405 p.u., and reduced UVE from 0.013528 to 0.001740 p.u.·h. Cross-year and seven-day tests preserved the principal economy-security pattern. The released 240-bus case study improved physical security and operating metrics at a small incremental economic cost. These findings support common-topology attribution, historical risk coverage and exact worst-day-retention auditing, and penalty-independent voltage metrics as practical elements of auditable ESS planning. Within the fixed-topology and finite-scenario setting considered in this study, the numerical results remain case-specific. They do not establish a universal storage size or location or economic savings for all feeders. Their main implication is methodological: ESS plans should be compared on a common network, evaluated across credible historical conditions, and judged through both total expenditure and physical security outcomes.
