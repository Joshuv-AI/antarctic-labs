EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents guidance to support the implementation of security objectives during the
design process for nuclear facilities using an organization’s quality management system. The
guidance in this document is intended for design vendors and operators of nuclear power facilities.
Additionally, this guidance document can be beneficial to regulatory bodies, industry partners,
customers, and other stakeholders within the nuclear power market. This report aims to ensure
security consequences are identified before designs are completed, which may lead to reduced
costs and higher security effectiveness and efficiency.
This report was created to address gaps in implementing security-by-design (SeBD) into industry
organization’s design processes to reduce operational costs and increase security efficiency
(Appendix D discusses this potential efficiency in more detail). Additionally, regulatory
requirements to include security in the design process may exist. However, holistic process
guidance does not currently exist for defining, implementing, and auditing SeBD methods. This
guidance document provides a foundational solution to that gap in the methodology.
This guidance document includes the following topics, and the layout of the document is depicted
in Figure 1:
1. A discussion on the implementation of SeBD within an organization’s quality management
 system (Section 2).
2. The methodology by which security objectives were assessed against design documentation

CHAPTER 1
1. INTRODUCTION
This report builds a framework for including security objectives into the design process for nuclear
facilities, which is frequently referred to as security-by-design (SeBD). This report proposes the
following definition of SeBD, which is slightly expanded from Nelson et al. (2024):

 SeBD is an integrated process by which the physical protection and cyber
 security of a nuclear facility is intentionally integrated into the full life cycle of
 the facility, including planning, design, construction, operation, and
 decommissioning through a combination of analytical, physical, technological,
 and procedural measures. SeBD is the intentional application and integration of
 security objectives with all other aspects of design and operation throughout a
 facility design life cycle to improve the overall site security posture while
 reducing the need for costly retrofits or added security resources post-design.

Incorporating an SeBD methodology within the iterative phases of design enables facility features
to be optimized to reduce security risks while still meeting the functional design requirements.
This definition guides security to a formal process that is integrated with other design activities and
placed into a similar importance as safety system design.
The framework described in this report provides the tools for a design vendor, licensee, competent
authority, regulatory body, or other stakeholder to implement, turnover, assess/inspect, and
compare SeBD practices across internal and external organizations.

1.1. OBJECTIVES
The SeBD framework was built to provide a process for organizations engaging in the design and
protection of nuclear facilities to holistically include security objectives into formal design
processes.
The primary objective of this report was to build an example policy and procedure that defines a
means to incorporate SeBD into the design and design change processes of organizations
responsible for creating or operating nuclear facilities. This policy and procedure provide each
organization with a means to quickly implement SeBD within their management systems. The
result of this primary objective is provided in Attachment 1.
The secondary objective includes a cross-reference of typical licensing application guidance
against security objectives, both cyber and physical. The cross-reference assessment documents
which security objectives are impacted by each design document. While not all inclusive, and not
specific to any individual facility, this assessment is a useful foundation for an organization to
begin SeBD. It is expected that the cross-reference assessment could expand and contract within

 12

an organization as lessons learned and experience elucidate the interaction between design
documents and security objectives for a facility. The result of this secondary objective is provided
in the “security objective impact checklist” in Attachment 1.
The tertiary objective of this report was to determine tools developed or maintained by the Office of
International Nuclear Security (INS) that can aid in analysis of, design for, or implementation of the
interfaces identified in the cross-reference assessment. This assessment provides the user with a
quick reference to understand both the tools available and their application. The result of this
tertiary objective is provided in Appendix G.
The output of this report also provides each organization with a means to prove to regulators and
stakeholders how SeBD was incorporated into the design of the facility. Regulatory bodies can find
an increase in efficiency of inspections if documentation and processes are well defined within the
organization being inspected. Having a policy and procedure within the quality management
system of the organization provides an excellent and well-understood foundation for a regulatory
body to determine the efficacy of the SeBD program. This report considers the consistency
provided by a formalized process to be regulatory predictability. This benefit continues with other
stakeholders, including investors and customers. If the design organization can show the policy
and procedures in use and their associated documentation, this may provide stakeholders with
confidence that the design organization is fully committed to and has implemented SeBD. This
regulatory predictability and stakeholder engagement is an additional benefit to the use of the
SeBD framework provided here.

1.2. Security by Design
Nuclear facility operators, design vendors, and other stakeholders are investigating methods, tools
and guidance to right-size their security strategies and systems. In competitive markets, operating
facilities are competing with market demands and lowering their respective operations and
maintenance (O&M) costs. The security strategy can cost around 20% of the O&M budget at a
nuclear facility (Yadav & Burli, 2020).

 The results of this report are intended to be added to the facility’s management
 systems (quality, documentation, design change, etc.) to provide a requirement
 for the designers and authors of design documents to engage security
 organizations, where identified, in the creation and revision of design
 documentation.

Design vendors have identified the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) limits above which sales for
new reactors may diminish. These LCOE targets can be significantly affected by security O&M
costs. Although those O&M costs are typically paid by the licensee or operator, the design vendor’s
objectives may be positively affected by right-sizing the security strategy.
The National Nuclear Security Administration’s (NNSA’s) INS, industry groups, and other
organizations have successfully built many useful tools in performing security analyses, written
papers to describe the benefits of SeBD, and established preliminary methods in the areas of

 13

security in design and safety. However, currently, a comprehensive guideline or framework does
not exist to tie these effective tools to an implementation plan, or better yet a procedural mandate.
The results of this process are intended to be added to the facility’s management systems (quality,
documentation, design change, etc.) to provide a requirement from the designers and authors to
engage security organizations, where identified, in the creation and revision of design
documentation.
Policy and procedure, as well as quality management, is a considerable driver to implementation of
methodologies in nuclear facility design and operation. Anything included within the management
system is committed by the organization and can be inspected by regulatory or quality
organizations. Although minimum requirements exist for management systems, the results of this
process would be a voluntary commitment by the organization to include in the management
system and audits/inspections therein. The organization must then meet the provisions in the
policy and procedure, ensuring security objectives are included as a main design input or
requirement.
It is expected that when security objectives are included in design decisions, security strategy
implementation during operation will be more efficient and cost-effective. The operator or licensee
will have a higher likelihood of right-sizing the strategy to meet economic goals without creating
vulnerabilities in the security system.
SeBD must be intrinsically included in the design process to achieve the greatest benefit. This
report offers a means to include SeBD throughout the design life cycle process.

CHAPTER 2
2. GUIDANCE ON THE USE OF THIS REPORT
This report is meant to provide an organization with a process and applicable tools, which will aid
in the organization’s ability to determine security impact on design decisions. The results of this
process are meant to be used within the organization’s management system, quality management
system, design process, and design change process. This section describes how this report can be
used within an organization and considers both the process and the tools available.

2.1. USING THIS REPORT
Updating or creating policies and procedures can be a significant effort for an organization. This
report aims to provide a straightforward process to implement both a policy and procedures from
that policy for SeBD.
The main proponent of building a SeBD procedure within an organization will be the security
organization. Therefore, this section provides an example set of stakeholders, processes, and
interactions to implement SeBD in the organization’s management system.

2.1.1. Expected Organizational Stakeholders
The first step in implementing a SeBD procedure within an organization is engaging the correct
stakeholders. This list is not all inclusive, but the following functional roles and corresponding
organizational stakeholders will be required. Each stakeholder should be aware of the intent to
incorporate the results of this report into the organization’s quality management system.
Figure 1 highlights the functional roles and responsibilities for key stakeholders within the
organization who are necessary for implementation of the SeBD framework. The following sections
describe the required resources within each organization.

 Organizational Leadership
Organizational leadership can include any resource from second line management up to the
executive level, depending on who is responsible for developing, reviewing, and approving policy
statements and objectives.
These resources are identified as “organizational leadership” in Figure 1. These resources can
commit the organization to SeBD and by doing so create a policy and objectives. In ISO 9001
(International Organization for Standardization, 2015) organizations, those policies and objectives
drive the development of procedures. Therefore, buy-in and commitment from organizational
leadership is critical to obtaining approvals for developing SeBD procedures.

 Design Organization
The design organization includes resources that are developing the design of the applicable facility.
For the purposes of this report, design refers to any documentation being developed for the
preliminary safety analysis report (PSAR), final safety analysis report (FSAR) supporting the
licensing or operation of the facility (to include technical reports, and operational procedures).

 15

These resources are identified as “design document author” and “design document verifier” in
Figure 2. These are the primary users of the procedure, and they will identify new documents or
revisions to documents and review the security impact assessments.

 Security Organization
The security organization includes the stakeholders developing physical or cybersecurity-specific
documentation and are responsible for developing and maintaining security objectives. Although
the security organization is likely a subset of the design organization, these resources should be
identified on a group or personal basis.
These resources are identified as security objective point of contact, security objective point of
contact verifier, and security organization leadership in Figure 2. These resources are experts in
security design, operations, or analysis. The security organization or technical leadership must
also identify the points of contact for each security objective.

The training and qualification organization or responsible parties are those that build or identify

These resources are identified as training and qualification in Figure 1. As the procedure is being

 Policy/Procedure Development
The policy/procedure development resources are those that identify and develop policies and

These resources are identified as “policy/procedure development” in Figure 1. These resources

 Internal Auditors and Inspectors
Internal auditors and inspectors, potentially within the quality organization, are resources that are

These resources could exist in individual working groups or in a stand-alone organization.

2.1.2. Initial Review
Upon receipt of this report, the security organization leadership or security-based technical leaders
should review the procedure, assessments, and results to confirm applicability of the data. The
methodology for creating the results and assessments is documented in Section 3. The security-
based technical leaders should understand the assessment inputs and results and the procedure
steps.
The results are provided in editable format for areas that may need to be updated based on
organization-specific differences or best practices. If the security leadership identifies any areas
that require change, they should make those alterations before confirming the applicability of the
procedure and data.

2.1.3. Approval to Initiate Policy Development
The security leadership should initiate a request to develop a policy and policy objectives for SeBD.
In many quality management systems, including ISO 9001, a policy is an organizational
commitment to meet objectives. A policy statement is generic and does not contain details in
execution but describes the commitment of the organization to the topic.
There likely exists a defined process for creating a policy and objectives, especially if the
organization meets or is expecting to meet ISO 9001.

 18

2.1.4. Approval to Initiate Procedure Development
Similarly, the security leadership should initiate a request to develop a SeBD procedure that meets
the commitments to SeBD from the policy described in Section 2.1.3.
There is likely a defined process for creating a procedure, especially if the organization meets or is
expecting to meet ISO 9001 (International Organization for Standardization, 2015).

2.1.5. Align Assessment Results with Documents or Naming Convention
The assessment results, documented in Attachment 1 and Section 3 are developed in a generic
fashion to align with regulatory documentation (i.e., regulations, guidance). If the organization has
a defined naming convention for documents and revisions, the resources assigned to building the
procedure should align the document categories identified in the assessment results with the
naming convention.
For example, if documents developed for siting have a specific document code or type code, that
code should be aligned with the assessment categories assigned to NUREG-0800, Standard Review
Plan for the Review of Safety Analysis Reports for Nuclear Power Plants: LWR [Light Water Reactor]
Edition (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2007), Chapter 2, Sites Characteristics and Site
Parameters. This builds an efficient protocol for users to quickly identify the applicable security
impacts without referencing NUREG-0800 directly.

2.1.6. Build Policy Statement
The security technical leadership or security organizational leads should work with organizational
leaders to develop the policy statement and the objectives of the organization from the policy
statement. This report has developed an example policy statement and objectives. The policy
template, objectives, and expected wording is provided in Attachment 1 of this report.

2.1.7. Build Procedure
The security technical leadership or security organizational leaders should work with the resources
identified as policy and procedure developers to take the procedure template from the results of
this report and incorporate it into the standard structure for procedures within the organization.
The procedure template, wording, and tools are provided in Attachment 1 of this report.

2.1.8. Amend or Append Training
Training will need to be developed or amended to account for the new policy and procedure. That
training should, at a minimum, have the applicable users (people in the design organization) read
and acknowledge the procedure. The resources assigned to training and qualification should build
the program based on internal processes.
Points of contact for security objectives may benefit from additional training as the determination
of impact between security objectives and design documentation can be more complicated than
simply reading the procedure and acknowledging its existence.

 19

2.1.9. Initiate Procedure
The last step is to initiate, or execute, the policy and procedure. With all required documentation
and tools in place and specific to the organization, the policy and procedure can be implemented
for new documents and revisions to documents.
The policy and procedure should have an agreed to effective date so that audit and inspections are
clear about when SeBD was officially part of the quality management system.

2.2. RESULTS PROVIDED
This report provides tools to make the transition to a proceduralized SeBD program simple and
efficient. These results are described and documented in this section.

2.2.1. Policy Template
ISO 9001 describes a hierarchy of quality management systems. Quality management can start
with a policy, which represents a commitment from organizational leadership to enact management
systems and protocols to meet the stated objectives. A policy is management’s edict to enact the
objectives.
The objectives of the policy are the means with which the policy’s success is graded. The
objectives of a policy are the direct linkage between policy and procedure or work process.
Attachment 1 includes a template for the policy and objectives for SeBD. This attachment can be
used during engagements with leadership and as a policy/procedure resource.

2.2.2. Procedure Template
The policy and objectives drive the development of procedures. Procedures can be complicated to
build and require subject matter expertise to be implemented successfully. This report includes a
procedure template that is designed to meet the objectives of the policy template in a manner
consistent with the best practices and guidance from applicable industry groups.
The procedure template provided by the report provides an outline that can effectively be included
within the organization’s quality management process and clearly provides other stakeholders with
the risks of the project. For proponents of SeBD, this template provides a strong foundation for a
procedure. The content is designed to harmonize with current management system practices.
Attachment 1 of this report includes a template for the procedure for implementing SeBD.
The procedure for SeBD implementation also includes a database that links design documentation
to security objectives. This output includes thousands of assessments and is structured in a matrix
format. Section 3 provides details about the methodology used for the assessment and the
documentation of results. Attachment 1 includes a link to the database/data files (Microsoft
Access and Microsoft Excel).

2.2.3. Security Impact Sheet
In using the SeBD procedure, design stakeholders and security resources will need to interact with
each other and document findings. The security impact sheet provides a template for design
stakeholders to populate to effectively engage with security points of contact. This sheet should be
included in the procedure.

A template for the security impact sheet exists as Appendix B in Attachment 1.

The project team performed an assessment across industry tools and areas in which design

CHAPTER 3
3. ASSESSMENT OF DESIGN DOCUMENTATION AGAINST SECURITY
 OBJECTIVES
Security objectives may not currently be included inherently in reactor safety design (Fissile
Materials Working Group, 2009). There is limited guidance available that includes security within
the design process, especially within the management system. In some cases, security experts
may provide insights during design reviews or based on informal interoffice meetings and
discussions. However, the inclusion of security objectives within the design process is critical to
the success of the reactors physical and cyber protection systems.

 Yet there is still today far less systematic attention to reactor security than to
 reactor safety. —Fissile Materials Working Group (2009)

Compared to safety objectives, security objectives are harder to include in the design phase. In
some cases, critical security objectives can be held in protected or sensitive information, which
only a limited number of resources can view. Additionally, security objectives tend to include
system perturbations that safety designers have not considered. Where deterministic analyses are
built on one or two equipment failures, adversary attacks can destroy as many pieces of equipment
as necessary to reach an undesirable state. Probabilistic analyses tend to rely on random failures,
which are not well-aligned with sabotage events.
Security leadership can have trouble incorporating these requirements and objectives in the same
fashion as safety requirements. Additionally, competent authorities and industry groups are
recommending (and potentially requiring) including SeBD in the design process. However, limited
guidance exists on that practice.

 Design features to enhance safety and security should be considered and
 resolved at an early stage in the regulatory process, preferably prior to receiving
 an application for a combined license. Early in the regulatory process, issues
 are easier to identify and can be more effectively addressed. —US Nuclear
 Regulatory Commission (2005)

 For a new nuclear facility, the site selection and design should take physical
 protection into account as early as possible and also address the interface
 between physical protection, safety and nuclear material accountancy and
 control to avoid any conflicts and to ensure that all three elements support each
 other. —International Atomic Energy Agency (2011)

 22

This project determined the most effective way to include security objectives in the design process
was to incorporate the practice into the quality management system through policy, objectives, and
procedures. Having organizational leadership commitment to SeBD practices ensures that high-
level support exists and will continue throughout the design process.
The formalization also provides an auditable and inspectable process, including outcomes and
decisions, to present to regulators and third-party stakeholders such as investors or future
customers.
This report aims to preliminarily assess the interaction between expected design documentation
and security objectives. In doing so, the authors determined design documentation from a
multitude of sources, security objectives from both domestic and international sources, and
assessed each combination for impact. This section describes the process and results of that
effort and how the results are to be incorporated in the procedure for implementing SeBD.

3.1. DESIGN DOCUMENTATION
SeBD requires an understanding of what design analysis and documentation exist to understand
where security objectives can exert influence or where security objectives can be influenced. The
challenge of defining an inclusive, representative list of design documentation expected is the vast
amount of guidance and regulation on the topic. This is especially true when considering domestic
and international deployments.
The analysis team chose NUREG-0800 as a surrogate or general list of expected design
documentation. NUREG-0800 contains 19 chapters of technical content areas for the regulatory
review of a commercial nuclear power plant and correlates to an FSAR structure. Although there
are differences between NUREG-0800 and other design criteria or application content, the level of
detail that this report required allows for the surrogate nature of NUREG-0800 to remain
applicable. A cross-reference between major application content guidelines and NUREG-0800 is
available in this section.
NUREG-0800 serves as an excellent surrogate for FSAR application content versus application
content guidance from the IAEA, as shown in Table 1.
NUREG-0800 has a multilayered document structure, starting with chapters then moving to
sections, and areas of review. This analysis documented all three layers but determined that only
the chapter and section were required for assessment against security objectives. The
assessments and results are documented to the section level.
The list of design analyses and documentation is included in Appendices A, B and C as well as the
“security objective impact checklist” in Attachment 1. Native files of each data point are provided
and are expected to be adapted to the specific needs of the report’s users. If new regulations or
guidance are developed and released, the user should be able to update documents, cross-
references, and objectives.

3.3. SECURITY OBJECTIVES
In this report, security objectives is a term used for objectives, analysis, documentation, or
processes that are needed for a facility’s security plan development. The term security objectives
generally represents any security requirement that could be assessed against design
documentation. Security objectives are broken into physical security and cybersecurity.
Native files of each data point are provided and are expected to be adapted to the specific needs
of the report’s users. If new regulations or guidance are developed and released, the user should
be able to update documents, cross-references, or objectives.

3.3.1. Physical Security Objectives
The project team built the physical security objectives primarily from regulations, like 10 CFR
73.55, Requirements for Physical Protection of Licensed Activities in Nuclear Power Reactors
against Radiological Sabotage (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2023), IAEA INFCIRC/225,
Nuclear Security Recommendations on Physical Protection of Nuclear Material and Nuclear
Facilities (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2011), and the Design and Evaluation Process
Outline (DEPO) method (Bland & Cipiti, 2022), developed by Sandia National Laboratories. The
DEPO method provides a general layout of the expected analyses that can be expected to develop a
physical security strategy.
The physical security objectives are provided in Appendix A and the security objective impact
checklist in Attachment 1.

 26

3.3.2. Cybersecurity Objectives
The project team built the cybersecurity objective from regulations and industry guidance primarily
10 CFR 73.54, Protection of Digital Computer and Communication Systems and Networks (US
Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2021), RG 5.71 R1, Cyber Security Programs for Nuclear Facilities
(US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2010), NEI 08-09 R7, Cyber Security Plan for Nuclear Power
Reactors (Nuclear Energy Institute, 2024), and National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) 800-82 R3, Guide to Operational Technology Security (National Institute of Standards and
Technology, 2023). These are broken into categories and subcategories.
The cybersecurity objectives are provided in in Appendix B and the “security objective impact
checklist” in Attachment 1.

3.4. ASSESSMENT OF DESIGN DOCUMENTATION AND SECURITY
 OBJECTIVES
The objective of this report was to identify design documentation that could impact security
objectives. The design document identification task found 282 document categories from
NUREG-0800. The project team determined 29 physical security objectives and 39 cybersecurity
objectives. This equates to more than 19,000 individual assessments between document
categories and security objectives.
The assessments were separated by security objective category and reviewed by subject matter
experts within the national laboratory system. Each assessment relied on a single question, “could
the output of this design document directly impact the security objective?” Figure 3 shows the
database form for the assessment.
The intent of the question is to be simple enough to answer within a few seconds but to still tie a
design document to a security objective. Direct impact was defined as an input to the security
objective analysis or document.
Native files of each data point are provided and are expected to be adapted to the specific needs
of the report’s users. If new regulations or guidance are developed and released, the user should
be able to update assessments if needed.

The results of the assessment are documented in the “security objective impact checklist” in
Attachment 1, including the objectives and design document categories. They are too expansive to
accurately represent in a report or tabular format. The security objective impact checklist in
Attachment 1 has a form and report structure for the results to be used within the procedure.
The results of this assessment are a generic starting point for users of this report. The assessment
should be reviewed and altered as seen fit by security leaders.

APPENDIX D. SUMMARY AND REVIEW OF BENEFITS, RISKS, AND
  GAPS IN IMPLEMENTATION OF SECURITY BY DESIGN

D.1. INTRODUCTION
This section summarizes available documentation to build a justification for including SeBD in an
organization’s quality management system. This section can be used to showcase the benefits and
challenges of SeBD to organizational leadership. The objective is to gain commitment from
leadership based on the benefits identified herein. That leadership commitment to SeBD can
manifest as the policy, objectives, and procedure from Attachment 1.
This appendix will discuss SeBD at a high level, with more detail included in the front matter of this
report. The discussion will focus on what SeBD is and why the need for SeBD exists. This appendix
identifies key benefits of SeBD combined from numerous sources and listed in a manner that is
quickly understood. This appendix discusses the current knowledge gap in SeBD research.

D.2. SEBD CONCEPTS AND LIFE CYCLE INTEGRATION

 SeBD is best achieved through a structured approach by which a State’s nuclear
 security objectives are fully integrated throughout the life of the project, starting
 with project planning and scoping, and specifically integrated throughout the
 entire design and construction process of the facility.—Snell et al. (2013)

In the current state, security requirements for systems that are not directly connected to the design
not well defined. A direct tie between system design requirements and security objectives has not
existed. As an example, codes and standards exist for designing mechanical and electrical
systems, for fire separation, for seismic loading, and for operating procedures, among numerous
others. Many of these requirements can be placed via requirement and compliance matrices on
system-level design. Nuclear facilities have been designed this way for decades.
The complexity arises from two parallel sources of design requirements from security. The first is
system-level, functional requirements. For example, the security computer system requires some
amount of power to operate, so there must be a certain number of cables routed in a certain way.
There is a second set of requirements that come from optimization of vulnerabilities around the
site. This is the core of SeBD. These requirements can materialize as separation of critical
equipment, material choice for components, pathways for efficiency of rounds or response, among
many others.
The system-level, functional requirements are straightforward to document. The requirements for
optimization of vulnerabilities are more obscure. This is because almost any design decision could
affect security objectives. The policy, objectives, and procedure included in Attachment 1 build a
solution for developing the requirements for optimization of vulnerabilities at the outset of the
design tasks.

 46

This solution allows the security objectives to be available to designers through their engagement
with security objective points of contact. The solution will likely reduce risk and vulnerability of the
facility, reduce life cycle costs, increase regulatory predictability and efficiency, and increase
strategic value and market position of the reactor design.

D.3. BENEFITS OF SEBD
D.3.1. Risk and Vulnerability Reduction
The IAEA states that “physical protection system is designed, implemented, operated and
maintained in a condition capable of effectively responding to the threat assessment or design
basis threat…” and that “risk can be managed by reducing the potential consequences of malicious
acts by modifying specific contributing factors, for example, the amount and type of nuclear
material and the design of the facility.” (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2011). These
statements show that the IAEA considers SeBD a means to reduce risk and vulnerabilities. This
likely propagates to international stakeholders.
Sandia National Laboratories notes that “Another useful design practice is to consider security
features that are intrinsic to the facility design, rather than considering security after the facility
has entered detailed design during the design–engineering phase” and that SeBD will likely
“achieve a more effective PPS [physical protection system]” (Jaeger, 2013).
Together, these documents demonstrate that integrating security requirements during the design
phase will likely reduce vulnerabilities and improve overall security effectiveness.

D.3.2. Life Cycle Cost Efficiency
Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) found “that a relatively small investment in an SeBD process
can save millions of dollars for the owner/operator over the 40 to 100 years of operation in the
back end of the nuclear fuel cycle—all while creating additional profit options for the vendor”
(Nelson et al., 2024). Sandia National Laboratories found that “security-by-design is highly
recommended to current and future nuclear reactor vendors to avoid costly retrofits, reduce long-
term operational costs, and enable assessment of the effectiveness of advanced security
technologies” (Evans et al. 2023)
Similarly, the US Department of Energy emphasizes that “cyber-informed engineering (CIE) provides
a way to greatly reduce, and in some cases eliminate, cyber risks from the outset and increase
overall efficiency and effectiveness” (US Department of Energy, 2022).
Finally, ORNL found that an analysis that was performed “demonstrates that the greatest benefits
accrue when SeBD is embedded during conceptual and basic design, offering substantial cost
avoidance compared to the escalating expenses and complexities of addressing security during
final design, construction, or operation. Neglecting early SeBD integration predictably leads to
costly retrofits, schedule delays, and technical design debt, undermining project economics, as
evidenced in past large-scale projects” (Nelson et al. 2025).
The introduction of security objectives into the design process from the initiation builds long-term
cost efficiency by eliminating risks inherent in the design.

 47

D.3.3. Regulatory Predictability and Licensing Efficiency
Regulatory bodies and the IAEA are prioritizing SeBD for regulations. The US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC), for example, has stated that “to complete the technology-neutral [regulatory]
framework, the physical protection strategy will have to be integrated with the safety strategy to
ensure a coherent approach to the safety and security of non-light-water and Generation IV
reactors” (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2005).
The NRC has also stated that “during the initial phase of advanced reactor development, the
Commission particularly encourages design innovations that enhance safety, reliability, and
security and that generally depend on technology that is either proven or can be demonstrated by
straightforward technology development program. Finally, the NRC also believes that it will be of
interest of the public as well as the design vendors and the prospective license applicants to
address security issues early in the design stage to achieve a more robust and effective security
posture for future nuclear power reactors” (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2008).
The proposed US NRC Part 53 performance-based rulemaking for the regulation of commercial
nuclear reactors contains a specific requirement for the inclusion of security by design in 10 CFR
53.440(f):
 “Safety and security must be considered together in the design process such that, where
 possible, security issues are effectively resolved through design and engineered security
 features.” (US NRC 2024)
Regulators and competent authorities also expect to see formalized processes to manage design.
NUREG-0800 and other content-of-application guidance routinely reference design control, change
control and configuration management as well as a quality management system. The IAEA, in its
General Safety Requirements Part 2 (IAEA, 2016), refers to a management system to maintain
configuration management and change control.
It is reasonable to conclude that having SeBD in an approved management system via policy and
procedure would lead to simplified regulatory review and more efficient licensing.

D.3.4. Strategic Value and Market Position
It is likely that a facility designed with security objectives placed directly within the design process
will hold higher long-term strategic value and market position, than if security objectives were
considered at the completion of the project or during the construction phase.
With A/SMR focusing heavily on operations and maintenance costs, and security accounting for a
significant amount of Operation and Maintenance (O&M), an A/SMR with highly efficient security
effectiveness will likely find a market advantage.

D.4. GAPS AND RESEARCH NEEDS
The research provided in these sources is based on assessments and has not had a direct
financial-based study as the process and designs are novel and yet to be formally initiated. Future
research will need to be conducted to document areas where SeBD practices definitively achieved
some of the benefits displayed above.

 48

For now, we must understand the limits of available research and use subject matter expertise and
the justifications above to see the value in SeBD practices.

E.5. USE CASE 3: DESIGN REVIEW AND VERIFICATION
E.5.1. Situation and Context
Use Case 3 describes a situation where the design organization implemented the SeBD policy and

system is preparing for a design review with both internal and external stakeholders, including

Although it is not always the case for a potential licensee to sit in on a design review, customers

E.5.2. Participants
The primary participants in this case are the designer or system design lead, design leads of other

E.5.3. Application of this Guidance or Procedure
E.5.3.1. System designer prepares for the design review
Design reviews require an extensive process to develop. The system design leadership must show
how the design of the system meets the requirements placed on it from regulations, industry
guidance, strategic goals, and customer requests.
In many cases, compliance can be shown via a compliance matrix. At the outset of design, it is
common for a system lead to develop requirements matrices that list everything the design must
include or meet. During the design process, the design authority meticulously creates and adjusts
the design to meet these requirements.
The security requirements for nonsecurity systems can be difficult to build into a requirements or
compliance matrix because they are not directly tied to systems throughout the site. The security
impacted documents checklist is the surrogate for a requirements matrix.
In this case, the security impacted documents checklist has been created and was available for the
design lead. The design lead was therefore able to compile the areas from the security impacted
documents checklist in which security requirements were provided. The design lead performing
this accumulation through the archived security objective impact sheets that were archived during
the implementation of the SeBD procedure.
The design lead now has a clear and concise description of each of the security objectives
required, which were included in the design, and how they were included.

too specific to be answered in the design review, the design organization has documented a policy
and procedure in the quality management system, and the proper archival of security objective
impacts demonstrates that SeBD is a primary concern and the organization is committed to it.

E.5.3.3. System designer addresses comments and provides follow-up
The system designer provides answers to any remaining follow-up questions and completes the
documentation for a design review.

E.5.4. Outcomes and Records
The outcome from this use case is a requirements and compliance matrix between the design
documentation and security objectives, archived security impact sheets, and the answer to follow-
up questions from a potential licensee.
Because the work was completed as part of the design, there are limited surprise inputs, and only
very small gaps that may exist.

E.6. USE CASE 4: SEBD IN DISPUTED DESIGN CHANGE
E.6.1. Situation and Context
Use Case 4 describes a situation in which the designer of an electrical power system has identified
a security impact within a document draft. The designer follows procedure and engages the
security objective point of contact through the security objective impact sheet.
However, the security objective point of contact provides a solution that is unacceptable to the
electric power system designer based on competing requirements.

E.6.2. Participants
The primary participants in this case are the designer and the security objective point of contact.”
In many organizations there would be multiple secondary participants, such as managers, leaders
trained in differing professional opinion programs, financial representatives, licensing
stakeholders, and possibly potential licensees.

E.6.3. Application of this Guidance or Procedure
E.6.3.1. Designer populates part A of the security objective impact sheet
The procedure has the designer check the security objective impact checklist for security objective
impacts. In this case, there is an impact in the target set objective. The designer populates Part A
with a general description of the document scope. The designer then sends the security objective
impact sheet” to the security objective point of contact.

 55

E.6.3.2. Security objective point of contact populates Part B of the security objective
 impact sheet
The security objective point of contact receives the security objective impact sheet and notices that
the designer must include a security requirement for the location of cables moving through the
facility. The security objective point of contact populates that information in Part B of the security
objective impact sheet and returns the document to the designer.

E.6.3.3. Designer populates Part C of the security objective impact sheet
The designer assesses the response and finds competing requirements between security objectives
and other objectives. The designer populates Part C of the security objective impact sheet, stating
that the security objective cannot be directly included in the design and signs the document. The
designer sends the security objective impact sheet to the security objective point of contact for
final signature.

E.6.3.4. Security objective point of contact does not sign security objective impact
 sheet
The security objective point of contact assesses the response from the designer and fundamentally
disagrees with the designer’s finding. The security objective point of contact does not sign the
document and replies to the designer.
At this point, barring any negotiating between the security objective point of contact and the
designer, this issue would be elevated to varying levels of management and decision-makers. The
procedure from Attachment 1 ends at this point. This is an organizational resolution issue now and
not included in SeBD processes.

E.6.3.5. Process moves to an organizational resolution program
Upon escalation, the organizational leadership will need to decide on the design solution based on
existing requirements. These could include regulation, financial, project execution, safety, security,
and customer impacts.
This decision-making effort would follow some internal process designed specifically for these
issues.

E.6.3.6. Designer and security objective point of contact justify their stance
All participants will likely build justification statements. For the security objective point of contact,
tools are available in the industry to aid in justification. Some of those tools are described in
Appendix F. Additionally, for financial assessments the Security by Design Business Case Study
from ORNL should be referenced, which includes a net present value assessment tool available via
request from Nuclear Nexus 3 (Nelson 2024).

3 https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/nuclear-nexus-contact-us

 56

E.6.3.7. Decision is made by leadership
Upon reviewing the justifications and project goals, the organizational leadership will decide on the
security objective. The decision justification should be used to update Part C of the security
objective impact sheet and all parties should sign and archive the document.

E.6.4. Outcomes and Records
The outcome of this use case is primarily to show the limits of the procedure developed in this
report. While the procedure provides interaction between designers and the security organization,
it does not solve the design decisions.
This report builds a clear security requirement for use by the design organization and just like any
other requirement the design organization has, they need to address the potential for some
requirements to be met in a nonideal state.
The organization should have means to determine how design changes are prioritized which may
include deployment strategy and financial concerns, among other inputs.
The security personnel should use tools identified in Appendix F to aid in building justifications for
prioritizing specific security requirements into the design. Additionally, financial justification tools
exist for understanding the long-term costs, such as the security by design business case study
from ORNL, which includes a net present value assessment tool available via request from Nuclear
Nexus 4 (Nelson 2024).

E.7. USE CASE 5: STAKEHOLDER REQUESTS OR INSPECTIONS
E.7.1. Situation and Context
Use Case 5 describes the situation where a regulatory body or other stakeholder (such as a
potential customer) inspects the SeBD program and its effectiveness.
In the case of the regulator, 10 CFR Part 53 will likely require an applicant to submit “confirmation
that safety and security were considered together in the design process, as required by 10 CFR
53.440(f)” (Prassad, 2023).
It is reasonable to assume that applicants under Part 53 may need to show how security and safety
were considered during the design process.

E.7.2. Participants
The primary participants in this case are personnel from the security organization and those from a
third-party organization, like a regulator or potential customer.

E.7.3. Application of this Guidance or Procedure
E.7.3.1. Third party requests an inspection or review of SeBD practices
The third party submits a formal request for review or inspection of the process in which security is
included in the design process. This request may include how security and safety are considered
together in the design process.

4 https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/nuclear-nexus-contact-us

 57

This request will likely come as a formal letter or as a portion of an application submittal. The
inspection or review initiates significant internal communication streams, meetings, and
scheduling.

E.7.3.2. Security organization builds documentation for review
The key party responsible for the inspection or review will be the security organization leadership.
They are tasked with developing documentation that shows how the organization incorporates
security into the design process and how security and safety are considered together.
The key documentation strategy is to start with the quality management system, document the
policy, objectives, and procedures associated with SeBD. The responsible party will also compile a
list of personnel trained in the procedure, all revisions of the security objective impact checklist, all
archived security objective impact sheets, any archived assessment change sheets, and any
archived revision of the security objective points of contact and their résumés.
This is an all-inclusive package that shows exactly how SeBD was used in the design process with
specific outcomes. Since this documentation was continuously built by procedure, the compilation
of this information was not a difficult task.

E.7.3.3. Review or inspection is held
The third-party representatives review the information provided. During the review of the security
objective impact checklist, the third-party representatives disagree with one of the assessments.
Essentially, they conclude that an assessment between site planning document categories and
target sets should read “yes” where they currently read “no.”
After discussion, the design organization agrees. Using the SeBD procedure, they initiate an
assessment change sheet and identify any adverse impacts from the alteration of the assessment.

E.7.3.4. Documentation is completed and submitted
The security organization completes a revision to the security objective impact checklist and an
assessment change sheet. The assessment change sheet is sent to the design organization for the
assessment of adverse impacts. Design changes that are required because of security objectives
are identified and initiated. The design vendor provides any documentation required to the third
party.

E.7.4. Outcomes and Records
This case shows how having a formal procedure for SeBD provides confidence to interested parties
and compliance to regulators that the design vendor has fully incorporated security objectives into
the design of the facility.

E.8. RESULT OF THE NARRATIVE EXAMPLES
These use cases show that the policy, objectives, and procedure provided in Attachment 1 of this
report build security into the design process. The procedure is agile and able to incorporate
changes to organizational structure, regulatory requirements, and workload.
