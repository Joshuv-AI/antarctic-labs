Mechanisms Underlying Pain Avoidance in Complex Regional Pain Syndrome
Authors: Raghavan Gopalakrishnan, Nitesh Singh Malan, Claire Sonneborn, Olivia Hogue, Pavan Tankha, Sylvain Baillet, Mathieu Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMaladaptive pain avoidance plays a critical role in chronic pain development and maintenance. Pain avoidance reinforced instrumentally through action‐outcome contingencies remains poorly understood, specifically in complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), a nociplastic condition. We aim to identify mechanisms underlying maladaptive avoidance to inform interventions and improve quality of life.MethodsTwenty‐eight lower extremity CRPS and twenty‐seven healthy (HC) participants performed an instrumental pain avoidance task in which they chose between two options linked to different probabilities of receiving pain, enabling assessment of avoidance learning and decision‐making. Pain was delivered to the affected or non‐affected extremity in CRPS and to either hand in HC. A reinforcement learning model estimated threat and safety learning, choice stochasticity, and trial‐wise pain expectancies based on task behaviour. Resting‐state magnetoencephalography was used to quantify intrinsic alpha‐band activity, a frequency linked to chronic pain experience, in relation to pain avoidance.ResultsThreat and safety learning did not differ between extremities in CRPS. CRPS exhibited significantly elevated threat and safety learning than HC, accompanied by increased choice stochasticity, despite similar pain exposure. Pain expectancies directly influenced avoidance decisions independent of perceived pain in both groups; however, this expectancy‐decision relationship was significantly weaker in CRPS. Increased threat learning in CRPS was statistically accounted for by elevated anxiety, sentimental personality, and reduced resting‐state alpha power in the prefrontal cortex.ConclusionsCollectively, compared to HC, CRPS exhibited higher learning from recent outcomes and diminished expectancy‐guided control of behaviour. Prefrontal alpha partially mediated the effects of pain severity on threat learning.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered in Clinicaltrials.gov [NCT04603417].Significance StatementCRPS is a debilitating condition associated with disproportionate pain, sensorimotor abnormalities, and autonomic dysfunction, often arising without underlying structural or organic causes. Maladaptive pain avoidance, driven by fear of pain, severely hinders physical therapy and rehabilitation by creating a cycle of inactivity, physical deconditioning, and increased pain sensitivity. This work highlights specific behavioural and neural mechanisms underlying pain avoidance for the development of novel psychosocial and neuromodulatory interventions that can be incorporated in clinical practice to treat CRPS.Keywords: complex regional pain syndrome, magnetoencephalography, pain avoidance, pain expectancy, peak alpha frequency, threat learning

Introduction

The experience of unexpected pain and pain absence respectively teach threat and safety related associations that in turn lead to motivated action of avoidance. An optimal balance between threat and safety learning is required for adaptive avoidance that helps to navigate everyday life effectively. However, attributing greater than normal threat value to pain or safety value to pain absence could result in avoidance of safe cues and valued life activities such as walking or exercising (Meulders 2019; Volders et al. 2015). Such maladaptive avoidance behaviour initially reduces pain‐related anxiety; however, over time leads to the development and maintenance of chronic pain (Vlaeyen and Linton 2000). Personality traits such as harm avoidance further contribute to pain maintenance and fuel a vicious cycle of fear and avoidance (Conrad et al. 2007; Monti et al. 1998).

While several classical conditioning studies have made inferences about maladaptive pain avoidance (Harvie et al. 2017), instrumentally reinforced pain avoidance behaviour is rarely studied in chronic pain (Volders et al. 2015). Pain avoidance is typically measured using standardized instruments (Traxler et al. 2025) that assess pain‐related fear and resulting extent of avoidance of physical activity. While these explicit retrospective accounts aid clinical evaluation, objective quantification of latent behaviour is critical to understand cerebral mechanisms underlying pain avoidance and decision‐making.

Previous studies have made considerable progress in our understanding of the brain mechanisms underlying pain in healthy humans (Zebhauser et al. 2023). Specifically, alpha oscillations have been found to encode aversive learning and pain avoidance decisions (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2025). Individuals with slower resting‐state peak alpha frequency (PAF) exhibit threat‐enhanced spinal and supraspinal nociception, suggesting that PAF may be a neurophysiological correlate of altered threat learning (Huber et al. 2025). PAF is reliably slowed across many chronic pain conditions (Furman et al. 2024), which is accompanied by alterations in the overall alpha power, specifically in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), which has been linked to pain severity (Malan et al. 2025). While slower and suppressed PAF indicates reduced inhibitory response to pain and an overall pronociceptive profile (Huber et al. 2025), whether this alteration facilitates pain avoidance behaviour is an outstanding question, especially in centrally driven conditions like complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS).

Here, we examined instrumental pain avoidance in CRPS, a debilitating nociplastic condition driven by altered CNS processing (Farzad et al. 2022; Ferraro et al. 2024). Psychological factors, specifically anxiety, have been found to be a significant contributor to CRPS maintenance (Bean et al. 2015; Cave et al. 2023). We studied unilateral lower extremity CRPS and tested pain avoidance behaviour separately on the non‐affected and affected hallux to examine if underlying mechanisms differ. We also included data from healthy participants, who were tested on their thumb, as an additional control. We used an instrumental pain avoidance learning (PAL) task in conjunction with a reinforcement learning model to dissociate pain avoidance into threat and safety learning. Participants also underwent resting‐state neurophysiology to quantify intrinsic brain activity and their mediating role in pain avoidance processes. We theorized increased learning from recent outcomes, increased choice stochasticity, and overall reduced expectancy‐driven control over action selection could characterize altered pain avoidance in CRPS. We hypothesized that threat and safety learning will be heightened in CRPS when encountering pain on their affected compared to non‐affected extremity. Although exploratory, we further hypothesized that anxiety and alpha power in the PFC (Malan et al. 2025) will mediate the effects of pain severity on threat learning, while fear avoidance traits will moderate the expectancy‐decision relationship.

Methods and Materials

Participants

The study enrolled 32 adult participants with CRPS (20–76 years old; 22 females and 10 males with mean age of 47 ± 13 years and 50 ± 12 years, respectively) and 33 adult HC (19–61 years old; 17 females and 16 males with a mean age of 33 ± 11 years and 30 ± 5 years, respectively). Patients were recruited from pain clinics within the Cleveland Clinic health system. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The current study deviated from the registered protocol [NCT04603417] in the following ways. First, opioid use disorder (OUD) risk was not included in the present analyses because the study focused on the neurobehavioural mechanisms underlying pain avoidance irrespective of OUD risk. Second, eligibility for participants with CRPS was restricted to individuals with lower extremity CRPS because upper‐extremity pain interfered with performance of the experimental task. Consequently, pain stimulation in participants with CRPS was delivered to the hallux rather than the thumb, as specified in the registered protocol. Finally, age‐ and sex‐matching of participants with CRPS and HC was not performed a priori to facilitate participant recruitment and ensure completion of the study within the project timeline. Consequently, a post hoc age‐ and sex‐matched subgroup analysis was conducted, and the results are presented separately.

Patients with CRPS (both Type I and II) were considered candidates after reviewing their history, neurological and psychological status. All patients were diagnosed with CRPS by pain physician (PT) based on the clinical Budapest diagnostic criteria (Harden et al. 2022). They also satisfied the research inclusion criteria including unilateral pain accompanied with allodynia and/or hyperalgesia, and an average daily pain ≥ 5 on 0–10 scale for past 30 days. Participation in the study did not alter routine care received by the patients. However, patients undergoing active stimulation therapies (e.g., spinal cord stimulators) were asked to turn off the stimulation 10–12 h before testing to reduce confounds related to stimulator induced paresthesia or analgesia. Patients with concurrent pain conditions (e.g., neuropathic pain) were excluded from the study. All HC met the inclusion criteria with no history of psychiatric, neurological, or pain disorders. All participants were recruited between Nov 2021 and Apr 2024.

Based on the effect sizes reported in previous studies on pain avoidance behaviour (Jepma et al. 2018, 2022; Roy et al. 2014), a priori power analysis for a paired samples ANCOVA indicated that approximately 30 participants per group would provide 80% power to detect a medium effect size at α = 0.05.

Data Collection

All participants, after providing their consent to participate in the study, reported to our facility on the day of testing. Five head position indicator (HPI) coils were affixed to the participant's scalp for continuous monitoring of head position inside the MEG scanner. In addition, a standard 10–20 EEG channel montage was affixed to the participant's scalp (data not reported). Fiduciary points (nasion, right and left auricular), along with head surface points and HPI locations, were collected using a Fastrack digitizer (Polhemus, Colchester, VT, USA) to allow for MEG‐MRI co‐registration (see MEG data analysis). A pair of Ag‐AgCl electrodes was taped across the participant's chest to capture the EKG (data not reported). Skin conductance responses were measured using a pair of gelled electrodes positioned on the palm of the right or the left hand (data not reported).

A 306‐channel MEG system (TRIUX neo, MEGIN Oy, Finland) was used to record resting‐state neural activity while the participants were seated upright inside a magnetically shielded room (Imedco, Switzerland). The height of the chair of the MEG device was adjusted so that the vertex of the scalp was in contact with the internal surface of the MEG apparatus. Participants were instructed to sit still with their eyes closed for 3 min while we collected continuous MEG data. Following this resting‐state segment, participants performed the PAL task (described in the next section). Subjects were monitored continuously with a video camera to ensure compliance with the instructions provided.

The participants were asked to rate their pain level in a numerical rating scale (NRS) of pain at the beginning of data collection and also completed the following standardized clinical and psychological questionnaires at the end of data collection, including Pain Disability Index (PDI), McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ), visual analog scale of pain (VAS), Beck anxiety inventory (BAI), Beck depression inventory (BDI), and temperament and character inventory (TCI). Though TCI measures both temperament and character, we only included the temperament dimensions (novelty seeking, harm avoidance, reward dependence, and persistence) in our analysis since they reflect habits related to associative learning.

Instrumental Pain Avoidance Learning (PAL) Task

The instrumental PAL task (Figure 1A) contained 150 trials. On each trial, participants made a choice between two visual cues (a diamond and a circle, or a star and a pentagon) to learn and select the option with the lowest probability of receiving a pressure pain stimulus outcome. The participants started out by trial and error but soon learned cue‐pain contingencies to avoid the option associated with a high probability of being followed by a pain outcome. The probabilities associated with each cue were independent of one another and dictated according to a pair of random walks that probabilistically reversed at least twice (Figure 1B). We used two different pairs of random walks administered randomly to each participant. Each trial started with the presentation of the two visual cues, randomly displayed to the left or right of the screen (1800 ms). The participants clicked the left or right button of a mouse to select the visual cue on the left or right side of the screen, respectively. The computer randomly selected a response if participant inactivity exceeded 2 s. The chosen option was displayed (200 ms) followed by an anticipatory period (2 s) during which a white crosshair appeared on the screen. Then the outcome—a brief pressure pain stimulus (see ‘Pressure pain’) or no‐stimulus—was applied (1300 ms). Outcome onset was accompanied by a change of the crosshair to a central asterisk (3 s). Following the outcome, a visual analog scale appeared on the screen (5 s), and participants were asked to rate their perceived pain by moving a slider on the computer screen, only when their choice resulted in a pain outcome. The next trial started after a jittered inter‐trial interval of 1–3 s with the same two cues presented on the screen. Based on the choice‐outcome contingency learning, the participants decided to either switch choices or stay with the current choice to avoid receiving a pain outcome. The trials that resulted in a pain outcome will be referred to as ‘pain trials’ and those that resulted in no‐stimulus will be referred to as ‘no‐pain trials’.

The full set of 150 trials was administered twice in each participant. While the same random walk was used for both trial sets, the random draws and visual objects were different. The two trial sets only differed by the site of pain delivery. In CRPS participants, pain was delivered either to the hallux nail bed on the affected (CRPS‐A) or non‐affected (CRPS‐NA) lower extremity. In HC participants, pain was delivered either to the left hand (HC‐L) or right hand (HC‐R) thumb nail bed. CRPS participants used their dominant hand for mouse clicks, whereas HC used the hand contralateral to pain delivery. See Figure S1 for a schematic illustration. The trial sets were separated by a short break lasting 5–10 min. The order of the pain delivery site between the first and second trial set was counterbalanced across participants. We utilized this task design to answer the question of whether pain avoidance learning differed significantly between CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A. Prior to data collection, on the same day, participants performed a practice session with a limited number of trials, and a different pair of random walk and visual objects from the ones they received during the test session. During the practice session, instead of actual outcomes, the words ‘Pain’ and ‘No Pain’ appeared on the screen. During practice, participants were carefully instructed about all aspects of the experiment, except the actual probabilities of pain they received during the actual test.

Since there was no significant difference between HC‐L and HC‐R (See Figures S2 and S3, and Table S1) for all analysis, we averaged the data from two trial sets in HC (HC‐L and HC‐R) which will be heretofore referred to as HC‐Avg. For group level analyses (CRPS vs. HC), we averaged the data from two trial sets in CRPS (CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A) which will be heretofore referred to as CRPS‐Avg.

Pressure Pain

Pressure pain was delivered to the participants' thumb nailbed (for HC) or hallux nailbed (for CRPS) using a computer‐controlled pneumatic device (Integrated Instrument Development Facility, University of Colorado, Boulder) capable of transmitting controlled pressure to a piston surface with 0.397 cm2. Each stimulus lasted for 1300 ms. HC participants held on to a handle and inserted their thumb into a groove with a snugly fit piston on top. CRPS participants rested their foot on a slant board and inserted their hallux into a groove with a snugly fit piston on top. Pressure levels were individually titrated to be at a level 80 on a continuous pain rating scale ranging from 0 to 100 (0, no sensation; 10, nonpainful; 20, low pain; 50, moderate pain; 80, maximum tolerable pain; 100—excruciating pain). When titrating the affected extremity of CRPS participants, they were asked to isolate and rate the pressure pain separately from their ongoing baseline pain. Pressure levels were always set between 1 and 9 kg/cm2 and never can exceed 10 Kg/cm2 which is the upper limit of the device output.

Task Behaviour

Task behaviour was characterized by using the following metrics: (1) Percentage of choice switches. The percentage of the trials (out of 150) that participants switched choices irrespective of the outcome received, (2) Percentage pain outcomes received. The percentage of the trials (out of 150) that participants received a pain outcome, (3) Percentage switches in pain trials. The number of pain trials (out of total pain reinforced trials) after which participants decided to switch choices, indicative of lose‐shift strategy, (4) Percentage switches in no‐pain trials. The number of no‐pain trials (out of total no‐pain reinforced trials) after which participants decided to switch choices, indicative of win‐shift (irrelevant) strategy, (5) Average task pain rating. The average pain rating provided by the participants after pain trials on the scale of 0 to 100 during the PAL task.

Reinforcement Learning Model‐Based Analysis

A prediction error (PE) driven model was applied where learning is proportional to PEs—mismatch between outcomes and prior expectations. In this model, worse‐than‐expected outcome drives threat learning and better‐than‐expected outcome drives safety learning. Participants' decisions in the task were modelled as a function of previous choices and outcomes using a temporal difference model with separate learning rates for pain αthreat and no‐pain αsafety outcomes and an inverse‐temperature parameter β that controlled the sensitivity of choice behaviour to learned expectancies, reflecting choice stochasticity and explore‐exploit tendencies. See Methods S1 for further description of this model.

Model parameters αthreat,αsafety, and β were estimated for each participant separately for task data from each extremity (HC‐L and HC‐R for controls; CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A for patients), using a hierarchical Bayesian inference through Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling (Ahn et al. 2017; Gopalakrishnan et al. 2025).

We applied the mean model parameters (the individual‐level posterior medians, averaged across all participants) to each participant's task data, that is, sequence of choices and outcomes to derive trial‐wise pain expectancy estimates and PEs. This approach was adopted to obtain stable trial‐wise pain expectancy estimates while minimizing noise and overfitting inherent to individual parameter estimates, particularly in mediation analyses that operate at the single‐trial level. This strategy has been used in prior work (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2025; Jepma et al. 2022; Roy et al. 2014) to dissociate learning‐related signals from individual differences in parameter magnitude and allows trial‐wise expectancies to reflect common task structure rather than idiosyncratic parameter variability.

Analysis of Trial‐Wise Pain Avoidance

To assess the factors that influence ‘in‐the‐moment’ learning and avoidance decisions, we performed a multilevel mediation analysis with individual trials as a unit of observation at the first level and participants at the second level. This analysis was conducted separately for CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A, and compared to HC‐Avg. Using only pain trials, we tested trial‐wise participant perceived pain rating to pressure pain in the nth trial (refer to Figure 1A) as a mediator of the relationship between pain expectation in the nth trial (equation 1 from Methods S1) and choice decisions in the n + 1th trial that is, whether they decided to stay with current choice or switch. We further applied clinical and psychological variables (age, sex, NRS, PDI, MPQ, VAS, BAI, BDI, and TCI) as moderators to test their influence on each mediation path. The mediation models were implemented with the CanLab mediation toolbox (Wager et al. 2008) (https://github.com/canlab/MediationToolbox), and we assessed the significance of each path using a bootstrap resampling procedure with 10,000 bootstrap samples. Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, we did not correct for multiple comparisons.

MEG Data Analysis

MEG data were preprocessed using standard procedures, and source localization of sensor level data was performed. For each source time series, we derived the power spectral density (PSD) from 1 to 40 Hz using Welch's method with a 4‐s sliding time window and 50% overlap. Individual PSD maps were then projected to ‘fsaverage’, as a common cortical reference surface obtained from FreeSurfer (see https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/FsAverage). Finally, we used the Desikan‐Killiany‐Tourville (DKT) atlas (Desikan et al. 2006) aligned with the ‘fsaverage’ cortical template, to parcellate the cortical sources into 68 regions of interest (ROIs). For each ROI, peak alpha frequency (PAF) that is, the frequency corresponding to the maximum power in the alpha band (8–12 Hz) was computed using the centre of gravity method (Brotzner et al. 2014; Jann et al. 2012, 2010) and the corresponding spectral amplitude was defined as PAF power. See Methods S2 for full description of MEG data analysis.

Recently published data from CRPS participants showed PAF power in the PFC region was significantly associated with pain severity independent of age, sex, and disease duration effects (Malan et al. 2025). Hence, of the 68 ROIs in the DKT atlas, we only considered the medial (superior frontal ROI in the DKT atlas) and lateral PFC (rostral middle frontal ROI in the DKT atlas) for further analysis. Given these ROIs encompass a greater section of the PFC, we subdivided them into smaller parcels for further analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All analysis were performed using custom written scripts in MATLAB and R. All data were inspected visually using Q plots and histograms for normality to select appropriate statistical tests. Group differences between CRPS and HC in age and sex were respectively tested using 2‐sample t‐test and chi‐square test. Group differences between CRPS and HC in clinical and psychological variables (NRS, PDI, MPQ, VAS, BAI, BDI and TCI), PAF and PAF power derived from PFC ROIs were tested using non‐parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test. Group differences in task behaviour (HC‐Avg vs. CRPS‐NA, HC‐Avg vs. CRPS‐A and CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A) were estimated using non‐parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test. Due to non‐normal distribution of datasets, we determined the effect of extremity (CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A) and task outcome (Pain [threat] or No‐pain [safety]) on computational learning parameters α using separate Wilcoxon rank sum tests. To determine the effect of group (HC‐Avg and CRPS‐Avg) and task outcome (Pain [threat] or No‐pain [safety]) on learning α, a 2‐way Anova was performed, since the data were normally distributed.

Analysis of Factors That Mediate Threat and Safety Learning

To investigate the factors that mediate the effects of chronic pain on overall threat and safety learning, we applied single‐level mediation model with individual participant as a unit of observation. The model parameters included pain severity (VAS) as independent variable, and RL model parameters (αthreat,αsafety, and β) from HC‐Avg and CRPS‐Avg as dependent variables. We tested demographical, clinical and psychological variables (age, sex, PDI, MPQ, BAI, BDI and TCI), neurophysiological variables (PAF and PAF power derived from the PFC ROIs) and task behavioural metrics (percent choice switches, percent pain outcomes received, percent lose‐shift trials, percent win‐shift trials and average task pain rating) as mediating variables. The mediation models were implemented with the CanLab mediation toolbox (Wager et al. 2008) (https://github.com/canlab/MediationToolbox) and we assessed the significance of each path using a bootstrap resampling procedure with 10,000 bootstrap samples. Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, we did not correct for multiple comparisons.

Results

Participant Demographics

Four CRPS and six HC participants' data were excluded from the analyses due to incomplete data collection, technical issues, or their inability to understand and perform the task as instructed. Hence, the final analysis included data from 28 CRPS (20–62 years old; 18 females and 10 males with mean age of 46 ± 11 years; 25 right handed, disease duration in years median: 4 [IQR = 1, 12]) and 27 HC (19–52 years old; 13 females and 14 males with mean age of 30 ± 8 years; 25 right handed) participants. Thirteen CRPS participants had pain in their left lower extremity, whereas the rest 15 had pain in their right lower extremity distributed across the ankle, feet, leg, and knee.

Our primary objective was to compare CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A. HC group was included as an additional control. Because CRPS and HC groups differed in age, all primary analyses were repeated in an age‐ and sex‐matched subsample as a robustness check. The matched subgroup was created using a hybrid of exact matching of sex and coarsened exact matching of age. Importantly, all major findings, including group differences in learning rates, choice stochasticity, and mediation effects, were preserved in the matched cohort, indicating that age did not account for the observed effects. The results of this ad‐hoc analysis are presented in the Supporting Information. Table S2 lists participant demographic, clinical, and psychological characteristics for the matched cohort.

Clinical Characteristics

CRPS patients exhibited significantly greater pain severity, anxiety, depression, and pain disability than HC. For personality traits, we found CRPS more orderly than HC (Disorderliness), more fatigable than HC (Fatiguability), and more sentimental than HC (Sentimentality). In the matched cohort, differences in disorderliness and fatigability remained significant, whereas the difference in sentimentality was no longer statistically significant despite a large effect size. Task Behaviour

As expected, participants switched choices more frequently in pain trials than no‐pain trials (p < 0.0001 for all groups in both full cohort and matched cohort using Wilcoxon rank sum test. See Table 2). The effects of pain on choice switching also decayed over time in CRPS‐NA, CRPS‐A, and HC‐Avg, as evidenced by logistic regressions assessing the effects of reinforcement history (pain delivered 1 through 6 trials back) on switches in pain trials (Figure 1C. See Figure S4 for results from matched cohort). These behavioural metrics provide evidence that participant choice selections during the task conformed to reinforcement‐based learning. However, odds of switching one trial back were significantly higher in CRPS than HC.

None of the task behaviour parameters (Table 2 and Table S4 for matched cohort) were significantly different between CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A. The percentage of overall trials with choice switches and the percentage of choice switches in pain trials were significantly higher in CRPS compared to HC, especially for CRPS‐A.

Participant inactivity led to automatic choice selections in 3% ± 3% trials in both CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A, and 2% ± 2% trials in HC‐Avg. Given the small proportion, these trials were not excluded from the analyses. Though the choice selections were automatic, participants still learned from the outcomes received.

Computational Model Parameters

CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A. Analysis comparing CRPS‐NA to CRPS‐A (Figure 2 top) revealed no significant difference in both threat and safety learning rates (CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A, αthreat = 0.64 [IQR = 0.3, 0.955] vs. 0.77 [IQR = 0.56, 0.925], αsafety= 0.94 [IQR = 0.735, 0.97] vs. 0.85 [IQR = 0.4725, 0.96], Wilcoxon rank sum test). When comparing αthreat vs. αsafety within CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A, there was a significant effect of outcome on learning rate only in CRPS‐NA (αthreat vs. αsafety, 0.64 [IQR = 0.3, 0.955] vs. 0.94 [IQR = 0.735, 0.97], p < 0.01, Figure 2, top). In other words, participants displayed greater learning from no‐pain outcome (αsafety) than pain outcome (αthreat) indicating a learning asymmetry. There was no significant difference in choice stochasticity between CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A (CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A, β = 4.97 [IQR = 3.825, 7.1375] vs. 4.27 [IQR = 3.09, 5.8525]).

A two‐way ANOVA (group [CRPS‐Avg/HC‐Avg] × task outcome [pain/no‐pain]) revealed that there was no significant interaction between the effects of group and task outcome (F(1,106) = 0.05, p = 0.8182). Simple main effects analysis of the effect of group revealed significantly greater threat and safety learning rates in CRPS‐Avg compared to HC‐Avg (CRPS vs. HC, αthreat= 0.64 ± 0.24 vs. 0.45 ± 0.15, αsafety= 0.78 ± 0.18 vs. 0.61 ± 0.12, F(1,106) = 27.22, p < 0.0001, Figure 2 bottom). Simple main effects analysis of the effect of task outcome revealed significantly greater safety learning rate compared to threat learning rate in both HC‐Avg and CRPS‐Avg (αthreat vs. αsafety, HC‐Avg = 0.45 ± 0.15 vs. 0.61 ± 0.12, CRPS‐Avg = 0.64 ± 0.24 vs. 0.78 ± 0.18, p < 0.0001, F(1,106) = 19.82, Figure 2 bottom) indicating a learning asymmetry. CRPS‐Avg exhibited a significantly lower inverse‐temperature parameter β indicative of increased choice stochasticity (CRPS‐Avg vs. HC‐Avg, β = 4.88 [IQR = 3.96, 6.32] vs. 7.7 [IQR = 6.72, 7.97], Wilcoxon rank sum test, p < 0.0001, Figure 2 top).

Our analysis of matched cohort reiterated the full cohort findings indicating age did not affect our original findings (Figure S5).

Factors Mediating Trial‐Wise Avoidance Decisions

Multilevel mediation analysis with pain ratings as a mediator of the effects of pain expectancy on choice decisions revealed that perceived pain level did not mediate trial‐wise avoidance decisions in CRPS‐NA, CRPS‐A or HC‐Avg (Figure 3A). Pain expectations positively influenced choice decisions that is, lower pain expectations resulted in lower odds of switching decisions irrespective of the pain level (unstandardized beta coefficients and standard error for path c. CRPS‐NA:1.52[0.32], CRPS‐A:1.32[0.30], HC‐Avg: 3.48[0.15]). However, this association was significantly weaker in both CRPS‐NA and CRPS‐A than HC‐Avg (Figure 3B) as determined by comparison of beta coefficients of path c between groups using Wilcoxon rank sum test (no significant difference for CRPS‐NA vs. CRPS‐A, p < 0.01 for HC‐Avg vs. CRPS‐NA and p < 0.001 for HC‐Avg vs. CRPS‐A). Additionally, in HC‐Avg, novelty seeking trait strengthened the positive relationship between pain expectations and choice decisions. In CRPS, however, harm avoidance and reward dependence traits significantly weakened the strength of relationship between pain expectancy and choice decisions. In other words, participants with greater negative traits such as fear uncertainty, choice decisions were less likely to be influenced by prior expectations. Our analysis of matched cohort reiterated the full cohort findings indicating age did not affect our original findings (Figure S6).

PAF and PAF Power

A significant suppression of PAF power was observed in the CRPS group compared to HC in left medial PFC (CRPS 0.41 [IQR = 0.37, 0.55] vs. HC 0.61 [IQR = 0.42, 0.78], p = 0.0082), right medial PFC (CRPS 0.44 [IQR = 0.35, 0.57] vs. HC 0.59 [0.41, 0.78], p = 0.0172), left lateral PFC (CRPS 0.39 [0.33, 0.49] vs. HC 0.53 [0.40, 0.72], p = 0.0033), and right lateral PFC (CRPS 0.42 [0.34, 0.50] vs. HC 0.52 [0.45, 0.58], p = 0.0180). A significant slowing of PAF was not observed in these ROIs.

Factors Mediating Effects of Pain Severity on Pain Avoidance Learning

Demographical Parameters

Mediation analysis revealed no evidence for the role of demographical parameters such as age, sex, or disease duration in mediating the effects of pain severity on avoidance learning.

Clinical Parameters

Of all the clinical variables, only the BAI score fully mediated the effects of pain severity on both αthreat and β (Figure 4A and Figure S7A). In other words, participants with greater pain severity showed greater threat learning and choice stochasticity when exhibiting greater anxiety.

(B) % switches in pain trials fully mediated the effects of pain severity on αthreat. Behavioural Parameters

Of all the behavioural parameters, only the percentage of switches in pain trials (lose‐shift trials) fully mediated the effects of pain severity on both αthreat and β (Figure 4B and Figure S7B). In other words, participants with greater pain severity showed greater threat learning and choice stochasticity when they resorted to lose‐shift strategy.

Trait Variables

Of all the TCI metrics, sentimentality—a sub‐category in the reward dependence trait—fully mediated the effects of pain severity on both αthreat and β (Figure 4C and Figure S7C). In other words, participants with greater pain severity showed greater threat learning and choice stochasticity only when they were highly sentimental. Sentimentality reflects a sensitivity to social cues and a strong emotional response to rewards.

Neurophysiology

Of all the ROIs tested, only the PAF power derived from the left medial PFC ROI partially mediated the effects of pain severity on αthreat (Figure 4D). In other words, participants with greater pain severity showed greater threat learning when they exhibited reduced alpha power in the medial PFC.

There was no evidence for a role of any of parameters in mediating the effects of pain severity on αsafety.

Discussion

In this study, using an instrumental PAL task in conjunction with a reinforcement learning model, we examined pain avoidance in unilateral CRPS. First, we show that pain‐related threat and safety learning are no different between the affected and non‐affected sites, supporting the idea of fear and avoidance generalization. Second, compared to controls, CRPS participants displayed significantly greater threat and safety learning, accompanied by increased choice stochasticity and diminished expectancy guided control of decisions. Third, the effects of pain severity on threat learning and choice stochasticity were fully mediated by increased anxiety and sentimentality trait and partially mediated by alpha rhythms in the medial PFC. The results provide novel mechanistic insights regarding altered pain avoidance in CRPS that could inform alternate therapies.

Spatial Generalization of Avoidance in CRPS

Several studies have shown generalization of pain‐related fear and avoidance in different chronic pain phenotypes, corroborating its role in pain‐related disability (Vandael et al. 2023). Chronic pain patients often extrapolate threat value based on perceptual or conceptual similarity (Meulders and Bennett 2018). Our study extends these previous findings to show that CRPS patients exhibit generalization of avoidance irrespective of spatial location of pain that is, affected vs. non‐affected extremity, supporting the notion that pain avoidance in CRPS cannot be explained by peripheral deficits alone, rather, supraspinal processes play a key role (Trojan et al. 2014). This spatial generalization of fear and avoidance could be a key contributor for the spontaneous spread of pain to other extremities that are often reported in CRPS without a secondary trauma or injury (van Rijn et al. 2011). Hence, interventions that target generalized fear, anxiety and avoidance could be key to prevent CRPS spread.

Increased Threat and Safety Learning in CRPS

Our data suggest that in CRPS, both threat and safety learning are altered, which adds to accumulating evidence from various chronic pain conditions (Harvie et al. 2020; Meulders et al. 2014; Schlitt et al. 2022). The higher learning rates observed for avoided pain than received pain indicates participants (both CRPS and HC) updated their expectations more following pain avoidance. Similar learning rate asymmetries have been found in reward learning that used monitory reinforcement (Lefebvre et al. 2017; Sharot and Garrett 2016). While this asymmetry is consistent with instrumental learning favouring pain avoidance, we speculate it may also reflect enhanced relief, particularly in CRPS, where avoidance may become habitual rather than optimally goal‐directed. The significantly greater threat and safety learning in CRPS implies patients updated their expectations more than controls following both pain and pain avoidance with choice decisions overweighting the most recent trials, rather than accumulating evidence over past several trials. In other words, patients were closer to win‐stay lose‐shift strategy. The increased threat learning in conjunction with increased choice stochasticity that is, selection of suboptimal choices could possibly indicate learned helplessness (Samwel et al. 2006). This is further corroborated by significantly greater number of pain‐switch trials without significant difference in number of pain outcomes received. Increased choice stochasticity may also reflect deliberate exploratory behaviour in response to unpredictable outcomes; however, this is unlikely based on current evidence for decision‐making deficits among chronic pain individuals (Horta et al. 2025). Although pain is a bodily threat, excessive fear and avoidance can compromise daily function. Exaggerated threat perception and learning can be key factors mediating chronicity resulting in changes to brain networks modulating stress responses (Timmers et al. 2019).

Fear and Anxiety Facilitate Pain Avoidance Learning and Decisions in CRPS

Our data demonstrates that rather than perceived pain, pain‐related fear and anxiety drive avoidance behaviour in CRPS. We show this at a subject and group level. Trial‐wise analysis of pain avoidance decisions showed the expectancy guided control of choice decisions was weak in CRPS, especially in patients with high harm avoidance and reward dependent traits. This aligns well with the notion that pain avoidance is no longer instrumental but rather has become habitual responses (Hofmann and Hay 2018). Group‐wise analysis showed that the effect of pain severity on threat learning and choice stochasticity was fully mediated by clinical anxiety and sentimentality—a trait associated with emotional dependence to rewarding situations and avoiding aversive situations. These findings iterate the relationship between anxiety, fear generalization and avoidance behaviours (Meulders et al. 2024). Fear and anxiety could exacerbate the pro‐inflammatory immune responses, sympathetic activation, central sensitization and overall overestimation of pain‐related threat (Devarajan et al. 2024). These often manifest as sensorimotor, vasomotor, and sudomotor abnormalities that are central to CRPS (Harden et al. 2022). Indeed, higher baseline anxiety level and pain‐related fear have been found to be predictors of poor outcomes in CRPS (Bean et al. 2015; Cave et al. 2023). The results support that multidisciplinary treatment approaches targeting fear and anxiety could normalize pain avoidance in CRPS (Bernardy et al. 2025; de Jong et al. 2005; Scarff 2022).

The Role of PFC Alpha Oscillations in Pain Avoidance

We found participants experiencing greater pain exhibited greater threat learning expressed through greater suppression of PFC alpha power providing novel support for the role of PFC alpha in pain avoidance learning. A growing body of evidence attributes threat and safety learning impairments to disinhibition in the PFC (Henderson et al. 2013; Lim et al. 2016; Ong et al. 2019; Silva‐Passadouro et al. 2022). The PFC is central to pain avoidance decisions (Roy et al. 2014), fear regulation (Giustino and Maren 2015), inhibitory pain control (Ong et al. 2019), and pain chronification (Qi et al. 2022). Specifically, the alpha activity in the PFC is known to facilitate inhibitory processes (Klimesch 2012; Klimesch et al. 2007), contributing to fear extinction (Bierwirth et al. 2023), valuation (Kaminska et al. 2021), learning (Brickwedde et al. 2019; Michael et al. 2023), and adaptive behaviour (Soltani and Koechlin 2022). The alpha activity in the PFC negatively correlates with pain severity in patients with CRPS (Malan et al. 2025). Taken together, attenuated PFC alpha oscillations could mediate exaggerated threat responses through PFC‐amygdala circuitry as it is often reported in anxiety disorders and PTSD (Carcamo 2016; Clancy et al. 2017). Indeed, PFC inhibits amygdala‐mediated habitual responses through instrumental control (Moscarello and LeDoux 2013), which might be lacking in CRPS. We speculate that suppressed PFC alpha in CRPS could be viewed as a central disinhibition syndrome that decreases the thresholds of endogenous systems altering the central pathway set‐point (Forstenpointner et al. 2020); however, paired‐pulse TMS or task‐based measures of inhibitory control are needed to confirm this view.

Clinical Implications

Given CRPS is a complex pathology, therapeutic interventions are largely focused on pain relief, psychological stabilization and functional restoration without standardized protocols (Doan and Chang 2025). Our study supports that CRPS could be managed through multidisciplinary approaches targeting pain‐related fear and anxiety. Given anxiety mediated the effects of pain severity on both threat learning and choice stochasticity, future studies could target anxiety through techniques such as CBT and study its effects on pain avoidance behaviour and overall clinical outcomes (Bernardy et al. 2025). In addition, complementary neuromodulation techniques combined with traditional approaches could improve CRPS outcomes. For instance, alpha activity has been a target for neuromodulation therapies (Millard et al. 2024). We found PFC alpha activity to mediate the effects of pain severity on threat learning. Hence, future causative studies could enhance PFC alpha activity using non‐invasive brain stimulation and study its effects on threat learning.

Limitations

Our sample size is limited but based on similar studies on pain avoidance (Jepma et al. 2022; Roy et al. 2014) with 80.0% power to detect medium effect size. We intended to use the non‐affected extremity as a control to the affected extremity from CRPS participants. However, we also used HC as an additional control. The significant age difference between the patient and HC is a limitation. To account for this, an ad‐hoc analysis was performed with 16 age‐ and sex‐matched CRPS and HC participants. The sub‐analysis results, although limited by sample size, were largely consistent with the main findings from the full cohort. Although pain stimulation sites differed between groups (hallux in CRPS, thumb in HC), the task required participants to learn abstract cue–outcome contingencies rather than sensorimotor mappings tied to a specific body location. The lack of difference in pain threshold between HC and CRPS could also be attributed to the different locations of pain delivery. Because pain intensity was individually titrated and learning was driven by probabilistic associations between cues and outcomes, it is unlikely that stimulation site substantially influenced behaviour. Analgesic drug regimen, another possible confound, has been accounted for to the best possible extent (Table 1). Although pressure pain stimuli do not specifically activate nociceptors, they resemble pain resulting from mechanical allodynia and hence improved the ecological validity of the study. Our task design involved self‐generated expectancies, rather than cue‐based expectancies that provide better experimental control. However, we believe that the effects of expectations on pain were stronger in our design likely due to greater task engagement (Kemper and Gaschler 2017). In our design, the MEG resting‐state data was collected prior to the PAL task; therefore, the observed alpha suppression could reflect a pre‐existing trait or a state which needs further investigation. Finally, the correlational data from the present study needs further evaluation by interventional studies to make inferences about causality.

Conclusions

In summary, our data supports generalized fear and avoidance behaviour in CRPS irrespective of the location of pain. CRPS participants showed significantly greater threat and safety learning compared to HC; however, expectancy guided choice decisions were weaker, possibly due to habitual systems fostered by traits such as harm avoidance and reward dependence. Increased threat learning in CRPS was fully mediated by anxiety and sentimentality trait. Further, PFC alpha oscillations play a vital role in mediating the effects of pain severity on threat learning. Our data provides psychological constructs that could be targeted using psycho‐social approaches and neurophysiological targets for neuromodulation approaches.
