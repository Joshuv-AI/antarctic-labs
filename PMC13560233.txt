Monitoring of breathing effort through pressure time product measurement using airway occlusion pressure (PTPPOCC study)
Authors: Emiliano Gogniat, Emilio Steinberg, Gustavo A. Plotnikow, Sebastián Fredes, Roque S. Moracci, Norberto Tiribelli, Javier H. Dorado, Sergio Giannasi, Mariano Setten, José Dianti, Matías Madorno, Pablo O. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonitoring respiratory effort is crucial to prevent ventilator-induced lung injury and diaphragmatic dysfunction, but esophageal manometry (the gold standard) is invasive an can be cumbersome. The airway occlusion pressure (ΔPOCC) correlates with inspiratory effort but does not account for inspiratory time (Ti) or respiratory rate (RR). We aimed to validate a novel non-invasive index (PTPPOCC) that combines ΔPOCC, Ti, and RR to estimate the esophageal pressure-time product (PTPESO).MethodsWe conducted a prospective, multicenter, physiological, crossover study. Three levels of inspiratory pressure (5, 10, and 15 cm H₂O) were randomly applied in patients with invasive mechanical ventilation in pressure support mode. We simultaneously measured PTPESO and calculated the non-invasive PTPPOCC (PTPPOCC−min = (0.75 × ΔPOCC × Ti / 2) × RR). Association was assessed using linear mixed-effects models. Diagnostic accuracy for detecting high (PTPESO >150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹) and low (PTPESO <50 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹) inspiratory effort was evaluated using ROC curves.ResultsA total of 49 patients were analyzed. A significant positive association was found between PTPESO−min and PTPPOCC−min (regression coefficient: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.86–1.1 p-value < 0.001). The mixed-effects model demonstrated good overall predictive performance (conditional R² = 0.80), although substantial inter-individual variability was observed (marginal R² = 0.40). The AUC for detecting high or low inspiratory effort was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.87–0.94), and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.94) respectively. Agreement analysis revealed proportional bias with wide limits of agreement.ConclusionsThe PTPPOCC index showed good performance for identifying extremes of respiratory effort in mechanically ventilated patients. Despite limited individual agreement, its strong association and high specificity may make it a useful tool for confirming very low or excessively high inspiratory effort and for identifying patients at risk of over or under assistance.Trial registrationThe study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06040138 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06040138), first submitted date 2023-09-05.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04113-2.

Background

Patients receiving positive pressure ventilation are at risk of developing ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and ventilator-induced diaphragm dysfunction (VIDD) [1, 2], and both these conditions are associated with worse outcomes [3, 4]. The risks of VILI and VIDD are in part mediated by the magnitude of respiratory effort; respiratory muscle inactivity can lead to diaphragm disuse atrophy and excessive effort can lead to diaphragm myotrauma, increased lung stress, and dyspnea [5]. Recent evidence suggests maintaining intermediate levels of respiratory effort is associated with improved outcomes in patients with acute respiratory failure [3].

Monitoring respiratory effort is crucial to detect patients at risk of VILI and VIDD. Esophageal pressure manometry is one of the reference methods commonly used to quantify the magnitude of respiratory effort in patients receiving mechanical ventilation [6]. More specifically, the esophageal pressure-time product (PTPESO), a parameter derived from esophageal manometry, considers the respiratory rate and the magnitude and duration of muscle contraction, and it is a variable that correlates well with the energy expenditure [7]. Unfortunately, esophageal manometry and PTPESO have several limitations that limit its application in clinical practice [8]. Bertoni et al. recently showed the noninvasive measurement of the change in airway pressure during an end-expiratory hold (ΔPOCC) correlates with the pressure generated by the respiratory muscles during inspiration [9]. Due to its simplicity, this technique has been rapidly adopted as a tool to monitor respiratory effort at the bedside. Although useful for identifying extremes of effort, as it is only a pressure gradient, it does not incorporate information about the duration of muscle contraction or the frequency with which the effort is repeated, two fundamental determinants in the genesis of fatigue.

The objective of this study is to validate the non-invasive estimation of PTP using a combination of ΔPOCC, ventilator inspiratory time (TiVENT), and respiratory rate (RR) in spontaneously breathing patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. The secondary objective is to derive cutoff values for the non-invasive PTP to predict excessive or insufficient respiratory efforts.

Methods

Study population and design

This is a prospective, multicenter, physiological, randomized-sequence crossover study conducted in five intensive care units in Buenos Aires, Argentina. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18-years-old or older, (2) receiving mechanical ventilation in pressure support ventilation mode, and (3) hemodynamic and metabolic stability as assessed by the attending clinician. Exclusion criteria were (1) any contraindication for esophageal balloon placement, (2) pregnancy, (3) patients with a do-not-resuscitate order or with an expected poor short-term prognosis. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06040138 on September 5, 2023 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06040138). The study was initially approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital Italiano de Buenos Aires (approved on September 19, 2024; approval number CEPI6810). Subsequently, the protocol was reviewed and approved by the local Ethics Committee at each participating center. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients’ next of kin before enrollment. This study was carried out according to the guidelines of Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT). Because there is no data on the relationship between PTPESO and PTP derived from ∆POCC to inform sample size calculation we decided to use a convenience sample size of 50 patients, given the exploratory nature of this analysis.

Physiological measurements

Prior to study initiation, patients were positioned in a semi-recumbent position at 45°, with proper body alignment. All equipment was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. An esophageal balloon catheter (7 cm balloon, MBMed, Buenos Aires, Argentina) was inserted following the standard technique used in mechanically ventilated patients [10]. Correct balloon positioning was confirmed using the end-expiratory occlusion maneuver [11] and with chest X-ray. In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the balloon was initially inflated with 5 mL of air and then deflated to reach a residual volume of 1 mL. To assess the quality of the acquired signal, an end-expiratory occlusion maneuver was performed, which was considered acceptable when the ∆PESO/∆PAW ratio (PESO: esophageal pressure; PAW: airway pressure) was between 0.8 and 1.2 [12].

Procedure

Three levels of inspiratory pressure (5, 10, and 15 cm H₂O) were randomly applied using an electronic randomization system. Each inspiratory pressure level was maintained for 15 min [13]. Trigger sensitivity, cycling-off criteria, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) were kept unchanged from the settings established before inclusion.

During the last 3 min of each phase, three end-expiratory occlusion maneuvers and three end-inspiratory occlusion maneuvers were performed to obtain the ∆POCC and the plateau pressure, respectively. Plateau pressure measurements were considered valid when they met the acceptability criteria proposed by Bianchi et al. [14]. Evita XL and V300 ventilators (Dräger, Germany), Maquet Servo-I ventilators (Maquet, Sweden), Puritan Bennett 840 and 980 ventilators (Medtronic, USA), and Brina ventilators (TECME, Argentina) were used. Airway and esophageal pressures, and flow data were collected with a FluxMed respiratory mechanics monitor connected to a PC using FluxView software (MBMed, Buenos Aires, Argentina). Offline analysis of the monitor signals was performed with FluxReview software (MBMed, Buenos Aires, Argentina). Chest wall compliance (CCW) was calculated as VTe/∆PESO, where VTe is the expiratory tidal volume and ∆PESO is the difference between end‑expiratory esophageal pressure and end‑inspiratory esophageal pressure measured during end‑inspiratory occlusions [15]. The esophageal pressure‑time product per breath (PTPESO−br) the recognized standard to quantify inspiratory effort, was calculated as the area between the chest wall elastic recoil pressure curve and the PESO curve, beginning at the point where patient effort starts and ending at the point where flow switches from positive to negative, which marks the end of inspiration (see Fig. 1). In each stage, we recorded the average PTPESO−br value from the five breaths preceding each expiratory occlusion maneuver. The PTPESO per minute (PTPESO−min) was calculated from the three PTPESO−br values obtained during the three occlusions. For each of them, the PTPESO−br was multiplied by the respiratory rate (RR) recorded during the minute immediately preceding the corresponding occlusion, thus obtaining three estimates of PTPESO−min. The final PTPESO−min value was established as the average of those three estimates (Additional File 1, Figure S1). Recordings with irregularities in the esophageal pressure signal that hindered its monitoring usefulness were also excluded from the analysis. For the calculation of the non-invasive pressure–time product per breath (PTPPOCC−br), we assumed that the muscle pressure waveform has a triangular shape [16]. The area of this triangle was estimated using the standard formula (base × height / 2), considering ΔPOCC as the height and the mechanical inspiratory time (TiVENT) as the base. This approach was chosen to simplify the calculation, as TiVENT is available on all ventilators, whereas there is insufficient information to accurately determine the end of inspiration during the occluded breath. Statistical analysis

Baseline continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with 25th and 75th percentiles, as appropriate. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on their distribution. Comparisons of breathing pattern and ventilatory effort variables across the different pressure support levels were performed using the Friedman test, followed by post hoc analysis with Dunn’s test and Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Missing data were planned to be handled using complete-case analysis; however, no missing data occurred. A linear mixed-effects regression model (fixed and random effects) was used to evaluate the relationship between PTPPOCC−min and PTPPOCC−br (independent variables) and PTPESO−min and PTPESO−br (dependent variables). In this model, PTPPOCC−min and PTPPOCC−br were included as fixed effects, and pressure support level and patient were included as random effects to account for within-subject association and to control for pseudoreplication. Regression coefficients for PTPPOCC−min and PTPPOCC−br were estimated along with their 95% confidence intervals. Additionally, given the exploratory nature of the study, post hoc analyses were performed to investigate the interaction between esophageal pressure–time product (PTPESO) and tidal volume (VT). This relationship was assessed using a linear mixed-effects regression model, with VT included as a fixed effect and PTPESO as the dependent variable, while patient and pressure support level were included as random effects. To explore the relationship between neural inspiratory time and ventilator inspiratory time across different pressure support levels, the index (1−(TiNEURAL/TiVENT)) was analyzed using a linear mixed-effects regression model. In this model, pressure support level was included as a fixed effect, 1−(TiNEURAL/TiVENT) as the dependent variable, and patient as a random effect. For accurate determination of TiNEURAL, we used the algorithm implemented in the FluxMed monitor software, which has been widely used in numerous published studies. Briefly, the onset of inspiratory effort is determined by analyzing the change in the slope of the esophageal pressure curve and the onset of inspiratory flow at the zero crossing. The cycle ends when inspiratory flow changes from positive to negative, marking the end of inspiration. Agreement between paired measurements was additionally assessed using the Bland–Altman method [17].

To assess the discriminative ability of our estimator for identifying high and low inspiratory efforts, acknowledging the uncertainty around cutoff values, we decided to use thresholds routinely used in the literature: >150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ for high effort and < 50 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ for low effort [6, 18]. The ability of PTPPOCC−min to discriminate between PTPESO−min values exceeding 150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ and those below 50 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The 95% confidence interval of AUC estimates were calculated using nonparametric bootstrapping with 2,000 replicates, resampling at the patient level to preserve the longitudinal data structure. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were calculated using the same bootstrap approach.

Cutoff values were determined based on predictions derived from the previously developed mixed-effects model, identifying the PTPPOCC−min values that best predicted PTPESO−min > 150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ and < 50 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with the pROC and dplyr packages.

There were no missing data or dropouts, and all enrolled patients completed the study protocol. Baseline characteristics of the 49 patients included in the analysis are presented in Table 1. No adverse events or harmful side effects were reported or observed in either group during the study period. There was a significant positive association between PTPESO−min and PTPPOCC−min (β 0.98, 95% CI: 0.86–1.1, p-value < 0.001). The mixed-effects model demonstrated good overall predictive performance, with a conditional R² of 0.81 (CI 0.75–0.86), although substantial inter-individual variability was observed, reflected by a marginal R² of 0.44 (CI 0.31–0.57). For PTPESO−br and PTPPOCC−br, the coefficient was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.80–1.06, p-value < 0.001), with a conditional R² of 0.77 (CI 0.71–0.84) and a marginal R² of 0.35 (CI 0.27–0.45). The relationships between these variables are shown in Figs. 2 A and 2B. Proportional bias was observed in the Bland-Altman analysis (Additional File 1, Figure S2A and S2B). The area under the ROC curve was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.87; 0.94) for classifying patients with PTPESO−min greater than 150 cm H₂O·s·min⁻¹, and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88; 0.94) for classifying patients with PTPESO−min less than 50 cm H₂O·s·min⁻¹ (Fig. 3A and B; Table 3). Diagnostic performance metrics of PTPPOCC−min for low or high respiratory effort are shown in Table 3. Cutoff values for PTPPOCC−min were derived from a previously developed linear mixed-effects model. The model predicted a PTPPOCC−min value of 114.4 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ corresponding to a PTPESO−min of 150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹, and a value of 12.6 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹ corresponding to a PTPESO−min of 50 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹. These PTPPOCC−min values demonstrated high specificity (98%) for identifying patients with both high and low effort. However, sensitivity were low in both cases (44% and 28% respectively). The PPV and NPV were moderate to high for both effort levels. Regarding the relationship between PTPESO−min and tidal volume, we observed a positive but very weak association with a conditional R² of 0.81 (CI 0.66–0.91) and a marginal R² of 0.04 (CI 0.02–0.10) (Additional File 1, Figure S3). Additionally, a significant difference between TiNEURAL and TiVENT was observed across all pressure support levels, with TiVENT becoming progressively longer than TiNEURAL as ventilatory support increased (Additional File 1, Figure S4).

Discussion

In this physiological study evaluating a novel noninvasive variable for monitoring inspiratory effort on a per-breath and per-minute basis in mechanically ventilated patients, we found that PTPPOCC showed good performance for identifying excessively high and very low levels of respiratory effort estimated by PTPESO. The estimator demonstrated a strong overall association with the reference method, although substantial inter-individual variability was observed. Accordingly, Bland–Altman analysis suggested limited reliability for estimating absolute individual PTPESO values.

The risk of respiratory muscle fatigue and ventilatory failure during weaning of ventilatory support depends not only on the intensity of inspiratory effort but also on the balance between muscle contraction and relaxation times [19]. We found PTPPOCC is a useful tool for confirming conditions of very low or excessively high inspiratory effort and could help identify patients at risk of over and under assistance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to validate a noninvasive tool for assessing respiratory muscle effort using PTP, a comprehensive variable that includes magnitude, duration, and repetition frequency. PTPPOCC showed a significant association with PTPESO, both on a per-minute and per-breath basis. The discrepancy between marginal and conditional R² suggests that the relationship between the estimator and inspiratory effort is substantially influenced by patient-specific characteristics. This finding likely reflects inter-individual variability in respiratory mechanics and neuromuscular coupling, which is expected in mechanically ventilated patients. However, agreement analysis revealed wide limits of agreement, and overall concordance between methods was lower than expected.

PTPPOCC showed excellent diagnostic capability for identifying extremes of inspiratory effort, with an excellent area under the ROC curve for both insufficient and excessive effort. Our results show that the PTPPOCC method has high specificity but low sensitivity for identifying extremes of inspiratory effort. This limitation in sensitivity is partly explained by a deliberate methodological decision: we chose not to use the optimal cut-off values derived from the Youden index, as the resulting thresholds differed substantially from those commonly reported in the literature and from values used in routine clinical practice. This discrepancy in cut-off values is consistent with findings from previous studies. Using the Youden index, Yang et al. [20] identified a ΔPOCC cut-off of 9.2 cm H₂O for detecting high inspiratory effort (PMUS >10 cm H₂O, calculated as the maximal difference between PESO and quasi-static recoil pressure of the chest wall), which is markedly lower than the corresponding threshold reported by de Vries et al. (14 cm H₂O) [21]. Similarly, the cut-off for low inspiratory effort reported by Yang et al. (6.2 cm H₂O) was lower than that reported by de Vries et al. (9 cm H₂O), despite the same reference criterion (PMUS <5 cm H₂O). Although this approach might have improved sensitivity and specificity in our cohort, the resulting cut-off points would likely have had limited clinical applicability.

We observed that the degree of agreement between PTPPOCC and PTPESO decreased as conditions of higher respiratory effort were evaluated. Additionally, the variance of the difference between measurements also increases with effort. One possible explanation for the increased dispersion observed at high effort levels is that PTPESO incorporates the pressure gradient associated with the elastic recoil of the chest wall in its calculation. Since this gradient is amplified at high tidal volumes, this could contribute to the greater discrepancy recorded at high effort values, where the volume input to the system was likely greater. However, we found no relationship between PTPESO and VT, suggesting many patients exhibited high VT with low effort and, conversely, low VT with high efforts. Another factor that may have contributed to the increased dispersion at higher effort levels is the relatively small number of observations (19%) with PTPESO values exceeding 150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹. This relative sparsity of high-effort data points likely explains the low sensitivity (44%) observed for detecting PTPESO, min > 150 cmH₂O·s·min⁻¹. In addition, we used the inspiratory time provided by the ventilator (TiVENT) for the calculation, rather than the integral of the airway pressure-time curve during the occlusion maneuver. This approach was chosen to simplify the calculation, as TiVENT is available on a breath-by-breath basis on all ventilators, whereas there is insufficient information to accurately determine the end of inspiration time during the occluded breath. This may also have contributed to the dispersion observed across the entire range of inspiratory effort. In the post hoc analysis, conducted to determine whether a difference existed between TiNEURAL and TiVENT, a discrepancy between the two was confirmed across all pressure support levels.

Our study has strengths. First, this is the first analysis evaluating a completely non-invasive method for estimating esophageal PTP. This is relevant because the main value of the PTPPOCC lies in the simplicity of its calculation and its capacity to non-invasively incorporate two key variables in the pathophysiology of muscle fatigue: respiratory rate and inspiratory time. Second, this is a multicenter study that included a heterogeneous cohort of patients with various reasons for intensive care unit (ICU) admission, allowing for the evaluation of the index across a wide spectrum of respiratory mechanics and inspiratory effort values, enhancing external validity. Third, several methodological aspects strengthen the findings: (a) PTPESO was used as the reference method, a widely validated technique; (b) a crossover design with randomization of pressure support levels was implemented; and (c) extended stabilization periods were established between each pressure change. However, the potential influence of confounding factors inherent to the crossover design such as changes in the patient’s clinical status or respiratory drive throughout the study, as well as possible carryover effects between the different pressure support levels, cannot be excluded.

This work also has limitations. First, the intra-individual variability of inspiratory effort implies that a single measurement might not represent the patient’s baseline state; to mitigate this, three occlusion maneuvers were averaged per evaluation phase, and the mean PTPESO from five preceding cycles were used. Second, the investigators were not blinded, which constitutes a potential source of bias. Third, the exclusion of five patients due to artifacts in the esophageal signal, while common in this type of study given the technical complexity of the method, could affect the generalizability of the results. Fourth, the limited sample size might restrict the statistical power for some secondary analyses and the applicability to specific subpopulations. Fifth, PTPPOCC inherits the methodological limitations inherent to the occlusion technique (ΔPOCC) in estimating muscular pressure. These include the inability to capture the isometric effort prior to the onset of inspiratory flow (especially relevant in patients with dynamic hyperinflation), the potential influence of expiratory muscle relaxation before the occlusion, and the non-inclusion of the chest wall’s elastic gradient, among others.

Sixth, airway opening pressure (AOP) was not assessed. As recently described by Docci et al. [22], the presence of airway closure may lead to an underestimation of the ∆PESO/∆PAW ratio used during the occlusion test to verify the adequacy of esophageal balloon positioning and the quality of the acquired PESO signal. Consequently, in patients with a PEEP level below AOP, the PESO used to validate PTPPOCC may have been erroneously calibrated. However, although we cannot completely rule out the presence of airway closure, this phenomenon is most commonly associated with obesity and ARDS. With an ARDS prevalence of only 4% in our cohort and body mass index values below the upper extremes typically associated with this phenomenon, we believe that its prevalence was likely low in our study population.

Conclusions

The non-invasive PTPPOCC index showed good performance for identifying extremes of respiratory effort in mechanically ventilated patients. Despite limited individual agreement, its strong association and high specificity may make it a useful tool for confirming very low or excessively high inspiratory effort and for identifying patients at risk of over or under assistance.
