4 Executive Summary
The Utility Managed Distributed Energy Resources Intelligent Community (UDERMS iCommunity) project, led by
PacifiCorp and supported by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Building Technologies Office (Award No. DE-
EE0009782), aimed to demonstrate an integrated, utility-managed “behind-the-meter” distributed energy
resource (DER) program connecting multifamily, commercial, institutional, and industrial buildings to deliver grid
services and improve energy efficiency.

Between project initiation on June 1, 2022, and termination on October 9, 2025, the UDERMS team completed
foundational design, modeling, and integration work across all major technical tasks for all of Budget Period 1 and
most of Budget Period 2. Achievements include grid modelling for all participating customer locations, the
configuration and testing of a Distributed Energy Resource Management System (DERMS) solution that was
integrated with an new Energy Management System (EMS) instance in a sandbox environment, deployment of
multiple Open Automated Demand Response (OpenADR) virtual top nodes (VTN) and virtual end nodes (VEN),
installation of advanced meter infrastructure (AMI) meters, connected to ~170 electric vehicle supply equipment
(EVSE) infrastructure at multiple pilot sites, detailed development and implementation of cybersecurity and
business model frameworks, demand response technology evaluation for water heater and HVAC retrofits, energy
efficiency (EE) modelling for all buildings, initial validation of hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulations
demonstrating distributed energy resource (DER) based grid services, and field testing of DER controls at the
microgrid facility.

The project successfully defined the control architecture, communication pathways, and grid service parameters
for frequency support, peak load management, and contingency reserves, while advancing EE modeling for six
participating facilities showing energy savings exceeding 30% relative to baseline. Key documents finalized prior
to termination include the DER initial control configuration, the inventory of all DERs and AMI installations, the plan
for business model development, Cybersecurity Plan (Phase B), Intellectual Property Plan, preliminary Evaluation
and Measurement (M&V) Plan, and all EE modeling reports.

Although the project was terminated prior to full field deployment and long-term demonstration, the work
completed established replicable frameworks for integrating DERs into utility operations and national connected
community initiatives. The technical progress and lessons learned from UDERMS provide valuable contributions
to ongoing industry and DOE efforts aimed at advancing secure, efficient, and scalable community energy
solutions.

5 Project Summary
The UDERMS iCommunity project planned to implement
a utility managed “behind the meter” DER control
program to integrate diverse types of buildings with a
range of flexible loads to optimize grid services and
improve building energy efficiency. The optimized grid
services included demand response such as peak load
management, frequency support, and contingency

to effectively integrate intermittent renewable resources,
enhance resiliency and outage response at the
distribution level, ensure resource adequacy particularly
during periods of disruptive events, and defer capital
expenses for distribution infrastructure upgrades that
serve the community of buildings. The objectives were to

5.1 Original Hypotheses
The project hypothesis was to demonstrate a combined 8.26 megawatts (MW) of flexible loads from the
participating buildings to be used for frequency support, demand response and other applicable grid services. The
EE objectives were to show that significant savings of at least 30% could be realized through design changes to
new buildings and operational improvements for existing buildings. Once the flexible loads and efficient savings
were demonstrated the plan was to develop viable business models that can be replicated across the country.

The team identified a diverse but representative set of buildings to include in the pilot project. The buildings range
from a large suburban apartment complex, downtown complex of mixed-use retail and apartments, University
laboratory and office building with a microgrid, a mass transit transportation center, manufacturing building, and
residential home. The buildings are all-electric and will have advanced energy efficiency technologies with efficient
heat pump-based HVAC (both central and mini-splits) and domestic hot water, adaptive building envelope, and
advanced lighting achieving a minimum of 30% energy efficiency compared to the baseline of typical buildings.
Each building contains multiple DERs with flexible loads, including renewable energy either onsite or part of a
community solar farm, battery storage, and electric vehicle charging equipment. Table 1 highlights the detailed
DERs, EE, and flexible loads at each building.

6.1.1 Task 1: Design Grid Services for DERs
The goal of this task was to develop and define the grid services for the UDERMS iCommunity project over multiple
time scales to support the distribution and bulk grid services. This was to be done through the development of a
detailed DER control strategy, supporting integrations, operational requirements, and integration designs to enable
peak load management, frequency support and contingency reserves. The specific supporting subtasks include:

 - Subtask 1.1.0: Develop DER control strategy
 - Subtask 1.2.0: Compile inventory of DERs and schedule for advanced metering infrastructure (AMI)
 installation
 - Subtask 1.3.0: Model distribution grid needs
 - Subtask 1.4.0: Design architecture and communication pathways

There were several actions taken to develop and define the grid services including: (1) development of control
parameters; (2) automation definition; (3) telecommunication and integration requirements; (4) development and
testing in the test environment of the EMS with the Open Systems International, Inc (OSI) Monarch and Integra
Distributed Energy Resource Management System (DERMS) solution; and (5) DER definition and configuration
requirements within the software.

6.1.1.1 Subtask 1.1.0: Develop DER Control Strategy

Grid Services Definition. The EMS support team worked to define parameters for frequency response control
such that thresholds for frequency could be set that would automatically trigger a frequency response event
through the EMS system if the frequency went above or below the defined allowable operating band over a certain
period of time. A calculation was developed to detect frequency events with the goal of catching most of the events
that fall under the BAL-003 reliability standards as defined by the North American Electric Reliability Corporation
(NERC). The speed at which frequency response events must occur dictated the telecommunication and
integration requirements for the DERs and further played a role with DER eligibility for this grid service.

While the initial grid services definition was focused on establishing requirements for the frequency support grid
service, PacifiCorp and the project team further defined the grid services through a series of grid services
workshops. The workshop meetings have covered the expected event response time, the duration of the event,
types of DERs most pertinent to each grid service, any telemetry and control signal requirements, and event
notification requirements. A high-level summary of the current definition set is shown in Table 2 below.

characteristics of various DERs including Solar, HVAC, and other energy consumption for a similar complex.

Concurrently, additional preliminary designs of optimization techniques for peak load management have been
completed incorporating the model for control of stationary storage and EV batteries combined with
representative usage of other DERs to maximize grid utilization while minimizing peak demands. Initial demand
response optimization-based strategies have been developed to incorporate signals from the utility to lower or
increase demand on the grid during defined windows. Additionally, investigation is ongoing into high- and mid-
fidelity models of DERs for facility control.

6.1.1.2 Subtask 1.2.0: Compile DER Inventory and AMI Installation Schedule

DER Device List. PacifiCorp and the project team completed a detailed DER device list for all existing and installed
DERs that are available for integration in the project. This list includes over 500 existing DER devices including
some DERs that were planned for replacement or additional control upgrades to meet the planned control
strategies defined for UDERMS. PacifiCorp and project team members compiled the known DER information and
augmented that information with additional details via initial site assessments. The site assessments provided
specific DER details including nameplate information, electrical connections, type of EVSE, model numbers, serial
numbers, communication identifiers, and specifics for any associated systems (i.e., mini-split HVAC, ducted split
systems, etc.). Through this process the project team identified updates to the DER installations and specifics that
were not detailed in other building models or diagrams. PacifiCorp also included the associated meter number for
each DER in the DER device list as this is needed for multiple tasks and evaluation in the future.

AMI Installation. PacifiCorp also worked with the PacifiCorp electric meter operations group to ensure that the
known and installed participating facilities were on the AMI meter deployment schedule. As of December 31,
2023, all installed and known controllable DERs and the associated main facility meters have been replaced with
AMI meters and programmed to collect 15-minute interval data. This was a significant effort that also included
evaluation of data to ensure that the data intervals and reporting were as expected. Additional metering
deployments were planned for additional DER device installations (i.e., smart thermostat deployments) or for
newly identified evaluation, measurement, and verification requirements.

6.1.1.3 Subtask 1.3.0: Model Distribution Grid Needs

Mapping Electrical Pathways. PacifiCorp has worked with the distribution engineering department to extract the
relevant distribution network model for each project location so that they can be analyzed by the project team. As
part of this process, PacifiCorp’s distribution engineering team also did an initial analysis of the electrical pathways

 9

between the substation and the participating buildings to understand the current state of the voltage, frequency,
and capacity for the grind infrastructure.

Grid Model. PacifiCorp compiled the utility network (CYME) files for the project locations. The CYME files were
extracted in a self-contained data version to include the detailed electrical mapping from the distribution network
to the substation, feeders, and participating locations as well as the other distribution parameters. The specific
service point locations for the participating buildings were clearly identified for use with the power flow analysis.
These files have been reviewed by the project team to determine compatibility for integration into other software
tools for network analysis (OpenDSS) and conversions from CYME to OpenDSS were completed. Additionally,
SCADA data for each of the substations and feeders were extracted to understand the maximum and minimum
loading data for each feeder and location to complete the baseline analysis including power flow analysis, voltage
fluctuation analysis, and short circuit analysis for each project location. The grid modelling work was completed
for all participating project sites.

6.1.1.4 Subtask 1.4.0: Design Architecture and Communication Pathways

Design Architecture. The PacifiCorp project team developed the first iteration of the solution and communication
in June 2023, and the initial draft was used for project discussions, DERMS implementation planning, and the first
round of the cybersecurity plan. Further discussions were held including meetings with Lawrence Berkeley
National Labs as the national coordinator to explain the project solution architecture and document it in a
consistent control framework across all the connected communities’ projects. A meeting was held on November
1, 2023, where PacifiCorp presented the project architecture, discussed internal and external systems, covered
the existing and planned communication pathways and protocols, and answered questions from other project
members and stakeholders. PacifiCorp’s UDERMS iCommunity is currently categorized as a hybrid archetype that
leverages both an aggregator and direct building / device integrations. The project team completed preliminary
simulation results for this hybrid building level/aggregator DER control archetype to simulate peak load
management with DERs including thermostats/HVAC, water heating, solar, EVSE, and battery for all project
locations.

Communication Pathways. PacifiCorp is focused on using open standard protocols and platforms for
communication pathways to support scalability, replicability, and interoperability. To that end, the UDERMS
project uses a combination of DNP3 (serial and IP), Open Automated Demand Response (OpenADR) 2.0b, Open
Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) 1.6, and VOLTTRON.

In 2023, PacifiCorp enabled controls with over 100 EVSEs through deployment, testing, and integrations
conducted with OCPP 1.6j and OpenADR 2.0b control signals. Detailed unit and field testing was completed to
demonstrate field controls of EVSEs. Additionally, testing was done at the USU microgrid facility with the
OpenADR lab virtual top node (VTN) communicating to the lab virtual end node (VEN), through OCPP to field EVSE
devices. Testing has included sending a real-time dynamic energy price from the utility-mimicking controller to the
microgrid test site and collecting reports on active power consumption at the site, which are stored in a database
for future use and analysis. By September 2025, the OpenADR VTN had been deployed at PacifiCorp in conjunction
with the EMS Sandbox DERMS and connectivity between the PacifiCorp VTN was completed with
communications verified to control all the DERs at the Microgrid facility.

6.1.2.3 Subtask 2.3.0: Pre-Deployment Occupant Recruitment and Experience Expectation
Significant pre-deployment planning was conducted for the customer experience baseline assessment. The
survey design was carefully crafted to ensure that the information and questions were tailored to the property,
including an understanding of the project and how customers perceive that they would be impacted by various
aspects of the project. The survey design also considered the time commitment for a survey respondent to ensure
that the time commitment was not viewed as a deterrent or barrier to participation in the survey. The survey
deployment planning was conducted to provide the highest level of survey awareness and opportunity for resident
participation while balancing survey deployment costs. Based on historical tenant engagement knowledge from
the property management company, the plan included a mix of digital and physical delivery. Residents that had an
email address on file were sent an email link to the survey while those that may not have email or see their emails
in a timely manner also had access and awareness of the survey through onsite flyers. In total, the survey was
deployed and made available to more than 600 multifamily residents to participate in the survey including
notifications through email, posted flyers in common areas, and posted flyers on individual tenant units.

Multiple meetings and sessions were also held with commercial property management and owners to discuss the
project, interest in project participation, expected goals and objectives from the project, and the baseline
operations for the area of the company that would be impacted.

6.1.2.4 Subtask 2.4.0 - Evaluate Consumer Preferences and Make Recommendations
The survey and occupant outreach were completed, and the results of the findings were captured in a document
for consumer preferences and recommendations. While limited, residents' responses regarding the project reflect
a spectrum of expectations and concerns. Many anticipated minimal personal impacts, foreseeing small
adjustments in energy usage that may not significantly disrupt their routines. There was some optimism about
potential benefits such as cost savings, improved environmental sustainability, and enhanced community
amenities like increased parking and better security measures for package deliveries. Some residents were eager
for technological advancements like smart home access and advanced lighting controls, viewing these
innovations as opportunities to improve efficiency and comfort. Overall, there was excitement about the potential
positive outcomes these projects could bring.

6.1.3 Task 3: Develop Cybersecurity Plan and Framework
The project team developed a detailed Cybersecurity Plan and framework for the project and provided updates on
this plan and framework on a regular cadence as the project progressed. Cybersecurity activities began in 2022
with the development of an initial cybersecurity framework that was discussed and reviewed among the project
team. The framework was also evaluated by BHE IT’s cybersecurity office (CSO).

An effective Cybersecurity Plan was documented based on the inventory of attack surfaces which can be exploited
on our project. An organization’s attack surface includes the many vulnerabilities, pathways, or methods (i.e.,
attack vectors) that threat actors can use to gain unauthorized access to the network or sensitive data, or to carry
out a cyberattack. For example, the inventory of attack surfaces for our project includes integrations between IT
systems as well as physical devices (e.g., EV chargers.)

6.1.3.1 Subtask 3.1.0: Develop Cybersecurity Plan
The Cybersecurity Plan was first completed and submitted on August 31, 2023. The plan was developed after
conducting in-depth discussions to document the detailed system architecture. By first developing a common
understanding of the system architecture for the project, the attack surfaces for the project became readily

Since August 2023, the project has gained additional insights into the interfaces, systems, and physical hardware

The cybersecurity framework and plan were implemented at the USU microgrid facility as part of the Budget Period
2 activities. In September 2025, PacifiCorp also conducted a 3rd party penetration test for all project systems

The Cybersecurity Plan was viewed as a living document that was updated at least every six months. The project
team completed the Phase A and Phase B cybersecurity checklists from PNNL’s cyber team and was working on
completing the Phase C checklist when the project was terminated.

6.1.4 Task 4: Develop Business Model Plan
In light of the significant transformations within the energy sector, driven by the swift adoption of DERs and
regulatory changes such as FERC Order 2222, the plan for a business model that leverages DERs for grid services
for aggregation and deployment at the wholesale market level is much needed. By advocating for this strategic
redirection, the plan positioned utilities to navigate and grow in the changing energy landscape, leveraging
opportunities for revenue generation through firm capacity, operating reserves, and the provision of energy.

This business plan also addressed the challenges and opportunities presented by the evolving energy sector
dynamics. As DERs become more prevalent, traditional utility models that rely heavily on energy sales are
becoming less viable. The plan, therefore, proposed innovative business models that incorporate DERs into
wholesale markets and utility planning, ensuring long-term sustainability and profitability. It stressed the need for
utilities to adapt to these changes by focusing on network management and ancillary services, thereby securing
their position in a rapidly evolving energy ecosystem.

PacifiCorp and project team members collaborated in sessions to discuss the approach to the business model
plan including meeting with the market policy and analytics department to engage the knowledge and expertise for
strategic guidance on this task. In its current state, the plan for the business model includes five core components:

 13

 1. Strategic Aggregation and Deployment of DERs. As part of the business model plan, the operational
 and technological frameworks are essential for effective DER aggregation. This includes the development
 of digital platforms for real-time management, the establishment of standardized communication
 protocols, and the implementation of advanced forecasting tools. Additionally, strategies for optimal DER
 deployment in wholesale markets are crucial. These strategies should take into account pricing models
 and the necessary investments in grid infrastructure to support DER integration.
 2. Facilitation of DERs as Non-Wires Alternatives (NWAs). As part of the business model plan, advanced
 grid analytics and GIS were to be utilized to identify locations where other DERs can serve as effective
 NWAs, specifically targeting areas experiencing congestion, aging infrastructure, or reliability risks. To
 guide the selection of NWA projects effectively, a comprehensive evaluation framework is essential. This
 framework should incorporate lifecycle cost analysis, grid impact simulations, and environmental
 assessments to ensure a thorough and informed decision-making process.
 3. Operational Coordination Models for TSO-DSO. As part of the business model plan, the merits and
 challenges of centralized versus layered structures for enhancing grid reliability and operational efficiency
 were to be investigated. It includes case studies and simulations to illustrate potential challenges and
 solutions. The document advocates for hybrid Distribution System Operator (DSO) models that
 amalgamate the strengths of both centralized and layered approaches. These models are to be supported
 by suitable technological and regulatory frameworks to ensure their effectiveness and sustainability.
 4. Integration into the Integrated Resource Planning (IRP) Process. As part of the business model plan,
 detailed scenarios considering technological advancements, market trends, policy changes, and
 consumer behavior would have been developed. An enhanced analysis framework, incorporating
 advanced modeling techniques and stakeholder engagement, will assess the robustness of each
 scenario.
 5. Implementation Roadmap. An implementation roadmap would have been developed that outlines
 short-term actions like launching a DER management platform pilot and initiating NWA feasibility studies,
 medium-term strategies for scaling successful pilots and formalizing support frameworks, and a long-
 term vision for a fully integrated DER-rich grid.
The business model plan is pivotal in transitioning to a DER-rich energy landscape, promising to enhance grid
reliability, flexibility, sustainability, and create new revenue streams and business opportunities for utilities.

6.1.5 Task 5: Analyze Scalability and National Replication Potential
As operator of the NASEO-NARUC GEB Working Group, NASEO engaged State Energy Offices, Public Utility
Commissions, and pertinent demand flexibility stakeholders on demand flexibility opportunities and impediments.
This included holding a NASEO annual meeting plenary session on October 18, 2023, in Portland, Oregon on
dynamic loads, virtual power plants, and connected communities’ session that featured speakers from U.S. DOE,
New Jersey Board of Public Utilities, California Energy Commission, and Google discussing demand flexibility,
Connected Communities, and related topics. NASEO staff also participated in a NASEO-NARUC State Microgrids
Working Group workshop in Bethesda, Maryland on October 3-4, 2023, which included a tour of the Brookville
Smart Energy Bus Depot microgrid, an integrated electric bus charging infrastructure project in Montgomery
County, Maryland. Demand flexibility and grid-interactive aspects were included in site visit and workshop
discussions and individual discussions with state officials. On November 3, 2023, NASEO attended virtually the
CalFlexHub Symposium on November 3, 2023, and on December 15, 2023, spoke with EPA ENERGY STAR staff
on the program’s Connected Criteria work. NASEO held other meetings and identified other resources pertinent

 14

to demand flexibility, grid-interactive functionality, and DER integration. While most of these activities were held
under separate project funding, they helped inform NASEO, the states, and this project on demand flexibility and
associated opportunities and impediments to deployment and scalability and replication potential for demand
flexibility, Connected Community, and related grid-edge solutions.

The learnings from these national forums served as inputs and details were included in the baseline scalability

6.1.6 Task 6: Data Coordination
PacifiCorp initiated monthly meetings for the data workstream portion of the UDERMS project to push the work
forward as needed in this task. Project stakeholders met on a monthly basis or more frequently to discuss data
related topics, conducted collaboration sessions, support project solutioning, and get updates on task and
deliverable status. The work on this task was also directly related to the work performed for Subtask 1.2.0 as
PacifiCorp and the project team reviewed and evaluated AMI meter data, deployment status of AMI metering at
the participating facilities, evaluation of OCPP data, and analysis of available BESS data.

The data coordination task was focused on data collection, data governance, data management, evaluation plan,
and the intellectual property plan. The data collection activities included understanding the specific data elements
that the project needs to capture for the various DER devices and the metering requirements including

Executing the defined UDERMS grid services across diverse devices at each project site required careful planning

 Figure 1: Network Architecture Diagram

OpenADR was leveraged as the main integration protocol before translating to device-specific protocols
downstream. There was also a second control signal originating from the “Grid Side” that steams from the
production DERMS platform using a DNP3 signal to control the Sonnen battery energy storage systems (BESS).
Improved communications between the sandbox environment and the production environment were slated as a

The connection between PAC and the USU EVR and microgrid facility relied on the OpenADR integration to send
the control signal to USU’s DERs and to receive telemetry reporting back from the facility. OpenADR was used
because it provides a standardized way to communicate between a utility and demand-side resources, and it was
easily extensible to provide a wide range of event and report types. OpenADR has two major communication
modes: pull mode and push mode. In pull mode, an OpenADR client, called a virtual end node (VEN) frequently
polls an OpenADR server, called a virtual top node (VTN) to check if the VTN has generated any new demand
response events. Conversely, in push mode, the VTN pushes demand response events to the VEN as soon as it
creates an event.

As shown in the figure above, the connection between the utility and the microgrid used both modes. For the
utility’s external connection to the VEN at USU’s microgrid facility, Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE) information
technology required the use of the push configuration in order to avoid the large number of requests from an
external VEN that would occur while using pull mode. However, the VTN connected to OSI’s EMS sandbox
deployed at PAC must operate in pull mode. The requirement to use pull mode to connect to the OSI VTN but push
mode in order to connect to locations external to the utility (e.g., USU’s microgrid VTN) necessitated the creation
of an OpenADR pull-to-push converter which lives on a server at RMP and helps connect the internal pull-VTN and
external push-VEN.

The pull-to-push converter had two antiparallel directions of data flow. To transmit OpenADR demand response
events from the pull-VTN to the push-VEN, the converter had to pull new events frequently from the push-VTN. It
did this using a pull-mode VEN. The converter’s pull-mode VEN ran in parallel with a push-mode VTN. When the
converter pulled a new OpenADR event from the pull-mode VTN, its pull-mode VEN passed along the event to its
push-mode VTN, which in turn pushed the event to USU’s push-mode VEN which was waiting to be pushed for
new events.

The second role of the pull-to-push converter was to pass telemetry reports from the push-mode VEN at USU to
the pull-mode VTN at RMP. This was handled similarly to the process used for passing events in the opposite
direction. The push-mode VEN at USU will generate telemetry reports and pass them to the converter’s push-
mode VTN at RMP. The converter’s push-mode VTN then wrote the new report to a stack of reports. The
converter’s pull-mode VEN then frequently checked the stack for new reports and repackaged the reports and
sent them to the pull-mode VTN. The pull-push converter had been tested successfully, demonstrating that it can
register reports received from the push-VEN with the pull-VTN, send new report update messages between those
two devices, and send events from the pull-VTN to the push-VEN.

In order to send telemetry reports from USU’s microgrid VEN to RMP’s VTN and to send events in the opposite
direction, firewall changes were made at both PAC and USU to allow the other to send web traffic through the
enterprise firewalls. The OpenADR connection between USU and RMP was successfully tested in August 2025,
with the USU VEN able to register itself with the RMP VTN, send reports to the RMP VTN, and receive events
pushed by the RMP VTN.

6.1.8.2 Subtask 8.2.0: Implement HIL models of distribution grid and DER

 Figure 4: HIL Simulation Model

USU developed a hardware-in-the-loop system to demonstrate the scalability of the microgrid’s demand
response capabilities. The work described in Task 8.1 demonstrated the communication and control techniques
required to integrate various DERs into the grid in order to provide grid services. Despite the large and varied DERs
under control as a part of this project, the total aggregation of all DERs was still a small fraction of the total load

 21

on the Western Interconnection, which means that the response of these resources would have a minimal impact
on the frequency of the system. One goal of the hardware-in-the-loop development is to model the impact of this
project’s DER control strategies at a larger scale, where the resources under control represent a much larger
fraction of the total interconnection’s load and generation.

This scaling-up of the project’s DERs was accomplished by taking the response of the DER devices in hardware
and applying them to a model of the grid in simulation that was much smaller than the real power system. This
includes a model of a local distribution network feeding the DERs and a model of a generation fleet whose
frequency changed as a result of imbalances between generation and load.

The distribution network is modeled in the Open Distribution Simulation System (OpenDSS). The network contains
a substation which feeds various distribution lines, uncontrolled loads, and DER devices. The power of the
uncontrolled loads is defined ahead of time, while the DER loads are updated in real-time using the data collected
from the real DER devices participating in this project. One additional input to the model is a source of a
disturbance in power, which can be a positive or negative value, representing unscheduled variations in total
system load. Key outputs of the distribution model include power flows on the power delivery equipment and
voltages at each node on the network. These outputs allow USU to estimate the quantity of grid services that can
be provided while constrained by the capacity of the distribution network, and how these devices can be used to
alleviate capacity challenges on the distribution network.

Another key output of the distribution model is the total power consumption of the network. This includes both the
controlled and uncontrolled loads but also the power losses in the distribution network that feeds the loads. The
losses are important for measuring the performance of the grid services at the regional level, as a change in power
output by a DER will result in a change in power equal to its measured power change plus the change in losses
induced elsewhere on the distribution network.

The total power change simulated in the distribution network is fed to a generator model, which models the
frequency of the synchronous grid. When power demand exceeds the scheduled total generation, the frequency
will decline, and the opposite will happen if demand is less than total generation. These dynamics are modeled in
Simulink, with a block representing a synchronous generator which follows the differential equation

The frequency modeled in Simulink by the generator block described above is sampled by a control block every
two seconds (0.5 Hz). The frequency error (the gap between the nominal and measured frequency) is given to a
proportional-integral controller which generates a control signal for the DERs to adjust output. In order to properly
model this full system in a way that accurately mimics the traditional control methods used by utilities to dispatch
such grid services, we use a similar OpenADR connection to deliver these control signals to the devices.

The power setpoint generated by the controller in Simulink is sent over UDP to another process on the same
machine which runs an OpenADR VTN. The VTN takes each new power setpoint and sends an OpenADR event to
a VEN which controls the devices as described in task 8.1. In this way, the HIL system can properly simulate a
power system and send commands to real hardware in real-time.

6.1.8.3.1.5 Electric Vehicles
EV load is managed by using OCPP 1.6 to send curtailment commands to vehicle chargers. The curtailment
commands contain a charge profile that specifies the intended shape of power draw, as well as the duration for
which the command is valid. After the command has expired the charger will fall back to a default charge profile,
or to normal free charging. Charge profiles may be set beforehand and last indefinitely, or they may be sent during
a charge session to dynamically impact charging behavior. The OCPP protocol provides semantic control interface
to the chargers which allows inner complexity to be managed by the charger itself. The USU microgrid facility uses
the default profile to set a hard limit on how much power can be pulled on any given day. When vehicles are
charging, in-session curtailments are used during the peak shaving process to reduce the load of the facility during
times of low solar, or otherwise high facility power usage.

based access details for each location and the Vulnerability, Threat, Risk, and a Mitigation Table that summarized
the identified vulnerabilities with their respective threats, the mitigation strategy associated to each vulnerability,
and the status of such strategy. The status of the mitigation strategies corresponds to:

 • Implemented – threat is mitigated due to implementation of the mitigation within the system
 • In Progress – the mitigation strategy is in the process of being implemented but it is still not completed
 • Transferred – the threat risk has been transferred to a different entity and is out of scope
 • Accepted – the threat risk is accepted, and no mitigation will be implemented
 • Pending – the threat and its mitigation are being considered by the project team and pending
 implementation

The project team was working to finalize the development and implementation of the mitigation strategies with
status Pending or In Progress. For instance, the project team was working on maintaining a detailed inventory of
software and hardware components and then making sure role-based access control was in place. Similarly,
components that integrate OpenADR were using TSL encryption in their communications.

6.1.12 Task 12.0: Evaluate Scalability and National Replication Potential
This work builds on the Baseline Scalability and Replicability Report from Budget Period 1, which examined
opportunities and barriers to scaling the UDERMS iCommunity approach. The report considered both the project’s
current context and the broader conditions that will influence adoption and adaptation by other utilities across
states and regions.

The analysis focused on key domains relevant to scalability, including:

 • Grid services design for distributed energy resources (DERs)
 • Cybersecurity frameworks
 • Evaluation, measurement, and verification (EM&V) methods
 • Environmental policies and regulations
 • ISO/RTO and wholesale market participation
 • Public policy objectives affecting DER integration

The report identified barriers to replication such as technology complexity, utility business models, regulatory
requirements, and the cost and valuation of grid services. It also emphasized the role of economies of learning.
Activities that are resource-intensive to establish the first time, such as cybersecurity planning, EM&V, participant
recruitment, and DER control strategies, become easier, less costly, and more effective with repetition and
knowledge sharing. These gains were to allow subsequent projects and utilities to scale more efficiently and avoid
duplicating effort.

While detailed quantitative lessons were not yet available, early findings suggest that replication will benefit from
standardized approaches to DER communication protocols, data pathways, and enrollment procedures.
Establishing these practices now creates a foundation for large-scale deployment and positions utilities to adopt
demand-flexible DER integration more effectively. Contextual factors such as policy environments, regulatory
frameworks, market design, and technology costs remain central in shaping utility motivation, and these will
continue to be key considerations for future replication efforts.

The evaluation conducted to date provides an initial framework for understanding the challenges and opportunities
that affect scalability and replication, satisfying Milestone 12.01. While it does not capture every barrier or
solution, it established a foundation that would have been expanded as additional project data and operational
experience had become available in Budget Period 3. This early work was to position the team to refine its
understanding, incorporate new insights, and strengthen the relevance of the framework as the project progresses.

6.1.13 Task 13: Implement Data Coordination
The data coordination task was focused on the consideration of sensor needs, data governance and the
implementation of the M&V assessment plan (Deliverable 6.0.2). These activities had postured the team with
Milestone 13.0.1 - “implementing the data M&V plan with data collection initiated from partner sites” completed
and on-track to produce Deliverable 13.0.1 - “Report on initial data collection as part of the M&V assessment
plan” by the end of the budget period. Task 8 and Task 13 also maintained a synergistic relationship throughout the

 34

budget period, enabling the alignment of DER control strategy, architecture, and communications with data
coordination efforts.

The consideration of sensor needs consisted of leveraging the updated network architecture diagram from Subtask
8.1.0 to understand how demand response signals would be communicated to each type of DER. This exercise
provided clear requirements for device solutioning. Specifically, water heater sensors were deemed necessary to
achieve measurements required by the M&V plan. This revealed that relays were necessary to integrate the water
heater temperature sensors with the ACE IoT controllers. Sensors and control devices that fulfill these
requirements were scheduled for Budget Period 3 procurement.

Data governance practices during this budget period included data acquisition from project partners through
secure channels (SFTP site with password and time-based access), secure data storage in an AWS S3 bucket,
and data anonymization for the data request placed on Connected Communities teams by the National
Coordinator in May 2025.

The implementation of the M&V plan consisted of solidifying a ground-truth list of DERs involved in the project,
bulk collection and inspection of data from project partners who have DERs active, and development of a relational
database designed to enable efficient calculations of the metrics laid out in Deliverable 13.0.1. The project team
is currently storing two years of one-minute resolution BESS/solar data from 600 residential batteries and PV units,
OCPP data from 181 EV chargers, 15-minute interval data from over 700 AMI meters, and BESS/solar data from
the USU microgrid facility which was the focal point of the Budget Period 2 Go/No-Go decision. As a part of the
extract, transform, load (ETL) process employed to manage incoming data, a data quality module was under
testing. This module reports on sampling period consistency (highlighting devices with missing data) and
compares measured values to device nameplate capacities to detect outliers.

Collectively, the accomplishments in Budget Period 2 have laid a strong foundation for continued progress in
Budget Period 3. The successful coordination of sensor requirements, secure and scalable data governance
practices, and the robust implementation of the M&V assessment plan have positioned the team to seamlessly
transition into active data collection and analysis. With Milestone 13.0.1 completed and Deliverable 13.0.1 on
track, the project team was poised to enter the next phase equipped with validated infrastructure, aligned control
strategies, and a growing repository of high-resolution DER data. These efforts not only fulfill key objectives but
had ensured that the team was well-prepared to meet the technical and analytical demands of Budget Period 3.
Data collection through AMI, OCPP, and BESS channels continued until project termination; no additional datasets
were added thereafter.

7 Conclusion
The UDERMS iCommunity project achieved significant progress toward its original objective of
demonstrating a utility-managed, grid-interactive community integrating DERs across diverse building
types. Through coordinated efforts among PacifiCorp, Utah State University, the University of Utah, Electric
Power Engineers, PNNL, OSI, and other partners, the project developed and tested technical
architectures, DER control strategies, cybersecurity frameworks, and data management structures
foundational to community-scale DER integration.

At the time of DOE’s termination of the award on October 9, 2025, all major Budget Period 1 and 2
milestones had been completed or substantially advanced, including successful HIL testing and the ability

 35

to control of 100% of DERs at the USU microgrid facility, installation of advanced metering infrastructure,
and initial execution of the cybersecurity framework. Planned field demonstrations, lighting and water-
heater control implementations, and extended data collection activities for Budget Period 3 were not
initiated.

Despite early closure, the UDERMS project produced a rich set of technical deliverables and cross-partner
learnings that remain applicable to future DOE Connected Communities and grid-edge programs. The
integration design, communication architecture, and cybersecurity methodologies serve as a tested
foundation for subsequent initiatives pursuing scalable, utility-managed DER coordination and community
energy resilience.
