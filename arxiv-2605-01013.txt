Modelling Intermediate-Current Transitions in Asymmetric-Valence Binary Electrolytes
Georgina C. Ryan, Mohit P. Dalwadi, Ian M. Griffiths[1ex] Mathematical Institute, University of Oxford, Oxford OX2 6GG, UK

Abstract
Asymmetric valences in a binary electrolyte can significantly affect the performance of systems such as reverse electrodialysis cells, batteries, and supercapacitors. To generate a theoretical understanding of this effect, we consider a steady one-dimensional Poisson–Nernst–Planck model of an electrolytic cell with imposed constant ionic fluxes, focusing on varying ion valences in a general asymmetric binary electrolyte. Numerical simulations reveal a smooth transition between the qualitatively distinct near-equilibrium and strongly non-equilibrium steady-state regimes. These regimes are distinguished by a valence-dependent transition point at an intermediate current where the classical Debye-scale boundary layer vanishes. We characterise this transition using asymptotic analysis, recovering the Gouy–Chapman and limiting-current results in the appropriate limits, and determining the correct transition results when neither is appropriate. We provide implicit solutions for the potential and ion concentrations of general asymmetric binary electrolytes and, notably, we provide explicit analytic expressions for the asymptotic composite solutions for 2 ​ z : z 2z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz , z : 2 ​ z z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621pt2z , and z : z z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz electrolytes. We show how the results can be presented in a collapsed phase diagram that can be used to predict qualitative intermediate-current steady-state behaviour in terms of ion valences and fluxes.

Introduction

The presence of multivalent ions can have a significant impact on the performance of electrochemical systems. For example, in reverse electrodialysis systems, where electricity is generated by mixing concentrated and dilute sodium chloride solutions, adding divalent ions to the electrolyte mixture reduces the open circuit voltage
[
8
,
44
,
35
]
. Moreover, these reverse electrodialysis systems are designed to generate energy from mixing natural water sources, which naturally contain multivalent ions
[
35
]
. In battery modelling, multivalent battery systems are a growing area of interest due to the potential of higher volumetric energy density than traditional Li-ion batteries
[
17
,
shahReviewRecentAdvances2025
]
. Additionally, the energy storage in supercapacitors is determined by the electrical double layer (EDL) at the electrolyte/electrode interface, and the properties of the EDL in a supercapacitor vary significantly with the valency of ions in the electrolyte
[
30
,
32
,
19
]
.

The impact of valence asymmetry on the fundamental properties of a binary electrolyte is a growing area of research. By studying modified Poisson–Boltzmann equations, Gupta et. al
[
19
,
18
]
found that asymmetric valences significantly impacted steric effects, dielectric decrement, ion–ion correlations, and relative ion motion in an electrolyte (diffusiophoresis and diffusioosmosis). From molecular dynamics simulations, Messias et. al
[
32
,
31
]
identified that asymmetric valence impacts many fundamental electrolyte properties, including EDL capacitance, cohesive energy, viscosity, and diffusion coefficients. Additionally, the coupling of diffusivity and valence asymmetry for the time-dependent Poisson–Nernst–Planck (PNP) equations with blocking-electrode conditions (i.e. not allowing a Faradaic current
[
6
]
) has been considered in flat-plate
[
2
]
and pore
[
21
]
geometries, with notable effects including producing oscillating diffusion layers and changing EDL charging timescales respectively.

Despite the noted effects and growing importance of valence asymmetry in electrochemical systems, the majority of mathematical modelling of ion transport in dilute solutions assumes a symmetric
z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz
electrolyte. This assumption creates symmetries in the governing PNP equations that significantly simplify the mathematics
[
5
,
34
]
.
Many foundational papers that have developed the field of asymptotic modelling of the PNP equations use the symmetric electrolyte simplification
[
9
,
5
,
6
,
10
]
. We note that solving the general
z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz
and the
1\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621pt1
electrolyte cases are equivalent under appropriate scalings in the non-dimensionalisation, so valence can be effectively scaled out of the mathematical problem in the symmetric case
[
5
]
. A simplification of this nature is not possible for asymmetric electrolytes; the valences must play some role in the dimensionless PNP equations.

The behaviour of an electrolytic cell can be divided into two distinct regimes: (thermodynamic) equilibrium and non-equilibrium
[
39
]
. Equilibrium refers to the regime where each ionic species has no net flux at the electrodes and hence there is no Faradaic current
[
34
]
. Non-equilibrium refers to systems with a Faradaic current, so there is a nonzero ionic flux at the electrodes and ion transport throughout the bulk. Non-equilibrium systems can be either transient (i.e. described by the time-dependent Poisson–Nernst–Planck equations) or steady. We use the term ‘strongly non-equilibrium’ to refer to systems that are approaching the limiting current, at which an electroactive ion is depleted to zero concentration at the electrode where it is consumed
[
34
]
.

Asymmetric valence electrolytes have been well-studied in the equilibrium case. In equilibrium, Gouy–Chapman theory describes the potential in the diffuse-layer of the EDL near an electrode as a solution to the Poisson–Boltzmann equation, a simplification of the PNP equations
[
16
,
13
]
. In the diffuse layer, the concentrations of both ion species are determined by the potential via a Boltzmann relation that depends on the ion valence
[
4
]
. There are only a few examples of analytic solutions to the Poisson–Boltzmann equation for asymmetric valence electrolytes in terms of elementary functions, namely the classic solutions for
z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621pt2z
and
2z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz
electrolytes at a single blocking electrode
[
16
,
1
]
. Analytic solutions for parallel-plate geometry have also been identified for symmetric and
2z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz
electrolytes in terms of Jacobi and Weierstrass elliptic functions respectively
[
46
,
45
]
.

Two studies relevant to our model have considered valence asymmetry in non-equilibrium systems. Firstly,
[
24
]
model the time-evolution of a general-valence PNP system, focusing on the coupling of EDL charging and redox reactions. The authors use asymptotic analysis to derive a set of differential equations that are much simpler to solve numerically compared to simulating the full PNP system. Since Jarvey et al. consider the time-evolution problem, their composite matching solutions for the concentrations and potential are given implicitly in the form of PDEs (bulk region) and ODEs and algebraic constraints (diffuse layers) alongside boundary and initial conditions.
The model explicitly includes a linear potential drop to model the Stern layer of the EDL and models redox reactions by setting the flux, which may be a function of the concentration and potential, at the boundary of the Stern and diffuse layers (i.e. the ‘outer-sphere approximation’
[
34
]
).

Secondly,

[
42
]
present a singular perturbation analysis of the PNP equations to find the steady-state electric potential of a general number of ion species and general valences. Their model uses Dirichlet concentration boundary conditions and unknown fluxes, a common choice for modelling ion channels in biological cells
[
25
,
47
,
39
]
. The solutions are presented in terms of implicit integrals and inverse functions, and are shown to agree with the classic
1\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621pt1

In this paper, we consider the steady state of a one-dimensional Poisson–Nernst–Planck model of an electrolytic cell. In contrast to
[
42
]
, we consider imposed constant ionic flux boundary conditions, focusing on varying ion valences in a general asymmetric binary electrolyte.
This choice is more appropriate than concentration boundary conditions for our model electrolytic cell because chemical reactions at the electrodes determine the ionic fluxes at the boundaries, rather than fixed concentration reservoirs. Using flux boundary conditions introduces additional mathematical complexity in order to fully close the problem, distinguishing this method from that taken by
[
42
]
. We note that choosing constant flux boundary conditions also makes the analytic analysis of the steady state of this system for asymmetric valences more mathematically tractable compared to existing models with nonlinear interfacial reaction boundary conditions
[
37
,
5
]
. Therefore, our minimal model provides a framework to examine, through an analytic approach, how asymmetric ion charges and fluxes alone impact the ionic concentrations and electric potential in a simple electrolytic cell. We provide a detailed analysis of the qualitative impacts of valence asymmetry on the system’s steady state.

In an initial numerical study of this system, we observe a smooth transition between the near-equilibrium and strongly non-equilibrium regimes, characterised by a transition point at an intermediate current where the classical Debye-scale boundary layer vanishes and the electric field is approximately constant throughout the cell. This transition point depends on the ionic valence ratio of the electrolyte. To investigate this phenomenon further, we solve the PNP equations for a general electrolyte using asymptotic analysis
[
7
]
. We produce explicit analytic formulas for the composite asymptotic solutions for the ionic concentrations and electric potential in the

,
2z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz

z\hskip-2.27621pt:\hskip-2.27621ptz

[
42

.
Further, we give implicit solutions for the diffuse layer in the general asymmetric case and evaluate these solutions numerically (with explicit solutions for the bulk region). Finally, we fully characterise the observed transition behaviour in terms of ionic valences and fluxes and represent this on a phase diagram that can be used to predict the qualitative behaviour of the system.

Non-dimensionalisation

We introduce the following scalings to non-dimensionalise the problem:

\displaystyle\hat{p}=\hat{C}\nu_{p}p,\quad\hat{n}=\hat{C}\nu_{n}n,\quad\hat{x}=\hat{L}x,\quad\hat{\phi}=\frac{R\hat{T}}{Fz_{n}}\phi,\quad\hat{V}=\frac{R\hat{T}}{Fz_{n}}V,

Firstly, for the

using

In general, (

and so cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions. However, in the cases where

[

using

To motivate why it seems as though only the
r=\{1/2,1,2\}
cases can be expressed in terms of elementary functions, we note that (
37
) is of the form
\int\mathrm{d}s/\sqrt{P(s)}
where
P(s)
is a finite Puiseux series
[
26
]
(i.e. a power series that may include negative or fractional exponents). Liouville’s theorem on elementary integration dictates that integrals where

is a cubic or higher-order polynomial are not representable by elementary functions
[
26
]
. The exception is if there is a repeated root in
P(s)
such that, after factorisation, the irreducible polynomial remaining under the square root is quadratic or linear. In this case, the integral is elementary
[
11
]
. This exception covers
r=1/2
and
r=1
, for which
P(s)
takes the form of a quartic polynomial with repeated roots.

The
r=2
case has a negative exponent in
P(s)
, so we cannot directly apply the same argument. However, an alternative non-dimensionalisation (where we scale by
z_{p}
instead of
z_{n}
in
[6d,e]
and (
8
)) would lead to a scenario where we can use this argument. Briefly, the alternative non-dimensionalisation leads to the scenario where, after writing the governing differential equation in the left boundary layer in terms of
p_{L}
, the resulting equation has a cubic form for
P(s)
with a repeated root, and hence has an elementary solution. Since this alternative non-dimensionalisation represents the same physical problem, the solutions under both non-dimensionalisations will be related by a simple change of variables. Hence, we expect an elementary solution in the
r=2
case with the scalings in
[6]
.

Right Diffuse Layer

We briefly outline the solution in the right boundary layer. Here, we transform to a boundary layer variable
\eta=O(1)
where
x=1-\varepsilon\eta
. We introduce notation to represent the leading-order contributions for the cation concentration
p(x)\sim\mathcal{P}(\eta)
, anion concentration

, and potential
\phi(x)\sim\varphi(\eta)
. Following an equivalent matching procedure to (

)–
[29a]
yields

\displaystyle p_{R}=\frac{(1-\mathcal{J})^{(1+r)}}{n_{R}^{r}},\quad\quad\varphi(\infty)=\phi_{0}(1)=\frac{1}{1+r}\log\left(\frac{p_{R}}{n_{R}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{1-\mathcal{J}}{n_{R}}\right).

(38a,b)

The restrictions that
p_{R}>0
in
[38a]
and
n_{L}>0
from
[29a]
necessitates that

There are now two degrees of freedom remaining:
n_{L}
and

from the outer solution (

n_{L}=\gamma^{2}n_{R}\quad\text{ where }\quad\gamma=\exp(V/2)\left(\frac{1-\mathcal{J}}{1+\mathcal{J}}\right)^{\frac{\mathcal{I}}{4\mathcal{J}}-\frac{1}{2}}.

Importantly, our solution procedure shows that information from each boundary layer (

) propagates through the outer region to the other boundary layer, affecting the solutions therein.

We proceed by solving the Poisson equation (

) in the right inner region,

\frac{\mathrm{d}{{}^{2}\varphi}}{\mathrm{d}{\eta^{2}}}=-\frac{\left(1-\mathcal{J}\right)^{(1+r)}}{n_{R}^{r}}\exp(-r\varphi(\eta))+n_{R}\exp(\varphi(\eta)),

(40)

using an analogous method to the left inner region (from (
31

36

n_{R}
. This process yields the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{R}}{\mathrm{d}\eta}=\text{sign}(\alpha_{R}-1)\sqrt{\frac{n_{R}}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{R}^{2(1+r)}}{r}u_{R}^{2-2r}+u_{R}^{4}-\frac{1+r}{r}\alpha_{R}^{2}u_{R}^{2}},

(41)

where

\displaystyle u_{R}(\eta)=\exp(\varphi(\eta)/2),\quad\alpha_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\mathcal{J}}{n_{R}}},

(42a,b)

with boundary and matching conditions,

u_{R}(0)=1,\quad u_{R}(\infty)=\exp(\phi(1)/2)=\alpha_{R}.

(43
a,b

The implicit solution in this boundary layer is therefore,

\eta=\text{sign}(\alpha_{R}-1)\,{\sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{R}}}}\int_{1}^{u_{R}(\eta)}\left(\frac{\alpha_{R}^{2(1+r)}}{r}s^{\,2-2r}+s^{4}-\frac{(1+r)}{r}\alpha_{R}^{2}s^{\,2}\right)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(44)

At this stage, there is one remaining degree of freedom,
n_{R}
, so the problem is not closed. We now derive the necessary closure condition to fix

Closing the problem

By deriving implicit solutions in the boundary layers, (
37
) and (
44
), we have determined implicit solutions for
\Phi(\xi)
and
\varphi(\eta)
by substituting back into

\displaystyle\Phi(\xi)=V+2\log(u_{L}(\xi)),\quad\varphi(\eta)=2\log(u_{R}(\eta)).

(45a,b)

We therefore have expressions for the potential and ionic concentrations over the whole domain and can write the composite asymptotic solutions at leading order via

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\sim\phi_{0}(x)+\Phi(x/\varepsilon)+\varphi((1-x)/\varepsilon)-\phi_{0}(0)-\phi_{0}(1),

(46a)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\sim c_{0}(x)+P(x/\varepsilon)+\mathcal{P}((1-x)/\varepsilon)-c_{0}(0)-c_{0}(1),

(46b)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\sim c_{0}(x)+N(x/\varepsilon)+\mathcal{N}((1-x)/\varepsilon)-c_{0}(0)-c_{0}(1),

(46c)

where
\phi_{0}
is defined in (
30
),
c_{0}
is defined in (
20
),
P,N
are defined in
[27]
, and

are defined by Boltzmann relations for the right inner region,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\mathcal{J})^{(1+r)}}{n_{R}^{r}}\exp(-r\varphi(\eta)),\quad\mathcal{N}(\eta)=n_{R}\exp(\varphi(\eta)).

(47a,b)

The solutions for the potential (
\phi_{0}
,
\Phi
and
\varphi
) still have one remaining degree of freedom,
n_{R}
. This is because the outer leading-order concentrations
p_{0}
and
n_{0}
are the same, so the two mass-conservation conditions [
12
e,f] represent a duplication of information at leading order.

One might expect that we would need to proceed up to
O(\varepsilon)
in our asymptotic analysis to close this problem. However, this would be overly cumbersome since the closure only requires considering the
O(\varepsilon)
contributions to the mass-conservation conditions. The mass-conservation conditions [
12
e,f] at
O(\varepsilon)
are

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!p_{1}(x;n_{R})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(P(\xi;n_{R})-c_{0}(0)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(\mathcal{P}(\eta;n_{R})-c_{0}(1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\eta,

(48a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!n_{1}(x;n_{R})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(N(\xi;n_{R})-c_{0}(0)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\eta;n_{R})-c_{0}(1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

(48b)

Therefore, the only additional contributions that we must determine at
O(\varepsilon)
in the mass-conservation conditions (
48
) are
p_{1}
and
n_{1}
in the outer region. Determining these contributions will generate an additional degree of freedom; however, since there are two equations in (
48
), there are sufficient constraints to close the problem.

To obtain solutions for
p_{1},n_{1}
, we substitute the outer region series expansions (
15
) into the Poisson equation (
13c
) and subsequently find that

p_{1}(x)=n_{1}(x):=c_{1}(x).

(49)

Therefore, the first integral terms in both mass-conservation equations (
48
) are equal, and we do not need to solve for
c_{1}(x)
explicitly to close the problem:
we can remove this degree of freedom by rearranging (
48
) to obtain

0=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\big(P(\xi;n_{R})-N(\xi;n_{R})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\big(\mathcal{P}(\eta;n_{R})-\mathcal{N}(\eta;n_{R})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

(50)

By evaluating the right-hand side of (
50
), we generate an equation for
n_{R}
that can be solved to close the problem.

In the
r=\{1/2,1,2\}
cases, the boundary layer concentrations are expressed in terms of elementary functions and (

for a range of

values (i.e.

) over a sufficiently large finite domain (i.e.

) for the integrals to converge. The following simplifications are helpful for calculating the concentration functions from the numerical solutions to (

\displaystyle P(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mathcal{J})^{(1+r)}}{(\gamma^{2}n_{R})^{r}}u_{L}(\xi)^{-2r},\quad N(\xi)=\gamma^{2}n_{R}u_{L}(\xi)^{2},

(51a,b)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{P}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\mathcal{J})^{(1+r)}}{n_{R}^{r}}u_{R}(\eta)^{-2r},\quad\mathcal{N}(\eta)=n_{R}u_{R}(\eta)^{2}.

(51c,d)

A fifth-order interpolating function is then constructed for the right-hand side of (

using

is used on the interpolating function to determine the value of

that satisfies (

[

]

\mathcal{I}
, the weighted current imbalance
\mathcal{J}
, the valence ratio
r
and the potential drop
V
. For example, these terms appear in the outer concentration solution
eq.

30
, and parameters defined in terms of
\mathcal{J}
and

appear in our boundary layer solutions
eqs.

37
and
44
. We recall further that the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
and weighted current imbalance
\mathcal{J}
implicitly depend on the valence ratio
r
.

First, we consider the impact of changing the weighted current imbalance

\mathcal{J}
controls the gradient of the concentrations in the bulk region but has minimal impact on the boundary layers. When
\mathcal{J}=0
, the concentration in the bulk region is spatially uniform and there is no net movement of particles in the system. When the weighted flux of anions,
rI_{n}
, is greater than the cation flux
I_{p}
(i.e. when
\mathcal{J}<0)
, the gradient is positive and vice versa. Therefore, when
\mathcal{J}>0
, the cations are relatively depleted in the bulk near the cathode (
x=1
) where they are consumed. In
fig.

\mathcal{J}

\phi
when the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
is fixed. The bulk potential increases slightly as
\mathcal{J}
increases and has a slight curvature change from being convex to concave.

In
fig.

5

\mathcal{I}=\{0.6,0.8,1.0,1.2,1.4\}
with a fixed weighted current imbalance
\mathcal{J}
. In
fig.

5
a,b, since the bulk ion concentrations
eq.

20
have no
\mathcal{I}
dependence, only the concentration boundary layers change as
\mathcal{I}
increases. For low currents, there is a greater concentration of cations at the cathode and anions at the anode (where they are consumed, respectively), consistent with being in a quasi-equilibrium slightly perturbed from Gouy–Chapman theory
[
40
]
. As
\mathcal{I}
increases, the concentration of cations depletes at the cathode and accumulates at the anode (and vice-versa for the anions). This process of accumulation and depletion in the boundary layers leads to a transition point where there are no visible boundary layers in the concentrations at
\mathcal{I}=1.0
for this electrolyte. As the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
increases further, the accumulation and depletion continues, producing boundary layers where the cations are now depleted at the cathode and accumulate at the anode (and vice-versa for the anions). This behaviour aligns with our physical expectations of observing a limiting current.

In
fig.

5
c, we see that the curvature of the potential
\phi
initially decreases (becoming more linear) as the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
increases. When
\mathcal{I}=1.0
,
\phi
is linear and there are no boundary layers. As
\mathcal{I}
increases further,
\phi
becomes non-monotonic. The gradient of the potential in the bulk region remains negative, so the bulk electric field continues to direct cations to the cathode and anions to the anode. However, the Poisson equation (
13c
) necessitates that the change in the relative amount of cations and anions corresponds to a change in the curvature of the potential in the diffuse layers. Therefore, the electric field changes direction throughout the cell at high currents.

Finally, we make some brief comments on the classical limiting current in this model. From the definitions of the boundary concentrations, such as
[29a]
and
[38a]
, we see that the limiting current condition of zero concentration at the boundary is achieved when
|\mathcal{J}|=1
. This corresponds to logarithmic divergences of the outer potential solution (
30
). The limiting current condition
|\mathcal{J}|=1
can be expressed equivalently as

|I_{p}-rI_{n}|=2(1+r).

(70)

For the classic example of a symmetric electrolyte with electroactive cations and spectator anions
[
29
,
27
]
, this becomes

|I_{p}|=4.

(71)

The factor of 4 appears in the classical current scaling to rescale the limiting current for
r=1
electrolytes to unity
[
33
,
14
,
10
,
9
]
. Therefore, (
70
) determines the values of the partial current densities
I_{p},I_{n}
that lead to a limiting current for general binary electrolytes. We note that this model’s strict limiting current condition is a restriction on the weighted current imbalance
\mathcal{J}
, not the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
(although, the appropriate boundary concentrations will tend to zero in the limit
\mathcal{I}\to\infty
). As an aside, we note that the
O(\varepsilon)
distinguished limit breaks down near the limiting current and the boundary region widens, allowing super-limiting currents
[
14
]
, so our observation of the limiting current in this model should be considered solely in the context of providing additional insight into the classical limiting current for asymmetric binary electrolytes.

From our asymptotic results, we can derive an analytic formula for the bulk potential value
\bar{\phi}
for a general binary electrolyte in the no-flux/blocking-electrode case (
I_{p}=I_{n}=0
). In this case, the right-hand side of (
21
) is zero. Therefore, the outer potential is an unknown constant
\bar{\phi}
that must be determined by matching into the boundary layers. The constant outer potential solution
\bar{\phi}
could be used as an effective boundary condition for situations where only information about the bulk potential is required with blocking electrodes.
We integrate the Poisson equation (
13c
) over the domain and apply the mass-conservation conditions to obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\phi}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\!p\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}\!n\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(72)

Transforming into our boundary layer variables
\xi,\eta
, (
72
) is equivalently written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}\xi}|_{{\xi=0}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}\eta}|_{{\eta=0}}.

(73)

Note that (
73
) applies for all values of
I_{p},I_{n}
and is automatically satisfied by our boundary layer solutions
eqs.

36
and
41
. In the no-flux case, (
73
) closes the problem and gives

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(r,V)=\frac{1}{1+r}\log\left(\frac{r\left(\mathrm{e}^{V}-1\right)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-rV}}\right).

(74)

Therefore, the bulk potential value
\bar{\phi}
in the blocking-electrodes case is determined by the valence ratio
r
and the potential drop
V
. We note that (
52
) is equivalent to (
74
) when
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}=0
.
The impacts of valence on this bulk potential (
74
) are most easily interpreted in dimensional quantities. Converting this bulk potential back to its dimensional form
\hat{\phi}_{0}
, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{z_{n}\to\infty}\hat{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z_{n}\to\infty}\frac{RT}{Fz_{n}}\bar{\phi}\left(\frac{z_{p}}{z_{n}},\frac{Fz_{n}}{RT}\hat{V}\right)=\hat{V},

(75)

\displaystyle\lim_{z_{p}\to\infty}\hat{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z_{p}\to\infty}\frac{RT}{Fz_{n}}\bar{\phi}\left(\frac{z_{p}}{z_{n}},\frac{Fz_{n}}{RT}\hat{V}\right)=0.

(76)

Therefore, as the magnitude of the anion valence increases, the dimensional outer potential increases, and vice versa for the cation valence.

Characterising the intermediate-current transition

From our asymptotic analysis, we have shown that the qualitative behaviour of the ion concentrations and electric potential depend on the weighted total current
\mathcal{I}
, the weighted current imbalance
\mathcal{J}
, and the valence ratio
r
. We now use the asymptotic results to characterise the transition behaviour seen in our numerical simulations by collapsing our results onto a phase diagram of
\mathcal{I}
versus
\mathcal{J}
in
fig.

7
.

To create the phase diagram, we first divide the behaviour we have observed in our numerical results (see
fig.

2
) into two distinct qualitative regimes: the near-Gouy–Chapman regime (GC), where cations accumulate at the cathode relative to the bulk, and the limiting-current regime (LC), where cations deplete at the cathode relative to the bulk. The observed transition where boundary layers disappear separates these two regions.

The physically relevant values for
\mathcal{J}
to consider for the phase diagram correspond to
I_{p},I_{n}\geq 0
(fluxes at the boundaries in the expected directions) and
|\mathcal{J}|<1
(below limiting current). Therefore, we impose the following physical restriction on
\mathcal{J}
,

-\min\!\left(1,\frac{\mathcal{I}r}{2}\right)\;<\;\mathcal{J}\;<\;\min\!\left(1,\frac{\mathcal{I}}{2}\right).

(77)

When
\mathcal{J}=0
, the system has a uniform unit bulk concentration (see (
20
)) and therefore the transition point occurs for
p_{L}=n_{L}=1
and
p_{R}=n_{R}=1
. Using the relationship between
n_{R}
and
n_{L}
in the
\mathcal{J}=0
case (
53
), we immediately see that the transition point for a general electrolyte when
\mathcal{J}=0
occurs at

\mathcal{I}=V.

(78)

Next, we derive an explicit expression for the transition line between Region GC and Region LC. From the definition of
p_{L}
in terms of
n_{L}

[29a]
, we find that the transition where
p_{L}=n_{L}
occurs when
n_{L}=1+\mathcal{J}
. Likewise on the right side,
p_{R}=n_{R}
when
n_{R}=1-\mathcal{J}
. Therefore, substituting these critical values of
n_{L},n_{R}
into the expression for
n_{L}
in terms of
n_{R}
(
39
), we find the transition between Region GC and Region LC occurs at a critical current,

\bar{\mathcal{I}}=\frac{2\mathcal{J}V}{\log(1+\mathcal{J})-\log(1-\mathcal{J})}.

(79)

The range of physically relevant
\mathcal{J}
values on the transition line is

\mathcal{J}\in[-\tanh\left(rV/2\right),\tanh\left(V/2\right)].

(80)

The extremal transition values of
\mathcal{J}
tend to
\pm 1
in the limit
V\to\infty
. Therefore, ionic concentrations in Region GC can tend to zero in the limit of infinite voltage. However, in our minimal model (ignoring the change in distinguished limit near the limiting current
[
14
]
), a limiting current is achievable for systems in Region LC with a finite voltage.

Conclusion

We have considered a one-dimensional steady-state Poisson–Nernst–Planck (PNP) minimal model of an electrolytic cell with constant-flux boundary conditions for a general binary electrolyte with valence ratio
r=z_{p}/z_{n}
, where
z_{p}
and
z_{n}
are the cation and anion valences, respectively. The constant-flux boundary conditions represent Faradaic reactions at the electrodes and hence a current throughout the cell (denoted by
I_{p}
for the cation concentration and
I_{n}
for the anion concentration), separating this work from studies of valence asymmetry with the Poisson-Boltzmann equations. From an initial numerical study of our minimal PNP equations, we identified a continuous, smooth transition from agreeing with Gouy–Chapman theory at zero current to limiting-current behaviour at high currents. We identified a transition point at a critical intermediate current that separates two distinct qualitative regimes (one where cations accumulate at the cathode relative to the electroneutral bulk concentration, and another where they are depleted). At this critical current, the potential has a linear profile, so the electric field throughout the cell is constant, and the electrolyte is electroneutral over the whole domain. We found that the valence ratio
r
controls this transition.

In order to characterise how the valence ratio
r
impacts the system, we reduced our minimal model using asymptotic analysis. This approach was possible because of the smallness of the parameter
\varepsilon
, which represents the ratio of the characteristic length of the diffuse layer near an electrode to the length of the cell, in the dimensionless PNP equations. We derived solutions for the cation concentration
p
, anion concentration
n
, and electric potential
\phi
in three distinct regions: the electroneutral bulk and two diffuse layers (boundary layers), one near each electrode. Through our analysis, we have characterised how this minimal model depends on the valence ratio
r
explicitly and also implicitly through two dimensionless parameters
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
, which represent a dimensionless weighted total current and dimensionless weighted current imbalance, respectively, and which contain the cation and anion fluxes.

For our model, we produced explicit analytic expressions for the composite asymptotic solutions for the
r=\{1/2,1,2\}
cases and we additionally generated implicit solutions for asymmetric electrolytes for any value of
r
. Our implicit solutions are simple to evaluate numerically for any value of
\varepsilon
, as our asymptotic techniques allow us to systematically scale out this small parameter from the appropriate reduced governing differential equations we derive. This overcomes a key difficulty faced by numerical modelling of stiff PNP systems. With slight modifications provided in the Supplementary Material, these solutions can also be used with Dirichlet concentration conditions, which are relevant to many mathematical biology applications, such as ion channels in cell membranes. While implicit asymptotic solutions for concentration boundary conditions for general electrolytes are known
[
42
]
, explicit elementary function representations of the
r=\{1/2,2\}
solutions are valuable as they require no additional numerical calculations.

We produced a phase diagram (
fig.

7
) that identifies whether the system will display the behaviour of cations accumulating or depleting at the cathode relative to the bulk (signifiers of the two distinct qualitative regimes) in terms of the valence ratio
r
and the cation and anion concentration fluxes
I_{p},I_{n}
. This phase diagram explains how binary electrolytes with different valence ratios can show significantly different qualitative behaviour for identical cation and anion fluxes, explaining observations that we had made from initial numerical simulations.

A natural extension to this work would be to incorporate Butler–Volmer reaction conditions
[
34
,
37
]
to explore how this valence ratio-dependent transition behaviour persists once interfacial reaction kinetics can limit the transport-driven effects. Additionally, we could extend the time-evolution work of
[
24
]
to explore how the system evolves temporally to the steady-states that we have found for different valence ratios. The model in
[
24
]
also captures the Stern layer, which we could incorporate into our analysis.

Our model analysis has shown that the valence ratio of a binary electrolyte determines many key qualities of the steady-state ionic concentrations and electric potential, including how the system transitions from the Gouy–Chapman regime towards limiting current behaviour as the total current increases. Our results provide a comprehensive picture of how multivalency in a binary electrolyte can play a fundamental role in the steady state of non-equilibrium electrochemical systems.
