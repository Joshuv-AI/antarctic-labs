Quadratic Objective Perturbation: Curvature-Based Differential Privacy
Daniel Cortild \orcidCortild ( daniel.cortild@maths.ox.ac.uk ) Coralia Cartis \orcidCartis ( coralia.cartis@maths.ox.ac.uk ) Mathematical Institute, University of Oxford, Oxford, United Kingdom

Abstract
Objective perturbation is a standard mechanism in differentially private empirical risk minimization. In particular, Linear Objective Perturbation (LOP) enforces privacy by adding a random linear term, while strong convexity and stability are ensured by an additional deterministic quadratic term. However, this approach requires the strong assumption of bounded gradients of the loss function, which excludes many modern machine learning models. In this work, we introduce Quadratic Objective Perturbation (QOP), which perturbs the objective with a random quadratic form. This perturbation induces strong convexity and enforces stability of the problem through curvature, thereby enabling privacy and allowing sensitivity to be controlled through spectral properties of the perturbation rather than assumptions on the gradients. As a result, we obtain ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -differential privacy under weaker assumptions, in the interpolation regime. Furthermore, we extend the analysis to account for approximate solutions, showing that privacy guarantees are preserved under inexact solves. Additionally, we derive utility guarantees in terms of empirical excess risk, and provide a theoretical and numerical comparison to LOP, highlighting the advantages of curvature-based perturbations. Finally, we discuss algorithmic aspects and show that the resulting problems can be solved efficiently using modern splitting schemes. Keywords. Differential Privacy, Objective Perturbation, Random Matrix Theory, Three-Operator Splitting.



1
Introduction

Modern machine learning is largely driven by Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) problems, where a model is obtained by minimizing a sum of losses over a dataset. Formally, given a dataspace
\mathcal{X}\subset\R^{p}\times\R
and a dataset
\D=\{z_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\X^{n}
of
n
individuals, we consider the problem

\theta_{\exact}=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\left\{\mathcal{J}(\theta;\D)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\theta;z_{i})+r(\theta)\right\},

(P)

where
\ell\colon\R^{d}\times\X\to\R
is a loss function convex in its first argument for any given datapoint
z\in\X
,
r\colon\R^{d}\to\R
is a convex regularizer that may depend on
n
but is otherwise independent of the dataset, and
C\subset\R^{d}
is a closed convex constraint set. In many applications, the parameter space is naturally constrained, for instance to enforce structural properties such as boundedness or feasibility. From a theoretical perspective, the constraint helps ensure well-posedness in the absence of strong convexity. This formulation captures a wide range of problems, from classical linear models to modern overparametrized systems. In many such settings, especially in large dimensions, the problem exhibits many difficulties; minimizers are in general not unique, curvature may be highly anisotropic, and classical regularity assumptions such as strong convexity or bounded gradients often fail. We refer to
[
25
]
and the references therein for a more complete overview.

Empirical risk minimization is often performed on sensitive data, which we aim to protect through the framework of
Differential Privacy
(DP)
(
14
)
. Informally, DP guarantees that an adversary cannot reliably infer whether any particular individual contributed to the dataset. Achieving this guarantee, however, comes at a price in terms of utility, especially in the absence of strong regularity, in which case the minimizers might be highly sensitive to perturbations in the data. Balancing privacy and utility has been a central challenge since the introduction of DP, and our work places itself within this line of research. Existing approaches mostly rely on the strong assumption of bounded gradients of the loss functions, which excludes many models and is unrealistic in modern machine learning. A core contribution is that we do not make such an assumption.

Linear Objective Perturbation.

The closest and most relevant prior work is
linear objective perturbation

(
7
;
22
)
, in which the private problem is obtained by adding a random linear perturbation to the model. Specifically, the mechanism considers

\theta_{\priv}^{\lin}=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{\priv}^{\lin}(\theta;\D)=\J(\theta;\D)+\frac{\Delta}{2}\|\theta\|^{2}+a^{T}\theta\right\},

(P-Priv-Lin)

where
a
is a random vector enforcing privacy, and
\Delta>0
is a deterministic scalar ensuring a unique solution. As linear perturbations might significantly shift the minimizer, the deterministic quadratic perturbation is required to achieve stability of the problem. Furthermore, this approach requires the strong regularity assumption of bounded gradients of the loss functions to achieve privacy, to ensure limited individual contributions of each datapoint. This is because the random perturbation does not control the curvature, and hence they must assume bounded variability instead. This suggests that a random linear perturbation might not be the right choice. Our model avoids this assumption, by simultaneously handling privacy and stability through the same perturbation.

The idea of linear objective perturbation was originally introduced in
7
, for pure
\varepsilon
-privacy, and later extended in
22
for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy. The empirical risk bounds were improved in
21
, and an excess population loss was provided in
4
. Practical aspects were taken into account in
20
, who showed that privacy is preserved even under inexact solves. More recently, in
28
, the notion of
per-instance differential privacy
was studied, and in
27
, a new analysis through Rényi differential privacy was provided, showing improved privacy guarantees.

The strong assumptions required in the above works suggest that linear perturbations are fundamentally insufficient in our setting, motivating the need for an alternative perturbation mechanism.

Quadratic Objective Perturbation.

To enforce privacy, we propose the
Quadratic Objective Perturbation Mechanism
. Instead of solving Problem (
P
), we draw a random
d\times d
matrix
W\sim\mathcal{W}
and solve instead the perturbed problem

\theta_{\priv}=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\left\{\Jp(\theta;\D)=\mathcal{J}(\theta;\D)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}W(\theta-\theta_{*})\right\},

(P-Priv)

where
\theta_{*}
is a common minimizer of all the loss functions
\ell(\cdot,z)
for
z\in\mathcal{X}
. The existence of a
\theta_{*}
is known as the
interpolation regime
, in which the model is sufficiently expressive to fit each datapoint individually. This assumption is standard in modern overparametrized learning, including deep neural networks and kernel methods, where zero training loss is often observed in practice
(
6
;
3
)
. We note this assumption is required for the privacy analysis, and is not necessary for the utility analysis.

The key feature is that the same random perturbation simultaneously provides privacy, controls the stability of the problem, and induces strong convexity. Unlike the previously mentioned linear perturbations, which shift the minimizers, the random quadratic perturbation modifies the curvature of the objective, ensuring the sensitivity of the output is controlled through the curvature of the objective rather than through assumptions on the gradients. As a result, we can guarantee privacy without requiring bounded gradients of the loss functions.

In practice, the perturbed problem is not solved exactly, and we instead allow for approximate solutions. Moreover, we assume access to an approximation
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
, for instance by warm-starting from a previous run, from a model trained on a similar dataset, or as part of a fine-tuning procedure
(
11
;
19
)
. Such approximations naturally arise in large-scale learning pipelines, where models are updated incrementally and solutions from previous iterations provide good initializations. As such, we approximately solve

\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\left\{\tJp(\theta;\D)=\mathcal{J}(\theta;\D)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{*})^{T}W(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{*})\right\}.

(P-Priv-App)

While approximate solves are known to degrade privacy guarantees, we show that meaningful guarantees can still be obtained.

We complement our theoretical findings with numerical experiments, showing that QOP exhibits a fundamentally different scaling behavior compared to LOP. In particular, while LOP’s risk increases with the constraint diameter, QOP remains stable, highlighting the advantage of curvature-based control over gradient-based control.

Related Works.

Besides objective perturbation, other ideas have been developed to ensure private ERM. We highlight output perturbation, which adds noise to the final solution
(
14
;
7
;
32
;
23
)
, gradient perturbation, which injects noise throughout the algorithm
(
5
;
29
;
1
;
4
)
, functional perturbation, which perturbs a representation of the objective
(
33
)
, input perturbation, which perturbs the data prior to training
(
12
;
16
)
, and sampling-based methods, such as the exponential mechanism, which selects outputs at random proportionally to the exponential utility score
(
24
)
.

Contributions.

Our contributions may be summarized as follows:

1.

We introduce a new objective perturbation mechanism based on random quadratic perturbations, eliminating the need for bounded gradient assumptions required by prior work.

2.

We develop a novel privacy analysis showing that the quadratic perturbation controls the sensitivity of the output through curvature, yielding
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-privacy under weaker assumptions than prior work, in the interpolation regime.

3.

We extend the method to approximate solves while preserving meaningful privacy guarantees.

4.

We provide utility guarantees by deriving bounds on the empirical excess risk.

Assumptions and Preliminaries

In Section
2.1
we formally state the problem assumptions. Before providing a privacy analysis, we define the notion of privacy and give some introductory properties in Section
2.2
. Our analyses, both of privacy and of utility, heavily rely on spectral properties of the random perturbation to control the optimization problem. Preliminaries from random matrix theory are given in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Problem Assumptions

Our goal is to solve the Problem (
P-Priv-App
). We make the following assumptions on the problem.

{assumption}
[Problem Assumptions]
We assume that

•

for each datapoint
z\in\X
, the loss function
\ell(\cdot;z)\colon\R^{d}\to\R
is convex, twice differentiable, is
L
-smooth (i.e. has
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradients), and has a Hessian with rank at most
\rho\leq d
;

•

the loss functions share a common minimizer, namely

\Theta_{*}=\bigcap_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\argmin_{\theta}\ell(\theta;z)\neq\emptyset
;

•

the constraint set
C\subset\R^{d}
is a closed and convex set; and

•

the regularizer
r\colon\R^{d}\to\R
is convex.

This assumption restricts the DP threat model to datasets without conflicting records. If violated, our formal guarantees no longer hold. We will explore the consequences of violating this assumption in Section
5.2
.

Approximate Solutions.

We now formalize the approximation errors arising both from estimating a common minimizer of the loss functions and from solving the perturbed problem. Firstly, we assume access to a point
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
, which remains hidden from the user, that satisfies

\dist(\tilde{\theta}_{*},\Theta_{*})\leq\eta.

(1)

We then define
\theta_{*}\in\Theta_{*}
such that
\|\tilde{\theta}_{*}-\theta_{*}\|\leq\eta
.

Secondly, we do not claim to solve Problem (
P-Priv-App
) exactly, but merely that we can solve it up to
\tau
-accuracy. Specifically, we mean that we identify a point
\theta_{\operatorname{app}}\in C
that satisfies

\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)=\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\min_{\theta\in C}\tJp(\theta;\D)\leq\tau.

(2)

While this criterion is not a practical stopping rule, it is sufficient for our analysis. We introduce a computable surrogate implying it in Appendix
G.1
.

Notation.

We denote by
\lx@sectionsign^{d}
and
\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}
the set of symmetric and symmetric positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices. The various optimization problems and their solutions are summarized in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Differential Privacy

We use the notion of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy, introduced in
14
. Intuitively, a randomized mechanism provides DP if it produces similar outputs on similar datasets. The following definitions formalize the notion of
similar datasets
and of
differential privacy
.

Definition 2.1

(Replacement Adjacency)
.

We say two datasets
\D
and
\D^{\prime}
are replacement adjacent, denoted by
\D\sim\D^{\prime}
, if they have the same number of datapoints and differ in at most one of them.

Definition 2.2

(Differential Privacy)
.

We say a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
provides
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if, for any two datasets
\D\sim\D^{\prime}
and any measurable set
S\subset\ran(\mathcal{M})
, it holds that

\lx@paragraphsign(\mathcal{M}(\D)\in S)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\lx@paragraphsign(\mathcal{M}(\D^{\prime})\in S)+\delta,

where
\mathcal{M}(\D)
and
\mathcal{M}(\D^{\prime})
are the outputs of
\mathcal{M}
under the data
\D
and
\D^{\prime}
.

Much work on differential privacy has been carried out since its introduction, and many results about enforcing privacy have since been introduced. We present some useful results that we will make use of in our main proof in Appendix
B
. For an overview of these results and a more thorough introduction to differential privacy we refer to
13
and the references therein.

2.3
Random Matrix Theory

In order to formally state Problems (
P-Priv
) or (
P-Priv-App
), we must first draw a random matrix
W\sim\mathcal{W}
from some distribution
\mathcal{W}
, which enforces privacy and controls the stability of the problem. We make some assumptions on
\mathcal{W}
, and show in Lemma
2.3
an example that satisfies it.

{assumption}

[Random Matrix Assumption]
Let
W
be distributed according to a probability distribution supported on
\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}
with density
q\colon\lx@sectionsign^{d}_{+}\to\R_{+}
. We assume that, given
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{3}
, and
\delta_{4}
, there exist
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\mu
such that

\lx@paragraphsign(\lambda_{\min}(W)\geq\alpha)\geq 1-\delta_{3},\quad\lx@paragraphsign(\lambda_{\max}(W)\leq\beta|\lambda_{\min}(W)\geq\alpha)\geq 1-\delta_{4},

\frac{\lx@paragraphsign(\alpha\leq\lambda_{\min}(W)\leq\alpha+\alpha_{1})}{\lx@paragraphsign(\lambda_{\min}(W)\geq\alpha)}\leq\delta_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\Exp{W}\preceq\mu I.

Moreover, we suppose that there exists a monotone
f\colon\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\R
such that, provided
\lambda_{\min}(W)\geq\alpha
,

\frac{q(W+U)}{q(W)}\leq e^{\|U\|_{\op}\cdot f(\rank(U))}\quad\text{for all $W\in\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}$ and $U\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}$ such that $W+U\in\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}$}.

Wishart Ensemble.
We denote by
\Wishart(d,m)
a
d\times d
Wishart matrix with hidden dimension
m
, namely that, for
G\in\R^{d\times m}
with entries
G_{i,j}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
W=GG^{T}\sim\Wishart(d,m)
.

The following lemma is a simplified version of Lemma
D.4
, which provides the exact values of all the required constants. For technical reasons the full lemma is postponed to the appendix.

(Wishart Distribution Satisfies Random Matrix Assumption)
.

Let
W\sim\Wishart(d,m)
be a
d\times d
Wishart matrix with hidden dimension
m>d
. Then the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
is satisfied for suitable values of
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\alpha_{1}
,
f
and
\mu
given in Lemma
D.4
.

3
Quadratic Objective Perturbation: Privacy and Utility Guarantees

Our approach to privacy relies on perturbing the objective through a random quadratic form, which modifies the curvature of the problem. This allows us to control the sensitivity of the output through spectral properties of the perturbation, rather than through bounded gradients. We now formulate our mechanism, and provide privacy (Section
3.1
) and utility (Section
3.2
) guarantees.

{algorithm}

[H]

\floatname
algorithmMechanism

(Quadratic Objective Perturbation Mechanism)

{algorithmic}

\Require
target privacy parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, solver accuracies
\tau,\eta
, and noise parameters
\sigma^{2},\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
.

\State
Compute, independently of
\D
, an
\eta
-approximate point
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
satisfying Equation (
1
).

\State
Draw
W\sim\mathcal{W}
and
b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma}^{2}I)
independently.

\State
Compute a
\tau
-accurate solution
\theta_{\operatorname{app}}
to Problem (
P-Priv-App
) satisfying Equation (
2
).

\State
Release

\theta_{\final}=\theta_{{\operatorname{app}}}+b
.

The anchor point
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
is assumed to be independent of the dataset
\D
, and is therefore computed from public or disjoint data. It may, in practice, be obtained from an already trained model, a warm start from a previous optimization run, or a fine-tuned checkpoint. This is standard in modern machine learning, where exact or near-zero training loss is often reached. The value of
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
, as well as the realizations of
W
and
b
, remain private throughout the mechanism, and are never exposed to the user.

We now delve into the privacy and utility properties of this mechanism.

3.1
Privacy Analysis

We now state our main result, the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-privacy guarantee of our mechanism. Intuitively, privacy is preserved if the noise level associated with the curvature addition and with the approximate release are sufficiently high. The following theorem provides quantitative parameters to ensure this.

Theorem 3.1

(Differential Privacy of Mechanism
3
)
.

Assume that the Problem Assumption
2.1
and the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
hold. Then Mechanism
3
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private, provided

\sigma^{2}\geq\max\left(\frac{2L}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left(f(2)+\frac{2(2\rho+2)}{\alpha}\right),\frac{2L}{\alpha_{1}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\sigma}\geq\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}}+\frac{\beta\eta}{\alpha}\right)\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{2}}\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta_{2})},

where
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
,
\delta=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}+\delta_{4}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\alpha_{1}
and
f(2)
are given in the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
.

Proof 3.2
.

We provide a proof idea here, and the full details are available in Appendix
E
.

We note that the perturbed objective function does not necessarily have a unique minimizer. We thus condition the mechanism on
W
having uniformly bounded below eigenvalues, making the perturbed objective strongly convex and hence have a unique minimizer. As this occurs with high probability, the Conditional Mechanism Lemma
B.3
guarantees that privacy is preserved.

In order to prove that the release of
\theta_{\final}
is private, we shall show that the joint release of
\theta_{\priv}
and
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}
is private, by applying the Composition of Mechanisms Lemma
B.2
. The latter two releases imply the first release, and hence can only worsen privacy. This idea is inspired by
20
. As such, the proof is split into two parts, proving the privacy of the release of
\theta_{\priv}
and proving the privacy of the release of
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}
;

1.

To show that the release of
\theta_{\priv}
is private, we first show it is private under a simplification assumption, namely that the regularizer
r
is twice differentiable and that the constraint set
C=\R^{d}
. This is the main novelty of the proof, presented in Lemma
E.1
, and largely extends the ideas in
7
and
22
. We then apply the Successive Approximation Lemma
B.4
to remove the simplification assumptions one-by-one, as done in
22
. This is described in Lemma
E.3
.

2.

To show that the release of
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}
is private, it is sufficient to note that
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}=\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}+b
, where
b
is Gaussian noise. In this step, we additionally condition the mechanism on
W
having eigenvalues uniformly bounded by above. In this case, by the Gaussian Mechanism
B.1
, the process is private if
\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}
is bounded uniformly in the noise realization, which it is. We then uncondition on the additional conditional event, introducing a slightly larger failure probability. This is described in Lemma
E.5
.

Theorem
3.1
shows that privacy is ensured by combining two mechanisms: the random quadratic perturbation, which controls the sensitivity of the exact minimizer through curvature, and the Gaussian mechanism, which accounts for approximation errors.

3.2
Utility Analysis

We now analyze the empirical excess risk of the proposed mechanism. The bound reflects the trade-off between privacy and solver accuracy.

Our utility analysis requires an additional assumption on the regularizer, given by the following.

{assumption}
[Bounded Subgradient of Regularizer]
We assume that the regularizer
r
has subgradients bounded by
G\geq 0
.

We note that one of our core contributions is that we do not require bounded gradients of the loss functions. The above assumption is not in contradiction with this, as it is an assumption on the regularizer. In fact, the assumption is quite standard for regularizers, and holds for the classical regularizers
r=\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
r=\|\cdot\|_{2}
or
r=\|\cdot\|_{*}
.

We now state our utility result, whose proof is postponed to Appendix
F
.

Theorem 3.3

(Empirical Excess Risk Bound)
.

Assume that the Problem Assumption
2.1
, the Bounded Regularizer Subgradients Assumption
3.2
, and the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
hold. Moreover, select
\sigma^{2}
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
as in Theorem
3.1
, to ensure
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy of Mechanism
3
. Then it holds that, for
\theta_{\final}
the output of Mechanism
3
and
\theta_{\exact}
the solution of the Unperturbed Problem (
P
),

\Exp{\J(\theta_{\final};\D)-\J(\theta_{\exact};\D)}\leq\frac{ndL\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{2}+G\sqrt{d}\tilde{\sigma}+\tau+\mu\sigma^{2}\cdot\left(\|\theta_{\exact}-\theta_{*}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\right).

In case the subproblem may be solved exactly, the above substantially simplifies, as many of the user-chosen parameters may be set to
0
.
Moreover, the Bounded Regularizer Subgradients Assumption

(Empirical Excess Risk Bound for Exact Solve)

and the Random Matrix Assumption

. Moreover, select
\sigma^{2}

, and

, to ensure

the output of Mechanism

4
Algorithmic Considerations

A key observation is that the perturbed problems arising from both LOP and QOP, namely Problems (
P-Priv-Lin
) and (
P-Priv
), admit a common composite structure. For a fixed realization of the perturbation, they can be written as

\min_{\theta\in\R^{d}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(\theta;z_{i})+r(\theta)+\iota_{C}(\theta)\right\},

where
\tilde{\ell}
is a smooth convex function,
r
is a convex (possibly nonsmooth) regularizer, and
\iota_{C}
is the indicator function of the convex constraint set
C
.

This naturally fits within the framework of
Three-Operator Splitting
methods
(
9
)
, which are designed to handle sums of one smooth convex term and two nonsmooth convex terms. In particular, this allows us to separate the smooth structure of
\tilde{\ell}
from the nonsmooth one of
r
and
\iota_{C}
.

In this work, we employ a stochastic variant of the method, leveraging the finite-sum structure of the smooth component. This was introduced in
31
;
30
under strong assumptions, and refined in
8
. Specifically, under suitable assumptions, the scheme converges to a solution of the perturbed problems, and is appropriate for the approximate solves required by our analysis. We provide additional details and the full algorithm in Appendix
G.2
.

5
Comparison to Linear Objective Perturbation

We now compare our Quadratic Objective Perturbation (QOP) mechanism to the classical Linear Objective Perturbation (LOP) mechanism
(
7
;
22
)
. While both approaches perturb the objective to ensure privacy, they differ fundamentally in how they control the stability of the problem. This comparison is performed on two fronts, one being theoretical, in Section
5.1
, the other being numerical, in Section
5.2
.

5.1
Theoretical Comparison

The LOP mechanism also solves a perturbed version of Problem (
P
), however with a random linear perturbation and a deterministic quadratic term. Specifically, it aims at solving

\theta_{\priv}^{\lin}=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{\priv}^{\lin}(\theta;\D)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\theta;z_{i})+r(\theta)+\frac{\Delta}{2}\|\theta\|^{2}+a^{T}\theta\right\}.

(P-Priv-Lin)

While follow-up works have considered inexact solvers
(
20
)
, they do not account for the presence of a regularizer. As such, we shall only compare the exact solve setting.

{algorithm}

[H]

\Require

•

target privacy parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
,

•

appropriate parameters
\sigma^{2}
and
\Delta
.

\State

Draw
a\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
.

\State

{assumption}

[Modified Problem Assumption]

5.2
Numerical Comparison

We now empirically demonstrate a fundamental scaling difference between Mechanisms
5.1
and
3
: in settings with unbounded gradients, LOP’s risk scales with the diameter of the constraint set, whereas QOP’s risk remains invariant. We compare only the curvature-based perturbations to the linear perturbations, and not the approximate solve, as the latter is identical for both mechanisms.

We consider the problem of linear least squares with LASSO regression. Specifically, given a dataset
\D=\{z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})\}\subset[-\xi,\xi]^{d}\times\R
, the problem is given by

\min_{\theta\in C}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}(x_{i}^{T}\theta-y_{i})^{2}+\omega\|\theta\|_{1}\right\},

where
\omega
is a regularization parameter. The regularizer has bounded subgradients by
G=\sqrt{d}\omega
, each loss function is
L
-smooth for
L=d\xi^{2}
and has a Hessian of rank at most
\rho=1
, and the loss function has bounded gradients when the domain is bounded, with, for all
z\in\X
,

\|\nabla\ell(\theta;z)\|\leq\zeta=(\diam(C)\xi\sqrt{d}+\sup|y_{i}|)\cdot\xi\sqrt{d}.

The resulting Problems (
P-Priv-Lin
) and (
P-Priv
) admit a composite structure, so we use the
Stochastic Three-Operator Splitting scheme
mentioned in Section
4
(and
described in Appendix
G.2
). The experiments are given in Appendix
G.3
, and the resulting empirical risks are plotted in Figure
2
. Statistical quantities, such as standard deviation and runtime, are reported in Section
G.4
.

We also compare the two aforementioned mechanisms to LOP-Clip, which clips the gradient used in LOP. This mechanism has provable privacy guarantees
(
27
)
, though dependent on the clipping threshold, which is an additional user-selected parameter.

We note that the empirical risk is higher for small values of
\kappa
. This is expected as the constraint set forces the minimizer to be far from the point
\theta_{*}
, thus forcing a large minimal risk. As
\kappa
grows, the constraint set grows and the minimal empirical risk diminishes.

Figure

shows the empirical risk in the interpolation regime. As predicted by theory, LOP requires noise proportional to the gradient bound, which scales with
\kappa
(appearing linear on the logarithmic scale), whereas QOP controls sensitivity via curvature and is therefore independent of

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced Quadratic Objective Perturbation (QOP) for differentially private empirical risk minimization. In contrast with Linear Objective Perturbation (LOP), which relies on strong bounded gradient assumptions, our mechanism enforces privacy through curvature while simultaneously inducing stability and strong convexity, yielding
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantees under significantly weaker assumptions.

We established privacy and utility guarantees, extended the analysis to approximate solutions, and showed that the resulting problem can be solved efficiently due to its composite structure.

Our theoretical and numerical results demonstrate that QOP and LOP satisfy fundamentally different scaling laws when the gradients are unbounded. In particular, LOP degrades with the constraint diameter, whereas QOP remains stable. These findings suggest that curvature-based perturbations provide a natural alternative to classical objective perturbation methods, particularly in modern machine learning settings where strong regularity assumptions are rarely met or unrealistic.

Future Work.

Several directions remain open. A first avenue would be to relax the assumption of a common minimizer of the loss functions, thus extending the applicability of our framework. Another direction is to strengthen the privacy analysis by considering Rényi differential privacy rather than standard differential privacy.

Lemma C.1

(Density Transportation)
.

Let
X\in\Omega\subset\R^{a}
be a random variable with density
q\colon\Omega\to\R_{+}
. Let
T\colon\Omega\to\R^{b}
be a locally Lipschitz continuous map with
a\geq b
, and define the random variable
Y=T(X)
. If
\|J_{b}T(x)\|>0
with probability
1
on
\{q>0\}
, then
Y
has density function
p\colon\R^{b}\to\R_{+}
given by

p(y)=\int_{T^{-1}(y)}\frac{q(x)}{\|J_{b}T(x)\|}d\H{^}{a-b}(x),

where
J_{b}T(x)
is the
b
-dimensional Jacobian whose determinant is given by
\|J_{b}T(x)\|=\sqrt{\det(DT(x)DT(x)^{T}}
, and
d\H{^}{a-b}
is the
a-b
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

Proof C.2
.

For any test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\R^{b})\subset L^{1}(\R^{b})
, it holds that, by the Coarea formula
(
15
;
26
)
,

\Exp{\phi(Y)}=\int_{\Omega}\phi(T(x))q(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(T(x))q(x)}{\|J_{b}T(x)\|}\|J_{b}T(x)\|dx=\int_{\R^{b}}\int_{T^{-1}(y)}\frac{\phi(T(x))q(x)}{\|J_{b}T(x)\|}d\H{^}{a-b}(x)dy.

We note that, on the fiber
T^{-1}(y)
,
T(x)=y
, such that

\Exp{\phi(Y)}=\int_{\R^{b}}\phi(y)\int_{T^{-1}(y)}\frac{q(x)}{\|J_{b}T(x)\|}d\H{^}{a-b}(x)dy.

As this holds true for all test functions, the statement holds true.

Lemma C.3

(Rank-1 Update Formula)
.

Let
A\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}
have full-rank and let
E\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}
have rank at most
1
. Then it holds that

\frac{\det(A+E)}{\det(A)}=1+\lambda,

where
\lambda
is the unique non-zero eigenvalue of
A^{-1}E
.

Proof C.4
.

Denote by
\lambda_{\min}(E)=\lambda_{d}(E)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{1}(E)=\lambda_{\max}(E)
. The result follows from

\frac{\det(A+E)}{\det(A)}=\det(I+A^{-1}E)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\lambda_{i}(A^{-1}E))=1+\lambda,

since
\rank(E)\leq 1
.

Lemma C.5

(Low-Rank Update Bound)
.

Let
A\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}_{+}
have full-rank and absolute eigenvalues lower-bounded by
\alpha>0
, and let
E\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}
have rank at most
r\leq d
and absolute eigenvalues upper-bounded by
L
. Then it holds that

\frac{\det(A+E)}{\det(A)}\leq\exp(Lr/\alpha).

Proof C.6
.

Denote by
\lambda_{\min}(E)=\lambda_{d}(E)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{1}(E)=\lambda_{\max}(E)
.
It holds that

\frac{\det(A+E)}{\det(A)}=\det(I+A^{-1}E)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\lambda_{i}(A^{-1}E))\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+|\lambda_{i}(A^{-1}E)|),

where the final inequality follows by
\rank(A^{-1}E)\leq\rank(E)\leq r
. By bounding
|\lambda_{i}(A^{-1}E)|\leq{L}/{\alpha}
,

\frac{\det(A+E)}{\det(A)}\leq\left(1+\frac{L}{\alpha}\right)^{r}\leq\exp(Lr/\alpha),

where the latter follows by exponential approximation.

Lemma C.7
.

Given a vector
u\in\R^{d}
and a vector
b\in\R^{d}\backslash\{0\}
, there exists a matrix
U\in\lx@sectionsign^{d}
such that
\rank(U)\leq 2
,
Ub=u
and
\|U\|_{\op}={\|u\|}/{\|b\|}
.

Proof C.8
.

Define
e=\frac{b}{\|b\|}
, let
\alpha=e^{T}u
, and let
w=u-\alpha e
, such that
u=\alpha e+w
and
e^{T}w=0
. Define

P_{v}=\begin{cases}\frac{vv^{T}}{\|v\|^{2}}\quad&\text{if $v\neq 0$},\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

and

U=\frac{1}{\|b\|}\left(\alpha(P_{e}-P_{w})+ew^{T}+we^{T}\right).

It is easy to see that
\rank(U)\leq 2
since its column space is contained in
\spa(e,w)
. It is then easy to check using
w^{T}e=0
that

Ub=\left(\alpha(P_{e}-P_{w})+ew^{T}+we^{T}\right)e=\alpha e+w=u,

as wanted. It remains to bound the norm of
U
. For this, denote
S=ew^{T}+we^{T}
and note that

S(P_{e}-P_{w})+(P_{e}-P_{w})S=0,\quad P_{e}^{2}=P_{e},\quad P_{w}^{2}=P_{w},\quad P_{e}P_{w}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad S^{2}=\|w\|^{2}(P_{e}+P_{w})=\|w\|^{2}(P_{e}-P_{w})^{2}.

As such,

U^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2}(P_{e}+P_{w})+\|w\|^{2}(P_{e}+P_{w})}{\|b\|^{2}}=\frac{\|u\|^{2}}{\|b\|^{2}}(P_{e}+P_{w}),

which implies that, since

\|U\|_{\op}=\sqrt{\|U^{2}\|_{\op}}=\frac{\|u\|}{\|b\|}\sqrt{\|P_{e}+P_{w}\|_{\op}}.

Using the definition of eigenvalues, one deduces that
\|P_{e}+P_{w}\|_{op}=\lambda_{\max}(P_{e}+P_{w})=1
, and hence all properties are fulfilled.

Lemma C.9

(
\thmcite
[Claim 23]kifer_private_2012)
.

Let

be a convex function, let

\theta_{\priv}=\theta_{*}
under
E_{\alpha}
, and hence releasing
\theta_{\priv}
is perfectly private, and hence certainly also
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP. As such, from now on, assume that
\theta_{*}\not\in\argmin r
. We note that
\nabla\Jp(\theta_{*};\D)=\nabla r(\theta_{*})\neq 0
in that case, and hence
\theta_{*}\not\in\argmin\Jp
, and hence
T_{\D}(W)\neq\theta_{*}
.

This proof is split into multiple parts. In Part 1, we derive the conditional density of the distribution of
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
under
E_{\alpha}
. In Part 2, we derive an upper bound on the ratio of this density.

Part 1. Conditional Density

Let us compute the conditional density
p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})
of
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
under
E_{\alpha}
. By Lemma
C.1
, it holds that
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
follows a distribution with density
p_{\D}
given by

p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})=\int_{T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)}\frac{q(W|E_{\alpha})}{\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|}d\H{^}{d(d+1)/2-d}(W)=\frac{1}{\lx@paragraphsign(E_{\alpha})}\int_{T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}}\frac{q(W)}{\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|}d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W),

(4)

where we canonically identified
\lx@sectionsign^{d}
with
\R^{d(d+1)/2}
. We note that we require
\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|>0
almost everywhere on
\{q(W|E_{\alpha})>0\}
and
T_{\D}
to be locally Lipschitz continuous under
E_{\alpha}
, which we shall show later. In order to evaluate
p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})
, we shall compute
\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|
. First note that
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
if, and only if,

F_{\D}(\theta,W)=\nabla\L(\theta;\D)+\nabla r(\theta)+\sigma^{2}W(\theta-\theta_{*})=0.

As such, along the curve
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
, we have
F_{\D}(\theta,W)\equiv 0
. We have that

D_{\theta}F_{\D}(\theta,W)[\delta\theta]=(\nabla^{2}\L(\theta;\D)+\nabla^{2}r(\theta)+\sigma^{2}W)(\delta\theta),

where we note the inverse is well-defined under
E_{\alpha}
, and

D_{W}F_{\D}(\theta,W)[\delta W]=\sigma^{2}(\delta W)(\theta-\theta_{*}),

and as such, by the Implicit Function Theorem, since
D_{\theta}F_{\D}
is invertible under
E_{\alpha}
,

D_{W}T_{\D}(W)[\delta W]=-\sigma^{2}(\nabla^{2}\L(T_{\D}(W);\D)+\nabla^{2}r(T_{\D}(W))+\sigma^{2}W)^{-1}(\delta W)(T_{\D}(W)-\theta_{*}).

Moreover, we know that
T_{\D}
is locally
C^{1}
, and hence locally Lipschitz-continuous. Specifically, if we define

A_{W}\colon\R^{d}\to\R^{d},\quad v\mapsto-\sigma^{2}(\nabla^{2}\L(T_{\D}(W);\D)+\nabla^{2}r(T_{\D}(W))+\sigma^{2}W)^{-1}v,

and

B_{\theta}\colon\lx@sectionsign^{d}_{+}\to\R^{d},\quad\delta W\mapsto(\delta W)(\theta-\theta_{*}),

then we can write

DT_{\D}(W)=A_{W}\circ B_{T_{\D}(W)}.

Now we compute the adjoint operator
B_{\theta}^{T}
by noting that

\langle B_{\theta}(\delta W),v\rangle=(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}(\delta W)v=\tr((\delta W)(\theta-\theta_{*})v^{T})=\langle\delta W,(\theta-\theta_{*})v^{T}\rangle=\left\langle\delta W,\frac{(\theta-\theta_{*})v^{T}+v(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}}{2}\right\rangle,

and hence
B_{\theta}^{T}(v)=\sym((\theta-\theta_{*})v^{T})
. As such,

B_{\theta}\circ B_{\theta}^{T}(v)=\frac{(\theta-\theta_{*})v^{T}+v(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}}{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})=\frac{(\theta-\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}+\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2}}{2}v,

or, in short-hand,

B_{\theta}B_{\theta}^{T}=\frac{(\theta-\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}+\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2}I}{2}.

Specifically, using the Rank-1 Update Formula
C.3
, we obtain

\det\left(B_{\theta}B_{\theta}^{T}\right)=2^{-d}\left(1+\frac{(\theta-\theta_{*})^{T}(\theta-\theta_{*})}{\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2}}\right)\det(\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2}I)=2^{1-d}\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2d}.

Moreover, we note that
A_{W}
is symmetric, and hence, with
\theta=T_{\D}(W)
,

\displaystyle\det\left(DT_{\D}(W)DT_{\D}(W)^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\det(A_{W}B_{T_{\D}(W)}B_{T_{\D}(W)}^{T}A_{W}^{T})

\displaystyle=\det(A_{W})^{2}\det(B_{T_{\D}(W)}B_{T_{\D}(W)}^{T})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{4d}\cdot 2^{1-d}\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{2d}}{\det(\nabla^{2}\L(T_{\D}(W);\D)+\nabla^{2}r(T_{\D}(W))+\sigma^{2}W)^{2}}.

If we define

\bar{A}_{\D}(W)=\nabla^{2}\L(T_{\D}(W);\D)+\nabla^{2}r(T_{\D}(W))+\sigma^{2}W,

then we can write

\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|=\sqrt{\det\left(DT_{\D}(W)DT_{\D}(W)^{T}\right)}=\frac{2^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\cdot\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{d}\cdot\sigma^{2d}}{|\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))|}=\frac{2^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\cdot\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{d}\cdot\sigma^{2d}}{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))},

where the final equality follows from
\bar{A}_{\D}(W)\succeq 0
. Plugging this into Equation (
4
) yields

p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{P(E_{\alpha})\cdot 2^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\cdot\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|^{d}\cdot\sigma^{2d}}\int_{T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}}q(W)\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W).

For simplicity of notation, we denote the data-independent scaling constant by
C_{\alpha}(\theta)
. We do note that
\bar{A}_{\D}(W)\succeq\sigma^{2}W\succ 0
and
\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|>0
, and hence
\|J_{d}T_{\D}(W)\|>0
almost everywhere on
\{q(W|E_{\alpha})>0\}
, as required for Lemma
C.1
.

Part 2. Upper Bound on Ratios of Densities

Now take two neighboring datasets
\D
and
\D^{\prime}
of size
n
, which differ only in the last datapoint (see Definition
2.1
). Define

E=\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;z^{\prime}_{n})-\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;z_{n}),

such that, for all
W\in T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)
and
W^{\prime}\in T_{\D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)
, it holds that

\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(W^{\prime})-\sigma^{2}W^{\prime}=\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-\sigma^{2}W+E.

Recall that the fiber
T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)
is described by
W(\theta-\theta_{*})=u_{\D}(\theta)
, and note that

u_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)=u_{\D}(\theta)-\sigma^{-2}\delta u,

where
\delta u=\nabla\ell(\theta;z^{\prime}_{n})-\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{n})
. Now define
U
such that
\|U\|_{op}=\frac{\|\delta u\|}{\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|}
, such that
U(\theta-\theta_{*})=\delta u
, and such that
\rank(U)\leq 2
. This is possible by Lemma
C.7
.
We note that by
L
-smoothness, we have that

\|U\|_{\text{op}}=\frac{\|\delta u\|}{\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|}\leq\frac{\|\nabla\ell(\theta;z^{\prime}_{n})-\nabla\ell(\theta_{*};z^{\prime}_{n})\|}{\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|}+\frac{\|\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{n})-\nabla\ell(\theta_{*};z_{n})\|}{\|\theta-\theta_{*}\|}\leq 2L.

As such,
(W,W^{\prime})\in T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\times T_{\D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)
, if, and only if,

W^{\prime}(\theta-\theta_{*})=W(\theta-\theta_{*})-\sigma^{-2}U(\theta-\theta_{*}).

Define the translation
\phi(W)=W+\sigma^{-2}U
. Specifically, if
W^{\prime}\in T_{\D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}
, then either
\phi(W^{\prime})\in T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}
or
\phi(W^{\prime})\not\in E_{\alpha}
. As such, we may define

G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)=\{W^{\prime}\in T_{D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}\colon\phi(W^{\prime})\in T_{D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}\}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)=\{W^{\prime}\in T_{D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}\colon\phi(W^{\prime})\not\in E_{\alpha}\},

such that
\phi(G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta))\subset T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}
, and
T_{\D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}=G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)\uplus B_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)
. As such,

\displaystyle p_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta|E_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}(\theta)\cdot\int_{T_{\D^{\prime}}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}}q(W^{\prime})\det(\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(W^{\prime}))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{C_{\alpha}(\theta)\int_{G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)}q(W^{\prime})\det(\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(W^{\prime}))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W^{\prime})}_{\text{(Part 2a)}}+\underbrace{C_{\alpha}(\theta)\int_{B_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)}q(W^{\prime})\det(\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(W^{\prime}))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W^{\prime})}_{\text{(Part 2b)}}.

By using the translation defined above, we have that, by change of variables,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{(Part 2a)}}{C_{\alpha}(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\phi(G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta))}q(\phi^{-1}(W))\det(\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(\phi^{-1}(W)))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W)

\displaystyle=\int_{\phi(G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta))}q(W-\sigma^{-2}U)\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-U+E)d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\sigma^{-2}L\cdot f(2)}\cdot\sup_{W\in\phi(G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta))}\left\{\frac{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-U+E)}{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))}\right\}\cdot\int_{\phi(G_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta))}q(W)\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\sigma^{-2}L\cdot f(2)}\cdot\sup_{W\in E_{\alpha}}\left\{\frac{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-U+E)}{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))}\right\}\cdot\int_{T_{\D}^{-1}(\theta)\cap E_{\alpha}}q(W)\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W)

\displaystyle=e^{2\sigma^{-2}L\cdot f(2)}\cdot\sup_{W\in E_{\alpha}}\left\{\frac{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-U+E)}{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))}\right\}\cdot\frac{p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})}{C_{\alpha}(\theta)},

where the first inequality follows by assumption. To bound the supremum, we recall that
E=\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;z^{\prime}_{n})-\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;z_{n})
has rank at most
2\rho
and all eigenvalues bounded by
2L
, and that
U
has rank at most
2
and all eigenvalues bounded by
2L
. We apply the Low-Rank Update Bound
C.5
with
\bar{A}_{\D}(W)\succeq\alpha\sigma^{2}I
on
E_{\alpha}
and
|\lambda_{i}(-U+E)|\leq 4L
for all
1\leq i\leq 2\rho+2
, where
\rank(-U+E)\leq 2\rho+2
, to obtain that

\frac{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W)-U+{E})}{\det(\bar{A}_{\D}(W))}\leq\exp\left(\frac{4L(2\rho+2)}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}\right).

As such, we conclude that

(\text{Part 2a})\leq\exp\left(2\sigma^{-2}L\cdot f(2)+2\sigma^{-2}L\frac{2(2\rho+2)}{\alpha}\right)\cdot p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha})\leq e^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdot p_{\D}(\theta|E_{\alpha}),

where the latter follows from the bound on
\sigma^{2}
.

For
(Part 2b)
, notice that

B_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)\subset\bar{B}=\{W\in\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}\colon\alpha\leq\lambda_{\min}(W)\leq\alpha+2\sigma^{-2}L\},

since
\|U\|_{\text{op}}\leq 2L
, and hence

\displaystyle(\text{Part 2b})\leq C_{\alpha}(\theta)\int_{T_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta)\cap\bar{B}}q(W^{\prime})\det(\bar{A}_{\D^{\prime}}(W^{\prime}))d\H{^}{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}(W^{\prime})=\frac{\lx@paragraphsign(\bar{B})}{\lx@paragraphsign(E_{\alpha})}p_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta|\bar{B}).

As such, for any measurable
S\subset\R^{d}
,

\int_{\theta\in S}(\text{Part 2b})d\theta\leq\frac{\lx@paragraphsign(\bar{B})}{\lx@paragraphsign(E_{\alpha})}\int_{\theta\in S}p_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta|\bar{B})\leq\frac{\lx@paragraphsign(\bar{B})}{\lx@paragraphsign(E_{\alpha})}\leq\delta_{1},

where the last step follows by assumption and by the bound on
\sigma^{2}
. Specifically, for any measurable
S\subset\R^{d}
, it holds that

\lx@paragraphsign_{\D^{\prime}}(\theta\in S|E_{\alpha})\leq e^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lx@paragraphsign_{\D}(\theta\in S|E_{\alpha})+\delta_{1},

and hence the conditional mechanism is
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP, as wanted.

The result of Lemma
E.1
holds without the Simplification Assumption
E.1
. This is detailed in the following.

Lemma E.3

(Conditional Release of Exact Minimizer)
.

Assume that the Problem Assumption
2.1
and the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
hold.
The release of
\theta_{\priv}
, conditioned on the event
E_{\alpha}
, is
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP for

\sigma^{2}\geq\max\left(\frac{2L}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left(f(2)+\frac{2(2\rho+2)}{\alpha}\right),\frac{2L}{\alpha_{1}}\right)

where
\alpha
,
\alpha_{1}
and
f
are given by the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
.

Proof E.4
.

The proof relies on the Successive Approximation Lemma
B.4
, following
22
. In fact, the proof closely follows the arguments laid out in
22
. The two assumptions we aim to prove are the twice-differentiability of the regularizer (Part 1) and the full-domain assumption of the constraint set (Part 2).

Part 1. Removal of Twice-Differentiability Assumption of the Regularizer.

In this part, we still assume the constraint set
C
to be the full domain
C=\R^{d}
. For this, we consider the function
\psi\colon\R\to\R
defined as

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\exp(-\frac{1}{1-x^{2}})\quad&{\text{if $|x|<1$}}\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

This function, known as the bump function, is
C^{\infty}
and all its derivatives vanish outside
(-1,1)
. Through this, we define the sequence of kernel functions
K_{i}\colon\R^{d}\to\R
defined as

K_{i}(\theta)=\frac{\psi(i\|\theta\|^{2})}{\int_{\R^{d}}\psi(i\|\theta^{\prime}\|^{2})d\theta^{\prime}}.

As such,
K_{i}
is also
C^{\infty}
and all its derivatives vanish outside its support
\supp(K_{i})=\{\theta\colon\|\theta\|\leq 1/\sqrt{i}\}
. Given the non-differentiable regularizer
r
, we define the approximate regularizer
r_{i}
as

r_{i}(\theta)=[r\star K_{i}](\theta)=\int_{\R^{d}}r(\theta-\theta^{\prime})K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}.

As
K_{i}\in C^{\infty}
, it also holds that
r_{i}\in C^{\infty}
, and as
r
is convex it also holds that
r_{i}
is convex. Now define
\Jp
to be the private objective with the regularizer
r
, and
\Jpi
to be the private objective with the regularizer
r_{i}
. We know that the release of
\phi^{i}(W)
is
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP by Lemma
E.1
. We shall thus show that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\phi^{i}(W)=\phi(W)\quad\text{for each realization $W\in\E_{\alpha}$},

where
\phi^{i}
is the unique minimizer of
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
and
\phi
is the unique minimizer of
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
, and thus conclude that the release of
\phi(W)
is also
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP by the Successive Approximation Lemma
B.4
.
We shall split the remainder of this part into two; proving uniform convergence of
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
towards
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
for each
W\in E_{\alpha}
(Part 1a) and therefrom proving pointwise convergence of
\phi^{i}
towards
\phi
(Part 1b).

Part 1a.
We consider a bounded set
\mathcal{I}\subset\R^{d}
and an arbitrary
W\in E_{\alpha}
, and show that
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
converges uniformly to
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
on
\mathcal{I}
. Since
r
is continuous over the compact
\bar{\mathcal{I}}+B(0,1)
, it is also uniformly continuous over
\bar{\mathcal{I}}+B(0,1)
. As such, given a
\xi>0
, there exists an
\eta>0
such that, if
\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|\leq\eta
for
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{I}+B(0,1)
, then
\|r(\theta_{1})-r(\theta_{2})\|\leq\xi
. Select
i>\max(1,1/\eta^{2})
, and any
\theta\in\mathcal{I}
, such that

\displaystyle|\Jpi(\theta;\D)-\Jp(\theta;\D)|

\displaystyle=|r_{i}(\theta)-r(\theta)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\R^{d}}r(\theta-\theta^{\prime})K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}-r(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\R^{d}}[r(\theta-\theta^{\prime})-r(\theta)]K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\R^{d}}\left|r(\theta-\theta^{\prime})-r(\theta)\right|K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\|\theta^{\prime}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{i}}\left|r(\theta-\theta^{\prime})-r(\theta)\right|K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\xi\int_{\|\theta^{\prime}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{i}}K_{i}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\xi.

As such,
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
converges uniformly to
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
on
\mathcal{I}
.

Part 1b.
Fix any
W\in E_{\alpha}
, choose any
0\leq\xi<\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{4}
, and define
\mathcal{I}=\{\theta\colon\|\theta-\phi(W)\|^{2}\leq 1\}
. By Part 1a we know that
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
converges uniformly to
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
on
\mathcal{I}
, and hence we may choose an
i_{\xi}
such that, for all
i\geq i_{\xi}
,
|\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)-\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)|\leq\xi
uniformly on
\mathcal{I}
. Now note that by
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strong convexity of
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
, we have that

\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta-\phi(W)\|^{2}\leq\Jp(\theta;\D)-\Jp(\phi(W);\D)\quad\text{for all $\theta\in\R^{d}$}.

Specifically, if
\theta\in\partial\mathcal{I}
, it holds that
\Jp(\theta;\D)-\Jp(\phi(W);\D)\geq\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}
, and by uniform convergence since
\phi(W),\theta\in\mathcal{I}
we thus get that

\Jpi(\theta;\D)-\Jpi(\phi(W);\D)\geq\Jp(\theta;\D)-\Jp(\phi(W);\D)-2\xi\geq\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}-2\xi>0.

We now show that
\phi^{i}(W)\in\mathcal{I}
. Assuming the negative, namely that
\|\phi^{i}(W)-\phi(W)\|>1
, we define

\tilde{\phi}^{i}=\phi(W)+\frac{\phi^{i}(W)-\phi(W)}{\|\phi^{i}(W)-\phi(W)\|}=\left(1-\lambda\right)\phi(W)+\lambda\phi^{i}(W)\in\partial\mathcal{I},

for
\lambda=\frac{1}{\|\phi^{i}(W)-\phi(W)\|}
. As such, by convexity of
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
, and since
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we get

\Jpi(\phi(W);\D)<\Jpi(\tilde{\phi}^{i};\D)\leq(1-\lambda)\Jpi(\phi(W);\D)+\lambda\Jpi(\phi^{i}(W);\D)\leq\Jpi(\phi(W);\D),

where the first inequality follows from the above and the last inequality follows from
\phi^{i}(W)
being the minimizer of
\Jpi(~\cdot~;\D)
. The above results in a contradiction, thus proving that we must have
\phi^{i}(W)\in\mathcal{I}
. As such, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\phi^{i}(W)-\phi(W)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Jp(\phi^{i}(W);\D)-\Jp(\phi(W);\D)

\displaystyle\leq\Jpi(\phi^{i}(W);\D)-\Jpi(\phi(W);\D)+2\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\xi.

As such,
(\phi^{i}(W))
converges to
\phi(W)
, and thus
(\phi^{i})
converges pointwise to
\phi
, as wanted.

Part 2. Removal of Full-Domain Assumption of the Constraint Set.

Consider the sequence of functions, indexed by
i\geq 0
,

\Jpi(\theta;\D)=\Jp(\theta;\D)+i\dist(\theta,C).

We denote by
\phi(W)=\argmin_{\theta\in C}\Jp(\theta;\D)
, and by
\phi^{i}(W)=\argmin_{\theta\in\R^{d}}\Jpi(\theta;\D)
(note the different domains), and shall prove that
\phi^{i}
converges pointwise to
\phi
. Since we know that the release of
\phi^{i}(W)
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP (consider
r+i\dist(\cdot,C)
as the regularizer) by Lemma
E.1
and Part 1 above, this would be sufficient to prove that the release of
\phi(W)
is
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
-DP by the Successive Approximation Lemma
B.4
.

We fix a
W\in E_{\alpha}
, and denote for convenience by
\phi^{i}\equiv\phi^{i}(W)
and
\phi\equiv\phi(W)
. We observe that
\phi\in C
, and if
\phi^{i}\in C
, then
\phi^{i}
minimizes
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
over
C
, and hence
\phi^{i}=\phi
. As such, to show convergence of
\phi^{i}
towards
\phi
, it is sufficient to show that there exists an index
N\geq 0
such that, for all
i\geq N
,
\phi^{i}\in C
.

Consider the set of points
\theta\in\R^{d}
such that
\Jp(\theta;\D)\leq\Jp(\phi;\D)
, and denote this set by
H
. Then, by
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strong convexity of
\Jp(~\cdot~;\D)
, it must hold that, if we define
D=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}[\Jp(\phi;\D)-\Jp(\phi^{0};\D)]}
, for all
\theta\in H
,

\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta-\phi^{0}\|^{2}\leq\Jp(\theta;\D)-\Jp(\phi^{0};\D)\leq\Jp(\phi;\D)-\Jp(\phi^{0};\D)=\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot D^{2}.

Moreover, since
\phi\in C
is clearly also in
H
, it must hold that, for any point
\theta\in H
,
\dist(\theta,C)\leq\|\theta-\phi\|\leq 2D
. Specifically, over
H
, the quantity
\lambda_{\theta}=\frac{2D}{\dist(\theta,C)}\geq 1
. Now we note that
\Jp
is convex, and hence, for
x=\theta
,
y=\proj_{C}(\theta)-\theta
and
\lambda=\lambda_{\theta}
, Lemma
C.9
gives that

\Jp(\proj_{C}(\theta);\D)-\Jp(\theta;\D)\leq\frac{\Jp(\theta+2D\cdot\frac{\proj_{C}(\theta)-\theta}{\dist(\theta,C)};\D)-\Jp(\theta;\D)}{\frac{2D}{\dist(\theta,C)}}\leq\frac{M}{2D}\cdot\dist(\theta,C),

where
M
is defined as the supremum of the function
(\theta,v)\mapsto\Jp(\theta+2D\cdot v;\D)-\Jp(\theta;\D)
over
\bar{H}\times\partial B(0,1)
(exists as the function is continuous over a compact). As such, we get that, for all
\theta\in H
such that
\theta\not\in C
, for
i>\frac{M}{2D}
,

\displaystyle\Jpi(\theta;\D)

\displaystyle=\Jp(\theta;\D)+i\dist(\theta,C)

\displaystyle=\Jp(\proj_{C}(\theta);\D)+i\dist(\theta,C)-\left[\Jp(\proj_{C}(\theta);\D)-\Jp(\theta;\D)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Jp(\proj_{C}(\theta);\D)+i\dist(\theta,C)-\frac{M}{2D}\cdot\dist(\theta,C)

\displaystyle>\Jp(\proj_{C}(\theta);\D)

\displaystyle\geq\Jp(\phi;\D).

We note that

\Jp(\phi^{i};\D)\leq\Jpi(\phi^{i};\D)\leq\Jpi(\phi;\D)=\Jp(\phi;\D),

and hence
\phi^{i}\in H
for all
i
. As such, the above strict inequality cannot hold for
\theta=\phi^{i}
, and we conclude that
\phi^{i}\in C
for
i>\frac{M}{2D}
, as wanted.

E.2
Release of Noisy Error

Lemma E.5

(Conditional Release of Noisy Error)
.

Assume that the Problem Assumption
2.1
and the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
hold.
The release of
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}
, conditioned on
E_{\alpha,\beta}
and the realized value of
\theta_{\priv}
, is
(\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2})
-DP, uniformly in the realization
W\in E_{\alpha,\beta}
, for

\tilde{\sigma}\geq\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}}+\frac{\beta\eta}{\alpha}\right)\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{2}}\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta_{2})},

where
\alpha,\beta
are given by the Random Matrix Assumption
2.3
.

Proof E.6
.

Fix any
W\in E_{\alpha,\beta}
.
First, observe that, for any
\theta\in\R^{d}
, it holds that

\Jp(\theta;\D)-\tJp(\theta;\D)=\sigma^{2}(\tilde{\theta}_{*}-\theta_{*})^{T}W\theta+c,

(5)

where
c
only depends on
\tilde{\theta}_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
and
W
, and specifically is independent of
\theta
. By denoting
h=\sigma^{2}W(\tilde{\theta}_{*}-\theta_{*})
, we obtain

\Jp(\theta;\D)-\tJp(\theta;\D)=h^{T}\theta+c,\quad\text{where $\|h\|\leq\sigma^{2}\beta\eta$.}

Releasing
\theta_{\final}-\theta_{\priv}
is equivalent to releasing a noisy version of
\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}
under the Gaussian Mechanism
B.1
. Under
E_{\alpha}
,
\Jp(\cdot,\D)
is
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strongly convex, and hence

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Jp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\Jp(\theta_{\priv};\D)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Jp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\Jp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)}_{\text{Term 1}}+\underbrace{\Jp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)-\Jp(\theta_{\priv};\D)}_{\text{Term 2}}.

For Term 1, we have, by Equation (
5
), definition of
\tau
and
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strong convexity of
\tJp(~\cdot~;\D)
,

\displaystyle\Jp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\Jp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)

\displaystyle=\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)+h^{T}(\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\tilde{\theta}_{\priv})

\displaystyle\leq\tau+\|h\|\|\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}\|

\displaystyle\leq\tau+\sigma^{2}\beta\eta\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}[\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv})]}

\displaystyle\leq\tau+\sigma^{2}\beta\eta\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}}.

For Term 2, we have, by Equation (
5
) and
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strong convexity of
\tJp(~\cdot~;\D)
,

\displaystyle\Jp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)-\Jp(\theta_{\priv};\D)

\displaystyle=\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)-\tJp(\theta_{\priv};\D)+h^{T}(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}-\theta_{\priv})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}-\theta_{\priv}\|^{2}+\|h\|\|\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}-\theta_{\priv}\|.

By regarding the latter as a quadratic in
\|\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}-\theta_{\priv}\|
, and maximizing the quadratic, we obtain

\Jp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)-\Jp(\theta_{\priv};\D)\leq\frac{\|h\|^{2}}{2\alpha\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}\eta^{2}}{2\alpha}.

Specifically, combining terms, we obtain

\frac{\alpha\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}\|^{2}\leq\tau+\sigma^{2}\beta\eta\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}\eta^{2}}{2\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{2\tau}+\frac{\sigma\beta\eta}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2}.

As such, the map
\D\mapsto\theta_{\operatorname{app}}-\theta_{\priv}
has
\ell^{2}
-sensitivity bounded by
2\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}}+\frac{\beta\eta}{\alpha}\right)
. Thus, by the Gaussian Mechanism
B.1
, the mechanism is
(\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2})
-DP. Since this holds for any realization
W\in E_{\alpha,\beta}
, the claim holds.

Consider the following decomposition

\displaystyle\Exp{\J(\theta_{\final};\D)-\J(\theta_{\exact};\D)}=

\displaystyle~~~~\Exp{\J(\theta_{\final};\D)-\J(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)}

(6a)

\displaystyle+\Exp{\J(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)}

(6b)

\displaystyle+\Exp{\tJp(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};\D)-\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)}

(6c)

\displaystyle+\Exp{\tJp(\tilde{\theta}_{\priv};\D)-\tJp(\theta_{\exact};\D)}

(6d)

\displaystyle+\Exp{\tJp(\theta_{\exact};\D)-\J(\theta_{\exact};\D)}.

(6e)

We first note that (
6b
) is nonpositive as
W\in\lx@sectionsign_{+}^{d}
, and that (
6d
) is nonpositive as
\tilde{\theta}_{\priv}
is the minimizer of
\tJp(\cdot,W)
. The quantity (
6c
) is bounded by
\tau
by assumption. We may bound (
6e
) by using

\displaystyle\Exp{\tJp(\theta_{\exact};\D)-\J(\theta_{\exact};\D)}

\displaystyle=\Exp{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\theta_{\exact}-\tilde{\theta}_{*})^{T}W(\theta_{\exact}-\tilde{\theta}_{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot(\theta_{\exact}-\tilde{\theta}_{*})^{T}\Exp{W}(\theta_{\exact}-\tilde{\theta}_{*})

\displaystyle\leq\mu\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot\|\theta_{\exact}-\tilde{\theta}_{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mu\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\|\theta_{\exact}-\theta_{*}\|^{2}+\|\tilde{\theta}_{*}-\theta_{*}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\sigma^{2}\cdot\left(\|\theta_{\exact}-\theta_{*}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\right).

Finally, by
L
-smoothness of
\ell(\cdot;z)
and since

has zero mean and is independent of

\displaystyle\Exp{\ell(\theta_{\final};z)}=\Exp{\ell(\theta_{\operatorname{app}}+b;z)}\leq\Exp{\ell(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};z)+\langle\nabla\ell(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};z),b\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|b\|^{2}}=\Exp{\ell(\theta_{\operatorname{app}};z)+\frac{L}{2}\|b\|^{2}},

G.1
Stopping Criterion

The stopping criterion in Equation (
2
) is not directly verifiable in practice as it requires the optimal value of Problem (
P-Priv-App
). We therefore introduce a computable surrogate condition, based on first-order optimality conditions. In fact, we consider the stopping condition

\dist(0,\partial\tJp(\theta;\D)+N_{C}(\theta))\leq\sqrt{2\tau\alpha\sigma^{2}},

(7)

where
N_{C}
is the normal cone of
C
, and
\alpha
is a lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of
W
, which is positive with high probability (see Section
2.3
). To see that Equation (
7
) implies the stopping condition in Equation (
2
), we note that
\theta\mapsto\tJp(\theta;\D)+\iota_{C}(\theta)
is
\alpha\sigma^{2}
-strongly convex, such that

\dist(0,\partial\tJp(\theta;\D)+N_{C}(\theta))^{2}\geq 2\alpha\sigma^{2}\left[\tJp(\theta;\D)-\min_{\theta\in C}\tJp(\theta;\D)\right].

G.2
Stochastic Three-Operator Splitting

While the proposed mechanism is conceptually simple, it requires solving a perturbed problem which is nontrivial due to its finite-sum structure, nonsmooth regularizer, and constraints. In this section, we show that the perturbed problem can be solved efficiently by leveraging its composite structure.

We consider a realization of
W
, and focus on how to (approximately) solve Problem (
P-Priv-App
). Specifically, the problem will be fixed, and the randomness will arise through the algorithm alone.

A key observation is that Problem (
P-Priv-App
) may be written as the sum of a smooth finite-sum problem with two convex regularizers, namely

\argmin_{\theta\in\R^{d}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(\theta;z_{i})+r(\theta)+\iota_{C}(\theta)\right\},

where
\tilde{\ell}(\theta;z)=\ell(\theta;z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{*})^{T}W(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{*})
is smooth and convex, and
\iota_{C}
is the indicator of the convex set
C
. We note this is not specific to our resulting problems, and would also be the structure of Problem (
P-Priv-Lin
), with
\tilde{\ell}(\theta;z)=\ell(\theta;z)+\frac{a^{T}\theta}{n}+\frac{\Delta}{2n}\|\theta\|^{2}
.

This structure suggests the use of the
Three-Operator Splitting
scheme
(
9
)
. The scheme was originally introduced in the deterministic setting, treating the finite-sum as a single operator, and has since been extended in the stochastic setting, in which a random term of the sum is used at each iteration
(
31
;
30
;
8
)
. The resulting algorithm is described in Algorithm
G.2
.

{algorithm}

[H]

(Stochastic Three-Operator Splitting)

{algorithmic}

\Require
a step-size
\rho
, a relaxation sequence
(\lambda_{k})
, and an initial guess
x_{0}\in\R^{d}
.

\For
k=0,\ldots,K

\State
Draw
i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
randomly and independently.

\State
Set
\theta_{k}=\prox_{\rho r}(x_{k})
.

\State
Set
z_{k}=\proj_{C}(2\theta_{k}-x_{k}-\rho n\nabla\tilde{\ell}(\theta_{k};z_{i_{k}}))
.

\State
Set
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\lambda_{k}\theta_{k}+\lambda_{k}z_{k}
.

\State
Update
k\to k+1
.

\EndFor

\Return
\theta_{K}

The work
31
introduced stochasticity into the scheme for strongly convex objectives, whereas
30
studied non-strongly convex objectives. Both works make a strong assumption resembling uniformly bounded variance of the stochastic oracle, which is not verified in our setting without additional assumptions. In contrast,
8
rely on a weaker assumption, by only assuming a finite variance of the stochastic oracle at a solution of the problem.

Convergence to a solution of the perturbed problem can be established in several important cases. In particular, when the constraint set
C
is an affine subspace (including the unconstrained setting), the method provably converges with accompanying rate statements. More broadly, more general conditions are considered in
8
.

We also note that when the problem is unconstrained, the function
\iota_{C}
is identically zero, and can therefore be disregarded. In this case, the Three-Operator Splitting algorithm reduces to the Backward-Forward algorithm
(
2
)
, and Algorithm
G.2
would reduce to a stochastic variant of it, in which the projection step is trivial.

G.3
Experimental Setup

The goal of the experiment in Section
5.2
is to isolate the dependence of the mechanisms on the constraint diameter
\kappa
, and thereby the dependence on the bounded gradients assumption required by LOP.

Data Generation.

To generate the data, we generate
n
independent samples
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, and rescale them to lie within
[-\xi,\xi]^{d}
. We then generate a random
\theta_{*}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, and define
y_{i}=x_{i}^{T}\theta_{*}

\tilde{\theta}_{*}=\theta_{*}+\tilde{c}

.

Problem Setup.

We consider the regularization parameter
\omega=1
, and constraint sets
C=[-\kappa,\kappa]^{d}
for varying
\kappa>0
.

Algorithmic Setup.
We solve the resulting problem through Algorithm
G.2
. For this purpose, we run
1000
iterations using the relaxation parameters
\lambda_{k}=(k+1)^{-1/2-2\cdot 0.001}
and the step-size

.

Mechanism Setup.
We consider the privacy budget
(\varepsilon,\delta)=(0.5,0.01)
. When running either mechanism, we omit the final Gaussian noise term

.

Evaluation Protocol.
For both cases, namely with and without interpolation, we evaluate the empirical risk. We run the algorithm

Computer Resources.
The experiment is written in Python 3.13, and was executed on an Apple Silicon MacBook Pro, with a M5 chip and 16GB of RAM.
