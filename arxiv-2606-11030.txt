Abstract
. Cellular generation, which generalises cofibrant generation, is an important categorical smallness condition on a class of morphisms. A general challenge is to determine whether a given class of morphisms ℳ \mathcal{M} is cellularly generated, in which ℳ \mathcal{M} -effective squares are often useful. These are commuting squares consisting of morphisms in ℳ \mathcal{M} , so that the induced morphism from the pushout square is also in ℳ \mathcal{M} . When we drop the requirement that the vertical morphisms in the square are in ℳ \mathcal{M} we obtain the weaker notion of ℳ \mathcal{M} -quasieffective square . We prove that, in a locally presentable category, ℳ \mathcal{M} is cellularly generated if and only if ℳ \mathcal{M} is almost everywhere quasieffective. The latter is a set-theoretic condition stating that for almost every partial elementary set-theoretic subuniverse 𝔑 \mathfrak{N} , we have that restricting any morphism in ℳ \mathcal{M} to 𝔑 \mathfrak{N} yields an ℳ \mathcal{M} -quasieffective square. For locally finitely presentable categories this yields an additional categorical characterisation in terms of filtrations of ℳ \mathcal{M} -quasieffective squares. If we additionally assume that ℳ \mathcal{M} is continuous (i.e., the corresponding wide subcategory is closed under directed colimits) then we obtain a stronger characterisation of cellular generation in terms of accessibility of the category of ℳ \mathcal{M} -effective squares. This improves on a theorem by Lieberman, Vasey, and the third author.



1.
Introduction

Smallness conditions have played an important role in category theory since its beginning. For instance, Freyd’s
solution set condition
guarantees that a limit preserving functor on a complete category has a left adjoint. Another example is Isbell’s important concept of a small
dense
subcategory (he called it left adequate). Both concepts can be found in
[
22
]
—and the latter entered the definition of a
locally presentable category
of Gabriel and Ulmer
[
10
]
or the more general concept of an
accessible category
of Makkai and Paré
[
25
]
. Recall that a category is locally
\lambda
-presentable if it is cocomplete and has a small dense subcategory formed by
\lambda
-presentable objects (
\lambda
is a regular cardinal), while in
\lambda
-accessible categories cocompleteness reduces to the existence of
\lambda
-directed colimits. Additionally, the density reduces to the fact that every object is a
\lambda
-directed colimit of
\lambda
-presentable objects, but the requirement that there is only a set of them remains. Locally presentable and accessible categories pervaded many areas, including algebra, homotopy theory, and model theory.

In homotopy theory, there is another smallness condition due to Quillen
[
26
]
called a
small object argument
. Its basis is that a given class
\mathcal{M}
of morphisms is generated from a set using pushouts, transfinite compositions, and retracts. In this case,
\mathcal{M}
is called
cofibrantly generated
, or
cellularly generated
if retracts are not used. In homotopy theory, if both cofibrations and trivial cofibrations are cofibrantly generated then they yield a model category which is called cofibrantly generated
[
12
]
. A general challenge is to determine whether a given class of morphisms is cofibrantly generated. In 2000, Hovey asked for examples of model categories which are not cofibrantly generated. Soon after, such examples appeared—for instance in
[
1
]
based on the fact that embeddings in posets are not cofibrantly generated.

In model theory, specifically in stability theory
[
27
]
, another smallness condition appears, often referred to as
local character
. A central role in stability theory is played by independence relations, generalising for example linear independence in vector spaces and algebraic independence in algebraically closed fields. Local character means that, given subsets
A
and
B
of some structure such that
A
is small (e.g., finite), then
A
can only depend on a small part of
B
. In
[
19
]
a categorical approach to model-theoretic independence was started. There, the independence relation itself can be viewed as a category, and local character can be expressed as the accessibility of that category.

In
[
3
]
Barr defined the notion of a category having
effective unions
. This means that for any pullback square that consists of regular monomorphisms, like below on the left, the induced morphism from the relevant pushout is a regular monomorphism, as pictured below on the right.

Borceux and the third author showed that this condition, with regular monomorphisms replaced by pure monomorphisms, implies cofibrant generation of pure monomorphisms
[

. However, cofibrant generation is not equivalent to having effective unions (e.g.,

). Still, for a fixed class of morphisms
\mathcal{M}
, the idea of

-effective squares
is useful in characterising cofibrant generation and cellular generation. By an
\mathcal{M}
-effective square we now mean a commuting square whose arrows are all in
\mathcal{M}
and where the induced morphism from the relevant pushout is in
\mathcal{M}
. In both
[
19
]
and
[
21
]
these were shown to induce a model-theoretic independence relation, and
[

, Theorem 3.1]
shows that a nicely behaved class
\mathcal{M}
in a locally presentable category
\mathcal{K}
is cofibrantly generated if and only if the induced independence relation, viewed as a category, is accessible. Hence, the result relates two seemingly disparate smallness conditions, one coming from homotopy theory and the other from model theory. By “nicely behaved” we mean that
\mathcal{M}
is cofibrantly closed (i.e., closed under pushouts, transfinite compositions, and retracts), continuous (i.e., the wide subcategory
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
of
\mathcal{K}
having
\mathcal{M}
as morphisms is closed under directed colimits in
\mathcal{K}
), and coherent (i.e., if
g,gf\in\mathcal{M}
then
f\in\mathcal{M}
).

The current paper further elaborates this equivalence. We fix a gap in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.1]
. This fix is not evident and uses a
fat small object argument
of
[
23
]
which is a variation of a small object argument. Furthermore, we remove the assumptions of continuity and coherence of
\mathcal{M}
at the expense of replacing
\mathcal{M}
-effective squares by a weaker notion of
\mathcal{M}
-quasieffective
squares, where only horizontal morphisms are in
\mathcal{M}
. Also, we have to replace accessibility of the resulting category by the existence of filtrations of objects by smaller objects. The proof combines categorical and set-theoretical methods. The set-theoretical tools that we rely on were developed by the first author, and are based on partially elementary subuniverses of the set-theoretic universe. They were used to prove the flat cover conjecture for certain categories of monoid acts
[
8
]
.

Main results.
We discuss, and state, the main results of our paper. The statements are precise, although not all components are defined yet and the actual statements contain more details.

We recall that a

of an object
A

A
is the colimit of this chain. Being “smaller” is made precise using the language of presentability of objects, which is a categorical way of measuring their size. Our main result is the following.

Let

Definition 2.3
.

Fix some language
\mathcal{L}
. A

\forall\bar{x}(\varphi(\bar{x})\to\exists!\bar{y}\psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})),

Languages may be multi-sorted, which requires no special treatment. When considering languages, logical formulas, and theories we stick to finitary logic (unless explicitly mentioned otherwise). While infinitary logic has a rich interaction with locally presentable categories, we will only be interested in the finitary case.

Fact 2.4

(
[
2

)

A category is locally finitely presentable if and only if it is equivalent to the category of models

Fact 2.5

(i)

(ii)

The Lévy hierarchy provides an important stratification of formulas in the language of set theory (which has the single binary relation symbol

in
\mathcal{K}^{2}

∎

Lemma 5.17

Define a functor

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{K}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathbf{Eff}(\mathcal{K})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{K}^{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

Appendix A

Lemma A.1
.

Let
T
be a limit theory in a finitary language
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\Phi(C,D,T,\mathcal{L})
denote the relation of
C
being a colimit of the diagram
D
in
\mathbf{Mod}(T)
. Then
\Phi
is
\Sigma_{1}
-expressible in
(V,\in)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{Str}(\mathcal{L})
denote the category of
\mathcal{L}
-structures, and the morphisms are homomorphisms. A representative colimit of
D
in
\mathbf{Mod}(T)
can be constructed in two steps:

(A)

First construct the colimit of
D
in
\mathbf{Str}(\mathcal{L})
.

(B)

Then take the reflection of that
\mathcal{L}
-structure in
\mathbf{Mod}(T)
.

So
\Phi(C,D,T,\mathcal{L})
holds if and only if
C
is isomorphic to that particular representative; i.e., if and only if the following assertion holds:

\displaystyle\exists C_{2}\ \exists C_{1}\ \exists f\ \Big(

\displaystyle\underbrace{C_{1}\text{ is the canonical }\mathbf{Str}(\mathcal{L})\text{-colimit of }D}_{\Phi_{1}(C_{1},\mathcal{L},D)}

\displaystyle\wedge\ \underbrace{C_{2}\text{ is the canonical reflection of }C_{1}\text{ from }\mathbf{Str}(\mathcal{L})\text{ into }\mathbf{Mod}(T)}_{\Phi_{2}(C_{2},C_{1},\mathcal{L},T)}

\displaystyle\wedge\ \underbrace{f\text{ is an isomorphism from }C\text{ to }C_{2}}_{\Phi_{3}(f,C,C_{2},\mathcal{L})}\Big)

It suffices to show that each of
\Phi_{1}
,
\Phi_{2}
, and
\Phi_{3}
is
\Sigma_{1}
-expressible.

The statement
\Phi_{3}(f,C,C_{2},\mathcal{L})
is in fact
\Delta_{0}
-expressible, because it is the conjunction of “
f
is an
\mathcal{L}
-homomorphism from
C
to
C_{2}
” and “
f
is a bijection from
C
to
C_{2}
”, each of which is expressible using only bounded quantifiers. The assumption that
\mathcal{L}
is a

language is crucial for the

-expressibility of “

is a homomorphism”, since it depends on the fact that if

is a set, the set

is
\Delta_{0}
-definable in ZF from the parameter

[
17
, Chapter IV, Theorem 5.3]
.

Next we show that

is
\Sigma_{1}
-expressible. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the (small) domain of the functor

. Then the colimit of

in

can be constructed as follows:

(1)

Let

be the disjoint union of the
\mathcal{L}
-structures indexed by

, i.e.,

This set is

-definable from the parameter

, because

if and only if
\exists a\in\Obj(\mathcal{A})
and

such that

. That last sentence still has some untranslated parts; e.g., since we view

as the tuple
(\Obj(\mathcal{A}),\Mor(\mathcal{A}),\circ_{\mathcal{A}},(id_{a})_{a\in\Obj(\mathcal{A})})
, the fragment “

such that …” is short for “there exists an

that is an element of the first coordinate of

such that …”. That this and the other shorthands are also

-expressible follows from the complexity computations in

, Chapter IV]
, which we will implicitly use throughout this argument.

(2)

Let

augment

by adding a new constant symbol

for every

be the set of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
-terms. There are various ways one could code up

, but the key point is that we are working in a finitary language, so the terms are members of

(3)

.
