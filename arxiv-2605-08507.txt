Abstract
In this paper, we provide a systematic comparison between the equisingularity of a 1-parameter unfolding F = ( f t , t ) F=(f_{t},t) of a finitely determined map germ f : ( ℂ 2 , 0 ) → ( ℂ 3 , 0 ) f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{3},0) and the equisingularity of its associated families of double point curves: D ⁡ ( F ) D(F) , F ⁡ ( D ⁡ ( F ) ) F(D(F)) , D 2 ​ ( F ) D^{2}(F) , and D 2 ​ ( F ) / S 2 D^{2}(F)/S_{2} . We also construct explicit counterexamples to several natural questions concerning the equisingularity of these loci. As a key application, we introduce new families of complete intersection curves — referred to as Henry-type families — which are topologically trivial but fail to satisfy Whitney equisingularity conditions. Finally, we generalize classical double point curve formulas, originally established for map germs from ( ℂ 2 , 0 ) (\mathbb{C}^{2},0) to ( ℂ 3 , 0 ) (\mathbb{C}^{3},0) , to the higher-dimensional setting of map germs from ( ℂ n , 0 ) (\mathbb{C}^{n},0) to ( ℂ 2 ​ n − 1 , 0 ) (\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0) for n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , providing the associated curves with a convenient analytic structure.

Equisingularity, double point curve, finite determinacy.

2.1
Double point spaces for map germs

Multiple point spaces of a map germ from
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{p},0)
with
n\leq p
play an important role in the study of its geometry. In this section, we will deal only with double points, which is a fundamental notion in the setting considered in this work.

We are interested in studying the space of double points, which is denoted by
D^{2}(f)
and its projection on the source of
f
which is denoted by
D(f)
. Roughly speaking,
D^{2}(f)
is the set of points
(\textbf{x},\textbf{x}^{{}^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\textbf{x}\neq\textbf{x}^{{}^{\prime}}
and
f(\textbf{x})=f(\textbf{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
or
x
is a singular point of
f
. In order to see
D^{2}(f)
as an analytic space, we need to present an appropriate analytic structure for it. We will follow the construction of
[
25
]
, which is valid for holomorphic maps from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}^{p}
, with
n\leq p
.

Let us denote the diagonals of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
by
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
, respectively, and denote the sheaves of ideals defining them by
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}_{p}
, respectively. We write the points of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
as
(\underline{x},\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
and
(\underline{X},\underline{X}^{{}^{\prime}})
, respectively.
Locally,

\mathcal{I}_{n}=\langle x_{1}-x_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},\cdots,x_{n}-x_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}\rangle
and
\mathcal{I}_{p}=\langle X_{1}-X_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},\cdots,X_{p}-X_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\rangle
.

Since the pull-back
(f\times f)^{\ast}\mathcal{I}_{p}
is contained in
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, there exist
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}}
, such that

f_{i}(\underline{x})-f_{i}(\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})=\alpha_{i1}(\underline{x},\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})(x_{1}-x_{1}^{{}^{\prime}})+\cdots+\alpha_{in}(\underline{x},\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})(x_{n}-x^{{}^{\prime}}_{n}),\ for\ i=1,\cdots,p.

If
f(\underline{x})=f(\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
and
\underline{x}\neq\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}}
, then every
n\times n
minor of the matrix
\alpha=(\alpha_{ij})
must vanish at
(\underline{x},\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
the ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{p}}
generated by the
n\times n
minors of
\alpha
. Then we define the
double point space
D^{2}(f)
(as a complex space) by

D^{2}(f)=V((f\times f)^{\ast}\mathcal{I}_{p}+\mathcal{R}(\alpha)).

Although the ideal
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
depends on the choice of the coordinate functions of
f
, in
[
25
]
it is proved that
\mathcal{I}^{2}(f)
does not, and so
D^{2}(f)
is well defined. It is easy to see that the points in the underlying set of
D^{2}(f)
are exactly the ones of type
(\underline{x},\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
with
\underline{x}\neq\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}}
,
f(\underline{x})=f(\underline{x}^{{}^{\prime}})
and the ones of type
(\underline{x},\underline{x})
such that
\underline{x}
is a singular point of
f
.

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{p},0)
be a finite map germ and take a representative of
f
defined on a sufficiently small open neighborhood of the origin. Denote by
I_{p}
and
R(\alpha)
the stalks at
0
of
\mathcal{I}_{p}
and
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
. We define the
double point space of the map germ
f
as the complex space germ

(D^{2}(f),0)=(V((f\times f)^{\ast}I_{p}+R(\alpha)),0)

Now we consider dimensions
n=2
and
p=3
. Other important spaces for studying the topology of
f(\mathbb{C}^{2})
are the double point curve
D(f)
in the source and its image under
f
, denoted by
f(D(f))
. According to the literature (see
[

), an appropriate analytic structure for these curves is the one given by Fitting ideals. In the sequel,

2.2
Finite determinacy, cross caps and triple points

The notion of a finitely determined map germ from
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
is central to this study, and we begin by providing its classical definition. Furthermore, the number of cross-caps and triple points appearing in a stabilization of such germs plays a fundamental role throughout this paper.

Definition 2.3

(a) Two map germs
f,g:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
are
\mathcal{A}
-equivalent, denoted by
g\sim_{\mathcal{A}}f
, if there exist germs of diffeomorphisms
\Phi:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
and
\Psi:(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
, such that
g=\Psi\circ f\circ\Phi
.

(b) A map germ
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
is
\mathcal{A}
-finitely determined (or “finitely determined” for simplicity) if there exists a positive integer
k
such that for any
g
with
k
-jets satisfying
j^{k}g(0)=j^{k}f(0)
we have
g\sim_{\mathcal{A}}f
.

Remark 2.4

Consider a finite map germ
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
. By Mather-Gaffney criterion
(
[
43
,
Th
. 2.1]
)
,

is finitely determined if and only if there is a finite representative
f:U\rightarrow V
, where
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
are open neighborhoods of the origin, such that
f^{-1}(0)=\{0\}
and the restriction
f:U\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow V\setminus\{0\}
is stable.

This means that the only singularities of
f
on
U\setminus\{0\}
are cross-caps (or Whitney umbrellas), transverse double and triple points. By shrinking
U
if necessary, we can assume that there are no cross-caps nor triple points in
U

(
[
23
,
p
. 208]
)
, we can take a stabilization
F
of
f

F:U\times T\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{4}
,
F(x,y,t)=(f_{t}(x,y),t)
,

where
T
is a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
. It is well known that the number of cross-caps of
f_{t}
(denoted by
C(f)
) and the number of triple points of
f_{t}
(denoted by
T(f)
) are independent of the particular choice of the stabilization

(see for instance

[
26
]
).

These are analytic invariants of

(see

[
25

2.3
Classical notions of equisingularity in families of curves

Let us begin the section by reviewing the concepts of topological triviality, Whitney equisingularity and bi-Lipschitz equisingularity of families of curves, and stating
the main criteria we will be using. Consider a germ of a reduced, equidimensional complex surface
(\textbf{X},0)
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
together with a flat analytic map
p:(\textbf{X},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
, and let
p:\textbf{X}\to T
be a representative, where
T
is an open neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
. The surface
X
can be viewed as a flat
1
-parameter deformation of the curve
\textbf{X}_{0}:=p^{-1}(0)
. Suppose that the singular locus of
X
is smooth of dimension one, and that there exists a section
\sigma:T\to\textbf{X}
, such that the image of
\sigma
is smooth and each fiber
\textbf{X}_{t}:=p^{-1}(t)
has a unique singular point at
\sigma(t)
(see Figure
2
).

Definition 2.6

Let
p:(\textbf{X},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
be a family of reduced curves and suppose that there is a good representative
p:\textbf{X}\rightarrow T
with a section
\sigma:T\rightarrow\textbf{X}
, such that both
\sigma(T)
and
\textbf{X}_{t}\setminus\sigma(t)
are smooth for
t\in T
.

(a) We say that
p:\textbf{X}\rightarrow T
is topologically trivial (or for simplicity,
X
is topologically trivial) if there is a homeomorphism
h:\textbf{X}\rightarrow\textbf{X}_{0}\times T
such that
p=p^{{}^{\prime}}\circ h
, where
p^{{}^{\prime}}:\textbf{X}_{0}\times T\rightarrow T
is the projection on the second factor.

(b) Let
p:\textbf{X}\rightarrow T
be a topologically trivial family of reduced curves. If in addition,
h
is bi-Lipschitz (that is,
h
and its inverse
h^{-1}
are Lipschitz maps with the ambient metric), then we say that
X
is bi-Lipschitz equisingular.

(c) We say that
p:\textbf{X}\rightarrow T
is Whitney equisingular (or for simplicity,
X
is Whitney equisingular) if the stratification
\{\textbf{X}\setminus\sigma(T),\sigma(T)\}
satisfies Whitney’s condition (b) at
0
, that is:

(Whitney’s condition b): For any sequences of points
(x_{n})\subset\textbf{X}\setminus\sigma(T)
and
(t_{n})\subset\sigma(T)\setminus\{0\}
, both converging to
0
, and such that the sequence of lines
(x_{n}t_{n})
converges to a line
l
and the sequence of directions of tangent spaces
T_{x_{n}}\textbf{X}
, to
X
at
x_{n}
, converges to a linear space
H
we have that the line
l
is contained in
H
.

A classical result asserts that
X
is topologically trivial if and only if the Milnor number
\mu(\textbf{X}_{t},\sigma(t))
of its fibers remains constant along
\sigma(T)
(see
[
6
, Th.
5.2.2
]
). Furthermore,
X
is Whitney equisingular precisely when it is topologically trivial and the multiplicity
m(\textbf{X}_{t},\sigma(t))
of
\textbf{X}_{t}
at
\sigma(t)
remains constant for all sufficiently small
t\in T
(see
[
3
, Th. III.3]
). Finally, we know that
X
is bi-Lipschitz equisingular if and only if the family of plane curves
p(\textbf{X}_{t})
is topologically trivial, where
p
is a generic linear projection from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
(see
[
3
, Ch. IV]
, see also
[
14
, Cor. 3.6]
).

\{\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus D(F),\ D(F)\setminus T,\ T\}.

(4)

In the target, the strata are

(

)

3
Equisingularity in families of double point curves

In this section we will provide an answer to Question
1
in the Introduction section. Consider that
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
is a finitely determined map germ and
F=(f_{t},t)
an unfolding of
f
. Initially, suppose that
F
is topologically trivial. In this case, what we can say about the equisingularity of
D(F)
,
F(D(F))
,
D^{2}(F),
and
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
?

In
[
7
]
, Callejas Bedregal, Houston, and Ruas showed that if
F
is topologically trivial then all the four families of double point curves
D(F)
,
D^{2}(F)
,
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
and
F(D(F))
are topologically trivial. We will present here an alternative presentation of this result. Our contribution in Lemma
3.1
is only item (b), the other items are already known in the literature.

Lemma 3.1

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
be a finitely determined map germ and consider an unfolding
F=(f_{t},t)
of
f
. If
F
is topologically trivial, then

(a)
D(F)
is bi-Lipschitz equisingular.

(b)
D^{2}(F)
is Whitney equisingular.

(c)
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
and
F(D(F))
are topologically trivial.

Proof.
(a) Suppose
F
is topologically trivial. Since the homeomorphisms in the source of
F
must send the double point curve
D(f_{t})
in
D(f)
, therefore
D(F)
is a topologically trivial deformation of
D(f)
. Since
D(F)
is a family of plane curves is also bi-Lipschitz equisingular (see
[
30
, Th. 1.1]
).

(c) For any
t
, it follows by
[
22
]
and
[
21
]
that

\mu(D(f_{t}))=\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}))+6T(f_{t})\ \ \ and\ \ \ \mu(D(f_{t}))=2\mu(f_{t}(D(f_{t})))+C(f_{t})-2T(f_{t})-1.

(6)

Since
C
and
T
are topological invariants (see
[
10
]
) and
F
is topologically trivial, by the upper semicontinuity of the invariants in (
6
) we obtain that
\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}))
and
\mu(f_{t}(D(f_{t})))
are constant. Therefore,
D^{2}(F)
and
F(D(F))
are topologically trivial. It follows by

\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}))=2\mu(D^{2}(f_{t})/S_{2})+C(f_{t})-1

(7)

that
\mu(D^{2}(f_{t})/S_{2})
is also constant. Therefore,
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
is also topologically trivial.

(b) It follows from the proof of (c) that
D^{2}(F)
is topologically trivial. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that the multiplicity of
D^{2}(f_{t})
is constant. Let
D(f)^{1}
be an irreducible component of
D(f)
with multiplicity
m_{1}
. We can take a Puiseux parametrization
\varphi^{1}
for
D(f)^{1}
defined by

\varphi^{1}(u):=\left(u^{m_{1}},\displaystyle{\sum_{i\geq m_{1}}}a_{1,i}u^{i}\right).

Suppose that
D^{2}(f)^{1}
is an irreducible component of
D^{2}(f)
such that
p(D^{2}(f)^{1})=D(f)^{1}
, where the map
p:\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the projection onto the second factor. Since
D^{2}(f)
is
S_{2}
invariant, then
there exists an irreducible component
D(f)^{2}
of multiplicity
m_{2}
of
D(f)
(not necessarily distinct from
D(f)^{1})
parametrized by Puiseux parametrization
\varphi^{2}

\varphi^{2}(u):=\left(u^{m_{2}},\displaystyle{\sum_{i\geq m_{2}}}b_{1,i}u^{i}\right)

and it follows by
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
that there exists an invertible element
\alpha
in
\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathbb{C}\{u\}
and a Puiseux parametrization
\tilde{\varphi}^{1}
for
D^{2}(f)^{1}
such that

\tilde{\varphi}^{1}(u):=\left(u^{m_{1}},\displaystyle{\sum_{i\geq m_{1}}}a_{1,i}u^{i},(\alpha u)^{m_{2}},\displaystyle{\sum_{i\geq m_{2}}}b_{1,i}(\alpha u)^{i}\right).

Therefore, the multiplicity of
D^{2}(f)^{1}
is the minimum of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. Since
D(F)
is Whitney equisingular, the multiplicity of
D(f_{t})^{1}
is constant. Since the multiplicity is additive, we obtain that the multiplicity of
D^{2}(f_{t})
is constant.

Ruas and the first author showed in
[
37

that if

is topologically trivial then

may not be Whitney equisingular. In the next proposition we will show that if

Theorem 3.5

Since

(respec.

) passing through the origin. Let

For each

the component
D(f)^{i}
of

can be parametrized by
\varphi_{i}(u)=(a_{i}u,b_{i}u),
for some

On the other hand, if a pair

, then the component

can be parametrized by

\phi_{i}(u)=(a_{i}u,b_{i}u,c_{i}u,d_{i}u),

(9)

where
\psi_{i}(u)=(c_{i}u,d_{i}u)
is a convenient reparametrization of the line

With an analogous argument, if the restriction of

to

is 2-to-1, then

has a parametrization in the from of (

). However,

is a reparametrization of

itself. Now, let

be the director vector of

and, for each

4
Families of curves of Henry-type

An important family of curves was presented by J. P. G. Henry. In the following example, we recall this family of curves (see, for instance,
[
3
, Ex. V.1]
):

Example 4.1
(J. P. G. Henry)

Define
\textbf{X}_{t}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
by equations

xy-tz=0\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ z^{6}+x^{15}+y^{10}=0.

Each
\textbf{X}_{t}
is a quasihomogeneous complete intersection curve singularity. For any
t
,
\mu(\textbf{X}_{t},0)=126,
therefore it is topologically trivial. However,
m(\textbf{X}_{0},0)=12,
while
m(\textbf{X}_{t},0)=10,
for all
t\neq 0
sufficiently small. This implies that
\textbf{X}_{t}
is not Whitney equisingular.

Some works have used this kind of family of curves to present counterexamples for certain problems (see, for instance,
[
3
, Ex. V.1]
,
[
6
, Ex. 7.2.1]
, and
[
19
, Ex. 4.21]
). Inspired by Example
4.1
, we say that a family of curves
\textbf{X}_{t}
is of “Henry-type” if each
\textbf{X}_{t}
is a quasihomogeneous complete intersection curve and the family is topologically trivial but not Whitney equisingular. In Proposition
3.2
, the family of curves
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
is a family of curves of Henry-type, and provides a counterexample to one of the problems that we consider in this work.

Since this type of family of curves is a powerful tool for constructing counterexamples, to finish this work, we present families of curves of Henry-type as in Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 4.2

For each odd integer
k\geq 1
, let
f^{k}_{t}:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
be the family of quasihomogeneous, corank 1 map germs given by

f^{k}_{t}(x,y)=(x,y^{4}+txy,y^{6k+1}+x^{2k}y).

Then,
f^{k}_{0}
is finitely determined,
D^{2}(f_{0}^{k})/S_{2}
is a quasihomogeneous complete intersection. Furthermore,
\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2},0)=(3k-1)(6k-1),
for all
t,\,m(D^{2}(f_{0}^{k})/S_{2},0)=4k,
while
m(D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2},0)=3k,
for all
t\neq 0
sufficiently small. In particular,
D^{2}(f^{k}_{t})/S_{2}
is a family of curves of Henry-type.

Proof.
Fix an odd integer
k\geq 1.
Note that
f_{0}^{k}
is a reflection map (see
[
33
]
). Then, by
[
1
, Th. 5.2]
(see also
[
12
, Prop. 6.3]
) we have that

\displaystyle D(f_{0}^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(((1-\xi^{3})y^{6k}+(1-\xi)x^{2k})((1-\xi^{2})y^{6k}+(1-\xi^{2})x^{2k})((1-\xi)y^{6k}+(1-\xi^{3})x^{2k})),

where
\xi\in\mathbb{C},\,\xi\neq 1,
is a primitive root of the unity of the fourth order. It can be verified that
D(f^{k}_{0})
is a reduced curve By
[
22
, Cor. 3.5]
, we conclude that
f_{0}^{k}
is finitely determined. In particular,
D^{2}(f_{0}^{k})/S_{2}
is a quasihomogeneous complete intersection. Now, consider the map
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}
given by
\psi(x,y,z)=(x,y+z,(y-z)^{2}).
This map is such that
\psi(D^{2}(f_{t}^{k}))\simeq D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2},
locally. Therefore,
D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2}
is generated by the ideal
(\psi^{*})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{2}(f_{t}^{k})^{S_{2}}),
where
\mathcal{I}_{2}(f_{t}^{k})
is the ideal that defines

In this case, it can be verified that

D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2}=V(\sigma_{1}^{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}+2tx,x^{2}+p_{k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})),

where
\sigma_{1}=y+z,\sigma_{2}=(y-z)^{2}
and
p_{k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
is a quasihomogeneous polynomial of degree
6k
, and the weights of

and

[
27
]

[

for all
t.
On the other hand, let

be a generic plane. Then,

m(D^{2}(f_{0}^{k})/S_{2},0)=dim_{\mathbb{C}}\dfrac{\mathbb{C}\left\{x,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right\}}{(\sigma_{1}^{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2},x^{2k}+p_{k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}),ax+b\sigma_{1}+c\sigma_{2})}=4k,

while

m(D^{2}(f_{t}^{k})/S_{2},0)=dim_{\mathbb{C}}\dfrac{\mathbb{C}\left\{x,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right\}}{(2xt+\sigma_{1}^{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2},x^{2k}+p_{k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}),ax+b\sigma_{1}+c\sigma_{2})}=3k,

for all
t\neq 0
sufficiently small.

5
Some double point formulas

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0)
be a finitely determined map germ, with
n\geq 3.
In this section, we present a convenient analytic structure for the curves
D(f)
and
f(D(f))
and we also provide a positive answer to Question 3 in the Introduction section.

We know that the curves
D^{2}(f)
and
D(f)^{red}
are reduced curves, while
D(f)
is only a generically reduced curve. In this case, we can consider the
\mathcal{A}
-invariants numbers of
f

\mu(D^{2}(f),0),\ \ \mu(D(f)^{red},0),\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mu(D(f),0).

Except when
f
has the Boardman symbol
\Sigma^{1,0}
(see
[
39
, Ex. 5.3]
), a natural question that arises at this point is whether it is possible to present a convenient analytic structure for
f(D(f)).
The most natural structure would be that given by the first Fitting ideal of a presentation matrix of
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{n},
as in the case of map germs from
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0).
However, little is known about the behavior of the analytic structure given by the Fitting ideals of
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{n}
when
n\geq 3.

In general, if
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{p},0)
is a finite map germ and
p-n\geq 2,
then there is no algorithm yet to effectively calculate the
presentation matrix of
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{n}
as in the case of finite map germs from
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0).
Therefore, as in
[
19
]
, let us consider
f(D(f))
from a geometric point of view. In this way, we can consider some geometric concepts that can be defined without the need for an analytic structure on
f(D(f))
, for example, the polar multiplicities
m(f(D(f)),0)
and
m_{1}(f(D(f)),0).

Remark 5.1

As in the case of map germ from
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0),
we can also define the quotient double point space
D^{2}(f)/S_{2}
for map germs from
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0),
with
n\geq 3.
For this space, we consider the reduced structure.

Now, let
F=(f_{t},t)
be an unfolding of a finitely determined map germ
f=f_{0}
from
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
to
(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0),
with
n\geq 3.
Consider the complex spaces
D^{2}(F)
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C},0),

D(F)
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C},0),

F(D(F))
in
(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1}\times\mathbb{C},0),
and
D^{2}(F)/S_{2}
in
(\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}/S_{2},0).
Let
p:D^{2}(F)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
be a projection onto the first factor. Denoting by
I_{F}=\sqrt{Fitt_{0}(p_{*}\mathcal{O}_{D^{2}(F)})}
, we will consider the reduced analytic structure for
D(F)
given by

D(F)=V(I_{F}).

Furthermore, let
J_{F}
be the ideal given by
\sqrt{Fitt_{1}(F_{*}\mathcal{O}_{n})}.
In this case, the analytic structure that we consider for
F(D(F))
is given by

F(D(F))=V(J_{F}).

One can verify that these double point curves do not depend on the choice of
F.
Now, we consider the analytic structure of
D(f)
given by

D(f)=V(I_{F},t),

(11)

and the analytic structure of
f(D(f))
is given by

f(D(f))=V(J_{F},t).

(12)

Let
\pi:D(F)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
be a projection onto the last factor. Since the Fitting ideals have the property of being compatible with respect to the base change (see
[
16
]
), we obtain that the analytic structure of the fibers of
\pi,
which coincides with the analytic structure of the curves
D(f_{t}),
given by each ideal
\sqrt{Fitt_{0}(p_{*}\mathcal{O}_{D^{2}(f_{t})})},
that is,
\pi^{-1}(t)=D(f_{t}).
In this case,
\pi:D(F)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
is an unfolding of the plane curve
D(f).

For more details on this construction, see
[
39
, Sec. 5.2]
. Now, inspired by the results of Jorge-Pérez and Nuño-Ballesteros in
[
19
]
, we have the following theorem, which provides formulas to the invariants
\mu(D(f),0)
and
\mu(D(f)^{red},0)
.

Theorem 5.2

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0)
be a finitely determined map germ, with
n\geq 3.
Then

(a)
\mu(D(f),0)=\mu(D^{2}(f),0).

(b)
\mu(D(f)^{red},0)=\mu(D^{2}(f),0)+2\epsilon(D(f),0),
where
\epsilon(D(f),0)
is the epsilon invariant of
D(f)
at the origin.

Proof.
(a) Let
F=(f_{t},t)
be a stabilization of
f.
By
[
19
, Prop. 2.6]
, we have that the projections
\pi:D^{2}(F)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
and
\pi:D(F)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
are unfoldings of the plane curves
D^{2}(F)
and
D(F),
respectively. Fix a representative
F:U\times T\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2n-1}\times T
, where
U
and
T
are neighborhoods at the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C},
respectively, such that
\pi:D^{2}(F)\rightarrow T
and
\pi:D(F)\rightarrow T
are good representatives, in the sense of
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
. Thus, for all
t\in D,
by
[
6
, Th. 4.2.2]
and
[
5
, Lemma 3.1.2]
, we have that

\mu(D^{2}(f),0)-\sum_{(x,x^{\prime})\in D^{2}(f_{t})}\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}),(x,x^{\prime}))=1-\chi(D^{2}(f_{t}))

(13)

and

\mu(D(f),0)-\sum_{x\in D(f_{t})}\mu(D(f_{t}),x)=1-\chi(D(f_{t})),

(14)

where
\chi(\textbf{Y})
denotes the Euler characteristic of the complex space
\textbf{Y}.
Since
F
is a stabilization of
f,
we have that
D^{2}(f_{t})
and
D(f_{t})
are smooth (see
[
19
, Prop. 2.6]
). Thus

\sum_{(x,x^{\prime})\in D^{2}(f_{t})}\mu(D^{2}(f_{t}),(x,x^{\prime}))=\sum_{x\in D(f_{t})}\mu(D(f_{t}),x)=0.

By
[
19
, Cor. 2.4]
, we have that the projection
p_{1}:D^{2}(f_{t})\rightarrow D(f_{t})
is a biholomorphism, for all
t\neq 0
. Therefore,
\chi(D^{2}(f_{t}))=\chi(D(f_{t})).
Thus, by (
13
) and (
14
), we conclude that
\mu(D^{2}(f),0)=\mu(D(f),0).

(b) The proof is analogous to item (a) using the formula
\mu(D(f),0)=\mu(D(f)^{red},0)+2\epsilon(D(f),0).

Example 5.3

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{5},0)

[

2.3]

2.14]

\mu(D^{2}(f),0)=1.
By Theorem

5.2
, we conclude that
\mu(D(f),0)=1.

Now, let
p:\mathbb{C}^{3}\times\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}

to

A presentation matrix of

Then,

\displaystyle D(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(Fitt_{0}(p_{*}\mathcal{O}_{D^{2}(f)}))

\displaystyle V(xy+yz^{3}+xz^{4}+z^{7},x^{2}z^{2}+xz^{5},x^{2}+2xz^{3}+z^{6},xy+x^{2}z+yz^{3}+2xz^{4}+z^{7},

\displaystyle-x^{2}-xz^{3},-y^{2}-xyz-x^{2}z^{2}-2yz^{4}-xz^{5}-z^{8}).
