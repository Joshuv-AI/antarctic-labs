On the independence of the slow and fast scales in multiple-scale expansions, with application to Van der Pol’s equation Journal: rspa
Gregory Kozyreff 1 and John R. King 2

Abstract
When implementing the method of multiple scales, one is instructed to treat the slow and fast time scales as if they were independent. Despite the intuitive motivation and the effectiveness of this perturbation method, one cannot fail to notice that these two scales relate to the same unique variable, so independence can only be formal. How sensible is it, then, to split a variable asymptotically into two independent ones? We elucidate this issue with Van der Pol’s equation, a simple example of a Hopf bifurcation. The discussion involves carrying the multiple-scale analysis up to arbitrarily large order and dealing with the divergent character of the resulting asymptotic series. Using the technique of optimal truncation, we re-connect the two scales. Specifically, we show that an initial translation of the fast coordinate leads to a nontrivial, exponentially small, phase shift that depends on the slow coordinate. This phase shift breaks the independence of the slow and fast scales and is found to result from the nonlinearity. Numerical simulations confirm its existence, as well as the predicted scaling. The calculation is carried out in sufficient detail to provide confidence in the generality of our result, both in its essence and in its form.

, we may then write

\tau_{0}-\tau=i\rho-\sqrt{\epsilon}s\sim i\rho e^{i\sqrt{\epsilon}s/\rho+\epsilon s^{2}/2\rho^{2}}=i\rho e^{-1-i(\tau_{0}-\tau)/\rho+\epsilon s^{2}/2\rho^{2}}.

(34)

Next, combining Stirling’s formula,
\Gamma(x)\sim(2\pi/x)^{1/2}(x/e)^{x}
, and the condition of optimal truncation,
J+\alpha\sim 2\rho/\epsilon
, we find

\left(\frac{i\epsilon}{2\left(\tau_{0}-\tau\right)}\right)^{J+\alpha}\Gamma(J+\alpha)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi\epsilon}{\rho}}e^{2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon}e^{-s^{2}/\rho}e^{-2i\theta}

(35)

in
Equation

22
. Hence, the equation for the remainder is

\mathcal{L}R=4\epsilon^{-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\epsilon}{\rho}}e^{2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon}e^{-s^{2}/\rho}e^{2i\phi}\left[f_{1}(\tau)e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}-f_{3}(\tau)e^{i(\theta+\phi)}\right].

(36)

Note the phase factor
e^{2i\phi}
. The right-hand side suggests to seek a multiple-scale solution of the form
R(\theta,s,\tau)
and one readily finds

R\sim R_{+}=2i\pi\epsilon^{-\alpha}e^{2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon}e^{2i\phi}\left[f_{3}(\tau)e^{i(\theta+\phi)}+f_{1}(\tau)e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}\right]\mathcal{S}\left(s/\sqrt{\rho}\right),

(37)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the Stokes function given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

\displaystyle\bigg(\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathcal{S}(x)

\displaystyle=1\bigg)

(38)

(cf.
[
22
]
) and the label
+
refers to the singularity
\tau_{0}
. Finally, a contribution
R_{-}
to the remainder comes from the singularity at
\tau=\bar{\tau}_{0}
. It must be such as to make
R
real on the real line. Hence, we have, simply,

R\sim 2i\pi\epsilon^{-\alpha}e^{2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon}e^{2i\phi}\left[f_{3}(\tau)e^{i(\theta+\phi)}+f_{1}(\tau)e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}\right]\mathcal{S}\left(s/\sqrt{\rho}\right)+\mathrm{c.c.}.

(39)

The size of
R
is controlled by the factor
\exp 2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon=\exp-2\pi/\epsilon
. The factor
2
appearing in the exponential is the value of
\Delta k
that was retained in the late-terms of the series. It comes from the fact that only odd harmonics are contained in the solution. Had there been a quadratic nonlinearity in Van der Pol’s equation, all harmonics of the fundamental oscillation would be present and
\Delta k=1
would be allowed, thus substantially increasing
R
.

Near the singularity

It remains to determine the constants
\lambda_{\theta,\tau}
and the offsets
\alpha_{\theta,\tau}
. The latter set is easy. Note, simply, that as
\tau\to\tau_{0}=i\pi
,
A_{0,\pm 1}\sim i/\sqrt{i\pi-\tau}
and, from there, deduce that

\displaystyle A_{j,k}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{B_{j,k}}{\left(i\pi-\tau\right)^{j+1/2}},

as

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\to i\pi,

with

\displaystyle B_{0,\pm 1}

\displaystyle=i.

(40)

This is to be compared with the
\tau\to i\pi
limit of the previously derived late-term expressions, for
k=1,3
:

\displaystyle A_{j,1}

\displaystyle\sim\left[\left(\frac{i/2}{i\pi-\tau}\right)^{j+\alpha_{s}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha_{s})}{\left(i\pi-\tau\right)^{1/2}}\lambda_{\theta}+\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{i/2}{i\pi-\tau}\right)^{j+\alpha_{\tau}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha_{\tau})}{\left(i\pi-\tau\right)^{3/2}}\lambda_{\tau}\right],

(41)

\displaystyle A_{j,3}

\displaystyle\sim\left[-\left(\frac{i/2}{i\pi-\tau}\right)^{j+\alpha_{s}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha_{s})}{\left(i\pi-\tau\right)^{1/2}}\lambda_{\theta}+\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{i/2}{i\pi-\tau}\right)^{j+\alpha_{\tau}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha_{\tau})}{\left(i\pi-\tau\right)^{3/2}}\lambda_{\tau}\right].

(42)

Matching the strengths of the singularities requires

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\tau}

\displaystyle=-1.

(43)

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{\theta}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{-j}\frac{B_{j,1}-B_{j,3}}{2\Gamma(j)},

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\gg 1.

(44)

Now if we substitute
eq.

40
into
eq.

12
and monitor
b_{j,k}=(i/2)^{-j}B_{j,k}
, we realise that the coefficients of the recurrence for
b_{j,k}
are all real. Hence, given that
b_{j,\pm 1}
are purely imaginary, so are all subsequently computed
b_{j,k}
. We may thus assert that the right hand side of
eq.

44
is purely imaginary. In the Appendix, we show that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-i\Lambda,

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\approx 0.023080.

(45)

Neglecting the contribution to
A_{j,k}
associated with
\lambda_{\tau}
, which is
O(J^{-1})
smaller and which tends to zero as
\tau\to\infty
anyway, we have, in
eq.

39
,

\displaystyle f_{3}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Lambda A_{0,1}(\tau),

\displaystyle f_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\Lambda A_{0,-1}(\tau).

(46)

As
s/\sqrt{\rho}\to\infty
,
\mathcal{S}(s/\sqrt{\rho}\to\infty)\to 1
and as
\tau\to\infty
,
A_{0,\pm 1}\to 1
. Hence, the remainder given by
eq.

39
becomes

R\sim 4\pi\Lambda\mbox{Re}\left\{e^{2i\tau_{0}/\epsilon}e^{2i\phi}\left[iA_{0,1}e^{i(\theta+\phi)}-iA_{0,1}e^{i(\theta+\phi)}\right]\right\}\\
=-8\pi\Lambda e^{-2\pi/\epsilon}\cos(2\phi)\sin\left(\theta+\phi\right)\\
=4\pi\Lambda e^{-2\pi/\epsilon}\cos(2\phi)\partial_{\theta}\left[2\cos\left(\theta+\phi\right)\right]

(47)

Comparing this last expression with
eq.

27
, and given that
2\cos(\theta+\phi)
is the leading-order approximation of the solution in the long-time limit, we thus find that

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\Delta\theta\sim 4\pi\Lambda e^{-2\pi/\epsilon}\cos(2\phi).

(48)

This last expression is the main result of this paper. According to it, the maximum variation of
\Delta\theta
as function of
\phi
is

\Delta(\epsilon)\equiv 8\pi\Lambda\,e^{-2\pi/\epsilon}\approx 0.58\,e^{-2\pi/\epsilon}.

(49)

4
Numerical investigation

To test our prediction, we compute a large set of numerical solutions
Y_{\text{num}}(t)
of
Equation

6
with varying initial phases of oscillation. Setting the initial amplitude of oscillations to the value
(1+e^{20})^{-1/2}
, the nonlinear term in
Equation

6
is completely negligible, so that
Y(t)
is very well approximated at early stages of its evolution by

\frac{\mbox{Re}[e^{(\epsilon+i\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}/4})t+i\phi}]}{\sqrt{1+e^{20}}}.

Hence, an initial condition with precise phase
\phi
is

\displaystyle Y_{\text{num}}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\phi}{\sqrt{1+e^{20}}}\,,

\displaystyle Y_{\text{num}}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mbox{Re}[(\epsilon+i\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}/4})e^{i\phi}]}{\sqrt{1+e^{20}}}\,.

(50)

At the same time, we define
Y_{\infty}(t)
as being the limit-cycle solution given by
eq.

7
subject to
Y_{\infty}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Since the limit cycle attracts all trajectories in the phase plane other than
Y\equiv 0
, we necessarily have
Y_{\text{num}}(t)\to Y_{\infty}(t+\Delta t(\phi,\epsilon))
as
t\to\infty
. The final phase of oscillations is, therefore,

\phi_{\infty}(\phi,\epsilon)=\omega(\epsilon)\Delta t(\phi,\epsilon),

(51)

where
\omega(\epsilon)
is given explicitly by
eq.

8
. It breaks down into

\phi_{\infty}(\phi,\epsilon)=\phi+\phi_{\text{dyn}}(\epsilon)+\Delta\theta(\phi,\epsilon).

(52)

Above,
\Delta\theta(\phi,\epsilon)
is the quantity of interest and
\phi_{\text{dyn}}(\epsilon)
is a dynamic phase. The latter results from the slow evolution of the frequency from
\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}/4}
to
\omega(\epsilon)
as the the amplitude ramps up towards the limit cycle, independently of
\phi
. Numerically,
\Delta t(\phi,\epsilon)
is computed as

\displaystyle\Delta t(\phi,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{s}\int_{40/\epsilon}^{40/\epsilon+2\pi}\left[Y_{\infty}(t)-Y_{\text{num}}(t-s)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\in[0,2\pi/\omega(\epsilon)].

(53)

In
Figure

3

\phi_{\infty}(\phi,\epsilon)-\phi
as a function of
\phi
for three values of

and find confirmation that the quantity oscillates as

, in agreement with
eq.

48
. The constant in the argument of the foregoing cosine is due to a slight change of the way
t=0
is defined in the present numerical section compared to
section

2
. We were able to compute the amplitude
\Delta(\epsilon)
of the phase shift numerically for values of

5
Conclusion

We have attempted to show in this paper to which extent the working assumption of scale independence is erroneous in the method of multiples scales (MMS). The interest of this perturbation method precisely lies in the fact that the two scales approximately decouple, in the sense that we can separately treat phenomena occurring on each scale, and thus significantly decrease the technical difficulty of the problem under consideration. The actual coupling between the two scales is indeed exponentially weak, as was already found in previous studies
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. It is characteristic of most of these calculations that, while they can be quite involved, the end result is often comparatively simple. This pattern clearly repeats itself in the present setting, namely a uniformly attracting limit cycle. The end formula
eq.

48
is indeed compact and easily verifiable. In the present case, the exponentially small phase shift
\Delta\theta
may be regarded as a benign consequence of slow scale-fast scale interaction. However, in other settings, the resulting effect can be much more dramatic, notably by conditioning the existence of localised oscillatory states and ruling their snaking bifurcation diagrams
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

The details of the calculation allows us to locate where the interaction between the slow and fast scales takes place: in the vicinity of the Stokes lines, which is also where the square of the amplitude of oscillation has its fastest growth. Well away from this Stokes lines, we know from the Stokes function
eq.

38
that the slow and fast scales practically do not interact, even under exponentially magnifying lenses. This confirms that the underlying assumption of MMS is reliable.

The present study also serves, we hope, to make the path of exponential asymptotics with multiple-scale problems a well-beaten one. One may now indeed envisage to study other Hopf bifurcations, with slightly more complicated nonlinearities than Van der Pol’s equation. As the calculation showed, the size of the exponentially small phase shifts born at the Stokes line is related to the set of harmonics generated by the nonlinearity. Including even harmonics in the picture is bound to change the factor
\exp(-2\pi/\epsilon)
in
eq.

48
to
\exp(-\pi/\epsilon)
, making beyond-all-order effects numerically more visible. Technically, this results from the values
\Delta k
allowed in the factorial-over-power ansatz
eq.

20
. Quadratic nonlinearities lead to the possibility that
\Delta k=\pm 1
. This in turns bring the order of optimal truncation to
J\sim|\tau_{0}-\tau|/\epsilon
instead of
2|\tau_{0}-\tau|/\epsilon
.

Appendix: determination of
\Lambda

We give here more details on the resolution of the multiple-scale recurrence in the vicinity of
\tau=i\pi
. Assuming the ansatz
eq.

40
, let us first define

\displaystyle D_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}B_{j_{1},k_{1}}B_{j_{2},k_{2}}B_{j_{2},k_{2}},

\displaystyle j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}

\displaystyle=j,

\displaystyle k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}

\displaystyle=k.

(54)

Then,
eq.

12
yields, in the limit
\tau\to i\pi
,

\left(1-k^{2}\right)B_{j,k}+2ik\nu B_{j-1,k}+\nu(\nu-1)B_{j-2,k}+\frac{1}{3}ikD_{j-1,k}+\frac{1}{3}\nu D_{j-2,k}=0.

(55)

where we have introduced the short-hand notation
\nu=j-1/2
. Above, we recall that
|k|\leq 2j+1
. Furthermore, we have
[
1
]

\displaystyle B_{0,\pm 1}

\displaystyle=i,

\displaystyle B_{1,\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{7}{16},

\displaystyle B_{1,\pm 3}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{8},

(56)

and, generally,
B_{j,-k}=(-1)^{j}B_{j,k}
.

For a given
j
, we thus only need to compute
B_{j,k}
for
k=1,3,5,\dotsb,2j+1
. In so doing, we have to distinguish the harmonic
k=1
from the others. Once the amplitude
B_{j-1,k}
have been determines, we may evaluate
D_{j-1,k}
and immediately compute

B_{j,k}=\left(k^{2}-1\right)^{-1}\left[2ik\nu B_{j-1,k}+\nu(\nu-1)B_{j-2,k}+\frac{1}{3}ikD_{j-1,k}+\frac{1}{3}\nu D_{j-2,k}\right],

(57)

for
3\leq k\leq 2j+1
. The computation of
B_{j,1}
, on the other hand, requires one to solve

2iB_{j,1}+\nu B_{j-1,1}+\frac{1}{3\left(\nu+1\right)}iD_{j,1}+\frac{1}{3}D_{j-1,1}=0,

(58)

and the complication resides in the fact that
B_{j,\pm 1}
are contained in the expression of
D_{j,1}
. In practice, the main difficulty lies in the computation of
D_{j,k}
. Given the set of numbers
\{B_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\}
for
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j
, the direct implementation of
eq.

54
turns out to be code-heavy and innefficient. Instead, we find it much more convenient to successively compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2j+1}\left(B_{j,k}z^{k}+B_{j,-k}z^{-k}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{Y}^{2}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}\mathcal{Y}_{j^{\prime}}\mathcal{Y}_{j-j^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{Y}^{3}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}\left(\mathcal{Y}^{2}\right)_{j^{\prime}}\mathcal{Y}_{j-j^{\prime}},

(59)

and extract
D_{j,k}
as the coefficient of
z^{k}
in
\left(\mathcal{Y}^{3}\right)_{j}
. Such manipulations can easily be done with a computer algebra system such as Mathematica, by way of the built-in command “Coefficient” (a code is provided as Supplementary Material).

Once the numbers
B_{j,k}
have been computed, we generate the series

\Lambda^{(0)}_{j}=i\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{-j}\frac{B_{j,1}-B_{j,3}}{2\Gamma(j)},

(60)

which is expected to tend to its limiting value
\Lambda
with only an
O(j^{-2})
error. Indeed,
eqs.

41
and
42
suggest that
O(j^{-1})
terms, which are proportional to
\lambda_{\tau}
, cancel each other out in
\Lambda^{(0)}_{j}
.
Figure

5
indicates that
\Lambda^{(0)}_{j}
tends to its limit in an oscillatory fashion. We first suppress this behaviour using Shanks transformation
[
1
]
:

\Lambda^{(1)}_{j}=\frac{\Lambda^{(0)}_{j+1}\Lambda^{(0)}_{j-1}-\left(\Lambda^{(0)}_{j}\right)^{2}}{\Lambda^{(0)}_{j+1}+\Lambda^{(0)}_{j-1}-2\Lambda^{(0)}_{j}}.

(61)

The resulting series, free from oscillations, is finally accelerated using Richardson’s extrapolation, taylored to remove
O(j^{-2})
terms:

\Lambda^{(2)}_{j}=\frac{(j+1)^{2}\Lambda^{(1)}_{j+1}-j^{2}\Lambda^{(1)}_{j}}{(j+1)^{2}-j^{2}}.

(62)

The plot of
\Lambda^{(2)}_{j}
demonstrates the efficiency of the procedure, from which we extract
\Lambda\approx 0.02380.
