Adult cervical embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma: a literature review of 41 adult cases with an illustrative aggressive case
Authors: Prapaporn Suprasert, Sopita Prasertpakdi, Tip Pongsuvareeyakul, Surapan Khunamornpong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdult cervical embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) is an exceptionally rare malignant mesenchymal tumor, and evidence regarding its clinicopathologic characteristics, optimal management, and prognostic factors remains limited. We report an aggressive case of adult cervical ERMS and provide a contemporary review of the literature.Case presentationA 50-year-old woman presented with abnormal vaginal bleeding and was diagnosed with cervical ERMS following histopathologic and immunohistochemical evaluation. She underwent total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy followed by adjuvant vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide (VAC) chemotherapy. Despite multimodal treatment, she developed rapid pelvic recurrence, progressive disease, and recurrent vaginal bleeding requiring palliative radiotherapy and died 13 months after diagnosis. To better characterize adult cervical ERMS, we reviewed English-language reports published between 2015 and 2026. Forty-one previously reported adult cases with sufficient clinical information were identified. The median age at diagnosis was 38 years, and the median tumor size was 5 cm. Most patients presented with stage I disease and were treated with surgery combined with chemotherapy, most commonly VAC-based regimens. Overall outcomes were generally favorable. Uncommon clinical presentations and associated conditions included uterine inversion, pregnancy-associated disease, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, melanoma, and DICER1-associated alterations.ConclusionAdult cervical ERMS demonstrates substantial clinical heterogeneity. Although most reported patients achieve favorable outcomes with multimodal treatment, a subset may experience aggressive disease characterized by rapid recurrence and poor prognosis. Greater molecular characterization, particularly regarding DICER1-associated alterations, may improve risk stratification and support more individualized treatment strategies.Keywords: Cervix, Adult, Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, DICER1

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a malignant mesenchymal tumor characterized by skeletal muscle differentiation and is most commonly encountered in children and adolescents. It accounts for approximately 3–5% of all pediatric malignancies; however, its occurrence in adults is rare and is generally associated with a more aggressive clinical course and poorer prognosis compared with pediatric cases (Sbaraglia et al., 2021; Sultan et al., 2009) According to the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumors, RMS is classified into four major histologic subtypes: embryonal, alveolar, pleomorphic, and spindle cell/sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma (Sbaraglia et al., 2021). Among these, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) is the most common subtype in children and adolescents, whereas adult-onset ERMS remains uncommon and poorly characterized (Sultan et al., 2009; Skapek et al., 2019).

In the female genital tract, ERMS most commonly arises in the vagina and cervix and frequently presents as the botryoid variant, particularly in younger patients (Devins et al., 2022). Cervical ERMS in adults is exceedingly rare and may demonstrate distinct clinicopathologic characteristics compared with pediatric disease (Devins et al., 2022). Because of its rarity, there is limited consensus regarding optimal management strategies, and treatment approaches are largely extrapolated from pediatric protocols involving multimodal therapy with surgery and chemotherapy (Devins et al., 2022; Sultan et al., 2009).

Adult-onset cervical ERMS poses several diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Clinically, large exophytic tumors may obscure the precise site of origin, making differentiation between cervical and vaginal lesions difficult. Furthermore, the characteristic grape-like (botryoid) gross appearance may not always be evident, potentially delaying or complicating the diagnosis. Histologically, the characteristic cambium layer, defined as a subepithelial condensation of tumor cells, may be obscured by surface ulceration or hemorrhage. In addition, adult patients frequently present with significant comorbidities that may limit tolerance to aggressive multimodal treatment and adversely affect clinical outcomes (Devins et al., 2022).

Herein, we report a rare case of adult ERMS of the cervix in a patient with complex comorbidities, including chronic type B aortic dissection and schwannoma involving the groin region, requiring multidisciplinary management. The patient demonstrated an aggressive disease course despite multimodal treatment. In addition, we reviewed the literature on adult cervical ERMS, focusing on clinical presentation, histopathologic features, treatment strategies, and oncologic outcomes.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman presented with profuse vaginal bleeding and a protruding necrotic mass from the vaginal canal. Physical examination revealed a large, friable, foul-smelling mass occupying the entire vaginal cavity. Due to the bulky exophytic nature of the lesion, the exact site of origin could not be clearly distinguished between the cervix and vagina. In addition, a palpable right inguinal lymph node measuring approximately 3 × 3 cm was identified.

Initial computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen performed at presentation demonstrated an approximately 10-cm rim-enhancing heterogeneous mass containing internal air bubbles, extending into and distending the vaginal canal without definite invasion of adjacent organs. The patient was referred to our institution two weeks after the initial presentation because of persistent fever and concern for superimposed infection. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed at that time demonstrated a pedunculated mass arising from the cervix or lower uterine segment and protruding through the cervical canal into the vagina. A heterogeneous-enhancing right inguinal lesion measuring 3.3 × 2.6 cm with internal necrotic change, corresponding to the palpable right inguinal lymph node, was also identified and initially suspected to represent metastatic lymphadenopathy. Multiple uterine leiomyomas were additionally noted, including a probable leiomyoma with myxoid degeneration at the uterine fundus. No gross adnexal mass was identified (Fig. 1).

Histopathologic examination of the biopsy revealed a malignant round to spindle cell neoplasm, consistent with rhabdomyosarcoma. Because of the necrotic and clinically infected tumor, the patient initially received intravenous ceftriaxone and clindamycin, which were later escalated to ertapenem.

The patient had a significant underlying comorbidity of chronic type B aortic dissection extending from the left subclavian artery to the aortic bifurcation. Given the complexity of the case, multidisciplinary management involving gynecologic oncology, cardiology, vascular surgery, interventional radiology, and infectious disease specialists was undertaken. Preoperative assessment demonstrated preserved cardiac function, and the patient was considered suitable for surgery with careful perioperative hemodynamic control. Due to the high risk of tumor-related hemorrhage, bilateral hypogastric artery embolization was performed one day before surgery.

The patient subsequently underwent total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and adhesiolysis approximately one month after the initial presentation. Intraoperatively, a fragile 10 × 10 cm mass arising from the cervix extended into the vagina and reached the lower vaginal canal without invasion of the vaginal mucosa. No enlarged pelvic or para-aortic lymph nodes were identified; however, an enlarged right inguinal lymph node measuring approximately 4 cm, corresponding to the preoperative imaging findings, was noted intraoperatively. Inguinal lymph node dissection was deferred to minimize operative time and cardiovascular risk.

Gross examination revealed a cervical mass with an extensively ulcerated and necrotic surface. Although focal polypoid projections were present within the endocervical canal, the characteristic edematous grape-like (botryoid) appearance was absent. The cut surface was fleshy and pale tan, and the tumor showed deep infiltration into the cervical wall. Tumor extension into the endometrial cavity was also identified, without evidence of myometrial involvement (Fig. 2A).

Extensive histopathologic examination of 25 sections from the tumor revealed ERMS composed of primitive round to spindle neoplastic cells with relatively uniform nuclei, arranged in alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas. In the endocervix, periglandular stromal hypercellularity reminiscent of a cambium layer was identified. Scattered aggregates of rhabdomyoblasts were also present. Marked nuclear pleomorphism or anaplasia was limited to approximately 2% of the tumor. The mitotic rate was 16 per 10 high-power fields (6.4/mm2). The tumor demonstrated extensive cervical wall infiltration with involvement of the right parametrial soft tissue and the cervical resection margin. Lymphovascular space invasion was identified. No phyllodes-like architecture or malignant epithelial component was identified. Immunohistochemically, the neoplastic cells were positive for myogenin and desmin and negative for h-caldesmon, supporting skeletal muscle differentiation (Fig. 2B–G).

Intraoperative microbiologic investigations, including bacterial, mycobacterial, fungal cultures, acid-fast bacilli staining, and polymerase chain reaction for Mycobacterium tuberculosis/non-tuberculous mycobacteria, were all negative.

Following postoperative recovery, the patient underwent vascular intervention approximately one week after gynecologic surgery. The procedure consisted of axillary–axillary and left common carotid artery bypass using a ringed polytetrafluoroethylene graft, followed by thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR). A postoperative endoleak subsequently developed and was successfully managed with coil embolization.

The patient subsequently received six cycles of vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide (VAC) chemotherapy every 21 days, beginning one month after surgery and completed over six months. The regimen consisted of vincristine 1.5 mg/m2 (maximum dose, 2 mg) administered on days 1, 8, and 15; actinomycin D 0.045 mg/kg (maximum dose, 2.5 mg) on day 1; and cyclophosphamide 1000 mg/m2 with mesna uroprotection on day 1. Treatment was well tolerated without major treatment-related complications.

Follow-up imaging demonstrated a slight reduction in the size of the right inguinal lesion. One month after completion of chemotherapy, the patient underwent delayed excision of the right inguinal lesion, which had initially been suspected to represent metastatic lymphadenopathy. Histopathologic examination unexpectedly revealed a schwannoma without evidence of metastatic disease (Fig. 3).

Despite multimodal treatment, the disease progressed. Approximately two months after completion of chemotherapy, the patient developed small bowel obstruction requiring emergency exploratory laparotomy, which revealed recurrent pelvic tumor associated with intra-abdominal bleeding. Reoperation for adhesiolysis and hemostasis was subsequently required. One month later, she developed recurrent vaginal bleeding caused by a necrotic vaginal mass measuring 5 × 3 × 4 cm, accompanied by acute kidney injury and bilateral hydronephrosis requiring right percutaneous nephrostomy. Her clinical course was further complicated by sepsis and progressive disease.

Given her deteriorating condition, the patient was transitioned to palliative management. One month later, palliative radiotherapy (400 cGy in five fractions) was administered to control vaginal bleeding. However, the disease continued to progress, and the patient died 13 months after the initial diagnosis.

Discussion and review of literature

Our patient demonstrated an aggressive clinical course despite surgery and chemotherapy, with radiotherapy subsequently administered for palliative control of recurrent vaginal bleeding. Although adult cervical ERMS is generally associated with favorable outcomes in previously reported cases, our patient experienced rapid pelvic recurrence and progressive disease, ultimately resulting in death 13 months after diagnosis.

To better characterize the clinicopathologic features, treatment patterns, and outcomes of this rare malignancy in adults, we conducted a literature review of English-language reports of cervical embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma published between January 2015 and April 2026. The literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar with the search terms “embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma”, “cervix,” and “cervical.” We focused on contemporary reports published since 2015 to reflect current diagnostic criteria, immunohistochemical evaluation, molecular characterization, and modern treatment approaches, while minimizing heterogeneity related to historical changes in diagnostic classification and management strategies. Only patients aged ≥18 years with cervical embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma and sufficient clinicopathologic information were included. Cases with non-embryonal histology, duplicate publications, or insufficient clinical data were excluded.

Using these criteria, a total of 41 previously reported adult cases were identified and included in the review. Detailed clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes of the individual cases are presented in Table 1, whereas the overall clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The median age at diagnosis was 38 years (range, 18–61 years), and 41.5% of patients were older than 40 years. The median tumor size was 5.0 cm (range, 1.5–17.0 cm). Among cases with available staging information, most patients presented with stage I disease and commonly manifested as a cervical polyp or exophytic cervical mass. Histologically, the botryoid variant was identified in 17 patients (41.5%). In contrast, a large series of cervical ERMS, including patients of all ages, has reported botryoid morphology as the predominant histologic subtype (approximately three-fourths of cases) (Devins et al., 2022). Surgery represented the cornerstone of treatment, most commonly hysterectomy or radical hysterectomy, and was frequently combined with chemotherapy. VAC-based regimens (vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide) were the most commonly administered systemic treatment. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was used in selected patients, whereas radiotherapy was generally reserved for advanced, recurrent, or high-risk disease. Notably, fertility-sparing procedures, including conization, trachelectomy, and partial cervical resection, were successfully performed in a subset of younger patients, reflecting the generally favorable prognosis and the importance of individualized treatment planning in women desiring future fertility. Overall, outcomes were favorable, with 27 patients (65.9%) achieving no evidence of disease during follow-up, whereas four patients (9.8%) died of disease. The median disease-free survival among reported cases was 33.2 months (Table 1, Table 2).

Several uncommon clinical presentations and associated conditions were identified in the reviewed literature. Uterine inversion associated with cervical ERMS was reported in two patients and was generally associated with aggressive disease behavior (Ambreen et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021). Pregnancy-associated cervical ERMS was also described, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing this rare malignancy during pregnancy (Ayas et al., 2015; Taghavi et al., 2015). Other unusual findings included cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (Meng et al., 2021) and coexisting melanoma (Ricciardi et al., 2020), both of which were reported in isolated cases. These observations underscore the clinical heterogeneity of adult cervical ERMS and suggest that rare presentations or associated conditions may further complicate diagnosis and management.

Previous studies have identified several clinicopathologic features associated with prognosis in cervical ERMS. Devins et al. (2022) analyzed 94 cases of cervical ERMS and demonstrated that advanced age and deep cervical wall invasion (>50%) were associated with extrauterine recurrence and decreased disease-specific survival. In contrast, tumor size and histologic parameters, including cellularity, mitotic activity, and anaplasia, were not prognostically significant in tumors with less than 50% cervical wall invasion. Recent evidence also suggests that advanced-stage disease, bulky tumors, and incomplete response to treatment may contribute to more aggressive clinical behavior and inferior prognosis (Gao et al., 2025). In our literature review of adult ERMS cases, most patients presented with stage I disease and achieved no evidence of disease after multimodal treatment. However, a small subset of patients experienced rapid recurrence, distant metastasis, or disease-related death. Our patient shared several unfavorable clinical features, including older age, bulky tumor size, positive surgical margins, rapid pelvic recurrence, and poor response despite multidisciplinary management, which may explain the particularly aggressive disease course observed in this case.

Histologically, cervical ERMS typically exhibits a botryoid or polypoid architecture, often accompanied by a hypocellular myxoid stroma and a subepithelial condensation of tumor cells (cambium layer). Primitive hyperchromatic spindle to ovoid cells admixed with differentiating rhabdomyoblasts and occasional strap cells are common microscopic findings. Entrapped endocervical glands, focal hemorrhage, and fetal-type cartilage may also be observed in a subset of cases (Alfaraidi et al., 2025; Devins et al., 2022).

The differential diagnosis of cervical ERMS includes fibroepithelial stromal polyp, Müllerian adenosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, carcinosarcoma, and other mesenchymal tumors of the cervix (Alfaraidi et al., 2025; Devins et al., 2022). Among these entities, Müllerian adenosarcoma is an important consideration because both tumors may demonstrate a polypoid architecture, subepithelial stromal condensation, and a cambium layer-like appearance. The presence of sarcomatous overgrowth in adenosarcoma, with predomination of rhabdomyosarcomatous component, may pose a particular diagnostic challenge. However, adenosarcoma typically contains a characteristic component showing leafy, phyllodes-like stromal proliferation with low-grade atypia, which helps distinguish it from ERMS. (Devins et al., 2022). Immunohistochemistry plays a crucial role in confirming skeletal muscle differentiation and excluding histologic mimics. Tumor cells in ERMS typically show positivity for desmin and variable nuclear expression of myogenin and MyoD1, the latter two markers being highly specific for skeletal muscle differentiation. In contrast, smooth muscle markers such as h-caldesmon are usually negative in ERMS, assisting in the exclusion of smooth muscle neoplasms (Devins et al., 2022).

It should also be noted that rhabdomyosarcoma is one of the most common heterologous sarcomatous components encountered in carcinosarcoma. Therefore, adequate tissue sampling is essential to exclude an associated carcinomatous component. Correlation of the morphologic findings with the immunohistochemical profile is therefore critical for establishing the diagnosis, especially in unusual presentations such as cervical ERMS in adults.

Recent studies have suggested a possible association between cervical ERMS and DICER1-related tumor predisposition syndromes. Devins et al. (2022) reported DICER1-associated manifestations in a subset of patients with cervical ERMS, particularly among younger individuals. In addition, Alfaraidi et al. (2025) identified a pathogenic DICER1 variant in an adult patient with cervical ERMS. Nevertheless, the relationship between cervical ERMS and DICER1 appears to be heterogeneous, and molecular testing has been performed inconsistently across published studies. Consequently, the true prevalence and clinical significance of DICER1 alterations in adult cervical ERMS remain uncertain. These findings suggest underlying molecular heterogeneity and indicate that additional genetic or biologic pathways may contribute to tumor development and clinical behavior.

Compared with previously reported cases, our patient demonstrated a particularly aggressive clinical course characterized by rapid pelvic recurrence despite surgery and chemotherapy. The patient also had coexisting chronic type B aortic dissection and groin schwannoma. Although cervical ERMS has been associated with DICER1-related tumor predisposition syndromes (Apellaniz-Ruiz et al., 2021), our patient did not demonstrate typical syndromic manifestations, and the relationship between these coexisting conditions and cervical ERMS remains uncertain. In addition, molecular testing was not performed in the present case; therefore, an underlying predisposing syndrome cannot be completely excluded. Further molecular characterization may help improve our understanding of the biologic heterogeneity and aggressive behavior of adult cervical ERMS.

To our knowledge, this report represents one of the largest contemporary reviews focusing specifically on adult cervical ERMS and includes recently published cases with detailed clinicopathologic and outcome data. However, this study has several limitations. First, the literature review was based primarily on published case reports and small case series, which are inherently subject to publication bias and selective reporting. Second, the reviewed studies demonstrated variability in reporting clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment details, and follow-up outcomes, which may limit direct comparisons across cases. Third, molecular testing, including DICER1 analysis, was inconsistently performed and reported among published studies, limiting assessment of the true prevalence and prognostic significance of molecular alterations in adult cervical ERMS. Despite these limitations, the present study provides a comprehensive review of adult cervical ERMS and highlights important clinical, pathologic, and molecular considerations in this rare disease entity.

Conclusion

Adult cervical ERMS is a rare malignancy that generally demonstrates favorable outcomes when diagnosed at an early stage and managed with multimodal treatment. However, our case illustrates that a subset of patients may exhibit aggressive clinical behavior with rapid recurrence despite surgery and chemotherapy. Histopathologic evaluation with appropriate immunohistochemical assessment remains essential for accurate diagnosis and differentiation from other cervical mesenchymal neoplasms. In addition, increasing recognition of DICER1-associated alterations suggests potential molecular heterogeneity and may have implications for genetic counseling and future risk stratification. Further multicenter studies and additional molecular characterization may improve understanding of prognostic factors and help optimize individualized treatment strategies in adult cervical ERMS.
