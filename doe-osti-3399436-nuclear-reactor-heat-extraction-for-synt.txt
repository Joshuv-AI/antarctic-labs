 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Audience
 The report is aimed at the United States (U.S.) Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy
(DOE-NE) and its federal manager; the Integrated Energy Systems (IES) program and its national
technical director; representatives of strategic development in the synfuels and closely related industries;
and advanced nuclear reactor developers.

Key Research Question
 This report looks at the viability and best approach when producing synthetic fuels using heat and
power supplied by advanced nuclear energy systems. The report looks at a low temperature integration
pathway with four types of advanced reactors: a pressurized water reactor (PWR), an advanced light
water reactor (A-LWR), a sodium fast reactor (SFR), and a high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR).
A failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) of the coupling system between the nuclear plant and the
synthetic fuel systems is performed with the goal of identifying the reliability of such a thermal delivery
system. Furthermore, a high temperature pathway is investigated for synfuel coupling to determine if this
is more efficient and more cost effective as a coupling approach.

Key Findings
 This report begins by examining multiple synfuel pathways for nuclear power integration. The
two-step methanol (CH3OH) process followed by the methanol-to-diesel (MTD) process is selected due to
its reduced complexity compared to other synfuel pathways. This process route allows the production of
traditional hydrocarbon-based fuels with the focus here being primarily on the production of diesel.
 The two key feedstocks used in the synfuels production process are hydrogen (H2) and carbon dioxide
(CO2). High-temperature steam electrolysis (HTSE) allows the efficient production of the H2 feedstock
while the CO2 for this process is obtained from solid-sorbent direct air capture (DAC). Both processes
utilize electricity and heat (steam) that can be sourced from advanced reactor systems. Obtaining H2 using
HTSE was found to use 18.5 MWe of electricity and 3.8 MWth of heat to produce 0.5 t/hour of H2.
Likewise, DAC required 4.6 MWe and 6.6 MWth to produce 3.1 t/hour of CO2. With these supply rates
of feedstock, the synfuel system produces 11.9 MWth of fuel. The synthetic fuel process requires
0.96 MWe of electricity but, due to its exothermic reactions, produces 3.8 MWth of heat, which then can
be used to provide the HTSE steam requirements without requiring steam from the nuclear system.
 To supply heat to the DAC process, steam is extracted from the nuclear plant steam turbines. The
report provides comprehensive diagrams for each reactor power conversion cycle in consideration and the
piping and instrumentation diagrams for the optimized thermal delivery system to the DAC process. For
each design, the maximum synfuel production rate is calculated assuming all power and steam from the
reactor was provided to the DAC, HTSE, and synfuel system. The SFR was found to give the highest
synfuel efficiency with 19.3% of the reactors’ thermal power converted to chemical energy within the
product diesel. The HTGR obtained a similar efficiency, reaching 18.8% synfuel production efficiency.
The lower electrical efficiencies of the light water reactor (LWR) designs led to synfuel efficiencies of
16.4% for the pressurized water reactor (PWR) and 14.2% for the A-LWR.
 An FMEA was conducted on the thermal delivery system that supplied heat from the turbine to the
DAC plant. Generic failure modes for components were evaluated along with specific failure probabilities
taken from literature for each component within the thermal delivery system. Component failure modes
were considered arising from load conditions, applied temperatures, external environmental conditions,
and wear. Using the failure probabilities from literature, a risk priority number (RPN) was assigned to
each component’s most high-risk failure mode.

 v

 Based on a 30-year operational duration and reactor-specific system demands, it was found that shell-
and-tube (S&T) heat exchangers, plate-and-frame (P&F) heat exchangers, the steam drum, and turbine
components yielded high RPN values while flow, level, and temperature sensors generally had low RPN
values. Aside from the RPN results, a few pump and turbine component failure probabilities were
calculated to be higher than 0.5, which significantly impacts the heat extraction design system reliability.
Using FMEA diagrams, heat extraction designs were identified as N+0 redundant series-reliability
systems and will require future modifications to meet synthetic fuel plant reliability standards of over
99% reliability with no downtime. It was recommended to increase redundancy for highly RPN-ranked
components and failure modes containing failure probabilities over 0.002.
 This report also describes the integration of high-temperature nuclear heat with synthetic fuel
production using natural gas reforming. Different coupling options are considered for heat integration,
and potential heat delivery fluid loops are evaluated. The requirements of the heat exchange system are
provided to document the challenges with high temperature delivery systems. For the heat delivery loop,
molten salts are found to outperform gases, but molten salt’s high freezing point requires a high return
temperature that affects system efficiency. A significantly higher synfuel production efficiency of 55% is
achieved using the high-temperature pathway compared to 19% for the low-temperature pathway. The
higher efficiency of the high-temperature pathway translates into a much lower levelized cost of fuel
(LCOF) of $66/MWh versus $675/MWh for the low-temperature pathway. These findings underscore the
substantial economic and efficiency benefits of high-temperature integration, despite the technical
challenges associated with heat delivery and integration.

Why This Matters
 The detailed consideration of the delivery of nuclear reactor heat to a synfuels system is important for
the following reasons:
 Reducing Supply Chain Risk. The research comprehensively demonstrates an efficient pathway for
future synfuel production using nuclear heat sources. This pathway helps secure access to a critical fuel
resource without the need for access to complex feedstocks. This reduces the risks of future dependence
on foreign fossil fuel imports by improving circularity, which is paramount for long-term domestic energy
security.
 Increasing Efficiency and Profitability. Comparing multiple integration scenarios, we identified
nuclear reactor designs and coupling approaches with the highest synfuel production efficiency to support
optimization of future synfuel designs in terms of potential tradeoffs between operating and system costs.
 Improving Reliability. The exploration of redundancy options supports economic evaluations of
designs with acceptable technical risks. The FMEA provides needed information to achieve the system
reliability needed for industrial applications.

 Nuclear Reactor Heat Extraction for Synthetic Fuel
 Plants
1. INTRODUCTION
 Petroleum-based hydrocarbon fuels are the largest source of energy in the United States (U.S.)
producing 35.4 quadrillion British thermal units per year (Quads/yr) [1]. This energy is primarily used for
transportation, which is the largest energy use sector in the U.S., using 28 Quads/yr—with 24.8 Quads/yr
being provided via petroleum-based fuels. Industry is also a large consumer of these fuels, using
8.5 Quads/yr [1]. Petroleum-based fuels are attractive due to their high-energy densities and
combustibility. High-energy densities are desired in the transportation sector to reduce refueling time and
frequency. The combustibility of these fuels allows them to generate heat at high temperatures, making
them useful for high-temperature applications commonly required in industry. To secure American
energy supplies against price shocks in global petroleum markets, it is imperative to find alternative
routes for obtaining these fuels.
 Synfuels, also known as E-fuels, present a possible solution for augmenting supplies of petroleum-
based fuels. These synthetic fuels are nearly chemically identical to traditional fossil-based fuels,
allowing for direct drop-in replacements with little or no needed modifications, unlike alternatives such as
electrification. However, the production of synfuels is highly energy intensive. In this report, nuclear
power is considered as a source for both the heat and electricity needed for synfuel production.
 This report focuses on optimal strategies for using heat and electricity from different advanced
nuclear reactors for the methanol two-step synthetic fuel process. We identify key pathways for producing
synfuels using hydrogen (H2) from water electrolysis and carbon dioxide sourced directly from the air.
Required redundancies in the heat delivery system are explored using failure modes and effects analysis
(FMEA) to ensure system reliability.

1.1 Synthetic Fuel Pathways
 For the reference synfuel plant, multiple pathways were initially considered. The feedstocks for all
synfuels are typically hydrogen (H2) and carbon dioxide (CO2); however, some pathways may utilize
carbon monoxide (CO). CO can be obtained via the water-gas shift reaction from CO2 or directly from
biomass or coal gasification.
                                         𝐶𝑂 + 𝐻2 𝑂 ⇌ 𝐶𝑂2 + 𝐻2                                              (1)
 There are six common pathways for synfuel production. Overall, the pathways are exothermic and
can generate the required process heat. These pathways are:
1. Thermocatalytic synthesis of H2 and CO2 into ethanol and higher alcohols and use of the biochemical
 alcohol-to-jet to convert these products into hydrocarbons [2].
2. Direct use of the Sabatier reaction to convert H2 and CO2 to methane [3].

 1

3. Direct methanol synthesis from H2 and CO2, also known as one-step methanol synthesis [4, 5].
 Methanol can be converted into dimethyl either via dehydration, or into liquid hydrocarbon fuels via
 the methanol-to-X (MT-X) process, typically methanol-to-diesel (MTD).
4. Methanol synthesis from H2 and CO, also known as the two-step process. The CO is generally
 obtained from CO2 through the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction [6]. Methanol can be
 converted into dimethyl either via dehydration or into liquid hydrocarbon fuels via the MT-X process.
5. Fischer-Tropsch process converting H2 and CO via Syncrude into hydrocarbons [4].
6. Use of the reaction between nitrogen (N2) and H2 via the Haber-Bosch process providing
 ammonia [7].
 Figure 1 illustrates these pathways and demonstrates the chosen pathway—using the MTD process—
highlighted in red. Methanol (CH3OH) synthesis has been an area of active research within the Integrated
Energy Systems (IES) program at the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) due to its reduced complexity
compared with the Fischer-Tropsch pathway that is more dominant in industry [8]. For this reason, a two-
step methanol synthesis process, followed by the MTD process is used. The reduced complexity may lead
to lower capital costs to improve the overall economics of synthetic fuel production.

1.2 Feedstocks
 The feedstocks CO2 and H2 can be obtained from many sources, as described below. For this work,
hydrogen is sourced from water and CO2 is sourced from the atmosphere, thus the feedstocks are widely
available and not reliant on natural gas. This choice allows operation of these facilities in generalized and
even remote locations. Sites that have access to concentrated CO2, such as from biorefineries, or low-cost
natural gas as a source of hydrogen, open additional opportunities that can be considered on a case-by-
case basis.

 2

1.2.1 Hydrogen
 Hydrogen gas can be produced using multiple methods. Most H2 is currently produced using steam
methane reforming, which converts natural methane gas into H2, CO, and CO2 [9]. Steam methane
reforming, along with other fossil fuel-based technologies, accounts for 95% of global H2 production.
Water electrolysis makes up the remainer of global H2 production at around 87 million tons/yr [10]. This
electrochemical process splits water molecules into H2 and oxygen. This report focuses on H2 production
via electrolysis to reduce reliance on natural gas sources.
 There are many electrolysis processes that operate at different conditions and use different
membrane technologies. For nuclear-integrated H2 production, the two main processes of interest are
low-temperature electrolysis using proton exchange membrane (PEM) technology and high-temperature
steam electrolysis (HTSE) based on solid oxide electrolysis cell (SOEC) technology. Of these two
processes, PEM is more mature and requires electricity and water as feedstocks. HTSE is less mature and
requires steam and electricity; however, it is more efficient than PEM electrolysis [11].
 Because of its higher efficiency and key utilization of mid- to low-temperature heat in the form of
steam, HTSE is of great interest for nuclear H2 production. With suitable heat recuperation, HTSE can use
steam at approximately 150°C, making integration possible with conventional reactors, such as light
water reactors (LWRs) [12, 13].

1.2.2 Carbon Dioxide
 Obtaining a CO2 source for the synfuel production processes is a key consideration. Two sources of
CO2 are considered in this work. The first, biomass, provides the ability to obtain CO2 from replenishable
crops. The second, direct air capture (DAC), uses a chemical process to capture CO2 directly from the air.

1.2.2.1     Biomass
 Biomass can be converted into syngas feedstock through a variety of processes. The most-developed
process is biomass gasification, which is a thermochemical process that converts biomass into syngas
(i.e., a mix of H2, CO, CO2, and CH4) at high temperatures. This can be done with a variety of gasification
agents, such as air oxygen or steam, in a sub-stoichiometric environment [14]. The syngas-mixture ratios
and production rates are dependent on the type of biomass used, the gasification system, the gasification
agent, the reactor temperature, and other system parameters [15]. There is little prospect for integrating
biomass gasification with nuclear, since the temperatures (700 – 1,500°C) required for this process are
generally greater than what can be provided by near-term nuclear reactors, and the process is generally
exothermic, thus requiring no external heat input. For methanol synthesis, a H2:CO ratio of 2:1 is best, but
biomass gasification generally results in a ratio of ~0.7:1; because of this, additional H2 is needed for
methanol synthesis [14]. Other complications with biomass as a synfuel feedstock include transportation
and availability. Many biomass feedstocks are low density and come from dispersed areas, making the
transportation of biomass to the synfuel plant expensive and inefficient [16].

1.2.2.2 Direct Air Capture
 DAC is a rapidly developing technology that uses chemical processes to capture CO2 from the air.
There are a variety of different DAC methods being developed, but the two that are the most mature are
liquid-solvent DAC and solid-sorbent DAC. Both processes require heat and electricity to operate;
however, the temperature of this heat varies between the two systems. Liquid solvent DAC requires
high-temperature heat, around 900°C, which is typically supplied by natural gas combustion. Solid
sorbent DAC requires low-temperature heat, around 100°C. In this work, only solid sorbent DAC is
considered, since the heat requirements for liquid solvent DAC cannot be directly met by a nuclear power
plant (NPP) [17, 18].

 3

 Solid sorbent DAC captures CO2 using a sorbent material undergoing a cyclic adsorption-desorption
process. Solid-sorbent DAC is a modular process, so the sorbent is contained within an array of adsorber
vessels. During the adsorption phase, air is forced through the adsorber vessel, where it contacts the
sorbent. The CO2 in the air binds with the sorbent separating it from the air stream. At the end of the
adsorption phase, the desorption process begins. The desorption process, typically a thermal vacuum
swing process, uses heat to cause the CO2 to unbind from the sorbent. This low-temperature heat can be
provided by an NPP in the form of steam. Following desorption, the CO2 is dried, compressed, and then
permanently stored underground or utilized. In this case, the CO2 is used as a feedstock for the synfuel
plant. The primary benefit of DAC in this work is that it does not require arable land, and can therefore be
deployed in most locations [19]. As a result, in this work, the focus is on obtaining CO2 via DAC.

1.3 Process Requirements
 The synfuel plant design used in this work uses CO2 and H2 as feedstocks. CO2 is obtained from a
DAC plant, while H2 is generated using HTSE. The CO2 feedstock is converted to CO via an RWGS
reactor where it is mixed with H2 to make syngas. The MTD process then converts the syngas into liquid
hydrocarbons (i.e., C5–C18), namely diesel, with a small amount of naphtha, and jet fuel (kerosene). The
process requirements for each of these systems are shown in Table 1 and a process flow diagram is

based on previous U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) reports on nuclear hydrogen and DAC, and used as
the basis for the energy requirements throughout this work [12, 17, 18].

 There are four different advanced reactor types considered in this work. For each integration case, the

1.4 Thermodynamic Modeling
 To quantify the capability of a NPP to generate heat and electricity to produce synthetic fuels, the
CHP cycle efficiencies must be determined. To obtain these efficiencies, the respective power conversion
cycles (PCC) for the advanced reactors were modeled using Cycle-Tempo, which is a steady-state
thermodynamic cycle modeling software [20]. In addition, to verify the results from the Cycle-Tempo
models, these PCCs also were modeled in Aspen HYSYS [21]. The Aspen models can be found in
Appendix C and are available in the HYBRID repository [22]. Once the PCCs were defined, these cycles
were adapted for CHP by finding the optimal heat extraction locations based on the energy requirements
of the DAC process.

1.5 Extraction Methods
 Using the reference PCC, a CHP configuration capable of providing the necessary heat and electricity
needed for the synfuel plant was created for each case. In all four cases, heat is provided to the synfuel
plant by extracting steam from one of the turbine sections to generate secondary steam via a series of heat
exchangers. Steam is tapped from existing steam extraction lines used for feedwater heating. This steam
is used to boil feedwater from the DAC system through evaporator and preheater heat exchangers. The
DAC side feedwater is heated in the preheater before entering a steam drum. Liquid in the steam drum is
circulated through a natural circulation evaporator loop where it mixes with the feedwater in the steam
drum. Steam generated in this loop is separated in the drum and sent to the DAC system. The P&ID for
this integration is shown in Figure 3. The bills of materials (BOMs) for the P&ID are provided in
Appendix A.

1.6 Affected Equipment
 The design changes required for CHP operation can impact the nominal conditions of certain
components, primarily the turbines. The extraction of additional steam from the turbines can cause them
to operate in an off-design state that reduces efficiency. In cases where the plant is not operating at
steady-state, the extraction point will need to be controlled extraction, which could potentially require an
entirely different turbine design. The additional extraction could potentially alter the extraction rate of
lower pressure uncontrolled extraction points. This in turn would affect the feedwater train, resulting in a
different final feedwater temperature or different extraction flow rates at other control extraction points.
The impact on equipment will increase with increasing extraction flow rate. In this work, the extraction
flow rate varies for each case and ranges from 7% to 23%. A previous study analyzed extracting 105 MW
of steam from the turbine system of a generic Westinghouse 4-loop PWR for hydrogen production [23].

3. FAILURE MODE AND EFFECTS ANALYSIS
 Having designed and evaluated the potential configurations for integrating synfuel and nuclear
systems, the reliability of these configurations is of interest. This analysis focuses on the reliability of the
thermal energy coupling system. To qualitatively and quantitively assess the reliability of the heat
delivery design, an FMEA is required to identify the high-risk failure modes. FMEA is a systematic,
proactive approach for identifying, evaluating, and prioritizing potential failures in a system, process, or
product. Originating from the aerospace industry, FMEA has since been adopted across various sectors—
including automotive, manufacturing, healthcare, and software development—to improve reliability,
safety, and quality. FMEA is a bottom-up, inductive reasoning technique that focuses on identifying and
analyzing failure modes and their potential effects. It aims to: (a) anticipate and prevent failures before
they occur; (b) prioritize and address the most severe and likely failures; (c) reduce the risk of failures by
implementing appropriate design and process improvements; (d) enhance system or product reliability,
safety, and quality; and (e) facilitate communication and collaboration among stakeholders, including
designers, manufacturers, and users. The key FMEA concepts are:
• Failure Mode: A way in which a system or product can fail its intended function. For identification
 purposes, the way that a system breaks down into its various functions and processes is required. The
 failure modes for each function and process are considered.
• Effect: The consequence or impact of a failure mode on the system, process, or user.
• Cause: The root reason or factor that leads to a failure mode. This reason must be determined for each
 identified failure mode and organized.
• Risk Priority Number (RPN): A numerical value assigned to each failure mode, based on the severity
 (S), occurrence (O), and detection (D) ratings, used to prioritize actions. The function is the product
 of all three components: 𝑅𝑃𝑁 = 𝑆 × 𝑂 × 𝐷. Higher RPN values imply high-risk failure modes
 requiring mitigation plans. Scales to evaluate the severity, occurrence, and detection are provided in
 Appendix D.
 Sources of failure can be attributed to assembly, service conditions, and/or maintenance. Thermal and
mechanical assembly-related issues include, but are not limited to, ambiguity in instructions,
inappropriate procedures, accidental error on the part of the operator, and misalignments. The other
sources of failure are more relevant for heat extraction designs as the utilization and maintenance of
components are controllable. Service conditions are the operational environments selected by system
engineers to satisfy the objectives to produce and deliver mechanical and thermal power effectively. It is
essential that the selected equipment and components can meet the requirements reliably. Failure during
service can be defined as an event in which the component did not function as intended. The four
dominant factors affecting the likelihood of service failure are:
• Load conditions: type, rate of loading, and magnitude of the load
• Operating or surrounding temperature: normal ambient temperatures, low temperatures -60°C ≤ 𝑇 ≤
 -20°C, and high temperatures 200°C ≤ 𝑇 ≤ 600°C
• Environment conditions: dry, moist, exposure to sea water or other corrosive material
• Wear: adhesive, abrasive, erosion, and cavitation.
 These factors are failure mechanisms that must be considered when choosing the equipment, design
materials and manufacturing methods. Maintenance strategies are another source of failure and can
accelerate the degradation of components, shortening the total service life. A well-developed and clear
maintenance plan with steps for maintenance—including instructions on what, when, where, who, and
how—can prevent failure and help detect potential failures. A lack of information on proper scheduled
maintenance, procedure, and acceptance criteria can frequently cause premature failure.

 15

3.1 Generic Failures of Components
 The following is the current interpretation of component failure relevant to the operational conditions
dictated by heat extraction designs:
 Valve. Control valves regulate fluid flow by adjusting passage size based on control signals that
manage flow rate, pressure, temperature, and liquid level. In general, pneumatic and electrical actuators
convert control signals into mechanical motion for valve operations. Common valve pre-failure issues
include sticking, leakage, cavitation, erosion, clogging, and actuator failure. Pneumatic actuators are
susceptible to air supply loss, air leakage, sticking, slow response, and over-pressurization, while electric
actuators can face power interruptions, mechanical failures, sensor issues, and hardware or software
malfunctions. Regular inspections, maintenance, and lubrication are essential preventive measures for
both actuator types.
 Pumps. Dynamic pumps, such as centrifugal pumps, impart velocity to fluid, converting kinetic
energy into a pressure head to facilitate flow. In contrast, displacement pumps move fluid by
mechanically trapping and displacing a fixed volume. This report focuses on centrifugal pumps. These are
ideal for precise flow control and handling viscous or abrasive fluids, using mechanisms like pistons or
gears to increase the pressure head. Centrifugal pumps use a rotating impeller and are ideal for
continuous, high-flow applications. Besides leakages, other potential failure modes for centrifugal pumps
include shaft deflection/bending and regular wear, impeller cracks or clogging, poor alignment or broken
bearings causing seizure, and failures in electrical components like the fan or capacitor. Additionally, the
motor armature and stator can malfunction.
 Steam Drum. The vulnerabilities of a steam drum, in terms of failure modes, are primarily caused by
material degradation. This includes slow-evolving degradation resulting from thermal stresses, material
oxidation, scaling on the internal walls leading to corrosion, and insulation losses on the exterior causing
uneven thermal stresses, to name a few. In contrast, ruptures at the welded joints, often resulting from
overpressure in the drum, are relatively fast-evolving and difficult-to-identify failure modes. Internal
embrittlement and fractures are also difficult to detect and require periodic material sampling and testing.
The shape and orientation of the steam drum also can mitigate or exacerbate the causes leading to the
listed failure modes. Finally, the control or tight regulation of water level inside the drum, all within safe
operating limits specified by the vendor, is crucial. In the event of a low water level, excessive heating of
the exposed metal can potentially result in drum deformation and material cracking/rupture.
 Heat Exchanger. This report focuses on shell-and-tube (S&T) and plate-and-frame (P&F) heat
exchangers. Both are susceptible to failure due to fouling, causing blockages in most instances. The issue
particularly arises due to the use of hard water without softening. The gasket malfunctioning is also a
common cause of failure. In addition, metal corrosion, vibration, steam hammer, uneven thermal stresses,
a tube’s or plate’s shape deformation, and damage at the joints or bends cause leakage-based and
blockage-based failure, and these may manifest as internal/external or slow/fast leakages, and reduced or
blocked flow, respectively.
 Sensors. Proper functioning of the sensors is necessary to ensure observability of the state variables in
the thermal delivery system. The most common failure mode in sensors, regardless of the physical state
variable being measured/observed, is gradual inaccuracy or bias build up in the measurements. Some of
the causes can be addressed via periodic recalibration, while factors such as aging of the components
within the sensor require hardware-based updates. Other factors contributing toward partial or complete
sensor failure include operation in harsh environments, vibrations typically resulting in loss or loosening
of cable connections, and movement of the sensor causing inaccurate placement/orientation. The
detection of a discrepancy or an anomaly in the sensor reading necessitates inspection or a subsequent
diagnostic. These anomalies are often observed by enabling sensor redundancy, wherein another sensor is
co-located for comparison of the measurements.

 16

 Turbine. The turbine is likely the most expensive part of the system. The common cause of failure is
often the rotating blades due to material fatigue at either the root, edges, or mid-section. Multiple factors
can contribute, such as vibrations, steam flow, steam quality, and even resonant frequencies that are
typically hard to abate. In addition, rotor shaft imbalance, rotor fatigue and cracking, the misalignment of
a ball bearing or insufficient lubrication, distortion in the turbine casing due to temperature fluctuations,
leakage through damaged/worn-out seals (on the shaft or on the control valve stem), dysfunctional control
valves, shaft deformations, etc., can contribute to turbine failure.

3.2 Component Failure Mode Ranking
 The identified components are evaluated based on the identified failure modes, as provided by the
Industry-Average Performance for Components and Initiating Events at U.S. Commercial Nuclear Power
Plants: 2020 Update [36] and the reference operational duration as noted in Table A-1 and Table A-2.
The principal components considered within this FMEA are pumps, heat exchangers, valves, sensors, and
steam drums. A more detailed component list can be found in Table A-2 and Table A-2. From the failure
distributions published by the “Summary of Standardized Plant Analysis Risk Component Unreliability
Data and Results” in [36], the average-failure rates for duration-derived failures and average-failure
probabilities for demand-derived failures are used to calculate the unreliability of the heat extraction
design. Since the gamma distribution is used for the average-failure rates, the calculated unreliability
based on operational duration is estimated using a Weibull distribution. Similarly, the average-failure
probability is provided using the beta distribution. Demand-based unreliability is estimated using the
binomial distribution. The estimations assume that the failure rates and failure probabilities are constant.
This assumption is mostly valid as failure behavior tends to be constant under the useful stages of
operation as dictated by the bath-tub curve. Although a complete FMEA will require distinct failure mode
cause and effect specific to a component type, the failure data provided from the industry-average
parameter estimates are general failure modes compiling all causes into a single- failure distribution. This
adds limitations to the current heat extraction FMEA as the provided data is not given for each cause. For
a full analysis of the reliability of a system, as noted in Section 3.1, a more complete FMEA is required;
however, a simplified FMEA is applied here. The following are recognized failure modes as identified by
the Industry-Average Performance for Components and Initiating Events at U.S. Commercial Nuclear
Power Plants: 2020 Update[36]:
• Valves: fail to open, fail to close, fail to control, spurious operation, internal leakage, and external
 leakage
• Pumps: fail to start during normal standby, fail to run within 1 hour during normal standby, fail to run
 over 1 hour during normal standby, external leakage, fail to start during normal operation, and fail to
 run during normal operation
• Heat Exchanger: plugging and loss of heat transfer, internal leakage, and external leakage
• Sensors: fail to operate on demand and fail to operate per hour.

 The severity, occurrence, and detectability scale rates for both S&T and P&F heat exchangers are
either moderate or high. To supply steam to the DAC system, the heat exchanger must be able to transfer
heat at the desired temperature and rate with acceptable fluid leakage. Several heat exchanger failure
types are considered. For example, heat exchanger tubes can accumulate buildup over time due to
upstream corrosion, debris, or scale formation, which may necessitate plugging. When accumulation is
significant, tubes are clogged and impede fluid flow preventing optimal heat transfer efficiencies.
Clogged tubes can cause uneven-temperature distributions, temporal-temperature fluctuations, and
damage to heat-exchanger internal components. To prevent unintended damage and maximize efficiency,
the clogged tubes are intentionally plugged to increase flow in other tubes. Another common failure is
internal leakage. Internal leakage is caused by corrosion, erosion (specifically in P&F heat exchangers),
vibrations, and irregular temperature distributions generating small to large ruptures in the tubing. The
tube internal pressure is higher than the external pressure, intensifying the fluid leakage. For the two types
of heat exchangers installed in the heat extraction design, internal leakage implies different effects. For
S&T heat exchangers, internal leakage has consequences of shell-side fluid contamination. Considering
the tube-side and shell-side are steam and water, shell-side fluid contamination will induce thermal
mixing and change shell-side output temperature and quality. For P&F heat exchangers, internal leakage
is unlikely due to requiring consecutive plate ruptures. Cross-contamination between the hot-side and
cold-side tubes is also unlikely as the hot-side, cold-side, and plate ruptures would have to exist at the
same time, and tube pressures will have to be equal or below ambient pressures. Perhaps the greatest
concern is the structure integrity of the P&F heat exchanger due to increased corrosion and erosion as it
ages. The mitigation in that case is replacement of the entire component. For external leakage, the
severity is rated higher than internal leakage due to unrecoverable loss of fluid. Loss of fluid is a loss of
heat, and if not detected, can persist until the point of cavitation impacting valves and pumps throughout
the operational lifetime.
 For letdown turbines, the main oil pump supplies high pressure oil lubricants to bearings to ensure
smooth operation and prevent wear and tear on the rotating shaft. An inadequate oil supply can lead to
premature failure of multiple turbine components, shortening the operational lifetime of the turbine.
Major causes of the main oil pump failure are corrosion, vibration, cavitation, and wear. The operational
environment calls for high pressure oil delivery and can corrode inner pipe walls causing generation of
fine solid particles. If the oil supply is not well-sealed, air can seep into oil lines and will impact pump
blades, causing cavitation. Excessive pump vibrations also can be observed when pump components are
unbalanced or misaligned. With continued operation under corrosive, cavitated, and vibrating conditions,
pump wear and tear can be accelerated significantly leading to total main oil pump failure.
 The effects of steam drum external leakage are loss of either steam or water depending on the location
of the leakage. If losses are significant, deviations to normal operations are unavoidable. External leakage
of the steam drum is a high-priority failure mode and merits mitigation plans before installation. Example
causes of external leakage include corrosion-fatigue attacks, fouling buildup on tube surfaces, and
mechanical stress from vibrations, and in high pressure regions can cause boiling roof explosions [37].

 19

 The next tier of high-risk failure modes includes pump external leakage and valve internal leakage.
Pump failure probabilities are relatively high. Pumps run continuously and often under pressurized
conditions and require various operational conditions, including liquid temperature limits and
backpressure to extend the operational lifetime. It is essential to ensure pump operating conditions are
within the manufacturing specifications. Regardless of the high-detectability of pump failures (i.e., ease to
detect from a reactor operator console), pump external leakage remains a concern due to low-
detectability. Typical detection methods include feedback from temperature, flow, and pressure sensors
deviating from normal conditions and visual inspections. Without scheduled regular maintenance, pump
external leakage discovery can be significantly delayed. Valve internal leakage high-failure risk is
attributed to detection difficulty. Each valve failure mode severity affects the heat extraction normal
operations in a different manner. For open and close failures, nonconforming flow initiation or stoppage
are high-impact conditions but are relatively easily detected due to large deviations from anticipated
operating conditions. For failure to control or spurious operations, unintended flow variation and unsteady
fluid supply are medium-impact conditions and can be detected with close attention to flow sensors.
Finally, valve internal and external leakage are low-impact conditions but moderately high-failure
probabilities with low-detectability. Especially for internal leakage, depending on the leakage magnitude,
detectability is uncertain and leads to higher risks.
 Reduced risks are exhibited in sensors. Sensors function to provide feedback to pumps, valves, and
reactor operators. The detection of sensor failures is high as regular calibration activities are planned at
sufficient frequencies to ensure sensor functionality. Although temperature sensor (e.g., thermocouple)
failures occur more frequently than level and flow sensor failures, risks are ranked lower due to decreased
severities in terms of heat extraction, delivery to the DAC system, and CO2 supply to synthetic fuel
plants. Invalid temperature sensor feedback to control valves may induce momentary flow changes that
have minimal consequences. For level and flow sensors, significant impacts to heat exchanger and steam
operations are anticipated. Feedback from level sensors control the steam drum water level as steam and
water are separated. Flow sensors provide feedback to control valves regulating the steam through both
the S&T and P&F heat exchangers. Failures from either sensor can affect heat delivery to the DAC
system and change the CO2-generation efficiencies. Continued failure will lead to moderate operational
disturbances.

3.3 Heat Extraction Reliability Analysis
 Heat extraction reliability can be measured by considering the failure probabilities provided in
Appendices E, F, and G. Reliability is the probability that a system, component, or device will perform
without failure for a specified period under specified operating conditions [41]. Reliability can be
determined by calculating the conditional probability based on discrete or continuous distributions. If the
failure probability is known, the reliability of a component also can be determined as the sum of the
reliability and failure probability are equal to 1. System reliability is overall reliability and considers all
component reliabilities existing within the boundaries of the selected system. For N+0 redundant systems,
the reliability is calculated in series and implies system failure if any one component fails. In this case,
system reliability is the product of all component reliability and can degrade reliability rapidly depending
on the component reliability and number of total number of components (e.g., regardless of all component
reliability to be 0.99, 25 components will result in 0.778 system reliability). For N+1 or higher redundant
systems, parallel reliability is made possible and can be calculated by taking the product of all redundant
system failure probabilities and subtracting the product value to 1. For system failure to occur in parallel
reliable systems, all redundant systems must fail leading to higher-system reliability. When full parallel
reliability cannot be achieved, partial active redundancy can be applied ensuring a fraction of full
operability or productibility. This is called n-out-of-m reliable systems where at least n components must
function to operate the system at partial capacity out of m components.
 For the heat extraction with and without steam letdown designs, a minimum set of each required
component is in place to ensure planned pressurized-steam supply to the DAC system. Figure 13 and
Figure 14 respectively show the reliability diagrams without and with the steam letdown designs using the
FMEA Assistant Tool developed by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) [42]. It
can be confirmed that all heat extraction designs are series-reliability systems operating at N+0
redundancy. From the FMEA sheets provided in Appendices E, F, and G, some components do exhibit
failure probabilities over 0.500 (e.g., failure to run over an hour pump reliability is 0.29 for an operation
of 30 years leading to an occurrence scale rate of 10 out of 10), which suggests the current designs are of
low reliability considering the expected operation of 30 years.
 Heat extraction reliability with and without steam letdown, acknowledging the design is in series
without redundancy, is 𝑅𝑠𝑦𝑠 = 0 due to the centrifugal pump that fails to run within 1 hour failure
probability is 1.00. Regardless of other high-reliabilities, a single low reliability component can massively
reduce system reliability dictated by the series-reliability calculation. Possible causes for a centrifugal
pump that fails to run within 1 hour are cavitation, vibration, corrosion, misalignment, wear and tear, and
clogging. The implication is unavoidable heat delivery delays for at least an hour as the heat exchangers
and steam drum will not receive a liquid feedwater supply and the primary condensers will not be
replenished with the same steam mass extracted from the turbines. Also, for cases with steam letdown
heat extraction designs, the letdown turbine failure probability for the shaft seal, bearing, main oil pump,
turbine control valve, turbine stop valve, and reducing gear were all determined to be 1.00 for the
operational lifetime of 30 years. Although a failure of each turbine component does not necessarily lead
to complete turbine failure, a single-failure event can accelerate other failure mechanisms, thus shortening
the operational lifetime of the turbine, and the turbine performance drop is inevitable. For common failure
causes, erosion, corrosion, and wear and tear are the leading contributors.

3.4 Future Work for Enhanced Reliability
 For higher-system reliability, reconsiderations of current heat extraction designs are recommended.
The following are identified corrective actions:
• Parallel Reliability: Introduce parallel or n-out-of-m reliable systems to current designs. Although
 multiple separate thermal extraction and delivery trains would increase system reliability, the upfront
 capital cost would multiply as well. There are also design-feasibility issues to consider when large
 amounts of components are in close proximity. To effectively implement redundancy, the FMEA
 results provided in Table 4, Table 5, and Table 6 can be used to identify the high-risk components.
 Components with the highest RPN values should be considered first for redundant installation to
 achieve the highest-reliability return per investment. Based on the RPN values, it is recommended to
 redundantly increase the number of heat exchangers, turbines (only for those with steam letdown heat
 extraction), and steam drums. It is also important to recognize that high-failure probability failure
 modes with an occurrence rating higher than 5 on the data sheets will impact the reliability
 calculations significantly. Along with the RPN ranking, the high-failure probability components
 should be candidates to increase redundancy.

 23

• Product Failure Data: As noted in Section 3.2, the FMEA conducted here is a simplified version due
 to restrictions from nuclear-sourced component failure data and generic power plant turbine failure
 data. To provide a more accurate FMEA for improved system redundancy, distinct failure modes
 need to be identified with the associated failure data. Close consultation with component suppliers is
 recommended to obtain accurate data at the required component size and specified operational
 conditions. Before commercialization, component suppliers follow industry standards investigating
 multiple scenarios leading to component failure. Statistical component failure from component
 suppliers will be instrumental to estimate the true failure probability. Using the upgraded failure data,
 a detailed FMEA will reveal changes in RPN ranking and provide an accurate list of components to
 add redundancy.
• Operational Lifetime Optimization: If parallel reliability implementation and component-supplier data
 acquisition does not increase system reliability to accepted industry standards, it is suggested to
 reconsider the operational lifetime of the heat extraction design. Currently, failure probabilities are
 calculated assuming an operational lifetime of 30 years. Based on reactor refueling cycle lengths, the
 number demands in Table 3 also are dictated by the operational lifetime. It is suggested to conduct an
 optimization study to determine the optimal operational lifetime providing acceptable system
 reliability. Since optimization accuracy will depend on the heat extraction design and component-
 supplier failure data, optimization studies should be initiated after the initial two corrective actions. If
 the parallel reliable heat extraction design is uncertain, a design optimization workflow with
 maximized reliability as an objection function can be developed instead.

4. A HIGH TEMPERATURE PROCESS DELIVERY PATHWAY
 As the U.S. nuclear industry moves toward advanced reactors, nuclear-integrated synfuel production
pathways that rely on high-temperature heat integration can be considered. So far, this report has focused
on a pathway that utilizes electricity and low-temperature heat from the NPP, which is henceforth referred
to as Pathway A. In this section, another synfuel production pathway is discussed that relies on high-
temperature integration. This pathway, referred to as Pathway B, is based on a report from the Next
Generation Nuclear Plant (NGNP) project [43].
 The NGNP report presents a nuclear-integrated synfuel production pathway that converts natural gas
into synthetic fuel. This pathway uses steam methane reforming to convert the natural gas into syngas,
which is then followed by the methanol two-step process as described above. Steam methane reforming
relies heavily on the endothermic reaction:

Because the reaction is so highly endothermic, significant heat is required to drive the reaction
equilibrium towards the products. This opens the potential for nuclear heat to be used in place of the
conventional combustion of methane feedstock, thereby improving the overall system natural gas
conversion efficiency.
 The steam reforming reaction can be undertaken in three ways: steam methane reforming only,
autothermal reforming only, or a two-step reforming with primary steam methane reforming followed by
secondary autothermal reforming. It is the latter approach that is utilized in the NGNP study [43] and is
also adopted here in Pathway B. The NGNP study found that the optimal temperatures of the two stages
were 739°C and 1,038°C for the steam methane reformer and autothermal reformer, respectively. It was
also proposed that the primary steam methane reformer be split into two stages. The first of these primary
reformers is set at a temperature of 655°C, and therefore can make use of nuclear heat delivered at 700°C.
The second of these primary reformers relies on purge gas to provide heat and the temperature is set at
739°C as before. After the syngas is formed, gasoline can be produced using the methanol two-step
process using nuclear produced electricity where required. Similarly, nuclear produced electricity can be

used as part of the air separation required to produce the O2 reactant for the autothermal reforming stage.

4.2 Extraction Methodology and Requirements of System
 While Pathway B is energetically promising, extracting and delivering heat at 700°C from a nuclear
system is a significant technical hurdle. Here we investigate the potential approaches for high-temperature
heat delivery from a HTGR and look to specify the requirements of the heat exchanger and heat
exchanger loop to deliver the heat to the reactants for the first primary reformer.
 In this work we assume that delivering high-temperature heat requires a thermal transfer loop. This
isolates the primary helium from the heated reactants for the steam reforming. This loop could utilize a
variety of different working fluids. Several options are discussed in greater detail below, including their
respective advantages and disadvantages.

4.2.1 Coupling Options to the Primary
 There are several options to couple the HTGR to the heat transfer loop. The optimal configuration
will depend on several trade-offs in terms of economics, reliability and overall system efficiency. These
options are examined qualitatively here, and in-depth work will be required to examine the trade-offs
quantitively for a specific coupling.

4.2.1.1       Parallel
 Figure 16 shows a parallel coupling configuration. This coupling assumes a conventional PCC for the
HTGR but adds an additional parallel flow within the primary system. This option results in a decrease in
helium mass flow rate to the steam generator.

 26

 Figure 16. Parallel coupling configuration where helium is split before the steam generator and used
 to transfer heat to a separate heat transfer loop.
 The primary advantage of parallel coupling is that there is flexibility in terms of how much heat and
electricity are delivered by each individual unit. Adding multiple units gives strong redundancy as
shortfalls in both power and electricity can be overcome from the flexibility. On top of this, such a
coupling could be retrofitted to existing designs and would not require redesign of the steam generator or
power conversion cycle. The drawback to such a design is that there is significant underutilization of
either the heat exchanger to the heat delivery system or the power conversion cycle. Both systems could
be specified to the exact balance of heat and electricity required but the option for flexibility (and hence
redundancy) would be lost.
 The other issue with such a coupling is that heat rejection may be required by either the heat user or
in the primary helium to ensure the cold leg of the heat delivery helium is sufficiently cool enough to not
overheat the core inlet. This would both effect the efficiency of the compressor but may also affect
nuclear considerations within the HTGR core. This topic is discussed in detail below with a calculation of
the impact on efficiency.

4.2.1.2     Series
 The series configuration in Figure 17 overcomes the concern with regards to high return temperature
in the parallel configuration. When the return temperature of the heat transfer loop is hot, then the exit of
the primary helium is used to run a Rankine cycle. This option may allow for better utilization of the
high-temperature heat and a higher overall CHP efficiency, but the Rankine cycle would have lower
electrical efficiency. Furthermore, this coupling would not enable flexibility in either the heat delivered or
the power produced without core load following or heat rejection which would reduce efficiency
significantly.

 27

 Figure 17. Series configuration where primary helium first delivers high-temperature heat to the user
 before generating power in a Rankine cycle.

4.2.1.3 Independent Units
 An alternative approach is to have separate units optimized for power delivery and thermal delivery.
This is demonstrated for N units of power production and M units of heat production in Figure 18. This
coupling option is only available where multiple units are required to meet the steam and electricity
demand. As with the series configuration, redundancy can only be provided by increasing the number of
units. However, in this configuration, to achieve redundancy for both heat and electricity supply, an
additional unit of each type (heat or power) would be required.
 On the other hand, this coupling option would likely be the simplest and most efficient. For the
power-only units, the standard power conversion cycle design could be utilized, reducing customization
costs. Likewise, the heat exchanger in the heat only system could be optimized, but again additional heat
rejection may be required to ensure core inlet temperature is sufficiently cool.

4.2.2.1 Gas Heat Transfer System
 Helium, air and carbon dioxide all perform relatively similar as heat transfer fluids with relatively
minor differences in heat capacity. It should be noted that the relatively high reactivity of air is
problematic in high temperature systems. In this analysis helium is considered as the gas heat transfer
fluid of interest.
 Helium’s primary advantage as a heat transfer fluid is its stability up to very high temperatures.
Helium’s stability has a significant impact on reducing corrosion within the components allowing simpler
design with more conventional metal alloys. However, helium (and other gases) suffer significantly in
comparison to molten salts due to their much lower volumetric heat capacity [44] and their much lower
thermal conductivity. There are multiple consequences of this reduced thermal performance compared to
molten salts. Namely larger and more complex heat exchangers, increased pumping and piping cost, and
larger pressure differentials leading to creep issues. The diagram in Figure 22 taken from Peterson et al.
[44] shows the impact of helium’s thermal performance compared to a fluoride salt for the design of a
high temperature heat exchanger.

 Figure 22. Total pumping power (dashed) and the total heat exchanger volume (solid) for a 600
MWth compact IHX as a function of LMTD for the various combinations of MS and He heat exchangers.
Shown are the impact on LMTD for similar sized power and volume heat exchanger of switching working

 In Figure 22 the trade-off between the heat exchanger volume (correlated with cost), achievable
LMTD, and pumping power is shown. The delivery of 700°C from a 750°C Helium HTGR poses a huge
challenge as it limits the allowable LMTD to 50°C with a nuclear grade heat exchanger. The design of a
feasible heat exchanger that can cost effectively achieve this LMTD is critical for such a heat delivery
design. Because of the high volumetric flow rates required for helium to transfer reasonable amounts of
heat, there exists another a trade-off in achieving a low LMTD. This trade-off is between the pumping
power and the additional heat exchanger surface area. This results in a pumping power a factor of 100
higher than an equivalent molten salt system [44]. This is a key limit in the heat transport cost and limits
the flexibility in siting of the reforming operations.
 Because of the differences in volumetric heat capacity, the helium is typically operated at high
pressure. The pressure differential between the steam reforming reactant flows and any helium system
would also likely pose a difficulty for any heat exchanger design. Handling this will significantly impact

 33

design choices for compact heat exchangers. The high pressures also cause safety issues due to the high
stored energy in the loop.

4.2.2.2 Molten Salt Heat Transfer System
 There are a variety of molten salts that can be used as heat transfer fluids up to the temperatures
required for the heat delivery in this system. Fluoride salts offer higher thermal conductivities but have
lower heat capacities and higher melting points. The melting point of fluoride salts is typically around
400°C, with chloride salts having the potential to achieve lower temperatures of around 200°C [45].
 The melting point could become a significant issue for the heat transfer loop required for HTGR heat
delivery, since it significantly limits the minimum return temperature of the loop. This limits the
achievable efficiency of the parallel heat delivery configuration if the core inlet temperature of the HTGR
is assumed to be fixed. Return temperatures of 400°C would require cooling of the primary helium via the
configurations shown in Figure 20.
 Despite the limitations of melting points in molten salts, their advantages over gases for heat transfer
functions are numerous. The molten salts have significantly higher volumetric heat capacities, higher
thermal conductivities, and can be operated at much lower pressures than the helium. These qualities
allow the heat exchangers in the loop to be much more compact for the same LMTD, as is clear from
Figure 22. Likewise, the reduced pumping power compared to helium allows for much greater heat
transfer distances to be a realistic option for molten salts. This greatly increases the likelihood the
required safety case siting distance may be realistically achievable.

4.2.3 Conclusions and Future Work
 The integration of high-temperature nuclear heat with synthetic fuel production pathways presents a
promising avenue for enhancing the energy efficiency of synfuel production. However, several challenges
must be addressed to realize this potential.
 1. Heat Delivery and Integration: Extracting and delivering high-temperature heat (700°C) from a
 HTGR to the steam reforming plant is a significant technical hurdle. The design of efficient and
 cost-effective heat exchangers that can achieve the required low LMTD is critical.
 2. Thermal Delivery Loop: The choice of working fluid for the heat transfer loop, whether inert
 gases like helium or molten salts, presents trade-offs. Helium, while stable at high temperatures,
 requires larger and more complex heat exchangers and higher pumping power. Molten salts,
 though more efficient in heat transfer, have high melting points that limit the minimum return
 temperature, affecting overall system efficiency.
 3. Coupling Configurations: Various configurations for coupling the HTGR to the heat transfer
 loop (parallel, series, and independent units) each have their own set of trade-offs in terms of
 efficiency, flexibility, and cost. The parallel configuration offers high electrical efficiency and
 redundancy, but requires careful management of heat rejection to maintain core inlet
 temperatures.
 4. Nuclear Safety: The integration of chemical processes with nuclear reactors necessitates
 stringent safety protocols to prevent contamination and ensure the safe operation of both the
 nuclear and chemical plants. This includes robust isolation systems and emergency response
 plans. Compared to the low-temperature pathway, this system has one less layer of isolation
 between the nuclear systems and the chemical plant and this will require a significant additional
 licensing burden.
 5. Costs: The development and deployment of advanced heat exchangers and thermal delivery
 systems requires significant capital investments. The choice of working fluid and coupling

 34

 configuration will impact the overall cost, with helium systems potentially requiring more
 extensive infrastructure due to their lower thermal performance. The economic feasibility of the
 high-temperature pathway depends on the balance between improved energy efficiency and the
 costs associated with the technical challenges and safety measures. Further research and
 optimization are needed to reduce costs and enhance the overall economic viability of the system.
 A preliminary cost comparison is completed in the following section as a way to initiate a
 discussion on this topic.
 In conclusion, the high-temperature pathway for synthetic fuel production using nuclear heat offers
significant potential benefits; however, addressing the technical challenges, ensuring safety, and
managing costs are critical to successful implementation. Continued research and development, along
with careful consideration of the trade-offs involved, will be essential to advance this promising
technology.

5.7 Cost Comparison Conclusions
 Two nuclear-integrated synfuel production scenarios have been presented – one with low-temperature
thermal integration (Pathway A) and one with high-temperature thermal integration (Pathway B). To
compare these two scenarios, the cost of each system was estimated. The resulting levelized cost of fuel is
$675/MWh for Pathway A and $66/MWh for Pathway B. This difference in cost is largely due to the
difference in efficiency between the two pathways (18.8% for Pathway A and 55% for Pathway B).

 46

 The results also show that the cost of the heat delivery system is not a major cost driver for the overall
system. The overall cost is dominated by the cost of the HTGR and synthetic fuel synthesis. While there
is still uncertainty surrounding the specifics of the high-temperature heat delivery system, and therefore
the cost, this analysis shows that even if the high-temperature heat delivery system costs several times
that of the low-temperature heat delivery system, the high-temperature option will likely result in a lower
levelized cost of fuel. These results motivate further investigation into the details of high-temperature heat
delivery from an NPP.

6. CONCLUSIONS
 This report describes options for using heat from NPPs to produce synthetic fuels. The methanol two-
step synthetic fuel production pathway is considered and HTSE is used alongside DAC to provide H2 and
CO2 feedstocks for the production process. Obtaining H2 using HTSE was found to use 18.5 MWe of
electricity and 3.8 MWth of heat to produce 0.5 t/hour of H2. Likewise, DAC required 4.6 MWe and 6.6
MWth to produce 3.1 t/hour of CO2. With these supply rates of feedstock, the synfuel system produces
11.9 MWth of fuel. The synthetic fuel process requires 0.96 MWe of electricity but, due to its exothermic
reactions, produces 3.8 MWth of heat, which then can be used to provide the HTSE steam requirements
without requiring steam from the nuclear system.
 Two P&IDs were designed for the heat delivery system, both with and without a steam letdown
turbine, based on steam extraction from advanced nuclear power cycles. For these power cycles,
optimized configurations were given to deliver steam to the DAC process at the highest efficiency. The
SFR was found to deliver the highest production efficiency of synfuels (synfuel efficiency) with 19.3% of
the thermal power of the reactor converted to chemical energy within the produced diesel. The HTGR
obtained a similar efficiency, reaching 18.8% synfuel efficiency. The lower electrical efficiencies of the
LWR designs led to synfuel efficiencies of 16.4% for the PWR and 14.2% for the A-LWR.
 This work also performed a FMEA for the thermal delivery system. Based on a 30-year operational
duration and reactor-specific system demands, it was found that both S&T and P&F heat exchangers,
steam drum, and turbine components yielded high RPN values, while flow, level, and temperature sensors
were generally low. Aside from the RPN results, a few pump and turbine component failure probabilities
were calculated to be higher than 0.5, which significantly impacts heat extraction design system
reliability. Using the FMEA diagrams, heat extraction designs were identified as N+0 redundant series-
reliability systems and will require future modifications to meet synthetic fuel plant reliability standards
of over 99% reliability with no downtime. It was recommended to increase the redundancy for highly
RPN-ranked components and failure modes containing failure probabilities of over 0.002.
 Finally, a high-temperature pathway is investigated which demonstrates a significantly higher synfuel
production efficiency of 55%, compared to 19% for the low-temperature pathway. This efficiency
translates into a much lower levelized cost of fuel (LCOF) for the high-temperature pathway, at
$66/MWh, versus $675/MWh for the low-temperature pathway. Despite the technical challenges
associated with heat delivery and integration, the high-temperature pathway offers substantial economic
and efficiency benefits. Its downside is that it does not significantly alleviate the demand for fossil fuels
within synfuel production due to its high demand for natural gas. To enable such a pathway will require
further research and development in this area including the design and economic analysis of a high-
temperature heat delivery system.
