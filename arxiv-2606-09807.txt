Pathological function spaces and an unsolved Analysis I problem Thanks: Research supported by NSERC Discovery Grant RGPIN-2026-04565
Thomas Ransford

Abstract
. The spaces in the title are holomorphic function spaces on the unit disk containing a function with the bizarre property that, although it can be approximated in norm by polynomials, it cannot be approximated by the partial sums of its own Taylor series, nor by any linear combinations thereof. The search for concrete examples of such spaces leads to an elementary problem about convergent series that is still unsolved.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
primary 30H45; secondary 40J05, 41A58

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a
holomorphic function space
on the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.
By this, we mean that
X
is a Banach space
of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
, containing the polynomials,
and with the property that convergence in the norm of
X
implies
local uniform convergence in
\mathbb{D}
.

For
f\in X
and
n\geq 0
, we write
s_{n}(f)(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}
,
where
\widehat{f}(k):=f^{(k)}(0)/k!
.
Note that
s_{n}(f)\in X
, as
X
contains the polynomials.
Of course
s_{n}(f)\to f
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
as
n\to\infty
.
What about convergence in the norm of
X
?

Various possibilities can arise:

(1)

For all
f\in X
, we have
\|s_{n}(f)-f\|_{X}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Many spaces exhibit this ‘good’ behaviour, for example the Hardy spaces
H^{p}
for
1<p<\infty
,
the Bergman spaces
A^{p}
for
1<p<\infty
, and the Dirichlet space
\mathcal{D}
.

(2)

It is not the case that
\|s_{n}(f)-f\|_{X}\to 0
for all
f\in X
, but
X

admits a convergent summability method
.
This means that there exists an infinite complex matrix
A=(a_{nk})
such that, for each
f\in X
and each
n\geq 0
,
the series
S_{n}^{A}(f):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{nk}s_{k}(f)
converges in the norm of
X
and

\|S_{n}^{A}(f)-f\|_{X}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
For example, this is the case if
X
is the disk algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
, the Hardy space
H^{1}
,
the Bergman space
A^{1}
, the space
VMOA
of analytic functions of vanishing mean oscillation,
the little Bloch space
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, and the local Dirichlet space
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.

(3)

Polynomials are not norm-dense in
X
. Evidently, in this case,
X
admits no convergent
summability method. Examples of spaces of this type include the Hardy space
H^{\infty}
,
the Bloch space
\mathcal{B}
, and the space
BMOA
of analytic functions of bounded mean oscillation.

For details on all these cases, we refer to the article
[
4
]
and the references cited therein.
However, the cases
1
—
3
listed above are not exhaustive. In principle, there is a fourth possibility:

(1)

Polynomials are norm-dense in
X
, yet
X
admits no convergent summability method.

The existence of function spaces
X
of the type
1
,
which we label ‘pathological spaces’, was established only
relatively recently. The first examples, presented in
[
7
]
,
were based on Banach spaces not having the bounded approximation
property. Later,
in
[
5
]
, it was shown that there even
exist pathological Hilbert function spaces. In fact, more is true:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2]
)
.

There exist a Hilbert holomorphic function space
H

and a function
f\in H
such that:

•

H
contains the polynomials as a dense subspace;

•

f
lies outside the closed linear span of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
.

Moreover, given any positive sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 0}

such that
\sum_{n}1/\omega_{n}<\infty
,
the space
H
can be chosen so that
\|z^{n}\|_{H}\leq 1+\omega_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Remarks
.

(i) The bound
\|z^{n}\|_{H}\leq 1+\omega_{n}
is useful in certain applications (see e.g.
[
5
, Corollaries 1.3 and 1.4]
).

(ii) As presented in
[
5
]
, the space
H
is an artificial
construct, not a naturally occurring function space. In seeking concrete Hilbert function spaces satisfying Theorem
1.1
, a natural place to look is
among the de Branges–Rovnyak spaces. It is known that
some of these spaces contain functions whose Taylor series have
‘bad’ summability behaviour
[
6
]
.

In the light of these remarks, it is reasonable to ask whether
Theorem
1.1
holds for some de Branges–Rovnyak space
\mathcal{H}(b)
, and if so,
whether
\mathcal{H}(b)
can be chosen so that, in addition,
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
.
Our main result shows that both questions have positive answers
provided that a certain elementary criterion is satisfied.

In what follows, given a complex sequence
\alpha=(\alpha_{j})_{j\geq 0}
, we write

\supp\alpha
for the
support
of
\alpha
, namely

\supp\alpha:=\{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\alpha_{j}\neq 0\}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

There exist a de Branges–Rovnyak space
\mathcal{H}(b)
and a
function
f\in\mathcal{H}(b)
such that:

•

\mathcal{H}(b)
contains the polynomials as a dense subspace;

•

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
;

•

f
lies outside the closed subspace spanned by

\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
.

(2)

There exist complex sequences
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
, not identically zero, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq k}\overline{\gamma}_{j-k}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}\gamma_{j-k}\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{j}\quad(j\in\supp\alpha).

(2)

We are thus led to consider the following problem.

Problem 1.3
.

Do there exist complex sequences
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})\setminus\{0\}
such that
(
1
) and (
2
) hold?

We call this an ‘Analysis I problem’ because of its elementary nature.
Indeed, a basic knowledge of infinite series suffices in order to understand the problem. However, solving the problem is a different matter! In fact, the solution remains elusive. One of the purposes of this paper is to advertise
the problem in the hope that it will eventually be solved.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
We develop the necessary background on general Hilbert function spaces
in §
2

and on de Branges–Rovnyak spaces in §
3
.
Theorem
1.2
is proved in §
4
.
The remainder of the article is devoted to a discussion of
Problem
1.3
. In §
5

we show that the possible solutions to this problem are subject
to certain quite restrictive constraints. Finally, in §
6
, we describe our best attempt at a solution to Problem
1.3
.
We show that the problem can be reformulated as a question about one sequence instead of three,
albeit with a more complicated condition on the sequence.

2.
Hilbert holomorphic function spaces

Let
H
be a Hilbert holomorphic function space on
\mathbb{D}
.
Recall that this means that
H
contains the polynomials, and that
convergence in norm implies local uniform convergence in
\mathbb{D}
.
It follows that, for each
k\geq 0
, the map
f\mapsto\widehat{f}(k):H\to\mathbb{C}
is a
continuous linear functional on
H
.
Therefore, for each
n\geq 0
, the map

s_{n}:f\mapsto s_{n}(f):H\to H
is a bounded linear projection.
We denote its operator norm by
\|s_{n}\|
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert holomorphic function space on
\mathbb{D}
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

there exists
f\in H
such that
f
lies outside the closed linear span of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
;

(2)

there exists
g\in H\setminus\{0\}
such that
\langle g,z^{n}\rangle_{H}=0
for all
n\in\supp\widehat{g}
.

If there exists
g
as in (ii), then
\supp\widehat{g}
must be infinite.
If, further, polynomials are dense in
H
, then
\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\widehat{g}
is also infinite.

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Suppose that (ii) holds.
Then
\langle g,s_{n}(g)\rangle_{H}=0
for all
n
, and hence

\langle g,h\rangle_{H}=0
for all
h
in the closed linear span of
\{s_{n}(g):n\geq 0\}
.
Since
g\neq 0
, we have
\langle g,g\rangle_{H}\neq 0
, and so
g
lies outside this closed linear span.
Thus (i) holds with
f=g
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Suppose that (i) holds. Set
S:=\supp\widehat{f}
and

M:=\{h\in H:\supp\widehat{h}\subset S\}
.
Then
M
is a closed subspace of
H
, hence a Hilbert space in its own right.
Obviously
f\in M
and also
s_{n}(f)\in M
for all
n\geq 0
. Since
s_{0}(f)=\widehat{f}(0)
and
s_{n}(f)-s_{n-1}(f)=\widehat{f}(n)z^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
,
it follows that the span of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
equals the span of
\{z^{n}:n\in S\}
.
As
f
is assumed to lie outside the closed linear span of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
,
it also lies outside the closed linear span of
\{z^{n}:n\in S\}
. Therefore
there exists
g\in M\setminus\{0\}
such that
g\perp z^{n}
for all
n\in S
.
Thus (ii) holds.

Suppose, if possible that (ii) holds with
\supp\widehat{g}
finite. Then

\langle g,g\rangle_{H}=\Bigl\langle\sum_{n\in\supp\widehat{g}}\widehat{g}(n)z^{n},g\Bigr\rangle_{H}=\sum_{n\in\supp\widehat{g}}\widehat{g}(n)\langle z^{n},g\rangle_{H}=0,

which is a contradiction. Therefore
\supp\widehat{g}
must be infinite.

Finally, assume that polynomials are dense in
H
, and suppose,
if possible, that (ii) holds with
\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\widehat{g}
finite.
Fix a positive integer
N
, chosen large enough so that

\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\widehat{g}\subset\{0,1,\dots,N\}
.
Let
\epsilon>0
. By density, there exists a polynomial
p
such that
\|g-p\|_{H}<\epsilon
.
Set
q:=p+s_{N}(g-p)
. Then
\widehat{q}(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\widehat{g}
,
so
\langle q,g\rangle_{H}=0
. It follows that

\displaystyle|\langle g,g\rangle_{H}|

\displaystyle=|\langle g-q,g\rangle_{H}|

\displaystyle=|\langle(g-p-s_{N}(g-p),g\rangle_{H}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|s_{N}\|)\|g-p\|_{H}\|g\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|s_{N}\|)\epsilon\|g\|_{H}.

As
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary,
it follows that
\langle g,g\rangle_{H}=0
, a contradiction.
We conclude that
\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\widehat{g}
must be infinite.
∎

Remark
.

It is not altogether obvious whether the situation described in Theorem
2.1
(ii) can ever arise.
However, it was shown in
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
that, for each set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that both
S
and
\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus S

are infinite, there exist a Hilbert holomorphic function space
H
containing the polynomials as a dense subspace and a
function
g\in H\setminus\{0\}
such that
\supp\widehat{g}\subset S
and
\langle g,z^{n}\rangle_{H}=0
for all
n\in S
.

3.
De Branges–Rovnyak spaces

The spaces now called de Branges–Rovnyak spaces
were introduced by de Branges and Rovnyak
in the appendix of
[
1
]

and further studied in
[
2
]
.
They were later popularized by the book of Sarason
[
10
]
.
It is now known that these spaces have a variety of connections
with other topics in complex analysis and operator theory,
and they have attracted the interest of many authors.
In this section we present a brief review of these spaces.
The interested reader may find much more information in the books
of Sarason
[
10
]
and Fricain–Mashreghi
[
3
]
.

Let
b\in H^{\infty}
with
\|b\|_{H^{\infty}}\leq 1
.
The map
(z,w)\mapsto(1-\overline{b(w)}b(z))/(1-\overline{w}z)
is
positive-definite on
\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}
, so it is the reproducing kernel
of a unique Hilbert space of functions on
\mathbb{D}
. This space is
called the
de Branges–Rovnyak space
associated to
b
,
and is denoted by
\mathcal{H}(b)
.

Each
\mathcal{H}(b)
-space is contractively contained
in the Hardy space
H^{2}
.
In particular, convergence in the norm of
\mathcal{H}(b)

implies local uniform convergence in
\mathbb{D}
.
This is one of the two qualifying conditions to be
a holomorphic function space on
\mathbb{D}
.
The other, namely that
\mathcal{H}(b)
contain the polynomials,
is only satisfied for certain
b
. This is the subject of the
following result. We write
\mathbb{T}
for the unit circle.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
10
, §§IV-2, IV-3, NIV-1, V-I]
)
.

Let
b\in H^{\infty}
with
\|b\|_{H^{\infty}}\leq 1
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{H}(b)
contains the polynomials;

(2)

b
is a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
;

(3)

\log(1-|b|^{2})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.

Moreover, if (i)–(iii) hold, then
the polynomials are dense in
\mathcal{H}(b)
.

Using the equivalence between (ii) and (iii) in Theorem
3.1
,
we see that every non-extreme
b
has a unique
Pythagorean mate
,
namely an outer function
a
on
\mathbb{D}
such that
a(0)>0

and
|b|^{2}+|a|^{2}=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
Conversely, if
b
has a Pythagorean mate
a
,
then it is non-extreme.

The next result characterizes those
\mathcal{H}(b)
for which
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
9
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
, let
a
be its Pythagorean mate
and let
\phi:=b/a
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
;

(2)

\phi\in H^{2}
;

(3)

1/(1-|b|^{2})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.

The next result is a formula for
\mathcal{H}(b)
-norms in terms of Taylor coefficients.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
, let
a
be its Pythagorean mate
and let
\phi:=b/a
. Assume that
\phi\in H^{2}
.
Then
f\in\mathcal{H}(b)
if and only if
f\in H^{2}
and

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\widehat{f}(j+k)\Bigr|^{2}<\infty,

in which case

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 0}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\widehat{f}(j+k)\Bigr|^{2}.

Using the polarization identity, we deduce a formula for inner products in
\mathcal{H}(b)
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
,
if
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(b)
, then

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\widehat{f}(k)\overline{\widehat{g}(k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{i\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(i)}\widehat{f}(i+k)\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\overline{\widehat{g}(j+k)}\Bigr).

We conclude by remarking that Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
may break down without the assumption that
\phi\in H^{2}
.
This phenomenon is discussed in detail in
[
8
]
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Proof that (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}(b)
and
f
exist as in (i).
By Theorem
3.1
,
the fact that
\mathcal{H}(b)
contains the polynomials implies that
b
is non-extreme,
so has a Pythagorean mate
a
.
By Theorem
3.2
, the fact that

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
implies that
\phi:=b/a\in H^{2}
.
Therefore Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
both hold.

Now we apply Theorem
2.1
:
since
f
lies outside the closed linear span
of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}
, there exists
g\in\mathcal{H}(b)\setminus\{0\}
such that

\langle g,z^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=0\quad(j\in\supp\widehat{g}).

(3)

As
g\in\mathcal{H}(b)
, Theorem
3.3
implies that

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\widehat{g}(j+k)\Bigr|^{2}<\infty.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, set

\alpha_{k}:=\widehat{g}(k),\qquad\beta_{k}:=\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\widehat{g}(j+k)\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{k}:=\widehat{\phi}(k).

Then
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
. From the definition of
\beta
, we have

\beta_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\gamma}_{j}\alpha_{j+k}\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

which is equivalent to (
1
). Also, by Corollary
3.4
, for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
we have

\langle g,z^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=\widehat{g}(j)+\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}\Bigl(\sum_{i\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(i)}\widehat{g}(i+k)\Bigr)\widehat{\phi}(j-k)=\alpha_{j}+\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}\gamma_{k-j}.

Combining this with (
3
), we deduce that

\alpha_{j}+\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}\beta_{k}\gamma_{j-k}=0\quad(j\in\supp\alpha),

which is equivalent to (
2
).
Finally, since
g\neq 0
, we have
\alpha\not\equiv 0
, and from (
2
) it follows that

\beta\not\equiv 0
and
\gamma\not\equiv 0
as well.

Proof that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

Suppose now that there exist
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
(
1
) and (
2
).
Define
\phi
by

\phi(z):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\gamma_{k}z^{k},

Since
\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
,
we have
\phi\in H^{2}
.
In particular
\log(1+|\phi|^{2})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,
so there exists a unique outer function
a
on
\mathbb{D}

such that
a(0)>0
and
|a|^{2}=1/(1+|\phi|^{2})
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
Set
b:=a\phi
. Then
b
lies in the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
and
a
is its Pythagorean mate,
so
b
is non-extreme.
By Theorem
3.1
, the space
\mathcal{H}(b)
contains the polynomials as a dense subspace.
Further, we have
b/a=\phi\in H^{2}
, so by Theorem
3.2
we have
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|z^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}<\infty
.

Now define

g(z):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha_{k}z^{k}.

Since
\alpha\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})\setminus\{0\}
, we have
g\in H^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
Also, using (
1
), we have

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(j)}\widehat{g}(j+k)\Bigr|^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{\gamma}_{j}\alpha_{j+k}\Bigr|^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 0}|\beta_{k}|^{2}<\infty,

so by Theorem
3.3
we deduce that
g\in\mathcal{H}(b)\setminus\{0\}
.
From Corollary
3.4
, we again have

\langle g,z^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=\alpha_{j}+\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}\gamma_{k-j}\quad(j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

whence, by (
2
),

\langle g,z^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=0\quad(j\in\supp\widehat{g}).

By Theorem
2.1
, it follows that there exists
f\in\mathcal{H}(b)
lying outside
the closed linear span of
\{s_{n}(f):n\geq 0\}

(and in fact the proof of Theorem
2.1
shows that we may take
f=g
).
This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Constraints on a possible solution to Problem
1.3

The following theorem suggests that finding a solution to
Problem
1.3
may be a delicate matter.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})\setminus\{0\}

satisfy (
1
) and (
2
).
Then:

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\alpha_{j}||\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|=\infty
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq k}|\alpha_{j}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}=\infty

and
\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}=\infty
.

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}|\alpha_{n}|=\sum_{n\geq 0}|\beta_{n}|=\sum_{n\geq 0}|\gamma_{n}|=\infty
.

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)|\alpha_{n}|^{2}=\infty
.

(5)

Both
\supp\alpha
and
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\alpha)
are infinite sets.

Proof.

(i) Multiplying (
1
) by
\overline{\beta}_{k}
, conjugating, and summing over
k
, we obtain

\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq k}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\beta_{k}\gamma_{j-k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}|\beta_{k}|^{2}>0.

On the other hand, multiplying (
2
) by
\overline{\alpha}_{j}
and summing over
j
, we get

\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\leq j}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\beta_{k}\gamma_{j-k}=-\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}<0.

Thus the two double series sum to different values.
This can only happen if

\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\alpha_{j}||\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|=\infty.

(4)

(ii)
Applying Cauchy–Schwarz to the outer sum in (
4
), we get

\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\alpha_{j}||\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

As the left-hand side is infinite and
(\alpha_{j})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
, it follows that

\sum_{j\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}=\infty.

Likewise, by Cauchy–Schwarz again,

\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq k}|\alpha_{j}||\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}|\beta_{k}|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq k}|\alpha_{j}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

As the left-hand side is infinite and
(\beta_{k})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
, it follows that

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq k}|\alpha_{j}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}=\infty.

(5)

(iii) Applying Cauchy–Schwarz directly to the double sum in (
4
), we get

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\alpha_{j}||\beta_{k}||\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}|\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}|\beta_{k}|^{2}|\gamma_{j-k}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{n\geq 0}|\gamma_{n}|\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}|\beta_{k}|^{2}\Bigr).

As the left-hand side is infinite and both
(\alpha_{j}),(\beta_{k})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
, it follows that

\sum_{n\geq 0}|\gamma_{n}|=\infty.

The proofs that
\sum_{n\geq 0}|\alpha_{n}|=\infty
and
\sum_{n\geq 0}|\beta_{n}|=\infty
are similar.

(iv) The relation (
5
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j+k}||\gamma_{j}|\Bigr)^{2}=\infty.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz to the inner sum, we get

\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j+k}||\gamma_{j}|\Bigr)^{2}\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j+k}|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j\geq 0}|\gamma_{j}|^{2}\Bigr).

As the left-hand side is infinite and
(\gamma_{j})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
,
it follows that

\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq 0}|\alpha_{j+k}|^{2}=\infty,

in other words, that

\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)|\alpha_{n}|^{2}=\infty.

(v) Since
\sum_{n\geq 0}|\alpha_{n}|=\infty
, it is obvious that
\supp\alpha
is infinite.
To prove that
\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\supp\alpha
is infinite,
we note that the proof of Theorem
1.2
shows that

\supp\alpha=\supp\widehat{g}
, where

g
is a function in a de Branges–Rovnyak space
\mathcal{H}(b)

in which polynomials are dense, and such that

\langle g,z^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(b)}=0
for all
j\in\supp\widehat{g}
.
The result therefore follows from the last part of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

6.
An attempt at a solution

In this section we describe our best attempt at a solution
to Problem

with this problem is that,

The basic strategy behind our attempt is to fix one of the sequences,

\gamma

In fact, rather than treating Problem

in the proof of our main result.
Here is the modified version with which we shall work.

(i)
\Rightarrow

Remark
.

The proof above shows that the statement of Proposition
6.6
remains true if the
combined conditions (
15
)–(
16
) are replaced either by the stronger condition

\lim_{J\to\infty}\|(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y_{J}\|_{\infty}=0,

or by the weaker condition

\lim_{J\to\infty}((I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y_{J})_{k}=0\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}).

Though the conditions (
15
)–(
16
) are more complicated than either of these,
they have the distinct advantage, as we shall now see, that they lead to an optimization problem that we can
solve explicitly.

Condition (ii) in Proposition
6.6
can be reformulated
more succinctly as follows:
for
J=0,1,\dots
,
there exist vectors
y_{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1}

and invertible diagonal matrices

\Delta_{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n(J)+1)\times(n(J)+1)}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\langle y_{J},e_{0}\rangle=1,\\
&\sup_{J\geq 0}\Bigl(\|y_{J}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Gamma_{J}y_{J}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y_{J}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)<\infty,\\
&\lim_{J\to\infty}(\Delta_{J})_{kk}=0\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}).\end{aligned}\right.

Let us suppose, for the moment, that
\Delta_{J}
has already been chosen.
How best to choose
y_{J}
?
The most efficient choice is to select
y_{J}
to be a solution,
if it exists, to the
minimization problem

\min\Bigl\{\|y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Gamma_{J}y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\|_{2}^{2}:y\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1},\,\langle y,e_{0}\rangle=1\Bigr\}.

The following proposition solves this problem.

Proposition 6.7
.

With the notation above, we have

\displaystyle\min\Bigl\{\|y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Gamma_{J}y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\|_{2}^{2}:y\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1},\,\langle y,e_{0}\rangle=1\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=1\Big/\Bigl((I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}-(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J})^{-1}\Bigr)_{00}.

The proof is based on the following simple lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
P
be a positive-definite
N\times N
matrix and let
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

\min\Bigl\{\langle x,Px\rangle:x\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\,\langle x,u\rangle=1\Bigr\}=1/\langle u,P^{-1}u\rangle.

The minimum is attained exatly when
x
is a unimodular
multiple of the vector
P^{-1}u/\langle u,P^{-1}u\rangle
.

Proof.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, if
x\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, then

|\langle x,u\rangle|^{2}=|\langle P^{1/2}x,P^{-1/2}u\rangle|^{2}\leq\|P^{1/2}x\|_{2}^{2}\|P^{-1/2}u\|_{2}^{2}=\langle x,Px\rangle\langle u,P^{-1}u\rangle.

Hence, if
\langle x,u\rangle=1
, then

\langle x,Px\rangle\geq 1/\langle u,Pu\rangle
.
Equality holds iff
P^{1/2}x
is a scalar multiple of
P^{-1/2}u

and
\langle x,u\rangle=1
, in other words, iff
x

is a unimodular multiple of
P^{-1}u/\langle u,P^{-1}u\rangle
.
∎

We shall also need a result from matrix theory
known as the Woodbury formula.
A proof and a discussion of its history can be found in
[
11
, §0]
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
N,M
be positive integers,
and let
A,B,X,Y
be complex matrices of dimensions

N\times N,M\times M,N\times M,M\times N
respectively.
Assume further that
A,B
are both invertible.
Then
(A-XB^{-1}Y)
is invertible iff
(B-YA^{-1}X)

is invertible, in which case we have

(A-XB^{-1}Y)^{-1}=A^{-1}+A^{-1}X(B-YA^{-1}X)^{-1}YA^{-1}.

Proof of Proposition
6.7
.

We begin by re-expressing the quantity to be minimized
in a form consistent with that in Lemma
6.8
.
We have

\displaystyle\|y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Gamma_{J}y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle y,y\rangle+\langle\Gamma_{J}y,\Gamma_{J}y\rangle+\langle\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y,\,\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,y\rangle+\langle y,\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}y\rangle+\langle y,(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})(\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J})^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle y,\,(G_{J}+G_{J}D^{-1}_{J}G_{J})y\bigr\rangle,

where
G_{J}:=(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})
and
D_{J}:=\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\min\Bigl\{\|y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Gamma_{J}y\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta_{J}^{-1}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})y\|_{2}^{2}:y\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1},\,\langle y,e_{0}\rangle=1\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\min\Bigl\{\langle y,(G_{J}+G_{J}D_{J}^{-1}G_{J})y\rangle:y\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1},\,\langle y,e_{0}\rangle=1\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=1\big/\bigl\langle e_{0},\,(G_{J}+G_{J}D_{J}^{-1}G_{J})^{-1}e_{0}\bigr\rangle,

where the last equality is from Lemma
6.8
,
with
P:=(G_{J}+G_{J}D_{J}^{-1}G_{J})
.
By Lemma
6.9
, applied with
A=X=Y:=G_{J}
and
B:=-D_{J}
, we have

(G_{J}+G_{J}D_{J}^{-1}G_{J})^{-1}=G_{J}^{-1}-(G_{J}+D_{J})^{-1},

whence

\displaystyle\bigl\langle e_{0},\,(G_{J}+G_{J}D_{J}^{-1}G_{J})^{-1}e_{0}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle e_{0},\,(G_{J}^{-1}-(G_{J}+D_{J})^{-1})e_{0}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=(G_{J}^{-1}-(G_{J}+D_{J})^{-1})_{00}.

The result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

Combining Propositions
6.5
,
6.6
and
6.7
, we see that
condition (i) in Theorem
6.4
holds if and only if there exist invertible diagonal
matrices
\Delta_{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n(J)+1)\times(n(J)+1)}
such that
\lim_{J\to\infty}(\Delta_{J})_{kk}=0

for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and

\sup_{J\geq 0}~1\Bigl/\Bigl((I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}-(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J})^{-1}\Bigr)_{00}<\infty.

This last condition is equivalent to
(
9
).

Thus we have almost proved the equivalence of (i) and (ii)
in Theorem
6.4
. The only difference is that,
in condition (ii), there is no requirement that the matrices
\Delta_{J}
be invertible.
However, this is not an obstacle, since non-invertible
\Delta_{J}
can be rendered
invertible by making small perturbations of their diagonal entries without changing
the validity of the condition (ii).
∎

Although Theorem
6.4
yields a necessary and sufficient
condition for our original problem to have a solution,
it suffers from the defect that the condition involves the
unknown matrices
\Delta_{J}
.
Our final theorem remedies this defect.
The price to be paid is that the result is now expressed in
terms of separate necessary and sufficient conditions.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
\gamma\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
and let
S
be an infinite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
Write
S=\{n(j):j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
, where
0\leq n(0)<n(1)<n(2)<\cdots
,
and let
(\Gamma_{J})_{J\geq 0}
be the matrices given in Definition
6.3
.
For each
J
, define an inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1}

by

\langle x,y\rangle_{J}:=y^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}x\quad(x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n(J)+1}).

Let
(m(J))
be a sequence of integers such that
0\leq m(J)\leq n(J)

for all
J
and
\lim_{J\to\infty}m(J)=\infty
,
and let
\theta_{J}
be the angle between

e_{0}
and
\spn\{e_{m(J)},\dots,e_{n(J)}\}
,
measured with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{J}
.

For there to exist
\alpha,\beta\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
with
\beta_{0}=1

satisfying (
6
), (
7
) and (
8
),
it is
necessary
that

\liminf_{J\to\infty}\langle e_{0},e_{0}\rangle_{J}>0,

(19)

and
it is
sufficient
that (
19
) hold, together with

\liminf_{J\to\infty}(\cos^{2}\theta_{J})>0.

(20)

To prove this theorem, we need a further lemma,
which is a case of the Woodbury
formula known as the Sherman–Morrison formula.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
A
be an invertible
N\times N
matrix, and let
u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Then
A+uv^{*}
is invertible iff
1+v^{*}A^{-1}u\neq 0
, and in that case we have

(A+uv^{*})^{-1}=A^{-1}-\frac{A^{-1}uv^{*}A^{-1}}{1+v^{*}A^{-1}u}.

(21)

Proof.

Apply Lemma
6.9
with
X:=u
and
Y:=v^{*}

and
B:=-1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.10
.

We have
\langle e_{0},e_{0}\rangle_{J}=(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}_{00}
, so clearly
(
9
) implies (
19
).
This proves the necessity of (
19
).

We now turn to sufficiency.
Suppose that (
19
) and (
20
) both hold.
For each
J\geq 0
,
there exists a
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{J}
-unit vector

u_{J}\in\spn\{e_{m(J)},\dots,e_{n(J)}\}

such that
\theta_{J}
is the angle between

e_{0}
and
u_{J}
, measured with respect to the inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{J}
.
Let
\Delta_{J}
be the
(n(J)+1)\times(n(J)+1)
diagonal matrix defined by

(\Delta_{J})_{kk}:=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq k<m(J),\\
(u_{J}^{*}u_{J})^{1/2},&m(J)\leq k\leq n(J).\\
\end{cases}

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
we have
\lim_{J\to\infty}(\Delta_{J})_{kk}=0
,
because
(\Delta_{J})_{kk}=0
when
m(J)>k

and
\lim_{J\to\infty}m(J)=\infty
.
We shall show that, with this choice
\Delta_{J}
,
the condition (
9
) is satisfied.

For each
J\geq 0
and each
x\in\spn\{e_{m(J)},\dots,e_{n(J)}\}
, we have

x^{*}(\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J}-u_{J}u_{J}^{*})x=(u_{J}^{*}u_{J})(x^{*}x)-|u_{J}^{*}x|^{2}\geq 0,

so
\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J}-u_{J}u_{J}^{*}
is positive semi-definite.
It follows that, for each
J\geq 0
,

\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle\leq\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+u_{J}u_{J}^{*}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle.

Also, by Lemma
6.11
,
applied with
A:=(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})
and
u=v:=u_{J}
,
we have

\displaystyle\Bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+u_{J}u_{J}^{*}\Bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}-\frac{(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}u_{J}u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}}{1+u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}u_{J}}

\displaystyle=(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}u_{J}u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}.

Combining these observations, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Bigl((I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}-(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J})^{-1}\Bigr)_{00}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+\Delta_{J}^{*}\Delta_{J}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,\bigl(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J}+u_{J}u_{J}^{*}\bigr)^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle e_{0},\,(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}u_{J}u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}e_{0},\,u_{J}^{*}(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}e_{0}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl|\bigl\langle u_{J},\,(I+\Gamma_{J}^{*}\Gamma_{J})^{-1}e_{0}\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl|\bigl\langle u_{J},e_{0}\bigr\rangle_{J}\bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle e_{0},e_{0}\rangle_{J}\cos^{2}\theta_{J}.

Since (
19
) and (
20
) both hold, (
9
) follows, as claimed.
