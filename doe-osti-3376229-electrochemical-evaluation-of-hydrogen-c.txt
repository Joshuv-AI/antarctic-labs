1 Technical Overview
1.1 Problem Statement
 Tritium breeding, confinement and extraction play central roles in development of a
tritium breeding blanket for the ARC fusion power plant, with implications for refueling,
radiological source terms, and worker safety. The successful design and implementation of tritium
management requires the ability to predict tritium transport rates and inventories in the liquid
breeding material from generation to release. FLiBe (i.e. molten Li2BeF4) is a candidate liquid
breeding material for ARC. Solubility and diffusivity of tritium in FLiBe are the two dominant
properties that govern the transport characteristics. The available experimental data for these two
key parameters are rare and unfortunately often come with large variation. As shown in Figure 1,
the data span of hydrogen isotopes’ solubility from studies can be as large as three orders of
magnitude. Besides the large span of the solubility values, the measured temperature dependencies
from different studies can go in completely opposite directions, which superimposes another layer
of deviation. The diffusivity exhibits a narrower variation, but still more than one order of
magnitude from different studies. The variation in transport properties puts a large uncertainty on
the design and operation of ARC.

Figure 1: Solubility and diffusivity of hydrogen isotopes in FLiBe and LiF-NaF-KF (FLiNaK)
salts as a function of temperature. Extracted from Humrickhouse and Fuerst’s Idaho National
Lab report. Note: markers represent measurements and lines represent models.1
 The variability in prior solubility and diffusivity data may be attributed to:

 1

 1. Different oxidation state of tritium (zero or +1, with -1 being unlikely).
 2. Different solvation state of tritium, mediated by contaminants in the salt.
 3. Mismatched equilibration and experimentation times.
 This motivates the need to develop a method to determine solubility and diffusivity of tritium
[1] in specific oxidation states, [2] with varying contaminants dissolved in the salt, and [3] as a
function of time. Electrochemical techniques would achieve these goals, since electrochemical
methods are oxidation-state specific (they rely on electron-exchange reactions, thus measurements
can only be made for specific oxidation states, i.e. zero, +1, -1 etc). Furthermore, electrochemical
measurements (whether dynamic or static), take seconds to tens of seconds to complete, thus
enabling the possibility of essentially continuous and repeated measurements. This then allows for
changing salt conditions (temperature, composition, partial pressures of species in the case)
followed by repeated measurements of species concentration and diffusivity in the salt. For these
reasons, electroanalytical methods are extremely valuable.
 Electroanalytical measurements of hydrogen in FLiBe are limited by (a) imprecise potential
measurements, leading to uncertainty in identification of electrochemical signals, and (b) a lack of
electrodes of controlled geometries suitable for quantitative electrochemical measurements,
including microelectrodes and rotating disk electrodes (RDEs). The work performed during this
project focused on developing components for rigorous electrochemical measurements of
hydrogen in molten FLiBe, which will improve our ability to precisely measure properties of
hydrogen dissolved in FLiBe. The work included the following thrusts:
 1. Development of a COMSOL multiphysics electroanalysis model to test the behavior of
 different microelectrode geometries when exposed to FLiBe.
 2. FLiBe-compatible microelectrodes capable of measuring steady-state current flows upon
 polarization.
 3. Development of FLiBe-compatible RDE components
 4. Development of thermodynamic reference electrode (TRE) chemistries suitable for
 FLiBe.

1.2 Work Scope
 The University of California Berkeley Principal Investigator has previously demonstrated
electrochemical methods for the evaluation of hydrogen species diffusivity and permeability in
fluorinated molten salts, including FLiBe. In their previous work2, a 1mm diameter platinum (Pt)
wire macro-electrode was used to obtain hydrogen species permeability, defined as D1/2C, due to
linear diffusion to the electrode surface. Therefore, the use of macroelectrodes requires
independent knowledge of diffusivity (D) or concentration (C) to determine the other from
electrochemical experiments. This work aimed to overcome this limitation by producing FLiBe-
compatible electrode designs that inherently enable access to distinct modes of mass transport
besides linear diffusion, specifically:
 1. Microelectrodes, where linear diffusion dominates at the short-time limit yielding a value
 for the aforementioned permeability D1/2C. In the long-time limit, the current approaches
 a steady or quasi-steady state proportional to a mass transfer coefficient whose functional
 form is proportional to DC and also depends on the shape of the electrode surface as shown
 in Table 1 below.

 2

Table 1: Form of steady state mass transfer coefficient for microelectrodes of common
geometries, modified from Bard et al. 3

 2. RDEs, where driven rotation of a shaft immersed in the melt establishes a steady-state
 convective-diffusion field at the electrode surface with a mass transfer coefficient
 proportional to D2/3C. Electrochemical measurements on an RDE can be taken while the
 shaft is held motionless and spinning to access the two modes of mass transfer.
 By employing measurement tools with built-in control over the modes of mass transport and
algebraically decoupling D and C within one experiment, variability from changing salt
conditions or residence time between separate measurements can be reduced or eliminated
entirely.
 A parallel approach was employed to address the limitations in electroanalytical
measurements of hydrogen in FLiBe. This effort involved:
 ● Development of novel microelectrode geometry designs guided by the creation and use of
 a COMSOL Multiphysics electroanalysis model. This model was used to model dynamic
 methods such as square wave voltammetry and chronoamperometry to different 2D
 axisymmetric microelectrode shapes. This module may also be extended to include other
 dynamic methods, post-process experimental data, or model 3D geometries.
 ● Fabrication of FLiBe-compatible microelectrodes with critical dimensions smaller than a
 target 50-micron axis, including:
 o Micron-sized Pt wires attached to metal leads and immersed in FLiBe with LiH
 additions.
 o Band microelectrodes produced using physical vapor deposition (PVD) of
 electrode metal on pyrolytic boron nitride wafers, connected via ring terminal, and
 encapsulated in silicon nitride.
 o Printed microelectrodes fabricated in Berkeley’s Marvell Nanofabrication Center
 with tunable electrochemical dimensions, allowing access to different diffusion
 modes, microelectrode arrays to increase signal-to-noise ratios, and the evaluation
 of additional electrochemical properties of FLiBe with LiH.
 ● Design and fabrication of an RDE constructed using FLiBe-compatible materials housed
 in a Modulated Speed Rotator assembly that can operate at the high temperatures of molten
 FLiBe experiments.
 ● Testing of Ni2+/Ni and Ag+/Ag reference electrodes to distinguish hydrogen redox
 reactions from possible interference from difficult-to-control oxide contamination in the
 FLiBe.

 The eventual development of a comprehensive electrochemical toolkit for hydrogen
evaluation in FLiBe will enable robust electroanalytical techniques sensitive to the low hydrogen
concentrations expected in ARC FLiBe.

2.1 Macroelectrode Measurements
 Experimental work on the INFUSE project was led by macroelectrode studies on purified
FLiBe to collect redox specific permeability data (D1/2C) and validate the approach with previous
H-FLiBe electrochemical data. Baseline measurements of the as-received/blank FLiBe are shown
in Figure 2. Cyclic voltammograms were collected by modulating the potential of a gold (Au) wire
(working electrode[WE]) against the potential measured at a tungsten (W) wire (quasi-reference
electrode [qRE]) in the molten FLiBe at 550॰C; the current passed through the cell during testing
was passed through a molybdenum (Mo) wire (counter electrode [CE]). A peak shape on the
current versus (vs) voltage plot indicates a redox reaction at the WE; the measured reaction
potentials were corrected against the Be reduction potential.

 4

Figure 2: Cyclic Voltammograms of Molten FLiBe showing potential contamination by moisture
and increased charging currents possibly due to buildup of reaction products on electrode surface.

 Though the FLiBe was received at <100 ppm oxide, the samples were seemingly
contaminated by moisture or oxide evidenced by the presence of electrochemical peaks at
approximately 1.0, 1.25, 2.5, and 3.0 V vs Be/Be2+. At 550॰C, reactions [4] and [5] occur at a
nearly identical potential, 2.47 and 2.43 V vs Be/Be2+ respectively; since reaction [4]
proton/elemental hydrogen redox was the target reaction, the oxide peak was identified as a
competing reaction dominating the electrochemical signals. The exact alignment of the measured
peaks assuming a Nernstian response, calculations of electrons transferred, and other experimental
data should be continued.
 One approach to manage the interference of oxide reactions in these measurements was to
electrochemically oxidize dissolved oxide to the form of oxygen gas to then bubble out of the
system. This approach was attempted for a short amount of time after the initial scans of the FLiBe
blank, but the low overpotential and short electrolysis times did not lead to any noticeable change
in the thermodynamic potentials of the moisture-related reactions in the FLiBe. In a subsequent
experiment using an identical setup to the previous study, square wave voltammograms were
recorded before and after holding an oxidizing potential (enough to bubble out O2 gas) for

 5

approximately 1 hour. The square wave voltammograms recorded before electrolysis,
chronoamperograms, and square wave voltammograms recorded after are shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3: Left: Square Wave Voltammograms measured before and after electrolysis. Right:
Chronoamperogram at Oxidizing potentials including a qualitative fit of Current-Time Response.
 The decay of current during electrolysis was approximately fit with an equation describing
linear diffusion to the electrode (t1/2), a linear decrease of dissolved gas concentration (t), and a
constant limiting current caused by natural convection within the melt. It should be noted that this
method is not rigorous, as the diffusive and convective terms assume a constant bulk concentration
of reactant; combining these expressions with one describing a linearly decreasing concentration
term is inherently paradoxical and meant as a starting point for data interpretation only. Before
and after electrolysis, the profile of the observed peaks in the ~2.4 V vs Be/Be2+ range changed,
with the relative difference in potential between the two increasing by 0.4 V; however, it is difficult
to compare these reactions due to drift of the quasi-reference electrode during the experiment. For
example, an operating circuit potential (OCP) hold for 30 seconds after each subsequent square-
wave voltammetry (SWV) scan was performed and is shown in Figure 4 below. Immediately after
a scan, the OCP of the cell would typically drift over 400 mV; approximately 10mV of drift was
retained between scans, which after correction aligned the centers of the peaks.

Figure 4: Left: Potential drift of system during testing. Right: Overlaid Square Wave Voltammograms after
correcting by the average potential drift.

 Besides work on Thermodynamic Reference Electrodes (described later in this report),
other methods to distinguish between oxide and hydrogen related reactions were explored by
changing the composition of the working electrode. Since Pt and Au are expected to have different
interfacial energies for hydrogen/oxygen adsorption, switching, and recombination, some cyclic
voltammograms were recorded in the same (moisture-contaminated) salt and are presented in
Figure 5.

Figure 5: Comparison of Cyclic Voltammograms on Au and Pt wires with currents normalized to
the peak on the reverse wave

 Since the radii of the two noble metal wires were different and the immersion depth of the
wires in the salt were not well controlled, the current densities of the two measurements were not
tabulated. By scaling the current to the same value at the reverse reaction observed at 1.68 V vs.
Be/Be2+, performance of the two metals in the moisture-contaminated salt can be compared, which
show two broad oxidizing peaks occur on Pt at a relatively lower current draw compared to the
Au. Upon the return, a sharper peak (indicating more electrons transferred compared to a broader
peak) was observed. Subsequent scans on Pt wire showed that the electrochemical window ranged
up to 2.55 V vs. Be/Be2+ and similar bubbling reactions proceeded after fPt2 similar to the behavior
after fAu2.
 Since this work is targeted to access different “modes” of electrochemical mass transport
enabled by novel electrode geometry, it was important to assess any modes of mass transfer present
to electrodes that would be modeled as a plane. The limiting currents at macroelectrodes were
examined using an unlabeled spare sample of FLiBe from other experiments; plots of the current
measured from different applied potential steps are shown in Figure 6. Fitting lines were modeled
using the linear combination of Cottrellian decay, steady-state current from a set hydrodynamic
boundary layer, and a charging current.

Figure 6: Chronoamperograms recorded at 1.8V and 2.0V vs qRE.

 Attempts to fit the data without target values for the parameterizations expectedly lead to
significantly varying results on identical experimental trials on the same setup. This analysis is
intended to demonstrate the importance of strict experimental control in molten salt
electrochemical work. Regardless, it is clear that the current-time plots did establish a steady state,
either due to large overpotentials accessing different reactions or varying natural convection
environments between trials. These observations became the key motivator for the development
of an RDE assembly, the progress of which is discussed in section 3.5.

2.2 Microelectrode Measurements
 The first attempt at the construction of a microelectrode for operation in FLiBe was
fabricated using a Shapal closed-end tube (AlN-BN, from supplier Ortech Ceramics) and a 38.1
µm radius Pt wire (3N5, from supplier ESPI Metals) sprayed with boron nitride aerosol (from
supplier ZYP Coatings). The end of the sprayed wire was then snipped off to expose the conducting
surface, forming a microdisk. Images of this electrode before and after testing in a blank FLiBe
sample are shown in Figure 7, as well as cyclic voltammograms measured of the FLiBe before and
after additions of LiH.

 8

Figure 7: Platinum Microelectrode Before and After Testing in Molten FLiBe at 600°C (Top) and
cyclic voltammograms measured using this electrode before (left) and after (right) testing. Note
the large increase in current at all data points after addition of LiH.

 Measurements of the blank are reported at 100 mV/s (red circles) and 500 mV/s (Black
Squares). At the low scan rate, a steady state current is established before reaching the end of the
electrochemical window (oxidation of the electrode material) and retraced on the return wave. As
the scan rate increases, a peak characteristic of diffusion-limited mass transport is established
before possibly giving way to a similar steady state on the return wave. After addition of 0.1 mol%
LiH to the melt, one voltammogram was measured at 250 mV/s showing an enhanced current with
onset pushed to the left, suggesting some amount of reactant (likely H2) was at a higher activity.
Unfortunately, after passing too far beyond the Be/Be2+ reduction potential, it is believed that a
large amount of Be metal was plated onto the Pt electrode, making further testing impossible.
Discoloration of the Shapal insulator could indicate some intrusion of FLiBe into the ceramic, but
no FLiBe was observed within the crucible.

 9

 Another test was performed using the 38.1 µm Pt wire on a batch of salt used in a test for
a thermodynamic reference electrode shown in Figure 8. This was to test the functionality of set
connectors as electrode connectors for molten salt electrochemistry.

Figure 8: (Left) Image of electrodes used in experiment. (Right) Voltammogram recorded in
contaminated FLiBe

 The contaminants in this FLiBe after other experiments preclude thermodynamic analysis
of any reactions occurring; however, a quasi-steady state current draw to this microelectrode
design in FLiBe is evident and should be considered for better controlled measurements of the H-
FLiBe system. Therefore, in this experimental campaign, 3 key issues were identified:
 a) Molten FLiBe electrochemical experiments require more precise control over the reference
 potential.
 i) Reaction potentials may be reliably corrected between measurements so long as
 continuity of OCP monitoring is maintained between measurements.
 b) The hygroscopic nature of these melts contribute to significant challenges in isolating the
 hydrogen peak.
 c) Modeling of mass transport to electrode surfaces in FLiBe is complex and must be handled
 by improved theoretical and numerical models.
2.3 COMSOL Multiphysics Simulations
 The numerical modeling portion of molten FLiBe electrochemistry was approached
through the development of a COMSOL Multiphysics Electroanalysis model. The model can
output electrochemical data from custom voltage/current input waveforms, kinetic and diffusive
parameters for electrode reactions and mass transport, and chemical activity models for the system
of interest. To bridge the gap from collected and experimental data (and calculate diffusion
coefficients), the chemical kinetics, activity coefficients, and output waveforms from the
electrochemical studies will need to be described. The COMSOL electroanalysis module allows
for the input of all these parameters; work first focused on the outputs of mass transport modes not
easily described analytically.

 10

 First, the model was validated by modeling potential-step input current-time outputs on
disk and hemispherical electrode geometries. The current response of a hemispherical
microelectrode is well approximated by the addition of linear transport and radial transport with
the mass transfer coefficient for the hemisphere given in Table 1. For more complicated
geometries, the transient response is not so easily treated, so the performance of micro-disk and
cylinders were modeled to simulate potential measurements. The 2D axisymmetric model was
built in COMSOL 6.1 using the built-in electroanalysis module. This model allowed for the
simulation of hemisphere, disk, and micro-wire electrodes through the construction of a quarter-
circle geometry consisting of an electrode region (constructed as a quarter-circle, line, or rectangle
respectively) and two electrolyte regions consisting of an inner region filled with a free triangular
mesh and an infinite element layer built using a mapped mesh. The maximum element size was
set to the electrode radius, and the minimum element size was set to the electrode radius divided
by 1,000. Example meshings are shown below in Figure 9.

 The Ag/AgF system was chosen to address the limitations of the Ni/Ni2+ TRE, as the low
melting point of AgF (Tmelt = 435°C) allows for the direct use of the pure salt in the electrode,
eliminating the time-consuming dissolution step required for NiF2. After facing challenges with
AgF reactivity with the crucible material (AX05 Boron Nitride), subsequent reevaluations of the
TRE design resulted in a final test using a porous graphite crucible with a boron nitride coating on
the outside only, a supporting salt of FLiBe that ~13mol% of AgF could be dissolved into instead
of reacting with moisture, and an Ag metal wire immersed in the FLiBe-AgF melt was performed;
again tested against a Be rod. A schematic of the electrochemical cell, images of the electrode
samples, and the result of ~18 hours of OCP monitoring of the cell are shown in Figure 14.

 14

 Figure 14: Results of the Ag/AgF TRE Experiment

 The investigation of Ag/AgF as a reference electrode system in FLiBe revealed significant
chemical compatibility challenges. While achieving a faster equilibration time (2 hours) compared
to traditional Ni/NiF2 electrodes (30 hours), the Ag/AgF system demonstrated unexpected
reactivity with both boron nitride containment materials and moisture contaminants, resulting in
the reduction of Ag ions and deviation from expected potentials. The observed equilibrium
potential of 1.4 V vs. Be2+/Be, corresponding to parts per trillion Ag+ concentration, suggests
substantial loss of Ag ions.

2.5 Rotating Disk Electrode Development
 To better understand the behavior of hydrogen in convecting salt systems, the development
of an RDE was initiated. This device rotates while applying a potential in order to obtain diffusivity
data solved using the solution to the Convective-Diffusion equation. It is constructed from a top
collar which connects to a Pine Instruments Modulated Speed Rotator, then a shaft (made of two
upper and lower pieces) that attaches to a noble metal electrode tip and insulated by AX05 boron
nitride sheathing. A sketch of the electrode construction, and image of the entire assembly is shown
in Figure 15 below.

3 Impact
3.1 Use of Project Results
 Strategies and best practices for electrochemical hydrogen detection in FLiBe are crucial
to developing on-line diagnostics for tritium breeding systems. The learnings from this study on
electrode material choice to detect hydrogen and deconvolute from oxygen evolution will
accelerate design of CFS’ electrode assemblies. Learning about the failure modes of different
thermodynamic reference electrode chemistries also will help design more successful ones in the
future. FLiBe compatible, high-sensitivity microelectrodes will significantly boost the ability to
measure the electrochemical signal of dissolved species in FLiBe at the lab and loop scale. The
COMSOL models developed in this INFUSE have been shown to closely obey their corresponding
analytical equations, indicating that they can be used for modeling electrochemical transport in
more complex geometries. This will feed into more robust transport models that can be used as
new electrodes and other salt-facing components are developed.
 The hardware developed for hydrogen sensing in this work will also have tangible impacts
on CFS’ FLiBe research and development efforts. The development of new fabrication processes
for microelectrodes, and their imminent testing, have derisked electrode design. As more
performance results come in, CFS will likely implement microelectrodes similar to the ones
designed in this work into test systems. The CAD drawings for RDE designs can also be directly
used for CFS’ lab systems.

blankets. In a practical molten salt engineering system, the direction chemical equilibria may shift
depending on dissolved structural materials, oxidant intrusion, radiation products, and tritium

tritium generated in FLiBe breeding blankets does not leak to the environment and is captured to
create more fuel. This may be quickly understood by examining the estimated solubilities of H2

 Suzuki et al. only detected HT as the permeating tritium species in their permeation
experiment, concluding that tritium confined as T+ in the salt cannot permeate through structural
materials6. It follows that knowledge of the redox state of the hydrogen in a system is necessary to
interpret solubility data, also evidenced by the large spread in diffusivity data measured in the
permeation tests shown in Figure 1. Furthermore, if the solvation mechanism to fluoride depends
on chemical states beyond simply the redox potential like the fluoroacidity of the melt7,8, changing
activities of fluorine in the salt due to an unknown suite of blanket conditions will lead to different
ratios of tritium which may or may not permeate through structural materials or permeators in the
system. Though this work was not able to deliver definitive measurements of hydrogen species
solubility and diffusivity in FLiBe, it systematically approached the wide range of experimental
challenges in characterizing these systems and developed approaches to tackle each through a
robust set of electrochemical, modeling, and thermodynamic techniques.
 The University of California Berkeley SALT group’s work in this system is currently
active. Each design covered in this report is steadily working towards completion, and a second
round of experimentation with the simpler designs is planned in collaboration with the University
of Edinburgh. Finally, an extensive review of the current and historic research regarding the
behavior of hydrogen, not just in FLiBe but in the wide range of halide melts is in progress. We
believe that reinterpretation of the field of available data in the context of tritium breeding blankets
will yield useful knowledge about the management of this volatile species and set the stage for
further exploration.
