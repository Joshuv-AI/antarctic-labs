 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 Projections of the number of high level waste (HLW) canisters to be produced in the
Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) (e.g., [1]) are based upon the
inventory of the tank wastes, the anticipated performance of the sludge treatment processes, and
current understanding of the capability of the borosilicate glass waste form. The WTP HLW
melter design, unlike earlier Department of Energy (DOE) melter designs, incorporates a glass
bubbler system. The bubblers create active glass pool mixing and thereby improve heat and mass
transfer and glass melting rate. The WTP HLW melters each have a glass surface area of 3.75 m 2
and depth of ~1.1 m. The two melters in the HLW facility together are designed to produce up to
7.5 MT of glass per day at 100% availability. Further increases in HLW waste processing rates
can potentially be achieved by optimization of the feed and glass formulations, increasing the
melter operating temperature above 1150C, and by increasing the waste loading in the glass
product. Increasing the waste loading also has the added benefit of decreasing the number of
canisters for storage.

 The baseline WTP glass formulation efforts have been conservative in terms of
achievable waste loadings. These baseline formulations have been specified to ensure that the
glasses are homogenous, contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule-
heated, ceramic-lined melters, and meet WTP contract requirements. The overall WTP mission
will require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are dominated by mixtures that
include aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as
waste loading limiting components. In order to improve waste loadings, DOE’s Office of River
Protection (ORP) initiated a testing program at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of the
Catholic University of America (CUA) to develop and characterize HLW glasses for wastes that
are limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, and Cr [1, 2]. Results of that work have demonstrated the
feasibility of increases in waste oxide loading from about 25 wt% to 33-50 wt% in the glass,
depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these higher waste loading glasses will reduce
the HLW canister production requirement by about 25% or more [3]. Furthermore, it has been
shown that a key technological risk area relates to the strong dependence of glass production rate
on waste composition [3]. The extent of this variation across the full spectrum of HLW waste
types needs to be quantified in order to accurately project waste treatment rates.

 Under a separate contract with Bechtel National Incorporated (BNI) to support the WTP,
VSL has developed and tested glass formulations for WTP HLW compositions to provide data to
meet the WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities [4-9]. That work
was based upon small-scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected
formulations were also tested in continuously fed, joule-heated melters (DM100 and DM1200)
[10-15]. That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then-planned early feed tanks
for the WTP (i.e., AZ-101, AZ-102, C-106/AY-102, and C-104/AY-101). These wastes are high
in iron (AZ-101, AZ-102 and C-106/AY-102) or thorium (C-104/AY-101) and are significantly
different from those used in subsequent enhancement tests performed for ORP (i.e., wastes

 9

limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, and Cr). Baseline glass formulations to treat these high-Fe wastes were
developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the throughput requirement for the HLW
melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was subsequently increased to 800 kg/(m2·day). As a
result, the baseline high-Fe HLW glass formulations for WTP perform only slightly better than
the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate requirement. Furthermore, the baseline waste loadings for
the Fe-limited HLW compositions are only slightly higher than the BNI contract minimum. In
subsequent work performed for ORP on other HLW compositions, VSL developed small-scale
test methods to assess processing rates of melter feeds and included them as an integral part of
glass formulation development. This methodology was used successfully to develop glass
formulations for high-Al Hanford HLW that showed processing rates in excess of
2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste loadings. The same methodology can be applied to
the development of improved glass formulations for other Hanford HLW streams in order to
provide ORP with a significantly more robust operating envelope with reduced risk of
throughput shortfalls. Consequently, a wide range of Hanford HLW streams rich in constituents
such as iron [16], bismuth [17], sulfur [18, 19], chromium [20], strontium [21], zirconium [22],
and manganese [23], which have the potential to limit waste loadings and production rates, are
being addressed in a series of glass formulation development and melter testing tasks.

 This report describes results from work performed to develop and test glass and feed
formulations designed to increase waste loadings for Hanford HLW containing aluminum, iron,
and sodium phosphates as well as to determine the effects of the form of phosphate in Hanford
HLW streams on melter feed processing rates. Phosphates are present in Hanford tank waste in a
variety of forms including iron, sodium, aluminum, and bismuth phosphates [24-26]. These
differences in phosphate form have the potential to affect waste and melter feed rheology, feed
processing rates, and the extent of crystalline phases in the glass product, as has been observed
with different forms of aluminum and iron in HLW waste simulants [27-29]. In previous work
for ORP one such HLW composition was studied to determine achievable glass waste loadings
and to determine the effects of the type of phosphate (e.g., sodium phosphate, aluminum
phosphate, iron phosphate) on feed properties and processing characteristics [30]. The results of
that work showed that high waste loading glass formulations developed for this HLW stream are
corrosive to Inconel 690 and show poor processing characteristics. Some formulations for this
waste stream, and formulations for other wastes high in phosphate [31], showed submicron
phosphate crystallization; however, this crystallization did not affect Product Consistency Test
(PCT) releases. Since reducing the waste loading to address these issues is not an economically
desirable solution, it is preferable to explore other glass formulation options. If, however,
reductions in waste loading are required to address these issues, it is necessary to define the
extent of such reductions and the maximum viable waste loadings for these streams.

 These issues were addressed by first focusing on the corrosion of Inconel 690 by these
high-P HLW glasses in order to understand the mode of corrosion and to identify glass
formulation changes to reduce corrosion. Subsequently, glass formulations for the Hanford tank
waste containing aluminum, iron, and sodium phosphates were developed, and selected
formulations were used for melter testing using feed containing three different phosphates likely
to be present in Hanford tank waste. The testing outlined in the Test Plan for this work [32] was
designed to identify glass and melter feed formulations that are less corrosive to Inconel 690 and
that optimize waste loadings and waste processing rates while meeting all processing and

 10

product quality requirements. The work included preparation and characterization of crucible
melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and the maximum waste
loadings that can be achieved. The testing included an assessment of melt rate using a Vertical
Gradient Furnace (VGF) system to develop new formulations with improved melt rates and high
waste loadings. Development of a suitable glass formulation was informed by earlier work with
high phosphate HLW compositions [30, 31]. The results from crucible-scale testing supported
subsequent melter tests on a DM100 vitrification system designed to examine the effects of
enhanced glass and feed formulations on waste processing rate and product quality. DM100
melter tests were conducted with feeds containing three different phosphates under nominal
conditions (1150°C glass temperature, 9 lpm bubbling) to assess the effects of the form of
phosphate on feed processing rate and processing characteristics. Samples of the glass product
were collected and analyzed for composition and crystalline phases. Data on processing rate and
any processing difficulties, off-gas composition, and system operating parameters were
collected. Inconel 690 bubblers and thermowells were inspected before and after the melter tests
to assess the extent of Inconel 690 corrosion in the new glass formulations. This work builds on
previous work performed at the VSL for ORP to increase waste loadings and processing rates of
HLW glass formulations [16-23, 27-31].

1.1 Test Objectives

 One objective of the present work was to investigate the increased Inconel 690 corrosion
and poor processing characteristics of feed and glass formulations containing a high phosphate
waste [30] and evaluate strategies for ameliorating these conditions. A further objective was to
assess the effects of phosphate form (aluminum, iron, sodium) on feed processing properties,
glass production rates, and product quality while vitrifying a high phosphorus HLW stream.

 The specific objectives of this work were to:

 • Determine the cause of extensive Inconel 690 corrosion by the HLW-HPA-07 glass
 formulation for the HLW composition from HTWOS Model Run Waste Batch 2636 [33].
 Results from Inconel 690 corrosion testing were used in the development of glass
 compositions for this waste stream that are less corrosive to Inconel 690.

 • Determine the cause of the poor processing characteristics of the HLW-HPA-07 glass
 formulation for the HLW composition from HTWOS Model Run Waste Batch 2636 [33].
 Results from Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) testing were used in the development of
 glass compositions for this waste stream.

 • Identify HLW glass compositions and glass forming additive blends that achieve high
 waste loadings and improved processing rates for the HLW composition from HTWOS
 Model Run Waste Batch 2636 [33] that meet all glass property requirements.

 11

 • Determine the effects of phosphate form (aluminum, iron, sodium, calcium) on feed
 processing properties, glass production rates, and product quality while vitrifying the
 HLW composition from HTWOS Model Run Waste Batch 2636 [33].

 These objectives were addressed through a combination of crucible-scale tests, vertical
gradient furnace tests, and confirmation tests on a DM100 melter system.

1.2 Quality Assurance

 This work was conducted under a quality assurance program that is based on Nuclear
Quality Assurance (NQA)-1 2008 that is in place at the VSL. The program is compliant with
applicable criteria of 10 CFR 830.120; Office of Civilian Waste Management DOE/RW-0333P,
Quality Assurance Requirements and Description (QARD) Revision 20; the American Society of
Mechanical Engineers (ASME) NQA)-1, 2008 including NQA-1a-2009 addenda; and DOE
Order 414.1 D, Quality Assurance. This program is supplemented by a Quality Assurance
Project Plan (QAPP) for ORP WTP work [34] that is conducted at VSL. Test and procedure
requirements by which the testing activities are planned and controlled are also defined in that
plan. The program is supported by VSL standard operating procedures that were used for this
work [35].

1.3 DM100 Melter System

          1.3.1   DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed from the
recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the peristaltic pump,
which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon-lined feed line and water-cooled feed tube into
the melter.

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed

 12

through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated, and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Methods

 1.4.1 Feed Conversion by Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) Testing

 Figure 1.3 shows a schematic diagram of the VGF setup. The temperature gradient inside
the VGF is maintained by two separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in
cylindrical form and aligned along their axes. The inner heater is set at 1150°C, which is the
nominal temperature of the glass pool, and the ambient heater is set at 600°C, which is similar to
the melter plenum temperature. A ceramic crucible (4 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting
melter feed. The temperature gradient in the test crucible is shown in Figure 1.4. For a typical
feed conversion test, 10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed
(expressed on an oxide basis) is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater
before the dried melter feed (to yield 17 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the
controlled temperature gradient are allowed to continue for the designated test duration
(typically, from 30 to 90 minutes) and then stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air.
The top surface of the reacted feed material is then inspected and photographed. The crucibles
with their feed contents are then cross-sectioned to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends.
The saw cuts of the crucibles are performed dry (without lubricant) to avoid loss of any soluble
material.

 To characterize the reacted feed material, visual inspection and digital imaging of the top
(by photography) and cross section (using an optical scanner) of the reacted sample are
performed. The VGF results are used to evaluate the melt rate on a relative scale using the degree

 13

of melting that has occurred, the structure of the feed materials that are undergoing reaction and
transformation, and the feed conversion progression with time. A VGF sample showing
relatively more feed conversion and melting is classified as fast whereas a sample showing
relatively less feed conversion and melting is classified as slow. In cases where multiple VGF
samples are tested, a numerical ranking of relative melt rate is assigned based on a correlation
using feeds that were tested on the VGF and whose melt rates had been determined previously in
DM100 melter tests [27]. The numerical rankings of feed samples based on the extent of feed
conversion from observation of the top and cross section of VGF samples is given in Table 1.1.
The rankings span the range of 1 to 6, with a feed sample ranked 1 expected to exhibit a fast
processing rate and a feed sample ranked 6 expected to show a slow processing rate.

 1.4.2 Feed Composition and General Properties

 Feed samples were taken directly from as-received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion was ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20-ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct-current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples were also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Crucible and Melter Product Glasses

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5-gallon steel pails periodically
using an air-lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the pails for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. In addition, sufficient glass from the end of tests with each form of
phosphate was collected to perform the Product Consistency Test (PCT, 7 days at 90°C) and the
Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP). Prior to those tests, characterization with
respect to PCT, TCLP, melt electrical conductivity, melt viscosity, and secondary phases was
performed on the crucible melt formulations to identify the selection for melter testing and to
ensure compliance with the present WTP contract requirements. All of these procedures are
routinely conducted at VSL and, therefore, standard operating procedures are in place [35].

 Sample preparation for chemical analysis begins with size reduction and sieving. All
samples are then subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all elements
except boron and lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
reference materials are used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium are determined
by total acid dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the resulting
solutions to DCP-AES analysis. Glass samples from melter tests with different phosphate forms
were also analyzed for iron redox state by Mössbauer spectroscopy using glass prepared for XRF
analysis.

                 1.4.3.1 Viscosity

 The melt viscosity, η, is measured using a Brookfield viscometer. Measurements are
performed in the temperature range of 950-1250ºC and the data are interpolated to standard
temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln η = [A/(T-To)]+B, where A, B, and To are
fitting parameters. The equipment is calibrated at room temperature using standard oils of known
viscosity and then checked at 950-1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711).
Both precision and accuracy of the viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±15
relative%.

 1.4.3.2 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity, σ, of each glass is determined by measuring the resistance of
the glass melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode probe
attached to an Agilent model 4194A impedance analyzer. Measurements are performed over
similar temperature ranges to those employed for the melt viscosity measurements. The results
are analyzed to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity data are then
interpolated to standard temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln  = [A/(T−To)] + B,
where A, B and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical conductivity
measurements are  20 relative%.

 1.4.3.3 Product Consistency Test (PCT)

 The product consistency test (PCT; ASTM C 1285) [36] is used to evaluate the relative
chemical durability of glasses by measuring the concentrations of the chemical species released
from 100-200 mesh crushed glass (75-149 μm) to the test solution (de-ionized water in this case).
PCTs on the HLW glasses are performed at 90ºC, in accordance with the current WTP contract
requirement. The ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume for this test is about 2000
m-1 (typically, 4 g of 100-200 mesh glass is immersed in 40 ml of deionized water). All tests are
conducted in triplicate, in 304L stainless steel vessels, and in parallel with a standard glass
included in each test set. The internal standard is the Argonne National Laboratory – Low
Activity Reference Material (ANL-LRM) reference glass [37] and/or the Defense Waste
Processing Facility – Environmental Assessment (DWPF-EA) glass [38], both of which have
undergone round-robin testing. The leachates are sampled at predetermined times, the first of
which is seven days. One milliliter of sampled leachate is mixed with 20 ml of 1M HNO3 and the
resulting solution is analyzed by DCP-AES; another 3 ml of sampled leachate is used for pH
measurement.

 1.4.3.4 Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

 The TCLP was performed at VSL using SW-846 Method 1311, which employs leaching
of crushed glass (< 3/8”) in a sodium acetate buffer solution for 18 hours at 22C with constant
end-over-end agitation. A mass of about 100 grams of glass is leached in 2 liters of TCLP

 15

extract, according to the extraction method for non-volatiles. The surface area to volume ratio for
this test is about 20 m-1, which is about two orders of magnitude lower than that in the PCT. The
leachates are analyzed by DCP-AES according to VSL standard operating procedures.

 1.4.3.5 Secondary Phases

 Secondary phases in the glass samples were determined by optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
in combination with X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Secondary phases due to crystallization
and phase separation can be identified using these methods. The samples for SEM analysis are
processed by grinding and sieving (-18 mesh, < 1 mm) in order to provide a representative
measure of the overall crystal fraction in the sample. Ground glass samples are mounted in
epoxy, polished to expose cross sections of the glass grains, coated with carbon, and examined
using SEM/EDS. The amount of crystals in glass samples was determined by SEM in
conjunction with image analysis.

 1.4.3.6 Crystalline Phases by X-Ray Diffraction

 Quantitative XRD was used to determine the crystal content in glasses and on solids from
the cyclone filter in the off-gas system. Powder diffraction data were gathered using a Thermo-
Fisher X-tra theta-theta diffractometer fitted with a Cu X-ray tube. Glass samples were ground
and sieved to 325 mesh particles. Five weight percent of a corundum internal standard was
mixed with the powdered sample for quantitative analysis of the crystalline phases. Each powder
pattern was scanned from 5 to 80 degrees 2 theta using a collection time of 2 seconds per data
point. JADE9 Software was used to perform the search-match identification of the crystalline
phases as well as the whole pattern fitting to determine the weight percent of each crystalline
phase in the sample.

 1.4.3.7 Mössbauer Spectroscopy

 Mössbauer spectroscopy is an analytical technique that utilizes the recoil-free emission
and resonant absorption of gamma rays by nuclei bound in solids. The energies of the gamma ray
emitter (source) and absorber (sample) have to be closely matched and, therefore, the number of
elements that can be studied using this technique is limited. One of the elements that has been
studied extensively using Mössbauer spectroscopy is iron. In this work, Mössbauer spectroscopy
was used to measure the fraction of iron in the Fe2+ and Fe3+ states, which can be used as a
measure of the redox state of the glass sample. Mössbauer spectroscopy was also used to analyze
the various iron minerals in the feed materials and deposits in the off-gas system.

 Mössbauer spectra were collected using an American Magnetic/Ranger Scientific
MS-1200 system equipped with a 57Co source in a rhodium matrix with glass powder as the
sample. Both divalent and trivalent iron show doublet peaks in the Mössbauer spectrum and the
peak areas are proportional to the concentrations of the respective species in the glass. Even

 16

though the peaks overlap partially, software can be used to deconvolute the peaks and calculate
the peak areas. The ratio of the areas of the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks is equal to the ratio of their
concentrations in the glass. The redox measurements are calibrated using a set of six standard
glass samples with Fe2+ contents ranging from 7 to 90% of the total iron (Fetotal). The standards
include a NIST traceable Obsidian Rock (SRM 278), five standard glasses analyzed by Corning,
Inc. for their redox state, and the DWPF-EA glass. The site populations provided by the fitting is
mol% Fe, assuming that the recoil-free fraction is the same for each site.

 The NIST standard iron foil (SRM 1541) is used to calibrate the instrument and
determine the zero-velocity channel. Since Doppler shifts in energy are measured in Mössbauer
spectroscopy, the velocity is a measure of the shift in energy; knowing the zero-velocity channel
therefore helps in identifying the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks.

 1.4.3.8 Inconel 690 Corrosion

 Rectangular coupons of Inconel 690 were machined from the bulk material and their
surfaces polished. The dimensions of the coupons were 0.300 inch by 0.150 inch in cross-section
and of sufficient length to be immersed in the molten glass to a depth of 0.7 inch (Figure 1.5).
All dimensions of the coupons were measured by a micrometer to better than 0.5 mil before the
tests. The precision of the parallel surfaces was better than 0.001 inch.

 The standard VSL open crucible corrosion tests with molten glass were conducted using
a modified American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) refractory corrosion procedure
[39] using the experimental arrangement shown schematically in Figure 1.6. The experimental
setup consists of a 50-ml platinum crucible placed into a quartz crucible holder. On the top of the
crucible there is a cover with a 5-mm slot. The coupon is placed through the slot and suspended
by a rod that is placed through a hole drilled through one end of the coupon. For each test, 70
grams of glass is melted at 1150ºC (nominal) for 30 minutes; this glass quantity gives a metal
surface area (S)/melt volume (V) ratio of 0.15 cm-1 and the crucible is filled to ¾ of its height
(29 mm) with glass. The coupon is preheated with the lid at 800ºC for 45 minutes, allowing an
oxide layer to form on the coupon, which is then placed into the crucible with the molten glass.
The setup is then placed inside a furnace that is preheated to 1150ºC. After the designated test
period (7 days), the setup is removed from the furnace, the coupon is extracted from the glass,
and the glass melt is poured onto a graphite mold for additional analyses, if required. The coupon
with the glass coating is annealed at 450ºC for 2 hours to reduce spalling of the glass coating.
The dimensional loss is calculated as half of the difference of the measured widths between the
original and reacted coupons at the neck and half-down locations, as in the case of K-3 refractory
corrosion. The dimensional loss at the bottom of the Inconel coupon is also measured and
reported (Figure 1.7).

2.3 Development of Glass Formulations

 The previous glass formulations developed and tested for HLW Batch 2636 constituted
the HLW-HPA glass series [30]. Waste loadings of the fifteen HLW-HPA glasses range from
42 wt% to 64 w%, which correspond to P2O5 concentrations in the glass of 4.60 wt% to 7.18
wt%. The Na2O concentrations in these glasses are higher than those found in typical HLW
glasses, ranging from 15.32 wt% to 23.91 wt%; all Na2O in the HLW-HPA glasses originate
from the waste. The principal constraint that limited waste loading in the HLW-HPA gasses was
nepheline formation after heat treatment, which was observed for glasses with 60 wt% waste
loadings and above. At waste loadings below 60 wt%, the HLW-HPA glasses showed
crystallization of phosphate and spinel phases upon isothermal and canister centerline cooling
(CCC) heat treatments. In spite of the relatively high Al2O3 and Na2O contents in these glasses
(up to 13.11 wt% Al2O3 and 21.67 wt% Na2O), no nepheline resulted from CCC heat treatment.
Based on the characterization data collected, HLW-HPA-07 was determined to meet all WTP
requirements for HLW glasses. The P2O5 and Na2O concentrations in HLW-HPA-07, which has
a waste loading of 58 wt% are 6.51 wt% and 21.67 wt%, respectively. Because of the unusually
high Na2O content, HLW-HPA-07 was also characterized for K-3 refractory corrosion and the
test data showed a moderate neck loss of 0.02”. No acceptability limit for K-3 refractory
corrosion is defined in the WTP specification. Earlier HLW glass formulation development work
adopted the K-3 corrosion limit recommended for low activity waste (LAW) glass formulations
[43], which limits the neck corrosion loss to no more than 0.040”. Consequently, HLW-HPA-07
was selected as the target glass formulation to support melter testing.

 During melter testing and idling between tests with feeds containing either Na3PO4 or
FePO4/Ca5(PO4)3(OH), HLW-HPA-07 was found to be highly corrosive to Inconel 690, with
damage observed on melter components including the bubbler, thermowell, and airlift lance [30].
After consultation with ORP, scheduled testing using the AlPO4 based feed was replaced with a
melter feed without phosphorus in order to assess the effects of phosphorus on the vitrification

 20

process. Results from both melter and bench scale tests showed that the formulation without
phosphorus was not overly corrosive towards Inconel. Severe damage to Inconel 690 bubblers
has also been observed in melter tests with high bismuth and phosphorus HLW glass
formulations, where formation of bismuth metal and phosphate phases was determined to be the
primary cause of metal corrosion [45]. For melter testing with HLW-HPA-07, however,
preliminary investigation identified phosphorus and sulfur as the agents of corrosion, as no
bismuth metal was observed in contact with the corroded metal [30]. Although phosphidation
and sulfidation are common degradation mechanisms in metal alloys including Inconels [46],
this was unexpected since comparable corrosion was not observed in earlier testing with even
higher levels of both phosphorus and sulfur [31].

 Only two additive oxides were included in the formulation of HLW-HPA-07: B2O3 and
SiO2. No alkalis were added to suppress the formation of secondary phases such as spinel since
Na2O was already present at a high concentration in Batch 2326. The high Na2O contents in
HLW-HPA glasses (e.g., 21.67 wt% Na2O in HLW-HPA-07) not only limited the achievable
waste loading by increasing the propensity for nepheline formation, it was also believed to
contribute to the corrosiveness of the glasses [30]. Therefore, in addition to meeting all the
processing constraints and product quality requirements, new glass formulations to be developed
for HLW Batch 2636 must limit the corrosion of K-3 refractory and Inconel 690 to acceptable
levels. This was achieved by reducing the phosphorus concentration in the glass and by
introducing a passivating additive.

 Corrosion data for the HLW-HPA glasses were collected previously on K-3 refractory
but not on Inconel 690 [30]. The present work also tested corrosion properties of these HLW

glasses HLW-HPA-17 through -19 were characterized with respect to K-3 refractory corrosion
and the results are given in Table 2.8 together with the previously collected data. It can be seen
that all of the new HLW-HPA glasses showed considerable improvements with regard to K-3
corrosion, yielding much lower dimensional losses at the neck and half-down positions of the
test coupons. In particular, the neck loss data for the new glasses ranged between < 0.001” and
0.005”, which are comparable to those measured for many HLW glasses that had been selected
for other melter tests [47]. In contrast, the earlier HLW-HPA glasses showed larger neck losses
of 0.009” to 0.033”. The half-down losses display similar decreases for the new glasses. Figure
2.2 correlates the measured K-3 neck dimensional losses with waste loading. Glasses formulated
without ZnO passivation show a fairly linear and steep decline in neck loss with waste loading.
A similar trend is observed with the Zn-bearing glasses, although the decrease is more moderate.
Overall, glass passivation with addition of ZnO is very effective in controlling the rate of K-3
corrosion. This passivation has been shown to result from the formation of a dense layer of
Zn/Cr spinel around the refractory-melt interface, thereby reducing the dissolution rate of the
K-3 solid solution [47].

 2.4.3 Vertical Gradient Furnace Tests

 The vertical gradient furnace has been used as a useful screening tool for ranking HLW
slurry feeds for their processing rate in waste glass melters [16, 21-23, 27-31]. Melter feed
samples with each of the three different phosphates, all producing the same HLW-HPA-17 glass
composition, were tested under the standard VGF conditions in an attempt to evaluate the
potential impact of the form of phosphate on melt rate. Aliquots from one-liter sized slurry feed
samples were dried and subjected to the test protocol described in Section 1.4.1. The chemical
compositions of the sampled feed are given in Tables 2.17 - 2.19. In a standard VGF experiment,
feed samples are examined for the extent of feed reactions, melting, appearance of the feed
sample such as the presence of foam, formation of a dome-like structure, etc., that are indicative
of the rate of feed conversion to glass. The conversion of each type of feed material was
evaluated primarily based on images of the tops and cross sections of the reacted feed samples
after 30- and 60-minute VGF tests, depending on the observed extent of completion of the
conversion reactions. The top and cross-sectional images of VGF samples are shown in Figures
2.4 and 2.5. Table 2.21 provides a summary of the visual observations and relative rankings of
the feeds as compared to the previously tested HLW-HPA-07 composition with various forms of
phosphate [30], the HLW-NG-Fe2 composition with various forms of iron [16, 29], the HLW-
HMn-20 composition with various forms of manganese [23], and the HLW-E-Al-27 and HLW-
Al-16 compositions with various forms of aluminum [3, 27].

 The results of the VGF tests indicate that the type of phosphate in the feed did not have a
significant effect on the conversion of feed to glass reaction rate. This finding is in contrast to
tests with the higher waste loading HLW-HPA-07 composition, which showed that feed with
iron phosphate was much slower to convert to glass than feeds with sodium or aluminum
phosphate. All feeds targeting the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition were slow to react, with a
thick foamy layer on the wall of the reaction vessel after 30- and 60-minute VGF tests, resulting
in a ranking of 3. Typically, formation of such a thick layer full of gas vesicles indicates episodes
of degassing, foaming, and subsequent collapsing of an over-arching dome structure, resulting in
the overall conversion rate from melter feed to glass being slower. Similar observations were
made while testing the HLW-HPA-07 composition with sodium or aluminum phosphates,
suggesting that higher glass production rates are not to be expected for the present lower waste
loading composition. Improved melting characteristics were observed for the HLW-HPA-17
formulation as compared to the HLW-HPA-07 for feeds with iron phosphate.

 25

 As compared to other feeds previously subjected to VGF testing, the only feeds that
melted more slowly than the feeds targeting the HLW-HPA-17 composition were feeds targeting
the HLW-E-Al-27 composition, which contained high concentrations of Al2O3 or Al(OH)3 and
was yet to be optimized to achieve maximum processing rate. Also of note is that, in general, the
phosphate-containing feeds from the present tests were converted to glass at slower rates than the
high iron, high manganese, and reformulated high aluminum formulations. Conversion rates of
feed to glass for the feeds from the present tests were also slower than that observed for the
previous high phosphate glass composition [31] indicating that slow melting is not exclusively
attributable to phosphate but rather to a combination of other feed components with phosphate.
The most apparent difference between the two feeds is that the previously processed high
phosphorus waste contains much less sodium and therefore lithium carbonate was used as an
additive [31].

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100-BL vitrification system between 9/18/18 and
 10/3/18 with the high phosphorus, HTWOS Model Run Waste Batch 2636 HLW simulant and
 the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition. Feed for the tests was obtained in a single batch without
 phosphorus, iron, sulfur, and calcium as well as deficits in aluminum and sodium, enabling the

 phosphate sources. These tests are to be compared to those conducted with a higher waste
 loading glass composition formulated for the same waste (HLW-HPA-07). In tests with the
 HLW-HPA-07 glass composition, extensive corrosion of the Inconel bubbler and thermowell
 occurred, and relatively low processing rates were achieved.

 In the present series of melter tests, over half a metric ton of glass was produced from
 tests processing over one and half metric tons of feed with various forms of phosphate. In all
 tests, the glass temperature was held constant at 1150°C, melt pool bubbling was held constant

 waste oxide and glass compositions were identical for all tests. This testing protocol permitted
 the determination of the effects of each phosphate compound on production rate and processing
 properties and facilitated comparison with previously conducted tests. Attempts were made to

 simulants [3, 11-17, 20-23, 27-31, 41-44]. These conditions include a near-complete cold cap,
 which is between 80-95% melt surface coverage for the DM100 since a 100% cold cap tends to
 lead to "bridging" in smaller melters. Prior to these tests, the composition of the melt pool was
 adjusted to approximate the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition. The planned 50-hour duration of
 each test was exceeded for the first two compositions but was not obtained for the last test due
 to the failure of the system blower at 44 hours into the test. Summaries of the tests are provided
 in Table 3.1; the tests were divided as follows:

 The feed and glass were processed with similar degrees of difficulty for feeds containing
different forms of phosphate present in the feed. This is in contrast to tests with the

deposits [30]. Cold cap conditions were similar to the range of conditions observed in many

incorporate into the glass pool. Glass discharging lowered the glass level in the melter and

pond on the cold cap surface and abruptly flow onto the melt pool surface causing minor

3.1 Corrosion of Inconel Components

 Following the completion of tests processing the HLW-HPA-17 formulation with each of
the phosphates, the Inconel 690 bubbler and thermowell were removed from the melter and
inspected for corrosion. The inspection took the form of visual observations, examples of which
are given in Figures 3.7.a – 3.8.b, XRF and XRD analysis of scale associated with the bubbler,
given in Table 3.5, SEM analysis of scale from the bubbler, given in Figures 3.9 and 3.10, and
caliper measurements along the lengths of the bubbler and thermowell. The bubbler appeared to
have a corroded layer surrounding it upon removal after each test. Once this material was
removed, the bubbler had a very shiny metallic finish, which increased in intensity over the
course of the tests. Analysis of the alteration layer reflects the glass composition with 10 to
20-fold enrichments of chromium and nickel, which are the major constituents of Inconel 690.
Mineral phases in this layer included relatively high proportions of magnetite (Fe3O4), eskolite
(Cr2O3), ludwigite (Ni2CrO2(BO3)), and niccoloferrite (Fe0.92Ni0.18(Fe0.93Ni1.07)O4). Phosphate
minerals were detected only in one sample, which reflects the overall glass composition more
than corrosion products. No enrichments of bismuth or phosphorus were observed in the
alteration material. SEM analysis of glass removed from the bubbler show layered minerals
containing zinc, chromium, and nickel, which increase in concentration with proximity to the
bubbler. Collectively, this indicates that the material spalled from the bubblers is glass enriched
in metals originating from the bubblers. Many of the minerals in this glass layer contain zinc,
which presumably limits the corrosion of the Inconel 690 bubbler. No metallic bismuth or
reduced phosphorus was observed, suggesting that neither is responsible for the corrosion. These
results are in contrast to secondary phases observed while processing the same waste and the
glass formulation HLW-HPA-07 [30]; phosphorus in the form of spherical nickel phosphide was
present in the glass near the metal interface, with some granulated and angulated nodules in the
molten metal with significant amounts of phosphorus in these particles. .

 Dimensional analysis of the bubbler and thermowell indicate minimal corrosion of these
two Inconel 690 components, as shown in Table 3.6. The average loss for each of the tests was

 30

less than a tenth of an inch for both the bubbler and thermowell. All measured points on the
bubbler lost less than 0.08 inches and only two points of measurement on the thermowell

corrosion. It appears that fluorine and sulfur reach only about half to three quarters of their
respective target concentrations due to volatilization from the glass pool and cold cap.

4.2 Chemical Durability of Discharge Glasses

 Samples of glass discharged from the end of the DM100 tests produced while processing
feeds with each of the phosphates were evaluated for chemical durability using the PCT and
TCLP methods. The PCT results are compared to those for the benchmark DWPF-EA and the
comparable crucible glasses in Table 4.7; the TCLP results are compared to the WTP delisting
limits [49, 50] and Universal Treatment Standard (UTS) limits in Table 4.8. Additionally,
concentrations of bismuth and manganese were measured in the TCLP leachates as a point of
comparison between the glasses; there are no regulatory limits for bismuth and manganese by the
TCLP procedure. The chemical durability determined for the melter and crucible HLW-HPA-17
glasses by both of these methods is excellent. All measured normalized PCT releases for the
HLW-HPA-17 discharge glass samples are greater than an order of magnitude lower than the
corresponding values for the DWPF-EA glass. The melter glasses all have lower PCT releases
than the crucible glass. All of the melter and crucible HLW-HPA-17 glasses have lower PCT
releases than the corresponding releases for the HLW-HPA-07 glasses [30]. Much of this
improvement from HLW-HPA-07 to HLW-HPA-17 may be attributable to reducing the oxide
waste loading from 58 to 55 wt% which results in a Na2O reduction from 21.67 to 20.55 wt%.

 All regulated TCLP leachate concentrations are well below the WTP delisting and UTS
limits for melter glasses targeting the HLW-HPA-17 composition. Measured sodium PCT
releases for the melter glasses were lower than those for the crucible glasses. No obvious trends
in PCT release or TCLP leachate concentrations are observed in the melter glasses generated
from feeds containing the aluminum, sodium, iron, and calcium phosphates. The TCLP leachate
concentrations for the melter glasses are more than an order of magnitude lower than the
delisting limits [49, 50] and at least five times lower than the UTS limits. The TCLP leachate
concentrations are from four to more than ten times higher for melter than crucible glasses for
some elements due to the incorporation of metals leached from melter components in the
discharged glasses. All measured TCLP leachate concentrations are less than those measured on
comparable HLW-HPA-07 glasses with the same high phosphate waste [30] due in part to more
extensive corrosion of the Inconel 690 bubbler and thermowell causing higher concentrations of
metals in the glass during the earlier tests. The chemical durability of the HLW-HPA-17 glasses

 33

is generally within the range measured on glasses produced from wastes limited by bismuth,
chromium, aluminum, and aluminum plus sodium [3], chromium and iron [20, 16], phosphorus
[31], strontium [21], manganese [23], and zirconium [22]. These results confirm that glasses can
be formulated with higher phosphate contents and meet glass product quality requirements. They
also show that the TCLP response for many metals is partially a function of the level of
corrosion of melter system components, and not only waste composition and glass formulation.

4.3. SEM, XRD, and Mössbauer Analysis of Melter Glass Samples

 Discharge glass samples from the end of tests processing feeds with different phosphates
were subjected to XRD, SEM, and Mössbauer analysis to determine the secondary mineral
content and iron oxidation state. The results are summarized in Table 4.9 and detailed
descriptions of observed mineral phases are given in Table 4.10. SEM images and EDS analysis
of secondary phases in the discharge glasses are given in Figures 4.2 to 4.4. Similar crystalline
phases were detected in samples analyzed by both XRD and SEM, although the SEM analysis
was complicated by the solubility of secondary phases that are lost during sample preparation
and the small crystal size of the secondary phases. In previous tests with HLW streams rich in
phosphates [30, 31], detection of alkali phosphates in glasses by SEM was similarly challenged
when large quantities of phosphate minerals were detected by XRD. Glasses discharged during
the present tests were measured by XRD to contain eighteen to twenty weight percent sodium
phosphate and apatite. The same glasses were determined to contain five to ten volume percent
sodium phosphate and five to ten volume percent sodium, calcium phosphate by SEM. The
combined phosphate mineral content measured accounts for the majority of the phosphorus
present in the melter feed. Similar observations were made for glasses made with the
HLW-HPA-07 formulation and the same high phosphate waste [30]. The canister cooled crucible
melt for the HLW-HPA-07 glass was reported to contain nearly ten percent voids, which are
assumed to have contained sodium phosphate before sample preparation, resulting in similar
amounts of crystalline phases as detected in the discharged glasses. This suggested that most of
the phosphate phases detected in discharge samples probably formed during cooling in the
discharge can. The phosphate crystals, if present as such at the processing temperature, which
seems unlikely, are not expected to affect glass processing properties due to their small size. No
traces of iron or aluminum phosphate were detected in any of the glass samples, while sodium
phosphate and apatite were measured in all discharges, regardless of their incorporation in the
feed being tested. Collectively, this indicates that the phosphate minerals in the feed are
incorporated into the glass pool, followed by crystallization of sodium and calcium phosphates
during cooling of the discharged glass. The amount of spinels detected in samples ranged from
0.11 to 0.18 volume percent, with the highest amount being associated with the feed containing
aluminum phosphate. The amount of divalent iron ranged from nondetectable for tests with
sodium and iron phosphate to 1.3% for tests with aluminum phosphate.

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40-CFR-60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions during each test segment. The concentrations of
off-gas species present as particulates and gaseous species were derived from laboratory data on
solutions extracted from air samples (impinger solutions, filters, and rinse solutions) together
with measurements of the volume of air sampled. Particulate collection required isokinetic
sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust at the same velocity that the air is
flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5). Typically, a sample size of 30 dscf was taken at a
rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total particulate loading was determined by combining
gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical analysis of probe rinse solutions.
An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the sampling train to ensure
complete scrubbing of acid gases. The collected materials were analyzed using DCP-AES for the
majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for anions. Melter emission fluxes are
compared to feed fluxes and emission samples taken while processing the same glass
compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the distinction that is made between constituents sampled as
particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally defined as those species that
are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has passed through a 0.3 µm heated
filter. All samples were well within the 90 - 110% limits for isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition with
phosphates constituted from 0.34 to 0.39 percent of feed solids. The level of carryover while
processing the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition was lower than the range measured while
processing various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants processed on the same melter:
AZ-102 (0.57 - 1.47 percent) [15]; C-106/AY-102 SIPP (0.61 to 0.81 percent) [12]; the former
C-106/AY-102 baseline (0.3 - 0.74 percent) [40, 41]; the C-106/AY-102 high waste loading
formulation (0.66 and 0.71 percent) [16]; high chromium HLW-HCr-16 formulation (0.70 and
0.62 percent) [20]; high strontium HLW-HSr-7 formulation (1.14 and 0.58 percent) [21]; high
zirconium HLW-ZrA-07 formulation (1.57 and 2.51 percent) [22] and high zirconium
HLW-Zr-11 formulation (3.05 and 1.77 percent) [22]. Carryover in the present tests was
comparable to that measured while processing the high bismuth HLW-Bi-F3 formulation (0.30
and 0.39 percent) [17]; high phosphate HLW-HP-16 formulation (0.34 and 0.23) [31]; high
manganese HLW-HMn-20 glass formulation (0.23 to 0.34 percent) [23]; and chromium and
bismuth limited HLW wastes (0.19 – 0.48) [3]. Particulate emissions while processing the
HLW-HPA-07 glass and the same high phosphate waste composition constituted only 0.13 to
0.16 percent of feed solids. The increased carryover in the present tests may be attributable to the
fine-grained zinc used as an additive in the HLW-HPA-17 composition or the minimal use of
manual intervention to dislodge deposits from the plenum. The relatively low particulate
carryover in the present tests was a result in part of the low volatile content of the feed. Two of
the volatile constituents in the HLW-HPA-17 composition, fluorine and sulfur, are emitted in

 35

large proportion as gaseous species and therefore do not contribute as significantly to the
calculated carryover of feed solids. Carryover values for tests conducted with sodium, calcium,
and iron phosphate vary only by a difference of 0.14 percent of feed solids, indicating that the
type of phosphate in the melter feed has a minimal effect on the amount of particulate carryover.

 The composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed
composition, with some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. Elements
carried over at the same or lower proportion than the overall particulate are aluminum, silicon,
iron, magnesium, nickel, manganese, phosphorus, strontium, zinc, and zirconium, while boron,
chromium, halides, alkalis, lead, and sulfur were emitted at rates considerably higher than that
for the total particulate. Bismuth carryover was the same as or a little higher than the overall
particulate, which is considerably less than that observed for feeds with added reductants [45].
The highest carryover was clearly observed for the most volatile elements, sulfur and halides, as
expected. Chlorine was detected in the emissions particularly in tests with sodium phosphate due
to its presence in chemicals as a contaminant and its highly volatile nature. No difference in
these trends was observed with the various phosphates over the course of the tests. Phosphorus is
one of the least volatile elements in the feed; the results show that the relative carryover of
phosphorus is not affected by the type of phosphate. Boron, chlorine, fluorine, and sulfur were
the only elements detected in the impinger solutions collected downstream of the heated particle
filter in the sampling train, which constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions.

5.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 The amount of material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler is
listed in Table 5.2. The amount of material removed from the cyclones accounts for 0.02 to 0.13
percent of the feed solids from the tests, which is lower than the particulate sampled in the melter
exhaust, suggesting that either the particulate is too fine to be removed by the cyclone or solids
are deposited in the line in between the exhaust sampling point and the cyclone. The analyzed
mineral content and chemical composition of material accumulated in the cyclones are provided
in Tables 5.2 and 5.3, respectively. Cyclone solids contain all of the feed components in similar
proportions to the feed for most elements, with enrichments over feed for the volatile elements
most notably sulfur (four-fold), as well as an up to twofold depletion of silicon. These
proportions of volatile and non-volatile elements in the cyclone particulate indicate bulk
carryover of feed from the melter coupled with the loss of some volatile components from the
glass pool and cold cap. Chlorine, lithium, and titanium were detected in the cyclone solids
despite not being included in the target feed composition due to contamination of the present
feed (chlorine, for example) or carryover of residual material from preceding tests. The results
from XRD analysis show that the most abundant mineral phases detected in the cyclone deposits
were present in the feed as either the additive quartz or the waste components eskolaite and
hematite, suggesting direct entrainment of feed components into the melter exhaust stream.
These were the same minerals observed in the deposits after processing the HLW-HPA-07 glass
and the same high phosphate waste composition [30]. No phosphate minerals were detected in
the deposits. Low concentrations of an aluminum sodium silicate mineral were detected in each
of the samples, which is not surprising since oxides of these three elements comprise over two
thirds of the target glass composition.

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 This report details work performed to develop and test new high waste loading glass
formulations for Hanford HLW containing aluminum, iron, and sodium phosphates as well as to
determine the effects of the form of phosphate in Hanford HLW streams on melter feed
processing rates. A number of HLW batches to be processed at the WTP have P2O5
concentrations that extend up to about thirteen weight percent on an oxide basis [33]. These
high-phosphorus HLW streams frequently contain other components such as bismuth, iron,
calcium, sulfur, and various rare earth elements, which when combined with phosphorus, can
cause secondary phase formation or amorphous phase separation that can limit waste loading or
lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow processing rates. In previous work for ORP one such
HLW composition was studied to determine achievable glass waste loadings and to determine
the effects of the type of phosphate (e.g., sodium phosphate, aluminum phosphate, iron
phosphate) on feed properties and processing characteristics [30]. The results of that work
showed that high waste loading glass formulations developed for this HLW stream are corrosive
to Inconel 690 and have poor processing characteristics. Some formulations for this waste stream
and formulations for other wastes high in phosphate [31] showed submicron phosphate
crystallization.

 These issues were addressed by first focusing on the corrosion of Inconel 690 by these
high phosphorus HLW glasses to assess the mode of corrosion and to identify glass formulation
changes to reduce corrosion. Subsequently, glass formulations for the Hanford tank waste
containing aluminum, iron, and sodium phosphates were developed and subjected to melter
testing using feed containing three different phosphates likely to be present in Hanford tank
waste. The testing was designed to identify glass and melter feed formulations that are less
corrosive to Inconel 690 with high waste loadings and improved processing rates while meeting
all processing and product quality requirements. The work included preparation and
characterization of crucible melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and
the maximum waste loadings that can be achieved. The testing included an assessment of melt
rate using a VGF system. The results from crucible-scale testing supported subsequent melter
tests on a DM100 vitrification system designed to examine the effects of enhanced glass and feed
formulations on waste processing rate and product quality. DM100 melter tests were conducted
with feeds containing three different phosphates under nominal conditions (1150°C glass
temperature, 9 lpm bubbling) to determine the effects of the form of phosphate on feed
processing rate and processing characteristics. Samples of the glass product were collected and
analyzed for composition and crystalline phases. Data on processing rate and any processing
difficulties, off-gas composition, and system operating parameters were collected. Inconel 690
bubblers and thermowells were inspected before and after the melter tests to assess the corrosion
of Inconel 690 components in the new glass composition.

 38

 The high-phosphorus HLW composition selected for glass formulation development is
batch Number 2636 from the HTWOS run given in the River Protection Project System Plan,
Revision 6 [33]. The most abundant components in HLW Batch 2636 are Na2O (35.86 wt%) and
Al2O3 (21.70 wt%), followed by P2O5 (10.77 wt%). The concentrations of Bi2O3 and SO3 are
moderately high at 5.25 wt% and 0.72 wt%, respectively. Glass formulations previously
developed for this HLW stream showed substantial corrosion of Inconel melter components and
poor processing characteristics [30]. The focus of the present work was, therefore, to address
these issues, especially Inconel corrosion. The work included preparation and characterization of
crucible melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading and the addition of ZnO to reduce Inconel
corrosion. The results from crucible-scale testing were used to select a glass formulation for
subsequent DM100 melter tests designed to further assess the newly developed glass and feed
formulations with respect to Inconel corrosion and feed processing.

 The previously developed glass formulation for this waste stream, HLW-HPA-07 has a
waste loading of 58 wt%. In order to improve the corrosion characteristics, new glass
formulations were designed and tested at waste loadings ranging from 52 to 55 wt%, and with
and without ZnO as an additive. The glasses showed less than 1 vol% crystals after heat
treatment for 70 hours at 900ºC and no nepheline on CCC heat treatment. The crystalline phases
were phosphates and spinels, as expected. Glass samples subjected to CCC heat treatment
showed higher concentrations of crystals, with about 2 to 7 vol% of phosphate crystals. PCT
releases were well within the WTP contract limits for both quenched and CCC heat treated glass
samples. K-3 refractory corrosion of the glasses formulated in this work ranged from <0.001” to
0.005” as compared 0.009” to 0.033” for glasses formulated previously for the same HLW
stream [30]. Neck loss on the K-3 refractory corrosion test decreased linearly with decreasing
waste loading in the glass, likely due to the high sodium content of the waste. As expected, ZnO
was particularly effective in reducing K-3 refractory corrosion. Similar behavior was observed
with Inconel corrosion, with ZnO again being very effective in reducing corrosion.

 From the new set of glasses that were tested, HLW-HPA-17, at a waste loading of
55 wt%, was selected as the candidate glass composition for melter testing. HLW-HPA-17 meets
all of the WTP processing and product quality requirements and the CCC heat treated glass
sample shows PCT releases well below the WTP limits. SEM examination of the CCC heat
treated sample for crystal content showed about 3.5 vol% phosphate containing Na and Ca and a
small amount of spinel. Processing characteristics of HLW-HPA-17 were assessed using the
VGF with different phosphate sources (FePO4 and Ca5(PO4)3(OH), AlPO4, and Na3PO4). All
three feeds showed moderate processing rates on the VGF test. DM100 melter tests with the
HLW-HPA-17 composition and the above forms of phosphate also showed similar processing
rates of 700 to 800 kg/m2/day of glass. Corrosion of the Inconel melter components (bubbler and
thermowell) were substantially less with the new HLW-HPA-17 formulation as compared to the
corrosion observed with the previous HLW-HPA-07 formulation.

 The HLW-HPA-17 glass formulation developed for the present tests was effective in
reducing corrosion of the Inconel 690 bubbler and thermowell. Dimensional analysis of the
bubbler and thermowell indicate minimal corrosion of these two Inconel 690 components. The
average loss for each of the tests was less than a tenth of an inch for both the bubbler and
thermowell. All measured points on the bubbler lost less than 0.08 inches and only two points of
measurement on the thermowell exceeded a tenth of an inch. The points on the bubbler and
thermowell that had the most dimensional loss were not consistent between any of the tests,
indicating no single point of failure. The limited amount of corrosion observed in the present
tests is in contrast to the corrosion observed with HLW-HPA-07 and the same high phosphate
waste; in that test, the bottom thirteen inches were missing from the thermowell and the
remaining portion was severely corroded up to the top of the glass pool [30]. The bubbler from
the tests with HLW-HPA-07 was corroded all the way up to the top of the glass pool with a
limited amount of corrosion around the orifice. SEM analysis of glass removed from the bubbler
after the present tests showed layered minerals containing zinc, chromium, and nickel, which
increase in concentration with proximity to the bubbler. Collectively, this indicates that the spall
from the bubblers is glass that is enriched in metals originating from the bubblers. Many of the
minerals in this glass layer contain zinc, which limits the corrosion of the Inconel 690 bubbler.
No metallic bismuth or reduced phosphorus was observed, suggesting that neither is directly
responsible for the corrosion. These results are in contrast to secondary phases observed while
processing the same waste and glass formulation HLW-HPA-07 [30], when phosphorus in the
form of spherical nickel phosphide was present in the glass near the metal interface, with some
granulated and angulated nodules in the metal with significant amounts of phosphorus in these
particles.

 Samples of the glass product were collected from each test and analyzed for composition,
crystalline phases, iron oxidation state, and product quality. Glasses produced from feeds
containing phosphates had ten to twenty weight percent of either sodium or calcium phosphate
finely divided crystalline phases, similar to that observed in previous high phosphorus HLW
compositions [30, 31]; however, this crystallization is thought to occur during cooling and
therefore should not affect glass processing properties. Spinels were present at less than two
tenths of a weight percent in all of the samples. The iron oxidation state of melter glasses ranged
from totally oxidized to 1.3 percent divalent iron. Glass from melter tests far exceeded chemical

releases than the crucible glass. All of the melter and crucible HLW-HPA-17 glasses have lower
PCT releases than the corresponding releases for the HLW-HPA-07 glasses developed
previously [30].

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW-HPA-17 glass composition with
sodium, aluminum, and iron phosphates vary from only 0.34 to 0.39 percent of feed solids,

filter in the off-gas system. XRD analysis of the particulate indicates entrainment of feed
minerals (quartz, eskolaite, and hematite) and amorphous feed components in the exhaust as well
as enrichments of the volatile elements, particularly sulfur.
