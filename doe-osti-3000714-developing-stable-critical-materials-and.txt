 Executive Summary
Reversible Solid Oxide Cells (RSOCs), which operate as either Solid Oxide Fuel Cells (SOFCs)
or Solid Oxide Electrolytic Cells (SOECs), hold great promise for clean, high-efficiency energy
conversion and hydrogen production. However, their commercial potential is hindered by stability
issues arising from temperature-induced materials degradation. While substantial progress has
been made in developing durable, reduced-temperature SOFCs - bringing them closer to
commercial deployment, SOECs still exhibit substantially higher degradation rates at both the cell
and stack levels under practical operating conditions.
Key degradation mechanisms in RSOCs include chromium poisoning and delamination of the
oxygen electrode (OE), nickel sintering and migration in the hydrogen electrode (HE), and high
gas flow resistance in the HE substrate. Overcoming these critical challenges is essential to
unlocking the full potential of RSOC technology.
This project aims to advance reduced-temperature (≤700oC), zirconia-based RSOC technology
for efficient and cost-effective power generation and green hydrogen production. The focus is on
addressing the aforementioned degradation issues through materials innovation, advanced
microstructure design, and a deep, physics-based understanding of failure mechanisms. The
ultimate goal is to raise the technology readiness level (TRL) from 3 to 4 by validating newly
developed materials, microstructures and cell designs at both laboratory and benchtop scales.
Key innovations and contributions include:
 • Oxygen electrode (OE): Development of both barrier-layer-free (BLF) and bilayer OE
 structures with enhanced activity for the oxygen reduction (ORR) and evolution (OER)
 reactions.
 • Hydrogen electrode (HE): fabrication of porosity-graded, open-channel HE substrates with
 low flow resistance and integrated catalytic nanoparticles to minimize concentration
 polarization.
 • Validation: Electrochemical performance validated by a national lab partner.
 • Modeling: a Multiphysics model, calibrated with experimental data, was developed to
 guide materials and design optimization.
Research highlights by task:
 1. Barrier-layer free oxygen electrode (SOPO Task 2):
       Optimized a BLF-OE comprising (Bi0.75Y0.25)0.93Ce0.07O1.5±δ (BYC) and La0.8Sr0.2MnO3
 (LSM) and achieved a polarization resistance (RP) of 0.08 Ω·cm² at 650°C. Key
 processing parameters for processing the BLF OE include 10 wt% PMMA as a pore-former
 for porous BYC skeleton, 750°C bonding temperature, and 25-30 wt% LSM loading. Met
 Milestone 2.2 and Succes Criteria specified in PMP.
 2. Bilayer oxygen electrode via ALD (SOPO Task 3):
 Developed a new hybrid fabrication method combining SrO infiltration and atomic layer
 deposition (ALD) of SrCo0.9Ta0.1O3- (SCT) as the capping layer of the bilayer OE,
 reducing processing time from 2 weeks to 1 day without sacrificing performance (<0.1
 Ω·cm² at 700°C). Met Milestone 3.2 and Succes Criteria specified in PMP.

 3. Hydrogen electrode substrate development (SOPO Task 4):
 Employed phase inversion (PI) techniques to fabricate a porosity graded HE substrate with
 dendritic open channels; optimized phase inversion reaction time, binder burnout and
 sintering conditions; developed infiltration process to ensure uniform catalyst distribution
 into the substrate. Met Milestone 4.1 specified in PMP.
 4. Cell testing (SOPO Task 5):
 Demonstrated strong performance of 1.3 cm² cells with BYC-LSM OE and PI-derived HE.
 Achieved ±600 mA/cm² at 650°C in electrolysis/fuel cell mode. Low degradation was
 observed over the 600-hour testing. Met Milestone 5.1. At the large-cell scale, further
 improvements are needed in electrolyte density and electrode microstructure before scale-
 up. Performance fell short of success criteria for Milestone 5.4 specified in PMP.
 5. Multiphysics modeling (SOPO Task 6):
 Developed a Multiphysics model based on synthetic 3D microstructures to analyze OE
 failure mechanisms. Validated against experimental data, the model revealed 13%
 performance improvement with bilayer OE and highlighted changes in transport
 mechanisms from fresh to aged cells. Met Milestone 6.0 specified in PMP.
Impact and Outcomes
The technologies and scientific insights developed in this project present great potential to advance
the commercialization course of RSOCs, particularly for distributed power systems and hydrogen
production. The materials and methods are broadly applicable across all RSOC platforms.
 • Technology Readiness Level: Advanced from TRL 3 (component-level validation) to
 TRL 4 (system validation in lab environment).
 • Education: Supported one PhD student and two part-time postdoctoral researchers.
 • Dissemination: Produced 6 peer-reviewed papers, 13 conference presentations, and one
 social media interview.
 • Project execution: All project milestones were met on schedule over the 4-year period.
Overall, this work lays a solid engineering foundation for future scale-up and commercialization
of advanced, reduced-temperature zirconia-based RSOCs.

 Introduction
Project Objectives
With the recent surge of renewable power generation and availability of low-cost renewable
electricity, a critical issue facing the renewable power industry to achieve a sustainable penetration
into the utility market is to find a suitable energy “buffering” solution for its power variability
mitigation and efficiency/stability assurance. Reversible solid oxide cells (RSOCs) capable of
reversibly producing power and chemicals with high efficiency and low emissions offer an
appealing solution to this purpose. This project aims to advance intermediate-temperature (IT)
RSOCs for efficient, reliable, and low-cost green H2 production by addressing RSOC’s critical and
unsolved foundational material and microstructure issues through innovations. The specific
objectives of this project include:
1) Development of a new barrier-layer-free, high-performance oxygen electrode (OE) for 600-
 650oC ZrO2-based RSOCs
2) Development of a high-performance and stable bilayer OE for 600-700oC ZrO2-based RSOCs
3) Development of graded porosity and open channel hydrogen electrode (HE) substrate
 embedded with active catalysts
4) Multi-level cell testing
5) Multiphysics modeling of failure modes/mechanisms and mitigation solutions
By implementing the developed innovative foundational materials/microstructure into large
cells/stacks, the project is poised to make impact on commercial development of RSOC technology
for distributed power and H2 generations with competitive efficiency, durability and cost.
Background: The importance of reversible solid oxide cells
Reversible solid oxide cells (RSOCs) are a class of high-temperature electrochemical devices
using solid oxides as the functional electrode and electrolyte materials. There are two basic
operating modes for RSOC: power generation from a source of fuels/oxygen and chemical
production from a source of electricity/feedstock. In the literature, the former and the latter are
often referred to as solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) and solid oxide electrolysis cell (SOEC),
respectively. Historically, the development of SOFCs has been a primary focus by academia and
industry alike because high-efficiency and low-emission are deemed the most important matter for
the power generation industry. After decades’ R&D efforts, the high-temperature SOFC power
generation technology has been developed to such a state that it is currently on a path to
commercialization. With the recent advancement of renewable power technologies and the widely
available low-cost renewable electricity [1], efficient transmission of the renewable electricity
across the existing power grid has now become the bottleneck for the renewable industry to expand
to the next level. To this regard, SOEC with the ability to produce H 2 efficiently and cost-
competitively has been viewed as a promising energy “buffering” solution (aka energy storage) to
mitigate the intermittency and demand-supply mismatch issues facing the renewable industry.
Alternate operation of SOFC and SOEC modes within RSOC coupled with a H2-storage/
distribution unit is also a very attractive approach to accelerating renewable penetration.
Efficiency and cost are the two most important factors for the RSOC technology to be competitive
with conventional internal combustion engines (ICEs) for power generation and the benchmark

steam methane reforming (SMR) for blue H2 production, respectively. High-temperature RSOCs
are inherently advantageous in these aspects. For example, unlimited by the Carnot cycle
efficiency upper bound, high-temperature SOFCs can produce power at an efficiency as high as
50+% and combined heat and power at an efficiency ≥85%, which are unmatchable by any ICEs.
Likewise, high-temperature SOECs can produce H2 from H2O at an electrical efficiency as high
as 95+% (HHV) at a working voltage close to thermoneutral potential, much higher than their low-
temperature counterparts, viz. alkaline electrolysis cell (AEC) and proton exchange membrane
electrolysis cell (PEMEC). [2-6] The profoundly high electrical efficiency of SOECs is
fundamentally rooted in the thermodynamics, allowing process heat (e.g. waste heat from an
industrial process and/or Joule heating from the cell) to be a part of the conversion. In addition,
SOECs can operate at high current densities, thus high H2 production rates, without imposing too
high a voltage (e.g. 1.3 V@800oC) due to its lower overpotentials. The high-flux H2 production is
essential to achieve a low H2 cost, but obviously at the expense of conversion efficiency. However,
the efficiency-flux tradeoff in SOECs is much less severe than the efficiency-power tradeoff in
SOFCs, thus placing SOECs in an advantageous position to meet the DOE’s target of producing
H2 at <$2/kg with an electricity cost of >$30/MWh. For comparison, today’s commercial alkaline
electrolysis cells (AECs) are normally operated at a higher voltage (1.7−1.9 V) than the
thermoneutral potential (1.23 V) to achieve a high H2 flux, resulting in low efficiency (<60-70%)
and a H2 cost of $3-5/kg, 2-3 higher than $1.25/kg for large SMR systems with a natural gas price
of $0.3/kg. [7]
Other advantages of SOEC-H2 system include modularity, scalability, low maintenance, and low
acoustic signature, all of which make it well suited for distributed generation. Another distinct
advantage of SOEC from other electrolysis technologies is its ability to electrochemically convert
CO2 and H2O, a common product of combustion, into a mixture of CO and H2 (syngas), an
industrial feedstock for methanol, methane, and synthetic fuels [8-21]. This unique capability
could potentially play an important role in closing the carbon cycle and achieving a sustainable
ecosystem of our planet.
Background: Fundamental aspects of RSOCs
Chemistries of RSOCs
Modern RSOCs can be categorized into two groups based on the type of solid oxide electrolyte
used, viz. oxide-ion conductor and mixed oxide-ion and proton conductor. Each type of RSOC has
a slightly different chemistry that enables the functionality of either power generation in SOFC
mode or H2 production in SOEC mode. Figure 1 summarizes these enabling chemistries. For
oxide-ion conducting RSOCs (denoted as O2--RSOCs), it is straightforward to understand the
oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and fuel oxidation reaction (FOR) at oxygen electrode (OE) and
hydrogen electrode (HE), respectively, under the SOFC mode, and the oxygen evolution reaction
(OER) at OE and water splitting reaction (WSR) at HE, respectively, under the SOEC mode.
For proton involved RSOCs, there have been debates and misunderstandings about the true ionic
conduction mechanisms in the literature. The proton conduction in solid oxides is not intrinsic.
Instead, it relies upon H2O-VO.. (oxygen vacancy) or H2-O2- (lattice oxygen) reaction at
gas/electrolyte surface to produce proton species such as OH- by which H+ hops across the oxygen
lattice (O2-) in the electrolyte. Therefore, it is reasonable to conceive OH- conduction as H+
migration accompanied by a counter O2- migration under an opposing H2 and O2 chemical potential
across the electrolyte. Such a concerted ion-migration leads to low energy barrier and high

conductivity, thus better suited for low-temperature RSOCs. In Figure 1, the enabling surface
reactions of such an O2-/H+ bi-ionic RSOC (denoted as O2-/H+-RSOC) working under SOFC and
SOEC modes are illustrated. With these electrode reactions, the SOFC mode of O2-/H+-RSOC, like
O2--RSOCs, generates power with air (O2) and H2 (with minor H2O) as the feedstock, producing

Materials of RSOCs
Electrolyte (EL): EL is a chemistry-defining component in RSOCs. An ideal solid-oxide
electrolyte should be a pure ionic conductor that is chemically stable in both oxidizing and
reducing atmospheres under which it is exposed, electrochemically stable over the voltage window
it operates and thermally compatible with its adjacent components. Good oxide-ion conductors are
generally found in the family of fluorite (AO2) and perovskite (ABO3) structured oxides, [31] two
examples of which are Y2O3-stabilized ZrO2 (YSZ) and Sr- and Mg-doped LaGaO3 (LSGM).
Proton conducting oxides are predominantly found in oxygen-deficient perovskites ABO3‑δ, e.g.
B-site doped SrCeO3, BaCeO3 and BaZrO3; [32-34] aliovalent doping in these perovskites is
necessary to create oxygen vacancies for mobile proton species. Another advantage of B-site
doping is that it transforms the original orthorhombic structure of ABO3 into primitive cubic
structure; the latter is favorable to both oxide-ion and proton migration. Proton-conducting oxides
have attracted significant interest in recent decades because of their high ionic conductivity at
lower temperatures, which could potentially enable RSOCs to operate at reduced temperatures for
improved reliability and cost.
Poor chemical stability is the major issue of these high-basicity perovskite-based mixed O2-/H+
conductors. High basicity is needed to ensure sufficient H2O uptake (hydration), thus proton
conductivity; on the other hand, however, it also invokes the formation of alkaline earth metal
carbonates and hydroxides, which would degrade the cell performance. [35-36] This chemical
stability issue has become a major challenge to the development of O2-/H+-RSOCs because they
need to operate in an environment containing significant amounts of H2O and CO2. Among the
most studied perovskite oxides, Y-doped BaZrO3 (BZY) is the most stable proton conductor,
showing negligible reactivity with H2O and CO2. However, it is difficult to densify even at very
high temperatures (>1600oC) due to the poor cation diffusion, large grain-boundaries, and loss of
Ba, thus resulting in low ionic conductivity. Adding sintering aid such as ZnO and NiO can
substantially lower the sintering temperature, but often at the expense of efficiency and stability
penalty due to the induced electronic leakage current and impurity. [37] Recently,
BaZr0.4Ce0.4Y0.1Yb0.1O3- (BZCYYb4411) has been shown to exhibit good CO2 tolerance. [38]
However, the cracking issue during thermal cycling and potential electronic conduction
(Ce4+/Ce3+) of the membrane has been concerned for future applications. [39]
Oxygen electrode (OE): An ideal OE should be a mixed ionic and electronic conductor, porous
in microstructure and thermally/chemically stable in oxidizing atmospheres and compatible with
the adjacent component. OEs for O2--RSOCs are generally identical for SOFC and SOEC
operations. The industrial benchmark material after many decades’ development is a composite of
La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−δ (LSCF) with Gd0.2Ce0.8O2- (GDC) for O2--RSOCs, by which mixed oxide-
ion and electron conductivity and thermal expansion match are achieved to maximize ORR activity
and thermal compatibility. A critical element of such a cell design is the GDC barrier layer between
OE and electrolyte, aimed to prevent the chemical reaction between LSCF and ZrO2-based
electrolytes. For O2-/H+-RSOCs, the concept of using composite of a mixed conducting oxide and
electrolyte is still valid, but the mixed conducting phase has been extended to other oxides, e.g.
Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3−δ (BSCF), PrBaCo2O5+δ (PBC), and Pr2NiO4+δ (PN), to achieve triple charge
carrier (O2-/H+/e-) conduction. [40] A general conclusion from these early studies is that LSCF is
not a good OE for O2-/H+-RSOCs as its polarization resistance (RP) increases with 𝑃𝐻2 𝑂 . Instead,
BSCF, PBC and PN all show a decrease in RP with 𝑃𝐻2 𝑂 , promising them to be a good OE for O2-
/H+-RSOCs. [40]

Hydrogen electrode (HE): An ideal HE should also be a mixed ionic and electronic conductor,
porous in microstructure and thermally/chemically stable in reducing atmospheres and compatible
with the adjacent component. HE is compositionally similar for both O2-- and O2-/H+-RSOCs, i.e.
a Ni/electrolyte cermet (e.g. YSZ+Ni and BZY+Ni) designed to achieve high mixed electronic/
ionic conductivity, catalytic activity for hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) and WSR, thermal
compatibility, and mechanical stability. However, with Ni as the major catalyst, coking and sulfur
poisoning need to be carefully managed when hydrocarbon fuels are used. Excessive coarsening
of Ni particles and loss of Ni over extended exposure to high temperature and high steam can lead
to performance degradation.
Problems statement
As a high-temperature device, the operational reliability of RSOCs is critically challenged by the
temperature-related materials degradation. In the past, significant efforts and investments have
been devoted to improving the durability of SOFC in the areas of electrodes, interconnects, and
seals, etc., specifically by lowering the operating temperature. With significant advancements in
materials and improved understanding in degradation mechanisms achieved in the past, SOFC is
now on a path to commercialization. In comparison, long-term degradation data for SOEC single-
cells and stacks under practical operating conditions are still lacking, but a general observation is
that SOEC degrades at a faster rate than SOFC [41-44]. Therefore, more R&D are needed to further
advance the development of SOEC technology.
Cr-poisoning of OE
The Cr-poisoning at OE as a major performance degradation mechanism has been recognized for
a long time in SOFC R&D. There are two OE Cr-poisoning pathways with a common source of
gaseous Cr6+ species derived from the oxidation of high-Cr alloy interconnects: 1) at triple phase
boundaries, where gas-phase Cr6+ species are electrochemically reduced into solid Cr2O3 and 2) at
the OE surface, where Cr6+ species chemically reacts with the segregated SrO to form SrCrO4. [45]
The insulating nature of both Cr2O3 and SrCrO4 blocks ORR, thus leading to performance
degradation. For the SOEC operation of RSOC, the above Cr-poisoning mechanisms are still valid,
but to a more severe degree, since thermodynamics predict that CrO2(OH)2(g) and CrO3(g) are the
two most stable species under a high-H2O and high-O2 environments under which SOECs typically
operate. Therefore, there exists a greater need to address Cr-poisoning issue for SOECs than
SOFCs. To facilitate the understanding of this new insight, Figure 2 illustrates the two Cr-
poisoning mechanisms for O2--RSOCs and O2-/H+-RSOCs operated under SOEC mode. For a
hybrid RSOC system operates alternately between SOFC and SOEC modes, Cr-poisoning
mechanisms will be prevalent in each cycle and could become a major performance degradation
mechanism if not properly addressed.
Delamination of OE
Another critical issue associated with OE is its delamination from the OE/electrolyte interface
during SOEC operation. Driven by a high polarization overpotential across the OE, delamination
is commonly perceived to originate from an oxygen pressure build-up in closed pores near the
OE/electrolyte interface. [46-47] When the built-up oxygen pressure exceeds the bonding strength
of OE to the EL, delamination occurs. The interfacial delamination of OE has been previously
identified as a major performance degradation mechanism for O2--SOECs at high current densities
and low temperatures. For O2-/H+-RSOCs under SOEC mode, the OE delamination issue is largely

unknown because of the lack of long-term testing data available in the literature, thus worth
investigating.

Ni sintering and migration of HE
Nickel sintering and migration are the two major issues associated with HE such as Ni+YSZ.
Under the high H2O condition, these two problems are exacerbated due to accelerated Ni-sintering
and formation of gas-phase Ni(OH)2. [48-51] The former leads to a reduction of the active surface
area of Ni and the latter results in the loss of Ni, both of which would lead to a loss of activity and
conductivity of HE. The performance loss was in some cases also attributed to an insulating glassy
phase originated from the glass seal; the silica impurities in the glassy phase accumulate at the
triple phase boundaries of HE, blocking the reactive sites. [52]
High flow/diffusion resistance of HE substrates
The modern design of RSOCs uses HE as a substrate for thin-film electrolytes and OEs to take the
advantages in thermal and electrical conductivity, thermal expansion coefficient, and catalytic
activity. However, gas transport (or concentration polarization and subsequently activation
polarization) through a thick HE substrate fabricated by conventional tape-casting technique could
become a performance-limiting factor, particularly under the conditions of lower temperatures,
high current density, high fuel/H2O utilization (high H2O concentration). [53-54] The traditional
sponge-like porous anodes made by tape-casting, uniaxial pressing, or gel-casting, have been
proven beneficial to achieve gas-tight electrolytes. However, such a microstructure, given the fact
that HOR/WSR only occurs at the HE/electrolyte interface, is not ideal for gas transport because
of its high tortuosity (usually 4~10), resulting in significantly lowered effective diffusivity of the
active species and thus high concentration (and subsequently activation) polarization and low
limiting current density. [55-57] Therefore, developing HE substrates with open channel
microstructure, adequate porosity gradient and low tortuosity is highly desirable for IT-RSOCs to
achieve high-performance at high current density (H2-flux). [58]
The overarching goal of this project is to advance reduced temperature (≤700oC) ZrO2-based
RSOCs technology for high-efficiency and low-cost power and green H2 production by addressing

the above critical and unsolved problems in OEs and HEs through foundational materials and
microstructure innovations and physics-based comprehensive fundamental understanding, and to
validate the developed new materials/microstructure in small- and large-area cell testing,
ultimately raising the TRL from the current level of 3 to 4.

The best solution to address the critical durability issue of solid oxide cells (SOCs) for high-

 SOPO Task-3: Development of Robust Highly Active Conformal
 Bilayer Oxygen Electrode with Atomic Layer Deposition
Prior to this project, we have identified LSCF-GDC skeleton overcoated with SrCo0.9Ta0.1O3-
(SCT) as a highly active oxygen electrode. [61] The method used to coat SCT cap layer is solution
infiltration, which takes long time to achieve the wanted SCT loading (20 wt%), making it
production unfriendly. To address this processing issue, atomic layer deposition (ALD) was
proposed to shorten the processing time from a few weeks to a few hours. The objective of this
task is to develop ALD supercycle conditions to deposit Sr, Co and Ta in multiple cycles over
LSCF-GDC skeleton.
Development of ALD Supercycle recipes for SCT@LSCF+GDC oxygen electrode (Subtask
3.1)
ALD equipment and precursors
The ALD depositions were carried out using our Savannah S200 deposition system shown in
Figure 3.1. The Savannah deposition system is equipped with high-speed pneumatic pulse valves
to deposit conformal, uniform films on substrates with ultra-high aspect ratio. The system can hold
substrates of different sizes (up to 200 mm for the S200 series). Our system is equipped with four
heated lines capable of handling gas, liquid, or solid precursors. As for SCT deposition, three lines
were connected to Sr, Co and Ta precursor, one line was connected to ozone oxidizer. The
maximum temperature for precursors is 180ºC. The maximum reactor deposition temperature is
270ºC.
In addition to the fundamental functions, our system is equipped with an In-Situ Quartz Crystal
Microbalance (QCM), Ozone Generator, and Low Vapor Pressure Delivery (LVPD) System. The
QCM assembly is integrated in the lid and allows for real-time thickness measurement with sub-
angstrom resolution, see Figure 3.2. It is the ideal instrument for in-situ thickness metrology based
on resonant frequency measurement, and provides rapid process characterization and optimization,
advanced process control and fault detection. QCM was used to indicate the film growth rate in
the early stage. Since ALD-SrO requires ozone as the oxidizer, the ozone generator is installed and
shown in Figure 3.3. The Savannah ozone generator is specifically designed to meet the purity and
flow requirements for ALD processing in small chamber applications. The oxygen flow rate can
be adjusted to determine the concentration of ozone in the delivery system, using flows from 0.1
to 1 LPM. The ozone concentration can be adjusted to a maximum output of ~120 mg/l (~7.5 wt%
ozone). To ensure enough precursor vapor pressure of Sr, the Low Vapor Pressure Delivery
(LVPD) System was installed, see Figure 3.4. It contains a nitrogen distribution line, an LVPD
bubble valve, and a special precursor cylinder with an inlet and outlet. It is designed to provide
improved precursor delivery for low vapor pressure precursors both solid and liquid (P vapor < 0.5
Torr) or providing larger doses for high surface area samples and batch processing. Nitrogen (N2)
carrier gas is used to bring precursor vapor into a reaction chamber.
The precursors used for SrO, Ta2O5 and Co3O4 are Sr(thd)2 (Strem Chemicals),
Pentaethoxytantalum (Sigma-Aldrich), Bis(cyclopentadienyl)cobalt (II) (Sigma-Aldrich), and
pentakis(dimethylamino) tantalum (PDMAT), respectively. Ultra-pure carrier (UPC) grade
oxygen is used as an oxygen source for ozone generation. Ultra-high purity nitrogen was used for

the LVPD system and ALD deposition purge gas. Silicon wafer is used as the substrate for recipe
development.

  SOPO Task-4: Development of Hydrogen Electrode with Porosity
 Graded Open Channel and Active Catalysts
For the modern hydrogen electrode (HE) supported thin-film electrolyte solid oxide cells (SOCs),
the microstructure of HE substrate plays a key role in its mechanical properties and electrochemical
performance. Since the most active area of H2-related reactions is located at the HE/electrolyte
interface, one rationale concept is to engineer a porosity graded HE microstructure where a thin
layer of electrochemical catalysts is at the HE/electrolyte interface and supported by an open
channel thick layer for easy gas diffusion. One existing fabrication technique that can achieve such
porosity-graded microstructure is the phase inversion (PI) process. The objective of this task is to
develop and optimize the PI-process tailored for HE-supported SOC manufacturing.
Phase Inversion process optimization (Subtask 4.1)
A typical PI process is operated on two miscible solvents, in this case, it is NMP (N-methyle-2-
pyrrolidone) and H2O. The solid (HE material such as YSZ+NiO) is first mixed with PESF
(polyethersulfone) binder, PVP (polyvinylpyrrolidone) surfactant and NMP solvent, forming a
slurry. The slurry is then casted to a thick film, followed by soaking into a DI water batch at room
temperature. According to the ternary phase diagram shown in Figure 4.1, the high miscibility of
NMP and H2O triggers fast exchange between the two solvents, leaving behind dendrite-like
binder-rich belts that later can be converted into porosity after high-temperature burnout process.

Figure 4.1. Phase diagram of polymer (PESF)-solvent (NMP) and nonsolvent (H2O).

extensive efforts to develop PI process tailored for SOC applications. The finalized process
conditions and slurry recipes are summarized in Figure 4.2.

Fabrication of cells by PI (Subtask 4.2)
After a porosity-graded HE is fabricated by PI method, it is first bisque-fired at 1000oC to gain
mechanical strength. Then, the HE substrate undergoes an electrolyte dip-coating process. By
controlling the viscosity (0.1 Pas) of the electrolyte slurry, a 10 m thick electrolyte film is
fabricated after three times of the coating cycle. The coated cell is then sintered at 1400oC for 5
hours to achieve high density, followed by infiltrating ~10wt.% catalyst (e.g., CeO2) into the HE
substrate pores. Finally, the developed BLF oxygen electrode is applied using the method

A new concept of PI cells (Subtask 4.2)
During the development of PI process, we accidentally observed that a dense electrolyte layer is
formed naturally during the process. Figure 4.6 shows the microstructure of such an all-electrolyte
bilayer. With such a structure, we can simply infiltrate HE catalysts into the porous structure and
then screen-printing OE on top of the dense electrolyte layer.

Following the same concept, we can also laminate two bilayer structures in face-to-face manner
and create a sandwich structure with the dense electrolyte layer in the middle. Figure 4.7 shows
the fabricated symmetrical structure with two porous electrolyte layers sandwiching one thin dense
electrolyte layer. With this symmetrical structure, OE and HE in the form of nanoparticles can be
incorporated to achieve high performance.

A slurry recipe for the PI process has been developed for HE substrate fabrication. Process
conditions including water-bath soaking time, lamination temperature and time, binder burnout
temperature and time, and final sintering conditions have been optimized. The characterization of
PI-fabrication confirmed a porosity graded dendritic open channel microstructure in the HE
substrate, and thin electrolyte film and uniform distribution of catalyst inside the HE
microstructure. The optimized slurry recipes and process conditions developed from this task are
ready for scale-up manufacturing of high-performance SOCs in the future. Cell testing results will
be presented in the next section – SOPO Task 5. Overall, this task has developed optimized
conditions for the PI process to produce quality porosity-graded open channel HE, meeting the
Milestone 4.1 specified in PMP.
A new concept of PI-cells has also been proposed for IT-SOCs. While the concept is interesting,
the major challenge is loading a sufficient amount of electronic conductors (e.g., Ni) to form a
percolative electron pathway and reduce ohmic resistance. This requires additional engineering
efforts to develop new slurry recipes to yield a more porous HE substrate but still retain sufficient
mechanical strength.

 SOPO Task 5: Multi-level Cell Testing
The objective of this task is to demonstrate the performance of the University of South Carolina
(USC)-developed oxygen electrode and hydrogen substrate in small and large cells. To achieve
this goal, we collaborated with Pacific Northwest National Lab (PNNL) in cell testing and large-
format cell fabrication/testing. This chapter summarizes the results of this effort.
Button cell fabrication (1.3 cm2) (Subtask 5.1)
The button cells with an active surface area of 1.3 cm2 were fabricated in USC’s lab using the
barrier layer free oxygen electrode (OE) – BYC infiltrated with LSM, and PI-derived hydrogen
electrode (HE) – YSZ-NiO substrate and ScSZ-NiO functional layer. A schematic of the overall
fabrication process is illustrated in Figure 5.1. For comparison, USC also fabricated baseline cells
with screen-printed BYC-LSM and LSCF-GDC as OE. The testing temperature is confined to
below 650oC to align with the overarching goal of this project, i.e., to develop intermediate
temperature (IT) SOCs.

Figure 5.1. Fabrication process of SOCs with BYC-LSM OE and YSZ-NiO HE.
The fabricated cells were tested at PNNL in both SOFC and SOEC modes. Figure 5.2 compares
V-I curves of three types of SOCs tested at 650oC with 50%H2-50%H2O as fuel; (1) screen-printed
BYC-LSM (Mix), (2) infiltrated BYC-LSM (Infl), and (3) the baseline LSCF-GDC. It is evident
that BYC-LSM with infiltrated LSM phase exhibits the best performance, displaying a factor of 4
better performance than the baseline LSCF-GDC and demonstrating the potential of BYC-LSM
OE and PI-derived HE for IT-SOCs.

Reversible operation of SOEC and SOFC (Subtask 5.2)
The long-term stability of the cell was also evaluated under alternate SOFC (at 0.8 V) and SOEC
(at 1.3V) modes, cell voltage shown in blue, with 10-hour cycle. Figure 5.3 compares the
performance of screen-printed BYC-LSM OE, shown in Figure 5.3 (a), and infiltrated BYC-LSM
OE, shown in Figure 5.3 (b), at 650oC with air and 50%H2O-H2 as the feedstocks. It is evident by
the current density, shown in red, that the screen-printed OE exhibits lower value of ~300 mA/cm2
and poorer stability than ~600 mA/cm2 of the developed infiltrated OE. Over the 550-hour testing
period, the degradation of the BYC-LSM (Infil) cell is negligible, further demonstrating the
readiness of the developed OE and HE technologies for scale-up.
We have also examined the repeatability of cell performance using 10 sister cells and tested them
under the same condition. The results confirm that the performance shown in Figure 5.3 is
reproducible.

Large cell (13 cm2) testing at PNNL (Subtask 5.4)
The large format cells with an active electrode area of 13 cm2 were fabricated by PNNL. The cells
are Ni-ScSZ supported (500 m) with 8 m 8ScSZ electrolyte and 20 m Ni-ScSZ HE functional
layer. All layers were made by tape casting, laminating, and co-firing the layers at 1350oC. The
oxygen electrode was applied at USC by screen printing BYC skeleton and LSM infiltration. The
BYC loading is 0.2 g and the LSM loading is 0.04g. The finished cells are shown in Figure 5.5.
Other cell configurations such as Ni-ScSZ and Ni-YSZ with YSZ electrolyte were also prepared
and tested.

Figure 5.5. Images of the large format cells and button cells (a) after screen-printing of BYC
skeleton and (b) after LSM infiltration.
To test the cell, electrode contacts are Ag paste/Ag mesh on the hydrogen and the oxygen sides.
The cell was placed into an alumina housing and sealed on the HE side using Schott’s glass rated
for 650oC. The Ni-ScSZ electrode was first reduced using dry hydrogen and then 50% steam was

added to hydrogen. The flow rates for H2 and air were set at 200 and 500 sccm, respectively. Two
batches of large cells were tested under the same condition. Both tests repeatedly show
underperformance over the button cells.
Initial current-voltage characteristics obtained at 650oC in 50%H2-H2O are shown in Figure 5.6
(a), followed by polarization curves (b) and the impedance spectra (c). Among these three cells,
the ScSZ-based cell performed the best with the lowest ASR and overpotential and highest current
density at a given voltage. However, the overall performance of these larger cells is much lower
than button cells, only achieving ~75 mA/cm2 @ 1.3V and ~70 mA/cm2 @ 0.8V vs. ~600 mA/cm2
@ 1.3V and ~500 mA/cm2 @ 0.8V for the button cells. The causes for the low performance could
be attributed to the unoptimized fabrication of half-cell ScSZ/Ni-ScSZ and BLF OE layer.

Figure 5.6. (a) V-I characteristics; (b) overpotential-I characteristics; (c) EIS spectra of all three
types of 13 cm2 cells measured at 650oC in 50% H2-H2O.
Long-term testing results under alternate SOFC (0.70 V) and SOEC (1.30 V) modes for three types
of cells are shown in Figure 5.7. The current densities at 1.30 V and 0.70 V shown in Figure 5.7(a)
are much lower than those shown in Figure 5.3. The corresponding ASR measurement over 700
hours shown in Figure 5.7 (b) is also higher and less stable compared to the button cell testing
results.

Figure 5.7. Current density variations with time under SOEC and SOFC modes at 650oC and

To understand the underlying reasons for the poor performance, we performed SEM/EDX analysis
of the post-tested 13 cm2 cell. The SEM image and the corresponding EDS mapping analysis are
shown in Figure 5.8, where HE, electrolyte, and OE are clearly distinguished. The cell has a 20
µm Ni-ScSZ HE functional layer, an 8 µm 8ScSZ electrolyte, and a 28 µm BYC-LSM oxygen
electrode. Small pinholes are seen in the selected area of 8ScSZ. However, it seems that the HE
functional layer is a little too dense and too thick. The OE exhibits a porous structure, with BYC
forming the primary skeleton, surrounded by LSM nanoparticles. However, the average size of the
BYC particles is around 5 µm, which is larger than normal (~1 m). The large BYC particle size
and the lack of active surface area may explain the low performance observed in the test. A later

project review determined the cause was not following the original procedure to ball mill the BYC
powder before use.

Pressurized testing (Subtask 5.3)
The team tried one pressurized testing, but the experiment failed due to unexpected leakage. Given
the safety concerns, complexity, and difficulty of performing pressurized testing, additional trials
were not conducted.
In this task, we have demonstrated excellent performance of intermediate temperature SOCs (1.3
cm2) with BYC-LSM barrier layer free oxygen electrode and PI-derived HE substrate through a
third-party independent institution. An improved performance was achieved at 650oC with 50%H2-
H2O is +600 mA/cm2 at 1.3 V and -600 mA/cm2 at 0.8 V; there is no noticeable degradation over
600 hours testing. This level of performance meets Milestones 5.1 and Success Criteria specified
in PMP. The benchmark performance obtained demonstrates that BYC-LSM OE and PI-derived
HE are promising materials for IT-SOCs.
At the large-cell level, we have performed two rounds of cell testing but performance fell short of
success criteria for Milestone 5.4 due to HE/electrolyte half-cell and cell assembly related issue.
Therefore, more work is needed in the future to optimize the fabrication techniques to achieve
denser electrolyte, thinner and more porous HE functional layer, finer OE microstructure and more
robust HE current collector attachment. After these engineering issues are resolved, the developed
materials and processes will be ready for scale-up and testing in stacks.

Mitigation strategies (Subtask 6.3)
In this task, we have built a Multiphysics model from a synthetic 3D microstructure, identified the
most sensitive parameters and validated the model with the experimental data. Microstructure
modeling allows us to build the correlation between structure and performance. Overall, the bilayer
OE shows superior performance (13% = (1.42-1.23)/1.42 improvement @ 1A/cm2), see Figure
6.9) against the baseline OE due to the alleviated oxygen stoichiometry and electric potential
variations. As the cracked surface area increases, the polarization overpotential in OE as well as
the polarization impedance increase. A transition in transport pathways from being predominantly
governed by 2PBs in the fresh cell to a co-domination of 3PBs and 2PBs occurs, meaning that both
2PBs and 3PBs play a major role in OE performance. To ensure safe operation without OE

delamination, matching applied current with OER rate is critical. In general, higher OE
performance leads to higher operating current density without OE delamination.
Overall, this task has established a Multiphysics model for predictions of OE performance, failure
mechanisms and modes, and provided mitigation strategy for performance enhancement, meeting
Milestone 6.0 and Success Criteria specified in PMP.

 Summary and Outlook
Overall summary
For Project Management and Planning Task (SOPO Task-1), the Recipient has updated and
submitted the Project Management Plan on September 13, 2021, meeting the requirement of
“submission of PMP 30 days after award”. Throughout the project, there were changes in 1) a
project management policy to cancel the effort for DEI activities; 2) no-cost extension for one year
(from September 9, 2024 to September 9, 2025); 3) adding a new task to explore a new concept of
cell structure. The results and status of the risk management process have been presented during
monthly project reviews and in quarterly progress reports with emphasis placed on the medium-
and high-risk items. The Recipient has developed a Technology Maturation Plan (TMP) that
describes the current technology readiness level (TRL) of the proposed technology/technologies
as TRL=3. The initial TMP was submitted on November 30, 2021. At the end of the project, TRL
was raised to level 4. A final TMP has been submitted on August 30, 2025, within 90 days of
completion of the project (September 9, 2025).
For Development of Stable Barrier Layer Free (BLF) OE (SOFC task-2), the project has developed
recipes and process conditions for fabricating high performance barrier layer free oxygen electrode
- BYC-LSM for intermediate-temperature ZrO2-based solid oxide cells. The final optimal process
conditions that yield the best cell performance at 650oC are summarized as: 1) BYC as skeleton
and LSM as NPs; 2) 740oC for sintering BYC skeleton to ScSZ electrolyte; 3) 10 wt% PMMA
pore former; 4) 25 wt% LSM loading. These engineering data are transferable to any industrial
partners for scaled-up manufacturing. The intermediate-temperature performance of BYC-LSM is
superior to the benchmark LSCF-GDC, exhibiting 5 lower polarization resistance at 650oC. The
low Rp is also confirmed by the high exchange current density and low activation energy measured
by STEHC methodology developed by the project. Long-term stability testing indicates that Rp is
stable over 500 hours operated in alternate SOFC and SOEC modes. The demonstrated
performance meets Milestone 2.2 and Success Criteria, i.e., overpotential ≤0.15V@±1A/cm2
@600oC, as specified in PMP.
A remaining issue to develop the BLF-OE is to demonstrate the performance repeatability on
larger cells (>4cm x 4cm). This will require significant engineering effort to develop scalable
process recipes and conditions.
For Development of Robust Highly Active Conformal Bilayer OE with ALD (SOPO Task-3), the
project has developed individual ALD cycle recipes for SrO, Co3O4 and Ta2O5 depositions. By
assembling these single ALD cycles, the project also developed supercycle recipes for SCT
coating. However, one challenge is the stability and consistency of ALD-Sr, which is caused by
the low vapor pressure of Sr precursor. To solve this problem, the project developed a hybrid
process consisting of SrO coating by solution infiltration, over which ALD-Co and ALD-Ta
coatings were deposited. The optimized process conditions for the hybrid SCT deposition are 2L
Sr(NO3)2 infiltration, followed by 10 cycles of Co and 1 cycle of Ta. The process repeats 10 times
to yield Rp= 0.08 cm2 at 700oC, equivalent to that made by infiltration method, but the overall
processing time has been reduced from 1 week to 1 day. The demonstrated performance meets
Milestone 3.2 and Success Criteria, i.e., overpotential ≤0.15V@±1A/cm2@700oC, as specified
in PMP.

A challenge to implement the developed infiltration/ALD hybrid bilayer OE into large practical
cells is the scale-up of ALD technique. The current lab-scale bench-type ALD reactors are not
suited for practical cells. Design and manufacturing of large ALD reactors are needed to scale up
the technology.
For Development of HE with Porosity Graded Open-Channel and Active Catalysts (SOPO Task-
4), the project has developed a slurry recipe for HE substrate fabrication using PI process. Process
conditions including water-bath soaking time, lamination temperature and time, binder burnout
temperature and time and final sintering conditions have been optimized. The post-fabrication
characterization confirm porosity graded, dendritic open channel microstructure in the HE
substrate, thin electrolyte film and uniform distribution of catalyst inside the HE microstructure.
The accumulated engineering knowledge from this work is ready for scale-up manufacturing. In
addition, a new concept of PI-derived symmetrical cells has also been proposed for IT-SOCs.
While the concept is interesting, its engineering requires efforts to address the high ohmic
resistance challenge due to the low electronic conductivity. Overall, this task has led to optimized
PI process conditions to produce quality porosity-graded open channel HE, meeting the Milestone
4.1 as specified in PMP.
Challenges to PI-derived HE substrates are: 1) limited catalyst loading, which causes high ohmic
resistance and 2) less reactive sites for hydrogen oxidation/evolution reaction (HOR/HER) than
conventional tape-cast HE cells, which causes higher polarization resistance. More work is needed
to engineer new PI slurry recipes that yield microstructure allowing more catalyst loading without
losing reactive sites. Different immiscible solvent pairs should be considered.
For Multi-Cell Testing (SOPO Task-5), the project has demonstrated excellent performance of
intermediate temperature SOCs (1.3 cm2) with BYC-LSM barrier layer free oxygen electrode and
PI-derived HE substrate through a third-party independent testing facility. Improved performance
achieved at 650oC with 50%H2-H2O is +600 mA/cm2 at 1.3 V and -600 mA/cm2 at 0.8 V; there is
no noticeable degradation over 600 hours testing. The benchmark obtained demonstrates that
BYC-LSM OE and PI-derived HE are promising materials for IT-SOCs. At the large-cell level,
more work is needed in the future to optimize the fabrication techniques to achieve denser
electrolyte, thinner and more porous HE functional layer and finer OE microstructure. After these
engineering issues are resolved, the developed materials and processes are ready for scale-up and
testing in stacks. Overall, this task has demonstrated superior cell performance of BLF-OE and PI-
HE on a button cell level, meeting Milestone 5.1 and Success Criteria specified in PMP. Due to
the issues associated with HE/electrolyte half-cell and cell assembly, large-cell performance fell
short of success criteria for Milestone 5.4 after two rounds of cell testing.
A challenge encountered during large-cell testing in this project was the procedure to apply current
collectors (CCs) for the OE and HE. If different CCs are used for OE and HE, and they require
different firing temperatures, the sequence of their applications must be considered. For example,
if HE-CC is Ni-mesh, which requires >900oC firing, it must be processed before the BLF-OE is
applied because the highest firing temperature for the latter is 750oC. Processing BLF-OE with the
HE-CC could present an issue for the screen-printing step.
For Development of Multiphysics Model of OE Failure Mechanisms and Modes (SOPO Task-6),
the team has built a Multiphysics model from a synthetic 3D microstructure, identified the most
sensitive parameters and validated the model with the experimental data. The model-predicted data
agree with experimental data within 5%. Overall, the cell with the bilayer OE exhibits 13% higher

performance than that with the baseline OE due to the reduced variations in oxygen stoichiometry
and electric potential. As the cracked surface area increases, the polarization overpotential in OE
as well as the polarization impedance increase. A transition occurs in transport pathways from
being predominantly governed by 2PBs in the fresh cell to a co-domination of 3PBs and 2PBs if
cracks develop during operation. To ensure safe operation without OE delamination, matching
applied current with OER rate is a critical criterion. In general, higher OE performance allows
higher operating current density without OE delamination. Overall, this task has established a
Multiphysics model for predictions of OE performance, failure mechanisms and modes, and
provided mitigation strategy for performance enhancement, meeting Milestone 6.0 and Success
Criteria specified in PMP.
The project has produced a comprehensive Multiphysics model for OE performance prediction.
However, the model was developed from a synthetic microstructure. The use of real 3D
microstructure of OE will significantly increase the fidelity of the model. To attain the real 3D
microstructure, a synchrotron-based computed tomography technique is needed.
Future Outlook
This project has successfully demonstrated the technical feasibility of BLF-OE, bilayer OE, and
PI-derived porosity-graded HE substrates for intermediate-temperature RSOCs. The developed
manufacturing procedures are both reproducible and scalable, with strong potential for scale-up to
bench- (4 cm by 4 cm) and pilot-scale cells and stacks (10 cm by 10cm).
It is recommended that the developed cell technologies be evaluated by current SOFC developers.
If validated, they may be optimized and considered for integration into production lines as part of
next-generation intermediate-temperature solid oxide cell products.
The new Multiphysics model established through this work also provides a robust foundation for
high-fidelity simulations that integrate real microstructural data from synchrotron-based 3D X-ray
tomography. Advancing this modeling capability will greatly benefit from future collaborations
with national laboratory user facilities.
In addition, the innovative symmetrical cell design enabled by the PI process shows promising
potential to further reduce operating temperatures to ~600°C. This is achieved through the
combination of an ultra-thin electrolyte layer and high-surface-area nanoparticle electrodes. To
fully realize the benefits of this concept, improvements in electrode conductivity will be essential,
representing an exciting direction for future research and development.
