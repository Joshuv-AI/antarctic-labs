 ABSTRACT

 This report summarizes FY26 progress under the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation
(NEAMS) program’s high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) application driver work, covering a wide
range of activities such as code validation and multi-physics code assessment. 1) A detailed SAM model
of the High-Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR) was developed using a unique-block grouping
approach, with an extended parallel thermal network method to capture block-to-block conduction and ra-
diation heat transfer, and applied to steady-state simulations of the HTTR 30 MW and 9 MW cases. 2)
In another activity, SAM’s newly implemented multi-component gas flow model was validated against the
Natural convection Shutdown heat removal Test Facility (NSTF) argon ingress experiment, correctly cap-
turing the density-driven suppression and thermal recovery of natural circulation observed when argon is
introduced into the air-cooled Reactor Cavity Cooling System (RCCS) loop. 3) For the OECD/NEA High
Temperature Test Facility (HTTF) benchmark, we co-led the international benchmark activities as well
as the OECD/NEA final benchmark report to be released at the end of this year. 4) Finally, the coupled
Griffin-SAM modeling capability for pebble-bed HTGRs was advanced by verifying the Griffin neutronics
solution against Serpent Monte Carlo for a realistic non-uniform temperature distribution, resolving several
deficiencies in the SAM-to-Griffin temperature transfer scheme, and enabling distinct fuel kernel, modera-
tor, and coolant temperatures for cross section feedback. These new features were demonstrated in a PBR
load-following transient.

1 Introduction

 High-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs) are advanced nuclear systems that operate at high outlet
temperatures and are typically helium cooled, graphite moderated, and fueled with tristructural isotropic
(TRISO) particles. For their high thermal efficiency, inherent safety, and potential to provide high-temperature
process heat, HTGRs have attracted worldwide interests. In the United States, X-energy is designing the
Xe-100 reactor, which is a pebble-bed type of HTGR. Valar Atomics and Nano Nuclear Energy are both
pursuing a prismatic type of HTGRs.

 The unique design features of HTGRs, such as using coated fuel particles embedded in spherical pebbles
or prismatic graphite blocks and passive decay heat removal systems, offer inherent safety and high thermal
efficiency. At the same time, the combination of high-temperature operation, complex core geometries, and
strongly coupled physical phenomena makes predictive modeling and simulating of HTGRs particularly
challenging. An HTGR analysis inherently requires multiphysics simulations capabilities that couple reactor
physics, fuel performance, thermal hydraulics, and chemical reactions (e.g., graphite oxidation). HTGR
modeling also presents multiscale challenges to capture the behavior of sub-millimeter TRISO particles
embedded in fuel pebbles and fuel rods in graphite matrix, pebble-to-pebble interactions in the core, full-core
neutronics and flow fields, and core-scale heat transfer for decay heat removal.

 Air-ingress accident scenarios in prismatic or pebble-bed HTGRs illustrate one such multiphysics
challenge. In the event of a breach in the pressure boundary of the primary system, the ingress of air can
initiate graphite oxidation, altering both structural integrity and heat transfer properties of the core and
potentially compromising the integrity of fuel, leading to the release of fission products. Accurate simulation
of this scenario requires coupling multicomponent gas transport, molecular diffusion-dominant and buoyancy-
driven flows, chemical kinetics of graphite oxidation, and the transport of fission products. Passive decay heat
removal following a loss-of-forced-coolant (LOFC) event presents a related challenge, requiring accurate
prediction of natural circulation and radiative/conductive heat transfer across the reactor core, vessel, and
surrounding structures under strongly coupled, low-flow conditions.

 The Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program aims to accelerate the
deployment of advanced nuclear energy technologies by developing predictive multiphysics modeling and
simulation capabilities, including reactor physics, thermal hydraulics, fuel performance, and structural
mechanics. Under the HTGR application driver work scope, we aim to support the modeling methodology
development and code assessment, benchmark, validation, and demonstration of NEAMS tools for HTGR
applications. Meanwhile, strong collaboration between NEAMS and U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
Advanced Reactor Technology - Gas-Cooled Reactor (ART-GCR) programs have been fostered to work on
identified problems of interest to both parties, including the ongoing High Temperature Test Facility (HTTF)
benchmark and the High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR) benchmark. The collaboration
creates mutually beneficial feedback between the two DOE programs and a synergy combining the strengths
of both programs, where the modeling and simulation capabilities developed under the NEAMS program are
applied to solve real-world reactor-specific challenges as identified by the ART-GCR program.

2 High-Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR) Benchmark

 As part of the ongoing SAM verification and validation efforts, SAM models of the High-Temperature
Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR) are being developed to evaluate SAM’s performance against available data
in the HTTR benchmark. The dimensions and properties of the HTTR components for developing the SAM
models were referenced from [3–8]. A vertical view of the HTTR is presented in Figure 2.1.

 Figure 2.1: Vertical view of the HTTR core, reproduced from [3, 4, 8].

 In Figure 2.1, coolant enters the reactor from the bottom and flows peripherally upwards through the
space between the permanent reflector blocks and side shield (i.e., plates adjacent to the permanent reflector
and attached to the core restraint mechanisms), and between the side shield and the reactor pressure vessel.
Once the coolant reaches the dome-shaped upper plenum, the flow changes direction and flows downwards
through the coolant channels in the main core structure. The main core structure contains various core block
types at various axial levels, with the most important being the active core, which is located between the third
and eighth block levels from the upper plenum (total of 5 axial blocks in the active core). As the coolant
traverses the main core structure beyond the active core, the flow is increasingly consolidated by the blocks
in each subsequent axial level, ultimately combining into a single flow at the hot plenum before leaving the
reactor core through the main coolant outlet pipe. An auxiliary coolant outlet pipe exists to the side of the
main outlet but is not used in the HTTR benchmark exercise, and is not modeled in this work.

2

 In the HTTR, the fuel comprises UO2 fuel kernels coated by layers of pyrolytic carbon and SiC in a
TRISO format. The TRISO particles are formed into hollow cylindrical fuel compacts, which are assembled
into fuel rods. The fuel rods are then placed in the coolant channels in the fuel blocks, before being stacked
to form the fuel columns, which in turn makes up part of the active core. Aside from the fuel makeup,
other parameters of the HTTR (e.g., material compositions, dimensions, component arrangements, etc.) are
referenced from available data [4, 5]. The referenced information is then used to develop the SAM HTTR
model.

2.1 SAM HTTR Model

 The SAM HTTR model consists of two distinct approaches, i.e., a ring homogenization model which
models each hexagonal ring of the HTTR core as one set of flow channels and heat structures, and a
unique-block grouping model which models each unique hexagonal block in the HTTR core as distinct
flow channels and heat structures. An illustration of the two approaches is presented in Figure 2.2. Model
development started with the ring model due to its simpler approach with less model components in order to
evaluate SAM’s heat structure coupling capabilities. However, the ring model development ceased with the
successful demonstration of energy balance and proper heat transfers in the model and was superseded by
the unique-block model, which offers much greater modeling detail and granularity with lesser assumptions
made to the material properties.

Figure 2.2: Modeling approaches in SAM with Ring Homogenization (left) having less modeled components
and coupling, and Unique-Block grouping having greater components and coupling complexity but enabling
greater model granularity and fidelity. Core diagram reproduced from [7].

 In the unique-block model, the flow channels in each unique-block are reduced to a basic pattern which
describes the representative physics for a single flow channel. This basic pattern is then extended to represent
the entire unique-block using a multiplication factor, i.e., the number of channels in the unique-block. Figure
2.3 illustrates the reduction of the F1 fuel block to the basic pattern used in the SAM model. In the figure, the
solid materials in the fuel rod are represented by an RZ axisymmetric 2-D mesh in the SAM model. This is
followed by a 1-D mesh for the fluid (helium) flow, before another 2-D mesh for the graphite in the hexagonal
block. Since this graphite is shared by all fuel flow channels, the thickness of the block graphite is adjusted to

conserve the total heat capacity. In addition, slight dimensional and specific heat capacity adjustments are
made to the fuel rod materials to conserve the heat capacity while retaining the characteristic lengths of the
components.

Figure 2.3: Reduction of the F1 fuel block to a basic pattern in the unique-block grouping approach. Diagram
of fuel rod and hexagonal block reproduced from [8].

 Similar treatment is performed on the control rod blocks, which has the same hexagonal block dimensions
as the fuel blocks and which consist of only three large helium channels each (two free-flowing and one
plugged) where the control rods and poison spheres can be inserted. The dimensions of the components in
the control rod blocks were chosen to preserve the approximate characteristic lengths of each flow channel.
However, because only two out of three helium channels are free-flowing, the heat capacity of the block
graphite around the plugged channel is redistributed to the two free-flowing channels, raising the specific heat
capacity multiplier to 1.5 times. Table 2.1 summarizes the dimensions and properties of the block components
in the unique-block model. The instrumentation blocks (“I” blocks) are modeled as R3 control rod blocks
while the replaceable reflector blocks (“RR” blocks) are modeled as solid graphite cylinders that preserve the
volume and heat capacity of the hexagonal RR blocks.

between the control rod channels and the fuel channels. For instance, the current SAM HTTR model features
an “open” control rod insertion channel where coolant from the upper plenum can freely flow between the
control rods and the channel walls. In reality, the top of the control rod blocks are connected to the control

tortuous, leading to much lower coolant flow through the control rod channels. This would in turn enhance the
flow through the fuel blocks and result in lower coolant and fuel temperatures in the fuel blocks. Additionally,
the leakage flow between the hexagonal blocks is assumed to be negligible in the SAM HTTR model, and is
neglected as a result. Future updates to the SAM HTTR model would enhance the model details as required.

3 NSTF Argon Ingress Code Validation

 To model the air-ingress scenario in HTGRs, a multi-component flow model is implemented in SAM
[11]. The NSTF heavy-gas ingress experiment is selected as a validation case for the SAM multi-component
gas flow modeling capability. This experiment measured the response of a buoyancy-driven loop when the
inlet gas composition transitions from air to a denser non-air gas. Under this transient, the system response is
governed not only by the imposed heat load and hydraulic resistance, but also by the spatial and temporal
evolution of gas composition, the corresponding changes in mixture density, and the heat up of the gas
inventory within the heated channels. The experiment therefore provides a valuable validation case of the
developed SAM multi-component gas flow model for air-ingress events analysis, especially for the system
level behavior associated with composition-dependent buoyancy effects.

3.1 NSTF Facility Description

 The NSTF is a large-scale integral effects experimental facility constructed at Argonne National
Laboratory to study passive air-cooled Reactor Cavity Cooling System (RCCS) behavior under prototypic
natural circulation conditions [2]. The facility is designed at half-scale of the RCCS of the 350 MWth
MHTGR[12] and preserves the major flow and heat transfer features of an air-cooled cavity cooling system,
including the inlet downcomer, inlet plenum, heated cavity with riser channels, outlet plenum, and chimney
section. The overall facility height is nearly 26 m, providing a substantial elevation difference between the
inlet and outlet regions and allowing development of a measurable natural circulation driving head. The
heated wall is driven by electric heaters to simulate the reactor pressure vessel (RPV) outer surface, and the
facility is instrumented with high resolution sensors that measure mass flow rate, temperature distributions,
and gas composition. These features make the NSTF well suited for validation of system analysis codes for
passive decay heat removal applications.

 During normal operation, ambient air is drawn into the facility through the inlet region and flows
downward through the inlet downcomer. The gas then enters the inlet plenum, where it is distributed to
twelve parallel riser ducts. Heat is supplied to the riser section through the electrically heated wall, increasing
the gas temperature and reducing its density as it flows upward. The heated gas enters the outlet plenum and
discharges through two chimney ducts to the atmosphere. The density difference between the relatively cool
gas in the inlet downcomer and the hot gas in the riser and chimney sections produces the buoyancy head that
drives the air circulation. The NSTF facility is illustrated schematically in Fig. 3.1.

3.2 Heavy-gas Ingress Experiment

 The heavy-gas ingress test was designed to examine the effects of introducing a dense, non-air gas
into the inlet of an air-cooled RCCS flow path. This scenario is motivated by the potential for an external
heavy-gas released from nearby chemical processing facilities that enters the passive air-cooled RCCS of
HTGRs. Ingress of a heavy gas can modify the natural circulation driving head and thereby reduce the
effectiveness of passive heat removal. The NSTF experiment therefore provides valuable data for validating
system codes used to model multi-component gas behavior in natural circulation systems.

 13

 Figure 3.1: NSTF layout and primary segments. Adapted from [1].

 In the test, the facility was first operated at the normal air-cooled condition. After the transient begins,
the inlet gas is switched to argon, which was selected as the heavy gas for the experiment. Although carbon
dioxide is also relevant to industrial ingress scenarios, argon provides a clear density effect while avoiding
some of the additional exposure hazards associated with carbon dioxide. To perform the test, an argon
enclosure with an internal volume of approximately 1,200 ft3 (roughly twice the internal gas flow volume
of the NSTF loop) was installed above the inlet downcomer. The enclosure is 7 × 10 × 15 feet, as shown
in Fig. 3.2. The enclosure operation modes are shown in Fig. 3.3. A 24-inch air duct was placed at the
center of the enclosure to provide the normal inlet path during steady-state air operation, with a mating
seal at the lower end of the duct that isolated the argon inventory from the NSTF loop. The enclosure was
filled with argon before the transient, and, prior to transient initiation, the measured enclosure composition
was greater than 98% argon with an oxygen concentration below approximately 0.4%. At the beginning
of the transient, bypass vent valves were opened and the mating seal at the lower end of the air duct was
broken. This transition established a flow path that allowed argon from the enclosure to be drawn into the
inlet downcomer.

 Oxygen sensors were installed in both the argon enclosure and the inlet plenum to track the local
evolution of gas composition during stagnation and recovery, while loop mass flow rate was measured
as the primary integral indicator of natural circulation performance. Together, the flow rate and oxygen
concentration measurements provide the principal validation targets for the SAM simulation. A companion
transient was also performed with air placed in the enclosure prior to transient initiation. This air transition
test confirmed that the mechanical actions associated with opening the enclosure path and breaking the mating
seal did not by themselves cause the observed loss of natural circulation; the strong response observed in the
argon test is therefore attributed to the gas composition change and associated density effect rather than to the
actuation procedure itself.

3.2.1 SAM Model Description

 The SAM model used for this validation extends the previously developed single-gas NSTF model [13]
to include two gas components, air and argon. The model preserves the major flow paths, heat structures,
control volumes, and boundary conditions of the NSTF. A schematic of the SAM model is shown in Fig. 3.4.

The NSTF flow path is represented using one-dimensional pipe components for the inlet downcomer, heated
riser section, and chimney, together with zero-dimensional PBVolumeBranch components for the inlet and
outlet plenums. The twelve physical riser ducts are combined into a single equivalent riser duct that preserves
the total flow area and the overall hydraulic response of the loop; the total electrical heat input is imposed as
a uniform heat flux on the equivalent riser wall. Similarly, the two chimney ducts above the outlet plenum are
modeled with a single equivalent chimney pipe, with a convective wall boundary condition that accounts
for the inlet-to-outlet temperature variation along the chimney. This simplified representation is appropriate
for predicting the overall natural circulation mass flow rate, although it cannot resolve the individual riser
channel behavior. A schematic of the SAM model is shown in Fig. 3.4.

 Figure 3.4: SAM model for NSTF heavy-gas ingress test.

 The upstream inlet region is modified to represent the heavy-gas ingress configuration. Two parallel flow
paths are added upstream of the downcomer: one representing the central air duct and the other representing
the argon enclosure. The argon enclosure itself is modeled as a zero-dimensional PBVolumeBranch with a
specified initial 98% argon composition. During the steady-state initialization, the argon path is isolated from
the rest of the loop by assigning large form loss coefficients at its inlet and outlet, while the air path remains
open with zero loss coefficients. At the transient initiation time, the form loss coefficients are switched so that

16

the enclosure path becomes open and the air duct path becomes restricted, approximating the experimental
transition in which the inlet flow is diverted from the central air duct to the surrounding argon enclosure.
Because zero-dimensional volume components are used for both the enclosure and the inlet plenum, SAM
predicts an average gas composition in these volumes rather than local sensor composition variations.

 To properly reproduce the ambient effects on the natural circulation loop [14], two virtual components
representing the atmosphere of the NSTF building are included above the inlet of the argon enclosure and air
duct. A vertical air column provides the correct hydrostatic head and ambient temperature of the air outside
the NSTF building, with its inlet boundary condition set to the measured outdoor pressure and temperature.
A horizontal pipe with wall heating is then used to match the measured NSTF inlet air temperature.

 The transient calculation is initialized from a converged steady-state air flow solution. Molecular
diffusion is neglected in the base model, so the gas composition evolution is governed primarily by advection.
Because air is treated as a single component in the two-component formulation, oxygen is not tracked as a
separate species; the calculated oxygen concentration is instead inferred from the computed air mole fraction
by assuming that oxygen accounts for 21% of air, which enables direct comparison with the oxygen sensors
used in the NSTF experiment.

3.2.2 Results and Discussion

 Transient simulation is performed for the full NSTF heavy-gas ingress test. A null transient is first run
to establish the nominal air-cooled steady-state operating conditions. The transient begins at 28.65 hr and is
initialized from this converged steady state by switching the form loss coefficients of the air duct and argon
enclosure paths, as described in the preceding section. The measured response and the corresponding SAM
prediction are compared below in three phases that follow the evolution of the transient: rapid argon entry
into the inlet region, degradation of natural circulation toward near stagnation, and heat up and recovery.

 In the experiment, the inlet plenum oxygen concentration decreased sharply from the normal air value
immediately after the transition, dropping to approximately 11% within about 2 s and to approximately 1.8%
within about 6 s. This rapid decrease indicates that the inlet plenum became argon-rich very quickly after
the enclosure was opened. The SAM calculation reproduces this rapid depletion: the predicted inferred
oxygen concentration in the inlet plenum, shown in Fig. 3.5, drops sharply after the argon path is opened
and then rises to an intermediate value of approximately 10% as a mixture of air and argon flows from the
enclosure into the inlet plenum. Because the enclosure and inlet plenum are modeled as zero-dimensional
well-mixed volumes, SAM predicts an average composition rather than the local measured value. Short
period oscillations are also observed in the predicted mass flow rate immediately after the transition (see
Fig. 3.6). The short initial spike and oscillations in the predicted mass flow rate are considered to be primarily
physical, arising from the abrupt reconfiguration of the inlet flow path when the enclosure path is suddenly
opened with higher buoyancy force. The spike and oscillations dissipate within a few seconds and do not
affect the predicted long-term flow degradation or recovery behavior, so they have no impact on the validation
conclusions.

 After argon entered the downcomer and inlet plenum, the measured loop flow rate decreased rapidly
and approached an essentially stagnant condition within approximately 90 s. The system mass flow rates
predicted by SAM and measured in the experiment are compared in Fig. 3.6. The SAM prediction follows the

simplifications affect local oxygen concentration and motivate higher fidelity modeling of the inlet plenum
and riser region in future work.

late-time increase in oxygen concentration. Overall, the NSTF heavy-gas ingress validation demonstrates

thermal recovery of natural circulation at the system level, which is precisely the mechanism controlling
the air ingress into the HTGR cores. The validation also identifies the expected limitations of the present
system-level model: the perfect mixing assumption in large volumes and the equivalent-riser approximation

4 High Temperature Test Facility (HTTF) Benchmark Status Update

 In the past year, the main effort focused on developing the OECD/NEA final report “Thermal- Hydraulic
Code Validation Benchmark for High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactors (HTGR) Using the High Temper-
ature Test Facility (HTTF) Data”. Argonne served as the lead for Problem 3 (P3) which consisted of two
exercises: SYS.P3Ex1 and SYS.P3.Ex2. Together they evaluated the capability of system codes to predict the
pressurized conduction cooldown (PCC) response of the HTTF. SYS.P3.Ex1 uses common, fixed boundary
conditions and material properties to isolate differences arising from code correlations, numerical approaches,
and modeling assumptions, while SYS.P3.Ex2 uses the actual operating conditions to assess code predictions
against experimental data. The exercises provide an important assessment of system code capabilities for
predicting both steady state heat transfer and transient natural circulation behavior.

 SYS.P3.Ex1 consisted of four sub-exercises covering full-power and partial-core heating under both
steady-state and transient PCC conditions. For the full-power cases (Ex1A and Ex1B), all ten HTTF heater
banks were assumed to operate at 2.2 MW before the transient, although the facility was not operated at this
power level. The participating codes generally predicted very similar core flow distributions. SAM, RELAP5
(INL), GAMMA+, and SPECTRA predicted similar coolant temperature distributions, with relatively uniform
temperatures in the heated core and lower temperatures in the reflector regions. Differences were more
pronounced in the predicted ceramic temperatures, particularly in the inner reflector, where differences
approaching 200 K were observed. In contrast, predictions of reactor vessel temperature and overall RCCS
heat removal were generally consistent among the codes. RCCS water heat removal predictions ranged from
12.2 to 16.3 kW, less than 0.6% of the 2.2 MW input power.

 The partial power cases (Ex1C and Ex1D) were based on the heating configuration of Test PG-27, with
two of ten heater banks energized and a total steady state power of 86 kW. Despite differences in how the
nonuniform heating and complex core geometry were represented, the system codes predicted reasonably
similar flow distributions. For the PCC transient (Ex1D), all codes captured the rapid reduction in flow
following the circulator trip and the subsequent establishment of natural circulation. The flow decreased by
more than three orders of magnitude from the initial forced flow value. SAM and RELAP5 (INL) showed
particularly close agreement throughout the transient. The codes also generally predicted similar temperature
evolution and peak temperatures, although larger discrepancies were observed in some inner-core and reflector
regions. CATHARE, for example, predicted peak temperatures approximately 150–200 K higher than several
other models in selected locations.

 SYS.P3.Ex2 extended the assessment by simulating the actual HTTF PG-27 experiment using measured
operating conditions and available test data. Unlike the fixed-boundary-condition exercise, participants were
allowed to use engineering judgment to estimate uncertain inputs, such as helium flow rate (not measured
in HTTF), and to adjust effective material properties, such as the thermal conductivity of the ceramic core,
to represent the complex heat conduction behavior of the core. This approach provides a more realistic
assessment of code predictive capability and allows direct comparison with experimental measurements.

 The comparison with PG-27 data showed reasonable agreement between several system codes with the
experiment, although notable differences remain. Steady state and transient temperatures were compared
at multiple locations, including the inner, middle, and outer core, reflectors, core barrel, and reactor vessel.
Differences among the predictions can be attributed in part to the different representations of the complex
HTTF geometry, heat transfer closure models, nodalization schemes, and effective thermal properties used by

 21

the individual codes. The benchmark therefore demonstrates both the overall capability of modern system
codes to reproduce the principal PCC behavior and the importance of continued model development and
validation against experimental data.

 Overall, SYS.P3.Ex1 and Ex2 demonstrate that the participating system codes consistently capture the
dominant PCC phenomena, including the loss of forced circulation, establishment of natural circulation,
temperature evolution. At the same time, the code-to-data comparisons identify areas where differences in
heat transfer correlations, geometric representation, and effective material properties can produce significant
variations in local temperature predictions. These results provide a basis for improving model fidelity and for
identifying priorities for future experimental and simulation work.

5 PB-HTGR Griffin/SAM Multiphysics Coupling

 This section describes the FY26 progress on the coupled Griffin-SAM modeling of a pebble-bed high-
temperature gas-cooled reactor (PB-HTGR), continuing the effort to establish the coupling scheme with
online cross section generation in FY25 [15]. First, the Griffin neutronics solution is verified against a Serpent
Monte Carlo reference for a realistic non-uniform temperature distribution taken from a converged coupled
calculation, so that the accuracy of the neutronics solver itself is established before it is exercised in a coupled
setting. Second, several deficiencies in the temperature update path from SAM to Griffin are identified
and resolved, covering the transfer scheme itself, the assignment of distinct temperatures to the fuel kernel,
moderator, and coolant, and the SAM capability for computing the TRISO fuel kernel temperature from an
external power source. Finally, the improved coupled model is applied to load-following transients, both to
demonstrate Griffin with online cross section generation and to quantify the impact of the improvements on
the predicted transient behavior.

5.1 Verification of Griffin for Non-uniform Temperature Case against Serpent

 To ensure the validity of a coupled simulation, the Griffin solution was verified against a Serpent
reference for a snapshot configuration obtained from a coupled simulation. The procedure is described below.

 1. The temperature distribution was edited from the coupled simulation of the gPBR problem solved in
 FY25 [15] and averaged over the 60 regions of the pebble-bed core shown in Figure 5.1.
 2. The Serpent reference model was built using these 60 temperature values for the 60 regions by
 separately defining a material composition for each region. For simplicity, the lower cone region was
 removed.
 3. A consistent Griffin model was built using the same region-wise temperatures and run stand-alone,
 without SAM involved.
 4. The Griffin eigenvalue and power distribution were then compared with the Serpent results.

 Table 5.1 and Figure 5.2 show the Griffin eigenvalue and power distribution errors against the Serpent
results. For comparison, the results for the uniform temperature case are also included. Both the eigenvalue
and the power distribution errors fall within good accuracy: less than 100 pcm for the eigenvalue and ±5%
for the power. Compared to the uniform temperature case, there is no noticeable bias introduced by the
non-uniform temperature condition. For the realistic temperature distribution shown in Figure 5.1, where
the upper regions of the core are cooler and the lower part is about 500 K hotter, the power is top-skewed
because of the weaker resonance absorption in the cooler condition. This result confirms that Griffin can
produce an accurate solution for a given non-uniform condition met during a coupled simulation.

5.2.3 SAM updates for TRISO fuel kernel temperature calculation with external power source

 This subsection describes the SAM code updates for computing the fuel kernel temperature based on
an external power source. SAM has two models: the TINTE model and a zero-dimensional (0D) model.
The TINTE model solves the transient heat-conduction equation within the TRISO particle, accounting for
the radial temperature distribution across the kernel and coating layers. In contrast, the 0D model treats the
fuel kernel as a lumped thermal mass with a uniform temperature, providing a computationally efficient
approximation of the kernel temperature. Initially, both models only supported the case with a user-specified

 29

 Figure 5.9: Impact of this FY improvements on steady-state power distribution results.

power source given in an input. With an external power source transferred from Griffin, the TRISO fuel
kernel temperature calculation was silently ignored.

 In this work, SAM was updated to support an external power source for the 0D model only. The
extension to the TINTE model is left for future work because of a variable type inconsistency issue of the
MOOSE framework. The TINTE model deals with a nodal LAGRANGE variable, whereas the external power
source is a CONSTANT MONOMIAL elemental variable. MOOSE forbids coupling elemental variables into
nodal objects.

 There were two fixes:

 1. An external power source is delivered as a fuel zone power density, deposited only in the fuel block,
 for the solid temperature calculation because heat is not produced in the graphite layer of a pebble.
 However, the 0D model class in SAM assumed the power density input of the class to be the power per
 pebble volume. This was fixed in the class so as to regard the power density input as the power per fuel
 zone volume.

 2. The kernel power density used in the 0D model is calculated by dividing the total pebble power by the
 fuel kernel volume. However, the total TRISO volume, including the volumes of all layers, was used
 for the division. This underestimated the fuel kernel power by the volume ratio of the fuel kernel to the

30

 TRISO particle, and the fuel kernel temperature was thus systematically under-predicted. This was
 fixed as well.

6 Conclusions and Future Work

 This report documented the FY26 progress under the NEAMS HTGR application driver work scope,
advancing SAM and Griffin-SAM modeling capabilities and validating them against HTTR, NSTF, and
HTTF data in continued collaboration with the ART-GCR program.

 A detailed SAM model of the HTTR was developed using a unique-block grouping approach, in which
each unique hexagonal core block is represented by its own flow channels and heat structures, coupled
through an extended parallel thermal network formulation that accounts for both conduction and radiation
heat transfer between blocks. Steady-state simulations of the HTTR 30 MW and 9 MW benchmark cases
produced coolant and fuel temperature distributions, as well as flow proportioning among fuel and control
rod block groups, consistent with expected reactor behavior, and identified modeling simplifications (e.g.,
open control rod channels, negligible block-to-block leakage) to be refined in future work.

 SAM’s multi-component gas flow model was validated against the NSTF heavy-gas ingress experiment,
in which argon is introduced into the normally air-cooled RCCS natural circulation loop. The SAM prediction
reproduced the near-stagnation of flow, and subsequent buoyancy-driven recovery observed experimentally,
confirming that the model captures the density-driven suppression and thermal recovery mechanisms that
govern air-ingress and RCCS heavy-gas scenarios in HTGRs.

 As for the OECD/NEA High Temperature Test Facility (HTTF) benchmark, the international benchmark
enters into the final stage as all participating organizations submitted their results. A final OECD/NEA
benchmark report is being composed and will be released at the end of this year.

 For the coupled Griffin-SAM modeling of pebble-bed HTGRs, the Griffin neutronics solution was first
verified against Serpent Monte Carlo for a realistic non-uniform core temperature distribution, confirming the
accuracy of the neutronics solver independent of the coupling. Several deficiencies in the SAM-to-Griffin
temperature update path were then identified and corrected, including an indexing artifact in the channel-
wise temperature transfer, use of the volume-averaged (rather than surface) pebble temperature, and the
assignment of distinct temperatures to the fuel kernel, moderator, and coolant regions rather than a single
lumped value. SAM was also extended to compute the TRISO fuel kernel temperature from an externally
supplied (Griffin) power source. Applying these improvements to FY23 load-following transients showed
that using the fuel kernel temperature, rather than the TRISO-smeared pebble temperature, for cross section
feedback meaningfully weakens the effective Doppler response, yielding 12–21% higher plateau power
and correspondingly larger predicted coolant temperature rise and reflector heating during load-following
operation.

 Future work will extend the HTTR SAM model to simulate the LOFC transient benchmark cases, build-
ing on the steady-state results presented in this report, and will support a broader code-to-code comparison
with other system analysis tools such as RELPA5. For the pebble-bed HTGR work, future efforts will
investigate how fuel depletion affects the local power distribution within the core, an important consideration
given the sensitivity of the predicted power and temperature response to the fuel temperature coupling scheme
demonstrated in this report.
