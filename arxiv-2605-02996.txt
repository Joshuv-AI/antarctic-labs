Wave interference as the origin of the cyclic magnetorotational dynamo in accretion disks: insights from weakly nonlinear theory and local shearing box simulations Preprint: APS/123-QED
Uddipan Banik, Amitava Bhattacharjee, James M. Stone

Abstract
Long-period cyclic reversals of the large-scale magnetic field are a prominent feature of the dynamo associated with the magnetorotational instability (MRI) in accretion disks, but their physical origin remains unclear. We develop a quasilinear theory (QLT) of the MRI dynamo where the electromotive force (emf) is computed from the linear eigenfunctions under the WKB approximation. The emf 𝜺 \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} depends on the mean field 𝐁 {\bf B} more generally than standard mean-field closures allow. In the unstratified case, the leading order contribution to the large-scale dynamo is the shear-current effect: the emf is given in terms of the current 𝐉 {\bf J} by 𝜺 = 𝜷 ⋅ 𝐉 \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J} , with a tensor 𝜷 ⁡ ( 𝐁 , t ) \boldsymbol{\beta}({\bf B},t) that oscillates with time t t and whose off-diagonal components generate the mean field. The oscillations arise from beats between the two branches of magnetorotational eigenfrequencies. Since the beat frequency varies only weakly with wavenumber, the beats remain coherent and drive the long-period butterfly cycle seen in local shearing box simulations. We predict a dominant cycle period ∼ 30 ​ 1 + a 2 ​ t orb \sim 30\sqrt{1+a^{2}}t_{\rm orb} , with a a the vertical-to-radial aspect ratio and t orb t_{\rm orb} the orbital period, and an amplitude scaling ∼ a 2 \sim a^{2} before saturation at a ≳ 5 a\gtrsim 5 . Both trends agree with zero-net-flux unstratified shearing box simulations with Athena++ . A carrier-envelope analysis of the simulation spectra shows that the same interference mechanism extends beyond strict QLT, through higher-order linear combinations of the eigenfrequencies, with observed cycles arising from pairwise beats within this spectral network. These results identify coherent interference between nearly degenerate eigenfrequencies as a key mechanism behind large-scale cyclic dynamos, with implications for magnetic variability in protoplanetary disks, X-ray binaries, and AGNs.



I
Introduction

Cyclic large-scale magnetic fields are a striking manifestation of self-organization in rotating conducting fluids and plasmas. They are observed or inferred across a wide range of systems. The 11-year sunspot cycle
(
Hathaway 2015
)
traces a large-scale oscillating field in the solar interior. Geomagnetic reversals are recorded in the paleomagnetic record
(
Amit et al. 2010
)
, while magnetic variability and reversals have been inferred for giant planets
(
Hathaway and Dessler 1986
)
. Quasi-periodic modulations of X-ray binary and AGN light curves
(
Alston et al. 2016
)
are often interpreted in terms of cyclic magnetic activity in accretion disks
(
O’Neill et al. 2011
;
Zhou and Lai 2024
)
. A common ingredient in many of these systems is differential rotation. In weakly magnetized accretion flows, the magnetorotational instability (MRI)
(
Balbus and Hawley 1991
;
Balbus 1995
)
is the dominant mechanism for amplifying seed fields to dynamically significant strengths. MRI-driven turbulence rapidly develops in both local and global MHD simulations
(
Hawley et al. 1995
;
Hawley et al. 1996
;
Stone et al. 1996
)
. Once the linear instability saturates, a large-scale dynamo generically emerges. In particular, the mean toroidal field undergoes quasi-periodic polarity reversals on timescales of order
10
–
100
orbital periods, producing the well-known butterfly diagram
(
Brandenburg et al. 1995
;
Stone et al. 1996
;
Gressel 2010
;
Dhang et al. 2024
)
. Similar cyclic behavior appears not only in stratified and/or net-flux simulations
(
Stone et al. 1996
;
Miller and Stone 2000
;
Simon et al. 2011
;
Lesur et al. 2013
;
Bai and Stone 2013
)
but also in unstratified zero-net-flux simulations
(
Hawley et al. 1996
;
Fromang and Papaloizou 2007
;
Fromang et al. 2007
;
Guan et al. 2009
;
Simon and Hawley 2009
;
Shi et al. 2016
)
, showing that shear alone can drive the cycle. The strength of the dynamo depends sensitively on box geometry: tall boxes exhibit pronounced cycles and converged turbulent (Maxwell and Reynolds) stresses, whereas equal-aspect-ratio boxes show weak or no cycles and stresses that decrease with increasing resolution
(
Fromang and Papaloizou 2007
;
Fromang et al. 2007
;
Shi et al. 2016
)
. Despite this extensive numerical evidence, the physical origin of the long cycle period and its aspect-ratio dependence remains unclear. Prior work has shown that magnetized shear-flow dynamos can be subcritical, sustained by finite-amplitude perturbations and non-modal growth much like hydrodynamic shear-flow turbulence
(
Rincon et al. 2007
;
Rincon et al. 2008
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2014a
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2014b
)
. The dynamo can also sustain itself through a non-linear feedback loop consisting of shear-driven poloidal-to-toroidal conversion followed by nonlinear regeneration of poloidal field through non-axisymmetric MRI perturbations
(
Rincon et al. 2007
;
Riols et al. 2013
)
. However, a quantitative theory for the period of these feedback loops, and of MRI dynamo cycles more generally, is still lacking.

The standard framework for large-scale dynamos is mean-field theory
(
Brandenburg and Subramanian 2005
;
Rädler 2014
;
Brandenburg 2018
)
, which expands the electromotive force (emf) in the mean magnetic field
{\bf B}
and its derivatives, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J}+\cdots
, where
{\bf J}=(c/4\pi)(\nabla\times{\bf B})
. In the classic
\alpha
–
\Omega
dynamo
(
Parker 1955
;
Steenbeck et al. 1966
)
,
\alpha
is proportional to the mean kinetic helicity. While this framework has been phenomenologically successful, it faces two major difficulties in a fully-developed MHD turbulence. First, the kinematic
\alpha
is quenched by the current helicity generated as the large-scale field grows
(
Pouquet et al. 1976
;
Gruzinov and Diamond 1994
;
Bhattacharjee and Yuan 1995
;
Blackman and Field 1999
;
Rädler and Rheinhardt 2007
)
, and at high magnetic Reynolds numbers relevant to astrophysical disks the
\alpha
undergoes catastrophic quenching
(
Vainshtein and Cattaneo 1992
)
. Second, the expansion is not a controlled closure: its convergence is not guaranteed, and the tensors
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are often inferred from simulations rather than derived from first principles. In non-helical flows, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is weak, the dominant contribution is instead the shear-current effect
\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J}
, whose off-diagonal component can amplify the mean field
(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2015a
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2015b
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2015c
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2015d
;
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
)
. Rotating systems can also admit an additional term
\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}\cdot(\nabla\times{\bf U})
, with
\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}
proportional to the cross-helicity
(
Yoshizawa 1990
;
Tripathi et al. 2026
)
. Even with these extensions, however, mean-field theory, in its standard form, has not helped to explain the long, geometry-dependent cycles seen in unstratified shearing boxes, where the dynamo operates primarily through an off-diagonal component of
\boldsymbol{\beta}

(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
;
Shi et al. 2016
)
.

These limitations motivate a more systematic route: quasilinear theory (QLT), in which the emf is computed directly from linear eigenmodes rather than postulated through a low-order closure. QLT is most appropriate when fluctuations remain weak relative to the mean field. It has been used in laboratory plasmas to determine the self-consistent dynamo in reversed-field pinches unstable to tearing modes
(
Bhattacharjee and Hameiri 1986
;
Strauss 1986
)
. The onset of the dynamo cycles before the MRI turbulence reaches a statistical steady state
(
Bhat et al. 2016
)
indicates that QLT should be able to capture at least the initial dynamo phase. Earlier work on accretion disks established important aspects of the quasilinear program.
Lesur and Ogilvie 2008
used a perturbative expansion for non-axisymmetric shearing waves and identified a non-linear feedback loop for dynamo cycles
(
Rincon et al. 2007
, c.f.)
, but did not derive the cycle period.
Ebrahimi and Blackman 2016
computed the quasilinear emf from linear MRI eigenfunctions, but did not evolve the time-dependent dynamo equations. Thus, a dynamical explanation for the butterfly period and its geometry dependence remains missing. A complementary line of work has approached the zero-net-flux MRI dynamo from a nonlinear dynamical systems perspective, identifying subcritical periodic orbits (identified as fixed points of the stroboscopic/Poincaré map constructed from the simulation data) and their bifurcations (with varying magnetic Reynolds number) as the organizing structures of MRI dynamo action
(
Riols et al. 2013
)
. Our goal here is different: rather than characterizing the phase-space skeleton of the fully nonlinear dynamo, we seek a first-principles physical explanation for the cycle period from a weakly nonlinear perspective. We show that toroidal cycles may arise from the interference of axisymmetric oscillatory modes, provided turbulence mediated by non-axisymmetric fluctuations supplies weak poloidal fields.

In this paper we focus on the weakly nonlinear theory for axisymmetric MRI modes. We compute the full quasilinear emf from the linear MRI eigenfunctions in the WKB approximation, without assuming a mean-field closure, and then integrate the resulting dynamo equations for the large-scale toroidal field. The central result is that the MRI dispersion relation contains two branches of shear Alfvén waves whose frequency difference
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}

is weakly dependent on wavenumber for
k^{\prime}\gtrsim k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, where
k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
is the marginally unstable wavenumber. Since the emf is an integral over contributions from all small-scale modes, the key factor that determines the level of coherence is the degree of phase-mixing across the spectrum. Because
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
varies weakly over the dominant range of
{\bf k}^{\prime}
, these contributions add nearly coherently instead of phase-mixing away. The large-scale induction therefore develops a slow envelope modulation whose characteristic period is set by the narrow spread of
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
across the contributing modes. In this sense, the cyclic MRI dynamo is a large-scale manifestation of wave-interference.

This mechanism leads to concrete, testable predictions. We show analytically in Section
III.4
that the dominant cycle period scales as

\displaystyle T_{\rm cycle}\sim 30\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\;t_{\rm orb},

(1)

where
a=L_{z}/L_{R}
is the vertical-to-radial aspect ratio of the shearing box and
t_{\rm orb}=2\pi/\Omega
is the orbital period. We also find that the cycle amplitude scales as
\sim a^{2}
before saturating at
a\gtrsim 5
. Both trends arise directly from the anisotropic character of the MRI eigenmodes. We test these predictions against unstratified, zero-net-flux
Athena++
shearing box simulations spanning
a=1
–
8
and extending to
500
orbital periods. The QLT-predicted dominant periods and their aspect-ratio dependence agree well with the simulations. We further show, through a carrier-envelope analysis of the simulation power spectra in Section
IV.4
, that the interference mechanism is not merely an artifact of the quasilinear approximation. The simulations contain higher-order linear combinations of the MRI eigenfrequencies, and the observed cycle periods arise from pairwise beats within this broader spectral network. The basic organizing principle of the MRI dynamo cycles is therefore wave interference.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
reviews the linear MRI and the eigenmode structure underlying the dynamo. Section
III
develops the quasilinear theory, derives the emf, integrates the dynamo equations numerically, and obtains analytic estimates for the cycle period. Section
IV
presents the
Athena++
simulations, and compares them with the quasilinear predictions. Section
V
discusses the physical interpretation, the limitations of QLT, and the astrophysical implications of the results.

\dfrac{2i\,\Omega\,{\bf k}\cdot{\bf B}_{0}}{\omega^{2}-{\left({\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\times\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\left(\omega\right),

(7)

where
\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\left(\omega\right)=\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\sum_{i}c_{i{\bf k}}\,\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i{\bf k}}\right)
is the Fourier transform of the radial velocity perturbation, with amplitude
\delta v_{{\bf k}R}
. The dispersion relation is quadratic in
\omega^{2}_{\bf k}
, so it has roots
\pm\omega_{1{\bf k}}
and
\pm\omega_{2{\bf k}}
. The
+
and
-
signs correspond to the right and left circular polarizations of the wave, i.e., positive and negative helicities. Taking the inverse Fourier transform of equation (
7
) yields the following solution for the linear perturbations in the time domain:

\displaystyle v_{1{\bf k}R}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\delta v_{{\bf k}R}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i=1,2}\Big[\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}+c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\cos{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}-i\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}-c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\sin{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}\Big],

\displaystyle v_{1{\bf k}\phi}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\sum_{i=1,2}\frac{\omega_{i{\bf k}}}{\omega^{2}_{i{\bf k}}-{\left({\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega}+\frac{{\left({\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{i{\bf k}}}\frac{{\rm d}\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)

\displaystyle\times\Big[\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}+c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\sin{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}+i\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}-c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\cos{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}\Big],

\displaystyle B_{1{\bf k}R}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=i\,{\bf k}\cdot{\bf B}_{0}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\sum_{i=1,2}\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle\times\Big[\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}+c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\sin{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}+i\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}-c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\cos{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}\Big],

\displaystyle B_{1{\bf k}\phi}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=2i\,\Omega\,{\bf k}\cdot{\bf B}_{0}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}R}\sum_{i=1,2}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{i{\bf k}}-{\left({\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\Big[\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}+c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\cos{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}-i\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}-c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)\sin{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}}t\right)}\Big].

(8)

where we ensure the reality condition,
c^{+}_{i-{\bf k}}=c^{-\ast}_{i{\bf k}}
,
c^{-}_{i-{\bf k}}=c^{+\ast}_{i{\bf k}}
, and
\omega_{i-{\bf k}}=\omega_{i{\bf k}}
(the imaginary
i
is not to be confused with the subscript
i
for the eigenmode index). There are three important points to note here. First,
\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}+c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)
denotes the total strength of fluctuations at each
{\bf k}
and
\left(c_{i{\bf k}}^{+}-c_{i{\bf k}}^{-}\right)
the net helicity of those fluctuations, i.e., the difference between the amplitudes of right and left circularly polarized waves. Second, the azimuthal perturbations are proportional to
\Omega
and the shear,
{\rm d}\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R
, which represents the shear-driven tangential stretching of the field lines and the associated development of azimuthal velocity and magnetic perturbations. Third,
v_{1{\bf k}\phi}
and
B_{1{\bf k}R}
get stronger near marginal stability
\left(\omega_{2{\bf k}}\to 0\right)
, implying that the strength of the emf and the large-scale field would be dominated by modes closer to marginal stability.

III
Weakly nonlinear theory

So far we have discussed the spatiotemporal behavior of linear perturbations on a fixed background shear flow with a large-scale magnetic field. The large-scale field itself, however, evolves through the back reaction of the fluctuations, on timescales longer than the linear wave period: of order the orbital time
2\pi/\Omega
on large scales and the Alfvén crossing time
2\pi/{\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}
on small scales. The large-scale field is
\left<{\bf B}\right>=\left<{\bf B}_{2}\right>={\bf B}_{0}
, where the brackets denote the limit
k_{i}\to k_{i,\rm min}=2\pi/L_{i}
. Here
i
labels the radial or vertical component of
{\bf k}
, and
L_{i}
is the corresponding box size. In this limit,
\left<{\bf B}_{1}\right>=0
, since
{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}}\propto{\bf k}\cdot{\bf B}_{0}=0
by
\nabla\cdot{\bf B}_{0}=0
.

More generally, we write
{\bf B}=\left<{\bf B}\right>+\delta{\bf B}
and
{\bf v}=\left<{\bf v}\right>+\delta{\bf v}
, where
\delta{\bf B}
and
\delta{\bf v}
are nonlinear fluctuations and
\left<{\bf B}\right>
and
\left<{\bf v}\right>
are the mean fields. In the present case,
\left<{\bf v}\right>=R\Omega\hat{\phi}
. Substituting into the induction equation yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left<{\bf B}\right>}{\partial t}=\nabla\times\left(\left<{\bf v}\right>\times\left<{\bf B}\right>\right)+\nabla\times\left<\delta{\bf v}\times\delta{\bf B}\right>,

(9)

where we used
\left<\delta{\bf B}\right>=\left<\delta{\bf v}\right>=0
. The first term is the mean-flow contribution, i.e. the
\Omega
effect that stretches radial field into toroidal field. The second is the dynamo term, driven by the inductive electric field from correlated fluctuations. This field,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\left<\delta{\bf v}\times\delta{\bf B}\right>
, is the electromotive force (emf). In fully nonlinear MHD, evaluating the emf is difficult. Mean-field theory aims to express it in terms of mean quantities, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\left<{\bf B}\right>+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\left<{\bf J}\right>+\cdots
, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
depend on properties of the fluctuations such as helicity. The truncation of this series is generally uncertain and often imposed through an ad hoc closure. Here, however, we show that both the full emf and the dynamo coefficients can be evaluated systematically in perturbation theory.

We now compute the emf at leading order using second-order perturbation theory, or quasilinear theory. In this approximation, the fluctuations are taken to be linear, and the large-scale field is generated by their correlations. The second-order magnetic perturbation then obeys

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf B}_{2}}{\partial t}=\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{0}\times{\bf B}_{2}\right)+\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{2}\times{\bf B}_{0}\right)+\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{1}\times{\bf B}_{1}\right),

(10)

where the subscripts
0
,
1
, and
2
denote the mean field, the linear perturbation, and the second-order perturbation. As noted above, the large-scale field
\left<{\bf B}_{2}\right>
is just
{\bf B}_{0}
, and the background flow is
{\bf v}_{0}=R\Omega(R)\hat{\phi}
. The second-order velocity perturbation
{\bf v}_{2}
would follow from the quasilinear force equation, but
\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{2}\times{\bf B}_{0}\right)
is
{\cal O}\!\left((kR)^{-1}\right)
and can be neglected in the WKB limit. The term
\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{0}\times{\bf B}_{2}\right)
reduces to
\left({{\rm d}{\Omega}}/{{\rm d}\ln{R}}\right)B_{2R}\hat{\phi}
, the usual
\Omega
effect. The nonlinear coupling of the linear perturbations, through
\left<{\bf v}_{1}\times{\bf B}_{1}\right>
, produces the emf that drives the quasilinear evolution of the magnetic field on timescales longer than the orbital time. In reality, the perturbations
{\bf v}_{1}
and
{\bf B}_{1}
are themselves modified by nonlinear mode coupling. Here we neglect those nonlinear terms and use their linear forms in the quasilinear induction equation. As shown below, this is sufficient to capture the dynamo periodicity, though not its amplitude. We briefly discuss this in section
III.5
but leave a detailed analysis for future work.

. The unstable (stable) region shrinks (expands) more at larger

, which implies that phase-mixing washes away cyclic behavior at stronger

and/or smaller (larger

) boxes.

III.1
Dynamo equations under the quasilinear approximation

The analysis of the large-scale dynamo is simplified in the Fourier space of
{\bf x}
. Substituting the Fourier modes of the linear perturbations from equations (
8
) in the induction equation (
10
), we obtain the following equation for the evolution of the fields at second order (see Appendix
A
for a detailed derivation):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{{\bf k}R}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle={\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}R}\left(B_{R},B_{z},t\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{{\bf k}\phi}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle={\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}\phi}\left(B_{R},B_{z},t\right)+\frac{{\rm d}{\Omega}}{{\rm d}\ln{R}}B_{{\bf k}R},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{{\bf k}z}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle={\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}z}\left(B_{R},B_{z},t\right).

(11)

Here, the wavenumber
{\bf k}
corresponds to the largest scale field that resides in the system, and the induction
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{\bf k}
is equal to
i{\bf k}\times\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}
, with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}=\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\times{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)
the Fourier transform of the emf,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}={\bf v}_{1}\times{\bf B}_{1}
. It is evident from equations (
8
) that
{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}}
is proportional to the large-scale field
{\bf B}
. Hereon we drop the subscript
0
from
{\bf B}_{0}
and denote the large-scale/mean field as
{\bf B}
instead of
\langle{\bf B}\rangle
or
{\bf B}_{0}
. We substitute the linear perturbations from equations (
8
). For compactness, define
s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=(c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})/2
and
d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=(c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})/2
and

\displaystyle\Psi_{ij}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\equiv s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\pm d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\Theta_{ij}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\equiv s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\pm d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\pm\frac{1}{\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pm\frac{\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\Lambda_{j}.

(12)

We find the following expression for the toroidal induction:

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left(k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}-k_{R}\right)\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}\;\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)+i{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)}{\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{ij}^{-}\,\Gamma_{ij}^{-}\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\Psi_{ij}^{+}\,\Gamma_{ij}^{+}\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{ij}^{-}\,\Gamma_{ij}^{-}\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle-\Theta_{ij}^{+}\,\Gamma_{ij}^{+}\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(13)

Similarly, the radial induction can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}R}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{z}}{2}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}\;\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\left[{\cal R}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)+i{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)\right],

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{ij}^{-}\,\Delta_{ij}^{-}\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\Psi_{ij}^{+}\,\Delta_{ij}^{+}\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\Theta_{ij}^{-}\,\Delta_{ij}^{-}\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\Theta_{ij}^{+}\,\Delta_{ij}^{+}\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(14)

The vertical induction
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}z}
is equal to
-k_{R}{\cal E}_{{\bf k}R}/k_{z}
, which ensures that
k_{R}B_{{\bf k}R}+k_{z}B_{{\bf k}z}=0
at all times.

The
({\rm d}\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R)B_{{\bf k}R}
term in the toroidal induction represents the
\Omega
effect, the shear-driven azimuthal stretching of the field lines.
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{\bf k}
denotes the evolution of the mean field due to the inductive electric field generated by small-scale
{\bf v}
and
{\bf B}
fluctuations. Since
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{\bf k}=i{\bf k}\times\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}
, we have
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}R}=-ik_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\phi}
,
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}=i\left(k_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}-k_{R}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}\right)
, and
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}z}=ik_{R}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\phi}
, and the emf
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
can be easily obtained from the above expression for the induction
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}
. Note that the imaginary part is zero if
d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=(c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})/2
, which is proportional to the net helicity, is zero. The real part, on the other hand, is non-zero even if the helicity
(\propto d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})
is zero. As we show in Appendix
C
, the leading order (in
{\bf k}
) term in the imaginary part corresponds to an emf of the form
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
explicitly depending on the mean helicity of the flow, while the real part results in a
\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J}
form where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
mainly depends on the Maxwell (
\langle\delta{\bf B}\otimes\delta{\bf B}\rangle
) and Faraday (
\langle\delta{\bf v}\otimes\delta{\bf B}\rangle
) stresses.

The induction and the emf are aggregate responses from fluctuations on all scales
k^{\prime-1}
smaller than the scale
k^{-1}
under consideration. A key ingredient of the emf is
i{\bf k}^{\prime}\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}
, which is the Fourier transform of
\delta v_{R}\nabla\delta v_{R}
, a quantity that is related to the derivative of the Reynolds stress responsible for turbulent transport. The coefficient
\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}
is related to the power-spectrum
{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}={\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}-\frac{k_{l}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}\left({\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\right)+...\,.

(15)

It is well known that the power spectrum for MRI turbulence in an unstratified box is typically of the form

\displaystyle{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\approx{\cal N}\,{\left|k^{\prime}_{\perp}\right|}^{-\delta},

(16)

with a positive exponent
\delta
and
{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\perp}={\bf k}^{\prime}\times\hat{{\bf B}}
the component of
{\bf k}^{\prime}
perpendicular to the large-scale field
{\bf B}
.

III.2
The quasilinear emf

Evidently, the emf has a non-trivial dependence on both
{\bf B}
and
t
. Beyond the overall proportionality to
{\bf B}
, the field dependence enters through the linear eigenfunctions and eigenfrequencies, while the time dependence is either exponential or oscillatory depending on whether the linear instability has saturated. Some insight may nevertheless be gained by expanding the emf order by order in
{\bf k}
. At zeroth order, the emf can be recast in an effective
\alpha
-dynamo form,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\left({\bf B},t\right)\cdot{\bf B}
, with
\alpha_{ij}
roughly proportional to
\lim_{{\bf k}\to 0}i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,(v_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}l}\,\varepsilon_{lin}\,k^{\prime}_{j}\,v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}n})/\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
(
\varepsilon_{lin}
is the Levi-Civita symbol and should not be confused with the emf). Here,
\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
is of order
\Omega
on large scales and
{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}
on small scales. Since the integral is dominated by large scales, where
\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
varies slowly, we may pull
\omega^{-1}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
outside the integral, identify it with the turbulent correlation time
t_{\rm corr}
, and write
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
approximately as
\alpha_{ij}=\alpha\,\delta_{ij}
with
\alpha\approx t_{\rm corr}\left<{\bf v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}\right>
. Here we used the identity that
\lim_{{\bf k}\to 0}i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,v_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}l}\,\varepsilon_{lin}\,k^{\prime}_{i}\,v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}n}
is equal to
\lim_{{\bf k}\to 0}i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cdot\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\times{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)=i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\bf v}^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cdot\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\times{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)
, namely the net kinetic helicity
\left<{\bf v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}\right>
, with
\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}=\nabla\times{\bf v}_{1}
. This argument is heuristic, however, and applies only to a kinematic dynamo, since it ignores self-consistency through the force equation. As shown in Appendix
C.1
, a more rigorous analysis of the toroidal induction using the linear eigenfunctions indeed yields the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}
form at zeroth order in
{\bf k}
, with
\alpha_{RR}=\alpha_{zR}
,
\alpha_{Rz}=\alpha_{zz}
, and
\alpha_{RR}+\alpha_{Rz}=\alpha_{RR}+\alpha_{zz}=\alpha_{zR}+\alpha_{Rz}=\alpha_{zR}+\alpha_{zz}\sim\int{\rm d}t\,\left({\cal H}_{\rm cur}-{\cal H}_{\rm kin}\right)
, where
{\cal H}_{\rm kin}=\left<{\bf v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}\right>
is the net kinetic helicity and
{\cal H}_{\rm cur}=\left<{\bf J}_{1}\cdot{\bf B}_{1}\right>/4\pi\rho_{0}
is the net current helicity. Hence, the flow must either be initialized with, or self-consistently develop, non-zero helicity for the
\alpha
dynamo to operate.

Magnetic-helicity conservation constrains the component of the emf parallel to the mean field. In a statistically steady (weakly resistive) state, small-scale helicity conservation in the inertial range of the MRI turbulence gives

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf B}=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}/2

(
Vishniac and Cho 2001
;
Ebrahimi and Bhattacharjee 2014
)
, where
\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}
is a small-scale helicity flux. This implies that the zeroth-order
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
emf must satisfy (see Appendix
C.3
for a detailed discussion)

{\bf B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}=0.

Using the tensor structure derived in Appendix
C.1
, this gives

\displaystyle\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}+B_{z}}=-\frac{\alpha_{RR}B_{R}+\alpha_{Rz}B_{z}}{\alpha_{\phi R}B_{R}+\alpha_{\phi z}B_{z}},

(17)

if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is non-zero. Estimating the ratio near marginal stability, where the integrals are dominated by
{\bf k}^{\prime}\sim{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, using

{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}/\Omega=\sqrt{4-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}}k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}
with

k^{\prime}/k^{\prime}_{z}=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
, and taking
B_{R}\approx B_{z}
yields

\displaystyle\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}}\right|\sim\sqrt{1+a^{2}},

(18)

which is found to approximately hold true, modulo order unity factors, in the
Athena++
simulations discussed later. Thus the helicity constraint naturally predicts a toroidal-to-poloidal ratio that increases with the aspect-ratio parameter
a=k^{\prime}_{R}/k^{\prime}_{z}
, consistent with the dominance of the toroidal field in the MRI dynamo.

The first-order contribution to the emf takes the form of the shear-current effect
(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
)
. At this order in
{\bf k}
, the emf may be written as
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\left({\bf B},t\right)\cdot{\bf J}
, where
{\bf J}=(c/4\pi)\,\nabla\times{\bf B}
is the mean current and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a tensor with non-trivial dependence on
{\bf B}
and
t
(see Appendix
C.2
). Unlike
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
which depends on the helicity (difference between the kinetic and current helicities),
\boldsymbol{\beta}
depends on the Faraday and Maxwell stresses, e.g.,
\beta_{R\phi}
depends on
\langle v_{1\phi}B_{1R}\rangle
(see Appendix
C.2
) and
\beta_{\phi R}
mainly on
\langle B_{1z}B_{1z}\rangle

(
Mondal et al. 2026
)
. The off-diagonal component
\beta_{R\phi}
produces a radial emf and hence toroidal induction, while the diagonal component
\beta_{\phi\phi}
generates a toroidal emf and radial induction. Past MRI saturation, both
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
oscillate in time, with periods much longer than the orbital time (see Section
III.4
). The mean part of
\beta_{R\phi}
is positive, whereas that of
\beta_{\phi\phi}
is negative, implying shear-driven growth of the toroidal field and turbulent diffusion of the radial field (see Appendix
C.2
). Note that the axisymmetric modes yield
\varepsilon_{R}=\beta_{R\phi}J_{\phi}
, which generates
B_{\phi}
in addition to the
\Omega
effect. The inclusion of non-axisymmetric modes, on the other hand, would yield
\varepsilon_{\phi}=\beta_{\phi R}J_{R}
(in addition to the axisymmetric
\beta_{\phi\phi}J_{\phi}
) with
\beta_{\phi R}
depending on the Maxwell and Reynolds stresses
(
Mondal et al. 2026
)
. This would grow
B_{R}
and defeat the turbulent diffusion due to
\beta_{\phi\phi}
sourced by the axisymmetric modes; in addition, it would contribute a
B_{\phi}
-draining term,
\varepsilon_{R}=\beta_{RR}J_{R}
, to the emf. The growth of the poloidal field due to the emf driven by a radial shear current through the non-axisymmetric modes
(\varepsilon_{\phi}=\beta_{\phi R}J_{R})
is the original shear-current effect proposed by
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
. Here we show instead that the toroidal field is driven by the azimuthal shear current through the axisymmetric modes, which gives rise to
\varepsilon_{R}=\beta_{R\phi}J_{\phi}
.

Since the helicities are small in an unstratified shearing box,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is small
(
Shi et al. 2016
)
; the unstratified MRI dynamo is therefore, to leading order, a
\beta
dynamo rather than a conventional
\alpha
dynamo. Still, as shown above, a small but non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is required to yield a saturated toroidal-to-poloidal ratio through small-scale helicity conservation. The
\beta
dynamo satisfies the helicity constraint,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf B}=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}/2
(see Appendix
C.3
). Note that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are only the lowest-order terms in a
{\bf k}
expansion of the emf. For practical calculations, we caution against truncating this expansion, since it may converge slowly when
{\bf k}^{\prime}\sim{\bf k}
or at large
t
. It is therefore preferable to evaluate the full emf directly from equations (
13
) and (
14
), which can be done at little additional computational cost.

There are several key features of the emf:

•

Under the quasilinear approximation, the emf is generated by interference between the linear eigenmodes. In the unstable regime, it grows exponentially at roughly twice the linear MRI growth rate. In the stable regime, after linear saturation, it oscillates due to interference between the oscillatory modes.

•

Averaged over the oscillations, the toroidal induction is generally positive, while the radial induction is negative for the axisymmetric modes (see Appendix
C.2
). Thus the radial (or vertical) field decays after the linear growth phase, whereas the toroidal field can grow to large amplitudes. This reflects the action of rotational shear through the explicit dependence of the toroidal induction on
\Omega
and
{\rm d}\ln\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R
(see equation [
13
]), in addition to the usual
\Omega
-effect term
\left({\rm d}\ln\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R\right)B_{{\bf k}R}
in
\partial B_{{\bf k}\phi}/\partial t
(see equation [
11
]). Non-axisymmetric modes would grow
B_{R}
as well
(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
)
.

•

In the toroidal induction, the coefficients of
\cos{\left(\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right)}
and
\cos{\left(\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right)}
differ; likewise for the oscillatory sine terms in the radial induction. This enables long-period cyclic behavior, which, as shown in Section
III.4
, is driven by the
\cos{\left(\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right)}
term. The key reason is that
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}
varies only weakly with
{\bf k}^{\prime}
, leading to inefficient phase mixing.

•

The prefactor of the
\cos{\left(\left(\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right)}
term in the toroidal induction tends to
1/2
for
{\bf k}^{\prime}\gg{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, whereas the prefactor of the corresponding
\sin{\left(\left(\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right)}
term in the radial induction tends to zero. Since the quasilinear cycles are driven by beats at large
{\bf k}^{\prime}
(see Section
III.4
), the toroidal field displays a much stronger cyclic signal than the radial field.

•

The toroidal field amplitude exceeds the radial one by a factor
\sim k^{\prime}/k^{\prime}_{z}=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
. Hence the cyclic behavior is more pronounced in
B_{\phi}
than in
B_{R}
, for
a=L_{z}/L_{R}>1
.

For dynamo cycles to emerge, the linear MRI must first saturate. Although quasilinear evolution can in principle amplify the large-scale field until the MRI criterion in equation (
6
) is no longer satisfied, zero-net-flux simulations indicate that QLT substantially overestimates the saturated field strength. In practice, saturation likely occurs earlier through nonlinear mode coupling: the perturbations
{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}
that generate the emf can couple to neighboring
{\bf k}^{\prime}
modes, producing a cascade, or get modified through the coupling of the eigenmodes at the same
{\bf k}^{\prime}
. These interactions modify the perturbation amplitudes through convolutions of the linear eigenfunctions evaluated at the resonances,
\sum_{i}{\bf k}^{\prime}_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}_{i}}=0
. Turbulent cascades as well as the non-linear coupling between stable (exponentially decaying) and unstable (exponentially growing) modes can shut off the linear instability and give rise to a saturated Maxwell stress. Moreover, parasitic instabilities like the Kelvin-Helmholtz and tearing mode instabilities may also saturate the MRI
(
Pessah and Goodman 2009
)
. The overall topic of non-linear saturation of the linear MRI lies beyond the current quasilinear framework and will be addressed in a follow-up paper.

In this paper, we therefore assume that such beyond-quasilinear effects saturate the linear MRI by driving the growth rate of the unstable mode,
\gamma_{\bf k}=\sqrt{-\omega^{2}_{2{\bf k}}}
, to zero, so that the unstable region becomes marginally stable. Equivalently, we set
\omega_{2{\bf k}}=0
in the linearly unstable region of
{\bf k}^{\prime}
. This is a crude approximation invoked to follow through with the current quasilinear calculation; a more general non-linear treatment of the MRI unstable region is deferred to future work. Since, within QLT, the long-period cycles of the large-scale field arise from the beat term
\sim\exp{\left[-i\left(\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]}
(see Section
III.4
), they are sourced only by the stable region, where the eigenmodes are oscillatory, i.e.,
\omega^{2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}\geq 0
.

III.3
Quasilinear evolution of the large-scale field

To evolve the large-scale
{\bf B}
, we evaluate the induction terms in equations (
11
) using equations (
13
) and (
14
), and then numerically integrate equations (
11
). In the axisymmetric WKB limit, all three induction terms depend on
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
but not on
B_{\phi}
, so the toroidal equation decouples from the radial and vertical ones. The poloidal equations yield a monotonic decay of
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
to very small values, because the mean part of the corresponding emf is negative (Appendix
C.2
). This is, however, purely an outcome of the turbulent diffusion caused by the axisymmetric modes.
Athena++
simulations show an initial poloidal decay (after linear MRI) followed by saturation at small but non-zero values, which shows that the emf driven by the non-axisymmetric modes (primarily through shear-current effect
(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
;
Mondal and Bhat 2023
;
Mondal et al. 2026
)
) can balance out the axisymmetric turbulent diffusion. Since the degree of variation of
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
is much less than that of
B_{\phi}
in the simulations (past MRI saturation), in this quasilinear treatment, we evolve
B_{\phi}
, holding
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
fixed at small constant values satisfying
k_{R}B_{R}+k_{z}B_{z}=0
. We explicitly check the validity of this long-term near-constancy of the large-scale poloidal field in the simulations, as shown in Fig.
7
.

We then integrate the azimuthal mean-field equation (the second of equations [
11
]) using the toroidal induction in equation (
13
), with initial condition
B_{\phi}=0
. The velocity power spectrum is taken to be
{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}={\cal N}{\left(k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{\perp}+k^{2}\right)}^{-\delta/2}
, where
k=\left|{\bf k}\right|=2\pi\sqrt{1/L^{2}_{R}+1/L^{2}_{z}}
is of order the smallest wavenumber in the system. Using Parseval’s theorem,
\int{\rm d}{\bf k}\,\left|{\bf v}_{{\bf k}}\right|^{2}=2\pi\int{\rm d}{\bf x}\,\left|{\bf v}\right|^{2}=2\pi L_{R}L_{z}\langle{\bf v}^{2}\rangle
, together with
{\bf B}_{\bf k}\approx{\bf B}/(k_{R}k_{z})
(
k_{R}=2\pi/L_{R}
,
k_{z}=2\pi/L_{z}
), we estimate the normalization
{\cal N}
. The variance
\langle{\bf v}^{2}\rangle
is measured from the simulation. We adopt
k_{R,\rm max}=64\,k_{R}
and
k_{z,\rm max}=64\,a\,k_{z}
, with
k_{z}=k_{R}/a
(
L_{z}=aL_{R}
), and verify that doubling the resolution changes the result negligibly. We assume that the helicity of the perturbations is zero, i.e.,
c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, in accordance with the non-helical nature of the flow in zero-net-flux simulations
(
Shi et al. 2016
)
. The field obtained this way is in the inertial frame; the shearing-frame
B_{\phi}
is then found by subtracting the
({\rm d}\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R)\,B_{R}\,t
term (see the second of equations [
11
]).

Fig.
2
shows the large-scale toroidal field in the shearing frame as a function of
t/t_{\rm orb}
, where
t_{\rm orb}=2\pi/\Omega
, for different
a
and
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega
. Here
v_{\rm A}=\sqrt{(B_{R}^{2}+B_{z}^{2})/4\pi\rho_{0}}
is the Alfvén speed. The toroidal field exhibits beats, with characteristic periods ranging from
\sim 10\,t_{\rm orb}
to a few
100\,t_{\rm orb}
. At fixed
a
, the beats are most pronounced for particular values of
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega
. These cycles arise from the
\cos{\left[\left(\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]}
term in the toroidal induction (equation [
13
]); as shown in Section
III.4
, the long periods result from the slow variation of
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}
with
{\bf k}^{\prime}
at large
{\bf k}^{\prime}
.

The oscillation amplitude generally increases with
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega
, consistent with the overall linear dependence of the emf on
{\bf B}
. For
a\lesssim 2
, the dynamo shows several comparable periods and is therefore more stochastic, whereas at larger
a
the cycles become cleaner and stronger. The longer periods at larger
a
reflect the approximate scaling of the cycle period as
\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
. Since the eigenfrequencies scale as
k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}
, they are more sparsely distributed in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
-space at larger
a
, which reduces phase mixing and enhances the cyclic behavior. The larger amplitudes at larger
a
also partly follow from the factor
\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A})^{2}
in the denominator, that scales as
\sim(k_{z}^{\prime 2}/2k^{\prime 2})\,\kappa^{2}\sim a^{-2}\kappa^{2}
at
k^{\prime}\gg k
(see equations [
5
] and [
13
]). By contrast, increasing
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega
shortens the overall cycle period and makes the evolution more stochastic. Overall, these trends reflect more efficient phase mixing at smaller
a
and larger
B_{R}
.

The trends can be understood from Fig.
3
, which shows
{\cal E}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}12}
, the part of the integrand proportional to
\cos{\left[\left(\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]}
, as a function of
k_{R}^{\prime}
and
k_{z}^{\prime}
for
a=1
and
8
and
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega=0.02
and
0.1
; the gray region marks the unstable modes. Since the quasilinear emf that yields the cycles is dominated by the stable (oscillatory) region, three features matter. First, the dominant contribution comes from a narrow strip around
{\bf k}^{\prime}\perp{\bf k}
, i.e.
{\bf k}^{\prime}\parallel{\bf v}_{\rm A}
, because the MRI turbulent power spectrum peaks for field-aligned modes (equation [
16
]). Second, a smaller fraction of this strip lies inside the box when the box is taller, i.e. at larger
a
. Third, the stable region expands as
kv_{\rm A}/\Omega
increases. Hence more modes contribute in smaller boxes and/or for stronger fields, which enhances phase mixing and makes the dynamo less cyclic and more stochastic. Overall, the long cycles originate from the MRI dispersion relation, namely the weak
{\bf k}
dependence of the difference between the fast and slow shear-Alfvén frequencies, while the trends with aspect ratio and field strength reflect the combined effects of this dispersion relation and the anisotropic, field-aligned MHD turbulence.

III.4
Origin of the dynamo cycles in QLT

In QLT, oscillations of the mean magnetic field arise from the interference between two normal modes with frequencies
\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
. Each
{\bf k}^{\prime}
mode contributes at the sum and difference frequencies,
\Sigma\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
. The long cycle is set by the low-frequency branch
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
. Since
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
varies only weakly with
{\bf k}^{\prime}
for
k^{\prime}\gtrsim k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, the spread
\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}
is small (see Fig.
1
), producing slow beats. Here
\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}=\Delta\omega_{k^{\prime}\approx 2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}}
and
\Delta\omega_{\infty}=\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\to\infty}
approximately denote the minimum and maximum values of
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
.

The dominant contribution to the radial emf, which drives the toroidal field, is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left[f_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cos{\left(\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}t\right)}+f_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cos{\left(\Sigma\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}t\right)}\right]\,,

(19)

where
f_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
f_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
are functions of
{\bf k}^{\prime}
involving the linear eigenfunctions and eigenfrequencies (see the first of equations [
13
]). Assuming
k<k^{\prime}
, we transform from
{\bf k}^{\prime}
to
\left(s=k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime},{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}\right)
and then from
{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}
to
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
at fixed
s
, so that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\int{\rm d}s\,\left[\int_{\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}^{\Delta\omega_{\infty}}{\rm d}\Delta\omega\,g_{1}\left(\Delta\omega\right)\,\cos{\left(\Delta\omega t\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{\Sigma\omega_{\rm c}}^{\Sigma\omega_{\infty}}{\rm d}\Sigma\omega\,g_{2}\left(\Sigma\omega\right)\,\cos{\left(\Sigma\omega t\right)}\right],

(20)

where
g_{1}\left(\Delta\omega\right)=f_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}/J_{1}
and
g_{2}\left(\Sigma\omega\right)=f_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}/J_{2}
, with
J_{1}=\left|\frac{s,\;{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}}{k^{\prime}_{R},\;k^{\prime}_{z}}\right|\left(\partial\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}\right)
and
J_{2}=\left|\frac{s,\;{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}}{k^{\prime}_{R},\;k^{\prime}_{z}}\right|\left(\partial\Sigma\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial{{\bf k}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{{\bf v}}_{\rm A}\right)
the Jacobians of the transformation. Since
f_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
contains terms scaling as
\omega^{-1}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
{\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)}^{-1}
, it is largest near the marginally stable wavenumber
{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, where
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}=0
(see equation [
6
]). However, the
\sin{\left(\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}t\right)}/\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
term in the emf, arising through
v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}
and
B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}
, can produce a large secular growth of the toroidal field at late times, which is not seen in the simulations. We avoid this by noting that nonlinear wave-wave interactions should broaden the marginal mode to
\omega^{\prime 2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}}=\omega^{2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}-{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}}\approx-\left(\partial\omega^{\prime 2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial k^{\prime}\right)\Delta k^{\prime}
, where
\Delta k^{\prime}=k^{\prime}-k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
is defined by
\left(\partial\omega^{\prime 2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial k^{\prime}\right)\Delta k^{\prime}\approx\left(\partial^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial k^{\prime 2}\right){\left(\Delta k^{\prime}\right)^{2}}/2
(with derivatives evaluated at fixed
s
and at
k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
). Assuming that three-wave interactions dominate this broadening, the linear dispersion relation yields
\Delta k^{\prime}\approx k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
. Thus, after accounting for nonlinear broadening, the minimum wavenumber that contributes to the quasilinear emf is approximately
2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
. Although, to evolve
B_{\phi}
, we consider both MRI stable and unstable regions to compute the emf (assuming
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}=0
in the unstable region), here we focus on the stable region, since this is where the cycles arise in QLT.

The long-period cycle comes from the first term in equation (
23
), which we denote by
\varepsilon_{1{\bf k}R}
. Integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1{\bf k}R}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}^{\Delta\omega_{\infty}}{\rm d}\Delta\omega\,g_{1}\left(\Delta\omega\right)\,\cos{\left(\Delta\omega t\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int{\rm d}s\,\Bigg[{g_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}\right)\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t\right)}-g_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}\right)\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}t\right)}}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}}^{\Delta\omega_{\infty}}{\rm d}\Delta\omega\,\frac{{\rm d}g_{1}(\Delta\omega)}{{\rm d}\Delta\omega}\sin{\left(\Delta\omega t\right)}\Bigg],

(21)

and repeated integration by parts yields the asymptotic series

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1{\bf k}R}(t)=\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\times\int{\rm d}s\,\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(2n)}_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}\right)\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t\right)}-g^{(2n)}_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}\right)\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}t\right)}}{t^{2n}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(2n+1)}_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}\right)\cos{\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t\right)}-g^{(2n+1)}_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}\right)\cos{\left(\Delta\omega_{{\rm c}}t\right)}}{t^{2n+1}}\right],

(22)

where
g^{(m)}_{1}(\Delta\omega)
is the
m^{\rm th}
derivative of
g_{1}
with respect to
\Delta\omega
. At long times,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1{\bf k}R}(t)\approx\frac{1}{t}\int{\rm d}s\,\Big[g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})\sin(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t)-g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})\sin(\Delta\omega_{\rm c}t)\Big]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{t}\int{\rm d}s\,\Bigg[\big(g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})-g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sin\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\big(g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})+g_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\Bigg].

(23)

with the second term dominant because
\Delta\omega_{\infty}
and
\Delta\omega_{\rm c}
are close. The same analysis applies to
\varepsilon_{2{\bf k}R}
(second term of equation [
23
]) after replacing
g_{1}\to g_{2}
and
\Delta\to\Sigma
.

Thus
\varepsilon_{1{\bf k}R}
exhibits fast oscillations modulated by slow
beats, with characteristic periods

T_{\Delta 1}=\frac{4\pi}{\left|\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}\right|},\quad T_{\Delta 2}=\frac{4\pi}{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}.

(24)

As shown in Appendix
B
, expanding the eigenfrequencies in the limit
{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}/\Omega\gg 1
gives

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}}\Omega\left[1+\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 2}}{4k^{\prime 2}}{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-2}\left(2-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{4}}{8\Omega^{4}}\right)+\cdots\right].

(25)

Thus
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
approaches

\Delta\omega_{\infty}=2k^{\prime}_{z}\,\Omega/k^{\prime}
at large
k^{\prime}
, so small-scale modes
oscillate at nearly the same beat frequency. This weak dispersion is
the key ingredient behind a long period cycle.

Getting back to the magnetorotational dynamo, the emf given in equation (
23
) decays as
\sim\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t\right)}/t
or
\sin{\left(\Delta\omega_{\rm c}t\right)}/t
, allowing the toroidal field to persist for a long time. Since
\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}
has the same form as
\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}
with
g_{1}
replaced by
g^{\prime}_{1}=(k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}_{R})g_{1}
, and since
\partial B_{\phi}/\partial t={\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}
with
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}=i\left(k_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}-k_{R}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}\right)
, the slowly varying part of
B_{\phi}
is

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\int{\rm d}s\,\left[h_{1}\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}\right){\rm Si}\left(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t\right)-h_{2}\left(\Delta\omega_{\rm c}\right){\rm Si}\left(\Delta\omega_{\rm c}t\right)\right],

(30)

where
{\rm Si}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}x\,\sin(x)/x
is the Sine integral, and
h_{1}=i\left(k_{z}g_{1}-k_{R}g^{\prime}_{1}\right)=i\left(k_{z}-k_{R}k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}_{R}\right)g_{1}
. Neglecting the fast contribution from
\varepsilon_{2{\bf k}R}
and using
{\rm Si}(t\to\infty)=\pi/2-\cos(t)/t
, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{t}\int{\rm d}s\,\Big[h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})\cos(\Delta\omega_{\rm c}t)-h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})\cos(\Delta\omega_{\infty}t)\Big]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{t}\int{\rm d}s\,\Bigg[\big(h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})-h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\rm c})+h_{1}(\Delta\omega_{\infty})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sin\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}{2}t\right)\Bigg].

(31)

Again the second term dominates because
\Delta\omega_{\infty}\approx\Delta\omega_{\rm c}
. Thus the toroidal field shows the same beating behavior as the emf, with slow modulation at the period
T_{\Delta 1}
in equation (
26
), as seen in Fig.
2
. Phase mixing remains weak because
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
varies only slowly with
k^{\prime}
. Physically, one mode is slightly faster because rotation reinforces magnetic tension in one branch and opposes it in the other. Their small frequency difference then drives the long-period modulation of the mean field. This is the quasilinear mechanism for the dynamo cycles. The simulations, however, are not limited to this channel and also contain additional frequencies generated by higher-order mode coupling.

III.5
Beyond quasilinear effects

So far we have considered the perturbations to be linear, i.e., they scale as
\exp{\left[-i\omega_{j{\bf k}}t\right]}
. However, in a saturated turbulent state, this is not true in general. The perturbations themselves couple to the other modes (at the same as well as other
{\bf k}
), which causes them to oscillate at not only their own eigenfrequencies but also frequencies that are linear combinations of the eigenfrequencies of the different modes. A generic perturbation therefore scales as

\displaystyle\delta Q^{i}_{{\bf k}}(t)=\sum_{j}c_{1j{\bf k}}U^{i}_{1j{\bf k}}\exp{\left[-i\omega_{j{\bf k}}t\right]}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\sum_{l}\sum_{n}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}c_{2j{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}}U^{i}_{2jln{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}}\exp{\left[-i\left(\omega_{l{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{n{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]}

\displaystyle+...,

(32)

where the superscript
i
represents the index of the state vector, the subscript
j,l
and
n
represent the index of the eigenmode, and
U^{i}_{1j{\bf k}}
,
U^{i}_{2j{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, etc. are complicated convolutions involving the linear eigenfunction
U^{i}_{j{\bf k}}
. In the linear regime, we have
U^{i}_{1j{\bf k}}=U^{i}_{j{\bf k}}
and
U^{i}_{2j{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}}
is comparatively small. Upon substituting these nonlinear perturbations in the induction equation, it is not hard to see that the emf gains terms that scale as
\exp{\left[-i\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{l{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}}+\omega_{n{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}+{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}}\right)t\right]}
,
\exp{\left[-i\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}-{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}}+\omega_{l{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime\prime\prime}}+\omega_{n{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}+{\bf k}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\right)t\right]}
and so on, in addition to the quasilinear terms
\sim\exp{\left[-i\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]}
(
{\bf k}^{\prime}
,
{\bf k}^{\prime\prime}
,
{\bf k}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, etc. are the dummy wavenumbers to be integrated over). In section
IV.4
, we explicitly show the presence of these frequencies in the power-spectrum generated from the simulation data. These additional terms have two key effects: (1) they give rise to a non-zero contribution to the cyclic dynamo from the unstable region in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
, which does not arise under the quasilinear approximation, and (2) they make the overall dynamo more turbulent and stochastic.

IV
Numerical simulations

Let us now compare the theory to simulations. The simulation setup is a local shearing box, which is well-described by the WKB approximation. However, besides being fully nonlinear rather than quasilinear, the simulations are more general than the theory in the sense that they (i) include both axisymmetric and non-axisymmetric (shearing waves) modes, (ii) show a turbulent fluctuation of
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
which are not constant, as assumed in the analytical theory, and (iii) self-consistently generate the turbulent power-spectrum of fluctuations, something that is beyond the scope of QLT. Despite these differences, it is interesting to see if some of the basic properties of the mean (large-scale) field in the simulations are reproduced by QLT.

IV.1
Governing equations and code description

We solve the compressible MHD equations for an unstratified shearing box corotating with the disk at an orbital frequency
\Omega\hat{{\bf z}}
. We concern ourselves with high
\beta
plasmas where the flow is fairly incompressible
(v\sim v_{\rm A}<c_{\rm s})
. Unlike the linear and quasilinear calculations, which we perform in a global inertial frame in cylindrical coordinates, the simulations are performed in a local shearing (non-inertial) frame in Cartesian coordinates. The equations include the continuity and induction equations (see equations [
2
]) along with the isothermal equation of state,
P=\rho c^{2}_{\rm s}
, and the force equation that includes the non-inertial Coriolis and centrifugal forces:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf v}}{\partial t}+{\bf v}\cdot\nabla{\bf v}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\left(P+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}\right)+\frac{1}{4\pi\rho}{\bf B}\cdot\nabla{\bf B}

\displaystyle-2\Omega\,\hat{{\bf z}}\times{\bf v}+2q\Omega^{2}x\,\hat{{\bf x}},

(33)

where
\hat{{\bf x}}
refers to the radial direction,
\hat{{\bf y}}
the azimuthal direction parallel to disk rotation, and
\hat{{\bf z}}
the vertical direction, while
q=-{\rm d}\ln\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R
is the shear parameter. Since we study an unstratified shearing box, we have omitted the vertical tidal acceleration
\Omega^{2}z\hat{{\bf z}}
. We also neglect the non-ideal viscous and resistive effects.

We use the
Athena++
MHD code to perform our numerical simulations in a Cartesian grid, adopting the CTU integrator, a second order piecewise linear flux reconstruction, and the HLLE Riemann solver as the basic algorithms. The orbital advection scheme is implemented to increase the computational efficiency and reduce the mismatch of flux integrals on the radial faces due to remap. The conservation of the vertical magnetic flux is ensured to machine precision by using a remapping method.

IV.4
Origin of the dynamo cycles in simulations

Unlike QLT, the simulation spectra of the velocity and magnetic fluctuations contain not only the linear eigenmodes but also frequencies given by their linear combinations. This is a natural consequence of higher-order wave-interference effects, as briefly discussed in Section
III.5
. As we show below, these additional frequencies produce a non-zero contribution to the dynamo cycles from the MRI unstable region, which is absent in QLT.

We compute the temporal power spectra of the
(k_{x},k_{y})=(0,0)
modes of
B_{y}=B_{\phi}
and
v_{x}=v_{R}
for the
a=8
and
a=1
simulations, and plot them as functions of
\omega
for different
k_{z}
in Figs.
9
and
10
, respectively. The vertical dashed lines mark the dominant frequencies. The
a=8
case shows clear spectral peaks, especially at large scales (small
k_{z}
), whereas the
a=1
case shows no comparably distinct peaks and is much more continuum-like. This difference originates from the fact that the eigenfrequencies scale as
k_{z}/k
: for larger
a
, they are more widely separated in
{\bf k}
-space, so phase mixing is weaker and discrete peaks remain visible, while for smaller
a
they are more closely packed and blend into a stochastic continuum.

For
a=8
, the peaks roughly correspond to the linear eigenfrequency
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{0}}\approx\Omega
and to combinations such as
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}\pm\omega_{2,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}\approx\sqrt{7}\,\Omega
,
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}-\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{0}}=(\sqrt{7}-1)\Omega
,
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}+\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{0}}\approx(\sqrt{7}+1)\Omega
, and
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}+2\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{0}}\approx(\sqrt{7}+2)\Omega
, where
k_{0}=2\pi/L_{z}=\pi/4h_{z}
(since
L_{z}=8h_{z}
) is the smallest vertical wavenumber and
k_{\rm c}
is the marginally unstable one. This follows from
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{0}}\approx k_{z}\kappa/k
and
\omega_{1,k_{z}=k_{\rm c}}\approx\sqrt{7}\,k_{z}\kappa/k
(see equations [
5
]), with
\kappa=\Omega
for Keplerian flow. The critical frequency appears prominently because the fluctuations are strongest near marginal stability, as seen from the growth of
v_{1{\bf k}\phi}
and
B_{1{\bf k}R}
in the
{\bf k}\to{\bf k}_{\rm c}
or
\omega_{2{\bf k}}\to 0
limit in equations (
7
). The exact peak positions vary somewhat with
k_{z}
because of nonlinear coupling among modes near
k_{0}
and
k_{\rm c}
. All of these modes lie in the MRI unstable region, since the large-scale
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
are quite small (
\sim 0.01
; see Fig.
7
).

We now show how interference between these rapidly oscillating waves can produce long-period modulations of the large-scale field. We extract the peak frequencies
\omega_{B_{y}}
from the
B_{y}
power spectrum at
k^{\prime}_{z}=2n\pi/L_{z}
with
n=2(1)6
, and the frequencies
\omega_{v_{x}}
from the
v_{x}
spectrum at
k_{0}-k^{\prime}_{z}
, where
k_{0}=2\pi/L_{z}
. The emf
\varepsilon_{z}
at the large-scale mode
k_{z}=k_{0}
, which drives the toroidal field
B_{y}
, therefore oscillates at the frequency combinations
\omega_{B_{y}}\pm\omega_{v_{x}}
. Denoting this set of frequencies by
\omega_{n}
, we may write
\varepsilon_{z}
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{z}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\cos(\omega_{n}t).

(34)

These frequencies cluster in groups, each of which has a narrow spread. Let us now study the emf in any one of these groups that has
N_{\rm G}
such frequencies. Choose the median frequency
\omega_{0}
as the representative carrier frequency for the group, and define
\delta_{n}\equiv\omega_{n}-\omega_{0}
.
Then

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}\cos\big[(\omega_{0}+\delta_{n})t\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}\left[\cos(\omega_{0}t)\cos(\delta_{n}t)-\sin(\omega_{0}t)\sin(\delta_{n}t)\right]

\displaystyle=A(t)\cos(\omega_{0}t)-B(t)\sin(\omega_{0}t),

(35)

where

\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}\cos(\delta_{n}t),\quad B(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}\sin(\delta_{n}t).

(36)

Hence the emf may be written in carrier–envelope form as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{z}(t)=E(t)\cos\!\big[\omega_{0}t+\phi(t)\big],

(37)

with

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A^{2}(t)+B^{2}(t)},\quad\phi(t)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{B(t)}{A(t)}\right).

(38)

The quantities
A(t)
and
B(t)
are sums over the individual offsets
\delta_{n}
, but the beating appears most directly in the envelope amplitude. Indeed,

\displaystyle E^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=A^{2}(t)+B^{2}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}^{2}+2\sum_{m<n}a_{m}a_{n}\cos\big[(\delta_{m}-\delta_{n})t\big].

(39)

Since
\delta_{m}-\delta_{n}=(\omega_{m}-\omega_{0})-(\omega_{n}-\omega_{0})=\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle E^{2}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm G}}a_{n}^{2}+2\sum_{m<n}a_{m}a_{n}\cos\big[(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})t\big].

(40)

Thus, the envelope is controlled by the pairwise frequency differences
\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}
, which are nothing but the beat frequencies. A similar exercise could also be performed for the radial emf
\varepsilon_{x}
. We club the frequencies
\omega_{B_{y}}\pm\omega_{v_{x}}
in groups, compute the pairwise differences
\Delta\omega=\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}
and the beating period
T_{\rm beat}
in each group. We plot the histogram of these beating periods in Fig.
11
. These periods match well with the periods derived from the temporal power-spectrum of
B_{y}
(see the
a=8
case in the left panel of Fig.
8(b)
). Especially, the peaks at
\sim 50
,
100
and
200\,t_{\rm orb}
are correctly recovered. In this analysis, we have smoothed the
v_{x}
and
B_{y}
power-spectra for the identification of prominent peaks, which could introduce potential error in the frequency estimation. For consistency check, we varied the smoothing kernel and found that the histogram of beating periods appears similar with persistent peaks at the aforementioned periods in each case, which shows that the periods in Fig.
11
are not an artifact of mis-estimation of frequencies due to smoothing. All in all, this exercise shows that wave-interference is ultimately responsible for the emergence of long period dynamo cycles. However, the relevant beats are not restricted to the quasilinear
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
channel; they also involve higher order combinations of eigenfrequencies and therefore include beyond-quasilinear contributions.

V
Discussion

V.1
Physical picture

The central result of this paper is that the long-period MRI dynamo cycle can be understood as a wave-interference phenomenon. Starting from the linear MRI eigenfunctions in the WKB approximation, we computed the quasilinear emf,
\langle{\bf v}_{1}\times{\bf B}_{1}\rangle
, without assuming an
a priori
mean-field closure, and used it to evolve the large-scale field. The resulting emf depends nontrivially on the mean field through the eigenfunctions and eigenfrequencies, and contains oscillatory contributions involving both sums and differences of the two eigenfrequency branches. The low-frequency part, associated with the difference,

\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}},

drives a slow modulation of the mean induction and hence of
B_{\phi}
. In this way, QLT provides a first-principles explanation for the origin of the dynamo cycles, i.e., the butterfly diagram.

The physical reason for the long period is that, for
k^{\prime}v_{\rm A}>\Omega
, the two frequency branches become nearly Alfvénic and differ slightly by a shear-dependent term, and
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
becomes small and weakly dependent on
k^{\prime}
. Physically, the fast and slow branches differ in how shear competes with magnetic tension: for the fast branch, tension is reinforced by shear, whereas for the slow branch they oppose each other. This small residual asymmetry leaves the two branches nearly degenerate, but not exactly so, producing a nonzero beat frequency. Since the emf is an integral over many
{\bf k}^{\prime}
modes, a long-lived large-scale modulation requires weak phase mixing across the contributing spectrum. The MRI eigenstructure satisfies this requirement for
k^{\prime}\gtrsim k^{\prime}_{\rm c}\sim\Omega/v_{\rm A}
, so the low-frequency beat survives spectral averaging and the narrow spread in the beat frequency across the spectrum produces a long dynamo cycle. Within the present theory, the cycle period is therefore set by the weak dispersion of
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
.

This picture also explains why the cycles depend strongly on box geometry. The aspect ratio
a=L_{z}/L_{R}
sets
k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}
and therefore the eigenfrequencies, which are roughly proportional to
k^{\prime}_{z}/k^{\prime}
. At larger
a
, the relevant frequencies are more sparsely distributed in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
-space, phase mixing is weaker, and the cycles become more pronounced and longer-lived. The same geometry also lowers
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, increasing the cycle period
T_{\rm cycle}
; within QLT this yields the approximate scaling:
T_{\rm cycle}=T_{\Delta 1}\sim 30\sqrt{1+a^{2}}t_{\rm orb}
. The linear response, and therefore the emf, also becomes stronger at larger
a
, increasing the cycle amplitude as
\sim a^{2}
before flattening off beyond
a\sim 5
. These trends are broadly consistent with local shearing box simulations.

V.2
Relation to mean-field theory

In the large-scale limit, the full quasilinear emf may be expanded in a form analogous to the usual
\alpha
–
\beta
mean-field expansion. In that sense, the quasilinear calculation contains the familiar mean-field terms, but is not limited to them. The lowest-order (in
{\bf k}
) contribution has the structure of an (helicity-dependent)
\alpha
effect, while the next-order term has the structure of a shear-current, or
\beta
, effect. For the unstratified zero-net-flux system studied here, the helicities are small, so the contribution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}
to the emf, is small. A small but non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is, however, required to yield a saturated ratio of the toroidal and poloidal components,
|B_{\phi}/B_{R}|\sim\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
, through the conservation of magnetic helicity on small scales. The dominant contribution to the emf is
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J}
, which is the shear current effect. In the axisymmetric sector, the off-diagonal component
\beta_{R\phi}
grows the toroidal field (along with the shear-driven
\Omega
effect). If non-axisymmetric modes are included,
\beta_{\phi R}
grows the poloidal field
(
Squire and Bhattacharjee 2016
;
Rincon et al. 2007
;
Riols et al. 2013
)
. We intend to generalize the quasilinear framework of this paper to non-axisymmetric MRI in future work.

Let us briefly compare our perturbative treatment to direct statistical simulations
(
Mondal and Bhat 2023
;
Mondal et al. 2026
)
.
Mondal et al. 2026
decompose each quantity
{\bf Q}
as
\bar{{\bf Q}}+\delta{\bf Q}
in the shearing frame, with
\bar{{\bf Q}}
the horizontal mean, and show how a positive off-diagonal
\beta_{\phi R}
(negative off-diagonal turbulent diffusivity) arises. The mean fields satisfy
\partial_{t}\bar{B}_{R}\simeq-\partial_{z}\varepsilon_{\phi}
and
\partial_{t}\bar{B}_{\phi}\simeq-q\Omega\bar{B}_{R}+\partial_{z}\varepsilon_{R}-\partial_{R}\varepsilon_{z}
, where
q=-{\rm d}\ln\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R
: shear amplifies
\bar{B}_{\phi}
from
\bar{B}_{R}
, while
\varepsilon_{\phi}\simeq\beta_{\phi R}\bar{J}_{R}
, with
\bar{J}_{R}=-\partial_{z}\bar{B}_{\phi}
and
\beta_{\phi R}\simeq(\rho\Omega)^{-1}[(2-q)^{-1}\overline{\delta B_{z}\delta B_{z}}/4\pi+q^{-1}\rho\,\overline{\delta v_{z}\delta v_{z}}]>0
, regenerates
\bar{B}_{R}
. The magnetic tension force
\delta B_{z}\partial_{z}\bar{B}_{\phi}
drives
\delta v_{\phi}
, and the Coriolis force converts this into
\delta v_{R}
, which yields
\varepsilon_{\phi}\simeq-\overline{\delta v_{R}\delta B_{z}}\propto\bar{J}_{R}
. Saturation occurs because nonlinear Lorentz-force triple correlations produce a term
\varepsilon_{\phi}^{(3)}
such that
-\partial_{z}\varepsilon_{\phi}^{(3)}
balances the current-driven source
-\partial_{z}(\beta_{\phi R}\bar{J}_{R})
in
\partial_{t}\bar{B}_{R}=-\partial_{z}\varepsilon_{\phi}
. The same
\bar{B}_{\phi}\to\delta v_{\phi}\to\delta v_{R}\to\bar{B}_{R}\to\bar{B}_{\phi}
loop occurs in the quasilinear framework, when both axisymmetric and non-axisymmetric modes are taken into account. What our framework offers, though, but direct statistical simulations
(
Mondal and Bhat 2023
;
Mondal et al. 2026
)
do not, is the timescale over which this loop is closed: the period over which the small-scale velocity and magnetic fluctuations get back into phase with one another and give rise to a coherent large-scale dynamo.

Our perturbative analysis shows that the MRI dynamo should not be viewed as a conventional
\alpha
–
\beta
–
\Omega
dynamo. Neither
\alpha
nor
\beta
should be regarded as a complete closure in this problem: they are only the first terms in the expansion of the full quasilinear emf. For practical purposes, it is more informative to evaluate the full emf directly (using equations [
13
] and [
14
]) than to truncate the series at low order.

V.3
What QLT captures, and what it misses

The main success of QLT is that it explains the existence of long-period dynamo cycles and their aspect-ratio dependence, without imposing phenomenological time-dependent dynamo coefficients. It identifies the relevant slow timescale with the interference between the two eigenfrequency branches. At the same time, the simulations reveal that the full dynamo is spectrally richer than QLT alone. In the quasilinear picture, the dominant long-period cycle arises from beats between the two eigenfrequencies in the MRI stable region. In the simulations, by contrast, the spectrum contains not only the linear eigenfrequencies but also higher-order linear combinations, and the observed cycle periods arise from pairwise beats among a broader set of peaks, including a nonzero contribution from the MRI-unstable region.

QLT has well-defined limitations. It builds the mean field from the interference of linear perturbations while neglecting their nonlinear coupling with other modes. MRI saturation, however, is governed by such mode couplings—both between different
{\bf k}
and within a given
{\bf k}
—which drain energy from the linearly unstable (exponentially growing) modes and redistribute it through the stable (exponentially decaying) modes. Preliminary calculations indicate that this redistribution of energy between the stable and unstable modes gives rise to dynamo cycles with periods similar to those arising from the interference of oscillatory modes or waves discussed in this paper. In a follow-up paper, we will present a more general theory based on a nonlinear dynamical systems analysis of the large-scale fields, complementary to the invariant solutions approach of
Riols et al. 2013
.

V.4
Astrophysical implications

Although the present theory and simulations treat an idealized system—a local, unstratified, magnetized shear-flow—they suggest a broader organizing principle for large-scale cyclic dynamos: when the eigenspectrum contains nearly degenerate branches whose frequency differences vary only weakly across the modes that dominate the emf, the contributions to the emf oscillate with nearly the same beat frequency and therefore do not phase-mix rapidly.

How far this mechanism extends beyond the present setting remains to be established. In stratified disks, buoyancy
(
Skoutnev et al. 2022
)
and an
\alpha
effect emerging from non-helical flows
(
Dhang et al. 2024
)
may open up additional dynamo channels. Explicit resistivity, finite magnetic Prandtl number
{\rm Pm}

(
Fromang et al. 2007
;
Riols et al. 2015
)
, non-axisymmetric modes
(
Lesur and Ogilvie 2008
)
, and global curvature
(
Pessah and Psaltis 2005
;
Das et al. 2018
)
would modify the spectrum and the saturation pathway. It would be important to test whether the same interference picture continues to organize the large-scale dynamo in more realistic models, especially in the low-
{\rm Pm}
regime relevant to many astrophysical disks. A related problem is the dependence of the turbulent stress on the geometry and
{\rm Pm}
. The sharp decline of the stress with decreasing
{\rm Pm}
and its turning off below a critical
{\rm Pm}>1
in
a=1
boxes with explicit dissipation
(
Fromang et al. 2007
)
appears to be connected to the reduction in turbulent stress with increasing resolution in ideal MHD
(
Fromang and Papaloizou 2007
;
Shi et al. 2016
)
. However, this behavior is strongly geometry-dependent:
Shi et al. 2016
found that vertically elongated boxes not only develop a coherent cyclic large-scale dynamo (which we have explained using QLT) but also resolution-independent stresses, while explicit-dissipation studies of elongated boxes show that sustained turbulence can persist to lower
{\rm Pm}
when the vertical domain is extended
(
Nauman and Pessah 2016
;
Nauman and Pessah 2018
)
.
Held and Mamatsashvili 2022
found a
{\rm Pm}^{\delta}
(
{\rm Rm}
dependent
\delta>0
) scaling of the stress. This scaling is further weakened in tall boxes, where a large-scale dynamo action makes the stress less sensitive to the dissipation scale. These results suggest that the geometry and
{\rm Pm}
dependence of turbulent transport is deeply connected to the ability of the domain to support a large-scale dynamo.

Despite the restricted setup of zero-net-flux, unstratified, ideal MHD, the physical principle behind long-period dynamo cycles is clear: they emerge from coherent beats between nearly degenerate MHD waves. In this paper, we have identified this beating mechanism explicitly for magnetorotational dynamos, but the principle is more general and need not be tied to the MRI. Kelvin–Helmholtz dynamos in shear layers
(
Tripathi et al. 2026
)
and buoyancy-driven dynamos in sheared flows with zero net rotation
(
Squire et al. 2025
)
are alternate cases where analogous beats are presumably in action, and we intend to study such non-magnetorotational dynamo pathways in future work. This broader interpretation has direct implications for cyclic magnetic activity across astrophysical systems, from stellar and planetary dynamos to quasi-periodic variability in accretion disks around stars, planets, and black holes. In accreting systems, magnetic cycles could lead to episodic accretion and quasi-periodic flaring and jet launching, which could in principle introduce cyclic features to observed light curves
(
Alston et al. 2016
)
, e.g., low-frequency quasi-periodic oscillations in AGNs
(
O’Neill et al. 2011
;
Zhou and Lai 2024
)
. In some cases, cyclic dynamo activity could mimic variability otherwise attributed to binaries, e.g., putative binary supermassive black holes in AGN disks
(
Zhou and Lai 2024
)
. The solar cycle is often attributed to a near-surface
(
Vasil et al. 2024
)
or near-tachocline MRI-type dynamo
(
Kagan and Wheeler 2014
)
. It would be interesting to see if our theory can be generalized to the study of stellar and planetary dynamos.

Appendix A
Derivation of the quasilinear emf

Here we compute the quasilinear emf from the linear perturbations in the velocity and magnetic fields. The evolution of the second order perturbations,
{\bf v}_{2}
and
{\bf B}_{2}
, is given by the force and induction equations perturbed up to second order:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial{\bf B}_{2}}{\partial t}&=\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{0}\times{\bf B}_{2}\right)+\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{2}\times{\bf B}_{0}\right)+\nabla\times\left({\bf v}_{1}\times{\bf B}_{1}\right),\\
\frac{\partial{\bf v}_{2}}{\partial t}&+{\bf v}_{2}\cdot\nabla{\bf v}_{0}+{\bf v}_{0}\cdot\nabla{\bf v}_{2}+\frac{\nabla\left({\bf B}_{0}\cdot{\bf B}_{2}\right)-{\bf B}_{0}\cdot\nabla{\bf B}_{2}}{4\pi\rho_{0}}\\
&=-{\bf v}_{1}\cdot\nabla{\bf v}_{1}-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}}\left[\frac{\nabla\left({\bf B}_{1}\cdot{\bf B}_{1}\right)}{2}-{\bf B}_{1}\cdot\nabla{\bf B}_{1}\right].\end{split}

(41)

The linear perturbations given by equations (
8
) need to be substituted above to obtain the time evolution of
{\bf v}_{2}
and
{\bf B}_{2}
.

The induction can be computed as follows:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{\bf k}&=i{\bf k}\times\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}=i{\bf k}\times\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\times{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\\
&=i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left[{\bf k}\cdot{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\,{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)-{\bf k}\cdot{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\,{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\right]\\
&=i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left(k_{R}-k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\right)\\
&\times\Big[B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)-v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}(t)\Big],\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}=0
and
{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}=0
. The azimuthal/toroidal and radial components of the induction are given by

\begin{split}{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}&=i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\left(k_{R}-k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\right)\\
&\times\Big[B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,v_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}(t)-v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,B_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}(t)\Big],\\
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}R}&=-\frac{k_{z}}{k_{R}}{\cal E}_{{\bf k}z}\\
&=-ik_{z}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\Big[B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,v_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)-v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\,B_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}(t)\Big].\end{split}

Plugging in the linear perturbations from equations (
8
) in the above equation, and defining
s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=(c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})/2
and
d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}=(c^{+}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-c^{-}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}})/2
, we can rewrite the toroidal induction in the following concise form:

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left(k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}-k_{R}\right)\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}\;\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)+i{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)}{\omega^{2}_{j\,{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(42)

Similarly, the radial induction can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}R}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{z}}{2}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}\;\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\,\delta v_{{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}R}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{i,j}\left[{\cal R}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)+i{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)\right],

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(43)

Since
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{\bf k}=i{\bf k}\times\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}
, we have

{\cal E}_{{\bf k}R}=-ik_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\phi}
,

{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}=i\left(k_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}-k_{R}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}\right)
,
and

The emf
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\bf k}=\left(\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R},\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\phi},\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}\right)
can thus be trivially obtained from the above expression for the induction.

Appendix B
Computation of the dynamo cycle period

The cycle period depends on the frequency difference between
the two eigenfrequencies,
\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}=\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, in the MRI stable regime,
{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}/\Omega\gtrsim 1
(see section
III.4
). Here we derive the large-wavenumber expansion of this frequency difference or beat frequency, which yields a simple expression for the cycle period. Define

x\equiv\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega},\quad\mu\equiv\frac{k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}},\quad s\equiv\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}.

(44)

The two branches may be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime},2{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}

\displaystyle=x^{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}s}{2}\left[1\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{16x^{2}}{\mu^{2}s^{2}}}\right],

(45)

where
\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
denote the larger and smaller positive frequencies. Expanding these in the limit of large
x
(MRI stable regime) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\Omega}

\displaystyle=x+\mu+\frac{\mu^{2}}{x}\left(\frac{s}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{\mu^{3}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{4}+\frac{s^{2}}{32}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mu^{4}}{x^{3}}\left(\frac{s^{2}-6s+10}{16}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu^{5}}{x^{4}}\left(\frac{7}{8}-\frac{5s}{8}+\frac{9s^{2}}{64}-\frac{s^{3}}{128}-\frac{s^{4}}{2048}\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-5}),

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\Omega}

\displaystyle=x-\mu+\frac{\mu^{2}}{x}\left(\frac{s}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{\mu^{3}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{4}+\frac{s^{2}}{32}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mu^{4}}{x^{3}}\left(\frac{s^{2}-6s+10}{16}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mu^{5}}{x^{4}}\left(\frac{7}{8}-\frac{5s}{8}+\frac{9s^{2}}{64}-\frac{s^{3}}{128}-\frac{s^{4}}{2048}\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-5}).

(47)

The common
x^{-1}
and
x^{-3}
terms cancel in the difference. Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\mu\Omega\,\Bigg[1+\frac{\mu^{2}}{4x^{2}}\left(2-s+\frac{s^{2}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu^{4}}{x^{4}}\left(\frac{7}{8}-\frac{5s}{8}+\frac{9s^{2}}{64}-\frac{s^{3}}{128}-\frac{s^{4}}{2048}\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-6})\Bigg].

(48)

Restoring
s=\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}
and

x=({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A})/\Omega
, this becomes

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}}\Omega\,\Bigg[1+\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 2}}{4k^{\prime 2}}{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-2}\left(2-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{4}}{8\Omega^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 4}}{k^{\prime 4}}{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-4}\left(\frac{7}{8}-\frac{5}{8}\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{9}{64}\frac{\kappa^{4}}{\Omega^{4}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\frac{1}{128}\frac{\kappa^{6}}{\Omega^{6}}-\frac{1}{2048}\frac{\kappa^{8}}{\Omega^{8}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left[{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-6}\right]\Bigg].

(49)

We next write this in terms of the shear parameter

q\equiv-{\rm d}\ln\Omega/{\rm d}\ln R
, for which

\kappa^{2}=(4-2q)\Omega^{2}
. The two correction coefficients become

2-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{4}}{8\Omega^{4}}=\frac{q^{2}}{2},

(50)

and

\displaystyle\frac{7}{8}-\frac{5}{8}\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{9}{64}\frac{\kappa^{4}}{\Omega^{4}}-\frac{1}{128}\frac{\kappa^{6}}{\Omega^{6}}-\frac{1}{2048}\frac{\kappa^{8}}{\Omega^{8}}=\frac{q^{3}(16-q)}{128}.

(51)

Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}}\Omega\,\Bigg[1+\frac{q^{2}}{8}\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime 2}}{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{q^{3}(16-q)}{128}\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 4}}{k^{\prime 4}}{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-4}+\mathcal{O}\!\left[{\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)}^{-6}\right]\Bigg].

(52)

We now evaluate the finite-
k^{\prime}
correction at
k^{\prime}=2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
(this is roughly the lowest
k^{\prime}
that contributes to the quasi-periodic emf; see section
III.4
). The marginal
MRI condition gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(4-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right)\frac{k_{z}^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime 2}}=2\mu^{2}q.

(53)

Therefore, at
k^{\prime}=2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
,
x^{2}=8\mu^{2}q
. Substituting this into
equation (
52
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{\rm c}\equiv\Delta\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\big|_{k^{\prime}=2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}}

\displaystyle=2\mu\Omega\left[1+\frac{q}{64}+\frac{q(16-q)}{8192}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mu\Omega\left[1+\frac{q(144-q)}{8192}\right].

(54)

The asymptotic value is
\Delta\omega_{\infty}=2\mu\Omega
. Thus, with

t_{\rm orb}=2\pi/\Omega
, the two oscillation periods of the emf (see section
III.4
) are given by

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{\left|\Delta\omega_{\infty}-\Delta\omega_{\rm c}\right|}=\frac{8192}{q(144-q)}\frac{k^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,t_{\rm orb},

(55)

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{\Delta\omega_{\infty}+\Delta\omega_{\rm c}}=\frac{8192}{16384+q(144-q)}\frac{k^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,t_{\rm orb}.

(56)

For a mode geometry characterized by
k^{\prime}_{R}/k^{\prime}_{z}\simeq k_{R}/k_{z}=a
, we have

k^{\prime}/k^{\prime}_{z}\simeq\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
. More generally, the geometry factor may be
written as

\frac{k^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}=\mathcal{A}(a)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1+a^{2}},&{\bf k}^{\prime}\parallel{\bf k},\\[3.0pt]
\sqrt{1+a^{-2}},&{\bf k}^{\prime}\perp{\bf k}.\end{cases}

(57)

Therefore

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 1}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{8192}{q(144-q)}\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb},

(58)

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 2}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{8192}{16384+q(144-q)}\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb}.

(59)

For Keplerian shear,
q=3/2
, these become

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 1}

\displaystyle\simeq 38.3\,\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb},

(60)

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 2}

\displaystyle\simeq 0.494\,\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb}.

(61)

For comparison, the periods may also be computed directly from the unexpanded
branch frequencies. At
k^{\prime}=2k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, define

s=4-2q,\quad\chi(q)=\left[1+\frac{128q}{s^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(62)

The beat frequency at this scale is

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{\rm c}^{\rm ex}

\displaystyle=\mu\Omega\,\Bigg[\left(8q+\frac{s}{2}\left[1+\chi(q)\right]\right)^{1/2}-\left(8q+\frac{s}{2}\left[1-\chi(q)\right]\right)^{1/2}\Bigg].

(63)

Writing
\Delta\omega_{\rm c}^{\rm ex}=\mu\Omega F(q)
, where

\displaystyle F(q)

\displaystyle\equiv\left(8q+\frac{s}{2}\left[1+\chi(q)\right]\right)^{1/2}-\left(8q+\frac{s}{2}\left[1-\chi(q)\right]\right)^{1/2},

(64)

the exact beat periods are

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 1}^{\rm ex}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|F(q)-2|}\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb},

(65)

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 2}^{\rm ex}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{F(q)+2}\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb}.

(66)

For Keplerian shear,
q=3/2
, the exact values are

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 1}^{\rm ex}

\displaystyle\simeq 37.6\,\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb},

(67)

\displaystyle T_{\Delta 2}^{\rm ex}

\displaystyle\simeq 0.493\,\mathcal{A}(a)\,t_{\rm orb}.

(68)

Appendix C
Computation of the dynamo coefficients

Since we are interested in the large-scale dynamo, we can perform a Taylor series expansion of the emf in
{\bf k}
. In other words, we can expand the induction
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}}
as

\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}}=\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}}^{(0)}+\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}}^{(1)}+\cdots
,
where
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}}^{(i)}
is
\left(i+1\right)^{(\rm th)}
order in
{\bf k}
. The corresponding emf is
i^{(\rm th)}
order in
{\bf k}
.

C.1
\alpha
dynamo

Taking
k\lesssim k^{\prime}
in
{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}
,
{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, noting that
{\bf Q}_{1-{\bf k}^{\prime}}={\bf Q}^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}
for
{\bf Q}={\bf v}
or
{\bf B}
,
s_{i-{\bf k}^{\prime}}=s^{\ast}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
d_{i-{\bf k}^{\prime}}=-d^{\ast}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, we obtain the following leading order contribution to the
\phi
induction:

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}^{(0)}_{{\bf k}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle={\bf B}\cdot\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left(k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}-k_{R}\right)\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\;{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)+i{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)}{\omega^{2}_{j\,{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(69)

It is not hard to see that, since
{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}
is even in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
, and so are
s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}
or
d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, the integrand for the real part is odd in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
and thus the real part is zero. The imaginary part involves the cross term between
s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}
, which makes it odd in
{\bf k}^{\prime}
. The already existing
{\bf k}^{\prime}
factor in the integrand then makes it even, and we have a non-zero imaginary part. And, since
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}\phi}=i\left(k_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}R}-k_{R}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}z}\right)
, we have
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
a tensor, whose components are given by

\begin{split}\alpha_{RR}&=\alpha_{zR}\\
&=\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{j\,{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}\,{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right),\\
\alpha_{Rz}&=\alpha_{zz}\\
&=\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,k^{\prime}_{z}\,{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{j\,{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}\,{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right).\end{split}

These are not the only non-zero components of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. In equation (
43
) for the radial induction, similar arguments as above yield the real (imaginary) part zero (non-zero). Noting that
{\cal E}_{{\bf k}R}=-ik_{z}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\phi}
and comparing it to equation (
43
), we can obtain the remaining non-zero components of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
:

\begin{split}\alpha_{\phi R}&=-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right),\\
\alpha_{\phi z}&=-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,k^{\prime}_{z}\,{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right),\end{split}

(70)

where
{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}
is given by

\begin{split}&{\cal I}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)\\
&=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]\\
&\quad-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].\end{split}

Now let us try to connect
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to known quantities. We will show that
\alpha_{ij}
(
i,j=R
or
z
) can be approximately expressed in terms of the mean kinetic helicity
\left<{\bf v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}\right>
and the mean current helicity
\left<{\bf J}_{1}\cdot{\bf B}_{1}\right>/(4\pi\rho_{0})
. Upon substituting the linear eigenfunctions, the mean kinetic helicity can be expressed as

\begin{split}{\cal H}_{\rm kin}&=i\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\bf v}^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cdot\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\times{\bf v}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\\
&=-2\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,{\rm Im}\left(v^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right)\\
&=\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}\\
&\times\left[-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}+\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right]\\
&\times\Big[\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]\\
&+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]\Big].\end{split}

Similarly, the mean current helicity can be written as

\begin{split}{\cal H}_{\rm cur}&=\frac{i}{4\pi\rho_{0}}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\bf B}^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\cdot\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\times{\bf B}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\\
&=-\frac{2}{4\pi\rho_{0}}\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}\,{\rm Im}\left(B^{\ast}_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right)\\
&=-2\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}\\
&\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}\left(\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}\right)}\\
&\times\Big[\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]\\
&-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]\Big].\end{split}

Now, the difference between these two helicities is given by

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\rm cur}-{\cal H}_{\rm kin}

\displaystyle=2\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\cal G}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}^{(\phi)}({\bf B},t),

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}^{(\phi)}({\bf B},t)

\displaystyle=-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)t\right].

(71)

Noting that
\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}\approx{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}
at large enough
{\bf k}^{\prime}
and
\omega_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}}<\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}
for all
{\bf k}^{\prime}
, it is not difficult to see by comparing the above equation to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
in equations (
C.1
) and
{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}
in equations (
69
), that

\displaystyle\alpha_{RR}+\alpha_{Rz}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{RR}+\alpha_{zz}=\alpha_{zR}+\alpha_{Rz}=\alpha_{zR}+\alpha_{zz}

\displaystyle\sim\int{\rm d}t\,\left({\cal H}_{\rm cur}-{\cal H}_{\rm kin}\right).

(72)

Similarly, it is not hard to see that
\alpha_{\phi R}
and
\alpha_{\phi z}
are related to but not quite equal to the radial part of the mean cross helicity
\left<{\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf B}_{1}\right>
,
\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,{\rm Re}\left(v_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}^{\ast}B_{1{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right)
.

C.2
\beta
dynamo

Let us now obtain the next order contribution,
\boldsymbol{{\cal E}}^{(1)}_{{\bf k}}
. The toroidal induction at this order is given by

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}^{(1)}_{{\bf k}\phi}

\displaystyle\approx\Omega\int{\rm d}{\bf k}^{\prime}\,\left(k_{z}\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}-k_{R}\right)\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf B}\sum_{n=1}^{3}{\cal J}^{(n)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right),

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{(1)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k_{l}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{(2)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-k_{l}\,{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}/\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}{{\left(\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}\right)}^{2}}{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right),

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{(3)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{l}}{2}\,{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}+\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{\ast 2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right){\cal S}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right).

(73)

where

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{\ast}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[1-\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\Bigg[\Bigg(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}k_{l}\Bigg)t\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{\ast}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[1+\frac{\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{2\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}-\frac{{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\Bigg[\Bigg(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}k_{l}\Bigg)t\Bigg],

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\Bigg[\Bigg(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}k_{l}\Bigg)t\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\!\Bigg[\Bigg(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}k_{l}\Bigg)t\Bigg].

(74)

Note that the superscript in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\omega^{2}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{R}}

\displaystyle=\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)v_{{\rm A}R}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}k^{\prime 2}_{z}}{2k^{\prime 4}}\,\kappa^{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{16\,k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime 2}_{z}}\frac{\Omega^{2}{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\kappa^{4}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\pm\frac{4\Omega^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\left(\dfrac{k^{\prime}_{R}}{k^{\prime 2}}\,{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}+v_{{\rm A}R}\right)}{\sqrt{1+\dfrac{16\,k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime 2}_{z}}\dfrac{\Omega^{2}{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\kappa^{4}}}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\omega^{2}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{z}}

\displaystyle=\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)v_{{\rm A}z}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{k^{\prime}_{z}k^{\prime 2}_{R}}{2k^{\prime 4}}\,\kappa^{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{16\,k^{\prime 2}}{k^{\prime 2}_{z}}\frac{\Omega^{2}{\left({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}\right)}^{2}}{\kappa^{4}}}\right)

\begin{split}\frac{k_{l}}{2}\frac{\partial\omega^{2}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}&=\left(k_{z}k^{\prime}_{R}-k_{R}k^{\prime}_{z}\right)\frac{k^{\prime}_{R}k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime 4}}\\

\displaystyle{\bf{\cal E}}_{{\bf k}\phi}

\displaystyle\approx\Omega\;\frac{{\left(k_{z}B_{R}-k_{R}B_{z}\right)}^{2}}{B_{z}}\,{\cal N}_{\phi}\sum_{n=1}^{3}{\cal L}^{(n)}_{{\bf k}\phi}\left({\bf B},t\right),

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i,j}\frac{{\cal R}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}k^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)}{\omega^{2}_{jk^{\prime}}-k^{\prime 2}v^{2}_{\rm A}},

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}+\frac{k^{\prime 2}v^{2}_{\rm A}}{\omega^{\ast 2}_{jk^{\prime}}}\frac{{\rm d}\ln\Omega}{{\rm d}\ln R}\right){\cal K}_{k^{\prime}j}\left({\bf B}\right){\cal S}^{(\phi)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right).

We can rewrite the radial induction

\displaystyle=-k_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}{\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}{\cal R}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}({\bf B},t),

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{(2)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}R}({\bf B},t)

\displaystyle={\left|\delta v_{{\bf k}^{\prime}R}\right|}^{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{k_{l}}{\omega^{2}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}{\cal S}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right),

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{(R)}_{{\bf k}{\bf k}^{\prime}ij}\left({\bf B},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sin\!\left[\left(\omega_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}+\frac{\partial\omega_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}}{\partial k^{\prime}_{l}}k_{l}\right)t\right]

\displaystyle+\left(s_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{i{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{j{\bf k}^{\prime}}\right)

{\bf k}^{\prime}

is equal to

of

dependence of the emf here arises from the axisymmetric modes, while the

.

C.3
Magnetic helicity conservation and saturation

The magnetic helicity flux places additional constraints on the dynamo coefficients. The helicity constraint is best understood by separating the large- and small-scale magnetic helicities. For a mean-field split,
{\bf B}_{\rm tot}={\bf B}+\delta{\bf B}
and the vector potential
{\bf A}_{\rm tot}={\bf A}+\delta{\bf A}
, the corresponding helicity equations are schematically

\displaystyle\partial_{t}({\bf A}\cdot{\bf B})=2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf B}-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm LS}-2\eta\,{\bf J}\cdot{\bf B},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\delta{\bf A}\cdot\delta{\bf B}\rangle=-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf B}-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}-2\eta\,\langle\delta{\bf J}\cdot\delta{\bf B}\rangle,

(85)

where
\eta
is the resistivity,
\delta{\bf J}=(c/4\pi)\nabla\times\delta{\bf B}
is the current fluctuation, and
\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm LS}
and
\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}
are the large-scale (LS) and small-scale (SS) helicity fluxes. Thus, if the small-scale helicity has reached a statistically steady state and resistive dissipation is negligible, the parallel emf must be balanced by a small-scale helicity flux,

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf B}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{\cal F}}_{\rm SS}.

This imposes the constraint

{\bf B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot{\bf B}=0

on the zeroth-order
\alpha
contribution to the emf, since it does not involve any derivatives of
{\bf B}
and thus cannot be written as a total divergence. For the MRI
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
tensor derived above, this condition is

(B_{R}+B_{z})(\alpha_{RR}B_{R}+\alpha_{Rz}B_{z})+B_{\phi}(\alpha_{\phi R}B_{R}+\alpha_{\phi z}B_{z})=0,

where we used
\alpha_{RR}=\alpha_{zR}
and
\alpha_{Rz}=\alpha_{zz}
. Therefore the tensor
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
need not vanish; rather, its contraction with the mean field must vanish. Since
B_{\phi}
is typically much larger than
B_{R}
and
B_{z}
in the MRI dynamo, even small
\alpha_{\phi R}
and
\alpha_{\phi z}
components, as in the unstratified case, can allow this helicity constraint to be satisfied.

If
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is non-zero, then the helicity constraint provides the relative saturated ratio of the toroidal and poloidal components:

\displaystyle\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}+B_{z}}=-\frac{\alpha_{RR}B_{R}+\alpha_{Rz}B_{z}}{\alpha_{\phi R}B_{R}+\alpha_{\phi z}B_{z}}.

(86)

This toroidal-to-poloidal ratio is controlled by the relative amplitudes of

{\cal I}^{(\phi)}_{ij}/[\omega_{j}^{2}-({\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A})^{2}]
and

{\cal I}^{(R)}_{ij}
. Near marginal stability,
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}\to 0
.
If the slow–slow helicity combination

H^{+}_{22}\equiv s_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}d^{\ast}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}-d_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}s^{\ast}_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}

survives, the
22
term is the most singular and gives

\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}+B_{z}}\right|_{22}\approx\frac{1}{4}\left(4-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right)\frac{\Omega}{\omega_{\rm br}},

where
\omega_{\rm br}=\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}((1+\epsilon)k^{\prime}_{\rm c})
is the broadened version of
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}(k^{\prime}_{\rm c})=0
. Writing

\Delta k^{\prime}=\epsilon k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, we take

\omega_{\rm br}^{2}\approx(\partial\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}/\partial k^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}_{\rm c}}\Delta k^{\prime}
. The width

\epsilon
may be estimated by equating the linear and quadratic terms in
the expansion of
\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}
about
k^{\prime}_{\rm c}
,

(\partial\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}/\partial k^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}_{\rm c}}\Delta k^{\prime}\approx(\partial^{2}\omega_{2{\bf k}^{\prime}}^{2}/\partial k^{\prime 2})_{k^{\prime}_{\rm c}}(\Delta k^{\prime})^{2}/2
,
which gives

\epsilon=\frac{2\left(8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}\right)^{2}+64}\approx 1.

Using

\frac{{\bf k}^{\prime}_{\rm c}\cdot{\bf v}_{\rm A}}{\Omega}=\left(4-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}},\quad\frac{k^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}_{z}}=\sqrt{1+a^{2}},

the surviving-
H^{+}_{22}
estimate becomes

\displaystyle\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}+B_{z}}\right|_{22}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}{4}\left[\frac{8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}{8}\left[\frac{\left(8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}\right)^{2}+64}{8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\approx 0.47\sqrt{1+a^{2}},\quad\kappa=\Omega.

(87)

Since
B_{R}\approx B_{z}
at saturation (as seen from the Athena++ simulations), we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}}\right|\approx\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{z}}\right|\approx\sqrt{1+a^{2}}.

(88)

If instead
H^{+}_{22}
vanishes, the leading
singular pieces are the cross-branch terms. Defining

H_{12}^{2}\equiv|H^{-}_{12}|^{2}+|H^{+}_{12}|^{2}
and similarly for
H_{21}
,
one finds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{B_{\phi}}{B_{R}+B_{z}}\right|_{12/21}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}{\left(8-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}\right)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\left[\left(4-\kappa^{2}/\Omega^{2}\right)/4\right]^{2}H_{12}^{2}+H_{21}^{2}}{H_{12}^{2}+H_{21}^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(89)

Thus both cases give a toroidal-to-poloidal ratio of order

\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
, with an order-unity prefactor set by

\kappa/\Omega
, the marginal-mode broadening, and the relative
cross-branch helicity amplitudes. Even if the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
term in the emf is typically small compared to the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
term for unstratified MRI, a non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is required for the saturation of the toroidal-to-poloidal ratio via helicity conservation. It should be noted, though, that above estimation is only an order-of-magnitude, approximate one.

The next order term in the emf, which is the shear current term
\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\bf J}
, is proportional to
J_{{\bf k}\phi}=i(k_{z}B_{R}-k_{R}B_{z})
, and can therefore be recast as a total divergence form (in the Fourier space). The same can be said of the higher order terms which typically scale as powers of
J_{{\bf k}\phi}
. Therefore, the quasilinear emf satisfies the conservation of magnetic helicity.
