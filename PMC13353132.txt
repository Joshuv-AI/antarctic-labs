Alternative Breakfast Service Models Are Associated With Greater Student School Breakfast Participation. Evidence From Middle and High School Students Across 8 States
Authors: Monica D. Zuercher, Dania Orta‐Aleman, Cailin D. French, Chrisina A. Hecht, Kenneth Hecht, Juliana F. W. Cohen, Punam Ohri‐Vachaspati, Anisha I. Patel, Lorrene D. Ritchie, Wendi Gosliner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEating school breakfast has benefits for students, yet participation remains lower than that of school lunch. This study examined associations between school breakfast service models, self‐reported participation, and perceived participation barriers, and whether these associations differed by school level (middle vs. high) and universal school meals policy (with vs. without).MethodsOne thousand ninety‐seven middle and high school students were surveyed. Data were analyzed using multilevel logistic regression.ResultsMost students (67%) reported access only to traditional before‐school breakfast. Higher odds of participation and lower odds of time‐related barriers were found for breakfast in the classroom (OR 3.88; 95% CI 2.49, 6.03) and “second chance” breakfast (OR 2.07; 95% CI 1.43, 3.00) compared to traditional before‐school breakfast only. Grab‐and‐go options were associated with modestly higher odds of participation (OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.01, 1.67) and fewer barriers among high school students.Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and EquityBreakfast after the bell approaches is associated with higher participation and fewer barriers. These strategies can help ensure more students have reliable access to school breakfast.ConclusionsAlternative school breakfast service models, particularly breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast, may increase participation and support student nutrition, health, and academic performance.

Introduction

Eating breakfast consistently is associated with better diet quality and positive impacts on cognitive function, memory, attention, and overall academic performance for children and adolescents [1, 2, 3]. The School Breakfast Program is a United States (US) federal meal program that requires participating schools to offer breakfast meeting prescribed federal nutrition standards for free or at a reduced price to all students deemed eligible through means‐testing, based on their family's income, while others may purchase breakfast meals at the full subsidized price [4, 5]. Participation in the School Breakfast Program has been associated with reduced absenteeism and tardiness, improved classroom behavior, and better food security [6, 7, 8, 9]. During the 2023–2024 school year, an average of more than 15.5 million students participated in the School Breakfast Program each school day nationwide [10]. While nearly all schools participating in the National School Lunch Program also participate in the School Breakfast Program, participation in the School Breakfast Program represents only 52% of National School Lunch Program participants [11]. This participation gap between school breakfast and lunch participation far exceeds any difference in school‐level program adoption, raising questions about whether specific barriers might prevent some students from accessing breakfast meals that may benefit them.

Serving breakfast in the cafeteria before school begins has been the most widely implemented model in US schools. However, the timing and location constraints of this approach create barriers to student participation [12, 13, 14]. Previous studies have identified common reasons why students skip school breakfast, such as not arriving on time, late bus schedules, and not feeling hungry before school [12, 15, 16]. To address some of these participation barriers, schools have implemented alternative breakfast service models (hereafter referred to as breakfast models), including serving breakfast in the classroom, “second chance” breakfast, and offering “grab and go” options [12, 13]. In the breakfast in the classroom model, meals are served to students in their classrooms at the beginning of the school day [13, 17]. In the “second chance” breakfast model, meals are offered in the cafeteria or elsewhere after the school day begins, frequently during a morning nutrition break or between classes [13, 17]. Grab‐and‐go breakfast options are typically pre‐packaged breakfast meals available for pickup from cafeterias or hallway kiosks, which students can eat while walking to or in class [13, 17]. Grab‐and‐go options are not a standalone breakfast model, but rather represent a food service strategy that can be incorporated into any model. Grab‐and‐go options have become increasingly popular as a strategy to increase breakfast participation [18].

Other significant barriers to school breakfast participation include the stigma associated with the perception that school breakfast is only for low‐income students, and meal costs that remain burdensome for both reduced‐price participants and families just above the eligibility threshold who are still struggling financially but have to pay the full price [12, 15, 16, 19]. Offering free breakfast to all students can increase participation by reducing the stigma associated with school breakfasts, removing the economic barriers, and facilitating breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast implementation by eliminating payment collection, eligibility verification, and meal tracking, allowing schools to deliver breakfast efficiently in classrooms or through grab‐and‐go stations [13, 20]. Providing free breakfast to all students can be accomplished through local, regional, statewide, or federal universal school meal (USM) policies or through the Community Eligibility Provision and Provisions 1–3, which allow high‐poverty schools to offer school meals free of charge to all students. Currently, nine US states offer USM programs that include breakfast and lunch [21], with others, including Pennsylvania and Arkansas, only offering breakfast free of charge to all students [13, 22]. USM policies have consistently been associated with increased breakfast participation [19]. For example, national data have shown that most US states experienced a reduction in breakfast participation after returning to means‐tested school meals when the COVID‐era waivers, which made meals free for all students, ended [13]. However, states that continued to offer free meals to all students through statewide USM programs, or in a significant number of their schools through high Community Eligibility Provision adoption, saw an increase in participation [13]. Despite evidence on the benefits of USM [23, 24, 25, 26], no research has compared the implementation of breakfast models between states with and without statewide USM policies, an important question given that USM may interact with breakfast delivery models by removing economic barriers for students and administrative barriers for schools that constrain breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast implementation.

Prior studies have examined differences in breakfast model implementation and participation rates among elementary, middle, and high school levels. This research has shown that elementary schools are more likely to offer breakfast in the classroom than middle and high schools, whereas middle and high schools are more likely to offer “second chance” breakfast and grab‐and‐go options [14, 20]. Participation in school breakfast has been reported to be higher among elementary school students than among middle and high school students [16, 27], yet middle and high school students face distinct scheduling and social barriers that may shape how breakfast models affect participation. However, most studies exploring breakfast models have been conducted in elementary schools, leaving it unclear whether findings about breakfast in the classroom, “second chance” breakfast, or grab‐and‐go options generalize to older students [8].

To address these research gaps, this study provides what is, to our knowledge, the first multistate examination of school breakfast models and student participation that simultaneously accounts for USM policy status and school level among middle and high school students. Specifically, the first aim of this study was to assess whether the different school breakfast models reported by middle and high school students in states with and without USM policies were associated with student self‐reported participation and selected barriers to participation. Additionally, a second aim was to assess whether student‐reported prevalence of implementing different breakfast models varied by school level (middle and high) and by USM policy status (with and without).

Methods

Participants

This analysis was part of a broader cross‐sectional study evaluating how various stakeholders—including food service directors, parents, and students—in eight US states view school meal programs. Data collection took place during the 2022–2023 school year, following the expiration of the federal government's temporary nationwide funding for USM, which was implemented during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Among the eight states included, four had implemented statewide USM policies (California, Maine, Massachusetts, and Vermont) following the pandemic, one had a policy to eliminate co‐pays for reduced‐price meals (Arizona), while the other three reverted to means‐tested meal provision (Illinois, New Hampshire, and Texas). Although Arizona was considered a hybrid state, as it did not fully implement USM or means‐tested meal provision, its relatively low percentage of students eligible for reduced‐price meals (in 2023, approximately 7% of the breakfast meals in Arizona were served to students eligible for reduced‐price meals) [28]. made it more similar to states without USM policies. Therefore, Arizona was categorized as a “no‐USM” state for analyses comparing states with and without USM policies.

In spring 2023, parents participating in a national research panel [29] were invited to complete an online survey about school meals and were asked to provide consent for one of their children in grades 6–12 to participate in a separate student survey. During summer 2023, students whose caregivers had granted consent were contacted via email or text and invited to complete an online survey about their experiences with school meals. Participating students received a $20 gift card as compensation.

We received survey responses from 1230 middle and high school students attending public or charter schools. In states where USM programs had ended (i.e., reverted to a means‐tested provision), students were not eligible to participate if they attended schools that continued to offer free meals to all students through Community Eligibility Provision or Provisions 1–3 during the 2022–2023 school year. Conversely, in states providing USM, students were not eligible to participate if their schools had participated in Community Eligibility Provision or Provisions 1–3 prior to the 2019–2020 school year. Quotas were established in each state to ensure diverse representation across various school levels (middle or high), race, ethnicity, eligibility for free or reduced‐price meals (FRPM), and geographic region. The current analysis excluded students from schools that did not offer breakfast (n = 127) or who reported not knowing the breakfast model implemented in the school (n = 6). The analytic sample included 1097 survey responses.

Instrumentation

The survey included a combination of validated items when available as well as newly developed, pilot‐tested questions [30, 31]. Questions included five screening items to determine eligibility for FRPM and collect demographic data, and 35 questions focused on students' school meal experiences during the 2022–2023 school year. The survey was available in both English and Spanish and took approximately 20 min to complete. It was programmed in Qualtrics (Qualtrics Version March 2022, Provo, UT) and could be accessed via smartphone, computer, or tablet.

Procedure

School breakfast models: Students reported how breakfast was offered at their school and whether grab‐and‐go options were available in response to the question, “How is breakfast offered at your school? (mark all that apply).” Response options included (1) breakfast served in the cafeteria before the start of school, (2) “grab and go” options (packaged, carry‐away options), (3) breakfast served in the classroom, (4) “second chance” breakfast (available after school begins or during first period but not served in the classroom), and (5) other (write in). Based on these responses, dichotomous variables were created for each breakfast model, with “1” indicating that the model was offered (either alone or in combination with other models) and “0” indicating that it was not. These variables were not mutually exclusive.

When examining associations between students' self‐reported school breakfast models, meal participation, and selected barriers to participation, the traditional breakfast‐before‐the‐bell model (“offering breakfast before the bell only”) was used as the reference category. This model was selected because it is the most commonly offered and has been identified as having timing and location constraints that may limit participation [12, 13, 14]. Breakfast models were analyzed separately as “second chance” breakfast versus breakfast before the bell only, and breakfast in the classroom versus breakfast before the bell only. “Grab and go” options were analyzed independently as a binary variable (offered vs. not offered), regardless of breakfast model.

Breakfast participation: Students self‐reported their participation in school breakfast with the question, “On a typical school week, how often do you usually eat school breakfast?” Response options included every school day, 3–4 school days, 1–2 school days, and no days. For analysis purposes, breakfast participation was categorized into students who typically ate school breakfast three or more days per week and those who typically ate it fewer than 3 days per week, including those who never ate school breakfast.

Barriers to breakfast participation: The survey asked students to select the top three reasons they don't eat school breakfast from a list of 15 potential barriers. The current analysis focused on student selection of two barriers: not arriving at school early enough to eat breakfast (“I don't get to school early enough”) and insufficient time to eat (“there is not enough time”) as these barriers could be affected by the different school breakfast models.

Other breakfast questions: The survey also included other questions related to breakfast access and preferences, including (1) whether they arrive at school early enough to get a school breakfast, (2) whether they would prefer to have the option to eat school breakfast in their classroom, and (3) where they typically get breakfast on a school day.

Covariates and stratification variables: Covariates included FRPM eligibility (eligible for FRPM, near eligible for FRPM, and higher income), race and ethnicity, gender, and state. FRPM eligibility was determined using parents' reports of household size and annual household income and applying federal poverty level guidelines for the corresponding household size [32]. The percentages reported represent income relative to the federal poverty level. Students were classified as eligible for FRPM if their household income was ≤ 185% of the federal poverty level, near eligible for FRPM if their household income was between 186% and 230% of the federal poverty level, and higher income if their household income was ≥ 231% of the federal poverty level [32]. For analysis purposes, race and ethnicity were combined into four categories: Non‐Hispanic‐Black or African American, Hispanic or Latinx, Non‐Hispanic‐White, and Non‐Hispanic‐Other or multiracial (due to small sample sizes, this category included Asian or Asian American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, other race, and more than one racial category). For analysis purposes, gender was combined into three categories: girl, boy, and other gender or prefer not to state (due to small sample sizes, this category included non‐binary, transgender, genderqueer, and prefer not to state). Stratification variables included school level (middle or high school) and USM policy status (with or without).

Data Analysis

Participants' general characteristics were described using frequencies and percentages. School breakfast models in the overall sample were reported as unadjusted percentages. When stratified by school level and USM status, adjusted prevalence of school breakfast models were estimated using marginal standardization with multilevel mixed‐effects logistic regression models. These models were adjusted for school level, FRPM eligibility, race/ethnicity, gender, and included state as a random effect.

The association between students' self‐reported school breakfast models, meal participation, and participation barriers was evaluated with separate multilevel mixed‐effects logistic regression models adjusted for school level, FRPM eligibility, race/ethnicity, gender, and included state as a random effect. Potential effect modification by school level and USM status was assessed by including interaction terms in separate models, followed by stratified analyses when indicated. All statistical analyses were conducted in Stata (Stata Statistical Software: Release 19, 2025; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX), using a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

A little more than half of the students who responded to the survey were from states without USM policies (59%), more than a third were non‐Hispanic‐White (37%), and about a third were Hispanic (34%) (Table 1). Responses were nearly equally distributed among middle and high school students (53% vs. 47%, respectively), students eligible for FRPM and those from higher‐income households (38% vs. 39%), and girls and boys (47% vs. 51%). Most students ate school breakfast fewer than 3 days per week (55%), arrived at school early enough to get a school breakfast (69%), preferred to have the option to eat school breakfast in the classroom (52%), and reported eating breakfast at home (64%). Students in states with USM policies were more likely to be in high school, eat school breakfast daily, and live in households with higher income (Table 1).

School Breakfast Models

The most reported school breakfast model was breakfast served in the cafeteria before the start of school (82%), and it was more common in states without USM policies (84%) than in states with them (75%) (Table 2). “Second chance” breakfast was reported to be available by 14% of students, and the adjusted prevalence was higher in high schools (19%) than in middle schools (14%). Breakfast served in the classroom was reported by 12% of students, with no statistically significant differences by school level (p = 0.15) or USM status (p = 0.75). Fewer than half of students reported that their school offers “grab and go” breakfast options (43%), with no statistically significant differences by school level or USM status.

School Breakfast Models and Student Participation

Students attending schools offering “second chance” breakfast had two times the odds of reporting eating school breakfast frequently than students in schools offering breakfast before the bell only (OR 2.07; 95% CI 1.43, 3.00), and 43% lower odds of reporting that they don't eat school breakfast because they don't get to school early enough (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.37, 0.90). The interactions with school level or USM status were not statistically significant (Table 4).

Students attending schools that served breakfast in the classroom had more than three times higher odds of reporting eating school breakfast frequently than students in schools offering breakfast before the bell only (OR 3.88; 95% CI 2.49, 6.03). Moreover, offering breakfast in the classroom was associated with fewer students reporting that they do not eat school breakfast because they do not get to school early enough (OR 0.54; 95% CI 0.32, 0.92) (Table 3); however, a significant interaction with school level was found (p = 0.02) (Table 4), and stratified analyses showed that this association was only statistically significant among high school students (OR 0.27; 95% CI 0.10, 0.70), but not among middle school students (OR 0.84; 95% CI 0.43, 1.64) (Table S1). The interactions with USM status were not statistically significant (Table 4).

Discussion

Students in this analysis reported that breakfast served in the cafeteria before the start of school was the most common way in which their school offered breakfast. For most students, this was their only option for eating breakfast at school, despite nearly one‐third reporting that they arrived too late to participate in this model and more than half expressing a preference for classroom breakfast options. Implementation of breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast in our analysis was low, with less than 15% of students reporting that these options were available at their schools. “Grab and go” breakfast options were somewhat common, with just over 40% of the students reporting that their school offers these options.

In this analysis, alternative breakfast models were associated with greater student school breakfast participation. Students with access to breakfast in the classroom, “second chance” breakfast, and “grab and go” options had higher participation rates and reported fewer participation barriers, indicating that these approaches could address the structural constraints that limit traditional school breakfast participation. These results align with previous research in California elementary schools, which found that schools serving breakfast in the cafeteria had the highest proportion of students not eating breakfast, compared with schools serving breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast [33]. It is important to acknowledge that breakfast model availability was based on student self‐report rather than administrative or school‐level verification. Students may not have fully understood or accurately identified the specific breakfast models implemented at their schools, which could have resulted in misclassification and attenuated observed associations. This limitation may be particularly relevant for models such as “grab‐and‐go,” which may be offered in conjunction with multiple breakfast service approaches.

Of these alternative breakfast models, breakfast in the classroom was most strongly associated with student participation; students attending schools with this breakfast model reported over three times the odds of frequently participating compared to those attending schools without this option. These results corroborate findings from previous studies in which breakfast in the classroom has been identified as the breakfast model associated with the highest participation rates [33, 34, 35]. Multiple factors have been reported to explain the success of breakfast in the classroom in increasing student participation, including removing the stigma associated with school breakfasts and making breakfast meals more accessible to students [20]. Moreover, breakfast in the classroom has been associated with reductions in food waste and improvements in dietary intake [8, 33, 36]. However, implementing this breakfast model can be challenging for schools due to the additional labor and costs associated with packaging, transporting, and cleaning up the breakfasts served outside the cafeteria, as well as the impact on classroom and instructional time, and opposition from teachers and school staff [17, 18, 37]. Moreover, breakfast in the classroom is offered less frequently in middle and high schools than in elementary or K–8 schools [14, 20]. These differences in implementation by school level may reflect additional challenges associated with implementing breakfast in the classroom in secondary schools, including the more complex structure of class schedules and student movement between classrooms.

In our analysis, “second chance” breakfast, in which a morning meal is served after the start of the school day but not in the classroom, was associated with two times the odds of students frequently participating in school breakfast, consistent with previous studies that reported that “second chance” breakfast was associated with increased participation among middle and high school students [20, 38]. Also consistent with the literature, in this analysis, “grab and go” options were associated with higher participation in school breakfast [20, 39, 40]; however, the magnitude of the association was smaller than that of breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast. The more modest effect size suggests that “grab and go” options may work best in tandem with alternative breakfast models. In addition to addressing the issues related to getting to school on time for breakfast, “second chance” breakfast and “grab and go” options offered at extended breakfast hours may also facilitate participation among students who might not be hungry when they first arrive at school in the morning (a situation that may also apply to breakfast in the classroom when it is not served at the beginning of the school day) [20]. Future studies should assess the quality of the foods offered in the different breakfast models, including “grab and go” options, to ensure that tradeoffs between healthfulness and convenience are not a concern. For example, if more students participate in school breakfasts in the classroom or when there are “grab and go” options, but those models make it more difficult for schools to serve fresh, healthy, minimally processed and highly nutritious options, this would be important to consider.

This study found some differences in the relationship between breakfast models offered and breakfast participation by school level. Offering breakfast in the classroom and access to “grab and go” options were associated with fewer reported barriers to breakfast participation due to not arriving at school early enough among high school students, but not among middle school students. This difference may reflect that this barrier is a greater issue for high school students, as indicated by its higher prevalence compared with middle school students (41% vs. 28%, respectively). These findings provide new evidence that the effectiveness of certain breakfast delivery strategies may differ by school level and highlight the need for additional research examining how breakfast models operate in middle and high school settings.

No statistically significant differences were found in the relationship between breakfast models offered and breakfast participation by USM policy status. Multiple factors that make it difficult for students to participate in breakfast before the bell have been identified [8, 33, 37]. While USM policies have been shown to reduce economic and stigma‐related barriers, logistical challenges addressed by breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast, such as early morning schedules, late arrivals, and bus timing, remain relevant across both policy contexts. Finally, schools in states with USM policies must still document which students are eligible for FRPM to maximize federal reimbursement. As a result, in the absence of a federal USM policy, state‐level USM policies may not fully eliminate the administrative barriers associated with breakfast in the classroom models since determining and documenting FRPM eligibility remains necessary in order to maximize federal reimbursement [41, 42]. In contrast, a federal USM policy could further reduce these administrative burdens [43], potentially facilitating broader adoption of breakfast in the classroom models and increasing student participation in healthy school breakfasts.

Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity

Findings from this analysis indicate that breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast are associated with higher participation and fewer participation barriers, whereas the traditional breakfast before the bell model creates barriers that limit student participation. These strategies can help ensure that all students, regardless of income or logistical challenges, can reliably access a nutritious school breakfast that supports their health and learning. In addition, attention should be paid to the nutritional quality of foods offered across models, so that convenience and participation gains do not come at the expense of healthfulness. Finally, the relatively low prevalence of breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast models observed in our sample, despite evidence of their effectiveness in increasing participation, underscores the need for continued efforts to better understand barriers and facilitators to their implementation.

Limitations

A strength of this study is that it provides updated prevalence estimates of school breakfast model implementation using representative samples from multiple US states and includes a large sample of students with racial and ethnic distributions comparable to national data across middle and high schools in states with and without USM policies [44]. One limitation is that the sample may not be fully representative of the national distribution of student eligibility for FRPM, as it included a lower proportion of students eligible for FRPM (38% of students in this study were eligible for FRPM versus 53% on the US) [45]. This likely resulted from the exclusion of schools implementing the CEP or Provisions 1–3 (those implementing it prior to SY 2019–2020 in states with USM and during SY 2022–2023 in states without USM), which may have disproportionately excluded lower‐income schools and thereby limited the generalizability of the findings. Another limitation is that the study's cross‐sectional design does not allow us to establish causality. Further, student participation and breakfast models were determined based on student reports rather than administrative data or in‐school observations. Additionally, there is a risk of potential misclassification of breakfast models due to ambiguity in the definition of breakfast models and the implementation of novel breakfast models in schools that may not fully align with the survey options provided. For example, in the survey, “second chance” breakfast was defined as available after school begins or during the first period but not served in the classroom. Another limitation is that the alternative breakfast models were relatively infrequent, creating small sample sizes that may have led to insignificant interaction terms impacting our ability to detect differences between breakfast models and USM status. Future studies should continue to assess this relationship. Further, we did not measure the quality of the foods being offered in various models and whether students were responding to different foods being available in these models rather than simply the timing, location, and convenience with which the food is being offered. However, some schools in California serve “brunch” after the second period, so our question could have misclassified that breakfast model. Misclassification of breakfast models is also possible when grab‐and‐go options are available, as some students may have selected only “grab and go” without identifying the underlying breakfast model through which the option was offered. Another limitation is that information on the specific school each student attended was not collected, which prevented us from assessing the consistency of reported breakfast models within schools. Moreover, our analysis focused exclusively on time‐related barriers to school breakfast participation and did not assess other important barriers, such as stigma or economic constraints, which may be alleviated by USM policies. Finally, although odds ratios were used due to the binary nature of the outcomes and their widespread application in epidemiologic research, they may overstate effect sizes when outcomes are common and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Students having access to alternative school breakfast models, particularly breakfast in the classroom and “second chance” breakfast, may be more likely to participate in school breakfast, as they face reduced participation barriers. However, the implementation of these breakfast models is low. Our findings suggest that alternative school breakfast models may increase student participation regardless of school level (middle or high) and USM policy status (with or without), which can support student nutrition, health, and academic performance.
