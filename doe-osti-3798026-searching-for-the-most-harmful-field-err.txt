 Abstract

 In this project, we improve beam stability for the Electron-Ion Collider. Magnetic field errors can
reduce beam stability, making it essential to identify the field errors that have the greatest impact on
accelerator performance. However, this is particularly challenging because beam stability depends on
the complex interactions of many magnetic field errors, resulting in a high-dimensional and nonlinear
optimization problem. We determine which field errors are the most influential for the large physical
aperture superconducting magnet B2PF, a critical magnet in the Interaction Region (IR) in the Hadron
Storage Ring (HSR). We complete and analyze nearly 30,000 simulations on the Brookhaven National
Laboratory Linux Cluster by varying 18 nonlinear magnetic field errors. We evaluate beam stability using
the dynamic aperture and the tune diffusion. We identify the field errors that most strongly influence
beam stability and establish quantitative field error tolerances that improve accelerator performance.

1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation for Studying the B2PF Magnet
The Electron-Ion Collider will study the internal structure of protons and nuclei with unprecedented pre-
cision. In order to achieve this feat, beam stability will be of utmost importance. Previous studies have
identified the B2PF magnet as the main contributor to reducing beam stability. This is because the beam
reaches its largest size at that magnet, making it prone to larger nonlinear field errors. These higher-order
field errors create nonlinear resonances and reduce the size of the dynamic aperture. In this project, we
identify which field errors in the B2PF magnet have the greatest impact on beam stability and provide the
optimal field error ranges to improve the dynamic aperture.

In this study, we investigate the 18 nonlinear magnetic field errors

 Our goal is to identify which of these field errors have the greatest impact on beam stability in the B2PF
magnet. This results in quite a challenging problem since beam stability depends on multiple nonlinear field
errors, creating a high-dimensional optimization problem.
 In order to achieve this, we first study the relationship between the field errors and the dynamic aper-
ture. Since dynamic aperture calculations are computationally expensive, we then demonstrate that the tune
diffusion can serve as an efficient substitute observable, allowing us to analyze a substantially larger dataset.
This new approach later allows us to identify the field errors that most strongly influence beam stability and
determine the field error ranges associated with improvement or harm in accelerator performance.

2 Using Dynamic Aperture as an Observable
The dynamic aperture, DA, is the maximum amplitude of particles in Phase Space below which the particles
will survive after a set amount of turns (typically 1, 000, 000) [2]. The goal would be to make DA as large
as possible. The dynamic aperture is measured in RMS beam size (σ).

2.1 Dynamic Aperture Calculation
In order to calculate the dynamic aperture, we need to find the boundary in which the particle is stable and
unstable. We define stable as the particle still being in orbit after 1, 000, 000 turns are completed. If the
particle is lost before the 1, 000, 000 turns are completed, then we would classify it as unstable. As seen in
Figure 1, we find this boundary by following the procedure:

 1. Check if the particle is stable at the origin ( σxx , σyy ) = (0, 0).

 2. If the particle is stable, increment a distance 0.1σ away from the origin at a 15◦ angle from the
 horizontal.

 3. Repeat step 2 until the particle is unstable.

 4. Once unstable, go back to the last increment in which the particle was stable. The distance between
 this point and the origin is the DA we use as our observable.

2.5 Limitations
Based on our findings in the previous section, we must produce a larger number of samples to improve the
accuracy of the machine learning model and, in general, be able to make more accurate conclusions. As
said before, directly calculating the dynamic aperture is computationally expensive, making it impractical
to generate the large datasets required for this study. By studying the field errors’ relationship with the tune
diffusion, also known as ∆Q, we will be able to produce sample data much faster and, consequently, a larger
sample size by orders of magnitude.

 In Section 3.5.3, we suggest that some field errors have a greater influence on the tune diffusion than
others. In order to quantify how influential a field error is, we introduce an ”influence score”. An influential
field error should satisfy two properties. First, the distributions corresponding to the Best and Worst tune dif-
fusion categories should be as separated as possible. A large difference between their mean values indicates

 15

that the field error shifts depending on beam stability. This suggests that the field error plays an important
role in determining the tune diffusion. Second, the field error should be tightly concentrated within the Best
category. If the lowest tune diffusions are only achieved when the field error is focused around a narrow
range of values, then small deviations would likely ruin beam stability. On the other hand, a large spread
implies that many values produce a similarly good tune diffusion, making the field error less influential.
Combining these two properties, we define the influence score as the separation between the mean values of
the Best and Worst categories, divided by the width of the densest 50% interval of the Best category:

4 Validation with Dynamic Aperture

diffusion corresponds to more predictable particle motion and, consequently, greater beam stability. In

diffusion. Since the dynamic aperture is an established way of measuring beam stability, it provides an

6 Outlook
Looking forward, we will investigate whether using resonance driving terms as the observable for beam
stability provides consistent results. Specifically, we want to confirm whether by using a different observ-
able, we reach the same conclusions about which field errors are the most influential and what their optimal
ranges are. An agreement between using resonance driving terms and tune diffusion as observables would
further validate the identified field error tolerances we provided.
 In addition, we will also investigate an improvement to the influence score. The current definition uses
the width of the densest 50% interval in the Best category. This assumes that the most relevant concentration
of a field error occurs at low tune diffusion. However, a field error may instead exhibit a narrow distribution
primarily in the Moderate or Worst categories. We will therefore investigate replacing

7 Conclusions
We used the dynamic aperture as an observable to identify the most dangerous field errors of the large
aperture superconducting magnet B2PF. It was extremely time consuming and led to inconclusive results
due to a small set of tracking data. Instead, we decided to use tune diffusion as an observable that is
more efficient to compute. We verified that tune diffusion can be used as an indicator of long-term beam
stability and, therefore, the dynamic aperture. We showed that a lower tune diffusion leads to a higher
dynamic aperture. For B2PF, we found that there is a correlation between the tune diffusion and low-
order field errors b3 , a3 , a4 , and b4 , and identified their optimal ranges to maximize dynamic aperture. We
independently verified these optimal ranges through systematic dynamic aperture scans of individual field
errors. These scans also identified b5 as the most harmful field error, as removing b5 produced the largest
dynamic aperture. We also revealed that there is a weak correlation between high-order field errors and tune
diffusion.
