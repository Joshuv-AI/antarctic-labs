 Overview
 National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) staff provided Technical Assistance (TA) to Moore
 Street Seniors, Inc., in Fairbanks, Alaska, under the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Energy to
 Communities (E2C) Expert Match Program. NLR provided, within the TA scope of work, a
 building survey of the Moore Street Senior Apartments. This consisted of an air leakage test,
 building survey/walkthrough, and the creation of an Energy Star Portfolio Manager (ESPM)
 profile for the building. NLR staff have provided the results of the building survey in this report
 along with a list of window replacement considerations and best practices, building ventilation
 considerations and best practices, and further considerations to reduce the annual energy costs
 and improve indoor air quality in the apartment building.

 1 Background
 Moore Street Seniors, Inc., a nonprofit organization in Fairbanks, Alaska, applied to the DOE
 Energy to Communities E2C Expert Match Program in the summer of 2024. NLR provided TA
 to Moore Street Seniors, Inc, from Nov. 2024 to Jan. 2025.

 1.1 Moore Street Seniors, Inc.
 Moore Street Seniors, Inc. is a nonprofit organization that supports seniors living in Interior
 Alaska by providing safe and affordable housing through the operation of a residential
 multifamily building1. The 20-unit Moore Street Senior Apartments building is located directly
 across from the Senior Center in Fairbanks, Alaska, and provides residents with a place to live
 and opportunities for social activities, access to the Senior Center for meals and services, and
 programming such as gardening and exercise classes.

 The Moore Street Senior Apartment building is approximately 30 years old (see Figures 1 and 2).
 The goal of Moore Street Seniors, Inc. is to renovate the building so that it is efficient, safe, and
 healthy for the next several decades to enable the continuation of their mission to serve seniors in
 Fairbanks.

 1.2 E2C Expert Match Program
 Through the U.S. Department of Energy's Energy to Communities (E2C) program, NLR, other
 national laboratory experts, and select organizations provide Expert Match—free, short-term
 technical assistance to address near-term energy challenges and questions. The Expert Match
 technical assistance timeframe is 40–60 hours over 3–4 months. Moore Street Seniors, Inc. was
 selected as a participant in the E2C Expert Match program because of their need for technical
 input and best practices for making critical building, mechanical, and envelope improvements to
 benefit their residents and save energy to reduce annual costs.

 2 Objective
 The objective of this Expert Match project is to provide technical input based on past energy
 bills, conversations with Moore Street Seniors, Inc. staff, and findings from a limited-scope on-
 site building survey, on the following:

 • A list of considerations for window replacements, because Moore Street Seniors, Inc., has
 obtained funding to replace all windows in the building.
 • The feasibility of installing a new ventilation system, as the building currently relies on
 exhaust-only ventilation. Considerations should include details on a potential system or
 different options if they exist, and the advantages and disadvantages of the options.
 • A list of other potentially cost-effective retrofit options identified during the building
 survey.
 This technical input is intended to assist Moore Street Seniors, Inc., to reduce energy costs and
 improve occupant comfort and indoor air quality.

 3 Moore Street Senior Apartments
 The Moore Street Senior Apartments is a 20-unit apartment complex with two common rooms
 and an office. The entire building is approximately 17,000 ft2 and individual units are
 approximately 480 ft2 in size. The apartments serve as low-income senior housing, and host

 residents from the Fairbanks area, as well as surrounding towns that do not have senior housing.
 Built approximately 30 years ago in the 1990s, the building currently lacks many energy

 improve comfort for residents.

 3.1 Lighting
 Lighting in the building is mostly T8 linear fixtures with Light Emitting Diode (LED) lamps in
 common areas and offices, with high-intensity discharge (HID) lamps outside. In individual
 units, the lighting consists of one T8 fixture with fluorescent lamps and two other fixtures with
 LED lamps, and a vanity fixture with LED lamps in bathrooms.

 controller and baseboard distribution system. The domestic hot water system has a recirculation
 control that is on during business hours. There is no cooling system in the building. The
 thermostats are manual and located in each building zone.

 3.5 Building Baseline – ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager
 NLR staff created an ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager (ESPM) profile for Moore Street
 Senior Apartments. ESPM is a free software program created and maintained by the United
 States Environmental Protection Agency. It allows building owners and others to track building
 metrics and energy use for properties, and it also calculates several different metrics that building
 owners can use to benchmark their property and then see improvements as retrofits are
 completed.

 Shown in Table 2 is the energy performance profile created in ESPM. To establish a baseline
 year, ESPM utilizes the first complete year of energy bills entered into the software, in this case
 starting in 2023. Similarly, the energy current year is based on the most recent full year of energy
 bills entered, for the calendar year of 2024. This profile includes Moore Street Seniors, Inc.’s
 electricity, natural gas, and fuel oil consumption (before the natural gas conversion). The metrics
 reported are defined as follows2:

 Site Energy Use Intensity (EUI) is the measure of how much energy a building uses in kilo
 British thermal units per square foot of space over a year (kBtu/ft2/yr. It is calculated using the
 energy consumed by the building on-site (electricity, natural gas, fuel oil) and does not include
 the energy associated with the generation of the energy at the source.

 The energy cost reported in the table is the total amount of money, measured in nominal U.S.
 dollars for the year associated, spent to provide energy to the building during the 12-month time
 periods established by the baseline and current years. This metric accounts for every energy type
 entered in the case of Moore Street Apartments, (electricity, natural gas, and fuel oil).

 Total (Location-Based) Emissions Intensity estimates the amount of emissions (carbon dioxide,
 methane, nitrous oxide, etc.) that the building produces per square foot over one year. Carbon
 dioxide equivalent (CO2e) is a general unit that combines the quantity and global warming
 potential of each greenhouse gas3. This is calculated based on the energy that the building uses,
 and the emissions associated with generating that energy in the local area.

 interior hallways of the building were treated as the outside reference pressure. Entry/exit doors

 3.7 Health and Safety
 The building has fire and smoke alarms with battery backup as well as fire extinguishers
 throughout the building. During the on-site survey, staff did not observe any mold on interior
 building surfaces.

 The building manager reported one issue that has arisen in the last decade of poor indoor air
 quality in the hotter summers in Fairbanks, especially when there are wildfires burning in the
 forests that neighbor the Fairbanks area. With no dedicated balanced ventilation system, the
 building relies on exhaust-only ventilation consisting of bath fans controlled by humidistats and
 range hoods with on/off controls in individual units. When these place the building into a
 negative pressure, air comes in through cracks in the building envelope. There is no filtration,
 and in the summer, residents bring in smokey air when they use exhaust ventilation. On
 particularly smokey days, the building manager has reported that you could see visible smoke
 when you look down the long hallway. Additionally, as residents close windows to keep out
 smoke, with no cooling system, the building becomes uncomfortably hot.

 If the building has not been tested for radon, Moore Street Seniors Inc may want to consider a
 radon test. Radon can enter buildings connected to the ground, such as with the Moore Street
 Senior Apartments’ crawlspace foundation. While the crawlspace does have a vapor retarder, in
 high radon areas, this may not be enough to keep radon levels low. More information on radon,
 including how to get a local test completed, is available from the University of Alaska Fairbanks
 Cooperative Extension Service (https://www.uaf.edu/ces/home-environment/radon/).

 3.8 Envelope Considerations
 The building currently has triple pane windows, which are generally adequate for the Fairbanks
 climate as long as the seals are intact. Moore Street Seniors, Inc., has obtained State of Alaska
 funding to replace the windows. Considerations for replacing windows would include:

 • If an additional funding source can be identified, the best practice would be to replace the
 siding and add exterior insulation at the same time as the window replacement; this
 would necessitate appropriate mechanical ventilation to provide dedicated outside air
 since it would air-tighten the building. The funding source would need to be
 comprehensive to both the wall retrofit and adding mechanical ventilation.

 • If there is any flexibility in the funding, it may be beneficial to replace the siding and add
 exterior insulation in place of window replacement. This would necessitate also installing
 mechanical ventilation. The added insulation and reduced air leakage would help to
 reduce drafts and could include details around the existing windows. A detailed building
 energy model would help to clarify if this upgrade is cost effective.

 • Windows can be replaced with new triple pane windows so they remain energy efficient
 and do not cause a rise in energy costs for the building.

 • In cold climates, it is beneficial to install casement windows (hinged on one side that
 open like a door to the outside) with locks and hinges at more than one place on the
 locking side for windows that need to open. This reduces air leakage and ice formation in
 the winter, and egress casement windows are less likely to freeze shut than sliding
 windows in cold weather. Unless it is necessary for egress, double casement windows can
 be replaced with single casement (casement on only one side of the frame) to reduce air
 leakage and reduce energy costs.

 • If the contractor replacing the windows follows best practice cold climate building
 science principles ensuring to detail around the window to reduce air leakage or water
 intrusion, it will ensure lower energy use and costs. For instance, contractors can follow
 the Building Energy Efficiency Standards5, which are one example of energy efficiency
 practices for commercial buildings that reduce energy costs.

 3.9 Ventilation
 The Moore Street apartment building currently has no central ventilation system. Ventilation is
 exhaust-only and consists of bath fans and range hoods in each of the 20 units. The bath fans are
 manually switch-operated with a humidistat back-up. The range hoods are operated by a manual
 on/off switch. This exhaust-only ventilation relies on passive air entry through small openings
 and air leakage pathways around windows and doors for fresh air. The building has one heat
 recovery ventilator (HRV) (see Figure 9), which provides ventilation to two storage closets when
 it is operational. The HRV was not operating at the time of the survey.

 12

 Figure 9. The building has one HRV in a storage closet, which is not operational.

 There are many things to consider when choosing a ventilation system:

 • Energy efficiency upgrades—such as new windows, adding insulation, and air sealing—
 will result in the building being more airtight. While this will help to reduce energy bill
 costs and increase comfort, it will also reduce the building’s ability to bring in outside air
 when exhaust ventilation is operating. A balanced ventilation system that can bring in
 fresh air when it exhausts stale air becomes necessary. Continuing to rely on exhaust
 only ventilation as the building is upgraded will likely not be able to maintain a healthy
 amount of fresh air.

 • It is likely not feasible to retrofit a whole building ventilation system. While ducting for
 the first floor could run through the crawlspace, there is not an obvious place to run
 ducting that is not visible for units on the upper floor.

 • Unit balanced ventilation options do exist. HRVs can be installed in closets and exhaust
 stale air and bring in fresh air to a single apartment unit. In a cold climate such as
 Fairbanks, using an HRV to recapture heat that would otherwise leave the building with
 stale air is beneficial as opposed to ventilation options that exhaust heated air and bring
 in untempered cold air. These units have variable programming options to operate a
 portion of each hour depending on occupancy. They can also have a filter box in line
 with the air intake to install higher minimum efficiency reporting value filters, which can
 help prevent particulate matter pollution from wildfires or exhaust from entering the
 building.

 3.10 Energy Efficiency Considerations
 Energy efficiency upgrades for Moore Street Senior Apartments can help reduce energy use and
 improve occupant comfort. A detailed building energy model could help to inform the cost
 saving potential of each of the following to help prioritize the retrofits. This was outside of the

 13

 scope of this particular technical assistance project but could be pursued in the future with other
 funding. Building management can prioritize retrofits in other ways, though, such as by the cost
 of installation, occupant comfort and health, life of the existing equipment, and/or the ability of
 maintenance staff to carry out the retrofit. Some considerations to potentially reduce energy costs
 are below:

 • Converting all lighting to efficient LED lamps. For the inside, lighting should match the
 current LED lamps for consistency of light quality and ease of lamp replacements.

 • Installing daylight or occupancy sensors for the lighting in the common rooms and
 laundry areas. Such sensors can dim or shut off lights when there is sunlight coming in
 through the windows and/or there are no occupants present.

 • Installing programmable thermostats for common areas with a temperature set back
 during nighttime hours.

 • Installing smart parking lot controllers for the head bolt heaters in the parking lot. These
 controls vary the on/off times for the outlets based on outdoor temperatures, so that in
 warmer temperatures they are on less often and reduce energy use.

 • Choosing appliances such as refrigerators, washing machines, and dryers with ENERGY
 STAR appliances as current appliances reach their end of life will save consumers
 money. ENERGY STAR appliances achieve the highest performance efficiencies and
 thus represent options with the lowest operating costs6.

 • Air sealing and insulating the walls of the crawlspace. This may be feasible from the
 interior with detailing the vapor barrier around penetrations at the wall boundary and
 using spray foam or foam board insulation on the walls and in the rim joists (replacing
 the fiberglass insulation that in some places is compressed or not extending all the way to
 the edge). However, it is better practice to do from the exterior, excavating around the
 foundation and adding foam board insulation. Exterior insulation will help keep the
 interior surfaces warm, so they do not experience condensation. In either case it is
 important to preserve the crawlspace ventilation. Information about energy retrofits for
 crawlspaces can be found in the Building America Solution Center
 (https://basc.pnnl.gov/building-components/crawlspaces).

 • Replacing the deteriorating siding. That is an ideal time to insulate the building from the
 exterior. Continuous exterior insulation has other advantages, such as stopping the
 thermal bridging that occurs through studs and serving as an air barrier to reduce air
 leakage. However, it is important to follow best practice cold climate building science
 principles when applying exterior insulation to avoid creating a double vapor barrier
 and/or the potential for condensation on the interior side of the wall.7 Both situations can
