Executive Summary
This report provides a framework for analyzing the operational effectiveness and resource
efficiency of micromobility and e-mobility—henceforth collectively referred as two-wheeled
mobility—operations for the U.S. Park Police fleet in the Washington D.C. metropolitan area.
Micromobility refers to small, lightweight transportation modes—such as bikes, and e-bikes—
designed for short-distance urban travel and first and last-mile connectivity. E-mobility refers to
electrified vehicles such as electric motorcycles. The Park Police currently have an inventory of
bikes, e-bikes, e-motorcycles, UTVs, and cars which they use to patrol National Monuments and
points of interest in the capital region. They want to understand how they can patrol these
locations by using new and existing two-wheeled mobility options.

The authors conducted a comprehensive analysis of the U.S. Park Police’s patrol operations by
assessing their patrol patterns, rapid response, and community‑engagement scenarios. Using this
data, the authors developed application criteria to guide optimal deployment, maintenance
scheduling, and future acquisition decisions of micromobility and e-mobility options to
maximize operational effectiveness and resource efficiency. These criteria were organized
around an optimization framework to support allocating mobility options to different stations
based on their inventory, usage pattern, and officers deployed.

The Mobility Optimization and Deployment Engine (MODE) model developed in this report
could be used to explore other micromobility and macromobility (long-distance, higher capacity
transportation) options for fleets across the country.

1 Introduction
The United States Park Police (USPP) is a federal police force tasked with patrolling and
maintaining law and order in and around the National Monuments in the Washington D.C.
metropolitan area. In the many pedestrian areas that cannot be patrolled by cars, the Park Police
must choose between the different two-wheeled mobility options available to them.

The USPP already have a fleet of bikes, electric bikes (e-bikes) and electric motorcycles (e-
motorcycles) that they acquired in 2017, 2024 and 2019 respectively. The fleet is currently
satisfying the needs of USPP, although some vehicles are due for replacement. The USPP is also
expecting a period of rapid growth in their operation between FY25 and FY26, almost doubling
their officer count. This increase in officers and responsibilities is expected to result in a need to
buy newer and more advanced micromobility and e-mobility vehicles. Researchers from the
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) were asked to develop a framework to identify
optimal two-wheeled mobility options for USPP’s patrolling duties considering both operational
needs and efficient use of resources.

To provide a solid framework of deployment options for the USPP, NLR analyzed the current
fleet and points of interest (POIs) that officers patrol, and explored the various two wheeled
mobility options that are currently available in the market. The authors then developed a travel
optimization algorithm to pair the most well-suited mobility options with the patrol duties using
the Mobility Optimization and Deployment Engine (MODE) model.

2 Overview of USPP Fleet Operations
The USPP currently has an inventory of bikes, e-bikes, motorcycles (both gas and electric), and
cars that they use to patrol National Monuments and POIs in the capital region. Since many of
these areas are not accessible by motorcycle or car and are walk/bike only, they also use different
micromobility options available to them. The USPP has five stations in the Washington, D.C.
metropolitan area from which they patrol these locations (USPP 2025). Each station has specific
patrol locations assigned to them based on proximity and officer count.

The USPP currently operates between 24 and 29 bikes and 12-15 e-bikes across five stations in
the D.C. metropolitan area. They also maintain a fleet of over 30 bikes as a training fleet for
initial police bike classes that are necessary to train officers for their bike certifications. They
have 8 e-motorcycles out of the District 1 station in downtown D.C. Additionally, they have 2
electric Polaris Ranger UTVs as well: one is assigned to the District 1 station, and one is
assigned to USPP headquarters. The bikes in the patrol fleet are roughly five to nine years old as
of this report. The training fleet is mostly over ten years old. The e-bikes are two years old, and
the e-motorcycles are seven years old. Based on fleet reporting, there are patrol cars available at
each of these police stations as well. However, the authors focused exclusively on two wheeled
mobility options for this study.

3 Two-wheeled mobility Options
The USPP wants to utilize the micromobility options at its disposal to patrol some non-motorized
traffic areas, such as the National Mall and the Lincoln Memorial. To that extent, they already
have a fleet of bikes, and e-bikes. Additionally, USPP has concerns that some of their current
two-wheeled mobility fleet is under-utilized and that their e-motorcycles have taken a long time
to get repaired when maintenance issues arise. Our market research hence started with finding
reliable mobility options that can withstand the usage patterns of USPP.

Of all the current options in the market, bikes and e-bikes stood out for their reliability,
popularity, and suitability to USPP operations. Additionally, there are police-specific options
available in the market, which reduces the costs of accessorizing and modifying them compared
to any other mobility option. E-bikes are one of the faster options in the micromobility space.
Bikes are one of the most economical forms of mobility available with a long police force
history. The patrol officers need to be certified before they operate any micromobility options;
owing to a long history of usage, it is easier for bike certification compared to other options like
scooters and one-wheels.

Manufacturer recommendations for maintenance are similar across bike types but return to
operation from maintenance has varied significantly in USPP operations. Bikes and e-bikes
typically require routine maintenance that is available at dozens of area shops, including
replacement of damaged parts such as brake pads and chains as well as greasing and gear tuning.
The e-motorcycles procured by USPP need to be fixed in specialty shops certified by their
manufacturer. There are few of these in the D.C. metropolitan area, and sometimes the
turnaround from these shops can take months. The USPP believes that with a greater market
share, the e-motorcycles may eventually yield maintenance savings compared to gasoline
motorcycles because they have fewer moving parts and fluids to replace.

Table 2 details some of the micromobility and e-mobility options that are currently available in
the market. The USPP currently operates Code 2 and Code 3 bikes (Police Bike Store 2026),
Recon Police Interceptor e-bikes (Recon Interceptor 2026), and Zero DSR (Zero Motorcycles
2025), all of which are police specific models. Livewire e-motorcycles – whose parent company
Harley Davidson has a long history with the police force – are a new option in the e-motorcycle
space (LiveWire Motorcycles 2026).

The largest advantage of bikes is their low cost. Even the top-of-the-line fully kitted police bike
is roughly 1/3 the cost of the e-bikes. They are also very maneuverable, excellent among crowds,
and easy to teach officers to ride and use properly.

E-bikes can cover short and long distances at speed, and officers arrive physically able to
respond to a call for service or emergency. Traveling any real distance on a bike to an emergency
leaves officers at a disadvantage physically. The capabilities of e-bikes are not equivalent to a
gas or electric motorcycle for covering large distances. However, for smaller geographical areas
and congested areas where low speeds are required, officers view e-bikes as an equally capable
tool. While the lights on the bikes function sufficiently, the e-bikes use the same top-tier lights as
emergency vehicles, the emergency lighting on the e-bikes is very good which is another
advantage of e-bikes.

 4

E-motorcycles can cover farther distances at a greater speed with very little physical exertion
when compared with bikes and e-bikes, though they are restricted to motor vehicle routes. They
can carry more equipment throughout the day compared to micromobility options. The compact
vehicle size allows officers to ride more comfortably and safely in and around traffic. The
quietness of the e-motorcycles avoids disturbing visitors or notifying bad actors.

With increasing market share, the service and spare part availability of e-motorcycles have also
improved significantly since 2019 when the USPP first purchased the Zero DSR e-motorcycles.
Newer models with more efficiency and longer range have since been introduced, successfully
addressing range and efficiency concerns among the officers. With less moving parts, e-
motorcycles are easier to service and have a longer time between services compared to
traditional motorcycles. With many of the new models supporting fast charging, and with easier
access to charging stations, e-motorcycles present a strong case financially and operationally.

Using a combination of two-wheeled mobility options helps maximize the advantages and
minimize the disadvantages of the various technologies, while balancing maintenance, cost, and
range requirements.

With a variety of micromobility and e-mobility options potentially at their disposal, the USPP
wants to re-think how to deploy their officers for patrolling duties. Each station in the D.C.
metropolitan area has specific POIs assigned to them. In addition to the mobility options and
service timelines, NLR was also tasked to prepare an optimum patrolling schedule and
framework. Therefore, the authors utilized an optimization tool to achieve realistic schedules and
mobility options for the service.

4 Framework for Deploying Micromobility in U.S. Park
  Police Operations
NLR developed an optimization framework called the Mobility Optimization and Deployment
Engine (MODE) to account for vehicle purchase cost, maintenance cost, vehicle range, and
patrol requirements to prioritize different mobility options based on the patrol radius and the
stopover time at each site. The framework utilizes an algorithm to optimize vehicle selection
using the number of officers and the numbers of places they have to patrol based on the
transportation options each station has and constraints described below.

To optimize the patrolling schedules and fleet allocation, MODE treats the patrol assignment as a
resource allocation problem. It looks at the mobility options available at each station, their travel
times, and their weighted cost factors. This section outlines the data parametrization, the
mathematical formulation of the optimization model, and the various constraints NLR utilized in
solving the problem.

First, an objective function is defined. In this case, the primary objective involves balancing two
sets of transportation constraints: travel time and the weighted cost factor attached to each mode
of transport. This optimizes for mobility efficiency. The weighted cost factor is dependent on the
price to acquire and maintain that mode of transport. Maintenance price includes the service
costs, spare parts cost and the cost to charge the vehicles. By utilizing the cost to acquire and
maintain these mobility options over a period of five years and by using the price data from
Table 2, NLR was able to calculate weighted cost factors for each mobility option. From foot
patrols to e-motorcycles, the cost factor rises steadily as their price increases. The final weighted
cost factors for each mode of mobility are detailed in Table 3.

Additionally, the algorithm also supports a secondary objective to maximize utilization by
minimizing unused shift time such that routes are assigned in such a way that every officer’s
time is utilized for the maximum possible duration.

In addition to the transportation constraints mentioned above, a few more operational constraints
are important to the functioning of the optimization algorithm:

 1. Complete Coverage Constraint: Every POI must be visited exactly once. Each POI is
 visited by only a single officer.

 2. Station Matching Constraint: Every patrol officer only patrols the POI location that
 belongs to the officer’s station.

 6

 3. Time Capacity Constraint: Total time spent by an officer patrolling should not extend
 their shift time.

 4. Officer Schedule Constraint: An officer can patrol only one location at a time and once
 they are assigned a schedule, they are not assigned any more schedules.

 5. Mode Compatibility: Our dataset has an “allowed modes” column which checks
 whether a particular POI allows for a particular mode of transport or not. For example,
 the solver wouldn’t allow an e-motorcycle borne officer to ride up to the Washington
 Monument since that POI would not allow an e-motorcycle.

 6. Shift Integrity: The total duty time (travel time + service time + lunch break) should not
 exceed eight hours of shift time.

 7. Service Requirements: Each POI is assigned a mandatory service time, representing the
 time an officer must be onsite for investigation or presence.

 8. Flow Conservation: Every officer must start at their respective station, visit the POIs in
 their schedule, and return to their origin station.

This approach generates a time-constrained heterogenous vehicle routing solution, which
includes three main aspects:

 1. Heterogeneity: The routing problem accounts for mixed modes of transportation, but
 each daily schedule uses a single mode per officer.

 2. Decision Variables: MODE uses both binary decision variables (𝑥𝑥𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 ) and continuous
 time variables (𝑡𝑡𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 ) to determine if officer 𝑘𝑘 travels from location 𝑖𝑖 to location 𝑗𝑗.

 3. Optimization Engine: For optimizing our routing, NLR utilizes a Mixed-Integer Linear
 Programming optimization package called PuLP (Mitchell et al. 2011) in Python.

With the constraints and algorithm defined, NLR used the geospatial intelligence and routing
APIs to arrive at a solution. OpenRouteService (HeiGIT gGmbH 2025) is utilized to fetch live
routing matrices between the stations and POIs. These matrices are different for different modes
of transport. If the API failed to fetch the data, MODE could use Haversine distances to calculate
these matrices, but that was not needed for the USPP patrol routes in Washington, D.C. Each
value in these matrices signifies the time it takes (in minutes) to cover the distance between each
POI and the stations.

Once these matrices are obtained, the MODE solver starts the optimizing process of assigning
different modes of transport to different POIs. It uses these matrices and the above-mentioned
transportation constraints to build multiple trip chains depending on the number of officers
available at each station and the number of POIs to be patrolled with each trip starting and
ending at the same station. Once the model evaluates the possible combination of POIs in the
form of a trip by cross checking with our specified operational constraints it then generates the
routes for each officer to travel from stations to cover each of the POIs with the lowest total
weighted cost accounting for transportation constraints.

5 Results
MODE was able to successfully find optimal transport modes and trip chains for officers to
cover all 45 POIs across 5 stations. By using OpenRouteService, NLR was able to obtain
accurate travel times for all the micromobility modes. The authors then developed complete
travel time matrices, and the model was able to build a valid patrol itinerary for officers in all 5
stations.

was successfully assigned to a patrol officer. The solver’s prioritization of mode was heavily

The results showcased how a diversity of micromobility options can serve a wider variety of
routes. In many cases, e-bikes were only slightly faster than bikes but with a cost factor of 4 to
2.5, respectively, thus lowering the cost-benefit factor of e-bikes over bikes. One factor NLR did
not include in the model was rider fatigue, which was higher in the case of bikes and hence
stations could prioritize e-bikes to avoid fatigue in some situations.

The solver prioritized e-motorcycles when it calculated the costs associated with the e-
motorcycle making up for the distance covered for a particular trip. Instead of splitting up the
distance travelled by an e-motorcycle into multiple e-bike or bike trips which led to more officer
usage, the solver chose to prioritize e-motorcycles.

Patrols in the dense, POI-heavy areas of central D.C. were assigned to foot and bike modes while
patrols done by the District 2 (GWMP) station were geographically dispersed and assigned to e-
motorcycle modes.

The model provides a baseline for USPP to allocate mobility assets to each district station as well
as feasible patrol routes for the officers. However, other factors, like weather and tourist
seasonality, could impact those schedules. Additionally, charging time and other associated
logistics were not included in the study but could be factored in as additional constraints. Given
that the trip distances analyzed for both e-bikes and e-motorcycles are well within their
respective operational ranges, mid-trip charging was not considered in this study. Instead, all
vehicles are assumed to be fully charged before starting the trip.

6 Conclusion
This report recommends transportation assignments for USPP officers, aiming to minimize both
travel time and total transportation costs. NLR’s model developed for this study, MODE,
generated logistically feasible schedules that organized routes and assigned a single patrol
schedule to a single transportation mode. Table 7 shows how MODE aligned the fleet
distribution with actual usage patterns and operational demand across stations. This efficient
allocation reduces downtime and improves asset utilization thereby helping in managing capital
and maintenance costs. This approach could provide USPP a more efficient and cost-effective
fleet mix for the future; however, USPP fleet administrators will need to verify whether there are
other considerations for asset assignment not considered in this analysis. For example, training
needs, weather, or peak events like Independence Day celebrations might require additional
assets.
