Molecular Mechanisms of Urea Interactions with Bovine Serum Albumin in an Acid-Expanded Conformation (pH 3.7) A footnote for the title
Y. Ricardo Espinosa, C. Manuel Carlevaro, C. Gastón Ferrara

Abstract
Understanding the molecular mechanisms by which denaturants modulate protein structure remains a central challenge in protein biophysics. In this work, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to investigate the effects of urea on the structural stability of bovine serum albumin (BSA) in its F isoform at pH 3.7, over a broad range of urea concentrations (0 M to a fully urea-solvated system). The results reveal that urea induces a concentration-dependent dehydration–rehydration mechanism within the protein hydration shell. At low urea concentrations, a marked reduction ( 40%) in protein–water hydrogen bonds is observed, accompanied by a corresponding increase in protein–urea interactions, consistent with a competitive solvation process. At higher concentrations, urea–urea self-association becomes significant, limiting direct protein–urea interactions and promoting partial rehydration of the protein surface. Despite these solvent rearrangements, the secondary structure of BSA remains largely preserved, whereas local and tertiary structural features, particularly in Domain III, exhibit increased solvent exposure and conformational flexibility. These findings support a dynamic compensation mechanism in which urea partially replaces water in the solvation shell without fully disrupting the hydrogen-bonding network. Overall, this study provides molecular-level insight into the interplay between preferential interactions, solvation dynamics, and protein stability under denaturing conditions.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the role of urea and other denaturing agents in aqueous solutions containing proteins or micelles has sparked intense debate, leading to considerable progress in our understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Although various mechanisms have been proposed to explain denaturation, and despite the extensive body of literature on the topic, direct and indirect mechanisms have traditionally been the most frequently cited—some proposals even combine both perspectives

More recently, a growing number of studies have questioned whether a common denaturation mechanism can be generalized across different systems
Rossky 2008
;
Das and Mukhopadhyay 2009
;
Niether et al. 2018
;
Hua et al. 2008

;
Paladino et al. 2022
;

Miller and Levinger 2022

These questions, together with more recent studies
Guckeisen et al. 2021
;
Tihonov et al. 2015
, suggest that the possibility of generalizing a denaturation mechanism for urea and other denaturing agents remains unclear and requires further investigation.

In this work, we use molecular dynamics simulations to examine how varying urea concentrations affect the structure of bovine serum albumin (BSA). Given that BSA undergoes reversible conformational changes in response to pH variations
Scanavachi et al. 2020
, we selected the N-isoform state of BSA at pH 7 as our initial reference for simulations. This state represents a folded, functional conformation. Our results show strong agreement with experimental findings and with previous simulation results reported in the literature, thereby validating the robustness of our approach.

Materials and Methods

2.1
Systems and Simulations

Urea-induced effects on the structural stability of bovine serum albumin (BSA) were examined in aqueous environments over a range of urea concentrations. Urea molecules were represented using the Boek model
Boek et al. 1994
, while all nonbonded interactions and covalent parameters were adopted from the GROMOS 54A7 force field
Schmid et al. 2011
.

Water molecules were described using the Simple Point Charge Extended (SPC/E) model
Berendsen et al. 1987
. The starting atomic configuration of bovine serum albumin (BSA) was taken from its crystallographic structure available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4F5S)
Bujacz 2012
. The protein topology corresponding to physiological conditions at pH 3.7 was adopted from prior studies and validated by Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) measurements in combination with molecular dynamics simulations
Scanavachi et al. 2020
. Under these conditions, BSA carries a net charge of -99, which was compensated by the addition of sodium (Cl
-
) counterions to ensure overall charge neutrality.

Intermolecular interactions were explicitly accounted for using the GROMACS 2023.4 molecular dynamics simulation package
Spoel et al.
. As a reference, a control system devoid of urea was first simulated, with water molecules randomly distributed around the protein (see Control System I in Table 1).

3
Results and discussion

All analyses are based on the average of ten independent simulations, each 10 ns in duration, performed for every system investigated (the control system without urea, urea–water mixtures ranging from 1 to 5 M and 7 M, and the system composed exclusively of urea in the absence of water). Before data collection commenced, all systems underwent 500 ns of hydration/solvation to ensure adequate equilibration.

3.1
Hydrogen Bond Analysis

In our initial analysis, we examined the formation of hydrogen bonds (HBs) between the protein and water, and between the protein and urea, as a function of urea concentration. For each concentration, the average was calculated from ten independent systems using the final 8 ns of each simulation. Figure 2 presents the sum of the averages corresponding to protein–water and protein–urea HBs, and compares these values with the urea-free condition (0 M).

Figure 1
:
Average total number of hydrogen bonds (HBs) as a function of urea concentration for protein–water and protein–urea interactions. The magenta line indicates the average number of hydrogen bonds for the protein in the 0 M condition.

The presence of urea in combination with water leads, in all cases, to an increase in the total number of hydrogen bonds compared to the control systems containing either only water or only urea. A monotonic loss of hydrogen bonds between the protein and water occurs as the number of urea molecules increases.

The observed decrease in protein–water hydrogen bonding is concomitant with an approximately 45% increase in protein–urea hydrogen bonds over the 1–7 M concentration range, suggesting a progressive replacement of water molecules by urea within the protein solvation shell. However, a direct comparison of the control systems (W and U) reveals that urea cannot reproduce water’s hydrogen-bonding capacity toward the protein. This difference indicates that, although urea can partially compensate for the loss of protein–water interactions,
it does not fully restore the original hydrogen-bonding network, potentially contributing to alterations in protein stability and structural integrity.

When analyzing the formation of hydrogen bonds (HBs) between the protein and water at the domain level, we observe that all domains experience a progressive reduction in HBs as urea concentration increases, with the most pronounced effects observed at 5 and 7 M. This decrease follows a nearly monotonic trend, whereas Domain III displays marked stability in its hydrogen-bonding pattern at lower urea concentrations. Notably, all three domains present comparable numbers of HBs under the 0 M and 1 M conditions.

Radius of gyration (RG), root mean square deviation (RMSD), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA)

All quantities reported in this section were calculated from the final 8 ns of the ten replica simulations performed at each urea concentration. The values presented correspond to averages over these trajectories.

In Figure
9
, we analyze the protein’s radius of gyration as a function of urea concentration. At low urea concentrations, the average radius of gyration remains essentially constant, with values comparable to those observed in the control W system. At 3 M, a pronounced increase in the radius of gyration is detected, and beyond this concentration, noticeable fluctuations are observed. This fluctuating behavior parallels that identified in the distribution of HBs with urea in Domain I.

Figure 9
:
Means Radius of Gyration (RG) vs urea concentration.

In this case, the presence and increase of urea does not consistently increase the average radius of gyration relative to the urea-free system (
Espinosa et al. 2025
). Moreover, the observed variations are modest, not exceeding 8%, suggesting that any structural changes remain limited in magnitude. At low urea concentrations, these findings are in agreement with the results reported by Yasuda and co-workers
Hayashi et al. 2007
, who, within the 0–5 M range and at the same temperature, observed that the protein structure remains largely preserved, with only minor fluctuations associated with solvent dynamics.

At high urea concentrations (7 M and control U), no substantial variations in the radius of gyration are detected. Unlike the behavior reported by Hasan and co-workers, our simulations do not reveal pronounced side-chain expansion in BSA nor a marked increase in hydrophobic hydration effects. This difference may be largely attributed to the initial structural model adopted in our study, which reflects physiological conditions at pH 3.7. Under these conditions, the protein is already partially unfolded, potentially limiting the extent of additional conformational changes induced by urea.

The analysis of the average RMSD as a function of urea concentration, shown in Figure

Relative to the control W system, RMSD values are reduced by more than 30% in the presence of urea in all cases, suggesting that urea may promote structural stabilization of the protein. Notably, the system containing only urea (control U) displays markedly reduced protein fluctuations, which may indicate a more stable and more expanded structure than that observed in the water-only system (control W), consistent with the behavior observed in the radius of gyration.

An analysis of the mean solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) [Fig.

In most cases, the presence of urea results in SASA values higher than those observed in the control W system, except in the complete absence of water (control U). At low urea concentrations, a monotonic increase in SASA is observed up to 2 M. Beyond a urea concentration of 3 M, however, this monotonic behavior is no longer evident; instead, only slight fluctuations in SASA are detected with increasing urea concentration. The percentage increase in average SASA values is to 9% only at a urea concentration of 2 M; in all other cases, the increase remains below this level relative to the protein in the absence of urea (control W). When comparing the control systems W and U, the difference in SASA is approximately 2%.

Although these deviations do not contradict the overall behavior observed for the other variables analyzed, they indicate the need for a more detailed examination, particularly one addressing how the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) varies across the individual domains of the protein. Given the structural complexity of proteins such as BSA, a domain-specific SASA analysis is essential to characterize how different regions of the protein respond to variations in urea concentration, with respect to solvent exposure and potential local conformational changes. To further refine this analysis, SASA was evaluated at the domain level in order to identify the protein regions most affected by changes in hydration or by variations in urea concentration (Fig.

The present analysis indicates that Domains I and III are the regions most influenced by the presence of urea, whereas Domain II exhibits the smallest variation in solvent-exposed surface area as the urea concentration changes. Domain I displays two distinct regimes in the evolution of SASA, with a monotonic increase observed between 0–2 M and again from 3–7 M, followed by a decrease to its minimum value in the absence of water (control U). It is also noteworthy that Domain I consistently shows the lowest average SASA values among the three domains, while simultaneously exhibiting the largest fluctuations, although these remain below 11%. Domain III presents slightly smaller SASA variations than those observed for Domain I. In this case, no clear monotonic dependence on urea concentration is detected, and the fluctuations in SASA reach approximately 10% in the most pronounced instance.

CONCLUSION

This study employed Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine the effects of urea on the structural properties of bovine serum albumin (BSA) at pH 3.7. Under these conditions, BSA adopts a partially unfolded conformation characterized by increased solvent exposure of its domains, commonly referred to as the F isoform. The simulated systems were constructed with urea concentrations ranging from 0 M (water control, Control W) to a system containing urea as the sole solvent component (urea control, Control U). Within this range of urea concentrations, our results indicate that urea does not significantly alter the overall secondary structure of BSA. In particular, the number of residues participating in secondary structural elements remains largely preserved, with
\alpha
-helical content showing minimal variation across all systems. These observations are consistent with previous computational and experimental studies reporting that moderate concentrations of urea primarily affect tertiary contacts rather than disrupting backbone hydrogen-bonding patterns that stabilize secondary structure.

A more detailed analysis of local conformational features, including S-bends, hydrogen-bonded turns, and helical conformations, reveals subtle effects of urea on protein structure. Specifically, the presence of urea increases in locally disordered configurations, suggesting enhanced conformational flexibility. However, these local perturbations do not translate into significant changes in the protein’s secondary structure. This behavior is consistent with the notion that urea weakens intramolecular interactions while stabilizing solvent-exposed regions, without substantially altering the secondary structural framework.
The observed structural resistance of BSA under denaturing conditions is consistent with prior experimental findings at different pH values, which indicate that the F isoform retains secondary structure despite partial unfolding. These results underscore the intrinsic stability of the F isoform and suggest that, within the studied concentration range, urea primarily modulates local structural dynamics rather than inducing significant loss of secondary structure.

Analyses based on the radius of gyration (Rg), root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) support the proposed interpretation. Specifically, no significant global structural changes are observed in the protein, and the presence of urea appears to stabilize the protein in its F isoform. Notably, Domain III is the most affected region, exhibiting increased solvent exposure in the presence of urea.

A key aspect emerging from the analysis is the progressive dehydration of the protein surface as urea concentration rises, driven by the replacement of protein–water hydrogen bonds with protein–urea interactions. This exchange mechanism, which maintains or slightly increases the total number of hydrogen bonds involving the protein, suggests the presence of a dynamic solvation equilibrium. A comparison between the two control systems indicates that, although urea can partially compensate for the loss of protein–water interactions, it does not fully restore the original hydrogen-bonding network in this case. This may account for the observed alterations in the local structural stability and integrity of the protein. A domain-level analysis further corroborates the previously described behavior, confirming the presence of a compensation mechanism whereby the total number of hydrogen bonds among the protein, water, and urea remains nearly constant. At the level of individual domains, Domain III exhibits particularly stable hydrogen-bonding patterns at low urea concentrations. This behavior can be rationalized within the framework of preferential interaction theory: in the F isoform, Domain III presents greater exposure of hydrophobic regions, which promotes preferential hydration at low urea concentrations, thereby stabilizing its local hydrogen-bonding network. As urea concentration increases, this preferential hydration is progressively reduced due to the accumulation of urea in the solvation shell, leading to a redistribution of intermolecular interactions without a substantial disruption of the overall hydrogen-bond network.

The presence of urea primarily affects hydrogen-bond (HB) formation between the protein backbone and water, leading to a marked reduction of approximately 40% in their number across all concentrations. Concurrently, the formation of HBs between urea and the protein backbone suggests a preferential interaction with urea, indicating that the attenuation of backbone–water hydrogen bonds is consistent with a competitive solvation mechanism. In this framework, urea molecules progressively replace water at critical hydrogen-bonding sites, a process that may facilitate urea-induced conformational destabilization.

Analysis of hydrogen-bond formation between protein side chains and either water or urea further supports the presence of a compensatory mechanism, whereby urea partially substitutes for water at accessible polar and charged residues, thereby preserving the local interaction network despite changes in the solvent environment.

Analysis of the radial distribution functions (RDFs) for the different groups present in the system indicates the presence of local hydration near solvent-accessible regions of the protein core. This hydration is maintained across all urea concentrations and may even be enhanced at higher concentrations. Consistently, the analysis of the normalized average number of hydrogen bonds between the protein and water confirms the presence of a rehydration phenomenon at the protein surface. Increasing urea concentration appears to favor interactions between water molecules and chloride ions, whereas interactions of these ions with urea or with the protein remain comparatively limited, except under water-depleted conditions. The propensity for urea–urea interactions increases with urea concentration, with self-association becoming dominant at higher concentrations.
