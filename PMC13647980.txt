Mathematical Framework for the Effects of Sample Absorption
on Raman Intensity for Quantification and Detection Thresholds of
Minerals in Two-Component Geological Mixtures

Yu Yu Phua, Bethany Ehlmann

Abstract

On Earth and other
planets, Raman instruments can characterize
minerals in situ in a nondestructive and rapid manner
without sample preparation. However, they commonly detect phases in
mixtures because field instruments are often limited in the magnification
that can be achieved (≳100 μm/spot). The effects of mixing
can create challenges in detecting and quantifying the prevalence
of phases of interest, particularly when there are dark minerals that
attenuate photons nonlinearly with their abundance. In this work,
we develop mathematical models that parameterize the effects of sample
absorption on Raman intensity for three types of two-component solid
mixtures: (1) low absorptivity + low absorptivity minerals, (2) low
absorptivity + high absorptivity minerals, (3) high absorptivity +
high absorptivity minerals. Subsequently, we verified these models
through laboratory measurements of Raman peak intensities for hydrated
sulfates and phyllosilicates in mixtures. We find that Raman intensity
as a function of concentration is linear for mixtures made of solely
low absorptivity minerals but follows a nonlinear relationship for
mixtures containing one or more high absorptivity minerals. Data quantify
the challenges of detection of dark minerals with Raman spectroscopy,
e.g., the hydration peak of 13 vol % nontronite is detected in a low
absorptivity anhydrite matrix, but three times that amount is required
to detect nontronite in a high absorptivity basalt matrix. On Mars
or other field settings with limited independent data, our models
can be used in conjunction with microimages or other quantitative
compositional instruments to estimate the detection thresholds and
abundances of minerals.

Introduction

Raman spectroscopy is becoming increasingly
common in Earth and
planetary science research as it enables characterization of minerals
while preserving textural relationships in rocks. Miniaturization
of Raman instruments now extends applications to in situ field use in a non-destructive and rapid manner without any sample
preparation. In addition to portable Raman instruments that are used
in terrestrial settings, there are currently
two Raman spectrometers operating in an extraterrestrial environment
on Mars on the NASA Mars 2020 Perseverance rover: the Scanning Habitable
Environments with Raman and Luminescence for Organics and Chemicals
(SHERLOC) that uses a 248.6 nm laser excitation source and the SuperCam Raman spectrometer that uses a 532 nm
laser excitation source. There are further
planned planetary missions with a Raman spectrometer, including the
Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) mission to Phobos, as well as the ExoMars mission to Mars.

While the SHERLOC and SuperCam Raman spectrometers
have provided
valuable data on the mineralogy of Jezero crater,
−

 one challenge of Raman spectrometers in the field
is that they are commonly measuring phases within mixtures because
field instruments are often limited in the magnification that can
be achieved. Benchtop Raman spectrometers are typically operated with
a high magnification objective lens to focus the laser spot size to
∼few μm, which is below the typical 10s to 100s of μm
grain size of the majority of mineral grains. In contrast, for commercial
portable field spectrometers, the laser spot size can be as small
as ∼10 μm but more typically is sub-millimeter.
,
 The laser spot size of SHERLOC is ∼100 μm, and that of the SuperCam Raman spectrometer is
few millimeters. Consequently, with field
instruments on Earth and Mars, the Raman scattered photons will originate
from an area that will often contain more than one phase. This outcome
has been observed with SHERLOC, where peaks originating from two different
minerals are observed in the same spectrum.
,

The effects of mixing can create challenges in detecting phases
of interest, particularly when there are dark materials in the field
of view of the laser. Here, we refer to “dark materials”
as materials with large absorption coefficients in the range of the
wavelength of the excitation laser and/or the Raman scattered light.
Attenuation of the exciting laser and any Raman scattered photons
can result in weak Raman peaks, and hence dark materials can be difficult
to detect and characterize with Raman spectroscopy. We can similarly
expect the Raman signal of light materials in a mixture with dark
materials to be diminished as a result of attenuation by the dark
materials.

Despite the importance of the effects of mixing on
the detectability
of minerals, few works have explored the phenomenology of this issue.
Vítek et al. explored the limits
of detection of organic molecules in a halite matrix using a portable
Raman instrument with a laser spot size of ∼100 μm and
a benchtop instrument with a laser spot size of ∼2 μm.
The portable instrument performed better only at very low concentrations
of 1 and 10 mg kg–1 β-carotene in halite,
which Vítek et al. attributed
to the larger spot size being better able to capture the average bulk
concentration of the organics dispersed in the halite matrix. Some
works have studied the Raman spectra of two- or three-component mineral
mixtures, focused on the goal of constructing calibration parameters
to quantify the abundances of the different components in the mixtures.
−

 Demaret et al. found that the intensity
ratio of the gypsum to quartz Raman peaks in a gypsum–quartz
mixture was not linear as a function of the weight fraction of gypsum
or quartz and attributed this to the differences in the Raman scattering
cross sections between gypsum and quartz. Breitman et al. studied the Raman spectra of two-component mixtures
of pigment material and noted that for an anatase–rutile mixture,
which are both low-absorptivity materials, the sum of the peak areas
of each component is linear to the proportions in which they are present
in the mixture. However, for mixtures made up of one light pigment
and one dark pigment such as rutile and ultramarine blue, Breitman
et al. described the relative intensities
of the peaks originating from each of the components as showing “a
nonlinear behaviour” that is “a complicated phenomenon”
that could be a result of reflection, absorption, transmission and/or
grain size.

Several works have examined the effects of absorption
on the intensity
of Raman peaks for an aqueous solution, whether the absorbing component
is the same as the Raman-scattering component (a phenomenon known
as self-absorption) or different.
−

 These works showed that the effects
of absorption on the Raman peak intensity can be characterized by
the Beer-Lambert law, which states that the emerging light decays
exponentially as a function of concentration of absorbing material
and distance traversed in the medium. Based on this, intuitively,
we can see that the Raman peak intensity of a particular mineral will
differ drastically, depending on whether it is mixed in an environment
with dark, highly absorbing minerals (such as iron-bearing silicate
minerals) or light, less absorbing minerals (such as evaporative salts).

Motivated by the question of detectability of minerals in a mixture
with field instruments of large laser spot size but broadly applicable
to any Raman system studying multi-component systems in analytical
chemistry applications, in this work, we present a mathematical model
that parametrizes the Raman peak intensity as a function of sample
absorption and Raman cross-section for two-component solid mixtures.
In geology, the effects of sample absorption are particularly important
as many minerals, especially Fe-containing minerals, have electronic
or crystal-field absorption features at the ultraviolet (UV), visible,
or near-infrared wavelengths, all of which are commonly used laser
excitation wavelengths in Raman spectroscopy. We have developed this
model based on the formalisms of Hong and Asher, Lin et al., Shriver and Dunn, and Wu et al., where
only one component is absorbing and/or Raman scattering, and extended
the approaches to describe a general two-component solid mixture,
where both components could be absorbing and Raman scattering at the
relevant frequencies. Subsequently, we verified this model through
laboratory measurements of the Raman spectra of three types of two-component
mixtures, focusing on the hydration (H2O/OH) peaks: (1)
low absorptivity + low absorptivity minerals, (2) low absorptivity
+ high absorptivity minerals, and (3) high absorptivity + high absorptivity
minerals. We will discuss how we can use these models to estimate
the quantitative values of absorptivity, Raman scattering cross section,
and Raman sensing depth and how these models have the potential to
provide a relatively straightforward way of developing an in situ
calibration model that enables some quantitative analyses from Raman
spectroscopy on Mars.

Parameterizing the Effects of Sample Absorption
and Raman Cross-Section
on Raman Intensity for a Two-Component Solid Mixture

Absent
other effects, the intensity of Raman peaks depends on the
Raman scattering cross-section in a linear manner. The Raman intensity
also depends on wavelength-dependent sample absorption, which can
attenuate both the incident beam as well as the scattered Raman photons,
causing the emerging light to decay exponentially as a function of
concentration of absorbing material, as described by the Beer-Lambert
law.
−

 Sample absorption can significantly affect the Raman intensity because
the absorption cross-section in the ultraviolet (UV) to visible wavelength
range (∼10–16 cm2 molecule–1 str–1
 is many orders of magnitude larger than the Raman cross-section
(∼10–30 cm2 molecule–1 str–1).
,

The effects of
absorption on the intensity of Raman peaks can be
understood and characterized by examining the segments of the path
of the incident laser excitation light: absorption by the sample as
the incident light travels through the sample (labeled 1 in ), Raman scattering
(labeled 2 in ), and subsequent absorption by the sample of the Raman-scattered
photons as they travel out of the sample into the detector (labeled
3 in ). Even though Hong and Asher, Lin et
al., and Shriver and Dunn developed their formalisms of the effects of
absorption on the intensity of Raman peaks to study liquid solutions,
the general optical phenomena of absorption, as described by exponential
attenuation and Raman scattering, are applicable for solid mixtures
as well. We also extend their formalisms to describe a general two-component
solid mixture, where both components could be absorbing and Raman
scattering at the relevant frequencies.

However, one important difference
between solid and liquid materials
is that the propagation of the incident and Raman-scattered photons
through solid materials such as the pressed pellets that we have used
in this work is complicated by the multiple elastic scatterings that
occur due to the inhomogeneities in the microscopic structure of the
samples. In models where photon travels only in one direction, scattering
is combined with absorption into an attenuation coefficient such that
the intensity of light at a distance z is given by I(z) = I
0
e
–αz
, where I
0 is the incident intensity and α = k + s, where α is the combined absorption
(k) and scattering coefficient (s).
,
 In a two-directional flux model, Schrader
et al., based on the Kubelka-Munk theory,
showed that the Raman intensity of powdered samples can be expressed
as a function of the absorption and scattering coefficient. However,
these models are approximations and in reality, photons can scatter
in any direction in three dimensions in a random-walk manner.

The effects of scattering on Raman intensity are dependent not
only on sample characteristics such as crystal orientation and grain
sizes but also the optics of the instrument. Smaller grain sizes are
expected to increase the amount of multiple scattering. However, whether
multiple scattering leads to enhanced or attenuated Raman intensity
depends also on the laser beam size used. The exponential attenuation
model that combines scattering into the attenuation coefficient and
the Kubelka-Munk theory predict that increased elastic scattering
will result in decreased Raman intensity. This trend is observed for small laser beam sizes (<5 μm)
with graphite and coal samples. However,
for instruments with a larger laser beam size (>100 μm),
multiple
scattering can lead to the excitation of more Raman-active components
within the volume that is probed by the laser, resulting in an increase
in Raman intensity that is not consistent with theoretical predictions. The competing effects of multiple scattering
due to the instrument optics and grain size appear to result in a
non-monotonic trend of Raman intensity as a function of grain size.
Specifically, previous works have found that the Raman intensity is
maximized at an intermediate grain size (∼30–100 μm)
for olivine, feldspar and carbonates but pyroxene does not show a
consistent trend.
−

 Indelicato et al. observed
an increase in Raman intensity as a function of grain size from 10–50
μm for calcite, with the Raman intensity at 50 μm ten
times greater than at 10 μm. They modeled this increase with
the formulation of Schrader et al. based
on the Kubelka-Munk theory, incorporating the thickness of the Raman-scattering
layer, Raman scattering cross-section, absorption coefficient, and
scattering coefficient. For grain size
50–900 μm, the Raman intensity of calcite was observed
to decrease as a function of grain size, with the Raman intensity
at 900 μm half of that at 50 μm. They modeled this decrease
with the reflectivity of the air–particle interface and the
absorption coefficient.

Due to
the nontrivial and not fully studied nature of the effects
of grain sizes and multiple scattering on Raman intensity, in this
work, we make a simplifying assumption that the contributions of elastic
scattering are small compared to absorption. This is not a bad assumption
for the pressed pellet samples that we have used, which are expected
to have few pore spaces and hence less scattering than powdered samples studied in previous works.
,−

 We verified the validity of this assumption in the “” section through laboratory measurements where
the grain sizes are not controlled and the laser spot size is relatively
large (∼30 μm). We emphasize that quantitative analyses
of Raman spectroscopy for solid samples including pressed pellet samples
will benefit from a future more sophisticated treatment of the elastic
scattering that arises from the microscopic heterogeneity of solid
materials. Considerations of multiple scattering in the model will
affect the measured Raman sensing depth, though our estimates with
this simplified model predict results to within an order-of-magnitude
or better (see “”).

We are
interested in modeling the Raman scattering contributions
from both components because, in some of our experimentally studied
samples (see “”), the hydration
peak can originate from multiple minerals. The hydration peak at ∼3000–4000
cm–1 is caused by the O–H symmetric and asymmetric
stretching of both molecular H2O and structural hydroxide
OH and can originate from many mineral groups, including hydrous salts
like sulfates and carbonates and silicates like phyllosilicates.

Experimental
Verification of Model As Relevant to Geologic Materials

Endmember Sample
Selection and Sample Preparation

To
characterize how the intensities of the Raman peaks depend on the
concentration of materials in mixtures of different absorptivities
and different Raman scattering cross sections, our sample suite includes
samples with qualitatively high and low absorptivity, as well as high
and low Raman scattering cross sections. Note that there are few quantitative
databases of absorptivity and Raman cross section, and these can vary
in ways specific to a particular sample (e.g., due to crystallinity,
minor/trace elements), so our knowledge of these parameters is qualitative/comparative
only.

The specific samples we have chosen include hydrated,
hydroxylated and anhydrous minerals that have either been detected
on Mars or proposed to be present (). Natural samples of a basalt (K1919), the
Fe-bearing phyllosilicate nontronite (NAu-2), serpentine and gypsum
were prepared by Thomas et al. as pressed
pellets from powdered samples of <250 μm. We made additional
pressed pellets with synthetic powder samples of anhydrite, gypsum,
kieserite and brucite, which were purchased from chemical supply companies
and used as is, without further independent characterizations. The
identities of these synthetic samples were confirmed through the Raman
spectra we collected ().

We mixed these different endmember powder samples
at various proportions
to produce three types of binary mixtures with different combinations
of absorptivity: (1) Low absorptivity + low absorptivity, (2) Low
absorptivity + high absorptivity, (3) High absorptivity + high absorptivity
(). To create
these mixtures, first we weighed out the masses of the powder samples
using an analytical balance (±0.1 mg) at the relative proportions
listed in .
We mixed these two components in a mortar and pestle and pressed them
into a 1.2 cm diameter pellet at a pressure of 124 MPa for 20 s. The
thickness of the pellets was not controlled but the average thickness
of 20 randomly measured pellets was 1.11 ± 0.26 mm.

Acquisition and Processing of Raman Spectra

We used
a Renishaw Raman inVia Spectrometer with a 514.5 nm excitation source
and 1,800 lines/mm grating to acquire Raman spectra from 100–4000
cm–1 at every 1 cm–1 interval.
The laser power was attenuated to 10%, equivalent to 10 mW. The wavelength
was calibrated with a silicon chip to within 0.1 cm–1. A 5× objective was used for all the mixtures except the serpentine
+ basalt mixture. The resulting laser beam size with a 5× objective
is ∼30 μm and spectral resolution 6 cm–1. Even though a lower objective lens results in a lower signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR), we used this to enable mixed phases to be probed in the
field of view of the laser. For the serpentine + basalt mixture, we
used a 20× objective, giving a laser beam size of ∼10
μm, to increase the SNR to get usable Raman spectra from the
weakly scattering serpentine.

No pre-treating was conducted
for all but the natural gypsum sample from Fremont County, Colorado,
which we pre-treated with 150 s of photobleaching before acquiring
Raman spectra to reduce the fluorescence background. For all samples
but the serpentine-basalt mixtures and serpentine endmember, we collected
a two-dimensional map of Raman spectra consisting of 100 points in
a 10 × 10 grid covering a 1 mm × 1 mm area of the sample.
The measurements were recorded using 10 s exposure time and one spectral
accumulation. For the serpentine-basalt mixtures, the Raman spectral
map consisted of 100 points in a 10 × 10 grid covering a 0.1
mm × 0.1 mm area of the sample. The measurements were recorded
using 10 s exposure time and two spectral accumulations. For the serpentine
endmember, we attempted to make measurements from hundreds of different
points on the sample using 10 s exposure time and two spectral accumulations.
However, the acquisition of a Raman spectrum was hindered by fluorescence
from the natural serpentine sample and we were only able to successfully
acquire one usable Raman spectrum of endmember serpentine.

The
acquired spectra were processed with self-written Python scripts.
We focused our analyses on the hydration peaks and removed the baseline
locally in the range within 2900–3900 cm–1 in the vicinity of the hydration peaks (see for the specific wavenumber range for the
different minerals), using a linear function to model the baseline
to offset the effects of fluorescence except for the gypsum-brucite
sample, as was done in Phua et al. Subsequently,
we fit the hydration peaks with up to two Gaussian components for
all samples except the gypsum-brucite sample, using the SciPy library
of Python. The hydration peaks can be
approximated with Gaussian profiles, as the intrinsic peaks for the
O–H symmetric and asymmetric stretch modes are inhomogeneously
broadened as a result of diverse microenvironments.
,
 For the gypsum-brucite sample, we modeled the baseline with the
improved asymmetrically reweighted penalized least squares method
using the pybaselines library of Python. In addition, we find that while the hydration peaks of gypsum from
molecular H2O are well approximated as Gaussian functions,
the narrow hydration peaks of brucite from structural OH (FWHM ∼
7) are better approximated as Lorentzian functions. This difference
is likely because the structural OH in brucite are arranged in a well-ordered
crystalline lattice, resulting in a homogeneously broadened peak that
is Lorentzian.
,

We filtered and removed
fitted peaks that are of lower confidence
in subsequent analyses based on the SNR. For all samples except the
serpentine + basalt samples, we calculated the SNR, which we defined

Results and Discussion

The representative spectra of gypsum-basalt mixtures, a low absorptivity
+ high absorptivity suite, show that the peak intensities of the SO4 symmetric and asymmetric stretch, as well as OH stretch,
all of which can be attributed to gypsum, increase with increasing
concentration of gypsum from 30 to 100 wt %, as expected ((A)). These spectra
were chosen because they have OH peak areas that are comparable to
the average OH areas calculated for the respective samples. However,
there is actually substantial heterogeneity in the peak intensity
spot-to-spot. The optical microscope image of the scan area for 50
wt % gypsum-basalt illustrates spatial heterogeneities in the distribution
of gypsum and basalt within the sample ((B)). Even though we have used a low magnification
objective lens (5×) to increase the laser spot size, the heterogeneity
of the sample is sometimes at a larger scale (>100 μm) than
the laser spot size (∼30 μm), also expected given the
particle size distribution of the mixtures. The heterogeneities in
the distribution of gypsum and basalt correlate to heterogeneities
in the Raman spectra recorded, whereby the highest OH peak areas are
correlated to locations with higher concentrations of white material
that is presumably gypsum ((B)).

While the compositional heterogeneity at the microscopic scale
of the gypsum-basalt mixture gives rise to variable OH peak areas
at each measured point ((B)), the average OH peak areas converge as the number of
sampled points increases (from n = 10 to n = 100; (C)). Mixtures with a higher concentration of gypsum converge
to a higher average OH peak area, consistent with our expectations.
All mixture samples showed that the average OH peak areas converge
as the number of sampled points increases (). However, for the nontronite-anhydrite
samples, the average OH peak area for 50 wt % nontronite converged
to a value that is larger than that of 80 wt % nontronite by ∼8%.

These results show that acquiring an average of 100 sampled points
over a defined region can bridge the gap between the microscopic compositional
heterogeneity that is measured by Raman spectroscopy and the macroscopic
bulk composition of the mixture samples. This is consistent with the
results of Haskin et al., who found that
it is possible to quantify the mineral proportions in a rock sample
by acquiring 100 Raman spectra from the sample and examining the proportion
of spectra that contain the mineral peak of interest. Consequently,
we use the average OH peak area as representative of the Raman signal
intensity for each mixture sample, to investigate how the Raman intensity
changes as a function of concentration and verify and .

The fit parameters for the models are derived from least-squares
fitting. To quantify the uncertainties of the fit parameters, we used
the nonparametric bootstrap method. Specifically, for a given mixture
series, for each concentration value, we randomly sampled 100 values
with replacement from our measured Raman OH peak areas to form a “new”
set of Raman OH peak areas. We computed the bootstrap mean for this
new set and fitted our model using the bootstrap mean at each corresponding
concentration. We repeated this procedure 2000 times for each concentration
value in a mixture series. The fit parameters we report here are the
mean from the 2000 fits conducted, while the uncertainty is the standard
deviation from the 2000 fits.

An effect of note, apparent for
mixtures containing high-absorptivity
materials (see “” and “”), is
a dominance of points with low signal. Approximately half the points
in the 50 wt % gypsum-basalt sample record very small OH peak areas
<10000 (48/100 points), with the remaining half of the points having
OH peak areas of 10000–94114 ((B)). This asymmetrical distribution of peak
intensities will cause the sample’s average OH peak area to
be weighted more strongly to low values closer to 0 than the arithmetic
average between the maximum OH peak area (=94114) and the minimum
OH peak area (=0). A similar phenomenon of asymmetrical distribution
of peak intensities is also observed for low absorptivity + low absorptivity
mixtures such as kieserite-anhydrite due to points that consist of
purely anhydrite contributing no OH peaks ().

Discussion

Observed Detection Thresholds
and the Dominant Effect of High
Absorptivity Materials

The dominant effects of high-absorptivity
materials are observed when comparing the Raman peak intensity of
the same material mixed in a low or high absorptivity matrix. For
example, when kieserite, a low-absorptivity material, is mixed with
anhydrite, another low-absorptivity material, at our lowest measured
concentration of 22 vol % kieserite, the Raman OH peak area recorded
was ∼37000 ((A)). In contrast, when kieserite is mixed with nontronite,
a high-absorptivity material, 87 vol % of kieserite is needed to produce
an equivalent Raman OH peak area of ∼37000 ((B)). Similarly, the Raman
OH peak of nontronite, a high-absorptivity material, was detectable
at 13 vol % mixed in low-absorptivity anhydrite ((C)), but this concentration
of nontronite in nontronite-basalt was insufficient to produce OH
peaks that pass the signal detection threshold, which requires ∼35
vol % nontronite in basalt ((A)). When the signal detection threshold is instead
set to SNR > 3, the detection threshold of nontronite in anhydrite
remains at 13 vol % but is increased to 56 vol % for nontronite in
basalt. The trend of requiring higher concentration of a phase for
its detection in a dark matrix is consistent regardless of the exact
signal detection threshold chosen. Therefore, these results support
our qualitative understanding that dark minerals can create challenges
in the detection of Raman peaks of materials of both low and high
absorptivities due to more significant attenuation.

In the context
of Earth and planetary sciences, these results imply that a mineral
with low absorptivity such as evaporative salts will need to be present
in higher concentration when mixed with high-absorptivity minerals
such as Fe-bearing silicate rocks compared to other low-absorptivity
minerals. Similarly, minerals of high absorptivity and/or low Raman
scattering cross sections such as phyllosilicates need to be nearly
pure or mixed with low absorptivity minerals such as evaporative salts
in the field of view of the laser to produce observable Raman signatures
with field Raman spectrometers such as SHERLOC on the Mars 2020 rover.
However, phyllosilicate minerals tend to be dispersed as micrometer-scale
alteration products in/around silicate or oxide mineral grains in
a matrix and are rarely present as filled veins or vugs that are pure
phyllosilicates. Their tendencies to form in dispersed textures could
explain the lack of detections of phyllosilicates by the SHERLOC Raman
spectrometer relative to widespread detections by orbital and in situ
near-infrared spectroscopy.

if we know the Raman sensing depth, Raman scattering cross sections,

Conclusion

We
developed mathematical models that quantitatively explain Raman
peak intensity as a function of concentration, Raman scattering cross
section, absorptivity, and the Raman sensing depth, all of which are
sample- and wavelength-dependent properties. Our analysis revealed
different functional forms for two-component mixtures, depending on
the relative absorptivities of the materials and whether one or both
materials have the Raman peak of interest. We verified the performance
of the mathematical models through laboratory Raman measurements of
prepared mixtures for three types of two-component mixtures: (1) low
absorptivity + low absorptivity materials, (2) low absorptivity +
high absorptivity materials, and (3) high absorptivity + high absorptivity
materials. Our systematic exploration of the Raman intensity as a
function of concentration for these different mixtures also demonstrates
quantitatively that it is challenging to detect minerals when they
are mixed in a matrix of high absorptivity minerals.

With knowledge
of the Raman scattering cross section and the absorptivity,
our models can be used to solve for the abundance of materials through
Raman spectroscopy. Conversely, our models potentially provide an
alternative way to deduce some of these properties if at least one
of these properties has been characterized and is known through other
lab measurements. Provision of the instrument response function for
a Raman system relating photon counts to irradiance would enable determining
Raman cross-section from experiment. Further development of methods
that can characterize sample absorptivity concurrent with Raman intensities
of the same target material can lead to a higher confidence in the
derived abundances and the Raman sensing depth, providing a pathway
to field-based Raman quantification of minerals.
