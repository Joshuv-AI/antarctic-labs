Abstract

53

Candida auris has emerged globally as a multidrug-resistant yeast that can spread via nosocomial

54

transmission. An initial phylogenetic study of isolates from Japan, India, Pakistan, South Africa, and

55

Venezuela revealed four populations (Clades I, II, III, and IV) corresponding to these geographic

56

regions. Since this description, C. auris has been reported in over 30 additional countries. To trace this

57

global emergence, we compared the genomes of 304 C. auris isolates from 19 countries on six

58

continents. We found that four predominant clades persist across wide geographic locations. We

59

observed phylogeographic mixing in most clades; Clade IV, with isolates mainly from South America,

60

demonstrated the strongest phylogeographic substructure. C. auris isolates from two clades with

61

opposite mating types were detected contemporaneously in a single healthcare facility in Kenya. We

62

estimated a Bayesian molecular clock phylogeny and dated the origin of each clade within the last 339

63

years; outbreak-causing clusters from Clades I, III, and IV originated 34-35 years ago. We observed

64

high rates of antifungal resistance in Clade I, including four isolates resistant to all three major classes of

65

antifungals. Mutations that contribute to resistance varied between the clades, with Y132F in ERG11 as

66

the most widespread mutation associated with azole resistance and S639P in FKS1 for echinocandin

67

resistance. Copy number variants in ERG11 predominantly appeared in Clade III and were associated

68

with fluconazole resistance. These results provide a global context for the phylogeography, population

69

structure, and mechanisms associated with antifungal resistance in C. auris.

Importance
In less than a decade, C. auris has emerged in healthcare settings worldwide; this species is

73

capable of colonizing skin and causing outbreaks of invasive candidiasis. In contrast to other Candida

74

species, C. auris is unique in its ability to spread via nosocomial transmission and its high rates of drug

75

resistance. As part of the public health response, whole-genome sequencing has played a major role in

76

characterizing transmission dynamics and detecting new C. auris introductions. Through a global

77

collaboration, we assessed genome evolution of isolates of C. auris from 19 countries. Here, we

78

described estimated timing of the expansion of each C. auris clade and of fluconazole resistance,

79

characterized discrete phylogeographic population structure of each clade, and compared genome data to

80

sensitivity measurements to describe how antifungal resistance mechanisms vary across the population.

81

These efforts are critical for a sustained, robust public health response that effectively utilizes molecular

82

epidemiology.

Introduction
In the last decade, Candida auris has emerged in healthcare settings as a multidrug-resistant

86

organism in over 30 countries worldwide (1). Primarily a skin colonizer, this pathogenic yeast can cause

87

bloodstream infections and other infections (2), is often resistant to multiple classes of antifungal drugs

88

(3, 4), and can spread via nosocomial transmission causing outbreaks of invasive infections (5-12).

89

Initial studies suggested that C. auris emerged simultaneously and independently in four global

90

regions, as phylogenetic analyses revealed four major clades of C. auris wherein isolates clustered

91

geographically (13). These clades are referred to as the South Asian, East Asian, African, and South

92

American clades or clades I, II, III, and IV, respectively (14). The isolates from these clades are

93

genetically distinct, differing by tens to hundreds of thousands of single nucleotide polymorphisms

94

(SNPs), with nucleotide diversity nearly 17-fold higher between clades compared to within clades (14).

95

All the clades, except Clade II, have been linked to outbreaks of invasive infections; uniquely, Clade II

96

appears to have a propensity for ear infections (15). The need for increased global efforts to understand

97

the population structure of C. auris was recently highlighted by the discovery of the first Iranian C. auris

98

case that yielded a single isolate representing a fifth major clade (16).

99

Molecular epidemiologic investigations of C. auris outbreaks generally show clusters of highly

100

related isolates, supporting local and ongoing transmission (7, 17, 18). The analysis of outbreaks and

101

individual cases has also revealed genetic complexity, with isolates from different clades detected in

102

Germany (19), United Kingdom (20) and United States (21), suggesting multiple introductions into the

103

countries, followed by local transmission. To date, each of the clades appears to have undergone clonal

104

expansion; while C. auris genomes have conserved mating and meiosis genes, only one of the two

105

fungal mating types are present in a given clade. Specifically, MTLa is present in Clades I and IV and

106

the other mating type, MTLα, is found in Clades II and III (14). Understanding whether mating and

107

recombination between clades is occurring is critical, especially in those countries, where isolates from

5

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

108

different clades and opposing mating types overlap in time and space. This information could help

109

contextualize complex epidemiologic findings or transmission dynamics.

110

In addition to its transmissibility, C. auris is concerning because of its high rates of drug

111

resistance. Three major classes of antifungal drugs are currently approved for systemic use – azoles,

112

polyenes, and echinocandins. Most C. auris isolates are resistant to fluconazole (azole) (22). Elevated

113

minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) to amphotericin B (polyene) have been reported in several

114

studies and resistance to echinocandins is emerging in some countries (22). Numerous mechanisms of

115

antifungal resistance have been described for C. auris. Echinocandin resistance has been linked to a

116

single mutation at S639P/F in FKS1, the gene that encodes the echinocandin target 1,3-beta-D-glucan

117

synthase (23). Most isolates display a mutation linked to fluconazole resistance in C. albicans; three

118

mutations, Y132F, K143R, and F126L, have been identified in ERG11, the gene that encodes the azole

119

target lanosterol 14-α-demethylase. These mutations have been shown to associate by clade where

120

Y132F and K143R are predominately found in Clades I and IV and F126L is exclusively in Clade III

121

(13, 24). Additionally, there have been suggestions that increased copy number of ERG11 may be a

122

mechanism for fluconazole resistance in C. auris (14).

123

To better understand C. auris emergence and population structure, we engaged in a global

124

collaboration involving 19 countries to produce a large dataset of C. auris whole-genome sequences

125

from hundreds of cases and associated environmental samples from healthcare surfaces. Our goal was to

126

generate a comprehensive genomic description of a global C. auris population to provide a population

127

genetic framework for the molecular epidemiologic investigations.

130

Geographic distribution of C. auris major clades

131

We first performed a phylogenetic analysis to characterize the global distribution of C. auris

133

sample collection clustered in one of the four major C. auris clades (Figure 1A). In this collection, 126

134

(41%) were classified as Clade I, seven (2%) as Clade II, 51 (17%) as Clade III, and 120 (39%) as Clade

135

IV. Globally, Clade I was the most widespread and found in ten countries (Canada, France, Germany,

136

India, Kenya, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, United Kingdom, United Arab Emirates, and United States);

137

Clade II was found in Canada, Japan, South Korea, and United States; Clade III in Australia, Canada,

139

States, and Venezuela (Figure 1B). Multiple clades were found in Canada, Kenya, and United States.

140

In contrast to an initial report (13), we observed a weaker phylogeographic substructure as

141

isolates from countries of most global regions appeared interspersed in phylogenies, although there was

142

notable clustering by country within Clade IV (Figure 1C). Within Clade I, there were three

143

predominant subclades, each including isolates from India and Pakistan. The smallest subclade included

144

the B8441 reference genome and three other isolates. The other two subclades were more closely related

145

to each other and included groups of highly related isolates from outbreaks in Kenya, United Kingdom,

146

and United States. Additionally, Clade I isolates from countries in Europe (Germany, France) and the

147

Middle East (Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates) appeared interspersed in the phylogeny, suggesting

148

multiple introductions of C. auris into these countries. Clade II was rarely observed and consisted of

149

seven diverse isolates from Japan, South Korea, United States, or Canada, where six were from cases

150

involving ear infections. Other examples of phylogeographic mixing included isolates from Australia

151

and Spain clustered with Clade III and isolates from Israel clustered with Clade IV, clades originally

152

described as the African and South American clades, respectively.

Evolutionary rate and molecular dating

154

To better understand the emergence of this species, we next estimated the divergence times of

155

the four major clades. We utilized collection dates for clinical isolates and associated environmental
7

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

156

samples, such as swabs from healthcare facilities, which ranged from 2004 to 2018; most (98%) were

157

collected from 2012 to 2018 (Figure 2A). By analyzing Clade III isolates from a single healthcare

158

facility in Kenya experiencing an outbreak of ongoing transmission (25), we confirmed that the

159

divergence level of these isolates are temporally corelated, supporting use of molecular clock analyses,

160

and calculated a mutation rate of 1.8695e-5 substitutions per site per year (Supplementary Figure 1).

161

This rate was used for a Bayesian approach for molecular dating of a phylogeny for all four clades.

162

Using a strict clock coalescent model, we estimated that the time to most recent common ancestor

163

(TMRCA) for each clade occurred within the last 339 years (Figure 2B, C). Clade IV emerged most

164

recently with a TMRCA of 1984 (95% HPD 31.5 - 37.1 years ago), while Clade II was the oldest with a

165

TMRCA of 1679 (95% HPD 321.2 - 356.4 years ago). Lastly, we observed the divergence within the

166

19th century for the two most closely related clades, Clades I and III, 1878 (95% HPD 132.8 - 149.0

167

years ago) and 1844 (95% HPD, 164.2 - 187.3 years ago), respectively (Figure 2B, C). These dates are

168

impacted by the inclusion of divergent isolates in both clades, which notably do not have ERG11

169

resistant mutations and are often drug susceptible, one isolate from Canada in Clade III and two isolates

170

from Pakistan and one from United States in Clade I (Figures 1C and 2B). Excluding these drug

171

susceptible outliers for Clades I and III, TMRCA estimates are for Clade I in 1984 (95% HPD, 17.5 –

172

21.74 years ago) and for Clade III in 1985 (95% HPD, 16.0 – 21.5 years ago); these more recent

173

estimates are more similar to that estimated for Clade IV. The estimated dates of TMRCA for each

174

individual clade supports the recent expansion during the ongoing outbreak. Together this suggests an

175

older separation of the four clades and the recent diversification of each clade in the years before the

176

detected outbreaks.

C. auris population structure

179

We next examined the global genomic data set for evidence of population substructure and

180

recent admixture. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identified four well separated populations
8

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint

181

corresponding to Clades I, II, III, and IV, and the tight clustering of isolates within each clade suggests

182

there is no recent admixture in any isolates (Figure 3A). To assess genetic diversity at the clade level,

183

we compared population genetic statistics, including nucleotide diversity (π), Tajima’s D (TD), fixation

184

index (FST) and pairwise nucleotide diversity (DXY) (Figure 3B to E). Overall, Clade I and III showed

185

the lowest genetic diversity (π = 1.51e-5 and π = 1.42e-5, respectively); Clade IV exhibited nearly three

186

times these levels (π = 4.23e-5) and Clade II presented the highest genetic diversity (π = 1.29e-4), nearly

187

nine times higher than Clades I and III (Figure 3B). Clade II was also the only clade that exhibited

188

positive TD (td = 1.153; Figure 3B and C), suggesting balancing selection as expected from the long

189

branches observed in Clade II phylogeny (Figure 1C). In Clades I, III, and IV we observed negative TD

190

values consistent with recent population expansions and the shorter phylogenetic branches (Figure 3B

191

and C, Figure 1C); however, Clade IV exhibited a highly variable distribution of TD relative to Clade I

192

and III (Figure 3C; Supplementary Figure 2), which suggests that these clades have experienced

193

distinct evolutionary processes, such as different degrees of population bottlenecks.

194

Genome-wide FST analysis highlighted substantial interspecific divergence and reproductive

195

isolation between C. auris clades (average genome-wide FST > 0.94 in all inter-clade comparisons;

196

Figure 3D; Supplementary Figure 2). Comparison of the two most closely related clades (Clades I and

197

III) revealed small regions with FST values close to zero; these regions of identity were distributed across

198

the genome (5.86% of the genome; Figure 3E; Supplementary Figure 2, 3). Phylogenetic analysis

199

revealed that these regions in isolates from Clades I and III are intermixed in a monophyletic clade

200

(Supplementary Figure 3). Comparison of DXY values across the genome highlighted regions of

201

population divergence between C. auris clades. Even between these clades with substantial interspecific

202

divergence, we detected large genomic tracts that exhibit either high or low DXY values. For DXY, we

203

observed that all chromosomes exhibited a bimodal distribution of regions of both high and low levels of

204

DXY; scaffolds 8 and 10, which correspond to chromosomes 1 and 3, respectively (14), show only low

We observed a single C. auris mating type in each clade. Isolates in Clades I and IV had MTLa

210

and those in Clades II and III had MTLα (Supplementary Figure 4); this confirms prior findings from a

211

smaller data set (14) in this larger global survey. Countries with multiple clades (i.e., Canada, Kenya,

212

213

214

observed contemporaneously in a single healthcare facility in Kenya based on the PCA analysis.

215

Together, these findings suggest that the C. auris clades have been genetically isolated and that variation

216

across the genome was likely impacted by karyotype variation that prevented equal chromosome

217

mixing.

Antifungal drug resistance and mechanisms of resistance
To examine resistance levels, we performed antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) to

221

fluconazole, amphotericin B, and micafungin – drugs representing each of the major classes. Of the 294

222

isolates tested, 80% were resistant to fluconazole, 23% to amphotericin B, and 7% to micafungin (Table

223

1, Figure 4). Clade II had the greatest percentage (86%) of susceptible isolates, including only one

224

isolate resistant to fluconazole, and Clade I had the greatest percentage of resistant isolates to

225

fluconazole (97%) and amphotericin B (54%). Additionally, Clade I had the highest rates of multidrug-

226

resistance (two antifungal classes; 49%) and was the only clade to have extensive drug-resistance (3%)

227

to all three major classes of antifungals, including isolates from two geographic regions (United Arab

228

Emirates and Kenya) that cluster together (Table 1; Figure 4). Amphotericin B resistance only appeared

229

in Clades I and IV, and was dispersed across the phylogeny in Clade I and detected in a Clade IV cluster

We next determined the genotype of specific drug mutations in the ERG11 gene that have been

234

235

Y132F spanning 11 countries in 53% of isolates from Clade I and 40% of isolates from Clade IV

236

237

from Clade IV. F126L was found only in Clade III, in nearly all isolates (96%) (Figure 4); all isolates

238

with F126L also carried the adjacent mutation V125A. Nearly all of the isolates with these changes in

239

ERG11 were resistant to fluconazole; 99% of the isolates with Y132F or K143R and 100% of the

240

241

polymorphisms in S639 in the hotspot1 of the FKS1 gene in 90% of the isolates with decreased

242

243

resistant to micafungin), and S639F and S639Y were found in micafungin-resistant isolates from Clade I

244

and III (Figure 4).

Analysis of the distribution of ERG11 copy number variation (CNV) revealed that of 304

246

isolates, 18 (6%) had either two or three copies. Of those 18 isolates, all were resistant to fluconazole

251

Discussion

263

In this study, we used whole-genome sequencing to describe a global collection of C. auris

264

isolates collected from patients and healthcare facilities between 2004 and 2018. We found that the four

265

predominant clades are genetically distinct with strong geographic substructure in Clade IV. Using

266

collection dates to estimate a molecular clock, we dated the origins of the four clades and confirmed the

267

recent emergence of C. auris. Furthermore, we characterized mutations associated with antifungal

268

resistance by clade, which varied between clades and country of isolation. While the clades appear

269

largely clonal in species phylogenies and represent a single mating type, we found that they have distinct

270

evolutionary histories and genome-wide patterns of variation. We provided a browsable version for C.

271

auris genomic epidemiology through Microreact (28) to explore phylogeny, geographic distribution,

272

timeline and drug resistance mutations (https://microreact.org/project/wfwXVjf9G).

273

In contrast to previous reports, we observed more phylogeographic mixing for C. auris (13).

274

While we found that isolates from additional global regions can be clearly assigned to one of the four

275

previously reported clades, we observed that three countries – Canada, Kenya, and United States – had

276

isolates corresponding to multiple C. auris clades (Figure 1B). Additionally, isolates from multiple

277

clades have been previously reported in Germany (19) and the United Kingdom (20). As travel has been
12

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

278

previously shown to play a major role in the spread of C. auris (21), global travel of persons with prior

279

healthcare exposures to C. auris has likely contributed to the observed phylogeographic mixing. Our

280

analysis of the likely geographic origin of infections observed in new geographic regions is limited by

281

incomplete travel history for most patients in this study set. We noted that the strongest geographic

282

substructure was observed in Clade IV for isolates from Colombia, Panama and Venezuela, with

283

additional distinct clades of isolates from Israel and United States (Figure 1C). This finding further

284

supports evidence of rapid localized transmission in some of these countries (18, 21).

285

These results have confirmed prior findings from the analysis of a smaller data set where isolates

286

in Clades I and IV had MTLa and those in Clades II and III had MTLα (14). Although mating between

287

C. auris clades has not been reported, it is concerning that the majority of countries reporting multiple C.

288

auris clades have clades of opposite mating types. This is especially concerning in Kenya, where

289

opposite mating types were observed in single healthcare facility experiencing ongoing transmission. In

290

such a situation, it could be possible to have mixed infections of opposite mating types. If mating

291

occurred, this would lead to increased genetic diversity and the possibility for enhanced virulence and

292

exchange of drug resistance alleles. Continued efforts to characterize C. auris infections at the genomic

293

level are essential for the rapid detection of potential C. auris hybrids.

294

Assessment of C. auris population structure by PCA and genome-wide FST analysis yielded no

295

evidence for admixture between the major clades. The close relationship of Clades I and III is

296

highlighted by the detection of regions with very low FST values, which suggest recent divergence or

297

genetic exchange between these clades. Given that these regions were short and spread across the

298

genome, we hypothesized that they are a result of incomplete lineage sorting rather than recent

299

introgression events. We also observed variation in the average divergence between clades (DXY) along

300

each chromosome. This may be due to genome rearrangements between the clades, whereby genomic

301

areas exhibiting high DXY levels, or low gene flow, arose in C. auris due to chromosomal
13

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

302

rearrangements, which prevents recombination and supports high rates of genetic differentiation.

303

Variation in chromosome number and size as measured by electrophoretic karyotyping as well as

304

deletions, inversions and translocations detected by comparing genome assemblies of different C. auris

305

clade isolates have been described in C. auris (14, 26, 27).

306

This global survey has provided a wider perspective of the mechanisms and the frequency of

307

mutations associated with resistance to antifungal drugs. The presence of both resistant and susceptible

308

isolates in the same populations along with the presence of genetically related isolates with different

309

alleles of resistance genes indicate that the resistance in C. auris is not intrinsic and has been recently

310

acquired. The most common mutation associated with azole resistance in Clades I and IV was ERG11

311

Y132F; however, both clades also included genetically related isolates with ERG11 K143R. In contrast,

312

all fluconazole resistant isolates in Clade III carried ERG11 F126L substitution. In addition to mutations

313

in genes associated with drug resistance, we found that increase in copy number of ERG11 is

314

predominantly observed in Clade III again suggesting clade specific variation in mechanisms of azole

315

resistance. All but three isolates with micafungin resistance had FKS1 S639Y/P/F mutations. Taken

316

together, these observations suggest recent emergence of antifungal resistance in C. auris populations,

317

most likely in response to some unknown environmental change, such as increased use of azole

318

antifungals in clinical practice, agriculture, or both.

319

By using a molecular clock, we estimated ages of the four clades by calculating time to the most

320

recent common ancestor (TMRCA) of each clade. Our estimates demonstrated that Clade II was the

321

oldest clade with TMRCA of 339 years, while Clade IV was the youngest with TMRCA of 34 years.

322

Clade I and III isolates coalesce 140 and 175 years ago, respectively; however, in both Clade I or Clade

323

III the clusters of isolates associated with ongoing drug resistant outbreaks worldwide, which display

324

increased resistance to fluconazole and harbor mutations in ERG11 associated with drug resistance, have

325

emerged within the last 35 years. These results are consistent with the other population characteristics:
14

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

326

even with the smallest sample set, Clade II had the highest genetic diversity compared to the three other

327

clades and a positive TD indicative of the balancing selection, characteristics of an older population.

328

Conversely, three other clades had low genetic diversity and negative TD consistent with the rapid

329

emergence. Notably, the oldest C. auris isolate was collected from a patient in South Korea in 1996 (2)

330

and no other strains were identified by searching the historic Candida culture collections. The absence

331

of C. auris in culture collections prior to 1996 and a rapid emergence after 2012 suggest that this

332

organism only recently emerged as a human pathogen and likely occupied a different ecological niche.

333

Other notable fungal outbreaks also have been estimated to be of recent origin. For example, the

334

BdGPL lineage of the amphibian pathogen Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis was estimated to have

335

arisen only ~100 years ago (29). While our reported mutation rate of 1.8695e-5 substitutions per site is

336

consistent with that (5.7e-5; R2 = 0.37) reported in a previous study (17), the mutation rate over longer

337

time spans than we sampled is likely slower. We used collection dates spanning from 2004 to 2018 to

338

inform our estimate, and rates of molecular evolution measured over short time-scales tend to be

339

overestimated, as some sites will be removed over time by natural selection (30). Therefore, the rate is

340

more similar to a spontaneous mutation rather than an evolutionary substitution rate. If our mutation rate

341

is substantially overestimated, the exact times of C. auris emergence and clade divergence would be

342

older than we have estimated. We also acknowledge that utilizing only currently known isolates, which

343

are highly similar within clades, provides a limited sampling of a larger source population, which may

344

be also be undergoing sexual recombination. The identification and characterization of a wider

345

population sample of C. auris will provide a higher resolution view of the nodes separating these major

346

clades. However, as there is only speculation to date about potential associations or locations of such a

347

source population, we suggest that the dates reported be used as a rough estimate that will need further

348

evaluation when sources of additional diversity are identified.

349

Our molecular clock estimates demonstrate that nearly all outbreak-causing clusters from Clades

350

I, III and IV originated 34-35 years ago in 1984-85. Such recent origin and nearly simultaneous

351

detection of genetically distinct clades suggest that anthropogenic factors might have contributed to its

352

emergence. Specifically: 1) In the 1980s, azole drugs first became widely used in clinical practice. The

353

first azole topical antifungal drug, miconazole, was approved in 1971 followed by clotrimazole in 1972;

354

both became widely used for treatment of superficial fungal infections in the late 1970s. In 1981, the

355

first oral azole drug, ketoconazole, was released for treatment of systemic fungal infections (31). 2) In

356

agriculture, the first azole fungicides, triadimefon and imazalil, were introduced in 1973, and by the

357

early 1980s, ten different azole pesticide formulations were available. It has been demonstrated that

358

azoles from the agricultural use can penetrate ground water and accumulate in soils. 3) Also noteworthy,

359

our predicated emergence of C. auris as a human pathogen coincided with the early stages of AIDS

360

epidemics; however, the wide use of antifungal drugs, such as fluconazole, for treatment of secondary

361

fungal infections, had not started until the 1990s (32-34). Other anthropogenic factors might also have

362

brought C. auris into contact with humans (35). Although the emergence of C. auris may be due to

363

multiple factors, the coincidence between the introduction of azoles and C. auris emergence is intriguing

364

and requires further investigation. Understanding processes that led to the emergence of C. auris in

365

humans is important to prevent emergence of other drug resistant fungi and pathogens.

366

Although a recent study reported an isolate from a fifth clade isolated from a patient in Iran (16),

367

all isolates in our collection were assigned to the previously described Clades I, II, III, and IV. This is

368

noteworthy because isolates from neighboring Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and United Arab Emirates were

369

represented in the analysis. Indeed, this highlights the unique nature of the divergent Iranian C. auris

370

case and advocates for increasing diagnostic capacity worldwide and continued phylogenetic studies to

371

understand C. auris diversity.

372

While we have included a set of diverse isolates, they likely differ from a random sample of the

373

C. auris population. The isolates were obtained by conventional sampling, and, therefore, our findings

374

do not represent country-specific characteristics of C. auris molecular epidemiology. Wider sampling

375

may also change the population structure and antifungal susceptibility profiles. Notably, at the time of

376

this analysis, Clade V had not yet been discovered, highlighting the importance of further sampling and

377

genomic characterization. Finally, since the environmental reservoir of C. auris remains unknown, our

378

analysis is based solely on the analysis of clinical isolates; higher genetic diversity, deeper divergence

379

times and different population structure is likely to occur in the natural populations of this fungus.

380

In conclusion, we have provided a comprehensive genomic description of a global C. auris

381

survey representing 19 countries on six continents. Given that C. auris is a transmissible multidrug-

382

resistant organism causing outbreaks of invasive infections in healthcare studies, an understanding of

383

how C. auris is spreading, evolving, and acquiring resistance to antifungal drugs is essential for robust

384

public health responses. Continued efforts to characterize the C. auris population, additional

385

mechanisms of antifungal resistance, and environments conducive for mating between clades is critical.

Methods

388

Sample collection

389

We performed genomic analyses on sequences from 304 C. auris isolates. This collection

390

included C. auris from 19 countries on six continents and were sampled from both C. auris cases and

391

environmental surfaces from healthcare facilities where ongoing transmission was occurring

392

(Supplementary Table 1). Samples from C. auris cases were derived from a variety of specimen source

393

sites including sterile sites, such as blood, and non-invasive sites, such as respiratory tract or urine. All

394

samples were a result of convenience sampling. For four countries (Colombia, Kenya, United States, and

395

Venezuela) where more than 50 samples were available, 50 representative samples were selected by
17

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

396

proportional random sampling: samples from each country were stratified by city, and then a subset was

397

randomly selected proportionally from each strata.

398
399
400

Sample preparation and whole-genome sequencing (WGS)
The sample collection comprised both publicly available sequences generated from previous

401

studies and newly sequenced isolates (Supplementary Table 1). For newly sequenced isolates, except

402

those from France, DNA was extracted using the ZR Fungal/Bacterial DNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo

403

Research, Irvine, CA, USA). For isolates from France, DNA was extracted using NucleoMag plant kit

404

extraction (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) in a KingFisherTM Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

405

Genomic libraries were constructed and barcoded using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep kit for

406

Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and were sequenced on either the Illumina HiSeq

407

2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the HiSeq Rapid SBS Kit v2 500-cycles or the

408

MiSeq platform using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 500-cycles. For the two isolates from France, libraries

409

were constructed using the Illumina Nextera Flex protocol and sequenced on an iSeq 100 to generate

410

paired 150 bp reads.

411
412
413

Variant identification
We used FastQC v0.11.5 and PRINSEQ v0.20.3 (36) to assess read quality and perform filtering

414

for low quality sequences using "-trim_left 15 -trim_qual_left 20 -trim_qual_right 20 -min_len 100 -

415

min_qual_mean 25 -derep 14". Paired-end reads were aligned to the C. auris assembly strain B8441

416

(GenBank accession PEKT00000000.2; (14)) using BWA mem v0.7.12 (37). Variants were then

417

identified using GATK v3.7 (38) using the haploid mode and GATK tools RealignerTargetCreator,

418

IndelRealigner, HaplotypeCaller for both SNPs and indels, CombineGVCFs, GenotypeGVCFs,

419

GatherVCFs, SelectVariants, and Variant Filtration. Sites were filtered with Variant Filtration using
18

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

420

"QD < 2.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQ < 40.0". Genotypes were filtered if the minimum genotype quality < 50,

421

percent alternate allele < 0.8, or depth < 10 (https://github.com/broadinstitute/broad-

422

fungalgroup/blob/master/scripts/SNPs/filterGatkGenotypes.py). Genomic variants were annotated and

423

the functional effect was predicted using SnpEff v4.3T (39). The annotated VCF file was used to

424

determine the genotype of known mutation sites in ERG11 and FKS1. To determine the mating type

425

locus (MTLa and MTLα) in each isolate, the average read depth at the locus was computed from the

426

aligned bam file and normalized by the total coverage depth.

Phylogenetic and phylodynamic analysis
For phylogenetic analysis, sites with an unambiguous SNP in at least 10% of the isolates (n =

430

222,619) were concatenated. Maximum likelihood phylogenies were constructed using RAxML v8.2.4

431

(40) using the GTRCAT nucleotide substitution model and bootstrap analysis based on 1,000 replicates.

432

Phylogenetic analysis was also performed for each clade using subsets of the entire VCF and visualized

433

using iTOL (41).

434

For phylodynamic analysis, we assessed for a temporal signal using a set of isolates from Kenya

435

Clade III using TempEst v1.5.3 (42) to quantify and to estimate an initial mutation rate from the R-

436

squared value. Bayesian phylogenies were generated using BEAST v1.8.4 (43) under strict molecular

437

clock (both lognormal and exponential priors). In addition, we applied both Bayesian Skyline coalescent

438

and Coalescent Exponential, and a GTR nucleotide substitution model. We obtained similar results

439

using the molecular rate estimated for a C. auris outbreak un United Kingdom (17). Specimen collection

440

dates (month and year) were used as sampling dates; the month of June (year midpoint) was assigned for

441

samples where month was unknown. Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were run

442

for 20 million steps using an unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) tree as a

443

starting tree and MCMC convergence was explored using Tracer v.1.7.1 (44). We generated a maximum
19

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.01.06.896548; this version posted January 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

444

clade credibility tree with TreeAnnotator v1.8.4 after discarding 47% as burn-in, and we visualized

445

phylogenies using FigTree v1.4.4.

448

Population genomic analyses
Population genomic analysis was performed using gdsfmt v1.14.1 (34), SNPRelate v1.12.2 (45),

449

and the PopGenome v2.6.1 (46) R packages. Genome-wide nucleotide diversity (π), Tajima’s D (TD),

450

fixation index (FST) and pairwise nucleotide diversity (DXY) were calculated and plotted per scaffold in 5

451

kb sliding windows. Genome-wide calculations are the average of all 5 kb windows for each metric.

452

Genomic regions that exhibit copy number variation (CNV; deletions and duplications) were identified

453

using CNVnator v0.3 (47) (genomic windows > 1 kb showing significant variation P-value < 0.01).

Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST)
AFST was performed on 294/304 (97%) isolates. The majority (N=270, 90%) of isolates were

457

tested at the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) as outlined by Clinical and

458

Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. Custom prepared microdilution plates (Trek Diagnostics,

459

Oakwood Village, OH, USA) were used for fluconazole and the echinocandin micafungin. Interpretive

460

breakpoints for C. auris were defined based on a combination of those breakpoints which have been

461

established for other closely related Candida species, epidemiologic cutoff values, and the biphasic

462

distribution of minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) between the isolates with and without known

463

mutations for antifungal resistance. Resistance to fluconazole was set at ≥32 µg/mL and at ≥4 µg/mL for

464

micafungin. Amphotericin B was assessed by Etests (BioMerieux), and resistance was set at ≥2 µg/mL.

465

For isolates not tested at the CDC, similar methods were employed and described previously (17, 19,

466

48). As there are no currently approved breakpoints for C. auris, for this manuscript breakpoints were

467

set at ≥32 µg/mL for fluconazole, >1 µg/mL for amphotericin B, and ≥4 for micafungin. These MIC
