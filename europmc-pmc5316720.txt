Royal Infirmary, Dundee.
As the etiology of acute perforating ulcers of the small intestine
is still obscure, I desire to place on record the notes of three
cases which have recently come under my care in the Dundee
Royal Infirmary.
Case I. (Register No. 4900/23).?Mrs A. M., a weaver, aged 53,
was sent to the Dundee Royal Infirmary on 13th November 1923,
complaining of pain in the abdomen of thirty-six hours' duration.
When at work on 12th November 1923, she was suddenly seized

to both iliac regions. She vomited and was taken home, but the
pain, which never abated, was intensified by movement, and everything
taken by the mouth was vomited. The bowels had not moved for
three days and there was pain during micturition.

Commentary.?The association with herniae and enter-
optosis in all three cases is interesting. The muscular develop-
ment was poor, and as the patients were senile the herniae
might be readily accounted for. There was very little space
between the posterior sheath of the rectus abdominis and the
lumbar vertebra in Case III., and considerable difficulty was
experienced in keeping gut out of the way when stitching up
the peritoneum. But a week later I operated on the brother
of Case III. for perforated duodenal ulcer, and here again the
same difficulty was met with. The abdominal cavity appeared
very small in the sagittal plane owing to marked lordosis.

It will be noted too that the site of rupture, in relation to

Etiology.?So far, the evidence available tends to support
the theory that these perforations are inflammatory, and the
infection is transmitted through the blood stream ; but this
theory is still far removed from proof. Material has not been
so abundant as to afford bacteriological comparison between the
offending organisms of the primary focus and of the perforation
of the small gut. On looking at these perforations one first
notices the absence of induration, and the dissimilarity between
them and perforated ulcers of the duodenum. To me, these
jejunal-ileal "ulcers" appeared like a partial tear of the bowel-
wall from within, the rent being completed by a bursting
pressure, also from within. The manner in which the mucous
membrane protruded showed this, the tear appearing bigger in
the mucous membrane than in the sero-muscular coat.
I made a hurried but unsuccessful local search for any
foreign body.

In dogs, however, where the alimentary canal is of the
carnivorous type, one finds cases of acute perforation of the
small intestine by sharp pieces of wood which have been chewed

proof of a foreign body causing acute perforation of the small
intestine.
Simmons and Von Glahn3 investigated "The effect of ground
Stass on the gastro-intestinal tract of dogs," and found that no
lesion was produced and that there was no toxic effect. But
the experiments were not repeated with pieces of broken glass
various sizes and with sharp edges.
Perhaps X-ray examination during early convalescence

Treatment.?It is my custom, in obscure cases of peritonitis,
to make a final examination of the patient before the reflexes
have disappeared with the deepening of anaesthesia. I make
my incision over the last area to lose its resistance or rigidity
under anaesthesia, and have found this sign a most valuable one
*n locating the position of any lesion. It is very useful in dif-
ferentiating one of the more silent types of perforation of the
duodenum from appendicitis, or in diagnosing the latter con-
dition from acute cholecystitis. Most of these cases of acute
Perforation of the ileum have been mistaken for appendicitis
with peritonitis, and the actual lesion would be very easily missed
if a grid-iron incision were used. It is therefore important to
incise right over the lesion. If faecal fluid is present, it is an
indication that a perforation has taken place, and the adhesion
of loops of gut covered with lymph is Nature's attempt to wall
off the site of the perforation, which can thus be readily found.

Conclusion.?There appears to be satisfactory evidence that
sharp foreign bodies sometimes perforate the wall of the small
intestine. The actual perforation may be preceded by sudden
or powerful contraction of the abdominal muscles. Possibly the
anterior abdominal wall, in contracting, is brought firmly into
contact with a portion of gut already partially penetrated by a
sharp foreign body and the penetration is thus completed.
Where there is enteroptosis, loops of small intestine are
"bunched" together in the lower abdomen and pelvis, and,
should a sharp foreign body be sticking in the mucous
membrane, contact with compression between anterior abdominal
wall and a lower lumbar vertebra or the promontory of the
sacrum, would give rise to perforation near the middle of the
small gut. I have likened the perforations, judging from their
appearance, to a " burst." Continued pressure with escaping
content, be it solid, fluid, or gaseous, will convert a pin-hole
perforation into a ragged tear, and will give a condition similar
to that which I found in those cases. It is doubtful whether
the uninjured or healthy wall of small gut could be burst by
sudden abdominal muscle contraction alone.
