Corticosteroids for Haemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis Secondary to Typhoid Fever: A Case Report
Authors: Sonia Baumard, Aurélie Brunet, Isabelle Quatresous, Claire Pluchart, Yohan N'Guyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnlike haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) induced by tuberculosis or rickettsial disease, there is no consensus for the treatment of HLH secondary to typhoid fever (Salmonella Typhi). After a short illustrative example, we searched among the previously described cases of HLH secondary to typhoid fever to determine: (i) Is the specific treatment of HLH mandatory; and (ii) which option to choose between corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins in association with antibiotherapy?

Introduction

Secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a potentially life‐threatening complication, due to a persistent activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, during a malignant, autoimmune or infectious process [1]. Besides viruses, a wide range of bacteria, such as Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Typhi (S. Typhi), have been described to lead to the development of secondary HLH [1, 2]. The diagnosis relies on the H‐score [3, 4] while corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIGs) and etoposide remain the first‐line treatments in adults and children [5, 6]. Recent guidelines [6] suggested that corticosteroids were not mandatory in cases of HLH induced by tuberculosis or rickettsial disease, but there are no guidelines about the treatment of HLH secondary to typhoid fever (HLHstf). After a short illustrative example, we searched in the literature to determine: (i) is a specific treatment of HLH is mandatory; and (ii) which option to choose between corticosteroids and IVIGs in association with antibiotherapy?

Case Report

We report the case of an 18‐year‐old French man of Senegalese ancestry without relevant past medical or recent international travel histories. He was admitted for a 2‐day‐long high fever with headache and abdominal pain. Abdominal examination did not reveal tenderness. A lumbar puncture ruled out the diagnosis of bacterial meningitis, and a computed tomography scan evidenced splenomegaly with a craniocaudal length greater than 120 mm (Figure 1A). A S. Typhi strain categorized as resistant to fluoroquinolones but susceptible to ceftriaxone grew on the blood and stool samples. Because of persisting fever despite ceftriaxone 2 g b.i.d, along with low platelets and high ferritin levels (Figure 1B) and a 2.25 mmol/L fasting triglyceride value (N < 1.71 mmol/L), a bone marrow aspirate was performed and yielded haemophagocytosis features (Figure 1C). The lower leucocyte, haemoglobin and fibrinogen values were 2.3G/L, 116 g/L and 3.2 g/L, respectively, whereas the higher ASAT value was 226 UI/L. Thus, the calculated H score and the probability of HLH were 229 and 97.8%, respectively [3]. The fever resolved only once a corticosteroid treatment (prednisone 40 mg the first evening then, 1 mg/kg qd) was prescribed for 14 days with rapid tapering, in association with ceftriaxone (Figure 1A). No fever recurrence was observed following corticosteroids and ceftriaxone withdrawal. No immune deficiency was further evidenced, specifically no oxidative burst or interleukin 12 receptor beta 1 (IL12Rβ1) deficiencies [7]. The patient gave his written consent for reporting his case.

A systematic literature search of other previously reported cases of HLHstf was performed (Table S1) before addressing the following questions.

Is the Specific Treatment of HLH Mandatory During HLHstf?

Several cases of the literature report a favourable outcome without any specific care other than an effective antibiotic therapy. Because the infection of rodents by Salmonella Typhimurium is considered a model of HLH [8] and because the HLH criteria [5] (fever, splenomegaly, cytopenia, hyperferritinaemia, etc.) overlap with the symptoms described during typhoid fever, it is suggested that some old cases of typhoid fever treated only by antibiotics could have been genuine secondary HLHs. Only half of the 53 patients received a specific treatment of HLH (corticosteroids, IVIGs and/or etoposide) in the review by Meena et al. [9]. The inclusion of all supplementary cases in the literature we found (Figure S1) gave a pooled odds ratio of death of 2.1956, 95% CI [0.0609, 79.1842]; p = 0.667 in the group of specific treatment (6 deaths out of 34 patients vs. 1 out of 29 untreated patients). However, the severity of patients could lead to a confounding bias, as the most severe patients with the highest risk of death had a higher chance of receiving a specific treatment for HLH. The severity of the patients receiving a specific HLH treatment is not directly compared to that of patients receiving no specific treatment in the review by Meena et al. [9], where a late admission in the third week of the disease and a ferritin value greater than 5000 ng/mL were observed in 60% of deceased patients and were independently associated with mortality, respectively. Two lethal cases of HLHstf treated by dexamethasone and dexamethasone plus etoposide on the 9th and the 20th days of symptoms, respectively [10, 11], suggested an association between late admission, severity and prescription of a specific HLH treatment. That is the reason why some authors recommend to consider antibiotics only if the patient seems stable and IVIGs or dexamethasone if the patient does not [12]. The use of etoposide as described in HLH protocol 2004 is only considered when there is no improvement after IVIGs or corticosteroids [12], as described for virus‐associated HLHs in adults [6].

Which Option Between Corticosteroids and IVIGs in Association With Antibiotherapy?

Both corticosteroids and IVIGs have been used in HLHstf, but case reports do not allow a direct comparison of corticosteroids versus IVIGs, which are far more expensive. Few cases like ours suggested that corticosteroids other than dexamethasone could be used. Indeed, dexamethasone was the most commonly prescribed corticosteroid with a posology of 10 mg/m2 up to 40 mg for 5–21 days with rapid tapering. It can be safely prescribed during typhoid fever cases in association with adequate antibiotic therapy [13], without increased risk of bowel perforation [14].

As S. Typhi strains are naturally resistant to aminoglycosides and may have acquired resistance to chloramphenicol, ampicillin, trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole and fluoroquinolones [15], azithromycin (bacteriostatic) and ceftriaxone (bactericidal) remain the first options for the empiric treatment of typhoid fever. Among the few cases of HLHstf involving extended spectrum β‐lactamase (ESBL) producing strains from India or Pakistan, the outcome was favourable with meropenem and specific HLH treatment.

Conclusion

To date, concerning the treatment of haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis secondary to typhoid fever, the published data do not allow definitive conclusions to be drawn: neither on the specific treatment to prescribe alongside with antibiotherapy nor when to start it. To address these questions, randomized controlled trials in highly endemic countries or master protocols should be conducted or built, respectively, with outcomes other than mortality (ferritin blood level, length of fever and length of hospital stay).
