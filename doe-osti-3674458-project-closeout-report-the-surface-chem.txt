Executive Summary: Project Key Findings and Impact on Accel-
eration of Hanford Tank Waste Clean Up Mission
This project addressed a central uncertainty in Hanford tank waste pretreatment as identified in the NNLEMS R&D
Roadmap for Hanford Tank Waste Mission Acceleration, NNLEMS-2022-00005, Rev. 0 report [1]: the process behav-
ior of plutonium in concentrated, strongly alkaline waste media relevant to Tank-Side Cesium-Removal (TSCR). Plu-
tonium in Hanford-relevant hyper-alkaline solutions does not solely exist as a dissolved molecular species. Instead,
a significant fraction may exist as PuO2 nanoparticles and their aggregates, with stability governed by electrolyte
composition, pH, and ligand chemistry. In concentrated electrolyte media, these nanoparticles can remain suspended,
increasing soluble Pu concentrations beyond expected solubility of crystalline PuO2 . Carbonate was shown to be
especially important because it stabilizes plutonium-bearing colloids under alkaline conditions and increases the
amount of plutonium remaining in the mobile phase.
These solution-dependent behaviors matter directly for TSCR because the crystalline silicotitanate (CST) columns
do not simply encounter a dissolved Pu concentration; rather, they encounter a dynamic mixture of dissolved, col-
loidal, and weakly aggregated plutonium-bearing species whose partitioning can change during waste staging,
dilution, mixing, and transport. Batch-contact experiments carried out in Hanford-relevant simulants showed that
plutonium partitioning to CST can be very efficient. Under baseline conditions representative of the TSCR process,
Pu distribution ratios of both PuO2 nanoparticles and molecular Pu(VI) species exceeded those measured for cesium
under the same chemical conditions. This observation is significant because CST is specifically designed for selec-
tive removal of Cs+ , yet the apparent affinity for plutonium-bearing phases can be of similar or greater magnitude.
The retention cannot be explained by the same pore-scale ion-exchange mechanism responsible for cesium uptake,
as both PuO2 nanoparticles and their aggregates as well as aqueous plutonium (VI) and (V) species are larger than
the CST pore apertures that are specifically designed for spherical monovalent cations like Cs+ and Sr2+ . Accord-
ingly, the results point to alternative retention pathways, including adsorption or association on external CST bead
and binder surfaces, interaction with CST-derived fines, and destabilization of plutonium colloids by CST leachate
components that promote aggregation and apparent removal from solution.
The project also identified several operationally important controls on unintended Pu uptake by CST. Increasing
carbonate concentration substantially suppresses Pu retention by stabilizing PuO2 nanoparticles in the mobile phase.
At the same time, experiments showed that fines generation and mechanical handling can strongly influence apparent
Pu removal. Under conditions that generate CST fines, a large fraction of the plutonium inventory can reside in this
potentially mobile fine fraction rather than exclusively on the macroscopic CST beads. This finding is important
because it means that simple two-phase interpretations of batch-contact data may overestimate true immobilization if
fines are capable of transport, deposition, and later remobilization in the DFLAW flowsheet.
Beyond short-term batch-contact behavior, the project also examined how plutonium-bearing oxide phases evolve
over time under Hanford tank waste conditions and how their retention compares across multiple oxide sorbents.
Aging experiments showed that PuO2 nanoparticles are not static phases, but instead continue to aggregate, partially
dissolve, repartition, and interact with secondary solids over periods of months as a function of pH, nitrate concen-
tration, and contact with CST, MST, and iron-bearing phases. In parallel, comparative sorption studies with goethite,
rutile, and CST showed that plutonium can be retained strongly by several oxide materials under alkaline, high ionic
strength conditions, with rutile and goethite providing useful context for interpreting CST behavior and suggesting
possible pre-filtration or pretreatment strategies to reduce plutonium transfer to CST.
Using the measured partitioning behavior together with available TSCR design and operating information, the
project developed first-order parametric estimates of plutonium loading on CST during DFLAW processing. These
estimates indicate that some CST loadings may remain below the 100 nCi/g transuranic waste threshold, whereas
others, especially for lead columns, may approach or exceed it depending on feed composition, processed volume,
carbonate concentration, and the distribution of retained Pu across the column setup. Agreement between these
projections and published CST digestion data from prior lab-scale TSCR testing indicates that this is a credible
operational and waste-classification concern rather than a purely conservative bounding case.
Overall, the project supports the Hanford cleanup mission by providing a mechanistic foundation for predicting
plutonium behavior in TSCR-relevant chemical environments and by identifying specific factors that can be used to
manage risk. The results support improved feed-chemistry control, more realistic interpretation of Pu partitioning
data, and better-informed strategies for TSCR operation and spent CST characterization and disposition.

1 Introduction & Motivation
The Hanford Site in Washington State is a former plutonium production site that produced large amounts of radioactive
waste during from the 1940s through the Cold War era. Today, approximately 55 million gallons of radioactive
waste are stored in 177 underground storage tanks, awaiting treatment and final disposition.[1, 2] These wastes are
chemically and radiologically diverse, consisting of saltcake, sludge, and alkaline supernatants. The supernatant phase
constitutes an especially difficult matrix because of its extremely high ionic strength and strongly caustic nature, with
Na+ concentrations exceeding 5 M, NO− −
 3 typically around 2 M, and free OH in the range of 0.8–1.2 M.[3, 4]

Under strongly alkaline brine conditions, plutonium speciation and phase distribution in tank supernatants are con-
trolled by the limited solubility of Pu(IV) oxide/hydroxide solids. These solids exist in equilibrium with submicron-
and nanoscale PuO2 particles and their aggregates, as well as with potentially dissolved plutonium species in the
+V and +VI oxidation states.[5–8] Soluble plutonium concentrations, likely comprising both colloidal and molecular
species, are typically found in the nanomolar range in the tanks, corresponding to 10−4 to 10−3 mCi/L.[5, 9, 10] Re-
cent evaluations of plutonium behavior in Hanford-relevant media highlight persistent uncertainties in predicting its
chemistry in tank supernatants and in understanding its interactions with solid surfaces, including tank solids such as
Al- and Zr-containing phases, as well as engineered materials used in waste processing.[5]
At the Hanford Site, the Direct-Feed Low-Activity Waste (DFLAW) program includes Tank-Side Cesium-Removal
(TSCR), designed to reduce the radiological dose of tank supernatants prior to vitrification by selectively removing
radioactive cesium from highly alkaline, high ionic strength liquid waste streams. The process relies on crystalline
silicotitanate (CST), an inorganic ion-exchange material with strong affinity for Cs+ . While CST is effective for its
intended purpose, prior testing at the radioactive waste test platform (RWTP, [11]) with Hanford tank waste showed
measurable retention of plutonium and other transuranic elements by the CST media [4, 9, 12, 13]. This creates an
important operational and waste-disposition concern because spent CST must remain below the regulatory threshold
for transuranic (TRU) waste classification (100 nCi/g). If the transuranium loading on CST exceeds that threshold, the
resulting waste form may not be compatible with the intended disposal pathway within the current DFLAW flowsheet.

Previous digestion studies of CST contacted with Hanford tank wastes from AP-107 and AW-102 showed that Pu
retained on the lead columns could approach or exceed the 100 nCi/g TRU limit [12]. These observations established
that unintended Pu uptake during TSCR is not merely a theoretical concern. At the same time, the mechanism of this
retention remained poorly constrained. This project was motivated by the need to understand plutonium behavior in
TSCR at the level of particle and surface chemistry.[1] Our central premise was that plutonium in Hanford-relevant
media should be treated not only as a dissolved thermodynamic component, but also as a population of reactive oxide
nanoparticles and aggregates whose behavior depends strongly on electrolyte composition, pH, and ligand chemistry.
If this plutonium is present as PuO2 nanoparticles, then its interaction with CST is unlikely to be governed by classical
ion exchange alone. Instead, it may depend on colloidal stability, aggregation, dissolution, particle-surface association,
and interactions with CST-derived fines or leachates. Understanding these processes is essential both for predicting
plutonium fate during TSCR and for developing mitigation strategies that minimize risk regarding the formation of
TRU-classified spent CST.
The plan for this research was therefore twofold: First, to establish a mechanistic understanding of PuO2 nanoparti-
cle formation, surface chemistry, stability, and reactivity under Hanford-relevant conditions; and second, to use that
understanding to develop pretreatment strategies that could mitigate or prevent Pu retention by CST (Figure 1).
Several working hypotheses guided the project.

 • First, plutonium in Hanford tank supernatants is present to a significant extent as nanocrystalline PuO2 particles
 and aggregates with a discrete and chemically responsive surface.
 • Second, the reactivity, charge, aggregation behavior, and stability of these particles can be strongly altered by
 electrolyte composition and ligand binding.

 • Third, those altered particle properties will in turn affect their partitioning to process materials such as CST and
 may offer opportunities for mitigation through feed chemistry control or pretreatment.
 • Finally, a predictive framework for plutonium behavior in TSCR must explicitly incorporate colloidal and
 nanoparticulate Pu phases rather than treating plutonium only as a dissolved species.

The sections that follow summarize the major outcomes of this project. They include development of surrogate
nanoparticle synthesis workflows, serving to refine synthetic approaches, characterize particle properties, and identify
key controls exerted by electrolyte and ligand chemistry (Chapter 2). These surrogate studies provided a foundation
for subsequent work on PuO2 nanoparticles themselves, including their aggregation, dissolution, and aging (Chapter
3). Complementing these colloid-focused studies, the project also used single-crystal oxide model systems to examine
interfacial processes relevant to tank waste chemistry (Chapter 4). These studies included Cs+ adsorption on alumina
under Hanford tank waste-relevant conditions, as well as exploratory measurements of CeO2 and PuO2 nanoparticle
association with alumina and yttria-stabilized zirconia surfaces. In these experiments, alumina served as a model for
Al-bearing phases present in tank sludges [2, 14], while zirconia served both as a model for Zr-bearing tank phases
and as a proxy for zirconium-containing binder components in CST [2, 15]. Together, these surface studies helped
connect plutonium colloid chemistry with interfacial processes relevant to both tank solids and engineered treatment
materials. The project also examined plutonium retention by CST under representative Hanford-relevant conditions
(Chapter 5) with emphasis on quantifying the magnitude of unintended Pu uptake and identifying the mechanisms
responsible for its retention. This work was extended by investigating three-phase Pu partitioning and the role of
CST fines in mediating potentially mobile Pu transport, providing a more process-relevant interpretation of apparent
Pu removal by CST. Chapter 6 examines the long-term evolution of PuO2 nanoparticles under Hanford tank waste
conditions, including the effects of pH, nitrate concentration, and contact with CST, MST, and Fe-bearing solids on
colloid stability and dissolved versus particulate Pu partitioning over time. Chapter 7 broadens the scope further by
comparing plutonium retention on goethite, rutile, and CST under alkaline Hanford waste tank conditions, placing
CST behavior in the context of other oxide sorbents and tank-relevant mineral phases, while simultaneously testing
these mineral phases as cost-efficient pre-treatment materials for TSCR. Finally, Chapter 8 translates these experi-
mental findings into a process-relevant framework by developing first-order estimates of plutonium loading on CST
during DFLAW processing and evaluating the corresponding implications for TSCR operations and spent-CST waste
classification. Together, these chapters provide an integrated picture of plutonium oxide surface chemistry, colloid
behavior, interfacial interactions, and process implications under Hanford-relevant conditions.

2 Surrogate Nanoparticles: Development of Synthesis Approaches
and Particle Characterization in Concentrated Electrolytes
2.1 Synthesis and Characterization of CeO2 NPs and their Reactivity in Concentrated
 Electrolytes
The first project year established a synthesis and characterization workflow for CeO2 nanoparticles as a non-radioactive
surrogate system for PuO2 nanoparticles. CeO2 nanoparticles were prepared by reflux synthesis and characterized by
dynamic light scattering, transmission electron microscopy, and small-angle X-ray scattering to assess particle size,
aggregation state, and morphology. The surface charging behavior of bulk CeO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles was ex-
amined as a surrogate framework for understanding charge development and surface complexation in actinide oxide
systems. Potentiometric acid-base titrations were conducted in nitrate and perchlorate electrolytes to determine net
proton charge behavior and points of zero net proton charge. The results showed clear differences between bulk and
nanoparticulate CeO2 , indicating that nanoparticle surfaces exhibit distinct charging behavior and ion interactions
relative to the bulk material. Initial surface complexation modeling further demonstrated that model parameters devel-
oped for bulk CeO2 do not adequately reproduce the behavior of CeO2 nanoparticles, underscoring the importance of
size-dependent surface chemistry. Reactivity studies in concentrated electrolyte solutions, including conditions rele-
vant for Hanford tank waste, showed that colloidal stability depends strongly on ionic strength and pH, with evidence
for aggregation at intermediate salt concentrations and partial resuspension at very high ionic strength. These studies
provided an important platform for developing experimental methods, sample environments, and data interpretation
approaches later applied to transuranium oxide systems. The experimental work for this task was completed during
the initial project period. A full description of the methods, results, and interpretation is available in the Year One
Progress Report.[16]

2.2 Synthesis of UO2 Nanoparticles via Coulometric Titration: Influence of Elec-
 trolytes and Ligands on Synthesized Particle Properties
Efforts in this task culminated in publication of a peer-reviewed article describing the controlled synthesis of uranium
dioxide nanoparticles (UO2 NPs) by coulometric titration and the effects of electrolyte identity, ionic strength, and
coordinating ligands on the properties of the resulting particles.[17] Although uranium is not the target transuranium
element for Hanford tank waste pretreatment, this work was an important component of the overall project because
it established a tractable and lower-radiotoxicity surrogate platform for probing the hydrolysis, nucleation, growth,
and colloidal behavior of tetravalent actinide oxide nanoparticles under solution conditions relevant to the broader
project. In particular, UO2 shares key structural and chemical similarities with PuO2 , including the fluorite-type
oxide structure and formation through hydrolysis and condensation of tetravalent actinide precursors. As a result, the
UO2 system provides a useful experimental analogue for isolating how solution composition controls nanoparticle
formation pathways and particle properties before extending analogous questions to plutonium-bearing systems.

The motivation for this work arose from a broader need to better understand how actinide oxide nanoparticles form and
evolve as a function of solution chemistry. Nanoparticle formation from tetravalent actinides is not simply a matter of
precipitation from a supersaturated solution. Rather, it can involve a sequence of hydrolysis, condensation, formation
of small oligomeric or prenucleation species, and subsequent growth through both classical and non-classical path-
ways.[18–20] These processes are strongly influenced by pH, the presence of (in)organic ligands, electrolyte com-
position, ionic strength, and redox stability. Because colloidal and nanoparticulate actinide phases are increasingly
recognized as important in both waste management and environmental transport [21, 22], a controlled synthesis route
that allows these parameters to be varied systematically is especially valuable. Within the context of this project, de-
velopment of such a synthesis platform supported later studies of PuO2 nanoparticles by helping define which solution
variables most strongly influence particle size, aggregation state, and stability.
The study used coulometric titration under ambient atmospheric conditions as a mild and experimentally flexible route
to UO2 nanoparticle formation [23, 24]. In this approach, uranyl-containing acidic solutions were electrochemically
reduced to U(IV) in a small electrochemical cell equipped with platinum electrodes. At the working electrode, re-
duction of U(VI) to U(IV) occurred together with local generation of hydroxide from water reduction, causing a pH
increase in the electrode boundary region. This increase in pH triggered hydrolysis of U(IV), followed by condensation
reactions that produced black colloidal suspensions of UO2 nanoparticles (Figure 2a). The use of coulometric titration
provided a high degree of control over the reaction pathway because the rate of reduction and local pH evolution are

mild compared to addition of concentrated hydroxide solutions. In situ UV-visible absorption spectroscopy was used
to monitor the reduction and hydrolysis sequence prior to nanoparticle formation.

A major focus of the paper was to determine how the identity of the background mineral acid anion affects the size
and properties of UO2 nanoparticles synthesized in dilute media. Experiments were performed in nitric, hydrochlo-
ric, and perchloric acid solutions, and the products were characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS), elec-
trophoretic light scattering, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and Raman
spectroscopy. Across these systems, successful syntheses yielded black colloidal suspensions whose dried solids were
consistent with nanocrystalline UO2 -type material. PXRD patterns showed broad diffraction peaks characteristic of
nanoscale fluorite-type UO2 (Figure 2c), while Raman spectra displayed the expected T2g mode near 460 cm−1 , along
with defect-related bands associated with lattice disorder and hyperstoichiometric UO2+x . These features confirmed
that the synthesis route produced nanocrystalline uranium dioxide materials rather than bulk precipitates of unrelated
uranium phases, although some degree of partial oxidation during drying or sample handling was also evident in
several spectroscopic measurements.
The particle size measurements demonstrated clear electrolyte-specific trends. In nitrate media, the dominant fraction
of particles observed by DLS was generally small, with a majority of the intensity distribution corresponding to par-
ticles below about 10 nm under several synthesis conditions ((Figure 2)b). Chloride media yielded somewhat larger
but more uniform particles, with lower polydispersity indices than the corresponding nitrate systems. Perchlorate
produced the largest and most polydisperse particles among the dilute electrolyte systems. Electrophoretic measure-
ments also showed that the synthesized UO2 colloids were positively charged in all three media, but the magnitude of

 4

the apparent zeta potential depended on the electrolyte. Particles synthesized in perchlorate had the highest positive
zeta potentials, whereas nitrate- and chloride-derived particles showed somewhat lower values, consistent with greater
surface interaction or partial charge compensation by these more coordinating anions.

TEM analysis clarified that the size distributions measured by DLS correspond to aggregates rather than isolated
primary crystallites. The primary particles in all tested media were only a few nanometers in size, typically on the order
of 3-5 nm, and had roughly spherical to weakly elliptical morphologies. Although the average crystallite size varied
only modestly across nitrate, chloride, and perchlorate systems, the breadth of the size distributions differed more
strongly, with perchlorate again producing the broadest range. TEM images also showed that the nanoparticles were
assembled into larger structures through multiple non-classical growth pathways. Some adjacent crystallites displayed
common crystallographic orientation, consistent with oriented attachment, whereas others appeared to have merged
without registry, consistent with coalescence [19, 20]. These observations are significant because they indicate that
particle growth is not governed solely by classical monomer addition or Ostwald ripening, but rather by aggregation
and fusion of nanocrystalline building blocks. This mechanistic picture is relevant to the broader project because
similar non-classical growth and aggregation pathways may also contribute to PuO2 nanoparticle formation and aging
[22, 25], including in waste-relevant media, as described in more detail in section 3.2.

The observed electrolyte effects were interpreted in terms of how anions influence hydrolysis, pre-nucleation species,
and interfacial interactions during nanoparticle formation. Perchlorate behaves as a weakly coordinating, structure-
disrupting ion that is relatively ineffective at stabilizing small hydrolyzed U(IV) oligomers. Under such conditions,
nucleation may be less favored and particle growth may dominate, leading to larger crystallites, larger aggregates, and
broader size distributions. By contrast, chloride and nitrate appear better able to participate in or stabilize precursor
species and/or interact with nanoparticle surfaces, promoting formation of smaller and more uniform products. These
trends can qualitatively be related to broader Hofmeister-type ideas and to prior observations of electrolyte effects
on the formation of tetravalent actinide oligomers [26, 27] and surface-mediated An(IV) nanoparticle formation [28,
29]. While this mechanistic interpretation provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding why nominally
similar syntheses can produce substantially different nanoparticle populations depending on background electrolyte
identity.
Our work also examined the influence of two more strongly coordinating ligands, sulfate and acetate, because ligand
effects are especially important for actinide hydrolysis chemistry and are directly relevant to waste-process conditions.
These experiments showed that ligand concentration can serve as a strong control on whether nanoparticle formation

 5

occurs at all. Sulfate at moderate to high concentrations largely suppressed U(IV) hydrolysis and prevented formation
of colloidal UO2 , consistent with strong complexation of the tetravalent metal center. At lower sulfate concentration,
nanoparticle synthesis became possible, but the resulting particles aggregated rapidly and formed a black precipitate
rather than a stable colloidal suspension. This behavior is consistent with sulfate adsorbing strongly to the particle
surface, compensating surface charge, and destabilizing the dispersion. In contrast, acetate had a much weaker effect.
At sufficiently low pH, most acetate remained protonated as acetic acid, so only a limited fraction was available to bind
U(IV) or act as a capping ligand. Under these conditions, UO2 nanoparticle synthesis still proceeded and produced
stable colloidal suspensions with particle sizes similar to those formed in nitrate alone, although modest differences in
polydispersity and oxidation behavior were observed.

These sulfate and acetate experiments are especially informative for the broader project because they show how ligand
identity can shift the balance among metal-ion complexation, hydrolysis, condensation, colloidal stabilization, and
aggregation. Sulfate illustrates that a sufficiently strong ligand can suppress nanoparticle formation altogether or
destabilize particles once formed. Acetate illustrates a weaker ligand regime in which hydrolysis and nanoparticle
formation still occur because ligand binding is limited under acidic conditions. This provides a useful analogue for
interpreting how other inorganic and organic ligands in tank-relevant systems may either inhibit, stabilize, destabilize,
or transform actinide oxide colloids depending on concentration and solution speciation.
A final part of the study explored synthesis in concentrated electrolyte solutions containing 2 M nitrate, chloride,
or perchlorate. These experiments were motivated by the broader relevance of high ionic strength media to nuclear
waste systems, including Hanford tank supernatants. In concentrated electrolytes, the higher conductivity accelerated
electrochemical reduction and caused more rapid local pH excursions, which in turn accelerated U(IV) hydrolysis and
nanoparticle formation. Although UO2 nanoparticles could still be produced under these conditions, the products were
of lower quality from a colloid-control perspective. The resulting suspensions were more polydisperse, more aggre-
gated, and more susceptible to oxidation than those formed in dilute media. Raman spectroscopy revealed stronger
signatures of oxidized uranium species, including features consistent with uranyl and U3 O8 -like products, indicating
reduced oxidative stability. DLS measurements likewise showed large aggregates and broadened size distributions.
Thus, high ionic strength enabled rapid synthesis but at the cost of poorer control over particle size, aggregation state,
and redox integrity.
Overall, this publication made several important contributions to the project. First, it established coulometric titration
as a flexible synthesis route for preparing UO2 nanoparticles under mild conditions with control over solution compo-
sition. Second, it showed that electrolyte identity has measurable and mechanistically informative effects on particle
size, polydispersity, and surface charge. Third, it demonstrated that strongly coordinating ligands can either suppress
nanoparticle formation or destabilize the resulting colloids, depending on ligand identity and concentration. Fourth,
it showed that concentrated electrolytes accelerate the electrochemical synthesis but often produce more aggregated,
less stable, and more easily oxidized materials. Together, these findings provided an experimental and conceptual
foundation for later plutonium-focused studies in the project, especially those examining how background electrolyte
composition, ligand chemistry, and ionic strength control formation, aggregation, dissolution, and process-material
interactions of PuO2 nanoparticles in Hanford-relevant environments.

More detailed experimental methods, results, and interpretation are provided in the publication: Neumann et al. "Syn-
thesis of UO2 Nanoparticles via Coulometric Titration: Influence of Electrolytes and Ligands on Synthesized Particle
Properties." Journal of Nuclear Materials (2025): 156279.

3 Synthesis and Characterization of PuO2 Nanoparticle Systems
3.1 Surface Charge Characterization of PuO2 Nanoparticles
This tasked aimed to better understand the acid-base behavior of PuO2 nanoparticles (NPs), as an extension of surface
charge characterization of CeO2 conducted in Year 1 of the project as described in section 2.1. Standardized potentio-
metric titration data was collected under inert conditions to exclude the impact of carbonate. To facilitate comparison
of surface acid–base behavior among samples, titration data were interpreted using a surface complexation model, and
the model-derived total surface proton loading (TOTH) was plotted as a function of pH. TOTH represents the net pro-
ton uptake or release associated with the oxide surface after standardization of the titration data, and therefore provides
a useful comparative metric for evaluating surface charging behavior across different oxide systems and solution con-
ditions. Positive values of TOTH correspond to net proton uptake by the surface, whereas negative values correspond
to net proton release. The zero crossing in these curves provides a convenient operational indicator of the pH at which
proton uptake and release are balanced, and thus serves here as a proxy for the apparent point of zero charge (PZC) or,
more conservatively, the point of zero net proton balance (PZNPB). Figure 4 compares these proton-balance curves
for CeO2 and PuO2 . Panel (a) shows bulk CeO2 and CeO2 NPs, while panel (b) shows PuO2 NPs measured at three
NaNO3 concentrations, together with CeO2 NPs at 0.1 M NaNO3 for reference. The figure displays only the calcu-
lated proton-balance points derived from the experimental titrations rather than the thermodynamic model predictions.
This distinction is important because the figure is intended to compare experimentally derived surface charging trends
among materials, not to present a forward model fit.
Several clear trends emerge from these data. First, comparison of bulk CeO2 and CeO2 NPs shows that decreasing
particle size shifts the apparent PZNPB to lower pH, from approximately 8.2 for the bulk material to approximately 7.3
for the nanoparticles. The value measured here for bulk CeO2 is consistent with previously reported values near 8.1
[30], and the value obtained for the CeO2 NPs is likewise within the range reported in the literature for nanocrystalline
ceria [31]. Thus, in the case of CeO2 , the nanoscale material becomes net deprotonated at a lower pH than the corre-
sponding bulk oxide. This shift is consistent with the general expectation that the surface charging behavior of metal
oxides can change substantially at the nanoscale, where particle size, defect density, crystallographic termination, and
reduced coordination environments alter the distribution and protonation behavior of surface hydroxyl sites [32]. Such
trends are not universal across oxide systems. Rather, the direction and magnitude of the shift depend strongly on the
specific material and its surface structure. Interestingly, systematic studies of TiO2 have shown the opposite behavior,
namely an increase in the point of zero charge with decreasing particle size [33]. The lower PZNPB observed here

rather than a universal consequence of particle-size reduction.
The PuO2 NP data reveal two additional points of interest. First, the measured curves at 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 M NaNO3
show only a modest dependence on ionic strength. All three proton-balance curves exhibit the same general shape,
with positive TOTH at low pH and negative TOTH at high pH, indicating progressive deprotonation of the surface
as alkalinity increases. The zero crossing is at roughly pH∼8.5 and shifts slightly to higher pH with electrolyte
concentration, but the effect is comparatively small relative to the differences observed between oxide compositions.
This suggests that within the nitrate concentration range examined here, background electrolyte primarily modulates
the details of interfacial charging without fundamentally changing the intrinsic acid-base character of the PuO2 NP
surface. The measured value is in good agreement with previous results obtained by zetapotential measurements, i.e.,
an isoelectric point at pH 8.6.[34]
Second, when the PuO2 NP data are compared directly with the CeO2 NP curve at 0.1 M NaNO3 , the PuO2 proton-
balance curve is shifted to higher pH. In practical terms, the PuO2 NP surface retains protons to higher pH and therefore
behaves as the more basic of the two nanoparticle oxides in this comparison. This upward shift from CeO2 to PuO2
is somewhat surprising, because Pu(IV) is expected to undergo stronger hydrolysis than Ce(IV), consistent with its
slightly smaller ionic radius and higher effective charge density [35], which promote strong polarization of coordi-
nated water and metal–oxygen interactions. Interestingly, however, the PuO2 NP proton-balance curve shifted toward
higher pH relative to CeO2 suggests that the PuO2 surface hydroxyl groups exhibit a higher apparent acidity constant
pKa . This apparent contrast between stronger aqueous hydrolysis and greater proton retention at the oxide surface
highlights that aqueous hydrolysis behavior does not directly predict surface acid–base chemistry. The differences
likely arise from the distinct coordination environments, metal–oxygen bonding, defect structure, and interfacial hy-
dration associated with nanocrystalline oxide surfaces. Although the origin of this behavior remains to be established,
the observation provides evidence that PuO2 surface chemistry cannot simply be inferred from known trends in the
aqueous chemistry of Pu(IV).
Overall, these results show that the surface charge behavior of PuO2 NPs is distinct from that of CeO2 and cannot be
inferred directly from simple analogies to either bulk oxides or aqueous hydrolysis trends, underscoring the importance
of studying Pu directly rather than relying solely on Ce(IV) as a surrogate for Pu(IV). The present dataset should be
viewed as an initial foundation rather than a complete description of PuO2 surface acid-base chemistry. Additional
work is needed to establish systematic trends in (de)protonation behavior, including measurements in a broader range
of electrolytes and electrolyte concentrations, more controlled comparisons across particle sizes, and complementary
measurements using bulk PuO2 , which were beyond the scope of the present project. Such data will be necessary
to develop and test surface complexation models capable of describing PuO2 surface protonation and deprotonation
in a predictive way. Thus, the titration results presented here provide an important starting point for future model
development, but a predictive description of PuO2 surface charging will require substantially broader experimental
constraints and further mechanistic refinement.

3.2 Ion-Specific Effects on PuO2 Nanoparticle Aggregation and Dissolution in Con-
 centrated Electrolytes
This task produced a peer-reviewed publication describing the ion-specific reactivity of PuO2 nanoparticles in concen-
trated electrolyte solutions and its implications for plutonium behavior in high ionic strength waste environments.[36]
The study addressed a major knowledge gap relevant to Hanford tank waste chemistry: although hydrolytic PuO2
nanoparticles are recognized as a mobile Pu-bearing phase in alkaline and saline nuclear waste systems [8], their be-
havior in concentrated, non-ideal electrolyte media has remained poorly constrained. In particular, previous work in
various chemical systems, including conditions relevant to Hanford tank waste, showed that apparent soluble pluto-
nium concentrations can remain elevated even under conditions where thermodynamic models predict very low soluble
Pu [5, 8, 37], suggesting an important contribution from colloidal or nanoparticulate Pu phases. The new publication
systematically resolved how electrolyte identity and concentration affect the partitioning of Pu between dispersed
nanoparticulate, aggregated/sedimented, and dissolved molecular forms.
The work examined freshly prepared and artificially aged PuO2 nanoparticle suspensions reacted with concentrated
sodium electrolytes containing chloride, nitrate, perchlorate, sulfate, and oxalate at concentrations up to 5 M. A multi-
technique workflow (Figure 5) was applied that combined liquid scintillation counting to quantify operationally defined
aqueous Pu concentrations in high ionic strength solutions, coupled with dynamic light scattering and small-angle
X-ray scattering to assess aggregate size and colloidal behavior, electrophoretic measurements to monitor changes in
particle charge, and UV-visible, Raman, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy to identify dissolved species, oxidation-
state changes, and solid-phase transformations. This combined approach was critical because bulk measurements of
aqueous Pu alone cannot distinguish whether plutonium remains in solution as molecular complexes or as suspended
nanoparticles. The study therefore provided a more complete and mechanistic picture of PuO2 nanoparticle reactivity
than was previously available.

Initial characterization showed that the hydrolytically prepared PuO2 materials consist of nanoscale primary crys-
tallites assembled into larger colloidal aggregates (Figure 6). Fresh and artificially aged suspensions both exhibited
aggregate sizes on the order of tens of nanometers in solution, while transmission electron microscopy and Raman
spectroscopy showed that the primary crystallites were only a few nanometers in size and had structures consistent
with nanocrystalline PuO2 . Artificial aging increased particle crystallinity and reduced structural disorder, produc-
ing a somewhat less reactive particle population while preserving the same general colloidal form. This comparison
between fresh and aged particles was important because it demonstrated that the qualitative electrolyte-dependent
reactivity trends are broadly transferable to more crystalline, tank-waste relevant PuO2 (nano)particulates.

The results demonstrated clear ion-specific differences in how concentrated electrolytes affect PuO2 nanoparticle sta-
bility. Among the weakly coordinating anions, perchlorate primarily promoted aggregation and partial sedimentation
through ionic-strength-driven compression of the electrical double layer, with relatively little evidence for specific
interaction with the particle surface or significant nanoparticle dissolution. Chloride and nitrate showed more com-
plex behavior. In both systems, measured aqueous plutonium concentrations remained relatively high even at elevated
salt concentrations, but spectroscopic measurements revealed that this apparent stability masked important shifts in
speciation. In concentrated nitrate media, a significant fraction of plutonium was present as dissolved Pu(IV) ni-
trato complexes, indicating partial nanoparticle dissolution. In concentrated chloride media, dissolution also occurred,
but the dominant dissolved products were instead Pu(VI) chloro complexes. Thus, similar bulk aqueous plutonium
concentrations could arise from very different chemical states, emphasizing that operationally defined aqueous Pu
concentrations alone are insufficient to describe plutonium behavior in concentrated brines.
The strongly coordinating ligands sulfate and oxalate produced even more dramatic effects (Figure 7). Sulfate sub-
stantially perturbed PuO2 nanoparticle suspensions by interacting strongly with the particle surface, reversing the sign
of the zeta potential at relatively low concentration, and promoting aggregation and removal of Pu from the mobile
fraction. Spectroscopic data further showed that sulfate also induced partial dissolution to molecular Pu(IV)-sulfate

 10

complexes at lower sulfate concentrations. At higher sulfate concentrations, the aqueous Pu concentration decreased
further as a secondary Pu(IV)-sulfate solid phase formed. The sulfate system therefore illustrated the coupled im-
portance of surface adsorption, charge compensation, particle aggregation, ligand-assisted dissolution, and secondary
phase precipitation. Oxalate behaved differently. Despite raising the solution pH into a regime where aggregation
and sedimentation would typically be expected, oxalate maintained high aqueous Pu concentrations by driving near-
quantitative dissolution of PuO2 nanoparticles to dissolved Pu-oxalate complexes. Raman and optical spectroscopy
confirmed the disappearance of characteristic PuO2 nanoparticle features and the formation of molecular oxalate com-
plexes and oxalate-derived solid products upon drying. Additional experiments with aged PuO2 nanoparticles showed
the same overall transformation pathway, although dissolution occurred more slowly, consistent with the lower reac-
tivity of the more crystalline aged particles.

A major outcome of the study is the demonstration that PuO2 nanoparticle behavior in concentrated electrolytes is
governed by two coupled pathways: aggregation/sedimentation and ligand-promoted dissolution. Which pathway
dominates depends strongly on the identity and concentration of the background electrolyte. Weakly coordinating
anions such as perchlorate primarily influence colloidal stability through double-layer compression and changes in in-
terparticle interactions, whereas nitrate and chloride also promote partial dissolution to distinct dissolved complexes.
More strongly coordinating ligands such as sulfate and oxalate substantially shift the balance toward dissolution and/or
secondary phase formation. The work further showed that particle aging affects the extent and rate of these transfor-
mations but does not fundamentally change the ion-specific ordering of reactivity. These findings help explain why
measured plutonium concentrations in concentrated waste solutions may deviate strongly from the predictions of con-
ventional dissolved-species thermodynamic models.
This publication is directly relevant to the goals of the present project because it provides a mechanistic and spectro-
scopically constrained foundation for modeling Pu behavior in Hanford-relevant chemical environments. The data set
links electrolyte identity and concentration to changes in particle aggregation, surface charge, oxidation state, ligand
coordination, and dissolved versus nanoparticulate partitioning. As such, it provides an important basis for future
extension of thermodynamic models that explicitly incorporate PuO2 nanoparticle phases [37] rather than treating plu-
tonium only as dissolved molecular species. These advances are needed to improve predictions of plutonium mobility,
colloidal stability, and partitioning to process materials under high ionic strength conditions relevant to tank waste
treatment and pretreatment strategies.

4.1 Sorption of Cs+ on Alumina (012) under Hanford Tank Waste-Relevant Condi-

Understanding the molecular-scale interactions of Cs+ at oxide-water interfaces is directly relevant to the TSCR pro-
cess at Hanford. Cs+ interactions with oxide surfaces, including its adsorption geometry, coverage, and sensitivity
to solution pH and ionic composition are needed to be determined to develop predictive thermodynamic models. We
conducted two sets of in situ crystal truncation rod (CTR) experiments [38, 39] on the alumina (012) surface to in-
vestigate Cs+ adsorption under (i) variable pH conditions and (ii) Hanford simulant (HS) compositions with varying
Cs+ concentrations. The alumina (012) surface serves as a structurally well-defined model oxide substrate exposing
surface sites relevant for a variety of Al-bearing hydroxide/clay/mineral interfaces.[40, 41]
Single-crystal α-Al2 O3 (012) substrates (MTI Corporation, 10 × 10 × 1 mm2 , miscut angle <0.1◦ , EPI polished) were
prepared following the procedures similar to earlier described methods [42]. Crystals were cleaned sequentially with
acetone and methanol under sonication (15 min each, repeated at least three times), followed by sonication in deionized
water (DIW, Milli-Q, resistivity ≥18.2 MΩ·cm). Crystals were annealed at 1050–1100 ◦ C in air for 4–12 h, cooled to
room temperature in the furnace, rinsed with DIW under sonication, and stored in DIW prior to X-ray experiments. In
situ specular CTR measurements were performed at Beamline 7-ID at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne
National Laboratory using an X-ray energy of 18 keV. The crystal surfaces were mounted in a thin-film X-ray cell in
contact with aqueous solutions, and data were collected over a momentum transfer range of ∼ 0 < q < 6 Å−1 .
Experiment 1 — pH Dependence: CTR data were collected for the alumina (012) surface in contact with 100 mM
CsNO3 solutions at pH values of 4.5-10. This series aims to understand Cs+ adsorption as a function of the surface
charge.
Experiment 2 — Cs+ Concentration Dependence in Hanford Simulant: CTR data were collected for the alu-
mina (012) surface in contact with a simplified Hanford simulant (HS) solution containing 0.7 M NaNO3 and 2 M
NaOH, with Cs+ concentrations of 10-1000 µM added as CsNO3 . This experiment directly probes Cs+ adsorption
under the high-ionic-strength, alkaline conditions characteristic of Hanford tank supernatants and provides insight into
competitive adsorption between Cs+ and Na+ at oxide surfaces. CTR data were analyzed using established model-
dependent fitting procedures to extract total electron density profiles along the surface-normal direction, providing sub-
Å-resolution information on surface relaxations, adsorbed water layer positions, and adsorbed ion distributions.[39,
42, 43]

4.2 Sorption of CeO2 and PuO2 Nanoparticles on Alumina (012) and Yttrium-Stabilized
 Zirconia (001) under Hanford Tank Waste-Relevant Conditions
This task explored how PuO2 nanoparticles interact with well-defined oxide surfaces under solution conditions relevant
to concentrated tank-waste chemistry. The broader motivation for this work is that as plutonium in Hanford-relevant
alkaline media may exist not only as dissolved molecular species, but also as colloidal or nanoparticulate oxide phases
[8], the plutonium transport and retention during waste pretreatment may depend not only on bulk solution thermo-
dynamics, but also on interfacial processes such as nanoparticle attachment to surfaces. To begin addressing these
questions in a controlled way, we used single-crystal oxide substrates as model surfaces and examined the behavior of
PuO2 nanoparticle systems upon contact with those surfaces.
As part of this effort, CeO2 was used as a non-radioactive surrogate to develop synthesis, handling, and surface-
analysis workflows before extending analogous studies to plutonium-bearing materials. The student-led portion of this
task focused on the preparation and characterization of CeO2 -containing colloids, building on the results described in
Section 2.1, and on exploratory adsorption experiments using metal-oxide single crystals. We selected α-Al2 O3 as a
model for Al-bearing solid phases in the tanks arising from dissolution of Al cladding, and yttria-stabilized zirconia
(YSZ) as a zirconium oxide substrate representative of Zr-bearing phases in Hanford tank sludges, as well as of
zirconium-containing components of the crystalline silicotitanate (CST) ion-exchange material relevant to TSCR.[2,
15]
A CeO2 -containing colloidal suspension was prepared by aqueous reflux of ceric ammonium nitrate. Raman spec-

of nanocrystalline CeO2 , supporting formation of a ceria-like oxide phase. Dynamic light scattering measurements
showed a dominant hydrodynamic particle population near 13 nm together with larger secondary aggregates, estab-
lishing that the reflux synthesis produced a colloidal CeO2 suspension suitable for exploratory surface-contact ex-
periments. The adsorption experiments were designed to test whether the CeO2 NPs would measurably associate
with model oxide substrates under tank-relevant electrolyte conditions. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to
compare α-Al2 O3 (012) and YSZ(001) surfaces exposed to deionized water, to a Hanford-relevant electrolyte control
consisting of 2M NaNO3 and 0.7M NaOH, and to electrolyte containing the CeO2 colloid.

raised topographic features after exposure to the electrolyte-only control, making it difficult to distinguish possible
CeO2 -derived deposits from residual contamination, incomplete surface cleaning, or drying artifacts associated with
the concentrated electrolyte. Because the YSZ substrates were not annealed prior to use, the initial surface condition
was less well defined than for the α-Al2 O3 (012) crystals, further limiting interpretation of the AFM images. These
observations highlight the importance of rigorous surface preparation and appropriate electrolyte controls when using
post-drying topographic methods to assess nanoparticle adsorption in high-salt media.
To provide a more direct comparison with the alumina system, we also carried out the corresponding radiometric
adsorption experiments using 242 PuO2 nanoparticle suspensions in Hanford electrolyte. Despite the uncertainty in the
AFM observations, the alpha-spectrometric measurements suggest that plutonium coverages on YSZ were approxi-
mately a factor of six higher than those measured on alumina under comparable conditions (approximately 3.5 µmol
Pu per m2 ). This result should presently be regarded as preliminary, since additional work is needed to confirm its
reproducibility, better constrain the role of substrate preparation, and determine whether the higher apparent uptake
reflects stronger nanoparticle attachment, enhanced retention of Pu-bearing surface precipitates, or another interfa-
cial process. Nevertheless, this trend is potentially important because Zr-bearing phases are a significant constituent
of the sludges in some of the Hanford tanks and Zr is also present as binder in crystalline silicotitanate (CST), the
ion-exchange material used in TSCR. If zirconium oxide-like surface environments exhibit enhanced affinity for Pu-
bearing nanoparticles or dissolved Pu species, they may contribute to the unexpectedly strong plutonium retention
observed for CST under Hanford-relevant conditions.

 19

Overall, this task provided an important foundation for understanding how actinide oxide nanoparticles interact with
metal oxide interfaces in concentrated, tank waste-relevant media. The alumina experiments support the interpretation
that PuO2 nanoparticles can associate to some degree with mineral-like oxide surfaces. The relatively low Pu surface
coverages measured on α-Al2 O3 suggest that Pu loading associated with filtered Al-bearing solid phases is likely to
be relatively low under comparable conditions. In contrast, the higher apparent Pu uptake observed for YSZ indicates
that filtrates containing larger amounts of Zr-bearing phases may exhibit correspondingly greater Pu loading, although
this interpretation remains preliminary and requires further confirmation.
To advance this work, element-specific and surface-sensitive techniques, including alpha spectrometry, electron mi-
croscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS), and resonant anomalous X-ray reflectivity (RAXR),
provide valuable means to quantify Pu affinity for metal oxide surfaces relevant to tank waste and to elucidate the
interfacial mechanisms responsible for Pu retention. Although this task did not yet provide a fully quantitative or
chemically specific description of CeO2 or PuO2 nanoparticle adsorption at model oxide interfaces, it established use-
ful methodological lessons, identified important limitations in substrate preparation and surface analysis, and pointed
to promising directions for future work. These results therefore provide a practical basis for more targeted studies of
plutonium-bearing colloids interacting with process-relevant solid surfaces, including materials relevant to Hanford
tank waste pretreatment and CST-based cesium removal.
In addition to its scientific contributions, this effort also served an important workforce development role by train-
ing an undergraduate student in nanoparticle synthesis, surface characterization, and data interpretation. These re-
sults therefore provide both a practical basis for more targeted studies of plutonium-bearing colloids interacting with
process-relevant solid surfaces and a useful example of student training aligned with DOE mission needs.

5 Retention of Pu by Crystalline Silico Titanate (CST) under Hanford-
Relevant Conditions
5.1 Pu Retention by CST and Mechanistic Insights
This task produced a new manuscript focused on plutonium retention by crystalline silicotitanate (CST) under strongly
alkaline, nitrate-rich conditions relevant to Tank-Side Cesium-Removal (TSCR) operations at the Hanford Site.[46]
This work addresses a central operational concern for TSCR: although CST is deployed specifically for selective
removal of radioactive cesium from Hanford tank supernatants, prior testing with actual tank waste indicated that
CST can also retain measurable amounts of plutonium and other transuranic constituents.[4, 9, 11, 12] Because spent
CST must remain below the regulatory threshold for transuranic waste classification, understanding the magnitude and
mechanism of Pu retention is important both for process control and for waste disposition planning. The present study
therefore sought to quantify Pu partitioning to CST in representative Hanford simulant solutions, compare Pu uptake
with the intended Cs uptake, and assess how solution chemistry influences retention behavior.

The experiments were carried out in a representative hyperalkaline Hanford simulant containing 2 M NaNO3 and 0.7 M
NaOH. Under these conditions, Pu was introduced either as pre-formed PuO2 nanoparticles (PuO2 NPs), representing
the colloidal Pu(IV) phases thought to dominate under many Hanford-relevant conditions [8], or as aqueous Pu(VI),
representing a more oxidized dissolved Pu species [5]. Batch-contact experiments were performed over a range of
initial Pu concentrations and solid-to-liquid ratios using pre-washed CST. Cesium sorption was measured in parallel

Our work further showed that Pu retention by CST is not restricted to systems initially containing colloidal Pu(IV).
When aqueous Pu(VI) was contacted with CST under similarly alkaline conditions, strong apparent retention was
again observed, with distribution ratios typically on the order of ∼103 to ∼3·103 mL/g (Figure 16c,d). The resulting
isotherm closely resembled that measured for the PuO2 NP system. This similarity suggests that the initial oxidation
state or physical form of Pu in the contacting solution may not fully determine the final retained Pu fraction under these
conditions. A plausible interpretation is that aqueous Pu(VI) undergoes reduction and/or hydrolysis during interaction
with CST and forms Pu(IV)-bearing surface-associated or particulate species. While direct spectroscopic confirmation
of the retained interfacial Pu speciation was not obtained in this study, the comparable uptake behavior for the PuO2
NP and Pu(VI) systems indicates that CST can strongly immobilize Pu from both colloidal and initially dissolved Pu
feed conditions.

One of the most important chemical controls identified in the study is carbonate concentration. Previous work in
the broader project [16, 48] showed that carbonate can stabilize PuO2 nanoparticles in alkaline brines by increasing
their colloidal stability and reducing their tendency to aggregate and settle. Consistent with that behavior, increasing
carbonate concentration in the CST contact experiments caused a marked reduction in PuO2 NP retention (Figure
17). In solutions with elevated carbonate, the amount of Pu remaining in the mobile phase after contact with CST
increased substantially, and the corresponding Pu distribution ratios decreased by roughly an order of magnitude, to
values near ∼102 to 3·102 mL/g at 1M Na2 CO3 . This result is operationally significant because it identifies carbonate
as a potentially useful solution variable for moderating unintended Pu uptake onto CST. At the same time, practical
use of this effect would need to account for broader waste chemistry, especially the possibility of carbonate mineral
precipitation in the presence of divalent cations such as calcium.

A major contribution of this task is its examination of the likely mechanisms responsible for Pu retention by CST.
Because CST is designed as a size-selective ion-exchange material for relatively small cations such as Cs+ and Sr2+
[49–51], direct ion exchange of PuO2 nanoparticles into CST pore structures is not physically plausible. The PuO2 NP
primary particles and their aggregates are far larger than CST pore apertures. Likewise, even aqueous plutonyl species
(and their hydrolysis products) are sterically and chemically distinct from the spherical cations for which CST was
designed. Therefore, Pu retention most likely occurs through a combination of alternative mechanisms, including:

(1) Association of Pu-bearing species with external CST bead and binder surfaces
(2) Co-settling or physical retention of PuO2 NP aggregates with CST fines, and
(3) Destabilization of PuO2 NP suspensions by dissolved CST leachate components, which can promote aggregation
and sedimentation.

Microscopy results strongly support these interpretations. Transmission electron microscopy of supernatants from
PuO2 NP-CST contact experiments showed nm-scale PuO2 -like crystallites and their aggregates associated with larger
particles identified as CST-derived fines (Figure 18). These observations provide direct evidence that Pu-bearing
nanoparticles can associate with CST-related solid material outside the internal ion-exchange pore network. Com-
plementary SEM-BSE and EDS mapping of CST contacted with aqueous Pu(VI) showed spatial coincidence of Pu
with Si- and Ti-rich regions associated with CST-derived material. This spatial overlap indicates direct association of
retained Pu with CST solids and provides important visual evidence for Pu immobilization on or with CST-derived
particles under Hanford-relevant conditions. The exact chemical form of Pu that is retained in this case, including
the interfacial Pu oxidation state, could not be fully resolved spectroscopically in this study; however, the microscopy
data clearly show that Pu accumulates on CST and CST-derived fines. Reduction of initially aqueous Pu(VI) to Pu(IV)
and formation of PuO2 nanoparticles upon interaction with CST-related surfaces remains a possibility and has been
reported in geochemical systems.[52–55]
An additional mechanistic insight came from CST leachate experiments. Pre-contact of CST with Pu-free simulant
showed release of measurable dissolved Si and smaller amounts of Ti, Nb, and Zr into solution. When this leachate
was mixed with a stable PuO2 NP suspension, the measured aqueous Pu concentration decreased relative to dilution-
only expectations [46]. This result indicates that dissolved leachate species from CST (Si, Ti, Nb, Zr) can destabilize
PuO2 NP suspensions, enhancing aggregation and reducing the mobile colloidal Pu fraction. Such a mechanism may
be especially important during batch mixing or column operation where fresh CST surfaces, fines generation, and
solution recirculation could increase contact between Pu colloids and CST-derived dissolved species. The finding also

5.2 Three-Phase Pu Partitioning and Risk of CST Fines-Mediated Pu Transport
Although batch-contact experiments provide a useful model platform for quantifying Pu partitioning to CST, TSCR
is ultimately operated as a column process in which the effective solid-to-liquid ratio, hydrodynamic conditions, and
extent of particle-particle contact differ substantially from those in batch systems. We therefore examined how solid-
to-liquid ratio influences the apparent extent of Pu retention by CST. Tests performed at higher solid-to-liquid ratios
and under prolonged strong shaking produced a substantial fine-particle fraction that partially remained suspended in
solution after contact. In these experiments, plutonium concentrations in the settled supernatants were often at or near
the detection limit (Figure 19a), corresponding to nearly complete removal of Pu from the clarified supernatant and
yielding very large conventional distribution ratios above 1,000 mL/g (Figure 19b), indicating strong immobilization
of Pu by CST.

However, interpretation of these data requires caution because the traditional two-phase description of a mobile aque-
ous phase and an immobile solid phase is not fully adequate for colloidal systems. In the present case, the system is
better described as a three-phase distribution involving the clear supernatant, a suspended CST fine fraction, and the
macroscopic CST beads. This distinction is operationally important because the CST fine fraction cannot be treated
unambiguously as either fully immobile or fully mobile. When the batch-contact data are reinterpreted in a three-
phase framework, a substantial fraction of the plutonium inventory, typically on the order of 60-90 percent, is found
to reside in the CST fine fraction (Figure 19c) rather than being associated solely with the macroscopic CST beads.
If these fines are treated as part of the mobile inventory, effective distribution ratios decrease by orders of magnitude
relative to the conventional two-phase values (1-10 mL/g). The mechanistic origin of this strong association of PuO2
nanoparticles to CST-fines remains unresolved. Several explanations are plausible and may operate simultaneously.
First, PuO2 nanoparticles may adsorb directly to CST fine particles that have a relatively large surface area compared
to the CST beads. Second, Pu-bearing particles and CST fines may co-settle or co-aggregate because of comparable
size scales. Third, dissolved species released from CST under caustic conditions may destabilize the Pu colloid and
thereby promote aggregation and association with the fine fraction, as described in section 5.1.
Regardless of the exact mechanism, each of these pathways could lead to strong apparent Pu removal from solution
while still allowing a fraction of Pu-bearing material to remain transportable through the process. In the TSCR pro-
cess, larger CST fine particles may be retained in lag/ polishing columns or by filters, but smaller Pu-bearing CST
fines may pass through these barriers, deposit in downstream piping or vessels, and later be remobilized by agitation
or flow changes. As a result, fines may contribute to unexpected plutonium transport even when bulk supernatant
measurements suggest efficient removal. This result highlights the importance of defining the operational boundary
when interpreting Pu retention by CST and suggests that fines management may be an important factor in predicting
Pu fate during and after TSCR.

6 Long-Term Evolution of PuO2 Nanoparticles under Hanford
Tank Waste Conditions
This task examined the behavior of PuO2 nanoparticles (NP) at elevated temperature (35 ◦ C) over an extended incu-
bation period (up to approximately 10 months) under a suite of conditions relevant to the Hanford waste tank systems.
The batch treatment conditions included a range of sodium nitrate concentrations (0.01 to 4 mol/L) and variable initial
pH values (6, 11, and 14) with or without system amendments (iron, crystalline silica titanate (CST), monosodium
titanate (MST), and iron with CST). The experimental matrix and objectives for each variable are described in Table
1.
The initial experimental set consisted of 39 reactor vessels which were held in an anoxic glovebox in a dry heat bath at
35 ◦ C. The samples were spiked to 0.20 mM total 239 Pu as PuO2 colloids and sampled after 56 (settled), 90 (filtered),
and 295 (settled and filtered) days of equilibration. Note that for initial experiments with CST and MST amendments,
the material was not introduced to the reactor vessels until 33 days after the initiation of the experiment. After the first
sampling event, three additional treatment vessels (for a total of 42) were prepared for the iron with CST group with
the addition of a buffer to mediate the strong pH shift to acidic conditions resulting from iron hydrolysis.
Sample processing at each time point consisted of removing two aliquots of each treatment vessel. The first aliquot
was obtained prior to mixing to evaluate the concentration of dissolved Pu plus colloidal PuO2 (referred to as “set-
tled” samples in this report). The second aliquot was obtained after gently swirling the vessel and passing the mixed
aliquot through a 3k polyesthersulfone (PES) ultrafiltration membrane to examine the dissolved Pu species. The pH
and oxidation reduction potential (ORP) of the aqueous phase were also measured at both sampling events. Pluto-
nium concentrations were determined via Liquid Scintillation Counting (LSC; 2910 Tricarb) and Inductively Coupled
Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS; Thermo iCAP RQ), which had approximately a 100–1000x lower detection limit
than the LSC.
The intent of these experiments was to age the samples for at least 6 months prior to detailed microscopic and spectro-
scopic characterization using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). During this reporting period, samples were aged for approximately 10 months total at the time
of the second and final sampling campaign. Multiple microscopic techniques were utilized to investigate nanoparticle
presence and morphology (TEM) and to visualize association of Pu and iron with CST (scanning electron microscopy,
SEM) for select samples.

6.3 Impacts of Fe addition over time
Several tanks also contain high levels of iron used during chemical processing. Thus, the project sought to examine
potential impacts of iron on PuO2 colloid stability. The addition of iron to samples initially at pH 6, 11, and 14 and
NaNO3 concentrations from 0.1 to 4 mol/L resulted in rapid precipitation, formation of goethite, and a reduction in pH
due to iron hydrolysis. The decreased pH had a major impact on the concentrations of Pu in solution wherein lower
pH values yielded much higher concentrations of Pu with 8.5–25% of the total Pu remaining stable in solution (Figure
22a). It is noteworthy that the Fe was added after the PuO2 colloids were spiked into the solutions. Thus, aggregation
of the PuO2 colloids likely occurred and the rapid pH decrease due to addition of Fe appeared to have caused re-
suspension, disaggregation, and possible dissolution of the colloids and/or some degree of temporary aqueous species
stabilization. The latter is suspected, particularly for samples where pH was <1, since filtered concentrations were
higher than settled concentrations at the first sampling time points (recall that initial settled samples were collected
at 57 days, while the first set of filtered samples were collected at 90 days). Additionally, changes in settled Pu
concentrations were minimal over the extended incubation period while filtered sample concentrations decreased by
about an order of magnitude, alluding to the temporary possibility of dissolved Pu species stabilization at earlier times
and potential re-condensation to colloidal species at longer times.

The impact of pH is also shown in samples where both Fe and CST were added due to the CST buffering the pH
near 5 at the first sampling time point, modulating the low pH conditions at shorter times (Figure 22b). Over the
extended time frame between the first and second sampling campaign, the buffering effect of CST was degraded with

the unamended group where pH 11 samples have the lowest filtered Pu concentrations overall that increase with time,
is also seen in this sample group at pH near 8 indicating some degree of time, pH, and NaNO3 dependent dissolution
of Pu. Overall, there is a systematic decrease in Pu concentrations with increasing pH in these data. It is particularly
noteworthy, that in the presence of Fe, Pu was nearly quantitatively removed from solution under high pH conditions
independent of CST of presence.
The nearly ubiquitous temporal shifts in Pu concentrations and trends across the full range of pH and NaNO3 con-
centrations examined are indicative of the inherent dynamic nature of these systems. Additional aging studies and/or
longer observation times would be valuable to continue to probe colloid behavior under these conditions and elucidate
additional mechanisms responsible for the variability of colloid stability and potential dissolution occurring in some
cases.

7 Comparative Plutonium Retention on Goethite, Rutile, and
CST under Alkaline Hanford Waste Tank Conditions
The scope of this task was to examine Pu partitioning to multiple candidate sorbents for the removal of radionuclides
while processing the salt solutions within the Hanford legacy nuclear waste tanks. Crystalline silica titanate (CST)
is currently intended for removal of fission products but partitioning of plutonium to CST could inadvertently result
in the solid being classified as a transuranic waste. Thus, along with partitioning of plutonium to CST, studies were
also performed using goethite and rutile. All studies were conducted under simulated high pH, high ionic strength
conditions found in the waste tanks. This work is meaningful in that it observes interactions of Pu with several sorbent
materials at a high pH and determines strong interactions with all sorbents, including indications of hysteretic or
potentially irreversible sorption and/or aggregation. The impact of this data is the application of a protocol by which
Hanford waste tank processing can efficiently trap Pu and prevent its propagation in the waste processing stream
utilizing CST columns, minimizing the formation of transuranic waste.
Adsorption of Pu to sorbent materials was carried out in a batch mode using the solids crystalline silicotitanate (CST),
iron oxide (goethite), and titanium dioxide (rutile). Sorption isotherms were collected in 0.7 M NaOH and 2 M
NaNO3 with PuO2 nanoparticle (NP) suspensions between 10−1 -10−5 mM. The PuO2 nanoparticles had primary
particles approximately 3-5 nm in diameter and formed aggregates into 200-400 nm ranges in experimental solutions,
consistent with observations in other tasks in the project [16, 48]. Samples were initially mixed for 24 hours before
the amount of PuO2 NP sorption was measured after centrifugation. Then the supernatant was decanted and fresh
0.7 M NaOH 2 M NaNO3 was added to initiate a desorption step which was measured after 1, 3, and 7 days. The
sorption trend of Pu to CST (Figure 23a) begins with a linear relationship between the concentration of Pu in the
aqueous and solid phases until the aqueous concentration of Pu reaches 0.01 mM, at which point there is no further
increase in aqueous Pu but a continued rise in solid phase Pu. This could suggest that the surface of the CST has
become saturated with Pu and that Pu-Pu interactions are now driving the increase in solid phase Pu concentrations.
Desorption of Pu from CST is linear and moves to lower aqueous concentrations at each subsequent desorption step
indicating only a small fraction of the total Pu was being desorbed. The fraction of Pu desorbed decreased with time
for nearly all samples indicating the removal of less-strongly bound Pu initially (Figure 23b). The consistent values in
fraction desorbed for higher initial Pu solid phase concentrations indicate a solubility type control of the aqueous Pu
concentration and the likely presence of PuO2 NP aggregates on the surface (Figure 23b).
The sorption and desorption of Pu to two additional sorbent materials, goethite and rutile, was explored with results
demonstrating strong Pu sorption on both sorbents. The sorption of Pu to goethite (Figure 24a) loosely fits a linear
trend until a concentration of 10−5 mM aqueous Pu and appears to display the same increase in solid phase Pu
without additional aqueous Pu, similar to the behavior seen with CST and likely indicating the aggregation of PuO2
NPs. Additional experiments using higher initial Pu concentrations may help verify this, which could indicate Pu-Pu
interactions on goethite as well as CST. Some initial desorption of Pu from the goethite solid phase appears to occur
after 24 hours but the fraction desorbed continually decreases with time for all samples, indicating little reversibility
within the experimental timeframe suggesting strong Pu-goethite or Pu-Pu interactions on the surface of the sorbent.

Comparing the Kd values for Pu sorption to CST, goethite, and rutile (Figure 24a), CST exhibits a flat, linear trend

the point where PuO2 NP aggregation is influencing Pu removal from the aqueous phase. Rutile and goethite also
follow somewhat linear trends, but rutile’s Kd is observed to increase and then sharply decrease at the greatest initial

phase rather than an aggregation process. However, further experiments are required to verify that mechanism. The
Kd values for CST are the lowest amongst the three sorbents, while rutile has the highest. Therefore, rutile has the
potential to be a favorable sorbent for a pre-treatment step to remove Pu from waste streams prior to CST columns.
This could prevent accumulation of PuO2 NPs on the CST which may lead to production of TRU waste.

8 Projected Pu Retention in TSCR: Operational Implications and
Waste Classification
8.1 Parametric Estimates for Pu Retention by CST in TSCR Columns
The experimental results shown in in this report show that Pu partitioning to CST can be substantial over a broad
range of Hanford-relevant solution conditions, including variations in plutonium concentration, nitrate concentration,
hydroxide concentration, and solid-to-liquid ratio [46]. These observations are consistent with prior lab-scale TSCR
studies using Hanford waste samples [4, 9, 12], which also indicate considerable Pu retention by CST. Building
on this experimental foundation, we used available TSCR operational information to develop first-order parametric
estimates of plutonium loading on CST during full-scale processing and to assess the implications for transuranic
waste classification.
For these estimates, we considered published TSCR design and operating information, including column size, CST
inventory, processed waste volumes, and reported feed Pu activities. Each TSCR column has a bed volume of 159.1
gal, corresponding to approximately 602 L [56], and contains about 1180 kg of CST (Table 3). Reported waste volumes
processed per batch during the first TSCR campaign, DFLAW-01, ranged from approximately 430,000 to 836,000 L
[57]. Using the reported Pu-239+240 activity for AP-107, the staging tank for DFLAW-01, of 5.43·10−4 µCi/mL [9,
12, 13], the total plutonium activity processed per batch is estimated to fall between 234 and 454 mCi Pu-239+240.
Using these values, Pu loading on CST can be estimated under different assumptions regarding the extent and dis-
tribution of Pu retention. In a first scenario, we use previously reported lab-scale TSCR data from PNNL [9, 12,
13, 58], which showed approximately 35 percent Pu recovery in the effluent, with reported fractions of Pu removals
of 60 percent for AP-105, 61 percent for AP-107, and 71 percent for AW-102. If this retained Pu is assumed to be
distributed homogeneously across the three columns in the TSCR setup, the resulting plutonium loading is estimated
to be approximately 43 to 83 nCi Pu-239+240 per gram of CST (Table 3). However, a homogeneous Pu distribution
across the columns is unlikely in actual operation. In fact, prior work [12] indicates that roughly 75 percent of the
retained Pu inventory may reside on the lead column. Under that assumption, the corresponding Pu loading on the
lead column increases to about 89.6 to 173.9 nCi/g, which spans and potentially exceeds the 100 nCi/g transuranic
waste threshold (Table 3).
In a second scenario, we use the isotherm data generated from the new CST adsorption data collected in this project
(section 5.1 and [46]). If carbonate in the tank supernatant is neglected, the Freundlich fit to the PuO2 nanoparticle
adsorption data corresponds to equilibrium Pu concentrations of about 2.0 to 3.7 nM under the relevant conditions,
implying Pu retention of approximately 90.6 to 94.9 percent (as shown in [46]). Using this higher retention in the
loading estimate gives Pu loadings of 62.3 to 115.4 nCi/g for homogeneous distribution over the three columns, and
130.9 to 242.3 nCi/g if 75 percent of the retained Pu is assumed to reside on the lead column. In a revised version
of this calculation, we additionally consider the reported inorganic carbon concentration for AP-107 [9, 12], corre-
sponding to a carbonate concentration of roughly 0.65 M. Based on the data shown in Figure 17, the corresponding
Kd value is substantially lower, estimated at 0.122 L/g. This lowers the predicted Pu loading to 22.5–43.6 nCi/g as-
suming homogeneous distribution across the three columns, and to 47–135 nCi/g on the lead column assuming that a
major fraction of Pu is retained there. These revised estimates, summarized in (Table 3), indicate that carbonate can
substantially reduce projected Pu loading on CST, although lead-column loadings may still approach or exceed the
TRU threshold depending on the actual carbonate concentration, Pu retention behavior, and distribution of Pu across
the column setup.
The same approach can be extended to the next planned TSCR campaign, DFLAW-02 (Table 3). The reported Pu-
239+240 activity in the AP-104 feed for that campaign is slightly lower, 4.56 × 10−4 µCi/mL, so somewhat lower
Pu loading would be expected if waste volumes processed per batch are similar to those in DFLAW-01. Under those
assumptions, the total Pu activity processed per batch is estimated to range from 196 to 381 mCi. Using the retention
values derived from our adsorption isotherm, the corresponding Pu loading would be 52.2 to 101.5 nCi/g if distributed
homogeneously across the three columns, and 109.6 to 213.1 nCi/g if 75 percent of the retained Pu is assigned to
the lead column. Because information on carbonate concentrations for AP-104 were not available here and were
therefore not included in this estimate, therefore, these values should be regarded as conservative. If carbonate is
present at appreciable concentration, which is likely the case, the actual Pu retention and resulting CST loadings could
be lower than estimated here. Thus, even for DFLAW-02, lead-column loadings may exceed the 100 nCi/g threshold

depending on the actual retention behavior, the carbonate concentration, and the extent of non-uniform loading across
the individual columns.

An important operational factor in these estimates is that cesium, not plutonium, defines the CST column changeout
point. Column replacement is triggered by the Cs breakthrough criterion of 0.1 percent c/c0 , meaning that feeds with
lower Cs concentrations allow more bed volumes to be processed before CST is replaced. Longer operation in turn

 33

permits greater cumulative Pu accumulation on the CST. Accordingly, the relative molar ratio of Cs to Pu in the feed
is a meaningful parameter for evaluating TRU loading risk. For DFLAW-01 and DFLAW-02, the estimated Cs-to-
Pu molar ratios are approximately 331:1 and 425:1 (Table 3), respectively, reflecting slightly lower Pu and slightly
higher Cs in DFLAW-02. This difference contributes to lower projected Pu loadings for DFLAW-02 compared with
DFLAW-01.

8.2 Implications for TSCR Operations and Waste Classification of Spent CST
Overall, the resulting estimates indicate that some projected CST loadings may remain below the 100 nCi/g TRU
threshold, while others, especially for the lead column, may exceed it. Importantly, these values are in good agreement
with published digestion data for CST from lab-scale TSCR tests, which reported 63.5 to 101 nCi Pu-239+240 per
gram CST on lead columns over multiple campaigns [4]. This agreement supports the conclusion that the present
estimates are realistic and that plutonium retention during TSCR represents a credible waste classification concern
rather than a purely conservative bounding scenario.
One practical mitigation strategy suggested by these estimates is blending CST from the lead, lag, and polishing
columns prior to final disposition of spent CST. Because Pu loading is expected to be highest on the lead column and
lower on downstream columns, blending would reduce the composite TRU loading of the spent CST inventory and may
help keep the overall waste form below the 100 nCi/g threshold. However, this benefit would not be realized if columns
are changed out and managed separately in a lead-lag-polish sequence, which is currently the expected operational
approach for both future TSCR deployments and the expanded Advanced Modular Pretreatment System (AMPS).
In the AMPS configuration, the polishing columns are intended to be rotated into the lead and lag positions during
column replacement, further reinforcing column-specific differences in loading history. In addition, while blending
may reduce the composite plutonium loading below the TRU threshold, final waste classification would still need to
account for other transuranic contributors, including neptunium, americium, and curium, which are not considered
in the estimates presented here. Any blending-based mitigation strategy would therefore require confirmation by
representative sampling and composite assay of the full TRU inventory.
Taken together, these parametric estimates show that plutonium retention by CST is not only chemically significant
but also operationally consequential. Depending on feed composition, processed volume, carbonate concentration,
and the distribution of retained Pu across the column train, spent CST from TSCR may approach or exceed the TRU
waste threshold. These findings reinforce the importance of understanding and managing Pu partitioning during TSCR
and support the development of feed chemistry controls, blending strategies, and improved predictive tools for CST
loading and waste classification.

9 Accomplishments per Task
The project was originally scoped across the three-year award period with continuous effort in particle synthesis and
laboratory characterization, followed by surface exchange and synchrotron studies beginning in the latter half of Year
1, and sustained experimental and modeling activities continuing throughout the award. Significant advances were
made in surrogate particle synthesis and characterization, which provided the basis for the broader research effort.
Development of CeO2 and UO2 nanoparticle systems established practical synthesis routes, characterization work-
flows, and mechanistic insight into how electrolyte identity, ionic strength, and ligand chemistry affect nanoparticle
formation, aggregation, and stability. These surrogate studies directly informed subsequent plutonium-focused work
and helped define key experimental variables for Hanford-relevant systems.
Experimental studies of PuO2 nanoparticle behavior were carried out extensively in simplified and concentrated su-
pernate simulants. These studies included surface charge characterization, ion-specific investigations of colloid aggre-
gation and dissolution, and evaluation of the long-term evolution of PuO2 nanoparticles as a function of pH, nitrate
concentration, and contact with process-relevant solids including CST, MST, and iron-bearing phases. Spectroscopic
and microscopic characterization of these colloidal systems was completed and showed that plutonium behavior in
concentrated alkaline media must be understood as a dynamic balance among dispersed nanoparticles, aggregates,
dissolved species, and secondary phases rather than as a purely dissolved thermodynamic component.
The project also expanded beyond colloid-only studies to include interfacial investigations using single-crystal oxide
model systems. In situ synchrotron X-ray scattering measurements were successfully performed to examine Cs+
adsorption on alumina under Hanford tank waste-relevant conditions, and exploratory nanoparticle sorption studies
were conducted with CeO2 and PuO2 on alumina and yttria-stabilized zirconia surfaces. While these model-surface
studies did not yield a fully complete mechanistic description of nanoparticle attachment at all interfaces considered,
they established important methodological capability, generated direct evidence for Pu association with oxide surfaces,
and provided useful insight into oxide-water interfacial behavior under highly caustic, high ionic strength conditions.

A major accomplishment of the project was the substantial advancement of CST interaction studies. Rather than
remaining at an initial exploratory stage, the work developed into a detailed examination of plutonium retention by
crystalline silicotitanate under Hanford-relevant alkaline conditions. Batch-contact experiments quantified Pu reten-
tion for both PuO2 nanoparticles and initially aqueous Pu(VI), identified carbonate as a major suppressor of unintended
Pu uptake, and showed that apparent retention can be strongly influenced by CST-derived fines and by CST leachate
effects on Pu colloid stability. Comparative sorption studies with goethite and rutile further broadened the context for
interpreting Pu retention on engineered and mineral-like oxide phases that can serve as pre-filters for the CST columsn
in TSCR. Considering TSCR operations without such a pre-filtration step, the project translated experimental parti-
tioning results into first-order parametric estimates of Pu loading in TSCR columns, demonstrating that under some
plausible operating scenarios spent CST may approach or exceed the 100 nCi/g transuranic waste threshold, especially
on lead columns.
Work on surface complexation and reactivity modeling, informed by the collected experimental data, was initiated but
was not advanced to a stage sufficient to fully represent the coupled chemistry of concentrated tank supernatants and
plutonium colloidal phases. As a result, development of a predictive mechanistic modeling framework was not com-
pleted within the project period. In addition, although the project substantially improved understanding of the factors
controlling Pu retention by CST and identified operationally important variables, the originally envisioned develop-
ment and validation of specific pretreatment or mitigation strategies could not be completed before project closeout.
Nevertheless, iron oxides and titanium oxides are identified as promising materials for pre-treatment strategies.
Overall, the project delivered major experimental advances in surrogate nanoparticle development, plutonium colloid
chemistry, oxide interfacial science, CST partitioning, long-term aging behavior, and TSCR-relevant waste classifi-
cation analysis. Although some originally planned modeling and pretreatment-development activities remain incom-
plete, the work established a strong mechanistic and experimental foundation for future efforts aimed at predicting and
managing plutonium behavior in CST-based treatment of Hanford tank waste.
