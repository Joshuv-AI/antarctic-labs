 Executive Summary:

This project aims were to develop and demonstrate a scalable, integrated process to convert sewage
sludge into renewable natural gas (RNG), enabling wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) to
become net energy producers. The system proposal integrates autothermal hydrothermal
liquefaction (AT-HTL), supercritical salt precipitation (SCSP), and hydrothermal gasification
(HTG), collectively forming the Supercritical Sludge-to-Gas (SC-S2G) platform.
Initially, batch hydrothermal liquefaction reactions were used to screen sewage sludge using AT-
HTL (later termed RI-HTL) conversion to biocrude, aqueous and char phases compared to
hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL). Significant improvement in biocrude yield using peroxide
addition at O:C ratio of 0.05 and under conditions of 300°C for 10 minutes gave 57% biocrude
yield and 85% fluid carbon yield (biocrude plus aqueous), while minimizing the loss of carbon to
char solids (~7%). Hence, RI-HTL was shown to be effective for conversion of real sewage sludge.
The corrosion of the alloy reactor tubes or vessels is an important factor when developing a process
that includes an oxidant and a chemically complex feed like sewage sludge. We investigated the
corrosion rates on metal alloys at 350°C for 240 hours. Corrosion rates of 0.21 and 0.26 mpy for
304L and 316L stainless steel were measured respectively. The corrosion information obtained in
this investigation was utilized by PNNL for design, materials sourcing and construction of the pilot
scale continuous flow system. Based on the measured 304L and 316L uniform corrosion rates,
these materials are acceptable and passed safety protocol for application in the PNNL HTL-2
continuous reactor system.
Deleterious anions such as sulfate and phosphates and organosulfur and phosphorus compounds
occur in the aqueous phase after HTL of sewage sludge. These S and P-compounds will poison
the gasification catalysts. We designed and built a supercritical salt precipitator (SCSP) in order
to remove the ions from aqueous phase by utilizing the solubility change of supercritical water
precipitating out the ions and solids. Later the SCSP use was de-scoped due to insufficient
reduction of the sulfur leading to poisoning of the gasification catalysts. We concluded that
converting >90% of organic sulfur contained within the sewage sludge into inorganic
sulfates/sulfites will not be possible without reducing the liquid yield obtained under HTL
conditions. Sulfur removal during HTL benefits the activity of the downstream HTG catalyst via
reduced poisoning. Sulfur can be removed either as inorganic sulfates/sulfites – which are
precipitated as solids in the SCSP step – or as inorganic sulfides, either hydrogen or metal sulfide.
While C-S bonds are more oxidatively reactive than C-C bonds, experimental measurements
indicate that the difference is insufficient for >90% C-S conversion without breaking C-C bonds
resulting in generation of >20% carbon yield of gas. We adopted a multi-layered mitigation
strategy to address this challenge, including evaluation of both oxidative and reductive methods
for sulfur removal and optimization of downstream HTG processes. We first evaluated the use of
activated carbon as an inexpensive oxidation catalyst. Activated carbon addition resulted in a
negligible increase in sulfur oxidation. Accordingly, we then evaluated reductive methods –
including a catalytic method and an adsorptive method involving metal oxides – for converting
sulfur into hydrogen and metal sulfide forms. Catalysts reduce sulfur in the biocrude to
approximately 50% of its value in the absence of catalyst (i.e., from 2 wt% to 1 wt%). Further
improvements are possible with optimization of conditions. Adsorptive methods achieve >80%
sulfur removal relative to values measured in the absence of adsorbent (i.e., from 2 wt% to 0.4
wt%). Further mitigation was performed by optimizing the robustness of the HTG step. Finally,
we investigated the removal of sulfate and phosphate anions from the aqueous phase using a
chemical precipitation with calcium salts. We show significant reduction in inorganic S- and P-
compounds with addition of calcium acetate.

The initial project goal was to convert the biocrude and aqueous phase organics using
hydrothermal gasification (HTG) to produce compressed renewable natural gas. HTG experiments
using model compounds including acetone, propanol and 1-butanol were studied. Time on stream
(TOS) studies of Ni/SiO2 catalyst showed excellent stability using model feeds (propionic acid),
producing >50% methane yield over 40 hours TOS. However, with using sewage sludge HTL
aqueous phase feeds, the catalyst exhibited significant degradation of activity over 2 hours TOS.
HTL aqueous phase contains significant levels of sulfur which likely poisoned the catalyst.
Hydrothermal gasification is likely not economic at this point without further development of
stable catalysts or significant sulfur removal from the aqueous phase. Hence, we changed the
scope of the project to focus on formation of biocrude from RI-HTL. The biocrude would be used
directly as a bunker fuel for combined heat and power (CHP) for a wastewater treatment plant.
RI-HTL has the dual benefits of removing the organics from sewage sludge and providing
operation heat and power for the WWTP. This will mitigate the CNG used for incineration of
sludge while producing fuel for the electricity and/or fuel used to run the plant.
PNNL modified their existing continuous hydrothermal liquefaction pilot plant system to include
hydrogen peroxide injection and preheating. Four different configurations were trialed to match
the heating and residence profile of the batch RI-HTL process. A maximum of 86% carbon yield
fluid phases (biocrude and aqueous) was achieved for configuration with premixing in a CSTR,
using peroxide at 300°C.
We observed an oil-like residue remaining on the blowdown solids. A hexane-toluene wash was
used on the blowdown solids to identify any extractable organics. A 20% increase in total biocrude
yield from extraction from the blowdown solids was shown to match the yield enhancement of RI-
HTL in batch reactions. We achieve >70% carbon yield in combined oil + aqueous products from
the continuous RI-HTL reactor system with detailed mass balances under steady-state operation.

A finalized techno-economic analysis for the RI-HTL of sewage sludge to biocrude process was
developed in this project using the experimental data. A project scope change decision was made
to produce biocrude which can be used as a bunker fuel for powering the WWTP. Burning the
biocrude oil directly for heat and energy to operate the WWTP was sufficient for the client’s needs
and the overall economic of the process. We compare the WWTP operating income (costs and
revenue) under different scenarios, including the base case where sludge is sent to landfill,
anaerobic digestion of the sludge produces biogas and hydrothermal liquefaction with and without
radial initiator (RI-HTL). HTL and RI-HTL show superior operating incomes at $400 and $500
per million gallons of wastewater processed respectively. We chose 110 dtpd for the sale of WWTP
to implement a RI-HTL system since plants above 100 MGD represent a large percentage (~30%)
of the wastewater processes in the US and these plants would have sufficient sludge for HTL
processing.
RI-HTL is the most favorable scenario with avoided transportation and landfilling costs compared
to AD and base case of landfilling sludge. Increased biocrude yields from RI-HTL result in more
CHP energy compared to HTL, due to the combustion of biocrude fuel. The return on investment
can be estimated from the net income from HTL and RI-HTL of wastewater sewage sludge. With
a five year payback on the capital expense (CAPEX). We estimate a CAPEX of $36.2 million for
the RI-HTL plant operating at 110 dry ton per day of sludge. This gives an estimated ROI of 64%
for HTL and 78% for RI-HTL for a 5 year payback.
In addition, the payback period for HTL and RI-HTL is 2.5 and 1.7 years respectively, which is
<5 year, achieving the overall project goal.

The lifecycle analysis for this process was constructed using GREET®. Within GREET, there is
a prebuilt system for the hydrothermal liquefaction of sludge based on PNNL’s 2022 state of
technology report. With this pre-built system, we were able to add the production of hydrogen
peroxide and adjust the oil yields to match our experimental values. This results in a footprint of
0.5320 kg CO2/kg biocrude produced. This is slightly higher (13%) than PNNL’s reported value
for HTL of sewage sludge, as we are adding an energy intensive step of producing hydrogen
peroxide. In addition we calculate the greenhouse gas (CO2) abatement for different processes
compared to the baseline of sewage sludge going to landfill. Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL and
RI-HTL) show low cost for the abatement of CO2 at $40/ton of sludge compared to state-of-the-
art processes of incineration (>$200/ton) and anaerobic digestion (~$50/ton). This is due to the
diversion of sludge from landfills which leads to methane formation (a much stronger greenhouse
gas) with the base case set to 500 kgCO2e/dry ton from landfill methane emissions, and
incineration uses significant amounts of natural gas (CNG) to combust the wet sludge. Hence, we
show that HTL and RI-HTL require >90% less cost of CO2 abatement per ton of sludge compared
to incineration and < 50% less energy for abatement of CO2 compared to anaerobic digestion of
sewage sludge.
For moving this technology to market, we developed a survey to gauge the readiness and interest
in adoption of RI-HTL with WWTPs. 31 participants from WWTPs gave feedback on the survey.
From the analysis of the survey data, a SWOT framework was developed. We found that
significant barriers to HTL implementation at WWTPs for sludge treatment exist, including the
quantity of sludge produced at many plants, cost of installation and the training required for
operating the HTL system and Federal and State policy and funding. There are opportunities to
address some of the barriers and increase the likelihood of HTL adoption. Namely, that WWTPs
exhibit willingness to convert their waste to usable fuel, as well demonstration plants and
standardized training would significantly help in their implementation.

 1. Background:
The specific project-wide baseline goals are:

 1. produce 0.65 kg C in methane per kg C in sludge on a carbon basis, sufficient to make
 Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs) a net energy producer (by a factor of 3.3)
 2. reduce quantities of landfilled carbon by >80%
 3. limit upfront capital investment for the plant improvement <5% of the WWTP total
 installed cost
 4. recover >50% of the incoming sulfur, nitrogen, and phosphorous in usable forms that
 provide additional economic and/or environmental benefits as fertilizers, soil amendments,
 and other materials
 5. handle variations in feed properties > 20% with <5% variation in methane yield
 6. be capable of safe startup and shutdown in less than 6 hours
 7. minimize costs of externalities, including oxidant to be used in autothermal hydrothermal
 liquification (AT-HTL) and catalysts.

Meeting these goals will result in a predicted annual return on investment of 19% and payback
period of 5.3 years. Our effort included pilot-scale testing of the AT-HTL and an integrated SCSP-
HTG process components, with a final project outcome raising the technology from TRL 4 to TRL
6 during the period of performance. The effort will de-risk the new technologies for follow-on
investments for design and testing as part of an upgrade to a first-generation HTL conversion plant
The project will be divided into two budget periods with the following objectives for each period:

 BP1: Lab-Scale Technology Evaluation and Preparing for Pilot Scale
 BP2: Refining Lab scale and models, and Pilot-Scale Testing

Sub-task 1.1.2 Sulfur Speciation. [10-14 months] Specific attention will be placed on
production of H2S and SO2, which are desirable forms of sulfur that can easily be separated
from the bio-crude and aqueous phase products. Elemental composition will be determined for
both liquid and solid products using full CHONS analysis combined with inductively coupled
plasma (ICP) spectroscopy for trace sulfur/phosphorous detection. Liquid products will be
analyzed using sulfur-selective gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS), GC×GC,
liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS), and ion or liquid chromatography to
identify non-volatile products. Relationships between char and liquid heteroatom content will be
examined to evaluate the potential for selective heteroatom removal. Liquid products will be
analyzed for sulfate, with the goal of converting >90% of the incoming sulfur into inorganic
sulfates, phosphate and nitrate. Unlike organic versions, these inorganic heteroatom forms will
easily separate from solution during SCSP. Inexpensive catalysts such as activated carbon or
TiO2 will be evaluated if necessary to achieve heteroatom oxidation goals.
MS1.2 Convert >90% sulfur to sulfates/sulfites in AT-HTL by using 1-10% stoichiometric
H2O2 and optimizing the conditions to enable sufficient sulfur removal for stable HTG.
(M12, Y1Q4)

Sulfur is important to remove for the following reasons; sulfur is corrosive, potentially damaging
equipment; Potential hazards to human health and the environment; and meeting stringent fuel
specifications.
In order to track the sulfur speciation throughout the hydrothermal liquefaction process (HTL and
RI-HTL), we quantified the feedstock sulfur composition using elemental analysis, then analyzed
each of the HTL products for sulfur species. Biocrude and biochar solids sulfur content was
determined form elemental analysis. Gas products were identified and quantified Analyzed by
Gas Chromatography with pulsed flame photometric detector (GC-PFPD) - from OI Analytical,
Xylem Lab Solutions. Aqueous phase products were measured using ICP-OES in order to give a
sulfur elemental balance to the products.

Sub-task 1.1.3 Corrosion [14-18 months] Metal corrosion is a potential risk when operating at
high temperatures with an oxidizing co-feed (e.g., H2O2). Use of corrosion resistant materials
mitigates this risk, but at added cost. As part of Task 1.1, we will perform coupon corrosion tests
using a series materials known for their high-temperature corrosion resistance (304-stainless steel,
316-stainless steel, Inconel-625, Hastalloy-C276) and analyze them for signs of corrosion,
including weight loss due to generalized corrosion and, by placing the coupon under stress, stress-
corrosion cracking. Only materials that pass these tests, as determined by measured rates of
generalized and localized corrosion and as based on TEA, will be studied at pilot scale.
MS1.3 Obtain corrosion rates under aggressive AT-HTL conditions for 2 grades of steel to
de-risk subsequent scale-up in continuous reactors.(M3, Y1Q1)

The selection of materials for high pressure reactors handling harsh feedstocks is important to
avoid excessive corrosion. Hydrothermal conditions (280-365°C at 5-20MPa) can be highly
corrosive to reactor metals. In this task we will studied corrosion of a range of metal alloys under
RI-HTL conditions with sewage sludge with an oxidant (hydrogen peroxide) for which little
literature exists on the corrosion rates under these specific conditions and feed. We established
specific corrosion rates at 350°C, ~18MPa for sewage sludge and oxidant feeds using static batch
reactor.

Experimental
Corrosion test coupons and conditions
Corrosion testing was undertaken following a similar method to that reported by Liu et al. [Liu et
al. NACE paper C2020-15439, 2020]. A total reaction of 240 h at 350°C, 2700 PSI was used. The
batch reactor was thermally cycled 11 times. After cooling, coupons were washed with water and
the reactor recharged with the feed sludge and oxidant after every ~20-24h reaction. The feedstock
consisted of 20 wt% sewage sludge and 10 wt% H2O2 feed with a volume of 100mL. A glass liner
in SS316 vessel was used to protect the coupons from the reactor wall corrosion. Four alloy
coupon were used; SS304L, SS316L, Inconel 625, and Hastelloy C276. In addition, strained U-
bend coupons of SS304L and SS316L were tested to compare the corrosion of the alloys under
stain.

Task 2.1. Batch mode testing of SCSP for mixed salts. [1-18 months] SCSP will be evaluated
in batch mode for fractionation of model salt solutions and solutions produced by HTL treatment
of sewage sludge. Ability to remove sulfates and fractionate inorganic salts will be key
performance parameters.

Initially we performed batch testing of supercritical salt precipitation to evaluate the salt
solubilities. The supercritical salt precipitation reactions were performed in a customized high-

A thermodynamic analysis reveals that water plays a critical impact in this system. Specifically,
since we are aiming to convert oxygenates into methane without a H2 co-feed, some CO2 formation
is required. Without adequate water in the system, one expects minimal conversion of the feed
molecule. See, for example, predicted equilibrium yields to methane, CO, and CO2 as a function
of water mole percentage during the HTG of 1-butanol at 500°C and 20 atm. In butanol rich
environments (mole fraction  1.0), little yield to HTG products is expected at equilibrium
because it is not possible to produce methane, CO, and CO2 in the absence of water. As the water
content becomes appreciable (butanol mole fraction 0.8 to 1.0), we predict a substantial increase
in the equilibrium yield to methane, which has a relatively broad peak just above 70% between
butanol mole fractions of 0.20 and 0.50. If the butanol mole fraction decreases below
approximately 0.2 (creating a very water-rich system), the equilibrium yield to methane drops
again as these conditions begin to favor steam reforming processes, resulting in increasing
formation of CO2 and H2. Accordingly, for 1-butanol, water contents between 20 and 50% are
allowable if targeting methane production.

HTG Deactivation and Pellet Tests
During BP1, research at Syracuse University demonstrated that Ni/SiO2 catalysts are effective for
the hydrothermal gasification of mixed organic species in water. A major reason for this is that
they are non-acidic and so minimize feed polymerization, coke deposition, and reactor fouling. In
contrast, acidic supports like γ-Al2O3 promote coke formation and we generally find that reactors
plug and must be frequently taken offline for regeneration. Initially, we have only tested powdered
formulations (~50-micron grain size) in bench scale reactors (1/4” OD). An important step in
scaleup and technology transfer is to transition to pelletized catalysts as these are the only viable
option in pilot and commercial scale reactors. The main challenge with pelletized catalysts is that
they suffer more severe diffusion limitations, and Ni utilization (compared to powdered
formulations) is low. For this reason, it is important to characterize the performance of pelletized
catalysts in larger test facilities, which is an essential step in adequately characterizing reaction-
diffusion phenomena (Thiele modulus, effectiveness factors) for reactor scale up. To this end,
Syracuse University prepared pelletized Ni/SiO2 catalysts using an identical protocol to that
employed for powdered catalysts. In general, we find that the pelletized catalysts have larger Ni
particles and a lower Ni surface area, which we tentatively attribute to the lower specific surface
area of the pelletized material (120 m2/g vs 342 m2/g)—generally speaking, this results in a higher
areal density of Ni in the pelletized material, which promotes nucleation and particle growth.
Samples have been characterized to determine textural properties, metal surface area, and particle
size; data are summarized alongside the powdered catalyst analog in Table 5.

Generally speaking, Ni/SiO2 catalysts exposed to HTG conditions experience three primary
deactivation modes, even in the presence of highly idealized model feeds. These include coke
deposition, sintering, and pore structure collapse. We expect that subsequent testing at PNNL with
unrefined feeds will additionally reveal poisoning in the presence of heteroatoms (e.g., S, N, Cl,
etc.) that are undoubtedly present in feeds derived from sewage sludge. Mitigating catalyst
deactivation is therefore challenging in that it requires a multi-tiered approach for stabilizing pore
structures, stabilizing metal dispersion, preventing coke deposition, and either preventing S- or N-
poisoning (unlikely) or avoiding contact between the catalysts and these heteroatoms.

Prior work at Syracuse University showed that Ni/SiO2 catalysts reliably deliver equilibrium yields
of methane at residence times below 1 hour at temperatures from 450 – 500°C. Testing at PNNL
employed standing protocols, which require operation under liquid phase conditions at 300 –
350°C. Here, it was found that Ni/SiO2 produces primarily H2 and does not completely convert
feedstocks. This can be rationalized by considering a map of equilibrium product distributions for
a model gasification system, namely equimolar quantities of 1-butanol and water. Here, we see
that low temperatures and high pressures generally favor methane formation, whereas high
temperatures and low pressures favor reforming reactions and syngas formation (CO and H2).
Although conditions employed by PNNL are attractive for maximizing equilibrium yields of
methane (300 – 350 °C, P > 20 bar), rates of reforming and gasification are too slow for the system
to reach equilibrium in workable residence times. Where the system at Syracuse was always
operating under equilibrium control, the system at PNNL at 300 – 350 °C operates under kinetic
control. Our proposed resolution for the next two quarters of the project is to increase operating
temperatures and pressures to kinetically favor gasification and reach equilibrium yields of
methane. A key takeaway from equilibrium analysis is that as long as temperatures remain below
~ 600°C and pressures remain above ~ 10 bar, methane yields at or above 80% are attainable as

Continuous Reactor Design
1. System design
The design for the continuous SCSP was adapted from previous work by Federic Vogel and his
team at the Paul Sherrer Institute. Figure 39 shows the final process flow diagram of the continuous
SCSP system along with an image of the current state of the reactor construction. The feed
(GRAY) is held in a 2 L glass jar and passes through a 15 𝜇𝜇m sparge filter before passing to the
pumping unit. The system currently uses a Knauer Azura P 4.1S (Knauer, APG12FA) with a
stainless-steel pump head. This pump can deliver fluid at 10 ml/min and pressures up to 400 bar,
well above the critical pressure of water. The Azura pump is additionally equipped with an inline
pressure sensor with an auto-stop feature which will terminate pumping functionality above 310
bar. This is a crucial safety precaution as the SCSP reactor will more than likely clog due to salt
deposition on the walls which will be discussed in the next section. This potential clogging will
cause a rapid increase of pressure on the upstream equipment and the auto-stop feature will prevent
any equipment failures. In the unexpected event of a power outage, or the pump self-stopping
feature malfunctioning, the bottom of the reactor is also fit with a pressure relief valve (Swagelok,
SS-4R3A) which can be set to 275 - 344 bar to allow for rapid depressurization. Alternatively,
previous work with supercritical water has also used ISCO pumps to achieve a wider range of flow
rates under high pressures, however there is a significant cost associated with purchasing and
maintaining these units.
The reactor body and fittings (BLUE) were sourced from SITEC’s high-pressure catalog. The
reactor body and fittings are made of 316L stainless steel. The reactor body is 400 mm long with
an outer diameter of 11/16” (SITEC, 730.1004). The top and bottom cross are 11/16” to fit the
reactor body and are rated to 2000 bar (SITEC, 725.1424). Attached to each cross are 1/8” low-
pressure reducers to fit the Swagelok tubing on the upstream and downstream components (SITEC,
625.2711). The reactor body is outfitted with six 1x1” 150 W nozzle heaters (Watlow,
BC104039C). As produced, the heaters are too large for the reactor body, so a 1” OD stainless
steel pipe (McMaster, 89495K269) was cut into 1” long pieces and bored out to have an ID of
11/16” to fit the SITEC reactor. Additionally, the steel spacers were equipped with a thermocouple
well to enable temperature control (Omega, KMQSS-062G-12). To prevent heat loss at the crosses,
two steel press plates are attached on both sides of the cross and equipped with two 3” 100 W
cartridge heaters which are controlled by a single controller (Watlow, E3A48-L12). The 8-zone
heating unit is controlled by a 8-zone custom controller produced by IES technical sales
(Middleton, MA 01949). While it is not shown in Figure 40 the reactor is additionally outfitted
with a front plate that helps to ensure the system is well insulated with ceramic fiber insulation
(McMaster, 93315K71).
The top, or organic-rich, stream (GREEN) passes from the top cross directly into a custom heat
exchanger. The heat exchanger is a custom build using Dernord sanitary stainless-steel equipment
as the frame and houses 20 ft of coiled 1/8” 316 SS Swagelok tubing (Swagelok, SS-T2-S-028).
Once cooled, the fluid product passes through a split filter apparatus which allows filter cups
(Swagelok, SS-2F-K4-15) to be changed or cleaned without stopping the reactor operation. Inline
filtration is critical because the system pressure is maintained with a back pressure regulator and
so preventing particulates from reaching the diaphragm of the regulator will help to extend its
overall lifespan. The backpressure regulator was sourced from Equilibar (U6L1SNN8-
NSGP5000T150K40WWWB) and made of 316 stainless steel with a maximum temperature of
                                            Page 44 of 96

150 °C and a maximum inlet pressure of 413 bar. Lastly, once the fluid passes through the back
pressure regulator, it will pass over a Mettler Toledo Cond Sensos conductivity meter (58031404).
From here, the organic-rich aqueous phase will be collected.
The bottoms, or inorganic-rich stream (YELLOW) operates in parallel to the tops product and
controls the split ratio of the reactor. The bottoms product leaves the reactor system and passes
through a tube-in-tube heat exchanger that is in series with the tops heat exchanger. The bottoms
product is withdrawn from the reactor using an Ametek-Chandler Quizix 5000 high-pressure pump
(Q5000-10K) which will act as a mass flow controller for the system. Similar to the tops stream,
the bottoms product then passes through a split filtration unit to remove particulates and prevent
clogging. Once cooled, it is passed to a glass collection vessel.
The entire reactor system is housed within a customized 80-20 strut (McMaster, 47065T101)
structure with 0.25” plexiglass on the exterior (McMaster, 8560K) to mitigate rapid
depressurizations of the system. The unit is equipped with two doors on the front to allow for easy
user access as well as a partition of the reactor side from the sample collection side, allowing the
user to maintain safe separation from the unit while it is running. Lastly, the reactor side is hooked
into the building’s ventilation system allowing it to act as a self-contained hood.

increasing the temperature range such as Configuration B, most of the radial gradient stays above
the critical point. The temperature at the wall reaches 550 °C, and remains above the critical point
for 4.75 mm. Beyond this point, the fluid dips below the critical point and settles at a steady state
centerline temperature of 350 °C. This would indicate that the innermost 4 mm would be a sub- or
near-critical fluid, while the outer 4.75 mm would be a supercritical fluid suggesting a biphasic
mixture. Since the supercritical fluid is likely to form closest to the walls, any salts that are
dissolved in the fluid will crash out, and deposit along the reactor walls. This is a phenomenon that
has been well documented in supercritical salt precipitation systems.

Conclusion
 Hydrothermal liquefaction aqueous phase is a potential source of energy that can be
unlocked through gasification techniques, however, for feeds with high ash contents the aqueous
phase will be concentrated with inorganics. These salts could pose a risk of poisoning precious
metal catalysts that are required for hydrothermal gasification to drive the dissolved organics to
                                           Page 48 of 96

either hydrogen or methane. Supercritical salt precipitation was evaluated for its potential as an
intermediate process between HTL and HTG, leveraging the non-polarity of supercritical water to
simultaneously retain dissolved organics while dispelling dissolved salts. Initial batch experiments
show great potential for the SCSP system to reduce the aqueous sodium sulfate concentration by
99%. However, batch experiments were found to be limited in their capability as the system could
not maintain a supercritical equilibrium after 10 wt% of the initially loaded sample was removed,
forcing the system into a subcritical regime. These limitations led to efforts to design and construct
a continuous flow SCSP system that can maintain supercritical equilibrium while simultaneously
separating the organic and inorganic rich phases. While the construction of the continuous SCSP
system is iterated to achieve the desired conditions, both first principal calculations and empirical
correlations were utilized to predict the thermal gradients within the flow system as well as the
expected concentration of common salts under these conditions. COMSOL solved the momentum
and energy balances within the system which indicated that the fluid exiting the reactor would be
a biphasic mixture of supercritical water closest to the walls and subcritical water closest to the
center of the flow profile. This finding is validated by the confirmed phenomenon of salt deposition
on the reactor walls for previous SCSP reactor designs. Knowing the physical properties of water
within the reactor then allowed for the modeling of salt solubilities based on previously published
models that show a reduction in solubility down to 0.0003 wt% for sodium sulfate. While chloride
salts appear to be less effected by the change in fluid density, the continuous SCSP will be a highly
tunable system to fractionate and recover salts within the HTL aqueous phase.

descoped because insufficient sulfur could be removed from the aqueous and biocrude phases.

We concluded that converting >90% of organic sulfur contained within the sewage sludge into
inorganic sulfates/sulfites will not be possible without reducing the liquid yield obtained under
HTL conditions. Sulfur removal during HTL benefits activity of the downstream HTG catalyst.
Sulfur can be removed either as inorganic sulfates/sulfites – which are precipitated as solids in the
SCSP step – or as inorganic sulfides, either hydrogen or metal sulfide. While C-S bonds are more
oxidatively reactive than C-C bonds, experimental measurements indicate that the difference is
insufficient for >90% C-S conversion without breaking C-C bonds resulting in generation of >20%
carbon yield of gas. We adopted a multi-layered mitigation strategy to address this challenge,
including evaluation of both oxidative and reductive methods for sulfur removal and optimization
of downstream HTG processes. We first evaluated the use of activated carbon as an inexpensive
oxidation catalyst. Activated carbon addition resulted in a negligible increase in sulfur oxidation.
Accordingly, we then evaluated reductive methods – including a catalytic method and an
adsorptive method involving metal oxides – for converting sulfur into hydrogen and metal sulfide
forms. Catalysts reduce sulfur in the biocrude to approximately 50% of its value in the absence of
catalyst (i.e., from 2 wt% to 1 wt%), with further improvements possible with optimization of
conditions. Adsorptive methods achieve >80% sulfur removal relative to values measured in the
absence of adsorbent (i.e., from 2 wt% to 0.4 wt%). Further mitigation was performed by
optimizing the robustness of the HTG step. Experimental HTG results obtained to date indicate
                                            Page 49 of 96

that the Ni/SiO2 catalyst selected for this process has sufficient activity to achieve equilibrium gas
concentration even after sintering. Further, Ni is an inexpensive material to be used in catalytic
quantities. Activity loss due to sulfidation will be evaluated in BP2. If Ni is insufficiently active
in the presence of carried over sulfur from the HTL and SCSP steps, we will evaluate the use of
sulfided Ru as the HTG catalyst. While Ru is more expensive than Ni, it is much more active and
retains most of its activity after sulfidation.

Task 4.2. HTG
HTG research during previous quarters has revealed that Ni/SiO2 catalysts are susceptible to
deactivation, which occurs by three key mechanisms: sintering; coking; and pore structure
collapse. Each of these is deactivation mechanisms is influenced (to varying extents) by three key
aspects of the HTG reaction environment: high temperature and pressure; high water content; and
the presence of carbonaceous (organic) species. In other words, the relatively severe reaction
conditions of HTG combine to drive sintering, coke deposition, and pore structure collapse.
                                           Page 60 of 96

Mitigation strategies must address each mode of deactivation, but this is not straightforward
because deactivation modes are somewhat coupled, and they typically respond differently to
changes in operating conditions and catalyst properties. For example, if trying to minimize
sintering, one might select Al2O3 as a Ni support; however, Al2O3 is acidic, and it will increase
rates of coke formation. In contrast, one might mitigate coke deposition by using an inert support,
like SiO2; unfortunately, SiO2 interacts weakly with metal nanoparticles, and Ni supported
thereupon is more prone to sinter. It is as yet unclear what catalyst design will offer the best
compromise and deliver the best stability under reaction conditions. To insightfully design a stable
catalyst, one must understand how various deactivation modes respond to changes in reaction
conditions as well as catalyst composition and provenance (metal identity, metal loading, support
identity, synthesis method, etc.) To this end, we have probed the impacts of varying organic
composition, support identity, and synthesis method on catalyst stability under HTG conditions.
First, we considered how variations in organic composition and in types of functional groups
present in the HTG feed will impact stability. This was accomplished by subjecting an HTG
catalyst operating at steady state to perturbations in (1) organic feed identity and (2) organic partial
pressure. Surprisingly, we found that once a catalyst reaches its “working” steady state under HTG
conditions, additional variations in organic identity/composition do not further induce deactivation
by coking, sintering, or pore structure collapse. This is summarized for a selected group of
compounds wherein exposure to increased partial pressures of each species, including challenging
molecules like THF and phenol, does not appear to change the activity of the catalyst (a value of
1 on this figure means that no deactivation has occurred after exposure to a compound) see Fig.
56.

 Figure 56. Catalyst stability following perturbations in the identity and partial pressure of
 organic species. T = 500°C, Pacetone = 10.5 bar, Pwater = 10.5 bar.

These observations suggest that, once a catalyst reaches its working state, it is robust to future
changes in composition. This is an important outcome for a technology like HTG, for which one
anticipates endless, near-continuous variations in the identity and composition of organic
compounds present in the feed.
It is difficult to mitigate sintering and pore structure collapse. One potential strategy for doing so
is to change the catalyst support. For example, supports like Al2O3 generally retain good metal
dispersion (small particles), whereas metals supported on SiO2 tend to sinter quickly and to a
larger extent. Accordingly, we have compared the onstream stability of a base Ni/SiO2 catalyst
(0.73 wt% Ni) with Ni/Al2O3 (1.1 wt% Ni), see Fig. 57.

Here we observe that Ni/Al2O3 has a lower initial activity than Ni/SiO2 per unit Ni. Further, it
undergoes extensive and continuous deactivation with time on stream, whereas Ni/SiO2 ultimately

characterization of these two samples is currently underway to determine the underlying cause of
deactivation and to determine whether changing the support identity to Al2O3 is useful in
preventing sintering, even if it may induce other modes of deactivation (like coke formation). The
insight as to the primary deactivation is still useful as it may inform future strategies for retaining
metal dispersion.
We additionally considered variations in Ni deposition technique. Generally, we have used
incipient wetness impregnation to disperse Ni on SiO2, but this technique usually achieves poor
control over Ni location. In addition, it not guarantee strong interactions between the metal (Ni)
and the support (Si); as such, catalysts prepared by incipient wetness impregnation may be prone
to sinter. One potential strategy to enhance control over metal dispersion and ensure strong
interactions between metal and support is to use atomic layer deposition to disperse Ni onto the
support. We have compared the performance of Ni/SiO2 catalysts synthesized using both methods,
and we generally observe that, ultimately, catalysts prepared using either method reach identical
steady states after roughly 30 – 40 hours on stream. This suggests that initial catalyst preparations
are metastable and, under the high temperatures of HTG, Ni/SiO2 catalysts ultimately reach similar
steady state structures regardless of the initial preparation method, see Fig. 58. The model
compound studies are particularly useful in understanding catalyst stability and deactivation
mechanisms under controlled conditions. By testing with specific organic compounds, we can
isolate the impact of individual variables on catalyst performance, allowing us to identify which
factors most influence deactivation and optimize the process accordingly.

We have determined that all Ni/SiO2 samples, regardless of preparation method, will undergo
severe coke deposition under HTG conditions. This is supported by our observation of graphitic
carbon in post reaction samples via X-Ray Diffraction. We subjected Ni/SiO2 samples prepared by
incipient wetness and ALD to carbon burnoff via calcination in air at 450°C. Generally, we find
that, independent of preparation method, Ni/SiO2 catalysts accumulate roughly 0.1 – 0.2 grams of
carbon per gram of catalyst. Curiously, we observe that while calcination does accomplish carbon
burnoff, it does not restore catalyst activity, even if it is followed by reduction of the Ni phase
under high temperature H2 (400°C). This suggests that, although coke deposition is prevalent and
may lead to reactor fouling, it does not appear to substantially impact the activity of Ni. The
implication is that the bulk of Ni deactivation must be associated with sintering and pore structure
collapse, which provides key insights that inform future mitigation strategies.
Scaling up production required only minor modifications on our part – we fabricated at 1” ID flow
reactor that can accept catalyst charges of 50 – 100grams, and we have since switched to using this
unit to pretreat pelletized Ni catalysts by thermal calcination in air followed by thermal reduction
in hydrogen. We have additionally characterized these materials using the standard battery of tests
(N2 physisorption, H2 chemisorption, and X-Ray Diffraction). Activity testing on these samples is
currently underway at PNNL using a combination of model compounds (acetone/water) to mimic
bench scale studies at SyracuseU and HTL liquids derived from wastewater treatment to
approximate a real HTG feed that will be encountered in a wastewater facility.
In parallel we investigated designing a more stable Ni catalyst. At present, we see that each material
tested has advantages and disadvantages, and we have yet to identify an “ideal” catalyst
formulation. Thus far, our general preference remains Ni/SiO2 as it is non-acidic and is able to
operate continuously for long periods of time at steady state (albeit at a reduced rate relative to
zero time on stream). Ni/Al2O3 has some advantages, primarily that it is slightly more sinter
resistant than Ni/SiO2. This means that activity losses thereupon are at least partially regenerable;
                                            Page 63 of 96

however, it appears to be perhaps more susceptible to deactivation by coke deposition and
oxygenate polymerization such that its steady state rate is lower than that of Ni/SiO2 and it may
induce reactor fouling, requiring one to place the entire system offline to change the catalyst bed.
As yet, we have been able to run Ni/Al2O3 samples for long periods on stream; however, these
studies were performed at very short residence times with small catalyst quantities so that we could
observe and quantify deactivation extents, and we have not yet determined if these materials will
remain robust at large contact times, which will be required to achieve high organic conversion in
commercial operation. In this study, our primary goal is to demonstrate that Ni/Al2O3 samples are
able to operate stably at high contact times and high feed conversion to achieve equilibrium yields
of methane. We have demonstrated that this is possible for a broad set of organic and water
compositions over Ni/SiO2, and that residence times on the order of 1 hour are required to reach
equilibrium. We observe that Ni/Al2O3 samples generally retain better metal dispersions than
Ni/SiO2 samples. Assuming that Ni surfaces are comparably active in each material, a lower extent
of sintering suggests that Ni/Al2O3 samples may be able to deliver equilibrium yields of methane
in relatively short contact times (0.1 – 1 hour); however, it remains to be seen if operating with
high mass loadings of Ni/Al2O3 (to achieve high conversion) is feasible due to anticipated reactor
fouling issues.
As a final direction for this project, we have seen that support identity has a significant impact on
Ni stability (e.g., by sintering) and on reactor fouling (e.g., acidic supports seem to induce severe
coke formation). One may propose then that an ideal catalyst formulation might utilize a support,
like Alumina, that is able to retain high Ni dispersion. One possible workaround for the challenge
of reactor fouling and coke deposition is to passivate the surface by neutralizing surface acid sites
through post-synthetic modification. For example, exchanging a Ni/Al2O3 catalyst with sodium
nitrate would replace acidic protons with relatively neutral sodium cations and may reduce coke
formation. We aim to explore this idea as well as continued variation in support identity during the
next quarter of this project.

The project team discussed the potential for sludge treatment technology implementation with a
variety of experts in wastewater treatment and biosolids handling. This document summarizes
the team’s current understanding of the market size, driving forces, and challenges to
implementing sludge treatment technologies.

There are over 15,000 wastewater treatment facilities (WWTPs) in the United States. Of these,
only about 3,000 WWTPs are large enough for most sludge treatment processes to be economical,
according to Jason Turgeon of EPA Region 1.

Figure 63. Distribution of Wastewater treatment plants based on sludge production rate.

A sampled analysis of about 4521 wastewater treatment plants, where the production rate is used
roughly as a measure of the size of the wastewater treatment plant confirms this notion. As shown
in Figure 64, only about 3% of the wastewater treatment plants in the sampled dataset churn out a
sludge production rate of greater than 50 Dry Tons Per Day (dtpd). Smaller facilities often contract
with larger facilities or standalone operations to handle some of their sludge treatment. The three
most prevalent sludge treatment pathways are incineration, land application/composting, and
landfilling. Since these pathways are operated under contract, making a change is difficult and
slow. Some of the large sludge treatment entities with which WWTPs contract include Synagrow,
Waste Management, and Resource Management Incorporated. The WWTP facilities and their
operators are risk averse, with few opportunities for enabling new technology to be introduced.
However, permits are a strong driver of change within this industry.
Another limitation when attempting to implement new sludge treatment technology is the need for
a civil engineering firm to design the facility. This creates a scenario where both the WRRFs and
civil engineering firms are considered the customers for the technology, and both stakeholders
must be convinced that a specific technology is worth installing for the project to move forward.
These firms tend to be risk-averse as well and are hesitant to provide designs for emerging

                                            Page 69 of 96

technologies without a pilot demonstration. Furthermore, regulators in each region must be
convinced that a given technology will work as a treatment method. Collectively, WRRFs,
engineering firms, and regulators all participate in the decision to purchase and implement new
technology.
An incentive to attracting stakeholder attention, however, would be to sell the idea of how much
revenue wastewater treatment plants stand to gain from extracting biocrude using sludge treatment
technology. A wastewater treatment plant stands to gain income if they can afford to sell biocrude
they produce at a rate per gallon comparable to that of existing conventional fuels. This will depend
on the size of the wastewater treatment plant and effectively the sludge production rate.
A techno-economic analysis of the wastewater treatment plants sampled earlier shows that less
than 3% of the sampled wastewater treatment plants can afford to sell their biocrude at the
Minimum Fuel selling Price (MFSP) of $4.00 per Gallon Gasoline Equivalent (GGE).

One major driver of change, especially in the Northeast United States, is the handling of “emerging
contaminants”, especially PFAS (according to Turgeon). Requirements around PFAS in the
Northeastern states limit the viability of the sludge treatment handling pathways, which creates a
strong need for an alternative option. For example, Vermont and New Hampshire need to export
all or nearly all of their sewage sludge due to the inability of the WRRFs to meet the stringent
PFAS regulations in these states. Smaller facilities are gravely in need of solutions to the
challenges associated with PFAS since most on-site processing options are prohibitively
expensive. Funding for addressing emerging contaminants was allotted in federal infrastructure
funding to the states, which may help facilitate the installation of new technologies.
For the 2,000+ WWTPs that are large enough to justify on-site sludge treatment, a window of
opportunity may be opening as new PFAS regulations come into play. If a proposed sludge
treatment technology can eliminate PFAS, the technology will be in high demand by the WWTPs.

The barriers most likely to impact implementation will be funding and convincing an engineering
firm to design and construct a treatment process. For the smaller sites, a separate entity would most

                                            Page 70 of 96

likely be needed to fund and operate the sludge treatment process. These sites could contract with
this separate facility and transport their sludge to the off-site location to be treated.
An article in the Winter 2023 NEWEA Journal listed different sludge treatment technologies and
their status as common practice, established technologies, innovative technologies, and emerging
technologies. HTL was listed among the emerging technologies, along with supercritical water
oxidation. This article laid out a map of how technologies at different levels of development could
be leveraged in a wholistic strategy for addressing any regulatory or other constraints that may
arise in the future. For example, products from digestion (considered common practice) could then
be fed to a thermal drying process (established technology), and on to gasification or pyrolysis
(both innovative technologies). It is helpful to consider that the path to adoption for a technology
perceived to require significant development, like HTL, may require some stop-gap approaches on
the road to implementation.

renewable natural gas was shown to be the best route to implement HTL technology initially at a

5. Changes/Problems:
  a) Scope issues, Risks and mitigation strategies:
 We concluded that converting >90% of organic sulfur contained within the sewage
 sludge into inorganic sulfates/sulfites will not be possible without reducing the liquid
 yield obtained under HTL conditions. Sulfur removal during HTL benefits the activity
 of the downstream HTG catalyst via reduced poisoning. Sulfur can be removed either
 as inorganic sulfates/sulfites – which are precipitated as solids in the SCSP step – or as
 inorganic sulfides, either hydrogen or metal sulfide. While C-S bonds are more
 oxidatively reactive than C-C bonds, experimental measurements indicate that the
 difference is insufficient for >90% C-S conversion without breaking C-C bonds
 resulting in generation of >20% carbon yield of gas. We adopted a multi-layered
 mitigation strategy to address this challenge, including evaluation of both oxidative and
 reductive methods for sulfur removal and optimization of downstream HTG processes.
 We first evaluated the use of activated carbon as an inexpensive oxidation catalyst.
 Activated carbon addition resulted in a negligible increase in sulfur oxidation.
 Accordingly, we then evaluated reductive methods – including a catalytic method and
 an adsorptive method involving metal oxides – for converting sulfur into hydrogen and
 metal sulfide forms. Catalysts reduce sulfur in the biocrude to approximately 50% of
 its value in the absence of catalyst (i.e., from 2 wt% to 1 wt%), with further
 improvements are possible with optimization of conditions. Adsorptive methods
                                      Page 87 of 96

 achieve >80% sulfur removal relative to values measured in the absence of adsorbent
 (i.e., from 2 wt% to 0.4 wt%). Further mitigation was performed by optimizing the
 robustness of the HTG step. Experimental HTG results obtained to date indicate that
 the Ni/SiO2 catalyst selected for this process has sufficient activity to achieve
 equilibrium gas concentration even after sintering. Further, Ni is an inexpensive
 material to be used in catalytic quantities.
