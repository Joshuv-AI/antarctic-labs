SECTION 1: Introduction
As the United States (U.S.) aims to achieve energy independence (Energy
Information Administration (EIA) [21], there is an increasing need for multiple
energy sources [74]. These recent efforts have led to the construction of many
new energy sources that are innovative, reliable, and have the potential to deliver
critically needed power [85]. Recent developments in advanced computing,
artificial intelligence (AI), data centers, electric vehicles, etc. are beginning to strain
the grid, requiring large amounts of energy in order to operate [70, 78]. A report
from Lawrence Berkely National Laboratory estimates that by 2028, data centers
alone may demand up to 132 GW, representing 12% of total U.S. electricity
consumption [72]. While critically needed energy production is expanding in
response, the infrastructure enabling transmission and distribution (T&D) of this
energy is equally critical to meet rising demands [7, 8].

Power T&D is the critical infrastructure that facilitates the delivery of generated
power from energy sources to end consumers. This system consists of high-
voltage transmission lines that transport electricity over long distances from
power plants to substations, where it is then stepped down to lower voltages
and distributed via distribution lines to homes, businesses, and industries.
T&D is essential for ensuring that electricity generated from various sources,
including renewable energy, reaches the consumer efficiently and reliably. The
importance of a robust T&D network cannot be overstated, as it plays a vital role
in maintaining grid stability, reducing losses, and is the backbone supporting
the increasing demands of modern society. As the energy landscape evolves,
the expansion and modernization of T&D infrastructure is imperative to meet
growing energy demands and achieve energy security [68]. Without upgraded
and increased capacity, energy goals will remain on hold, and the industry will
lack sufficient power for advancement.

However, due to economic challenges and physical constraints, the expansion
of T&D has slowed in recent years [68]. According to the American Society of
Civil Engineers’ (ASCE) 2021 Infrastructure Report Card, some grid components
are “over a century old—far past their 50-year life expectancy—and 70% of
T&D lines, are well into the second half of their lifespans,” indicating that T&D
infrastructure needs to be replaced and/or upgraded for safety and increased
efficiency [1]. These deteriorating infrastructure components can affect grid
operators’ ability to import and provide power, especially during summer peak-
load situations resulting from high temperatures. This can potentially lead to
transmission congestion or even unplanned outages [24]. Combined with aging
wear and tear, extreme weather events raise the number of outages each year
due to damage inflicted on T&D systems [29], adding to the cost of replacement
and repair. For example, in 2017, Hurricane Harvey’s extreme wind and flooding

 1

were responsible for damaging and knocking down more than 6,200 distribution
poles and 850 transmission structures [7]. It is estimated that by 2050, individual
utilities may see up to $1.7 billion in storm-damage costs and lost revenue due
to subsequent weather-related outages [7]. Utilities and grid-network owners are
constantly planning and preparing to address these concerns, expanding their
T&D capacity as quickly as possible [24]. However, construction may not even
begin until all approvals and permits are obtained, which slows progress and can
result in replacement taking over a decade [37].

With their potential to directly destroy T&D infrastructure, wildfires create
hazardous conditions for repair and inflict hundreds of millions of dollars in
damage. Wildfires also present a particularly severe threat to power grids.
Smoke and particulate matter from wildfire can also ionize the air, leading
to arcing and short circuits in electrical equipment. Moreover, the increasing
frequency and intensity of these weather events strain the resilience of
the grid, making it more challenging to maintain a reliable power supply.
The cumulative impact of these natural hazards underscores the need for a
more resilient and adaptable power grid to ensure continuous and reliable
electricity delivery in the face of changing conditions.

Extreme heat can cause power lines to sag due to thermal expansion, increasing
the risk of contact with vegetation or other structures, leading to outages or fires.
Additionally, elevated temperatures can reduce the efficiency of transformers
and other electrical equipment, potentially causing them to overheat and fail.
Ice storms can coat power lines and equipment with heavy layers of ice, adding
substantial weight and potentially causing lines to snap or poles to collapse.
High winds can bring down power lines, damage infrastructure, and blow debris
into electrical components, leading to widespread outages.

1.1 Solution Landscape
Many solutions, aside from siting and building new transmission capacity,
have the capability to meet transmission-capacity demands. This is
highlighted in the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 1920,
which emphasizes the importance of incorporating advanced technologies
and innovative solutions into transmission-planning studies to enhance the
resilience, reliability, and efficiency of the power grid. The order encourages
utilities to consider a broad range of technologies to address various
challenges, including those posed by incorporating diverse-energy sources,
aging infrastructure, and evolving grid demands [83].

According to Order 1920, utilities should consider technologies, such as advanced
conductors, which offer higher capacity and efficiency. Solutions, such as
dynamic line-rating (DLR) systems, optimize the use of existing transmission
lines based on real-time conditions, and energy storage systems can provide
grid stability and support during peak demand periods. Additionally, Order 1920
highlights the importance of incorporating grid-enhancing technologies, such
as power-flow controllers (PFCs), which can manage congestion and improve
the distribution of power, and advanced monitoring and diagnostic tools, which
enhance situational awareness and predictive maintenance [25].

1.1.2 Advanced Conductors

24-51094

Advanced conductors are composite conductors, often incorporating a carbon-
fiber core for strength. Advanced conductors typically sag much less because
of external and resistive heating, allowing for higher transmission capacity for
the same conductor weight. These conductors can often be applied to existing
towers and poles, simplifying installation and circumventing lengthy right-of-way
and regulatory processes involved in building new transmission lines.

The application of advanced conductors does require replacing existing
conductors and taking transmission lines temporarily out of service. Advanced
conductors can be an excellent solution to transmission-capacity constraints;
however, they do not directly solve issues related to conductor icing, fire
vulnerability, or galloping and vibration from wind.

A separate Advanced Conductors Scan Report has been produced by the Idaho
National Laboratory [37]. For this reason, advanced conductors will not be
covered in this report.

1.1.3 Active Solutions

24-51094

This is a broad category of grid-enhancing technologies (GETs), including
DLR [78], PFCs [28], and other power electronics, topology optimization,
energy-storage optimization, reactive-power flow control, and advanced
central processing [27]. These strategies can be used to successfully reduce
congestion and increase power flow with minimal modifications to existing
physical equipment. One thing all active or digital solutions have in common
is their reliance on system awareness, which is achieved through advanced
sensing, communications, and control.
Active GETs play an important role in increasing power-system transmission
capacity and have been the subject of extensive research and numerous case
studies. FERC Order 881, issued in 2021, mandates the use of ambient adjusted
ratings (AARs), a less-precise precursor to DLRs, on transmission lines to increase
their capacity [23].
The main downside of active GETs is their reliance on communications and
new technology, making implementation difficult when integrating with
existing operational controls. There is also hesitancy to adopt some of these
technologies due to their perception as stopgap solutions and concerns
about misoperation and lack of longevity. Similar to advanced conductors,
active GETs do not directly protect against extreme weather events, although
they can make a power system more resilient to disruption overall.
More information on active and digital GETs can be found in the series of
reports found on the Grid-Enhancing Technologies Landing Page. These
technologies will not be covered in this report.

1.1.4 Mechanical Solutions

24-51094

Mechanical solutions are defined as passive or mechanical innovations
that are not advanced conductors and do not rely on digital processing or
communications. The category of mechanical solutions, the topic of this scan

SECTION 2: Overhead Conductors

24-51094

Overhead conductors are key components of power systems, primarily used
to transmit and distribute electrical energy across long distances via overhead
power lines. These conductors are typically made of materials, such as
aluminum, aluminum alloy, or a combination of aluminum and steel (such as
an aluminum conductor, steel reinforced [ACSR]), chosen for their balance of
conductivity, strength, and cost-effectiveness. ACSR conductors have largely
been the standard in overhead conductors’ installation for the past several
decades. As a result, T&D networks are composed primarily of ACSR lines,
many of which have been in use for several decades [79].

 8

2.1 Concerns Associated with Traditional Conductors
While aluminum-based conductors offer numerous benefits, they also present
several challenges that pose potential hazards, risks, and inefficiencies in
energy T&D. These concerns include the following:

 • Sagging: Where overhead lines, as a result of higher temperatures
 from increased power flow and environmental conditions, begin
 to expand and sag closer to the ground, the result is a phenomena
 called conductor sag [40]. Sagging can lead to increased line losses
 and safety hazards if the conductors come too close to the ground or
 other objects [32].
 • Aeolian/Vortex-Induced Vibration: Where wind-induced oscillations
 cause overhead lines to vibrate at their resonant material frequency,
 leading to fatigue and, eventually, mechanical failure of the
 conductors [39].
 • Galloping and Ice Loading: Large amplitude, low-frequency
 oscillations induced by wind, often exacerbated by ice loading, can
 cause severe mechanical stress and damage to conductors and
 supporting structures and connections [76, 25].
 • Structural Instability/Deterioration/Aging: The compromise of
 traditional conductors’ mechanical integrity and overall performance
 [1] over time, caused by material degradation.

Presently, several mechanical solutions address these particular challenges;
these were developed to improve the reliability, safety, and efficiency of
overhead conductors.

2.2 Conductor Coatings
Conductor coatings are advanced material additions to the exterior of
power lines. Coatings with hydrophobic properties reduce ice accumulation
and acoustic noise [87] while other coatings specialize in heat emissivity,
reducing sag by lowering the overall temperature and safely providing
elevated operating ampacity [3]. The most important considerations for
implementation are expected use case and ability to be applied to existing
live or strung conductors. Two ampacity-boosting coatings are already in
commercial production while anti-ice coatings are still in case-study phases.

 9

2.2.1 Prysmian E3X®
Prysmian’s E3X technology, which stands for Enhanced Energy Efficiency, is a
thin coating that improves thermal performance by helping to dissipate heat
more effectively, both reducing conductor sag and increasing transmission
capacity. This coating can be applied to TransPowr overhead conductors
at the manufacturing facility, where it can be applied in different stages
of conductor layers or, alternatively, to just the outer surface [50]. Also, a
robotic system has been developed to clean lines and apply E3X to already
installed (but not live) overhead lines. E3X may also increase the service life of
overhead lines by shielding conductors from such environmental degradation
as ultraviolet (UV) exposure and moisture [38].

Company Profile Figure 2.1. Prysmian’s E3X Robotic Applicator.
Headquartered in Milan, Italy, Prysmian Group provides a wide range
of products, including high-tech cables for various telecom and power
applications. They are a large global company, with active sales and regional
offices and research and development facilities in the U.S. [64].

The coatings developed by Prysmian are at a high TRL of 8, indicating that
they have been tested and validated in both laboratory settings and real-
world applications [3] . These coatings are commercially available and have
been deployed in various projects worldwide. The robotic application of E3X
is at a slightly lower TRL of 6, having been deployed in a case study on over
half a mile of overhead ACSR line in Illinois in 2021 [31].

Testing conducted at Oak Ridge National Laboratory confirms that E3X
conductors have up to 25% increased ampacity and 30% reduced operating
temperature relative to uncoated conductors in similar conditions [38].

BENEFITS
 • Up to 25% increase in ampacity without reconductoring.
 • Robotic application on existing lines is possible.

 • The capability of coating to withstand wildfire is unknown.

2.2.2 AssetCool—Photonic Cooling Technology

Figure 2.2. Conductor coated with AssetCool’s photonic cooling technology.

AssetCool’s photonic cooling technology is a solution designed to improve
the thermal performance of overhead conductors by leveraging photonic
materials. These materials are engineered to reflect sunlight and radiate
heat more effectively, thereby reducing the operating temperature of the
conductors. This technology may enhance the ampacity of power lines (up
to 33% relative to traditional, uncoated conductors) and reduce thermal sag,
leading to improved transmission capacity and reliability at lower cost than total
conductor replacement [2]. The photonic cooling technology can be applied to
new conductors during the manufacturing process and can also be retrofitted
to existing lines. Beyond its ampacity-increasing capabilities, AssetCool’s
coating may reduce corona noise, enhance mechanical stability to resist
aeolian vibrations, and achieve consistent cooling performance of up to 24%
temperature reduction from previous temperature under similar environmental
conditions for uncoated conductors for as long as 16 months [35].

AssetCool is a United Kingdom (UK)-based company specializing in advanced
materials and technologies for improving the performance and efficiency of
power-transmission systems. The company focuses on solutions that enhance
the thermal management of electrical conductors and sells their products and
solutions in the United States.

 11

The photonic cooling technology developed by AssetCool is at an intermediate
TRL of 6–7. This indicates that the technology has been validated in relevant
environments and is transitioning from the research phase to commercial
deployment. Field trials and pilot projects have been conducted to demonstrate
its effectiveness and reliability.

BENEFITS
 • Significant reduction in conductor operating temperature.
 • Potential for increased ampacity without the need to
 replace conductors.
 • Enhanced reliability and lifespan of conductors by reducing
 thermal stress.

 • The long-term durability of the photonic materials under various
 environmental conditions is still being evaluated.
 • Initial installation and retrofitting costs may be higher compared to
 traditional solutions.

2.3 Composite Wraps
While coatings are applied in a thin, even layer onto the surface of a
conductor, composite wraps are physically wrapped around each conductor.
Unlike coatings, composite wraps provide additional structural and
mechanical rigidity to overhead conductors. The type of material, application,
and secondary-line effects can be different. Application method and cost
remain important considerations.

2.4 Dampers and Anti-Vibration Devices
Conductor dampers and anti-vibration devices are essential tools in reducing
the adverse effects of galloping and aeolian vibration in conductors.
Galloping, a low-frequency oscillation caused by wind and ice accumulation,
and aeolian vibration, a high-frequency oscillation induced by steady wind,
can both lead to conductor fatigue, hardware damage, and power outages.
Conductor dampers and anti-vibration devices work by absorbing and
dissipating the energy from these vibrations, protecting the integrity of the
power lines. Local wind and weather conditions are important considerations,
as are historical vibration issues, the criticality of the power line, the cost of
installation and maintenance, and the potential impact on system reliability
and longevity [82].

2.4.1 Technologies
Due to the similarity and advanced TRL of these products, this section will
provide a general description of these technologies separate from the
company profiles below. Stockbridge dampers, spiral dampers, and spacers
all effectively absorb and dissipate vibrational energy. This reduction in
vibrational energy reduces oscillations, protects conductors from fatigue,
minimizes hardware wear, and prevents power outages due to component
failures and strong winds. These devices may enhance transmission-network
reliability and longevity.

Stockbridge Dampers

Figure 2.4. Tuned counterweights of the Stockbridge damper.

Stockbridge dampers reduce aeolian vibration, utilizing counterweights to
dissipate energy. Named after George Stockbridge, who invented them in
1924, they absorb inertial energy via balanced weights tuned to vibrate for
specific higher frequency (5-150Hz for power systems) aeolian vibrations.
These dampers are a type of tuned mass dampers, a general stabilizing
technology which is also used in different forms for earthquake resiliency in

 15

large buildings. The connective material effectively transfers the mechanical
energy from the conductor to the weights and into the surrounding air.
Stockbridge dampers are typically ordered based on conductor size along
with mounting equipment but can be customized based on field vibration
measurements and studies [35].

Spiral Dampers

Figure 2.5. Signature spiral outer layer of the spiral damper.

Spiral dampers, sometimes called helical or aeolian dampers, are used to
mitigate aeolian vibration. These dampers use semi-rigid PVC rods wrapped
in a loose spiral around a conductor, holding the conductor at each end
and dissipating and disrupting standing waves. Offering flexibility and easy
installation by hand, they can be installed anywhere on a cable to avoid
conflicts with corona prevention and cable termination equipment. Spiral
dampers are typically recommended over Stockbridge dampers for smaller
cables less than 0.75 inches in diameter [37].

Spacers

Figure 2.6. A standard conductor spacer.

 16

Spacers do not dissipate vibrational energy, but can be used either between
bundled conductors in the same phase or different phases of conductors to
reduce susceptibility of the more rigid system to wind-induced vibrations. Inter-
phase spacers can also prevent conductor slap or flashover by simply holding
conductors a distance apart – these spacers reach across voltage potential, so
must be durable insulators. Bundle spacers on the same phase may be much
smaller, made of aluminum or hardened plastic, and may be conductive [39].

Stockbridge dampers, spiral dampers, and tuned mass dampers are all TRL 9,
indicating full maturity and successful deployment in operational environments.

Towers and poles are critical components of the power system, providing
the necessary support for overhead conductors to transmit and distribute
electrical energy across long distances. These structures are typically made of
materials—steel, concrete, and wood—chosen for their durability, strength,
and cost-effectiveness. Steel-lattice towers and monopoles are commonly
used in high-voltage transmission lines while wooden and concrete poles are
often used in lower-voltage distribution networks. These structures have been
the backbone of the power T&D infrastructure for several decades.

3.1 Concerns Associated with Traditional Towers and Poles
While traditional towers and poles have proven their general reliability,
they also present several challenges that pose potential hazards, risks, and
inefficiencies in energy T&D. These concerns include:

 • Corrosion/Rusting and Mechanical Fatigue: Steel structures are
 susceptible to corrosion and rusting over time, especially in harsh
 environmental conditions. Combined with corrosion, repeated
 mechanical stress from wind, conductor vibration, and other factors
 can lead to fatigue and eventual failure of towers and poles [19].

 26

 • Wood Decay and Termite Damage: Wooden poles can suffer
 from decay and damage caused by termites and other pests,
 compromising their strength and stability [4].
 • Adverse Weather Events: Towers and poles are exposed to extreme
 weather conditions, including high winds and ice loading, which can
 cause structural damage, bending, or even collapse [19].
 • Wildfires: In fire-prone areas, wooden poles are particularly
 vulnerable to damage or destruction from wildfires, posing risks
 to the stability and continuity of power supply, especially in
 emergencies [50].
 • Aging: Many towers and poles in existing networks have been in
 service for several decades, leading to concerns about aging and
 the need for replacement or reinforcement to ensure continued
 reliability and safety [1].

Presently, there are numerous mechanical solutions that address these tower and
pole concerns with respect to grid hardening and reliability.

5.2 Spacer Bundles
Spacer bundles are devices used in high-voltage power transmission lines
to maintain the separation between conductors in a bundled configuration.
Spacer bundles help reduce electrical losses, minimize conductor movement
caused by wind or other environmental factors, and prevent electrical faults due
to conductor contact by keeping conductors at a consistent distance from each
other. This contributes to the efficiency and reliability of power transmission
systems by ensuring stable delivery of electricity over long distances.

Power T&D is the critical infrastructure that delivers generated power from
energy sources to end consumers. This system consists of high-voltage Conductor
transmission lines that transport electricity over long distances, from power Wraps/Coatings
plants to be stepped down at substations to lower voltages and distributed to
homes, businesses, and industries. As energy generation and load demands
grow, the expansion and modernization of T&D infrastructure are imperative to
maintain grid stability and reliability. Without upgraded capacity, the industry’s
ability to meet rising energy demands is compromised.

However, due to economic challenges and physical constraints, the Pole Wraps/
expansion of T&D has slowed in recent years. According to the ASCE’s 2025 Coatings
“Infrastructure Report Card,” many grid components are well past their life
expectancy, indicating a need for replacement and upgrades [122]. These
aging components can affect grid operators’ ability to provide power,
especially during peak-load situations caused by high temperatures, leading to
transmission congestion or unplanned outages. Extreme weather events, such Tower/Pole
as hurricanes, ice storms, and wildfires, further strain the grid, causing damage Design
to infrastructure and increasing the number of outages each year. Utilities
face significant storm-damage costs and lost revenue due to weather-related
outages, emphasizing the need for a more-resilient power grid to ensure
reliable electricity delivery amidst growing energy demands and challenging
environmental conditions. Adjacent
 Components

ensuring continuous and reliable operation even in the face of severe weather

SECTION 9: Appendix A
Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs)
Identify the readiness level of the technology associated with the project as
well as the planned progression during the course of project execution. A
detailed explanation of the rationale for the estimated technology readiness
level should be provided. Specific entry criteria for the next higher technology
readiness level should be identified. The following definitions apply:

 1. TRL-1. Basic principles observed and reported: Scientific problem or
 phenomenon identified. Essential characteristics and behaviors of systems
 and architectures are identified using mathematical formulations or
 algorithms. The observation of basic scientific principles or phenomena
 has been validated through peer-reviewed research. Technology is ready to
 transition from scientific research to applied research.
 2. TRL-2. Technology concept and/or application formulated: Applied
 research activity. Theory and scientific principles are focused on specific
 application areas to define the concept. Characteristics of the application
 are described. Analytical tools are developed for simulation or analysis of
 the application.
 3. TRL-3. Analytical and experimental critical function and/or characteristic
 proof of concept: Proof of concept validation has been achieved at this
 level. Experimental research and development is initiated with analytical
 and laboratory studies. System/integrated process requirements for the
 overall system application are well known. Demonstration of technical
 feasibility using immature prototype implementations are exercised
 with representative interface inputs to include electrical, mechanical, or
 controlling elements to validate predictions.
 4. TRL-4. Component and/or process validation in laboratory environment-
 Alpha prototype (component): Standalone prototyping implementation
 and testing in laboratory environment demonstrates the concept.
 Integration and testing of component technology elements are sufficient to
 validate feasibility.

5. TRL-5. Component and/or process validation in relevant environment- Beta
 prototype (component): Thorough prototype testing of the component/
 process in relevant environment to the end user is performed. Basic
 technology elements are integrated with reasonably realistic supporting
 elements based on available technologies. Prototyping implementations
 conform to the target environment and interfaces.
6. TRL-6. System/process model or prototype demonstration in a relevant
 environment- Beta prototype (system): Prototyping implementations
 are partially integrated with existing systems. Engineering feasibility
 fully demonstrated in actual or high fidelity system applications in an
 environment relevant to the end user.
7. TRL-7. System/process prototype demonstration in an operational
 environment- Integrated pilot (system): System prototyping demonstration
 in operational environment. System is at or near full scale (pilot or
 engineering scale) of the operational system, with most functions
 available for demonstration and test. The system, component, or process
 is integrated with collateral and ancillary systems in a near production
 quality prototype.
8. TRL-8. Actual system/process completed and qualified through test
 and demonstration- Pre-commercial demonstration: End of system
 development. Full-scale system is fully integrated into operational
 environment with fully operational hardware and software systems.
 All functionality is tested in simulated and operational scenarios with
 demonstrated achievement of end-user specifications. Technology is ready
 to move from development to commercialization.
