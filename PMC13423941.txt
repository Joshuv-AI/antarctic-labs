Normative Reference Values for Vibration, Warm and Cold Perception Thresholds in an Indian Population Using the Vibrasense + T Device
Authors: Srihari Sharma KN, Sunjyoth HS, Bhajish Bharathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic peripheral neuropathy is a major complication of diabetes and a leading cause of foot ulcers and amputations. Vibrasense + T is a handheld quantitative sensory testing device that integrates vibration, warm and cold perception threshold testing into a single probe.PurposeThis study aimed to establish normative reference values for Vibrasense + T device in a healthy adult Indian population.MethodsIn this prospective cross-sectional study, 345 healthy adults aged 18–93 years were recruited. Individuals with diabetes, neuropathy or other conditions affecting sensory function were excluded. Vibration perception threshold (VPT), warm perception threshold (WPT) and cold perception threshold (CPT) were assessed bilaterally at standard hand and foot sites using the method of limits. Normative reference values for VPT, WPT and CPT were derived using non-parametric percentile distributions, and the influence of age, sex and anthropometric variables on sensory thresholds was evaluated.ResultsAll participants completed testing without adverse events. Sensory thresholds were consistently higher at the foot compared to the hand across all modalities (p < .001). Age demonstrated a moderate association with VPT and a weaker but significant association with WPT, with thresholds increasing progressively across age groups (all p < .001). In contrast, CPT showed minimal and inconsistent age-related changes. Females exhibited greater sensitivity than males across most modalities. Side-to-side differences were small and not clinically meaningful. Height, weight and BMI did not meaningfully influence sensory thresholds.ConclusionThis study establishes normative reference values for vibration, warm and cold perception thresholds using the Vibrasense + T device in an Indian population. These age- and sex-stratified values can assist interpretation of sensory thresholds measured with this device in clinical and research settings.

Introduction

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is among the most common and debilitating complications of diabetes, contributing significantly to morbidity and reduced quality of life. 1 It is also a major risk factor for diabetic foot ulcers and lower-limb amputations, making early detection and management crucial.2, 3 The burden of DPN is considerable, with prevalence estimates of approximately 30% among individuals with diabetes. 4 Current guidelines from the American Diabetes Association recommend screening for diabetic neuropathy at the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis and 5 years after type 1 diabetes diagnosis, followed by annual screening thereafter. 5

Quantitative sensory testing (QST) is a psychophysical method that evaluates sensory nerve function through the standardised application of thermal and mechanical stimuli.6, 7 Various sensory modalities, including vibration, warmth, cold, pressure and pain, can be assessed using standardised quantitative methods, enabling evaluation of both peripheral and central nervous system function.6, 7 QST provides valuable insights into the presence or absence of specific sensations, as well as their detection thresholds. 8 It is particularly useful for longitudinal monitoring and comparative assessment in conditions such as diabetic neuropathy, small fibre neuropathy and central pain syndromes.8, 9

Conventional QST methods require specialised equipment and standardised protocols.6, 7 Given the need for rapid and practical screening approaches in routine care, simpler bedside tools for quantitative sensory assessment are desirable.8, 9 Vibrasense + T (Ayati Devices Pvt. Ltd., India) is a portable, handheld QST device designed to assist in neuropathy evaluation. It integrates vibration, warm and cold perception threshold testing into a single compact (1.2 cm diameter) probe, enabling multi-modality evaluation within a unified device. The device is battery-powered and designed for use in point-of-care settings.

QST values vary with anatomical site and individual factors such as age and sex, and are influenced by methodological factors; therefore, device-specific normative reference values are required for clinical interpretation.10, 11 This study aimed to establish normative reference values for vibration, warm and cold perception thresholds across age groups and sexes at standard hand and foot sites using the Vibrasense + T device. These data will provide a reference framework for defining impaired sensation and support its use in neuropathy assessment.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the College of Physiotherapy, Dayananda Sagar University, Karnataka, between September 2024 and January 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the College of Physiotherapy, Dayananda Sagar University, and all procedures adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The trial was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/09/073936). Volunteer recruitment was carried out through health screening camps and outreach programmes. All participants provided written informed consent. Screening included demographic details, handedness/footedness and structured questions regarding neurological or vascular symptoms.

Healthy adults aged ≥18 years were eligible, irrespective of gender. Exclusion criteria included a history of diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or sensory symptoms (e.g., numbness, dysesthesia, hyperesthesia and altered thermal perception). Individuals with a history of conditions known to cause neuropathy (e.g., vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, renal disease and malignancy) were excluded. Participants on neurotoxic drugs or medications affecting sensation/alertness, as well as those with spinal injuries, sensory disorders, active ulcers or deformities at test sites, were also excluded.

Study Procedure

Physical examination included measurements of height and weight and basic neurological assessments. Eligible participants underwent testing of vibration perception thresholds (VPT), warm perception thresholds (WPT) and cold perception thresholds (CPT) using the Vibrasense + T device. Testing was conducted in a quiet, air-conditioned room maintained at 20 °C–24 °C, with 10 min of acclimatisation prior to assessment. The method of limits was used for all sensory assessments, and the reported thresholds include the participant’s reaction time. The sequence of test administration was vibration, followed by warm and cold perception testing. The sensory testing procedure required approximately 10 min per participant.

Vibration Perception Threshold

VPT was assessed at the pulp of the index finger of the hand and the dorsal aspect of the distal interphalangeal joint of the great toe of the foot. An ascending method of limits was used, with the stimulus initiated at 1 V and increased at a rate of 1 V/s up to a maximum of 25 V. Participants verbally indicated the first perception of vibration, and the corresponding stimulus intensity was recorded as the VPT.

Warm Perception Threshold

WPT was assessed at the thenar eminence and the dorsolateral aspect of the foot using the ascending method of limits. The probe temperature was initialised at 32 °C and increased at a rate of 1 °C per second until the subject first perceived warmth. The maximum stimulus temperatures were preset in the device at 50 °C, serving as automated safety cut-offs.

Cold Perception Threshold

CPT was assessed at the thenar eminence and dorsolateral foot using the descending method of limits. The probe temperature was initialised at 32 °C and decreased at a rate of 1 °C per second until the participant indicated the first perception of cold. The minimum stimulus temperatures were preset in the device at 15 °C.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was determined a priori to ensure adequate age-category-specific estimation of thermal thresholds, using published normative data from Yarnitsky and Ochoa. 12 The calculation was based on the parameter exhibiting the greatest variability: foot warm detection threshold measured by the method of limits. From the reported mean and upper normative limit for this parameter, the standard deviation was estimated at approximately 2.85 °C. Assuming a clinically meaningful difference of 1 °C, a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 62–64 participants per age category, corresponding to a target number of 186–192 across three age categories. The final study cohort exceeded this requirement, enrolling 345 participants overall.

All participants who completed the testing procedures were included in the final analysis. Side-to-side differences were evaluated before pooling bilateral data. No significant side-to-side differences were observed for warm or cold perception thresholds. A modest but statistically significant side-to-side difference was observed only for VPTs, with slightly higher values on the right side. However, the absolute magnitude of these differences was small, with mean side-to-side differences of 0.36 V at the hand and 0.31 V at the foot, both below the 1 V step size of the device. Therefore, data from both sides were pooled for subsequent analyses to enhance statistical robustness and simplify interpretation.

Normative values were therefore presented for the hand and foot, based on pooled data from both sides, and further stratified by gender (male and female) and age group (18–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years). As the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated non-normal distributions, non-parametric methods were employed. Descriptive statistics were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The outer reference limit was defined as the 97.5th percentile for VPT and WPT and the 2.5th percentile for CPT, reflecting the direction of abnormal sensory thresholds for each modality.

Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons across age groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A p value < .05 was considered statistically significant; for pairwise comparisons, Bonferroni-adjusted p values were used. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.

Effect of Age on Sensory Thresholds

Sensory measures were compared across three (18–39, 40–59 and ≥60 years) age groups (Figure 1). VPT at both the hand and foot showed a clear increase across age groups (both hand and foot p < .001). Significant differences were observed in all pairwise group comparisons at the hand and foot (adjusted p < .001). These findings indicate a consistent stepwise increase in VPT across the three age groups at both sites.

Sensory Thresholds Across Three Age Groups at the Hand and Foot.

WPTs increased with age at both the hand (p < .001) and foot (p = .001). At the hand, thresholds rose early, with the 40–59 group already showing significantly higher values than the 18–39 group (p < .001), and remained similarly elevated in the ≥60 group (no difference between 40 and 59 vs ≥60, p = .521). In contrast, at the foot, thresholds did not differ between adjacent age groups, and a significant difference was observed only between the youngest and oldest groups (18–39 vs ≥60, p < .001), indicating that changes become apparent mainly at older ages.

CPTs showed a modest difference across age groups at the hand (p = .012), primarily driven by a significant difference between the ≥60 and 18–39 groups (p = .002), while other pairwise comparisons were not significant after Bonferroni correction (40–59 vs ≥60: p = .041; 18–39 vs 40–59: p = .616). At the foot, no significant differences were observed across the age groups (p = .125).

Consistent with these findings, Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated a moderate positive association between age and VPTs at both the hand (ρ = 0.43, p < .001) and foot (ρ = 0.50, p < .001). WPTs showed weaker but statistically significant correlations with age at both sites (hand: ρ = 0.24, p < .001; foot: ρ = 0.19, p < .001). In contrast, CPTs did not show a significant correlation with age at either the hand (ρ = − 0.07, p = .090) or foot (ρ = −0.07, p = .077).

Linear regression analysis demonstrated that age was a significant predictor of VPTs, explaining 16%–24% of the variance, with a stronger effect at the foot (R² = 0.24, p < .001) than at the hand (R² = 0.16, p < .001). For WPTs, age explained only 4%–5% of the variance (hand: R² = 0.05, foot: R² = 0.04, both p < .001). In contrast, CPTs showed minimal to no meaningful association with age, with variance explained below 1% (hand: R² = 0.005, p = .061; foot: R² = 0.008, p = .018). Overall, these findings indicate a progressive decline in vibration and warm sensitivity with age, whereas cold perception remains more variable and less strongly associated with ageing.

Effect of Gender on Sensory Thresholds

Females demonstrated greater sensory sensitivity than males, with consistently lower thresholds across most modalities (Table 4). Vibration thresholds were lower in females at both the hand and the foot. Warm thresholds showed no sex difference at the hand but were lower in females at the foot. CPTs were lower in females at both sites, indicating a consistent sex-related difference in thermal sensitivity.

In side-to-side analyses, VPTs were modestly higher on the right than the left at both the hand (mean difference = 0.36, p < .001) and foot (mean difference = 0.31, p = .030). In contrast, no significant right-to-left differences were observed for warm or cold perception thresholds at either site.

Effect of Anthropometric Variables on Sensory Thresholds

Anthropometric variables showed only weak associations with sensory thresholds. Height demonstrated weak correlations with thermal thresholds at the foot (WPT: ρ = 0.13, p = .001; CPT: ρ = −0.11, p = .004). Weight showed similarly weak correlations with WPTs at both the hand and foot (both ρ = 0.14, p < .001) and with cold perception at the foot (ρ = −0.10, p = .006). No meaningful associations were observed with BMI.

Discussion

This study established normative reference values for vibration, warm and cold perception thresholds using the Vibrasense + T device in an Indian population. The RLs across all age groups remained within the device’s measurement and safety range, supporting its suitability for clinical assessment of sensory thresholds.

The thermal threshold ranges observed in the present study were wider than those reported with larger surface area thermodes. At the thenar eminence, warm and cold thresholds extended up to +8 °C and −10 °C from baseline, respectively, while at the foot they extended up to +11 °C and −12 °C. In comparison, an Indian study using the Medoc TSA-II with a 9 cm² probe reported narrower ranges of approximately +1.4 °C to +2.7 °C for warm and −1.7 °C to −2.3 °C for cold thresholds at the thenar eminence, and +3.9 °C to +8.6 °C for warm and −3.5 °C to −6.0 °C for cold thresholds at the dorsolateral foot. 10 A Swedish study using a 12.5 cm² thermode similarly reported narrower thermal threshold ranges. 11

A key methodological factor underlying these differences is the smaller probe surface area of the Vibrasense + T device (1.13 cm²), compared to the 9–16 cm² thermodes used in other devices. This reduced contact area likely contributes to higher and more variable detection thresholds, as smaller stimulus areas require greater deviation from baseline for perception. This is consistent with findings by Dyck et al., who demonstrated lower detection thresholds with larger (10 cm²) thermodes compared to smaller (2.7 cm²) ones. 13

The age-stratified vibration RL generated in this study may facilitate more context-specific clinical interpretation than the use of a single fixed threshold. This is particularly relevant because vibration perception values increase with age, and the overall foot reference limit in the present study (18 V) was slightly higher than the 15 V threshold previously used for neuropathy detection with the predecessor Vibrasense device. 14

The observed site-dependent pattern, with greater sensitivity at the hand than the foot, is in line with previous thermal QST studies demonstrating lower thresholds at upper-limb/proximal sites and higher thresholds at lower-limb/distal sites.10, 11 This pattern likely reflects underlying neurophysiological factors, including longer conduction pathways to distal sites and the inherently slower conduction velocities of Aδ and C fibres involved in thermal perception, contributing to delayed sensory processing for distal sites.12, 15

Side-to-side differences were small and not clinically meaningful, supporting the pooling of bilateral data for the generation of reference values. This is consistent with previous reports indicating largely symmetric thermal thresholds and minimal or non-significant lateral asymmetry in VPTs.7, 10, 16–18

Advancing age was associated with higher sensory thresholds, most prominently for vibration perception and less consistently for thermal modalities. This pattern is in agreement with prior studies demonstrating greater age-related sensory decline at distal compared with proximal regions and evidence of length-dependent peripheral nerve ageing.19, 20 The stronger age effect on vibration perception may reflect age-related changes in the somatosensory system, including preferential loss of distal large myelinated sensory fibres and vibration-sensitive mechanoreceptors such as Pacinian corpuscles.21, 22 The more modest and variable age-related changes in thermal thresholds are also in keeping with previous thermal QST studies.10, 11, 23

The observed sex-related differences are broadly consistent with prior QST studies reporting greater sensory sensitivity in females, particularly for thermal detection.10, 18, 23 The underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, although differences in central sensory processing, hormonal influences and higher epidermal nerve fibre density in women have been proposed as possible explanations.24, 25

Anthropometric variables showed limited clinical relevance for interpretation of sensory thresholds in this cohort, broadly similar to previous QST studies reporting limited or inconsistent associations between anthropometric factors and sensory thresholds.11, 17 Overall, the findings suggest that age- and sex-stratified reference values are more clinically relevant than anthropometry-based adjustments.

The strength of this study is the relatively large sample size and age- and sex-stratified analysis across both hand and foot sites. Several limitations merit consideration. Participants were recruited as apparently healthy volunteers from health screening camps and underwent structured eligibility screening before inclusion. However, screening was based on medical history and limited clinical assessment, and subclinical or unreported conditions that could influence sensory thresholds may not have been completely excluded. Sensory data were collected from only two sites in the hand and foot, excluding more proximal regions that may have provided additional insights into sensory gradients. The thresholds reported in this study were obtained using the method of limits and are therefore reaction-time inclusive, which is particularly relevant in older participants. Had reaction-time-independent algorithms, such as the method of levels, been included, they might have helped better distinguish sensory decline from delayed reaction. Formal reliability testing of the Vibrasense + T device, including test–retest, intra-rater and inter-rater reliability, was not performed in this study, as each participant underwent a single testing session. Future studies should specifically evaluate the reliability and repeatability of the device across repeated measurements and different examiners. Finally, this was a single-centre study, and multicentric studies across diverse regions of India would improve the generalisability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study establishes normative reference values for vibration, warm, and cold perception thresholds using the Vibrasense + T device in an Indian population. These values provide a framework for objective assessment of peripheral sensory function, particularly in conditions such as diabetes where early detection of neuropathy is critical. Future studies should evaluate the diagnostic performance of these thresholds in clinical populations and explore their role in risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring.
