Ad5/52s fiber substitution alters Ad5 tropism, bioavailability, and plasma sensitivity
Authors: Marc Garcia-Moure, Esther Rodríguez, Francisco M Barriga, Guillem Chillón, Javier del Rey, Eduard Batlle, Assumpció Bosch, Miguel Chillón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdenovirus type 5 (Ad5) is a well-established platform for gene transfer but relies on the coxsackie and adenovirus receptor (CAR), limiting efficacy in CAR-deficient tissues and influencing biodistribution through interactions with blood cells. Human adenovirus 52 (Ad52) encodes two fibers: a long CAR-binding fiber (F52L) and a short fiber (F52s) that engages sialylated glycoconjugates. Here, we engineered a chimeric Ad5/52s vector by replacing the Ad5 fiber with F52s. Ad5/52s production was initially inefficient due to delayed replication, but optimization of harvest timing markedly improved infectious titers and particle quality. In vitro, Ad5/52s efficiently transduced different Schwann cell lines and other Ad5-refractory lines. A CAR-independent tropism was demonstrated for Ad5/52s, along with the ability to transduce all tested CAR-positive cell lines. In vivo, systemic delivery revealed strikingly reduced liver tropism and rapid loss of infectivity in circulation, despite comparable genome persistence. Fractionation experiments implicated plasma factors as mediators of Ad5/52s inactivation. Bioinformatic analysis identified a putative thrombin-sensitive cleavage motif within F52s, and mutation (R67N) conferred enhanced viral stability in blood. The Ad5/52s platform highlights both opportunities for targeted local applications and the potential to rationally modulate systemic safety profiles.Keywords: adenovirus, Ad52, fiber protein, bioavailability, biodistribution

Introduction

More than 100 human adenovirus types are currently recognized and grouped into seven species (A–G).1 Among them, adenovirus C type 5 (Ad5) is one of the most widely used and best characterized viral platforms for gene delivery because of robust yields in producer cells, efficient infection of dividing and non-dividing targets, and an overall favorable safety record.2 Adenoviruses mainly recognize their target cell through the contact between the fiber protein and a specific viral receptor on the outer cell surface,3 followed by internalization driven by penton base-integrin interactions mediated through the Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) motif.4,5,6 Consequently, receptor availability and distribution critically determine cell and tissue permissivity. The Ad5 fiber recognizes the coxsackie and adenovirus receptor (CAR),7,8,9,10 which acts also as a fiber receptor for certain human adenovirus types from the species A, C, D, E, F, and G,11,12 as well as for non-human adenoviruses, such as canine adenovirus type 2 (CAV-2)13 and fowl adenovirus 1(CELO).14 However, CAR is absent or downregulated in multiple clinically relevant contexts, including subsets of tumors.15,16,17 Moreover, the presence of CAR on the surface of human platelets and erythrocytes also impacts on the biodistribution of Ad5 capsids.18,19,20,21 Together, these factors motivate the development of vectors whose cellular entry is not constrained by CAR.

Human adenovirus 52 (Ad52), the sole member of species G, was isolated from patients with gastroenteritis and is considered an enteric adenovirus alongside Ad40 and Ad41 of species F.22 Like Ad40/41, Ad52 encodes two fibers of different lengths. The long fiber (F52L) can use CAR, but it has been reported that the short fiber (F52s) mediates entry into CAR-negative cells by binding sialylated glycoconjugates on the cell surface.11,23 This dual receptor repertoire suggests a straightforward strategy to expand or redirect Ad5 tropism: introducing the Ad52 short fiber into an Ad5 capsid could enable CAR-independent attachment and entry while preserving the manufacturing familiarity and genomic architecture of Ad5.

Results

Optimization of the production protocol

The Ad5/52s genome was generated through a four-step cloning strategy as described in the materials and methods section. Briefly, first generation Ad5/52s vectors (so carrying the F52s fiber) were constructed (Figure 1A), and later produced in HEK293 cells, harvested 40 h post-infection, and purified by CsCl gradient centrifugation. Compared with conventional Ad5 preparations, both yield, and infectious titers (IUs) versus physical particles (PPs) or IU/PP ratio of Ad5/52 vectors were suboptimal. Since fiber replacement is known to delay adenoviral propagation,24 we hypothesized that premature harvesting accounted for the poor productivity. To test this, Ad5/52s-GFP was used to infect HEK293 cells at MOI 1, and cultures were harvested at sequential time points. Infectious virus was negligible at 15–22 h, increased markedly by 40 h, and peaked between 44 and 48 h (Figure 1B). Thus, fiber substitution extends the Ad5 replication cycle from ∼36 to ∼44 h.

When harvested at 44 h, yields improved substantially. Across six independent productions, Ad5/52s titers increased from 50 to 570 IU/cell (11.5-fold), while at the same time the IU/PP ratio improved from 1:73 to 1:21 (Table 1). Productivity in terms of total particles rose more modestly (2.8-fold). These findings demonstrate that the earlier poor yield reflected premature collection before virion maturation, rather than intrinsic defects in genome replication or assembly.

The F52s protein conferred a new tropism on the Ad5 capsid

Fiber proteins mediate initial viral binding to cell-surface receptors. Replacing the Ad5 fiber with F52s was expected to alter tropism. To investigate, a panel of cell lines from different tissues and species was used: S16 and RT4-D6P2T (rat Schwann cells); C2C12, (murine myoblasts); HeLa (human adenocarcinoma); A549 (human epithelial lung cells); DKZeo (dog kidney cells); Jurkat (human T lymphoblasts); PC12 (rat adrenal gland cells); Caco2, (human epithelial colon cells). Cell lines were infected with Ad5/52s-GFP or Ad5-GFP at increasing doses (10, 100 or 1,000 PP/cell), and GFP expression was quantified by microscopy and flow cytometry (Figures 2A and 2B).

Cell lines were segregated into three groups. S16, RT4-D6P2T, and C2C12 were efficiently transduced by Ad5/52s, reaching 59%–87% infection at 1,000 PP/cell but showed minimal susceptibility to Ad5. A549 and HeLa cells were equivalently transduced by both vectors, with >80% infection at the highest dose. Conversely, DKZeo, Jurkat, and PC12 cells were far more permissive to Ad5 than Ad5/52s, with Ad5 achieving 32%–72% infection versus <10% for the chimera.

It must be considered that most of these cultures are immortalized stable cell lines with undifferentiated features. Therefore, the expression levels of viral receptors might be biased by culture conditions and differ substantially from those observed in their tissue counterpart. For this reason, S16 cells were culture for three days in starvation conditions (normoglycemic serum free medium), which has been reported to induce differentiation of these cells into a Schwann cell-like phenotype.25 Then, the cells were infected with both viruses at 10, 100 or 1000 PP/cell and 48 h later the infectivity was observed by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 2C). Differentiated S16 remained refractory to Ad5 even at 1,000 PP/cell, while Ad5/52s mediated detectable transduction at 10 PP/cell and nearly complete infection at 100–1,000 PP/cell (Figure 2C). Primary mouse Schwann cells obtained from mice sciatic nerve and cultured in vitro until reaching 70% of confluence were similarly tested: Ad5 yielded negligible GFP expression up to 500 PP/cell, whereas Ad5/52s mediated efficient transduction at 50 PP/cell and complete saturation at 500 PP/cell (Figure 2D). Altogether, these results suggest that F52s may confer Schwann cell tropism.

Because Ad52 was originally isolated from human stool samples, an enteric preference was hypothesized. Colon-derived cell lines (Caco-2, SW1116, and LoVo) and RAW264.7 macrophages were infected at 500 PP/cell and analyzed 48 h later. Ad5 transduced epithelial lines more efficiently (75%–92%) than Ad5/52s (35%–59%) (Figure 3A).

The same experiment was done with the murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7, since it has been reported that intestinal macrophages also mediate in the enteric tropism of chimeric viruses carrying the short fiber of the naturally intestinal adenovirus Ad40.24 Of note, RAW264.7 macrophages were more permissive to Ad5/52s (16%) than Ad5 (2%). To assess receptor accessibility in polarized tissues, murine colon organoids generated from intestinal stem cells obtained from Rosa26 transgenic mice (expressing GFP upon recombination mediated by Cre recombinase) were infected with Cre-expressing Ad5 or Ad5/52s, at an estimated dose of 200 PP/cell and reporter activation was evaluated 72 h post-infection. Both vectors infected organoids comparably (Figure 3B). Collectively, fiber replacement did not confer authentic enteric tropism.

Fiber substitution reduces in vivo hepatic transduction of Ad5

We next analyzed in vivo distribution following intravenous administration. Mice received Ad5-or Ad5/52s-lacZ (5 × 10ˆ10 PP/animal). Three days later, β-galactosidase activity was quantified in multiple tissues. As expected, Ad5 preferentially transduced the liver (22.4 ng/mg), with modest activity (about 8 times less) in heart and pancreas. In contrast, Ad5/52s failed to produce efficient expression in any organ compared with PBS controls (Figure 4A). Notably, β-galactosidase requires tetramerization for full enzymatic activity, so low-level expression may yield disproportionately little detectable signal (i.e., a non-linear readout), which could contribute to the minimal activity observed for Ad5/52s throughout. Thus, loss of hepatic sequestration was not accompanied by redirection to other tissues.

To exclude intrinsic infectivity defects, both vectors were delivered intramuscularly (1 × 10ˆ10 PP/animal). Reporter expression was observed in tibialis muscle (54.8 ng/mg for Ad5 vs. 30.2 ng/mg for Ad5/52s; difference not significant), confirming that Ad5/52s virions remain competent in vivo (Figure 4B). Similarly, intranasal delivery (3 × 10ˆ10 PP/animal) also produced robust lung expression (4.0 ng/mg for Ad5; 1.8 ng/mg for Ad5/52s), but no liver signal (Figure 4C). These data indicate that the systemic defect reflects blood-specific inactivation rather than loss of general infectivity.

Ad5/52s exhibits reduced viability in circulation

To evaluate stability in blood, Ad5-RFP and Ad5/52s-GFP were co-injected intravenously (5 × 10ˆ10 PP of each vector/animal), and viral genomes were quantified 5, 20, and 60 min after the injection. Both vectors were rapidly cleared, with ∼15%–27% genomes still detectable at 5 min and progressively declining at later time points. Genome clearance rates were indistinguishable (Figure 5A).

Clearance of Ad5/52s from blood

(A) Viral genomes (VG) and (B) infectious titers of Ad5-RFP and Ad5/52s-GFP present in blood samples collected 5, 20, and 60 min after intravenous administration of the viruses (5 × 10ˆ10 PP/animal) (mean ± SD) (n = 8 per group). p values are indicated as p < 0.01∗∗ or not significant (ns).

However, titration of infectious units revealed striking differences. Within 5 min, Ad5 retained 1.9% infectivity, whereas Ad5/52s dropped to 0.08% (24-fold faster decay). At 20–60 min, infectivity was equally diminished for both (Figure 5B). Hence, Ad5/52s fails not due to faster clearance but because its particles lose infectivity in circulation.

Plasma factors mediate Ad5/52s inactivation

To determine whether viral loss of function occurred in plasma or cells, blood was fractionated 5 min after intravenous co-injection of Ad5-RFP and Ad5/52s-GFP (5 × 10ˆ10 PP of each vector per animal). IUs revealed that compared with Ad5, Ad5/52s was disproportionately inactivated, in both plasma (5.8% vs. 0.4%) and white blood cells (1.6% vs. 0.07%) (Figure 6A), despite similar recovery of viral genomes across fractions (Figure 6B). Since plasma contained the vast majority of viral genomes, these findings implicate soluble factors as the primary mediators of Ad5/52s inactivation.

However, ex vivo co-incubation of both vectors with isolated plasma, erythrocytes (RBCs), or white blood cells (WBCs) did not reproduce the selective loss of Ad5/52s infectivity (Figure 6C). This discrepancy suggests that coagulation factors—active in vivo but inactivated during ex vivo processing with anticoagulants—may account for the observed inactivation.

Mutation of a putative thrombin cleavage site in F52s enhances Ad5/52s stability in blood

One difference between the in vivo and ex vivo experiments is the exposure of the viruses to clotting factors in blood in vivo that will be inactive ex vivo since blood was treated with sodium citrate as an anticoagulation agent to prevent clotting of the blood before its fractionation. The formation of blood clots is mediated by the activation of the coagulation cascade, which consists of a sequential activation or blood factors, proteases being the most preeminent. Using proteolytic sites prediction software (Peptide Cutter; Expasy.org), a putative thrombin cleavage motif (GRG, aa66–68) was predicted within the F52s fiber shaft (Figure S1). Proteolytic cleavage here would detach the knob, compromising receptor binding and virion stability. To test this, a mutant vector (AdF52sR67N) was generated by replacing GRG with the native Ad5 GNG sequence. Comparative in vitro studies using different cell lines showed that Ad5/52s and AdF52sR67N have similar relative infectivity profiles across all the cell lines tested (Figure S2), even though the IU/PP ratio of the AdF52sR67N vector batch was approximately 4-fold lower than that of Ad5/52s in HEK293 cells (Table 1). The same vector batches were subsequently used in the in vivo experiment; therefore, this approximately 4-fold difference in infectivity was also reflected in the titers measured at the pre-administration (0 min) time point (). Following intravenous co-administration of either AdF52sR67N-GFP or Ad5/52s-GFP (5 × 10ˆ10 PP/animal) together with Ad5-RFP (as an internal control), the 5-min titers were 0.5% for Ad5, 0.02% for Ad5/52s, and 0.16% for AdF52sR67N-GFP (Figure 7A). In contrast, the clearance of physical particles expressed as a percentage of the initial virions administered, was similar among the three vectors (Figure 7B). Thus, despite the approximately 4-fold lower infectivity of the AdF52sR67N batch at baseline, this mutant displayed an approximately 8-fold higher IU than Ad5/52s at 5 min, indicating significantly greater stability in circulation and approaching the levels observed for Ad5. Since physical particle clearance was comparable among the vectors, the differences in IUs cannot be attributed to differential removal of virions from the circulation. Collectively, these findings suggest that thrombin-mediated cleavage may contribute to the inactivation of Ad5/52s; however, further mechanistic studies are required to demonstrate direct processing.

Discussion

Ad52 is an enteric virus that, like intestinal adenoviruses Ad40 and Ad41, encodes two distinct fibers of different lengths.22 The long fiber binds the canonical adenoviral receptor CAR, whereas F52s engages sialic acid moieties on glycoproteins.11,23 In this study, we engineered a chimeric vector, Ad5/52s, based on Ad5 but pseudotyped with the Ad52 short fiber. This substitution replaces CAR-dependent entry with sialic acid-mediated interactions, thereby redirecting viral tropism while retaining the safety profile of Ad5 capsids.

Initial Ad5/52s production was inefficient, with low IUs and a high fraction of immature capsids. Analysis of the replication cycle revealed that fiber substitution delayed viral maturation to ∼44 h, compared with ∼36 h for Ad5. Such cycle alterations have also been reported for other chimeric adenoviruses24,26,27 and are likely driven by altered receptor usage and downstream trafficking. Adjusting the harvest to 44 h increased infectious yields more than 11-fold and reduced non-infectious particles over 3-fold, demonstrating that the earlier low productivity reflected premature collection.

Tropism analysis confirmed that fiber substitution reshaped infection profiles. CAR-positive cell lines (DKZeo, Jurkat, and PC12) were efficiently infected by Ad5 but poorly transduced by Ad5/52s, consistent with the loss of CAR recognition.28,29,30 In contrast, cell lines refractory to Ad5 (S16, RT4-D6P2T, and C2C12) were permissive to Ad5/52s, a trend reproduced in differentiated S16 and primary Schwann cell cultures. A549 and HeLa cells, which express both CAR and sialylated receptors11,12,31,32 were efficiently transduced by both vectors. Unexpectedly, even though Ad52 has been described as a gastrointestinal virus, Ad5/52s did not show enhanced enteric tropism in colon-derived lines or organoids. This may reflect the non-polarized nature of stable colon cancer lines (Caco-2, SW1116, and LoVo), which display receptor distribution patterns unlike polarized intestinal epithelium.33 Since sialic acid residues localize preferentially to apical membranas,34 polarized epithelial models may better reveal potential enteric advantages. Notably, Ad5/52s, like Ad5/40s,24 transduced RAW264.7 macrophages more efficiently than Ad5, indicating possible engagement of resident intestinal immune cells.

Following intravenous delivery, Ad5 preferentially targeted the liver, consistent with previous studies.35,36,37 Ad5/52s, however, showed almost no hepatic infection and did not redirect to other tissues. Similar findings have been reported for other short-fiber-pseudotyped vectors.38,39,40,41,42 Importantly, Ad5/52s remained functional after local intramuscular or intranasal administration, ruling out intrinsic defects. Circulating genome levels of both vectors declined at similar rates, but Ad5/52s IUs dropped more rapidly, pointing to loss of infectivity rather than enhanced clearance. Fractionation experiments implicated plasma factors as key mediators of inactivation, though this effect was not reproduced ex vivo under anticoagulated conditions.

These results align with literature indicating that blood components—particularly coagulation factors and complement—govern adenoviral stability, shielding, and tissue access.43,44,45,46 The observed pattern of stable genome persistence with rapid loss of infectivity is consistent with coagulation-linked inactivation. The absence of an ex vivo effect is explained by citrate anticoagulation, which suppresses coagulation cascades and underestimates in vivo phenomena.

Sequence analysis identified a putative thrombin cleavage motif (GRG) within the F52s shaft domain. Cleavage at this site would be predicted to shed the fiber knob, disrupt trimerization, and destabilize virions.47,48 Supporting this model, mutation of the GRG site (R67N) improved Ad5/52s stability in blood, yielding IUs closer to those of Ad5.

Given the widespread use of adenoviruses as vaccines and gene therapy vectors, their interactions with blood are critical for biodistribution, efficacy, and safety. The binding of Ad5 hexon to factor X, which shields capsids from innate immunity, is a well-established example.46 Our findings support the concept that plasma-derived factors can selectively reduce vector infectivity without eliminating genomes, and suggest that capsid motifs may contribute to modulating this balance. Although thrombin is a plausible mediator, direct biochemical evidence of cleavage remains to be demonstrated.

Coagulation-sensitive sites within adenoviral fibers may act as natural “safety-by-design” elements, restricting systemic spread after intravenous delivery not only of natural virus but also of recombinant viral vectors as it seems for Ad5/52s vectors. This property could minimize off-target transduction and adverse effects, while rational engineering of these motifs could allow systemic biodistribution when desired, which is of evident interest in intravenous administration of gene therapy strategies for human diseases.

In summary, we developed a novel Ad5/52s chimeric vector that combines the Ad5 backbone with the short fiber of Ad52. Optimizing production improved particle maturation, the vector acquired tropism for Schwann cell lines with reduced CAR dependence, and intravenous delivery revealed genome persistence but rapid loss of infectivity consistent with plasma-mediated inactivation. These insights advance the rational design of adenoviral vectors for tailored local or systemic applications and highlight opportunities to exploit natural safety features for clinical benefit.

Materials and methods

Generation of the Ad5/52s vectors

The Ad5/52s genome was generated through a four-step cloning strategy. Two intermediate plasmids (p8900.F5 and pK18) were first derived from pKP1.4, which encodes the E1-deleted Ad5 genome.49 Digestion of pKP1.4 with SpeI or SnaBI followed by religation yielded plasmids p8900.F5 and pK18, respectively. The Ad52 short fiber gene (GenBank: DQ923122.2) was synthesized and cloned into the fiber locus of p8900.F5 to generate p8900.F52s shuttle plasmid. The pK18.F52s was obtained by homologous recombination in chemically competent bacteria (BJ5183 strain) between p8900.F52s and pK18. Finally, the F52s gene was introduced into the Ad5 genome by homologous recombination between pK18.F52s and pKP1.4 plasmids to obtain the pMG plasmid containing the Ad5/52s genome. The construct was verified by PCR and western blot (Figure 1A). The F52sR67N fiber variant (amino-acid substitution R67N; AGA→AAC) was synthesized and cloned into the fiber locus of p8900.F52s. pMG.F52sR67N, containing the AdF52sR67N genome was obtained by the same homologous recombination strategy explained above.

Shuttle plasmids pTG6600-CMV-GFP, pTG6600-CMV-LacZ and pTG6600-CMV-Cre were digested with FspI and BstEII. The restriction fragments containing the reporter genes were cloned into the plasmids containing Ad5 (pKP1.4), Ad5/52s (pMG), and AdF52sR67N (pMG.F52sR67N) genomes, previously linearized with SwaI.

Production of adenoviral capsids

Plasmids containing adenoviral genomes were linearized with PacI. Linear DNA (6 μg) was transfected into HEK293 cells (1 × 10ˆ6 cells per well in a 6-well plate). Three days later, cultures were harvested, viruses were amplified in HEK293 cells, and purified by double cesium chloride (CsCl) gradient ultracentrifugation.50 IUs were determined by serial dilution on HEK293 cells, and physical particle counts were determined by absorbance at 260 nm.51

Incorporation of the chimeric fiber was confirmed by PCR using primers flanking the fiber gene (Fwd primer5′-TTATTCAGCAGCACCTCCTTG-3′, Rv primer5′-ATAAACACGTTGAAACATAACAC-3′), and by western blot using the anti-adenovirus fiber (monomer/trimer) antibody clone 4D2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). This antibody recognizes the epitope MKRARPSEDTFNPVYPY conserved within the tail domain of all HAdV.11

TheAd5/52 vectors were amplified in 4 × 10ˆ8 HEK293 cells, harvested 40 h post-infection, and purified by CsCl gradient centrifugation. A single band was observed, and harvested. IUs were determined by using an anti-hexon antibody based-system as described.50 Briefly, serial dilutions were prepared in infection medium, and 100 μL of each dilution was added to HEK293 cells seeded in 96-well plates. After 48 h at 37°C, transduced cells were identified by immunocytochemistry and counted, and IU/mL calculated based on the initial virus volume and dilution factors. Immunocytochemistry was performed using the AdEasy Viral Titer kit (Agilent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To determine the concentration of viral PPs, 5–10 μL of purified virus were DNAse treated. Later, viral capsids were added to TE buffer containing 0.1% SDS and denatured by incubation at 56°C for 10 min, after which absorbance was measured at 260 nm. Viral particle concentration was then calculated using the formula (PP/mL = Abs260 × 1.1 × 10ˆ12) described by Maizel and collaborators,51 with the final value corrected according to the sample volume used.

Cell line cultures

Cell lines HEK293 (ATCC, CRL-1573), RT4-D6P2T (ATCC, CRL-2768), S16 (kindly provided by R. Quarles52), C2C12 (ATCC, CRL-1772), DKZeo (kindly provided by E. Kremer53), A549(ATCC, CCL-185), and HeLa (ATCC, CCL-2), were cultured in DMEM (Lonza) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Biowest), except for A549, which was maintained in 5% FBS. DKZeo cultures were additionally supplemented with 1% non-essential amino acids (PAA). Jurkat cells (ATCC, TIB-152) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Lonza) containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 4 mM L-glutamine (Biowest). S16 cultures were differentiated for 72 h to a Schwann cell-like phenotype by glucose and serum deprivation.25

Primary Schwann cell cultures

Primary Schwann cells were isolated from the sciatic nerves of postnatal day-4 ICR mice (Harlan Laboratories). Nerves were incubated for 40 min at 37°C in HBSS containing Ca2+/Mg2+ (PAA), DNase I (0.11 mg/mL; Sigma), and collagenase (0.89 mg/mL; Sigma), then mechanically dissociated. Cells were plated at 1.2 × 10ˆ5 cells per well in 24-well plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (0.2 mg/mL; Sigma) and cultured in DF-10 medium consisting of 50% DMEM/Ham’s F-12 (PAA), 40% MEM with Earle’s salts (PAA), and 10% FBS (Biowest), supplemented with 1% MEM vitamins (PAA), 0.2% non-essential amino acids (PAA), 0.5% gentamicin (PAA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Biowest). After 24 h, half of the conditioned medium was replaced by AraC (Sigma) was added to a final concentration of 10 μM. Following 30 h of AraC treatment, the medium was replaced with 50% conditioned/50% fresh medium containing bovine pituitary extract (20 μg/mL; Sigma) and forskolin (0.5 μM; Sigma), and cells were cultured until ∼70% confluence.

Ad5/52s viral cycle

HEK293 cells were seeded at 1.5 × 10ˆ5 cells per well in 24-well plates and infected with Ad5/52s-GFP at MOI of 1. After 12 h, the supernatant was removed and cultures were washed with PBS to eliminate residual virus. Whole-cell lysates where then harvested 22, 36, 40, 44, 48, and 60 h post-infection, and IUs were determined by serial dilutions on HEK293 cultures.

Ad5/52s tropism in vitro

Cell cultures were infected with Ad5-GFP or Ad5/52s-GFP for 48 h. GFP expression was observed by fluorescence microscopy, and the percentage of transducted cells was quantified by flow cytometry (FACSCanto, BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences).

Viral biodistribution in mice

For systemic biodistribution, mice were injected intravenously (tail vein) with 5 × 10ˆ10 PP of either Ad5-LacZ or Ad5/52s-LacZ; PBS served as control. For local administration, doses were 1 × 10ˆ10 PP (intramuscular) or 3 × 10ˆ10 PP (intranasal). For local administration, doses were 1 × 10ˆ10 PP (intramuscular) or 3 × 10ˆ10 PP (intranasal). After 72 h, animals were euthanized and β-galactosidase activity was measured (Galacto-Light Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in protein extracts from adipose tissue, spleen, brain, sciatic nerve, heart, liver, tibialis muscle, lung, pancreas, testis, and kidney.

Clearance of virus in blood

ICR mice received tail-vein injections of both Ad5-RFP and Ad5/52s-GFP (or AdF52sR67N-GFP) at 5 × 10ˆ10 PP per virus per animal. Blood samples were collected from the facial vein at 5, 20, and 60 min post-injection.

Viral DNA was extracted from 10 μL of whole blood (QIAamp DNA Blood Mini kit, QIAGEN) and viral genomes were quantified by qPCR using fiber-specific primers (F5 Fwd 5′-TTACCTCAGAAGCCCTAACT-3′, F5 Rv 5′-TGGCAATGCTAAGTTTGGAG-3′, F52s Fwd 5′-TTTACCTCACCTCTCCGTTA-3′, F52s Rv 5′-AGGACTTAGGTTTGTACCTA-3′).

Infectious titers were measured by serial dilution of blood samples on HEK293 cells; GFP-positive (Ad5/52s) or RFP-positive (Ad5) cells were counted by fluorescence microscopy.

Blood fractionation

For fractionation, mice were exsanguinated and blood was collected into sodium citrate (11 mM; PanReac) to prevent coagulation. Samples were centrifuged at 100×g for 10 min to obtain plasma. The cell pellet was resuspended in PBS containing 11 mM sodium citrate and layered over Ficoll-Paque Premium 1.084 (GE Healthcare) to separate red blood cells and white blood cells.

Viral titers in blood fractions

To determine viral titers across blood compartments in vivo, ICR mice received tail-vein injections of Ad5-RFP and Ad5/52s-GFP (5 × 10ˆ10 PP per virus per animal). Five minutes later, animals were exsanguinated and blood fractions were separated as aforementioned. Infectious titers were determined on HEK293 cells, and viral genomes were quantified by qPCR using fiber-specific primers.

Intestinal organoids

Intestinal organoids were derived from 6 to 8-week-old Rosa26 mTmG mice54 (Jackson Laboratories) and cultured as previously described.55 Briefly, the proximal 10 cm of the intestine were dissected, cut longitudinally to expose villi, washed, and incubated with HBSS-EDTA (8 mM) on ice for 15 min. Crypts were mechanically dissociated from the muscle layer; this procedure was repeated three times to enrich for crypts. Crypts were embedded in Matrigel and cultured in organoid medium favoring an intestinal stem-cell phenotype56 by supplementing basal medium with EGF, Noggin, R-spondin 1, CHIR99021, and valproic acid. Organoids were released from Matrigel and infected with Ad5- or Ad5/52s-Cre particles for 2 h on Matrigel-coated wells. After infection, cells were re-embedded in Matrigel and recombination was assessed 72 h later by detecting EGFP-positive cells using a Nikon SP-E confocal microscope.

Animal procedures

ICR mice (Envigo) were housed under standard laboratory conditions (food and water ad libitum, 22 ± 2°C, 12 h light:dark cycle starting at 08:00). This study was carried out in strict accordance with the current National regulations (Generalitat de Catalunya Decret 214/97, 30th July 1997). The Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona approved all procedures described in this study (protocol number: CEEAH 2196/DMAH 7370).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Differences between two groups were assessed using the Student’s t test, while comparisons among more than two groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. The null hypothesis was rejected at a p value <0.05.
