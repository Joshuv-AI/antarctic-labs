Structural and
Enzymatic Basis for Impaired Deacylation
of N‐Acyl‐N‐Methyl
Lysine Residues

Sebastian Hilscher, Paul Horst Böttcher, Florian Friedrich, Martin Pannek, Fady Baselious, Marat Meleshin, Oliver Einsle, Cyril Barinka, Clemens Steegborn, Wolfgang Sippl, Manfred Jung, Mike Schutkowski, Cordelia Schiene-Fischer

Abstract

Posttranslational
lysine acetylation and methylation
are generally
considered mutually exclusive modifications of the ε-amino group.
The recent discovery of N-acetyl-N-methyl lysine (KAcMe) on histone H4 showed that both marks can coexist
on the same lysine side chain, raising the question of whether this
dual modification is enzymatically reversible. Here, we synthesized
model peptides containing N-acyl lysine or N-acyl-N-methyl lysine residues and tested
their processing by Zn2+-dependent HDACs and NAD+-dependent sirtuins. While the corresponding unmethylated substrates
were deacylated by the expected enzymes, KAcMe-containing peptides
were not detectably deacetylated by either enzyme family. N-methylation also blocked HDAC8/11-mediated demyristoylation
and SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation, whereas SIRT2 and SIRT6 retained
weak demyristoylase activity toward N-myristoyl-N-methyl lysine. X-ray crystal structures of sirtuin–peptide
complexes, supported by inhibition studies and molecular docking,
reveal that N-methylation disrupts productive substrate
positioning and misaligns the scissile amide bond for catalysis. These
findings suggest that KAcMe is resistant to canonical lysine deacetylases
and may represent a persistent chromatin-associated modification.

Introduction

The lysine side chain can undergo a wide
variety of posttranslational
modifications (PTMs). They include methylation and acetylation, but
also other acylations including propionylation, butyrylation, crotonylation,
malonylation, succinylation, glutarylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation,
as well as modifications by proteins like ubiquitination or SUMOylation.
,
 The lysine PTM status of histones is of major importance for the
epigenetic regulation of gene expression by influencing the chromatin
structure.
,
 In nonhistone proteins, lysine PTMs modulate
different properties of the respective proteins, like stability, activity,
or subcellular localization.

In
terms of PTM crosstalk, different PTMs at one individual site
can occur at different times, however, a single PTM is generally considered
to establish a covalent bond to one specific atom at a given time.
As an exception, up to three methylations may modify incrementally
a single nitrogen atom leading to the existence of mono-, di-, or
trimethylated lysine side chains or the formation of asymmetric dimethylarginine.
Similarly, oxidation of cysteine leads to sulfenic, sulfinic, and
sulfonic acids by successive covalent attachment of oxygen to the
sulfur atom. However, in both cases, a single atom is modified by
more than one of the same groups.

Despite its chemical feasibility,
it has just been shown recently
that two different functional groups, namely acetylation and monomethylation,
are not mutually exclusive at the ε-amino group of a lysine
residue. Both modifications have been found to exist simultaneously
on the side chain of a single lysine residue forming a dual PTM. The resulting N-acetyl-N-methyl lysine (KAcMe) on histone H4 was shown to be associated
with active transcription start sites. Thus, uniquely, in KAcMe, one
atom of an amino acid side chain is covalently modified by two different
functional groups at the same time.

Enzymatic covalent modifications
of lysine side chains are generally
considered to be reversible reactions. They are generally controlled
by two classes of enzymes, the so-called writers and erasers that
add and remove the respective PTMs and thus determine the levels of
lysine PTMs. The removal of a PTM by erasers
to switch off a signal is a prerequisite for timely control of signaling
by the modification. Acetylation of the lysine side chain is regulated
by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) as writers, which are counterbalanced
by histone deacetylases (HDACs) as erasers.
,
 To
date, 18 different HDACs have been characterized in humans, which
can be divided into four classes based on sequence homology. Classes
I (HDAC1–3 and 8) and II further divided into subclasses IIa
(HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9) and IIb (HDAC6 and 10), and IV (HDAC11) can be
summarized as Zn2+-dependent HDACs based on their catalytic
mechanism. Class III HDACs are NAD+-dependent enzymes known as sirtuins (SIRTs), comprising SIRT1–7. Lysine methylation is regulated by lysine methyltransferases
(KMTs) as writers and lysine demethylases (KDMs) as erasers. The lysine demethylases LSD1 and LSD2 both are
flavin-dependent monoamine oxidases and only demethylate monomethyl
and dimethyl lysine residues. In contrast,
Jumonji C (JMJC) domain-containing proteins use an oxygenase mechanism
to demethylate monomethylated, dimethylated, and trimethylated lysine
residues.
,
 The levels of the dual PTM KAcMe are probably
also subject to dynamic regulation as implied by their changes in
response to cellular signals and transcriptional activity. The known reaction mechanisms of KMTs and HATs
suggest that an initial monomethylation of lysine is followed by acetylation
to form KAcMe in a sequential mode of action. Accordingly, the HAT
p300 has been described to be able to acetylate N-methyl lysine.

Enzyme-mediated
deacetylation of KAcMe modifications leading to
the formation of N-methyl lysine residues was suggested
by in vivo inhibition experiments. On the other hand, an additional methyl group at the scissile
amide bond of N-acetyl lysine residues has been described
to convert substrates of Zn2+-dependent HDACs into inhibitors
that are stable against enzymatic deacetylation.

The aim of this study was to systematically analyze
the ability
of different HDACs to deacetylate KAcMe residues. Here we show that
neither Zn2+-dependent HDACs nor sirtuins can remove the
acetyl residue from KAcMe in model peptides in vitro, suggesting that the presence of the methyl group renders a per
se reversible acetylation irreversible. N-Methylation
also blocked HDAC8/11-mediated demyristoylation and SIRT5-mediated
desuccinylation, whereas SIRT2 and SIRT6 retained weak demyristoylase
activity toward N-myristoyl-N-methyl
lysine.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis

Chemicals
and General Methods for Peptide Synthesis

All Fmoc-protected
amino acids (except lysine) were purchased from
GL Biochem Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fmoc-Lys­(Ns)-OH was prepared as
described elsewhere.
N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), piperidine, OxymaPure, pentafluorophenol,
and Rink amide MBHA resin were purchased from Iris Biotech (Germany). N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) was purchased from Carl
Roth (Germany). All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich
if not stated otherwise. Purification of peptides was done on a Shimadzu
LC system with a Phenomenex Kinetex 5 μm XB-C18 (250 ×
21.1 mm, 100 Å) column using different gradients of 0.1% trifluoroacetic
acid (TFA) in H2O (solvent A) and 0.1% TFA in ACN (solvent
B) solutions. The wavelength used was 220 nm. UPLC-MS analysis was
performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC-MS system (Milford, USA) with
a Waters ACQUITY-UPLC-MS-BEH C18, 1.7 μm (2.1 × 50 mm;
30 Å) column. As the mobile phase, 0.1% formic acid in H2O (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (ACN) (solvent
B) solutions were used. A typical gradient from 95:5 (v/v) H2O:ACN to 5:95 (v/v) H2O:ACN in 6 min was used for most
of the runs. Data analysis was performed using Waters MassLynx software.

Solid-Phase Peptide Synthesis

The peptides were synthesized
using Fmoc-SPPS on Rink Amide resin using an automated microwave peptide
synthesizer Liberty Blue (CEM Corporation, Matthews, NC, USA). The
coupling of amino acids was performed with DIC/OxymaPure at 90 °C
for 2 min, and each coupling step was performed twice. Fmoc deprotection
was accomplished with 20% piperidine in DMF at 90 °C for 1 min.
Once the peptide had been modified as desired, it was cleaved from
the resin using a mixture of TFA/H2O/1-dodecanethiol
(97:1:2 v/v/v) for 1 h twice.

Peptide Modification

Subsequent to the SPPS, the nosyl
protecting group was cleaved and the lysine side chain was modified
on solid support. Removal of the nosyl group was done by adding β-mercaptoethanol
(10 equiv) and DBU (5 equiv) in DMF and incubating for 1 h. For acetylation,
a solution containing acetic anhydride (4 equiv) and DIPEA (5 equiv)
in dry DMF was used and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 30
min while shaking. For trifluoroacetylation, a solution of ethyl trifluoroacetate
(5% v/v) and DIPEA (5% v/v) in DMF was added to the resin and incubated
overnight. For succinylation, a solution of succinic anhydride (4
equiv) and DIPEA (5 equiv) in dry DMF was added for 30 min. For fatty
acylation, a solution of the corresponding fatty acid (5 equiv), DIPEA
(5 equiv), and HATU (5 equiv) in dry DMF was added and allowed to
react for 1 h. The reaction was repeated once. For alkylation, the
alkyl groups were introduced in the presence of the nosyl group. The
methyl, propyl, pentyl, and benzyl groups were introduced by adding
a solution of triphenylphosphine (5 equiv) and the corresponding dry
alcohol (10 equiv) in dry THF. After 5 min, a solution of diisopropyl
azodicarboxylate (DIAD) (5 equiv) in dry THF was added and the reaction
was left shaking for 24 h. Isopropylation and neopentylation were
performed by addition of acetone or pivalaldehyde (10 equiv) in dry
DMF with 1% (v/v) acetic acid, followed by NaBH3CN (10
equiv) in dry DMF and shaking for 48 h. UPLC-MS analysis of the synthesized
compounds was performed (Table S1, Figures S1–S22).

For HDAC1, 2, 3, and HDAC6, the recombinant proteins were purchased
from Enzo Life Sciences AG (Lausen, Switzerland), whereas HDAC4, 5,
and 7–11 were produced as described.

 Sirtuins 2, 3, 5, and 6 were expressed and purified as described.

(GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 25 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 300 mM NaCl,
1.5 mM DTT.

Enzymatic Assays

HPLC-MS-Based Deacylation
Assays

The in vitro deacylation of the HDACs
was monitored using UPLC-MS. The assays
were performed in the following buffers: HDAC buffer (HDAC1–9)
contained 20 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 1 mM
TCEP, pH 7.4; HDAC10 buffer consisted of 20 mM HEPES, 0.1 mg/mL BSA,
pH 7.4; HDAC11 buffer contained 20 mM HEPES, 0.5 mg/mL BSA, 70 μM
TCEP, pH 7.4; and the SIRT buffer contained 20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl,
5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 50 μM TCEP, 1 mM NAD+, pH 7.8.

Peptide derivatives were analyzed for their
substrate properties at concentrations of 50 μM for the unmethylated
and 100 μM for the methylated derivatives. Product formation
was quantitatively evaluated by the product peak area in the photometric
analysis of the UPLC separation. Conversion of less than 1% after
a 24 h reaction with the respective HDAC was considered as not detectable
by evaluation of UPLC-MS as assessed by analysis of a series of different
concentrations of product peptide 2. Absence of the formed
product was confirmed by mass spectral scans for the presumed cleavage
product.

The demyristoylation of peptides 5 and 6 by SIRT2 and SIRT6 was monitored using HPLC. SIRT2 and SIRT6
(0.5–2.0
μM) were incubated for different times at 30 °C in SIRT
buffer with the substrate peptide (15 μM) followed by analyzing
demyristoylation activity by HPLC separation. Analytical runs were
performed on an Agilent 1100 system (Boeblingen, Germany). Separation
was done with a linear water/ACN gradient within 6 min and a flow
rate of 0.5 mL/min on a 3.0 × 50 mm reversed-phase column (Phenomenex
Kinetex XB-C18, 2.6 μm). Absorbance was monitored at 280 nm.
Product formation was calculated as the ratio of product peak area
to total peak area.

To control the activity of the respective
HDACs independently,
the following standard substrates optimal for the individual HDAC
tested were used: the acetylated p53-derived peptide substrate Ac-RHKK­(Ac)-AMC
for HDAC1–3; the trifluoroacetylated
artificial peptide S1 for HDAC4–9 and 11;
,
 the myristoylated TNFα-derived peptide substrate Ac-EALPKK­(Myr)­TGG-NH2 for SIRT2, 3, and 6; the acetylated
TNFα-derived peptide substrate Ac-EALPKK­(Ac)­TGG-NH2 for SIRT1; and the acetylated CPS1-derived peptide substrate Bz-GVLK­(Suc)­EYGV-NH2 for SIRT5. HDACs 1–11
(50 nM), SIRT2, 3 (300 nM), and SIRT1, 5, and 6 (2 μM) were
incubated for 24 h at 30 °C in the corresponding buffer with
the peptide derivative to check for deacetylation activity. In the
control reactions, buffer conditions and DMSO concentrations corresponding
to the deacylation experiments for the dually modified peptides and
their unmethylated counterparts were used.

Fluorescence-Based Inhibition
Assays

Inhibition of
HDACs was analyzed in fluorescence-based enzymatic in vitro assays
using 384-well plates in a PerkinElmer EnVision 2104 multilabel plate
reader. After 5 min of incubation of the compounds with the respective
enzymes (HDAC1 = 10 nM, HDAC2 and HDAC3 = 3 nM, HDAC4 = 5 nM, HDAC5
= 10 nM, HDAC6 = 1 nM, HDAC7 = 5 nM, HDAC8 = 2 nM, HDAC9 = 20 nM,
HDAC11 = 20 nM), the reactions were started by the addition of substrate.

For HDAC1, 2, 3, and 6, an acetylated peptide substrate derived
from p53 (Ac-RHKK­(Ac)-AMC) was used in a discontinuous assay. All reactions were performed in assay buffer
(20 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM TCEP, and
0.2 mg/mL BSA, pH 7.4, adjusted with NaOH) at 37 °C. The reaction
was quenched after 1 h by adding trypsin and suberoylanilide hydroxamic
acid (SAHA). The fluorescence intensity was measured after 1 h of
incubation with an excitation wavelength of 380 nm and an emission
wavelength of 430 nm. HDAC4, 5, 7, 8, and 9 were measured in a continuous
manner using the thiotrifluoroacetylated artificial peptide substrate
Abz-SRGGK­(TFAc)­FFRR. The fluorescence
increase was followed for 1 h with two reads per min with an excitation
wavelength of 320 nm and an emission wavelength of 430 nm. HDAC11
activity was measured in a continuous manner using the peptide substrate
Ac-EALPKK­(11-Abz-Aun)­Y­(NO2)­GG-NH2, inhibition of HDAC11 was analyzed as described. Positive (enzyme, substrate, DMSO, and buffer)
and negative (substrate, DMSO, and buffer) controls were included
in every measurement and were set as 100% and 0%, respectively, and
the measured values were normalized accordingly. All tests were done
in three replicates. If no IC50 value could be determined
in the concentration range used, an inhibition assay with 100 μM
inhibitor was performed to determine possible binding.

Computational Methods

For the molecular docking study,
Schrödinger Suite 2025-2 was used. LigPrep panel with OPLS2005
−

 force fields was used to prepare the substrates. The optimized HDAC11-AF2
model bound to SIS17 in the vertical pose was utilized as the receptor. To generate a suitable grid for substrate docking,
the HDAC11 model was merged with a water molecule and a lysine residue
derived from the HDAC8 crystal structure complexed with a substrate
(PDB 2V5W
). The merged complex was then minimized using
Desmond minimization with OPLS5. For this, the complex was solvated
in an orthorhombic box using the SPC water model and a buffer distance
of 10 Å. The system was neutralized by adding chloride ions 4
Å away from the bound ligand.

Receptor grids were generated
using the receptor grid generation panel by utilizing the centroid
of the ligand. The Glide module
−

 was used for docking with flexible
ligand sampling and standard precision using the OPLS2005 force field.

For docking of H4K5Myr peptide 5 and the artificial
KAc peptide S2, a fragment of N-myristoylated
lysine was docked using core comparison constraints specifying the
core atoms with the maximum common substructure. The docked fragment
was used to generate a new grid as described above while increasing
the length of ligands to be docked to 35 Å and increasing the
inner box size to 15 Å to account for the difference in ligand
size.

For docking of H4K5MyrMe peptide 6 and the
artificial
KAcMe peptide 10, a fragment of N- methylated, N-myristoylated lysine was aligned flexibly to the N-myristoylated lysine in the HDAC11 model using the Ligand
Alignment Panel. The receptor was then minimized in the presence of
the new fragment using the Protein Preparation Panel
,
 specifying all atoms with a cutoff of 0.3 Å, and the grid was
generated as mentioned above.

Different conformers of the substrates
were generated using the
ConfGen application. The generated conformers
were subjected to core-constrained docking using Glide by applying
enhanced sampling and subjecting 100 poses to postdocking minimization
while reporting 5 poses. Docked poses were then clustered using the
Conformer Cluster Panel by atomic RMSD using average linkage to generate
20 clusters for each substrate. The members of each cluster were arranged
according to their Glide score, and the top-scoring member was selected
as the representative of the cluster. Selected poses of the substrates
were compared by an RMSD distance matrix, and similar poses (with
the lowest RMSD) were selected for further analysis.

Results

Synthesis
of Model Peptides Containing Dually Modified Lysine
Residues

Dual modification of the lysine residue by an acetyl
and a methyl residue KAcMe was found at H4K5. To analyze the substrate properties of acyl residues in the additional
presence of a methyl residue at the same lysine ε-amino group
for enzymatic deacylation by HDACs, the 11-mer peptide Ac-SGRGKGGKGWW-NH2
1 representing positions −4 to +4 of
H4K5 was used. The two additional tryptophans at the C-terminus were
introduced for efficient peptide detection by UV-Vis. We synthesized
derivatives of this sequence carrying an acetyl (3, 4), myristoyl (5, 6), or succinyl
(7, 8) group as the acyl moiety either in
combination with a methyl group (4, 6, 8) or a hydrogen atom (3, 5, 7) at the side-chain amino group of the central lysine (). Peptides were
prepared using automated Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis
(SPPS). The lysine side chain selected for modification was protected
with a nosyl group. The alkyl groups were introduced after SPPS by
treating the resin-bound peptide with the corresponding alcohol under
Mitsunobu conditions. Acylations were performed after removal of the
nosyl group. Peptides 1 and 2 contain no
acyl residue at the central lysine, thus representing the potential
products of a deacylation reaction. To get access to the enzymatic
activity of the catalytically less active class IIa HDACs, the artificial
sequence SRGGKFFRR already described as the basis for very good HDAC
substrates and the trifluoroacetyl group
which is readily hydrolyzed from the lysine side chain were used to obtain peptide derivatives S1 and 9. The N-terminal Abz
group allows fluorescence detection in the HPLC-based activity assay.
The lysine of 9 is modified with a methyl group in addition
to the trifluoroacetyl group (KTFAcMe). Peptide 10 with
the N-terminal nonfluorescent Bz group allowed enzyme
binding analysis at higher peptide concentrations. S2 is the unmethylated counterpart of 10 ().

Zn2+-Dependent
HDACs Are Unable to Deacylate Dually
Modified Lysine Residues

Initially, we analyzed the ability
of the different Zn2+-dependent HDACs to use peptides based
on the H4K5 sequence as substrates. After incubation of the respective
isozyme at 50 nM with 50 μM of 3 for 3 h at 30
°C, UPLC-MS analysis was performed. It was found that HDACs 1–3
and 6 were able to deacetylate the H4K5Ac peptide 3 (), whereas the other
HDACs were not able to use 3 as a substrate. HDAC10 was
omitted from the analysis because it is a poor lysine deacylase but
rather recognizes acetylated polyamines as substrates. To analyze if a deacylation reaction is catalyzed
by the respective HDAC also in the presence of an additional methyl
group at the ε-amino group of the lysine, 50 nM of HDAC1–3
or 6 were incubated with 100 μM of the H4K5AcMe peptide 4 for 24 h at 30 °C. No deacetylation could be detected
(, Figures S23–S26, S38). Since HDAC8 and
HDAC11 are known to exhibit activity against long-chain acyl groups,
,,
 for these enzymes demyristoylation
of the H4K5Myr peptide 5 was analyzed after incubation
of the respective isozyme with 5 for 24 h at 30 °C.
Both HDAC8 and 11 were found to be able to demyristoylate 5 (). However,
an additional methyl group at the amino group of the lysine residue
prevented demyristoylation. After incubation of 50 nM of HDAC8 or
HDAC11 with 100 μM of the H4K5MyrMe peptide 6 for
24 h, no product of demyristoylation could be detected (, Figures S31, S32, S39). As already described, class IIa HDACs are known
to be inert toward acetyl lysine residues even in the optimized artificial
HDAC substrate peptide, whereas trifluoroacetyl lysine peptides are
readily hydrolyzed by these HDACs.
,
 Thus, the
artificial N-trifluoroacetyl N-methyl
lysine peptide 9 in comparison to the artificial N-trifluoroacetyl lysine peptide S1 as a standard
substrate was used to analyze the enzymatic activity of HDACs 4, 5,
7, and 9. Reactions of the unmethylated
peptide derivative S1 were performed for 1 h, whereas
peptide 9 was incubated for 24 h with the respective
HDAC. Clearly, the dually modified peptide lacks HDAC substrate properties
().

In order to analyze if the peptides with an additional N-methyl residue at the N-acetylated or N-myristoylated lysine are still able to bind to the enzyme’s
active site, inhibition experiments were performed (). To this end, peptides 4 and 6, representing the doubly modified H4K5
sequence, were tested for their inhibitory potential. Additionally,
the doubly modified nonfluorescent artificial peptide 10 was used.

The different inhibition patterns
of the different
HDACs indicate
that acceptance of the additional methyl group in the HDAC active
site was influenced by the peptide sequence context in a way specific
to each HDAC isoform. For HDACs 1–5 and HDACs 7–9, the
affinities of the peptides analyzed were too low to determine IC50 values. Peptide 4 was found to bind only to
HDAC6 with an IC50 value of 59 μM but not to the
other HDACs. Peptide 10 significantly inhibited HDAC11
with an IC50 value of 8.2 μM. In addition, the inhibitory
potential of 6 against HDAC8 and 11 already described
to be able to accommodate long-chain acyl groups was evaluated. HDAC11
was found to be potently inhibited by 6 with an IC50 value of 17.2 μM. This reflects an increased affinity
compared to the unmethylated peptide 5 with an IC50 value of 81.4 μM. Determination of the IC50 value was performed using a fluorescence-based HDAC11 inhibition
assay in the presence of 6 to 200 μM 5 and 20 nM
HDAC11. Short-time measurements for 20 min allowed evaluation of the
inhibitory potency of 5 despite it being a substrate
of HDAC11.

For HDAC8, only moderate inhibition of 34.5% at 100
μM of
peptide 6 was found. Thus, N-methylation
of N-acyl lysine causes either (i) abolition of binding
to the respective HDAC or (ii) perturbation of a chemical step of
catalysis, depending on the HDAC isoform and the sequence of the peptide.

Since the HDAC11 binding site turned out to be quite promiscuous,
we used an inhibition approach to further analyze the spatial requirements
for efficient binding of peptides containing a lysine residue dually
modified by different acyl and alkyl residues. To this end, a series
of 7-mer H3K9 peptide derivatives (Ac-TARKSTG-OH) were synthesized,
as H3K9 has been documented as a natural deacylation site for HDAC11. At the side-chain amino group of the central
lysine, a range of alkyl modifications in combination with fatty acyl
residues of different chain lengths has been introduced yielding compounds 11–22. The inhibitory potential of the
compounds for HDAC11 was determined (, Figures S36, S37).

It turned out that all peptide derivatives
investigated
except
for 11 and 14 were able to bind to the HDAC11
active site, as inhibition was detected. In combination with an N-methyl residue, N-myristoylation of lysine
induced the highest inhibitory potential of the 7-mer H3K9 peptide
as indicated by an IC50 value of 19 μM for compound 12. As the steric demand for the alkylation increased, palmitoylation
emerged as beneficial for HDAC11 binding, as evidenced by a comparison
of 15 and 16.

It should be noted that
in 17, as the most potent
inhibitor in this series with an IC50 in the lower micromolar
range, the lysine is modified by a propyl residue beside a palmitoyl
residue indicating a rather large alkyl-binding site of HDAC11. Even
sterically demanding alkyl residues, such as the benzyl residue (21) and the neopentyl residue (22), can be accommodated.
However, the increased size of the alkyl residue found in 18–20 as well as the use of branched alkyl residues
as exemplified by comparing 17 and 18 or 20 and 22 reduced affinity for HDAC11.

To get insight into the molecular basis for the diminished catalytic
activity, docking studies were utilized to analyze HDAC11 binding
to the H4K5MyrMe peptide 6 and to the artificial KAcMe
peptide 10, which bound with relatively high affinity
but no enzymatic catalysis was found to occur (). The binding mode of the myristoylated
or acetylated, additionally methylated peptides was compared to the
analogous peptides without methylation 5 and S2, respectively. The binding modes were studied by molecular docking
using an optimized AlphaFold2 model of HDAC11. The model was extensively
validated and employed to study the binding mode of HDAC11 inhibitors
in previous work and was further utilized
to identify and design novel selective inhibitors.
,
 The obtained docking poses of the peptides revealed that the carbonyl
oxygen of the acetyl/myristoyl group coordinated the Zn2+ ion while forming a hydrogen bond with Y304. For the unmethylated
substrates 5 and S2, the NεH is positioned
within a suitable distance to form a hydrogen bond with G151. The
myristoyl chain of peptides 5 and 6 is conveniently
accommodated in the foot pocket and the extended hydrophobic tunnel
of HDAC11 along loop 3 and loop 7, while forming hydrophobic interactions
with residues lining the tunnel such as F37, W42, F141, and F152.
Comparing the docking poses of 5 (KMyr)/6 (KMyrMe) and S2 (KAc)/10 (KAcMe) revealed
that additional lysine N-methylation of the peptide
derivatives results in the loss of possible hydrogen-bond formation
with G151 as well as a slight shift in the positioning of the lysine
Nε, which might justify the lack of deacylation of the dually
modified peptides.

Sirtuins Are Unable to Deacetylate and Desuccinylate
Dually
Modified Lysine Residues

Next, we examined the ability of
sirtuins to deacylate dually modified lysine residues. We first analyzed
the substrate properties of H4K5-based peptide derivatives lacking
additional methylation and carrying different N-acyl
modifications at the lysine with respect to the major sirtuins. Sirtuins
SIRT1–3 and SIRT6 were able to deacetylate 3 (). The well-known
demyristoylases SIRT2, SIRT3, and SIRT6 could demyristoylate 5 (). SIRT5 as a deacylase specific for negatively charged acyl residues
was able to desuccinylate 7 (). Using peptides 4 and 8 methylated additionally to the respective acylation showed
that neither deacetylation of H4K5AcMe (4) nor desuccinylation
of H4K5SucMe (8) could be achieved by any tested sirtuin
(, Figures S27–S30). Obviously, the additional
methyl group at lysine prevents the enzymatic cleavage of the respective
amide bond.

Inhibition experiments using peptide 4 were performed
to analyze if binding to the active site of the sirtuins can occur.
At up to 60 μM of 4, no influence on the activity
of SIRT2, SIRT3, and SIRT6 was observed, indicating that the methyl
group in addition to the acetyl group on the lysine prevented efficient
binding.

Interestingly, complex formation between SIRT5 and
an N-succinyl-N-methyl lysine peptide
indicated significant
peptide binding and allowed crystallographic analyses of the complex
to examine why the catalytic activity is diminished (). The crystal structure of
the zebrafish SIRT5 ortholog in complex with a KSucMe peptide (2.8
Å resolution; A, B; Table S2) reveals the absence
of interaction between the lysine side-chain Nε atom and V221
in the SIRT5 active site, which is present in the zSIRT5–KSuc
peptide complex (PDB 4UTN; A, C, D).
A twist of the elongated lysine side chain of the KSucMe peptide brings
its succinyl carboxyl group near the NAD+ binding pocket
and prevents hydrogen bond formation with the R105 and Y102 side chains
necessary for catalysis (D). The carbonyl oxygen of the tertiary amide bond
of KSucMe is turned by about 145° away from the NAD+ binding pocket and thus is probably not correctly positioned to
attack the 1′ carbon of the nicotinamide ribose in NAD+ which initiates the deacetylation process (A).

Myristoylated Dually Modified Lysine Residues Form Substrates
of the Deacylases SIRT2 and SIRT6

Next, we analyzed the ability
of the known deacylases of the sirtuin family SIRT2, SIRT3, and SIRT6
with respect to their activity against peptides containing a lysine
residue doubly modified with methyl and myristoyl groups. In contrast
to SIRT3, SIRT2 and SIRT6 were found to be able to demyristoylate
the H4K5MyrMe peptide (6) besides their ability to demyristoylate
the H4K5Myr peptide (5) (A, Figures S33–S35). The omission of NAD+ in
the reaction mixture as a control resulted in the prevention of the
reaction, verifying that demyristoylation of 6 occurs
via enzymatic catalysis (Figure S41).

In order to quantify the substrate properties of 5 and 6 for SIRT2 and SIRT6, progress curves
of the demyristoylation
reactions at different enzyme concentrations were recorded (B) and demyristoylation
rates were obtained (C). As expected, SIRT2 efficiently demyristoylated 5 with a rate of 3.39 μM s–1. This rate was
approximately 135-fold higher than that for SIRT6 with 0.0251 μM
s–1. The additional methylation of the myristoyl-lysine
caused about a 600-fold reduction of the demyristoylation rate of
SIRT2 and about a 4-fold reduction of the demyristoylation rate of
SIRT6. Both enzymes catalyze the demyristoylation of 6 with similar rates of about 0.006 μM s–1 (C). Taken
together, the enzymatic activity was reduced but still existent in
the case of myristoyl-methyl-lysine in the substrate peptide for SIRT2
and SIRT6.

The structural basis for the reduced yet detectable
demyristoylation
of KMyrMe by SIRT2 was examined using peptide 12, whose
H3K9 sequence represents a well-established SIRT2 substrate. Two structures were solved: SIRT2–12 (PDB 30KD, 1.45 Å resolution; A) and SIRT2–12–NAD+ (PDB 30KE, 2.80 Å resolution; B) (Table S2). In the SIRT2–12 complex, the myristoyl group occupies the large hydrophobic
acyl-lysine channel formed at the interface of the Zn2+-binding and Rossmann-fold domains. The KMyrMe dual modification
introduces steric bulk that forces the N-methyl group
to rotate toward the Rossmann-fold domain. Consequently, KMyrMe adopts
a cis-amide conformation in which the N-methyl and carbonyl groups orient toward H187, a catalytically essential
residue (A, S42). This rotation moves the amide away from
V233, whose backbone carbonyl normally forms a key hydrogen bond with
the amide nitrogen in the unmethylated substrate, as presented by
comparison of the structures of the complexes SIRT2–12 and SIRT2–H3K9Myr (PDB 4Y6L) (Figure S42). Loss of this stabilizing interaction weakens substrate anchoring
in the channel, consistent with the rather weak electron density observed
for the KMyrMe moiety of peptide 12.

The SIRT2–12–NAD+ structure
was obtained by soaking NAD+ into preformed SIRT2–12 crystals for 6 h (B). Clear electron density for the nicotinamide (NAM)
moiety of NAD+ aligns well with the biochemical data, which
show markedly reduced demyristoylation of KMyrMe compared to KMyr
(see ). This
reduction likely arises because nucleophilic attack of the amide CO
on the C1′ of NAD+ ribose, required for NAM cleavage,
is impaired. Moreover, NAD+ binding induces an unusual
ribose conformation, in which the 2′–OH and 3′–OH
groups kink to avoid steric interference with the atypical KMyrMe
carbonyl orientation.

Notably, the myristoyl amide bond of KMyrMe
switches from cis to trans in the
NAD+-bound
structure. Superposition of SIRT2–12 and SIRT2–12–NAD+ reveals the structural basis for
this isomerization. In the absence of NAD+, SIRT2–12 adopts an open, unproductive conformation. NAD+ binding triggers closure of the Zn2+-binding domain (C), which would position
the N-methyl of the cis amide bond
only 2.2 Å from His187 (D). This steric clash potentially forces isomerization
to the trans-species. Because NAD+ was
soaked into preformed crystals, the reduced resolution of the NAD+-bound structure likely reflects slight disruption of the
crystal lattice caused by SIRT2 domain movement.

Comparison
of SIRT2–12–NAD+ with SIRT2–H3K9Myr
(PDB 4Y6L) further
clarifies the reduced catalytic turnover. To accommodate peptide 12 in the acyl-lysine channel, the K9 Cα atom shifts
by 1.4 Å relative to KMyr, allowing an 81° rotation of KMyrMe
compared to KMyr and preventing a clash with V233 (E,F). However, this repositioning
yields a suboptimal angle and distance between the amide CO
and the C1′ of the NAD+ ribose (3.7 Å versus
3.2 Å for KMyr), reducing the efficiency of nucleophilic attack.
This structural arrangement aligns with the observed diminished yet
measurable enzymatic activity.

Discussion

The in vivo existence of
a dually modified lysine
residue carrying both a methyl and an acetyl group at the ε-amino
group was recently found on histone H4 in a range of species and mammalian
tissues. The modification was shown to
be generated enzymatically by the successive action of a KMT and a
HAT (, 1, 3). KMTs are described to be specific for the amino
group of lysine and do not readily methylate other nitrogen nucleophiles,
making an initial HAT-mediated lysine acetylation (, 2) followed by KMT-mediated
methylation unlikely (, 4).

The removal of the KAcMe modification
could be
envisaged by either
deacetylation followed by demethylation or the other way around ().

In the study
presented here, we have shown that in the analyzed
context, acetyl groups of methylated acetyl lysines are virtually
not enzymatically removable by lysine deacetylases, neither by Zn2+-dependent HDACs nor by sirtuins ( and ).

For
deacylation analyses, we used model peptides either with a
sequence derived from histone H4K5 or with an artificial sequence
described to interact very efficiently with HDACs, so that the corresponding
unmethylated peptide formed a substrate for the lysine deacetylase
investigated. As a more reactive analogue of the acetyl residue, the
trifluoroacetyl residue was used for HDACs of class IIb. Possibly, HDAC substrate properties of peptides
may differ from those of folded proteins forming in vivo substrates. However, since histone tails, where the KAcMe modification
was found, are intrinsically disordered and devoid of three-dimensional
structure, peptides form useful models to study the enzymology of
histone PTMs.
−

 The in vitro assay used was designed
to be suitable to find even very slow deacetylation of KAcMe residues
by the use of high enzyme concentrations and long reaction times.
The conditions used would allow detection of enzymatic reactions for
substrates with very low catalytic efficiencies, with k
cat/KM values as low as 2 M–1 s–1.

Several lysine deacylases are known
to have distinct specificity
for the acyl group they remove. Whereas HDAC8 and 11 and sirtuins
SIRT1–3 and SIRT6 efficiently deacylate long acyl chains,
,,,,
 SIRT5 is specific for cleavage of negatively
charged acyl groups like succinyl residues from lysine. Therefore, we analyzed for these deacylases
their ability to remove the respective acyl group in the additional
presence of a methyl residue at the ε-amino group of lysine.
The natural occurrence of these modifications is not known; however,
spontaneous acylation of methyl lysine residues by the respective
CoA derivatives is chemically feasible. Furthermore, the acetyltransferase
p300 is known to transfer different acyl residues besides acetyl residues
like the succinyl, propionyl and butyryl residues.
,
 Despite their specific ability to deacylate the unmethylated counterparts,
the respective Zn2+-dependent HDACs were not able to deacylate N-myristoylated N-methylated peptide derivatives
and SIRT5 was not able to remove a succinyl group from the respective
dually labeled peptide derivatives in our experiments.

For most
HDACs, KAcMe deacetylation was precluded because the KAcMe-containing
peptides were unable to bind to the active sites of the respective
enzymes. Only HDAC6, 10, and 11 could interact with a KAcMe-containing
peptide as indicated by the efficient inhibition of enzymatic catalysis
(). Docking
studies for HDAC6 and 11 showed that
the overall binding of dually modified peptides to the HDAC active
sites to be similar to that of unmethylated peptides. This similarity
of the interactions during binding may explain the relatively high
affinity of the dually modified peptides toward the HDAC active sites.
However, the additional lysine methylation of the substrates causes
a loss of possible hydrogen-bond formation and a slightly disturbed
binding mode of the lysine side chain which may account for the lack
of catalysis (). Similarly, the crystal structure analysis of SIRT5 reveals a disturbed
binding of the KAcMe peptide derivative (). Whereas the Nε of the lysine side
chain of the KSuc peptide interacts with Val221 in the SIRT5 active
site, this interaction is missing for the KSucMe peptide derivative.
A turn of the peptide leads to a misalignment of the carbonyl group
of the scissile bond in relation to NAD+ precluding the
deacetylation reaction.

The inability of HDACs to remove acetyl
groups from KAcMe suggests
KAcMe to be a quasi-permanent mark in vivo taking
into account that demethylation of KAcMe has not been envisioned yet.
If the additional modification does not interfere with readers of
the single modifications, permanence would prevent deactivation. In
the case of histones, permanent histone acetylation would mean a permanent
permissive “switched on” state of chromatin allowing
access by chromatin-remodeling factors. Alternatively, KAcMe may constitute
a completely new signal recognized by specific readers with yet unknown
biological implications. The dual modification may also mask one of
the single modifications supplying an additional level of readout
control. In either case, the irreversibility of the acetylation event
implies the importance of long-term control and the existence of an
alternative possibility of displacement.

There are other PTMs
of amino acid side chains in proteins known
to be irreversible. Often they are caused by spontaneous chemical
processes connected with protein aging;
,
 however, also
enzymatic modifications like the deimination of arginine into citrulline
represent nonreversible PTMs. Irreversible
PTMs are considered to enable rather longer-lasting signal transduction
and regulation of biochemical processes.

The in vivo results of Lu-Culligan et
al. showing that the addition of the pan-HDAC inhibitor trichostatin
A (TSA) increased the level of H4K5AcMe peptides from cellular histone
proteins imply that HDAC-mediated reversibility of H4K5AcMe formation
may not necessarily arise from Zn2+-dependent HDACs involved
in the removal of the acetyl group of methylated acetyl-lysine residues. A possible alternative explanation may be offered
by the TSA-induced inhibition of HDACs leading to acetylation and
thereby the activation of the histone acetyltransferase p300 shown
to acetylate methyl-lysine residues.
,

Surprisingly,
a fatty acyl group in the additional presence of
a methyl group on the lysine could be removed by the two sirtuins
SIRT2 and SIRT6, even though with very low activity (). The structural analysis
of SIRT2-12 complexes implies that interactions of the
hydrophobic tail of the fatty acyl chain anchor the substrate peptide
in the active site in a position with sufficiently correct placement
of the scissile bond to allow catalysis (). The reduction in enzymatic activity may
result from repositioning of the lysine Cα atom to prevent a
clash with V233 in the SIRT2 active site, which leads to a suboptimal
angle and distance between the amide CO and the C1′
of the NAD+ ribose (E,F) for nucleophilic attack. Generally, the reversibility
of fatty acylation on methylated lysine residues may be important
in a biological context because of the large impact of fatty acylations
on protein localization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we show that HDACs are virtually
not able to remove
the acetyl group from KAcMe residues in the context of H4K5. Also,
other acyl residues could not be removed in the additional presence
of a methyl residue at the ε-amino group of lysine by the HDACs
known to be specific for the respective acyl group. The origin of
the prohibition of catalysis could be structurally clarified by a
disrupted binding mode of the substrate peptide in the enzyme’s
active site. Surprisingly, as an exception, SIRT2 and SIRT6 were found
to be able to demyristoylate the H4K5MyrMe peptide even though with
very low activity. Our results suggest that dual modification may
feature an additional level of regulation by lysine side-chain modifications
especially with regard to long-term control.
