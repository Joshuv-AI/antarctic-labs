 Beam Tuning Setups for Drift Tube Linacs
 S. I. Sosa Guitron, Y. K. Batygin
 Los Alamos National Laboratory, Los Alamos, NM, 87507, USA

Abstract
 Various matching methods for tuning Drift Tube Linacs are overviewed. Special emphasis is
on the methods utilizing signature phase scans with single and pairs of Beam Position Monitors
(BPM) placed inside or outside of the tuned-up cavity, and that using a combination of phase scans
with energy scans.

 1. Introduction

 Ion beam facilities use various Radio Frequency (RF) accelerating sections to increase beam
energy. A series of different accelerating structures is employed to match the beam velocity at each
stage. At the initial part of accelerators, the Radio Frequency Quadrupoles are typically employed
to accelerate the beam up to an energy of 3 - 5 MeV. The Drift Tube Linac (DTL), or Half-Wave
and Quarter-Wave resonators are used in the energy range from several MeV till ~ 100 MeV. In
high-energy linacs, the Coupled Cavity Linacs (CCL) and superconducting cavities are used.
 After fabrication of a new DTL tank, the radio-frequency parameters are typically found to be
different from design values, which can be attributed to fabrication errors, misalignments, etc.
After fabrication and initial assembly of a DTL tank, low power testing are required to achieve
design cavity frequency and the field “flatness” along the structure (similar voltage across each
accelerating gap). Typically, a perturbative method (bead-pull) is used to determine the field
flatness through the structure. Both the cavity frequency and the field flatness can be varied with
post-couplers in the tank walls located at different locations. Adjusting the position of post-
couplers can locally modify the capacitance of the structure, allowing control of the frequency and
field flatness.
  During start-up of operations, the amplitude and phase of the DTL tanks need to be tuned to
design values that provide the correct output beam energy at the exit of each tank. Incorrect setting
of RF parameters of cavities results in excessive beam losses. The amplitude and phase are
controlled by adjusting the parameters of feeding klystrons, or any other amplifier technology
being used.
  One of the original methods of DTL tuning was based on the determination of the threshold RF
field required for acceleration when synchronous phase still exists [1]. The next step in tuning
procedures was obtained with the development of the Delta-t method [2]. The useful approach is
also based on the absorber-collector technique [3]. Wide application of tuning procedures was
achieved by a phase scan procedure when the cavity phase is scanned in a range of up to 360◦ and
the beam energy is measured. As mentioned in [4], the measurement could be done in different
ways, using a calibrated dipole magnet [5] or in a combination with a beam position detector [9],

using silicon detectors [6, 7], or various time of- flight techniques by means of gamma-ray
detectors [5], fast current transformers [8], beam position/phase monitors (BPPMs or BPMs) [10,
11] and using cavities as the beam phase detectors [12, 13]. The measurements are usually fitted
into a model, which sets cavities to a given accelerating (also known as synchronous or reference)
phase.
 The focus of this report is on the tuning of the DTL accelerator section and, in particular, on
compiling the techniques used at multiple facilities to characterize the fields in the DTL during the
commissioning phase and in adjusting the field settings for optimal operation during the start-up
phase.

 The Delta-t tuning procedure has certain limitations. It assumes that the input energy in each
tank is equal to the design value. This assumption results in errors in the procedure. Equations (6),
(7) are derived in linear approximation to particle dynamics, where a nonlinear RF field is
approximated by a linear function within small deviations from the design values. Practically, it
means that the Delta-t method uses only a small phase range (~ 10o) for tuning, assuming that

deviation of beam energy from design at the entrance and at the exit of the accelerating module,
 WA , WB , are small ( W / W 10 −3 ). Therefore, values of amplitude and phase, as well as input
energy before the delta-t search, have to be selected as close as possible to design values, which
limits the applicability of the method.

 4. Absorber-collector method

 The more accurate values of amplitude in a drift tube linac can be obtained using the absorber-
collector method [3]. An energy absorber is installed after each tank of the drift tube linac (see Fig.
2). The thickness of the absorber is selected to allow propagation only for particles with energy
equal to or larger than the nominal energy after the structure. After the absorber, the collector is
installed, which measures the current of the accelerated particles. During the tuning process, the
phase of the accelerating structure changes within the interval of 360o. The preliminary bunched
beam is used as a probe to determine the phase area of beam capture into acceleration, which is
the width of separatrix,  s  3  s . The result of measurement is a signal of accelerated particles
versus the RF phase of the accelerating structure (see Fig. 3). The finding of the required value of
the accelerating field in the resonator is achieved by reaching the correct value of the phase width
of the separatrix with the variation of the field.

 5. Phase Scan Signature Matching Method

  A signature phase scan matching method was developed at Fermilab [14]. The basic idea behind
the phase-scan measurements is illustrated in Figure 4. The tank N is the tank being tuned. All
upstream tanks are turned on, and all downstream tanks are turned off. The RF power for tank N
is initially turned off. The phase of the beam-induced signals (beam phase) at a downstream
monitor is recorded. Radio-frequency power to tank N is next turned on, and the beam phase is
recorded as a function of tank N phase. The tank phase is varied over 360 degrees. The measured
response is a unique signature curve that, in general, is different for different structures.
 Measured phases are compared to model predictions and typically show good agreement. Once
the best manual curve fit to the target curve is obtained, a least-squares procedure is implemented
to find the theoretical curve that best matches the measured data. After the phase scan, the tank
phase is adjusted to the appropriate position along the phase-scan curve following preliminary
simulation of beam dynamics (see Fig. 5). The signature matching technique does not have a linear
response restriction and can be implemented over the full 360-degree range, without a priori guess
on the phase and amplitude. Use of BPMs is favored as they are non-intercepting beam devices;
however, the phase can be measured using intercepting devices (Faraday Cups).

Figure 5: Phase-scan curves for tank 6 of the Fermilab drift-tube linac. Beam phase is measured

 6. Phase Scan and Tuning Application (PASTA)

 Figure 6: SNS linear accelerator [15].
 Further development of the phase scan signature method, Phase Scan and Tuning Application
(PASTA), was developed at SNS [16-18]. SNS linac consists of 2.5 MeV RFQ, 87 MeV DTL, 186
MeV CCL, and 1 GeV superconducting linac (see Fig. 6). The DTL tuning method involves
varying an RF cavity amplitude and phase settings over a fairly large range and comparing the
measured the phase difference between two downstream BPMs “signatures” to model predictions
(see Figs. 7, 8). The tuning procedure includes minimization between the measured phase
difference and the calculated one over the range of scanned RF phase of the tank being tuned. The
procedure was repeated for different RF amplitude settings (i.e., simultaneously matching different
phase signatures). At the time of tuning, this method was followed by Delta-t tuning.

 Figure 7: Schematic plot of phase scan with two downstream BPM [17].

Figure 8: Example signature phase scan method for setting a DTL linac cavity phase and
amplitude for two cavity amplitudes [18].

 7. Phase Scan Signature Method with Single BPM Inside RF Cavity

  Later, the originally implemented method for SNS RF cavity setup that used the BPMs in the
next downstream cavity was replaced by Phase Scan Signature (PSS) with the usage of a single
BPM inside the tuned-up cavity [15, 19]. In this case, the tuning of only part of the cavity is

equivalent to tuning up the whole cavity if the model correctly reproduces the cavity design. The
method was applied both to SNS DTL and Coupled Cavity Linac (CCL). Figure 9 illustrates the
measurement scheme.
  The cavity phase scan is performed in a 360° phase range at the existing RF amplitude. The
position of BPM should be close to the beginning of the cavity to provide good beam transmission
and bunching for strong signals from this BPM, but also should be far enough away from the
beginning (several RF gaps) to give a non-sinusoidal BPM phase response during the scan.
  Figure 10 shows the result of the phase scan cavity CCL1. The non-sinusoidal shape of the curve
allows to find not only the cavity’s amplitude and phase, but also to determine the entrance beam
energy after the fitting procedure. The accuracy of the fitted model beam energy for the case shown
in Fig. 10 was 21 keV for the found energy 86.897 MeV. The design energy is 86.828 MeV. The
operational setup of cavity phases and amplitudes appears to be significantly different from the
calculated one to reduce beam loss in the linac (see Fig. 11).

 8. RF Amplitude and Phase Tuning with Energy Scans
 The Japan Proton Accelerator Research Complex (J-PARC) linear accelerator comprises three
DTL tanks (DTL1, DTL2, and DTL3), followed by a Separated-type Drift Tube Linac (SDTL)
(see Figure 12). During the beam commissioning of the J-PARC linac, RF amplitude and phase
tuning were performed using a phase scan method, while monitoring the output beam energy from
each DTL tank [20]. Each DTL tank at the exit has a Fast Current Transformer (FCT) for the beam
phase measurement. The output energy from each tank was measured with the time-of-flight
method utilizing two downstream FCTs. The FCT layout for the DTL tuning is shown in Fig. 12.
Phase-scan curves, which show the phase dependence of the output energy, were obtained for
several tank amplitudes and then compared to those from the modeling to determine the set-points
of RF phase and amplitude (see Fig. 13). The optimum set-points were determined from the
characteristic shapes of the phase-scan curves, such as a flat part or an intersection of two specific
curves.

Figure 12: J-PARC Fast Current Transformer (FCT) layout for DTL tuning. A dot shows the
location of an FCT [20].

Figure 13: Phase-scan curves for J-PARC DTL1. A filled circle shows a measured beam energy,
and the solid line shows that from modeling. The RF amplitude for each phase-scan is also
shown with relative to the design tank level [20].

Summary
  We review various techniques for fine-tuning the components of a Drift Tube Linac (DTL)
particle accelerator. The focus is on methods that use special measurements, called 'signature phase
scans,' with detectors that measure the beam's position, and methods that combine these phase
scans with energy measurements.
