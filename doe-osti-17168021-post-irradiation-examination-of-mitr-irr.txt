 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Yttrium hydride (YHx, x < 2) is a leading candidate moderator material for compact space nuclear
reactor systems because of its high hydrogen density and favorable performance at elevated
temperatures. Qualification of YHₓ requires demonstrating that it can retain its hydrogen inventory
while maintaining dimensional stability, thermal performance, and mechanical integrity during
prolonged exposure to reactor-relevant temperatures and neutron irradiation. To support this
objective, Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) fabricated the YHₓ specimens and associated
encapsulations, the specimens were irradiated in the Massachusetts Institute of Technology reactor
(MIT-R), and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) performed a focused post-irradiation
examination (PIE) campaign on specimens from Capsules 5, 6, and 13.
The ORNL PIE campaign combined thermal diffusivity measurements, dilatometry, ultrasonic
testing, and multiscale characterization using scanning electron microscopy, electron backscatter
diffraction, and scanning/transmission electron microscopy. The results showed that the irradiated
YHx retained its overall structural integrity. The material maintained a continuous, predominantly
coarse-grained microstructure without extensive cracking, porosity, or detectable nanoscale
cavities. Comparable oxide layers on irradiated and unirradiated specimens indicate that surface
oxidation was more likely associated with fabrication, storage, or handling. Localized Ti-, Zr-, and
Mo-rich precipitates in the irradiated material were consistent with interactions involving the TZM
capsule and molybdenum spacer disks, identifying material compatibility as a consideration for
extended operation.
The thermophysical and ultrasonic measurements showed meaningful specimen-to-specimen
variations. Irradiation altered the temperature dependence of thermal diffusivity, with the most
significant reduction occurring at elevated temperatures. The thermal expansion response of the
irradiated specimens also became more pronounced and variable above approximately 500 °C.
Ultrasonic measurements indicated changes in the effective elastic response from sample-to-
sample for both irradiation and unirradiated test articles. Some measurements suggested
differences in hydrogen-to-yttrium ratio among specimens, but the indications from thermal
diffusivity, dilatometry, and sound-speed measurements were not fully consistent. These
differences emphasize the importance of improved fabrication control, comprehensive
characterization of the as-fabricated material, and stronger product-quality assurance before
irradiation.
Variations among specimens from the same irradiation capsule further support previous PIE
observations of in-capsule hydrogen redistribution. Because hydrogen distribution within the YHx
specimens directly affects thermal transport, thermal expansion, elastic behavior, and neutron-
moderation performance, capsule temperature gradients must be carefully controlled. Capsule
designs should, therefore, minimize hydrogen redistribution and chemical interaction with
surrounding materials, accommodate irradiation-dependent dimensional changes, and preserve
the intended hydrogen inventory. Any changes to an established capsule design should be
supported by rigorous engineering evaluation and configuration control.

September 2026 iv

Overall, the results indicate that the post-irradiation behavior of YHx was governed primarily by
hydrogen retention, hydrogen redistribution, and local phase constitution rather than by extensive
irradiation-induced physical damage. Hydrogen redistribution or loss could reduce moderator
effectiveness while also decreasing high-temperature heat spreading, increasing dimensional
variability, and generating localized thermal and mechanical stresses. Execution of the ORNL PIE
campaign represents an important step in advancing hydride-based moderators for space nuclear
and microreactor applications. Continuation of PIE examinations on remaining capsules are also
critical. Future work should strengthen fabrication quality assurance, establish more complete pre-
irradiation property baselines, directly quantify local and bulk hydrogen content, evaluate capsule-
design effects, and complete the ultrasonic measurements needed to determine the full elastic
response of irradiated YHx.

1 INTRODUCTION
Yttrium hydride (YHₓ, x < 2) is being developed as an advanced moderator material for space nuclear
reactor systems because of its high hydrogen density and favorable performance at elevated
temperatures. Its successful deployment requires demonstrating that the moderator can retain its
hydrogen inventory while maintaining dimensional stability, thermal performance, and mechanical
integrity under reactor-relevant temperatures and neutron irradiation.
To support this qualification effort, Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) fabricated hydrides and
their encapsulations, and samples were irradiated at Massachusetts Institute of Technology reactor
(MIT-R). followed by a post-irradiation examination (PIE) campaign at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL). Critical PIE activities were performed to evaluate hydrogen retention and structural integrity
and to characterize irradiation-induced changes in the specimens’ thermal, mechanical, and
microstructural properties.
The results of these examinations provide experimental data needed to assess the post-irradiation
performance of YHₓ and identify material changes that could affect its use as a reactor moderator.
This work contributes to the broader qualification of advanced moderator systems and the
continued development of yttrium hydride technology for future compact space nuclear
applications.

2 OVERVIEW OF THE FISSION SURFACE POWER (FSP) IRRADIATION
  CAMPAIGN
A critical requirement for compact space fission reactors is the ability of the neutron moderator to
retain hydrogen while maintaining structural integrity during prolonged operation at temperatures
approaching 700-800°C. To evaluate moderator performance under representative reactor
operating conditions, an irradiation campaign was conducted at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology Reactor (MITR) from April through July 2025 (FY2025). The irradiation matrix included
multiple moderator materials (YHx and ZrHx pellets) and encapsulation (TZM alloy and HILDR)
concepts [1]. The present report is exclusively concerned with the TZM-encapsulated YHx
specimens.

September 2026 1

 Figure 1: Schematic representation of the HYD1 irradiation capsule showing the arrangement of the sealed
 TZM specimen capsules within the different temperature zones. Adapted from [1].

The irradiation campaign, designated HYD1, utilized a custom-designed in-core dry-irradiation
capsule installed at the MITR A1 core position. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the irradiation capsule
accommodated multiple TZM (titanium–zirconium–molybdenum alloy) specimen capsules
positioned within different temperature zones, enabling simultaneous irradiation of specimen
capsules under different thermal conditions. Each TZM capsule, in turn, contained multiple
cylindrical YHx specimens (with a fixed diameter of 8 mm but varying heights) arranged in a vertical
stack and separated by molybdenum spacer disks to prevent direct specimen-to-specimen contact
during irradiation. During experiment design, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) thermal modeling
was performed to achieve the desired temperature profile within the irradiation capsule. Target
irradiation temperatures of 700°C and 800°C were achieved through multi-zone helium-neon gas
flow regulations and monitored real-time using embedded thermocouples. The irradiated YHX
specimens exhibited H/Y ratios ranging from 1.931 to 1.984. Although Ti and Zr constituents of the
TZM capsule may have absorbed some hydrogen released from the YHx specimens post-irradiation,
capsule hydrogen uptake was not measured and its effect on the specimen H/Y ratios could not be
quantified. Figure 2 shows a representative irradiated TZM capsule following completion of the HYD1
campaign.

Following irradiation, the TZM specimen capsules were transferred to ORNL for PIE activities. The
present work focuses on detailed characterization of the thermophysical, mechanical, and
microstructural properties of irradiated YHx specimens. The study is limited to three specimen
capsules containing YHx specimens: two capsules irradiated at 800°C (Capsules 5 and 6), and one
can irradiated at 700°C (Capsule 13). Corresponding unirradiated YHx samples, from the same
fabrication run, were also characterized to provide a baseline for comparison. A summary of the
specimens examined in this study is provided in Table 1. These measurements provide a
comprehensive assessment of irradiation-induced changes in material behavior in encapsulated
yttrium hydride specimens.

3 CAPSULE DISASSEMBLY
Following receipt at ORNL, the irradiated TZM specimen capsules were transferred to the Irradiated
Materials Examination and Testing (IMET) hot cell facility for disassembly. Prior to opening, the
external surface of each capsule was visually examined in-cell using a high-definition camera
system (referred to as copy stand) to assess the condition of the TZM housing and verify the absence
of visible defects or irradiation-induced cracking. Visual inspection confirmed that all three capsules
remained structurally intact, with no evidence of damage to the external housing, as shown in Fig. 3.

September 2026 4

 Figure 3: As-received irradiated TZM specimen capsules following the HYD1 irradiation campaign:
 (a) Capsule 5, (b) Capsule 6, and (c) Capsule 13.

To assess the irradiated hydride specimens, each capsule was sectioned below the top end cap
using a low-speed precision saw. The cutting operation was successfully completed for all three
capsules without inducing cracking or other damage to the TZM housing. Following sectioning, the
internal specimen stack was carefully recovered from the capsule. Because the specimens were not
laser-engraved prior to irradiation, individual specimen identification relied solely on their loading
sequence within each capsule. Consequently, the disassembly procedure was performed carefully
to preserve specimen traceability. Each opened capsule was gently tilted to allow sequential
recovery of the specimens, beginning at the top end cap and progressing toward the bottom end cap,
while maintaining the original specimen order. Representative photographs of the capsule
disassembly process are shown in Fig. 4.

Following specimen recovery, the irradiated YHx specimens were allocated between ORNL and

4 PIE PLAN

(SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and scanning/transmission electron microscopy
(S/TEM). Corresponding unirradiated YHx specimens were also characterized using the same
experimental techniques to establish baseline material properties for comparison with the
irradiated specimens. The complete PIE matrix adopted for the present study is summarized in

neutron irradiation on YHx. Visual inspection and mass measurements were used to establish the
initial condition of the specimens and identify any macroscopic degradation. Sound-velocity
measurements provided a non-destructive assessment of the elastic properties and structural
integrity of the material. LFA and dilatometry quantified changes in thermal diffusivity and thermal
expansion behavior, respectively, both of which are important parameters for evaluating moderator
performance at elevated (reactor-operating) temperatures. SEM imaging and EBSD mapping were
performed on the polished specimen faces in the as-irradiated condition to characterize grain
morphology, cracking, porosity, and crystallographic features. Site-specific S/TEM foils were
prepared from selected regions of the as-irradiated faces, enabling examination of the surface and
near-surface microstructure at the nanoscale.

5.1 VISUAL EXAMINATION AND MASS MEASUREMENTS
Prior to thermophysical, mechanical, and microstructural characterization, each specimen was
subjected to visual examination and mass measurements to document its as-received condition
following irradiation and capsule disassembly.

  Figure 6: Keyence digital optical microscope used for visual inspection of the YHx specimens at LAMDA.

Optical examination was performed using a Keyence VHX-6000 digital optical microscope (shown in
Fig. 6) located in LAMDA. High-resolution images were acquired for each specimen to document the

5.2 THERMAL PROPERTY CHARACTERIZATION
Thermal properties govern the ability of a moderator material to transfer heat and maintain
dimensional stability under reactor operating conditions. Accordingly, thermal diffusivity and
thermal expansion behavior of the YHx specimens were characterized using LFA (Section 5.2.1) and
dilatometry (Section 5.2.2), respectively.

5.2.1 LASER FLASH ANALYSIS (LFA)
LFA was performed to determine the thermal diffusivity of irradiated and unirradiated YHx
specimens, in accordance with ASTM E1461-13 [2]. Thermal diffusivity is an important

September 2026                                                                                         10

thermophysical property that describes the rate at which heat propagates through a material and
provides insight into irradiation-induced changes in thermal transport behavior. Such changes may
arise from microstructural modifications in the material, including defect formation, porosity
evolution, cracking, and phase transformations.

 Figure 7: Netzsch LFA 467 HT HyperFlash system used for thermal diffusivity measurements.

Measurements were conducted using a Netzsch LFA 467 HT HyperFlash system (Fig. 7) located in
LAMDA. Prior to testing, the thickness of each specimen was measured at multiple locations, and
the average thickness was used for the thermal diffusivity calculations. During each measurement,
a short-duration laser pulse was applied to one surface of the disk-shaped specimen, while the
transient temperature rise on the opposite surface was monitored using an infrared detector. The
measured rear-face temperature–time response was analyzed using the Cowan model
implemented in the Netzsch Proteus software to determine the thermal diffusivity of the specimen
as a function of temperature.
Each specimen was subjected to two consecutive heating cycles from room temperature (~25°C) to
800°C. The specimens were heated under a flowing argon atmosphere at a flow rate of 40 mL/min
and a constant heating rate of 20°C/min. Thermal diffusivity measurements were acquired at
approximately 50°C intervals during the heating cycle. All measurements were performed under an
inert atmosphere without controlling the hydrogen partial pressure. As a result, the specimens were
not maintained under pressure-concentration-temperature (PCT) equilibrium conditions during
testing. Therefore, the measured thermal diffusivity values represent the material condition during
thermal cycling rather than equilibrium thermophysical properties.
Owing to the potential for hydrogen desorption at elevated temperatures, only data from the first
heating cycle were used to determine the material properties, as measurements from subsequent
cycles could reflect changs in hydrogen stoichiometry or microstructure. The measured thermal
diffusivities of the unirradiated and irradiated YHx specimens are presented in Section 6.2.1.

5.2.2 DILATOMETRY
The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the irradiated and unirradiated YHx specimens was
determined by push-rod dilatometry, in accordance with ASTM E228-22 [3]. Dilatometry measures
the dimensional change of a specimen as a function of temperature, providing the linear thermal
expansion behavior and CTE, both of which are important for evaluating dimensional stability of
moderator materials under reactor operating conditions.

 Figure 8: Netzsch DIL 402 CD push-rod dilatometer used for CTE measurements.

Measurements were conducted using a Netzsch DIL 402 CD dilatometer (Fig. 8), with one specimen
tested at a time under a flowing helium atmosphere to minimize oxidation during thermal cycling.
Each specimen was subjected to two consecutive heating-cooling cycles. The first cycle extended
from room temperature to the corresponding irradiation temperature of the specimen (700°C or
800°C), while the second cycle extended from room temperature to 1000°C. Heating and cooling
were performed at a constant rate of 3°C/min with a helium flow rate of 150 mL/min. A dwell time of
1 min was maintained at the maximum temperature of each cycle, before cooling. Prior to specimen
testing, the instrument was calibrated using a tungsten reference specimen in accordance with the
manufacturer’s recommended calibration procedure.
During testing, the specimen was positioned between a fixed support and an alumina push rod
maintained under a small constant compressive load. As the specimen expanded or contracted
during thermal cycling, the corresponding displacement of the push rod was continuously recorded
by the instrument as a function of temperature. The measured displacement data were converted to
relative linear expansion (Δ𝐿⁄𝐿! ), where L0 is the initial specimen length at room temperature.
Instrumental contributions, including thermal expansion of the push rod and the measurement
system, were corrected automatically using the calibration data and the instrument’s built-in
correction procedure. The CTE was subsequently calculated from the corrected linear expansion
data using Netzsch Proteus analysis software. The CTE results obtained for the unirradiated and
irradiated YHx specimens are presented in Section 6.2.2. To minimize the influence of thermally

September 2026                                                                                  12

induced hydrogen desorption, the reported dilatometric properties were derived exclusively from the
initial heating cycle.

5.3 ELASTIC PROPERTY MEASUREMENTS
The elastic properties of the unirradiated and irradiated YHx specimens were evaluated using
ultrasonic sound-velocity measurements. Ultrasonic techniques provide a non-destructive means
of assessing the elastic response of materials by measuring the propagation velocity of elastic waves
through a specimen. Measurements of both longitudinal- and shear-wave velocities were performed
to characterize the elastic response of the YHx specimens.

  Figure 9: Olympus 5077PR square-wave pulser/receiver system used for sound-velocity measurements.

Sound-velocity measurements were performed at room temperature using an Olympus 5077PR
square-wave pulser/receiver system (Fig. 9), in accordance with ASTM C1419-14 [4] and ASTM
C769-15 [5]. Measurements were conducted using a through-transmission configuration, with
separate transmitter and receiver transducers positioned on opposite faces of the specimen.
Longitudinal-wave measurements were performed using Panametrics V106-RM, 12.7 mm-diameter
transducers, while Panametrics V154-RM transducers were used for shear-wave measurements.
Ultragel II ultrasonic gel couplant was applied between the longitudinal-wave transducers and the
corresponding specimen surfaces to ensure efficient transmission of the ultrasonic pulse. A shear
gel couplant was used for the shear-wave measurements to provide appropriate acoustic coupling
between the V154-RM transducers and the specimen surfaces.
Prior to data collection, the performance of the ultrasonic measurement system was verified using
an acrylic reference specimen with a known sonic velocity. For each specimen, the thickness and
diameter were measured prior to testing. The measured thickness was used to determine the
ultrasonic travel distance, while the measured dimensions, together with the specimen mass, were
used to calculate the specimen density. During testing, the instrument generated a short ultrasonic
pulse through the transmitter transducer that propagated through the specimen and was detected
by the receiver transducer on the opposite face. The analog ultrasonic signal output was processed

September 2026                                                                                    13

using a digitizer and recorded as a digital signal through the onboard data-acquisition system. The
time required for the pulse to travel through the specimen (time-of-flight) was determined from the
recorded signal, and the corresponding sound-velocity was calculated by dividing the specimen
thickness by the measured travel time. Measurements were repeated until at least three consistent
velocity readings were obtained for each configuration, and the resulting values were averaged to
obtain representative longitudinal- and shear-wave velocities for each specimen.
The elastic properties were calculated using the relationships provided in ASTM standards [4,5]. The
Young’s modulus (E) was calculated from the longitudinal- and shear-wave velocities (vL and vS,
respectively) and specimen density (r), while the shear modulus (G) was determined from the
measured shear-wave velocity and specimen density, using Equations (1) and (2) [6].

5.4 MICROSTRUCTURAL CHARACTERIZATION
Complementary electron microscopy techniques – SEM, EBSD, and S/TEM – were used to investigate
irradiation-induced features in the YHx specimens over multiple length scales. SEM/EBSD
(Section 5.4.1) provided information on surface morphology, grain structure, and crystallographic
orientation, whereas TEM (Section 5.4.2) enabled characterization of nanoscale defects.

5.4.1 SEM IMAGING AND EBSD MAPPING
SEM was used to characterize the surface condition and microstructure of selected non-irradiated
and irradiated YHx specimens, while EBSD in SEM was used to examine their grain morphology,
crystallographic orientation, and phase distribution. In SEM, a focused electron beam is scanned
across the specimen surface, generating signals that include secondary electrons, backscattered
electrons, diffracted electrons, and characteristic X-rays. These signals are collected using different
detectors to provide information on surface morphology, microstructural contrast, and elemental
composition. EBSD patterns are generated by the diffraction of backscattered electrons from
crystalline regions of the specimen and can be indexed to obtain spatially resolved information on
crystallographic orientation and crystal structure.

September 2026                                                                                      14

 Figure 10: TESCAN MIRA3 Schottky field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) equipped with
 EDS and EBSD detectors.

SEM imaging and EBSD characterization were performed using a TESCAN MIRA3 Schottky field-
emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM), equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS), back-scattered electron (BSE) and Oxford Symmetry EBSD detectors (as shown
in Fig. 10). SEM imaging and EBSD analysis were performed using an accelerating voltage of 30 kV.
SEM micrographs were acquired at magnifications ranging 32× to 5000× to document the specimen
surfaces and examine microstructural features at multiple length scales. Two representative regions
were selected from each specimen for detailed characterization. EBSD maps were acquired from
the selected regions at a magnification of 150× to characterize the phase distribution and
crystallographic orientation within the YHx specimens.
The SEM and EBSD characterization results for the YHx specimens are presented in
Sections 6.4.1and 6.4.2, respectively.

5.4.2 S/TEM IMAGING
S/TEM was used to characterize the microstructure of selected non-irradiated and irradiated YHx
specimens at the nanoscale. S/TEM requires electron-transparent specimens through which a high-
energy electron beam can be transmitted. Interactions between the electron beam and the
specimen generate transmitted, diffracted, and inelastically scattered electrons, as well as
characteristic X-rays. These signals can be collected using different detectors to obtain information
on the morphology, crystallographic structure, and elemental composition of microstructural
features.

September 2026                                                                                    15

 Figure 11: (a) FEI Versa3D DualBeamTM and (b) FEI Talos F200X S/TEM in LAMDA.

Electron-transparent specimens were prepared using focused ion beam–scanning electron
microscopy (FIB–SEM) lift-out techniques. An FEI Versa3D DualBeamTM (Fig. 11a) and an FEI Quanta
3D 200i DualBeamTM were used to obtain micron-scale lift-outs from selected regions of the YHx
specimen surfaces. Prior to lift-out, a platinum layer was deposited over the region of interest using
the FIB to protect the specimen surface during subsequent milling. A portion of the extracted lift-
outs was subsequently thinned to a thickness of approximately 150 nm or less to produce electron-
transparent foils suitable for S/TEM characterization. During thinning, the gallium ion beam current
was progressively reduced from 1.0 nA to 0.3 nA to minimize gallium implantation and ion-beam
damage to the foil. Final polishing was performed at 5 kV and 2 kV on both sides of each foil to further
reduce the damage introduced during FIB preparation. TEM lift-outs were placed on copper grids
rather than molybdenum grids to facilitate detection of any molybdenum potentially incorporated
into the specimens from the Mo spacer disks during irradiation.
TEM characterization was performed using an FEI Talos F200X TEM (Fig. 11b). Scanning transmission
electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (S/TEM-EDS) was used to
characterize the elemental composition and distribution of features at the surface and sub-surface
regions of the YHx specimens. S/TEM-EDS mapping was also used to identify changes in the yttrium
hydride matrix, such as oxidation. Because hydrogen cannot be detected using conventional S/TEM-
EDS, this technique alone could not be used to distinguish metallic yttrium from YHx. Selected-area
electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were, therefore, acquired from selected grain regions to provide
additional crystallographic information for phase identification. TEM bright-field imaging, including
through-focus imaging, was also performed to examine nanoscale features within the YHx matrix,
including cavities.
The TEM characterization results for the YHx specimens are presented in Section 6.4.3.

7 PRELIMINARY DISCUSSION
Unirradiated and irradiated specimens’ thermophysical properties showed variations. Firstly,
thermal diffusivity of unirradiated specimen was lower than the irradiated specimen which indicated
that the H/Y ratio of the unirradiated specimen was lower than that for irradiated specimens. On the
other hand, dilatometry data of unirradiated specimens showed no clear transition behavior,
suggesting high H/Y ratio. Comparison of H/Y ratio and sound–speed measurements for the
10-mm-thick specimens showed that several non-irradiated specimens exhibited lower sound
velocities than expected for their compositions. All these imply that further examination on

September 2026                                                                                       40

fabrication process is important to assure the product quality. Secondly, variation of thermophysical
properties for the specimens in the same host capsule reinforces the previous PIE results on in-
capsule hydrogen redistribution. Thus, capsule design must be done accordingly and no diversion
from the planned designs must be done except for severe critical issues.
Microstructural characterization provides the principal basis for interpreting the measured property
changes. SEM, EBSD, and TEM examinations showed that the irradiated specimens retained a
continuous, predominantly coarse-grained microstructure without observable cracking or porosity.
The coexistence of coarse d-YHx grains and striped a-Y regions indicates local heterogeneity in
hydrogen distribution throughout the hydride matrix. The stripe-like features may represent
localized, layered arrangements of metallic yttrium within or near the grain structure.
Comparable surface oxide layers on the unirradiated and irradiated specimens indicate that
oxidation was more likely associated with fabrication, storage, or handling than with irradiation. In
contrast, localized Ti-, Zr-, and Mo-rich precipitates within the irradiated matrix are consistent with
interaction between the hydride specimens, the TZM capsule, and the molybdenum spacer disks.
These precipitates do not appear to have controlled the measured bulk-property response, but they
identify a potential material-compatibility concern for long-duration operation.

8 CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK
ORNL has conducted PIE on MITR-irradiated YHx specimens. This study combined thermal
diffusivity, dilatometry, ultrasonic measurements, and multiscale microscopy to evaluate the post-
irradiation behavior of YHx specimens from Capsules 5, 6 and 13. The main conclusions are as
follows:

 • Structural integrity and microstructural evolution: The irradiated YHx retained a
 continuous, coarse-grained structure without extensive cracking, porosity, or nanoscale
 cavities. Irradiation introduced phase heterogeneity (d-YHx and a-Y) within the YHx matrix.

 • Coupled thermal and elastic response: Irradiation altered the temperature dependence of
 thermal diffusivity and thermal expansion, with the most consequential differences
 developing at elevated temperatures. The ultrasonic measurements also suggest a modified
 effective elastic response. Together with the observed phase heterogeneity (d-YHx and a-Y),
 these results are consistent with hydrogen redistribution or loss during irradiation.

 • Implications for moderator application: Hydrogen distribution or loss can modify heat
 transport and increase dimensional variability. Reduced high-temperature diffusivity may
 limit transient heat spreading, while enhanced expansion may affect thermal contact and
 generate stresses within a moderator assembly. The localized capsule-derived precipitates
 additionally identify compatibility with surrounding structural materials as a consideration
 for extended operation.
In summary, completion of the ORNL PIE represents an important step toward advancing hydride-
based moderators for microreactor applications. The recommended future activities will further

enhance technology readiness and support the development of other advanced nuclear
components.
