1) Abstract / Executive Summary

This project establishes a Cybersecurity Center for Offshore Wind Energy with the objective of
designing and operating a cyber-physical testbed for wind energy farms (WEFs) that enables
comprehensive cybersecurity research. The testbed incorporates a Supervisory Control and Data
Acquisition (SCADA) system connected to turbine models via industrial-grade programmable logic
controllers (PLCs) and remote terminal units (RTUs). It supports side-channel data acquisition,
implementation and analysis of various cyberattack scenarios, and development of attack detection,
mitigation, and best-practice guidance tailored to wind energy systems.

During the project, the team expanded the number and fidelity of mathematical turbine models
(MTMs), integrated these models with SCADA infrastructure, and deployed a scaled physical turbine
and associated sensors. High-resolution operational and side-channel data streams were collected
and used to refine machine-learning (ML)-based attack detection systems and to extend the
WindCRAFT framework to multi-turbine threat scenarios. The project demonstrated a realistic,
scalable environment for evaluating cyber threats, validated attack detection approaches using
enriched datasets, and identified new multi-turbine and inter-turbine communication attack vectors.
The resulting testbed, models, and security mechanisms provide a foundation for ongoing R&D and
deployment of cyber-resilient offshore wind energy systems.

2) Introduction and Project Overview

The Cybersecurity Center for Offshore Wind Energy aims to develop a dedicated, high-fidelity
environment for analyzing and improving the cybersecurity posture of offshore wind energy farms.
The overarching goals are to design a testbed that supports specialized research on wind energy
cybersecurity, to integrate operational and side-channel data sources, and to explore and mitigate
representative cyberattack scenarios targeting wind turbine control and communication systems.

The testbed includes a SCADA system that connects and controls turbine models and the WEF
through industrial-grade PLCs and RTUs. The project records side-channel information generated
by wind energy farm components and implements a range of attack scenarios, including
eavesdropping attacks. From these experiments, the team develops attack detection models,
resolution strategies, and best practices for securing wind energy systems.

3) Major Activities and Accomplishments

1. Expansion of Turbine Models and Data Acquisition

During the reporting period, the project continued to build on foundational work by expanding the
testbed infrastructure and increasing the fidelity of model turbines to support advanced threat

analysis. The number of mathematical turbine models (MTMs) integrated into the SCADA system
was extended to simulate a more realistic and scalable offshore WEF environment. The MTMs were
enhanced by incorporating additional quality parameters, including temperature profiles and
electromagnetic signatures of PLCs, to support detailed threat modeling.

The MTM setup was expanded to establish a data acquisition pipeline that collects high-resolution
operational data from both model turbines and a deployed field turbine. This pipeline enables precise
analysis of side-channel emissions and control signals. The extended MTMs were calibrated and
validated against real-world turbine benchmarks to ensure accurate emulation of turbine dynamics
under varying wind conditions. A small turbine was installed in the field and connected to the SCADA
server to provide real-time operational parameters. A modular framework for turbine model
deployment was implemented, supporting seamless integration of new turbine configurations and
sensor types into the existing SCADA infrastructure.

With these enhancements, the team-initiated threat analysis experiments using newly collected
quality parameters. These experiments targeted potential vulnerabilities and attack vectors specific
to turbine control and communication subsystems. Detection models were refined using enriched
datasets that incorporate the new turbine parameters, improving detection accuracy and reducing
false positives. The WindCRAFT framework was expanded to encompass multi-turbine threat
scenarios and vulnerabilities in inter-turbine communication.

During the project period, the team achieved several milestones in establishing the scaled-down
turbine model. A flexible and scalable prototype architecture was designed to support small, medium,
and large-scale wind turbine models. The laboratory-scale prototypes were constructed to replicate

The team expanded partnerships with 3D-printing facilities at the Virginia Modeling, Analysis and

Initially, motors and sensors were controlled using Raspberry Pi microcontrollers, with plans to

manufacturer to align the test environment with the security needs of future offshore installations.
Information exchanged with this company informed the architecture of the prototype, which draws
heavily on standard critical-infrastructure command-and-control systems.

4. PLC/RTU Integration and Control Architecture

The turbine models were integrated into a supervisory control system using a diverse set of field
devices, including industrial PLCs, RTUs, and custom-emulated PLCs. Industrial PLCs used in the
project include the Siemens Simatic S7-1200, Schneider Electric TM221CE16T, and Omron CP1H-
XA40DT-D. These PLCs are interfaced with the model turbines to bring distinct capabilities into the
system. Emulated PLCs were implemented using Raspberry Pi devices running OpenPLC software
to control both scaled-down and modeled turbines.

The Raspberry Pi-based OpenPLC setup provided a cost-effective, open-source control platform
programmed to manage turbine operations. This configuration created an effective bridge between
industrial control standards and educational prototyping. The combination of industrial and emulated
PLCs supported a comprehensive control architecture in which the SCADA system monitored real-
time turbine parameters, issued control commands, and enforced operational safety—for example,
initiating turbine shutdown when rotational speed exceeded configured thresholds. This integrated
architecture closely replicates real-world wind energy systems and aligns industrial technologies with
innovative, accessible solutions to create a functional supervisory framework.

4) SCADA System Design, Security, and Monitoring

 6

1. Rapid SCADA Overview and Configuration

Rapid SCADA, an open-source industrial automation platform, serves as the central SCADA system
for monitoring and controlling wind turbine processes in the testbed. It provides real-time data
acquisition from sensors and controllers, supports multiple communication protocols (including
Modbus, OPC UA, MQTT, and SNMP), and offers a web-based human–machine interface (HMI) for
system visualization. The platform includes configurable alarms and notifications, historical data
logging, and extensibility via custom drivers, scripts, and third-party integrations. Its core applications
are the Administrator (for configuration), Communicator (for device polling), and Server (for data
storage, processing, and visualization).

In the current implementation, Rapid SCADA is configured with multiple Modbus device connections
to interface with PLCs managing turbine operations. The SCADA system issues commands such as
“start” and “stop” to turbines and monitors key operational parameters. Communication with multiple
PLCs is maintained over Modbus TCP/IP, providing reliable and scalable connectivity across the
wind farm infrastructure.

Modbus integration is achieved through the Rapid SCADA Communicator module, which supports
both serial (Modbus RTU) and network-based (Modbus TCP) devices. Data mapping is configurable
to match each PLC’s register layout, and custom scripts written in C# can be embedded in drivers
or calculated channels for advanced data manipulation. Modbus polling options include configurable
read/write intervals, polling rates, and batch operations to balance communication efficiency with
timely data updates.

2. Secure Communications and OPC UA Integration

To secure Modbus TCP communication, Rapid SCADA traffic can be routed through an encrypted
Stunnel gateway. Communicator settings specify gateway IP and port, session timeout, and
reconnection policies, which together maintain secure and stable connectivity between devices and
the SCADA server. Sequential connection handling avoids persistent TCP connections, reducing
risks of port conflicts with other networked devices, and supports redundant connections for failover.

Rapid SCADA also supports integration with OPC UA servers through a dedicated client module.
This enables secure read/write access to OPC UA nodes, utilizing X.509 certificates for mutual
authentication and TLS transport security. Polling parameters, including polling interval, timeout, and
retry count, are configurable, and OPC UA nodes are mapped to SCADA channels to connect server
data to visualization and control. OPC UA commands can write numeric, string, or binary values to
specific nodes, giving SCADA operators full control over OPC UA-enabled devices

3. Authentication Proxy and SSI-Backed Trusted Device Access

 7

A key security innovation in the project is a Rapid SCADA device authentication workflow
implemented via a Python-based proxy server and device-side authentication client. This workflow
enforces trusted device communication using X.509 certificates and a Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)
signed credential. SCADA communication from a device is permitted only after successful
authentication.

In this architecture, a trusted Certificate Authority (CA) issues certificates to devices, each of which
is provisioned with a private key and CA-signed certificate. To authenticate, a device creates a
signed message containing a fixed integer value (0) and sends this, along with the device certificate,
CA certificate, and SSI credential, to the proxy. The proxy verifies the message content, validates
the certificate chain, confirms that the device certificate is signed by the CA, verifies the message
signature, and checks the SSI credential. Only if all checks succeed is the device IP authorized and
subsequent Modbus TCP traffic forwarded to Rapid SCADA. The proxy listens on port 1502 and
forwards authorized traffic to the SCADA server, typically on port 502, thereby enforcing an identity-
based access control perimeter for industrial communication.

5) SCADA System Design, Security, and Monitoring

1. Simulink-Based Turbine Model

The project developed an emulated wind turbine model through three stages that combined
simulation, embedded control, and industrial communication. The first stage used MATLAB Simulink
to build a turbine model that incorporates a wind turbine power block, blade pitch and generator
speed control, and an Arduino analog input to emulate varying wind speeds. The model computes
corresponding power and torque outputs as functions of these inputs.

The Simulink model represents a 2 MW nominal mechanical output turbine and implements tracking
characteristic speeds, a maximum pitch angle of 45 degrees, a pitch controller gain of 500, and a
maximum pitch angle rate of change of 2 degrees per second. Power curves, as shown in Figure 4,
derived from the model demonstrate how power output varies with generator speed at different wind
speeds, reveal optimal operating points, and indicate cut-in and power-limiting behavior. This
modeling framework enables maximum power point tracking, power prediction, control strategy
development, and definition of safe operational boundaries.

2. Wind Turbine Model Simulation and Cyber-Physical Analysis

The Simulink-based steady-state simulation characterizes variable-pitch turbine power behavior
(Figure 5). It captures relationships among wind speed, blade pitch angle, generator speed, and
mechanical power output without requiring direct manipulation of underlying equations. This virtual
testbed allows users to evaluate turbine behavior under diverse environmental conditions, test
control algorithms, and explore performance under different cyberattack scenarios.

Users can vary generator speed, pitch angle, and wind speed inputs to observe mechanical torque
output, enabling analysis across the full operational envelope from startup to generating and pitch-
brake modes. The simulation can be extended to include electrical components, grid connections,
and control systems, thereby supporting end-to-end modeling of wind energy conversion systems
and their response to cyberattacks.

3. Arduino-Based Turbine Emulation
To overcome limitations in cross-platform communication among MATLAB, Arduino, and Raspberry
Pi, the Simulink model was re-engineered into C code and executed on an Arduino microcontroller.

 10

This transition enabled tighter integration with external hardware and real-time visualization via an
LCD display. Wind speed is emulated using a potentiometer, and a DHT sensor captures ambient
temperature and humidity, introducing environmental realism into the model.

The model follows established wind energy design parameters, including a tip-speed ratio of 7, a
100-meter rotor diameter, a 4 m/s cut-in wind speed, and a 12 m/s rated wind speed corresponding
to 2.4 MW maximum power output. Blade pitch is adjusted in response to wind speed to maintain
smooth operation, and power output is computed using simplified physics equations that emphasize
data exchange and system behavior rather than exhaustive physical fidelity. The resulting Arduino
implementation embodies key operational characteristics of utility-scale turbines while remaining
accessible for integration with testbed infrastructure.

4. Integration with Raspberry Pi, OpenPLC, and MODBUS
In the final stage, the Arduino-based model was integrated with a Raspberry Pi running OpenPLC to
emulate industrial control systems. In this configuration, the Raspberry Pi acts as the supervisory
“principal,” issuing commands and monitoring turbine state, while the Arduino serves as the “agent”
executing those commands. The Raspberry Pi monitors turbine RPM, and when RPM exceeds a
threshold of 35 (reflecting safety limits based on real turbine designs), it sends a shutdown signal to
the Arduino. The Arduino then executes a SHUTDOWN routine that sets RPM, blade pitch, and
power output to zero, emulating protective behaviors in real turbines.

Communication between the Raspberry Pi and Arduino uses the MODBUS protocol, enabling
reliable sharing of RPM readings and control commands. This protocol choice also facilitates
integration with the larger SCADA environment. As a result, the project establishes a miniature but
highly representative control system that mirrors the communication and control patterns found in
operational wind farms, thereby supporting realistic cybersecurity experimentation.

6) Cybersecurity Research

1. WindCRAFT
 Central to the project’s cybersecurity efforts is the Wind Cyber Resilience Assessment
 Facilitation Tool (WindCRAFT), as shown in Figure 7, built using Bayesian networks with
 pgmpy 0.1.22 and PyMC3 3.11. This tool integrates threat intelligence from the MITRE
 ATT&CK framework (version 13.0) and the National Vulnerability Database to quantitatively
 assess cyber resilience. It empowers analysts to shift from conceptual risk assessments to a
 precise, data-driven platform, enhancing the development of targeted cybersecurity strategies.

 Figure 7. WindCraft: Output Window

 Machine learning models, developed with TensorFlow 2.12 and PyTorch 2.0, were trained on
 datasets derived from simulated cyberattacks. These models address scenarios including
 blade pitch manipulation, which forces pitch angles to extreme values like 90 degrees to halt
 power generation; overspeed attacks, which push generator speeds beyond safe thresholds;
 false data injection, which distorts sensor inputs like wind speed; remote access takeovers,
 which simulate unauthorized control; and power curve manipulation, which subtly degrades
 efficiency, defined in Table 1. Side-channel analysis further explored vulnerabilities, focusing

 12

 on electromagnetic signal leakage from microcontrollers and Programmable Logic Controllers,
 using a Rohde & Schwarz MXO-5 Oscilloscope to measure signal amplitudes across a 100
 MHz to 1 GHz frequency range.

 power generation
 Overspeed Attack Increases generator speed beyond safe limits, risking physical

 Remote Access Takeover Simulates unauthorized control, leading to erratic turbine behavior.

2. AI and Chatbot Development
 The CyberWind Assistant, a specialized chatbot, was designed to support users in interacting
 with wind energy data systems. It employs Retrieval-Augmented Generation techniques,
 incorporating Sentence Window Retrieval and Hybrid Search, which blends keyword and
 semantic vector search via SentenceTransformers 2.2.2, with post-retrieval re-ranking

 language model, the chatbot stores embeddings in a Qdrant vector database and operates on
 a Kubernetes cluster for scalability. It excels in tasks such as dataset navigation, real-time data
 analysis, and technical support, enhancing user accessibility and operational efficiency. The

 The project introduced two innovative platforms, detailed in papers accepted for the 21st
 International Wireless Communications & Mobile Computing Conference (IWCMC 2025). The
 Bassa-Llama platform focuses on real-time network attack detection in wind power plants,
 utilizing a fine-tuned Llama-3 large language model optimized with Quantized Low-Rank
 Adaptation. It integrates the Rahasak blockchain with smart contracts and a custom NFT
 schema (k-528) to encode malicious packet capture data, ensuring data integrity and
 traceability, with a data lake storing preprocessed datasets featuring attributes like packet size,
 timestamps, and protocol types. The VindSec-Llama platform offers a multilayered data
 security architecture, combining Generative AI, blockchain, federated learning, and Product Bill
 of Materials. It features a coordinatorless federated learning system for privacy-preserving
 machine learning across distributed wind farms, using a fine-tuned Llama-3 model to generate
 remediation scripts, such as Ansible playbooks, and Product Bill of Materials in JSON schema,
 adhering to NIST SP 800-53 guidelines with OpenSCAP for vulnerability scanning.

 Two of our papers have been published in the 21st International Wireless Communications and
 Mobile Computing (IWCMC) Conference, May 12–16, 2025.

7) Significant Results, Lessons Learned, and Discussion

Across the project, the team achieved several key milestones that significantly advance
cybersecurity research for offshore wind energy:
  Deployment of a multi-turbine model testbed that enhances realism and scalability of the
 research environment.
  Collection and analysis framework of high-quality turbine data, enabling deep insights into
 operational behavior and vulnerabilities.
  Improved accuracy of attack detection models driven by enriched datasets and advanced ML
 techniques.
  Identification of new threat vectors, such as coordinated multi-turbine attacks and exploitation
 of inter-turbine communication protocols.
  Extension of the WindCRAFT framework to simulate and mitigate complex, multi-component
 cyberattack scenarios.
  Validation of a modular turbine modeling approach that supports rapid prototyping and

 14

 evaluation of new configurations and control strategies.

Lessons learned include practical insights into using Rapid SCADA across operating systems,
resolving software dependencies (e.g., ModRSsim2 installation issues), and troubleshooting
common SCADA communication problems such as illegal data address errors, connection refusals,
and missing log files. Additional experience was gained in debugging custom C# drivers and
managing SCADA web services via IIS. These lessons will inform both future development of the
testbed and best-practice for model turbine connectivity and related issues.

8) Conclusions and Future Work

The Cybersecurity Center for Offshore Wind Energy successfully established an integrated cyber-
physical testbed combining physical turbine prototypes, mathematical turbine models, industrial-
grade PLCs, emulated PLCs, and a secure SCADA environment. The project demonstrated realistic
multi-turbine operations, collected diverse operational and side-channel datasets, and developed
and refined detection models and security workflows that address emerging threats in offshore wind
energy systems. Future work can build on this foundation by:
  Expanding the scope and complexity of attack scenarios, including coordinated attacks and
 stealthy side-channel-based intrusions.
  Further integrating grid-level models to study impacts of turbine-level attacks on broader
 power system stability.
  Enhancing LLM- and ML-based detection systems with additional real-world data and
 adversarial testing.
  Transitioning testbed capabilities into training and operational support tools for utility and
 offshore wind operators.
These directions will help translate the project’s research outcomes into practical guidance and
technology solutions that enhance the cybersecurity and resilience of offshore wind energy
infrastructure.
