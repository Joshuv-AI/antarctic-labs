Convex geometries and directed paths on three vertices
Júlio Araújo, Mitre C. Dourado, Marisa Gutierrez, Fabio Protti, Silvia Tondato

Abstract
A convexity space is an ordered pair ( V , 𝒞 ) (V,\mathcal{C}) , where V V is an arbitrary set and 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is a family of subsets of V V , called convex sets , which contains { ∅ , V } \{\emptyset,V\} and is closed under intersections and nested unions of its elements. For any S ⊆ V S\subseteq V , the convex hull of S S is the inclusion-wise minimum convex set C ∈ 𝒞 C\in\mathcal{C} such that S ⊆ C S\subseteq C . For a convex set C ∈ 𝒞 C\in\mathcal{C} , an element p ∈ C p\in C is an extreme of C C if p p does not belong to the convex hull of C ∖ { p } C\setminus\{p\} . A convexity 𝒞 \mathcal{C} defined over V V is a convex geometry (a.k.a. geometric convexity ) if any convex set C ∈ 𝒞 C\in\mathcal{C} is the convex hull of its extreme elements. Given an oriented graph D = ( V , A ) D=(V,A) , the family 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of subsets of V V is the P_3 \opthree -convexity defined over D D if 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is formed by all (convex) sets C ⊆ V C\subseteq V satisfying the following property: no vertex v ∈ V ∖ C v\in V\setminus C is the central vertex of a directed path P = ( u , v , w ) P=(u,v,w) with { u , w } ⊆ C \{u,w\}\subseteq C . In the P_3^* \opthrees -convexity defined over D D , we have that 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is formed by all (convex) sets C ⊆ V C\subseteq V satisfying the following property: no vertex v ∈ V ∖ C v\in V\setminus C is the central vertex of a directed path P = ( u , v , w ) P=(u,v,w) such that { u , w } ⊆ C \{u,w\}\subseteq C and ( u , w ) ∉ A (u,w)\notin A . In this work, we present necessary and sufficient conditions over an oriented graph D D so that the P_3 \opthree -convexity over D D is geometric, or the P_3^* \opthrees -convexity over D D is geometric. While the first case implies a polynomial-time algorithm to decide whether the P_3 \opthree -convexity over D D is a geometric, we show that it is 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{coNP}} to decide whether the P_3^* \opthrees -convexity over D D is a convex geometry. We also present a family of oriented graphs, termed acyclic indifference oriented graph, and demonstrate that deciding whether the P_3^* \opthrees -convexity over an acyclic indifference oriented graph is geometric can be solved in polynomial-time. Keywords: Graph convexity; Oriented graphs ; P_3 \opthree -convexity; P_3^* \opthrees -convexity

For further notions on Graph Theory and Abstract Convexity, the reader is referred to

A
convexity space
is an ordered pair
(V,\mathcal{C})
, where
V
is an arbitrary set, and
\mathcal{C}
is a family of subsets of
V
, called
convex sets
, that satisfies:

a)

\emptyset,V\in\mathcal{C}
;

b)

For all
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, we have
\bigcap\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{C}
;

c)

Every nested union of convex sets is a convex set.

In the case of a finite set
V
, the condition of nested unions is always satisfied, and we can only consider the first two.

We often say that the family
\mathcal{C}
is a
convexity
over
V
. For any
S\subseteq V
, the
convex hull
of
S
is the inclusion-wise minimum convex set
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
S\subseteq C
. For a convex set
C\in\mathcal{C}
, an element

is an
extreme
of
C
if
p
does not belong to the convex hull of
C\setminus\{p\}
.

For a positive integer

, it is well-known that a convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is any subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{d}
such that, for any two points
p_{1},p_{2}\in S
, the points in the straight-line segment linking
p_{1}
to
p_{2}
must also belong to
S
. In this context, a well-known result is that any convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is closed and bounded corresponds to the convex hull of its extreme points
[
KreinMilman1940
,
Minkowski1911
]
. More generally, a convexity
\mathcal{C}
defined over
V
is a
convex geometry
(a.k.a.
geometric convexity
) if any convex set
C\in\mathcal{C}
is the convex hull of its extreme elements. When it is not necessarily the case, given a convex set
C
, we say that
C
is
geometric
if
C
is the convex hull of its extreme elements.

When the set
V
is the set of vertices of a (directed) graph, the convexity space
(V,\mathcal{C})
is a
graph convexity
. There are several graph convexities defined in the literature
[
ADPS2025
]
. The study of graph convexities has received a lot of attention from the community, especially in the last 25 years. Most of them study (non-directed) graphs, even if some of the older works in this domain address the oriented case
[
erdHos1972some
,
pfaltz1971convexity
]
.

Most graph convexities are defined in terms of (directed) paths of the input (resp. directed) graph. More precisely, the convex sets
C
in the family
\mathcal{C}
satisfy the following property: if
u,w\in C
, then all internal vertices of (resp. directed)
(u,w)
-paths satisfying some constraint
\Pi
must also belong to
C
. When the constraint
\Pi
is that the path is a shortest path, then the graph convexity is called

. If
\Pi
corresponds to the property of being an induced path, then the convexity is called
monophonic
. When
\Pi
requires that the path has length two, we have the
\opthree
-
convexity
, while if the path is further required to be an
induced/shortest
path on three vertices, then such convexity is known as
\opthrees
-
convexity
.

Note that, by definition, for any
u,w\in C
, one must consider all directed
(u,w)
-paths and all directed
(w,u)
-paths satisfying the constraint
\Pi
, which of course, may not necessarily contain the same internal vertices in the directed case.

Graph convexities defined over directed graphs in the literature usually assume that they are indeed oriented graphs, which is a hypothesis that we also consider in this work. Recall that
D
is an
oriented graph
if it is an orientation of a simple graph
G
, i.e.
D
is obtained from
G
by replacing each edge of
G
by exactly one arc with the same endpoints. Consequently,
D
is a directed graph without loops, nor parallel, or symmetric arcs.

Although graph convexity has been more extensively studied than digraph convexity, there is a substantial body of literature dedicated to
\opthree
-convexity
[

,

,

In a recent work

Our goal in this work is to study which are the oriented graphs

\opthree

We start our main contribution by presenting a characterization of convex geometries in the
\opthree
-convexity that naturally leads to a polynomial-time algorithm to recognize such graphs.

Theorem 3
.

An oriented graph
D
is a convex geometry in the
\opthree
-convexity if and only if all the following conditions are valid:

1.

D
is acyclic;

2.

for all distinct
x,y\in V(D)
such that
y
is a descendant of
x
, we have
\overrightarrow{\dist}(x,y)\leq 2
; and

3.

if
D
has an induced subdigraph isomorphic to an
\oofour
with vertex set
\{a,b,c,d\}
, being
a
and
d
its endpoints, then
\{b,c\}\subseteq\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthree}^{D}(\{a,d\})
.

Proof.

Assume first that
D
is a convex geometry in the
\opthree
-convexity. If
D
contains an oriented cycle
C
, then
\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthree}^{D}(V(C))
is a convex set with no extreme vertices, thanks to Proposition
1
and to Observation
1
, a contradiction. Thus, Condition
1
is valid.

Suppose now that
\overrightarrow{\dist}(x,y)=k>2
, for some
x,y\in V(D)
such that
y
is a descendant of
x
. Let
P
be a directed path from
x
to

of length
k
in

. Since

is acyclic and

is a shortest directed
(x,y)
-path, note that

is an induced subdigraph of

isomorphic to
\overrightarrow{\Path_{k}}
,

. Then

is a convex set such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq\ext(X)
, but

, a contradiction. Thus, Condition
2
is valid.

Finally, suppose that

is a directed path such that the subdigraph
H
of

induced by

is isomorphic to
\opfour
or
\oofour
. Let

. Note that
Y
is a convex set,

(recall Observation

), and

. Since

. Thus, Condition
3
is valid.

Conversely, suppose that Conditions
1
,

, and

are valid and

is a convex set in the
\opthree
-convexity defined over

such that

.

By Condition

, note that

. Thus, let

. As

. Let

4.1
Characterizing the oriented geometric graphs

An oriented graph
D
is
H
-free
, given the oriented graph
H
, if
H
is not an induced subdigraph of
D
. For a family of oriented graphs
\mathcal{F}
,
D
is
\mathcal{F}
-free
if
D
is
H
-free for every
H\in\mathcal{F}
. Recall that DAG is an abbreviation for
directed acyclic graph
.

In the sequel, we define the class of oriented graphs that we prove in this section to be the class of
\opthrees
-geometries.

Let
u,v
be vertices of a DAG
D
. If there is a
(u,v)
-path in
D
, then we say that
u
is an
ancestor
of
v
and that
v
is a
descendant
of
u
in
D
.

Definition 1
.

We say that
H
belongs to class
\mathcal{H}
if
H
is a vertex minimal DAG that is
\{\opfour,\oofour\}
-free such that
V(H)
can be partitioned into three sets
B,C_{\ell}
and
C_{r}
such that there is a circular ordering
c^{1},\ldots,c^{\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}
, where
\ell^{\prime}=|C_{\ell}|
and
r^{\prime}=|C_{r}|
, of the vertices of
C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}
satisfying:

1.

\min\{|B|,\ell^{\prime},r^{\prime}\}\geq 2
;

2.

|B|\leq\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
;

3.

no vertex of
C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}
is the internal vertex of an induced path with both endpoints in
B
;

4.

for every positive
i\leq\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
, if
c^{i}\in C_{\ell}
, then there is
b\in B
such that
(b,c^{i},c^{i+1})
is an induced path; and

5.

for every positive
i\leq\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
, if
c^{i}\in C_{r}
, then there is
b\in B
such that
(c^{i+1},c^{i},b)
is an induced path.

In Definition
1
, we emphasize that
c^{i+1}=c^{1}
when
i=\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
. This is what we mean by circular ordering of
c^{1},\ldots,c^{\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider an
H\in\mathcal{H}
with partition
(B,C_{\ell},C_{r})
of

satisfying Definition
1
. Then

i)

For every positive
i\leq\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
, if
c^{i}\in C_{\ell}
, then
(c^{i},c^{i+1})\in A(H)
; otherwise,
(c^{i+1},c^{i})\in A(H)
.

ii)

No vertex of
C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}
is extreme.

iii)

For any
c\in C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}
, it holds
V(H)=\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthrees}^{H}(B\cup\{c\})
.

Proof.

Item
i)
and Item
ii)
follow directly from the conditions in Definition
1
, namely Item
4
and Item
5
.
To prove Item
iii)
, it suffices to show that for any
i\in[\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}]
, the predecessor of
c^{i}
in the given circular ordering belongs to
\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthrees}^{H}(B\cup\{c^{i}\})
. First, consider that
c^{i-1}\in C_{\ell}
.
Since
(b,c^{i-1},c^{i})
is an induced path, for some
b\in B
, we have that
c^{i-1}\in\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthrees}^{H}(B\cup\{c^{i}\})
.
Now, consider that
c^{i-1}\in C_{r}
.
Since
(c^{i},c^{i-1},b^{\prime})
is an induced path for some
b^{\prime}\in B
, we have that
c^{i-1}\in\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthrees}^{H}(B\cup\{c^{i}\})
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Every oriented graph of
\mathcal{H}
is not a convex geometry in the
\opthrees
-convexity.

Proof.

Let
H
be any member of
\mathcal{H}
. It suffices to present a subset

such that

is convex, but it is not the convex hull of its extreme vertices. Take

.
By definition,

is a convex set.
By Item

and Item

of Definition

is non-empty and composed of vertices that are not extreme.
By Item

, we have that
\hull_{\scriptscriptstyle\opthrees}^{H}(B)=B
, which means that

is not the convex hull of its extreme vertices.
∎

\par\vskip 12.0pt plus 4.0pt minus 4.0pt\par For the necessity, consider that there is a truth assignment $\phi$ that satisfies all clauses of ${\cal E}$. For every $i\in[m]$, denote by $\ell_{i,j_{i}}$ one of the literals of $C_{i}$ that has value true under $\phi$. Construct a set $S\subset V(G)$ as follows. For every $i\in[m]$, add $w_{i,j_{i}}$ and $z_{i,j_{i}}$ to $S$; for $i\in[t]$, add $w^{\prime}_{i}$ and $z^{\prime}_{i}$ to $S$; and add all vertices of $X\cup Y$ to $S$.
\par We claim that $S$ is a convex set. By contradiction, suppose that there are $a,c\in S$ and $b\not\in S$ such that $(a,b,c)$ is an induced directed path. By the construction of $G$, every vertex of $W$ is a source of $G$ and every vertex of $Z$ is a sink of $G$, which implies that $b\in X\cup Y$. But this yields a contradiction because $X\cup Y\subset S$. Therefore, $S$ is a convex set.
\par Next, we show that $S\cap(W\cup Z)=\extps(S)$.
Every vertex of $X\cup Y\subset S$ is not an extreme vertex of $S$ because of the following facts.
\par\begin{itemize}\par\itemize@item@for every $i\in[m]$, $x_{i}$ is not extreme in $S$ because $y_{i}\in S$, there is $w_{i,j}\in S$ for some $j\in[3]$ and $(w_{i,j},x_{i},y_{i})$ is an induced directed path;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@the vertex $y_{1}$ is not extreme in $S$, because $x^{\prime}_{t}\in S$ and there is $z_{1,j}\in S$ for some $j\in[3]$, and the dipath $(x^{\prime}_{t},y_{1},z_{1,j})$ is induced;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@for every $i\in\{2,\ldots,m\}$, $y_{i}$ is not extreme in $S$ because $x_{i-1}\in S$, there is $z_{i,j}\in S$ for some $j\in[3]$ and $(x_{i-1},y_{i},z_{i,j})$ is an induced dipath;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@for every $i\in[t]$, $(w^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})$ is an induced dipath and $w^{\prime}_{i},y^{\prime}_{i}\in S$;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@the vertex $y^{\prime}_{1}$ is not extreme in $S$ because $x_{m},z^{\prime}_{1}\in S$ and the dipath $(x_{m},y^{\prime}_{1},z^{\prime}_{1})$ is induced; and
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@for every $i\in\{2,\ldots,t\}$, $(x^{\prime}_{i-1},x^{\prime}_{i},z^{\prime}_{i})$ is an induced dipath, and $x^{\prime}_{i-1},z^{\prime}_{i}\in S$.
\par\end{itemize}\par Hence, since every vertex of $W$ is a source of $G$ and every vertex of $Z$ is a sink of $G$, we have that $S\cap(W\cup Z)=\extps(S)$.
\par Now, we claim that $\hullops(\extps(S))=\extps(S)$. By contradiction, suppose the contrary, i.e., there are $w,z\in\extps(S)$ and $u\not\in\extps(S)$ such that $(w,u,z)$ is an induced dipath. By the previous arguments, it holds that $w\in S\cap W$, $z\in S\cap Z$, and $u\in X\cup Y$.
\par First, consider that $u=x_{i}$ for some $i\in[m]$.
Since $N^{-}(x_{i})=\{w_{i,1}$,$w_{i,2},$ $w_{i,3}\}$ and $N^{+}(x_{i})=\{z_{i,1},$ $z_{i,2},$ $z_{i,3},y^{\prime}\}$ for some $y^{\prime}\in Y$, we have that $w=w_{i,j}$ for some $j\in[3]$, and $z=z_{i,j^{\prime}}$ for some $j^{\prime}\in[3]$, which is not possible because $(w_{i,j},z_{i,j^{\prime}})$ is an arc of $G$.
\par Now, consider that $u=x^{\prime}_{i}$ for some $i\in[t]$. By the construction of $G$, every out-neighbor of $x^{\prime}_{i}$ which belongs to $Z$ is also an out-neighbor of $w^{\prime}_{i}$. Then we can assume that $w=w_{i,j}$ for some $i\in[m]$ and $j\in[3]$. We also have that every in-neighbor of $x^{\prime}_{i}$ belonging to $W$ is also an in-neighbor of $z^{\prime}_{i}$. Then we can assume that $z=z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}$ for some $i^{\prime}\in[m]$ and $j^{\prime}\in[3]$. Since $(w,u,z)$ is an induced path, $(w_{i,j},z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\not\in E(G)$, which means that the literals corresponding to $w_{i,j}$ and $z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}$ is a pair of ${\cal T}$, i.e., they belong to different clauses and one is the negation of the other, which contradicts the construction of $S$.
\par Next, consider that $u=y_{i}$ for some $i\in[m]$. Then,
$w=w^{\prime}_{t}$ if $i=1$ or $w=w_{i-1,j}$ for some $j\in[3]$ if $i\geq 2$,
and $z=z_{i,j^{\prime}}$ for some $j^{\prime}\in[3]$. But note that $(w,z_{i,j^{\prime}})$ is an arc of $G$, which is not possible.
\par At last, consider that $u=y^{\prime}_{i}$ for some $i\in[t]$.
For $i=1$, note that $y^{\prime}_{i}$ has only three in-neighbors belonging to $W$, namely $w_{m,1},w_{m,2}$ and $w_{m,3}$, and has only one out-neighbor belonging to $Z$, the vertex $z^{\prime}_{1}$. Moreover, we have that $(w_{m,j},z^{\prime}_{1})\in E(G)$ for every $j\in[3]$, which is not possible.
For every $i\geq 2$, note that $w^{\prime}_{i-1}$ is the onlyl in-neighbor of $y^{\prime}_{i}$ belonging to $W$, and $z^{\prime}_{i}$ is the only out-neighbor of $y^{\prime}_{i}$ belonging to $Z$. However, $(w^{\prime}_{i-1},z^{\prime}_{i})\in E(G)$, which implies that $G$ is not a convex geometry.
\par\vskip 12.0pt plus 4.0pt minus 4.0pt\par For the sufficiency, consider that $G$ is not a convex geometry.
We begin by showing that $G$ is $\{\opfour,\oofour\}$-free.
Note that $W,X,Y$ and $Z$ are independent sets. Note also
that there is no arc from a vertex of $X\cup Y\cup Z$ to a vertex of $W$,
that there is no arc from a vertex of $Y\cup Z$ to a vertex of $X$,
and that there is no arc from a vertex of $Z$ to a vertex of $Y$.
Therefore, if $G$ has an induced $\opfour$ or an induced $\oofour$ with arcs $(w,x),(x,y)$ and $(y,z)$, we would have that
$w\in W$,
$x\in X$,
$y\in Y$, and
$z\in Z$.
Now, note that among the out-neighbors of every $x^{\prime}\in X$, two belong to $Y$, say $y^{\prime}$ and $y^{\prime\prime}$.
Note that one of these two vertices, let us say $y^{\prime}$, has as in-neighbors all in-neighbors of $x^{\prime}$.
Therefore, $x^{\prime}$ and $y^{\prime}$ do not belong to an induced $\opfour$ nor an induced $\oofour$.
Note also that $x^{\prime}$ is an in-neighbor of all out-neighbors of $y^{\prime\prime}$.
Therefore, $x^{\prime}$ and $y^{\prime\prime}$ do not belong to an induced $\opfour$ nor an induced $\oofour$, which means that $G$ is $\{\opfour,\oofour\}$-free.
\par Therefore, by Theorem~\ref{thm:ops-geometries2}, $G$ has an induced subgraph $H\in{\cal H}$ such that $V(H)\setminus\hullops(B^{\prime})\neq\emptyset$ where $B^{\prime}=\extps(\hullops(V(H)))$. Let $(B,C_{\ell}=\{c_{\ell}^{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\},C_{r}=\{c_{r}^{1},\ldots,c_{r}^{r^{\prime}}\})$ be a partition of $V(H)$ and $c^{1},\ldots,c^{\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}$ be a circular ordering of the vertices of $C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}$ satisfying Definition \ref{def:familyH}.
\par By Item \ref{ite:Clnoextreme} of Definition \ref{def:familyH},
for every $i\in[\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}]$, if $c^{i}\in C_{\ell}$, then there is $b\in B$ such that $(b,c^{i},c^{i+1})$ is an induced path. Therefore $C_{\ell}\subset X\cup Y$.
By Item \ref{ite:Crnoextreme} of Definition \ref{def:familyH},
for $i\in[\ell^{\prime}+r^{\prime}]$, if $c^{i}\in C_{r}$, then there is $b\in B$ such that $(c^{i+1},c^{i},b)$ is an induced path. Therefore $C_{r}\subset X\cup Y$.
\par Now, note that in the subgraph $G[X\cup Y]$, every vertex of $X$ has in-degree zero and out-degree two, while every vertex of $Y$ has in-degree two and out-degree zero. Therefore, using Item \ref{ite:lrcycle} of Lemma \ref{lem:cons}, we conclude that $C_{\ell}\subseteq X$ and $C_{r}\subseteq Y$.
\par Furthermore, for every $x\in X\cap C_{\ell}$, among the two out-neighbors of $x$ in the graph $G[X\cup Y]$, only one can be the vertex $y\in Y\cap C_{r}$ such that $(b,x,y)$ is an induced path for some $b\in B$.
Similarly, for every $y\in Y\cap C_{r}$, among the two in-neighbors of $y$ in the graph $G[X\cup Y]$, only one can be the vertex $x\in X\cap C_{\ell}$ such that $(x,y,b)$ is an induced path for some $b\in B$.
Therefore, by the circular ordering of $C_{\ell}\cup C_{r}$, we conclude that $C_{\ell}=X$ and $C_{r}=Y$.
\par Since every vertex of $W\cup Z$ is extreme in $G$ and $B\subset W\cup Z$, we have that every vertex of $B$ is an extreme of $\hull(V(H))$, which means that $B=B^{\prime}$ and $\hull(V(H))\setminus B\subseteq X\cup Y$.
Since $V(H)\setminus\hullops(B)\neq\emptyset$, using Item \ref{ite:one} of Lemma \ref{lem:cons}, we conclude that $\hullops(\extps(\hullops(V(H))))=\hullops(B)$ does not have any vertex of $X\cup Y$.
\par Using Item \ref{ite:Cnoextreme} of Lemma \ref{lem:cons}, we have that every vertex of $C_{\ell}$ has at least one in-neighbor $w_{i}$ of $B$ belonging to $W$.
Note that for different positive values $i,j\leq m$, we have that $N^{-}(x_{i})\cap N^{-}(x_{j})=\emptyset$, which means that $w_{i}\neq w_{j}$ as long as $i\neq j$.
Let $S=\{w_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}$.
Analogously, we have that every vertex of $C_{r}$ has at least one out-neighbor $z_{i}$ of $B$ belonging to $Z$. Note that for different positive values $i,j\leq m$, we have that $N^{+}(z_{i})\cap N^{+}(z_{j})=\emptyset$, which means that $z_{i}\neq z_{j}$ as long as $i\neq j$.
Furthermore, for $w_{i}=w_{i,q}$ and $z_{i}=w_{i,q^{\prime}}$, we have that $q=q^{\prime}$ because if $q\neq q^{\prime}$, then $(w_{i,q},z_{i,q^{\prime}})\not\in E(G)$ and $(w_{i,q},x_{i},z_{i,q^{\prime}})$ is an induced path, which means that $\hullops(B)$ contains a vertex of $X\cup Y$, which is not true.
\par To complete the proof, it suffices to show that for any two vertices of $S$, the corresponding literals belong to different clauses and that one is not the negation of the other.
By the construction of $S$, it is clear that any two vertices correspond to literals of different clauses. Then, suppose by contradiction that there are positive values $i,j\leq m$ such that the vertices $w_{i}=w_{p,q}\in S$ and $w_{j}=w_{r,s}\in S$ correspond to literals, with one being the negation of the other.
Due to the assumption that there is no positive $i\leq m-1$ such that $C_{i}$ contains a literal that is the negation of a literal of $C_{i+1}$, we have that $|p-r|\geq 2$.
Then $\{w_{p,q},w_{r,s}\}$ is a pair of vertices associated with a pair $\{\ell_{p,q},\ell_{r,s}\}\in{\cal T}$. Assume that $\{\ell_{p,q},\ell_{r,s}\}$ is the $k-th$ pair of ${\cal T}$ in the considered ordering to construct $G$.
Note that $(w_{p,q},x^{\prime}_{k},z_{r,s})$ is an induced path where $w_{p,q},z_{r,s}\in B$, which means that
$\hullops(B)$ contains vertices of $X\cup Y$, a contradiction.
\end@proof
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Acyclic indifference oriented graphs: a polynomial case.}
\par\par\par In this section, we present a new class of oriented graphs, to the best of our knowledge, in which deciding if an oriented graph is a geometry with respect
to $\opthrees$-convexity is polynomial-time solvable. The definition of this class is inspired by the class of indifference graphs.
\par A graph $G$ is an \emph{indifference} graph if and only if there is a total order $\prec$ over $V(G)$ such that if $u\prec v\prec w$ and $uw\in E(G)$, then $uv,vw\in E(G)$~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{LO1993}{}{}]}. Instead of a total order, we consider a partial order, and the maximal cliques are replaced by maximal transitive tournaments. A related subclass was presented in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{tesisGuada}{}{}]}, where the author considers a tree-like order in which the Hasse diagram of the partial order is a tree.
\par\par\par\begin{definition}Let $D$ be an oriented acyclic graph. We say that $D$ is {acyclic indifference} if there is a partial order $\prec$ on $V(D)$ such that:
\par\begin{enumerate}\enumerate@item@if $(x,y)\in A(D)$, then $x\prec y$; and
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\enumerate@item@if $x$, $y$, $z$ are vertices of $D$ with $x\prec y\prec z$ and
$(x,z)\in A(D)$, then $(x,y)\in A(D)$ and $(y,z)\in A(D)$.
\end{enumerate}\end{definition}\par\par See Figure \ref{fig:exampleToAcyclicIndif} for an example of an acyclic indifference oriented graph.

\@add@centering\end{figure}\par An equivalent way to define the graphs in the class is as follows.
\begin{observation}A DAG $D$ is an {acyclic indifference} if and only if for any vertices $x$, $y$, $z$ of $D$ such that there are an $(x,y)$-path, a $(y,z)$-path, and $(x,z)\in A(D)$, it holds that $(x,y)\in A(D)$ and $(y,z)\in A(D)$.
\end{observation}\par Note that this class is hereditary and $\oofour$-free.
\par\par\begin{theorem}An acyclic indifference oriented graph $D$ is a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$- convexity if and only if $D$ is $\overrightarrow{P_{4}}$-free.
\end{theorem}\@proof Let $D$ be an acyclic indifference oriented graph. By definition, recall that $D$ is a DAG and $D$ is $\oofour$-free. If $D$ is a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity, then $D$ must be $\opfour$-free, by Theorem~\ref{thm:ops-geometries2}.
\par Conversely, every convex subset of an acyclic indifference oriented graph $D$ that is $\overrightarrow{P_{4}}$-free induces a subdigraph in the same class, as the family is hereditary, and vertex removal cannot create an induced $\overrightarrow{P_{4}}$. Thus, it suffices to prove that $V(D)$ is the hull of its extreme vertices. Let $\prec$ be a partial order over $V(D)$ as in Definition \ref{def:acyclic-indifference}.
\par Since $D$ is acyclic, it has at least one source and at least one sink. Then $V(D)$ has extreme vertices.
Suppose that $V(D)$ has at least one non-extreme vertex $a$, as otherwise $V(D)$ is trivially the convex hull of its extreme vertices.
\par Thus, there exist $x,y\in V(D)$ such that $(x,a),(a,y)\in A(D)$, but $(x,y)\notin A(D)$. Among all maximal transitive tournaments that contain both $x$ and $a$, let $T$ be one whose source $s$ is a minimal element, with respect to the order $\prec$, in the subset of vertices of $V(D)$ composed of all sources of all maximal transitive tournaments containing both $x$ and $a$. Let us prove that $s$ is a source of $D$.
\par By contradiction, suppose that it is not the case and let $s^{\prime}\in V(D)$ be such that $(s^{\prime},s)\in A(D)$. By the choice of $T$, we claim that $(s^{\prime},a)\notin T$. Otherwise, by the definition of acyclic indifference oriented graphs, note that $s^{\prime}$, $s$, $x$, and $a$ would belong to another maximal transitive tournament $T^{\prime}$ in which $s$ is not a source, contradicting its minimality in $\prec$. Note also that $(s^{\prime},y)\notin A(D)$ and $(s,y)\notin A(D)$, as $(x,y)\notin A(D)$ and $D$ is an acyclic indifference oriented graph. Consequently, $\{s^{\prime},s,a,y\}$ induces a $\opfour$, a contradiction. Therefore, $s$ must be a source of $D$.
\par Analogously, one can prove that there is a sink $t$ of $D$ such that $(a,t)\in A(D)$. Note that $(s,t)\notin A(D)$ as $(x,y)\notin A(D)$ and $D$ is an acyclic indifference oriented graph. Therefore, $a$ is in the convex hull of the extreme vertices of $D$, for any non-extreme vertex $a$ of $D$, which completes the proof.
Thus, by a simple brute-force algorithm to look for induced $\opfour$, one can deduce that deciding whether a given acyclic indifference oriented graph $D$ defines a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity can be done in $\mathcal{O}(n^{4})$-time, $n$ being the number of vertices of $D$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par Actually, the proof in Theorem \ref{thm:acyclic-indifference} implies that $D$ is a convex geometry if and only if each vertex is either a source, a sink, or lies in an induced $\opthrees$ whose endpoints are a source and a sink of $D$. Therefore, we may deduce that:
\par\begin{corollary}Deciding whether a given acyclic indifference oriented graph $D$ on $n$ vertices and $m$ arcs defines a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity can be done in ${\cal O}(n^{3})$-time.
\end{corollary}\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Further research}

One can observe that such oriented graphs must be still acyclic, but our proof of $\oofour$-freeness does not hold in this case. Actually, see Figure \ref{fig:badforP3} for an example of a convex geometry in the $\opthree$ convexity that is not a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity as it contains an induced directed path on four vertices.

\par\par Another interesting direction is to study the $\opthree$-convexity and try to determine which oriented graphs $D$ are convex geometries in the $\opthree$-convexity. One could also try to improve the polynomial-time algorithm to decide whether an oriented graph is a convex geometry in the $\opthree$-convexity, which is a consequence of Theorem \ref{thm:P3-convexity}.
\par Since deciding whether a directed graph is a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity is ${\mathsf{coNP}}$-complete, we can ask whether there are polynomial-time algorithms to decide whether a directed graph is a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity when it is restricted to particular graph classes. For example, for graphs with bounded treewidth, one can use Courcelle's theorem to decide whether an oriented graph $D$ is a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity in polynomial time. Indeed, the authors in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{AMM+2026}{}{}]} present MSO formulas to represent the property of $S$ being a convex set and for $S^{\prime}$ being the convex hull of $S$ in the $\opthrees$-convexity. It is straightforward to propose formulas for the properties of a vertex being extreme, and thus for $D$ being a convex geometry in the $\opthrees$-convexity.
\par However, the time-complexity of these algorithms is known to be highly exponential in the value of the treewidth. Adhoc algorithms for specific graph classes are still of interest. One can also address this problem for other graph classes, such as bipartite graphs, planar graphs, etc.
