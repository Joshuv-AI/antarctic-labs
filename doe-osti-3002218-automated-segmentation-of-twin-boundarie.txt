 ABSTRACT
Coated particle fuels, such as the tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel particle, are essential for
high-temperature gas reactor (HTGR) applications due to their efficiency and stability under normal
and off-normal conditions. However, widespread commercialization and deployment of this technology
for next-generation nuclear applications require robust quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)
methods linking fabrication, properties, and performance. Of the many important metrics for TRISO
QA/QC, quantification of the silicon carbide (SiC) microstructure is critical because it correlates with
fission product retention during irradiation. Previous work has shown extensive twinning of the SiC
microstructure, which strongly affects microstructural metrics; however, twin grain boundaries are
not expected play a significant role in fission product diffusion. This report summarizes the initial
development, training, and testing of a machine learning image processing algorithm to detect twin grain
boundaries in a backscattered electron image, which can be removed so that microstructural metrics can be
recalculated for legacy data. Further development and deployment of this model will provide automated,
scalable improvement of potential QA/QC methods for the SiC layer of TRISO particles.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Tristructural isotropic (TRISO)–coated fuel particles have been engineered as high-performance nuclear
fuels for advanced reactor technologies, including high-temperature gas reactors (HTGRs), molten salt
reactors, and as fully ceramic encapsulated fuels within light-water reactors (LWRs) [1, 2, 3]. A standard
TRISO particle features a uranium-based fuel kernel surrounded by a sequence of coatings: a porous
carbon buffer layer, a dense inner pyrolytic carbon layer, a silicon carbide (SiC) layer, and a final outer
pyrolytic carbon layer. Each of these coatings serves a distinct but complementary function, collectively
providing mechanical integrity and containment of fission products during reactor operation. Among these
layers, the SiC layer functions as the main structural barrier and is primarily responsible for retaining
fission products that escape the fuel kernel [4]. The microstructural characteristics of the SiC—including
grain size, shape, aspect ratio, and grain boundary character—play a critical role in the fission product
transport pathways [5]. The effective diffusion coefficient (De f f ) of these fission products can be described
by the Maxwell-Garnett equation (Eq. 1):

Accordingly, evaluating the SiC microstructure is a component of TRISO fuel quality assurance and
quality control (QA/QC). Presently, QA/QC practices for the SiC layer rely on qualitative visual inspection,
whereby samples are broadly classified based on micrographs. There is thus an opportunity to enhance
current methodologies by incorporating modern, quantitative image processing and analysis techniques.
Recent work at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has focused on the development of an
edge-detection image processing algorithm to map SiC grain boundaries from a backscattered electron
(BSE) image, allowing quantification of grain size via an ASTM standard [6, 7]. This approach represents

 1

an essential first step toward potentially establishing a quantitative QA/QC procedure for the SiC
layer microstructure of TRISO and other coated particle fuels. However, previous work on irradiated
TRISO fuel from the US Department of Energy’s Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) Fuel Development and
Qualification Program’s first and second irradiation campaigns, AGR-1 and AGR-2, showed that grain
boundary twinning in the SiC layer is prominent and significantly affects microstructural measurements.
For example, the average grain major axes and diameter nearly doubled in value when twins were removed
[5]. Despite this large quantitative impact on microstructural metrics, twin boundaries in SiC do not
contribute significantly to percolation pathways facilitating fission product release and are therefore less
important for TRISO or coated particle fuel QA/QC assessment [8, 9]. Presently, identification of twin
boundaries in a microstructure necessitates crystallographic information obtained via electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD). EBSD, though it is a robust and powerful characterization technique, is time and
labor-intensive and demands specialized equipment that is generally available only at universities and
national laboratories. Thus, there is a clear need for a simple, efficient, and industrially applicable method
to refine the existing QA/QC procedures for TRISO particle SiC microstructure quantification.
One method to address this need and implement modern data processing technologies is the use of
computer vision (CV). Computer vision is a field of machine learning that comprises data extraction and
hierarchical feature recognition in images. Some commonly known examples of CV applications in recent
years include facial recognition, handwriting identification and interpretation, and object recognition
in security camera footage. In addition to these general applications, academic work in recent years
has demonstrated CV’s great potential for microstructural analysis and identification [10, 11, 12, 13].
Specifically, the U-Net segmentation algorithm [14] has emerged as an excellent CV model for grain
structure and grain boundary analysis due to its ability to segment images on a pixel-by-pixel basis rather
than assign a single label to a whole image [15]. In the context of TRISO SiC QA/QC, this pixel-by-pixel
classification is promising for identifying twinned grain boundaries in a grain structure map based on
crystallographic training data.
The work documented in this report summarizes an ongoing effort at ORNL to refine existing workflows
for quantifying and assessing TRISO SiC microstructure properties for QA/QC methods development
[7]. As part of this effort, ORNL is exploring the use of a U-Net CV algorithm to identify twin grain
boundaries in the SiC layer of TRISO particles, remove them from the grain boundary structure, and then
re-calculate microstructure metrics such that they more closely correlate with fuel performance. While
previous work in the CV microstructure space typically uses the method for automated grain boundary
segmentation via edge detection, the work described herein is a novel implementation in which certain
grain boundaries (twins) are identified in the crystallographic training data, and the model is trained to
identify this distinct grain boundary character without any crystallographic input. Further development
and ultimate deployment of this model will offer efficient, automated re-assessment of legacy SiC BSE
images from past AGR irradiation campaigns to improve quantification of microstructural metrics for
relevant QA/QC analysis. Furthermore, commercial deployment of such a trained machine learning
model requiring only BSE images will allow high-throughput QA/QC analysis of coated particle fuels
fabricated at an industrial scale, with the ultimate goal being streamlined regulatory approval of advanced,
next-generation safety-tolerant fuel forms.

 2. METHODS
2.1 EXPERIMENTAL SAMPLE PREPARATION
To build a twin-detecting CV model from the ground up, the first step was to collect EBSD crystallographic
data of the SiC layer from past AGR TRISO particles to build a model training dataset. The training
dataset was generated from surrogate TRISO particles (STPs) representative of true TRISO particles
fabricated and tested for the AGR-2 campaign. The total dataset used for model training, testing,
and validation comprised 8 EBSD maps collected from the SiC layer of STPs from a previous study;
data collection and EBSD parameters are detailed in Gerczak et al. [5]. In addition, 11 EBSD maps
were collected from a separate AGR-2 STP to supplement the existing legacy dataset. For these data,
7 individual STPs were mounted using a Struers hot mounting system for metallographic sample
preparation. The mount was polished using a Struers Tegramin-30 polisher within 5 µm of the estimated
particle midplane, starting from a 9 µm suspension and concluding with extended polishing steps using
submicron colloidal suspensions. Colloidal suspension polishing provides chemical and mechanical
polishing during the final polishing steps, yielding a high-integrity surface that is ideal for EBSD data
collection. The full polishing procedure is shown below in Table 1.

2.2 EBSD TRAINING DATA COLLECTION AND POST-PROCESSING
Electron backscatter diffraction data were collected using a TESCAN MIRA3 scanning electron microscope
(SEM) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Microstructural maps were acquired at 10,000× magnification
with a step size of 0.075 µm to enable high-resolution characterization of grain boundaries and twin
structures in the SiC matrix. The SEM chamber was maintained under high vacuum, and samples were
tilted to 70° to optimize Kikuchi pattern quality during EBSD acquisition. All scans were performed on
the polished cross-sections to ensure a flat surface and reduce pattern degradation, and multiple scans were
performed at different regions of each STP SiC layer. It is noteworthy that the EBSD data from previous
work comprising a large portion of the dataset (i.e., the data from Gerczak et al. [5]) were collected at a
significantly lower magnification (2,300×) than the new data to capture the full width of the SiC coating.
However, this difference is beneficial for the model training as exposure to different conditions during
training is conducive to generalization of output predictions. This is further discussed in a subsequent
section.
For post-processing, raw EBSD data were indexed against the β-SiC (3C) polymorph, as previous work
characterizing the SiC layer of TRISO particles has found this to be the primary composition [16].

 3

Restriction of the indexing space to β-SiC yielded consistent map indexing with a confidence index
(CI) of greater than 0.5 on average, suggesting an overall good indexing of the SiC layer. Raw data
were cleaned in EDAX OIM Analysis; cleaning comprised, sequentially, a per-grain CI standardization,
neighbor orientation correlation, and grain dilation. OIM allows generation and representation of various
microstructural and crystallographic information from a single map: grain boundary maps, orientation
maps, and de-twinned microstructure maps. The most prominent twin grain boundary structures in β-SiC
are 60◦ <111> and 38.9◦ <110> [5]; these are identified in the EBSD training data as twin boundaries and
were incorporated into the data processing and preparation as discussed in subsequent sections.

2.3 BSE GRAIN BOUNDARY EDGE DETECTION
Ultimately, successful deployment of this U-Net model will consist of twin removal from SiC grain
boundary maps. Previous work at ORNL has successfully developed a manual edge-detection method for
grain boundary segmentation of BSE micrographs, which can be used for extraction of the SiC boundary
maps. The full procedure is described elsewhere [6, 7]; but, briefly described, this process consists of a
sweeping Canny edge detection across entire micrographs, followed by edge-linking to connect stray
segments. This algorithm was applied in the work described here to extract grain boundary maps from
various SiC BSE images; an example is presented in Figure 1. As shown in the figure, this technique
is applicable to variable magnification micrographs, demonstrating the versatility of this algorithm
across scales, which is essential for subsequent generalized model testing and deployment. From the
edge-segmented micrographs, the average grain area for each microstructure was determined using ASTM
standard E1382, which specifies automatic or semiautomatic image analysis methods [17].

2.4 U-NET MODEL DESIGN AND ARCHITECTURE
For complex microstructural segmentation and/or classification tasks, the U-Net algorithm is a strong
candidate due to its proven ability with limited training datasets and capability to resolve fine features
such as grain boundaries [14]. The network architecture comprises an encoder–decoder pathway that

 4

 Figure 2. Schematic of the U-Net machine learning model architecture [14].

resembles a U-shape as an input image is downsized and upsized; a schematic of the network architecture
is shown in Figure 2. The encoder (contracting path, left side of the U) downsizes an input image to extract
and capture features within the image, whereas the decoder (expanding path, right side of the U) upsizes
the learned images to predict a segmentation map from the input image. The encoder and decoder are
connected by a bridge (bottom of the U), which passes information from encoder to decoder. A key feature
of the U-Net architecture relative to other convolutional neural networks is the skip connection steps,
illustrated by the gray arrows in Figure 2 [18, 19]. During these steps, images are sent directly from the
contracting path to the expanding path without undergoing subsequent pooling and convolutions (red and
blue arrows, respectively). This allows both high-level features (via the skip connections) and low-level
features (via multiple convolutions and pooling) to be learned from the input images.
The U-Net model applied for this work was built and parameterized using MATLAB’s Deep Learning
Toolbox. It contains four encoder and decoder blocks, connected by a latent bridge. Each encoder
layer comprises two convolutional layers with 3×3 kernels and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations.
Downsampling through the encoder was achieved through 2×2 max pooling. The decoder mirrors this
structure, comprising 2×2 transposed convolutions to upsample and paired 3×3 convolutional layers with
ReLU activation, with skip connections using center cropping and feature concatenation. The final layer is
a softmax activation layer assigning a pixel-wise classification in the predicted microstructure map with a
specific probability.
Crystallographic EBSD maps to be used for model training were split into training/validation/test sets
with a ratio of 70/15/15. From each bulk EBSD map (≈ 2500×2000 pixels on average), small square
patches of size 256×256 were extracted randomly to focus on grain boundary features while maintaining
a representative dataset for each map; this patch size was iteratively determined to provide an optimal
balance of training speed/performance and spatial information of the boundary network. Each dataset
was further supplemented by data augmentation steps, including flips, rotations, and small translations to
increase the robustness of the training set [20]. Such augmentations are common in U-Net model training

 5

and allow spatial flexibility in the feature learning.
The main challenge in building an image segmentation model for this task is class imbalance; that is,
most pixels in the training and validation images correspond to grain interiors, and the relatively sparse
boundaries are the features of interest. To account for this, a weighted binary cross-entropy loss function
was used to assess model performance during training and validation, as shown in Equation (3):

                                  L = −W(yi log(pi ) + (1 − yi ) × log(1 − pi ))                           (3)
where L is the prediction loss, W is a weighting matrix for false negative and false positive twin boundary
predictions, yi is the ground truth (0 or 1) and pi is the output prediction probability (0 < pi < 1). For
sparse, imbalanced data like those under study for the current task, the vast majority of pixels in a
predicted image are likely to be ‘grain interior’ (as mentioned previously), which is a correct prediction
regardless of the accuracy of twin boundary classification. Thus, W is crucial for this training task, as
it is designed to weight the penalty for incorrect boundary predictions much more highly than that for
grain interiors, thereby guiding the model learning toward the sparse boundary structures. For all model
development approaches discussed in this report, W is defined as having a value of 1 for a correct pixel
classification, a value of 7.5 for a false positive (i.e., a predicted boundary probability > 0.5 where the
ground truth is a grain interior), and 15.0 for a false negative (i.e., a predicted boundary probability < 0.5
where the ground truth is a grain boundary). Both penalty weights are high to account for the sparsity
of the training data, and the false negative is weighted more heavily than the false positive to strongly
incentivize boundary identification within the sparse microstructure predictions. For initial development
of this model and technique, the specific classification labels vary based on the selected approach to
identify twinning in the SiC microstructure; two approaches for the initial stages of model development are
discussed in this report. In all cases, model prediction performance was assessed by calculating common
ML metrics (precision, recall, and F1 score) for changing values of a probability mask threshold applied to
the predicted microstructure map. Additionally, model parameterization was the same for all tested cases,
and a summary of the training parameters is given in Table 2.

 3. INITIAL MODEL TRAINING AND TESTING
An inherent challenge in the development of an image processing machine learning model with the
ultimate goal of wide-scale deployment for various BSE datasets is the implementation of physically
meaningful training data that are relevant to the intended application. Because accurate identification of
twin grain boundaries requires crystallographic data, EBSD data, though not industrially relevant, must be
incorporated into the model training. The following sections describe two approaches to pre-processing
and preparation of these data in ways that are relevant and, ideally, translatable to the end goal application
of wide-scale model deployment for automated twin boundary segmentation in TRISO SiC layers.

3.1 TRAINING WITH SIMULATED CHANNELING CONTRAST
Electron channeling contrast (ECC) in BSE SEM imaging arises from the dependence of backscattered
electron yield on the crystallographic orientation of individual grains. When the incident beam is
aligned with a low-index crystallographic direction of a grain, electrons can channel between rows of
atoms, resulting in reduced backscatter and darker image contrast. Misalignment from these channeling
conditions increases electron scattering, producing a higher backscatter yield and brighter contrast. While
EBSD capability is unlikely to be relevant for industrial end-users of this model, SEM/BSE is a much
more ubiquitous capability, and a model trained on physically meaningful data that are representative of
SEM conditions offers potential for more feasible generalization of this model for the desired downstream
applications.
Accordingly, one approach investigated for developing microstructural training data comprised translating
EBSD maps into simulated BSE images by visually representing expected ECC based on grain orientations.
The mathematical basis for this procedure is shown in Equation (4):

                                             gi = R(ϕ1,i , Φi , ϕ2,i ) · ẑ                                   (4)
where gi is a grayscale intensity value assigned to grain i, R(ϕ1,i , Φi , ϕ2,i ) is the Euler angle rotation matrix
for the orientation of grain i, and ẑ is the surface normal vector, (0,0,1). Simply, the angle between the
surface normal direction and the mean vector orientation of a given grain is calculated, and a correspondingly
scaled grayscale value which reflects the expected backscattered electron yield is calculated and assigned
to that grain. For training data preparation, Eq. (4) is calculated for every grain in the microstructure, and
the determined grayscale value is applied to each grain to simulate the expected ECC during BSE imaging
of the microstructure. For this approach, the simulated ECC BSE micrograph was used as a training image,
while the ground truth labels comprise a map of twin grain boundaries in the microstructure. The intention
of this approach is that the U-Net algorithm can learn the relationship between magnitude differences in
grain orientations at a given grain boundary (i.e., the difference in physically meaningful grayscale value)
and the associated likelihood of that boundary being a twin.

3.2 TRAINING WITH TWIN-EXCLUSIVE BOUNDARY MAPS
As discussed, the end goal of model implementation is the further development of recent quantitative
approaches to improve the existing standard of TRISO SiC QA/QC assessment. While the existing
BSE segmentation approach offers rapid extraction of microstructural metrics, the purpose of this
model development effort is to identify twin boundaries and remove them from these analyses. With the
simulated ECC pipeline discussed in the previous section, which predicts the twin boundaries themselves,
an additional step of data superposition is required to subtract those predicted twin boundaries from
a cumulative grain boundary map. Ongoing work is focused on implementing and streamlining this
superposition process and is discussed in Section 4. As an alternative approach to circumvent this step
completely and minimize downstream analytical uncertainty arising from the image superposition, this
section describes a model training designed to predict a microstructure that already possesses removed
twin boundaries from an input of a cumulative grain boundary map.
A summary of the data-preprocessing and preparation for model training is shown in Figure 7. Figure 7a
shows a plot of only grain boundaries from a full EBSD map, while Figure 7b shows grain boundaries
of the same microstructure with those characterized as twins (via the criteria described in Section 2.2)
removed. These images represent the image and ground truth labels, respectively, used for training to
predict the twin-exclusive boundary map (TEBM). As with the previous model, the training and validation
datasets comprised patches of size 256×256 extracted from the bulk images. Unlike the previous model,
however, the training of this model consisted of both the legacy low-magnification (2,300×) and the
recently-collected higher magnification maps (10,000×) SiC EBSD datasets. Although the training patch
size is the same for all data, incorporation of both EBSD datasets provides an augmented training set while
potentially training the model to identify and generalize any differences due to variation in pixel resolution.
While the previous model is trained to identify twin boundaries on simulated microstructural data
grounded in physical phenomena, the workflow shown in this section is also well-attuned to the desired
downstream application. As shown in Figure 1, previous work at ORNL has developed and demonstrated
a grain boundary segmentation algorithm which is designed for BSE images. The output of this algorithm
when applied to industry-relevant BSE SEM micrographs of the SiC layer is, accordingly, a map of all
grain boundaries. Thus, development of a machine learning model trained to remove twin boundaries
from a map of all boundaries in a microstructure is equally, if not more so, applicable to the intended
downstream application of this algorithm.

3.2.2 Model Prediction Performance on Twin-Inclusive Boundary Map
A summary of training, testing, and performance assessment is shown in Figure 9. Figure 9a–c provides a
visual summary of the model performance pipeline. Figure 9a shows the twin-inclusive grain boundary
map reserved as the testing microstructure (i.e., had not previously been seen by the model), while
Figure 9b shows the performance metric sweep determined similarly to that shown in Figure 5b. A
noteworthy difference in the performance metrics between the TEBM approach and the simulated ECC
approach is that the absolute values of the metrics are much improved. While the precision/recall crossover
point occurred at a value of ≈ 0.20 with the simulated ECC model, here the intersection point occurs
at ≈ 0.75. While this may lead one to believe that this model is a far superior option for generating
twin-exclusive grain boundary maps from twin-inclusive data, this is not necessarily the case and once
again highlights the limitation of applying these general performance metrics to this highly imbalanced
classification task. As shown in the Figure 7c, this model was trained on image–label pairs comprising
twin-inclusive microstructures and TEBMs, respectively, and the training is intended to teach the model
to predict the absence of features (i.e., twin boundaries) rather than the presence thereof. While some
local regions in SiC microstructure are dominated by high fractions of twinning, the opposite is also
true, and some image–label pairs are nearly identical on a pixel-wise basis. This may be incentivizing
the model to be overly cautious with removing boundary segments, which is reflected by the probability
map in Figure 9c, which closely resembles the twin-inclusive input image. The high performance metric
values simply reflect the fact that for this segmentation task, there is a much higher fraction of the minority
class; whereas the simulated ECC model is only ‘correct’ when it segments grain boundaries of specific
crystallographic character, the TEBM model is ‘correct’ with segmentation of any grain boundary, which
contributes to higher overall performance metrics that are not necessarily reflective of accomplishing the
desired task. This further highlights the need to implement alternate metrics which are better tailored
towards imbalanced class segmentation tasks like this one.
Nonetheless, probability thresholding and boundary plotting are performed to assess potential translatability
of the TEBM segmentation to refining SiC microstructural metrics, similarly to the previous model.
These results are shown in Figures 9d–f, which depict paired images of the ground truth TEBM (left)
with the predicted twin map at probability thresholds of 0.525, 0.550, and 0.575, respectively (right),
as labeled and highlighted by the red lines on the plot. Unlike with the simulated ECC model, the
precision/recall intersection point is not a useful parameter for identifying a threshold for twin removal.
While the intersection occurs at ≈ 0.500, the segmentation is already too aggressive at a more conservative
probability threshold (0.525, shown in Figure 9d), with many non-twin boundaries remaining in the
predicted map. Only by p > 0.550 (Figure 9e) do the twin boundaries become sufficiently sparse such
that post-processing removal should be an achievable task. Even so, because the raw probability map
itself assigns relatively high prediction probability to essentially all grain boundaries in the microstructure
(Figure 9c), the non-twins also become sparse and may pose a challenge in post-processing twin removal.

 4. SUMMARY, CURRENT LIMITATIONS, AND NEXT STEPS
The results presented and discussed in this report are a part of an ongoing effort at ORNL to quantify and
assess SiC microstructure on TRISO particles for QA/QC methods development. Although the recent
advancements in microstructure quantification via BSE segmentation and grain size analysis represent
a marked improvement upon the classical ‘visual standard’ of SiC QA/QC microstructural analysis
[6, 7], further work is needed to remove twinned grain boundaries from this quantification to establish
appropriate QA/QC methods to capture the relevant SiC microstructure. Here, the conceptualization,
initial development, and testing of two CV machine learning models with varied analytical approaches to
recognize, and ultimately remove, twin grain boundaries in SiC is reported. These results are summarized
below.

4.1 SUMMARY OF INITIAL MACHINE LEARNING MODEL DEVELOPMENT
4.1.1 ECC Model
Firstly, as described in Section 3.1, a model fed an input of simulated BSE images from EBSD data,
colored based on orientation-dependent electron channeling contrast, shows a strong ability to identify
a sparse network of grain boundaries after being trained on crystallographically determined twin grain
boundaries in SiC. The probability threshold sweep (Figure 5b) shows a convergence of precision, recall,
and F1 score between thresholds of 0.375–0.425, suggesting a stable operating region where segmentation
performance is balanced. The corresponding probability maps (Figure 5d–f) indicate that the model
consistently captures the twin features while reducing noise as the threshold is refined. Furthermore, this
model architecture generalizes well to a real BSE image and is similarly able to output a sparse boundary
map from non-crystallographic input data at similar probability thresholds to that of the simulated ECC
microstructures. These initial results are promising and demonstrate a strong foundation for downstream
application of de-twinned SiC microstructural analysis.

4.1.2 TEBM Model
Secondly, as described in Section 3.2, a model fed an input of a twin-inclusive map of grain boundaries
was trained to output a twin-exclusive boundary map (TEBM). The probability map (Figure 9c) indicates
that the model is broadly detecting boundary-like features across the microstructure, though not with a
clear separation between twins and non-twins. As the threshold increases from 0.525 to 0.575 (Figures 9d–f),
the predicted twin map becomes progressively sparser, but this reduction appears to affect both true twin
boundaries and non-twin boundary detections in a roughly even manner. Comparison against the ground
truth suggests that the model is not strongly discriminating twin features, and the effect of thresholding is
largely a uniform filtering of predictions rather than a targeted refinement. These results indicate that while
the TEBM-trained model can produce tunable outputs through thresholding and may be applicable for true
BSE images, its ability to selectively distinguish twins from other boundaries appears limited and further
model development is required.

4.2 CURRENT LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
4.2.1 ECC Model
Although the simulated ECC-trained model shows the most promise for deployment in the near-future,
application to SiC microstructural refinement requires the additional steps of image superposition
and subtraction of the pixels identified as twin boundaries from the complete grain boundary map.

 19

Accordingly, an existing limitation for broader testing and implementation of this model is misalignment
between the EBSD data used for training and the model output prediction map. This issue is illustrated
in Figure 11, which shows the superposition of the ground truth map of all grain boundaries (in green),
and a predicted twin boundary map (in red). The dimensions of the raw images vary and thus required
image resizing before masking and twin subtraction. While this resizing results in some well-overlaid
twin boundaries (Figure 11b), there are also regions of slight offset (Figure 11c), which prohibits simple,
direct twin subtraction for subsequent calculation of updated twin-exclusive SiC microstructural metrics.
This challenge can be feasibly overcome and should be resolved in the near future. Simultaneously, to
circumvent the need for superposition and subtraction, an additional model architecture will be developed
in the near future that comprises the simulated ECC input data and TEBM ground truth labels. This
approach should offer a balance between information-rich input data (to avoid sparsity among all of
the inputs, labels, and predictions) and streamlined processing and deployment of the model since the
prediction does not require additional operator steps.
Additionally, a necessary consideration is that although the accurately sparse predictions and generalization
to real micrographs from the ECC model are promising, the predictive output of the model is not truly a
twin map. As shown in Figure 5b, the maximum precision of the model prediction only reaches as high
as ≈ 0.35, indicating that current model performance can accurately predict no better than 35% of true
twin boundaries present in the ground truth. This relates to the aforementioned inherent challenges of
imbalanced CV segmentation tasks, for which it is more difficult for the machine to learn quantitative
features and relationships when they are highly sparse. Nevertheless, even at the initial development
stage, there is a significant fraction of twin boundaries being identified, and once the data can be properly
aligned, twin-subtracted grain boundary maps will be analyzed and the change in grain morphology
metrics validated against previous literature on de-twinned SiC microstructure.

4.2.2 TEBM Model
Although designed to expedite downstream application and deployment by directly outputting the
de-twinned microstructure, the TEBM model’s performance was less encouraging than that of the ECC
model. This is most likely a factor of both the sparsity and similarity between the input images and the
de-twinned ground truth labels. Improved twin deletion performance using this model architecture will
likely require changes in the model parameterization. The modifications that would perhaps be the most
likely to improve performance are (1) changing the initialization of W in Eq. (3) to very strongly penalize
false positives, which should better disincentivize the inclusion of non-twins in the de-twinned prediction,
and (2) increasing the image and label patch size from 256 to 512, which should provide more variation
between image and label by incorporating more de-twinned regions in the TEBM. However, development
and testing of the aforementioned combined ECC and TEBM model are likely to be prioritized over
parametric investigation of refined TEBM model design, as this approach will offer the streamlined twin
deletion of the TEBM model while avoiding the sparsity in grain boundary maps in both the input and
output.

4.2.3 General
Of prime importance for broad and industrial deployment and application of these model(s) is automation,
and the removal of as much operator input as possible to maintain consistency. Currently, the primary
need for automation is selection of the optimal probability threshold, which in this work was done by
visual inspection. Based on the ECC results, the precision/recall intersection point may seem like a
logical option; however, as seen in Figure 5e, the balance threshold appeared to be too aggressive in
detecting twins. Moreover, downstream application of the model will be similar to the true BSE test
shown in Figure 6, which does not have an associated ground truth and therefore cannot have an associated
probability sweep to determine a threshold. Thus, it may be necessary to tune and optimize the model for
performance at a single universal probability threshold; this will be a focus of ongoing work.
The development of a twin or de-twinning model that outputs dense, continuous boundaries while also
not oversampling non-twins is likely unfeasible because of the nature of this sparse segmentation task.
Accordingly, there remains a need for developing and establishing a post-processing algorithm for the
thresholded prediction map, whether that be a twin boundary output or a TEBM output. Through some
preliminary testing, the primary operations of this post-processing procedure will be skeletonization to
reduce all boundary predictions to a single line, small feature removal to discard isolated 1–2 pixel regions
predicted to be boundaries which are likely to be majority class, and Canny edge detection to connect
remaining neighboring pixels and generate a structure that more closely resembles a boundary network
than scattered and isolated pixels. This post-processing will need to be generalized across microstructures
but should greatly improve the connectivity and subsequent workability of the boundary predictions.
Lastly, and perhaps mostly importantly, is demonstrating the application of the model prediction for
calculating de-twinned microstructural metrics of SiC. The algorithmic infrastructure for executing this
is already in place, as similar calculations were performed on the BSE-segmented SiC microstructure
in the previous work [6, 7]. This stepwise workflow will comprise (1) BSE segmentation on a test
microstructure, (2) calculation of grain morphology metrics, (3) twin removal (via superposition or
a TEBM prediction model) from the BSE/segmented boundary map input, (4) post-processing of the
twin/de-twinned prediction and superposition/subtraction as needed, and (5) repeated calculations of grain
morphology metrics for comparison against the full grain boundary map. The quantitative comparison
between grain metrics from the full boundary map and from the de-twinned map will be validated against
previous literature [5], which has recorded comprehensive microstructural metrics for TRISO SiC with and
without twin boundaries removed.
