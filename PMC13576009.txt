Breaking Silos: Improving Continuing Medical Education Planning Through Interdisciplinary Collaboration
Authors: Katherine Staab, Noah Johnson, Ana Chiddington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

CONTEXT: SETTING AND STAKEHOLDERS

High-quality continuing medical education (CME) requires coordinated planning to meet learner needs and accreditation standards, including gap analysis, evidence review, disclosure management, and outcomes evaluation. In practice, however, these activities often occur in silos, resulting in fragmented communication and inefficiencies.

National expectations for CME are also evolving. The Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education increasingly emphasizes outcomes-based education, the documentation of planning processes, and impact reporting through systems such as the Program and Activity Reporting System. These requirements increase both the complexity of CME planning and the need for coordinated high-quality workflows.

At Southern California Permanente Medical Group, CME activities span multiple regions, departments, and stakeholders, creating additional challenges in terms of coordination, consistency, and scalability. Historically, CME planning at Southern California Permanente Medical Group occurred within decentralized site-based “clusters.” Although this approach supported locally tailored programming, it also led to duplicated effort, inconsistent processes, and limited visibility and collaboration across sites. Team members often worked sequentially rather than collaboratively, increasing the likelihood of delays and inefficiencies. In response, Southern California Permanente Medical Group transitioned toward a “one-system” accreditation model, emphasizing shared standards and cross-site collaboration. Building on this shift, the project team redesigned the CME workflow to promote the early, concurrent engagement of key stakeholders. The project team consisted of a consultant, a project manager, and a medical librarian who collaboratively implemented the redesigned workflow.

To address these challenges, the project team initiated a test of change focused on redesigning the CME planning workflow. The intervention introduced an early, team-based consultation model in which consultants, project managers, and medical librarians participated together at the initial planning stage. This pilot was implemented within a defined group of CME sites within Southern California Permanente Medical Group, including San Diego, San Bernardino County, and Riverside, with the goal of improving coordination, reducing inefficiencies, and enhancing the quality and consistency of CME planning. The evaluation focused on stakeholder experience, perceived preparedness, time and effort required, and perceived quality and educational value. This shift moved CME planning from siloed workflows to a collaborative learning ecosystem, enabling shared expertise and a more coordinated planning approach.

RATIONALE FOR CHANGE, COLLABORATIVE APPROACH, AND BENEFITS

Despite producing high-quality CME activities, the project team identified inefficiencies in its planning process related to fragmentation, delayed communication, and inconsistent coordination across stakeholders. Consultants, project managers, faculty, planners, and medical librarians often worked sequentially rather than collaboratively, resulting in misaligned timelines and duplicated efforts.

A key limitation was the delayed involvement of certain team members, particularly medical librarians and project managers. Librarians frequently received topic information too late to conduct comprehensive literature reviews, and project managers were often positioned reactively, addressing issues after they emerged rather than proactively supporting planning.

These challenges were further amplified by the scale and complexity of Southern California Permanente Medical Group, where multiple regions and departments contributed to CME activities. Faculty, who often operate under substantial time constraints, faced fragmented communication and lacked consistent access to coordinated support early in the process.

EXISTING LITERATURE AND PRACTICE

A growing body of literature highlights the expanding role of medical librarians in CME, particularly in supporting evidence-based practice, instructional quality, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Across studies, 3 key themes have emerged: the value of librarian involvement in identifying educational needs, the contribution of librarians to content quality and equity, and the importance of collaborative approaches in CME planning.

First, several studies have demonstrated that medical librarians contribute meaningfully to identifying practice gaps and aligning educational activities with learner needs. For example, Bartkowiak et al found that librarian participation in CME committees increased the identification of actionable practice gaps, directly informing more relevant educational programming. 1 Similarly, Swanberg et al emphasized aligning instruction with learner needs and clinical milestones, highlighting shared goals between librarians and CME professionals. 2

Second, the literature underscores the role of medical librarians in enhancing content quality, particularly in evidence synthesis, critical appraisal, and equity-focused education. Gerber described how librarians support topic development, speaker selection, and content neutrality, by drawing on their expertise in research methods and pedagogy. 3 More recent work has also highlighted the importance of integrating perspectives such as cultural and linguistic competency and implicit bias into educational design.

Third, studies have highlighted the benefits of sustained interdisciplinary collaboration in CME. Glover and Reagan described a model of close partnership in which librarians and CME professionals collaborated in real time to support planning, evaluation, and content development. 4 Their findings suggest that collaboration across roles can improve both process efficiency and educational quality. 4

Despite these contributions, gaps remain in the literature. A recent scoping review by Phinney et al noted that many reports lack structured program design, formal evaluation, and alignment with accreditation frameworks. 5 In addition, few studies have described integrated, early-stage collaboration models that include medical librarians, project managers, consultants, faculty, and planners working together from the outset of CME planning.

This study addresses these gaps by examining a structured, team-based approach to a redesign of the CME planning workflow that embeds interdisciplinary collaboration at the initial consultation stage and evaluating its impact on process and stakeholder experience.

COLLABORATIVE INTERVENTION

In response to the challenges, the project team at Southern California Permanente Medical Group implemented a redesigned CME planning workflow centered on early interdisciplinary collaboration. The intervention introduced a model in which consultants, project managers, and medical librarians participated together in the initial consultation meeting with faculty and planners.

This approach shifted the workflow from a sequential to a parallel model, enabling stakeholders to align on key elements (including topic selection, learning objectives, evidence needs, and accreditation requirements) at the outset of planning. The redesign also clarified roles and expectations, established shared timelines, and created opportunities for real-time communication among team members.

A key feature of the intervention was the earlier integration of medical librarians into the consultation process, allowing for direct engagement with faculty, clarification of information needs, and more targeted evidence support. Similarly, early involvement of project managers enabled proactive coordination of logistics and workflow planning.

This collaborative model was piloted within the San Diego, San Bernardino County, and Riverside medical centers of the Southern California Permanente Medical Group, with the goal of improving coordination, reducing inefficiencies, and supporting a more integrated approach to CME activity development.

PURPOSE/AIM

The purpose of this study was to describe and evaluate a redesigned CME planning model that integrates early, interdisciplinary collaboration among consultants, project managers, medical librarians, faculty, and planners.

Specifically, this study examined the implementation of a team-based consultation approach in which key stakeholders participated jointly in the initial planning phase of CME activities. The evaluation focused on stakeholder experience, perceived preparedness, time and effort required, and perceived quality and educational value, with the goal of assessing the feasibility and perceived impact of this collaborative model.

By situating this intervention within the existing literature on interdisciplinary collaboration and medical librarian involvement in CME, this study aims to contribute a structured and potentially replicable approach to CME workflow design.

Methodology

STUDY DESIGN AND INTERVENTION

In January 2024, the project team implemented a redesigned CME planning workflow that introduced early, interdisciplinary collaboration. This model brought together the consultant, project manager, and medical librarian in the initial consultation meeting with faculty and planners, replacing a prior sequential approach in which these stakeholders engaged at different points in the planning process.

To evaluate this intervention, a cross-sectional survey was used to compare faculty and planner experiences before and after implementation of the revised model.

PARTICIPANTS AND SETTING

Eligible participants were CME faculty and planners who had been involved in CME activities both before and after implementation of the new workflow. Participants were identified through a retrospective review of CME activity records from January 2022 through March 2025.

Inclusion criteria required that individuals had served as faculty presenters or planners for at least 1 CME activity prior to January 2024 and participated in at least 1 CME activity following the implementation of the revised process.

Exclusion criteria comprised individuals who participated only before or only after the intervention, as well as those serving in other stakeholder roles (eg, consultants, project managers, medical librarians), because the study focused specifically on the end-user experience of the planning process.

A total of 18 eligible faculty and planners were invited to participate in the study, and 13 completed the survey.

DATA COLLECTION

A 12-item survey was developed to assess participant perceptions of the CME planning process across both time periods. The survey included a combination of Likert-scale, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. The complete survey instrument and deidentified responses are available in Supplementary Material 1.

DATA ANALYSIS

Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and summary distributions for Likert-scale responses. Comparisons were made descriptively between participants’ reported experiences before and after implementation of the revised model.

Qualitative data from open-ended responses were analyzed using thematic analysis. The responses were reviewed and coded iteratively to identify recurring patterns and key themes related to participant experience and perceived benefits and challenges of the revised workflow. Themes were refined through comparison across responses to ensure consistency and representativeness. All 3 members of the project team independently reviewed the responses to enhance the consistency in theme identification.

Institutional Review Board

This project was reviewed by the Kaiser Permanente Southern California Institutional Review Board and was determined not to meet the regulatory definition of human participant research; therefore, it was not subject to further institutional review board review.

Overall, participants reported consistently high levels of support under both models, with a notable shift toward more uniformly positive ratings under the new process. Under the previous model, some respondents identified challenges related to administrative burden, unclear expectations, and inefficiencies in communication. These concerns were less frequently reported with the revised model, for which participants instead highlighted clearer guidance, improved coordination, and earlier access to resources.

Perceived preparedness remained high across both models; however, most participants indicated feeling more prepared under the new collaborative approach. Responses related to time and effort were more variable, with approximately half of participants reporting reduced workload and others noting no substantial change.

The participants also reported perceived improvements in presentation quality and educational value, although some respondents indicated uncertainty regarding changes in educational impact.

QUALITATIVE FINDINGS

Open-ended responses reinforced these trends, emphasizing the value of early, coordinated support. Participants described the revised process as “streamlined and easy” and appreciated having “all resources available at the outset.” Several respondents also noted that the new model clarified CME requirements and facilitated more effective preparation.

Because of the small sample size, the analyses are descriptive and intended to identify trends rather than establish statistical significance.

Discussion

This study examined participant perceptions of a redesigned, collaborative CME planning model that introduced early interdisciplinary engagement. Overall, the findings suggest that the participants experienced the revised model as more coordinated and supportive, with several reporting improvements in communication, clarity of expectations, and access to resources.

Participants reported high levels of support under both the previous and revised models, with a shift toward more consistently positive ratings following implementation of the collaborative approach. Reported challenges associated with the prior model (including administrative burden, unclear expectations, and communication inefficiencies) were less frequently noted for the revised process. These findings suggest that the early engagement of multiple stakeholders may help address coordination challenges inherent in complex CME environments.

The results related to preparedness and time optimization were more mixed. Although most participants reported feeling more prepared under the revised model, all had already reported adequate preparedness under the previous model. Similarly, perceptions of reduced time and effort varied across respondents, with some indicating improvements and others reporting no change. These findings suggest that, although the redesigned workflow may enhance aspects of the planning experience, its impact on efficiency may be more variable and context dependent.

Perceptions of presentation quality and educational value also showed modest change. Some participants reported perceived improvements in quality, whereas others noted no difference. Despite reported improvements in coordination and planning experience, perceived educational value did not show a consistent change across respondents. This finding may reflect the already high baseline for the quality of CME activity, as well as the complexity of measuring educational impact through self-reported perceptions. It also suggests that improvements in planning processes do not necessarily translate directly into perceived learning outcomes. Future research could explore this issue further by examining the perspectives of participants who did not perceive changes in educational value, with particular attention to identifying factors that influence how educational impact is experienced and assessed. Incorporating more targeted survey questions or follow-up interviews may provide deeper insight into how workflow redesigns influence both the planning process and downstream educational outcomes.

Qualitative feedback provides additional insight into participant experience, with respondents emphasizing the value of early, coordinated engagement and access to multiple forms of support at the outset of planning. These findings align with the existing literature highlighting the benefits of interdisciplinary collaboration and early involvement of medical librarians and project managers in CME design. However, unlike many descriptive accounts in the literature, this study provides preliminary evaluative data on a structured, team-based intervention.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The sample size was small (n = 13), and the participants were drawn from a single institutional setting, limiting the study’s generalizability. In addition, the reliance on self-reported perceptions introduces the potential for response bias. The survey instrument did not include standardized or validated measures, and key constructs such as “quality” and “educational value” were not explicitly defined for respondents. Future studies could strengthen evaluations by incorporating clearer measurement frameworks, larger samples, and objective outcome measures.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to a growing body of work exploring interdisciplinary approaches to CME planning. The findings suggest that the early integration of key stakeholders may support improved coordination and participant experience, although further research is needed to better understand its impact on efficiency and educational outcomes. Given the descriptive nature of the data and the small sample size, the findings reported herein should be interpreted cautiously and are intended to identify preliminary trends rather than establish definitive evidence of impact.

Conclusion

This study describes the implementation and evaluation of a collaborative CME planning model that integrates early interdisciplinary engagement among consultants, project managers, medical librarians, faculty, and planners.

The findings suggest that this approach may support improved coordination, clearer communication, and a more structured planning experience for faculty and planners. However, the results related to efficiency, preparedness, and perceived educational value were more variable and should be interpreted in the context of a small, single-site sample and descriptive study design.

For institutions considering similar approaches, early joint consultation and clearly defined roles may facilitate more coordinated workflows. At the same time, additional research is needed to evaluate the impact of such models across larger populations and with more robust measures of educational outcomes.

Although the collaborative model may support a more coordinated planning experience, its relationship to perceived educational impact remains an important area for further study. Future work could explore the integration of interdisciplinary collaboration across additional phases of CME planning, including evaluation and outcomes assessment, as well as the role of medical librarians in supporting equity-focused and evidence-based educational design.
