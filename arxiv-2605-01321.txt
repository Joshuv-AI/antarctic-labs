Exact WKB and Quantum Periods for Extremal Black Hole Quasinormal Modes Preprint: RUP-26-7
Yasuyuki Hatsuda, and Tomohito Shiga

Abstract
We apply exact WKB analysis to the spectral problem arising in black hole perturbation theory. The boundary conditions for quasinormal modes lead to exact quantization conditions for the complex frequencies. To solve these conditions, one needs to evaluate the so-called quantum periods, or Voros symbols. For scalar perturbations of extremal Reissner–Nordström and Kerr black holes, we compute these quantities up to very high orders in the WKB expansion and perform Borel–Padé resummation. The resulting resummed quantization conditions successfully reproduce the correct quasinormal mode frequencies with high precision.

Introduction

Quasinormal modes (QNMs) are among the most fundamental observables in black hole perturbation theory
Kokkotas:1999bd
;
Berti:2009kk
;
Konoplya:2011qq
. They are defined by imposing purely ingoing behavior at the horizon and purely outgoing behavior at infinity, and their complex frequencies encode both the oscillation and decay of black hole ringdown. While QNM spectra have traditionally been studied by numerical and semi-analytic methods, it is highly desirable to formulate the problem in a controlled analytic framework. Exact WKB analysis
Voros:1983
offers such a framework. In this approach, the boundary conditions for QNMs naturally lead to exact quantization conditions (EQCs)
Imaizumi:2022qbi
;
Imaizumi:2022dgj
;
Miyachi:2025ptm
;
Miyachi:2025dyk
, whose basic building blocks are quantum periods, or Voros symbols.

There are two independent approaches to evaluating the quantum periods. One efficient method is to express them in terms of the Nekrasov partition function
Nekrasov:2002qd
in the so-called Nekrasov–Shatashvili limit
Nekrasov:2009rc
, or equivalently in terms of the semiclassical (irregular) conformal block
Zamolodchikov:1986
;
Zamolodchikov:1987
, through the correspondence with Seiberg–Witten (SW) theory
Seiberg:1994rs
;
Seiberg:1994aj
and two-dimensional CFT
Belavin:1984vu
. This approach is useful because we know a combinatorial formula of the Nekrasov partition function or a recursive method to obtain the conformal block.
In the black hole QNM problem, this approach works well in practice
Aminov:2020yma
.
However, as emphasized in
Arnaudo:2025kof
, the instanton expansion parameter of the Nekrasov partition function is not always a suitable expansion parameter for the black hole problem. In particular, the convergence deteriorates as the overtone number increases. To circumvent this difficulty, one may instead re-expand the partition function in terms of a different parameter, but no combinatorial closed formula is known in such a case.

The other approach is to evaluate the cycle integrals defining the quantum periods directly.
For genus-one spectral curves, these quantum periods can in principle be expressed in terms of elliptic integrals.
In practice, however, obtaining explicit analytic expressions is not always straightforward, since the relevant integrals typically involve complicated parameter dependence.
Nevertheless, in certain special cases the structure of the spectral curve simplifies sufficiently, and the quantum periods can then be computed in a much more systematic and explicit manner
Grassi:2019coc
. This direct approach ultimately leads to TBA equations for the Borel resummed quantum periods
Ito:2018eon
;
Ito:2019jio
.

In this work, we focus on the QNM problem for scalar perturbations of four-dimensional asymptotically flat extremal black holes.

Very recently, the QNM problem for scalar perturbations of the extremal Reissner–Nordström black hole was analyzed using its connection with
\mathcal{N}=2
SW theory
Wang:2026kue
. In that work, the Nekrasov–Shatashvili free energy was used to solve the problem. Although there is a slight overlap, the approach is clearly different from ours.
The advantage of the extremal limit is that the computation of the quantum periods becomes much simpler. We compute the quantum periods up to very high orders in the WKB expansion. Such high-order data allow us to perform Borel–Padé resummation of the quantum period. As a result, we can solve the resummed quantization conditions, and they provide the correct QNM frequencies with high precision.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we study scalar perturbations of the extremal Reissner–Nordström black hole.
We first reduce the radial equation to the doubly confluent Heun equation, then review the basic ingredients of exact WKB analysis, derive the exact quantization conditions for the QNM frequencies, and finally evaluate the quantum periods and compare the resulting resummed quantization conditions with numerical QNM data.
In Section 3, we carry out the analogous analysis for the extremal Kerr black hole.
Section 4 is devoted to our conclusions.
In Appendix
A
, we explain how to compute the quantum periods analytically.

2
Extremal Reissner–Nordström Black Hole

In this section, we analyze scalar perturbations of the extremal Reissner–Nordström black hole using exact WKB analysis. We first reduce the radial equation to the doubly confluent Heun equation and review the basic ingredients of exact WKB analysis relevant to our problem. We then derive the exact quantization condition for the QNM frequencies and evaluate the corresponding quantum periods. Finally, we compare the resulting Borel–Padé resummed quantization condition with numerical QNM data.

2.1
Reduction to Doubly Confluent Heun Equation

We begin with the extremal Reissner–Nordström background,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}),\qquad f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}},

(1)

where
M
and
Q
are the mass and the charge of the black hole.
At linear order in the perturbation, the scalar field equation is simply given by the Klein–Gordon equation on this background:

\displaystyle(-g)^{-1/2}\partial_{\mu}[(-g)^{1/2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}]\Phi=0.

(2)

By the separation of variables

\displaystyle\Phi=e^{-i\omega t}\frac{\phi(r)}{r}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\phi),

(3)

where
Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\phi)
is a spherical harmonic,
we obtain the radial ordinary differential equation (ODE),

\displaystyle\biggl[f(r)\frac{d}{dr}\biggl(f(r)\frac{d}{dr}\biggr)+\omega^{2}-V_{\text{RN}}(r)\biggr]\phi(r)=0,

(4)

where

\displaystyle V_{\text{RN}}(r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\biggl(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{r}\biggr)

(5)

\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{2M}{r^{3}}-\frac{2Q^{2}}{r^{4}}\biggr).

For
0\leq Q<M
,
f(r)=0
has two distinct real roots,

\displaystyle r_{\pm}=M\pm\sqrt{M^{2}-Q^{2}},

(6)

where
r=r_{+}
corresponds to the event horizon.
We also introduce the tortoise coordinate
r_{*}
by

\displaystyle r_{*}=r+\frac{1}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\Bigl(r_{+}^{2}\log(r-r_{+})-r_{-}^{2}\log(r-r_{-})\Bigr).

(7)

The limit
r_{*}\to-\infty
corresponds to the event horizon, while
r_{*}\to+\infty
corresponds to spatial infinity.
Then, the QNM boundary conditions are given by

\displaystyle\phi(r)\to\begin{cases}e^{-i\omega r_{*}}\quad&(r_{*}\to-\infty)\\
e^{i\omega r_{*}}\quad&(r_{*}\to+\infty)\end{cases}.

(8)

For
0\leq Q<M
, the ODE (
4
) turns out to have two regular singular points at
r=r_{\pm}
and one irregular singular point at
r=\infty
. Therefore it is equivalent to the confluent Heun equation.

In the extremal case
Q=M
, the situation changes drastically. In this case,
r_{-}=r_{+}
,
f(r)
has a double zero at
r=M
, and the tortoise coordinate is given by

\displaystyle r_{*}=r-\frac{M^{2}}{r-M}+2M\log(r-M)+\text{const}.

(9)

The ODE (
4
) has two irregular singular points at
r=M,\infty
. It is equivalent to the doubly confluent Heun equation (DCHE).
To see it more explicitly, let us change the variables,

\displaystyle r=M(1+z),\qquad\phi(r)=\biggl(1+\frac{1}{z}\biggr)\psi(z).

(10)

We immediately obtain the following ODE:

\displaystyle\biggl(-\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-(M\omega)^{2}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{z}\biggr)^{4}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{z^{2}}\biggr)\psi(z)=0

(11)

For this ODE, one can easily see that
z=0,\infty
are irregular singular points.
Near
z=0
, we have the two asymptotic solutions,

\displaystyle\psi(z)\sim e^{\pm iM\omega/z}z^{1\mp 2iM\omega}\quad(z\to 0).

(12)

Similarly, the two asymptotic solutions around
z=\infty
are given by

\displaystyle\psi(z)\sim e^{\pm iM\omega z}z^{\pm 2iM\omega}\quad(z\to\infty).

(13)

By carefully tracking the change of variables, one finds that the QNM boundary conditions (
8
) are mapped to the following condition in the
z
-variable:

\displaystyle\psi(z)\to\begin{cases}e^{iM\omega/z}z^{1-2iM\omega}\quad&(z\to 0^{+})\\
e^{iM\omega z}z^{2iM\omega}\quad&(z\to+\infty)\end{cases},

(14)

where
z\to 0^{+}
means that
z
goes to zero along the positive real axis.

2.2
Review of Exact WKB

We would like to apply exact WKB analysis to the perturbation equation (
11
) to solve the QNM problem.
In this subsection, we review the basics of exact WKB analysis
Voros:1983
;
Aoki:1991
;
Delabaere:1997srq
.

Let us consider the one-dimensional Schrödinger-type equation:

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+Q(x)\biggr)\psi(x)=0,

(15)

where we assume that
Q(x)
takes the following form:

\displaystyle Q(x)=Q_{0}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{2k}Q_{2k}(x).

(16)

We construct the WKB solution of the form

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\exp\biggl(\int^{x}S(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\biggl),

(17)

where
S(x)
satisfies the Riccati equation,

\displaystyle\hbar^{2}\bigl(S(x)^{2}+S^{\prime}(x)\bigr)=Q(x)

(18)

We can construct a formal power series solution,

\displaystyle S(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{j-1}S_{j-1}(x).

(19)

At the leading order, we find

\displaystyle S_{-1}(x)=\pm\sqrt{Q_{0}(x)},

(20)

which provides two independent WKB solutions.
We divide
S(x)
into the odd/even power parts,

\displaystyle S_{\text{odd}}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{2k-1}S_{2k-1}(x),\qquad S_{\text{even}}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{2k}S_{2k}(x).

(21)

Then it is well-known that the following identity holds:

\displaystyle S_{\text{even}}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx}\log S_{\text{odd}}(x).

(22)

We obtain the following two independent WKB solutions:

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S_{\text{odd}}(x)}}\exp\biggl(\pm\int_{x_{0}}^{x}S_{\text{odd}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\biggr),

(23)

where
x_{0}
is a reference point.

The WKB solutions have formal series in
\hbar
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})=\exp(\pm\hbar^{-1}y_{0}(x))\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi_{\pm,n}(x)\hbar^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(24)

where

\displaystyle y_{0}(x):=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\sqrt{Q_{0}(x^{\prime})}dx^{\prime}.

(25)

These series turn out to be divergent in general. We use Borel resummation to construct analytic solutions.
The Borel resummations of (
24
) are given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})=\mathcal{S}[\psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})]=\int_{\mp y_{0}(x)}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y}{\hbar}}\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x,y)dy,

(26)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x,y):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{\pm,n}(x)}{\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2})}(y\pm y_{0}(x))^{n-\frac{1}{2}}

(27)

are referred to as the Borel transforms. The symbol
\mathcal{S}
represents the Borel resummation.

\Psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})
are analytic solutions to the original equation (
15
).

An important point is that the Borel resummed WKB solutions
\Psi_{\pm}(x;x_{0})
exhibit the Stokes phenomenon.
The zeros of
Q_{0}(x)
are called turning points. In particular, a simple zero
x=a
of
Q_{0}(x)
is called a simple turning point.
Now we define the Stokes curves. The Stokes curves emanating from a turning point
x=a
are defined by

\displaystyle\im\biggl[\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{Q_{0}(x^{\prime})}\,dx^{\prime}\biggr]=0.

(28)

If
x=a
is a simple turning point, then three Stokes curves emanate from
x=a
.
On a Stokes curve, the dominant WKB solution is not Borel summable.
When one crosses a Stokes curve, this solution changes discontinuously.
This phenomenon is called the Stokes phenomenon.
Let us denote the dominant and subdominant solutions by
\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}
, respectively.

Three Stokes curves emanate from a simple turning point. Across each Stokes curve, one of the Borel resummed WKB solutions undergoes a discontinuous jump, which is the Stokes phenomenon.

Then, as shown in Fig.
1
, the analytic continuation from sector I to sector II, obtained by crossing the Stokes curve counterclockwise around the turning point, is described by the following connection formula:

2

Here we assume that no Stokes curve emanating from a turning point terminates at another turning point.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}^{\mathrm{I}}(x;a)\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\mathrm{I}}(x;a)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}^{\mathrm{II}}(x;a)\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\mathrm{II}}(x;a)\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

Conversely, the analytic continuation from sector II to sector I, obtained by crossing the Stokes curve clockwise around the turning point, is given by the inverse relation

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}^{\mathrm{II}}(x;a)\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\mathrm{II}}(x;a)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}^{\mathrm{I}}(x;a)\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\mathrm{I}}(x;a)\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

Note that
\Psi_{\mathrm{dom}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{sub}}
should not be confused with
\Psi_{+}
and
\Psi_{-}
. The latter are simply the solutions constructed from the definition (
23
). In applying the connection formula, one must always keep track of which of
\Psi_{\pm}
is dominant and which is subdominant.

2.3
Exact Quantization Conditions for QNM Frequencies

In this subsection, we derive the EQCs that determine the QNM frequencies for the extremal RN black holes. To apply exact WKB to this system, we need to introduce a formal Planck parameter by hand. There are infinitely many possibilities to do so.
To fix one natural choice, we use the connection with the quantum Seiberg–Witten curve
Ashok:2016yxz
;
Grassi:2021wpw
. The corresponding gauge theory is
\mathcal{N}=2

SU(2)
SQCD with
N_{f}=2
flavors. The SW curve of this theory is given by

\displaystyle p^{2}+2\Lambda^{2}\cosh 2x+2\Lambda(m_{1}e^{x}+m_{2}e^{-x})=2u,

m_{1}
and
m_{2}

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+2\Lambda^{2}\cosh 2x+2\Lambda(m_{1}e^{x}+m_{2}e^{-x})\biggr)\varphi(x)=2u\varphi(x)

We perform the following change of variables:

\displaystyle z=e^{x},\qquad\varphi(x)=z^{-1/2}\psi(z)

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+Q_{0}(z)+\hbar^{2}Q_{2}(z)\biggr)\psi(z)=0,

\displaystyle Q_{0}(z)=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{z^{4}}+\frac{2m_{2}\Lambda}{z^{3}}-\frac{2u}{z^{2}}+\frac{2m_{1}\Lambda}{z}+\Lambda^{2},\qquad Q_{2}(z)=-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}.

\displaystyle\hbar

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\qquad 2u

\displaystyle=-iM\omega,

(38)

Using the leading WKB form

\psi_{\pm}\sim Q_{0}(z)^{-1/4}\exp\left(\pm\int^{z}\sqrt{Q_{0}(z^{\prime})}\,dz^{\prime}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}

\displaystyle\sim\exp\biggl[\pm iM\omega\int^{z}\biggl(1+\frac{2}{z^{\prime}}\biggr)dz^{\prime}\biggr]

\displaystyle\sim z^{\pm 2iM\omega}\exp(\pm iM\omega z),

We then analytically continue the same branch to the near-horizon region

\displaystyle Q_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\sim-(M\omega)^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{z^{4}}+\frac{4}{z^{3}}+O(z^{-2})\biggr),

Now we can derive the EQC for this type of Stokes geometry. Along the positive real axis, we cross the two Stokes curves. We start with the Borel resummed WKB solution

We have chosen the reference point

because the first Stokes curve that we encounter emanates from this turning point. After crossing this Stokes curve, the dominant solution
\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{I}}
changes discontinuously due to the Stokes phenomenon.
As we have already seen, this change is precisely determined by the connection formula. Hence we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{+}^{\mathrm{I}}(z;\beta_{-})\\
\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{I}}(z;\beta_{-})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

\displaystyle V_{ab}:=\exp\biggl[\mathcal{S}\biggl(\oint_{\gamma_{ab}}S_{\text{odd}}(z)dz\biggr)\biggr].

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{+}^{\mathrm{II}}(z;\beta_{+})\\

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{+}^{\mathrm{I}}(z;\beta_{-})\\

\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{III}}(z;\beta_{+})\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

In particular, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{I}}(z;\beta_{-})=-iV_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}^{1/2}\Psi_{+}^{\mathrm{III}}(z;\beta_{+})+(V_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}^{1/2}+V_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}^{-1/2})\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{III}}(z;\beta_{+}).

\Psi_{-}^{\mathrm{III}}(z;\beta_{+})

Therefore we arrive at the exact condition

\displaystyle V_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}=-1,

(52)

which leads to the EQC:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\biggl(\oint_{\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}}S_{\text{odd}}(z)dz\biggr)=2\pi i\biggl(n+\frac{1}{2}\biggr),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(53)

The left-hand side is referred to as the (Borel resummed) quantum period.
In the next subsection, we will discuss how to treat this EQC.

2.4
Quantum Periods and QNM Frequencies

To solve the EQC (
53
), we have to evaluate the Borel resummation of the quantum period for the cycle
\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}
. For this purpose, we recall the formal power series (
21
). This leads to the formal power series of the quantum period,

\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}}S_{\text{odd}}(z)dz=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{2k-1}\oint_{\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}}S_{2k-1}(z)dz,

(54)

by interchanging the summation and the integration.
Our strategy is to evaluate

\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}}S_{2k-1}(z)dz,\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,

(55)

order by order and then perform the Borel resummation of the resulting formal power series using Padé approximants.
The analytic evaluation of the quantum periods is not simple in general. However, in the extremal case, it can be done up to quite large
k
. We have obtained the analytic data up to
k=160
. The computation becomes more involved in the non-extremal case or for gravitational and electromagnetic perturbations.

We now present our analytic results for the quantum period.
We notice that the identification (
36
) is not the only choice of the parameters because there is a rescaling freedom

\displaystyle(\hbar,\Lambda,m_{1},m_{2},u)\to(\kappa\hbar,\kappa\Lambda,\kappa m_{1},\kappa m_{2},\kappa^{2}u),

Extremal Kerr Black Hole

In this section, we perform an analogous analysis for the extremal Kerr black hole. The computation is straightforward but technically more involved. We begin with the Kerr metric in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\frac{\Delta}{\Sigma}(dt-a\sin^{2}\theta d\phi)^{2}+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}dr^{2}+\Sigma d\theta^{2}+\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\bigl((r^{2}+a^{2})d\phi-adt\big)^{2},

\displaystyle\biggl[\frac{d}{dr}\biggl(\Delta\frac{d}{dr}\biggr)+\frac{K^{2}}{\Delta}+2ma\omega-\lambda\biggr]R(r)=0,

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we applied exact WKB analysis to the QNM problem for scalar perturbations of four-dimensional asymptotically flat extremal black holes. For both the extremal Reissner–Nordström and Kerr backgrounds, the radial equations reduce to the doubly confluent Heun equation with two irregular singular points, and the QNM boundary conditions can be translated into exact quantization conditions. The central object in this formulation is the Borel resummed quantum period associated with the relevant cycle.

A main advantage of the extremal limit is that these quantum periods can be computed analytically to very high orders. In the extremal RN case, using the relation to the
N_{f}=2
quantum Seiberg–Witten curve, we obtained the formal power series of the quantum period to very high order and performed its Borel–Padé resummation. The resulting resummed quantization condition reproduces the QNM frequencies with high precision. We then carried out the analogous analysis for the extremal Kerr black hole. Owing to the similar Stokes geometry, the same exact quantization condition applies, while the quantum periods can be evaluated after an appropriate parameter identification. We again found good agreement between the resummed quantization condition and numerical QNM data.

Our results show that exact WKB provides a practical analytic framework for black hole QNMs, and that the direct evaluation of quantum periods is especially powerful when the spectral curve simplifies, as in the extremal case. There are several natural directions for future work. It would be interesting to extend the present analysis to non-extremal black holes, where the computation of the quantum periods is more involved, as well as to gravitational and electromagnetic perturbations.
A preliminary result for the quantum period of the Schwarzschild black hole was obtained in
Hatsuda:2021gtn
.
It is also desirable to clarify the TBA description of the resummed quantum periods in the present black hole setting. The TBA for the
SU(2)

N_{f}=2
quantum Seiberg–Witten theory has been studied in
Imaizumi:2021cxf
, and this is expected to be useful in the present context (see also
Fioravanti:2021dce
). Another interesting problem is to analyze the behavior of QNMs at high overtone number from the EQC obtained in this paper. In the extremal Kerr case, the sector with
m=\ell>0
appears to be subtle, and a more careful treatment of this case remains an important open problem.

The exact WKB approach developed here may also be applicable beyond the computation of QNMs, since the connection coefficients directly encode the scattering data
Bonelli:2021uvf
. In particular, it would be worthwhile to explore possible applications to greybody factors and, more ambitiously, to excitation factors
Berti:2006wq
;
Zhang:2013ksa
through the frequency dependence of the corresponding connection formulae.

Appendix A
Analytic Computations of Quantum Periods

In this appendix, we explain how to obtain the high-order quantum periods analytically.
We use the
N_{f}=2
quantum SW curve. Throughout this section, we consider the equal mass case

\displaystyle m_{1}=m_{2}=\mu.

(96)

The Schrödinger equation is given by

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+Q_{0}(z)+\hbar^{2}Q_{2}(z)\biggr)\psi(z)=0,

(97)

where

\displaystyle Q_{0}(z)=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{z^{4}}+\frac{2\mu\Lambda}{z^{3}}-\frac{2u}{z^{2}}+\frac{2\mu\Lambda}{z}+\Lambda^{2},\qquad Q_{2}(z)=-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}.

(98)

Sometimes, it is more convenient to use the original

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+2\Lambda^{2}\cosh 2x+4\mu\Lambda\cosh x\biggr)\varphi(x)=2u\varphi(x).

(99)

We emphasize that both expressions give the same quantum periods.
We further rewrite it as

\displaystyle\biggl(-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+(2\Lambda\cosh x+\mu)^{2}\biggr)\varphi(x)=E\varphi(x),

(100)

where

\displaystyle E:=2u+2\Lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}=A^{2}.

(101)

By using the rescaling freedom (
56
), we set
\Lambda=1
.
For
0<\mu<A-2
, the four turning points of

,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{-A-\mu\pm\sqrt{(A+\mu-2)(A+\mu+2)}}{2},

(102)

\displaystyle\beta_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{A-\mu\pm\sqrt{(A-\mu-2)(A-\mu+2)}}{2},

(103)

are all real. We have
\alpha_{-}<\alpha_{+}<0<\beta_{-}<\beta_{+}
.
Note that
\log\beta_{\pm}
correspond to two real turning points in the

We compute the quantum periods in this situation, and then perform analytic continuation to complex parameters.

At the classical level, we would like to evaluate the integral:

\displaystyle\Pi_{B}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=i\oint_{\gamma_{\beta_{-}\beta_{+}}}\sqrt{Q_{0}(z)}dz=2\int_{\beta_{-}}^{\beta_{+}}\frac{\sqrt{(z-\alpha_{-})(z-\alpha_{+})(z-\beta_{-})(\beta_{+}-z)}}{z^{2}}dz.

(104)

It is not easy to do so. For this purpose, we perform the following change of variables:

\displaystyle s=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}.

(105)

Then, we obtain the relatively simple expression:

\displaystyle\Pi_{B}^{(0)}=4\int_{1}^{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{E-(2s+\mu)^{2}}{s^{2}-1}}ds,

(106)

where we have used the symmetry
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}=1
and

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{-A-\mu}{2},\qquad\beta=\frac{A-\mu}{2}.

(107)

We see that the derivative of
\Pi_{B}^{(0)}
with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dE}\Pi_{B}^{(0)}=4\int_{1}^{\beta}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{(s^{2}-1)(E-(2s+\mu)^{2})}}=\frac{8}{\sqrt{(-\alpha+1)(\beta+1)}}\eK(\mathsf{m}).

(108)

The classical period
\Pi_{B}^{(0)}
itself does not have such a simple structure, but we can still write down its analytic form in terms of the elliptic integrals, as in (

).
Once we obtain the exact results, we can easily analytically continue them to complex domains.
We can define other classical periods,

\displaystyle\Pi_{A}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=4\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{\frac{E-(2s+\mu)^{2}}{1-s^{2}}}ds,

(109)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\widetilde{B}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=4\int_{\alpha}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{E-(2s+\mu)^{2}}{s^{2}-1}}ds,

(110)

where
\alpha<-1
and
\beta>1
for

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-\biggl(2-\mathsf{n}^{\prime}-\frac{\mathsf{m}^{\prime}}{\mathsf{n}^{\prime}}\biggr)\ePi(\mathsf{n^{\prime}}|\mathsf{m^{\prime}})\biggr],

(111)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\widetilde{B}}^{(0)}

(112)

where

(113)

It is well-known that the classical period integral

(117)

Let us now turn to the computation of the quantum periods. Here we employ an efficient method, known as the differential operator approach
