 ABSTRACT
Online synthesis of power distribution is critical in the operation and control of nuclear power reactors to
ensure that the core is operating within safety margins and to provide essential knowledge associated with
the burnup of the fuel. In light-water reactors, power synthesis is achieved by using some a priori knowl-
edge of the state of the reactor core and updating based on the signals coming from in-core sensors—
namely, self-powered neutron detectors (SPNDs). This report examines the effects of fuel burnup and sen-
sor degradation on the ability to accurately synthesize the power distribution in a pressurized water reactor
(PWR), considering the typical low-enriched uranium (LEU, 3%–5% enrichment) fuel cycle as well as the
higher enrichment LEU+ (5%–8% enrichment) fuel cycle. Several modeling tools were used to simulate
power synthesis based on the responses of SPNDs, with emitters made out of Rh or V. A representative
PWR LEU core was modeled using the Polaris/Purdue Advanced Reactor Core Simulator (PARCS) ap-
proach. The Monte Carlo N-Particle Transport 6 (MCNP6) code was used as well to calculate response
functions between different segments of fuel to individual SPNDs; this is a crucial parameter for power
synthesis. The Oak Ridge Isotope GENeration (ORIGEN) package in the Standardized Computer Anal-
yses for Licensing Evaluation (SCALE) code was used to model the time-dependent isotopic transmuta-
tion in the SPND emitters. All these data were fed into a custom code that enacted the point-based itera-
tive method to simulate power synthesis. Developmental work was also performed on high-fidelity SPND
models in the GEometry ANd Tracking 4 (Geant4) code, which enables higher-accuracy modeling of the
current responses from SPNDs.
In this work, five sets of time-dependent power synthesis test cases were conducted. In these test cases,
systematic changes in the input conditions enabled an analysis of the effect of (1) slightly inaccurate a pri-
ori power distribution assumptions with respect to fuel burnup, (2) highly inaccurate a priori power distri-
bution assumptions with respect to fuel burnup (such that burnup is not included in the a priori assumed
distribution), and (3) differences between Rh and V SPNDs in terms of downstream consequences of the
transmutation in the emitters and the extended nature of the LEU+ fuel cycle in comparison with LEU.
The authors discovered that one may permissibly have slightly inaccurate a priori assumptions of the fuel
burnup (such that the level of burnup may be slightly underapproximated or overapproximated by the ac-
cumulated burnup in approximately 9.3 full power days), but to not account for burnup at all in the a priori
assumptions leads to severe levels of error, approaching 25% at maximum (for LEU). The authors also dis-
covered that V SPNDs are extraordinarily robust in both the LEU and LEU+ fuel cycles considered in this
modeling work, whereas Rh SPNDs undergo significant transmutation that can result in large errors in the
synthesized power distribution.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Traditionally, nuclear power reactors such as light-water reactors (LWRs) have an in-core neutron flux
monitoring system, which typically includes either self-powered neutron detectors (SPNDs) or minia-
ture fission chambers. The data collected from these sensors are used to synthesize the 3D power distri-
bution in the core. A variety of power synthesis methods exist, and they have been described in a variety
of patents (Nishizawa 1982; Impink 1987; Impink and Grobmyer 1988; Impink 1988; Impink and Grob-
myer 1989a, 1989b; Heibel 1996; Fushimi et al. 2011) and studied in peer-reviewed publications and re-
ports (In et al. 1991; Park and Shin 2014; Fu, Zhengpei, and Yongming 1997; Kai et al. 2019; Ramezani
and Ghofrani 2021; Shim et al. 2015; Urase, Endo, and Yamamoto 2024; Makai and Végh 2017; Saeed
and Rashid 2020; Dias and Silva 2016; Li et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2024). Furthermore, to some extent,
these methods have been adopted by industry and used in core-monitoring codes to perform this power

reconstruction (Boyd 2002; Boyd, Book, and Morita 2004; Boyd et al. 2009; Boyd and Krieg 2010; Di-
Giovine and Noël 2003; Molnar and Vocka 2014; Beard and Morita 1994; Morita 2000; General Electric
Company 1999; Global Nuclear Fuel 2016). The power synthesis process, which enables accurate deter-
mination of the power distribution, is crucial for several reasons, including the following: ensuring that the
reactor power distribution complies with safety limits, such as the heat flux hot channel factor; calculat-
ing burnup levels in the various fuel assemblies within the reactor core; and calibrating ex-core sensors by
using in-core sensors to determine the axial neutron flux distribution.
In the power synthesis field, it can be challenging to develop a full understanding of the efficacy of a par-
ticular synthesis method in a given reactor design. Efficacy, in this case, can be understood by various
factors, including the following: how optimal the choices of sensor placement are in or surrounding the
core; whether the fidelity of the synthesized power distribution is sufficient without resulting in a signifi-
cant computational demand to perform the synthesis in real time; whether the method used for synthesis
for a given reactor design is appropriate or whether a different method would perform better in terms of
error reduction or calculation time; and whether certain reactor physics aspects, such as fuel burnup and
sensor degradation, are appropriately considered. Unfortunately, methodological details and supporting
studies performed for the development of codes that are implemented commercially are typically limited
because of their proprietary nature. Therefore, research that is conducted academically or at research insti-
tutions and is publicly disseminated is the key to elucidating a qualitative and quantitative understanding
of reactor power synthesis. Specifically, this research must consider the appropriateness of different meth-
ods in different contexts, the effect of sensor placement and sensor uncertainty on synthesis accuracy, and
the effect of different physics-based perturbations in the reactor power distribution. The authors of this re-
port have conducted several studies to gain a better understanding of such variables and how they affect
the ability to synthesize the power distribution in advanced LWRs (Birri and Blue 2020; Birri, Sweeney,
and Ezell 2022; A. Birri et al. 2023; Birri, Gates, et al. 2024; Birri, Goetz, et al. 2024). Additionally, var-
ious peer-reviewed studies have been conducted with a similar intent (Wenfeng et al. 2001; Xia, Jiang,
and Luxat 2014; Pomerantz, Calabrese, and Grant 2002; Terman, Kojouri, and Khalafi 2018; Bahuguna,
Mukhopadhyay, and Tiwari 2021; Peng et al. 2019; Dall’Osso, Martinez, and Rio 2005; Jang et al. 2004;
Cao, Cao, and Gan 2021).
In the context of reactor power synthesis, the landscape is changing regarding the design, development,
and operation of nuclear reactors in the near and distant future. One aspect of the current landscape to con-
sider is the push for lifetime extensions of both Generation II and Generation III reactor designs (Fernández-
Arias, Vergara, and Orosa 2020). These extensions have implications regarding the degradation and pos-
sible replacement of sensors that are used both in-core and ex-core to support reactor power monitor-
ing. Furthermore, the use of higher-burnup and accident-tolerant fuels is increasingly being promoted for
LWRs (Hall et al. 2021; Cumberland et al. 2021; Hu, Mertyurek, and Wieselquist 2022), which would re-
sult in longer fuel cycles and thus more significant in-core and potentially ex-core sensor drift over the
course of these fuel cycles. Finally, next-generation reactors (i.e., Generation IV reactors), which are under
development, will have higher temperatures and higher in-core fluxes (Chen et al. 2021). The flux spec-
tra for certain Generation IV reactors will also be significantly different (e.g., significantly more weighted
toward fast neutrons) in comparison with traditional LWRs. Thus, for these reactors, sensor degradation,
sensor placement limitations, and implications of fuel burnup must be considered.
Given the future direction of the nuclear industry, the factors that impact the performance of the sensors,
which are used in the in-core and ex-core neutron flux monitoring systems, must be considered. These fac-
tors include the variations of power distributions due to fuel burnups and the burnups of the sensor emitters
themselves, especially in reactors with longer fuel cycles. In this report, particular focus is given to the
in-core sensors, which are exposed to particularly high levels of neutron flux; they are perhaps most rel-
evant for inferring more localized perturbations to the power distribution that can arise due to burnup on

 2

the assembly-to-assembly and pin-by-pin level. For in-core neutron flux monitoring, the two most promi-
nent sensors used for direct measurements are SPNDs and fission chambers. In general, SPNDs are used
in pressurized water reactors (PWRs) (Todt 1996), and fission chambers are used in boiling water reactors
(BWRs) and are called local power range monitors (LPRMs) (Prasad et al. 1998). Both types of detectors
are subject to a loss of sensitivity over time in the reactor because of burnup of the material in question,
which is responsible for driving the current signal. In the case of fission chambers, the fissioning of U-235
results in detector sensitivity reduction as a function of time; in the case of SPNDs, detector sensitivity is
reduced because of transmutation of the primary emitter isotope(s) responsible for ultimately generating
high-energy electrons via neutron/beta, neutron/gamma/electron, or gamma/electron nuclear reactions.
This loss of sensitivity is important to consider because it will result in error in both the inferred thermal
power output magnitude and the synthesized power distribution if not appropriately accounted for via cali-
bration or model-informed corrections.
Clearly, interplay occurs between the burnup of the fuel and the loss of sensitivity in the sensors; both
are dependent on the energy-dependent neutron fluence in the location of that fuel segment or sensor.
Therefore, the impact of both effects, both independently and simultaneously, must be studied. This re-
port presents results regarding the effects of fuel burnup and isotopic inventory changes in the emitters of
SPNDs on the ability to accurately synthesize the 3D power distribution in a representative PWR core.
Two different fuel cycles are considered: one which is an approximately 18 month fuel cycle in which low-
enriched uranium (LEU) (< 5% enrichment) fuel is utilized and one which is an approximately 24 month
fuel cycle in which LEU+ (> 5%, < 8% enrichment) fuel is utilized. Both Rh and V are considered as ma-
terials for SPND emitters for comparison; Rh is more sensitive but exhibits sensitivity loss at a faster rate,
and these are the two predominant emitter materials used in SPNDs. Different levels of fuel burnup are as-
sessed, with different a priori assumptions for burnup used as the initial estimates for the power synthesis
method used herein, which is the point-based iterative (PBI) method. Thus, the consequence associated
with different levels of error in the assumed level of burnup can be functionally understood. SPNDs have
been primarily modeled with simple steady-state analytical models in this work. However, this work also
includes enhancements of SPND models using the GEometry ANd Tracking 4 (Geant4) code so that the
implications of carefully treating electron transport in realistic sensor geometries can also be understood in
the context of fuel burnup and sensor degradation.

2022). The core models were generated using the Polaris/Purdue Advanced Reactor Core Simulator (PARCS)

lations for regulatory reviews. This work focused on the LEU (with < 5% enrichment) core models and on
LEU+ (with enrichments varying between 5% to 8%) core models generated in that project.

2.2 MCNP6 INCREMENTAL FLUXES
Monte Carlo N-Particle Transport 6 (MCNP6) was used to determine incremental fluxes (i.e., flux con-
tributions from individual segments of fuel to individual SPNDs) by using a homogenized model of the
representative PWR core, which was heterogeneously modeled in PARCS. This approach was taken to sig-
nificantly reduce computational demand associated with calculating incremental fluxes because the total
number of incremental fluxes is equal to the number of SPNDs in the core multiplied by the number of fuel
segments for each reactor state. (Note that, for the models described herein, “fuel segments” are assumed
to be axial discretizations of each individual fuel assembly.) These incremental fluxes are used to calculate
response functions, which are the ratio of the electrical current contribution (which comes from one fuel
assembly segment to an SPND) and the power generated in that fuel assembly segment. The consequence
of using homogeneous response functions, as opposed to heterogeneous response functions, is explored in
a previous conference paper (Birri, Goetz, and Ezell 2025); in short, homogeneous response functions will
result in appropriate trends in perturbation synthesis error but may underestimate error magnitude because
homogeneous response functions do not account for SPND “blind spots.”
The particle transport simulations were conducted in a geometry of a homogenized sphere, which was seg-
mented such that it contained a 0.5 cm radius core and 199 0.5 cm thick shells, as shown in Figure 4(a).
This homogenized geometry for response function calculations was developed based on another study
(Blue and Jones 2023). The central core of the homogeneous sphere had fissions enabled, whereas fissions
were not enabled in any of the shells (where secondary fissions were ignored in this approach, given that
the focus is to account for neutron flux from the central core to the sensor). This design allows the core of
the sphere to act as a respective fuel segment, whereas the shells act as potential regions in which a sen-
sor may exist and come in contact with neutrons being emitted from the central core. This geometry thus
effectively enables the determination of incremental flux as a function of distance between a fuel assem-
bly segment and an SPND. In terms of MCNP6 functionality, the fissions were disabled in all shells by

 9

using the NONU card, and F4 tallies were applied to each shell with log-scaled energy binning to deter-
mine the per source particle flux distributions. A point source was placed in the core of the sphere using
the Maxwell fission spectrum and the default Maxwellian temperature in MCNP6.
The homogeneous sphere material included the UO2 fuel, Zircaloy cladding and fuel assembly structures,
and water. The ZrB2 in the integral fuel burnable absorber (IFBA) rods as well as the B4 C in the wet an-
nular burnable absorber (WABA) and control rods were ignored because these boron-containing materials
would reduce the thermal peak of the incremental fluxes in a manner that is nonrepresentative of the bulk
heterogeneous core. The densities of the UO2 , Zircaloy, and water were assumed to be identical to those
determined in a previous study on an AP1000 (Birri, Sweeney, and Ezell 2022). The neutron cross sec-
tion data were collected from the ENDF/B-VII.0 libraries (Trellue et al. 2009), where the cross sections
for the isotopes in the fuel were evaluated at 1,200 K, whereas all other isotopes were evaluated at 600 K.
The isotopic fractions in the homogenized sphere were determined based on the volume specifications of
fuel assemblies (Godfrey 2014). The molar fractions of the different isotopes in the representative PWR
are shown in Figure 4(b), where Nb, Sn, Fe, O, and H are assumed to have natural isotopic abundances.
These molar fractions are very similar to those in an AP1000 (Birri, Sweeney, and Ezell 2022), except that
the total content of U in this representative PWR is lower because the total volume (i.e., number of fuel as-
semblies) is higher. Calculations were performed in MCNP6 for LEU and LEU+; as shown in Figure 4(b),
LEU+ calculations involved a notably higher molar fraction of U-235.

2.3 SCALE/ORIGEN
Burnup calculations were performed to calculate the isotopic transmutations in the SPND emitters (i.e.,
V, Rh) because of the neutron irradiations in the core. Such calculations were performed on both the maxi-
mum power assemblies in the respective LEU and LEU+ cores. ORIGEN Assembly Isotopics (ORIGAMI)—
a module in the SCALE code suite (Wieselquist and Lefebvre 2024)—was used in the first step to calcu-
late the time-dependent total neutron flux in the respective fuel assembly. For the maximum power assem-
bly, the power history (i.e., specific power and on-power days in each time step) calculated by PARCS was
multiplied with the maximum axial power factor among the five SPND axial locations before being fed
into the ORIGAMI calculations, which output the total neutron flux for this assembly into multiple time
steps.
In the second step, the time-dependent flux obtained from the previous step was then fed into the Oak
Ridge Isotope GENeration (ORIGEN) (Wieselquist and Lefebvre 2024) calculations to simulate the irra-

 11

diation of the SPND emitters. A typical reactor cross section library for a 17 × 17 fuel assembly (with ini-
tial enrichments set based on the enrichments in the maximum power locations in both the LEU and LEU+
cores) was used in both the ORIGAMI and ORIGEN calculations. An initial quantity of 1.9195 g was used
for the V emitter (with natural abundance), and an initial quantity of 0.8250 g was used for the Rh emit-

2.4 GEANT4
Geant4 simulations, especially those involving neutron reactions, are very computationally expensive be-
cause they require a large number of events to achieve statistical significance. An added complication for
power inferencing specifically is that numerous runs are required to account for the contributions of all
the different fuel elements. Therefore, significant efforts were made in this work to streamline the Geant4
simulation while simultaneously adding features relevant to the task at hand.
Fortunately, power inferencing does not require the same level of detail as detector design. The original
branch of the SPND simulation included tracking to identify the source of each electron generated in the
simulation. This tracking is useful during the design phase to tune a detector’s response to, for example,
neutrons over gamma rays. This feature is also useful in designing for detector response time through iden-
tifying which isotope is producing electrons if the chosen element in the emitter has many stable isotopes
or if there is a strongly produced daughter isotope with a nonideal half-life. This tracking is highly compu-
tationally expensive and was removed, yielding a 2.5× increase in speed on four cores.
One feature that has not previously been included in SPND simulation is the space charge effect. Space
charge is the buildup of an electromagnetic field in the insulator of an SPND operating at steady state. This
electromagnetic field will direct an electron leaving the emitter back into the emitter or, if it is energetic
enough to pass over the potential barrier, eject it straight to the collector, where it then counts as signal
produced in the detector. Geant4 has a native module for electromagnetic fields, but it is very computa-
tionally expensive, as observed during previous efforts on fission chambers. Therefore, the authors decided
that the best way to handle the electromagnetic field generation was through an empirical model. Sang et
al. demonstrated that this can be achieved by providing an energy cutoff, emin , to the electrons leaving the
emitter, where it is assumed that all electrons with energy greater than emin will pass the potential barrier to
the collector and produce a signal (Sang et al. 2021). To find emin , one must first solve for electric potential
in the insulator using Poisson’s equation, shown as Eq. (1), where ρ (r) is the space charge density in the

3.2 SENSOR CONSIDERATIONS
SPNDs were arranged in the representative PWR core as sensor strings in specific fuel assemblies through-
out the core. Although this representative PWR core has differences from a Westinghouse AP1000 core,
the arrangement of SPNDs in the representative PWR was based on the arrangement of SPNDs in an AP1000
(Sun et al. 2017). The authors assumed that the sensor strings were placed in the central location in the
17 × 17 fuel pin bundle, the instrumentation guide thimble. Each sensor string had four SPNDs arranged
in the axial direction of the assembly. In total, 50 sensor strings were placed strategically in 50 fuel as-
semblies throughout the core, resulting in a total of 200 SPNDs in the core. Although this may seem like
a large number of SPNDs, it is on par with the number of SPNDs in an AP1000, which is 210 (Sun et
al. 2017). The arrangement of sensor strings throughout the representative PWR core studied in this work
is depicted in Figure 9. This arrangement applies identically to both the LEU and LEU+ cores.

This work considered and compared Rh and V SPNDs. The benefit of using Rh as the emitter material is
that the neutron sensitivity is quite high, resulting in a relatively high electrical current response, which
is easier to amplify and maintain a high signal-to-noise ratio. The trade-off is that the primary stable iso-
tope that drives the current response following neutron absorption, Rh-103, will burn up at a relatively fast
rate, resulting in a drop-off of signal as a function of time throughout the fuel cycle. Meanwhile, a V emit-
ter will burn up at a far slower rate (due to the much lower neutron absorption cross section of the stable
isotope V-51), although its neutron sensitivity is far lower than that of Rh, resulting in a more challenging
signal amplification problem and a lower signal-to-noise ratio. Implications of signal processing are not
considered in this work. These implications must be considered when comparing the synthesis results that
are informed from Rh or V SPNDs.

3.3 DESCRIPTION OF SYNTHESIS TEST CASES
In total, five sets of power synthesis test cases were considered in this study (with each set containing a
case for V and a case for Rh emitters), such that each test case involved simulating power synthesis based
on an array of SPNDs (per the configuration in Figure 9) over the course of the fuel cycle at a time step of
approximately 9.2 days for LEU and 11.9 days for LEU+ to study the impacts of the a priori power dis-
tribution and burnup effects of the emitter materials. A summary of the different test cases is provided in
Table 3. For the V and Rh emitters, the test cases are designated accordingly with a “-V” or “-Rh” at the
end of the test designation.
In each test case, a different a priori distribution is used; it serves as an estimated distribution that initial-
izes the iterative process, which is inherent to synthesis via the PBI method. This a priori distribution rep-
resents the modeled power distribution that would serve as the initial estimate in an actual power distri-
bution synthesis, with real sensors and instrumentation, before conducting the PBI method based on data

array. Evidently, the exact values of the error are indifferent to whether the burnup conditions are over-
approximated or underapproximated. Also, the error values are indifferent to whether V or Rh SPNDs is
used. The emitter material is indifferent because the sensor degradation effects are not considered in these
test cases and because the PBI method operates based on the normalized distribution of sensor signals (i.e.,
the magnitude of the signals is not a factor).
For test cases 1d-V and 1d-Rh, where the burnup was not accounted for in the a priori distribution (and
thus the t = 0 distribution was used), the errmax and erravg rapidly increased as a function of time (as Pa
and Pt depart from one another significantly), thus leading to large errors in P s . In fact, errmax values be-
came unacceptably high, exceeding 20%. Therefore, in the context of this particular reactor fuel loading

 22

pattern, the a priori distribution must account for burnup to some extent so that an accurate synthesis can
be performed. For this particular reactor design, the need for this is likely because of the cross-hatching
pattern that burns into the power distribution such that fresh fuel assemblies start to exhibit higher power
densities in comparison to once- or twice-burned assemblies (Figure 6). These variations in the power dis-
tribution occur on a spatial scale that is smaller than the spacing associated with the SPNDs in the radial
direction. Previous systematic studies of power perturbations have demonstrated that spatially small per-
turbations are particularly challenging to synthesize accurately (Birri, Goetz, et al. 2024).
To understand how the assembly-segment-level error (i.e., errA ) is distributed among the fuel assembly
segments, errA was plotted for five axial segments associated with P s for test cases 1d-V and 1b-V, as
shown in Figure 13. Notably, errA can be mathematically defined as

                                      errA = (100%)|Pt,A − P s,A |/Pt,A .                                  (9)

For test case 1d-V, the error generally took on the cross-hatching pattern associated with the burnup, in-
dicating that the PBI method had difficulty resolving such small perturbations when they were not in any
way included in Pa . Conversely, for test case 1b-V, when compared on the same color scale, the errA val-
ues were essentially negligible.

4.3 COMPARISON OF SENSOR DEGRADATION EFFECTS BETWEEN V AND RH
The final test cases to analyze were 2-V, 2-V+, 2-Rh, and 2-Rh+, which included perfect a priori distribu-
tions as well as sensor degradation effects based on Eq. (5) and Eq. (6). The resultant percent reductions in
ISV and ISRh at end of cycle for all sensors distributed throughout the core are shown in Figure 14. Regard-
ing the V SPNDs in test cases 2-V and 2-V+, the current reduction was negligible on the color scale that
allows for comparison with the Rh SPNDs. This is an initially promising sign that V is a particularly ro-
bust emitter for both the LEU and LEU+ fuel cycles. Conversely, the Rh SPNDs in test case 2-Rh showed
generally high reductions in signal (as high as 45%); the reductions in signal for Rh SPNDs in test case
2-Rh+ were even higher (as high as 57%). The axial dependence in this current reduction was relatively
slight compared with the drop-off in the current reduction in the corners of the core (where the neutron flu-
ence values are the lowest). The variable changes in current, as a function of locations in which Rh SPNDs
exist, are problematic because they will be erroneously interpreted as variations in the power distribution if
the signal processing schemes do not correct for them in some manner.
The resultant errmax and erravg values associated with test cases 2-V, 2-V+, 2-Rh, and 2-Rh+ were plot-
ted as a function of time, as shown in Figure 15. For test cases 2-V and 2-V+, the errmax and erravg values
stayed remarkably low. In fact, errmax only reached 0.4% by end of cycle for LEU in test case 2-V, and
it only reached 0.6% by end of cycle for LEU+ in test case 2-V+. Conversely, for test cases 2-Rh and 2-
Rh+, the errmax and erravg values were approximately 2 orders of magnitude higher for all times through-
out the fuel cycles for LEU and LEU+. One interesting feature of Figure 15 is the extreme rate at which
errmax and erravg increased as a function of time in the early portion of the fuel cycle, followed by a more
moderate increase; the exact reason for the phenomenon is unclear at this time.
To understand the distribution of errA and how test cases 2-Rh, 2-Rh+, 2-V, and 2-V+ compare, the errA
distributions were plotted for each test case at end of cycle, as shown in Figure 16. As expected, for test
cases 2-Rh and 2-Rh+, the error was consolidated to the corners of the core, where the drop-off in ISRh was
less severe. This error was relatively extreme. In contrast, the errA values toward the radial center of the
core were relatively reasonable. These findings demonstrate that in regions where the spatial dependence
of the burnup was relatively flat, the Rh SPNDs burned up their Rh-103 content at similar rates, and thus
the PBI method performed reasonably well in these regions of the core.

 5. CONCLUSION
This work demonstrates that time-dependent physics associated with the reactor core and in-core SPND
sensors can significantly influence the ability to accurately perform power synthesis in an online control
algorithm for representative LEU and LEU+ fueled PWR cores. The a priori assumptions regarding power
distribution, which come from neutronic model estimates, must be sufficiently accurate relative to the true
state of the reactor core at any given time. The need for accurate a priori power distribution assumptions
specifically relates to the manner by which the burnup of fuel causes local perturbations in the radial power
distribution due to the fuel loading pattern of the fresh, once-burned, and twice-burned fuel assemblies.
Not accounting for burnup at all in the a priori assumed distribution would result in significant errors in
the synthesized power distribution, which would likely prevent ensuring that safety margins are being
maintained throughout the fuel cycle. Furthermore, V SPNDs are significantly more robust than Rh SP-
NDs throughout the fuel cycle because V has significantly less emitter material burnup than Rh; V SPNDs
therefore enable highly accurate power synthesis, and this has been demonstrated to be true for both the
LEU and LEU+ fuel cycles. This conclusion, of course, ignores the challenges associated with amplifica-
tion of the signal from a V SPND. The results from this work provide useful quantitative data to support
the design and optimization of in-core sensor arrays and to establish the degree to which the level of bur-
nup in the core must be accurately predicted to perform power distribution synthesis with sufficiently low
error. These data may be useful to industry stakeholders who are looking to undergo power uprates for
their plants, are interested in improving their sensing systems and associated control systems, or are seek-
ing to adapt well-characterized synthesis methods for advanced reactor designs. Furthermore, the synthesis
data from modeled V SPNDs herein give confidence in the notion that extended fuel cycles with higher-
enrichment fuels will not introduce unreasonable synthesis errors as a consequence of SPND degradation
so long as the emitters are constructed out of V.
