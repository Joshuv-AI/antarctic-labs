Classical correlation functions at strong coupling from hexagonalization
Benjamin Basso 1 , Erkan Kaluç 1,2, and Didina Serban 1,2

Abstract
We study correlation functions of half-BPS operators in planar 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 Super-Yang-Mills at strong coupling, in the classical limit where operator dimensions scale with the coupling. We focus on the two-dimensional kinematics corresponding in the dual description to strings propagating in A ​ d ​ S 3 × S 3 AdS_{3}\times S^{3} . Using the hexagon formalism, we show that correlation functions exponentiate in this regime and are governed by the free energy of an associated set of Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz (TBA) equations. These equations are structurally equivalent to the Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke equations encoding BPS spectra in 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 supersymmetric field theories. Exploiting this correspondence, we apply wall-crossing techniques to extend the TBA framework and formulate a χ \chi -system applicable both to polygonal hexagon tilings and to closed geometries describing correlators of single-trace operators. In particular, for four-point functions, this construction generalizes the results of Caetano and Toledo for minimal surfaces in A ​ d ​ S 2 × S 1 AdS_{2}\times S^{1} .

Introduction and summary of results

Integrability provides a powerful framework for computing correlation functions of local operators in planar
\cal N=4
Super-Yang-Mills (SYM) theory. In the case of two-point functions, it has led to the Quantum Spectral Curve

;

In some cases, precise control over spectral data has also led to significant progress in the study of higher-point functions. Notable recent examples include correlation functions on the circular Wilson-Maldacena loop and four-point functions of stress-tensors
Cavaglia:2021bnz
;
Cavaglia:2022qpg

Caron-Huot:2024tzr
;

At strong coupling, detailed knowledge of the string energy levels—combined with insights from the string worldsheet theory and analyticity constraints—have driven further advances, for instance in the computation of curvature corrections to Virasoro–Shapiro amplitudes
Alday:2022xwz
;

;
Alday:2023mvu
;

While spectral data provide powerful constraints, a direct integrability-based treatment of general multi-point correlators must go beyond the spectral problem. The state-of-the-art framework for correlators is the hexagon form factor formalism for structure constants

Geometrically, the con- struction parallels the triangulation of punctured surfaces, with the punctures representing the operator insertions and the edges of the triangulation their planar Wick contractions. Each hexagon has three mirror edges obtained by cutting along the corresponding propa- gators, while the remaining edges correspond to the operators themselves, represented as spin chains or strings. The full correlator is obtained by gluing hexagons, with the gluing implemented by summing over complete sets of mirror magnons propagating across the cuts.

This framework applies at arbitrary coupling and has led to numerous results, particularly for three- and four-point functions, where hexagonalization reproduces perturbative data to high loop orders

While inspiring, the methods developed for the octagon are difficult to extend to higher-point functions or to configurations involving sums and integrals over mirror states on multiple cuts. In practice, such computations are extremely challenging and typically limited to the lowest nontrivial orders at weak coupling

;
Bargheer:2018jvq

DeLeeuw:2019dak

Crisanti:2024vnd

The regime of interest corresponds to the classical limit, in which both the coupling constant and the operator dimensions scale to infinity simultaneously. In this regime, a classical string description becomes reliable, and correlation functions are expected to be exponentially suppressed and governed by minimal surfaces in
AdS_{5}\times S_{5}
. String theory suggests that a general integrable description should exist in this limit, rooted in the integrability of the worldsheet theory.

Before the development of the hexagonalization approach, an important step in that direction was already taken by Caetano and Toledo
Caetano:2012ac
who, working directly from the worldsheet theory, studied four-point functions of half-BPS operators in a one-dimensional kinematic setup. Their methods were based on earlier approaches for three-point functions of spinning strings

AdS_{2}\times S^{1}

;
Alday:2010vh
;
Alday:2009dv

In this paper, we revisit the classical problem from the hexagonalization perspective and generalize the findings of
Caetano:2012ac
to higher-point functions of half-BPS operators in the large-charge regime
L_{i}\sim g\gg 1

O(4)
scalar subsector, or equivalently to string configurations in an
AdS_{3}\times S^{3}

Our main result is a set of TBA equations governing the classical strong-coupling limit of these correlation functions. Starting from hexagonalization, the correlators are expressed as partition functions
\mathbf{Z}
of magnon systems associated with triangulations of the worldsheet. In the classical regime, this representation simplifies dramatically: the system reduces to weakly interacting magnons with purely diagonal interactions, in contrast to the finite-coupling case where the matrix structure of the form factors leads to substantial technical complications.

These simplifications allow the partition function to exponentiate, with a free energy governed by a Yang–Yang functional associated with a set of TBA equations, in close analogy with null polygonal Wilson loops

;
Alday:2009dv
;
Alday:2010vh
;
Basso:2013vsa

;
Fioravanti_2015

\mathbf{Z}\approx\exp{-2g \cA}\,,\qquad\cal A=\sum_{\gamma}\cal A_{\gamma}\,,

(1)

\cal A_{\gamma}=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}\theta}\,\left[R(Y_{\gamma}^{\textrm{Sph}}(\theta))+R(\bar{Y}^{\textrm{Sph}}_{\gamma}(\theta))-R(Y^{\textrm{AdS}}_{\gamma}(\theta))-R(\bar{Y}^{\textrm{AdS}}_{\gamma}(\theta))\right]\,,

(2)

where
\theta

R(Y)

R(Y)\equiv\textrm{Li}_{2}(-Y)+\frac{1}{2}\log{(Y/\cY)}\log{(1+Y)}\,.

(3)

Here the
Y

Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)
come in four species, reflecting the
PSU(2|2)
symmetry of the hexagon construction
Basso:2015zoa

AdS_{3}\times S^{3}
. They satisfy TBA equations of the form

\log{Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)}=\log{{\cY}_{\gamma}^{I}(\theta)}+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}\sum_{J}(-1)^{F_{J}+1}\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle K^{IJ}\star\log{(1+ Y^{J}_{\gamma'}(\theta))}\,,

(4)

where
(-1)^{F_{J}}

F_{\textrm{Sph}}=0,F_{\textrm{AdS}}=1

\cal Y_{\gamma}^{I}(\theta)

K^{IJ}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
capture magnon interactions. Remarkably, these kernels are directly determined by the strong-coupling limit of the magnon
S
-matrix, essentially arising from its logarithm.
We present several equivalent formulations of these equations. In one, the kernel matrix becomes block-diagonal, effectively decoupling the AdS and sphere sectors at the cost of redefining the driving terms. In another, the kernels are recast in a relativistically invariant form.

A key aspect of the construction is the sum over magnon states labeled by
\gamma
. In simple cases, these labels correspond one-to-one with the cuts of the triangulation, with each cut supporting four
Y
-functions coupled to those on neighboring cuts. The structure of these interactions is encoded in skew-symmetric pairings
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
, which define the quiver of the triangulation.

This quiver structure is closely related to the convolution terms in the TBA equations. Since the kernels develop pole singularities when rapidities coincide, one must prescribe an ordering of the integration contours
\{\cal C_{\gamma}\}
associated with the
Y
-functions, consistent with kinematical limits. For linear quivers this ordering is straightforward, but it becomes nontrivial in the presence of cycles. Wall-crossing transformations
Gaiotto:2010okc
;
Alday:2010vh
;
Alday:2009dv
;
Toledo:PhDthesis
resolve this issue by allowing contour reordering at the cost of introducing composite
Y

SU(2)
Hitchin systems.

As usual, the integral equations admit an equivalent formulation in terms of functional relations of
Y
-system type. In our case, this system reflects the singularity structure described above and can be written as

\displaystyle Y_{\gamma}^{++}\,\bar{Y}_{\gamma}=\prod_{\gamma^{\prime}\succ\gamma}(1+Y_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{++})^{\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\rangle}\prod_{\gamma^{\prime}\prec\gamma}\left(1+\bar{Y}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right)^{\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle}\,,

(5)

together with the conjugate system obtained by
Y\leftrightarrow\bar{Y}
, where
Y^{++}\equiv Y(\theta+i\pi)
and
\succ
denotes the contour ordering. In this form, the equations are identical in the AdS and sphere sectors, which therefore appear completely decoupled.

Following
Gaiotto:2010okc
, the system can be further reformulated in terms of a
\chi
-system, providing an alternative set of functional equations with a universal geometric structure. We carry out this analysis in detail for polygonal hexagon tilings, which generalize the octagon case. We then discuss the extension to closed-string geometries corresponding to correlators of single-trace operators, focusing in particular on the four-point function. In the
AdS_{2}\times S^{1}
subkinematics, we find perfect agreement with the functional equations and minimal-area results obtained by Caetano and Toledo
Caetano:2012ac

Hexagonalization and cluster algebras

2.1
Hexagonalization of correlation functions

In this section we review the hexagonalization framework introduced in
Fleury:2016ykk
;
Fleury:2017eph
. In this work we apply it to
n
-point correlation functions of the simplest protected operators, namely the chiral primary operators built from the six real scalar fields
\Phi_{I}(x)
of the theory, with
I=1,\ldots,6
. In the integrability picture these operators correspond to vacuum states of the associated spin chains.
They take the form

\mathcal{O}_{L_{i}}(x_{i},y_{i})=\Tr\,\left(y_{i}\cdot\Phi(x_{i})\right)^{L_{i}}:=y_{i}^{I_{1}}\ldots y_{i}^{I_{L_{i}}}\Tr\,\Phi_{I_{1}}(x_{i})\ldots\Phi_{I_{L_{i}}}(x_{i})\;,

(6)

where
L_{i}
is the R-charge of the operator (equivalently the spin-chain length). Since these operators are protected, their scaling dimension is
\Delta_{i}=L_{i}
to all loops. The coordinates
x^{\mu}_{i}
denote spacetime positions, while
y^{I}_{i}
are null polarization vectors in six dimensions satisfying
y_{i}\cdot y_{i}:=\sum^{6}_{I=1}y_{i}^{I}y_{i}^{I}=0
.

Throughout this paper we restrict to two-dimensional kinematics,
x_{i}^{0}=x_{i}^{3}=0
, so that the operator insertions lie in a Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We then parametrize each insertion point as
x_{i}=(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i})
with complex coordinates

\mathbf{x}_{i}=x_{i}^{1}+ix_{i}^{2}\,,\qquad\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=x_{i}^{1}-ix_{i}^{2}\,.

(7)

We also restrict the polarization vectors to an
O(4)
subsector by imposing
y^{5}_{i}=y^{6}_{i}=0
. The resulting vectors
y_{i}=(y^{a}_{i},0,0)
define complex null rays in four dimensions and can be parametrized by points on another Riemann sphere,

\mathbf{y}_{i}=y_{i}^{1}+iy_{i}^{2}\,,\qquad\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=y_{i}^{1}-iy_{i}^{2}\,.

(8)

In this way, the R-symmetry variables mirror the structure of the spacetime coordinates. On the dual gravity side, these kinematical restrictions correspond to focusing on strings propagating on
AdS_{3}\times S^{3}
.

The hexagonalization conjecture expresses the
n-
point correlation functions at large
N
as a sum over planar graphs dressed by dynamical factors,

\langle\mathcal{O}_{L_{1}}(x_{1},y_{1})\ldots\mathcal{O}_{L_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n})\rangle_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{L_{1}\ldots L_{n}}}{N^{n-2}}\sum_{\text{graphs}}\left(\prod_{E}(d_{E})^{\ell_{E}}\right)\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{E}\}]\,,

(9)

where the formula applies to the connected part of the correlator and for properly normalized operators (see
Fleury:2016ykk
). In the weak coupling limit,
g^{2}\rightarrow 0
, the dynamical factors reduce to
\mathbf{Z}=1+\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
, and the sum reproduces the standard Feynman graph decomposition of the tree-level correlator. Each graph corresponds to a pattern of Wick contractions, while each edge
E
encodes a set of contractions between pairs of operators. More precisely, a Wick-contraction between scalar fields in
\mathcal{O}_{L_{i}}(x_{i},y_{i})
and
\mathcal{O}_{L_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})
contributes a factor
d_{E}
associated with the edge
E\equiv ij
,

d_{E}:=\frac{y_{ij}^{2}}{x_{ij}^{2}}\;,

(10)

where
x_{ij}^{2}=(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}
is the spacetime distance squared and
y_{ij}^{2}=(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}=-2y_{i}\cdot y_{j}
the invariant product of polarization vectors. The number of such contractions defines the bridge length
\ell_{E}
of the edge
E
.

The factor
\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{E}\}]
encodes the all-loop dynamical contributions associated with a given graph in (
9
). By construction, it is invariant under conformal and R-symmetry transformations and therefore depends only on spacetime and R-symmetry cross-ratios. These enter through a set of group elements
\{\textrm{g}_{E}\}
associated with the edges, as explained below. According to the hexagon proposal, this dynamical factor can be computed at any value of the coupling by decomposing the graph into hexagons and gluing them along their mirror edges.

To obtain this decomposition, it is useful to view the planar graph as defining a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of a
n
-punctured sphere, where each puncture corresponds to an operator and each graph edge maps to an edge of the triangulation. This identification is straightforward when all bridge lengths are nonzero. When some bridge lengths vanish, however, the triangulation is no longer unique: the graph defines an equivalence class of triangulations related by flips of zero-bridge-length edges (see Figure
5
). In such cases, one may choose any triangulation compatible with the graph, as the final result is independent of this choice.

An example of triangulation for the four-point function is shown in Figure
1
. The hexagon picture emerges by replacing each puncture with a small circle, as in the spin-chain or string description of the operator, so that each triangle becomes a hexagon representing a non-local form factor. Each hexagon thus has three physical edges, corresponding to arcs of these circles, and three mirror edges, corresponding to the edges of the triangulation. The latter are also referred to as mirror cuts, and in what follows we use the terms
edge
and
cut
interchangeably.

The nontrivial step is to glue hexagons along their common mirror edges. This amounts to inserting a complete basis of mirror magnons
\psi_{E}
on each edge. Schematically, one obtains

\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{E}\}]=\sum_{\{\psi_{E}\}}\prod_{E\,\in\,\mathcal{T}}\mu_{\psi_{E}}\;\textrm{g}_{\psi_{E}}(\ell_{E},z_{E},\bar{z}_{E},\alpha_{E},\bar{\alpha}_{E})\prod_{E_{i},E_{j},E_{k}\,\in\,\mathcal{T}}\mathfrak{h}(\psi_{E_{i}}|\psi_{E_{j}}|\psi_{E_{k}})\;,

(11)

where
\psi_{E_{i,j,k}}
are the states associated with the three edges of a triangle in
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathfrak{h}(\psi_{E_{i}}|\psi_{E_{j}}|\psi_{E_{k}})
is the corresponding hexagon form factor, and
\mu_{\psi_{E}}
is the measure for the sum over mirror magnons.

Each state
\psi_{E}
propagating along an edge
E

\textrm{g}_{\psi_{E}}

\ell_{E}
and the spacetime and R-symmetry cross-ratios

\textrm{g}_{\psi_{E}}=\langle\psi_{E}|\textrm{g}_{E}|\psi_{E}\rangle

2.2
Preliminaries on quivers and cluster algebras

To handle more efficiently the triangulation data underlying the hexagonalization, it is useful to introduce the related notions of quivers and cluster algebras. These structures have been extensively studied in the mathematical literature, particularly in the context of marked surfaces and the surface realization of cluster algebras (see, for instance, the foundational work
Fomin2008
and the pedagogical introduction
iwaki2014
). Here, we restrict attention to the structures relevant for our purposes and refer the reader to the literature for further details.

Quivers from triangulations.

In our setup, we consider a marked surface
\mathcal{S}
, possibly with boundary, where the marked points correspond to operator insertions. These points may lie either on the boundary (vertices) or in the interior of the surface (punctures). We are interested in triangulations of the surface
\mathcal{S}
, and we denote by
\mathrm{N}
the number of edges in a triangulation and by
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
the set of all triangulations of
\mathcal{S}
. As mentioned above, to define cross-ratios unambiguously, we fix an orientation for each triangulation, indicated by an orange arrow in Figure
2
and by orange circles in Figure
3
.

To ensure the existence of a well-defined cluster algebra structure, certain degenerate cases must be excluded, such as a monogon with zero or one puncture. In the present work, we also disregard pathological configurations in which triangulations contain self-folded triangles. The latter necessitates tagged triangulations. Further details may be found in the references cited above.

The combinatorial data of a triangulation, together with the choice of orientation, can be encoded in a quiver, i.e., an oriented graph. Given a triangulation

From one-cut to

As reviewed above, hexagonalization reduces a planar correlator to the partition function

3.1
Octagon as a one-cut resummation

Before turning to more general cases, let us revisit the simplest example, in which two hexagons are glued into an octagon along a bridge of length
\ell
. This object first appeared in the study of four-point functions of large-charge BPS operators, where four large bridges effectively split the sphere into two octagons
Coronado:2018cxj
. At strong coupling, it takes the form of a TBA-like free energy for a four-flavor system
Bargheer:2019exp
. As this expression will serve as a basic building block for higher-point polygons, we briefly review its derivation and set up the notation.

Kinematically, this configuration can be viewed as a triangulation of a quadrilateral. It is characterized by the cross ratios
z,\,\bar{z}
and
\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}
, defined as in (
12
), which we parametrize as

\displaystyle z=e^{-\sigma^{\textrm{AdS}}+i\phi^{\textrm{AdS}}}\,,\quad\alpha=e^{-\sigma^{\textrm{Sph}}+i\phi^{\textrm{Sph}}}\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\bar{z}=e^{-\sigma^{\textrm{AdS}}-i\phi^{\textrm{AdS}}}\,,\quad\bar{\alpha}=e^{-\sigma^{\textrm{Sph}}-i\phi^{\textrm{Sph}}}\,.

In this case, there is a single mirror cut and thus a single mirror sum is needed in (
11

\textbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})
and bound-state indices
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
, with
a_{i}=1,2,\ldots
and where
a_{i}=1
denotes the fundamental magnons. Since the magnons transform in non-trivial representations of the spin-chain symmetry group, an additional summation over their internal degrees of freedom (flavors) is required.

The result at arbitrary coupling reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}]=\sum_{N\,=\,0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\int d{\textbf{u}}\,\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{u})\,T_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{u};\textrm{g})\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{u})=\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{N}\mu_{a_{i}}(u_{i})\;\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}H_{a_{i}a_{j}}(u_{i},u_{j})\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad d{\textbf{u}}=\prod_{j\,=\,1}^{N}\frac{du_{j}}{2\pi\epsilon}\,.

Both

and

\displaystyle x(u)=u+\sqrt{u^{2}-1}\,,\qquad x^{[\pm a]}(u)=x\!\left(u\pm\frac{ia\epsilon}{2}\right)\,,

which can be found in

Jiang:2016ulr

The sum over magnon flavors, together with the dependence on the cross ratios, is encoded in the average of the generalized transfer matrices

3.1.3
Exponentiation and clustering

The measure factors (
20
) also simplify enormously at strong coupling, approximating to

\displaystyle H_{a_{i},a_{j}}(u_{i},u_{j})\approx\frac{(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}+\frac{(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}}{4}\epsilon^{2}}{(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}+\frac{(a_{i}+a_{j})^{2}}{4}\epsilon^{2}}\,,\qquad\mu_{a}(u)\approx\frac{1}{a}\,,

(40)

up to smooth terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, which will be irrelevant here. This shows that interactions between magnons die off rapidly for
u_{i}-u_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, so that the multi-particle sum in (
19
) exponentiates.

However, there remains a subtle but important effect coming from regions where two rapidities approach each other within separations of order
u-v\sim\epsilon
in (
40
). In these regions, the rational factors in the two-particle measure develop poles that pinch the integration contours. These singularities modify the combinatorics of the bound-state expansion, giving rise to a phenomenon known as
clustering
. This phenomenon was analyzed from both the combinatorial and functional perspectives in
Jiang:2016ulr
, and applied to the octagon computation in
Bargheer:2019exp
.
6
6

6

This phenomenon is common in integrable systems, appearing for instance in the Nekrasov partition function
Nekrasov:2009rc
;
Bourgine_2014
, stochastic processes
borodin2013macdonaldprocesses
and in resummations of the Pentagon OPE for null Wilson loops
Fioravanti_2015
.
In the functional approach, clustering amounts to combining the two-particle interaction terms into a Cauchy determinant
Bettelheim_2014
, which allows the full sum to exponentiate and be rewritten as a Cauchy–Fredholm determinant. A brief review is provided in Appendix
A
.

The net effect of clustering at strong coupling is to modify the one-particle measure according to

\displaystyle\mu_{a}(u)\,T^{\omega}_{a}(u)\;\approx\;\textrm{Str}_{\mathscr{V}_{a}^{\omega}}\,\frac{\textrm{g}}{a}\quad\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\textrm{clustering}}}\quad-\,\textrm{Str}_{\mathscr{V}_{1}^{\omega}}\,\frac{(-\textrm{g})^{a}}{a^{2}}=-\,\textrm{Str}\,\frac{(-\mathcal{Y}_{\omega})^{a}}{a^{2}}\,.

(41)

The interpretation of (
41
) becomes natural once bound states are viewed as unbound stacks of magnons. More precisely, in the classical limit the constituents of a bound state effectively become unbound, so that the sum over bound states and their associated transfer matrices can be reorganized as a sum over stacks built from the four fundamental excitations.

Here, the symbol
Str
, when used without an explicit specification of the representation, denotes the graded sum over the four fundamental magnon flavors, with positive signs assigned to the sphere (Sph) excitations and negative signs to the AdS ones. More generally,
Str
is defined for an arbitrary function

as

\displaystyle\textrm{Str}\,F[\mathcal{Y}]\equiv\sum_{I}(-1)^{F_{I}}F[\mathcal{Y}^{I}]=F[\mathcal{Y}^{\textrm{Sph}}]+F[\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\textrm{Sph}}]-F[\mathcal{Y}^{\textrm{AdS}}]-F[\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\textrm{AdS}}]\,.

(42)

After clustering, the strong-coupling resummation yields a remarkably compact expression for the octagon free energy,

\mathbb{O}\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\,=\,\pm}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cal A_{\omega}\right]\,,\qquad\cal A_{\omega}=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}\theta}\,\textrm{Str}\,\textrm{Li}_{2}\left(-\cal Y_{\omega}(\theta)\right)\ ,

(43)

where the integration over the mirror rapidity
u
has been replaced by the relativistic rapidity
\theta
according to

du=\frac{d\theta}{\cosh^{2}\theta}\,.

(44)

The appearance of the dilogarithm in the effective action is a direct consequence of the renormalization of the measure in (
41
) by clustering.

In this form, the result appears to differ slightly from that obtained in eq. (2.21) of ref.
Bargheer:2019exp
. In fact, one can show that the two results coincide after performing an integration by parts in eq. (
43
) and going to the subkinematics
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}=-1
considered in that reference. In this restricted kinematics, the two dressings are equivalent and it is possible to combine the various
Y
–functions into a single effective one, using

1+Y_{\textrm{\cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{Bargheer:2019exp}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}}}\Big(\theta-\frac{i\pi}{2}\Big)\equiv\frac{(1+\cal Y^{\textrm{Sph}}(\theta))\,(1+\bar{\cal Y}^{\textrm{Sph}}(\theta))}{(1+\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta))\,(1+\bar{\cal Y}^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta))}\,.

(45)

To recover eq. (2.23) from
Bargheer:2019exp
, we substitute
\phi^{\textrm{AdS}}\to\phi+\pi
and
\sigma^{\textrm{AdS}}\to\varphi
, as well as
\phi^{\textrm{Sph}}\to\pi
and
\sigma^{\textrm{Sph}}\to 0
, and shift the rapidity variable as indicated above.

As a final remark, let us note that the dressed transfer matrices (
25
) exhibit a holomorphicity property with respect to the R-symmetry cross ratios, namely

\partial_{\bar{\alpha}}\,T^{+}=\partial_{\alpha}\,T^{-}=0\,.

(46)

This can be seen, for instance, from the expressions for
\cal Y_{\omega}^{\textrm{Sph}}
and
\bar{\cal Y}_{\omega}^{\textrm{Sph}}
in (
38
), taking into account that the factor
U=\sqrt{\alpha\bar{\alpha}/z\bar{z}}
cancels the dependence on
\alpha
or
\bar{\alpha}
for
\omega=+1
or
\omega=-1
, respectively. This property immediately implies an analogous relation for the free energy (
43
) in the two dressings,

\partial_{\bar{\alpha}}\,\cal A_{+}=\partial_{\alpha}\,\cal A_{-}=0\,.

(47)

Remarkably, this structure is quite general and persists for higher-point functions. It shows that the sum over dressings can be interpreted as a decomposition of the correlator into holomorphic and anti-holomorphic components with respect to the R-symmetry variables.

From a string-theory perspective, this feature may seem surprising, as it suggests that each component is associated with its own area
\cal A_{\omega}
and hence with its own classical string solution. It is also somewhat unexpected that the spacetime and R-space dependence are treated asymmetrically, since no relation analogous to (
47
) holds for the spacetime cross ratios, which enter in the same way in both dressings (see, e.g., (
34
)). We will return to this apparent puzzle and its resolution in Section
7.3
, where a precise comparison of the hexagon formalism with string-theory results will be carried out.

As in the octagon case (

\omega=\pm

3.2.1
Poles and contours

As it stands, the expression for the hexagon sum contains several poles in the rapidity plane. To understand their structure, let us start by examining the zeros and poles originating from the dynamical factor
h_{ab}
. Its basic building block is the hexagon form factor for fundamental magnons
Basso:2015zoa

h_{11}(u,v)=\frac{u-v}{u-v-i\epsilon}\frac{\left(1-1/x^{-}y^{+}\right)^{2}}{(1-1/x^{+}y^{+})(1-1/x^{-}y^{-})}\sigma(u,v)^{-1}\,,

(52)

where
\sigma(u,v)
is the Beisert-Eden-Staudacher dressing phase
Beisert:2006ez
. The bound-state expression is obtained by the usual fusion procedure of the fundamental form factor,

h_{ab}(u,v)=\prod_{j\,=\,0}^{a-1}\prod_{k\,=\,0}^{b-1}h_{11}\left(u^{[1-a+2j]},v^{[1-b+2k]}\right)\,,

(53)

where
u^{[\pm n]}=u\pm i\epsilon n/2
, and similarly for
v^{[\pm n]}
. Substituting the rational factor in
(u-v)
from (
52
) into (
53
), one finds that the rational part of
h_{ab}(u,v)
is given by

h_{ab}(u,v)\big|_{\textrm{rational part}}=\frac{\left(u-v+\frac{|a-b|}{2}\,i\epsilon\right)\ldots\left(u-v+\frac{|a+b|-2}{2}\,i\epsilon\right)}{\left(u-v-\frac{|a-b|+2}{2}\,i\epsilon\right)\ldots\left(u-v-\frac{|a+b|}{2}\,i\epsilon\right)}\,.

(54)

This introduces a finite set of poles and zeros in the integrand (
48

\begin{cases}{\textrm{ poles at }}\quad u-v=-i\frac{|a-b|\epsilon}{2}-ik\epsilon\,,\\
{\textrm{ zeros at }}\quad u-v=i\frac{|a-b|\epsilon}{2}+i(k+1)\epsilon\,,\end{cases}

(55)

with
k=0,\ldots,\textrm{min}\{a,b\}-1

The matrix part
\cal R_{ba}(v,u)
likewise develops poles that couple the rapidities
u
and

-matrix are rather involved

DeLeeuw:2019dak
;
DeLeeuw:2020ifb
;
Arutyunov:2008zt
;
Arutyunov:2009mi

R
-matrix occur precisely at the locations where the dynamical part has zeros, and are therefore canceled by them.

7

7

The remaining factors in

(56)

3.2.2
Diagonal approximation

The absence of pinching singularities in
u-v
implies that all clustering and combinatorial structures arise exclusively from the integration measures associated with each cut individually, which remain unchanged. Interactions between different cuts, by contrast, can be evaluated directly in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
with
u,v
held fixed. In this regime, the hexagon form factors simplify drastically. In particular, one finds

h_{ab}(u,v)\approx\sqrt{S_{ab}(u,v)}=1+\epsilon\,ab\,K(u,v)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(58)

where
S_{ab}
denotes the dynamical part of the magnon
S
-matrix in the
SL(2)
sector. Here,
K
is given by

K(u,v)=\frac{2ixy(xy-1)}{(x^{2}-1)(x-y)(y^{2}-1)}\,,

(59)

where
x=x(u)
and
y=x(v)
. A similar simplification occurs for
R
-matrix, which asymptotes to the identity in this limit, leading to

\frac{\cal R_{ba}(v,u)}{h_{ab}(u,v)}\approx 1-\epsilon\,\mathbb{K}_{ab}(u,v)\,,

(60)

where
\mathbb{K}
relates to the classical
R
-matrix studied in refs.
deLeeuw:2008dp
;
Beisert:2007ty
;
Moriyama:2007jt
;
Torrielli:2007mc
. Here, we will refer to it as the kernel, since it enters as the integral kernel in the TBA equations.

According to (
60
), at zeroth order in
\epsilon
, interactions between cuts are trivial, and the hexagon sums factorize into a product of two decoupled octagons. At the next order, magnons on different cuts interact through the matrix kernels
\mathbb{K}_{ab}(u,v)
. However, since these interactions are weighted by two magnon measures, each scaling as
1/\epsilon
, they cannot be neglected.

To systematically isolate the contributions that survive in the strong-coupling limit of the correlator, one may employ a diagrammatic expansion similar to that developed in
Kostov:2017vwz
. In this setup, each magnon is represented as a vertex and each kernel as a branch connecting vertices, as depicted in the right panel of Figure
9
. When computing the free energy, i.e. the logarithm of the correlator, we can restrict attention to the connected diagrams. The leading contributions then correspond simply to connected tree diagrams.

This can be seen through a simple power counting in
\epsilon
. Each integral contributes a factor
\epsilon^{-1}
, while each kernel contributes a factor
\epsilon^{+1}
. Therefore, for a connected diagram arising from the expansion of
\cal R_{\textbf{ba}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{power of}\ \epsilon\ \ \ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits}}\ \

\displaystyle\#\ \textrm{of kernels (dots)}\,-\,\#\ \textrm{of integrals (lines)}\quad(\textrm{l.h.s. of Figure }\ref{fig:tree_example})

(61)

\displaystyle{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits}}\ \

\displaystyle\#\ \textrm{of branches}\,-\,\#\ \textrm{of vertices}\geq-1\quad\quad\quad\ \ \ (\textrm{r.h.s. of Figure }\ref{fig:tree_example})\,.

The leading connected contribution is thus of order
\epsilon^{-1}=2g
and is encoded by tree diagrams, such as one depicted in Figure
9
.

\textrm{g}_{1}(u)

\textrm{g}_{1}(u)

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{\text{tot}}=\mathscr{V}_{G1}\otimes\mathscr{V}_{G2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathscr{V}_{R1}\otimes\cdots.

(62)

The supertrace can be evaluated recursively by starting from the
leaves
of the tree, i.e. vertices attached to the rest of the diagram by a single

in

\mathscr{V}_{R1}

This “leaf stripping” procedure can be iterated. At each stage, tracing over a leaf enforces flavor conservation along the corresponding branch. Iterating until all leaves are removed shows that only the diagonal matrix elements of

3.2.3
Clustering and simplified partition function

According to the previous discussion, in the classical limit we can restrict ourselves to the diagonal part of the scattering matrix (
60
). For fundamental magnons
\in\mathscr{V}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{V}_{1}
, this amounts to retaining only

K^{IJ}(u,v)\equiv\mathbb{K}_{IJ}^{IJ}(u,v)\,,

(63)

where
I,J\in\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\,|\,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\}
. Equivalently, one may replace (
60
) by a diagonal matrix
\mathbb{D}(u,v)
, defined by
\mathbb{D}_{IJ}^{KL}=\delta_{I}^{K}\delta_{J}^{L}\mathbb{D}^{IJ}
, with components

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{IJ}(u,v)\equiv\exp\!\left[-\,\epsilon\,K^{IJ}(u,v)\right]\approx 1-\epsilon\,K^{IJ}(u,v)\,,

(64)

to leading order in
\epsilon
.

The same reasoning applies to bound states
\in\mathscr{V}_{a}\otimes\mathscr{V}_{b}
. At strong coupling, these decompose into stacks of fundamental magnons, so the diagonal components of their scattering matrix factorize into products of fundamental ones and can be expressed in terms of
\mathbb{D}
. This structure becomes most transparent after performing the clustering on each cut. The algebra is the same as for the octagon and allows one to reorganize the sums over bound states directly in terms of stacks of fundamental magnons with a given flavor. The net result is that the weights take the form (
41
), while the interaction between a stack of
a
magnons of flavor
I
and a stack of
b
magnons of flavor
J
is simply given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{IJ}_{ab}(u,v)=\left(\mathbb{D}^{IJ}(u,v)\right)^{ab}\,,

(65)

or equivalently,
\mathbb{D}_{ab}(u,v)=\mathbb{D}(u,v)^{ab}
, at the matrix level.

Introducing the shorthand notations,

\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{1})^{\textbf{a}}}{{\textbf{a}}^{2}}=\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{N}\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{1}(u_{i}))^{a_{i}}}{a_{i}^{2}}\,,\qquad\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{2})^{\textbf{b}}}{{\textbf{b}}^{2}}=\prod_{j\,=\,1}^{M}\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{2}(v_{j}))^{b_{j}}}{b_{j}^{2}}\,,

(66)

and,

\mathbb{D}_{{\textbf{a}}{\textbf{b}}}({\textbf{u}},{\textbf{v}})=\prod_{i,j}\mathbb{D}(u_{i},v_{j})^{a_{i}b_{j}}\,,

(67)

we conclude that, in the strong-coupling limit, the two-cut partition function (
48
) can be replaced by

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}[\textrm{g}_{1},\textrm{g}_{2}]\approx\sum_{N,M\,=\,0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{N+M}}{N!\,M!}\sum_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}\int d{\textbf{u}}\,d{\textbf{v}}\,\textrm{Str}_{\mathscr{V}_{\textrm{tot}}}\!\left[\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{1})^{\textbf{a}}}{{\textbf{a}}^{2}}\,\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{2})^{\textbf{b}}}{{\textbf{b}}^{2}}\,\mathbb{D}_{{\textbf{a}}{\textbf{b}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\right]\,,

(68)

where the supertrace is taken over the total space of fundamental magnons,

\mathscr{V}_{\textrm{tot}}\equiv\mathscr{V}_{1}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathscr{V}_{1}^{\otimes M}\,.

(69)

In this form, the partition function is expressed entirely in terms of the weights and scattering data of fundamental magnons. In particular, one can parametrize
\textrm{g}^{I}_{1,2}
in terms of the functions
\cal Y^{I}_{1,2}
as before. Explicit expressions for
K^{IJ}
are provided in Appendix
B
, including the effects of the dressing by the
\mathcal{Z}
-markers.

3.3
Generalization to the
\mathrm{N}
-cut partition function

The arguments leading to the simplified expression in (
68
) extend naturally to multi-cut systems obtained through more elaborate gluings of hexagons. The precise form of the integrand will not be needed here; what matters is that the rule governing interactions between magnons on the various cuts has a local and factorized structure
Basso:2015zoa
.

The basic observation is that any pair of magnons located on two cuts of a hexagon acquires an
R
-matrix contribution from that hexagon, exactly as in the case discussed above. The resulting matrix part for the
\mathrm{N}
-cut system is then obtained schematically by assembling all such pairwise interactions arising from the individual hexagons. A concrete example is shown in Figure
10
for a three-cut configuration associated with a linear quiver (fan or zigzag).

Figure 10
:
Left:
Connected configuration associated with a linear quiver with three cuts (
R,G,B
), labeled “red”, “green”, “blue”, and
N=M=P=3
magnons on each cut.
Right:
The corresponding tree configuration, with
N+M+P
vertices and
N+M+P-1

R1,R2,R3
and
B3
are leaves. A graph containing a cycle, where “red” and “blue” magnons also scatter, can be treated similarly; the only difference is that the tree will contain branches connecting “red” and “blue” vertices.

The proof that the matrix part can be organized as a sum over trees relies solely on power counting, and not on the detailed pattern of
R
-matrices. As a result, the diagonal approximation applies to each
R
-matrix individually, regardless of the global gluing or the number of cuts involved. For the three-cut case, the relevant tree diagrams are illustrated in Figure
10
, where the same recursive leaf-stripping argument applies without modification.

It is nevertheless important to keep track of the connectivity of the mirror cuts in the triangulation, as it determines the resulting pattern of diagonal interactions. In addition, since the basic interaction is antisymmetric under the exchange of flavors and rapidities,
K^{IJ}(u,v)=-K^{JI}(v,u)
, one must also account for the relative orientation of the cuts.

To formalize this picture, we use the language introduced in Section
2.2
and associate a quiver with
\mathrm{N}
nodes to the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
. Recall that each node of the quiver corresponds to an edge
E\in\mathcal{T}
and a charge
\gamma_{E}\in\Lambda
. In this terminology, a mirror magnon on
E
can be identified with a state of charge
\gamma_{E}
, and its couplings to other states are encoded in the signed adjacency matrix (
15
), or equivalently in the pairings between edges,
\langle\gamma_{E},\gamma_{E^{\prime}}\rangle
.

The resulting diagonal interaction between magnons on a pair of edges
(\gamma_{E},\gamma_{E^{\prime}})
can then be written compactly as

\mathbb{D}(u,u^{\prime})^{\langle\gamma_{E},\gamma_{E^{\prime}}\rangle}\,,

(70)

where the directionality of the interaction is encoded in the sign of the exponent. This expression trivially reduces to unity when the edges are not adjacent in the triangulation (
\langle\gamma_{E},\gamma_{E^{\prime}}\rangle=0
), as expected.

It is also convenient to introduce notation for the full set of states associated with the triangulation,

\mathcal{B}\equiv\{\gamma_{E}\mid E\in\mathcal{T}\}\,.

(71)

In principle, one should further equip
\mathcal{B}
with a partial order encoding the ordering of the integration contours, and generalizing (
56
) to the multi-cut case. However, determining this poset structure is subtle, and we defer a detailed discussion to Sections
4
and
5
. For now, we ignore singularities arising from decoupling poles and the associated contour-ordering issues, and instead focus on the formal structure of the system.

One may then collect all factors and write down the general partition function. Dropping the edge index
E
for notational simplicity, the group elements
\textrm{g}_{\gamma}
, bound-state indices
{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma}
, and rapidity sets
\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}
associated with each cut all carry the state label
\gamma\in\cal B
. As in the two-cut case, we first perform clustering on each edge, reorganizing bound states into stacks, which now live in the full tensor product of fundamental modules,

\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{tot}}\equiv\bigotimes_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\mathscr{V}_{1}^{\otimes N_{\gamma}}\,.

(72)

Using these notations together with (
67
), the two-cut expression (
68
) for the partition function at strong coupling generalizes to the
\mathrm{N}
-cut case, which can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\}]\approx\prod_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\sum_{N_{\gamma}\,=\,0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{N_{\gamma}}}{N_{\gamma}!}\sum_{{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma}}\int d{\textbf{u}}_{\gamma}\,\,\textrm{Str}_{\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\left[\prod_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\frac{(-\textrm{g}_{\gamma})^{{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma}}}{{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma}^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{D}_{{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma}\,{\textbf{a}}_{\gamma^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma},\mathbf{u}_{\gamma^{\prime}})^{\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle}\right]\,.

(73)

Up to the aforementioned subtleties concerning the choice of integration contours, this expression applies to arbitrary configurations of
\mathrm{N}
cuts and is built from the same basic ingredients as in the one- and two-cut cases.

3.4
Path integral description

Having simplified the partition function in the general
\mathrm{N}
-cut case, we now proceed to its resummation. In the strict strong-coupling limit,
\mathbb{D}\rightarrow 1
in (
73
) and
\mathbf{Z}
appears to factorize into independent one-cut contributions of the octagon type,
\mathbf{Z}\sim\prod_{\gamma}\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma}]
. To recover the full free energy at strong coupling, however, one must go beyond this approximation and incorporate trees of
K
-interactions arising from the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
-corrections to the individual
\mathbb{D}
factors, as discussed in Section
3.2.2
. A description of this tree-based resummation is given in Appendix
C
. Here, we adopt an alternative approach based on an effective field description, which applies to diagonal pairwise interactions and allows us to recast
\mathbf{Z}
in the form of a path integral.

The starting point is to extract the
\mathbb{D}
-terms from the integrals and sums in (
73
). This is achieved by representing them through the action of a suitable operator. To this end, we introduce auxiliary fields
\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)
, labeled by a fundamental flavor index
I
and a state
\gamma
, together with their conjugate functional derivatives satisfying

\left[\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)}\,,\Phi^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u^{\prime})\right]=\delta^{IJ}\delta_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\,\delta(u-u^{\prime})\,,

(74)

where
\delta_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
and
\delta^{IJ}
denote Kronecker deltas. We then define the operator

\cal D=\prod_{\gamma,I}\prod_{\gamma^{\prime},J}\exp{-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\! \int du\, du' \,\qK_{\gamma\gamma'}^{IJ}(u, u') \frac{\delta^2}{\delta\qPhi^I_{\gamma}(u) \delta\qPhi^J_{\gamma'}(u')}}\,,

(75)

where the kernel encodes both the interactions and the adjacency relations between cuts,

K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}(u,u^{\prime})=\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle K^{IJ}(u,u^{\prime})\,,

(76)

and satisfies the antisymmetry properties

K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}(u,u^{\prime})=-K_{\gamma^{\prime}\gamma}^{IJ}(u,u^{\prime})=-K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{JI}(u^{\prime},u)\,.

(77)

This operator is constructed so as to reproduce the
\mathbb{D}
-terms in (
73
) when acting on exponentials of the auxiliary fields. More precisely, one first applies
\cal D
and then sets the auxiliary fields to zero. Introducing the convenient notation

\langle F[\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}]\rangle=\lim_{\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}\rightarrow 0}\,\cal D\,F[\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}]\,,

(78)

for any functional
F[\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}]
, one finds—using (
75
) and the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula—that the
\mathbb{D}
-terms can be written compactly as

\prod_{(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}\mathbb{D}({\textbf{u}}_{\gamma},{\textbf{u}}_{\gamma^{\prime}})^{\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle}=\langle\exp\sum_{\gamma,i,I}\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(u_{\gamma,i})\rangle\,.

(79)

Substituting this representation into (
73
), the resummation becomes straightforward, since all contributions are now fully factorized, as in the case of decoupled octagons. In particular, the dependence on the auxiliary fields in (
79
) can be absorbed into a redefinition of the group elements,

\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\to\textrm{g}_{\gamma}e^{\Phi_{\gamma}}\,,

(80)

or equivalently,
\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma}\rightarrow\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma}\,e^{\Phi_{\gamma}^{I}}
for the eigenvalues, so that the
\mathrm{N}
-cut partition function (
48
) takes the compact form

\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\}]=\langle\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma_{1}}e^{\Phi_{\gamma_{1}}}]\,\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma_{2}}e^{\Phi_{\gamma_{2}}}]\ldots\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}e^{\Phi_{\gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}}]\rangle\,.

(81)

Here,
\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma}e^{\Phi_{\gamma}}]
denotes the octagon with the modified argument (
80
), namely

\mathbb{O}[\textrm{g}_{\gamma}e^{\Phi_{\gamma}}]\approx\exp{-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\, \str\, \textrm{Li}_{2}\left(-\cY_{\gamma} e^{\qPhi_{\gamma}(u)}\right)}\,.

(82)

To evaluate (
81
), it is then convenient to pass to Fourier space by introducing momenta
\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}
conjugate to the fields
\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}
. Denoting the functional measures by
D\Phi
and
D\Pi
, any functional can be expressed via its Fourier transform as

\displaystyle F[\{\Phi_{\gamma}\}]

(83)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int\!D\Pi\,\exp[\sum_{\gamma,I}\int\frac{du}{2\pi\epsilon}\,\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)\,\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)\Big]\!\!\int\!D\Phi^{\prime}\,\exp[-\sum_{\gamma,I}\int\frac{du}{2\pi\epsilon}\,\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)\,{\Phi^{\prime}}^{I}_{\gamma}(u)\Big]F[\{\Phi^{\prime}_{\gamma}\}]\,,

up to an overall normalization and with suitable integration contours. In this representation, the action of
\lim_{\Phi\rightarrow 0}\cal D
is straightforward. Namely, it amounts to replacing the exponent in the first exponential by

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,I}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},J}\int\frac{dudu^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}\epsilon}\,\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)\,K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}(u,u^{\prime})\,\Pi^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u^{\prime})\,.

(84)

Applying this to the product of octagons in (
81
), and using (
82
), one finally obtains the path-integral representation

\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\}]\approx\int D\Phi\,D\Pi\,\exp{-\frac{1}{\epsilon} S[\qPhi, \qpi]}\,,

(85)

with the action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\gamma,I}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\Pi_{\gamma}^{I}(u)\,\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,I}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},J}\int\frac{dudu^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\Pi_{\gamma}^{I}(u)\,K^{IJ}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(u,u^{\prime})\,\Pi^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\gamma}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\,\textrm{Str}\,\textrm{Li}_{2}\left(-\cal Y_{\gamma}e^{\Phi_{\gamma}(u)}\right)\,.

(86)

Here, the indices
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
run over the full set
\mathcal{B}
and
I,J
over the four fundamental flavors. At this stage, one could integrate out the momenta
\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}
by formally inverting the kernels, thereby obtaining a path-integral representation involving the fields
\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}
alone. This representation would make contact with the resummation method used in the context of null polygonal Wilson loops
BSV
;
Fioravanti_2015
;
Bonini_2019
. However, this step is unnecessary for the classical limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0

4
TBA equations and free energy for linear quivers

In what follows, starting from the action (
3.4
), we derive the saddle-point equations for the fields
\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}
, as well as the value of the partition function
\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\}]
at the saddle point, and cast them into the form of a system of TBA equations. To facilitate comparison with the equations obtained in the string-theory context, we switch to the variable
\theta
and rewrite the action (
3.4
) accordingly, keeping the rest of the notation unchanged.

Throughout this section, we restrict our attention to polygon triangulations corresponding to linear (acyclic) quivers, with
\mathrm{N}=n-3
, such as those shown in Figure
4
. This restriction is necessary because the formulation of the TBA equations requires a definite prescription for the integration contours. As discussed in Section
3.2.1
, this prescription is fixed locally for each pair of cuts by the decoupling pole and is implemented by shifting the contours along the imaginary
\theta
-direction, thereby inducing a partial ordering on the states. Denoting by
\cal C_{\gamma}
the integration contour associated with a state
\gamma
, and writing
\gamma\succ\gamma^{\prime}
for
\textrm{Im}\,\cal C_{\gamma}>\textrm{Im}\,\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, the decoupling pole condition then requires

\displaystyle\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\gamma\succ\gamma^{\prime}\,.

(87)

This ordering is only partial: if two nodes in the quiver are not connected (
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
), no ordering is imposed on the corresponding contours.

The main challenge is to implement this constraint consistently for all
\gamma\in\cal B
. For linear quivers, this is immediate, as their structure induces a compatible ordering. In this case, the set
\mathcal{B}=\{\gamma_{E}\mid E\in\mathcal{T}\}
naturally acquires a poset structure determined by the orientation of arrows in the quiver, which we refer to as the
canonical poset
.

The simplest example is provided by the fan triangulation shown in the left panel of Figure
4
. Labeling its diagonals as
\gamma_{i}
, with
i=1,\ldots,n-3
from bottom to top, one has
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}\rangle=1
for all
i<n-3
, leading to the ordering

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\succ\gamma_{2}\succ\cdots\succ\gamma_{n-3}.

(88)

More general linear quivers, such as those arising from zig-zag triangulations, are obtained by flipping some arrows in the fan quiver, which modifies the relative ordering while preserving consistency.

In summary, for acyclic quivers, the contour ordering is naturally encoded in the quiver itself, enabling a direct formulation of the TBA equations. For quivers with cycles, however, the pairings
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
no longer define a consistent partial order, and a more elaborate construction is required, as discussed in Section
5
.

4.1
First form of the equations

The path-integral representation (
85

3.4
) with respect to the conjugate fields
\Pi
and
\Phi

\displaystyle\Phi^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)=-\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\cal B}\sum_{J}\int_{\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi\cosh^{2}\theta^{\prime}}\,K^{IJ}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,\Pi^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\Pi^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)=(-1)^{F_{I}}\,\log{\left(1+\cY^I_\gamma e^{\qPhi^I_{\gamma}(\theta)}\right)}\,.

Defining

Y^{I}_{\gamma}\equiv\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma}\exp{\Phi^I_\gamma}\,,

(90)

we find that the equations can be recast into the form of TBA equations

\log{Y^{I}_{\gamma}}(\theta)=\log{{\cY}_{\gamma}^{I}(\theta)}+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\sum_{J}(-1)^{F_{J}+1}K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}\star L^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)\,.

(91)

Here the convolution
\star

K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\star L^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta):=\int_{\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi\cosh^{2}{\theta^{\prime}}}\,K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,L_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{J}(\theta^{\prime})\,,

\theta

99

Substituting the solution (

\mathbf{Z}[\{\textrm{g}_{\gamma}\}]\approx\exp{-\frac{1}{\epsilon} \cA}\,.

It decomposes into contributions from each cut, all of which are controlled by the same expression,

\cal A=\sum_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\cal A_{\gamma}\,,\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\cal A_{\gamma}=\sum_{I}(-1)^{F_{I}}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}\theta}\,R(Y_{\gamma}^{I})\,.

(94)

The function

R(Y)\equiv\textrm{Li}_{2}(-Y)+\frac{1}{2}\log{(Y/\cY)}\log{(1+Y)}\,,

(95)

\cal Y

It naturally extends the formula found for the octagon (

Inspecting the kernels in Appendix

In particular,

\displaystyle\log{Y_{\gamma}(\theta)}

(104)

4.2
Second form of the equations

The TBA equations (
91
) derived in the previous section take a natural form dictated by the hexagonalization construction. In this formulation, the driving terms
\cal Y
have a clear interpretation, directly encoding the weights of the magnons in terms of the bridge lengths and cross-ratios.

The main complication lies in the kernels (
98
), which are not of relativistic difference type. A similar situation arises in the study of null Wilson loops, where the kernels obtained from the Pentagon-OPE approach also exhibit a complicated structure
Basso:2013vsa
;
Bonini:2015lfr
, despite the fact that the TBA description derived from the minimal surface analysis admits a universal relativistic form
Alday:2009dv
;
Alday:2010vh
. This mismatch is only superficial and is resolved by simple redefinitions
Alday:2010ku
;
Gaiotto:2014bza
.

An analogous reformulation exists in the present case. The TBA equations can be recast in a “relativistic frame” where the kernels depend only on rapidity differences. This requires a further redefinition of the driving terms and leads to the equations

\displaystyle\log{Y_{\gamma}(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\log{\sfchi_{\gamma}(\theta)}+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\left[G_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}*L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)+\bar{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}*\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)\right]\,,

(107)

\displaystyle\log{\bar Y_{\gamma}(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\log{\bar\sfchi_{\gamma}(\theta)}+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\left[\bar{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}*L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)+G_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}*\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)\right]\,,

with the same functions
L_{\gamma}(\theta)
and
\bar{L}_{\gamma}(\theta)
as before. Here
G_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle G
and
\bar{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\bar{G}
, and the relativistic kernels are

G(\theta)=\frac{i}{2}\,\textrm{coth}{\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}\,,\qquad\bar{G}(\theta)=-G(\theta+i\pi)=-\frac{i}{2}\,\textrm{tanh}{\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}\,.

(108)

Moreover, the new convolution
*
now involves translationally invariant measure, e.g.

G_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}*L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta):=\int_{\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,G_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})\,,

(109)

and similarly for
\bar{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)
and
\bar{L}_{\gamma}(\theta)
.
The kernel
G(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
has a simple pole at
\theta=\theta^{\prime}
, with unit residue,

G(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\sim\frac{i}{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\,.

(110)

As before, the integration contours are ordered according to the poset structure of
\mathcal{B}
.

Again, the two sectors decouple and the equations depend on the sector only through the driving terms, that are given by

\log{\sfchi^I_{\gamma}(\theta)}=C^{I}_{\gamma}+Z_{\gamma}e^{\theta}+\bar{Z}_{\gamma}e^{-\theta}\,.

(111)

The so-called central charges
Z_{\gamma}
and
\bar{Z}_{\gamma}
depend on the geometric data (cross-ratios and bridge lengths) and, remarkably, are the same for all flavors, both in the sphere and AdS sectors. They satisfy

Z_{\gamma}+\bar{Z}_{\gamma}=-l_{\gamma}-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\delta l_{\gamma}}\,,

(112)

and

Z_{\gamma}-\bar{Z}_{\gamma}=i\sigma^{\textrm{AdS}}_{\gamma}+i\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\int\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{4\pi\cosh{\theta'}}\left[L_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta^{\prime})+\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta^{\prime})\right]\,.

(113)

The coefficients
C^{I}_{\gamma}
are less universal and depend on the type of excitations. In the AdS and sphere sectors, respectively, we find

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}^{\textrm{AdS}}

\displaystyle=-\bar{C}^{\textrm{AdS}}_{\gamma}=i\phi^{\textrm{AdS}}_{\gamma}+i\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\int\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{4\pi}\textrm{tanh}{\,\theta^{\prime}}\left[L_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta^{\prime})-\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{AdS}}(\theta^{\prime})\right]\,,

(114)

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}^{\textrm{Sph}}

\displaystyle=-\bar{C}^{\textrm{Sph}}_{\gamma}\,=i\phi^{\textrm{Sph}}_{\gamma}\,+i\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\int\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{4\pi}\textrm{tanh}{\,\theta^{\prime}}\left[L_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{Sph}}(\theta^{\prime})-\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\textrm{Sph}}(\theta^{\prime})\right]+\omega\log{\qU_{\gamma}}\,,

where
\omega
is the dressing parameter and we introduce

\log{\qU}_{\gamma}:=\log U_{\gamma}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\delta\log U_{\gamma}}\,.

(115)

Finally, the free energy can be rewritten in the more canonical form

\cal A=\sum_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}(Z_{\gamma}e^{\theta}+\bar{Z}_{\gamma}e^{-\theta})\,\textrm{Str}\,L_{\gamma}(\theta)+\sum_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}l_{\gamma}\,\kappa_{\gamma}\,,

(116)

which is standard for this class of TBA equations, up to the presence of the
\kappa
-terms. This expression follows from (
94
) after integrating by parts the dilogarithmic terms and using the TBA equations to simplify the result. As the intermediate steps are lengthy and not particularly illuminating, we defer the full derivation to Appendix
D
.

4.3
Y
-system

As emphasized at the beginning of this section, the construction of the TBA system comes equipped with a canonical set of integration contours, such that each function
Y^{I}_{\gamma}
appearing in (
91
) is naturally defined on its corresponding contour
\cal C_{\gamma}
. As is standard in the TBA framework, however, these functions admit analytic continuation beyond their original domains, allowing one to explore their behavior in other regions of the rapidity plane.

In particular, by considering the continuation under the shift
\theta\rightarrow\theta+i\pi
, one finds that the
Y
-functions functions satisfy a set of functional relations of
Y
-system type, similar to those arising in conventional TBA equations
Zamolodchikov:1991et
;
Kuniba:1993cn
. The derivation of the
Y
-system does not depend on which form of the TBA we adopt, so here we use equations (
102
). Moreover, since the result is identical in the AdS and sphere sectors, we suppress the sector index in what follows.

The key observation is that the kernels and driving terms transform simply under the shift
\theta\rightarrow\theta+i\pi
. This shift implements relativistic crossing symmetry, flipping the sign of the energy and momentum of the magnons and relating different
R
-matrix elements (see eq. (
253
)). In terms of the TBA data (
100
), one has

\displaystyle\log\cal Y_{\gamma}(\theta+i\pi)=-\log\bar{\cal Y}_{\gamma}(\theta)\,,\qquad\log\bar{\cal Y}_{\gamma}(\theta+i\pi)=-\log\cal Y_{\gamma}(\theta)\,,

(117)

and similarly for
\delta\log{\cY}
’s in (
103
), as well as

K(\theta+i\pi,\theta^{\prime})=-\bar{K}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\qquad\bar{K}(\theta+i\pi,\theta^{\prime})=-K(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,.

(118)

Naively, this suggests that

Y^{++}_{\gamma}\equiv Y_{\gamma}(\theta+i\pi)

(119)

maps to
1/\bar{Y}_{\gamma}
, and vice versa. However, this ignores that the kernel (
98
) has a pole at coincident rapidities,

\displaystyle K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\equiv\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\,K(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\ \ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{\theta\to\theta^{\prime}}}}\ \ \frac{i\,{\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle}}{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\,\cosh^{2}\theta\,.

(120)

As a result, the right-hand side of the TBA equation for
\log{Y_{\gamma}(\theta)}
jumps whenever
\theta
crosses a contour
\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}
with
\gamma^{\prime}\succ\gamma
. Taking the residue at
\theta=\theta^{\prime}
yields

\left(K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\star L_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right)(\theta-i0)=\left(K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\star L_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right)(\theta+i0)-\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\log{(1+Y_{\gamma'})}\,,

(121)

for
\theta\in\cal C_{\gamma^{\prime}}
. Similar poles arise from
\bar{K}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\star\bar{L}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
at the end of the continuation, that is when the rapidity reaches
\theta+i\pi
, as seen in (
118
). These cross all contours with
\gamma^{\prime}\prec\gamma
and contribute with the opposite sign.

After combining all these effects, we obtain that the
Y
-functions obey the relations

\displaystyle Y_{\gamma}^{++}\,\bar{Y}_{\gamma}=\prod_{\gamma^{\prime}\succ\gamma}(1+Y_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{++})^{\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\rangle}\prod_{\gamma^{\prime}\prec\gamma}\left(1+\bar{Y}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right)^{\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle}\,,

(122)

together with a similar equation obtained by exchanging
Y\leftrightarrow\bar{Y}
. Since the driving terms cancelled out in the derivation, the AdS and sphere sectors are completely decoupled in this formulation. In turn, the
Y
-system encodes all the information about the TBA equations, except for the driving terms: these are zero-mode solutions of the homogeneous difference equations,
\cal Y_{\gamma}^{++}\bar{\cal Y}_{\gamma}=1
, which must be fixed by additional conditions.

We note that the
Y
-system (
122
) has a somewhat unusual structure since its right-hand side contains both shifted and unshifted
Y
-functions. This feature reflects the presence of decoupling poles near the initial contour of integration in the hexagon construction. It would be interesting to investigate whether this system can be brought to a more canonical form by redefining some
Y
-functions through shifts of their arguments.

Similar structures have been observed in the context of null polygonal Wilson loops
Bonini:2015lfr
.
As in that case, the
Y
-system is extremely efficient for solving the TBA equations in particular regimes
Alday:2010vh
;
Alday:2009dv
, such as the zero bridge-lengths limit, where the
Y
-functions become constant (
Y^{++}=Y=\text{const}
). We refer the reader to Appendix
E
for a construction of this solution.

5
Quiver cycles and wall crossing

Up to this point we have restricted our attention to triangulations associated with linear quivers. This allowed us to work with the contour ordering induced from the quiver (
87
). However, when the quiver contains oriented cycles, the integration contours can no longer be consistently ordered in this way.

:
Left:
Symmetric triangulation of a hexagon, with implicit orientation as in Figure
3
.
Right:
The corresponding cyclic quiver.

The simplest example is the 3-cycle shown in Figure
11
, corresponding to a symmetric triangulation of a hexagon. In this case,

\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}\rangle=1\,,\qquad i=1,2,3\,,

(123)

with
\gamma_{i+3}=\gamma_{i}
, which makes the prescription (

A natural attempt to resolve this issue is to adopt a Feynman-like prescription, integrating around each pole separately by introducing infinitesimal imaginary shifts determined by the pairings,

\qquad K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\rightarrow K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta+i\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle 0,\theta^{\prime})\,.

(124)

In this formulation, no global contour ordering is required, and the TBA equations can be written for arbitrary quivers, with all contours taken along the real
\theta
-axis. The drawback is that this prescription is not fully compatible with the hexagon construction and, in fact, violates the decoupling condition.

The origin of this violation can be traced to contact terms arising in products of kernels. The first instance appears in the three-magnon configuration, with one magnon on each cut of the inner triangle. The hexagon decoupling property requires that the
connected
contribution to this process be smooth at coincident rapidities. Instead, expanding the free energy to order
\propto\cal Y_{\gamma_{1}}\cal Y_{\gamma_{2}}\cal Y_{\gamma_{3}}
yields the following kernel combination
11

11

The regular part in the product of distributions is
\frac{1}{4}\prod_{i=1,2,3}\cosh{\theta_{\gamma_i}}(\cosh{\theta_{\gamma_1}}+\cosh{\theta_{\gamma_2}}+\cosh{\theta_{\gamma_3}})
.

K_{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}K_{\gamma_{2}\gamma_{3}}+\textrm{cyclic}\,=\,(2\pi)^{2}\cosh^{4}{\theta_{\gamma_{1}}}\delta(\theta_{\gamma_{1}}-\theta_{\gamma_{2}})\delta(\theta_{\gamma_{2}}-\theta_{\gamma_{3}})+\textrm{regular}\,,

(125)

which exhibits unphysical
\delta
-function singularities when combined with (
124
).
12
12

12

This follows by inserting the pole (
120
) of

into the left-hand side of the equation. Summing over cyclic images cancels all poles, up to delta-function terms.

Equivalently, these singularities signal a breakdown of the OPE factorization depicted in Figure
12
.

Figure 12
:
In the double OPE limit where the three left vertices approach each other, the six-point function factorizes into a product of two four-point functions. Since these contributions are ultra-local, they can in principle be cancelled by introducing additional degrees of freedom in the form of new

A more elegant resolution is obtained by inverting the pole prescription for a pair of states, e.g.,
i\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle 0\rightarrow-i\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle 0

5.1
Wall-crossing transformation

As noted above, handling cycles requires consistently swapping integration contours in the TBA equations, introducing a partial ordering for the set of contours. This prevents contact terms like (

Toledo:PhDthesis

5.2
General solution to state ordering

Motivated by the preceding discussion, we now present a general procedure for associating a poset of states to an arbitrary triangulation of a polygon. For quivers arising from polygon triangulations, the most complicated cyclic substructures are tree-like configurations of corner-sharing 3-cycles, as illustrated in Figure

The tree-like structure ensures that the elementary wall-crossing operation discussed for the

6
\chi
-system formulation

As discussed above, a consistent ordering of states for a general triangulation can be constructed via wall-crossing transformations. The resulting equations, however, are not unique, and establishing their equivalence becomes increasingly cumbersome for large quivers.

A more invariant formulation is obtained by following the framework of Gaiotto, Moore, and Neitzke, developed for the TBA equations arising from the Hitchin-system description of 4D
\mathcal{N}=2
theories
Gaiotto:2010okc
. The emphasis is on the discontinuities of the TBA equations, which induce the action of Kontsevich–Soibelman (KS) transformations
Kontsevich:2008fj
on a set of
\chi
-functions serving as Fock-Goncharov coordinates on the Hitchin moduli space. In the GMN framework, these transformations occur across rays encoding the spectrum of BPS states in a given chamber---i.e. a region of the parameter space where the spectrum is constant. While the spectrum itself changes between chambers due to wall-crossing, the total discontinuity remains the same, providing an intrinsic description of the system.
15
15

15

This formalism is closely related to the exact WKB analysis of Schrödinger-like equations. In that setting, the role of the
\chi
–variables is played by the Voros symbols
iwaki2014
, and the KS transformations are realized as Stokes automorphisms describing how these symbols jump when a Stokes line is crossed.

The same structure underlies our equations, with
\cal B
playing the role of the chamber in the
\mathcal{N}=2
setting. The system is governed by the same KS transformations acting on suitably defined
\chi
-functions. We explain below how these functions map to the
Y
-functions constructed earlier and, following
Gaiotto:2010okc
, derive the
\chi
-system form of the TBA equations from the spectrum generator, which encodes the total discontinuity.

This formulation bypasses the need to construct a specific poset/chamber and can instead be read off directly from the geometric data of the triangulation. It also makes clear that our TBA equations are essentially equivalent to those of GMN, as they share the same discontinuity structure. As discussed in Appendix
G
, the differences are mainly cosmetic: after suitable changes of variables and redefinitions, the two formulations coincide, both for AdS and for the sphere considered separately.

6.1
From
Y

\chi

To handle the singularity structure more efficiently, it is convenient, following
Gaiotto:2010okc

\log{X_{\gamma}(\theta)}=\log{\sfchi_{\gamma}(\theta)}+\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\,\in\,\mathcal{B}}\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\left[G*\log(1+X_{\gamma'}(\theta))+\bar{G}*\log(1+\bar X_{\gamma'}(\theta))\right]\,,

To make the structure more transparent, one may further separate the contours by assigning them a finite but arbitrary spacing, thereby opening finite domains between consecutive contours. This deformation does not affect the equations, provided the contours remain within the fundamental strip and assuming the integrals remain convergent. It also singles out two distinguished domains, denoted by

6.2

As mentioned above, is also useful to consider the total jump obtained by traversing all contours in
\mathcal{B}

(146)

which follows directly from the definition of the KS transformations (
140

\cal K_{\gamma_{i}}\,\cal K_{\gamma_{j}}=\cal K_{\gamma_{j}}\,\cal K_{\gamma_{i}}\,,

(147)

The

7
Closed string correlation functions

In this section, we extend our analysis to punctured spheres, which correspond to actual single-trace correlators of the theory.
The presence of punctures introduces additional complications due to cycles that encircle them. Magnons propagating along cuts around a puncture can generate wrapping divergences that cannot be removed by contour deformation, and instead give rise to various finite-size corrections, see e.g.
Basso:2017muf
;
Basso:2022nny
;
Ferrando:2025qkr
.

For half-BPS operators, however, the wrapping contributions cancel once we sum over magnon flavors. This can be verified explicitly in the classical limit considered here. In this limit, a magnon wrapping a puncture
P
carries a charge

\gamma_{P}=\sum_{E:P}\gamma_{E}\,,

(177)

where the sum runs over all edges
E
of the triangulation ending on
P
. This state interacts trivially with any external state
\gamma^{\prime}

\langle\gamma_{P},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0,\forall\gamma^{\prime}
. Furthermore, when
P
corresponds to a half-BPS operator, the product
\prod_{E:P}\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma_{E}}
is independent of the flavor index
I

Fleury:2016ykk
. Thus, the state
\gamma_{P}
decouples, and its contribution vanishes upon taking the supertrace.

As a result, the TBA equations (
91
) and the free energy (
94

\chi
-system as in the GMN formalism. Below, we highlight the minor modifications of this formalism associated with punctures, referring to
Gaiotto:2010okc
for a more complete discussion.

The quivers associated with triangulations of punctured geometries are in general more intricate, typically featuring multiple cycles, which makes the construction of a consistent poset more delicate. We illustrate this in the case of the four-point function, using wall-crossing transformations on
A_{3}
subquivers to construct a consistent ordering. More general methods, such as the mutation method
Alim:2011ae
;
Alim:2011kw
reviewed in Appendix

We then analyze the effect of summing over surface moduli, which corresponds to integrating over bridge lengths in the hexagon formalism. This step enables a precise comparison with the equations derived by Caetano and Toledo from the string-theory perspective.

7.3
Sum over bridge lengths

To make precise contact with string theory, we must also sum over the bridge lengths assigned to the edges of the triangulation, which in the hexagonalization procedure are interpreted as the moduli of the geometry. This leads to

\mathbf{Z}[\{d_{E}\}]=\sum_{\ell_{E}}\left(d_{E}\right)^{\ell_{E}}\,\mathbf{Z}[\{\ell_{E}\}]\,,

(195)

where
d_{E}=y_{E}^{2}/x_{E}^{2}
denotes the scalar propagator (see eq. (
10
)).

In the classical limit
\ell_{E}\rightarrow\infty,\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, with
l_{E}=\epsilon\ell_{E}
held fixed, the set of allowed lengths becomes dense. The discrete sum may then be replaced by an integral, which can be evaluated using a saddle-point approximation in the
l_{E}
variables,

\mathbf{Z}[\{d_{E}\}]\sim e^{-\cal F/\epsilon}\,,

(196)

where
\cal F
is given by

\cal F=\cal A-\sum_{E}l_{E}\log{d_{E}}\,,

(197)

evaluated at the saddle-point configuration
l_{E}=l^{\textrm{saddle}}_{E}
.

To derive the saddle-point equations for the bridge lengths, we must determine how
\cal A
varies under changes of the external parameters. Remarkably, this variation is entirely governed by the driving terms
\cal Y
appearing in the TBA equations,

\delta\cal A=-\sum_{I,E}(-1)^{F_{I}}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}{\theta}}\delta\log{\cY^{I}_{E}(\theta)}\times\log{(1+A^{I}_{E}(\theta))}\,.

(198)

This relation follows from evaluating the variation of the free energy density using the TBA equations (
91
), as detailed in Appendix
D
.

The bridge lengths cannot be varied independently, since they are constrained by charge conservation conditions at each puncture,

\sum_{E:P}l_{E}=\cal L_{P}\,.

(199)

The saddle-point analysis therefore naturally involves Lagrange multipliers. Rather than introducing them explicitly, one may instead exploit the fact that, if
E
is a diagonal of a quadrilateral as in Figure
20
, the lengths of the adjacent edges can be varied in such a way that the R-charges at its vertices remain fixed. This variation is given by

\delta_{E}l_{E^{\prime}}=\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle\,,

(200)

and, under it, one finds

\delta_{E}\log{\cY^{I}_{E'}}=-\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle\cosh{\theta}\,,\qquad\textrm{for all flavors}\,\,I\,.

(201)

Combining (
198
) and (
201
) leads to the saddle-point equations

0=\delta_{E}\cal F=\sum_{E^{\prime}}\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle(\kappa_{E^{\prime}}-\log d_{E^{\prime}})=-\log{\qU^2_{E}}\,,\qquad\forall E\,,

(202)

or equivalently,

0=\sigma^{\textrm{Sph}}_{E}-\sigma^{\textrm{AdS}}_{E}+\sum_{E^{\prime}}\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle\int\frac{d\theta^{\prime}}{4\pi\cosh{\theta'}}\textrm{Str}\,L_{E^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})\,,\qquad\forall E\,.

(203)

Similarly,
\log{\qU_{\gamma}}=0
for all
\gamma
, by linear extension to any chamber. Here we used the definition

\log z_{E}=\sum_{E^{\prime}}\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle\log\textbf{x}_{E^{\prime}}\,,

(204)

and analogously for
\bar{z}_{E},\,\alpha_{E},\,\bar{\alpha}_{E}
, to rewrite

\sum_{E^{\prime}}\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle\log d_{E^{\prime}}=\log{\left(\frac{\alpha_E \bar\alpha_E}{z_E \bar z_E}\right)}=\sigma_{E}^{\textrm{Sph}}-\sigma_{E}^{\textrm{AdS}}=\log{U^2_{E}}\,,

(205)

together with the definition (
115
).

Not all of the equations (
202
) are independent. Indeed, taking the product of
\mathscr{U}_{E}
over all edges ending on
P
automatically yields

\mathscr{U}_{P}=\prod_{E:P}\mathscr{U}_{E}=1\,,

(206)

even without imposing (
202
) on the individual factors. However, since each puncture comes with the conservation equation (
199
), the total number of independent constraints matches the number of edges in the triangulation, namely
3(n-2)
for an
n

202
) and (
199

These constraints have important consequences, bringing the system closer to the structure expected from string theory. First, they restore a full symmetry between the AdS and sphere sectors. Indeed, as can be seen from (
114
), on the support of (
202

\propto\omega\log{\qU_{\gamma}}
in the TBA equations for the sphere
Y

Y

\phi_{\gamma}
in (
114

An equivalent way to see this symmetry restoration is to examine the values of the
\chi

\theta=i\pi/2
in (
171

172

\boldsymbol{\chi}_{E}
and are the only potential source of discrepancy between the two sectors. Using (

\displaystyle\chi_{E}^{\textrm{AdS}}(i\pi/2)

\displaystyle=z_{E}\,,\qquad\bar{\chi}_{E}^{\textrm{AdS}}(i\pi/2)=\bar{z}_{E}\,,

(207)

\displaystyle\chi_{E}^{\textrm{Sph}}(i\pi/2)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{E}\,,\qquad\bar{\chi}_{E}^{\textrm{Sph}}(i\pi/2)=\bar{\alpha}_{E}\,,

showing that the AdS and sphere
\chi

(z_{E},\bar{z}_{E})\leftrightarrow(\alpha_{E},\bar{\alpha}_{E})

The second important consequence is that the bridge lengths
l_{E}
can be completely eliminated in favor of the central charges
Z_{E}

(209)

\sum_{E,E^{\prime}}\langle E,E^{\prime}\rangle(\kappa_{E}-\log{d_{E}})f_{E^{\prime}}=\sum_{E^{\prime}}f_{E^{\prime}}\log{\qU^2_{E'}}=0\,.

In conclusion, when

8
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we derived a TBA description of correlation functions of half-BPS operators from hexagonalization in the classical strong-coupling limit of planar
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM theory, focusing on two-dimensional kinematics. By combining standard integrability techniques with the wall-crossing framework of Gaiotto, Moore, and Neitzke
Gaiotto:2010okc
;
Gaiotto:2009hg
, we showed that the problem admits a natural reformulation in terms of
Y
- and
\chi
-systems. Our analysis applies to both open and closed geometries. In the four-point case, we recover, in the
AdS_{2}\times S^{1}
limit, the
\chi
-system previously obtained from semiclassical string theory
Caetano:2012ac
.

Our results open several directions for future investigation. First of all, from a practical perspective, it would be interesting to study the TBA equations numerically and to test the saddle-point approximation over moduli space performed in Section
7
, which played an important role in achieving agreement with string-theory results. This should clarify how bridge lengths—and the underlying triangulation—are dynamically fixed in terms of cross-ratios. A related question is whether the free energy varies smoothly with these cross-ratios
Caetano:2012ac
;
Sikorowski:2015wzd
, or if phase transitions arise when the saddle point shifts between triangulations.

On the string-theory side, our findings call for a direct extension of minimal-surface techniques to
AdS_{3}\times S^{3}

Erkan2026
. The emerging picture suggests a factorization into two
SU(2)
Hitchin systems, one for the AdS sector and one for the sphere, sharing the same central charges (stress tensors). Understanding whether a similar structure persists in more general kinematics, and how it relates to higher-rank Hitchin systems
Alday:2009dv
;
Alday:2010vh
;
Gaiotto:2012rg
;
Gaiotto:2012snakes
, is an important open problem. On the hexagon side, it would entail exploring how to efficiently handle the matrix part of the hexagon sums in these more general settings.

An important ingredient for a complete comparison with the classical string analysis is the construction of flat sections of the auxiliary linear problem associated with the Hitchin equations. In the minimal-surface/GMN framework
Gaiotto:2009hg
;
Caetano:2012ac
, these sections serve as the fundamental building blocks. They determine the underlying triangulation through WKB analysis and define the
\chi
-functions in terms of Fock–Goncharov (cluster) coordinates. In this formulation, the
\chi
-system arises more naturally and follows directly from the analytical properties of the sections, in contrast with the more indirect TBA derivation adopted here.
In the integrability formalism, the auxiliary linear problem can be derived from the Lax connection associated with the string sigma model
Bena:2003wd
; obtaining it directly within the hexagonalization formalism would provide a better understanding of how these geometric structures enter the TBA in this particular framework.

Throughout this paper, we have restricted attention to half-BPS operators. A natural generalization would be to consider operators dual to spinning strings, whose non-trivial spectral data are encoded, at strong coupling, in their quasi-momenta
Kazakov:2004qf
. An important question is whether the
\chi
-system is sufficiently universal to accommodate this generalization. If so, we expect the quasi-momenta of the operator insertions to enter the
\chi
-system through the driving terms.
The available results for the classical three-point functions of spinning strings
Janik_2011
;
Kazama_2011
;
Kazama_2012
can serve as a guide. However, for higher-point functions of non-BPS operators, where it is necessary to sum over bridge lengths, disentangling the contributions to the driving terms might be more challenging. Minimization over the bridge lengths may also bring some important simplification, in analogy to what we have seen in this work.

Another challenge when considering non-BPS operators in this formalism is the handling of wrapping corrections, which are built into the QSC formalism. Once these problems are solved, we expect to be able to take into account in an unified manner both the spectral data, encoded in the quasi-momenta and the
Q
-functions, and the geometric data encoded in the cross ratios. Alternatively, further insight can be gained from the Separation of Variables (SoV) method, used in
Kazama:2016cfl
to retrieve the semiclassical three-point function at weak coupling. Recent progress on the SoV representation of the three-point functions at weak coupling, see e.g.
Bercini:2022jxh
;
Bargheer:2025kli
;
Bargheer:2026qsov
and references therein, may shed additional light on the connection between correlation functions and the spectral problem via
Q
-functions.

A further natural extension of the present classical analysis is to higher-genus surfaces relevant for non-planar corrections
Bargheer:2017nne
;
Eden:2017ozn
;
Bargheer:2018jvq
;
Bargheer:2019kxb
. In the
Handling Handles
framework, this involves polygonization, hexagonalization, and sprinkling, i.e., summing over tree-level Wick-contraction graphs and their discrete moduli, decomposing the resulting geometry into hexagons, and including mirror-state contributions along the bridges. If our methods for closed geometries extend to higher genus, the last two steps may already be largely under control in the classical regime. The main open problems are the treatment of more general triangulations, a more systematic implementation of the moduli sum, and the resolution of Riemann-surface degenerations through stratification. These advances would bring the present framework closer to a genuine non-planar extension.

Finally, going beyond the classical limit remains an outstanding problem. While some quantum corrections should be captured by modifications of the TBA kernels and by fluctuations around the path-integral saddle point, others—such as flavor loops in the hexagon matrix part—lie outside the present framework. A key challenge is to understand how to incorporate these contributions efficiently. Another open question concerns whether, and in what way, wall-crossing transformations extend to loop corrections and, conversely, whether these transformations can be used to constrain the overall structure of such corrections.

Appendix A
Octagon at strong coupling

In this appendix, we review the strong-coupling computation of the octagon using an approach similar to
Jiang:2016ulr
;
Bargheer:2019exp

\mathbb{O}=\sum_{N\,=\,0}^{\infty}\,\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{{\textbf{a}}}\int d{\textbf{u}}\,\mu_{{\textbf{a}}}({\textbf{u}})\,T_{{\textbf{a}}}({\textbf{u}};\textrm{g})\,,

(232)

where we adopt the notation of Section
3.1
.
The weight

is defined as the average over the two dressings,

\displaystyle T_{\textbf{a}}({\textbf{u}};\textrm{g})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(T^{+}_{\textbf{a}}({\textbf{u}};\textrm{g})+T^{-}_{\textbf{a}}({\textbf{u}};\textrm{g}))\,,

(233)

so that the octagon can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{O}^{+}+\mathbb{O}^{-}\right)\,.

(234)

We are interested in the strong-coupling regime, where the measure
H_{a_{i},a_{j}}(u_{i},u_{j})
and
\mu_{a}(u)
are given in (
40
). Defining shifted rapidities

\displaystyle\mu_{\textbf{a}}({\textbf{u}})\,T^{\pm}_{\textbf{a}}({\textbf{u}})\,=\frac{\epsilon^{N}}{i^{N}}\det\frac{T^{\pm}_{a_{i}}(u_{i})}{u_{i}^{[-a_{i}]}-u_{j}^{[+a_{j}]}}=\frac{\epsilon^{N}}{i^{N}}\det\frac{T^{\pm}_{a_{i}}(u_{i})}{u_{i}^{[-2a_{i}]}-u_{j}}\,,

(235)

where, in the second equality, we shifted

in the denominator and neglected the corresponding shift in the numerator.

Taking the logarithm of
\mathbb{O}^{\pm}
in (
234
) isolates the connected contributions. These correspond to the longest cycles in the cycle expansion of the determinant at each
N

(236)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N>0}\frac{(-1)^{N+1}}{N}\Tr(\KK^\pm)^{N}\,.

The sum in (

\displaystyle\Tr(\KK^\pm)^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i^{N}}\sum_{{\textbf{a}}}\int\,\frac{du_{1}}{2\pi}\ldots\frac{du_{N}}{2\pi}\,\frac{T^{\pm}_{a_{1}}}{u_{1}^{[-2a_{1}]}-u_{2}}\ldots\frac{T^{\pm}_{a_{N}}}{u_{N}^{[-2a_{N}]}-u_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}\sum_{\textbf{a}}t^{|{\textbf{a}}|}\,T^{\pm}_{\textbf{a}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}\,(G^{\pm}(t)-1)^{N}\,.

(238)

Here
|{\textbf{a}}|=\sum_{j}a_{j}
, and

is the generating function for the characters,

\displaystyle G^{\pm}(t)=\frac{(1+t\,\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}})(1+t\,\bar{}\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}})}{(1+t\,\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}})(1+t\,\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}})}=1+\sum_{a\geq 1}t^{a}\,T^{\pm}_{a}\,,

(239)

such that for fundamental particles
T_{1}^{\pm}=\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}}+\bar{}\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}}-\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}}-\bar{}\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}}
. Writing the overall factor in
T^{\pm}_{1}
as
\textrm{q}=\exp{i\sigma^{\AdS} \cP-\ell\cE}
and comparing with (

\cal Y

\displaystyle\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}}=\textrm{q}\exp{{\pm\varphi}+i\phi^{\Sph}}

\displaystyle\,,\quad\bar{}\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}}=\textrm{q}\exp{{\mp\varphi}-i\phi^{\Sph}}\,,

\displaystyle\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}}=\textrm{q}\exp{i\phi^{\AdS}}

\displaystyle\,,\quad\bar{}\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}}=\textrm{q}\exp{-i\phi^{\AdS}}\,.

(240)

We can now compute the octagon at leading order in
\epsilon
. Substituting the last line of (
A

237

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{O}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\,\frac{du}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}\sum_{N>0}\frac{(1-G^{\pm}(t))^{N}}{N}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}\log G^{\pm}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\frac{du}{2\pi}\Big[\textrm{Li}_{2}(-\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}})+\textrm{Li}_{2}(-\bar{}\cal Y_{\pm}^{\textrm{Sph}})-\textrm{Li}_{2}(-\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}})-\textrm{Li}_{2}(-\bar{}\cal Y^{\textrm{AdS}})\Big]\,,

(241)

in agreement with (

The interpretation of this result is that, if we work at fixed total weight

of the bound states and arbitrary number

of bound states, the net effect of the clustering procedure is to combine the

\displaystyle{\rm weights}=\frac{T^{\pm}_{a}}{a}\quad\rightarrow\quad\sum_{I}(-1)^{F_{I}+1}\frac{(-\cal Y_{\pm}^{I})^{a}}{a^{2}}\,.

(243)

Appendix C
TBA equations and tree expansion

As alluded to in the main text, the strong coupling expansion in Section
3.4
can be reformulated as an expansion over trees. In Section
3.2.2
, this tree expansion was used to show that only the diagonal part of the scattering matrix contributes in the strong-coupling limit.
Here, we outline the main ingredients for deriving the TBA equations from (
73
) within this framework.

Each tree is constituted by vertices and branches, and they contain no cycles. The power counting in
\epsilon
is exactly the same as in (
61
). Here, after taking the diagonal approximation for the scattering matrices and performing clustering, vertices correspond to stacks of
a
fundamental magnons with weight

\textrm{vertex}_{\,(\gamma,I,a,\theta)}\ \to\ (-1)^{F_{I}+1}\frac{[-\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)]^{a}}{a^{2}}\equiv(-1)^{F_{I}}\,\cal Y_{\gamma,a}^{I}(\theta)\,,

(254)

where
\gamma
labels the edge,
I
the flavor index, and
a
the stack index. Branches connect vertices and they are assigned the weight

\textrm{branch}_{\,(\gamma,I,a,\theta)\to(\gamma^{\prime},J,b,\theta^{\prime})}\ \to-ab\,K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=-ab\,\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\,K^{IJ}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,.

(255)

The expansion of the
\mathrm{N}
-cut partition function (
73
) takes the form of a sum over forests, where each connected component is a tree.

We define a rooted tree
(-1)^{F_{I}}\,Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}(\theta)
as a tree whose root is the vertex
(\gamma,I,a,\theta)
, with an arbitrary number of attached branches. At each vertex other than the root we sum over
(\gamma^{\prime},J,b,\theta^{\prime})
.
Rooted trees satisfy a Schwinger-Dyson-type equation, reflecting the fact that any tree can be constructed recursively from smaller trees
Kostov:2017vwz
. In the present context, this equation takes the form

Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}(\theta)=\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}(\theta)\exp[a\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},J,b}(-1)^{F_{J}+1}b\,K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}\star Y^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime},b}(\theta)\Big]\,.

(256)

The equations for
a>1
can be derived from the one for
a=1
, corresponding to the fundamental
Y
-functions
Y^{I}_{\gamma,1}(\theta)\equiv Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)
, by setting

Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}(\theta)=-\frac{[-Y_{\gamma}^{I}(\theta)]^{a}}{a^{2}}\,.

(257)

This relation is consistent with the definition of the bare weights
\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}
for the stack of
a
magnons in (
254
), and ensures that the sum over
b
in the exponent of (
256
) reconstructs the expansion of
\log{(1+Y_{\gamma'}^J)}
.

The fundamental
Y
-functions then satisfy

Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)=\cal Y^{I}_{\gamma}(\theta)\exp[\sum_{\gamma^{\prime},J}(-1)^{F_{J}+1}\,K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{IJ}\star\log\left(1+Y^{J}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right)(\theta)\Big]\,.

(258)

which is equivalent to the TBA equation (
91
).

To obtain the expression for the action, one observes that summing (and integrating) over the variables associated with the root
Y^{I}_{\gamma,a}
effectively removes the root, thereby turning rooted trees into unrooted ones. The combinatorial counting of such trees differs from that in
Kostov:2017vwz
, and leads to the appearance of the Rogers dilogarithm (
95
).
A related tree representation for the solution to the GMN equations was discussed in
neitzke2013
.

Appendix F
Non-standard TBA for cyclic quivers

In this appendix, we analyze the TBA equations for polygon triangulations within a nonstandard framework, where Feynman-like prescriptions (
124
) are employed to regulate the decoupling singularities that arise in the kernels. The cases of interest correspond to quivers with cycles. For clarity, we focus on the simplest triangulations involving cyclic quivers, namely those containing one and two 3-cycles, as illustrated in Figure
25
. These examples correspond to systems with three cuts
(A_{3})
and five cuts
(A_{5})
, respectively.

As we will show, reformulating the TBA equations in this Feynman-like framework leads to the appearance of new types of “composite”
Y
-functions. These differ from those encountered in the wall-crossing approach: they are associated with nonlocal states supported on 3-cycles and satisfy distinct defining (or composition) relations that express them in terms of the elementary
Y
-functions.

To streamline the notation, we label the edges of the triangulation (equivalently, the nodes of the quiver) by
i
, and denote the corresponding
Y
-functions by

Y_{\gamma_{i}}\ \rightarrow\ Y_{i}\,.

(301)

Similarly, we write
Y_{ij\ldots}
for the composite
Y
-functions associated with states of charges
\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{j}+\ldots\,
. Their zero-mode contributions satisfy the standard multiplicative relation

\cal Y_{ij...}=\cal Y_{i}\cal Y_{j}...\,.

(302)

In this framework, the Feynman prescription assigns to the kernel
K_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
an
\pm i0
deformation of the form
\theta\rightarrow\theta+i\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle 0
, where the sign is determined by the pairing. As a result, there is no fixed ordering of integration contours; instead, all contours may be taken along the real rapidity axis. Consequently, the standard wall-crossing formalism and the associated KS transformations are not directly applicable.

Nevertheless, one can derive relations connecting the
Y
-functions in this non-standard framework to the corresponding
\chi
-functions, thereby establishing the equivalence between the two descriptions, as we demonstrate below for the specific cases under consideration.

Aside from the modified contour prescription, the TBA equations for the elementary functions
Y_{i}
retain the same structure as in the standard formulation, with the right-hand side involving a sum over a suitable set of states. For brevity, we present only the associated
Y
-system relations. Since these take the same form in both the AdS and sphere sectors, we suppress the flavor index throughout.

Cyclic
A_{3}
.

As discussed in Section
5
, a peculiarity of using the Feynman prescription to regulate the poles in the kernels of the TBA equations is the appearance of spurious contact terms, which are in tension with the expected decoupling properties of hexagon form factors. The simplest example arises in a three-magnon configuration, with one magnon located on each edge of the inner triangle of the 3-cycle triangulation.

The connected contribution of this configuration to the free energy can be extracted by expanding in the small-
\cal Y
regime and isolating terms proportional to
\cal Y_{1}\cal Y_{2}\cal Y_{3}
. Restricting to three identical magnons (say
\psi_{1}

\mathcal{I}_{123}=\int\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\cal Y_{i}(\theta_{i})d\theta_{i}}{(2\pi)\cosh^{2}{\theta_{i}}}(K_{12}K_{23}+\textrm{cyclic})\,,

(303)

where

K_{ij}=\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle K(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\,.

(304)

We fix the orientation of the circular quiver such that

\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{1}\rangle=1\,.

(305)

With the Feynman prescription, each kernel is evaluated with a shifted argument
\theta_{i}\rightarrow\theta_{i}+i\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle 0

\mathcal{I}_{123}=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}{\theta}}\cal Y_{1}(\theta)\cal Y_{2}(\theta)\cal Y_{3}(\theta)+(\textrm{regular triple integrals})\,,

(306)

The problem with the ultra-local term is that it does not vanish in the OPE limit (see Figure
12

\quad Y_{123}(\theta)\approx-\cal Y_{1}(\theta)\cal Y_{2}(\theta)\cal Y_{3}(\theta)\,,

G.3
Flip invariance

Finally, let us discuss the flip invariance of the free energy. As recalled in Section
2
, when the bridge length
l_{\gamma_{i}}=0
, one may switch to a different triangulation by flipping edge
i
without affecting the result. As discussed above, this operation corresponds to an analytic continuation of the associated
Y
-functions under the
++
shift. The previous analysis ensures that the TBA equations transform consistently under this operation, namely through a mutation of the geometric data entering the driving terms.

To make this precise, it is convenient to view the driving terms as functions of the cross ratios,

\cal Y_{\gamma}(\theta)=\cal Y(z_{\gamma},\bar{z}_{\gamma},\alpha_{\gamma},\bar{\alpha},l_{\gamma};\theta)\,.

(347)

Then the claim is that upon analytically continuing all functions of
Y_{\gamma_{i}}
outside of the fundamental strip, using the
++
shift, one finds that the TBA equations retain their form, up to the replacement

\cal Y_{\gamma}(\theta)\rightarrow\cal Y_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\theta)=\cal Y(z^{\prime}_{\gamma},\bar{z}^{\prime}_{\gamma},\alpha^{\prime}_{\gamma},\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}_{\gamma},l^{\prime}_{\gamma};\theta)\,,\qquad\gamma\neq\gamma_{i}\,,

(348)

with

l^{\prime}_{\gamma}=l_{\gamma}\,,

(349)

and where the primed cross ratios follow from the KS transformation,

z^{\prime}_{\gamma}=z_{\gamma}(1+z_{\gamma_{i}})^{\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma\rangle}\,.

(350)

A straightforward calculation on the driving terms for
\gamma\neq\gamma_{i}
confirms that this is indeed the case. Instead, for
\gamma_{i}
, the transformation is induced by the shift itself,

\cal Y_{\gamma_{i}}(\theta)\rightarrow\cal Y_{-\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}(\theta)=\cal Y^{++}_{\gamma_{i}}(\theta)\,.

(351)

We stress that we work in the same setup as before, choosing the shifted state to lie at the top of the chamber. In particular, when shifting
\gamma_{i}
-quantities by
++
, we do not cross any contours and so no wall-crossing is involved here. At the end, the resulting contour for the new state
-\gamma_{i}
lie
below
all the others in the fundamental strip, as in the Figure
28
for instance.

The TBA equations with these new source terms and contour configuration can be identified with those of the flipped triangulation. To see that this is the result of a mutation requires changing the basis of charges, as recalled earlier. This follows from the mutation (
16
), which maps the charge
\gamma_{j}
for the edge
j
in the initial triangulation to the charge
\gamma^{\prime}_{j}=\mu_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma_{j})
of the corresponding edge in the flipped triangulation. By linearity, we also extend this transformation to all other charges
\gamma
in the chamber, using

\gamma\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}=\mu_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma)=\gamma+\max\bigl(0,\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma\rangle\bigr)\gamma_{i}\,.

(352)

There is an important point worth mentioning here: since no transformation really occurs on the charges
\gamma\neq\gamma_{i}
, as we transport
\gamma_{i}
outside the fundamental strip, the set of charges
\cal B\backslash\{\gamma_{i}\}
in the chamber must be same before and after we flip. This means that the latter set must be invariant under the action of
\mu_{\gamma_{i}}
. This observation, and similar ones, are genuine constraints on the set of allowed chambers for a given quiver, and can be turned into efficient algorithms for building them, such as the mutation method. Here, we simply admit them.

That being said, we may formulate the flip invariance as the statement that

Y_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime}=Y_{\mu_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma)}\,,\qquad\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma_{i}\,,

(353)

and

Y^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}_{i}}=\bar{Y}_{\gamma_{i}}^{++}\,,\qquad\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}_{i}}=Y_{\gamma_{i}}^{++}\,,

(354)

solve the TBA equations associated with the flipped triangulation, with the driving terms

\cal Y^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\theta)=\cal Y(z^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}},\ldots,l^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}};\theta)\,,

(355)

where
z^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\mu_{\gamma_{i}}(z_{\gamma}),\ldots,
are the mutated cross ratios, and
l^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}=l_{\gamma}
. We stress that this holds for
l_{\gamma_{i}}=0
here; when
l_{\gamma_{i}}\neq 0
, additional terms show up in the transformation of the driving terms, inducing shifts of the bridge length
l_{\gamma}
notably.

To finish establishing flip symmetry, it remains to check whether the free energy transforms in the same way. As this calculation is slightly more subtle, we discuss it in more detail. Starting form the free energy,

\cal A=\cal A_{\gamma_{i}}+\sum_{\gamma\,\neq\,\gamma_{i}}\cal A_{\gamma}\,,

(356)

we deform the integration contour in

\cal A_{\gamma_{i}}=\textrm{Str}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}{\theta}}R(Y_{\gamma_{i}})\,,

(357)

across the line
\textrm{Im}\,\theta=i\pi/2
, while keeping all other contributions in (
356
) unchanged. Due to the pole in the measure, this deformation produces an additional contribution from the residue at
\theta=i\pi/2
,

\cal A_{\gamma_{i}}=\textrm{Str}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi\cosh^{2}{\theta}}R(Y^{++}_{\gamma_{i}})+\textrm{Res}\,,

(358)

with the residue given by

\textrm{Res}=\frac{1}{2i}\textrm{Str}\left[\log{(Y_{\gamma_i}/\cY_{\gamma_i})}\partial_{\theta}\log{(1+Y_{\gamma_i})}-\partial_{\theta}\log{(Y_{\gamma_i} \cY_{\gamma_i})}\log{(1+Y_{\gamma_i})}\right]_{\theta\,=\,i\pi/2}\,.

(359)

An important simplification comes form the fact that the first term in brackets vanishes at
\theta=i\pi/2

\gamma_{i}
is the highest element in the poset, one has
Y_{\gamma_{i}}=\chi_{\gamma_{i}}
, and hence
Y_{\gamma_{i}}/\cal Y_{\gamma_{i}}\rightarrow 1
at this point. This holds exactly in the AdS sector. In the sphere sector, there is a slight mismatch due to dressing effects. Fortunately, the mismatch is such that whenever
\log{(Y_{\gamma_i}/\cY_{\gamma_i})}
is non zero, the factor multiplying it in (
359
) vanishes at
\theta=i\pi/2
if
l_{\gamma_{i}}=0
. Thus, when
l_{\gamma_{i}}=0
, we can discard the first term in (
359
). A similar simplification applies to part of the second term, since

\partial_{\theta}\log{\cY_{\gamma_i} (i\pi/2)}=-il_{\gamma_{i}}=0\,.

(360)

As a result, the residue reduces to

\textrm{Res}=\frac{i}{2}\textrm{Str}\left[\log{(1+Y_{\gamma_i})}\partial_{\theta}\log{Y_{\gamma_i}}\right]_{\theta\,=\,i\pi/2}\,.

(361)

The remaining derivative can be evaluated using the TBA equations (
107
). The algebra is straightforward and shows that the net effect of (
361
) is to shift the contributions proportional to
\log{\cY_{\gamma}}
in
\sum_{\gamma\,\neq\,\gamma_{i}}\cal A_{\gamma}
to their final, mutated form given in (
348
). In other words, both the free energy and the TBA equations transform identically under this shift, preserving their general structure up to the KS action on the kinematic data (
350
). This completes the proof of flip invariance.

Finally, let us comment on convergence. To justify the above manipulations, one must ensure that the
Y
-functions remain well behaved at large rapidity when analytically continued across the line
\textrm{Im}\,\theta=\pi/2
. This can be achieved by taking
\sigma_{\gamma_{i}}
to be sufficiently large and positive, so that convergence is ensured by the factor
e^{i\sigma_{\gamma_{i}}\sinh{\theta}}
. In the opposite regime, where
\sigma_{\gamma_{i}}
is negative, one should instead perform the analytic continuation in the opposite direction, that is
\theta\rightarrow\theta-i\pi
.

Throughout the analysis, we have also implicitly assumed that
1+Y_{\gamma_{i}}
does not vanish in the relevant domain. Otherwise, extra residues—analogous to excited-state contributions in conventional TBA analyses—would arise from the contour deformation.

Appendix H
Mutation method

In order to construct chambers in a more systematic manner, one may employ the powerful methods developed in the study of BPS quivers, in particular the mutation method proposed in
Alim:2011kw
;
Alim:2011ae
. This approach provides a systematic algorithm for determining chambers directly from the quiver data. It relies on general structural constraints that the chambers must satisfy, together with the mutation properties of the underlying quiver. We refer the reader to the original references for a detailed discussion of these aspects and give in this appendix only a brief outline of the method.

The mutation method involves performing successive mutations on the charges
\gamma
associated with the nodes of the quiver. After each mutation, these charges transform linearly according to the general rules described in (
16
). To construct a chamber, one performs mutations on nodes associated with positive linear combinations of the elementary
\gamma
’s, continuing the process until the quiver returns to its original form, up to an overall reversal of the signs of the initial
\gamma
’s.

In the case of a linear quiver, this procedure readily reproduces the minimal chamber associated with the canonical ordering, starting from the sinks (nodes with all arrows directed toward them) and proceeding recursively until the sources (nodes with all arrows directed outward) are reached. Other chambers, corresponding to configurations with additional states, can be obtained by following alternative sequences of mutations.

\gamma

For instance, in the maximal chamber of the

\gamma_{2}

\gamma_{1}\leftarrow\gamma_{2}\,\,\underset{\mu_{\gamma_{2}}}{\Rightarrow}\,\,\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\rightarrow-\gamma_{2}\,\,\underset{\mu_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}}{\Rightarrow}\,\,-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\leftarrow\gamma_{1}\,\,\underset{\mu_{\gamma_{1}}}{\Rightarrow}\,\,-\gamma_{2}\rightarrow-\gamma_{1}\,.

(362)

\gamma_{2}\succ\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\succ\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{1}\leftarrow\gamma_{2}\,\,\underset{\mu_{\gamma_{1}}}{\Rightarrow}\,\,-\gamma_{1}\rightarrow\gamma_{2}\,\,\underset{\mu_{\gamma_{2}}}{\Rightarrow}\,\,-\gamma_{1}\leftarrow-\gamma_{2}\,.

(363)
