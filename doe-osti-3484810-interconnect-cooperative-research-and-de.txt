 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This CRADA modification involves analyses on a variety of CdTe-PV related materials, test
 structures, solar cells, and modules produced at FSLR and/or NLR and adds analysis related to
 module degradation and reliability Materials will be provided by FSLR, NLR, and/or by
 interleaving FSLR and NLR layers and processes. Module reliability activities will include
 technical risk assessment, materials characterization, module and test structure characterization,
 modeling, accelerated test development, and outdoor testing.

 1

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 Benefit to Participant: Identify performance-limiting attributes over a range of common and
 uncommon CdTe device structures. These fundamental studies will help the participant identify
 limiting mechanisms to advance the efficiency of the commercial CdTe module, and prescribe
 solutions to the manufacturing processes that not only increase efficiency but also reduce cost.

 Benefit to DOE: Success of this project will help enable CdTe solar technology to realize the
 DOE 2030 LCOE goal of $0.03 per KWh.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Modification 14A2:

 Task 1: Risk assessment and mitigation strategy

 NLR develops a risk assessment tool for the tandem product design. This tool is developed and
 used collaboratively to assess the risks and develop mitigation strategies for these risks. The
 initial tool and risk assessment is due at the end of month 2. The tool is updated continuously
 throughout the course of the project. Significant updates and reports are expected every two
 months.

 Task 1 (Mod 14A2) Results:

 This work focused on identifying the technical safety, performance, and market risks for a thin
 film on silicon tandem photovoltaic (PV) module product and compiling a risk register. We
 ranked the risks according to severity, prioritizing those that would lead to failure or safety risks,
 then those that would limit performance, and finally those that might cause minimal degradation.
 The high deposition temperatures required for thin film device fabrication were identified as a
 primary design constraint limiting the possibility of depositing the front cell directly on the
 silicon bottom cell, requiring a dielectric layer between the two devices in most configurations.
 The integrity and electrical properties of this layer, along with the interconnection design to
 connect the two devices were identified as high severity technical risks. The performance and
 reliability of the silicon bottom cell under tandem operating conditions with low light was an
 example of a moderate technical performance risk.

 The technical market risks focused on the lack of standards for the evaluation and testing of
 tandem devices. We identified aspects of both the thin film and silicon sections of International
 Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 61215 that could apply to these devices and triaged which
 aspects of standards development would be most time consuming and important for the eventual
 commercialization of tandem modules. These included efficiency measurements, performance
 assessment/energy-yield, safety, and environmental reliability. The current standards for single
 junction devices do not account for the possible failure modes of tandem interconnections since
 they were devleoped for single junction devices.

 Task 4 (Mod 14A2) Results:

 Candidate silicon bottom cells were tested for their durability using accelerated stress tests to
 identify issues related to potential-induced degradation (PID), light- and elevated-temperature-
 induced degradation (LeTID) and Thermal Cycling (TC).

 The samples tested included cells provided by EEPV, M-CELL, Longi, Tongwei, and control
 cells provided by NLR.

 Potential-Induced Degradation (PID)

 PID testing measures a PV cell's susceptibility to performance loss under high-voltage stress.
 Devices are subjected to string-level voltages to simulate field conditions and evaluate the
 impact of charge migration and ion-induced damage. For this study, PID testing was conducted
 at ±1500 V and 60°C for 96 hours, which represents an industry-standard accelerated aging test.
 Sample configurations used encapsulants and cells supplied by First Solar, laminated at NLR
 using vacuum lamination procedures designed to replicate glass/transparent conductive oxide
 (TCO)/encapsulant/cell/encapsulant/glass stack geometries.

 Round 1: Evaluation of Tongwei Cells with Commercial EVA and XUR Encapsulants

 The first round of testing used Tongwei cell samples paired with commercial ethylene-vinyl
 acetate (EVA) and XUR (a polyurethane-based protective resin) encapsulants. The test
 configuration included a glass/TCO/encapsulant/cell/encapsulant/glass stack, as illustrated in
 Figure 1. The results of PID testing on EVA and XUR encapsulants (Figure 2) highlighted
 significant performance losses, particularly under negative bias conditions. PID mechanisms
 causing degradation from the front surface (PID-s) and rear surface (PID-p) were detected.
 Specifically:

 • EVA exhibited the most severe degradation under both negative and positive biases, with
 the rear-side PID-p effects dominating.
 • XUR encapsulants showed better performance compared to EVA but still underwent
 noticeable degradation under the same conditions.

 To evaluate whether degradation effects could recover under field conditions, where diurnal

 overestimated compared to the dynamic field environment, where natural light assists in
 mitigating PID-p.

 Round 2: Expanded Testing with Additional Encapsulant Configurations

 The second round of testing introduced additional encapsulant materials to investigate
 performance variability and explore alternative strategies to mitigate degradation. New
 encapsulants included a Tri-Layer design, a proven polyolefin encapsulant (POE) material, and
 an updated XUR formulation (referred to as XUR-New). For further analysis, the effects of
 preconditioning on the performance of the older XUR formulation (XUR-Old) were studied
 under two storage conditions—dry storage in a nitrogen box (XUR-Old-D) and ambient
 laboratory conditions (XUR-Old-A).

 The degradation results for the expanded sample set (Figure 4) provided valuable insights:

 1. EVA and XUR encapsulants underwent the most significant degradation, with EVA
 showing the most severe rear-side PID (PID-p).
 2. XUR-New exhibited slight improvements in performance over XUR-Old, suggesting
 better resistance to PID effects with improved formulation.
 3. Preconditioning XUR-Old encapsulants in a dry environment (XUR-Old-D) reduced
 degradation relative to those stored in ambient conditions (XUR-Old-A). This highlights
 the role of moisture in exacerbating PID susceptibility.

 Figure 4. Summary of PID testing on Tongwei cells using EVA, POE, Tri-Layer, XUR-New, XUR-
 Old-A, and XUR-Old-B

 7

 Unexpectedly, the Tri-Layer and POE samples exhibited front-side short-circuit current (Isc)
 reductions. Post-test visual inspection (Figure 5) revealed a purple discoloration on the front
 surface of the device, indicating a possible interaction between the encapsulant and the TCO
 layer. This coloration was hypothesized to result from chemical changes or ion migration, which
 may impair the TCO’s conductivity and lead to unexpected performance losses.

 Figure 5. Photographs of the front side of the Tri-Layer sample post PID testing. A purple
 discoloration was observed, particularly when viewed from an angle

 To address this issue, additional samples were fabricated without the TCO layer on the front
 glass to investigate its role in this degradation mode. Results from these modified samples
 (Figure 6) showed that Isc degradation was fully eliminated, confirming the TCO layer as the
 primary cause of the observed front-side performance losses. Devices with POE and Tri-Layer
 encapsulants, absent the TCO layer, demonstrated excellent PID resistance and no rear-side
 degradation.

 Round 3: Final Testing with Pseudo-Tandem Device Configurations

 The final round of PID testing explored the performance of advanced encapsulants as well as a
 pseudo-tandem device stack that included a cadmium telluride (CdTe) top cell. This setup was
 designed to evaluate whether the inclusion of CdTe might introduce new degradation modes.
 CdTe top cells, provided by First Solar, were fabricated at NLR as non-electrically active
 components to simulate tandem stacks. A photograph of the configured samples is shown in
 Figure 7.

 9

 Figure 7. A photograph of the completed pseudo-tandem sample

 The results (Figure 8) further reinforced the findings from earlier rounds:

 • EVA exhibited significant performance degradation, most notably from rear-side PID-p
 effects.
 • Both Tri-Layer configurations (Tri-Layer-1 and Tri-Layer-2) performed exceptionally
 well, mitigating degradation in all conditions.
 • The pseudo-tandem device with CdTe top cells showed no measurable degradation of the
 silicon bottom cell, confirming that there was no adverse impact from CdTe migration or
 interaction.

 10

 Figure 8. Summary of PID testing on EVA, Tri-Layer 1, Tri-Layer 2 and a pseudo-tandem device

 The findings drawn from these tests underscore the importance of optimizing encapsulant
 materials and device stack configurations to mitigate PID and enhance long-term device stability.
 Several overarching conclusions emerged:

 1. EVA encapsulants, while common and cost-effective, are highly susceptible to PID and
 are not well-suited for high-voltage applications or tandem photovoltaic structures.
 2. Advanced materials like Tri-Layer encapsulants and POE exhibited superior
 performance, particularly in configurations where the TCO layer was omitted.
 3. Moisture uptake impacted PID susceptibility, as evidenced by the better performance of
 preconditioned XUR-Old encapsulant stored in a controlled dry environment.
 4. The pseudo-tandem configuration with a CdTe top layer introduced no observable
 degradation to the silicon bottom cell, indicating the feasibility of tandem device
 integration without compromising durability.

 The mitigation of degradation effects through light soaking further suggests that fielded
 conditions, with regular diurnal sunlight exposures, could aid in naturally recovering some PID-p
 losses observed during accelerated laboratory testing.

 Task 1 (Mod 21) Results:

 Long-term Si degradation

 Multiple candidate silicon solar cells were tested for their susceptibility to LeTID and boron-
 oxygen light-induced degradation (BO-LID). Two stress conditions were used for each test and
 are described below.

 LeTID:

 After an initial IV curve measurement, bare cells were subjected to a current injection equivalent
 to maximum power point current (Jmpp) at 90 or 120 °C for 2 hours or until voltage stabilized.
 After a post-stress IV curve measurement, the cells were subjected to the same stress conditions
 for 48 hours to assess possible regeneration.

 14

 BO-LID:

 After an initial IV curve measurement, bare cells were subjected to a pre-condition at 200 °C for
 30 minutes with no bias or current. After pre-conditioning the cells IV curves were measured
 again and then subjected to a degradation stress of 120 °C for 2 hours with a current injection
 equivalent to Jmpp. After a post-degradation IV curve, a regeneration condition of 120°C with
 bias was used for 12–24 hours, followed by a final IV curve.

 Known-bad cells (CNH) were used to benchmark and verify the tests as they exhibited
 significant degradation under LeTID and BO-LID stress conditions.

 8 different candidate cells were tested. These included a mix of monofacial, bifacial,

 Task 2 (Mod 21) Results:

 Background

 First Solar has developed a transparent back contact for their CdTe-based PV devices. This
 enables their modules to be bifacial in the same way as has been accomplished with Si passivated
 emitter and rear cell (PERC) cells. But because the bandgaps of CdTe and Si differ substantially,
 this development also enables the production of a stacked tandem with CdTe in front and a
 bifacial Si PERC cell on the backside. This configuration could create a significant jump in
 module efficiency.

 One of the markets that First Solar would like to work in is the residential PV market. Because of
 the significantly higher installation and marketing costs, the advantage of a higher efficiency
 module here is much greater making this niche a better place to leverage a higher efficiency
 module. However, when working on residential rooftops keeping a module’s weight below the
 50 lb (22.7 kg) Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) weight limit is
 important. In typical First Solar modules a large fraction of the module weight comes from the
 use of two plates of glass. Here the objective was to replace the second back piece of glass with a
 backsheet containing an aluminum (Al) foil to keep moisture out. Al foil is used because it is
 durable and inexpensive.

 16

 Testing Plan and materials

 The testing plan consisted of five components:

 1. Water Vapor Permeation measurement
 2. Solder Bump Test
 3. Ca-Film testing
 4. Scratch Test
 5. Adhesion Testing

 Each of these tests was designed to address one of the various requirements of a backsheet.

 Ca-Film Ingress test

 Ca-film test samples, as shown in Figure 13, were prepared as described by Kempe et al. In
 summary, glass substrates were scrubbed using a 4F-pumice slurry, followed by scrubbing and
 rinsing using isopropanol and deionized water, and then dried by blowing nitrogen (N₂) over it.
 Ca was deposited to a thickness of 100 nm on 3.18-mm-thick square glass plates with side
 dimensions of 51 mm. Lamination of this plate to another piece of glass was accomplished by
 first drying the polymeric materials at an elevated temperature (approximately 100°C to 150°C)
 in an argon-filled glovebox followed by assembly using a heated press at approximately 100°C
 to 150°C.

 Two pieces of 0.25 mm-thick fluoropolymer-coated fiberglass sheets with a square hole in the
 center were positioned between the Ca test sample glass plates as a spacer to help keep them
 parallel and separated at a distance of approximately 0.5 mm. A total thickness of 0.5 mm was
 chosen to be consistent with typical industrial use and to minimize possible effects of three-
 dimensional (3-D) moisture diffusion. However, variation in polymer thickness was not expected
 to significantly change these results as the amount of moisture necessary to consume the Ca film
 was small compared to the amount of desiccant in the polyisobutylene (PIB). The spacers used
 for sample assembly were subsequently removed resulting in the square Ca pattern shown in
 Figure 14. Two replicates of each sample were made for each test condition.

 For any diffusion-controlled process, the characteristic distance varies with the time squared. In
 Figure 17 19 the measured data is shown with markers and dotted lines and the best fit to the
 data is shown with a solid line with a slope of ½ on a log-log plot. At distances below 1 mm
 there is considerable variability in the estimate of the moisture ingress. The thickness of the edge
 seal and the polymeric layers of the backsheet are about 1 mm which helps to explain one reason
 why significant uncertainty was expected in this range. At length scales around 1 mm the
 assumption of this being a 1-D diffusion problem was not good, but at longer length scales, this
 assumption was much better.

 For this experiment, Helioseal 101 was used instead of LPO3. This choice was made for
 convenience because fresh material was already on hand, and this material did not contain trace
 amount of water at the sides of the edge seal tape which over time created a striped pattern on the
 sample which could make the data workup slightly more difficult. The question remained: would
 the results be the same? Both edge seal types have approximately the same breakthrough times
 within the experimental uncertainty. However, the moisture coming through the polymeric layers
 in the backsheet was absorbed by not just the Ca, but also by the desiccant in the edge seal. At
 sufficient distances from the edge and sufficiently long-time frames, the moisture profile in the
 edge seal and the backsheet layers can be approximated as a linear slope from the perimeter
 environmental equilibrium concentration to zero at the edge of the Ca oxidation front. The added
 Ca front velocity added by the contribution to moisture permeation in the backsheet layers is
 linearly proportional to the permeation in the saturated polymer and inversely proportional to the
 adsorptive capacity of the edge seal. For Helioseal 101 and LPO3 these ratios are 8.7 cm²/day
 and 6.7 cm²/day respectively. Based on this, it is estimated that the increased moisture ingress
 rate for Helioseal is 30% faster. In other words, if Helioseal would be required to be 30%
 relatively wider with a given backsheet, then LPO3 would be expected to be required to be 23%
 absolutely wider. However, it is acknowledged that this represents about one standard deviation
 in this experiment which is in addition to ±15% absolute error from the more comprehensive
 estimate. From this, revised estimations for LPO3 can be obtained (Table 6).

 Most of the backsheets would have required about 18%–20% wider edge seals to perform as
 well, with respect to edge seal breakthrough times, as compared to a module designed with glass.
 The main exceptions are the 3M 512 barrier film where only a 14% increase is estimated. This
 implies that there is relatively little polymeric material on the side of the film that is inward from
 the barrier layers. Conversely, Coveme SPV-P with 9 µm Al had a much larger, 39%,
 requirement as compared to the same film with a 20 µm Al foil. This was unexpected, but
 considering that this was found consistently with two different samples for both foil thicknesses,
 this does not seem to be an experimental error. If the increased moisture ingress rate was
 attributable to ingress through the Al foil, it would need to be higher than what was measured
 (see section 0) and would be a 2-D diffusion process that would not produce the straight lines
 shown in Figure 17. We believe that something more substantial than just a change in Al foil
 thickness occurred.

 Another sample that did not seem to be ordered correctly was the Krempel material. The
 specification sheet indicates that it has a much thinner 0.150 mm thick PET layer and would be
 expected to similarly have reduced ingress rates compared to the other materials but it does not.
 An analysis of the layer thicknesses and compositions might help identify the source of these
 differences.

 The Al-foil containing samples showed greater moisture ingress at higher temperatures. For the

 A permeation rate of 1 x 10¯⁴ (g/m²/day) will transmit the equivalent of a 1 µm layer of water
 over the course of 20 years. EVA holds about 0.00213 g/cm³ of water at 25°C which would raise
 the RH of a 0.5 mm EVA layer to 9.4% RH. If the absorption capacity of the encapsulant was
 10× less, as is common in polyolefin encapsulants, the RH would instead increase by 94% (if
 you ignore reductions in permeation rate as the humidity in the module rises and the permeation
 rate slows down). Realistically, it would be equilibrated with the environment assuming there
 isn’t substantial consumption of the water by reaction with a PV component. Exactly what
 amount of water is detrimental depends on the kinetic nature of the degradation. If every
 molecule of water is detrimental, then much lower permeation rates are needed, but if a more
 highly absorbing encapsulant is used and the RH at high temperatures is important then rates <1
 x 10¯⁴ (g/m²/day) would be adequate.

 Prior work with edge seals has shown that respect to water ingress, constant conditions of about
 45°C and 25% RH are equivalent to the time varying conditions in Bangkok, Thailand. The
 distance moisture gets into an edge seal varies as the square root of the relative humidity which
 means that the condition of 45°C/85% RH is about 1.8× faster than Bangkok, Thailand. Any of
 the Al-containing films are very likely to be adequate for this application, and even the 3M films
 should be adequate if they are assembled properly.

 Inspection of Figure 20 shows that the Coveme SPV-P 9-µm Al backsheet did not perform
 poorly relative to the other 20-µm Al films. This means that 9 µm of Al can at least initially be
 adequate as a moisture barrier. However, long term there is a greater possibility that corrosion of
 the Al foil may cause problems with moisture permeation. Similarly, as a thinner foil it is more
 susceptible to damage, see section 0. So unless there is a significant cost savings with the use of
 this material, we would not recommend using it.

 Scratch Durability Testing

 Typically, the scratch test, IEC 61730 MST 12, is looking for scratches that would pose an
 electrical risk. To do so, the scratch would have to go all the way through the backsheet and the
 encapsulant to come in contact with the cells using 1 kgf (9.8 N) of weight. In practice, this test
 only discriminated against thin coating layers and rarely serves a practical purpose in testing
 standard, especially since IEC 61730 2nd edition (2016) came out with prescribed distances
 through relied upon insulation. Essentially the scratch test is a legacy requirement dating back to
 testing issues in the 80s and 90s. Regardless, it provided a good way to repeatably mechanically
 stress the backsheets to qualitatively evaluate their durability.

 In Table 7 the results are shown and the barrier films are given a qualitative rank ordering. All of
 the films survived the minimum 1 kgf (9.8 N) test as prescribed by IEC 61730 MST 12. But all
 of them were harmed to some degree by the 5 kgf weight. Here we also see that for nominally
 identical Coveme films with either 9 µm or 20 µm Al foils that the 20 µm Al foil was more
 durable. While either one of these should be adequately durable, there is an improvement by
 going to the thicker foil. Therefore, if the costs are similar, we recommend using the 20 µm Al
 foils.

 Adhesion Testing

 The samples were examined using the tapered cantilever beam test after 0 h, 500 h, and 1,000 h
 of exposure to 85°C and 85% RH (Table 9). At the time of writing, not all of the results had been
 completed and tabulated. Previous tests on fielded samples consistently found adhesion values >
 100J/m² for samples which did not delaminate or have bubbles in them and adhesion values <
 10J/m² for modules that always had bubbles or delaminations. For values between 10J/m² and
 100J/m² delaminations might or might not occur. This could be due to specific environmental
 conditions, specifics of the design, insufficient time for failure, or some other random effect.

 34

 While the damp heat test does not have a specific correlation to equivalent time outdoors for
 adhesion retention, 1000 h of damp heat is very extreme and likely to be more harsh than actual
 exposure, especially for materials such as PET because of the hydrolysis issues. Here we
 evaluated the materials as being adequately adhered if the adhesion strength is always above
 100J/m², which with the data to date is always the case.

 Conclusions and Recommendations

 Using these five testing regimes, WVTR measurement, solder bump testing, Ca-film testing,
 Scratch testing, and adhesion testing, we obtained insight into how these Al-barrier films can
 work. From the WVTR measurements we found that the Al foils are not as good as glass for
 keeping moisture out, but are very close. There is a bit of a higher permeation with the 9 µm foil
 as opposed to the 20 µm foil. However, all of the foils had permeation rates low enough to be
 qualified for use in this application. There were two foils provided by 3M that were composed of
 polymer/oxide barrier layers which performed about an order of magnitude more poorly than the
 20 µm films, but these would still be adequate. However, the 3M barriers were more sensitive to
 handling.

 The Solder Bump testing was only completed to about one-third of the total intended testing. We
 found issues only with the FAM material, presumably because it just used a thin fluoropolymer
 coating on the outside of the Al foil. Unless it is significantly cheaper, we recommend not using
 it because of this issue.

 The Ca-film testing indicates that edge seal widths would need to be between 14% (3M 512
 barrier) and 39% (Coveme 9 µm Al) wider when compared to glass. The rest of the films needed
 between 18% and 20% wider edge seals. If backsheets were designed better with the minimum
 required remaining useful insulation (RUI) thickness on the inside, this added width could be
 half as large. To put this into perspective, the uncertainty in the required width is about ±15%
 making these added widths not very substantial. From this, the 9 µm Coveme film is not
 recommended, but the rest of them should be adequate with respect to the need to use wider edge
 seals.

 The scratch test was used to assess the mechanical durability of the films. The two 3M
 transparent barrier films were only able to pass the IEC 61730 MST12 minimum which is
 probably adequate and is what one might use if designing bifacial modules. Next the Coveme
 SPV-C and Coveme SPV-P materials with 20 µm Al foils performed the best. However, like the
 3M barriers all of them were able to handle the IEC minimum weight and cannot be conclusively
 ruled out.

 In adhesion testing, so far, no issues with loss of adhesion strength were found under the highly
 severe conditions of damp heat.

 In summary, the best materials tested were the Coveme SPV-C and Coveme SPV-P materials
 with 20 µm Al foils. The Krempel material was not fully tested because we were only given one
 sheet of it, but it could be equal to these. There is reduced performance in the Coveme 9 µm film
 which would only be justified for use if the cost was significantly lower. And lastly the 3M
 barrier film could work but would only be recommended when designing a bifacial module.
