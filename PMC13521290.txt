The PHO1 protein family in phosphate export and beyond
Authors: Yves Poirier, María Victoria Aparicio Chacón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Arabidopsis thaliana PHOSPHATE 1 (PHO1) was first identified as an inorganic phosphate (Pi) exporter mediating Pi loading into the root xylem. PHO1 is the prototypical member of a family of eukaryotic proteins possessing both an SPX and EXS domain. Recent cryo-EM structures of both the Arabidopsis PHO1 homolog PHO1;H1 and its mammalian ortholog Xenotropic and Polytropic Retrovirus Receptor 1 (XPR1), revealed that these proteins work as Pi channels that use a double-gated mechanism controlled by inositol polyphosphate. Key amino acids involved in binding Pi and gating the channel are highly conserved in PHO1 homolog throughout eukaryote superphyla, including in unicellular organisms, indicating a possible conservation of function during evolution. Beyond their structural homology, both plant and animal PHO1 ortholog are primarily localized to intracellular vesicles, raising interesting questions about the cell biology of Pi export. Arabidopsis PHO1 also contributes to the response of guard cells and seeds to abscisic acid (ABA) through yet unknown mechanisms. In this review, we will highlight how the recently gained knowledge about PHO1 and XPR1 structure and its cell biology is shaping our views about how these proteins contribute to Pi homeostasis at the cellular and organismal levels.

Inorganic phosphate (Pi) is one of the most limiting nutrients for plant growth and reproduction. Pi is integral to energy metabolism, fundamental in the structure of nucleic acids and phospholipids, and central in signaling through phosphorylation reactions (Lambers and Plaxton 2015; Poirier et al. 2022). Once soluble Pi has been imported from the soil into the root, Pi must be transferred to the shoot via the loading of Pi into the root xylem. This step is mediated by the export of Pi from the root xylem parenchyma to the xylem vessels. PHOSPHATE 1 (PHO1) was first identified in Arabidopsis thaliana by map-based cloning from a mutant defective in root xylem Pi loading and the PHO1 promoter was active in the root xylem parenchyma and passage cells (Poirier et al. 1991; Hamburger et al. 2002). PHO1 defined a new class of Pi transporters, named SPX–EXS transporters, characterized by the presence of an N-terminal SPX and C-terminal EXS domains (Wang et al. 2004). These domains were named after the proteins in which they were first identified, namely SYG1 and PHO81 in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Xenotropic and Polytropic Retrovirus Receptor 1 (XPR1) in mammals for SPX, and ERD1 in yeast, XPR1 and SYG1 for EXS. PHO1-mediated Pi export was demonstrated in Arabidopsis transgenic lines overexpressing PHO1 in leaves (Stefanovic et al. 2011; Arpat et al. 2012), by transient expression in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) (Arpat et al. 2012; Wege et al. 2016), and through heterologous expression in mammalian cells and Xenopus oocytes (Ma et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2025).

The characterization of the plant PHO1 was important in subsequently assigning functions to the orthologous SPX–EXS proteins XPR1 in animal (Giovannini et al. 2013; Wege and Poirier 2014) and SYG1 in fungi (Takado et al. 2023) as Pi exporters to the extracellular space. XPR1 activity is essential to maintain cellular Pi balance in mice as its deletion is lethal (Ansermet et al. 2017). Mutations in XPR1 in humans result in diseases linked with perturbation in Pi homeostasis, such as primary familial brain calcification (Legati et al. 2015), implicating calcium phosphate deposits in brain microcapillaries, as well as kidney tubule malfunction in Pi reabsorption found in the Fanconi syndrome (Ansermet et al. 2017). XPR1-mediated Pi export is also associated with the survival of ovarian carcinoma cells that overexpress Pi importers (Bondeson et al. 2022). Current knowledge on the function of the SYG1 ortholog in fungi is more limited. A truncated form of the S. cerevisiae SYG1 protein was found to interact with the β subunit of the trimeric G protein complex, but gene deletion was initially not associated with obvious phenotypes (Spain et al. 1995). More recently, mutation of the Schizosaccharomyces pombe SYG1 ortholog was linked with reduction in Pi export and increased in cytotoxicity from the accumulation of cytosolic Pi in mutants unable to store vacuolar polyphosphate (Takado et al. 2023).

The A. thaliana genome possesses 10 additional PHO1 homologs (PHO1;H1–PHO1;H10) displaying highly diverse tissue expression patterns, including root epidermal and cortical cells, trichomes, hydathodes, and pollen, suggesting broad biological functions (Wang et al. 2004). Complementation analysis of the pho1 mutant using individual homologs driven by the PHO1 promoter showed that only PHO1 and its closest homolog PHO1;H1 could restore Pi loading in the root xylem (Stefanovic et al. 2007). Phylogenetically, plant PHO1 proteins form 2 clades (Secco et al. 2010). Monocots possess only clade I members whereas gene diversification in dicots generated 2 clades. In A. thaliana, clade I includes PHO1 and PHO1;H1 while clade II regroups all other PHO1 homologs (PHO1;H2 to PHO1;H10) (Fig. 1). Clade I proteins are closely associated with Pi export, whereas clade II members may have more divergent functions that largely remain to be identified.

In plants, the SPX domain is also found combined with other domains; SPX-MFS (Major Facilitator Superfamily) proteins which include the PHT5 vacuolar Pi transporters (Liu et al. 2015, 2016; Wang et al. 2015) and SPX-RING (Really Interesting New Gene) proteins, such as the E3 ubiquitin ligase NLA regulating degradation of the PHT1 Pi importer (Kant et al. 2011; Lin et al. 2013; Park et al. 2014). Four stand-alone SPX proteins (SPX1-SPX4) (Duan et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2010) influence the Pi starvation response through their physical interaction with the PHR1 transcriptional factor, mediated by the high affinity binding of inositol polyphosphates (InsP), particularly inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6), diphosphoinositol pentakisphosphate (InsP7) and bisdiphosphoinositol tetrakisphosphate (InsP8), to conserved lysine- and arginine-rich surfaces on SPX (Rubio et al. 2001; Bustos et al. 2010; Wild et al. 2016; Jung et al. 2018; Ried et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2021). Although the SPX domain is dispensable for PHO1-mediated Pi export activity when transiently expressed in tobacco cells (Wege et al. 2016), mutations in the conserved lysine-rich region of PHO1's SPX domain abolish its ability to complement the pho1 mutant, demonstrating the importance of InsP binding in regulating PHO1 Pi export activity (Wild et al. 2016).

The EXS domain is essential for both Pi export activity and localization of PHO1 to the Golgi apparatus and trans-Golgi network (Golgi/TGN) (Wege et al. 2016). Fungal and plant proteins having only an EXS domain, collectively known as ERD1, function as resident cis-Golgi Pi exporters (Snyder et al. 2017; Hsieh et al. 2023). Their activity maintains Golgi Pi homeostasis by exporting to the cytosol Pi generated during nucleotide sugar-dependent glycosylation reactions occurring in the Golgi.

Recent cryo-EM studies of the Arabidopsis PHO1 homolog PHO1;H1 (Fang et al. 2025), the human XPR1 (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a), and Schizosaccharomyces pombe Syg1 (Yang et al. 2026) have considerably advanced our understanding of SPX–EXS transporters. These studies reveal a high degree of structural and mechanistic conservation, particularly between the plant and animal PHO1 orthologs (Khan et al. 2026). It is thus useful to consider the information from both PHO1;H1 and XPR1 to get an overall comprehension of the mode of action of the SPX–EXS protein family.

Overall domain organization of PHO1 orthologs

In A. thaliana, PHO1 shares highest sequence homology with PHO1;H1 (46% and 61% amino acid identity and similarity, respectively; Wang et al. 2004) and both function in Pi export to the root xylem parenchyma cells (Wang et al. 2004; Stefanovic et al. 2007). Thus, conclusions obtained from the structure of PHO1;H1 are essentially applicable to PHO1. Both PHO1;H1 and the human XPR1 form homodimers composed of a cytosolic N-terminal SPX domain, 4 transmembrane (TM) helices forming a scaffold region involved in dimerization, an EXS domain forming a 6 TM barrel-shaped pore, and a short flexible C-terminal tail (Fig. 2A) (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). Dimerization is mediated by van der Waals interactions centered on a conserved GxxxG motif in TM1 (Fig. S1). Mutations of this motif in XPR1 disrupt dimerization, reduce protein stability and plasma membrane (PM) localization, and strongly impair Pi export (Yan et al. 2024b; He et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025). This motif is conserved in plant PHO1 proteins (Fig. S1).

The EXS domain forms the Pi channel

The EXS domain forms an anion-selective pore that adopts open and closed conformations. All studies identify a conserved central Pi-binding site located midway through the pore transmembrane domain implicating D560 (TM5), K635/Y636 (TM7), D676 (TM8), R716 (TM9) and R747/R748 (TM10) (numeration according to PHO1;H1, Fig. 2B, Fig. S1), and mutagenesis in PHO1;H1 or XPR1 demonstrated their essential role in Pi transport activity (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). These amino acids are also conserved in the plant and fungal ERD1, supporting their role as Golgi Pi exporter (Fig. S2).

The study of Arabidopsis PHO1;H1 showed that Q672 also form hydrogen bond with Pi (Fig. 2B) and that Q672A mutation resulted in a reversal in potential in HEK293T cells expressing PHO1;H1, indicating a potential alteration in ion selectivity (Fang et al. 2025). Interestingly, Q672 is only conserved within clade I PHO1 orthologs involved in Pi export, including the Arabidopsis PHO1 and PHO1;H1, rice (Oryza sativa) PHO1;2, and barrel medic (Medicago truncatula) PHO1;1 and PHO1;2 (Secco et al. 2010; Nguyen et al. 2021) (Figs. S1–S3) whereas clade II homologs (eg A. thaliana PHO1;H2 to PHO1;H10) lack this residue despite conserving all other Pi-coordinating amino acids (Fig. S3). This observation suggests that clade II PHO1 homologs may have evolved different substrate specificities while maintaining the overall channel architecture. This hypothesis may be tested by determining channel conductance and substrate specificity of clade II PHO1 orthologs through expression in Xenopus oocytes and/or in human embryonic kidney 293T cells.

Within the EXS pore of the Arabidopsis PHO1;H1, W719 present in TM9 (W573 in XPR1) forms a hydrogen bond with Y610 (TM6) and seals the pore, preventing further movement of Pi toward the apoplast (Fig. 2C, Fig. S1) (Fang et al. 2025). Channel opening in both PHO1;H1 and XPR1 involves a pronounced rotation of the C-terminal segment of TM9, displacing the blocking tryptophan from the pore axis and creating a continuous pathway to the extracellular space (Fig. 2C) (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). Mutation of this tryptophan in either XPR1 or PHO1;H1 strongly impairs Pi transport activity (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a), indicating that this residue does not simply act as a roadblock but rather contributes to positioning Pi in the channel to support the conformational changes of TM9 allowing Pi export.

The C-terminal tail acts as a gate

In addition to the intrapore gate formed by TM9, Pi entry is controlled by a second gate located at the cytosolic entrance of the channel (Fig. 2A and 2D). In XPR1, residues surrounding F623 (corresponding to F767 in PHO1;H1) anchor the C-terminal plug to the pore entrance through a network of electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, effectively preventing Pi entry (Zuo et al. 2025a; Wang et al. 2025b). Mutations in these residues in XPR1 increase Pi efflux and stabilize an inward-open conformation, providing direct evidence that the C-terminal tail acts as a bona fide autoinhibitory gate (Zhang et al. 2025; Zuo et al. 2025a). Although deletion of the C-terminal plug in PHO1;H1 inactivates Pi transport, evidence suggests that this is due to protein instability (Fang et al. 2025). Conservation of residues forming the C-plug suggests that PHO1;H1 likely uses a similar regulatory mechanism (Fig. S1).

The SPX domain binds InsP and regulates pore opening

The SPX domain is too flexible for its structure to be determined by cryo-EM unless InsP is added during the purification procedure, namely either InsP6, InsP7, or InsP8 for XPR1 and InsP6 for PHO1;H1. In both proteins, 2 SPX domains organize in a head-to-tail dimeric arrangement that creates a positively charged interface for InsP binding (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). Unlike XPR1, PHO1;H1 displays a domain-swapped organization in which the 2 SPX domains are interlocked (Fig. 2A and 2D) (Fang et al. 2025).

Studies of PHO1;H1 and XPR1 show that the conserved positively charged residues in the SPX domain are essential for InsP binding and Pi export (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). Two InsP molecules bind at the interface between adjacent SPX domains through conserved lysine and tyrosine residues. For example, conserved Y23, K27, and K320 of PHO1;H1 SPX protomer A are involved in binding InsP6 with K370 and K373 of the neighboring SPX B protomer (Fig. S1) (Fang et al. 2025). Mutagenesis of these conserved residues disrupts ligand-dependent activation of Pi export, showing that InsP binding is essential for activity (Lu et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024b; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b; Zuo et al. 2025a). A second InsP binding pocket is present in XPR1 at the interface between the SPX domain and a region between TM4 and TM5 (TMD) (Lu et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a; Zuo et al. 2025a). Since the lysine residues involved in this interaction are not conserved in PHO1 and SYG1 orthologs, this additional InsP binding site appears specific to animals. Likewise, XPR1 associates with the scaffold protein KIDINS220, which promotes XPR1 PM localization and transport activity, whereas no homologous protein is found in plants or yeast (Bondeson et al. 2022).

Current structural models indicate that InsP binding induces major conformational rearrangements of the SPX domains. InsP binding at the SPX-SPX interface stabilizes the dimer and promotes the displacement of the C-terminal plug, thereby opening the intracellular gate (Fig. 2D). In mammals, the additional interaction between SPX and TMD may further facilitate removal of the C-plug. A major unresolved question is how the opening and closing of the cytosolic and intra-channel gates are coordinated, with some studies suggesting that opening of the 2 gates is not obligatorily coupled but may occur independently (Zhu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a).

Together, cryo-EM and functional studies establish PHO1;H1 and XPR1 as double-gated Pi channels whose activity is controlled by InsP through the SPX domain (Fig. 2D). Under Pi-deficient condition, where cytosolic levels of InsP, and particularly InsP8, are low, absence of interaction between the SPX domains maintains the channel closed, both at the intracellular site by the C-terminal plug and at the intra-channel site via the interaction between the amino acids W719 and Y610 (Fig. 2D, left panel). Increase in intracellular Pi is associated with a rise in cytosolic InsP, which promotes SPX-SPX interaction, resulting in a conformational change removing the C-terminal plug from the channel entrance (Fig. 2D, middle panel). Entry of Pi into the channel likely contributes to the rearrangement of TM9 orientation, moving W719 and opening the intra-channel gate, allowing free passage of Pi across the membrane (Fig. 2D, right panel). This mode of Pi transport is fundamentally distinct from Pi importers, such as yeast PHO90 or plant PHT, which rely on an alternating-access mechanism (Schneider et al. 2024; Du et al. 2026).

Although several cryo-EM publications on PHO1;H1 and XPR1 have used the commercially available InsP6 to stabilize the SPX domain and enable structure determination, studies have shown that the pyrophosphate derivative InsP8 is the key endogenous activator of XPR1 (Wilson et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2020). Furthermore, the SPX domain of PHO81 in S. cerevisiae controls the Pi starvation response program via InsP8 (Chabert et al. 2023) and the same molecule also contributes to phosphate starvation signaling via the interaction between free-standing SPX proteins and PHR1 in Arabidopsis (Dong et al. 2019; Ried et al. 2021). Further complexity is found in Arabidopsis where multiple isoforms of inositol pyrophosphate are present in different concentrations, including 1-InsP7, 5-InsP7, 4/6-InsP7, and 1,5-InsP8 (Yadav et al. 2025). Further studies should determine which InsP isoform is the principal physiological regulator of PHO1 Pi export activity.

The evolution of PHO1 proteins

Identification of the structural determinants underlying PHO1 function provides new insights into the evolution of the SPX–EXS family. PHO1 proteins are remarkably conserved across not only the green plant lineage, but in all major eukaryotic supergroups, which includes the Archaeplastida (land plants, green and red algae), Opisthokont (fungi and animals), Amoebozoa (slide molds and amoebae), Rhizaria (marine protists), Alveolates (paramecium, plasmodium), Stramenopiles (oomycetes, diatoms, brown algae), Hacrobians (unicellular marine algae), and Excavates (giardia, euglena, trypanosome) (Figs. S4 and S5). Conservation is not limited to the overall domain structure of PHO1 but also includes the residues involved in dimerization (GxxxG motif), InsP binding within SPX, and channel gating. However, neither the SPX nor the EXS domains have been identified in Bacteria or Archaea, indicating that SPX–EXS transporters arose in the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor. While the structural conservation of SPX–EXS family throughout eukaryotes suggests some level of conservation in function, study of PHO1 homologs belonging to various eukaryotic lineages will be essential to establish the function of these genes throughout evolution.

Evolution also resulted in the modular diversification of both the SPX and EXS domains. In plants, the SPX domain was recruited into stand-alone SPX proteins, SPX-MFS transporters, and SPX-RING ubiquitin ligases, whereas the EXS domain was retained in ERD1 proteins functioning as Golgi Pi exporters. In fungi, the SPX domain also became associated with regulatory proteins such as PHO81. Thus, while the ancestral SPX–EXS architecture has been highly conserved, both domains were subsequently co-opted into distinct signaling and transport pathways.

Cell biology of PHO1

A striking feature of the Arabidopsis PHO1 is its primary localization to the Golgi/TGN. Functional PHO1-GFP fusion that complements the pho1 mutant consistently localize to the Golgi/TGN in both tobacco epidermis and Arabidopsis xylem parenchyma (Arpat et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2012; Wege et al. 2016). Similar localization was observed for PHO1;H1 and PHO1;H3 in xylem parenchyma (Khan et al. 2014; Vetal and Poirier 2023). This distribution initially appeared inconsistent with the role of PHO1 in Pi export, since other transporters mediating root-to-shoot nutrient transfer, including BORON TRANSPORTER1 (BOR1, for boron; Takano et al. 2002, 2005) and NITRATE TRANSPORTER 1.5 (NRT1.5, for nitrate; Lin et al. 2008a), are localized to the PM. Nevertheless, direct measurements of Pi export in Xenopus oocytes and mammalian cells expressing either the Arabidopsis PHO1;H1 or rice PHO1 orthologs firmly establish PHO1 as a Pi exporter (Ma et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2025). Interestingly, expression of M. truncatula PHO1 orthologs in tobacco epidermal cells (Nguyen et al. 2021) and rice PHO1 in rice leaf protoplasts and onion cells (Che et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2021), show distinct localization, from the Golgi/TGN to the PM, indicating that cellular context is likely an important factor in PHO1 subcellular localization. It is thus important to examine the expression PHO1 homologous proteins in their native tissues using endogenous promoters and functional fluorescent protein fusions to confidently identify their subcellular localization (Fig. 1).

Arabidopsis PHO1 localization to the Golgi/TGN was not influenced by Pi deficiency, the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme PHO2, or the potential ubiquitination of cytosolic lysines in the EXS domain (Vetal and Poirier 2023). However, inhibition of clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) stabilizes PHO1 and PHO1;H1 at the PM in both tobacco and Arabidopsis xylem parenchyma cells (Vetal and Poirier 2023). Other transporters moving solutes across the PM but primarily localized to the Golgi/TGN and/or endosomes include the iron importer IRON-REGULATED TRANSPORTER 1 (IRT1) (Barberon et al. 2011), the nicotianamine apoplastic exporter NA EFFLUX TRANSPORTER1 (NAET1) (Chao et al. 2021) and the manganese exporter MTP11 (Delhaize et al. 2007; Peiter et al. 2007). IRT1 is observed at the PM when its recycling via endocytosis is inhibited by blocking mono-ubiquitination (Barberon et al. 2011) while NAET1 and MTP11 are re-localized to the PM when CME is inhibited (Vetal et al. 2025). This later response appear selective, as several other resident Golgi proteins remained unaffected by CME inhibition (Vetal et al. 2025). The fact that stabilization of IRT1 at the PM resulted in severe growth defects, while PM-stabilized PHO1 showed reduced root-to-shoot Pi export activity, indicate that their localization to the endomembrane system is important for optimal ion homeostasis (Barberon et al. 2011; Vetal and Poirier 2023).

At present, 2 main models can be envisaged to reconcile PHO1 subcellular localization at the Golgi/TGN with its apoplastic Pi export activity. The first is that PHO1 is mainly active when localized at the PM, even though this localization is transient and short-lived (Fig. 3A). An alternative hypothesis proposes that PHO1 loads cytosolic Pi into Golgi-derived vesicles using an electrochemical gradient generated by the vacuolar ATPase. Pi unloading from these vesicles to the apoplastic space would occur through exocytosis, followed by the rapid recycling of PHO1 from the PM by CME and endosomal trafficking (Fig. 3A). A similar model was also proposed for NEAT1 activity in nicotianamine apoplastic export (Chao et al. 2021).

Models for the action of PHO1. A) Two main models are presented to explain the Pi export activity of PHO1 in the root xylem parenchyma cells. After transiting through the Golgi and TGN, PHO1 could be transiently localized and active at the PM (red arrow) before being rapidly re-internalized by clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME). Alternatively, PHO1 localized to the TGN and early endosomes (EE) could transport Pi into intracellular vesicles. These Pi-filled vesicles subsequently fuse to the PM to release Pi in the apoplast (magenta arrow). PHO1 transiently present at the PM following vesicular fusion would then be recovered from the PM via CME. The clathrin-coated vesicles containing PHO1 would be recycled via the TGN/EE to undergo another cycle of Pi transport or be directed for degradation via multivesicular bodies. B) Hypothesized model to explain the contribution of PHO1 and homologs in signaling pathways. In response to phytohormones, such as ABA, or to biotic/abiotic signals, such as pathogens or light, vacuolar anion channels (partially) permeable to Pi are activated, transiently releasing Pi in the cytosol. PHO1 transports this excess cytosolic Pi into vesicles to maintain Pi homeostasis. Vesicles containing Pi can be redirected toward the vacuole via multivesicular bodies.

Interesting parallels exist between the subcellular localization of the Arabidopsis PHO1 with that of its orthologs in mammals and insects. XPR1 acts as the surface receptor for entry of xenotropic retroviruses into the cell, implying at least a partial localization at the PM (Battini et al. 1999; Tailor et al. 1999). However, functional fluorescent-tagged XPR1 is localized to intracellular vesicles rather than at the PM (Li et al. 2024). In Drosophila, the PHO1 ortholog Pxo is localized to intracellular multilamellar organelles storing phospholipids that are degraded during Pi starvation to release Pi (Xu et al. 2023). Finally, the yeast SYG1 is at least partially localized to the vacuolar membrane through the AP-3 pathway, which is involved in cargo transport from the Golgi to the vacuole (Eising et al. 2022).

While the necessity of Pi export to the extracellular space in some specific tissues (eg xylem parenchyma in plants, kidney tubule epithelia in mammals) is manifest in complex multicellular organisms, the role of PHO1 orthologs in unicellular organisms is less apparent. In S. pombe, SYG1 was shown to contribute to cell proliferation and survival under artificially high external Pi concentration when the vacuolar Pi transporters were defective (Takado et al. 2023). However, the extracellular Pi concentration is expected to be very low and relatively stable in many natural environments, for example in the sea. This raises questions as to whether PHO1 orthologs in marine unicellular eukaryotes are really involved in Pi export to the extracellular space. Considering the localization of plant and animal PHO1 orthologs in intracellular organelles and vesicles, it can be hypothesized that PHO1 proteins evolved primarily to move Pi from the cytosol into vesicles. Depending on the cell type and physiological need of the organism, this would have created the possibility of modulating Pi storage and intracellular release, as exemplified for the Drosophila PHO1. Studies of the localization and function of PHO1 orthologs in model organisms, such the marine unicellular algae Ostreococcus tauri, would shed important light on the evolution of PHO1 function in eukaryotes.

Function of PHO1 belonging to clade 1

PHO1 proteins belonging to clade I are associated with Pi export and Pi transfer from root to shoot. This was shown for the Arabidopsis PHO1 and PHO1;H1 (Hamburger et al. 2002; Stefanovic et al. 2007), the rice PHO1;2 (Secco et al. 2010) as well as clade I PHO1 orthologs in tomato (Zhao et al. 2019) and chickpea (Mani et al. 2024). However, expression of some clade 1 PHO orthologs beyond the root vasculature, such as the Arabidopsis PHO1;H1 expression in the vascular tissues of leaves, stems, and floral tissues, indicate a role in Pi distribution between tissues beyond the root (Wang et al. 2004; Vogiatzaki et al. 2017). The rice PHO1;2 promoter is active in xylem parenchyma, phloem sieve elements, and companion cells of vascular bundles of leaf blades as well as in nodes (Jabnoune et al. 2013; Che et al. 2020). In line with this expression pattern, rice PHO1;2 contributes to Pi transfer between shoot tissues (Che et al. 2020). Similarly, rice PHO1;1 and PHO1;3 genes are mostly expressed in the phloem of the shoot and phenotypes of the corresponding mutants show alteration in Pi distribution within leaves or between nodes (Saenchai et al. 2016; Yan et al. 2024a).

Beyond transfer of Pi to the vascular cylinder, Pi export to the apoplastic space is expected to be necessary in tissues that are not symplastically connected. In plants, the filial tissues of the developing seeds, namely the endosperm and developing embryo, are not symplastically connected to the maternal seed coat layers (Patrick and Offler 2001; Stadler et al. 2005). Both the Arabidopsis PHO1 and PHO1;H1 are expressed in the chalazal seed coat (CZSC) of developing seeds, where the vascular bundle terminates at the end of the funiculus (Vogiatzaki et al. 2017). Using pho1 mutant in micrografting experiments as well as CZSC-specific promoter to downregulate PHO1 with RNAi, it was shown that expression of PHO1 in the CZSC was contributing to the transfer of Pi from the maternal seed coat to the developing embryos (Vogiatzaki et al. 2017). A similar contribution of the rice PHO1;2 to seed development was also demonstrated. PHO1;2 expression in developing seeds was observed primarily in the ovular vasculature and maternal nucellar epidermis and rice pho1;2 mutant seeds had higher Pi level in the maternal husk and caryopsis coat but lower in the filial starchy endosperm, resulting in seeds that are smaller by weight compared to wild-type and showed reduced starch content (Che et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2021; Ko et al. 2024). It was concluded that the maternally inherited pho1;2 phenotype resulted from a reduction in Pi transfer from the maternal ovular vasculature and nucellar epidermis to the filial endosperm (Ko et al. 2024). Similar phenotypes were observed in mutants of the maize (Zea mays) PHO1;2a and PHO1;2b genes (Ma et al. 2021).

Nitrogen-fixing bacteroids in root nodules are surrounded by the peribacteroid membrane, which participates in extensive exchanges of solutes between the plant cell and the bacteroids, such as ammonia, malate, and numerous essential nutrients, including Pi (Whitehead and Day 1997). Since the peribacteroid membrane is derived from the PM, it was hypothesized that in legumes, some PHO1 orthologs may mediate Pi transfer from the cytosol to the bacteroids across the peribacteroid membrane. This hypothesis was tested in M. truncatula where 2 genes encoding clade I PHO1 homologs (PHO1.1 and PHO1.2) were broadly expressed across the various nodule zones in addition to the root vascular system (Nguyen et al. 2021). Expression of these proteins in tobacco epidermal cells mediated specific Pi export. Nodule-specific downregulation of both M. truncatula PHO1.1 and PHO1.2 by RNAi specifically reduced the transfer of Pi from the plant cells to the bacteroids, negatively impacting nodule development and nitrogen fixation (Nguyen et al. 2021).

Export of Pi across the PM is expected to be a key feature for the transfer of Pi from mycorrhizal fungi toward the plant cell, both at the arbuscules for arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and at the Hartig net for ectomycorrhizal fungi. While the mycorrhizal Pi transporters in charge of acquiring the Pi from the soil are known, transporters involved in Pi transfer from the mycorrhizae to the plant cells are unidentified (Ferrol et al. 2019). PHO1 homologs exist in major groups of mycorrhizal fungi and sequence alignment show that they share with the Arabidopsis PHO1;H1 and mammalian XPR1 the key amino acids involved in Pi binding and Pi export (Fig. S6). Thus, mycorrhizal PHO1 homologs are good candidates for transporters involved in Pi transfer from mycorrhizae to plant cells. Similarly, the symbiotic interaction and Pi exchange between fungi and green algae found in lichen may also involve a PHO1 homolog in the fungal symbiont (Fig. S6).

An interesting connection between PHO1 and abscisic acid (ABA) responses has been described. The Arabidopsis PHO1 is expressed in the leaf guard cells and is upregulated by ABA (Zimmerli et al. 2012). The stomatal responses of the pho1 mutant to ABA treatment were severely impaired, both in terms of induction of closure and inhibition of opening. Using a combination of hypocotyl micrografting and stomata-specific expression of PHO1 in the pho1 mutant and PHO1 downregulation in wild-type plants, impairment in stomatal response to ABA was demonstrated to be largely independent of the shoot Pi content but closely related to PHO1 expression (Zimmerli et al. 2012). ABA is also known to inhibit seed germination and Arabidopsis pho1 mutants are hypersensitive to ABA-mediated germination inhibition (Huang et al. 2017). Expression of PHO1 in germinating seedlings is regulated by ABI5, which contributed to this phenotype (Huang et al. 2017). The mode of action of PHO1 in ABA-stimulated guard cell movement or seed germination remains to be elucidated. ABA induces rapid and large fluxes in both cation and anion movement across the PM and tonoplast in both guard cells and germinating seeds (Planes et al. 2015; Eisenach and De Angeli 2017; Zuo et al. 2025b). Many of the identified channels regulated by ABA have rather broad anion specificity, such as the vacuolar ALMT channels, enabling transport of chloride, nitrate, and several organic acids (Eisenach and De Angeli 2017; Lee et al. 2025). While the permeability of several of these anion channels to Pi has not been experimentally addressed, it is possible that activation of tonoplast anion channels by ABA would impact cytosolic Pi content, at least transiently, considering that the vacuole is the major site of Pi storage. In this perspective, a hypothesized role of PHO1 in guard cells and germinating seeds may be to re-adjust cytosolic Pi concentration following an ABA-mediated Pi flux from the vacuole, by transiently moving the excess cytosolic Pi into vesicles before eventually releasing it back to the vacuole (Fig. 3B). Further experiments aimed at understanding the impact of ABA on Pi transport and homeostasis are essential to understand the implication of PHO1 in ABA-mediated responses.

Function of PHO1 homologs belonging to clade II

The function of PHO1 homologs belonging to clade II has remained largely enigmatic. Alignment of Arabidopsis clade II members with PHO1, PHO1;H1, and the human XPR1 shows conservation of the overall structure as well as of key amino acids involved in InsP binding in the SPX domain, dimer formation in the scaffold domain and Pi binding and gating in the EXS channel (Fig. S3). As described above, of the 8 key amino acid residues in PHO1;H1 involved in Pi binding within the EXS channel, all are conserved among class II PHO1 homologs except Q672 (Fig. S3). Interestingly, mutation of Q672 of PHO1;H1 resulted in a shift in reversal potential in Xenopus oocytes, suggesting a change in ion selectivity (Fang et al. 2025). These results suggest that while class II PHO1 homologs are likely to be ion channels regulated by InsP, the ion selectivity of this group may not be limited to Pi. Analysis of the transport specificity of the class II PHO1 homologs for Pi and/or other ions should be experimentally tested in heterologous systems. Systematic expression of all PHO1 gene family members under the PHO1 promoter in the pho1 mutant showed that besides PHO1 and PHO1;H1, none of the class II PHO1 homologs could complement the pho1 mutant phenotype (Stefanovic et al. (2007). This could be due either to distinct ion selectivity of class II PHO1 proteins or their inappropriate regulation or subcellular localization required for Pi export to the xylem (Arpat et al. 2012; Vetal and Poirier 2023).

The first mutant described for a clade II PHO1 homolog was pho1;h4, initially named short hypocotyl under blue1 (shb1) (Kang and Ni 2006). The phenotype of the pho1;h4 null mutant is a reduction of hypocotyl elongation under red, far-red, and blue light, while a dominant overexpressing allele showed increased hypocotyl elongation under the same conditions (Kang and Ni 2006). Subsequent analysis also showed phenotypes associated with seed size and endosperm proliferation (Zhou et al. 2009; Kang et al. 2013). Interpretation of the mode of action of the PHO1;H4 in these phenotypes raises numerous questions. PHO1;H4 is described as a nucleoplasmic protein recruiting specific transcription factors to the promoter of various genes, including the transcription factors PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) and MINISEED 3 (MINI3) (Kang et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2019). It remains enigmatic how a protein possessing 10 conserved transmembrane alpha-helices could maintain structural integrity to act in the hydrophilic environment of the nucleoplasm. Considering that PHO1;H4 contains the conserved amino acids involved in the binding of InsP to the SPX domain, Pi binding and channel gating in the EXS domain of PHO1;H1 and XPR1, it would be important to determine which of the pho1;h4 mutant phenotypes are dependent on these key features (Fig. S3).

The PHO1;H3 gene is expressed in the root vascular cylinder and is overexpressed in zinc deficiency (Khan et al. 2014). The pho1;h3 mutant shows higher shoot Pi compared to wild-type, suggesting that expression of PHO1;H3 would restrict Pi xylem loading, perhaps though an inhibitory interaction with PHO1 (Khan et al. 2014; Pal et al. 2017). A PHO1;H3 ortholog in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) has recently been implicated in the resistance to powdery mildew through an unknown mechanism (Sun et al. 2024).

Expression of the PHO1;H10 gene is strongly increased following numerous abiotic and biotic stress, including wounding, pathogen attack, as well as by the application of ABA and jasmonic acid (Ribot et al. 2008). However, work with pho1;h10 mutant has yet to reveal robust phenotypes associated with either biotic or abiotic stress other than a small decrease in anthocyanin production in detached leaves under high light (Sheng et al. 2022).

The conservation in clade II PHO1 homologs of key amino acids identified in Pi channel activity and InsP binding, as well as the connection of some of these genes with the response of plants to their environment (i.e. blue light for PHO1;H4, biotic and abiotic stress for PHO1;H10) raises the possibility that members of this gene family may act as anion channels more specifically involved in signaling than long-distance Pi export. The response of plants to numerous external stimuli, including light (Babla et al. 2019), pathogens, and hormones implicates unidentified anion channels (Barbier-Brygoo et al. 1999; De Angeli et al. 2007). For example, a rapid and transient plasma membrane depolarization resulting from the activity of an anion channel has been one of the earliest response of hypocotyls to blue light, yet the molecular nature of this anion channel still remains unknown (Cho and Spalding 1996). Furthermore, activities of numerous anion channels have been measured in different cells of the root, including the xylem parenchyma cells (Roberts 2006). It would be interesting to analyze whether any members of clade II PHO1 homologs could be involved in such anion channel activities.

Regulation of PHO1 expression and activity

PHO1;H1 mRNA is strongly upregulated under Pi deficiency and this is modulated by PHR1, the master transcription factor responsible for the activation of numerous genes under Pi deficiency, including Pi transporters PHT1;1 and PHT1;4 (Stefanovic et al. 2007; Bustos et al. 2010) (Fig. 4A). PHR1 activity is regulated by its interaction with stand-alone SPX proteins. Under Pi-sufficient conditions, high cellular levels of inositol pyrophosphates InsP7 and InsP8 mediates the interaction between SPX proteins and PHR1, essentially preventing PHR1 from binding to the promoter of Pi-regulated genes such as PHO1;H1 (Wild et al. 2016; Jung et al. 2018; Ried et al. 2021). Under Pi-deficient conditions, low InsP7 and/or InsP8 prevents PHR1 sequestration by SPX proteins, enabling PHR1 to activate transcription (Fig. 4A).

Under Pi-sufficient condition, PHO1 transcription is attenuated by the action of HHO3 (in rice) and WRKY (in rice and Arabidopsis) transcription factors (top left). Under Pi-deficient condition, PHO1 transcription is increased via a combination of decreased expression HHO3 and degradation of WRKYs via ubiquitination by the E3 ligase PRU1, which corresponding gene is overexpressed under these conditions (top right). Transcription of PHO1;H1 in Arabidopsis is under the control of the PHR1 transcription factor. Under Pi-sufficient conditions (bottom left), high level of InsP, in particular InsP8, promotes the interaction between PHR1 and SPX proteins, preventing PHR1 from binding to the P1BS sequence present in the promoter of PHO1;H1. Under Pi-deficient condition (bottom right), low InsP levels prevents the sequestration of PHR1 by SPX, which can then activate PHO1;H1 transcription. Under high Pi (top), expression of cis-NATPHO1.2 is low and a strong secondary structure in the PHO1.2 mRNA inhibits translation. Under low Pi (bottom), expression of cis-NATPHO1.2 increases. Interaction between cis-NATPHO1.2 and PHO1.2 mRNA destabilizes the inhibiting secondary structure present in the mRNA, enhancing translation. C) The presence of a µORF upstream or overlapping with the AUG start codon of PHO1 inhibits translation of the PHO1 main ORF. D) PHO1 (in rice and Arabidopsis) is degraded via ubiquitination by the action of the E2 ubiquitin conjugase PHO2. PHO2 mRNA is positively regulated by the transcription factor PHABULOSA, which is itself negatively regulated by the expression of miR165/160 promoted by SHR-SCR. At the same time, PHO2 mRNA is negatively regulated by miR399, which is strongly upregulated by Pi deficiency. The activity of the PHO2 protein is inhibited by phosphorylation via CASEIN KINASE 2 and stimulated by h-type thioredoxin.

While the upregulation of PHO1 mRNA under Pi deficiency is much weaker compared to PHO1;H1, PHO1 expression appears to be mainly controlled by transcriptional repression in both rice and Arabidopsis. In rice, the protein HRS1 HOMOLOG3 (HHO3) initially identified as a transcriptional repressor of AMMONIUM TRANSPORTER1 (AMT1) (Liu et al. 2023), negatively regulates the rice PHO1;1 gene by binding to semi-palindromic sequences related to GAATC found in its promoter (Yang et al. 2025) (Fig. 4A). Regulation of both nitrogen and Pi transporters by HHO3 enables some level of crosstalk between nitrogen and Pi nutrition.

In Arabidopsis, transcriptional repression of PHO1 involves 2 WRKY transcription factors, namely WRKY6 and WRKY42, that bind to W boxes present in the PHO1 promoter (Chen et al. 2009; Su et al. 2015) (Fig. 4a). Under Pi deficiency, WRKY6 protein is degraded via ubiquitination modulated by the PRU1 E3 ligase, and the PRU1 mRNA is itself upregulated by Pi deficiency (Ye et al. 2018). While overexpression of PHO1 via knockout of WRKY6 and/or WRKY42 transcription factors resulted in increased shoot Pi content (Chen et al. 2009; Su et al. 2015), application of similar knockout approaches in crops to improve phosphate use efficiency might have negative consequences as these transcription factors are also involved in the adaptation to several other stresses, such as response to plant pathogens (Robatzek and Somssich 2002; Cheng et al. 2015). In an innovative approach, CRISPR-mediated mutation in the W box of the rice PHO1;2 promoter resulted in increased PHO1;2 expression, greater root-to-shoot Pi export, enhanced root Pi uptake, and greater seed yield without affecting seed quality (Maurya et al. 2025).

The translation of PHO1 mRNA is regulated by distinct mechanisms in rice and Arabidopsis. In rice, all 3 PHO1 genes possess a cis-natural antisense (cis-NAT) that overlap with the 5′ end of the genes, and the expression of cis-NATs associated with the PHO1.1 and PHO1.2 genes are upregulated under Pi deficiency (Secco et al. 2010). Similar cis-NATs to PHO1 have been found in maize but not in Arabidopsis (Salazar-Vidal et al. 2016). Although expression of antisense RNA is often associated with cognate mRNA downregulation via the generation of siRNA, no change in mRNA level of the sense rice PHO1;2 mRNAs could be detected following cis-NAT expression (Secco et al. 2010; Jabnoune et al. 2013). Instead, translation of the PHO1.2 mRNA was enhanced by cis-NATPHO1.2 expression resulting in greater PHO1.2 protein amount under Pi deficiency (Jabnoune et al. 2013). Further studies showed that interaction between PHO1.2 mRNA and cis-NATPHO1.2 did not lead to widespread intermolecular double-stand RNA formation, but rather mediated local changes in nucleotide base pairing, leading to higher flexibility of PHO1.2 mRNA at a key high guanine-cytosine regulatory region inhibiting translation downstream of the start codon (Reis et al. 2021). Sense–antisense RNA interaction increased formation of the 80S complex in PHO1.2, possibly by inducing structural rearrangement within this inhibitory region, thus making this mRNA more accessible to 60S and enhancing translation (Fig. 4b) (Reis et al. 2021). Further association of cis-NAT expression with enhanced translation of the cognate mRNA has been identified in Arabidopsis in response to Pi deficiency (Deforges et al. 2019).

A distinct mode of translational control of PHO1 mRNA was found in Arabidopsis, where an upstream open reading frame (µORF) present in the 5′ untranslated region was demonstrated to inhibit its translation (Fig. 4C) (Reis et al. 2020). Point mutation removing the PHO1 µORF resulted in increased association of its PHO1 mRNA with polysomes and higher PHO1 protein levels. Interestingly, natural accessions lacking the PHO1 µORF exhibit higher PHO1 protein levels and shoot Pi content. Similar µORF were identified in PHO1 genes from rice, maize, barley and wheat (Reis et al. 2020). Since deletion of the µORF in Arabidopsis led to higher shoot Pi content and improved shoot growth under low external Pi supply, genome editing of µORFs in PHO1 by CRISPR appears a promising strategy to improve crop phosphate use efficiency.

Control over PHO1 protein stability plays an important role in the adaptation of plants to Pi deficiency. In Arabidopsis, PHO2 is a key E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme that stimulates the degradation of both PHT1 Pi importers and PHO1 through ubiquitination and multivesicular body-mediated vacuolar proteolysis (Liu et al. 2012; Huang et al. 2013; Park et al. 2014) (Fig. 4D). A similar function for the rice PHO2 ortholog on PHO1;2 degradation was demonstrated (Ying et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2020). PHO2 expression is itself under complex regulation. The microRNA miR399 is strongly upregulated by the PHR1 transcription factor under Pi deficiency and targets the PHO2 mRNA for degradation (Fuji et al. 2005; Lin et al. 2008b). PHO2 transcription is also stimulated by the PHABULOSA transcription factor (Xiao et al. 2021). PHABULOSA is subject to negative mRNA degradation by the miR165/166, which are positively regulated by SHORT-ROOT (Xiao et al. 2021). Abundance of the rice PHO2 is decreased by its phosphorylation via the casein kinase 2 (CK2) (Wang et al. 2020) while h-type thioredoxins regulates its activity though modulation of its redox state (Ying et al. 2017).

Concluding remarks

Determination of the molecular structure of PHO1;H1 and XPR1 has provided key information on how PHO1 orthologs act as Pi channels and how their activities are modulated by InsP. Furthermore, identification of key amino acids involved in binding Pi and gating the channel showed that such features are conserved in PHO1 across the green plant lineage and more broadly across eukaryotes, including in unicellular organisms, pointing to a potential conservation of function through eukaryotic evolution. Despite these major advances, many relevant questions regarding the mode of action and function of PHO1 family members remain unanswered, some of which are further underlined in the accompanying “Outstanding questions” box. Regarding the structure of PHO1, finding out the mechanism that coordinates the opening of the external and intra-channel gates to allow effective Pi flux across the channel is pivotal. Studies on PHO1 and XPR1 have highlighted a major gap in our understanding of the cell biology of Pi homeostasis in both plants and animals. For example, a central question is how PHO1 orthologs that are primarily localized in intracellular vesicles can effectively act as apoplastic (or extracellular) Pi exporters. Would the role of PHO1/XPR1 in Pi export have evolved from a more ancestral function of these proteins in managing intracellular Pi homeostasis via transient Pi storage in intracellular vesicles? If so, how is the extracellular release of Pi from such vesicles being controlled? Research on the role of the 9 clade II PHO1 homologs in Arabidopsis is complicated due to the likely high level of functional redundancy. There is thus a need to expend the study of PHO1 genes to other species, for example dicotyledon models with reduced number of PHO1 homologs. Importantly, studies in ancestral unicellular eukaryotes will be of key importance to elucidate how PHO1 proteins, and the cell biology underlying their function, evolved prior to the emergence of multicellularity.

Management of phosphate availability in soils is key to agricultural productivity. Phosphate limitation in crops impacts numerous aspects of the plant physiology, including photosynthesis, flowering, and seed yield, and PHO1 expression has an important role in all these traits (Ma et al. 2021, 2024; Da et al. 2024). Greater understanding of the structure, regulation, and mode of action of PHO1 opens the door for the development of novel crops that maintain optimal growth and yield with reduced input of Pi fertilizers.
