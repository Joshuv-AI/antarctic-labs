Response to anti‐seizure medications in children carrying novel or previously reported HCN1 gene variants
Authors: Marium N. Khan, Nicholas P. Poolos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveVariants in the HCN1 gene cause a syndrome of childhood epilepsy and developmental disability with a broad phenotypic range. Many affected children manifest with early infantile epileptic encephalopathy (EIEE) and highly drug‐resistant epilepsy. There are anecdotal reports that seizures in this syndrome are exacerbated by some anti‐seizure medications (ASMs), including lamotrigine and lacosamide. However, the efficacy of most ASMs in this disorder is unknown. We sought to understand which ASMs were most effective.MethodsWe compiled a registry of 10 children with HCN1 variants and refractory epilepsy via outreach to parent members of an online social affinity group, and from unsolicited parent outreach to us. We also provide a novel survey of ASM efficacy. INTRODUCTION

Variants in the human hyperpolarization activated, cyclic nucleotide‐gated cation channel type 1 (HCN1) gene cause a rare syndrome of early childhood epileptic encephalopathy manifested in many patients by medically refractory epilepsy and developmental disability (DD). 1 In most probands, HCN1 variants arise de novo without parental inheritance and cause onset of seizures and developmental delay or regression in the first year of life. The syndrome resembles in many ways Dravet syndrome (DS), which mostly arises from variants in the voltage‐gated Na+ channel gene SCN1A. 2 However, some children with HCN1 variants exhibit a milder syndrome, without seizures but with an autistic behavioral phenotype. 3

Seizures in children with HCN1 variants generally prove to be medically refractory. Anecdotal reports suggest that anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) with mechanisms of action (MOAs) that target Na+ channels are ineffective in this disorder and may even exacerbate seizures. 3 , 4 However, it is unclear whether any of the 20 or more licensed ASMs demonstrate efficacy in this difficult to treat syndrome. We sought to address this knowledge gap by assembling a registry of children with HCN1 variants by contacting parents who had joined a social media affinity group for this disorder, and also through unsolicited outreach to us by other parents. These parents filled out a survey on the clinical characteristics of their affected children, results of genetic testing, and response to ASMs. The results of this survey disclosed several previously unreported HCN1 variants and found wide variation in the response to ASMs. Knowledge of these variations in ASM efficacy may be clinically useful in guiding their epilepsy treatment.

METHODS

We assembled a registry of 10 children with HCN1 variants by either reaching out to parents who were members of a Facebook affinity group, “HCN1 Warriors,” or from unsolicited contacts from parents to the senior author. We received Institutional Review Board approval from the University of Washington to acquire these families' self‐reported medical information. Parents gave written consent to join the study and filled out an eight‐page survey (Supporting Information) detailing their child's and parents' genetic testing results, child's developmental status, and response to ASMs. In addition, outside medical records were provided that documented genetic testing results.

RESULTS

The demographics of the study population are shown in Table 1. Of the 10 subjects, five had HCN1 variants previously reported in the literature: M153I, S272P, M305L, I380F, and A387S. These variants all resulted in heterozygous, de novo single amino acid substitutions on the HCN1 protein sequence. All of these were associated with medically refractory epilepsy and moderate‐to‐severe DD. We also identified five subjects with novel HCN1 variants that have not been previously reported in the literature to the best of our knowledge. One subject was compound heterozygous for M215V amino acid substitution (paternally inherited) plus a deletion at p.73‐74 (maternally inherited). The remainder had heterozygous amino acid substitutions at M215V, M287L, F389S, and A670T. All subjects had associated DD ranging from mild to severe, with the exception of subject #10 (A670T) who was developmentally normal, even though that subject had epilepsy.

The inheritance pattern of three of the five novel HCN1 variants was familial. Subject #2 had unique compound heterozygous variants: a novel M215V variant that was paternally inherited and a novel short deletion (p.73‐74) that was maternally inherited. This subject had high seizure frequency (see Table 2) and DD whereas both parents had neither. We surveyed the likelihood of pathogenicity of these variants by reference to ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar) and GnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org) databases. ClinVar collects genetic testing data from clinical labs conducted for diagnostic purposes and provides a prediction of pathogenicity, whereas GnomAD aggregates data from large scale genomic sequencing projects, providing an estimate of gene variant frequency in the population at large. M215V had conflicting estimates of pathogenicity, with three submissions reported as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and one as Likely Benign; GnomAD reported this variant with an allele frequency in the general population of 10−5. Variant p73‐74del / c.218_223del was reported by ClinVar as Likely Benign with a relatively high allele frequency in GnomAD of 10−3. The severe clinical presentation in this subject could represent variable expression of the M215V variant inherited from the father or a possible additive effect on HCN1 channel function from two separate variants. Subject #3 also had the novel, maternally inherited heterozygous M215V variant. This subject had moderate seizure frequency and mild DD. Like subject #2, the M215V variant has inherited with no parental history of either epilepsy or DD. This suggests that while this variant in isolation can produce an epileptic phenotype, albeit one that is less severe than seen in Subject #2, its phenotypic expression is variable. There are other examples in the literature of inherited HCN1 variants where only some family members carrying the variant are affected by epilepsy and others are clinically unaffected (e.g., V414M, S680Y). 3

The locations of variants on the HCN1 ion channel structure are shown in Figure 1. The five previously described HCN1 variants mapped to one of the six HCN1 channel transmembrane domains, which have been common sites for other previously reported HCN1 variants. 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 Of the five novel variants, F389S was on the S6 transmembrane domain, associated with severe epilepsy and mild–moderate DD, similar to that seen with the nearby previously reported variants I380F 5 and A387S. 8 Two novel variants were found on linker regions between transmembrane domains: M215V and M287L. Both subjects had epilepsy of less severe intensity compared with those carrying variants on channel transmembrane domains and only mild DD. Both of these subjects became seizure‐free with medical management. The novel A670T variant, on the C‐terminal region distal to the cyclic nucleotide binding domain (CNBD), was associated with frequent nocturnal seizures but normal development. The p.73‐74 deletion seen on the intracellular N‐terminus occurred in association with the M215V variant in Subject #2 and produced a more severe phenotype than with the M215V variant alone (subject #3). In summary, variants occurring on HCN1 transmembrane domains tended to yield more severely affected phenotypes than those occurring on other regions of the protein sequence.

All of the children in this study had epilepsy. As shown in Table 1, the age of seizure onset was in the first year of life for most subjects, with the exceptions of subjects #5 (M287L) and #10 (A670T), both of whose seizures began at age 5. Table 2 describes the characteristics of seizures observed in the study cohort. Six of the 10 subjects had seizures provoked by fever; seven subjects had a history of status epilepticus (SE). Subject #8 (A387S) was deceased from an episode of SE. Parents filling out the survey endorsed multiple seizure types. The frequency of seizure occurrences was quite variable, ranging from multiple daily (subject #10, A670T) to just a few per year (subjects #4 S272P, #5 M287L, and #6 M305L). Interestingly, there was no strong correspondence seen between seizure frequency and DD: there were subjects with only several times yearly seizures who nonetheless had moderate to severe DD (subjects #4, S272P and #6, M305L) and a subject with daily seizures with normal development (#10, A670T).

We surveyed subjects' parents on their impression of the effectiveness of ASMs that the subjects had been exposed to. We asked them to rate ASMs based on whether each drug had significantly decreased, increased, or had little effect on seizure frequency. For each ASM, we calculated an Efficacy index (%) as follows: ((# of subjects with decreased seizures − # of subjects with increased seizures)/total number of ASM exposures in which efficacy was reported) * 100. Efficacy index was not calculated for fewer than three ASM exposures. The results of this survey are shown in Figure 2.

All of the subjects in our survey had been exposed to multiple ASMs and were medically refractory. Subjects had been exposed to a median eight different ASMs (range 1–10). The ASMs in Figure 2 are listed on the x‐axis in decreasing number of exposures, with levetiracetam (LEV) the most frequently used drug, and of ASMs with at least three exposures, cannabidiol (CBD) the least used. There was wide variability in the perceived effectiveness of ASMs. The most commonly used ASM, LEV, had an efficacy index not much different from zero. However, clobazam (CLB) and valproate (VPA) demonstrated significantly better efficacy, with VPA the most effective of all ASMs. Two subjects became seizure‐free with exposure to either CLB (#3, M215V) or VPA (#5, M287L). Two ASMs were rated as causing a net worsening of seizure frequency: lamotrigine (LTG) and oxcarbazepine (OXC). Other ASMs perceived as having no net benefit were lacosamide (LAC), phenobarbital (PB), vigabatrin (VGB), and carbamazepine (CBZ). The one subject (#10, F389S) who was deceased due to SE was being treated at the time with LTG and topiramate (TPM).

DISCUSSION

Summary of findings

Variants in the HCN1 gene are now well recognized rare causes of epilepsy and DD. These studies have generally relied on referring physicians reporting the characteristics of patients under their care. We took a different approach, reaching out to a social media affinity group for parents with children carrying an HCN1 variant (“HCN1 Warriors”). This outreach, plus unsolicited contacts from parents to the senior author, yielded a registry of 10 children. These children had all undergone evaluation by a neurologist as well as genetic testing, revealing that HCN1 mutation was likely the cause of their syndrome. The results of this study yielded the discovery of five novel HCN1 variants. In addition, we compiled a novel survey of ASM efficacy that suggested some ASMs are markedly more effective than others, while some ASMs caused an exacerbation of seizure frequency.

Novel variants discovered and association with pathogenesis

We report here five novel HCN1 variants. Their location on the HCN1 channel protein structure can be correlated with subject phenotype and compared to previously reported variants. The F389S variant, mapped to the S6 transmembrane domain, was in close proximity to the previously reported I380F and A387S variants. 5 , 8 , 9 These variants were all associated with moderate‐to‐severe DD and frequent seizures, with the subject carrying the novel F389S variant deceased at 10 months of age due to SE. Other reported variants on S6 include V390A and G391D, both associated with relatively severe phenotypes. 3 , 7 In general, variants mapping to the transmembrane domains of the HCN1 channel, such as our reported M153I 3 and S272P, 1 have been associated with significant DD and severe epilepsy.

Two novel variants were found on intracellular linking regions between transmembrane domains. M287L, mapped to the S4‐5 linker region, was associated with a more mild phenotype, with only mild DD and yearly seizures, compared to variants mapped to a transmembrane region; however, other reported variants mapping to the S4‐5 linker, H279Y, and R297T have been associated with more severe phenotypes. 1 A novel S2‐3 linker region variant, M215V, was seen in two subjects. When identified in isolation (subject #3), it was also associated with a relatively mild phenotype as was the S4‐5 linker variant M287L. Interestingly, this variant was maternally inherited, and the subject's mother had no history of epilepsy or DD. However, the M215V variant in subject #2 showed paternal inheritance with no history of seizures in the father and was found in heterozygosity with a p.73‐74 deletion inherited from the mother. The p.73‐74del variation was assessed in the subject's genetic testing as likely benign. Neither parent had a history of epilepsy or DD, and the M215V variant is as VUS versus likely benign in its pathogenicity. However, it is striking that this novel M215V variant appears twice in our small cohort, so we hypothesize that M215V can cause a mild syndrome in isolation. Whether its appearance in compound heterozygosity with the likely benign p.73‐74del is causative of a more severe syndrome is unclear. Our reading of the literature did not identify any other reports of S2‐3 linker variants.

The A670T variant was seen on the distal C‐terminal region. This variant was maternally inherited and was associated with frequent, nocturnal seizures in the subject (but not in the mother), and development was felt to be normal. Although the pathogenicity of this variant is assessed in ClinVar as being VUS versus likely benign, variants in the distal C‐terminal region have been associated with mild epilepsy and DD (S710Rfs71) 7 or with mild epilepsy and normal development (S680Y). 3 In summary, variants mapping to the transmembrane domains were associated with severe phenotypes, whereas variants mapping to the linker regions or N‐ and C‐termini generally had more mild phenotypes.

Response to ASMs

Parents of children in our registry in the aggregate retrospectively identified VPA and CLB as the most effective ASMs, whereas LTG and OXC appeared to exacerbate seizure frequency, not lessen it. Two subjects exposed to either CLB or VPA became seizure‐free. It is worth noting that the one subject (#10, F389S) who was deceased from SE was exposed to LTG at the time of his death. Other commonly used ASMs, such as LEV, LAC, and KLP, were neither particularly effective nor detrimental.

These findings have correlates with drug testing in a transgenic mouse line carrying the human M305L HCN1 variant, where LTG and PHT increased interictal epileptiform discharge rate, while VPA reduced it. 10 , 11 Our subject carrying the M305L variant likewise had exacerbation of seizure frequency by LTG and improvement with VPA. LTG or PHT administered to mice carrying other HCN1 mutations (human G391D or M153I) showed provocation of spontaneous convulsive seizures. 10 The pattern seen here where ASMs that are effective in generalized epilepsy syndromes (VPA for example) were most useful, while drugs that are indicated for focal epilepsy syndromes only (OXC) are exacerbating, is seen in Dravet syndrome as well. 12 , 13 The reasons for this are not entirely clear but may result from the differential effects of these ASMs on principal excitatory neurons versus inhibitory interneurons: animal models of DS demonstrate a specific deficit in interneuron excitability, 14 , 15 and ASMs active against voltage‐gated Na+ channels may have a disproportionately inhibitory effect on interneuron excitability. 16

The exacerbating effect of LTG in children with HCN1 variants is an interesting observation with regard to its demonstrated cellular and molecular MOAs. LTG is known to inhibit voltage‐dependent Na+ channels, but unlike PHT and OXC, which also inhibit activation of voltage‐gated Na+ channels and are clinically indicated only in the treatment of focal epilepsy, LTG is indicated in the treatment of both generalized and focal epilepsy. This points to a MOA beyond Na+ channels alone. 17 LTG has been demonstrated in vitro to upregulate the activation of HCN1 channels in hippocampal pyramidal neurons (likely not due to a direct effect on the channels themselves). 10 , 18 , 19 HCN1 channel expression and function are decreased in animal models of acquired epilepsy, producing hyperexcitability of excitatory principal neurons, 20 , 21 thus it would seem that LTG might exert a beneficial effect on HCN1 variant‐associated seizures. The consequences of human HCN1 variants on HCN1 channel biophysical properties have been studied at a neuronal level using electrophysiology techniques in ex vivo mouse brain tissue, but the results of these studies paint a complicated picture. The human M305L variant produced a modest loss of HCN1‐mediated current (I h) in neocortical pyramidal neurons and hyperexcitability in provoked action potential firing, consistent with the role of I h in acquired epilepsy animal model data. 4 The G391D and M153I variants appear to reduce the expression and function of HCN1 channels in excitatory hippocampal pyramidal neurons, but paradoxically, intrinsic excitability of these neurons was reduced not increased. 10 These conflicting findings are similarly seen in other studies (for example 22 ) that have failed to find consistent correlation of changes in HCN1 channel biophysical properties with clinical phenotypes. How HCN1 variants affect the excitability of inhibitory interneurons—which are critical to the pathogenesis of epilepsy in Dravet syndrome—has been less well studied. Thus, we conclude that the deleterious actions of LTG on seizure frequency in children with HCN1 variants are most likely via an inhibitory action on Na+ channels and not via an upregulation of HCN1 channel activity.

Study limitations

The conclusions regarding ASM efficacy drawn from this study include that they derive from a small patient sample with retrospectively recalled impressions from parents. These are subjective, non‐quantitative measures and should not be interpreted as head‐to‐head comparisons among various ASMs. Thus, the potential benefit or exacerbating effects from the ASMs listed should be interpreted as exploratory evidence and not as therapeutic guidelines in this disorder.

The novel HCN1 variants reported here have not been functionally characterized with in vitro electrophysiology, thus their pathogenicity is uncertain, as exemplified by “VUS” or lesser pathogenic class characterization in ClinVar for most of them. Further study of these novel variants would add to our understanding of this rare genetic disorder.
