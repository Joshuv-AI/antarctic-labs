Healthcare insurance status and hypertension control before and after the Affordable Care Act
Authors: Brent M Egan, Jiexiang Li, Susan E Sutherland, Michael K Rakotz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood pressure control among adults <65 years old with public, private, or no healthcare insurance after the US 2010 Affordable Care Act (ACA) is unknown.MethodsNational Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys were analyzed pre- (2001-2010) and post-ACA (2015-2023) on adults aged 20-64 years (pre-Medicare eligibility) with hypertension (systolic ≥140 or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or antihypertensive therapy). Relationships of blood pressure control (<140/<90 mmHg) to healthcare insurance and social determinants (race/ethnicity, low income [<200% federal poverty]) were assessed.ResultsAmong adults 20-64 years with hypertension pre- vs post-ACA, the uninsured (17.2% vs 11.4%) and the privately insured declined (64.3% vs 56.0%), while the publicly insured increased (16.2% vs 28.5%). Non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic adults comprised lower percentages of privately than publicly or uninsured adults, respectively, pre-ACA (18.9% vs 37.3%, 42.4%) and post-ACA (24.1% vs 38.3%, 50.3%). Low incomes were less frequent for privately insured adults than for publicly insured or uninsured adults, respectively, pre-ACA (13.0% vs 64.4%, 64.9%) and post-ACA (11.3% vs 67.6%, 63.1%). Blood pressure control was not different pre-ACA for publicly and privately insured adults (54.3% vs 50.4%, P = .064) and lower among uninsured adults (30.5%) than both insured groups, with a similar pattern post-ACA (54.5% [public], 50.2% [private], 30.3% [uninsured]).ConclusionsIn this US population-based study, blood pressure control was not different for publicly and privately insured adults pre- and post-ACA, although lower for uninsured adults. Public healthcare insurance appeared to continue mitigating the effects of adverse social determinants on blood pressure control post-ACA, despite the increase in publicly insured adults.Keywords: Affordable Care Act, hypertension, social determinants of health, healthcare insurance, blood pressure

Introduction

Blood pressure (BP) control was similar among adults with and without healthcare insurance in 1988-1994. By 2010, BP control rates doubled for publicly and privately insured adults while stagnant for uninsured adults.1 In this population-based study, publicly insured and uninsured adults had similar percentages of racial and ethnic minorities and lower incomes than the privately insured. Nevertheless, publicly and privately insured adults had similar BP control, suggesting healthcare insurance attenuated social barriers to control. However, more recent clinic-based data identified persistently lower BP control rates in Black than White adults, despite efforts to limit racial disparities.2

The adult population with hypertension who were uninsured increased from 12.3% in 1988-1994 to 17.4% in 2005-2010,1 representing an impediment to national BP control goals.3,4 Greater frequency of healthcare in publicly and privately insured than uninsured adults coincided with higher rates of awareness and treatment as well as BP control among the insured during 2001-2010.1,5

Key steps in Affordable Care Act (ACA) implementation included coverage of young adults until age 26 years on their parent’s policy and full coverage for recommended preventive services in 2010, Medicaid expansion in 2014, and mandated coverage for large employers in 2015. After ACA passage, the uninsured population in the US declined from 16.3% in 2010 to 8.0% in 2023,6–8 mainly reflecting an increase of public healthcare insurance.8,9 The ACA also increased healthcare insurance for low-income adults <65 years old with chronic conditions at Federally Qualified Health Centers.10 Systolic BP (SBP) was 3 mmHg lower in 2015-2016 among low-income adults in states with than without Medicaid expansion.11 Neither study compared changes in low-income adults to privately insured adults.

This study aimed to determine in a US population-based study if growing numbers of publicly insured adults, with adverse social determinants, maintained BP control rates equivalent to the privately insured post-ACA as reported pre-ACA. This information may help inform healthcare delivery, policy, and payment initiatives to equitably advance national BP control goals.3,4

Methods

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES) provide cross-sectional data on a representative sample of the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey protocols are approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Participants provided written informed consent.

NHANES were performed in 2-year cycles from 2001 to 2002 through 2017 to 2018. Five 2-year cycles were combined for NHANES 2001-2010 to represent the period before the 2010 ACA.6–8 NHANES 2015-2016, 2017-2020, and 2021-2023 were combined to represent the post-ACA period. NHANES 2017-2018 and 2019-2020 were combined, as the latter cycle was terminated prematurely by COVID-19. NHANES data are available from the Centers for Disease Control, National Center for Health Statistics.12 Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed.13

Study population. The primary analysis centered on adults 20-64 years old with hypertension, as most adults are Medicare-eligible at age 65 years.

Demographic variables. Participants self-reported age, sex, and race/ethnicity as non-Hispanic White (NHW), non-Hispanic Black (NHB), non-Hispanic Asian (NHA), Hispanic ethnicity of any race, and other. The NHA category was absent from NHANES before 2011.

Blood pressure was measured in triplicate during a single encounter at the Mobile Examination Center in most adults. As recommended in NHANES reporting guidelines, BP was defined by averaging the second and third values.14 When only 2 BP values were recorded, the second reading was used for analysis, while the single value was used when 1 BP was recorded. Adults without BP values were excluded.

Blood pressure was measured using mercury sphygmomanometry in NHANES 2001-2018. Blood pressure was measured by oscillometry in 2017-2018 and thereafter. Based on comparative analysis of 2017-2018 data, oscillometry values for 2017-2023 were altered by +1.2/−1.4 mmHg for ages 20-39, +1.1/−0.7 mmHg for 40-59, and +2.2/−1.9 mmHg for 60-64 years to match values by mercury sphygmomanometry.15

Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥140 or diastolic (D) BP ≥90 mmHg, or a positive response to the question “Are you currently taking medication to lower your BP?”.1,16,17 Hypertension prevalence, awareness, treatment, treatment intensity (number of antihypertensive medications/treated individual), treatment effectiveness (control/treated), and BP control to <140/<90 mmHg were previously defined.1,16,17 Blood pressure control to <130/<80 mmHg was assessed in secondary analyses.18,19

Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated from height and weight obtained at the Mobile Examination Center.20

Diabetes mellitus was defined by glycosylated hemoglobin ≥6.5%, having been told by a doctor that they have diabetes, or self-report of taking prescription insulin or other medications to treat diabetes.21

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined by adults reporting a physician told them they had a heart attack, stroke, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or angina.22,23 Rose Questionnaire data to assess angina24 were unavailable in 2021-2023.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g; eGFR was estimated using the race-neutral CKD-EPI creatinine equation.25

Social determinants. Income relative to the federal poverty level (FPL) was determined by dividing family income by the number of family members appropriate for each year and state in the United States per NHANES. Low income (<200% of the FPL), education (less than high school), and healthcare utilization (less than 2 encounters in the prior year) were previously defined and associated with hypertension awareness, treatment, and control.26–28 The number of healthcare encounters was unavailable for 2021-2023.

Healthcare insurance status 22 , 23 was defined as insured or uninsured, based on an affirmative or negative response, respectively, to the question “Are you covered by health insurance or some other kind of healthcare plan”? Private healthcare insurance was defined by a positive response to “Are you covered by private insurance”? Public healthcare insurance was defined as a positive response to coverage by Medicaid, Medicare, CHAMPUS/VA/military health care, Indian Health Service, a state-sponsored health plan, or other government insurance. Individuals with Medicare and/or Medigap were classified as having public healthcare insurance. Individuals reporting public and private insurance were not included.

Statistical analysis

SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA) survey analysis procedures were used to account for sample weights, stratification, and clustering of the complex sampling design and ensure nationally representative estimates. Mobile Examination Center exam weights (WTMEC2YR for NHANES 2000-2010, 2015-2016, and 2021-2023 and WTMECPRP for NHANES 2017-2020) were used in the analysis. Appropriate weights were constructed when combining NHANES cycles (eg, 5, 2-year cycles from 2000 to 2010 each with weight 1/5 and cycle 2015-2016 with weight 2/7.2, 2017-2020 with weight 3.2/7.2, 2021-2023 with weight 2/7.2). When estimating population counts, the unadjusted (crude) prevalence of the health condition or risk factor was estimated. Then, relevant population totals from the government census current population survey (CPS) were used to determine population estimates in NHANES. Finally, the prevalence of the risk factor or health condition was multiplied by the corresponding CPS-based population total to estimate the number of affected US adults. Missing data were not imputed.

Mean values and 95% CIs of continuous variables were calculated using Surveymeans. Percentages and 95% CIs of categorical variables were calculated using Surveyfreq. For the pre- and post- ACA period, the Wald F test was used to assess for differences in continuous variables, and the Rao-Scott Chi-Square Test was used to assess differences in categorical variables. To compare the pre- and post-ACA periods, the Wald F test in Surveyreg for continuous variables and Surveylogistic for categorical variables, with NHANES 2000-2010 coded 0 and NHANES 2015-2023 coded 1, was used to obtain P values.

Prevalence ratios and 95% CIs for hypertension control by insurance status were calculated using svyglm in R with a quasipoisson family. Model 1 was adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, and sex. Model 2 included model 1 and adjustment for BMI (<30 ref), diabetes, CVD, and CKD (null reference), which have been linked to treatment resistant hypertension and variably to control.29 Model 3 included model 2 with adjustment for income (<200% FPL reference) and education (less than high school reference), which have been linked to hypertension control.22,23 Model 4 included a usual care source, a key component of access to care linked to hypertension control.22,23 P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Age-adjusted values for mean SBP, DBP, hypertension awareness, antihypertensive treatment, treatment/aware, control, and control/treated were calculated. Age adjustment was performed using direct standardization to NHANES 2017-2023. The age categories used for standardization of privately insured, publicly insured, and uninsured adults, respectively, were 8.5%, 11.9%, and 15.2% for ages 20-34 years; 28.3%, 25.0%, and 27.7% for ages 36-50 years; and 63.2%, 63.1%, and 57.1% for ages 51-64 years.

Discussion

The key finding in this US population-based study is that adults with public healthcare insurance had absolute BP control rates of <140/<90 mmHg that equaled those with private healthcare insurance pre- and post-ACA. This finding is striking given the numerical doubling of publicly insured adults with adverse social determinants pre- to post-ACA. These observations suggest that care quality for publicly insured adults with adverse social determinants continued to match those with private insurance as numbers of the former rose. Yet, BP control to <130/<80 mmHg, which is associated with fewer cardiovascular events than higher BP levels, is infrequent irrespective of insurance status. Attaining higher rates of control to <130/<80 mmHg will require better care quality, including more appropriate rates of therapeutic intensification for uncontrolled BP.30–33

The numerical increase of publicly insured adults included the previously uninsured, who would have higher BP and lower rates of hypertension awareness and treatment, as well as BP control upon attaining public insurance. This finding coincides with a report that SBP was lower post-ACA among low-income adults in states with than without Medicaid expansion.11

Adults 20-64 years with incomes 100%-400% of the FPL were eligible for tax credits to obtain private insurance. However, percentages of privately insured adults with hypertension and incomes <200% of the FPL were lower post- than pre-ACA, suggesting that few adults with low incomes used insurance tax credits. In fact, >70% of the numerical increase in publicly insured adults pre- vs post-ACA reflected Medicaid expansion.

The findings in this population-based report contrast with clinic-based studies2,34 where BP control rates were lower in Black than White adults. In this study, Black adults had greater hypertension awareness and treatment rates than White adults, which offset lesser treatment effectiveness. In clinic-based settings where hypertension awareness and treatment rates are likely high across racial groups, lower BP control rates among Black than White adults on antihypertensive treatment (less treatment effectiveness) explain lower control rates in the former.2,34

Blood pressure control was similar for adults with and without healthcare insurance in 1988-1994.1 In this study, BP control rates for uninsured adults were lower than for those with public or private insurance from 2001 to 2023. Blood pressure control for uninsured adults in 2015-2023 was comparable to levels reported in 1988-1994.1 Lower BP control rates among uninsured than insured adults reflect lower rates of hypertension awareness and treatment from 2001 to 2023 and lower treatment effectiveness post-ACA (2015-2023). Demographic and risk factor changes pre- to post-ACA may confound control rate comparisons among the uninsured. Among the uninsured, age, sex, race/ethnicity, hypertension awareness, and treatment were similar pre- and post-ACA. However, chronic kidney disease, linked to uncontrolled and treatment-resistant hypertension,29 was more prevalent post- than pre-ACA.

Healthcare insurance was estimated to explain 16%-70% of healthcare disparities between underserved Black and Hispanic, compared to White adults.35 With reference to BP control, that percentage may be higher, although the explanation is unknown. Among possible explanations, most low-income adults are knowledgeable about the health risks of hypertension5,36 and may pursue public insurance to mitigate risk. The higher treatment rate for publicly insured than for higher-income privately insured adults in the current report is consistent with this notion. Alternatively, adults with public healthcare insurance are more likely to receive care in federally qualified healthcare centers than those with private insurance. Federally qualified health centers may provide more effective hypertension care than national averages at equivalent time points, especially in states with Medicaid expansion.7,10,11

Adverse social determinants, such as lower income, were similar among uninsured and publicly insured adults both pre- and post-ACA, although educational attainment was lower post-ACA in the uninsured. Hispanic adults were more prevalent among uninsured than publicly insured adults pre- and post-ACA. In other words, healthcare insurance for all remaining uninsured adults may not raise their hypertension control rates to levels observed in adults with public healthcare insurance. Additionally, the fully adjusted prevalence ratios for BP control pre- and post-ACA were consistently higher among those with Medicaid only and Medicaid with other public insurance, but not those with public insurance other than Medicaid, suggesting that differences in care between or characteristics of members enrolling in various public insurances may affect outcomes.

Antihypertensive therapy was less frequent in uninsured than insured adults. Yet, among adults who reported taking antihypertensive medications during the previous month, the uninsured reported taking a similar number of antihypertensive medication classes as the insured as noted previously.1 Even when reportedly taking similar numbers of antihypertensive medications, uninsured adults were less likely to attain BP control than insured adults post-ACA. Potential explanations include higher pre-treatment BP, lower adherence to antihypertensive medication, or more limited access to medication.34,37 Limited cost for effective generic antihypertensive medications and 340B pricing on proprietary drugs38,39 could mitigate the adverse effects of low-income on access to antihypertensive medications. In fact, prescription fills for amlodipine, lisinopril, and losartan in the United States increased as cost per fill declined.39

Blood pressure control was generally higher during the ACA transition than post-ACA, irrespective of insurance status. Previous studies also reported better BP control during than after the ACA transition.23,40 The decline in BP control after the ACA transition was largely attributed to a fall in care quality.23

Limitations of the current study are multiple. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey is a cross-sectional, single-time-point assessment of the US civilian population. Blood pressure exhibits inter-visit variability, which is missed by NHANES, and may misclassify hypertension status and control for some individuals. While NHANES reflects the US civilian population, the number of adults studied is limited. Confidence limits are often wide, especially in key subgroups, which limit the power to detect significant differences. To address this concern, we combined several NHANES cycles for the pre- and post-ACA periods. The multiple statistical comparisons within and between groups by insurance status may have led to statistically significant differences by chance. Another consideration is that NHANES does not provide data to address several potentially important social, environmental, political, and secular changes that could influence BP control. Our study did not address secular changes in BP control, which were reported previously,23,40–42 but focused on control rates by insurance status pre- and post-ACA.

Summary and conclusions

Since the ACA implementation, healthcare insurance has shifted proportionately from the uninsured and privately insured to the public sector. Despite doubling numbers of publicly insured adults with adverse social determinants post-ACA, BP control remains comparable in publicly and privately insured adults. Blood pressure control was higher in publicly than privately insured adults pre- and post-ACA after adjustment for sociodemographic confounders. Blood pressure control is much lower among uninsured adults who have adverse social determinants more closely aligned with the public than privately insured. Uninsured adults declined to proportions reported in 1988-1994 when control rates were similar in insured and uninsured adults. Despite declining numbers, BP control rates have not risen for the uninsured. One key to better BP control may be to provide healthcare insurance for uninsured adults.
