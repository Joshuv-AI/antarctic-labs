Conserved Influenza Epitopes Delivered via MHC-I–Functionalized Nanocarriers Induce Influenza-Specific CTL Responses
Authors: Sang-Hyun Kim, Bill Thaddeus Padasas, Dong-Su Kim, Doo-Jin Kim, Jeong-Ki Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeasonal influenza evades vaccine-induced immunity via ongoing antigenic drift, highlighting the critical necessity for universal vaccines that elicit broadly cross-reactive CD8+ T cell responses targeting conserved internal viral epitopes. In pursuit of this objective, we prepared biocompatible and biodegradable poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanocarriers (NCs) and functionalized them with MHC-I molecules. Among various murine and human epitopes tested, PB1703–711 and M158–66 showed the highest stabilization of H-2Kb and HLA-A2.1 molecules, respectively, thereby promoting sustained Ag presentation. These NCs exhibited specific peptide-loading onto MHC-I molecules while minimizing non-specific peptide surface binding. In vivo immunization of C57BL/6 mice with PB1703–711-loaded NCs elicited epitope-specific CD8+ T cell responses. Furthermore, the successful specific peptide-loading of HLA-A2.1-attached NCs highlighted the system’s human translational applicability. Ultimately, by combining effective Ag delivery with specific CTL induction, this designed NC platform provides a promising strategy for developing next-generation universal vaccines.Keywords: PLGA nanocarrier, Influenza virus, Peptides, Cytotoxic T lymphocytes, Universal influenza vaccine

INTRODUCTION

Seasonal influenza continues to represent a significant public health challenge, resulting in an estimated 3 to 5 million cases of severe illness and between 290,000 and 650,000 deaths globally each year (1,2). The primary agents responsible for these epidemics are influenza viruses, notably influenza A virus (IAV) and influenza B virus. Clinically, influenza infection is characterized by acute respiratory symptoms such as fever, cough, headache, myalgia, and sore throat, with extended complications occurring predominantly among vulnerable populations, including children and the elderly (3,4,5). The IAV genome consists of 8 segmented RNA strands that assemble into viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complexes, which comprise single-stranded RNA, polymerase subunits (PA, PB1, PB2), and nucleoprotein (NP). Beyond the vRNP components, the influenza virion contains the structural matrix protein (M1) and several non-structural proteins, all encapsulated within a lipid envelope embedded with hemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), and the M2 ion channel protein (6,7).

Current seasonal influenza vaccines primarily stimulate the production of Abs against highly variable regions of HA and NA (8). However, the frequent occurrence of antigenic drift enables viral evasion of vaccine-induced immunity, necessitating annual vaccine reformulation and often leading to suboptimal protective efficacy (9,10). To resolve these issues, the development of universal influenza vaccines targeting conserved viral epitopes has been proposed (11). Such vaccines are designed to elicit broadly cross-reactive immune responses by inducing neutralizing Abs against the extracellular domain of the M2 protein or the stalk region of HA, as well as by promoting CD8+ T cell responses against conserved internal viral Ags, including NP, M1, PA, PB1, and PB2 (12,13).

Among the strategies aimed at inducing CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity, peptide-based vaccines targeting conserved influenza epitopes represent a safe and precise approach to eliciting Ag-specific CD8+ T cell responses (14). These epitopes are readily synthesized, exhibit no oncogenic or hypersensitivity risks, and specifically activate epitope-directed CTLs in vivo (15,16). However, free peptide epitopes have a short biological half-life, limited immunogenicity, and must be presented by professional Ag-presenting cells (APCs) that express co-stimulatory molecules; presentation by non-APCs can trigger immune tolerance (17,18,19). To overcome these challenges, we adapted our previously established poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based nanocarriers (NCs) surface-functionalized with MHC-I molecules (20), expanding their application to the development of a novel influenza vaccine. PLGA is a biocompatible, biodegradable, and versatile platform that undergoes hydrolysis in vivo, yielding lactic and glycolic acids, and has been widely used in pharmaceutical formulations as a carrier for drugs and vaccine Ags (21,22,23,24,25,26,27). The immunogenic efficacy of PLGA NCs is significantly influenced by particle size; specifically, particles within the 500 nm to 2 μm range are effectively internalized by professional APCs through phagocytosis, whereas smaller particles (<200 nm) exhibit reduced recognition and limited cellular uptake (28,29,30). By formulating our PLGA NCs to target this optimal size range, we intrinsically promoted preferential uptake by APCs.

Furthermore, while conventionally used poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) serves as a stabilizing agent during NC preparation (31), we substituted PVA with poly(ethylene-alt-maleic anhydride) (PEMA) to produce carboxylated NCs. Crucially, this modification enabled the stable immobilization of MHC-I molecules onto the NC surface. This was achieved by utilizing mAbs against MHC-I molecules, conjugated via a carbodiimide-mediated coupling method (31,32), as anchors to secure the MHC-I complexes. By directly incorporating MHC-I molecules on the NC surface, this modification is designed to facilitate the efficient loading and delivery of influenza-derived peptide epitopes. In the present study, we evaluated the applicability of this platform for delivering influenza-derived epitopes. Our results indicate that the functionalized NCs can induce epitope-specific CTL responses in vivo, suggesting a scalable strategy toward the development of universal influenza vaccines.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice and cell lines

Female C57BL/6 mice (5–6 wk old) were purchased from Kosa Bio Inc. (Seongnam, Korea). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Korea University (KUIACUC-2023-0031) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations.

EG7-OVA cells, a mouse EL4 lymphoma cell line expressing ovalbumin (OVA, H-2b), and RMA-S cells, a mouse T cell lymphoma line derived from Rauscher leukemia virus-induced RBL-5 cells and deficient in Ag processing due to a TAP2 mutation, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA).

Peptides, Abs, and reagents

OVA257–264 (OVA257; SIINFEKL), NS1136–144 (NS1136; VIFDRLETL), PB1703–711 (PB1703; SSYRRPVGI), PB2496–504 (PB2496; VSIDRFLRV), and FITC-labeled PB1703 (FITC-PB1703) were synthesized by Peptron Co. Ltd. (Daejeon, Korea). Dipeptide glycyl-glutamic acid (GE) was from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). mAb specific to mouse H-2Kb (Kb; clone Y-3) was from BioXCell (West Lebanon, NH, USA). FITC-labeled mAb specific to Kb (clone AF6-88.5) was from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). Poly-IC was from InvivoGen (San Diego, CA, USA).

MHC-I surface stabilization assay

The stabilization of surface Kb molecules by binding with peptides was assessed using RMA-S cells, as previously described (33). RMA-S cells were plated at 2×105 cells/well, incubated overnight at 26°C with each peptide at various concentrations (0–100 µM) in serum-free DMEM, and then shifted to 37°C for an additional 5 h. After incubation, cells were washed with staining buffer, stained with FITC-labeled Kb-specific mAb, and analyzed by flow cytometry. To assess the time-dependent stability of peptide–Kb complexes, peptide-loaded cells were washed to remove unbound peptides and cultured with 5 μg/mL brefeldin A for 0, 3, 6, or 9 h at 26°C, followed by staining and flow cytometric analysis.

Preparation of carboxylated NCs

Carboxylated PLGA NCs were prepared using a solvent evaporation method, as described previously (20). Briefly, 4 ml of 5% PLGA (Evonik Industries, Essen, Germany) in ethyl acetate (oil phase) was mixed with 8 mL of 1% PEMA (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA, USA) aqueous solution and emulsified using a high-speed homogenizer (HG-15D; DAIHAN Scientific, Seoul, Korea) to form a water-in-oil emulsion. The emulsion was transferred into a solution containing 0.2% PEMA and 0.1% PVA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and stirred at room temperature for 18 h to allow NC solidification. The resulting carboxylated NCs were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended in 10 ml of sterile distilled water. The mean particle size and ζ-potential of the PLGA-NC were measured using a particle size analyzer (ELS-Z, Otsuka, Japan).

Isolation of membrane proteins containing MHC-I molecules

Membrane proteins containing Kb molecules were isolated from an EG7.OVA tumor mass, which serves as a high-yield source of Kb molecules, as described previously (20). Briefly, 1 g of tumor tissue was dissociated in 10 mL of extraction buffer (0.15 M NaCl, 0.05 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) supplemented with 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) using gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Following removal of cell debris and lipids, the supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 80,000 ×g for 45 min at 4°C. The resulting pellet was homogenized in 0.5 ml of extraction buffer using Tissue Grinder (SHS-30E; SciLab, Seoul, Korea). The homogenate was then mixed with 0.5 ml of solubilization buffer containing 2% n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside and 0.4% 3-([3-cholamidopropyl] dimethyl ammonio)-1-propanesulfonate hydrate (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was incubated for 90 min at 4°C to solubilize membrane proteins. After centrifugation at 17,000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C, the supernatant containing the membrane proteins was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a micro bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA).

Preparation of anti-MHC-I mAb-conjugated NCs and MHC-I-attached NCs

Anti-MHC-I mAbs were conjugated to the surface of carboxylated NCs using a modified carbodiimide coupling method, and the resulting mAb-conjugated NCs (NC-Ab) were subsequently attached with MHC-I molecules, as described previously (20). Briefly, 10 mg of carboxylated NCs was resuspended in 1.2 ml of 50 mM activation buffer and activated by adding 4 μl of 300 mM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 8 μl of 300 mM N-hydroxysuccinimide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. The EDC-activated NCs were washed twice with 50 mM activation buffer and then resuspended in 1 ml of the same buffer containing anti-mouse Kb mAb (600 μg/ml; clone Y-3), followed by incubation for 18 h at room temperature. To block non-specific conjugation, mAb-conjugated NCs were treated with 30 μl of ethanolamine (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min and washed twice with PBS. The mAb-conjugated NCs were then incubated with 5 mg of tumor-derived membrane proteins for 24 h at 4°C. The resulting Kb-attached NCs (NC-Kb) were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 1 ml of PBS.

Peptide exchange reaction on MHC-I-attached NCs

To replace native peptides bound to MHC-I molecules, 1 mg of NC-Kb was incubated with each peptide labeled with FITC in the absence or presence of 1 mM dipeptide GE for 18 h at 37°C. After incubation, the NCs were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 0.5 ml PBS. Peptide-loading efficiency was assessed by flow cytometry.

In vivo peptide-specific CTL activity

C57BL/6 mice were immunized intramuscularly with either 50 μg/mouse of PB1703 only or 0.4 mg/mouse of PB1703-pulsed NCs (NC-PB1) in the presence of poly-IC (50 μg/mouse) on days 0, 7, and 14. On day 21, PB1703-specific CTL activity was evaluated using an in vivo CTL assay, as described previously (20). In brief, splenocytes from naïve syngeneic mice were pulsed with 1 μM PB1703 for 1 h at 37°C, then labeled with a high concentration (25 μM) of CFSE to generate target cells. Control target cells consisted of syngeneic splenocytes labeled with a low concentration (5 μM) of CFSE without peptide pulsing. A 1:1 mixture (1×107 cells/mouse) of peptide-pulsed and unpulsed target cells was administered intravenously into the immunized mice. After 18 h, spleens were collected from recipient mice, and the relative frequencies of CFSEHigh (PB1703-pulsed) and CFSELow (unpulsed) target cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Specific cytotoxicity was determined by calculating the ratio of CFSELow to CFSEHigh cells.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Stability of surface peptide–MHC-I complexes

To identify a suitable peptide candidate for the NC vaccine, the peptides restricted by Kb or H-2Db (Db) molecules are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Previous studies have established that PB1703, among the Kb-restricted peptides, elicits PB1703-specific CTL responses, which are facilitated by dendritic cells, a representative professional APC, during infection with the A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1) (PR8) strain of IAV (34,35,36). In contrast to PB1703, peptide-specific CTL responses have not been reported for the other Kb-restricted peptides NS1136 and PB2496. These peptides were selected based on the highest predicted binding scores to MHC-I molecules, as determined by T cell epitope prediction algorithms available through the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource (37).

The stabilization of Kb molecules on TAP-deficient RMA-S cells induced by Kb-restricted peptides was assessed using flow cytometry. All tested peptides enhanced Kb surface expression in a concentration-dependent manner compared with the untreated control. The control peptide OVA257–264 (OVA257; SIINFEKL) induced a clear dose-dependent increase in Kb expression, thereby validating the assay (Fig. 1A). Among the influenza-derived Kb-restricted peptides tested, NS1136 exhibited only modest stabilization effects, which were apparent mainly at the highest concentration tested (Fig. 1B). In contrast, PB1703 demonstrated the most potent stabilizing effect, as indicated by a pronounced rightward shift in the Kb fluorescence histogram and the largest increase in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) change. Statistically significant stabilization was observed at intermediate to high peptide concentrations, with maximal effect at 100 µM (Fig. 1C). PB2496 also contributed to Kb stabilization, though to a lesser extent than PB1703, with significant increases primarily observed at concentrations of 50 µM and above (Fig. 1D).

RMA-S cells were incubated with (A) OVA257 (control peptide), (B) NS1136, (C) PB1703, or (D) PB2496 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM in serum-free medium at 26°C for peptide-loading. After shifting to 37°C and incubating for 5 h, the surface expression levels of Kb molecules were analyzed by flow cytometry following staining with FITC-labeled anti-Kb mAb. The binding of peptides restricted by Db molecules (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table 1), which are known to induce peptide-specific CTL responses, was similarly evaluated (34,35,36). NP55–63 (NP55; RLIQNSLTI) exhibited minimal stabilization effects on Db molecules (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, NP366–374 (NP366; ASNENMETM) demonstrated strong stabilization, which aligns with its established immunodominant status (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Additionally, PB1141–149 (PB1141; TALANTIEV) elicited moderate stabilization, suggesting that it is effectively presented on the cell surface, though with lower binding affinity than NP366 (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Given that the fluorescence intensities observed for Kb-restricted peptides were relatively higher than those for Db-restricted peptides, subsequent studies were primarily focused on the Kb-restricted epitopes. To evaluate the time-dependent stability of influenza virus-derived peptide–Kb complexes, Kb-restricted peptide-treated RMA-S cells were cultured with brefeldin A for 9 h. In the absence of peptide-loading, Kb molecules were unstable and rapidly declined within 3 h, reflecting the inherent instability of empty Kb molecules (Fig. 2A and E). Conversely, cells loaded with peptides exhibited enhanced stabilization of Kb molecules, with distinct differences among the peptides tested. NS1136 provided only transient stabilization, with a sharp decline evident from 3 h and substantial loss of Kb surface expression by 9 h (Fig. 2B and E). PB1703 conferred the most sustained stabilization, maintaining elevated Kb surface expression with only a gradual reduction in MFI throughout 9 h (Fig. 2C and E). This prolonged stabilization suggests a robust peptide–MHC interaction and an extended half-life of the PB1703–Kb complexes. PB2496 also stabilized Kb molecules, but surface expression was lower and decayed more rapidly at 9 h compared with PB1703, suggesting reduced persistence of PB2496–Kb complexes (Fig. 2D and E). The superior stability of PB1703–Kb complexes highlights its potential as a dominant epitope capable of sustaining Ag presentation and supporting prolonged CD8+ T cell responses during influenza infection.

Characterization and in vivo cytotoxicity of peptide-loaded NCs

Therefore, PB1703 was selected for optimization of the NC vaccine platform because it conferred the most robust stabilization of Kb molecules among the peptides tested. We next characterized the functional properties of PB1703-loaded NCs (NC-PB1). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the successful conjugation of both Abs and Kb molecules to NCs (Fig. 3A); a schematic illustration of the Kb-functionalized NC platform (NC-Kb) is shown in Fig. 3B. In our previous study on tumor vaccines, the mean diameter of NC-Kb was measured at 903.1±32.2 nm, with a ζ-potential of −18.68±3.64 mV. Relative to unmodified NCs, the diameter increased and the ζ-potential decreased, indicating effective surface conjugation of mAbs and Kb molecules (38). To evaluate the extent of PB1703 loading via peptide exchange on Kb molecules of NC-Kb, NC-Kb was incubated with FITC-PB1703. NC-Kb exhibited strong peptide-loading signals, while mAb-conjugated NCs (NC-Ab), which do not contain Kb molecules, showed minimal fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3C and D). This finding indicates that the presence of Kb molecules specifically facilitates peptide capture. Additionally, incubation of FITC-PB1703 with 1 mM dipeptide GE further enhanced PB1703 binding on NC-Kb relative to 0 mM GE (Fig. 3E). The dipeptides efficiently support peptide exchange on MHC-I molecules by promoting a peptide-receptive conformation that rapidly binds high-affinity peptides (32,39).

To examine the efficacy of NC-PB1 in mediating peptide-specific target cell killing, we first investigated the administration route using OVA257-pulsed NCs (NC-OVA), which have been previously demonstrated to elicit Ag-specific CTL responses (38). C57BL/6 mice were immunized either intravenously (i.v.) or intramuscularly (i.m.) on days 0, 7, and 14, and Ag-specific activity was assessed on day 21 (Supplementary Data 1). In vivo CTL assays showed that both i.v. and i.m. administration of NC-OVA induced robust OVA257-specific cytotoxic activity, without significant differences observed between the 2 groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To further validate these results, OVA257–Kb tetramer staining of peripheral blood CD8+ T cells was performed on day 21. The frequency of OVA257-specific CD8+ T cells was comparable between i.v. and i.m. immunized mice, thereby indicating that both routes effectively expanded Ag-specific T cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Based on these results, PB1703-specific CTL activity was assessed in vivo using the i.m. route, which is commonly employed for vaccine administration. C57BL/6 mice received i.m. immunizations with NC-PB1 formulations combined with poly-IC as an adjuvant on days 0, 7, and 14 (Fig. 4A). The in vivo CTL assay performed on day 21 showed that NC-PB1–vaccinated mice generated PB1703-specific CTL responses, with significantly enhanced lysis of PB1703-pulsed target cells relative to untreated controls (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these results establish NC-PB1 as an effective delivery platform capable of stabilizing influenza virus-derived epitopes through MHC-I molecules and eliciting significant Ag-specific CTL activity in vivo.

Translational applicability using HLA-A2.1 model

To explore the translational applicability of this NC platform, we assessed the binding affinity of the human influenza virus-derived epitope M158–66 (M158; GILGFVFTL) (Supplementary Table 2). This epitope is a highly conserved and immunodominant epitope restricted by HLA-A2.1 (40). Consistent with this, M158-specific CTL activity has been previously reported in transgenic mice immunized with the M158 peptide (41,42). For the binding assay (Supplementary Data 1), we used TAP-deficient T2 cells expressing HLA-A2.1, a representative human MHC-I molecule (43). After incubation, M158 elicited a pronounced, dose-dependent increase in HLA-A2.1 surface expression, indicating effective peptide binding and stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

HLA-A2.1 transgenic lung tissue was selected as the source for isolating membrane proteins containing HLA-A2.1 molecules. The anti-pan-HLA class I (HLA-I) mAb was chosen for the preparation of anti-HLA-A2.1 Ab-conjugated NCs due to its availability for bulk procurement and its cross-reactivity with other HLA-I alleles. We next optimized the assembly of HLA-A2.1–functionalized NCs (NC-A2.1). Carboxylated NCs were conjugated with anti-pan-HLA-I mAb and then incubated with membrane proteins containing HLA-A2.1 molecules (Supplementary Data 1). The successful conjugation of the Abs to the carboxylated NCs was confirmed, and the level of conjugated mAbs on NC-A2.1 was maintained, similar to NC-Ab (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The attachment of HLA-A2.1 molecules reached its maximum at membrane protein concentrations of 0.5 and 1 mg/ml, indicating an optimal concentration range for the efficient recruitment of HLA-A2.1 molecules onto NC-Ab (Supplementary Fig. 3C). The peptide-loading capacity of NC-A2.1 was assessed using FITC-labeled M158 (FITC-M158). The specific binding of FITC-M158 was highest in NC-A2.1 treated with 0.5 mg/ml of membrane proteins, whereas control NC-Ab exhibited negligible signal, confirming minimal non-specific binding of FITC-M158 on the NC surface (Supplementary Fig. 3D). These results show that the NC platform successfully supports specific peptide-loading onto human HLA-A2.1 molecules, suggesting that the Ag-specific CTL responses demonstrated in the murine model may potentially be translatable to human settings.

DISCUSSION

The influenza virus continually changes through antigenic drift and periodically undergoes antigenic shift in its surface glycoproteins, which makes current seasonal vaccines less effective than ideal and raises the risk of pandemic-related failures. Consequently, the focus of universal influenza vaccine development has moved toward highly conserved internal viral Ags, such as M1 and PB1, to trigger broadly cross-reactive CTL responses. In this study, we developed a PLGA NC platform functionalized with MHC-I molecules, demonstrating that it can efficiently load conserved immunodominant epitopes and induce significant Ag-specific CTL responses in vivo.

The efficacy of peptide-based vaccines is fundamentally dependent on the stability of the peptide-MHC-I complex, which governs both the duration of Ag presentation and the magnitude of the subsequent CD8+ T cell response (44). Among the murine epitopes tested, PB1703 demonstrated the most pronounced and sustained stabilization of Kb molecules on RMA-S cells. This strong stabilization profile is characteristic of an immunodominant epitope capable of maintaining prolonged Ag presentation, which is a critical factor for the regulation of CD8+ T cell responses during viral infection (45). Furthermore, the incorporation of the dipeptide GE significantly enhanced the loading efficiency of PB1703 onto the NC-Kb. Dipeptides act as molecular chaperones by stabilizing the peptide-receptive “open” conformation of MHC-I molecules, thereby reducing the thermodynamic activation energy required for the rapid exchange and binding of high-affinity exogenous peptides (46). Ultimately, this dipeptide-mediated loading strategy appears to enhance the density and stability of functional peptide-MHC-I complexes on the NC surface, potentially fulfilling a key requirement for the development of effective peptide-based vaccines.

A noteworthy feature of this PLGA-based NC platform is that the conjugation of Abs to the surface of NCs not only enables the capture of MHC-I molecules but may also enhance the immunological interaction between the NCs and professional APCs. When Abs are attached in an orientation that exposes their fragment crystallizable (Fc) region, this configuration can induce Fc receptor–mediated opsonization, thereby promoting the efficient phagocytic uptake of NCs by professional APCs (47). Crucially, the efficient cellular internalization of these NCs acts as a strong trigger that induces APC maturation, thereby providing the secondary signals required for effective CD8+ T cell priming. Indeed, in our previous study using the same NC platform as a cancer vaccine, we experimentally confirmed in vitro that the uptake of these NCs promotes the upregulation of endogenous co-stimulatory molecules—specifically CD80 and CD86—and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by APCs (38).

When comparing our MHC-I-functionalized NC strategy with conventional peptide-encapsulated nanovaccines, the fundamental mechanism of Ag presentation is likely similar, relying primarily on the internalization and cross-presentation by APCs. However, our platform has a clear manufacturing advantage. Conventional nanovaccines are often limited by low peptide encapsulation efficiency, which results in the permanent loss of costly unencapsulated peptides. Our system solves this problem with an external peptide exchange strategy. This external loading not only allows for accurate control over loaded peptide density but also enables the recovery and potential reuse of unbound peptides from the reaction mixture, providing meaningful economic and practical advantages for large-scale manufacturing. Nevertheless, to fully realize this economic benefit, additional optimization of the processes for peptide-loading, recovery, and recycling steps will still be required.

Beyond its use as a vaccine, this platform may also be useful as an in vitro tool for isolating Ag-specific CD8+ T cells. Because these NCs are relatively large (approximately 900 nm), they can easily precipitate under moderate centrifugation. As a result, engaged Ag-specific CD8+ T cells can be efficiently enriched using a simple centrifugation-based separation method, making the platform a promising experimental tool for studying T cell phenotypes and functions in various immunological settings.

While the platform can provide considerable potential as an experimental tool, its primary aim is directed toward clinical application. To validate the translational potential of this platform, we expanded our investigation into the human HLA-A*02:01 allele, focusing on the highly conserved M158 epitope. The M158 peptide exhibited remarkable dose- and time-dependent stabilization of HLA-A2.1 molecules, underscoring its strong immunodominance. The clinical relevance of targeting M158 is substantial; a recent study has demonstrated that M158-specific central memory CD8+ T cells (TCM) are established during early childhood and remain remarkably long-lived (41). By preserving key public TCR clonotypes throughout adulthood, these durable TCM populations provide a highly reliable immunological foundation for vaccine boosting strategies (48). The effective loading and stabilization of this epitope, which induces lifelong memory, allows our NC platform to serve as a promising strategy for universal pandemic preparedness. Notably, the conjugation of the NCs with cross-reactive pan-HLA-I Abs provides a flexible anchoring approach. This design enables the NCs to effectively bind diverse HLA-I alleles, including HLA-A, B, and C, regardless of whether they originate from recombinant sources, individual patients, or population-specific HLA-I pools. Consequently, this platform demonstrates considerable versatility, supporting its potential use in both personalized and broadly applicable vaccine development.

Expanding on the versatility of this platform, optimizing the route of administration represents a pivotal advancement for targeting respiratory pathogens. Although the present study successfully employed the clinically established i.m. route to induce systemic CTL responses, the development of universal vaccines against respiratory infections will require an emphasis on eliciting mucosal immunity. Protective immune responses against respiratory mucosal pathogens, such as the influenza virus, are most effectively and rapidly mediated by lung tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM), which act as crucial gatekeepers against infection (49). These CD8+ TRM cells, characterized by the expression of residency markers such as CD69 and CD103, reside permanently within the interstitial spaces and respiratory epithelium to sustain frontline memory, enabling immediate, localized cytotoxic activity and antiviral cytokine production upon viral re-exposure, independent of systemic recirculation (50,51). Current evidence indicates that conventional i.m. vaccination strategies are suboptimal for establishing these essential mucosal immune niches (52). Considering that our PLGA NCs have an average diameter of approximately 900 nm, an optimal aerodynamic size conducive to aerosol delivery (53), future research should investigate intranasal administration or a “prime-and-deploy” regimen, involving an i.m. prime followed by an intranasal boost (54,55). Such approaches could exploit the targeted delivery potential of our platform to generate durable, epitope-specific TRM populations directly at the primary site of influenza virus infection.

Despite the considerable translational potential and versatility of this platform, the inherent limitations of the present study must be objectively addressed. First, although our findings show that these nanovaccines can induce strong Ag-specific CTL responses in vivo, we did not directly evaluate their efficacy in conferring protection against viral infection. While neutralizing Abs mainly block viral entry, they alone often do not suffice to clear intracellular influenza infections. In this regard, CTL responses are crucial for controlling viral replication and supporting recovery (56). After influenza infection, pre-existing memory CTLs elicited by vaccination can recognize and eliminate virus-infected respiratory epithelial cells, thereby limiting viral dissemination (57). This cellular clearance mechanism has been shown to correlate with reduced lung viral titers and attenuated morbidity (58). Therefore, the Ag-specific CTL activity induced by our NC platform is expected to contribute to viral clearance and provide cross-reactive cellular immunity against viral variants. Future studies should use in vivo viral challenge models to directly evaluate the protective efficacy of this platform.

Second, unlike conventional long-peptide vaccines, which enable APCs to naturally process multiple epitopes and thereby accommodate a broad range of MHC-I haplotypes, the current platform is restricted to specific MHC-I alleles. Thus, a vaccine delivering a single MHC-I-restricted epitope would solely offer protection to individuals expressing the corresponding allele. To achieve broad global coverage, future clinical formulations of this NC platform will need a multiplexed design, simultaneously carrying multiple highly conserved internal epitopes (from M1, NP, PA, and PB1) that can bind to a wide range of common HLA supertypes. Furthermore, advancing toward practical clinical application will require thorough optimization of the purification protocols to isolate single MHC-I molecules. Additionally, careful bioprocess engineering for this NC platform will be necessary to overcome manufacturing scale-up challenges, precisely control Ab conjugation densities, and ensure batch-to-batch consistency.

Ultimately, by facilitating the delivery of conserved epitopes and the induction of specific T cell responses, this flexible NC platform provides a practical and scalable basis for developing next-generation universal influenza vaccines. Moreover, this approach may have broader applications beyond the influenza virus as a potential strategy against other highly mutable viral pathogens.

Stability of surface peptide–H-2Db complexes on RMA-S cells in the presence of influenza-derived peptides. Supplementary Figure 2

Injection route of OVA257-pulsed NCs does not affect OVA257-specific CTL responses. (A) OVA257-pulsed NCs were administered either i.v. or i.m. to C57BL/6 mice on days 0, 7, and 14. On day 21, OVA257-specific cytotoxic activity was assessed using an in vivo CTL assay. CFSE-labeled target cells were injected intravenously, and the specific cytotoxicity was determined 18 h later by flow cytometric analysis of splenocytes. (B) On day 21, whole blood samples collected from the facial vein were stained with OVA257–Kb tetramer and anti-mouse CD8α mAb. The frequency of OVA257-specific CD8+ T cells was analyzed by flow cytometry. Data are presented as the means ± SEM (n=5 mice/group).

Supplementary Figure 3

Characterization of HLA-A2.1-attached NCs (NC-A2.1) with or without M158 peptide. (A) T2 cells were incubated with M158 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM in serum-free medium containing 2 μg/ml β2-microglobulin at 37°C for peptide-loading. The surface expression levels of HLA-A2.1 molecules were analyzed by flow cytometry following staining with FITC-labeled anti-HLA-A2.1 mAb. (B) Conjugation of anti-HLA mAb was confirmed by flow cytometry following staining with FITC-labeled anti-mIgG mAb. (C) Conjugated mIgG level of NCs. NC-Ab was treated with HLA-A2.1 transgenic lung-derived membrane protein at indicated concentrations for 24 h. Attachment of HLA-A2.1 molecules was confirmed by flow cytometry following staining with FITC-labeled anti-HLA-A2.1 mAb. (D) Pulsed M158 peptide level of NCs. Attached HLA-A2.1 level of NCs. NC-A2.1 were incubated with FITC-labeled M158 at 37°C for 18 h, washed, and the peptide-loading efficiency was quantified by flow cytometry. Non-specific binding of NC-Ab to FITC-labeled anti-HLA-A2.1 mAb or FITC-labeled M158 was assessed as a control. Data are presented as means ± SEM (n=3).
