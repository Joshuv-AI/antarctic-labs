Long-time asymptotics of the autocorrelation function of the transverse Ising chain at the critical magnetic field Revisited
Noah Hout, Kenta Miyahara, Dustin Newland, Maxim Yattselev

Abstract
. Following [ 1 ] , we analyze the long-time asymptotics of the autocorrelation function of the transverse Ising chain at the critical magnetic field (a special case of the spin- 1 2 \frac{1}{2} XY model in a magnetic field) via the associated Riemann–Hilbert problem. We refine the original Deift-Zhou’s result by determining the subleading growing term in the asymptotics. Keywords : Quantum spin chain; correlation function; Riemann-Hilbert problem; steepest-descent method



1 Introduction and Main Results

Following the work of Deift-Zhou
[
1
]
, we consider the 1D transverse Ising model at the critical transverse magnetic field. In this formulation, spins on a 1D lattice interact via nearest-neighbor exchange interaction along the
x
-axis and are subjected to a transverse magnetic field along the
z
-axis. The Hamiltonian for this model is given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sigma_{l}^{x}\sigma_{l+1}^{x}+\sigma_{l}^{z}\right),

where
\sigma_{l}^{x}
and
\sigma_{l}^{z}
are the spin Pauli matrices at the
l
-th site of the 1D lattice. The main object of study in
[
1
]
is the autocorrelation function
\chi(t)
between the first spin component
\sigma^{x}_{0}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}t}\sigma^{x}_{0}(0)e^{\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}t}
after time
t
and the one at time
0
, i.e.,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}t}\sigma^{x}_{0}(0)e^{\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}t}\sigma^{x}_{0}(0)\right)}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}\right)},

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature. More precisely, the long-time behavior of
\chi(t)
is the main interest of Deift-Zhou. Prior to that, McCoy-Perk-Shrock
[
14
]
showed that
\chi(t)
is given by the Fredholm determinant

\chi(t)=e^{-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{det}\left(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{K}_{t}\right),

where
\mathcal{K}_{t}
is a trace-class operator acting on
L^{2}((-1,1))
with kernel

K_{t}(z,w)=g(z)\frac{\sin(\mathrm{i}t(z-w))}{\pi(z-w)},\quad z,w\in[-1,1].

Here, we set
g(z)=\tanh(\beta\sqrt{1-z^{2}})>0
on
(-1,1)
, which fixes the branch of the square root. As explained in
[
7
]
, using the general theory of integrable operators, see
[
8
,
13
,
2
]
for example, one can show that this operator is associated with the following Riemann-Hilbert problem.

Riemann-Hilbert Problem 1
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix function
\Psi(z,t)
such that

(1)

\Psi(z,t)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
;

(2)

one-sided traces
\Psi_{\pm}(z,t)
exist a.e. on
(-1,1)
, are bounded there, and satisfy

\displaystyle\Psi_{+}(z,t)=\Psi_{-}(z,t)G_{\Psi}(z,t),\quad z\in(-1,1),

where
(-1,1)
is oriented from
-1
to
1
and

(1.3)

\displaystyle G_{\Psi}(z,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1+g(z)&-g(z)e^{2zt}\\
g(z)e^{-2zt}&1-g(z)\end{pmatrix},\quad z\in(-1,1);

(3)

it holds that
\Psi(z,t)=I+z^{-1}\Psi_{1}(t)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-2})
as
z\to\infty
.

Throughout the article, when we discuss the Riemann-Hilbert problems, we may omit explicit mention of the
t
-dependence of functions if no confusion is likely. Let

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sigma(t):=\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\ln\chi(t).

The connection of the solution to RHP
1
and
\chi(t)
is given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(t)=-2[\Psi_{1}(t)]_{11}.

Thus, by solving RHP
1

\sigma^{\prime}(t)
from which Deift and Zhou obtained the following result.

Theorem (Deift-Zhou)
.

As
t\to\infty
, one has

(1.6)

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\exp\left[\frac{t}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\ln|\tanh\beta\zeta|d\zeta+\mathcal{O}(\ln t)\right].

Our goal is to find a more explicit expression of the error term
\mathcal{O}(\ln t)
in (
1.6
).
This is the problem posed by Percy Deift as one of the open problems at the conference
1

in Seoul to celebrate his 80th birthday.

This type of problem has attracted significant attention in related settings. Indeed, let us recall that the 1D transverse Ising model at the critical transverse magnetic field is a particular case of the spin-

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\left((1+\gamma)\sigma_{l}^{x}\sigma_{l+1}^{x}+(1-\gamma)\sigma_{l}^{y}\sigma_{l+1}^{y}\right)-h\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\sigma_{l}^{z},

Note that

Based on the sign of the chemical potential, there are three cases: (i) the case of negative chemical potential, (ii) the case of positive chemical potential in the spacelike direction, and (iii) the case of positive chemical potential in the timelike direction, see

Theorem 1

As

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\exp\left[\frac{t}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\ln|\tanh\beta\zeta|d\zeta+\frac{\ln^{2}(\tanh\beta)}{2\pi^{2}}\ln t+C+\mathcal{O}\big(t^{-1/4}\big)\right].

2 Deformations of RHP
1

In what follows,
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are the Pauli matrices

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

For our first step, we transfer RHP
1
to the unit circle
S^{1}=\{|w|=1\}
. To this end, let

\hat{\Psi}(w):=\Psi(z(w)),\quad z(w):=\frac{w-w^{-1}}{2\mathrm{i}},

which is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus S^{1}
. Since it must hold that
g(z(w))\geq 0
on
S^{1}
, we get that

(2.1)

\displaystyle g(z(w))=\begin{cases}h(w),&w\in S^{1}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(w)\geq 0\},\\
-h(w),&w\in S^{1}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(w)\leq 0\},\end{cases}

where

(2.2)

\displaystyle h(w):=\tanh\left(\beta\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}\right).

As standard, we orient
S^{1}
counter-clockwise. Notice that
z(w)
maps
S^{1}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(w)\leq 0\}
into
[-1,1]
, but oriented from
1
to
-1
. Thus, the jump matrix of
\hat{\Psi}(w)
satisfies

(2.3)

\displaystyle G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w)

\displaystyle:=\begin{Bmatrix}G_{\Psi}(z(w)),&w\in S^{1}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(w)\geq 0\}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
G_{\Psi}(z(w))^{-1},&w\in S^{1}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(w)\leq 0\}\end{Bmatrix}

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1+h(w)&-h(w)e^{-\mathrm{i}t(w-w^{-1})}\\
h(w)e^{\mathrm{i}t(w-w^{-1})}&1-h(w)\end{pmatrix},\quad w\in S^{1}.

Therefore,
\hat{\Psi}(w)
solves the following Riemann-Hilbert problem.

Riemann-Hilbert Problem 2
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix function
\hat{\Psi}(w,t)
such that

(1)

\hat{\Psi}(w,t)
is analytic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S^{1}
;

(2)

one-sided traces
\hat{\Psi}_{\pm}(w,t)
exist a.e. on
S^{1}
, are bounded there, and satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{+}(w,t)=\hat{\Psi}_{-}(w,t)G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w,t),\quad w\in S^{1},

where
G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w,t)
is given by (
2.4
);

(3)

it holds that
\hat{\Psi}(w,t)=I+2\mathrm{i}w^{-1}\Psi_{1}(t)+\mathcal{O}(w^{-2})
as
w\to\infty
.

Let us point out that RHP
1
and RHP
2
are simultaneously solvable and the solutions are related via
\hat{\Psi}(w)=\Psi(z(w))
. One direction is obvious by construction. In the other direction, given a solution of RHP
2
, one needs to observe that it must satisfy

\hat{\Psi}(w)=\hat{\Psi}(-1/w)

since
G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w)=G_{\hat{\Psi}}(-1/w)
as
h(w)=-h(-1/w)
, see
[
1
, Lemma 2.17]
(the ratio of these two functions is analytic across
S^{1}
and is equal to
I
at infinity; the claim then follows from Liouville’s theorem). This, in turn, allows one to define
\Psi(z)
out of
\hat{\Psi}(w)
. In a similar vein, observe that
G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w)=\sigma_{1}G_{\hat{\Psi}}(-w)\sigma_{1}
, which then induces the relation

(2.5)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(w)=\sigma_{1}\hat{\Psi}(-w)\sigma_{1},\quad w\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S^{1}.

Next, we make the diagonal entries of jump matrix to be oscillatory by defining

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(w):=\begin{cases}\hat{\Psi}(w)e^{\mathrm{i}tw^{-1}\sigma_{3}},&|w|>1,\\
\hat{\Psi}(w)e^{-\mathrm{i}tw\sigma_{3}},&|w|<1.\end{cases}

Then, the jump matrix for
\Phi(w)
is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle G_{\Phi}(w)

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathrm{i}tw^{-1}\sigma_{3}}G_{\hat{\Psi}}(w)e^{-\mathrm{i}tw\sigma_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}(1+h(w))e^{-t\varphi(w)}&-h(w)\\
h(w)&(1-h(w))e^{t\varphi(w)}\end{pmatrix},

where
\varphi(w)
is purely imaginary on
S^{1}
and is given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle\varphi(w):=\mathrm{i}(w+w^{-1}).

Furthermore, RHP
2
(3) becomes

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Phi(w,t)&=\left(I+\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{w}\Psi_{1}(t)+\mathcal{O}\left(w^{-2}\right)\right)\left(I+\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{w}\sigma_{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(w^{-2}\right)\right)\\
&=I+w^{-1}\left(2\mathrm{i}\Psi_{1}(t)+\mathrm{i}t\sigma_{3}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(w^{-2}\right),\quad w\to\infty.\end{aligned}

Once one has an oscillatory jump matrix, the next standard step in non-linear steepest descent analysis is to open the lenses, see (
2.13
). However, one technical issue arises from the fact that
h(w)
vanishes on the imaginary axis including at
\pm\mathrm{i}\in S^{1}
, which complicates the desired factorization. This leads us to deform the jump contour
S^{1}
away from
\pm\mathrm{i}
. To this end, we introduce some notation. Let

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{r}(a):=\{w\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|w-a|<r\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{D}:=\mathbb{D}_{1}(0).

Also, we observe that
G_{\Phi}^{-1}(w)
is analytic near
S^{1}
. Then, for
\rho\in(0,1)
sufficiently small so that the disks
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}(\pm\mathrm{i})
do not contain other zeros of

besides
\pm\mathrm{i}
, we define
\tilde{\Phi}(w)
by

(2.11)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(w):=\begin{cases}\Phi(w)G_{\Phi}^{-1}(w),&w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}(\pm\mathrm{i})\cap\mathbb{D},\\
\Phi(w),&\text{elsewhere}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle G_{\tilde{\Phi}}(w,t)=\begin{pmatrix}(1+h(w))e^{-t\varphi(w)}&-h(w)\\

Clearly, RHP

Next, we perform the Bruhat decomposition of

. Namely, it holds that

(2.13)

\displaystyle G_{\tilde{\Phi}}(w,t)=L_{ext}(w,t)P^{+}(w)L_{int}(w,t)=U_{ext}(w,t)P^{-}(w)U_{int}(w,t),

where

(2.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{ext}(w,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{h(w)-1}{h(w)}e^{t\varphi(w)}&1\end{pmatrix},&\quad L_{int}(w,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{h(w)+1}{-h(w)}e^{-t\varphi(w)}&1\end{pmatrix},\\
P^{+}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-h(w)\\
\frac{1}{h(w)}&0\end{pmatrix},&\quad P^{-}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{1}{h(w)}\\
h(w)&0\end{pmatrix},\\
U_{ext}(w,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{h(w)+1}{h(w)}e^{-t\varphi(w)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},&\quad U_{int}(w,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{h(w)-1}{-h(w)}e^{t\varphi(w)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}

It readily follows from the explicit expression (

\begin{cases}\mathrm{Re}(\varphi(w))>0,&\{|w|<1~\&~\mathrm{Im}(w)>0\}\cap\{|w|>1~\&~\mathrm{Im}(w)<0\},\\
\mathrm{Re}(\varphi(w))<0,&\{|w|<1~\&~\mathrm{Im}(w)<0\}\cap\{|w|>1~\&~\mathrm{Im}(w)>0\}.\end{cases}

\Gamma_{gl}^{+}:=\Gamma_{gl}\cap\{\mathrm{Im}(w)\geq 0\}

2.13
) around
\Gamma_{gl}^{-}:=\Gamma_{gl}\cap\{\mathrm{Im}(w)\leq 0\}
