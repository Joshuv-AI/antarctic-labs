Broadband Electromagnetic Field Prediction at Unseen Wavelengths via Physics‐Guided Neural Operators
Authors: Joonhyuk Seo, Chanik Kang, Dongjin Seo, Haejun Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurrogate solvers that deliver full‐wave accuracy across broad spectral ranges can unlock the rapid design and analysis of nanophotonic devices. We present a physics‐guided neural‐operator surrogate solver that predicts electromagnetic field distributions throughout the visible band from a permittivity map and a query wavelength, including wavelengths unseen during training. Our approach is built on two key ideas: (i) spectral consistency, which formalizes the intrinsic relationship between wavelength‐dependent field variations and spatial frequency, and (ii) a conditional embedding framework that comprises a refined wave prior. We validate the approach on three representative nanophotonic platforms—a single‐layer metasurface (metalenses), a five‐layer volumetric metasurface (spectrum‐splitters), and a freeform waveguide for photonic integrated circuits—achieving full‐wave fidelity while reducing mean field‐prediction error relative to prior operator‐learning baselines by up to 80.3% (single‐layer), 56.8% (multilayer), and 32.3% (waveguide). The solver remains compact (0.43 M parameters; 86.9% fewer than FNO) and accelerates simulation by ∼42× compared with a finite‐difference frequency‐domain solver while yielding robust broadband generalization. By providing continuous‐wavelength field predictions with full‐wave accuracy, this surrogate solver eliminates a key bottleneck in modeling and inverse design of metalenses, spectrum splitters, and emerging large‐area meta‐optics, offering a drop‐in engine for system‐level co‐optimization in nanophotonics.

Introduction

Recent advances at the intersection of deep learning (DL) and photonics have led to significant breakthroughs across various domains, including the inverse design of photonic structures [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], high‐resolution imaging and computational microscopy [6, 7, 8], optical signal processing and communication systems [9, 10, 11], and the realization of optical neural networks [12, 13, 14]. Specifically, DL‐based approaches have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in rapidly predicting electromagnetic phenomena traditionally analyzed through numerical methods [2, 15, 16, 17]. By training neural networks on datasets derived from simulations or experiments, DL approaches achieve orders‐of‐magnitude acceleration in predicting electromagnetic fields, device performance, and structural optimizations, all while maintaining accuracy comparable to traditional numerical solvers [2, 15, 16, 17].

One important application of DL in photonics is the development of surrogate solvers that approximate electromagnetic simulations. These surrogate solvers predominantly utilize deep neural networks due to their rapid inference and notable learning capability. Traditional data‐driven neural network‐based surrogate solvers [15, 16, 18, 19, 20] learn end‐to‐end mappings from structure/excitation to fields or responses, approximating the input–output operator of Maxwell's equations. However, these approaches frequently encounter limitations in accurately capturing the underlying physical characteristics of Maxwell's equations [2].

To address these shortcomings, physics‐informed neural networks (PINNs) [21, 22, 23] and physics‐regularized learning methods [2, 24] have been widely explored. PINNs incorporate Maxwell's equations into the training objective by penalizing PDE residuals and boundary/source conditions, enabling training without labeled simulation data. Physics‐regularized methods, by contrast, augment supervised/data losses with physics‐based penalties, reducing but not eliminating the need for labeled data. Despite these advantages, standard PINNs are largely instance‐specific: unless the network is explicitly conditioned on simulation parameters (e.g., wavelength and relative permittivity ϵr) and trained across their ranges, they typically require retraining for each parameter setting. Even with parameter conditioning, accuracy often degrades at parameter values not covered during training, leading to reduced field‐prediction fidelity on unseen simulation settings.

Recently, surrogate solvers utilizing neural operators have gained attention, as these methods effectively learn mappings between infinite‐dimensional function spaces, reflecting the intrinsic characteristics of partial differential equations (PDEs) [25, 26]. Notable progress in operator learning includes transformer‐based neural operators [27, 28, 29, 30], convolutional neural operators [31], and Fourier neural operators (FNO) [17, 26, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Among these, FNO has demonstrated strong inference performance across various electromagnetic applications [17, 36, 37], making it particularly promising for accelerating electromagnetic simulations.

Nevertheless, recent surrogate solver studies, including FNO‐based methods, exhibit limited prediction accuracy under the untrained simulation conditions, thereby failing to demonstrate the generalization capability of these models. The notable decline in predictive accuracy at intermediate wavelengths between discretely sampled training points raises fundamental concerns about whether these models adequately capture the underlying physics governing electromagnetic phenomena.

In this work, we present a surrogate solver that delivers highly accurate, full‐wave solutions of Maxwell's equations across a continuous, broadband spectrum, even when training data are available only at sparsely sampled wavelengths (Figure 1). Our model maintains robust accuracy on untrained intermediate wavelengths, overcoming the generalization limits of existing surrogate solvers. Moreover, by leveraging the intrinsic scale invariance of Maxwell's equations [38], our demonstrations satisfy the solutions of Maxwell's equations at any target frequency band by multiplying a scaling factor.

Wave Prior

As illustrated in Figure 3, the polarization‐dependent wave prior W=Wx,Wy is a synthetic wave pattern derived from homogeneous‐medium solutions of the wave equation. It is defined as Wx=expi2πλϵrx1⊤Δℓx,Wy=expi2πλϵr1y⊤Δℓy, where ϵr∈CH×W is the spatial map of relative permittivity, λ is the wavelength, Δℓx and Δℓy are the grid spacings, x and y denote the coordinate index vectors along x and y, and 1 is a vector of ones used to tile coordinates over the 2D grid. Because it originates from a homogeneous‐medium solution, W reproduces the wavelength‐dependent oscillation patterns observed in simulator‐generated fields within uniform regions.

TM and TE wave priors. The TM wave prior Wx=ej2πεrλx1TΔlx incorporates the spatially varying relative permittivity εr; the pronounced vertical seam marks interfaces where εr changes and the normal electric field is discontinuous. In contrast, the TE wave prior W˜x=ej2πλx1TΔlx omits the εr factor, yielding a smooth, plane‐wave‐like pattern.

At material interfaces, the fields satisfy electromagnetic boundary conditions. For transverse‐electric (TE) polarization, the tangential electric field is continuous, Et,1=Et,2. For transverse‐magnetic (TM) polarization, the normal component of the electric displacement is continuous, ϵ1En,1=ϵ2En,2, where ϵ1 and ϵ2 are the relative permittivities of the adjoining media.

However, the wave prior used in this work is derived solely from the macroscopic permittivity map. It thus does not account for other complex electromagnetic phenomena in sub‐wavelength structures, such as wavelength‐dependent resonances and evanescent fields. Consequently, it exhibits sharp and physically implausible features in regions where the permittivity changes.

Spectral Consistency

As discussed earlier, complex optical effects such as scattering and resonance induce nonlinear, wavelength‐dependent variations in electric field profiles, which can impede accurate predictions at wavelengths not encountered during training. To address this challenge, the model must encode the relevant physics at arbitrary wavelengths, including unseen ones, by (i) constructing conditional embeddings that reliably capture wavelength‐dependent features and (ii) designing conditioning mechanisms that effectively integrate these embeddings throughout the network.

As illustrated in Figure 4, shorter wavelengths generally yield field patterns with higher spatial frequencies, whereas longer wavelengths produce sparser, lower‐frequency structures. This inverse relationship is inherent to solutions of Maxwell's equations and follows from ν=1/λ, where λ and ν denote the wavelength and spatial frequency, respectively [40]. We define spectral consistency as the requirement that wavelength‐dependent conditional embeddings reflect this inverse relation and that their spatial‐frequency content aligns with that of the desired electromagnetic‐field distributions. Embeddings that satisfy spectral consistency provide an informed prior on the expected field complexity at target wavelengths, including those absent from the training set, thereby facilitating robust broadband generalization in surrogate models.

Spectral consistency. Across wavelengths, the latent wave prior (top) and the corresponding predicted electric‐field distributions (bottom) exhibit similar spatial‐frequency content, indicating that their spectra remain aligned under wavelength variation.

In this setting, conditional information that satisfies spectral consistency encodes the expected field complexity and spatial‐frequency distribution at target wavelengths, including those not seen during training. Enforcing spectral consistency, therefore, substantially enhances the surrogate model's broadband generalization.

Result and Discussion

In the following, we compare the field prediction performance for the continuous‐spectrum interpolation within the targeted bandwidth, including untrained intermediate wavelengths, in various simulation environments. We select three representative optical structures to investigate the generalization performance of our model under diverse electromagnetic phenomena.

Single Layer Metasurface: Metalens

Metasurfaces enable precise wavefront engineering by imposing arbitrary, spatially varying phases and amplitude profiles on transmitted or reflected waves. This versatility has been exploited in a wide range of applications, including beam steering [41, 42, 43], high‐resolution imaging [6, 7, 8], holography [44, 45, 46], vortex beam generation [47, 48], and communications [9, 10, 11]. Owing to their subwavelength thickness, metasurfaces provide a compact alternative to bulky optics and can be manufactured at scale using CMOS‐compatible lithographic processes [49].

Meta‐atoms in metasurfaces are individual scatterers and resonators that produce a prescribed macroscopic wavefront. Due to the resonance property of the meta‐atoms, the meta‐atoms project Maxwell's solutions nonlinearly over the varying wavelengths. This hinders large‐scale metasurface simulations; therefore, most of the metasurface studies assume a local periodic assumption for domain decomposition. This assumption often greatly degrades metasurface efficiency when the required phase and amplitude vary rapidly over the design region [50]. Therefore, it is very crucial to develop full‐scale metasurface simulators. Here, we apply our model to predict broadband solutions of Maxwell's equations in a single metasurface example, which can be applied to a metalens design.

Metasurface unit cells, often referred to as meta‐atoms, serve as subwavelength scatterers and resonators that collectively synthesize a prescribed macroscopic wavefront. Because each meta‐atom's resonant response varies nonlinearly with wavelength, simulating an entire metasurface at full scale becomes computationally expensive. To mitigate this, most designs invoke a local periodicity assumption for domain decomposition [51], but this approximation may not hold whenever the required phase and amplitude profiles change rapidly across the surface [50]. Such limitations impose the necessity for full‐scale metasurface simulators. Here, we apply our broadband surrogate model to predict full‐wave Maxwell solutions for a single‐layer metasurface [52, 53], illustrating its potential applicability to metalens design.

Multi‐Layer Metasurface: Spectrum Splitter

Although metasurfaces offer arbitrary wavefront control, their meta‐atoms exhibit inherently frequency‐dependent resonances that reduce efficiency outside their design wavelength [57]. This bandwidth limitation restricts critical imaging applications, such as achromatic metalenses [50] and spectral beam splitters [58], to narrow operating ranges. One promising solution is a volumetric, multilayer metasurface architecture, where stacking successive patterned layers increases the effective design volume [59, 60], enabling tailored phase and amplitude responses over a wider spectrum and bringing performance closer to that of conventional bulky optics.

Although multilayer metasurfaces remain uncommon in the infrared and visible regimes, primarily due to the fabrication complexity of volumetric architectures, recent advances in two‐photon polymerization lithography [61, 62] have enabled their realization. To demonstrate the versatility of our approach, we apply our broadband surrogate model to a representative multilayer metasurface, thereby validating its applicability to complex optical structures.

Freeform Waveguide: Photonic‐Integrated Circuit

Integrated photonics [66, 67] has emerged as a leading platform for next‐generation high‐performance computing, leveraging light's intrinsic advantages, low latency, immense bandwidth, and multiplexing capabilities. Advances in photonic integrated circuits (PICs), which integrate a diverse array of optoelectronic components on a single chip, have paved the way for photonic–electronic co‐integrated systems. These hybrid circuits hold great promise as hardware accelerators for both conventional high‐performance computing and emerging AI workloads. Recent advances in photonic integrated circuits have demonstrated on‐chip optical tensor convolution [14, 68], enabling fully optical neural networks that can dramatically accelerate convolutional operations.

Adjoint‐based inverse design has become one of the prevailing standards in PIC optimization because it efficiently computes gradients of performance metrics. However, each optimization step still requires full‐wave Maxwell solutions, so a single device often demands hundreds of electromagnetic simulations. As a result, adjoint methods have been largely confined to individual components of PICs (e.g., wavelength demultiplexers [66], mode converters [69, 70], or grating couplers [71, 72]). Here, we leverage our broadband surrogate model to predict the field profile of the freeform waveguide, achieving grid‐size precision while significantly reducing the computational cost.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although the proposed physics‐guided neural operator demonstrates robust performance and a highly compact architecture across diverse nanophotonic platforms, certain limitations present clear opportunities for future research. A primary simplification in our current baseline test cases is the assumption of constant, nondispersive refractive indices. In physical broadband applications, material dispersion and loss are dominant effects that dictate the lineshape and spectral position of optical resonances. Although our extended analyses indicate that the model's predictive capability remains stable across a wide range of arbitrary permittivities, incorporating wavelength‐dependent complex refractive indices into the conditional embedding is an essential next step.

Furthermore, it is important to clarify that the current study demonstrates high‐fidelity continuous‐spectrum interpolation within a targeted operational bandwidth (400–700 nm), rather than unbounded spectral extrapolation outside the training range. Although interpolating across this wide fractional bandwidth is a substantial achievement, predicting electromagnetic phenomena entirely outside the trained domain remains an active challenge for data‐driven solvers. Nevertheless, by leveraging the intrinsic scale invariance of Maxwell's equations, the interpolative capabilities demonstrated here can be mathematically mapped to different physical bandwidths using scaling factors, thereby broadening the model's applicability without requiring direct spectral extrapolation.

Another important consideration is the training data sampling strategy. The current model relies predominantly on fixed uniform spectral sampling (20 nm gaps) to systematically validate the architectural contributions of the proposed physics‐guided priors. However, the ability to perfectly resolve highly abrupt spectral features, such as sharp resonances, is intrinsically tied to this sampling distribution. Future studies investigate optimal data acquisition strategies, including sparse, nonuniform, random, or Chebyshev sampling, to determine the most data‐efficient methods for capturing complex spectral landscapes while avoiding the harmonic blind spots inherent to uniform grids [73].

Finally, although the proposed model exhibits orders‐of‐magnitude acceleration compared to single‐instance FDFD calculations, a more comprehensive computational benchmark is necessary for a holistic assessment. In practical broadband nanophotonics design, conventional workflows often utilize single‐shot FDTD simulations or highly parallelized FDFD runs distributed across multi‐node clusters. Future work will benchmark the proposed neural operator against these massively parallel and inherently broadband numerical environments to definitively quantify its computational advantages in true industrial and academic workflows.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present an electromagnetic surrogate solver that generates highly accurate, full‐wave solutions to Maxwell's equations over a continuous, broadband spectrum, even when the training set contains only sparsely sampled wavelengths. Our model inherently maintains strong predictive accuracy at intermediate wavelengths not seen during training, thereby overcoming the generalization limitations of existing surrogate solvers in electromagnetism. Moreover, by exploiting the intrinsic scale invariance of Maxwell's equations [38], the solutions obtained at one frequency can be uniformly extended to any other target frequency band through simple multiplication by an appropriate scaling factor.

To ensure broad‐spectrum generalizability, we introduce two foundational concepts. First, spectral consistency, which formalizes the inherent link between wavelength‐dependent field variations and spatial frequency as dictated by Maxwell's equations. Second, a conditional embedding framework supported by an efficient conditioning mechanism. Concretely, we use the polarization‐dependent wave prior and a wave‐informed element‐wise multiplicative encoding, each explicitly crafted to enforce spectral consistency. By uniting these components, our solver produces reliable, high‐fidelity predictions at wavelengths beyond those seen during training, thereby overcoming the shortcomings of prior surrogate approaches and markedly advancing the generalization capabilities of deep‐learning‐based electromagnetic simulators. We evaluate our surrogate solver on three representative structures, namely a single layer metasurface, a multilayer metasurface, and a freeform waveguide, each with distinct operating mechanisms and geometric complexity. Across their target bandwidths, our model reduces average electric‐field prediction error relative to the state of the art [17] by 80.3%, 56.8%, and 32.3% for three examples, and lowers model complexity by 86.9% compared with FNO2d [32]. Extension of our framework to macroscopic length scales is attainable through integration with emerging fast‐solver techniques [74, 75, 76]. Although enlarging the simulation domain naturally raises concerns about model complexity and training efficiency, contemporary deep‐learning architectures comprising hundreds of millions—or even billions—of parameters can be trained effectively on modern hardware. Consequently, developing a large‐scale, three‐dimensional extension of our surrogate solver represents a significant advance toward replacing conventional numerical methods for solving Maxwell's equations.
