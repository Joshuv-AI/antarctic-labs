Project Overview
Implementing 230Th-234U radiochronometry of environmental uranium particles by large geometry
secondary ion mass spectrometry (LG-SIMS) requires assessment, validation, and technical support
before safeguards conclusions can be drawn from the information. This project investigates the most
challenging aspects of LG-SIMS particle radiochronometry 230Th-234U measurements through a
collaboration between LANL and NIST, to provide best practices and procedures for determining high-
quality ages with optimized uncertainties. This includes exploration of reducing detector backgrounds,
investigating the best ways to report uncertainties, and establishing recommended instrument setups.

In Quarter 2 of FY26 LANL determined the abundance sensitivity factor behavior related to peak tailing
signal, as part of the “Best Practices For Reporting Uncertainties” task of the Project Work Plan. Below is
a summary of the efforts

1. Abundance Sensitivity Determination (Best Practices for Reporting Uncertainties;
Deliverable Due Date: 05/29/2026; Status: In Progress)
Methods: LANL performed 33 consumption analyses of CRM U-005 particles via monocollection and
peak hopping the magnet, counting half-masses from 229.5 to 237.5. The signal from U-234 was counted
and the equivalent signals from U-235, U-236, and U-238 were calculated using the certificate reference
isotope compositions of CRM U-005 (Meinke, 1969). Each analysis consisted of 15 cycles of the
following: each half-mass and U-234 was counted for 5 seconds per cycle, with wait times of 2 seconds
at mass 229.5 and 1 second for all other masses. A mean dark noise detector background of 8.33 × 10−4
counts per second was applied, calculated from overnight and weekend measurements from January 25,
2026 to February 12, 2026, covering the time span of sample characterization. A 30% relative uncertainty
of the dark noise was applied based on long term measurements (see also: Figure 9 from Tenner et al.,
2026). The signals at each half-mass and U-234 from all analyses were aggregated, and the Mid68 and 95
percent confidence interval upper and lower limits were calculated following methods from Groopman
et al. (2025); the uncertainties of the aggregated backgrounds were propagated into the 95% confidence
intervals. The expanded counting statistical uncertainty of the equivalent U-238 counts was also
propagated when calculating the abundance sensitivity ratio (defined as the tail counts divided by the
equivalent U-238 counts).

 As CRM U-005 is depleted uranium, the signals measured at the half-masses predominantly
represent the tailing from the peak of U-238. However, CRM U-005 contains small but appreciable
amounts of U-234 (0.00218 atom percent), U-235 (0.4895 atom percent), and U-236 (0.00466 atom
percent) (Meinke, 1969). To account for their tailing contributions we did the following:

 • one half-mass before the peak of interest: the signal at mass 237.5 is assumed to represent only
 the low-mass tail from U-238. Therefore, the ratio of 237.5 to U-238 signal represents the
 abundance sensitivity one half-mass before the peak of interest.
 • 1.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal at mass 236.5 is assumed to be represent only
 the low-mass tail from U-238. Therefore, the ratio of 236.5 to U-238 signal represents the
 abundance sensitivity 1.5 masses before the peak of interest.

• 2.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 235.5 is assumed to have
 contributions from the low-mass tails of U-236 and U-238 (we assume the high-mass tail signal
 from U-235 is negligible). As such we multiply the U-236 signal by the 0.5-mass abundance
 sensitivity factor (calculated from the ratio of 237.5 / U-238 signal) and subtract its contribution
 from the raw counts to generate a corrected abundance sensitivity 2.5 masses before the peak
 of interest.
• 3.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 234.5 has is assumed to have
 contributions from the low-mass tails of U-235, U-236, and U-238 (contributions from the high
 mass tail of U-234 is considered negligible). To account for this we multiply the U-235 signal by
 the 0.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-236 signal by the 1.5-mass
 abundance sensitivity factor. We then subtract their summed contributions from the raw counts
 to generate a corrected abundance sensitivity factor 3.5 masses before the peak of interest.
• 4.5 masses from the peak of interest: the signal from mass 233.5 is assumed to have

 relevant high-mass tails are considered negligible). As such we multiply the U-234 signal by the
 0.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-235 signal by the 1.5-mass abundance
 sensitivity factor, and we multiply the U-236 signal by the corrected 2.5-mass abundance
 sensitivity factor. We subtract their summed contributions from the raw counts to generate a
 corrected abundance sensitivity factor 4.5 masses before the peak of interest.
• 5.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 232.5 is assumed to have

 relevant high-mass tails are considered negligible). As such we multiply the U-234 signal by the
 1.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-235 signal by the corrected 2.5-mass
 abundance sensitivity factor, and we multiply the U-236 signal by the corrected 3.5-mass
 abundance sensitivity factor. We subtract their summed contributions from the raw counts to
 generate a corrected abundance sensitivity factor 5.5 masses before the peak of interest.
• 6.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 231.5 is assumed to have

 relevant high-mass tails are considered negligible). Therefore, we multiply the U-234 signal by
 the corrected 2.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-235 signal by the
 corrected 3.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, and we multiply the U-236 signal by the
 corrected 4.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor. We subtract their summed contributions from
 the raw counts to generate a corrected abundance sensitivity factor 6.5 masses before the peak
 of interest.
• 7.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 230.5 is assumed to have

 relevant high-mass tails are considered negligible). As such we multiply the U-234 signal by the
 corrected 3.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-235 signal by the corrected
 4.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, and we multiply the U-236 signal by the corrected 5.5-
 mass abundance sensitivity factor. We subtract their summed contributions from the raw counts
 to generate a corrected abundance sensitivity factor 7.5 masses before the peak of interest.
• 8.5 masses before the peak of interest: the signal from mass 229.5 is assumed to have

 relevant high-mass tails are considered negligible). Therefore, we multiply the U-234 signal by

 the corrected 4.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, we multiply the U-235 signal by the
 corrected 5.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor, and we multiply the U-236 signal by the
 corrected 6.5-mass abundance sensitivity factor. We subtract their summed contributions from
 the raw counts to generate a corrected abundance sensitivity factor 8.5 masses before the peak
 of interest.

236, and U-238, as well as the aggregated detector background and uncertainties. Table 2 lists the raw
and corrected signals measured at the half-masses, the corresponding mid68 and propagated 95CI count
values, and the abundance sensitivities and uncertainties for each half-mass before the peak of interest
(here compared to the equivalent U-238 signal). Most corrections to the raw signals from the tails of U-

mass 235.5 (36.41 counts) and that for mass 233.5 (13.13 counts).

2. Future Work
Future work will include comparing calculated tailing signal to measured tailing signal from other
reference materials, to verify the predicted values are sound. We’ve measured tailing signals from CRMs
U-900, U-630, U-200, and U-030 for which these comparisons can be made. Making these calculations
for unknown particles requires measuring their isotope compositions. This is typically done immediately
after a radiochronometry measurement on leftover material from the particle not completely consumed.
We plan to incorporate this into radiochronometry measurements later in FY26. Finally, the tailing signal
and its uncertainties will need to be propagated into the uncertainty of measured Th-230 signal, as part
of determining the age uncertainty, as part of the “Best Practices for Reporting Uncertainties”
deliverable.
