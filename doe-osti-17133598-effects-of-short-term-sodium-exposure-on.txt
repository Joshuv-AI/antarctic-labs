Effects of Short-Term Sodium Exposure on Alloy 709 and LPBF 316H Stainless Steel September 2026

ABSTRACT
This report investigates the effects of short-term liquid sodium exposure on Alloy 709 (A709) and
laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H stainless steel for structural applications in sodium-cooled
fast reactors. Sub-sized sheet tensile specimens of A709 in solution-annealed and precipitation-
treated conditions, along with LPBF 316H specimens with as-printed and mechanically ground
surfaces, were exposed to liquid sodium in a forced-convection loop at 550°C and 600°C for up to
~3000 h. Parallel thermal-aging experiments on A709 were conducted to decouple thermal aging
effects from sodium-induced environmental degradation. Chemical analysis of the sodium
environment and thermodynamic modeling of carbon transfer predicted carburization in both
alloys. Post-exposure evaluations revealed limited overall corrosion, with negligible thickness
changes and minimal weight loss. However, microstructural characterization confirmed surface
oxide formation, elemental redistribution, subsurface precipitation, and localized near-surface
hardening in both materials. In addition, uniaxial tensile testing showed that while sodium
exposure and thermal aging had a negligible effect on the strength of A709, they led to a reduction
in ductility, particularly at 600°C and for extended exposure times. The results demonstrate
favorable short-term sodium compatibility for both alloys and suggest that carburization-driven
near-surface microstructural evolution is a primary mechanism influencing the mechanical
behavior of these materials.

1 Introduction
Advanced nuclear reactors require both advanced materials and advanced manufacturing
capabilities that exceed current industry standards. Many next-generation reactor concepts operate
under extreme conditions, including high temperatures, intense neutron irradiation, and chemically
aggressive coolants, which can alter microstructure, accelerate corrosion, and degrade mechanical
properties over time. These demanding environments make the development of advanced materials
essential for ensuring component durability, extensive service lifetime, and operational safety. At
the same time, the nuclear industry also faces significant supply chain challenges that hinder the
timely and cost-effective production of critical components. Advanced manufacturing
technologies are therefore crucial, providing the flexibility, speed, and quality needed to support
the scalable deployment of advanced reactors [1].
Sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs) are a leading advanced reactor concept with considerable
technological maturity for commercial development [2]. With inherent and passive safety features,
SFRs offer safe, efficient power generation and can support a closed fuel cycle. Liquid sodium is
particularly attractive as a coolant because of its high thermal conductivity and broad liquid-phase
temperature range (98–883°C) [3]. With a significant margin below sodium’s boiling temperature,
an SFR does not require pressurization and can operate at or near the ambient pressure. Decades
of experience from the design, construction, and operation of experimental SFRs in the US and
abroad have established a strong technical foundation for continued development [2].
Despite these advantages, liquid sodium also presents a demanding environment for structural
alloys. In liquid sodium, the protective oxide films that normally stabilize alloy surfaces can be
reduced or removed, allowing metallic elements to dissolve either uniformly or selectively into the
coolant [4]. Nonmetallic elements, especially carbon, can also migrate across the sodium–alloy
interface, affecting phase stability and microstructural evolution [5][6]. As a result, both corrosion
behavior and mechanical performance must be carefully assessed for materials in direct contact
with sodium. In particular, a clear understanding of carburization and decarburization, and their
effects on long-term material degradation, is essential for ensuring the reliability and durability of
SFR components.
Alloy 709 (A709, ASTM A213 TP310MoCbN, UNS S31025) is an advanced austenitic stainless
steel originally developed for high-temperature boiler service [7]. Because of its excellent
corrosion resistance and high-temperature mechanical properties, A709 has great potential for SFR
applications. A substantial mechanical property database is currently being developed to support
qualification of A709 for high-temperature reactor construction under ASME Section III, Division
5. However, information on the behavior of A709 in sodium environments remains limited, and
the degradation mechanism of A709 in liquid sodium is unclear. As a highly alloyed steel with
significant carbide-forming elements, A709 may undergo microstructural changes with
precipitation of carbides or intermetallic phases. These microstructural changes can alter
mechanical properties, affecting the long-term performance of A709 in liquid sodium
environments. One objective of the current study is to evaluate the effects of sodium exposure on

 1

A709 microstructural evolution and tensile properties, providing both data and mechanistic
understanding relevant to SFR service conditions.
In parallel, this study also supports additive manufacturing (AM) development for advanced
nuclear systems. Rapid progress of AM technologies in recent years has created new pathways for
producing high-quality, application-specific components with tailored properties [1]. Potential
benefits include rapid prototyping (shortened design-test-fix cycles), increased design flexibility,
and reduced supply-chain dependency. Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H stainless steel (SS)
exemplifies this potential, offering a route to custom-designed components and a more agile supply
chain for advanced reactors. While AM materials may be compositionally similar to their
conventionally fabricated counterparts, they can exhibit different defects, microstructures, and
internal stress states due to manufacturing processes. These differences can translate into different
in-service behavior and long-term durability. To adopt AM technologies for advanced nuclear
systems, LPBF 316H SS is being evaluated under SFR-relevant conditions. The focus of this study
is on the effect of surface finish on the corrosion and carbon transfer behavior of LPBF 316H SS.
This study conducts sodium exposure experiments on both A709 and LPBF 316H SS in a forced-
convection sodium loop. For A709, a commercial heat produced by Allegheny Technologies Inc.
is used, and the work focuses on microstructural evolution and the resulting changes in tensile
properties, with the goal of identifying the conditions that favor carburization or decarburization
in the A709–sodium system. For LPBF 316H SS, the study evaluates sodium compatibility with
particular attention to AM-specific characteristics and the mechanisms and extent of carbon
transfer. These efforts aim to generate the performance data and mechanistic understanding needed
to support qualification and, ultimately, deployment of A709 and LPBF 316H SS for SFR
applications.

operated at different temperatures, and each vessel has two sample loading ports. After more than
10 years of operation, the cold trap and associated piping of the system were modified and
replaced. The system was re-commissioned in late 2025, and the two vessels have been operated
at 550°C and 600°C since then. Figure 3 shows the updated loop configuration.

 Figure 3. Forced-convection loop used for sodium exposure experiment
To perform a sodium exposure experiment, a group of samples were assembled on a SS holder
about 17 inches long as shown in Figure 4. The holder was then inserted into the vessel in its length
direction with the majority of the holder length submerged below the liquid sodium surface and
the holder head (right-hand side in Figure 4) above the liquid sodium surface. On the sample
holder, there are multiple positions where exposure samples can be mounted. Several samples (2
to 6 samples) can be stacked together with spacers in between at each exposure position. For the
current study, four sample holders, each containing 24 tensile samples, were assembled. Two
nickel foil coupons were also installed on one of the sample holders to assess the carbon activity
of the test environment. Figure 4 shows a prepared sample holder assembly ready for insertion.

In our system, the interior of a sodium exposure vessel is isolated from the outside with a set of
ball and gate valves. To minimize the risk of sodium vapor leak, sample insertions and withdrawals
were carried out at relatively low temperatures (<300°C). For insertion, a sample holder assembly
is loaded into a sample exchange chamber (Figure 5) consisting of a sliding rod that can be
dynamically sealed. After the exchange chamber is secured to the vessel access port, the chamber
is purged clean with several cycles of evacuating and argon backfilling. With the chamber cleaned
and secured in an inert environment, the gate and ball valves of the vessel are opened, and the
sample holder is lowered into the vessel using the sliding rod. After the sliding rod is detached
from the sample holder head, the holder is suspended inside the vessel with the samples submerged

2.3 Corrosion and Mechanical Property Evaluation
Before sodium exposure, each sample was weighed using an analytical balance with a precision
of 10 µg. Sample thickness was also measured with a micrometer. Sodium exposure and thermal-
aging experiments are currently ongoing, and samples will be retrieved at planned intervals for
evaluation.

After sodium exposure, the samples were cleaned with ethanol followed by distilled water to
remove residual sodium. Samples were then visually inspected and re-measured for weight and
thickness. These measurements were compared with pre-exposure values to assess corrosion
performance. Sodium exposed samples were also evaluated with SEM and EDS, focusing on the
surface layers in contact with liquid sodium during exposure. Attention was on the distribution of
alloying elements near surfaces.

3 Sodium Chemistry
3.1 Carbon Content in Sodium
Carbon is a key element influencing the mechanical properties of steels. Changes in carbon
concentration in A709 and LPBF 316H SS during sodium exposure can significantly affect their
microstructure and mechanical properties. The extent of carburization or decarburization in these
alloys depends on temperature and the carbon activity of the sodium. Therefore, the carbon content
in sodium needs to be measured.

Accurate determination of carbon concentration in sodium is challenging due to low carbon
activity. Several techniques have been developed in the past, including the foil equilibration
method, electrochemical and diffusion-type carbon meters. Among these, the equilibration method
is relatively simple and cost-effective, with acceptable accuracy. The equilibration material should
have a low corrosion rate in liquid sodium, sufficiently high carbon diffusivity, and a large carbon
distribution coefficient between the material and sodium [12]. In this study, nickel was selected as
the equilibration material. High-purity (99.999%) nickel foils were placed in the 600 °C vessel
along with sodium-exposure samples. The composition of the nickel foil used in this study is
shown in Table 5.

3.2 Oxygen Content in Sodium
Oxygen content in sodium was also assessed using a foil-equilibration method. Vanadium was
selected as the equilibration material based on the early work by Smith [14]. Four vanadium foils
with nominal dimensions of 10 x 65 x 0.125 mm were installed on a sample holder. After insertion
into the vessel, the loop temperature increased to 600°C and the vanadium foils were submerged
in sodium until equilibrium was reached.
After the vanadium foils were retrieved from the vessel, sodium adhering to the foils was removed
by immersion in ethanol at room temperature. After the sodium-alcohol reaction was complete,
the foils were rinsed in distilled water and dried. The foils were then electrothermally polished in
a solution of 80 vol.% absolute methanol and 20 vol.% concentration sulfuric acid. Two platinum
cathodes separated by 30 mm and a 7.5-V open-circuit voltage were used for electropolishing.
After polishing, the vanadium foils were analyzed with a Leco 836 oxygen analyzer. Several

6 Thermodynamic Analysis of Carbon Transfer in Sodium
6.1 Carbon Transfer Between A709 and Sodium
Carbon is a key element affecting the corrosion rate, microstructural stability, and mechanical
performance of A709. Given its highly alloyed composition containing carbide forming elements,
A709 may be particularly sensitive to its carbon content. Carbon concentration changes resulting
from environmental exposure could influence the microstructural evolution of A709 at elevated
temperatures, giving rise to deteriorated mechanical performance. A careful analysis of the
carburization/decarburization condition of A709 is therefore critical for understanding the
interactions between A709 and sodium environment.

The carbon activities of austenitic alloys have been studied by Natesan and Kassner [12]. Based
on the data of Fe-Cr-Ni model alloys, an empirical model was developed to calculate carbon
activities. Note that the model includes only the effects of Cr, Fe, and Ni on carbides. On the other
hand, A709 contains carbide-forming elements such as Nb and Ti which could impact the carbon
activity significantly. Figure 30 shows the carbon activities of carbides as a function of
temperature. TiC and NbC are much more stable than Cr23C6. As a result, their effects of the
carbon activity must be included for A709.

 Figure 30. Carbide stability calculated by HSC chemistry.
In the thermodynamic analysis of A709, we considered three types of carbide, M23C6, NbC, and
TiC. The content of carbon in each type of carbide was calculated with the method developed by
Saltelli [16]. The total content of carbon was the sum of these carbides.

The calculation results are shown in Figure 31 at 550 and 600°C. The orange color curves are the
total carbon concentration, and the blue, green, and red curves are contributions from M23C6, TiC,
and NbC, respectively. Among the three types of carbides, M23C6 is the most unstable. TiC and
NbC are much more stable at low carbon activities, and they could play a significant role in
stabilizing the microstructure of A709 in low-carbon-activity environments.

7 Conclusions
To evaluate the effects of sodium exposure on Alloy 709 and LPBF 316H SS, short-term sodium
exposure experiments were conducted with a forced-convection sodium loop. Small tensile
samples were exposed to flowing sodium at 550°C and 600°C, with exposure times up to ~3,000
hours for A709 and ~2,000 hours for LPBF 316H. In parallel, a thermal aging experiment was also
conducted on A709 samples to separate the effects of sodium exposure from thermal aging. Post-
exposure evaluation included corrosion behavior, hardness evolution, and tensile properties.

The sodium environment was characterized with equilibration methods. Carbon measurements
using nickel foil indicated sodium carbon concentrations of about 0.23–0.33 ppm, corresponding
to carbon activities of about 0.06–0.09 at 550°C and 0.03–0.04 at 600°C. Oxygen measurements
using vanadium foil showed the oxygen level decreased over time due to cold trapping and was
about 2 wppm when specimens were inserted. Thermodynamic analysis suggested that, under
these carbon activities, both A709 and LPBF 316H should undergo carburization in the loop.

Corrosion of A709 with sodium exposure was mild with small but detectable weight losses over
~1000 and ~3000 hours. SEM/EDS results showed oxide layers on all A709 samples and some
chromium/nickel segregation near the surface, likely associated with oxidation. At 600°C, very
fine precipitates were observed beneath the surface after ~3000 hours. Microhardness
measurements showed a clear near-surface hardness increase after sodium exposure, especially in
SA samples, which suggests that carburization occurred locally near the exposed surface.

For LPBF 316H SS, samples with as-printed surfaces showed a clear weight loss after ~2,085
hours in sodium, likely because partially melted surface particles and rough surface features can
detach during exposure. All LPBF 316H samples developed oxidation, and both ground and as-
printed conditions showed near-surface hardening after sodium exposure. Ground samples
exhibited a thin near-surface layer with fine precipitates, while as-printed samples showed more
extensive grain-boundary precipitation below the surface. EDS also showed chromium enrichment
and nickel depletion near surfaces, again consistent with oxidation-related chemical redistribution.

Mechanically, the main effect of short-term sodium exposure and thermal aging on A709 was a
reduction in ductility rather than a large change in strength. Baseline tensile testing showed good
strength and excellent elongation of A709 in both SA and SA+PT conditions. After thermal aging
or sodium exposure, yield strength changed little in most cases, but uniform elongation often
decreased, especially at 600°C and after longer exposure times in sodium. Sodium exposure up to
3,000 hours did not produce a simple trend across all heat-treatment conditions, but the data
indicate that short-term sodium exposure modestly affects strength while more noticeably
degrading ductility.

Sodium exposure experiments on A709 and LPBF 316H SS are continuing, aiming at assessing
longer-term effects of liquid sodium on both materials for SFR applications. Additional effort is
planned to characterize the precipitate and deformation microstructure, carbon transfer, and the
influence of surface conditions on sodium corrosion of these materials.
