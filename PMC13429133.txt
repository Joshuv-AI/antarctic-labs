Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes in Women with HELLP Syndrome Undergoing Caesarean Delivery Under General and Neuraxial Anaesthesia: A Systematic Review and Narrative Synthesis
Authors: Noy Norman Kambuaya, Iwan Fuadi, Annisa Isfandiary Ismandiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen with haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome may require urgent caesarean delivery under general or neuraxial anaesthesia. This review aimed to describe the maternal and neonatal outcomes reported in women with HELLP syndrome undergoing caesarean delivery under general and neuraxial anaesthesia.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL were searched from inception to September 2025. Eligible studies reported the anaesthetic technique together with at least one maternal or neonatal outcome. Risk of bias was assessed using tools matched to study design — the JBI checklist for single-arm case series and ROBINS-I for non-randomised comparative studies — and the certainty of evidence was rated using GRADE. Across these studies, the review describes 369 caesarean deliveries in women with HELLP syndrome; an anaesthetic technique was documented for 328 of these — 169 underwent general anaesthesia and 159 underwent neuraxial anaesthesia — while for the remaining 41 the technique could not be reliably determined. Background

HELLP syndrome is a severe hypertensive disorder of pregnancy defined by haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and thrombocytopenia,1 complicating approximately 0.2–0.8% of pregnancies and carrying a markedly elevated risk of severe maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality.2 Disease severity is frequently stratified by the maternal platelet nadir using the Mississippi classification.1

Because definitive treatment is delivery, most women with HELLP syndrome undergo caesarean delivery, and the choice between neuraxial and general anaesthesia in this population is genuinely difficult rather than a matter of institutional preference.3 Neuraxial anaesthesia is generally preferred in obstetrics, but the defining thrombocytopenia of HELLP syndrome introduces a theoretical risk of spinal–epidural haematoma. The precise minimum platelet threshold for safe neuraxial blockade remains a subject of ongoing clinical controversy. On the other hand, general anaesthesia circumvents this bleeding risk but exposes to the hazards of difficult airway and haemodynamic responses to intubation. Furthermore, this coagulopathy is highly time-dependent; platelet counts often fall rapidly into the postpartum period, complicating both the initial choice of technique and the safe timing of epidural catheter removal. Recognising these complexities, recent guidance, such as the Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology (SOAP) 2021 consensus, offers a framework for navigating obstetric thrombocytopenia within the overall clinical context rather than by a fixed cut-off.4

Despite this clinical importance, the evidence base guiding anaesthetic choice in HELLP-syndrome caesarean delivery remains limited. Previous reviews of anaesthesia in HELLP syndrome have been largely narrative,5,6 and, to our knowledge, no systematic review has specifically described the maternal and neonatal outcomes reported under general and neuraxial anaesthesia in this condition. Because the choice between neuraxial and general anaesthesia in HELLP syndrome is strongly driven by disease severity, platelet count, and obstetric urgency, any observed difference in outcomes is subject to substantial confounding by indication, and a randomised comparison is neither feasible nor ethically justifiable. A descriptive (non-comparative) synthesis is therefore the appropriate design: it can characterise how each technique is currently used, in whom, and with what reported outcomes, without implying causal superiority of one over the other.

This review aimed to describe the maternal and neonatal outcomes reported in women with HELLP syndrome undergoing caesarean delivery under general and neuraxial anaesthesia, and to identify the specific evidence gaps that future studies would need to address.

Methods

This systematic review was reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020 and was registered in PROSPERO.7,8

Eligibility Criteria

We included published English-language primary studies involving women with HELLP syndrome who underwent caesarean delivery and reported the anaesthetic technique together with at least one maternal or neonatal outcome. Eligible anaesthetic techniques were general anaesthesia and neuraxial anaesthesia, including spinal, epidural, and combined spinal–epidural techniques. Eligible study designs included observational studies and case series with at least 10 women. We excluded case reports, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without sufficient data, studies that did not report the anaesthetic technique, studies without extractable HELLP data, and studies that did not allow extraction of outcomes for caesarean delivery.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL from inception to September 2025, and screened reference lists and used citation searching to identify additional studies. The full search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Selection and Data Collection

One reviewer screened titles and abstracts and assessed full-text reports against the eligibility criteria; uncertain cases and a sample of exclusions were checked by a second reviewer. Data were extracted using a standardised form by one reviewer and checked by a second reviewer, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Study authors were not contacted for additional information. Extracted items included study design, setting, participant characteristics, HELLP severity, anaesthetic technique, platelet counts and their timing relative to anaesthesia, peri-procedural transfusion, and maternal, neonatal, and anaesthesia-related outcomes.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using tools matched to study design. Two single-arm studies, which described outcomes under one anaesthetic technique without an internal comparison group, were assessed with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series.9 Five non-randomised comparative studies, which reported outcomes or laboratory parameters for more than one anaesthetic technique, were assessed with ROBINS-I.10 For the JBI checklist, the following items were evaluated: inclusion criteria, diagnostic validity, consecutive and complete inclusion of participants, reporting of demographic and clinical detail, outcome measurement, follow-up, reporting of setting, and statistical analysis. The non-randomised comparative studies were assessed with ROBINS-I, with attention to bias due to confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Assessment was performed by one reviewer and checked by a second, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Synthesis and Certainty of Evidence

Because the evidence was observational and clinically heterogeneous, a structured narrative synthesis was performed and no meta-analysis was undertaken. Studies were summarised by the anaesthetic techniques and outcomes reported, considering clinical context (HELLP severity, platelet count, transfusion) where available. A formal statistical assessment of reporting bias was not performed because the number of included studies was small and no quantitative synthesis was undertaken; potential reporting bias was considered narratively. The overall certainty of evidence for key outcomes was assessed using the GRADE approach.

Amendments to the Registered Protocol

The registered PROSPERO protocol was framed primarily as a comparative review of general versus neuraxial anaesthesia. After study identification and appraisal, the final review was presented as a descriptive synthesis of reported maternal and neonatal outcomes, because the eligible evidence was observational, clinically heterogeneous, and strongly affected by confounding by indication, which limited valid direct comparison between techniques.

Results

Study Selection

The search identified 165 records via databases and registers (30 from PubMed, 127 from Embase, and 8 from CENTRAL) and 44 records via reference-list and citation searching. In the databases-and-registers arm, 34 duplicates were removed, 131 records were screened, 105 were excluded, 26 reports were sought for retrieval, 10 could not be retrieved, and 16 were assessed for eligibility, of which 11 were excluded (did not report the variable of interest, n=3; did not report the outcome of interest, n=1; non-English article, n=7), leaving 5 included studies. In the other-methods arm, 5 duplicates were removed, 39 records were screened, 37 were excluded, and 2 reports were assessed and included. Study Characteristics

Seven observational studies were included, published between 1988 and 2022 in tertiary or high-acuity settings; six were retrospective and one was a prospective single-arm cohort. Across the seven studies, the review describes 369 caesarean deliveries in women with HELLP syndrome. An anaesthetic technique was documented for 328 of these — 169 under general anaesthesia and 159 under neuraxial anaesthesia (spinal, epidural, or combined spinal–epidural) — while for the remaining 41 the technique could not be reliably determined from the source reports. Detailed study characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table S2.10–16

Risk of Bias

The two case series (Crosby et al; Fathy et al) were at low risk of bias on the JBI checklist, meeting all appraised criteria (Table 1). For the five non-randomised comparative studies, ROBINS-I judged every study to be at serious risk of bias overall (Table 2). In all five, the serious rating was driven by uncontrolled confounding by indication: the anaesthetic technique was selected on the basis of the platelet count and clinical severity — the same factors that determine outcome — without randomisation or analytical adjustment. One study (Osmanagaoglu et al) was additionally downgraded for missing data, because its narrative technique counts (general anaesthesia, 12; spinal, 25) summed to the whole cohort (n = 37) rather than to the 27 caesarean deliveries and could not be reconciled with the report that no vaginal delivery received regional anaesthesia, so the caesarean-specific technique split could not be recovered.

Maternal Outcomes

Maternal outcomes were reported across the seven studies, but reporting was inconsistent. Among studies that explicitly reported maternal mortality, study-level mortality ranged from 0% to 30%. The highest value (11/37, 30%) was from the intensive-care cohort of Osmanagaoglu et al; because that denominator is the whole ICU-admitted HELLP population rather than the 27 women who underwent caesarean delivery, and the study reports mortality against the full cohort rather than by technique, a caesarean- or technique-specific mortality cannot be derived from it (of the 11 deaths, 10 followed caesarean delivery).14 Crosby et al reported no maternal deaths;12 Ramanathan et al reported two maternal deaths in the general anaesthesia group;11 and Fathy et al reported one maternal death in a general-anaesthesia-only cohort.16 Other studies did not report maternal mortality in extractable detail.

Other reported maternal complications included postpartum haemorrhage, transfusion, disseminated intravascular coagulation, pulmonary oedema, renal failure, neurological complications, and intensive care admission, but were not reported uniformly or with technique-specific detail across studies. Detailed maternal outcomes and anaesthesia-related complications by study are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Anaesthesia-Related Complications

No study reported a confirmed neuraxial haematoma or a permanent neurological deficit after neuraxial anaesthesia.10–14,16 Palit et al reported one bloody tap during combined spinal–epidural anaesthesia with conversion to general anaesthesia, but no epidural haematoma.15 Vigil-De Gracia et al reported no neurological or epidural-space bleeding complications.13 Ramanathan et al reported hypotension after lumbar epidural anaesthesia and a difficult intubation with severe laryngeal oedema in the general anaesthesia group.11 Several studies stated that no anaesthesia-related complications were observed but did not describe how these were assessed or the duration of follow-up.12,13,17

Neonatal Outcomes

Neonatal outcomes were variably reported. Ramanathan et al reported birthweight, stillbirth, neonatal death, and Apgar scores by group;11 Crosby et al reported birthweight, stillbirth, Apgar scores, and no neonatal deaths;12 GünaydIn et al reported gestational age, birthweight, stillbirth, Apgar scores, and neonatal intensive care unit admission;17 and Fathy et al reported Apgar scores in a general-anaesthesia-only cohort.16 In other studies, neonatal outcomes were not reported in extractable detail.12–14 Detailed neonatal outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of evidence for the main maternal, neonatal, and anaesthesia-related outcomes was very low, owing to the serious risk of bias from confounding by indication, inconsistent outcome reporting, and imprecision. The detailed GRADE summary is presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Discussion

Principal Findings

Maternal and neonatal outcomes were reported across the included studies, but reporting was inconsistent in outcome definitions, settings, and detail. Maternal outcomes should be interpreted mainly as a reflection of disease severity and clinical setting rather than as a direct effect of anaesthetic technique, because the included studies did not involve comparable groups: women who received general anaesthesia were often more unstable, had lower platelet counts, or required more urgent delivery, whereas neuraxial techniques were generally used in more selected patients. The review can therefore describe what outcomes were reported under each technique, but cannot show that one technique caused better or worse outcomes than the other.

The same applies to neonatal outcomes, which were inconsistently reported and closely linked to prematurity, fetal condition, and urgency of delivery. In Ramanathan et al, lower Apgar scores and birthweight were reported in the general anaesthesia group, but that group also included more urgent, higher-risk cases.11 The neonatal findings are therefore best read as descriptive outcomes reported in the literature rather than evidence that the two techniques lead to different neonatal results in HELLP syndrome.

Interpretation in the Context of Consensus Guidance

These findings should also be interpreted in the context of the SOAP consensus statement on obstetric thrombocytopenia, which is one of several lenses through which the evidence can be viewed. SOAP emphasises that the decision to proceed with a neuraxial procedure should be made within the overall clinical context, taking into account comorbidities, obstetric risk, airway considerations, the risk of general anaesthesia, and patient factors.4 SOAP supports platelet-based risk stratification while recognising that this should not be reduced to a single universal cut-off; Neuraxial Safety

One finding is that serious neuraxial complications were not reported, but this should be interpreted carefully. No confirmed neuraxial haematoma or permanent neuraxial neurological injury was reported in the studies that addressed neuraxial safety; however, follow-up was not standardised, postpartum neurological surveillance was rarely described, and platelet timing at neuraxial placement or catheter removal was often missing.10–14,16 The available evidence is therefore reassuring only in a limited sense: it shows that neuraxial anaesthesia has been used in selected women with HELLP syndrome without reported catastrophic complications, but it does not establish safety at low platelet counts. Because technique was allocated according to clinical severity in every comparative study, none of the reported differences between techniques can be attributed to the technique itself.

Neuraxial risk in HELLP syndrome may also be time-dependent, particularly with epidural or combined spinal–epidural techniques, because catheter removal may occur later, when platelet counts have changed. One included study explicitly described delayed epidural catheter removal until platelet recovery, whereas most others did not report this in detail,15 highlighting that catheter management may be an important part of how neuraxial anaesthesia is reported and interpreted in this population.4,15

Limitations of the Included Evidence

All included studies were non-randomised and most were retrospective; outcome definitions were heterogeneous, and results were not always stratified clearly by technique.10–16 The five comparative studies were at serious risk of bias from confounding by indication, and one study (Fathy et al) evaluated only general anaesthesia in a single-arm design, contributing to the description of outcomes under general anaesthesia but providing no information on neuraxial techniques and therefore little to a technique-level description across the two approaches.16

The included studies span more than three decades (1988–2022), and this temporal range warrants caution. Anaesthetic practice changed over this period, a shift visible within the studies themselves: the earliest report compared lumbar epidural with general anaesthesia, whereas later studies used single-shot spinal and combined spinal–epidural techniques with defined catheter-management protocols. Platelet-based frameworks such as the SOAP consensus postdate most of the included studies and could only have informed the most recent reports. Transfusion thresholds, coagulation monitoring, and neonatal care are also likely to have evolved across this interval, so outcomes reported in earlier studies may not fully reflect current practice.

Interest has also grown in whether viscoelastic testing (thromboelastography/rotational thromboelastometry) could refine neuraxial risk assessment in thrombocytopenia beyond the platelet count alone; however, current consensus guidance concludes that there is insufficient evidence to recommend its routine use for this purpose in obstetric patients.4 The included studies, which relied on platelet count and conventional coagulation testing, therefore reflect an evidence base in which viscoelastic guidance is not established.

Limitations of the Review Process

Screening and eligibility assessment were performed by a single reviewer, with uncertain cases and a sample of exclusions verified by a second reviewer; data extraction was performed by one reviewer and checked by a second rather than independently duplicated. Full dual independent screening and extraction is the methodological standard for a systematic review; its absence may have introduced selection and extraction bias, cannot be remedied retrospectively, and represents the principal limitation of the review process. Only English-language published studies were included, unpublished data were not sought, and authors were not contacted for missing information.

Implications for Future Research

The gaps identified here point to concrete priorities: standardised, prospectively defined outcome definitions; consistent reporting of the platelet count at the time of neuraxial placement and at catheter removal; defined postpartum neurological surveillance; and prospective, ideally registry-based, data collection with a minimum reporting dataset for anaesthesia in HELLP syndrome. Such standardisation would allow future work to move beyond describing reported outcomes toward evidence capable of informing technique-related risk in this high-acuity population.

Conclusion

Caesarean delivery under both general and neuraxial anaesthesia has been reported in women with HELLP syndrome, without catastrophic neuraxial complications, but the evidence cannot be used to compare the safety of the two techniques: all five comparative studies included here were at serious risk of bias, because women who were more unstable, or had lower platelet counts, were more likely to receive general anaesthesia. Reported differences in outcome therefore most likely reflect the severity of the underlying disease rather than the anaesthetic itself. Future studies should record platelet counts at the time of neuraxial placement and catheter removal, define neurological follow-up, and report outcomes prospectively, so that this question can eventually be answered with reliable evidence.
