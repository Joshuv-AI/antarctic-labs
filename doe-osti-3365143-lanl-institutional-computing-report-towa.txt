1 Scientific and Programmatic Impact
1.1 Project goals
Our goal is to develop a digital twin of Arctic sea ice that combines high-resolution predictive modeling
with observational products. This effort will provide an optimized model for use in seasonal to sub-seasonal
forecasting and a tool for policymakers to better anticipate, plan for, and mitigate the national security
impacts of rapidly changing Arctic conditions. The modeling component uses the Discrete Element Model
for Sea Ice (DEMSI), which uses discrete elements to represent the sea ice with an explicit representation of
forces between them. This year three major areas of development have been accomplished at LANL:

1.2 Data Assimilation
To perform accurate sea ice predictive simulations a realistic initial condition is required. Interpolated simu-
lation results from other Earth System Models are often inconsistent with the used model, while data projects
have patchy spatial coverage. Data assimilation provides an effective methodology for using observations
to derive an initial condition consistent with the model. We have developed a data assimilation system for
DEMSI that uses the ensemble Kalman filter methodology. This method runs an ensemble of model mem-
bers and uses statistics of the model spread in that ensemble to move the model state consistently towards
observations. Both sea ice concentration (Fig. 1) and thickness have been assimilated. The method has also
been extended to allow ongoing updates to the model state during predictive simulations.

1.3 High fidelity sea ice-ocean coupling
Current Earth System Models use an explicit-in-time modeling framework, where coupling delays are present
between components. In the case of the ocean and sea ice these delays can generate numerical instabilities
as well as significantly reduce the flux of momentum between the components. To ameliorate this issue, a
new coupling methodology has been developed whereby coupled velocities, instead of being held fixed, are
rotated by the Coriolis frequency during model sub-cycling, better accounting for the effect of Coriolis forces
between coupling intervals. Idealized simulations and simulations with MPAS-Ocean, the DOE ocean model,
reveal a significant change in the magnitude of sea ice/ocean coupling stresses and improvements to coupling
stability, which have potential utility for E3SM, the DOE Earth system model.

1.4 Enhanced sea ice deformation analysis
Sea ice deformation is an important characteristic of sea ice physics that sea ice models needs to capture.
This deformation takes the form of narrow linear kinematic features, which models are not expected to ever
perfectly capture the location of. Consequently, simple difference errors cannot be used to assess model
fidelity. Instead models aim to reproduce the statistics of these features, such as frequency and intersection
angle. Here we have explored several new techniques to compare sea ice deformation between model and
observations. The first explored a spectral decomposition of the sea ice deformation, while the second used
