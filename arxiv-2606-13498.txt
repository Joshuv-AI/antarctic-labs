Population dynamics of surface-mediated autocatalytic processes
Denis S. Grebenkov, Yilin Ye

Abstract
We investigate the population dynamics of surface-mediated autocatalytic processes, in which particles diffuse in a complex environment towards surface regions where they can be either killed or replicated. These opposite mechanisms compete with each other and lead to a sophisticated stochastic evolution of the population size. We provide a systematic analysis of the generating function of the population size. We also deduce its distribution, mean, variance and higher-order moments. For this purpose, we employ several equivalent descriptions of these quantities in terms of nonlinear integral equations and partial differential equations with nonlinear boundary conditions. We inspect the long-time behavior of the population dynamics in three regimes when the mean population size vanishes, reaches a steady-state level, or grows exponentially. A numerical solution of the underlying integral equations and independent Monte Carlo simulations support our theoretical predictions.

III
Generating function

The central quantity of our study is the probability generating
function

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{s^{\mathcal{N}(t)}\}\qquad(0\leq s\leq 1)

(2)

of the population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
, initiated by a single particle started
from
\bm{x}_{0}
at time
0
(i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(0)=1
). Here the expectation

{\mathbb{E}}
is taken with respect to all degrees of freedom in the system,
and the subscripts highlights the starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
. Rewriting
the above expectation as

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}s^{k}\,Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(3)

one can determine the probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
to have
k
particles
at time
t
:

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\mathcal{N}(t)=k\}=\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{k!}\partial_{s}^{k}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(4)

The knowledge of
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is thus equivalent to that of the
distribution of
\mathcal{N}(t)
. By construction, the generating function
monotonously grows from
s=0
to
s=1
, at which
G_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1

due to the normalization of probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

III.1
Integral equation

We recall the probabilistic renewal-type argument from

Grebenkov26a
to establish an integral equation for the
generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. For this purpose, we introduce two
random variables: the first-reaction time
\tau_{\rm a}
on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
,
and the first-branching time
\tau_{\rm c}
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
(see Appendix

A
for their formal definitions). Depending on which

\tau_{\rm c}

\displaystyle G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\left\{s^{\mathcal{N}(t)}1_{t<\min\{\tau_{\rm c},\tau_{\rm a}\}}+s^{\mathcal{N}(t)}1_{\tau_{\rm a}<\min\{t,\tau_{\rm c}\}}+s^{\mathcal{N}(t)}1_{\tau_{\rm c}<\min\{t,\tau_{\rm a}\}}\right\}

\displaystyle=s\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{t<\min\{\tau_{\rm c},\tau_{\rm a}\}\}+{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\tau_{\rm a}<\min\{t,\tau_{\rm c}\}\}+{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[G_{s}(t-\tau_{\rm c}|\bm{X}_{\tau_{\rm c}})]^{2}1_{\tau_{\rm c}<\min\{t,\tau_{\rm a}\}}\}.

(5)

In fact, if there is neither branching, nor absorption before

(i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-D\Delta P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(6b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm a}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(6c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

\displaystyle P^{+}(\bm{x},t=0|\bm{x}_{0})

When restricted to

\displaystyle G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=sS^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm a}}d\bm{x}\,q_{\rm a}DP^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

where we used the Robin boundary condition (

\displaystyle G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1-(1-s)S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\quad-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,\bigl(1-[G_{s}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})]^{2}\bigr).

III.2
PDE reformulation

Even though the integral equation (
11
) fully
describes the generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, it is instructive to
transform this integral equation into an equivalent PDE problem

Grebenkov26a
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G_{s}

\displaystyle=D\Delta G_{s}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(12a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G_{s}

\displaystyle=q_{\rm c}(G_{s}^{2}-G_{s})\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(12b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G_{s}

\displaystyle=q_{\rm a}(1-G_{s})\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(12c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G_{s}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(12d)

\displaystyle G_{s}(0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=s,

(12e)

where both operators
\Delta
and
\partial_{n}
act on the starting
point
\bm{x}_{0}
. In fact, the integral equation (
11
)
can be recognized as a standard integral representation of the
solution of Eqs. (
12
), see Appendix
C
.

Let us provide the intuitive interpretation of the initial-value
problem (
12
): (i) as absorption and branching events
occur exclusively on the boundary, ordinary diffusion in the bulk does
not affect the population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
, yielding the usual diffusion
equation (
12a
); (ii) on the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
,
one particle splits into two offsprings, so that the change in the
generating function is
G_{s}^{2}-G_{s}
, with the catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
,
that gives the Robin-type boundary condition (
12b
); (iii)
similarly, the absorption event removes the particle and yields the
change
1-G_{s}
, with the absorption rate
q_{\rm a}
, in the Robin
boundary condition (
12c
) on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
; (iv) the remaining
reflecting region
\Gamma_{\rm r}
does not affect the population size, with
no change in the generating function and Neumann boundary condition
(
12d
); finally, (v) as the system starts from a single
particle,
\mathcal{N}(0)=1
, one imposes the initial condition
(
12e
). A more elaborate discussion of this probabilistic
interpretation in terms of boundary local times is given in Appendix

The

12b

difference with respect to conventional linear PDE descriptions (such

6

diffusion-controlled reactions and related first-reaction times. This
nonlinearity is the reminiscent feature of branching processes.
However, most former works were dedicated to branching processes in
the bulk, so that nonlinear terms appeared in the diffusion equation,
e.g., in the Fisher-KPP equation

;
Kolmogorov37
;
Grindrod
. In turn, the nonlinear term in
the boundary condition is the salient distinction of
boundary-catalytic branching processes. Delmas and Vogt provided a
rigorous construction of such processes and highlighted their relation
to PDEs with nonlinear boundary conditions

steady-state regime
Delmas05
. In turn, our focus is the
time-dependent population dynamics and the asymptotic behavior of the
population size.

III.3
Dual representations

In the following, we aim at understanding the long-time behavior of
boundary-catalytic branching processes. For this purpose, we deduce
another integral equation for the generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.
Setting

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1-G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(13)

we first rewrite the initial-value problem (
12
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{G}_{s}

\displaystyle=D\Delta\bar{G}_{s}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(14a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\bar{G}_{s}-q_{\rm c}\bar{G}_{s}

\displaystyle=-q_{\rm c}\bar{G}_{s}^{2}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(14b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\bar{G}_{s}+q_{\rm a}\bar{G}_{s}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(14c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\bar{G}_{s}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(14d)

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{s}(0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=1-s.

(14e)

This elementary modification removed the inhomogeneous term from the
Robin boundary condition on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
. The curious point of this
representation is that the catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
in the left-hand side
of Eq. (
14b
) appears with the
negative
sign, in
sharp contrast with the conventional positive sign of the absorption
rate
q_{\rm a}
.

To exploit this feature, we introduce another single-particle
propagator, denoted as
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-D\Delta P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(15a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(15b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm a}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(15c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(15d)

\displaystyle P^{-}(\bm{x},t=0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}).

(15e)

The only but crucial difference from the former propagator

P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the negative sign in front of the catalytic rate

q_{\rm c}
(as highlighted by the superscript minus). In Appendix

E
, we argue that
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the mean
population density in
\bm{x}
at time
t
.
Considering the right-hand side of Eq. (
14b
) as a “flux
source” on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
, one can express the solution of the
initial-value problem (
14
) as (see Appendix

C
for details):

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=(1-s)S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}

(16)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

where

S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(17)

In this way, we deduced a dual integral representation for the
generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in terms of the propagator

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. The dual nature can be more explicitly seen by
rewriting the former integral equation (
11
) for

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
as

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=(1-s)S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}

(18)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\biggl(\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x})-2\bar{G}_{s}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\biggr).

Comparing these two expressions, one can appreciate the flexibility of
handling the Robin boundary condition via the integral term. In fact,
even though the integral over
\Gamma_{\rm c}
was originally needed to
incorporate the quadratic term in the boundary condition
(
14b
), it can also be used to accommodate the linear
term
q_{\rm c}\bar{G}_{s}
as well. In other words, one has a freedom in
choosing a suitable propagator by moving the linear term from the
boundary condition to the integral term.

An immediate consequence of this freedom is yet another
representation:

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=(1-s)S(t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}

(19)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\biggl(\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x})-\bar{G}_{s}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\biggr),

with the third propagator
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
satisfying

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-D\Delta P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(20a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(20b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm a}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(20c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(20d)

\displaystyle P(\bm{x},t=0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}),

(20e)

and

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(21)

In this representation, the propagator itself corresponds to a
setting, in which the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
is treated as
reflecting, whereas the catalytic effect is incorporated into

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
through the integral over
\Gamma_{\rm c}
in
Eq. (
19
). The major advantage of this presentation is
that
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
does not depend on
q_{\rm c}
.

In the following, we will exploit the advantages of all three
representations. In particular, Eqs. (
16
) and
(
18
) will be used in Sec.
IV
for the
long-time asymptotic analysis, whereas the integral equation
(
19
) turns out to be more suitable for a numerical
solution (see Appendices
F
and
G
).

III.4
Distribution of the population size

According to Eq. (
4
), the generating function

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
allows one to determine the probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of
having
k
particles at time
t
via
k
-fold differentiation with
respect to
s
. However, a numerical computation of these derivatives
may be challenging, especially for large
k
. It is therefore
convenient to provide an alternative way to access the probabilities

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. The probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is obtained by setting

s=0
to
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, i.e., one can use any of the above
representations for
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. For instance,
Eq. (
11
) reads

\displaystyle Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1-S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

(22)

\displaystyle\quad+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,\bigl([Q_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})]^{2}-1\bigr).

For
k>0
, one can differentiate the integral equation
(
11
)
k
times with respect to
s
and evaluate it
at
s=0
to get

\displaystyle Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\delta_{k,1}

(23)

\displaystyle\quad+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,H_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

where

H_{k}(t|\bm{x})=\frac{1}{k!}\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\partial^{k}_{s}[G_{s}(t|\bm{x})]^{2}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}Q_{j}(t|\bm{x})Q_{k-j}(t|\bm{x})

(24)

is obtained by using the general Leibniz rule to differentiate the
product of two functions (note that the binomial coefficient is
compensated by the factorials that appear from the definition
(
4
) of
Q_{j}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
). Note that the function

H_{k}(t|\bm{x})
is linear with respect to
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x})
, i.e., it does not
include quadratic terms like
Q_{k}^{2}(t|\bm{x})
. As a consequence,
Eq. (
23
) is actually linear in
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, in
sharp contrast to the nonlinear integral equation
(
22
) for
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Once
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
found, the probabilities
Q_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),Q_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),\ldots
can be obtained by solving iteratively the linear integral
equations (
23
) for
k=1,2,\ldots
.

As for the generating function, one can get alternative integral
equations for
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
based on the propagators
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

or
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. For instance, the
k
-fold derivative of the
integral equation (
19
) with respect to
s
, evaluated
at
s=0
, yields for
k>0

\displaystyle Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=S(t|\bm{x}_{0})\delta_{k,1}+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times\biggl(H_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})-Q_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\biggr),

(25)

and

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=S(t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\bar{Q}_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})-\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\biggr),

(26)

where
\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1-Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Setting
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
, the latter integral equation determines
\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

Yet another description is obtained by applying the
k
-fold
derivative to the PDE problem (
12
) to get for any
k>0
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{k}-D\Delta Q_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(27a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}Q_{k}+q_{\rm c}Q_{k}

\displaystyle=q_{\rm c}H_{k}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(27b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}Q_{k}+q_{\rm a}Q_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(27c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}Q_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(27d)

\displaystyle Q_{k}(0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,1}.

(27e)

Once again, this PDE problem is linear in
Q_{k}
and can be solved
iteratively for
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
, etc., once
Q_{0}
is found.

IV
Long-time behavior of the generating function

In this section, we discuss the long-time asymptotic behavior of the
generating function. Since
G_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\equiv 1
due to the
probability normalization, we exclude
s=1
below by considering
0\leq s<1
. For convenience, we will inspect the complementary
function
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
given by Eq. (
13
). We
first introduce three distinct regimes and then derive the asymptotic
behavior of
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
separately in each regime.

IV.1
Three asymptotic regimes

The negative sign of the catalytic rate in the boundary condition
(
15b
) drastically changes the asymptotic behavior of the
propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
as compared to
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In fact,
when both absorbing and catalytic regions were treated as killing
(with the positive rates
q_{\rm c}
and
q_{\rm a}
), there was no branching, and
the particle had to disappear. This resulted in the exponential decay
of the propagator
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
as
t\to\infty
, as in
conventional diffusion-controlled reactions. In turn, the propagator

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
describes the diffusive dynamics, in which the
catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
produces new particles via branching
events, i.e.,
\Gamma_{\rm c}
plays the role of a source
Grebenkov26
.
If the production of particles via branching events is stronger than
their absorption on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
, the population size grows.

Since the domain
\Omega
is bounded with a smooth boundary, the
Laplace operator governing the diffusive dynamics has a discrete
spectrum, despite the presence of the catalytic region with negative
reactivity
Levitin
. In other words, there are infinitely many
eigenpairs
\{\lambda_{k}^{-},u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x})\}
satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}^{-}u_{k}^{-}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(28a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{-}-q_{\rm c}u_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(28b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{-}+q_{\rm a}u_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(28c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}.

(28d)

The eigenvalues are enumerated by index
k
in the increasing order,

\lambda_{0}^{-}<\lambda_{1}^{-}\leq\lambda_{2}^{-}\leq\ldots\nearrow+\infty,

(29)

whereas the associated eigenfunctions
\{u_{k}^{-}\}
form a complete
orthonormal basis of the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
of square-integrable
functions on
\Omega
, i.e.,

\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{j}^{-}(\bm{x})\,u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x})=\delta_{j,k}.

(30)

Since the domain
\Omega
is connected (as we always assume), the
principal (smallest) eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}
is simple (of
multiplicity
1
), whereas the associated eigenfunction
u_{0}^{-}
does
not change the sign in
\Omega
and can thus be chosen to be positive
(see, e.g.,
Hassannezhad24
).
As the propagator admits the spectral expansion over this eigenbasis,

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x})\,u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{k}^{-}},

(31)

the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}
determines the long-time
asymptotic behavior of the propagator and the related quantities (such
as
S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
).

According to the variational principle, one can represent the
principal eigenvalue as

\lambda_{0}^{-}=\inf\limits_{u\not\equiv 0}\biggl\{\frac{\int\nolimits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,|\nabla u|^{2}+q_{\rm a}\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\rm a}}d\bm{x}\,u^{2}-q_{\rm c}\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u^{2}}{\int\nolimits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u^{2}}\biggr\},

(32)

where the infimum is taken over all functions
u\not\equiv 0
from a
suitable functional space
H^{1}(\Omega)

Levitin
. Let us
comment on several particular situations. (i) When there is no
catalytic region (i.e.,
\Gamma_{\rm c}=\emptyset
and
q_{\rm a}>0
), the last
term in the numerator of Eq. (
32
) disappears, while
the remaining terms are positive. As a consequence, the principal
eigenvalue is strictly positive,
\lambda_{0}^{-}>0

Levitin
,
ensuring the exponential decay of the propagator at long times, as
expected for conventional diffusion-controlled reactions. (ii) In the
special case of no reactivity (
\Gamma_{\rm c}=\Gamma_{\rm a}=\emptyset
), the
infimum is achieved on a constant function, so that
\lambda_{0}^{-}=0
,
and the system reaches a steady-state limit. Moreover, setting the
test function
u
to be a constant, one gets an elementary upper bound
in the general situation:

\lambda_{0}^{-}\leq\frac{q_{\rm a}|\Gamma_{\rm a}|-q_{\rm c}|\Gamma_{\rm c}|}{|\Omega|}\,.

(33)

(iii) In the purely catalytic regime (i.e.,
q_{\rm c}>0
and
\Gamma_{\rm a}=\emptyset
), this inequality implies that the principal eigenvalue is
strictly negative, yielding an exponential growth of the population.
In general, however, the absorption and branching events compete with
each other, and the sign of the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}

depends on both rates
q_{\rm a}
and
q_{\rm c}
, as well as on the shape of the
domain and on the geometric properties of the absorbing and catalytic
regions.
Figure
3
illustrates the behavior of the principal
eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}
as a function of
q_{\rm c}
and
q_{\rm a}
for the
circular annulus.

\lambda_{0}^{-}>0

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\lambda_{0}^{-}=0

in which
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

IV.2
Subcritical regime

In the subcritical regime (
\lambda_{0}^{-}>0
), both
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

and
S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
decay exponentially as
e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}
so that

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, obeying Eq. (
16
), is expected to
inherit this decay. To analyze this behavior, we use the Laplace
transform, which is defined for a function
f(t)
as

\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}(p)=\tilde{f}(p)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,f(t),

where both tilde and
\mathcal{L}
notations are introduced. The application
of the Laplace transform removes convolution in time in the second
term of Eq. (
16
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\{\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\}(p)

\displaystyle=(1-s)\tilde{S}^{-}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

(34)

\displaystyle-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathcal{L}\{\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p).

According to the spectral expansion (
31
), the Laplace
transforms
\tilde{S}^{-}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
and
\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
have the
poles at
p_{n}=-D\lambda_{n}^{-}
, and the simple largest pole
p_{0}

controls the long-time asymptotic behavior. If
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

decays as
e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}
as
t\to\infty
, the largest pole of

\mathcal{L}\{\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p)
is
-2D\lambda_{0}^{-}
, so that this
function has no singularity at
p_{0}
. As a consequence, the
right-hand side of Eq. (
34
) has a simple pole at

p=p_{0}
and can thus be written near
p_{0}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\{\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\}(p)\simeq\frac{1}{p-p_{0}}\biggl[(1-s)C_{0}^{-}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})

(35)

\displaystyle-q_{\rm c}Du_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\,\mathcal{L}\{\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p_{0})\biggr],

where

C_{k}^{-}=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x}).

(36)

The inverse Laplace transform of this relation yields the long-time
asymptotic behavior

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq g_{s}\,C_{0}^{-}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(37)

with the dimensionless coefficient
g_{s}
given by

g_{s}=1-s-\frac{q_{\rm c}D}{C_{0}^{-}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t|\bm{x}).

(38)

This representation is rather formal because it requires the knowledge
of
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x})
for all times
t
. At the same time, the
asymptotic relation (
37
) captures correctly the
dependence of
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
on both
\bm{x}_{0}
and
t
in the
long-time regime.

To illustrate the behavior of
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, we consider again
diffusion in the circular annulus
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}~:~R<|\bm{x}|<L\}
and restrict our attention to the case
s=0
, for which case

\bar{G}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Moreover, we fix the
starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
to be on the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
so that

|\bm{x}_{0}|=R
, and use the shortcut notation
\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\bar{Q}_{0}(t|R)
for the probability that at least one particle is
present at time
t
when started from the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}

(similar results for other values of
s
and
\bm{x}_{0}
are not shown).
We compute this function by solving numerically the integral equation
(
19
), as described in Appendix

G
.

IV.3
Critical regime

In the critical regime,
S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
approach
constants. Taking the time derivative of Eq. (
16
), we
get then at long times

\partial_{t}\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(t|\bm{x}).

One sees that the space dependence is given again by
u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})
,
whereas the remaining ordinary differential equation with respect to
time can be solved exactly, yielding

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq\frac{C_{0}^{-}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})}{q_{0}q_{\rm c}Dt}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(39)

with the characteristic inverse length

q_{0}=C_{0}^{-}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,[u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})]^{3}.

(40)

This asymptotic behavior is illustrated on
Fig.
4
(b).
As the next-order term in Eq. (
39
) may give
the timescale for the applicability of this asymptotic relation, its
computation presents an interesting perspective.

IV.4
Supercritical regime

In the supercritical regime, both
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

grow exponentially as
t\to\infty
. In analogy to the subcritical
regime, one might naively expect that the structure of the integral
equation (
16
) would imply an exponential growth of

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. However, this function must remain bounded
between
0
and
1
, so that such a growth is not possible. Even
though this equation remains valid, it is not suitable for the
analysis of the supercritical regime, and we switch to
Eq. (
18
), which involves the decaying functions

P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. This propagator also admits the
spectral expansion

P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}^{+}(\bm{x})\,u_{k}^{+}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{k}^{+}},

(41)

where
\lambda_{k}^{+}
and
u_{k}^{+}
are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions
of the Laplace operator with the positive rate
q_{\rm c}
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
:

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{k}^{+}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}^{+}u_{k}^{+}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(42a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{+}+q_{\rm c}u_{k}^{+}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(42b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{+}+q_{\rm a}u_{k}^{+}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(42c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}^{+}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}.

(42d)

As previously, the eigenvalues are enumerated by index
k
in the
increasing order,

0<\lambda_{0}^{+}<\lambda_{1}^{+}\leq\lambda_{2}^{+}\leq\ldots\nearrow+\infty,

(43)

whereas the associated eigenfunctions
\{u_{k}^{+}\}
form a complete
orthonormal basis of the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
:

\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{j}^{+}(\bm{x})\,u_{k}^{+}(\bm{x})=\delta_{j,k}.

(44)

When the domain
\Omega
is connected (as we always assume), the
principal (smallest) eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{+}
is simple (of
multiplicity
1
) and strictly positive due to the positive
reactivities on both
\Gamma_{\rm a}
and
\Gamma_{\rm c}
. This expansion
immediately implies that the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{+}

determines the long-time asymptotic decay of the associated propagator
and the related quantities.

Since
S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
vanishes in the long-time limit, it is evident
from Eq. (
18
) that
\bar{G}_{s}\equiv 0
is always a
solution in the limit
t\to\infty
. Is it possible to get a nonzero
steady-state solution? Let us first assume that such a nontrivial
solution exists and then inspect the conditions for its existence.
Replacing
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
by its limit
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})
in
Eq. (
18
), we get in the leading order

\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm c}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})\bigl(2\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})-\bar{G}_{s}^{2}(\infty|\bm{x})\bigr),

(45)

where

{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,D\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u_{k}^{+}(\bm{x}_{0})u_{k}^{+}(\bm{x})}{\lambda_{k}^{+}}

(46)

is the Green’s function of the Laplace operator satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(47a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(47b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm a}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(47c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}.

(47d)

Since
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})
is independent
s
, the solution of the integral
equation (
45
) does not also depend on
s
. Given that

\bar{G}_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\equiv 0
due to the probability distribution
normalization, one might expect that
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})\equiv 0

for any
s
. We will argue below that this conclusion is valid in the
subcritical and critical regimes. In turn, a strictly positive
solution
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})>0
does exist in the supercritical
regime for
s<1
, and this solution determines the long-time
asymptotic behavior:

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})\qquad(t\to\infty).

(48)

To gain some probabilistic insights, one can rewrite
Eq. (
45
) as

G_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=\pi(\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})\,[G_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})]^{2},

(49)

where

\pi(\bm{x}_{0})=1-q_{\rm c}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0}).

(50)

The integral equation (
49
) has a simple probabilistic
interpretation: in the steady-state regime, a particle started from

\bm{x}_{0}
is either absorbed on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
with probability
\pi(\bm{x}_{0})
,
or branches at a point
\bm{x}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
, creating two identical
independent offsprings. The probability density of branching at

\bm{x}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
is precisely
-\partial_{n}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})\bigr|_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}=q_{\rm c}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})\bigr|_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}
. This extension of the spread
harmonic measure density
Grebenkov06b
;
Grebenkov15b
includes the
condition that the particle is not absorbed on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
. In turn,

\pi(\bm{x}_{0})
is known as splitting probability, i.e., the probability
of reacting first on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
.

IV.5
Criterion of existence

To inspect the existence of a nonzero solution, one can reformulate
the problem in a more formal way in terms of operators. In fact,
restricting
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
in Eq. (
45
), one can
rewrite it as

\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm c}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}\bigl\{2\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})-[\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})]^{2}\bigr\},

(51)

where
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}
is an integral operator with the kernel
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})
,
acting on a suitable function on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
. While the Green’s
function
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})
admits the spectral expansion
(
46
) over Laplacian eigenbasis, it is not
suitable for the analysis of the operator
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}
, which acts in the
functional space
L^{2}(\Gamma_{\rm c})
. To overcome this limitation, we
consider the eigenpairs
\{\mu_{k},v_{k}\}
of the following Steklov
spectral problem
Grebenkov26

\displaystyle\Delta v_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(52a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v_{k}

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}v_{k}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(52b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v_{k}

\displaystyle=-q_{\rm a}v_{k}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(52c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}

(52d)

(in the case
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, the Robin condition (
52c
) is
replaced by the Dirichlet condition
v_{k}=0
on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
). The
peculiar feature of the Steklov problem is that the spectral parameter

\mu_{k}
stands in the boundary condition (
52b
). The
spectrum is known to be discrete
Levitin
, with infinitely many
eigenvalues
\{\mu_{k}\}
, which are enumerated to form an increasing
sequence,

0<\mu_{0}<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty,

(53)

whereas the restrictions of the associated eigenfunctions
\{v_{k}\}

onto
\Gamma_{\rm c}
form a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Gamma_{\rm c})

such that

\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,v_{j}(\bm{x})\,v_{k}(\bm{x})=\delta_{j,k}.

(54)

The principal eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
is simple (of multiplicity
1
),
whereas the associated eigenfunction
v_{0}(\bm{x})
does not change the
sign in
\overline{\Omega}
and can thus be chosen to be positive due
to the Courant nodal domain theorem.
Extending the derivation from
Grebenkov20
, one can show that
the kernel of the operator
{\mathcal{G}}^{+}
admits the spectral expansion over
the eigenfunctions
\{v_{k}|_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}\}
:

{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{k}(\bm{x})v_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})}{\mu_{k}+q_{\rm c}}\qquad(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}).

(55)

Since the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}
are positive and accumulate at infinity,

{\mathcal{G}}
is a compact self-adjoint operator. Denoting by
{\mathcal{I}}

the identity operator, one can rewrite Eq. (
51
) as

\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathcal{A}}\bigl\{\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})-[\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})]^{2}\bigr\}\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}),

(56)

where
{\mathcal{A}}=[{\mathcal{I}}-q_{\rm c}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}]^{-1}q_{\rm c}{\mathcal{G}}^{+}
. Using the
spectral expansion (
55
), one can see that the new operator

{\mathcal{A}}
is again a compact self-adjoint positive-definite operator acting
on a function
f
as

[{\mathcal{A}}f](\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm c}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})}{\mu_{k}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}(\bm{x})\,f(\bm{x}).

(57)

The problem of finding a nontrivial solution of the integral equation
(
49
) is thus reformulated in terms of the operator
equation (
56
). This is a standard problem in the
bifurcation theory of nonlinear equations. While
\bar{G}_{s}\equiv 0

is always a solution of this equation, the existence of another
solution depends on the largest eigenvalue of the operator
{\mathcal{A}}
, which
is equal to
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}
: if
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}<1
,
\bar{G}_{s}\equiv 0
is
the unique solution, whereas for
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}>1
, there exists a
nontrivial solution. This criterion naturally appears by considering

\bar{G}_{s}^{2}
as a perturbation and inspecting the linearized problem

\bar{G}_{s}\approx{\mathcal{A}}\bar{G}_{s}
, in which the repeated application of

{\mathcal{A}}
would either contract the solution to
0
(if
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}<1
),
or blow it up to infinity (if
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}>1
). Since
0\leq\bar{G}_{s}\leq 1
, the correction term
\bar{G}_{s}^{2}
cannot alter the
contraction in the first case, yielding
\bar{G}_{s}\equiv 0
as the
unique solution. In turn, the correction term amends blowing up in
the second case, ensuring the convergence to a nontrivial solution. A
rigorous proof of the above statements is beyond the scope of this
work.

In the marginal case
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}=1
, one can show that there is no
nontrivial solution. For this purpose, one can multiply
Eq. (
56
) by
v_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})
and integrate over
\Gamma_{\rm c}

that yields, due to the self-adjoint character of the operator
{\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\biggl(1-\frac{q_{\rm c}}{\mu_{0}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}_{0}\,v_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{q_{\rm c}}{\mu_{0}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,v_{0}(\bm{x})[\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})]^{2}.

(58)

Since both
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})
and
v_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})
are nonnegative,
this equality is only possible if
q_{\rm c}/\mu_{0}>1
. In the marginal
case, the left-hand side is zero, implying
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|\bm{x})\equiv 0
.

As discussed in
Grebenkov26
, the smallest eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
,
which depends on
q_{\rm a}
, separates the subcritical (
q_{\rm c}<\mu_{0}
) and
supercritical (
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
) regimes. In fact, the PDE problems
(
52
) and (
28
) become identical when

\lambda_{0}=0
and
\mu_{0}=q_{\rm c}
, i.e.,
\mu_{0}
determines the
critical catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
, at which the system reaches a nonzero
steady-state limit (the critical regime). In turn, the variational
principle and monotonicity imply that
\lambda_{0}>0
for
q_{\rm c}<\mu_{0}
(the subcritical regime) and
\lambda_{0}<0
for
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
(the supercritical regime). Note that, in
Grebenkov26
,
a slightly different Steklov problem was used, in which the spectral
parameter was set on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
, whereas
q_{\rm c}
was fixed on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
.
In that case, the smallest eigenvalue determined, for a given
q_{\rm c}
,
the critical absorption rate
q_{\rm a}
that separates the subcritical and
supercritical regimes.

The asymptotic approach to the limiting value
\bar{G}_{0}(\infty|R)
is
illustrated on Fig.
4
(c).

IV.6
Long-time behavior of the distribution

The above asymptotic analysis can be easily transferred to the
probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
that are determined by the generating
function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
via Eq. (
4
). Since the behavior
of
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=G_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was already discussed in the previous
subsections, we sketch the asymptotic results for other probabilities
with
k>0
for three regimes:

(i) In the subcritical regime, Eq. (
37
)
immediately implies the same asymptotic behavior,

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq q_{k}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,C_{0}^{-}\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\quad(t\to\infty),

(59)

where the prefactor
q_{k}
is obtained by differentiating the amplitude

g_{s}
from Eq. (
38
):

\displaystyle q_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{k!}\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\partial_{s}^{k}g_{s}

(60)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,1}+\frac{q_{\rm c}D}{C_{0}^{-}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{Dt^{\prime}\lambda_{0}^{-}}H_{k}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

with
H_{k}(t|\bm{x})
given by Eq. (
24
).

(ii) In the critical regime, the leading-order term of

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in Eq. (
39
) does not
depend on
s
and thus disappears after differentiation. One can
therefore expect that the probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
decay faster
than
\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto 1/t
. We conjecture the following
asymptotic behavior for
k=1,2,\ldots
:

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq q_{k}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,t^{-2}\quad(t\to\infty),

(61)

with some prefactors
q_{k}
.

(iii) In the supercritical regime, we showed that
\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

approaches a nontrivial limit, and so does the probability

Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. As this limit does not depend on
s
, the
probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
should vanish for any
k>0
. The
asymptotic analysis of their decay presents an interesting open
problem.

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
also determines the moments of

k=1,2,\ldots

(62)

For instance, the first derivative with respect to
s
of
Eqs. (
12

s=1

(63d)

(63e)

linear

negative
on the catalytic region.

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=2[N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2},

(69a)

\displaystyle F_{3}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=6N_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(69b)

\displaystyle F_{4}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=6[N_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}+8N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})N_{3}(t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(69c)

While the mean population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
satisfies the
homogeneous
Robin boundary condition (
63b
) on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
,
the second and higher-order moments involve the inhomogeneous Robin
condition (
66b
), with an additional “source” term
q_{\rm c}F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
that is expressed through the lower-order moments. The
crucial point is that the initial-value problem (
66
)
is
linear
in
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, whereas the nonlinearity of the
boundary condition (
12b
) for the generating function is
reflected in the nonlinear structure of the source term
F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

The linear structure of the PDE allows one to expression its solution
in terms of the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Following the standard
technique (see Appendix
C
), we get

\displaystyle N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

(70)

\displaystyle+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,F_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}).

Since
F_{k}(t|\bm{x})
depends on the lower-order moments, one can evaluate
the moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x})
iteratively, if the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

is known (see Appendix
G
for details).

We complete this section by inspecting the long-time behavior.
First, we write the spectral expansion for the mean population size:

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}C_{k}^{-}\,u_{k}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{k}^{-}},

(71)

where the coefficients
C_{k}^{-}
were defined in Eq. (
36
).
One sees that

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq C_{0}^{-}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\quad(t\to\infty).

(72)

Next, according to Eq. (
69a
), this expansion determines
the source term
F_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for the second moment, which determines
the source term
F_{3}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for the third moment, and so. As the
asymptotic behavior depends on the sign of the principal eigenvalue

\lambda_{0}^{-}
, we distinguish three regimes.

V.1
Subcritical regime

When
\lambda_{0}^{-}>0
, one can employ the same analysis as in
Sec.
IV.2
to show that

N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq n_{k}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})C_{0}^{-}e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\quad(t\to\infty),

(73)

with a dimensionless amplitude
n_{k}
. Since
F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
expressed via Eq. (
68
) in terms of products of
lower-order moments, one can resort to the induction argument, i.e.,
one can assume that Eq. (
73
) holds for

j=1,2,\ldots,k-1
and then check it for
k
. This assumption implies
the long-time behavior

F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq(u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})C_{0}^{-})^{2}e^{-2Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}n_{j}n_{k-j},

(74)

so that the Laplace transform of
F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
does not have a pole at

p_{0}=-D\lambda_{0}^{-}
. As a consequence, the long-time asymptotic
behavior of the right-hand side of Eq. (
70
) is

\displaystyle N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq C_{0}^{-}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}

\displaystyle+q_{\rm c}Du_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{Dt^{\prime}\lambda_{0}^{-}}\,F_{k}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

that is reduced to Eq. (
73
) by setting

n_{k}=1+\frac{q_{\rm c}D}{C_{0}^{-}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{Dt^{\prime}\lambda_{0}^{-}}\,F_{k}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}).

(75)

As in Sec.
IV.2
, the inconvenience of this
representation is that one needs to know
F_{k}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x})
for all
t^{\prime}>0
. If the long-time relation (
74
) could be used for
the whole range of times, the above expression (
75
)
would be significantly simplified:

n_{k}\approx 1+\frac{q_{0}q_{\rm c}}{\lambda_{0}^{-}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}n_{j}n_{k-j},

(76)

with
q_{0}
defined by Eq. (
40
). Using
n_{1}=1
according to
Eq. (
72
), one can iteratively compute these
amplitudes; for instance, one has
n_{2}\approx 1+2q_{0}q_{\rm c}/\lambda_{0}^{-}
.

Figure
6
(a) illustrates the exponential decay of the
second moment
N_{2}(t|R)
when the starting point is located on the
catalytic region of the circular annulus. The asymptotic relation
(
73
) with the approximate form (
76
)
for
n_{2}
provides an accurate approximation. Since
N_{2}(t|R)

rapidly decreases, its accurate estimation from Monte Carlo
simulations requires too many realizations (see dispersion of crosses
at long times).

One practical consequence of the asymptotic relation
(
73
) is that the coefficient of variation, i.e.,
the ratio between the standard deviation and the mean,

\gamma(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{N_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})-N_{1}^{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})}}{N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})}\propto e^{Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}/2},

(77)

diverges as
t\to\infty
. In other words, fluctuations become more
and more relevant in the subcritical regime so that rare realizations
of the stochastic process
\mathcal{N}(t)
can significantly affect its
moments, as illustrated on Fig.
2
(a).

V.2
Critical regime

In the critical regime (
\lambda_{0}^{-}=0
), both
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

F_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
reach nonzero steady-state limits so that the integral
over
t^{\prime}
in Eq. (
70
) yields
t
in the leading order.
More explicitly, we have

N_{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})C_{0}^{-}\bigl(1+2Dt\,q_{\rm c}q_{0}\bigr),

(78)

with exponentially decaying correction terms. In the Laplace domain,
this behavior means that the product of two functions with a simple
pole
p_{0}=0
yields a function with a double pole
p_{0}=0
.
Repeating this procedure iteratively, one gets

N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto t^{k-1}\quad(t\to\infty).

(79)

In this case, the fluctuations are also dominant, and the coefficient
of variation exhibits a square-root growth with time:

\gamma(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto\sqrt{t}
.

Figure
6
(b) illustrates the remarkable accuracy of
the asymptotic relation (
78
) in the case of a
circular annulus (the dashed line is barely distinguishable from the
solid line). In turn, deviations of Monte Carlo estimates at long
times highlight the difficulty in performing accurate simulations in
the critical regime.

V.3
Supercritical regime

In the supercritical regime (
\lambda_{0}^{-}<0
), the Laplace-transform
argument is still applicable but gives a different result. In fact,
the Laplace-transformed propagator
\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
still has
the largest simple pole
p_{0}=-D\lambda_{0}^{-}
, which is now positive.
In turn, the product of lower-order moments in
F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
results
in the largest pole
-kD\lambda_{0}^{-}
, which is greater than
p_{0}
, and
thus it controls the long-time behavior. However, since the
asymptotic behavior of the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
is not the
dominant factor, one loses the explicit dependence on the starting
point
\bm{x}_{0}
via the principal eigenfunction
u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})
. In turn,
one can still employ the induction argument, i.e., to assume that

N_{k^{\prime}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq n_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{k^{\prime}Dt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\quad(t\to\infty)

(80)

holds for
k^{\prime}=1,2,\ldots,k-1
and then check it for
k
, where

n_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{0})
are (yet) unknown space-dependent amplitudes. The
relation (
80
) implies
F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}
and this function determines the asymptotic
behavior of
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Using the Laplace transform and the above
argument for the poles, we get the long-time behavior

\displaystyle N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\simeq q_{\rm c}De^{kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,\biggl(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}n_{j}(\bm{x})n_{k-j}(\bm{x})\biggr)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-kDt^{\prime}|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0}).

(81)

The dependence on the starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
is thus formally given as

\displaystyle n_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\simeq q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,\biggl(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}n_{j}(\bm{x})n_{k-j}(\bm{x})\biggr)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-kDt^{\prime}|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0}).

(82)

If the propagator is known, one can determine
n_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})
iteratively,
starting from
n_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})=C_{0}^{-}u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})
.

Using the spectral expansion (
31
), one
formally gets

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-kDt^{\prime}|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{u_{j}^{-}(\bm{x})u_{j}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})}{D(\lambda_{j}^{-}+k|\lambda_{0}^{-}|)}\,,

(83)

so that the dependence on
\bm{x}_{0}
involves all eigenfunctions

u_{j}^{-}(\bm{x}_{0})
. However, if all eigenfunctions except
u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})

could be neglected, the spatial dependence of
n_{k}(\bm{x})
would be
determined by the principal one,

n_{k}(\bm{x})\approx n_{k}\,C_{0}^{-}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x}),

(84)

in which case the dimensionless coefficients
n_{k}
would satisfy

n_{k}\approx\frac{q_{0}q_{\rm c}}{|\lambda_{0}^{-}|(k-1)}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}n_{j}n_{k-j},

(85)

with
q_{0}
being defined by Eq. (
40
). Using
n_{1}=1

according to Eq. (
72
), one can iteratively compute
these amplitudes; for instance, one has
n_{2}\approx 2q_{0}q_{\rm c}/|\lambda_{0}^{-}|
. Quite surprisingly, this rough approximation
turns out to be rather accurate for a circular annulus, see
Fig.
6
(c).

The asymptotic behavior (
80
) has an important
consequence. In this regime, the coefficient of variation

\gamma(t|\bm{x}_{0})
reaches a constant limit. More generally, any
higher-order moment
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
after rescaling by
[N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{k}

reaches a constant limit. This indicates that the rescaled population
size,
\mathcal{N}(t)/N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, is expected to achieve a stationary
distribution at long times. Once this distribution is found, the
long-time behavior of the system is fully described by the mean
population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

VI
Population extinction time

The main focus of the paper was on the stochastic dynamics of the
population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
. A complementary insight onto this dynamics
can be achieved through first-passage times

Redner
;
Metzler
;
Grebenkov
. Among various temporal aspects, the
population extinction time
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
plays the crucial role in many
applications as a proxy to quantify sustainability and robustness of
the considered diffusion-reaction system. This is the first time
instance when the number of particles drops to
0
. After reaching
this state, the system cannot produce any particle, so that
\mathcal{N}(t)

remains zero after
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
. As a consequence, the probabilistic event

{\mathcal{T}}_{0}<t
implies
\mathcal{N}(t)=0
so that

Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}<t\}.

(86)

In other words, the probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of having no particle at
time
t
is actually the cumulative distribution function of the
extinction time
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}

Grebenkov26a
. In turn, its probability
density is simply obtained by taking the time derivative:
J_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\partial_{t}Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. One sees that our former analysis of the
probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
gives an immediate access to the
distribution of the extinction time
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
. In this section, we
briefly discuss some basic properties of this distribution, while its
detailed study is beyond the scope of this paper.

We first recall that the probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
can be found by
solving the integral equation (
22
) or
(
26
), or by solving the initial-value problem
(
27
) with
k=0
. The time derivative of
Eq. (
22
) yields an integral equation for the
probability density:

\displaystyle J_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=J_{\mathrm{a}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+2q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times Q_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})J_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

(87)

where

J_{\mathrm{a}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm a}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm a}}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

(88)

is the flux of particles onto the absorbing boundary
\Gamma_{\rm a}
at time

t
. In turn, the time derivative of Eq. (
26
) gives an
equivalent integral equation:

\displaystyle J_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}S(t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}P(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times\bigl[1-2Q_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\bigr]J_{0}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}).

(89)

As discussed in Sec.
IV
, the long-time asymptotic
behavior of the probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is controlled by the
catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
:
1-Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}
in
the subcritical regime,
1-Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto 1/t
in the critical
regime, and
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\to Q_{0}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})<1
in the
supercritical regime. As a consequence, the mean and higher-order
moments of
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
are infinite in the critical and supercritical
regimes. In turn, in the subcritical regime, one can find them as

{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{k}\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{k}\,J_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=k\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{k-1}\,(1-Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})).

(90)

Let us briefly discuss the mean extinction time in the subcritical
regime. Substituting
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
from Eq. (
22
),
we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,(1-Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0}))=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,\bigl[1-Q_{0}^{2}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x})\bigr].

The time integral in the second term can be interpreted as a sort of
the Laplace transform of a convolution evaluated at
p=0
, yielding

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\}

\displaystyle=T^{+}(\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,\tilde{P}^{+}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl[1-Q_{0}^{2}(t|\bm{x})\bigr],

(91)

where

T^{+}(\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,S^{+}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

(92)

is the mean first-reaction time on either of regions
\Gamma_{\rm c}
or

\Gamma_{\rm a}
. While the representation (
91
) still depends
on the probability
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x})
, the spatial dependence of the mean

{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\}
is now explicitly captured via the Green’s
function
D\tilde{P}^{+}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0})
. In addition, this relation
identified
T^{+}(\bm{x}_{0})
as a natural lower bound for the mean
extinction time.

VII
Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we presented a systematic study of surface-mediated
branching processes for ordinary diffusion in bounded domains. The
competition between absorption and branching events results in a
sophisticated dynamics of the population of particles. We focused on
the population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
and its probabilistic characterization via
the generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, the probabilities

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, and the moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. We established three
types of closed-form integral equations for these quantities, which
were based on different single-particle propagators:
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
,

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, and
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. These propagators differ in how
the catalytic region is treated for a single particle: as absorbing
for
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, as neutral for
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, or as catalytic
for
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In the first two cases, the branching mechanism
on the catalytic region is incorporated via an additional term in the
integral equation. This flexibility was particularly helpful in the
long-time asymptotic analysis. While these integral equations played
the main role in our analysis, an equivalent PDE description with a
nonlinear Robin-type boundary condition was also provided.

The nonlinear nature of the branching mechanism presented the major
difficulty for understanding the behavior of the population dynamics.
The remarkable feature of surface-mediated branching processes is that
the mean population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
still obeys a linear PDE. In
fact, this quantity is equivalent to the survival probability in the
framework of conventional diffusion-controlled reactions, but with a

negative
effective reactivity on the catalytic region. On one
hand, the linear character of the problem allows one to employ
standard tools such as spectral expansions or Laplace transform
techniques. In particular, the long-time behavior of
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
fully controlled by the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}
of the
associated Laplace operator:
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}
.
In this way, we could distinguish the subcritical (
\lambda_{0}^{-}>0
),
critical (
\lambda_{0}^{-}=0
), and supercritical (
\lambda_{0}^{-}<0
)
regimes. Moreover, further spectral analysis permitted us to identify
the principal Steklov eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
as the critical value of the
catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
that distinguishes these three regimes. Indeed,
if
q_{\rm c}<\mu_{0}
, the branching events are not frequent enough to
maintain the population of particles, and the mean population size
decreases exponentially (i.e.,
\lambda_{0}^{-}>0
). In the opposite
case
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
, the branching events are too proliferative, and
the population grows exponentially. Finally, tuning the catalytic
rate
q_{\rm c}=\mu_{0}
allows one to balance branching and absorption
events, yielding a steady-state critical regime for the mean
population size
Grebenkov26
.

While the above classification into three regimes is informative, the
population dynamics is not reducible to the mean value. We have shown
that the probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of having
k
particles at time
t

vanishes as
t\to\infty
for any fixed
k>0
. This conclusion is
intuitively expected in the subcritical regime when the population
progressively disappears at long times. In particular,
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

decreases exponentially fast, with the same rate
D\lambda_{0}^{-}
, as
the mean population size.
In the critical regime, the situation is subtler because the mean
population size does not vanish, whereas higher-order moments

N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
grow as
t^{k-1}
. This is a manifestation of the
critical nature of this regime: while the population
\mathcal{N}(t)
vanishes
in the overwhelming majority of random realizations, very large
population sizes may be achieved in a small number of random
realizations to ensure the steady-state limit of the mean value and
the growth of higher-order moments. Accordingly, the probability of
having at least one particle slowly vanishes:
\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto 1/t
. This situation is quite common in probability. For
instance, if a random variable takes the values
0
and
n
with
probabilities
1-1/n
and
1/n
, its expectation is equal to
1
for
any
n
, even though the probability of getting
0
approaches
1
as

n\to\infty
. We also conjectured a power-law decrease of the
probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
as
1/t^{2}
.

The long-time behavior in the supercritical regime may look even more
surprising. While the mean population side grows exponentially, the
probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
still vanishes for any fixed
k>0
. At
t=0
, there is a single particle so that
Q_{k}(0|\bm{x}_{0})=\delta_{k,1}
.
For any
k\geq 2
fixed, the probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
starts
growing, reaches a maximum, and then vanishes, as it is getting more
and more unlikely to keep a fixed number of particles in the growing
population. Curiously, the probability of having no particles

Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
does not approach
1
as
t\to\infty
, in contrast to
two other regimes. We established a nonlinear integral equation for
its limit
Q_{0}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})
. The limiting value
Q_{0}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})<1
may seem to violate the probability normalization. This is a
consequence of the non-commuting limits
t\to\infty
and
k\to\infty
. In fact, the normalization is preserved for any
t
, but
does not necessarily hold in the limit
t=\infty
. A similar
“paradoxical” situation is well known for a Gaussian propagator

e^{-x^{2}/(4Dt)}/\sqrt{4\pi Dt}
for ordinary diffusion on the line:
the propagator is correctly normalized for any
t>0
, but it
formally vanishes as
t\to\infty
, thus “violating” the
normalization at
t=\infty
.

Another specific feature of the supercritical regime is the long-time
behavior of the moments:
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}
.
As a consequence, the moments of the rescaled population size,

\eta(t)=\mathcal{N}(t)/N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, have finite limits, suggesting that the
stochastic process
\eta(t)
reaches a steady-state distribution at
long times. Once this distribution is found, the long-time behavior
of the population dynamics would be fully characterized by the mean
population size. An exact computation of this limiting distribution
remains an open problem.

The above theoretical conclusions were validated by two independent
numerical techniques: (i) a numerical solution of the integral
equations, and (ii) Monte Carlo simulations. In order to facilitate
the numerical treatment, we focused on diffusion in a circular
annulus, for which the propagators and the survival probabilities can
be accurately computed via the known spectral expansions or via the
inverse Laplace transform of exact formulas. The major practical
simplification came from the rotational invariance of the annulus so
that the remaining radial-coordinate problem was one-dimensional, in
which the catalytic and absorbing regions were reduced to two points
at
|\bm{x}|=R
and
|\bm{x}|=L
. This simplification eliminated the
spatial integration in the integral equation that was reduced to a
nonlinear Volterra equation with a weakly singular kernel. We
proposed a basic scheme for its numerical solution. This computation
can be easily extended to other basic examples such as an interval or
a concentric spherical shell. Further improvements of the proposed
numerical technique and its extension to general domains present an
interesting perspective. In particular, one can employ finite-element
methods for solving nonlinear PDEs.

In this light, Monte Carlo simulations may be considered as a suitable
alternative to finite-element methods. While simulations of random
absorption and branching events for each particle can be realized by
existing tools (see, e.g.,
Schumm23
;
Ye25
), an accurate
approximation of the population size may be challenging, especially at
long times. The practical difficulties depend on the considered
regime: (i) in the subcritical regime, the population vanishes so that
an accurate estimation of exponentially small values may be hard due
to the usual statistical error
\propto 1/\sqrt{M}
for
M
random
realizations; (ii) in the supercritical regime, the number of
particles grows rapidly thus raising memory-overflow issues and
dramatic slowing down of each simulation; and (iii) in the critical
regime, minor errors in the simulation procedure may switch the
dynamics to subcritical or supercritical regime, and eventual
deviations would be amplified with time. This brief outline urges for
future developments of accurate numerical techniques for simulating
surface-mediated branching processes.

The presented study can be further extended in various
directions. (i) The ordinary diffusion can be replaced by a general
Markov process governed by a Fokker-Planck operator

Grebenkov26a
; the effect of external forces or diffusion
heterogeneities onto the population dynamics can thus be inspected.
(ii) The diffusion equation that governs the time evolution of the
generating function and of all the related quantities may incorporate
an additional term
-\nu G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
to describe eventual first-order
reactions in the bulk with the rate
\nu
; in this way, one can treat
“mortal” particles with an exponentially distributed finite lifetime

Yuste13
;
Meerson15
;
Grebenkov17
. (iii) While we focused on the
stochastic dynamics of the number of particles, spatial aspects of the
autocatalytic processes can be further analyzed, in particular, the
role of proximity to the catalytic region. (iv) Moreover, temporal
aspects of this dynamics are also accessible such as the
first-extinction time discussed in Sec.
VI
; in a
similar way, one can investigate the first-crossing times when the
population size exceeds a prescribed threshold. (v) An extension to
the initial state with many particles presents an interesting
direction. (vi) Finally, various optimization problems emerge such as
the geometric control of the population growth by reshaping the domain
or redistributing the absorption regions (see
Grebenkov26
).

In summary, surface-mediated branching processes represent an
important and yet poorly understood class of out-of-equilibrium
stochastic dynamics, with potential applications in chemical physics,
heterogeneous catalysis, and population dynamics. For example, an
extension to diffusion in exterior domains brings open mathematical
problems even for the mean population size. Furthermore, the
intrinsic out-of-equilibrium nature of these systems offers a
promising perspective for non-equilibrium physics. The negative
reactivity naturally raises the fundamental question of
time-reversibility at reactive boundaries. Evaluating the
thermodynamic cost required to sustain such surface-mediated critical
states, and quantifying the dynamic irreversibility driven by the
asymmetry between boundary absorption and branching, remain
fascinating for future research.

Appendix A
Probabilistic description of absorption and branching events

In this Appendix, we provide a more accurate probabilistic description
of absorption and branching events.

In the conventional framework of a first-order bulk reaction with a
rate
\nu
, a particle would react in the bulk during a time interval

(t,t+dt)
with the probability
\nu dt
. As we consider here
exclusively surface reactions, the bulk reaction rate
\nu
should be
replaced by a surface reaction rate
q_{\rm a}>0
, whereas the physical
time
t
is substituted by the boundary local time
\ell_{t,\rm a}

spent by the particle on the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\rm a}

Grebenkov20
. The latter can be defined as the properly
rescaled residence time of the particle in a thin layer of width
\varepsilon

near the absorbing region:

\ell_{t,\rm a}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{D}{\varepsilon}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\Theta(\varepsilon-|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\Gamma_{\rm a}|),

(93)

where
|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\Gamma_{\rm a}|
is the Euclidean distance between the
random position
\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}
of the particle at time
t^{\prime}
and the
absorbing region
\Gamma_{\rm a}
, and
\Theta(z)
is the Heaviside step
function:
\Theta(z)=1
for
z>0
and
0
otherwise

Ito
;
Freidlin
. In this definition, the boundary local
time
\ell_{t,\rm a}
, despite its name, has units of length;
accordingly, the surface reaction rate
q_{\rm a}
, despite its name, has
units of inverse length. In analogy with bulk reactions, at each
arrival onto the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\rm a}
, the particle may
disappear with the probability
q_{\rm a}d\ell_{t,\rm a}
, so that
q_{\rm a}
is
the surface reaction rate per unit boundary local time
\ell_{t,\rm a}
.
In this Poissonian setting, the absorption event occurs at a random
time

\tau_{\mathrm{a}}=\inf\{t>0~:~\ell_{t,\rm a}>\hat{\ell}_{\rm a}\}

(94)

when the boundary local time
\ell_{t,\rm a}
exceeds an independent
random threshold
\hat{\ell}_{\rm a}
with the exponential
distribution
{\mathbb{P}}\{\hat{\ell}_{\rm a}>\ell\}=e^{-q_{\rm a}\ell}
. This
definition is a direct analogy of the exponential random lifetime

\hat{t}
of a particle disappearing in the bulk,
{\mathbb{P}}\{\hat{t}>t\}=e^{-\nu t}
, with a given rate
\nu
. The above probabilistic
description corresponds to a partially reactive boundary (with a
reactivity
\kappa=q_{\rm a}D
) and yields a standard Robin boundary
condition for macroscopic quantities such as the survival probability

Grebenkov20
.

In the same vein, branching events on the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}

can be described by using the boundary local time
\ell_{t,\rm c}
on

\Gamma_{\rm c}
:

\ell_{t,\rm c}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{D}{\varepsilon}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\Theta(\varepsilon-|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\Gamma_{\rm c}|).

(95)

At each arrival onto
\Gamma_{\rm c}
, the particle may branch into two
identical copies (offsprings) with the surface branching rate
q_{\rm c}\geq 0
per unit boundary local time
\ell_{t,\rm c}
. In other
words, the branching event occurs with the probability
q_{\rm c}d\ell_{t,\rm c}
at each arrival onto
\Gamma_{\rm c}
or, equivalently,
at a random time

\tau_{\mathrm{c}}=\inf\{t>0~:~\ell_{t,\rm c}>\hat{\ell}_{\rm c}\}

(96)

when
\ell_{t,\rm c}
exceeds an independent random threshold

\hat{\ell}_{\rm c}
with the exponential distribution:
{\mathbb{P}}\{\hat{\ell}_{\rm c}>\ell\}=e^{-q_{\rm c}\ell}
. Two newborn particles
are released from the location of the branching event and diffuse
independently. Each of these particles will disappear or branch at a
later time, and so on.

Even though the above probabilistic constructions of individual
absorption and branching events are identical (except that
q_{\rm a}
and

q_{\rm c}
may take different values), their effects onto the population
dynamics are dramatically distinct, as described in the main text.

Appendix B
Monte Carlo simulations

In this Appendix, we briefly describe Monte Carlo simulations that we
developed to illustrate our theoretical results. As both absorption
and branching events are triggered according to the boundary local
time, we adopt the recent escape-from-a-layer (EFL) approach to
simulate efficiently both the position
\bm{X}_{t}
of each diffusing
particle and its boundary local times
\ell_{t,\rm a}
and

\ell_{t,\rm c}

Ye25
. When the particle diffuses away from
the boundary, its boundary local times do not change, and its random
trajectory can be sampled by using the classical walk-on-spheres
algorithm
Muller56
. In turn, when the particle arrives into a
thin boundary layer, a basic simulation of multiple reflections on the
boundary would require very small timesteps. We replace this
time-consuming simulation step by a single escape of the particle from
the boundary layer, with an appropriate random increment of the
acquired boundary local time. This technique was shown to be
efficient for estimating various diffusion-reaction quantities such as
the first-reaction time distribution.

As the absorption and catalytic rates
q_{\mathrm{a}}
and
q_{\mathrm{c}}
can be
significantly different, we allow for using different widths

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\varepsilon_{c}
of boundary layers near the absorbing and
catalytic regions
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. We use the EFL
approach for simulating diffusion of each particle until its
absorption event on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
or branching event on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The simulation for one particle is stopped when either
\ell_{t,\mathrm{a}}

exceeds a random threshold
\hat{\ell}_{\mathrm{a}}
(drawn from the
exponential distribution with the rate
q_{\mathrm{a}}
), or
\ell_{t,\mathrm{c}}

exceeds another random threshold
\hat{\ell}_{\mathrm{c}}
(drawn from the
exponential distribution with the rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}
), as defined in
Appendix
A
. In both cases, the particle is killed, and
one records its position and the time interval from its release time

t_{\rm ini}
to the (current) stopping time
t_{\rm end}
. If the
particle was stopped on the catalytic region, the branching event
occurs, i.e., two new particles are released from the recorded
position, and the above simulation is repeated for each of them. In
this way, one creates a list of particles with their release and
stopping times. The simulation is continued until either there is no
active particle (extinction of the population), or the total number of
active particles exceeds a prescribed limit
n_{\rm max}
, or if the
release times of all active particles exceed a prescribed limit

t_{\rm max}
. Two last conditions are imposed artificially to handle
an eventual explosive growth of the population size. Once the
simulation is stopped, one can use the created list to evaluate the
number of particles co-existed at any given time
t
. Repeating the
above simulation
M
times, one can estimate the distribution of the
population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
, its moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, the generating
function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, and the probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
at any
time
t
.

For validation purposes, we focus on the geometric setting discussed
in the main text, namely, a circular annulus between two concentric
circles of radii
R
and
L
that represent the catalytic and
absorption regions, respectively. For illustrations in the main text,
we used
R=0.1
,
L=1
,
q_{\mathrm{a}}=\infty
,
D=1
, and three
values of
q_{\mathrm{c}}
to cover three regimes. The parameters of Monte
Carlo simulations are:
M=10^{5}
,
n_{\rm max}=10^{5}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}=10^{-4}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{a}}=10^{-20}
, and
t_{\rm max}=10
(since the
outer circle is considered here to be perfectly absorbing, the
simulation of any particle is stopped upon the first arrival onto this
circle, so that the use of a very small thickness
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{a}}
does not
slow down simulations). Monte Carlo results are in excellent
agreement with our theoretical predictions, as discussed in the main
text.

Appendix D
Probabilistic derivation of PDE

In this Appendix, we sketch an alternative derivation of
Eqs. (
12
), which is independent of the integral equation
(
11
), employs the generator of the underlying
stochastic process and relies on the properties of the boundary local
times. As its rigorous derivation is exceedingly elaborate and thus
beyond the scope of this paper (see, e.g.,
Delmas05
), we
restrict our discussion to the main ideas.

(i) As the number of particles cannot change in the bulk, the time
evolution of the generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
obeys the same
diffusion equation as the survival probability in the conventional
setting without catalytic branching:

\partial_{t}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=D\Delta G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(99)

where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator acting on
\bm{x}_{0}
. This is a
standard backward Fokker-Planck (or Kolmogorov) equation that
represents the time evolution due to diffusive displacements governed
by the Laplace operator (the generator of ordinary diffusion). This
equation is completed by the initial condition

G_{s}(0|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{s^{1}\}=s,

(100)

which states that a single particle was released at time
0
(strictly
speaking, one has to speak about the terminal condition but they are
equivalent for ordinary diffusion). As absorption and branching
events occur on the boundary, all the nontrivial features appear in
boundary conditions.

(ii) The key point is that each particle, after branching on

\Gamma_{\rm c}
, produces a tree of descendants, which is independent of the
others. Even through many particles may be present at time
t
, the
boundary conditions for the generating function can be deduced from
the very first branching event. To get a boundary condition, we set

\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
at time
0
and consider two options: (1) the
branching event may occur with the probability
q_{\rm c}d\ell_{0,\rm c}

(see Appendix
A
), in which case two newborn particles
produce their independent trees of descendants, yielding the total
population size
\mathcal{N}(t)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{N}_{2}(t)
and the contribution

{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{s^{\mathcal{N}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{N}_{2}(t)}\}=[G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}
; or (2) the
particle leaves the proximity of
\Gamma_{\rm c}
without branching, keeping
a single tree of descendants for a while (until the next return to

\Gamma_{\rm c}
) and thus contributing
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. As a consequence, the
generating function after this initial branching attempt is

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigl(1-q_{\rm c}d\ell_{0,\rm c}\bigr)+[G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}\,q_{\rm c}d\ell_{0,\rm c},

i.e., its change before and after an infinitesimal increment

d\ell_{0,\rm c}
is

dG_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm c}d\ell_{0,\rm c}\bigl([G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}-G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr).

If the right-hand side was proportional to an infinitesimal increment

dt
of physical time
t
, one could divide this relation by
dt
and
take the limit
dt\to 0
to express the left-hand side as a time
derivative. In our case, this change occurs on the boundary and is
proportional to the infinitesimal increment of the boundary local time
that results in the
spatial
normal derivative:

\partial_{n}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm c}\bigl([G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}-G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr)\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c}.

(101)

This is a
nonlinear
Robin-type boundary condition, which is
reminiscent to the boundary-catalytic branching. Note that if the
particle was split into
m
offsprings, the quadratic term would be
replaced by
[G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{m}
.

(iii) A similar argument is applicable to the absorbing region

\Gamma_{\rm a}
, in which case the absorption event destroys the particle and
thus gives the contribution
s^{0}=1
instead of
[G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{2}
,
resulting in a linear Robin boundary condition:

\partial_{n}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=q_{\rm a}\bigl[1-G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr]\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a}.

(102)

Finally, no change in
\mathcal{N}(t)
happens on the reflecting region

\Gamma_{\rm r}
, that yields the Neumann boundary condition:

\partial_{n}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}.

(103)

Appendix E
Interpretation of the propagator

As discussed in Sec.
III.1
, the conventional propagator

P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
has a standard probabilistic interpretation as the
probability density of finding the particle in a vicinity of point

\bm{x}
at time
t
, given that it started from
\bm{x}_{0}
at time
0
and
has not reacted on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
, nor on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
. In contrast, an
eventual exponential growth of the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
urges
for another interpretation for this quantity.

In the Supplementary Material to
Grebenkov26a
, the formalism of
surface-mediated autocatalytic dynamics was extended to describe the
statistics of the number of particles
\mathcal{N}^{A}(t)
in a subset
A\in\Omega
. In particular, the mean population size
N^{A}_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\mathcal{N}^{A}(t)\}
was shown to satisfy the initial-value problem:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N^{A}_{1}-D\Delta N^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(104a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}N^{A}_{1}-q_{\rm c}N^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(104b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}N^{A}_{1}+q_{\rm a}N^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(104c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}N^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r},

(104d)

\displaystyle N^{A}_{1}(0|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=1_{A}(\bm{x}_{0}),

(104e)

where
1_{A}(\bm{x}_{0})
is the indicator function of
A
. Comparison of
this PDE with Eqs. (
63
) for
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
reveals the
only difference in the initial condition. Moreover, as the PDE
problem (
104
) is linear, one can write its solution as

N^{A}_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{A}d\bm{x}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(105)

As the mean population size in any subset
A
of the domain can be
obtained by integrating the propagator over
A
,
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})d\bm{x}

can be interpreted as the mean population size in a
d\bm{x}
vicinity of
the point
\bm{x}
. In other words,
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
represents the
density (or concentration) of particles at time
t
for a population
that was initiated by a single particle released from
\bm{x}_{0}
at time

0
. As a consequence, the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
provides the
spatial information on the autocatalytic dynamics.

Appendix F
Circular annulus

As a basic example, we consider the circular annulus
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}~:~R<|\bm{x}|<L\}
with the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
at
the inner circle of radius
R
and the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\rm a}
at
the outer circle of radius
L
(here
\Gamma_{\rm r}=\emptyset
). In
Secs.
F.1
and
F.2
, we
determine the principal Steklov eigenmode and the principal Laplacian
eigenmode that control the asymptotic behavior. Section

F.3
shows the computation of the Laplace-transformed
propagator, whereas Sections
F.4
and

F.5
present the results for the mean
population size and the long-time limit of the generating function,
respectively.

F.1
Principal Steklov eigenmode

As discussed in the main text, the principal eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
of the
Steklov problem (
52
) determines the condition for the
critical regime. As the principal eigenfunction is rotationally
invariant, one can search it as
v_{0}(\bm{x})=A(\ln(|\bm{x}|/R)+B)
, with
two unknown constants
A
and
B
. Two boundary conditions fix the
eigenvalue,

\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{R[1/(q_{\rm a}L)+\ln(L/R)]}\,,

(106)

and the constant
B
so that

v_{0}(\bm{x})=A\biggl(\ln(L/|\bm{x}|)+\frac{1}{q_{\rm a}L}\biggr).

(107)

The remaining constant
A
is found from the normalization condition:

1=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,v_{0}^{2}(\bm{x})=2\pi RA^{2}(1/\mu_{0}R)^{2}

and thus

A=\mu_{0}\sqrt{R/(2\pi)}\,.

(108)

We also evaluate two integrals

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,v_{0}^{2}(\bm{x})=\mu_{0}^{2}R\biggl[\frac{L^{2}-R^{2}}{8}\bigl((1+2/(q_{\rm a}L))^{2}+1\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{R^{2}\ln(L/R)}{2}\bigl(1+2/(q_{\rm a}L)+\ln(L/R)\bigr)\biggr]

(109)

and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,v_{0}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi R}\,\mu_{0}}{4}\biggl[(L^{2}-R^{2})(1+2/(q_{\rm a}L))

\displaystyle-2R^{2}\ln(L/R)\biggr].

(110)

For a given rate
q_{\rm a}
, the eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
distinguishes the
subcritical (
q_{\rm c}<\mu_{0}
), critical (
q_{\rm c}=\mu_{0}
), and
supercritical (
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
) regimes. In the limit
q_{\rm a}=\infty
,
we have

\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{R\ln(L/R)}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}

(111)

and

\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,v_{0}^{2}(\bm{x})=\frac{L^{2}-R^{2}-2R^{2}\ln(L/R)(1+\ln(L/R))}{4R[\ln(L/R)]^{2}}\,.

(112)

F.2
Principal Laplacian eigenmode

In this section, we summarize the standard formulas to compute the
principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{-}
and the associated eigenfunction

u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})
by solving Eqs. (
28
). While this
computation can be easily adjusted to get other Laplacian eigenmodes

Carslaw
;
Thambynayagam
;
Grebenkov13
, we focus on the principal
eigenmode that determines the long-time behavior. Moreover, the
derived formulas are valid for the principal eigenpair
\{\lambda_{0}^{+},u_{0}^{+}(\bm{x})\}
of Eqs. (
42
) by replacing
q_{\rm c}
by
-q_{\rm c}
,
and for the principal eigenpair
\{\lambda_{0},u_{0}(\bm{x})\}
associated to

q_{\rm c}=0
and satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}u_{k}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(113a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm c},

(113b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}+q_{\rm a}u_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm a},

(113c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\rm r}.

(113d)

In the following, we treat separately three regimes.

Subcritical regime

In the subcritical regime (
q_{\rm c}<\mu_{0}
), the principal eigenvalue

\lambda_{0}^{-}
is strictly positive. A rotationally invariant
Laplacian eigenfunction
u^{-}(\bm{x})
can be searched as a linear
combination
c_{1}J_{0}(\alpha r)+c_{2}Y_{0}(\alpha r)
, where
r=|\bm{x}|
,

J_{n}(z)
and
Y_{n}(z)
are the Bessel functions of the first and second
kind, and
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
\alpha
are three parameters to be fixed
via two boundary conditions and normalization. Using the properties
of Bessel functions
Watson
, one has

u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})=A\,w_{0}(|\bm{x}|),

(114)

where

\displaystyle w_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\bigl[-\alpha Y_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}Y_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr]J_{0}(\alpha r)

\displaystyle-\bigl[-\alpha J_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}J_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr]Y_{0}(\alpha r),

(115)

and
A
is the normalization constant such that

1=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,[u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})]^{2}=A^{2}2\pi\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r).

(116)

In turn, the principal eigenvalue is determined as
\lambda_{0}^{-}=\alpha^{2}
, where
\alpha
is the smallest positive zero of the equation

\displaystyle\bigl(-\alpha Y_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}Y_{0}(\alpha L)\bigr)\bigl(-\alpha J_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}J_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(-\alpha J_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}J_{0}(\alpha L)\bigr)\bigl(-\alpha Y_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}Y_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr).

(117)

When
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, it is reduced to

\displaystyle Y_{0}(\alpha L)\bigl(-\alpha J_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}J_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle=J_{0}(\alpha L)\bigl(-\alpha Y_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}Y_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr).

(118)

Using the properties of Bessel functions
Watson
, one gets

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}\biggl(R^{2}\bigl([w^{\prime}_{0}(R)]^{2}+\alpha^{2}[w_{0}(R)]^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle-L^{2}\bigl([w^{\prime}_{0}(L)]^{2}+\alpha^{2}[w_{0}(L)]^{2}\bigr)\biggr).

(119)

We have
w_{0}(R)=2/(\pi R)
,
w^{\prime}_{0}(R)=-q_{\rm c}w_{0}(R)
, and
w^{\prime}_{0}(L)=-q_{\rm a}w_{0}(L)
so that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q_{\rm c}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}-L^{2}(q_{\rm a}^{2}+\alpha^{2})[w_{0}(L)]^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}\,.

For
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, this relation becomes

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q_{\rm c}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}-L^{2}[w^{\prime}_{0}(L)]^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}\,.

We also have

C_{0}^{-}=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})=2\pi A\frac{Rw^{\prime}_{0}(R)-Lw^{\prime}_{0}(L)}{\alpha^{2}}\,.

(120)

Critical regime

In the critical regime (
\lambda_{0}^{-}=0
), the PDE problems
(
28
) and (
52
) for the Laplacian and
Steklov eigenmodes are identical, so that the principal Laplacian
eigenfunction
u_{0}^{-}
is actually proportional to the principal
Steklov eigenfunction
v_{0}
(we recall that these eigenfunctions have
different normalizations):

u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})=A\biggl(\ln(L/|\bm{x}|)+\frac{1}{q_{\rm a}L}\biggr),

(121)

where the normalization constant
A
is obtained from

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,[u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})]^{2}=A^{2}2\pi\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,\bigl(\ln(L/r)+1/(q_{\rm a}L)\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi A^{2}}{4}\biggl(L^{2}\bigl[(2/(q_{\rm a}L)+1)^{2}+1\bigr]

\displaystyle-R^{2}\bigl[(2/(q_{\rm a}L)+1+2\ln(L/R))^{2}+1\bigr]\biggr).

Integrating
u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})
over
\Omega
, we also get

C_{0}^{-}=\pi A\biggl((L^{2}-R^{2})(1/2+1/(q_{\rm a}L))-R^{2}\ln(L/R)\biggr).

(122)

In the limit
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, we have

\displaystyle A^{-2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\biggl(L^{2}-R^{2}-2R^{2}\ln(L/R)(1+\ln(L/R))\biggr)

and

C_{0}^{-}=\frac{\pi}{2}A\bigl(L^{2}-R^{2}-2R^{2}\ln(L/R)\bigr).

(123)

Supercritical regime

In the supercritical regime (
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
), the principal eigenvalue

\lambda_{0}^{-}
is negative, so that
J_{n}(z)
and
Y_{n}(z)
should be
replaced by modified Bessel functions
I_{n}(z)
and
K_{n}(z)
of the
first and second kind, respectively. The remaining computation is
almost identical to that in the subcritical regime. The eigenfunction
reads

u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})=A\,w_{0}(|\bm{x}|),

(124)

where

\displaystyle w_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\bigl[-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr]I_{0}(\alpha r)

\displaystyle-\bigl[\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr]K_{0}(\alpha r),

(125)

and
A
is the normalization constant such that

1=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,[u_{0}^{-}(\bm{x})]^{2}=A^{2}2\pi\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r).

(126)

The associated eigenvalue is
\lambda_{0}^{-}=-\alpha^{2}
, where
\alpha

is the smallest positive zero of the equation

\displaystyle\bigl(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}K_{0}(\alpha L)\bigr)\bigl(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}I_{0}(\alpha L)\bigr)\bigl(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr).

When
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, it is reduced to

\displaystyle K_{0}(\alpha L)\bigl(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle=I_{0}(\alpha L)\bigl(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R)\bigr).

Using the properties of the modified Bessel functions
Watson
,
one gets

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}\biggl(R^{2}\bigl([w^{\prime}_{0}(R)]^{2}-\alpha^{2}[w_{0}(R)]^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle-L^{2}\bigl([w^{\prime}_{0}(L)]^{2}-\alpha^{2}[w_{0}(L)]^{2}\bigr)\biggr).

(127)

We have
w_{0}(R)=-1/R
,
w^{\prime}_{0}(R)=-q_{\rm c}w_{0}(R)
, and
w^{\prime}_{0}(L)=-q_{\rm a}w_{0}(L)
so that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q_{\rm c}^{2}-\alpha^{2})-L^{2}(q_{\rm a}^{2}-\alpha^{2})[w_{0}(L)]^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}\,.

For
q_{\rm a}=\infty
, this relation becomes

\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,w_{0}^{2}(r)=\frac{(q_{\rm c}^{2}-\alpha^{2})-L^{2}[w^{\prime}_{0}(L)]^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}\,.

(128)

We also have

C_{0}^{-}=2\pi A\frac{Lw^{\prime}_{0}(L)-Rw^{\prime}_{0}(R)}{\alpha^{2}}\,.

(129)

F.3
The propagator

Since the Laplacian eigenmodes are known for the circular annulus, the
propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
can be found through the spectral
expansion (
31
). To avoid technical details, we focus
on the Laplace-transformed propagator, for which the computation is
simpler:

\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(130)

Moreover, as our setting is rotationally invariant, we average

\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
over the angular coordinates. Its
rotationally-invariant contribution reads (see,
e.g.
Grebenkov21g
)

\tilde{P}^{-}(r,p|r_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi DW}\begin{cases}v^{L}(r_{0})\,v^{R}(r)\quad(r\leq r_{0}),\cr v^{L}(r)\,v^{R}(r_{0})\quad(r>r_{0}),\end{cases}

(131)

where
\alpha=\sqrt{p/D}
and

\displaystyle v^{R}(r)

\displaystyle=(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R))K_{0}(\alpha r)

\displaystyle-(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R))I_{0}(\alpha r),

(132a)

\displaystyle v^{L}(r)

\displaystyle=(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}I_{0}(\alpha L))K_{0}(\alpha r)

\displaystyle-(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}K_{0}(\alpha L))I_{0}(\alpha r),

(132b)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}K_{0}(\alpha L))(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R))

\displaystyle-(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R))(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha L)+q_{\rm a}I_{0}(\alpha L)).

(132c)

Since
v^{R}(R)=1/R
due to the Wronskian of the modified Bessel
functions, one gets

\tilde{P}^{-}(R,p|r_{0})=\frac{v^{L}(r_{0})}{2\pi RDW}\,.

(133)

Using the properties of modified Bessel functions, we also compute

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,v^{L}(r)=-\frac{q_{\rm a}}{\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{R}{\alpha}\biggl[\alpha\bigl(I_{1}(\alpha L)K_{1}(\alpha R)-K_{1}(\alpha L)I_{1}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle+q_{\rm a}\bigl(I_{0}(\alpha L)K_{1}(\alpha R)+K_{0}(\alpha L)I_{1}(\alpha R)\bigr)\biggr]

(134)

and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,v^{R}(r)=\frac{q_{\rm c}}{\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{L}{\alpha}\biggl[\alpha\bigl(I_{1}(\alpha L)K_{1}(\alpha R)-K_{1}(\alpha L)I_{1}(\alpha R)\bigr)

\displaystyle-q_{\rm c}\bigl(I_{0}(\alpha R)K_{1}(\alpha L)+K_{0}(\alpha R)I_{1}(\alpha L)\bigr)\biggr].

(135)

In the limit
q_{\rm a}=\infty
,
v^{R}(r)
remains unchanged, whereas

v^{L}(r)
and
W
are reduced to

\displaystyle v^{L}(r)

\displaystyle=I_{0}(\alpha L)K_{0}(\alpha r)-K_{0}(\alpha L)I_{0}(\alpha r),

(136a)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=K_{0}(\alpha L)(\alpha I_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}I_{0}(\alpha R))

\displaystyle-I_{0}(\alpha L)(-\alpha K_{1}(\alpha R)+q_{\rm c}K_{0}(\alpha R)).

(136b)

F.4
Mean population size

For the circular annulus, an explicit computation of the mean
population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is possible in the Laplace domain. For
this purpose, it is sufficient to integrate the Laplace-transformed
propagator
\tilde{P}^{-}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\Omega
that yields

\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})=\frac{1}{p}\biggl(1+\frac{q_{\rm c}\,v^{L}(r_{0})-q_{\rm a}\,v^{R}(r_{0})}{W}\biggr),

(137)

where
v^{L}(r_{0})
,
v^{R}(r_{0})
and
W
are given by
Eqs. (
132
). When the starting point is uniformly
distributed in the bulk, one deals with

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{1}(p|\circ)=\frac{2}{L^{2}-R^{2}}\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\,\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r)

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{2}{(L^{2}-R^{2})pW}\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r\bigl[q_{\rm c}\,v^{L}(r_{0})-q_{\rm a}\,v^{R}(r_{0})\bigr],

which can be found explicitly by using Eqs. (
134
,

135
). While it is possible to invert the Laplace
transform by identifying the poles of
\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})
and
applying the residue theorem, we employ the numerical inversion via
the Talbot algorithm
Talbot79
.

F.5
Long-time limit of the generating function

Since the annulus is rotationally invariant, the dependence of the
generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
on
\bm{x}_{0}
is reduced to the radial
coordinate
r_{0}=|\bm{x}_{0}|
. As a consequence, the Green’s function

{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})
in the integral equation (
45
) is
averaged over the angular coordinate, yielding

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(r_{0})

\displaystyle=q_{\rm c}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln(L/r_{0})+1/(q_{\rm a}L)}{\ln(L/R)+1/(q_{\rm c}R)+1/(q_{\rm a}L)}\,.

(139)

Averaging the integral equation (
45
) over the angular
coordinate of
\bm{x}_{0}
, we reduce this integral equation to the
algebraic equation:

\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|r_{0})=\mathfrak{g}(r_{0})\,\bigl(\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|R)-[\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|R)]^{2}\bigr).

(140)

Setting
r_{0}=R
, we arrive at the quadratic equation, which has two
solutions:
\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|R)=0
and

\bar{G}_{s}(\infty|R)=2-\frac{1}{\mathfrak{g}(R)}=1-\frac{1/(q_{\rm c}R)}{\ln(L/R)+1/(q_{\rm a}L)}\,.

(141)

Using Eq. (
106
) for the principal Steklov eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
,
one can rewrite the nontrivial solution as

G_{s}(\infty|R)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{q_{\rm c}}\,.

(142)

Since the generating function cannot exceed
1
, the nontrivial
solution is possible if and only if
q_{\rm c}>\mu_{0}
, i.e., in the
supercritical regime, in agreement with our general conclusion in
Sec.
IV.4
. In turn,
G_{s}(\infty|R)=1
is the
only possible solution for the subcritical and critical regimes. In
these regimes, Eq. (
140
) immediately implies
that
G_{s}(\infty|r_{0})=1
for any starting point.

When the starting point is uniformly distributed, one has

G_{s}(\infty|\circ)=1+\mathfrak{g}(\circ)\,\bigl([G_{s}(\infty|R)]^{2}-1\bigr),

(143)

where

\mathfrak{g}(\circ)=\frac{(L^{2}-R^{2})(2/(q_{\rm a}L)+1)-2R^{2}\ln(L/R)}{2(L^{2}-R^{2})[\ln(L/R)+1/(q_{\rm c}R)+1/(q_{\rm a}L)]}\,.

(144)

We also note that the above analysis was done for
s<1
. In turn,
one has
G_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1
due to the normalization of probabilities in
any regime.

Appendix G
Numerical solution of integral equations

In this Appendix, we present a numerical scheme for solving integral
equations (
25
,
26
) to determine the
probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, as well as the generating function

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, in the case of the circular annulus with radii
R
and

L
. As this domain is rotationally invariant,
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
are functions of the radial coordinate
r_{0}=|\bm{x}_{0}|
.
As a consequence, the integral equation (
26
) becomes

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=S(t|R)+q_{\rm c}D2\pi R\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P(R,t^{\prime}|R)

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\bar{Q}_{0}(t-t^{\prime})-\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}(t-t^{\prime})\biggr),

(145)

where we used the shortcut notation
\bar{Q}_{0}(t)=\bar{Q}_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

for any
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}
such that
|\bm{x}_{0}|=R
, whereas
S(t|R)

and
P(R,t^{\prime}|R)
are shortcut notations for
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

P(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})
evaluated at
|\bm{x}|=|\bm{x}_{0}|=R
, see
Eqs. (
20
,
21
). As the equation for

\bar{G}_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
just includes the additional factor
(1-s)
in
front of
S(t|R)
, see Eq. (
19
), we do not discuss it
separately.

The above equation (
145
) is a nonlinear Volterra
equation of the second kind. Despite the convolution form, the common
Laplace transform is not efficient because of nonlinearity. Another
difficulty is that the kernel
P(R,t^{\prime}|R)
is weakly singular that
follows from the short-time behavior of the propagator (see below).
In the following, we describe a basic scheme to overcome these issues
and to get a numerical solution of Eq. (
145
) and
related integral equations. We expect that the computational
efficiency of our numerical method may be further improved.

G.1
Short-time behavior of the kernel

Let us first discuss the weak singularity of the kernel
P(R,t^{\prime}|R)
. To
see this point, we inspect the auxiliary function

f(t)=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}_{0}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

(146)

in the general setting. We assume that
\Gamma_{\rm a}
and
\Gamma_{\rm c}
are
disconnected regions of the boundary (as in the case of the circular
annulus). As a consequence,
\Gamma_{\rm a}
and
\Gamma_{\rm c}
are separated
from each other by a strictly positive distance, so that the
short-time behavior of
f(t)
is not affected by
\Gamma_{\rm a}
. The
asymptotics of
f(t)
as
t\to 0
can thus be found by considering
reflected Brownian motion started on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
. In the leading order,
the curvature of
\Gamma_{\rm c}
does not matter so that one can approximate
the propagator by that in the upper half-space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
,
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx 2e^{-|\bm{y}-\bm{y}_{0}|^{2}/(4Dt)}/(4\pi Dt)^{d/2}
, where the points

\bm{x}=(\bm{y},0)
and
\bm{x}_{0}=(\bm{y}_{0},0)
lie on the hyperplane
\partial{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
(the factor
2
comes from the reflecting condition). At
short times, the integral over the lateral coordinate
\bm{y}
yields

1/\sqrt{\pi Dt}
, whereas the second integral over
\bm{y}_{0}
gives the
area of
\Gamma_{\rm c}
:

f(t)\simeq\frac{|\Gamma_{\rm c}|}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}+O(1)\quad(t\to 0).

(147)

In the particular case of the circular annulus, the rotational
invariance implies
f(t)=|\Gamma_{\rm c}|^{2}P(R,t|R)
, from which

P(R,t|R)=\frac{f(t)}{|\Gamma_{\rm c}|^{2}}\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi R\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\quad(t\to 0).

(148)

In order to treat this weak singularity explicitly, we define

p(t)=2\pi Rq_{\rm c}D\,P(R,t|R)\,\sqrt{t},

(149)

which has a finite limit as
t\to 0
:
p(0)=q_{\rm c}\sqrt{D/\pi}
. Note
also that
S(t|R)
is exponentially close to
1
at short times.
We rewrite the above equation (
145
) as

\bar{Q}_{0}(t)=S(t|R)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,t^{\prime-1/2}\,p(t^{\prime})\,v(t-t^{\prime}),

(150)

with the initial condition
\bar{Q}_{0}(0)=1
, and

v(t)=\bar{Q}_{0}(t)-\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}(t).

(151)

G.2
Numerical computation of the kernel

To proceed, we need to evaluate
S(t|R)
and
p(t)
. These quantities
can be found either in terms of spectral expansions over the Laplacian
eigenfunctions, or via the inverse Laplace transform.
In the Laplace domain, we can use Eq. (
133
) to get

\tilde{P}(R,p|R)
and Eq. (
137
) to get

\tilde{S}(p|R)
, by setting
q_{\rm c}=0
. We employ the Talbot
algorithm for the numerical inversion of the Laplace transform. As
both
P(R,t|R)
and
S(t|R)
rapidly decay at infinity, one needs a
more accurate computation for large
t
. In the long-time regime, we
approximate these functions by the leading term of their spectral
expansions:

\displaystyle P(R,t|R)

\displaystyle\approx[u_{0}(R)]^{2}\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}},

(152)

\displaystyle S(t|R)

\displaystyle\approx u_{0}(R)\,C_{0}\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}},

(153)

where the eigenpair
\{\lambda_{0},u_{0}\}
was defined in
Eq. (
113
),
C_{0}
is the integral of
u_{0}
over

\Omega
, and their computation is described in
Sec.
F.2
(where we have to set
q_{\rm c}=0
).

G.3
Discretization

Choosing a small timestep
\delta=t/n
and equally spaced grid, we
discretize Eq. (
150
) as

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{0}(n\delta)\approx S(n\delta|R)+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}w_{j}\,v((n-j)\delta),

(154)

where
w_{j}
are suitable weights.

In a basic quadrature, we use piecewise-constant approximations for
functions
p(t^{\prime})
and
v(t-t^{\prime})
on each interval
(\delta j,\delta(j+1))
, so that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t^{\prime}}}\,p(t^{\prime})\,v(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{v_{n-j}+v_{n-j-1}}{2}

\displaystyle\times\underbrace{(\sqrt{t_{j+1}}-\sqrt{t_{j}})(p_{j}+p_{j+1})}_{=a_{j}},

for each
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, with
p_{j}=p(j\delta)
. As a
consequence, we have

w_{j}=\frac{a_{j}+a_{j-1}}{2}\quad(j=0,1,\ldots,n),

(155)

where we set
a_{-1}=a_{n}=0
.

Next, we separate the term with
j=0
to get the quadratic equation:

\bar{Q}_{0}(n\delta)=w_{0}\bigl(\bar{Q}(n\delta)-\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}(n\delta)\bigr)+f_{n},

(156)

where

f_{n}=S(n\delta|R)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\,\bigl[\bar{Q}((n-j)\delta)-\bar{Q}_{0}^{2}((n-j)\delta)\bigr].

(157)

A suitable solution of the quadratic equation reads

\bar{Q}_{0}(n\delta)=\frac{2f_{n}}{1-w_{0}+\sqrt{(1-w_{0})^{2}+4w_{0}f_{n}}}\,.

(158)

For the examples shown in the main text, we used
\delta=10^{-3}

that required the evaluation of
\bar{Q}_{0}(n\delta)
for
n
ranging
from
1
to
10000
to get the probability
\bar{Q}_{0}(t)
up to
t=10
. The above scheme was implemented in Matlab and took less than a
minute on a personal laptop. Our numerical results remained almost
unaffected upon diminishing
\delta
by factor
2
.

G.4
Solutions for
k>0

In the same way, one can discretize Eq. (
25
) with
k>0

as

Q_{k}(n\delta)\approx S(n\delta|R)\delta_{k,1}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}w_{j}\,v((n-j)\delta),

(159)

with

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=Q_{k}(t)\bigl(2Q_{0}(t)-1\bigr)+v_{0}(t),

(160a)

\displaystyle v_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}Q_{i}(t)Q_{n-i}(t),

(160b)

where we wrote separately the term
2Q_{0}(t)Q_{k}(t)
from
H_{k}(t|\bm{x})
.
We get then

\displaystyle Q_{k}(n\delta)\approx w_{0}Q_{k}(n\delta)\bigl(2Q_{0}(n\delta)-1\bigr)+f_{n},

(161)

where

f_{n}=S(n\delta|R)\delta_{k,1}+w_{0}v_{0}(n\delta)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\,v((n-j)\delta).

(162)

As a consequence, one obtains

Q_{k}(n\delta)\approx\frac{f_{n}}{1-w_{0}\bigl(2Q_{0}(n\delta)-1\bigr)}\,.

(163)

G.5
Computation of the moments

The same technique can be used to compute the moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
by
solving the integral equation (
70
). In contrast to the
generating function, the moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, as well as the kernel

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
in Eq. (
70
) grow exponentially in the
supercritical regime. This issue can be easily resolved by rescaling
the moment
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
by an appropriate exponential factor given by
the asymptotic relation (
80
). In fact,
setting

\bar{N}_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})e^{-kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|},

(164)

one can rewrite Eq. (
70
) as

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\bar{S}^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

(165)

\displaystyle+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\bar{P}^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,\bar{F}_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

where

\displaystyle\bar{S}^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=S^{-}(t|\bm{x}_{0})e^{-kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|},

\displaystyle\bar{P}^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})e^{-kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}

and

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{k}(t|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=F_{k}(t|\bm{x})e^{-kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}\bar{N}_{j}(t|\bm{x})\bar{N}_{k-j}(t|\bm{x}).

(166)

In this way, the integral equation (
165
) does not contain
any exponentially growing functions in the supercritical regime.

Since the right-hand side of Eq. (
165
) does not contain

\bar{N}_{k}
, this equation can be solved iteratively, starting from

\bar{N}_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which can be obtained directly via the inverse
Laplace transform of the exact expression (
137
).
Moreover, one can apply a fast Fourier transform for rapidly
evaluating the convolution in time. Despite the numerical efficiency
of this technique, we keep using the basic discretization scheme
discussed above that handles the weak singularity of the kernel.

For any fixed
k=2,3,\ldots
, we define the kernel

p(t)=2\pi Rq_{\rm c}D\,P^{-}(R,t|R)e^{-kDt|\lambda_{0}^{-}|}\,\sqrt{t}

(167)

and approximate the above integral equation (
165
) as

\bar{N}_{k}(n\delta)\approx\bar{S}^{-}(n\delta|R)+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}w_{j}\bar{F}_{k}((n-j)\delta),

(168)

with the weights
w_{j}
given by Eq. (
155
), except that
p_{j}=p(\delta j)
are now determined by the propagator
\bar{P}^{-}(R,t|R)

via Eq. (
167
). Note that the leading-order term in the
short-time behavior of this propagator does not depend on the
reactivity so that
p(0)=q_{\rm c}\sqrt{D/\pi}
remains unchanged.
