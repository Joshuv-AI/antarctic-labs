Distortion-engineered C1-symmetric inorganic molecular cages enable tunable chiroptical nonlinear optics with broad infrared transparency
Authors: Chao Wang, Chensheng Lin, Xiaoying Shang, Xingwang Zhu, Shunda Yang, Tao Yan, Chengming Hu, Min Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chiroptical materials combining chirality with second-harmonic generation are attractive for polarization-resolved photonics, yet examples integrating strong chiroptical nonlinear activity, broad infrared transparency and inorganic robustness remain limited. Here we report a purely inorganic strategy in which molecular-level geometric distortion of intrinsically C1-symmetric phosphorus–chalcogen cages tunes second-harmonic generation circular dichroism (SHG-CD). Site-selective homologous substitution yields enantiopure molecular cage crystals, S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se and S/R-AsP3S4Se, spanning a controlled distortion series. Across this series, SHG-CD correlates with distortion-driven molecular asymmetry, indicating that the cage dipole moment can serve as a practical descriptor for chiroptical nonlinear response within this isostructural molecular cage family. These results establish inorganic molecular cages as a tunable chiroptical nonlinear platform for infrared-transparent chiroptical nonlinear materials.

Introduction

Chirality endows solids with optical and electronic responses that are inaccessible to achiral media, including circular dichroism (CD), circularly polarized luminescence and chirality-dependent carrier or spin behavior1–5. When chirality is coupled to second-order nonlinear optics (NLO), a particularly information-rich response emerges: a chiral crystal can generate second-harmonic light with an intensity that depends on the handedness of the incident circular polarization6–10. Under excitation with left- versus right-circularly polarized light, the second-harmonic generation (SHG) intensities can differ, producing second-harmonic generation circular dichroism (SHG-CD)9,11–14. Because SHG is intrinsically sensitive to symmetry and electronic polarization, SHG-CD is not only a performance metric but also a mechanistic readout of how microscopic chiral polarization is translated into macroscopic, polarization-selective frequency conversion. A central objective for chiroptical NLO materials is therefore to move beyond empirical discovery and establish transferable structure–property relationships that predict the magnitude and sign of SHG-CD from chemically tunable descriptors.

Despite major progress, such predictive control remains difficult. Organic and organic–inorganic hybrid chiroptical materials dominate the field because synthetic chemistry provides numerous handles to introduce chirality and tailor electronic structure and packing (Fig. 1a)15–19. In these platforms, large asymmetry factors and sizeable SHG coefficients have been achieved, and systematic molecular modification is often straightforward17,20–25. However, organic constituents impose intrinsic constraints that become particularly acute for infrared photonics. Electronic absorption edges and vibrational bands can restrict transparency as one extends beyond the near-infrared26, while their systematic long-term stability and laser-induced damage thresholds (LIDTs) may be insufficient for high-power or harsh-environment applications. These considerations motivate the development of purely inorganic chiroptical materials that could couple broad infrared transparency to improved stability. Yet in all-inorganic systems, the mechanistic levers that control SHG-CD are rarely explicit, and chemical dials for tuning the response across a closely related family are often limited. A common pathway to chirality in inorganic crystals is crystallographic: chirality emerges from long-range helical assemblies, chiral channels or framework-level distortions in the lattice (Fig. 1b)27,28. While such routes can yield chiral space groups, the local chirality density of the underlying building blocks may be low, and SHG-CD responses can be modest. More fundamentally, conventional design strategies for inorganic second-order NLO materials focus on ensuring noncentrosymmetry and enhancing polar alignment. These principles are effective for rationalizing whether SHG is allowed and for maximizing SHG intensity, but SHG-CD additionally requires handedness selectivity in the nonlinear response. Lattice-level symmetry arguments alone rarely provide a quantitative handle for tuning the SHG-CD response across a chemically related series. This gap suggests that a mechanism-forward strategy should begin with a local descriptor that is (i) chemically addressable, (ii) directly coupled to electronic polarization, and (iii) capable of propagating from molecular structure to bulk chiroptical response.Fig. 1The train of thought to construct novel purely inorganic chiroptical crystals.a Typical strategy for the construction of previous organic-inorganic chiroptical crystals. b The construction method of traditional purely inorganic chiral crystals. c The strategy and advantages for constructing C1-symmetric inorganic molecular cage crystals in this work.

Here, we adopt such a strategy by using intrinsically chiral, C1-symmetric inorganic molecular cages as the primary chiral building blocks and by treating geometric distortion and the associated molecular dipole moment as mechanistic control parameters for SHG-CD. The premise is that geometric distortion redistributes electron chirality within a discrete chiral unit, modulating the degree of molecular asymmetry that can be captured by the molecular dipole moment. When such units assemble into enantiopure crystals, the collective chiroptical polarization should reflect how distortion-controlled molecular polarization is encoded in a dense array of chiral cages and transmitted through the crystal packing. In this view, the macroscopic SHG-CD response is not merely an emergent property of a helical lattice, but the bulk manifestation of a tunable degree of molecular asymmetry in intrinsically chiral inorganic units.

Phosphorus–chalcogen molecular cages provide an attractive scaffold for implementing this concept. Strong covalency within the phosphorus–chalcogen framework supports robust cage formation and can favor sizeable second-order susceptibilities. Importantly, C1 symmetry represents the lowest point-group symmetry: the molecule contains no inversion center, mirror plane or rotation axis. In enantiopure form, C1-symmetric molecular cages therefore embody ultimate symmetry breaking and offer maximal structural degrees of freedom (Fig. 1c)29,30. Compared with higher-symmetry chiral motifs (for example, C2- or C3-symmetric systems), C1-symmetric molecular cages permit a broader landscape of distortional modes, enabling tuning of molecular asymmetry through multiple chemically accessible pathways without altering the overall platform, a feature required for mechanism-led design. However, C1-symmetric phosphorus–chalcogen molecular cages remain rare, and general strategies to translate their intrinsic chirality into strong and tunable SHG-CD responses in purely inorganic crystals are still underdeveloped.

We therefore propose molecular-level geometric distortion engineering as a route to controllably tune SHG-CD response in inorganic molecular cage crystals. Our working hypothesis is that site-selective homologous substitution within the cage can systematically vary geometric distortion, thereby influencing the degree of molecular asymmetry. Specifically, substituting As for P and Se for S provides a chemically conservative yet structurally impactful perturbation: it modifies bond lengths, angles and local electron distribution while preserving an isostructural family of chiral cages. This substitution series is designed to create a graded set of distortions, enabling a direct test of whether the cage dipole moment tracks macroscopic SHG-CD behavior as a molecular descriptor. In contrast to lattice-centric approaches, this design is bottom-up: it prioritizes control of chirality and asymmetry at the molecular-unit level, while still enabling crystallization into enantiopure solids with strong second-order responses and broad infrared transparency. Using this strategy, we synthesize a family of enantiopure C1-symmetric molecular cage crystals, including S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se and S/R-AsP3S4Se. Across this series, we observe strong SHG responses, pronounced SHG-CD behavior, broad infrared transmission and high LIDTs, demonstrating that inorganic molecular cage crystals can serve as a tunable chiroptical NLO platform with broad infrared transparency and measurable infrared SHG activity. Crucially, the controlled substitution series provides a chemical dial to interrogate mechanism: by comparing cages with systematically varied distortion, we identify how changes in molecular asymmetry correlate with changes in SHG-CD performance, supporting a distortion–dipole–response relationship that elevates molecular geometry and electron chirality asymmetry to explicit predictors of macroscopic chiroptical NLO behavior. This mechanistic handle expands the design toolbox for polarization-resolved frequency conversion and supports the development of robust chiroptical materials with broad infrared transparency, motivating future exploration of their potential in infrared photonics.

Results and discussion

Molecular cage configuration design

Among the four P sites and five S sites in the [α-P4S5] configuration, the relatively lowest-energy P and S sites are identified as P4 and S5, respectively (Fig. 2). To maximally preserve cage integrity and symmetry, new C1‑symmetric molecular cages were designed via single or dual substitution at these lowest-energy sites with homologous elements. The formation of the C1‑symmetric [P4S5] from the C3v‑symmetric [P4S3] precursor can be rationalized as a symmetry-lowering process through sulfur addition. It can be inferred that the process occurs in two key steps. First, the C3v symmetry is reduced to Cs by cleaving one P–P bond in the basal phosphorus triangle of [P4S3] and inserting a sulfur atom into the cleaved region, where it bonds to the involved P atoms, ultimately yielding the Cs‑symmetric [P4S4] (Figs. 2a – c). Subsequently, symmetry is further lowered from Cs to C1. To achieve this, the final sulfur atom must attach to one of the two basal P atoms located outside the molecular mirror plane of [P4S4] (Fig. 2c); attachment to P sites within the mirror plane would not alter the symmetry. Since these two basal P sites in [P4S4] are equivalent but reside on opposite sides of the mirror plane, sulfur can attack either site to form a P = S double bond, resulting in the C1‑symmetric enantiomers [S-P4S5] and [R-P4S5], respectively (Fig. 2c–g). For the case of selenium substitution at the S5 site, the first step involves inserting a Se atom into the cleaved bond region of [P4S3] to form the Cs‑symmetric [P4S3Se] (Fig. 2a–d). In the second step, a S atom is added to the two basal P sites on either side of the mirror plane in [P4S3Se], respectively, yielding C1‑symmetric [S-P4S4Se] and [R-P4S4Se] (Fig. 2d–h). Notably, the design of [S/R-P4S5] and [S/R-P4S4Se] described above does not involve P-site substitution. To incorporate arsenic substitution at the P4 site, the precursor [P4S3] must first be replaced with the isostructural, C3v‑symmetric [AsP3S3] (Fig. 2b). Following an analogous two-step symmetry-lowering pathway—first to the Cs‑symmetric [AsP3S4] (Fig. 2b–e) and then to the C1‑symmetric [S/R-AsP3S5]—the target cage is obtained (Fig. 2e–i). Similarly, for concurrent substitution of As at P4 site and Se at S5 site, the process begins with [AsP3S3]. A Se atom is inserted into the cleaved bond region to form Cs‑symmetric [AsP3S3Se] (Fig. 2b–f), followed by sulfur addition to the two basal P sites, resulting in [S-AsP3S4Se] and [R-AsP3S4Se], respectively (Fig. 2f–j). It is important to note that the C1‑symmetric cages outlined in this design scheme (Fig. 2g–j) subsequently undergo rearrangement to reach their respective energy-minimized configurations, which represent the final, stable structures of interest (Fig. 2k–m). Crystal structure

Compared to the parent [S/R-P4S5], [S/R-AsP3S5], [S/R-P4S4Se], and [S/R-AsP3S4Se] exhibit only minor and reasonable geometric distortion in bond lengths and angles (Supplementary Fig. 1). Here, we synthesized S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se, and S/R-AsP3S4Se using a mild solution method similar to that for α-P4S531,32. The absolute configurations of all chiral crystals were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. The absolute values of the Flack parameter and μ were less than 0.1, meeting the strict reliability criteria for absolute stereochemical determination. Interestingly, the eight chiral crystals are completely isostructural, all crystallizing in the chiral space group P21 with very similar unit cell parameters. As shown in Fig. 3, the crystal structures of all eight consist of their respective C1-symmetric molecular cages stacked along the 21 screw axis (crystallographic b-axis). The crystal structures of the S- and R- enantiomers are mirror images, with the overall dipole moments maximally aligned along the b-axis (Supplementary Fig. 2). Since the achiral physicochemical properties of the S- and R- enantiomers are identical, for convenience in discussion not involving chirality, the prefixes S- and R- will be omitted. Unlike most previously reported chiral organic or organic-inorganic crystals synthesized via solution processing, the purely inorganic P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se exhibit excellent stability against water and oxygen. Furthermore, as molecular crystals, P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se have thermal decomposition temperatures above 165 °C (Supplementary Fig. 3), showing strong thermal stability among molecular crystals33–37. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the expected stoichiometry, and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) verified the high phase purity of the tested samples (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5).Fig. 3Crystal structure.Constituent C1-symmetric molecular cages and their crystal structures for a S/R-P4S5. b S/R-AsP3S5. c S/R-P4S4Se. d S/R-AsP3S4Se.

Performance characterization

To probe the linear optical characteristics of P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se, UV-vis-NIR diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a, the experimental bandgaps span 2.40–2.71 eV, noticeably larger than those of established IR NLO crystals such as AgGaSe2 (1.8 eV)38,39, ZnGeP2 (1.9 eV)38,40, LiInSe2 (1.8 eV)41, and GaSe (2.0 eV)42. First-principles calculations yield indirect bandgaps of 2.353 eV (P4S5), 2.287 eV (AsP3S5), 2.095 eV (P4S4Se), and 2.056 eV (AsP3S4Se), which are systematically lower than the experimental results (Supplementary Fig. 7). Partial density of states (PDOS) analysis reveals that in P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se, both the valence band maxima (VBMs) and conduction band minima (CBMs) are dominated by P-3p and S-3p orbitals (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results confirm that the optical responses of these crystals are largely dictated by the electronic structures of their molecular cage motifs. IR transmission spectra reveal pronounced absorption bands between 500–700 cm−1, accompanied by weaker features in the 400–500 cm−1 region (Supplementary Fig. 6b–e). These absorptions are assigned to stretching vibrations of P–S, As–S, and P–Se bonds, consistent with density functional theory (DFT) simulations (Supplementary Fig. 9). The most intense bands in both experiment and theory correspond to terminal P = S double-bond stretching modes. The first strong vibrational absorptions occur at 666 cm−1 for P4S5, 658 cm−1 for AsP3S5, 648 cm−1 for P4S4Se, and 645 cm−1 for AsP3S4Se, corresponding to approximately 15.0, 15.2, 15.4, and 15.5 μm, respectively. The short-wave optical limits are given by the diffuse-reflectance-derived electronic absorption edges of the four crystals, which lie in the visible region (Supplementary Table 5). For clarity, we now distinguish the individual optical limits of each crystal from the conservative common high-transmission interval shared by the whole family. In this sense, the transmission windows of P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se cover from the visible region to the long-wave infrared (LWIR) region (0.52–15 μm), which denotes the overlapping high-transparency interval common to all four crystals, in contrast to many previously reported chiroptical materials containing organic constituents whose transparent ranges were confined to the near-IR. Additionally, we observed that the optical bandgap and IR transparency range of these molecular cage crystals correlate positively with the highest occupied molecular orbital-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO-LUMO) gap and highest vibrational frequency of their respective building blocks, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This micro-to-macro property tuning also stems from geometric distortion engineering.

Due to laser source wavelength limitations and the materials’ own absorption edges, we employed a combination of Kurtz–Perry powder SHG measurements43 at 2050 nm, micro-area SHG measurements (Supplementary Fig. 11) in the 920–1080 nm range, and frequency-dependent calculations to assess the infrared NLO potential of P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se. Using commercially available AgGaS2 (AGS) crystal (0.47–13 μm) as a reference, which also has a broad transmission window, the SHG responses of polycrystalline P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se samples within the 150–212 μm particle size range were measured to be 1.1, 2.0, 2.4, and 2.8 times that of AGS at a laser wavelength of 2050 nm, respectively, and they exhibited phase-matching behavior (Supplementary Fig. 6f). The macroscopic SHG response of these crystals is positively correlated with the hyperpolarizability of the constituent molecular cages (Supplementary Fig. 10c). As shown in Fig. 4a–d and Supplementary Fig. 12a-d, our custom-built micro-area SHG testing system demonstrated very strong SHG responses from single crystals of S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se, and S/R-AsP3S4Se over a broad excitation wavelength range of 920–1080 nm. Subsequently, we calculated the frequency-dependent NLO coefficients of these crystals based on first-principles calculations (Supplementary Fig. 13). Together with the experimentally observed SHG response at 2050 nm, these results suggest the broadband infrared NLO potential of P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se, while direct LWIR chiroptical verification remains for future study. Furthermore, the SHG intensities of the single crystals P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, and AsP3S4Se exhibited a quadratic dependence on pulsed laser power, consistent with the two-photon nature of the second-order NLO process. When the excitation power continued to increase, causing a decrease in SHG intensity, the corresponding power density was defined as the LIDT. The LIDT values for R-P4S5, R-AsP3S5, R-P4S4Se, and R-AsP3S4Se were 150.34 GW/cm2, 133.86 GW/cm2, 115.67 GW/cm2, and 104.30 GW/cm2, respectively (Fig. 4e–h). For context, Supplementary Table 6 summarizes representative LIDT data of reported chiroptical crystals together with their laser wavelength, pulse duration, repetition rate, spot size, and threshold power when available. Because LIDT is highly sensitive to testing conditions, this literature comparison should be regarded as qualitative rather than as a strict benchmark across different systems. Nevertheless, our crystals compare favorably with reported chiroptical materials, particularly under broadly similar femtosecond excitation conditions, highlighting their promise for high-power photonic applications. The inherent structural asymmetry of C1-symmetric molecular cage crystals dictates that they should exhibit highly anisotropic SHG responses. Therefore, we investigated the SHG response anisotropy of single crystals S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se, and S/R-AsP3S4Se by measuring SHG intensity as a function of linear polarization angle. As shown in Fig. 5a–d and Supplementary Fig. 14a–d, the polarization-dependent SHG intensities in these single crystals exhibit significant anisotropy with different patterns, likely related to the different measured crystal planes. Subsequently, we determined the measured crystal planes using the Rigaku single-crystal diffractometer (Supplementary Fig. 15). Using Supplementary Equations (3) and (9), we fitted the angular-dependent linearly polarized SHG profiles to capture their overall anisotropic behavior. The fits reproduce the main features of the experimental profiles well, with R2 values of 0.94–0.99 and the root-mean-square error (RMSE) values of 0.012–0.073 (Supplementary Table 7). Standardized residual-versus-angle plots are provided in Supplementary Fig. 16. These residual plots reveal noticeable systematic deviations in some angular regions, indicating that the fitting equations capture the dominant angular dependence but do not fully reproduce all local experimental details. Such deviations are likely associated with the use of naturally exposed and unpolished crystal faces, alignment sensitivity inherent to the reflective micro-area SHG geometry, finite numerical aperture and collection conditions, and possible inhomogeneity arising from internal crystal defects. We therefore treat the fitting as a semi-quantitative analysis of the overall angular dependence. To investigate the chiroptical properties of these C1-symmetric molecular cage crystals, we replaced the half-wave plate with a quarter-wave plate to modulate the polarization of the fundamental light. When the incident laser polarization was set to 0°, rotating the fast axis of the quarter-wave plate to 45° and –45° generated right-handed circularly polarized (RCP) and left-handed circularly polarized (LCP) excitation, respectively. Although these C1-symmetric molecular cage crystals exhibit transmission across the visible-to-LWIR spectrum, we employed a conventional 1064 nm laser as the excitation source due to the wavelength limitations of our laser system. Figure 5e–h and Supplementary Fig. 14e–h show the circularly polarized SHG intensity of single crystals S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se, and S/R-AsP3S4Se as a function of the quarter-wave plate rotation angle φ under 1064 nm excitation, together with fitted curves based on Supplementary Equation (15). The fitted curves reproduce the overall circular-polarization-dependent SHG behavior well, and the corresponding fitting quality metrics and residual-versus-angle plots are provided in Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Fig. 17. Similar to the linearly polarized case, the residual analysis indicates that some curves exhibit noticeable systematic deviations in certain angular regions, showing that the fitting captures the dominant angular dependence but does not fully reproduce all local experimental details. Therefore, the fitting is used here mainly as a semi-quantitative description of the overall angular behavior. The differences in curve shapes for different measured crystal planes may be attributed to variations in the projection of the second-order nonlinear susceptibility tensor, which includes magnetic dipole coupling contributions, onto the p- and s-polarization directions. For all chiral crystals in Fig. 5e–h, the SHG intensity lacks mirror symmetry over the quarter-wave plate rotation range of 0–180°, which is a typical characteristic of chiroptical effects46. Notably, under LCP excitation, the SHG signal from R-type C1-symmetric molecular cage chiral crystals is significantly higher than that under RCP excitation, while S-type crystals show the opposite trend (Supplementary Fig. 14e–h). Structure–property relationship

Electronic chirality distribution has been established as a reasonable metric for quantifying the degree of chirality in materials, defined as the difference between left- and right-handed electron densities48. The total chirality of a material can be quantified by integrating its distribution. To investigate the structure–property relationship between electronic chirality and chiroptical response, we computed the electronic chirality distributions for these chiral crystals. Since the distributions for the R- and S-forms are mirror-symmetric and opposite in sign, the following discussion focuses on the R-configuration. Figure 6a displays the electronic chirality distribution of R-AsP3S5, where positive and negative regions are localized around each molecular cage, confirming that each cage acts as a chiral source. To better visualize the spatial distribution within the unit cell, a three-dimensional sectional view and its projection are presented in Fig. 6b. The asymmetry in the number and density of positive and negative regions further confirms the chiral nature of the material. Integration of the spatial electronic chirality density for R-AsP3S5 yields a total chirality C of 0.063. In comparison, the total chirality of Te crystal is on the order of 10−548, underscoring the strong chirality of R-AsP3S5. The electronic chirality distributions and total chirality of the other chiral crystals are similar to those of R-AsP3S5 (Supplementary Fig. 18). To better understand the relationship between total chirality C and chiroptical performance, we performed analogous electronic chirality calculations on a previously reported high-performance chiroptical crystal, P-DMA4[InCl6]Br17, to serve as a reference for comparison. As shown in Fig. 6c, the positive and negative electronic chirality regions are distributed around the organic groups rather than the inorganic [InCl6] octahedra in P-DMA4[InCl6]Br, indicating that the overall chirality originates from the helical arrangement of the organic moieties. The sectional view and projection (Fig. 6d) reveal that the electronic chirality distribution in P-DMA4[InCl6]Br is more diffuse than that in R-AsP3S5, likely reflecting a lower density of chiral sources in the unit cell. Given the significant differences between these two crystals in terms of chemical composition, crystal structure and the origin of their chirality, it is insufficient to establish a reliable cross-system relationship between electronic chirality and chiroptical properties. However, the calculated total chirality C for P-DMA4[InCl6]Br is 0.039, which still indicates that high-density localized electronic chirality is consistent with a strong chiroptical nonlinear response, further suggesting that electronic chirality may indeed be a fundamental factor influencing chiroptical properties of crystals. For these C1-symmetric molecular cage crystals, the crystal chirality C varies with the geometric distortion of the molecular cage, which we characterize using the molecular cage dipole moment (Fig. 6e). As shown in Fig. 6f, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mbox{g}}}_{{\mbox{SHG}}-{\mbox{CD}}}$$\end{document}gSHG−CD values exhibit a positive trend with increasing molecular cage dipole moment within this isostructural C1-symmetric molecular cage series, although the dataset is limited and the uncertainties of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mbox{g}}}_{{\mbox{SHG}}-{\mbox{CD}}}$$\end{document}gSHG−CD measurements are now explicitly indicated by error bars (Supplementary Table 10). When these closely related molecular cages crystallize with similar stacking arrangements, distortion-induced changes in the local electronic asymmetry are reflected in the macroscopic chiroptical response. In summary, we have introduced a bottom-up strategy to access purely inorganic chiroptical NLO materials by tuning molecular-level geometric distortion in intrinsically C1-symmetric phosphorus–chalcogen cages. Conservative, site-specific As/P and Se/S substitution generates enantiopure molecular-cage crystals (S/R-P4S5, S/R-AsP3S5, S/R-P4S4Se and S/R-AsP3S4Se) that combine measurable SHG-CD responses under 1064 nm excitation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mbox{g}}}_{{\mbox{SHG}}-{\mbox{CD}}}$$\end{document}gSHG−CD = 0.48–1.70) with SHG outputs of 1.1–2.8 × AGS at 2050 nm, broad IR transparency, and high LIDTs (104.30–150.34 GW/cm2). Importantly, the controlled substitution series also provides a mechanistic basis for connecting molecular structure with bulk chiroptical nonlinear response. Within this isostructural C1-symmetric molecular cage family, the experimentally measured SHG-CD behavior follows the distortion-dependent change in molecular asymmetry and cage dipole moment. This relationship identifies the molecular cage dipole moment as a useful, chemically addressable descriptor for understanding and tuning chiroptical nonlinear response in this specific family, rather than as a universal predictor across unrelated material systems. Together, these findings demonstrate that C1-symmetric inorganic molecular cages offer a versatile design platform for infrared-transparent chiroptical nonlinear materials. Future experiments using appropriate long-wavelength infrared excitation and detection configurations will be required to directly evaluate their nonlinear optical and chiroptical performance in the LWIR spectral region.

Methods

Reagents

P (Adamas, 6 N), S (Sinopharm, 3 N), As (Adamas, 5 N), SeS2 (Maclin, 99%), I2 (Adamas, 99.995%), and carbon disulfide (Sinopharm, 99.9%) were used as received.

Synthesis

P4S5: first, P4S3 was prepared by heating a stoichiometric mixture of P and S in an evacuated silica tube at 300 °C for 72 h. The solid obtained was recrystallized in CS2 to yield pure P4S3. The mixture of pure P4S3 (2.0 g) and S (0.58 g) was added in a well-closed container containing 100 mL CS2 and stirred until dissolved, then 0.1 g of iodine monomer was added and dissolved by shaking. The container was then exposed to diffuse sunlight, some yellow block crystals appeared in the container after a few days. The crystals were removed from the solution, washed with CS2 and alcohol and dried.

AsP3S5: first, AsP3S3 was prepared by heating a stoichiometric mixture of P, As, and S in an evacuated silica tube at 600 °C for 72 h. The mixture of obtained AsP3S3 (1.32 g) and S (0.35 g) was added in a well-closed container containing 100 mL CS2 and stirred until dissolved, then 0.1 g of iodine monomer was added and dissolved by shaking. The container was then exposed to diffuse sunlight, and some small yellow crystals appeared in the container after a few days. The crystals were removed from the solution, washed with CS2 and alcohol and dried.

P4S4Se: first, P4S3 was prepared by heating a stoichiometric mixture of P and S in an evacuated silica tube at 300 °C for 72 h. The solid obtained was recrystallized in CS2 to yield pure P4S3. The mixture of pure P4S3 (1.5 g) and SeS2 (0.98 g) was added in a well-closed container containing 100 mL CS2 and stirred until dissolved, then 0.1 g of iodine monomer was added and dissolved by shaking. The container was then exposed to diffuse sunlight, some yellow block crystals appeared in the container after a few days. The crystals were removed from the solution, washed with CS2 and alcohol and dried.

AsP3S4Se: the mixture of AsP3S3 (1.32 g) and SeS2 (0.72 g) was added in a well-closed container containing 100 mL CS2 and stirred until dissolved, then 0.1 g of iodine monomer was added and dissolved by shaking. The container was then exposed to diffuse sunlight, some crystals appeared in the container after a few days. The crystals were removed from the solution, washed with CS2 and alcohol and dried.

Caution: considering the toxicity and volatility, all operations with liquid bromine, iodine and CS2 should be carried out in a fume hood.

Structure determination

The crystal structures of the title compounds were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction on the Rigaku Oxford X-ray diffractometer XtaLAB Synergy-R fitted with Mo-Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å), the Rigaku Mercury CCD diffractometer equipped with graphite-monochromatic Ga-Kα radiation (λ = 1.3405 Å), and the Rigaku Oxford X-ray diffractometer XtaLAB SynergyCustom fitted with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). Single crystals were randomly selected for single-crystal X-ray diffraction to determine their absolute configuration. The cell refinement and data reduction were performed with the program CryAlisPro. The crystal structure was determined by the intrinsic phasing methods and refined by full-matrix least-quares fitting on F2 using SHELXL in Olex2 program49,50. All atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement parameters. The PLATON program was used to further check the crystal structures, and no higher symmetries were found51. The crystallographic data are summarized in Supplementary Table 1, atomic coordinates and equivalent isotropic displacement parameters are presented in Supplementary Table 2. The anisotropic displacement parameters, as well as bond lengths and angles are listed in Supplementary Table 3 and 4.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)

The PXRD patterns for polycrystalline samples were collected at room temperature on a Miniflex 600 Powder X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.540598 Å) in the 2θ range of 10–80°, at a step size of 0.01° and a step time of 2 s. The measurement results show that the PXRD patterns of the as-synthesized samples match well with the calculated ones derived from their single crystal data.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)

The elemental composition analysis was performed on a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, SU-8010) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy.

Thermal analysis

The thermal properties were measured on a NETZCH STA449F3 simultaneous analyzer. Reference (Al2O3) and crystal samples (5–10 mg) were enclosed in Al2O3 crucibles and heated from 30 °C to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1 under a constant flow of nitrogen gas.

UV–Vis–NIR diffuse reflectance spectrum

The UV-vis-IR diffuse reflectance spectra of the title compounds were measured using a PerkinElmer Lambda-950 UV/Vis/NIR spectrophotometer with BaSO4 as the reference material scanning in the range of 300–2500 nm. The reflection spectra were converted into absorption spectra using the Kubelka-Munk function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R=\frac{K}{S}=\frac{{(1-R)}^{2}}{2R}$$\end{document}R=KS=(1−R)22R52, with R, K and S denoting reflectance, absorption and scattering, respectively. IR transmittance spectrum

The IR transmittance spectrum was measured on a Bruker VERTEX 70 Fourier IR spectrometer in the range of 4000−400 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1 at room temperature.

Second harmonic generation (SHG) measurements

Powder SHG responses were measured with a Q-switched Nd: YAG solid-state laser of wavelength 2050 nm using the Kurtz-Perry method43. Polycrystalline samples and AgGaS2 (as the reference) were ground and sieved into several distinct particle size ranges of 25−45, 45−63, 63−74, 74−106, 106−150, and 150−212 μm, and then pressed into the container with a thickness of 1 mm and diameter of 8 mm. The intensities of the SHG signals were detected by a photomultiplier tube attached to a RIGOL DS1052E 50 MHz oscilloscope.

Crystals identified as S or R by single-crystal X-ray diffraction were selected for measurements. A customized inverted confocal microscope (Nikon, Ti-U) was used for the micro-area SHG measurements, which was equipped with a fs-pulsed laser as an excitation source. The fs-pulsed laser was generated by a femtosecond Ti-sapphire oscillator (80 MHz, pulse width of 140 fs, 680–1080 nm, Chameleon Vision II, Coherent). The fs-pulsed laser passed through a 750 nm short-pass dichroic mirror (Thorlabs, DMSP750R) and then was focused with a microscope objective lens (40 × 0.75 NA, Nikon) to the sample. The spot radius of the fs pulses on the samples was ~1 μm. The SHG signal was collected by the same objective, spectrally filtered using a 750 nm short-pass filter (Thorlabs, FESH0750), and then captured by the spectrometer (Acton, SpectraPro-2300i). For linear polarization-resolved SHG measurements, a λ/2 waveplate was placed in the incident light paths. For circular polarization-resolved SHG measurements, the λ/2 waveplate was replaced by a λ/4 waveplate and placed in the incident light path to modulate excitation polarization between LCP and RCP states. Computational methods

The electronic band structures, partial density of states, and optical properties of the title compounds were calculated using the CASTEP package based on density functional theory (DFT)53,54. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional in the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was adopted to describe the exchange-correlation energy55,56. The norm-conserving pseudopotential was used for the calculations57. The following orbital electrons were treated as valence electrons: P-3s23p3, S-3s23p4, As-4s24p3, Se-4s24p4. The cut-off energy was set to be 650 eV for P4S5, AsP3S5, P4S4Se, AsP3S4Se. The self-consistent convergence of the total energy was 1.0 × 10−5 eV per atom. Due to the discontinuity of exchange correlation, bandgaps calculated by the GGA method are usually smaller than experimental values, so a scissor operator was adopted to raise the conduction band to match the experimental value for optical property calculation.
