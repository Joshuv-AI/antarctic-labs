Convergence and Emergence of In-Context Reinforcement Learning with Chain of Thought
Zixuan Xie, Xinyu Liu, Rohan Chandra, Shangtong Zhang

Abstract
In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) refers to the ability of RL agents to adapt to new tasks at inference time without parameter updates by conditioning on additional context. Recent empirical studies further demonstrate that Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation can amplify this ICRL capability. This paper is the first to provide a theoretical understanding on how CoT interacts with ICRL. We conduct our analysis in a policy evaluation setup with linear Transformer. We prove that with specific Transformer parameters, the CoT generation process is equivalent to repeatedly executing temporal difference learning updates. Additionally, we provide finite sample convergence analysis showing that the policy evaluation error decreases geometrically with CoT length and eventually saturates at a statistical floor determined by the context length. We also prove that the desired Transformer parameters are a global minimizer of the pretraining loss, providing a theoretical understanding on the empirical emergence of those parameters.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL,
56
) provides a foundational framework where agents learn sequential decision-making through interaction.
A standard paradigm in deep RL consists of training an agent with an RL algorithm on a target problem and deploying the fixed policy within that same domain
(
39
;
50
;
51
)
. However, this workflow lacks flexibility against task variations. If the underlying goals or environment dynamics change, the specialized policy often fails, requiring the agent to be retrained from scratch to adapt, which can be computationally expensive.
In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL,
27
;
41
) mitigates this limitation by enabling adaptation at inference time without parameter updates. In this framework, an agent is pretrained on a diverse distribution of tasks, typically formulated as either policy evaluation or control problems
(
41
)
. Policy evaluation involves estimating value functions, whereas control focuses on searching for optimal policies.
At inference time, the network parameters are frozen and the agent conditions on additional context generated within the new task.
A standard form of such context is the trajectory of recent interactions, comprising past observations, actions, and rewards
(
27
)
. Empirical evidence
(
27
;
2
;
16
;
46
;
18
;
19
;
34
;
3
)
shows that performance often improves as the context grows, even on test tasks that deviate significantly from the pretraining distribution.
Thus, this improvement is difficult to explain by memorization of the pretraining tasks.
It instead suggests an implicit implementation of an internal RL process within the network’s forward pass that leverages the context for adaptation
(
41
)
.
We refer to this capability of reinforcement learning at inference time as ICRL.

This ICRL capability has been shown to arise under a diverse array of pretraining algorithms, which can be broadly divided into supervised pretraining and reinforcement pretraining.
In supervised pretraining
(
27
;
52
;
26
;
29
;
76
;
13
;
32
)
, the network is trained by imitation to match the behavior of a known RL algorithm on curated learning trajectories, so it is less surprising if the forward pass resembles that algorithm.
A more intriguing phenomenon arises from reinforcement pretraining, where the agent network can be a sequence model such as a Transformer
(
63
)
and the pretraining algorithm is simply a variant of standard deep RL algorithm

(
15
;
65
;
18
)
.
Here, the network is trained to output good actions for control tasks or to make accurate value predictions for policy evaluation tasks, yet the resulting forward pass can still behave like an RL algorithm.
This ICRL capability is also observed in large language models (LLMs).
For example,

53

report that multi-round prompting with scalar rewards can yield consistent inference-time improvement for LLMs in control tasks.
Particularly,
53
rely on Chain-of-Thought (CoT,
69
) generation to amplify LLM’s ICRL capability.

Many works have been done to theoretically understand ICRL
with most focusing on supervised pretraining.
See Section
7
for a more detailed exposition.
The volume of works to theoretically understand ICRL with reinforcement pretraining is much thinner.
Notably works include
66
;
67
that study ICRL under a policy evaluation setup.
Specifically,

66
prove that with certain parameters,
the layer-by-layer forward pass of linear Transformers is equivalent to step-by-step update of temporal difference (TD) learning
(
59
)
,
arguably the most powerful algorithm for policy evaluation.

67
further prove that those parameters are a global minimizer of some reinforcement pretraining loss.
However,

66
;
67
do not explain the role of CoT in ICRL,
a gap that we shall close in this paper.

Specifically, we make three contributions toward closing this gap. First, we constructively prove that under specific parameters, stepwise CoT generation in a linear Transformer is equivalent to iterating TD updates on the context. Second, we establish inference-time convergence guarantees with context constructed from both known and unknown dynamics, showing that the policy evaluation error decreases geometrically up to a statistical floor controlled by the context length. Third, we prove that the desired parameters above is a global minimizer for a TD-based pretraining algorithm and we empirically validate the emergence of those parameters in controlled environments.

2.1
Reinforcement Learning and Policy Evaluation

We consider an infinite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP,
5
) defined by a tuple

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p_{\text{MDP}},r_{\text{MDP}},\gamma,p_{0})
.
Here
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space,

p_{\text{MDP}}(\cdot|s,a)
is the transition kernel,
r_{\text{MDP}}(s,a)
is the reward function,

\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor,
and
p_{0}
is the initial distribution.
In this paper, we focus on the policy evaluation problem, where the goal is to estimate the value function of an arbitrary policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
.
An initial state
S_{0}
is sampled from
p_{0}
.
At timestep
t
, given the current state
S_{t}
, the agent samples an action
A_{t}
from its policy
\pi
.
Consequently, it obtains a reward
R_{t+1}
determined by
r_{\text{MDP}}(S_{t},A_{t})

and moves to the next state
S_{t+1}
according to the transition dynamics
p_{\text{MDP}}(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})
.
A fixed policy
\pi
reduces the MDP to a Markov Reward Process (MRP) with transition kernel

p(s^{\prime}|s)\doteq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)p_{\text{MDP}}(s^{\prime}|s,a)

and reward function

r(s)\doteq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)r_{\text{MDP}}(s,a).

In this case, transitions and rewards are determined solely by the current state:

S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t})
and
R_{t+1}\doteq r(S_{t})
.
We focus on policy evaluation under the fixed policy
\pi
, so it suffices to work with the MRP
defined by
(p_{0},p,r)
and a trajectory
(S_{0},R_{1},S_{1},R_{2},\dots)
sampled from it.
We use
P_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
to denote the state transition matrix induced by the policy
\pi
, i.e.,
P_{\pi}\quantity[s,s']=p(s^{\prime}|s)
.
Assume the Markov chain induced by
P_{\pi}
on
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible and aperiodic, so it admits a unique stationary distribution
d_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
.
We use
D_{\pi}
to denote the diagonal matrix whose diagonal is
d_{\pi}
.
The value function is

v_{\pi}(s)\doteq\mathbb{E}\quantity[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} | S_t=s].

It is the unique fixed point of the Bellman expectation operator
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}
defined by

(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}v)(s)\doteq\mathbb{E}\quantity[R_{t+1} + \gamma v(S_{t+1}) | S_t=s].

2.2
Linear TD and the MSPBE Objective

To estimate
v_{\pi}
in large state spaces, we employ linear value function approximation.
We use a feature mapping
x:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We approximate the value at state
s
as
v_{w}(s)\doteq x(s)^{\top}w
.
Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{{\absolutevalue{\fS}}\times d}
denote the feature matrix whose
s
-th row is
x(s)^{\top}
.
The vector of approximate state values across all states is written as
Xw\in\mathbb{R}^{{\absolutevalue{\fS}}}
.
Our objective is to find
w
such that
Xw
closely approximates
v_{\pi}
.

A widely used method to learn
w
is Temporal Difference learning (TD,
59
), which updates
w
iteratively. Using
x_{t}\doteq x(S_{t})
for brevity, the update rule is

w_{t+1}=w_{t}+\alpha_{t}\quantity(R_{t+1} + \gamma x_{t+1}^\top w_t - x_t^\top w_t)x_{t},

(1)

where
\{\alpha_{t}\}
is a sequence of learning rates.
Let

A\doteq X^{\top}D_{\pi}(I-\gamma P_{\pi})X,\,b\doteq X^{\top}D_{\pi}r,\,C\doteq X^{\top}D_{\pi}X,

(2)

where
r\in\mathbb{R}^{{\absolutevalue{\fS}}}
is the reward vector whose
s
-th entry is
r(s)
.
We assume
X
has full column rank.

62
proves that
\quantity{w_t}
converge to the TD fixed point

w_{*}=A^{-1}b

(3)

almost surely.
Since linear approximation is restricted to the feature subspace
\mathrm{span}(X)
, we measure convergence to
w_{*}
via the mean-squared projected Bellman error (MSPBE)

\mathcal{L}(w)\doteq\|\Pi\mathcal{T}^{\pi}(Xw)-Xw\|_{D_{\pi}}^{2},

(4)

where

\Pi\doteq X(X^{\top}D_{\pi}X)^{-1}X^{\top}D_{\pi}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}(X)
.
For linear function approximation, MSPBE admits the equivalent quadratic form

\mathcal{L}(w)=\norm{C^{-\frac 12}(Aw-b)}_{2}^{2}.

(5)

In particular,
\mathcal{L}(w_{*})=0
and
\mathcal{L}(w)=0
if and only if
Aw=b
.

2.3
Transformers and Linear Self-Attention

We study linear self-attention, where the softmax nonlinearity in standard self-attention
(
63
)
is replaced by the identity map.
This form is widely used in efficient Transformer architectures and is a common proxy in theoretical analyses of in-context computation

(
24
;
11
;
64
;
1
;
70
;
17
;
74
;
21
;
66
)
.
Given a prompt matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(n+1)}
, we define linear self-attention as

\text{LinAttn}(Z;P,Q)\doteq PZ(Z^{\top}QZ),

(6)

where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(3d+1)}
consolidates the value and projection matrices, and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(3d+1)}
is the merge of regular key and query matrices.
We consider a residual linear-attention layer

\mathcal{F}(Z;P,Q)\doteq Z+\frac{1}{n}\text{LinAttn}(Z;P,Q),

(7)

where
\frac{1}{n}
is a normalization that aligns the attention update with batch updates that average over
n
samples.
The behavior of this update is fully governed by the matrices
P
and
Q
, which serve as the learnable parameters in our analysis.

3
Transformers with CoT Can Implement Iterated TD Updates

Drawing inspiration from
21
,
we cast policy evaluation as an
in-context weight learning task
.
Given a finite trajectory
\tau_{n}=(S_{0},R_{1},\dots,S_{n})
,
we require the Transformer to approach the TD fixed point
w_{*}
defined in (
3
).
Completing this task purely in-context with a shallow network is hard, as it requires performing an iterative computation over
\tau_{n}
.
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation is a powerful way to improve the power of such a shallow network.
CoT is commonly understood as producing intermediate computations before outputting the final prediction.
In our setting, this corresponds to generating a sequence of iterates
\{w_{k}\}
, where each generation conditions on the growing prompt.

In this section, we claim that a single linear Transformer layer with CoT can solve the in-context weight learning task by simulating iterated batch TD updates.
Specifically, following
56
, batch TD updates
w
using the sample average over
\tau_{n}
as

w_{k+1}=w_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\quantity(R_{j+1}+\gamma x_{j+1}^\top w_k - x_j^\top w_k)x_{j},

(8)

where
\alpha>0
is a constant stepsize.
Batch TD can be viewed as an offline variant of TD defined in (
1
).
It repeatedly reuses
\tau_{n}
to drive the iterates toward
w_{*}
, with the improvement saturating at a statistical floor, which we quantify later.

To support the claim above,
we use
\tau_{n}
as the context and organize it into a prompt matrix
Z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(n+1)}
as

Z_{0}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\cdots&x_{n-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\gamma x_{1}&\cdots&\gamma x_{n}&\mathbf{0}\\
R_{1}&\cdots&R_{n}&1\\
\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&w_{0}\end{bmatrix}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}X_{\tau}&\mathbf{0}\\
\gamma X_{\tau}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}\\
R&1\\
\mathbf{0}&w_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(9)

where the first
n
columns store the fixed context, and the last column initializes the weight component
w_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
This block-structured prompt separates the context columns from the iterate column, following
21
.

We now formalize CoT generation under the prompt construction (
9
).
Specifically, given the current generated prompt
Z_{k}
at the
k
-th step, we take the last column of the output matrix
\mathcal{F}(Z_{k};P,Q)_{[:,-1]}\in\mathbb{R}^{3d+1}
as the next iterate column and append it to form

Z_{k+1}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}Z_{k}&\mathcal{F}(Z_{k};P,Q)_{[:,-1]}\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Therefore, at step
k
, the prompt has the form
Z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(n+k+1)}
with iterates
(w_{0},\dots,w_{k})
in the buffer

Z_{k}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}X_{\tau}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\gamma X_{\tau}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
R&1&1&\dots&1\\
\mathbf{0}&w_{0}&w_{1}&\dots&w_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

Surprisingly, with
(P,Q)
constructed as

P\doteq\begin{bmatrix}0_{(2d+1)\times d}&0_{(2d+1)\times(2d+1)}\\
\alpha I_{d}&0_{d\times(2d+1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad Q\doteq\begin{bmatrix}0_{d\times 2d}&0_{d\times 1}&-I_{d}\\
0_{d\times 2d}&0_{d\times 1}&I_{d}\\
0_{1\times 2d}&1&0_{1\times d}\\
0_{d\times 2d}&0_{d\times 1}&0_{d\times d}\end{bmatrix},

(12)

the last-token output
\mathcal{F}(Z_{k};P,Q)_{[:,-1]}
exactly produces the batch TD iterate
w_{k+1}
from
w_{k}
in (
8
) for every structured prompt
Z_{k}
in (
11
).
This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the linear attention layer (
7
) with
(P,Q)
defined in (
12
).
For any sequence
\{Z_{k}\}
generated by (
10
) with
Z_{0}
defined in (
9
), the weight component
w_{k}
in
Z_{k}
, as in (
11
), coincides with the
k
-th iterate of the batch TD update (
8
).

The proof is in Section
B.1
.
Theorem
1
shows that under our structured prompt, a single linear Transformer layer can implement one batch TD update in its forward pass.
Combined with the CoT recursion (
10
),

k
steps of CoT generation repeatedly apply this update on the fixed context
\tau_{n}
, yielding iterates
\{w_{k}\}
, as claimed above.
Notably, the sparse construction (
12
) is one valid choice; other parameter configurations may also realize the same one-step update on structured prompts.

4
Inference-Time Convergence with Known Dynamics

In this warm-up section, we analyze the CoT-generated TD iterates under known dynamics, where the transition matrix
P_{\pi}
is available at inference time and may be used to construct the context.
Following the setting in prior analyses of inference-time convergence for ICRL
(
67
)
, we replace the sampled successor features by their conditional expectations.
Let
m\doteq|\mathcal{S}|
and let
(s_{0},\ldots,s_{m-1})
be an enumeration of the states in
\mathcal{S}
.
For each
i\in\quantity[m]
, let
x_{i}\doteq x(s_{i})
, we define the expected successor feature

\textstyle\bar{x}_{i}\doteq\mathbb{E}\quantity[x(S')\mid S=s_i]=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}x_{j}P_{\pi}[s_{i},s_{j}].

(13)

Using shorthands
R_{i+1}\doteq r(s_{i})
, we then construct the initial prompt
\bar{Z}_{0}
as

\bar{Z}_{0}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{0})}x_{0}&\cdots&\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{m-1})}x_{m-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\gamma\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{0})}\bar{x}_{0}&\cdots&\gamma\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{m-1})}\bar{x}_{m-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{0})}R_{1}&\cdots&\sqrt{d_{\pi}(s_{m-1})}R_{m}&1\\
\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&w_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

where
d_{\pi}
is the stationary distribution of
P_{\pi}
.
Define
\bar{Z}_{k}
analogously to (
11
) by appending the CoT buffer columns while keeping the first
m
context columns fixed as in (
14
).
With the same
(P,Q)
defined in (
12
), applying one CoT step to
\bar{Z}_{k}
and reading out the weight component yields a sequence of deterministic iterates
\{\bar{w}_{k}\}
satisfying (Lemma
7
)

\bar{w}_{k+1}=\bar{w}_{k}+\eta(b-A\bar{w}_{k}),

(15)

where
\eta\doteq\alpha/m
is a constant stepsize inherited from
P
, with the factor
1/m
arising from the normalization in (
7
).
This recursion has the unique fixed point
w_{*}
defined in (
3
).
Define the symmetric part
H\doteq\tfrac{1}{2}(A+A^{\top})
and the spectral constants

\mu\doteq\lambda_{\min}(H),\quad L\doteq\|A\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

A standard contraction argument on (
15
) then yields the geometric convergence in the MSPBE.

Proposition 1
.

For any constant stepsize
\eta\in(0,\mu/L]
, there exists a constant
C_{\text{Prop}\text{\ref{prop:expect}}}
such that the iterates generated by
(
15
) satisfy for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{k})\leq C_{\text{Prop}\text{\ref{prop:expect}}}\bigl(1-\eta\mu)^{k}\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{0}).

(17)

The proof is in Section
C.2
.
Proposition
1
immediately yields a tradeoff between numbers of steps and precision in the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Fix any constant stepsize
\eta\in(0,\mu/L]
.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,C_{\text{Prop}\text{\ref{prop:expect}}}\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{0})]
, it holds that
\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{k})\leq\varepsilon
whenever

k\geq\frac{1}{\eta\mu}\log\Big(\frac{C_{\text{Prop}\tref{prop:expect}}\fL(\bar w_0)}{\varepsilon}\Big).

(18)

The proof is in Section
C.3
.
Recall
\bar{w}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{0})
is a constant.
Therefore, reaching
\mathcal{L}(\bar{w}_{k})\leq\varepsilon
requires only
k=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
CoT steps for a fixed stepsize
\eta
.

5
Inference-Time Convergence with Unknown Dynamics

Section
4
analyzes an idealized known-dynamics setting, where the context is constructed using the transition matrix
P_{\pi}
.
This assumption is restrictive in typical RL settings, where the dynamics are unknown and the model only observes a single finite trajectory
\tau_{n}=(S_{0},R_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
.
This is also a limitation of prior work on inference-time convergence for ICRL in
67
.
In this section, we analyze the convergence of CoT-driven ICRL as specified in Theorem
1
.
This extends
67
in three aspects:
(i) we move from tabular representations to linear function approximation,
(ii) we replace the state-enumeration construction by an arbitrary trajectory
\tau_{n}
,
and (iii) we use purely sample-based quantities without expected successor features.
Compared with the known-dynamics setting in (
15
), the CoT iterates generated under (
10
) is harder to analyze because they rely on a single Markovian trajectory
\tau_{n}
, which leads to estimation error under dependence.
Our goal is to quantify how the loss
\mathcal{L}(w_{k})
decreases with the number of CoT steps
k
until it reaches a statistical floor controlled by the context length
n
.

Throughout this section, we use the same prompt construction
Z_{k}
introduced in (
11
).
We define the empirical moments as

\textstyle\widehat{A}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}x_{t}\bigl(x_{t}-\gamma x_{t+1}\bigr)^{\top},\quad\widehat{b}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}R_{t+1}x_{t}.

(19)

Equivalently, (
8
) can be written as

w_{k+1}=w_{k}+\alpha\bigl(\widehat{b}-\widehat{A}w_{k}\bigr),

(20)

with
w_{0}=\mathbf{0}
as in our prompt construction.
Our finite sample analysis depends on the deviation of
(\widehat{A},\widehat{b})
from
(A,b)
in (
2
).
Define

\textstyle\varepsilon_{A}=\norm{\widehat A-A}_{2},\,\varepsilon_{b}=\norm{\widehat b-b}_{2}.

(21)

Since the chain induced by
P_{\pi}
is ergodic, we couple
\{S_{t}\}
to a stationary chain via Lemma
9
, apply a blocking argument with Berbee’s coupling (Lemmas
3
,
5
) to handle dependence, and conclude with the matrix Bernstein inequality (Lemma

(\widehat{A},\widehat{b})
in terms of an effective sample size

.

For any

C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},1},C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},2},C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},3}>0

such that letting

\textstyle m\doteq\Bigl\lceil C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},1}\log\Bigl(\frac{C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},2}n}{\delta}\Bigr)\Bigr\rceil,\quad n_{\mathrm{eff}}\doteq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2m}\Bigr\rfloor,

, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\varepsilon_{A}\leq\varepsilon_{A}(\delta),\,\varepsilon_{b}\leq\varepsilon_{b}(\delta),

where

\displaystyle\textstyle\varepsilon_{A}(\delta)\leq C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},3}\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(4/\delta)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}},\quad\varepsilon_{b}(\delta)\leq C_{\text{\ref{lem:concentration}},3}\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(4/\delta)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

(22)

The proof is in Section
D.1
.
Define the aggregated deviation

\textstyle\varepsilon(\delta)\doteq\varepsilon_{A}(\delta)+\varepsilon_{b}(\delta).

(23)

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
denote the event in Lemma
1
, which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.
On
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, (
20
) satisfies a contraction inequality with an additive perturbation term controlled by
\varepsilon(\delta)
.
Iterating this inequality for
k
steps yields the following finite-sample bound.

Theorem 2
.

Fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,\mu/L]
.
Then there exist constants

C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:finite}},1},C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:finite}},2}>0

such that on
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, if

n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:finite}},1}\Bigl(d+\log\frac{4}{\delta}\Bigr)
,
then for all
k\geq 0
,

\textstyle\mathcal{L}(w_{k})\leq C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:finite}},2}\quantity(\qty(1-\frac{\alpha\mu}{2})^k \fL(w_0)
+\varepsilon(\delta)^2).

(24)

The proof is in Section
D.2
.
On
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, (

Emergence under Reinforcement Pretraining

Previously, we established that the
(P,Q)
constructed in (
12
) is desirable for in-context policy evaluation, as it induces an iterative batch TD update of the form (
8
).
We now take an optimization perspective and show that these parameters attain global optimality for a pretraining loss over a finite dataset of trajectories.

Recall that the linear self-attention layer in (
7
) is parameterized by matrices
(P,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{(3d+1)\times(3d+1)}
.
Following a common practice in the in-context learning theory literature
(
64
;
1
;
36
;
74
;
10
;
17
;
21
;
67
)
, we restrict the learnable parameters
\theta
to the block-sparse pattern defined as

\displaystyle\textstyle\Theta^{\mathrm{CoT}}\doteq\Bigg\{\theta=(P,Q)\bigg|P=\begin{bmatrix}0_{(2d+1)\times d}&0_{(2d+1)\times(2d+1)}\\
P_{1}&0_{d\times(2d+1)}\end{bmatrix},Q=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2d\times 2d}&0_{2d\times 1}&\tilde{Q}\\
0_{1\times 2d}&1&0_{1\times d}\\
0_{d\times 2d}&0_{d\times 1}&0_{d\times d}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},

(26)

where
\tilde{Q}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}\\
Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}
with
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Under CoT autoregressive generation, all steps share the same parameter
\theta
.
In our parameterization,
\theta
has
3d^{2}
degrees of freedom given by the free blocks
(P_{1},Q_{1},Q_{2})
.
Throughout this section, we interpret
\nabla_{\theta}(\cdot)
as the gradient with respect to the vectorized entries of
(P_{1},Q_{1},Q_{2})
.
Let
\theta_{*}\in\Theta^{\mathrm{CoT}}
denote the constructed choice in (
12
), which yields the batch TD update in Theorem
1
.

We consider a finite pretraining dataset
\mathcal{D}\doteq\quantity{\tau_n^{(i)}}_{i=0}^{N_{\mathrm{train}}-1}
with
N_{\mathrm{train}}
trajectories sampled from the same MRP under a fixed policy
\pi
, where each trajectory is
\tau_{n}^{(i)}=(S_{0}^{(i)},R_{1}^{(i)},\dots,S_{n}^{(i)})
.
Fix any
\theta\in\Theta^{\mathrm{CoT}}
and an index
i\in\quantity[N_{\mathrm{train}}]
.
Let
\tau_{n}\doteq\tau_{n}^{(i)}
.
For notational simplicity, we suppress the superscript
(i)
and write
\tau_{n}=(S_{0},R_{1},\dots,S_{n})
.
Let
w_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})
denote the weight iterate produced after
k
CoT steps.
For any
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, the TD error at step
k
is

\displaystyle\delta_{k,j}(\theta;\tau_{n})\doteq R_{j+1}+\gamma x_{j+1}^{\top}w_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})-x_{j}^{\top}w_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n}).

(27)

With the value estimate
\hat{v}_{k}(s;\theta,\tau_{n})\doteq x(s)^{\top}w_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})
, we define the empirical TD update direction at step
k
as

\textstyle\Delta_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})\doteq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta_{k,j}(\theta;\tau_{n})\nabla_{\theta}\hat{v}_{k}(S_{j};\theta,\tau_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{3d^{2}}.

(28)

A classical TD objective is the norm of the expected update (NEU)
(
58
;
57
)
, originally defined as the squared norm of
\mathbb{E}_{d_{\pi}}[\delta(w)\nabla v_{w}(S)]
under the stationary distribution
d_{\pi}
, with zeros characterizing TD fixed points.
Since we operate in the unknown-dynamics regime with a single Markovian trajectory
\tau_{n}
, the stationary expectation is not directly accessible, and we replace it by a trajectory average over
\tau_{n}
, giving the empirical update norm (EUN) loss

J_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})\doteq\|\Delta_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})\|_{2}.

(29)

A zero of
J_{k}(\cdot;\tau_{n})
corresponds to
\Delta_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n})=0
, i.e., a batch TD fixed point on
\tau_{n}
, which makes the EUN a natural pretraining objective for studying whether the desirable parameter
\theta_{*}
in (
12
) attains global optimality.

Aggregating over
\mathcal{D}
, we define the empirical pretraining objective at step
k
as

\textstyle J_{k}(\theta;\mathcal{D})\doteq\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{\tau_{n}\in\mathcal{D}}J_{k}(\theta;\tau_{n}),

(30)

where
\sum_{\tau_{n}\in\mathcal{D}}
abbreviates
\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathrm{train}}-1}

when the trajectory is a dummy variable. We aim to show that
\theta_{*}
globally
minimizes
J_{k}(\cdot;\mathcal{D})
as
k\to\infty
.

Theorem 3
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and a stepsize
\alpha\in\bigl(0,\mu/(8L)\bigr]
.
There exists a constant
C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:emergence}}}>0
such that if for each trajectory
\tau_{n}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}
,

\textstyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:emergence}}}\left(d+\log\tfrac{4N_{\mathrm{train}}}{\delta}\right),

(31)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{k}(\theta_{*};\mathcal{D})=0
.

The proof is in Section
E.6
.
Since
J_{k}(\cdot;\mathcal{D})\geq 0
by definition, Theorem
3
implies that
\theta_{*}
attains the global minimum of the pretraining objective in the limit
k\to\infty
.
To interpret this limit, for each trajectory
\tau_{n}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}
define
\widehat{w}_{*}^{(i)}
by
\widehat{A}^{(i)}\widehat{w}_{*}^{(i)}=\widehat{b}^{(i)}
.
Under the same conditions, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and a stepsize
\alpha\in\bigl(0,\mu/(8L)\bigr]
.
If (
31
) holds, then with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
i\in[N_{\mathrm{train}}]

\mathcal{L}(\widehat{w}_{*}^{(i)})\leq C_{\text{Thm}\text{\ref{thm:finite}},2}\,\varepsilon(\delta^{\prime})^{2},

(32)

where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/N_{\mathrm{train}}

The proof is in Section
E.7
.
Consequently, under the
\theta_{*}
parametrization, the pretraining objective recovers the per-trajectory batch-TD fixed points, and the remaining population MSPBE error relative to
w_{*}
is purely statistical, governed by
\varepsilon(\delta^{\prime})
and vanishing as
n\to\infty
.

In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) studies agents that adapt at inference time without parameter updates by conditioning on additional interaction history.
ICRL is often viewed as a form of black-box meta-RL

A useful categorization of ICRL pretraining is supervised pretraining versus reinforcement pretraining

Supervised pretraining is typically imitation or algorithm distillation

In this setting, in-context algorithmic behavior is expected since training explicitly imitates an RL learning procedure.
Theory shows that such objectives can induce in-context implementations of decision-making procedures under suitable assumptions
(
29
;
32
)
.
We instead study reinforcement pretraining and focus on the inference-time dynamics induced by autoregressive chain-of-thought (CoT) token generation for policy evaluation.

In terms of reinforcement pretraining, a long line of work shows that training agents with RL objectives can induce inference-time learning behaviors from interaction history
(

;

;

;

Related empirical work investigates scalable architectures and training recipes for ICRL and its variants

On the theory side,

The closest works related to us are

Our work differs from

(ii) beyond the idealized known-dynamics regime studied in

(iii) we further show that our constructed parameterization is learnable under certain reinforcement pretraining objective even under unknown dynamics, which is not covered by

A concurrent submission

also extends

9
Conclusion

This paper provides a white-box account of how CoT generation implements inference-time policy evaluation in linear Transformers.
Under a simple prompt construction, each CoT step realizes one batch TD update on the context.
We establish geometric convergence guarantees in two regimes, namely MSPBE contraction under known dynamics and geometric improvement under unknown dynamics that saturates at a statistical floor governed by an effective sample size.
We further connect the CoT computation to reinforcement pretraining by showing that the constructed parameterization is one of the global minimizers of the empirical pretraining objective.
Controlled experiments on Boyan’s chain demonstrate the emergence of the predicted block structure across multiple policy-evaluation tasks.
Limitations include linear self-attention and the small scale of our empirical study, and addressing them may further advance the theoretical understanding of in-context reinforcement learning.

15

\displaystyle\|\bar{w}_{k+1}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bar{w}_{k}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-2\eta(\bar{w}_{k}-w^{*})^{\top}A(\bar{w}_{k}-w^{*})+\eta^{2}\|A(\bar{w}_{k}-w^{*})\|_{2}^{2}.

For any

v^{\top}Hv\geq\mu\|v\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|Av\|_{2}^{2}\leq L\|v\|_{2}^{2}
, so (

(43)

When

, we have
1-2\eta\mu+\eta^{2}L\leq 1-\eta\mu<1
, yielding a contraction.
By (
43

\|\bar{w}_{k+1}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1-2\eta\mu+\eta^{2}L)\|\bar{w}_{k}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

By Lemma

Iterating over

\displaystyle\leq\|C^{-\frac{1}{2}}A\|_{2}^{2}(1-\eta\mu)^{k}\|\bar{w}_{0}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

C.3
Proof of Corollary

Proof.

, for any constant stepsize

Lemma 9

(Doeblin coupling for finite ergodic chains)
.

Let
P_{\pi}
be an irreducible and aperiodic transition matrix on
a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
with stationary distribution
d_{\pi}
.
For any initial distribution
p_{0}
, there exists a coupling

(S_{t},S_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
with
S_{0}\sim p_{0}
,
S_{0}^{\prime}\sim d_{\pi}
,
both marginally Markov with kernel
P_{\pi}
, such that the
coalescence time

\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}\doteq\inf\{t:S_{s}=S_{s}^{\prime}\text{ for all }s\geq t\}
satisfies

\Pr(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}} > t)\;\leq\;C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},1}C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},2}^{t},

for some constants
C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},1}\geq 1
and
C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},2}\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}
is finite and
P_{\pi}
is irreducible and aperiodic,
by Lemma
4
there exists
r\geq 1
such that

P_{\pi}^{r}(s,s^{\prime})>0
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
. Define

\varepsilon\doteq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\pi}^{r}(s,s^{\prime})>0,\qquad\nu(s^{\prime})\doteq\varepsilon^{-1}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\pi}^{r}(s,s^{\prime}).

Then for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
, define

Q_{s}(\cdot)\doteq\frac{P_{\pi}^{r}(s,\cdot)-\varepsilon\,\nu(\cdot)}{1-\varepsilon},

so that
P_{\pi}^{r}(s,\cdot)=\varepsilon\,\nu(\cdot)+(1-\varepsilon)\,Q_{s}(\cdot)
,
where
Q_{s}
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{S}
for each
s
.
Construct the split coupling as in Section 4.2 of
48
):
at block times
0,r,2r,\ldots
, draw a common

\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\varepsilon)
coin; if it succeeds, set
both chains’ next state to a common draw from
\nu
; if it
fails, update using the residual kernels
Q_{S_{t}}
and

Q_{S_{t}^{\prime}}
independently. Once the chains meet, use identical
transitions thereafter. Each block has conditional coupling
probability at least
\varepsilon
, so

\Pr(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}} > kr)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{k}
for

k\geq 0
. Setting
C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},2}=(1-\varepsilon)^{1/r}
and

C_{\text{\ref{lem:doeblin-coupling}},1}=(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 10

Appendix F
Additional Experimental Details

Multi-task TD pretraining (Algorithm
1
).

Algorithm
1
summarizes our multi-task reinforcement pretraining loop.
Each training task samples an MRP and a feature map from
d_{\textnormal{task}}
, rolls out a trajectory, and performs semi-gradient TD updates while the model generates a CoT sequence of weight iterates
\{w_{t}\}
from the structured prompt.
In practice, pretraining is stabilized by gradient clipping with max norm
1.0
and a small weight decay
10^{-6}
(Table
1
).
We monitor the mean squared error between the Transformer’s value prediction and the batch TD solution once every 10 MRPs on the current training MRP.
We early-stop when this error falls below
\xi=10^{-3}
for 30 consecutive checks, meaning it stays below the threshold for
300
MRPs.
We use the hyperparameters listed in Table
1
for experimentation.

Emergent block-sparse structure and equivalent solutions.

We observe a clear emergence of block-sparse structure in
(P,Q)
, as shown in Figure
1
.
As in
66
, this emergence admits two equivalent solutions related by a global sign flip:

(P,Q)
and
(-P,-Q)
implement the same in-context update, and different random seeds may converge to different sign conventions.
When aggregating across seeds, we fix a canonical representative by choosing the sign such that
Q_{[2d,2d]}>0
.

Boyan’s chain (Figure
2
and Algorithm
2
).

Figure
2
illustrates the sparse transition topology of Boyan’s chain used in our experiments.
Algorithm
2
gives the randomization procedure. We sample a reward vector
r
, an initial distribution
p_{0}
, and a transition kernel
p
with two nonzero forward transitions per state and a reset from the last state. We also sample an i.i.d. uniform feature map
x(\cdot)
.

Compute resources.

All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.
The complete set of reported experiments, including all 10 random seeds in Figure
1
, takes approximately 10 minutes of wall-clock time.
This corresponds to roughly 0.17 H100 GPU-hours for the reported experimental results.
The experiments use only synthetic Boyan-chain data, and the storage requirement is negligible.
Preliminary and debugging runs used comparable compute and did not
substantially exceed the reported experimental budget.
