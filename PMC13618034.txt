Activating Photoreaction
of Indazole Metastable-State
Photoacid through Two-Photon Excitation

Ying-Zhong Ma, Pavithra Liyanage, Yi Liao

Abstract

The photoreaction
and functionalities of metastable state
photoacids
(mPAHs) have been examined almost exclusively through one-photon excitation
so far. Here, we report two-photon excited fluorescence measurements
on an aqueous solution of a highly water-soluble indazole mPAH. The
readily observable fluorescence emission was found to not only follow
the anticipated quadratic intensity dependence but also possess a
sizeable two-photon absorption cross-section of ∼53 GM. The
distinct time-resolved fluorescence decays and emission spectra observed
upon one- and two-photon excitation offer indirect evidence for the
activation of different isomers and their associated pathways in the
respective photoreactions. Our findings pave a promising way of unlocking
new functionalities and applications of these mPAHs that are inaccessible
to one-photon excitation.

Metastable-state photoacids
(mPAHs) are capable of reversibly generating a high proton concentration
and a long-lived acidic state during their photoreaction.
−

 These unique capabilities have stimulated not only numerous exciting
applications
,−

 but also fundamental studies to identify the underlying photoinduced
structural changes (PSCs) and competing electronic excited-state processes
using a variety of optical spectroscopic techniques. So far, all the
available reports have been based on either steady-state or time-resolved
optical spectroscopic measurements upon one-photon excitation,
,,,−

 except two studies.
,

Two-photon excitation of
mPAHs offers direct access to the electronic
excited-state(s) that are often inaccessible to one-photon excitation.
This can lead to potentially distinct photoreaction pathways, PSCs,
and excited-state relaxation processes, which can in turn promote
new capabilities and applications. Here, we report a two-photon excited
fluorescence (TPEF) spectroscopic study on a highly water-soluble
indazole mPAH using time- and frequency-resolved fluorescence (TFRF)
spectroscopy. Upon excitation with ∼50 fs pulses centered at
804 nm, we observed a readily detectable fluorescence emission in
the similar spectral region as commonly found with one-photon excitation,
which is also directly visible to the unaided eye. The fluorescence
intensity scales quadratically with excitation intensity, validating
its two-photon excitation origin. This validation also enabled us
to estimate the two-photon absorption cross-section (σ) for
this mPAH using a comparative method. Direct comparison of the time-resolved
fluorescence (TRF) decays upon one- and two-photon excitation, as
well as the time-integrated TPEF emission spectrum with one-photon
excited steady-state emission spectrum further reveals both similarities
and differences. The observed differences offer indirect evidence
for the involvement of different photoproducts and pathways in the
respective photoreactions. This finding suggests a potential route
towards unlocking new functionalities and applications of these mPAHs
via two-photon excitation.

The water-soluble indazole mPAH employed
here differs structurally
from other mPAHs with the attachment of a SO3® substituent
on the indazole moiety and the addition of an ester group to the benzothiazolium
side as shown in a. The reaction mechanism of this indazole
mPAHs has been the subject of a previous study. Specifically, it was revealed that photoirradiation of
this indazole mPAH generates a quinoidal cis isomer
in water, which possesses a higher acidity than that of the trans isomer due to the unusual quinoidal structure. The
deprotonated cis isomer was further found to be the
intermediate for both the spiro formation and the
reverse cis-to-trans reaction. In
view of the slightly higher acidity of the cis isomer
than that of the trans isomer, but the acidity of
the spiro form is significantly greater, it was suggested
that the proton transfer occurs either synchronically with or sequentially
after the conversion of the cis isomer to the spiro form. In either case, this proton transfer is known
to be preceded by the corresponding trans–cis photoisomerization, and based on the determined time
scale of approximately 25 ps for the trans–cis photoisomerization of a different indazole mPAH in neat
water, it is reasonable to consider that
the proton transfer takes place in a time scale greater than 25 ps
for the indazole mPAH examined in this study. Moreover, the reverse
reaction rate of this indazole mPAH was measured by acquiring absorption
spectra at different times upon switching off irradiation. As shown
in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information,
the spectra recover to the initial trans isomer in
20 min. By fitting the data to a first order reaction equation, which
is commonly used for mPAHs, the reverse
reaction rate was calculated to be 1.6 × 10–3 s–1, corresponding to a half-life of 433 s or
7.2 min.

Upon excitation with ∼50 fs pulses centered
at 804 nm, we
found that the TPEF signal from an aqueous solution can be readily
observed. The resulting TPEF is also directly visible to the unaided
eye as shown in Figure S2. TRF decays acquired
at 560 nm under different excitation intensities of ∼50 fs

integrated fluorescence intensity over the entire temporal range of

anticipated quadratic intensity dependence for the obtained TPEF signals.
This result, to the best of our knowledge, represents the first observation
of TPEF emission from all currently available mPAHs.

While Tsutskiridze et al. have shown that the
photoreaction of a merocyanine-based photoacid can be initiated by
two-photon excitation with a lower efficiency than its one-photon
excitation, it remains unexplored if
the underlying reaction pathway resembles or differs from the one
induced by one-photon excitation. Measurements of the TPEF decays
and time-integrated emission spectrum enable us to gain insight into
this unanswered question. First, we note that the two-photon excitation
leads to a distinct TPEF decay from the one acquired using one-photon
excitation. As shown in , the TPEF decay at 560 nm exhibits a noticeably slower decay
than that measured upon one-photon excitation. Quantitative analysis
of these decay profiles was performed employing a least-squares deconvolution
fitting algorithm with explicit consideration of the finite instrument
response function (IRF) in combination with a model function consisting
of a sum of exponential components. Using an IRF with a full width
at half maximum (FWHM) of 48 ps determined using a pyridinium styryl
dye, 2-[2-(4-dimethylamino-phenyl)-vinyl]-1-methylpyridinium iodide
(o-DASPMI) in 10 mM phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4) at the same
emission wavelength, we found that a
model function consisting of two exponential components is needed
for satisfactory fit of the one-photon decay with the resulting lifetimes
(relative amplitudes) of 25 ps (50.3%) and 60 ps (49.7%), respectively.
In contrast, a model function consisting of three exponential components
is necessary to describe satisfactorily the TPEF decay with the resolved
lifetimes (relative amplitudes) of 23 ps (42.9%), 69 ps (56.0%), and
409 ps (1.2%), respectively. Although the relative amplitude of this
slowest decay component is very small, its inclusion in the fitting
is necessary to describe the long-lived fluorescence signal that is
observed only with two-photon excitation. We note that this small
relative amplitude corresponds to a much greater fractional intensity,

As shown in b, while the resulting time-integrated spectrum exhibits considerable
similarity to the corresponding one-photon excited steady-state spectrum,
a clear red shift and a distinct feature on the longer wavelength

overlap between the absorption and TPEF emission spectra shown in b may contribute

To assess the distinct feature on the longer wavelength side, we performed
spectral deconvolution analysis for the TPEF spectrum. As shown in c, two Gaussian components
with peak wavelengths at 564.4 and 637.1 nm in combination with their
corresponding FWHM of 71.8 and 75.5 nm are necessary to satisfactorily
describe the TPEF spectrum. The observed distinct TPEF decay and time-integrated
emission spectrum strongly suggest that different isomers are involved
in one- and two-photon excitation. Existence of these isomers including
those transient species has been reported by several groups.
,,−
,
 These isomers may be structurally similar but differ
in their polarizability or hyperpolarizability, leading to distinct
behavior for one- and two-photon excitation.
,
 Since the donor and acceptor parts of these isomers are the same,
the difference in polarizability is directly related to their conjugation.
Specifically, an isomer with the least conjugation and polarizability
should possess moderate one-photon fluorescence but no or very weak
two-photon fluorescence. Conversely, those with the largest conjugation
and polarizability should possess high two-photon fluorescence but
none or very weak one-photon fluorescence. This observation further
indicates activation of different photoreaction pathways upon one-
or two-photon excitation. Given the complicated reaction pathways
of such mPAHs and associated electronic structures of various isomers,
,,,
 any attempt to identify the possible pathways induced by two-photon
excitation will necessitate detailed theoretical calculations, which
are beyond the scope of this letter.

Two-photon excitation occurs either within a finite
volume for
a focused laser beam or along the laser propagation path for a non-focused
high intensity laser beam. Consequently, the photoreaction and the
resulting proton release is spatially confined, making its detection
challenging for both pH and absorption spectral measurements. As detailed
in the Supporting Information, upon irradiating
with a non-focused beam with 1/e
2 diameter
of 936 μm (see Figure S3) and an
average power of 220 mW, which was kept below the threshold of white-light
continuum generation, our pH measurements on aqueous solutions with
a total volume of 8-10 ml using a conventional pH meter (Orion Star
A211 with 8107BNUMD probe) lead to a pH change too small to be reliably
validated. This is in stark contrast to the pH change from 6.55 to
3.88 upon irradiating the sample with a 470 nm LED for 5 min. This unsuccessful attempt is consistent with
the estimated low proton concentration as detailed in the Supporting Information. While continuous irradiation
of a sample solution over 7 h leads to a noticeable absorbance decrease
of the trans isomer absorption peaking at 418 nm,
we observed no absorption recovery upon keeping the sample solution
in darkness for 1 h (see Figure S4). This
observation does not mean that the two-photon initiated photoreaction
is irreversible, but instead the resulting changes are too small for
ensemble spectroscopic measurements. Future verification of this reversibility
may be achieved through a microscopic approach capable of probing
selectively the irradiation volume of two-photon excitation or, alternatively,
irradiation with a high intensity, continuous wave near-infrared light
source will create a sufficiently large effect for spectroscopic detection
at an ensemble level.

In this study, we report, to the best
of our knowledge, the first
observation of TPEF emission from an aqueous solution of a highly
water-soluble indazole mPAH. The readily observable fluorescence emission
was found to not only follow the anticipated quadratic intensity dependence
but also possess a sizeable two-photon absorption cross-section of
∼53 GM. This value is only about 30% smaller than the 80 GM
determined for a merocyanine in chloroform at 700 nm using Z-scan technique. We further
found that the TRF decay and time-integrated emission spectrum observed
upon two-photon excitation differ clearly from the corresponding results
obtained with one-photon excitation. This difference serves as indirect
evidence for the activation of different isomers and their associated
pathways in the photoreactions upon one- and two-photon excitation.
Our findings pave a promising way of unlocking new functionalities
and applications of these mPAHs that are inaccessible to one-photon
excitation. Specifically, it can promote the formation of two-photon
absorbing isomers with large conjugation and polarizability, which
may offer new functionalities inaccessible for one-photon excitation.
Direct two-photon excitation with enhanced penetration depth and tight
focal volume will enable precise and targeted drug delivery and activation
without the need of generating visible light for one-photon excitation
using upconversion nanoparticles.
,
 It will further
offer better temporal and spatial control of multiple mPAH-mediated
processes such as activation of ion channels, ion sensing, dissipative assemblies,
,,
 and transformations in synthesis. Moreover, such two-photon excitation will provide
significant technical convenience to probe and unambiguously identify
the different isomers involved in the complicated photoreaction of
such a mPAH using ultrafast optical spectroscopic techniques. Since
all irradiated mPAH molecules will be excited upon one-photon excitation,
the slower reverse reaction rate than the repetition rates of commonly
used pulsed laser systems means that repeated excitation of same or
similar sample volume will unavoidably lead to accumulation of different
isomers. Note that none of those commonly used approaches (rapid sample
flow through excitation volume or use of fast-spinning sample cell)
is fast enough to refresh the sample for each successive laser pulse.
Consequently, identification of the spectral and dynamics associated
with each isomer is challenging, if not impossible. In contrast, the
well-defined excitation volume afforded by two-photon excitation using
focused laser beam should make access to a fresh sample volume possible
for each successive laser pulse and thus may overcome this technical
challenge.

Experimental Methods

A. Materials.
Synthesis, purification and characterization
of the water-soluble indazole mPAH with a solubility of about 30 mM
was performed following the procedure described previously. An aqueous solution of 1 mM was prepared for
all TPEF measurements using ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm).
Upon two-photon excitation with both focused and non-focused laser
beams of various average powers ranging from ∼1 mW to 220 mW,
our measurements on this and other samples with comparable concentrations
over continuous irradiation from 7 to 16 h show the largest absorbance
decrease of ∼5% at 418 nm, the peak wavelength of trans isomer absorption. This observation is strikingly distinct from
the substantial changes observed for one-photon excitation of a different
indazole mPAH over a much short exposure time (>1 h), and thus it can serve as direct evidence for
an exceptional
chemical stability of the indazole mPAH under two-photon excitation
and the resulting confined excitation volume. An example of the absorption
spectra acquired after 7 h continuous irradiation to a non-focused,
high intensity laser beam with an average power of 220 mW is shown
in Figure S4.

Rhodamine 6G (R6G,
Exciton) was chosen as the standard for two-photon
absorption cross section (σst) determination. A methanol
solution with a concentration of 8 × 10–6 M
as used for measuring the reported σst value was
prepared using spectroscopic grade methanol. Accurate concentration
adjustment was made by setting its absorbance at 530 nm to 0.928 per
cm based on the reported molar extinction coefficient of 116000 cm–1 M–1 for R6G in methanol (529.8
nm).

B. Absorption Measurements. A Shimadzu UV-1900i
spectrometer was used to measure all absorption spectra reported in
this work.

C. Steady-State Fluorescence Measurements. Relative
fluorescence quantum yield of the mPAH was determined following the
procedure described in ref. using 6G as the standard. A Fluorolog-QM spectrofluorometer
equipped with single grating excitation and emission monochromators
was used to record steady-state fluorescence spectra of the mPAH sample
and the R6G standard. Corrected fluorescence emission spectra were
collected from 435 to 800 nm upon excitation at 350 nm using a 2 nm
bandwidth for both excitation and emission monochromators, a step
size of 2 nm and an integration time of 1 s. A dilute aqueous mPAH
solution with a maximum absorbance of 0.073 in 1 cm × 1 cm quartz
cuvette was prepared using a fresh stock solution to avoid occurrence
of its photoreaction prior to measurement. Similarly, a methanol R6G
solution in a same size cuvette was also diluted to a maximum absorbance
of 0.085.

D. Time-Resolved Fluorescence Measurements. TFRF
measurements upon one- and two-photon excitation were performed using
a time-correlated single-photon-counting (TCSPC) apparatus (PicoHarp
300, Picoquant) as described previously. Briefly, the excitation light was a small portion of the output
of a 250 kHz Ti:sapphire femtosecond regenerative amplifier (RegA
9050, Coherent) capable of generating typically 50 fs, 6 μJ
pulses at 804 nm. For one-photon excitation measurements, the 804
nm pulses were further frequency doubled using a 2 mm thick BBO crystal
to obtain excitation pulses centered at 402 nm. The detection combines
an ultra-stable Gemini Interferometer (Nireos SRL) for frequency-resolved
detection capability, and a hybrid photomultiplier detector assembly
(PMA Hybrid 07, PicoQuant) with a timing resolution <50 ps (FWHM). Measurements of the intensity dependence of
both mPAH and R6G samples were performed by acquiring TRF data at
a fixed emission wavelength of 560 nm using a bandpass filter with
a FWHM of 10 nm (FBH560-10, Thorlabs) under different excitation intensities.
A short pass filter (770/SP, Semrock) was also used to ensure complete
elimination of the pump light scattering from reaching the detector.
During these measurements, the Gemini Interferometer was set at a
fixed, zero-time delay. A 4.0 ps channel time and a fixed integration
time were chosen for all TRF data acquisition. To determine the fluorescence
intensity at a given excitation intensity, we integrated the TRF data
over the entire time range of the detectable signal.

Acquisition
of TFRF data on the aqueous mPAH solution upon two-photon
excitation was performed following a similar procedure as described
previously for one-photon excitation. To detect the emission over
the entire spectral range, we replaced the 560 nm bandpass filter
with a second short pass filter (FESH0750, Thorlabs). A laser power
of 4.5 mW was chosen for the two-photon excitation.
