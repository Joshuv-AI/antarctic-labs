Primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma: An extraordinarily rare malignancy with a challenging diagnosis
Authors: Connor W. Smith, Elias Lugo-Fagundo, Huili Li, Elliot K. Fishman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma is an exceedingly rare vascular endothelial neoplasm, accounting for a minute fraction of sarcomas with fewer than 60 cases reported in the medical literature. We report the case of a 70-year-old female with primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma, presenting with a rapidly enlarging right adrenal mass and associated severe right upper quadrant pain. Biochemical testing revealed that the mass was nonfunctional, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a large, heterogeneously enhancing lesion with inferior vena cava compression. Given rapid interval growth and severe symptoms, she underwent laparoscopic adrenalectomy, and histopathology with immunohistochemistry confirmed a diagnosis of primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma. Postoperative management included paclitaxel chemotherapy, and the patient has remained disease-free for 2 years post-operation. This case emphasizes that the clinical and imaging features of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma are nonspecific, and definitive diagnosis requires surgical pathology with vascular marker staining to avoid misclassification as metastatic carcinoma. Timely resection in combination with chemotherapy can yield favorable outcomes despite this tumor’s aggressive behavior and overall poor prognosis.

Introduction

Angiosarcomas are a rare, aggressive subtype of soft tissue sarcomas that arise from vascular or lymphatic endothelial cell lines [1,2]. Although angiosarcomas most commonly affect the skin and soft tissue, particularly in the head and neck regions, they have been shown to occur in other visceral and internal organs as well. Adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma is an exceedingly rare entity that accounts for less than 1% of all sarcomas, with less than 60 cases reported in the literature to our knowledge [3]. Given the poor prognosis of this disease, with a 5-year overall survival of less than 30%, timely diagnosis is of great concern [4]. However, nonspecific imaging features render diagnosis challenging, ultimately requiring pathological and immunohistochemical study. Treatment typically entails adrenalectomy with complete resection of the mass, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. However, due to the rarity of this disease, there are no widely established management guidelines. We report the case of a 70-year-old female with primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma. We highlight the central role of diagnostic imaging in tandem with clinical and pathological evaluations to diagnose and manage this highly malignant entity.

Case report

A 70-year-old female with unexplained weight loss and right upper quadrant pain with a known enlarging adrenal mass was referred to our institution for further evaluation and management. She reported a past medical history of non–small cell lung cancer 9 years prior to presentation with an associated right lower lobectomy. Previous laboratory findings at an external institution revealed normal DST cortisol suppression, no androgen excess, and normal levels of aldosterone, free urine metanephrines, and DHEA-S. This mass was initially favored to be a hemangioma or other benign etiology. Upon presentation to our institution, the patient reported unexplained weight loss and severe right upper quadrant pain that radiated into her back and ipsilateral shoulder. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis were performed and revealed a heterogeneously enhancing mass centered in the right adrenal gland (Fig. 1). It was noted that the mass had grown since its previous measurement of 5 × 4.9 × 4.8 cm-7.1 × 5.5 × 6.3 cm in under 2 weeks. The heterogeneous enhancement and rapid interval growth of the mass suggested that a hemorrhage may have occurred. It was noted that the tumor extended anteriorly and posteriorly to the inferior vena cava with significant compression of the IVC right wall. No evidence of metastasis was observed. Given the severity of symptoms and the interval growth of the mass, a laparoscopic right adrenalectomy was performed. Pathology was performed on the surgical specimen and confirmed a diagnosis of primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma. A well-circumscribed mass was present grossly (Fig. 2A). H&E sections demonstrated epithelioid neoplastic cells, forming clusters and vascular channels, and invading adrenal parenchyma (Fig. 2B). Tumor was positive for vascular markers (ERG and CD31) (Fig. 2C and D). Keratin marker OSCAR was positive in tumor, but the other keratin marker AE1/3 was negative (Fig. 2E and F). In addition, the tumor was also negative for TTF1, Napsin A, and p40, excluding a diagnosis of metastatic lung cancer. A hematoma in the adrenal bed was identified postoperatively on subsequent CT imaging but remained stable throughout follow-up. Discussion

Due to the profound rarity of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma, little is known about its epidemiology or etiology. Angiosarcomas at large, however, are known to predominantly affect the skin and soft tissue, with cutaneous angiosarcoma comprising about two thirds of all angiosarcoma cases [5]. It has been suggested that environmental exposure to certain chemicals may be linked to epithelioid angiosarcoma, such as vinyl chloride, thorium dioxide, anabolic steroid therapy, and arsenic [2,4,6,7]. However, no scientific evidence has been established to support this theory. Clinically, adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma often presents with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, abnormal bowel movements, and unintentional weight loss [1,4]. However, it is possible that this entity is diagnosed incidentally in asymptomatic cases, as advancements in imaging have led to increased rates (4%) of adrenal incidentaloma discovery [8]. In such cases, correlation with clinical presentation and biochemical analysis is crucial. Incidentally discovered adrenal masses should be thoroughly evaluated for hormonal function and for malignant potential, which may necessitate surgical intervention.

In any event, the first-line imaging modality for adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma is CT. However, ultrasonography (US), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) have also been employed in some cases [1,9,10]. MRI can be particularly useful in cases of localized soft tissue sarcoma, facilitating tumor cellularity analyses and disease extension with advanced sequences such as diffusion-weighted imaging [11]. Particularly in adrenal angiosarcoma, cross sectional imaging is especially useful as many cases are metastatic from other primary tumors [9]. Angiosarcomas generally appear on CT as large, cystic, heterogeneous masses with necrotic areas and peripheral contrast enhancement [12,13]. Due to its rarity, no pathognomonic imaging features have been described for adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma on CT, unlike other anatomical variants of this etiology. Reported cases of primary adrenal angiosarcoma encompass a broad range of imaging appearances. Wannasai et al. [14] reported a case of primary adrenal angiosarcoma appearing on CT as a large, lobulated mass with punctuate calcifications and focal necrosis with no evidence of metastasis [14]. Feng et al. [15] reported another case that demonstrated variable density and decreased enhancement peripherally [15]. Given the nonspecific imaging features, thorough clinical, laboratory, and pathological evaluation are required for appropriate management of this entity. Other etiologies commonly in the differential diagnosis of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma include pheochromocytoma, adrenal cortical carcinoma, adrenal lymphoma, adrenal adenoma, primary hyperaldosteronism, and metastasis from other sites [9]. Pheochromocytoma is known to frequently hemorrhage, with spontaneous adrenal bleeding occurring in nearly 50% of cases. Furthermore, other benign adrenal tumors may exhibit heterogeneity due to bleeding as well, with instances of hemorrhage reported in adrenal myelolipomas, adrenal cysts, and adrenal ganglioneuromas [16]. Therefore, evidence of hemorrhage on imaging can lead to diagnostic uncertainty, as neither malignant or benign entities can be excluded by its presence.

In the case reported above, thorough laboratory testing was performed to exclude hormonally functional etiologies, such as pheochromocytoma, primary hyperaldosteronism, and Cushing’s disease. CT imaging comparisons highlighted the rapid enlargement of the mass and heterogeneous enhancement indicative of hemorrhage, which was correlated to her severe right upper quadrant pain. Furthermore, compression was observed around approximately half of the IVC’s circumference, exhibiting diffuse mass effect on the IVC. Due to the symptomatic severity and rapid interval growth, however, an adrenalectomy was performed. Ultimately, a definitive diagnosis of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma cannot be achieved without pathological and immunohistochemical confirmation.

The pathological diagnosis of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma is often challenging. Its positive immunoreactivity with cytokeratin, in tandem with the epithelioid histologic pattern, may lead to a misdiagnosis of metastatic carcinoma of the adrenal gland [13]. Furthermore, it is not recommended to perform transcutaneous biopsy of adrenal neoplasms due to the high risk of complications. Studies have reported between 3% and 11% complication rates following transcutaneous adrenal biopsy, including hemorrhage, needle-tract tumor seeding, pneumothorax, and pancreatitis [[16], [17], [18]]. Furthermore, a study by Williams et al. [17] demonstrated that 12% of patients who underwent transcutaneous adrenal biopsy yielded nondiagnostic test results [17]. Especially in the case we report, the clinical severity of this patient’s presentation indicated surgical intervention as an appropriate option without adrenal biopsy. Pathological analysis is more frequently performed on species obtained via surgical resection of the tumor. Histologically, adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma typically appears with a solid epithelioid pattern, unlike most other angiosarcomas which have vasoformative patterns [19]. It is important to perform immunostaining for vascular markers such as CD31, FLI-1, CD34, and factor VIII to prevent misdiagnosis as metastatic carcinoma given this entity’s positive reactivity for cytokeratin [13]. In the case reported above, pathological analysis revealed positive immunostaining for ERG, CD31, and OSCAR with a negative lung triple stain (TTF1/Napsin-A/P40) to rule out metastasis from her previous non–small cell lung cancer. Furthermore, pathological evidence of hemorrhage was consistent with the heterogeneous enhancement and rapid enlargement identified on imaging.

At the time of writing, there are no putative guidelines for treatment of adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma. However, patients typically undergo surgical intervention according to the extension of disease [4,20]. This may entail adrenalectomy alone or include additional resection of locoregional tissue and organs that are affected by the disease process. In the case of localized disease, the use of adjuvant chemotherapy remains controversial and requires further study before widespread recommendation. However, in cases of local recurrence or distant metastasis, radiotherapy and chemotherapy have been suggested as viable management strategies [21]. The patient described above demonstrated excellent outcomes following adrenalectomy with paclitaxel administered postoperatively, showing no evidence of recurrence at the latest follow-up.

Conclusion

In conclusion, primary adrenal epithelioid angiosarcoma is an exceedingly rare malignancy with a challenging diagnosis. Its nonspecific clinical and radiological presentation require laboratory study to guide management, distinguishing functional and nonfunctional adrenal tumors in its evaluation. A definitive diagnosis of this entity can only be definitively made by pathological and immunohistochemical study, in which immunostaining with vascular markers is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis. This case report highlights the crucial role of imaging in the evaluation of adrenal neoplasms and its correlation with clinical symptoms and laboratory studies. Prompt diagnosis and intervention can facilitate favorable outcomes as demonstrated by the patient in this manuscript.
