Mechanism for Aromatic
Nitration Catalyzed by Cytochrome
P450 Enzymes from Rufomycin Biosynthesis

Alicia M Kirk, Jemma Gullick, Yongwei Zhao, Ralf B Schittenhelm, Ferran Feixas, Hiroya Tomita, Yohei Katsuyama, Yasuo Ohnishi, James J De Voss, Max J Cryle, Marc Garcia-Borràs

Abstract

During the biosynthesis
of the peptide antibiotic rufomycin,
the
cytochrome P450 (P450) enzyme RufO catalyzes the aromatic nitration
of a tyrosine residue in a ribosomal pentapeptide that serves as a
precursor for nonribosomal peptide synthesis. To understand the mechanism
of this unusual P450-mediated reaction, a series of pentapeptides
were tested as substrates alongside molecular dynamics simulations
and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM, ONIOM approach)
calculations. A new substrate-bound crystal structure of the homologue
NsRufO was also obtained. These experiments revealed the intimate
and necessary involvement of a histidine residue within the pentapeptide
substrate of RufO in supporting effective nitration, with the protonation
state of the intermediate compound II proposed to influence the production
of nitrated tyrosine over unwanted nitrate formation. These findings
provide key insights into the mechanism of P450-mediated nitration
that explain how sequence differences in RufO and its homologues,
along with the structure of the pentapeptide substrate, facilitate
aromatic nitration of tyrosine by RufO.

Introduction

The rufomycins are peptide natural products
of nonribosomal origin
that exhibit potent antibiotic activity toward Mycobacterium
tuberculosis. Among key tailoring enzymes found within the
rufomycin biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC), RufO is a cytochrome P450
(P450) that has been implicated in the nitration of a Tyr residue
within rufomycin, although the exact nature of this transformation
has proved enigmatic until recently. The
native substrate of RufO has now been identified as the ribosomally
synthesized pentapeptide MRYLH, encoded by a bytA homologue found upstream of the rufNO operon in
the rufomycin BGC. RufO and its homologues
catalyze nitration of the MRYLH tyrosine residue at the 3-position,
requiring nitric oxide (NO) as a cosubstrate; the latter is provided
by the nitric oxide synthase (NOS) RufN ().
,
 Subsequent digestion
of the modified peptide by RufB releases 3-NO2-Tyr as a
noncanonical precursor for incorporation into the nonribosomal peptide
synthase (NRPS)-mediated biosynthesis pathway of rufomycin. Nitration
of amino acid precursors had previously been observed only for NRPS
pathways and not as part of a ribosomally
synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide (RiPP), particularly
one that acts as a source of a modified amino acid for an NRPS. The
biosynthesis of rufomycin provides an intriguing example of the integration
of both RiPP and NRPS pathways to generate a single natural product,
in which the RiPP pathway provides a noncanonical amino acid for the
NRPS machinery.

Within the vast catalogue of ∼1,000,000
sequences, P450s
are known to catalyze an incredible array of distinct chemical transformations,
−

 with only three other P450s known to catalyze aromatic nitration:
TxtE,
,
 IlaN, and Laj2. These are all also accompanied by a nitric oxide
synthase: TxtD, IlaM, and Laj3, respectively. Additionally, a P450BM3 variant has been engineered to catalyze nitration, but
it uses nitrite rather than NO as a nitrogen source. Previous investigations of TxtE, other model systems, and
most recently on RufO have proposed the diffusion-controlled reaction
of NO and ferric-superoxide to form a peroxynitrite intermediate.
−

 Unlike the canonical P450 cycle, this proposed mechanism requires
only a single electron transferred from a reductase partner (). Homolytic cleavage
of the peroxynitrite would generate the nitrogen dioxide radical, •NO2, and a deprotonated Compound II (dp-Cpd
II), with subsequent steps remaining unclear.

For tryptophan/indole nitration by either TxtE
or a model heme
catalyst, the following two mechanisms have been proposed: either •NO2 adds directly to the aromatic ring followed
by ipso-hydrogen abstraction to form the nitrated
product, or alternatively in solution, one equivalent of •NO2 activates the substrate (through abstraction of the
indole N–H hydrogen) and a second equivalent then forms the
nitrated product through radical recombination.
,
 Typically, accepted mechanisms of phenol and protein tyrosine nitration
involve initial oxidation to generate a phenolic or tyrosyl radical,
which then couples with •NO2. We propose that initial oxidation of tyrosine
is also a key step in RufO-catalyzed nitration.

Crystal structures
of RufO (PDB ID: 8SPC, 8SPP, 9EBY, 9DUJ) show a high structural similarity along with
45% sequence identity to the biaryltide cross-linking P450Blt (PDB ID: 8U2M
), for which MRYLH is also a substrate,
albeit one in which a cross-link is formed between the Tyr-3 and His-5
residues of this peptide.
−

 A key difference in the RufO
active site is the mutation of the widely conserved I-helix alcohol
residue Ser239 to Val. This change is thought to prevent the protonation
of the ferric-superoxide/ferric-peroxide required to form Compound
I and to potentially alter the redox potential of the substrate Tyr-3
residue.
,
 However, the P450Blt S239V mutant
cannot catalyze nitration of (nor cross-link) the MRYLH substrate
in the presence of NO, suggesting this difference alone does not dictate
the change in catalytic activity from cross-linking to nitration. Nolan and co-workers recently suggested that
differences in the hydrogen-bonding network to the substrate His-5
may contribute to enzymatic selectivity by increasing the Tyr-3 to
His-5 distance by 0.7 Å in the RufO-bound MRYLH substrate (based
on aromatic ring centroids in the crystal structures), which could
hinder peptide cross-linking.
,
 Complicating this further
is data from previous studies that show RufO has the capacity to catalyze
cross-linking of the non-native MRYLW substrate in addition to its
capacity for catalyzing nitration of the native MRYLH substrate.
,,
 Thus, the underlying mechanisms
that allow these two homologous P450s to catalyze different chemistry
remain elusive.

Now, through a combination of substrate turnover
experiments, molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations, and quantum mechanics (QM) calculations,
we present results that strongly implicate tyrosyl radical formation
as essential in the proposed mechanism of nitration and suggest that
differences in the hydrogen-bonding network in RufO may facilitate
a key proton-transfer step to avoid nitrate formation. Elucidating
these mechanisms advances our understanding of selective nitro group
incorporation in natural product biosynthesis and informs future efforts
to engineer effective nitration biocatalysts with potential medicinal,
pharmaceutical, and agrochemical applications.

Results and Discussion

Substrate
Turnover

A series of peptides were subjected
to in vitro nitration assays to further investigate
RufO selectivity and gain insights into the mechanism of this P450.
In a yet unexplained phenomenon, all RufO enzymes exclusively utilize
the MRYLH peptide as the substrate. The
reason for the lack of variation in the natural substrate is not obvious.
Therefore, a series of mutants were generated across positions 2–5
of the analogous peptide Nle-RYLH (Nle being preferred to Met to avoid
facile sulfoxidation that has a deleterious effect on binding to the
P450) (Figure S1). Across all of the experiments conducted for this study, a dramatic
reduction in the level of nitration by this enzyme was observed relative
to previous studies with the substrate Nle-RYLH. This reduction in activity appeared to result from degradation
of the NO source (diethylamine NONOate) despite best efforts to preserve
it; similar activity levels have also recently been reported by Nolan
et al. Further attempts to rectify this
using new enzyme preparations also afforded the same results, and
hence, the results are reported here relative to the nitration observed
for the control peptide (Nle-RYLH) using the same batches of P450
and NO source. The enzymatic reaction of a RufO homologue, called
NsRufO, with Nle-RYLH was also compared, displaying 46% conversion
compared to that of RufO.

All changes to the substrate reduced
activity relative to the control (A and Figures S73 and S74). Truncation of the position 2 side chain to an alanine
residue (Nle-AYLH) reduced activity to 17% of that observed
with the native peptide. Similarly, mutation of position 4 from Leu-4
to the aromatic Tyr (Nle-RYYH) reduced activity to 23%
while maintaining selectivity for nitration of Tyr-3 (E,F).
Mutation of the crucial Tyr-3 to Phe (Nle-RFLH) or O-Me-tyrosine
(Nle-R-(OMe)­Tyr-LH) prevented nitration altogether, while
nitration of the peptide containing meta-tyrosine
(3-hydroxyphenylalanine) in the central position (Nle-R-
mTyr-LH) was reduced to 48%. It is important to note
that the lack of nitration of Nle-RFLH was not related
to a loss of binding efficiency for this substrate compared to Nle-RYLH
(B,C). It should be noted that a higher concentration
of RufO was used in the binding assays compared to previous studies, as we assumed that the non-native substrates
would display weaker affinities toward RufO; thus a higher apparent
Kd was calculated for RufO with Nle-RYLH. In position 5,
glutamic acid was tolerated to a small extent (Nle-RYLE, 13%), but not thiazolalanine (Nle-RYL-Thz, 0%) or
glutamine (Nle-RYLQ, 0%), with the Nle-RYL-Thz peptide also exhibiting a 10-fold loss of binding affinity (D). The tolerance
of 5-Glu but not 5-Gln is curious, as Gln is typically seen as a closer
replacement for His than Glu. Finally, and as introduced above, limited
nitration was observed for tryptophan in position 5 (Nle-RYLW); instead, cross-linking occurred preferentially with this
substrate. While Nle-RYLH displayed the tightest binding to RufO,
as indicated by the lowest K
d value, all
three of the peptides with alternative residues in position 3 (Nle-RFLH, Nle-R-(OMe)­Tyr-LH, and Nle-R-mTyr-LH) displayed increased binding efficiencies, whereas peptides with
modifications in positions 2, 4, and 5 had decreased binding efficiencies
(Table S1).

The results obtained from the position 3 mutants
of the Nle-RYLH
substrate implicate the phenolic hydrogen as an important contributor
to the mechanism of nitration. Somewhat counterintuitively, the histidine
in position 5 also seems to be intimately involved in facilitating
efficient nitration, as loss of this residue prevents or greatly decreases
nitration, and replacement with a reactive coupling partner like tryptophan
results in cross-linking. To assess whether altered binding or intrinsic
reactivity could account for impaired catalysis across the mutant
series, structure prediction, MD simulations, and reaction pathway
QM calculations were employed.

Predicted Binding Conformation
of MRXXX to RufO

The
Alphafold3 (AF3) server was used to predict the binding conformations
of the pentapeptide substrates MRYLH (1), MRYLW (2), MRYLE (3), MRYLQ (4), MRFLH (5), and MRYYH (6) to RufO
with the heme cofactor present. A typical
P450 trigonal-prism fold was generated with high confidence with the
substrate bound (Figures S83–S85). During the preparation of this manuscript, the structure of 1-bound
RufO was published (PDB ID: 9EBY). The RMSD between the
top AF3 predicted structure and 9EBY was 0.60 Å across all 390
pairs of residue backbones (including bound 1) or 1.04
Å with the inclusion of all side chain atoms.

For the native
substrate 1, the predicted binding conformation was in
general agreement with that observed in the crystal structure (PDB
ID: 9EBY) and
similar to the conformation found in the substrate 1-bound
crystal structure of P450Blt (PDB ID: 8U2M) (). Specifically, the substrate
Tyr-3 and His-5 side chains were positioned over the heme, with the
latter hydrogen bonded to the I-helix H235 residue along with hydrogen
bonding between the substrate backbone and R231, R188, and Q84. The
predicted E239 side chain rotamer aligned more closely to that seen
in the P450Blt crystal structure as opposed to the RufO
crystal structures. The predicted H235 side chain position deviated
slightly from that observed in the substrate-bound RufO crystal structure;
however, the overall conformational change that occurs between the
substrate-free and substrate-bound enzyme conformations was captured,
including the shifts in R188 and H235.

For all of the other substrates, the predicted
binding conformation
was largely unchanged with respect to that predicted for 1 (). The side
chains of substrate positions 3 and 5 remained bound over the heme.
To accommodate the larger substrate 2, the H235 side
chain rotated to adopt a conformation analogous to that found in the
substrate-free RufO crystal structure, accompanied by rotation of
the E329 side chain. For 6, selectivity for nitration
of Tyr-3 can be rationalized based on Tyr-4 facing away from the heme
when bound. For 3 and 4, H235 was able to
hydrogen bond to Glu-5 and Gln-5, respectively, which both, in turn,
could hydrogen bond to Tyr-3; no structural reasons for the reduction
in nitration of these two substrates were immediately apparent.

In parallel with the computational mechanistic investigations that
follow, a substrate-bound crystal structure of a RufO homologue, referred
to as NsRufO, was pursued to obtain further experimental insights
into substrate positioning. This crystal structure will be discussed
further below; however, in brief, the Nle-RYLH substrate was found
to be bound similarly to the conformation observed for 1bound RufO. There were, however, variations in the conformations
of the H235 and E239 side chains (C).

Quantum Mechanics Calculated Potential Reaction
Mechanisms

Using a truncated model system and density functional
theory (DFT),
two potential nitration mechanisms were investigated: (A) direct radical
addition of •NO2 to tyrosine versus (B)
radical recombination of •NO2 with a
tyrosyl radical (). Both begin with homolytic cleavage of the s-cis-peroxynitrite intermediate (TS1) to form the radical •NO2. Cleavage of the s-trans-peroxynitrite was calculated to occur via a pathway with a higher
energy barrier (Figure S86). Here, unlike
in the models of TxtE-catalyzed nitration of l-tryptophan,
,,
 direct addition of •NO2 to the aromatic carbon (TS2) (of phenol
as opposed to tryptophan) in path A, with subsequent abstraction of
the ipso-hydrogen (TS3), was calculated
to be the highest energy pathway. Subsequent abstraction of the phenolic
hydrogen (TS4) to form the tautomeric product could be
an alternative. However, path B (blue) was found to be lower in energy,
involving phenolic hydrogen abstraction by deprotonated Compound II
(TS5) followed by energetically barrierless radical recombination
in the gas phase (refer to Figures S87 and S88 for the calculated PES). The key involvement of the phenolic group
in the mechanism of Path B agrees with the importance of the phenolic
OH group demonstrated experimentally in the alternative substrates
explored above, where a lack of reactivity resulted from its removal
or its substitution to eliminate the abstractable hydrogen.

Furthermore, abstraction of a phenolic hydrogen
would seem logical
if RufO has diverged from the cross-linking P450Blt for
which a diradical mechanism involving abstraction of the phenolic
hydrogen is proposed. Abstraction of
phenolic hydrogen atoms by both Cpd I and Cpd II in the cross-linking
mechanism of the P450 OxyB was also calculated to have extremely low
energy barriers. Moreover, for a P450
TxtE model complex, nitration of tryptophan was also proposed to proceed
through a diradical mechanism where a cage-escaped •NO2 first abstracts the NH hydrogen of the indole ring. By analogy, we might postulate that for substrate 2 (MRYLW) with RufO, where cross-linking is mainly
observed, •NO2 could likewise abstract
the N–H hydrogen of the tryptophan indole ring.
If Cpd II abstracts the phenolic hydrogen, then this facilitates a
diradical coupling mechanism. Abstraction of an indole N–H was calculated to be 7.0 kcal mol–1 lower
in energy than abstraction of an imidazole hydrogen (Figure S89), which would be congruent with no cross-linking
being observed for the native MRYLH.

Finally, as
noted with other model complexes, heme–nitrate
complexes are an alternative product
resulting from those •NO2 radicals that
do not escape from the caged radical intermediate. Formation of nitrate
as a byproduct through •NO2 rebound (TSNR) was found to be low in energy and may be a competitive
pathway subject to diffusion of the •NO2 radical (, red). Consistent with this possibility, nitrogen dioxygenases (NODs)
convert nitric oxide to nitrate through a mechanism proposed to involve
the formation of a heme–peroxynitrite intermediate.
,
 In NODs, homolytic O–O cleavage forms •NO2, which then recombines with Fe­(IV)-oxo. Furthermore,
nitric oxide synthases (NOSs) are reported to form nitrate upon reaction
with peroxynitrite, again via isomerization of heme–peroxynitrite
through •NO2 formation and radical recombination. Therefore, nitrate formation seems a plausible
competing pathway in RufO-catalyzed nitration given the same initial
O–O cleavage is proposed, unless additional features of RufO
suppress it.

Previous studies have demonstrated that H-bonding
from a proximal
histidine stabilizes tyrosine radicals. Moreover, small models of P450Blt cross-linking indicated
that histidine protonation states and H-bonding interactions altered
the relative energetics of different coupling pathways. Whether involvement of the substrate His-5 residue
and the larger H-bonding network influences the partition between
nitration and nitrate formation is further explored using MD simulations
and QM/MM modeling (see below). Refer to the SI (Figure S90) for a brief discussion of a comparative small-model
study including imidazole.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

MD
simulations of substrate-bound
RufO in the superoxide and peroxynitrite intermediate forms were performed
in triplicate (500 ns each independent trajectory), starting from
the AF3 predicted bound structure with manual addition of dioxygen
or peroxynitrite. The substrate remained bound throughout the entire
duration of the simulations, indicating a stable binding conformation
(see RMSD measurements in Figure S91).
For substrate-bound P450Blt, only the superoxide intermediate
was simulated. Key hydrogen-bonding patterns were analyzed as summarized
in and S4.

H-bonding was observed from Tyr-3 to Fe–O more
often for
the peroxynitrite intermediate (, entry 1): 60% (peroxynitrite) versus
15% and 11% (superoxides). A higher prevalence of an H-bond from His-5
to Tyr-3 was observed in RufO compared to that seen with P450Blt:12–16% versus 4% (, entry 3). This appears to
result from the slight change in the binding position of His-5 between
the two enzymes. The transience of this H-bond is consistent with
the His-5 to Tyr-3 contact observed in the substrate-bound RufO crystal
structure (PDB ID: 9EBY), which, after hydrogen placement, should
be regarded as a borderline preorganized contact rather than as evidence
for a persistent hydrogen bond under the stricter quantitative criterion
used for MD analysis. Furthermore, a reduction in H-bonding from H235(234)
to His-5 in RufO, 56–60% versus 82% (, entry 6), implies a greater
flexibility in the positioning of H235 and His-5 in the modified H-bonding
network for RufO. This is accompanied by greater solvent accessibility
of H235:29–37% versus 0% (, entry 7).

Representative snapshots
of the most populated conformational cluster
from accumulated MD simulations are shown in . The larger peroxynitrite ligand, compared
to the oxo ligand, did not appear to significantly alter the binding
position of the substrate. Nor did the absence of an H-bond from S239
(V240) change the mean distance of the 3-Tyr hydroxyl to the heme
Fe in the superoxo intermediate MD simulations: RufO 4.7 ± 0.4
Å versus P450 Blt 4.4 ± 0.3 Å. The 3-Tyr phenolic hydrogen
remains well positioned for abstraction during the catalytic nitration
mechanism in RufO.

Quantum Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics Calculations

To further
explore the role of the protein environment on the RufO
catalytic nitration mechanism, the energetic landscape of proposed
mechanisms was calculated using QM/MM calculations based on the ONIOM
,
 approach as implemented in Gaussian 16 Software (). The initial conformation was obtained by selecting a representative
snapshot from the MD simulations based on clustering of the substrate
and active site residues (Figure S92).
The quantum mechanics (QM) model region was expanded compared to the
small truncated DFT model to include the substrate His-5 and I-helix
residue H235, while the rest of the protein and solvent were treated
at the molecular mechanics (MM) level.

First, the energy pathway for a mechanism analogous
to the small
truncated DFT model reported in was calculated ( black and Figure S93).
A relatively small 8.2 kcal mol–1 barrier for homolytic
O–O cleavage of the heme–peroxynitrite initiates the
reaction (TS-1) with the resulting •NO2 radical and dp-Cpd II (I-1) stabilized
relative to the reactant complex. The substrate Tyr-3 is well-positioned
in the active site to facilitate hydrogen atom abstraction (TS-2, DE+ZPE‡ = 13.7 kcal mol–1), forming a tyrosyl radical and Fe­(III)­OH (I-2). Radical
recombination of •NO2 and the tyrosyl
radical (TS-3, DE+ZPE‡ = 5.5 kcal mol–1) forms a nitrated keto-product (P-1)
before deprotonation facilitated by the Fe­(III)­OH (TS-4, DE+ZPE‡ = 1.6 kcal mol–1) and
rearomatizes the product (P-2), which provides the major
thermodynamic driving force of the reaction. Here, P-2 corresponds to the phenolate form of the product, consistent with
the reduction in pK
a upon tyrosine nitration.
,
 This mechanism seems energetically feasible; however, deprotonated
Cpd II is reported to be very basic (Cpd II pK
a > 10).
−

 Therefore, alternative mechanisms were considered
in which dp-Cpd II becomes protonated, considering Tyr-3 as a potential
proton donor.

The transition state for an isolated proton transfer
from Tyr-3
to Fe­(IV)O (i.e., to form a phenolate and Fe­(IV)­OH) could
not be located. Instead, an energetically favored proton relay transition
state was found, where a proton is shuttled from protonated H235 through
His-5 and Tyr-3 to Cpd II (TS-5, DE+ZPE = 6.6 kcal mol–1) ( blue and Figure S94). In the resulting
intermediate (I-3), Tyr-3 remains in the neutral phenol
form; the relayed proton resides on Cpd II in the Fe­(IV)­OH species;
both H235 and His-5 now take neutral forms. Therefore, the 4 kcal
mol–1 energy difference from I-1 to I-3 reflects both the proton relay and active-site hydrogen-bond
reorganization rather than an isolated Tyr-3 deprotonation/protonation
equilibrium.

From I-3, further reorganization of
the hydrogen-bonding
to facilitate hydrogen abstraction from Tyr-3 forms a high-energy
intermediate (I-4, DE+ZPE‡ = 17.2 kcal
mol–1) that prevents this pathway ( green). Alternatively, from I-3, a multisite proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET, TS-6, DE+ZPE‡ = 9.5 kcal mol–1, blue) can
generate the key tyrosyl radical and Fe­(III)­OH (i.e., I-2 in the initially described black pathway) and proceed as before
through a diradical recombination. This alternative mechanism intimately
involves His-5, which acts first as an acid to indirectly protonate
Fe­(IV)O and then as a base to deprotonate Tyr-3 during the
PCET.

Preorganization of the protein environment to facilitate
protonation
of the Fe­(IV)O intermediate might provide a mechanism to avoid
dominant nitrate formation. Nitrate formation was considered as a
competing pathway at two points along the reaction mechanisms. From I-1, a small barrier was identified for radical rebound of •NO2 (TS-9) to irreversibly
form nitrate (P-4) ( red). TS-9 was 7.4 kcal mol–1 lower in energy than TS-2, which in
the absence of another pathway would imply exclusive nitrate formation;
however, TS-9 was similar in energy to proposed TS-5, enabling the subsequent nitration pathway via Cpd II
protonation I-3 and PCET TS-6. Furthermore,
from I-3, •NO2 rebound with
Cpd II (TS-10) to form nitric acid (P-5)
( pink) was
within 0.7 kcal mol–1 of TS-6, suggesting
the PCET would indeed be kinetically competitive. Nitric acid is also
a much less thermodynamically favorable product. Given the computed
barriers separating productive nitration from nitrate/nitric-acid
formation in the present QM/MM model differ by less than 1 kcal mol–1, these calculations, rather than quantitatively proving
kinetic suppression of nitrate formation, should be interpreted as
supporting a plausible role of the Tyr-3/His-5/H235/water proton relay
in biasing product formation. If protonation of the Fe­(IV)-oxo is
required to avoid nitrate formation and to allow for productive nitration
of Tyr-3, this would explain why the substrate NleRYLQ was not nitrated while NleRYLE demonstrated some nitration:
Glu-5 can facilitate proton transfers while Gln-5 can only act as
an H-bond donor.

Crystal Structure of Substrate-Bound RufO
Homologue NsRufO

To support the computational investigations,
we attempted to obtain
a bound structure of RufO in complex with a pentapeptide substrate
(Nle-RYLH). Due to the low yield of RufO in E. coli, we turned to its homologue (NsRufO) from Nonomurae solani (with 46% identity), whose activity was confirmed with in
vivo complementation using the ΔrufO strain constructed in the previous study (Figure S95). By soaking 100 mM substrate
into the apo crystals obtained from PEG-containing conditions (0.1
M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 30% (w/v) PEG4000, 0.35 M lithium sulfate), yielding
needle-shaped crystals with good diffraction, we successfully obtained
the substrate-bound structure (space group P21212) to a resolution of 1.59 Å (PDB ID: 22YX, Table S7).

NsRufO adopts the canonical
P450 fold, with the thiolate-coordinated heme moiety at the center
of the protein. Density surrounding the active site is well-defined,
except for residues 178–180, which could not be resolved, consistent
with relatively high flexibility between the F and G helices. The
density of the substrate was found in the active site of NsRufO and
was well resolved via fitting the Nle-RYLH pentapeptide (a). Nle-RYLH is accommodated
in the active-site pocket of NsRufO in an almost identical way to
the structure of Nle-RYLH bound to RufO (PDB ID: 9DUJ). Specifically,
Tyr-3 and His-5 are located directly above the heme, with Tyr-3 H-bonding
to a water molecule W1 that remains coordinating to the heme iron
(b). The Arg-2
side chain forms a cation–pi interaction with F69, and the
terminal carboxylate of the Nle-RYLH substrate forms a salt bridge
with R231 and a water-mediated hydrogen bond with Q84. A slight shift
in the position of R231 accommodates a 0.7 Å shift in the position
of the His-5 terminal carboxylate compared to RufO, which creates
a better H-bond alignment between His-5 and Tyr-3 (more linear N–H–O
angle: 161° versus 134°; shorter N–O distance: 3.0
Å versus 3.2 Å). The Nle side chain is stabilized in a hydrophobic
cage formed by L44, L48, T308, and M310. Direct interaction between
the N-terminal substrate amine and NsRufO could not be observed, potentially
due to the unresolved region (residues 178–180) of the protein
backbone. Although the substrate conformation is highly similar to
that seen in RufO, the I-helix H235 of NsRufO does not H-bond to His-5
as is seen in RufO. Instead, H235 adopts
a conformation similar to that found in apo RufO, pointing “away” from the active site while
His-5 interacts with E239, bridged by a water molecule W2. The different
orientations of H235 could reflect distinct protonation states under
varied crystallization buffer conditions and support the observation
that this residue can explore different conformations; such states
may represent different stages of the nitration reaction.

Protonation State of HIS-5

Based on the results obtained
thus far, the most plausible mechanism for RufO-catalyzed MRYLH nitration
is proposed to involve His-5 acting as a proton shuttle to facilitate
protonation of Cpd II, followed by tyrosyl radical formation and radical
recombination with •NO2. However, it
might be envisaged that a protonated His-5 could also directly participate
as the proton source. Therefore, alternate protomeric forms of the
substrate His-5 residue were explored through a series of classical
MD simulations of RufO in the superoxide intermediate state in order
to evaluate the feasibility of different protonation states. A comparison
to the homologue NsRufO was also made. Furthermore, the protomeric
forms of the nearby I-helix H235 and E239 residues were considered
as changes in the local hydrogen bonding network were thought to influence
the protonation state of His-5 (refer to Table S5 for summary of combinations employed).

First, we considered
the acidic E239 residue. In the AF3 structure generated for RufO,
E239 interacts with R243 and N165 and is therefore predicted to be
deprotonated at pH 7 (H++ server).
−

 However, once protonated,
E239 rotates and establishes an H-bond with D169, assuming a conformation
similar to that found in the substrate-bound crystal structure (A). Unsurprisingly,
in the conformation from the substrate-bound crystal structure of
RufO, E239 is predicted to be protonated (pH 7, H++ server). MD simulations
initialized from the crystal structure and protonated form of E239
show that after 75 ns the initial H-bond between H235 and His-5 is
lost in favor of H235 to E239 H-bonding; a water bridge forms between
the F-helix D169 and His-5 while the substrate remains bound over
the heme (B).
Comparatively, in the crystal structure of NsRufO, the I-helix H235
residue does not form an H-bond with His-5; instead, a water molecule
bridges an interaction with the nearby, deprotonated (as predicted
by H++ server at pH 7) E239. This indicates that the H-bond provided
by H235 does not appear to be essential for the substrate positioning
in RufO as it does in P450Blt.

Finally, protonated His-5 was
considered. When
neutral H235 and
protonated His-5 are modeled, the His-5 H-bond to H235 is persistently

In all
of the studied cases, the substrate remained bound in the
active site over the heme. The population of the H-bond between His-5
and Tyr-3 remained modest, suggesting transient formation of catalytically
competent conformations: 15% for HIP-HIE-GLH, 7% for HIP-HIP-GLU,
and 11% for HIE-HIP-GLH. Taken together, these simulations indicate
that upon binding, His-5 can adopt either a neutral or a protonated
state, both of which are stabilized by interactions with the active-site
residues E239 and D169, irrespective of whether H235 is neutral or
protonated. These results further support the proposal that His-5
can participate directly in the nitration mechanism and suggest that,
in both protonation states, it can act in tandem with H235 as part
of a proton relay that facilitates the protonation of dp-Cpd II and
the subsequent PCET pathway.

Conclusion

RufO
and its homologue NsRufO can catalyze
tyrosine nitration of
the pentapeptide substrate MRYLH. The proposed mechanism involves
tyrosyl formation through either a hydrogen abstraction or a proton-coupled
electron transfer. Radical recombination of the tyrosyl radical with •NO2 generated from homolytic cleavage of
heme–peroxynitrite results in a keto tautomer
of 3-NO2-Tyr that must undergo tautomerization to provide
the main thermodynamic driving force for the reaction. This contrasts
with the proposed monoradical mechanism of TxtE-catalyzed tryptophan
nitration. Loss of the abstractable phenolic hydrogen, as seen in
potential substrates such as MRFLH, prevents nitration. Moreover,
the imidazole of the substrate His-5 residue seems intimately involved
through either H-bonding to Tyr-3 or as modeled here, as part of a
proton relay. Based on the combined experimental evidence and extensive
computational modeling, we propose that the hydrogen-bonding network
involving the I-helix residues H235 and E239, together with an active
role for His-5, operates cooperatively as a proton shuttle, a mechanism
to disfavor nitrate formation and promote efficient tyrosine nitration.
By elucidating the steps following heme–peroxynitrite formation,
this study provides a stronger foundation for engineering P450s for
selective peptide nitration, with the next challenge being to define
the enzymatic features that determine peroxynitrite formation.
