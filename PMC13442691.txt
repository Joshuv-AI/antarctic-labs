Social interaction enhances the pleasurable urge to move to music in people with Parkinson's disease
Authors: Jan Stupacher, Peter Vuust, Victor Pando-Naude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusic- and dance-based interventions can improve motor symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease (PD), but two key questions remain: whether the rhythmic complexity of music influences intervention outcomes, and whether social interaction further enhances these effects.ObjectivesTo examine the effects of rhythmic complexity and interpersonal synchronization on subjective and objective movement responses to music in PD.MethodParticipants tapped to the beat of music with varying levels of rhythmic complexity across three cueing conditions: music alone, music with a visual beat cue, and socially with an assistant who also tapped to the beat. We measured pleasurable urge to move to music (PLUMM – or groove), felt synchrony with the music, beat-related movement energy, and synchronization variability in 40 individuals with mild-to-moderate PD and 36 age-matched controls.ResultsCompared to music alone, social interaction increased PLUMM, felt synchrony, beat-related movement, and synchronization stability, with the strongest effects for moderate to high rhythmic complexity. These effects were stronger in participants with PD than in controls. In contrast, visual cues had no effect. Moreover, PLUMM ratings were positively associated with social closeness toward the assistant, trait empathy, and the experience of social reward through music.ConclusionSocial interaction while moving to the beat of music enhanced both the urge to move and objective motor performance. Our findings show that improvements of motor symptoms through music- and dance-based interventions extend beyond enhanced temporal prediction through motor simulation; they also include socio-emotional mechanisms that promote pleasure and strengthen social bonding.Plain language summary titlePeople with Parkinson’s move more to music when they move with othersKeywords: rhythm, cueing, groove, PLUMM, pleasurable urge to move, interpersonal synchronization, sensorimotor synchronization, social bonding

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is characterized by its well-known motor symptoms – including slowed and reduced movement, tremor, and muscle stiffness – and by social and emotional difficulties that can substantially diminish quality of life. 1 Moving to music with others can promote social bonding and may therefore help address socio-emotional challenges, such as isolation and loneliness.2–5 Although music- and dance-based interventions for people with PD can improve motor symptoms,6–11 there is mixed evidence for their effects on socio-emotional factors and quality of life.9,10 Two key questions remain open: whether rhythmic complexity of music influences PD intervention outcomes, and whether social interaction enhances these outcomes. 9 To address this gap, we investigated how music with varying levels of rhythmic complexity influences both subjective and objective measures of sensorimotor synchronization in a beat-tapping task across three cueing conditions: music alone, music with a visual guide showing the beat, and music in a social context with a trained assistant tapping in time with the beat. We compared subjective ratings of the pleasurable urge to move to the music (PLUMM) – often referred to as groove – and the felt synchrony with the music, as well as objective measures of beat-related spontaneous movement and beat-tapping stability between 40 participants with mild-to-moderate PD and 36 age-matched controls.

Music is inherently social. It is predominantly performed in groups and for groups, often with the aim of inducing body movement and fostering social cohesion.12,13 In these contexts, music provides a shared temporal structure that facilitates the coordination of body movements within 14 and between 15 individuals, for example in dance. When the music is rhythmically engaging, movements can emerge effortlessly and almost automatically, leading to an increase in PLUMM. Empirical evidence shows that PLUMM follows an inverted U-shaped relationship with rhythmic complexity: compared to very simple and highly complex rhythms, moderately complex rhythms induce the strongest PLUMM.16–19 Within the framework of predictive processing, this inverted-U of PLUMM emerges because rhythms with moderate levels of complexity elicit the largest number of strongly weighted prediction errors,20–22 which arise when notes fall outside the regular pattern of beats. Because moderately complex rhythms hit a good balance between predictability and surprise, they maximize the amount of strongly weighted and resolvable prediction errors that can be used to update the internal model of beat and meter. The process of resolving these prediction errors has been shown to engage reward and motor networks involving dopamine.20–22

The dopaminergic system is dysfunctional in PD, leading to characteristic motor symptoms, such as akinesia and bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity. 23 In contrast to healthy participants, people with PD experience greater PLUMM with simple rhythms, indicating a preference for high predictability and low surprise. 24 This suggests that the disruption of beat-based predictive processes in PD may occur due to dopamine depletion within the motor cortico-striatal loop. 25

Rhythmic auditory stimulation with simple external timekeepers, such as a metronome, can improve motor synchronization in PD by providing regular cues.11,26–29 Additionally, commercially available music that is rated higher in groove increases gait velocity and cadence in people with PD. 8 Dance-based PD interventions in groups can improve gait symmetry and reduce disease severity.6,30 In these social contexts, the visual feedback of co-dancers’ movements may improve perception-action links. For example, perceiving someone else's movements activates the observer's own action system for the same movement, making it more likely and easier for them to initiate a similar action. 31 This shared representation of perception and action may extend to shared informational dynamics for self and other, promoting social bonding.32–34 In line with this perspective, a recent study showed that group activities with higher levels of interpersonal movement synchronization – such as choir singing – can lead to stronger social bonds and oxytocin release in people with PD. 35

Perceiving another person moving in time with music may also improve one's own predictive model of beat and meter. 15 This could increase the rhythmic complexity of music that individuals can synchronize with and that elicits the strongest PLUMM. Recent research supports this notion showing that social closeness, like PLUMM, follows an inverted-U shape in relation to rhythmic complexity. 36 This indicates that a moderate amount of complexity is needed to keep people interested and engaged in social interactions. 37 It has been argued that moderately complex rhythms offer an ideal stimulus for both group unity and individual creativity: the shared mental model of the beat fosters cohesion, while unexpected rhythmic accents invite creative expression.21,38 In people with PD, however, it remains unclear which levels of rhythmic complexity in social interactions optimally promote engagement, PLUMM, and synchronization accuracy.

Here, we investigate the effect of rhythmic complexity and social interaction on subjective and objective measures of motor engagement in people with PD. We further examine how these measures correlate with socio-emotional traits, including empathy and the experience of social reward through music. We expected that tapping with another person would increase PLUMM, felt synchrony with the music, beat-related movement energy, and stability of tapping compared to tapping alone or with the visual beat-guide, especially for participants with PD. We also hypothesized that being able to mirror another person facilitates detecting and synchronizing with the beat, particularly when tapping with higher levels of rhythmic complexity.

Methods

Participants

The PD group consisted of 40 participants (27 male, 13 female, mean age = 68.5 years, SD = 7.7, range = 46–78) and the control group of 36 age-matched participants (17 male, 19 female, mean age = 67.2 years, SD = 8.7, range = 42–80). Age did not significantly differ between groups (p = .518). Four additional participants with PD were excluded from data analysis; 3 scored less than 26 points on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), 39 indicating cognitive impairment, and 1 could not sit upright enough to see the monitor and the assistant for most of the time. One additional control participant was excluded because their Major Depression Inventory (MDI) 40 score of 31 indicated a major depression. Participant characteristics for the PD and control groups are summarized in Table 1. PD participants scored higher on the motor part of the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS-III) 41 and MDI; all other comparisons were nonsignificant (Table 1). All PD participants were tested on their usual medication. Individual information about medication and a complete list of test scores for PD participants are provided in Table S1.

Stimuli

Sixteen musical stimuli were created by the experimenters and were unfamiliar to the participants. Each stimulus was a unique combination of 1 of 8 drum rhythms (kick, snare, and hi-hat) and 1 of 2 harmonic chord progressions performed on an electric organ. The stimuli included 2 isochronous, 2 low, 2 moderate, and 2 highly complex rhythms with similar numbers of events. The structure of each rhythm was 4 bars in an ABAC pattern, repeated once for a total of 8 bars. All rhythms had an underlying eighth-note hi-hat and a tempo of 96 beats per minute (BPM) or 625 ms. The mean syncopation indexes (a measure of deviation from the beat)17,18,42 were 0 for isochronous, 27.5 for low, 59.0 for moderate, and 111.5 for high levels of complexity. Syncopations occur when a note on a metrically weak position is followed by a rest on a stronger position. In 4/4 meter, as used in the current experiment, the meter can be counted as “1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &”, with 1 and 3 carrying the strongest metrical weight and the offbeats (“&”) the weakest. For example, a syncopation occurs when a note on the “&” after the “2” is followed by a rest on “3”. Such syncopations increase perceived rhythmic complexity and are linked to prediction error.17,18,21 At the beginning of each stimulus, a metronome with 4 woodblock sounds indicated the beat. Each stimulus lasted 22.5 s, including the woodblock count-in. Audio files are available online: https://osf.io/78w62.

Design and procedure

Data were recorded at Aarhus University Hospital. Participants tapped a self-selected finger of their right hand in time with the beat of the stimuli in 3 different cueing conditions: audio only, audio-visual with a blinking circle on a laptop screen that indicated the beat, and with a trained assistant who accurately tapped in time with the beat (Figure 1A). Participants tapped on a round aluminum plate with a diameter of 4 cm connected to an Arduino based circuit board (MaKey MaKey, JoyLabz). In all 3 cueing conditions, the assistant was seated on the opposite side of the table, facing the participants, such that physical co-presence was held constant across conditions. Both, the participants and the assistant wore headphones. The assistant always tapped with a metronome and could not hear the audio of participants.

A) three cueing conditions used in the experiment. Left – audio only: participants tapped the beat of music they heard; the assistant was present but did not tap along. Middle – audio-visual: participants heard the rhythm and saw a flashing dot marking the beat on a laptop screen; the assistant was present but did not tap along. Right – social: participants tapped in synchrony with the assistant who was trained to tap in time with the beat independently of the participants’ taps. B) Visualization of the data processing for beat-related whole-body movement energy.

Participants completed one or more randomly selected practice trials in each cueing condition to ensure that they understood the task. Stimuli were presented and taps were recorded with the software Max (version 8, Cycling ‘74). After each stimulus, participants rated PLUMM and how well they felt in synchrony with the music on 7-point Likert scales. In the social condition, they additionally rated how close they felt to the co-tapping assistant, as measured by a 7-point Inclusion of Other in the Self scale (IOS). 43 Participants were not informed about the specific hypotheses concerning social interaction and rhythmic complexity until after they had completed the experiment.

After the tapping task, participants completed the following assessments and questionnaires in Danish: UPDRS-III 41 (range 0–132 points, higher scores indicate greater motor symptom severity), MoCA 39 (version 7.0, range 0–30 points, scores below 26 suggest potential cognitive impairment), MDI 40 (range 0–50 points, 20–24 points suggest mild depression, 25–29 points suggest moderate depression, 30 points or more suggest severe depression), the musical training subscale of the Goldsmith Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI 44 ; range 7–49; higher scores indicate greater formal musical training and musical expertise), the social reward subscale of the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ 45 ; range 1–5; higher scores indicate greater social reward experienced through music), and the brief version of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (B-IRI 46 ; range 1–5; higher scores indicate greater trait empathy). In total, the experiment took between 70 and 90 min.

Data processing

Beat-related movement

Movement analysis was performed by quantifying frame-to-frame pixel changes in video recordings of participants’ whole bodies (Figure 1B). Videos were recorded at a spatial resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels and a temporal resolution of 25 frames per second. Recordings began after the 4-count tapping cue and ended when the auditory stimulus stopped. For each trial, the difference between consecutive frames was calculated as the mean pixel change across all 3 RGB channels, producing a time series of frame-to-frame movement. To analyze beat-related movement, a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) was applied to each trial's movement time series using MATLAB's fft function (MathWorks, Natick, MA). Beat-related movement was operationalized as the amplitude at 1.6 Hz, corresponding to the 625 ms beat interval.

Finger tapping

The standard deviation of inter-tap intervals (ITIs) was calculated as the time between each tap n and the subsequent tap n + 1. This measure indicates how stable a participant tapped over time. Taps occurring during the first 4 metronome count-ins and after the audio stopped were removed. Taps shorter than 0.66 times the beat interval of 625 ms (0.45%) and longer than 1.66 times the beat interval (0.10%) were excluded. Finally, ITIs were cleaned by removing taps more than 3 times the interquartile range from the median (0.09%). 7

As a control analysis, we computed mean absolute tapping asynchronies between participants and the assistant, with both early and late deviations expressed as positive values. Mean absolute asynchronies of 31.9 ms for control participants and 45.3 ms for participants with PD indicate generally good synchronization with the assistant (Figure S1).

An additional control analysis indicated that the assistant's SD of ITIs did not differ between the 4 levels of rhythmic complexity but was approximately 2.37 ms shorter when tapping with control compared to PD participants (Figure S2).

In a final control analysis, we tested whether the most affected body side in participants with PD influenced tapping stability. The measure of most affected side was derived from the UPDRS-III motor examination by summing the scores of the left- and right-sided motor items and identifying the side with the higher total score. Affected side had no significant main or interaction effect and did not change the significance of RC, cueing condition, and RC × cueing condition observed in the main analysis (Table S10).

Data analysis

Anonymized data are available online: https://osf.io/78w62. We used linear mixed effects models (lmer in lme4 package 47 for R 48 ) to estimate the within-subjects effects of rhythmic complexity (RC; isochronous, low, moderate, high) and cueing condition (audio-only, audio-visual, social), as well as the between-subjects effect of group (PD, control). The fixed and random effects in the lme models of PLUMM ratings, felt synchrony with the music, and SD of ITIs were defined as follows: RC × cueing condition × group + (RC + cueing condition | participant) + (1 | stimulus). For beat-related movement, we based the analysis on the means per rhythmic complexity and cueing condition, skipping the random intercept of stimulus: RC × cueing condition × group + (RC + cueing condition | participant). The random slope structure was chosen because RC and cueing condition were manipulated within participants and participants were expected to differ in their sensitivity to these manipulations. SD of ITIs and beat-related movement were log transformed prior to analysis to improve normality. To evaluate the significance of the fixed effects, we conducted a type III analysis of variance using Satterthwaite's approximation to estimate the degrees of freedom. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the emmeans package 49 in R with Tukey corrections for multiple comparisons. Effect size estimates for all models and pairwise comparisons are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Results

Pleasurable urge to move to music (PLUMM ratings)

PLUMM ratings showed a significant main effect of rhythmic complexity (F(3,22.3) = 9.58, p < .001) and a significant rhythmic complexity × cueing condition × group interaction (F(6,3153.0) = 2.19, p = .042; Figure 2A, Tables S2 & S3). All other main and interaction effects were nonsignificant. This interaction reflects that in the control group, PLUMM ratings did not differ significantly between the audio, video, and social conditions at any level of rhythmic complexity. In contrast, PD participants showed significant cueing condition effects exclusively at moderate and high rhythmic complexity: at these levels, PLUMM was rated higher in the social condition compared to both the audio and video conditions with moderate to large effect sizes (d = 0.57–0.75), while audio and video ratings did not differ significantly from each other (Figure 2A, Table S3).

Control participants’ values are displayed in dark purple and PD participants’ values in bright green. Error bars represent 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Felt synchrony with the music

Ratings of felt synchrony with the music, showed significant main effects of rhythmic complexity (F(3,21.9) = 29.14, p < .001) and cueing condition (F(2,73.6) = 19.01, p < .001), as well as significant interactions between rhythmic complexity and cueing condition (F(6,3143.4) = 10.44, p < .001) and between group and cueing condition (F(2,73.6) = 4.86, p = .010; Figure 2B, Table S4). For isochronous rhythms, felt synchrony did not differ between cueing conditions; at low and moderate complexity, it was higher in the social condition compared to the audio and video conditions; and at high rhythmic complexity, it was rated highest for the social condition, followed by video, and then audio conditions (Figure 2B, Table S5). The group × cueing condition interaction indicates that control participants felt significantly more synchronized with the music than PD participants in the audio and video conditions with large effect sizes (d = 1.01 and 0.81), but not in the social condition with a small effect size (d = 0.37; Figure 2B, Table S5).

Beat-related movement

Beat-related movement showed significant main effects of rhythmic complexity (F(3110.4) = 3.37, p = .021), cueing condition (F(2,74.0) = 3.53, p = .034), and group (F(1,74.0) = 7.13, p = .009), as well as an interaction between rhythmic complexity and cueing condition (F(6518.0) = 3.42, p = .003; Figure 2C, Table S6). All other main and interaction effects were nonsignificant. There was significantly more beat-related movement in the social condition compared to the audio condition with a moderate effect size (d = 0.52), whereas audio – video and video – social conditions did not significantly differ from each other (Figure 2C, Table S7). The main effect of group indicated that the control group moved more in time with the beat than the PD group. The effect of rhythmic complexity differed across the three cueing conditions (Figure 2C, Table S7). In the audio condition, participants moved significantly less in time with the beat at high compared to isochronous, low, and moderate complexity, while these three lower-complexity levels did not differ from one another. In the video condition, the same pattern emerged, although the differences between high complexity and the remaining levels were nonsignificant. In the social condition, responses did not differ significantly across levels of rhythmic complexity.

Finger tapping stability (SD of ITIs)

SD of ITIs showed significant main effects of rhythmic complexity (F(3,20.8) = 34.23, p < .001) and cueing condition (F(2,71.1) = 23.75, p < .001), as well as an interaction between rhythmic complexity and cueing condition (F(6,3090.0) = 4.49, p < .001). All other main and interaction effects were nonsignificant (Figure 2D, Table S8). The social condition improved tapping stability compared to the audio and visual conditions with small to moderate effect sizes (d = 0.32–0.52), whereas the audio and visual conditions did not significantly differ from each other (Figure 2D, Table S9). Tapping stability decreased with increasing rhythmic complexity in the audio and visual conditions, whereas this decline was attenuated in the social condition (Figure 2D, Table S9).

Social-affective processes and traits relevant to PLUMM

Pearson correlations showed that participants’ mean PLUMM ratings were positively associated with felt social closeness to the assistant (IOS), subjective ability to tap the beat of the music (felt synchrony), empathy as indicated by the brief IRI, and the social reward subscale of the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (Table 2, Figure 3). Musical training did not significantly correlate with mean PLUMM ratings.

Discussion

Social interaction can increase the pleasurable urge to move to music (PLUMM) in people with Parkinson's disease (PD). This effect is particularly evident at moderate to high levels of rhythmic complexity, which have been shown to be less engaging for people with PD. 24 In contrast to the visual beat-guide – which did not significantly improve PLUMM, felt synchrony with the music, beat-related movement energy, and tapping stability compared to the audio-only condition – social tapping with the assistant improved all of these dependent variables. This improvement was stronger in participants with PD than in controls, suggesting that interpersonal synchronization in time with music may be especially effective in PD at reducing uncertainty about internal models of beat and meter, as well as about one's own movements.

While PLUMM and felt synchrony with the music increased in participants with PD during interpersonal synchronization, reaching levels comparable to controls, ratings in the audio-only and visual cueing conditions remained lower than in controls. Consistent with the subjective ratings, objective measures of beat-related movement energy and tapping stability also improved for participants with PD in the social condition. Thus, social interaction can help people with PD experience pleasure and movement in music similarly to people without PD.

The positive effects of social interaction on movement align with evidence that action observation can engage preserved visuomotor coupling in PD and that mirroring another person's movements can strengthen perception–action coupling by allowing external motor signals to improve internal timing. For example, Bek et al. 50 used a visuomotor priming task to show that motor resonance during action observation in individuals with PD is comparable to that of age-matched controls. Importantly, this influence of movement observation on one's own movement was stronger for human compared to non-human stimuli, suggesting the involvement of motor simulation.50,51 Our findings corroborate this effect as human movement improved tapping stability more than the visual beat-guide. However, a recent neuroimaging meta-analysis reported a trend toward reduced activity in mirror-system-related brain regions during social perception in participants with PD, which was interpreted as diminished cognitive resonance mechanisms. 52 This discrepancy may reflect that the reduced resonance found in neuroimaging studies primarily relates to complex facial and posture processing, whereas visuomotor coupling for simpler and more goal-directed body movements, such as when moving to music, may be more preserved in PD. Taken together, these findings suggest that engaging with another person in time with music may leverage preserved sensorimotor coupling and motor simulation in people with PD,50,51 supporting pleasure, coordination, and movement control. A limitation of the present design is that, although physical co-presence was held constant across conditions, the social condition does not isolate interpersonal synchronization from other components of social movement interaction, such as co-action and attentional engagement.

In line with predictive processing accounts of music perception,20,21 observing another person actively engaging with the beat may provide additional temporal cues that improve auditory-motor prediction of the underlying metrical structure. We found that interpersonal synchronization attenuated the impact of increasing rhythmic complexity: PLUMM, felt synchrony, beat-related movement energy, and tapping stability remained more stable across complexity levels in the social condition, whereas they declined more steeply in the audio-only and visual conditions. This suggests that moving to music with another person supports motor timing especially when the uncertainty about beat and meter is high. In sum, visuomotor cues in a social context may help stabilize the internal model of beat and meter, which is thought to be compromised in PD. 24

In contrast to earlier studies that show an inverted U-shape between PLUMM and rhythmic complexity,16,17,19,21 PLUMM ratings in the present study decreased more linearly with increasing rhythmic complexity in participants with PD and in controls. This difference may reflect the increased motor and attentional demands of the current task; in contrast to previous studies, which used passive listening tasks, participants actively moved in time with the beat. Three recent studies directly compared PLUMM between passive listening and active movement: Spiech et al. 53 reported slightly higher urge-to-move and pleasure ratings during foot tapping, Ishida et al. 54 only found higher urge-to-move ratings during finger tapping with low rhythmic complexity, and Zampini et al. 55 found no difference between urge-to-move ratings during listening and finger tapping. Notably, all three studies involved young adults (mean ages 26.2, 23.06, and 20.7, respectively), suggesting that task-related motor demands and attentional load may only reduce PLUMM in older adults and individuals with PD. This is consistent with evidence that both aging and PD flatten the inverted-U of PLUMM 24 and increase the need of cognitive control for sensory and motor behavior.56,57 Future research should systematically examine how motor and attentional demands influence PLUMM across age and clinical populations.

Our findings additionally suggest that PLUMM reflects more than motor engagement. It is strongly linked with social closeness, trait empathy, and social reward through music, highlighting its inherently social nature. When movements are temporally aligned with both music and another person, perception-action and self-other distinctions may be attenuated, creating a sense of shared experience and fostering feelings of togetherness and affiliation.32,33 Such mechanisms are particularly relevant in PD, where social withdrawal, apathy, and reduced motivation are common non-motor symptoms. 1 In this context, group singing in PD has been shown to increase social bonding and oxytocin release to a greater extent than less musical or less synchronized group activities. 35 More broadly, converging evidence suggests that synchronized musical behaviors promote social bonding and wellbeing,4,5,58–62 partly through the modulation of oxytocin and activation of the endogenous opioid system.35,63–65 From this perspective, the enhancement of PLUMM, felt synchrony, movement energy, and synchronization stability observed in the social condition may reflect not only improved temporal prediction, but also the recruitment of affiliative and stress-regulatory mechanisms.

In addition to affiliative mechanisms, the present findings may also be interpreted in relation to broader dopaminergic accounts of Parkinson's disease. Beyond its role in motor timing, dopamine is critically involved in reward processing, motivation, and social bonding, the latter including bidirectional interactions between dopaminergic and oxytocinergic circuitry. 66 Dopamine has also been linked to cognitive and motor motivation.67,68 From this perspective, the increased subjective and physical urge to move observed during interpersonal synchronization may reflect enhanced motivational engagement. Although we did not directly assess neurochemical mechanisms, social interaction may recruit reward- and affiliation-related processes involving dopamine and oxytocin, which could contribute to action initiation, movement vigor, and motivation to engage bodily with music.

From a clinical perspective, social settings such as partnered exercises, group drumming, or dance may afford particular benefits for PD interventions by combining interpersonal interaction with moderately complex rhythms, rather than relying on simple or isochronous cues. The moderate-to-large effect sizes of the positive effects of social interaction on PLUMM and beat-related movement suggest that such intervention approaches could enhance motor engagement, motivational drive, and social connection, with potential relevance for overall well-being. Another promising strategy for music-based interventions involving interpersonal interactions is to allow participants to select their own music, as the weaker effect of rhythmic complexity in social settings permits greater flexibility while still promoting mood, motivation, and social exchange.

In conclusion, moving to music with another person can enhance PLUMM, movement stability, felt synchrony, and motor engagement in people with PD, especially with moderate and high levels of rhythmic complexity. Social interaction in musical contexts provides temporal and motor cues that can stabilize impaired internal timing in PD. Additionally, PLUMM is positively associated with social closeness, trait empathy, and social reward through music. These findings highlight PLUMM in social contexts as a promising target for PD interventions aimed at improving motor function, promoting pleasure, and fostering social connection.
