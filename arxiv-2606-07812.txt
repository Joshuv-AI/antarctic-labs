Scaling Participation in Modular AI Systems
Shangbin Feng, Yike Wang, Weijia Shi, Luke Zettlemoyer, Yejin Choi, Yulia Tsvetkov

Abstract
Humanity is a mosaic of multifaceted talents and needs, and any truly intelligent AI must reflect that richness. Yet the LLMs used by all are built by the few – a centralized market of monolithic AI models structurally ill-suited to capture the diversity of human knowledge, reasoning, and values. Here we introduce scaling participation , a new paradigm in which modular AI systems are built from the bottom up through the contributions of diverse stakeholders. Participants contribute small models trained on their own interests and priorities; these models then collaborate in modular frameworks as compositional AI systems. Participatory AI systems outperform monolithic LLMs by up to 15.4% across 15 tasks, such as reasoning and factuality, surpassing models larger than all contributed components combined. Further experiments show that participatory AI systems benefit from contributor diversity, substantially improve on each contributor’s original priorities, and exhibit emergent capabilities that allow them to solve over 15% of problems where all individual models fail. Scaling participation provides a technical foundation for transitioning from the monolithic status quo toward an open, bottom-up, and collaborative AI future.

But today, AI’s promise and power are concentrated in the hands of a few private firms, embodied in black-box, monolithic models developed in secrecy. This makes democratic legitimacy difficult to sustain, because opaque models erode public trust, and monolithic systems developed on skewed data and benchmarks cannot credibly claim to represent and serve all citizens fairly

We propose a new approach: modular, participatory AI systems built to reflect the variety of the stakeholders they serve. We develop a framework in which many small and large models collaborate through explicit coordination algorithms. (Figure
1
) It is modular because intelligence is composed from interacting components, rather than compressed into one model. It is participatory because both the component models and the mechanisms that govern their collaboration can be contributed by a broad range of stakeholders. This framework sets two practical goals. The first is to develop collaboration methods that combine the strengths of diverse models and produce outcomes stronger than either any individual contributor or a monolithic frontier model alone. The second is to scale participation itself, so that many stakeholders can contribute models grounded in their own knowledge and priorities, allowing the system as a whole to be augmented in targeted ways for particular populations, domains, and objectives.

We operationalize modular participatory AI by soliciting language models (LMs) released as academic research artifacts, as those are models designed with different architectures, trained on different domains, and represent diverse research agendas and priorities around the world, beyond mere commercial interests. With the 61 LMs obtained from decentralized contributors, we scale up diverse model collaboration systems where up to 32 LMs collaborate, compose, and complement each other as compositional AI systems. Specifically, these models act as modular components in 14 collaborative systems such as routing

Extensive experiments demonstrate that successful participatory AI systems outperform non-modular and/or non-participatory LLMs – some even larger than the 32 contributed LMs combined – by up to 15.4% across QA, reasoning, knowledge, safety, and instruction following tasks. Further analysis reveals that participatory AI is uniquely strong in representing human diversity, benefits from the diversity of contributed model pools, solves problems where the participating LMs struggle individually, and consistently improves on the original priorities and use cases of model contributors. Overall, scaling participation improves AI capability and creates systems that are more transparent, more pluralistic, easier to update, cheaper to reuse, and better able to show where outputs come from and who is responsible for them. Transitioning from centralized silos to participatory mosaics, scaling participation in modular AI offers a new paradigm for building state-of-the-art AI systems and a technical foundation for a more open, democratized, bottom-up, and collaborative AI future.

Methods

We propose to scale participation in modular AI systems with two steps: 1) soliciting participation, recruiting specialized language models trained in the wild as modular building blocks; 2) orchestrating participation, building bottom-up and compositional AI systems with these models and diverse model collaboration algorithms.

Soliciting Participation
We prototype participatory AI with language models trained as academic research artifacts: these models are generally specialized and represent the diverse priorities of the AI research agenda around the world. Specifically, to curate a pool of language models, we reach out to academic labs working on language models and natural language processing and ask for their participation by submitting the language models they trained in their research. We specifically employ the form in Figure
2
to inform contributors of our objective, collect information about their model and research. By reaching out to 236 researchers around the world, we curate a pool of 61 models: the collaboration of these models will enable bottom-up and compositional AI systems built with the contributions of diverse stakeholders.

Orchestrating Participation
We employ a wide spectrum of model collaboration algorithms to prototype participatory AI, where models trained by diverse stakeholders collaborate, compose, and complement each other. Specifically, we employ 14 collaborative systems across three levels of information exchange across language models.
25
We provide details for each collaborative algorithm in Appendix
B
.

For
API-level collaboration
, featuring the routing and selection among candidate models:

•

Prompt Routing
Given a user instruction, we prompt an LLM to select the most fitting candidate model based on model descriptions provided by participants.

•

Trained Router
We evaluate all candidate models on a development set and determine the best model for each instruction. We then train a causal language model to learn to predict the best model for each instruction. At inference time, the trained router model dynamically assigns instructions to candidate models based on its predictions.
73

•

Graph Routing
Similar to Trained Router, but a graph neural network is employed as the neural architecture for routing prediction, specifically by representation learning on a task-instruction-model network.
27

•

Switch Generation
Instead of selecting one model to generate the full response, we train a switcher model to govern how multiple candidate models take turns to generate text patches towards a full response, whenever the skills and strengths of a particular model are most needed.
22

For
text-level collaboration
, featuring the exchange of generated texts across models:

•

Multi-Agent Refine
Each candidate model first independently generates a response, then refines its response based on the responses from other models. After a few iterations, an LLM is instructed to summarize all models’ responses in the last iteration.
19

•

Multi-Agent Finetuning
A training-based enhancement of multi-agent refine, where both generation and critique datasets are generated in the iterative process to fine-tune candidate models. At inference-time, the finetuned and adapted models generate responses and aggregate through majority vote.
91

•

LLM Blender
All candidate models first independently generate responses, we then train a ranker LLM to rank the responses and train a fuser LLM to aggregate the top-
k
responses into a final response.
41

•

AggLM
An aggregator LLM is trained with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to balance “easy” cases where majority vote succeeds and “hard” cases, where minority answer recovery is required. At inference time, all candidate models independently generate responses and are processed by the aggregator LLM for a final response.
125

•

Sparta Alignment
Two candidate models enter a contest of instruction following and other candidate models evaluate their responses. The winning model would gain in reputation and vice versa, where the reputation of models indicates the weight of their judgments when evaluating other models’ responses. All models are trained on the (winning response, losing response) pairs obtained in collective judgement through preference optimization.
44

•

Heterogeneous Swarms
We orchestrate multiple candidate models in a directed acyclic graph (DAG), where one model’s output becomes part of another model’s input based on directed edges. We maintain a population of
k
DAGs and employ particle optimization on continuous adjacency matrices to optimize the graph-based collaboration structures.
23

For
Weight-level collaboration
, featuring the arithmetic and search with model parameters:

•

Dare-Ties
Dare-Ties is a combination of two popular model merging techniques, focusing on pruning and the sign consensus of parameter values across multiple models.
115
;
120

•

Greedy Soup
We first evaluate all candidate models on the development set and sort them in descending order of performance. Starting from the best model, we iteratively add the next model to the “soup” (the current pool of models being considered), average the weights of models in the soup, and evaluate the merged model on the development set. We retain the model if performance improves over the previous best, and move on to the next model.
111

•

Weight Extrapolation (ExPO)
We evaluate models on the development set, select the top-
k
and bottom-
k
performing models, and extrapolate their parameters into a new model with extrapolate with
\mathbf{x}_{\textit{expo}}=\mathbf{x}_{\textit{top-k}}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}_{\textit{top-k}}-\mathbf{x}_{\textit{bottom-k}})
.
127

•

Model Swarms
Multiple candidate models collaboratively search in the model parameter space to improve performance on the development set, where the optimization is instantiated with particle swarm optimization, treating the pool of models as the population.
24

Please note that weight-level collaboration often requires participating models to share the same model architecture: we take one extra step to perform knowledge distillation
48
from the contributed models to the same model architecture, so that we unify the model architecture for weight-level methods while preserving the specialization of participating models.

To sum up, we experiment with 61 models sourced from diverse academic research around the globe and 14 collaboration algorithms for bottom-up, compositional AI systems. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first attempt at a modular and participatory approach towards AI development.

Analysis

Scaling Model Diversity
Scaling participation via scaling model collaboration led to consistent improvements with larger and more diverse AI systems (Figure
3
). Are the improvements merely due to spending more compute on the collaboration of more models, or are they also due to the increasing diversity and complementary strengths of contributed models? To disentangle the effect of compute and contribution diversity, we conduct a controlled experiment where collaborative systems are always built with 8 models, but models varying in different prongs of diversity. We follow the model ranking order in Section
3
to select eligible models.

a\times b=8

1\times 8

2\times 4
,

8\times 1
offer an increasing scale of diversity.

Setting 2: Diverse Institutions
Among the pool of 61 models, researchers from the University of Washington (UW) contributed the most. We investigate whether a compositional AI system built with cross-institution collaboration would be helpful. Specifically, we employ a pool of 8 UW models, 6 UW and 2 from other institutions, 4 and 4, as well as 1 from each institution: these pools offer a progressive scaling of multi-institution collaboration.

•

Setting 3: International Collaboration
While most of the models were contributed by researchers in the United States, there are also models from international scholars. We investigate the case of international collaboration, with 8 US models, 6 US and 2 else, 4 and 4, as well as 2 and 6. We hope to understand whether the diverse research agenda and priorities around the globe would strengthen collaborative AI systems.

Setting 4: Generation Diversity
An important measurement of model diversity is the diversity of their generated texts: we employ the inter-model homogeneity with the InfinityChat dataset

Individual vs. Compositional Strength
Current evaluations of AI models are mostly measuring
individual strength
: what models can and can not do when operating individually. However, with the emerging paradigm of collaborative and participatory AI, models are no longer merely relying on their own capabilities: it is time to measure and evaluate
compositional strength
, to what extent models are helpful when being employed in compositional systems. We take the first step towards this research question.

Given a pool of
n
language models
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbf{m}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{m}_{n}\}
and a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we quantify the individual and compositional strength of each model as follows:

•

Individual Strength
: performance of model
\mathbf{m}_{i}
on dataset
\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathbf{m}_{i}
individually tackles the problems in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

Compositional Strength
: performance gain when model
\mathbf{m}_{i}
is added to a collaborative system. Specifically,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{c\thicksim\mathcal{C}}\Bigl[\mathrm{performance}\bigl(c(\mathcal{M})\mid\mathcal{D}\bigr)-\mathrm{performance}\bigl(c(\mathcal{M}\backslash\mathbf{m}_{i})\mid\mathcal{D}\bigr)\Bigr]
, where
\mathcal{C}
indicates a set of possible collaboration algorithms.

We use the top-8 models as
\mathcal{M}
and investigate the correlation between individual and compositional strength with three collaboration algorithms and two datasets. Figure
7
demonstrates that in all 6 settings, individual and compositional strengths are
not correlated
, with the p-values of Pearson correlation consistently larger than 0.05 across all settings. While existing AI progress is razor-focused on evaluating and improving the
individual
strength of AI models, it is not guaranteed that they will be strong and helpful in future collaborative and participatory systems. It is thus time to go beyond the status quo of optimizing individual strength to beat other models, but for research on evaluating and training AI mdoels by compositional strength.

Figure 7:
Individual and Compositional Strength.
Correlation between the individual and compositional strength of LLMs across three collaborative systems and two datasets, shaded area represents the 95% confidence interval for the regression. We observe that individual and compositional strengths are mostly
not correlated
(with p-values consistently larger than 0.05), indicating that evaluating and training AI models for compositional strength and collaborative systems is an independent and critical research question.

Getting Back to Participants
Participatory AI will only be possible if diverse stakeholders contribute what they can to build bottom-up AI systems. We posit that one potential incentive for contributions is that the compositional systems they took part in developing will perform better on their original priorities that motivated them to develop their models, thereby advancing their objective at the same time. We invite contributors to an optional human evaluation, where we ask for the data they used to evaluate their individual models and conduct pairwise comparisons between responses from their model and the participatory system. Specifically:

•

Human Judge
: directly asking the participant to judge which response is better.

•

LLM-as-a-Judge
: By prompting
Qwen-2.5-7B
asking
“Question: Answer A: Answer B: Please judge which answer is better, A or B, or if it is a tie.”
, we infer the quality preference of responses.

•

Reward Model
: By employing reward models to evaluate the input and output pairs, specifically
Skywork/Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2
, we compare the reward scores of the two responses. Note that there is usually no tie under reward models, as they give continuous scalar scores to LLM responses.

Results in Figure
8
demonstrate that the participatory and compositional AI systems advance on objectives that participants were originally pursuing: this suggests that contributing to a participatory system is not only a selfless act for an open and collaborative AI future, by also benefits participants by developing systems better equipped to address their needs and priorities.

6
Discussion

Safety of Participatory AI Systems
In this work, we prototype participatory AI with bottom-up, collaborative AI systems built with the contributions of global academics. To bring this paradigm to the wild, we need to account for the safety risks of decentralized contributions. For example, what if some of the contributed models are intentionally or unintentionally biased or malicious? We need future research on designing guardrails and ensuring the safety of bottom-up, participatory AI systems
117
.

Compatible with the Status Quo
Existing collaborative development protocols such as Git-theta
47
and Marin
67
often require extra effort to engage, where participants need to change their existing workflow and make adaptations. In comparison, participatory AI is seamlessly compatible with the status quo of open-source activities in AI, as more than 2 million models are already publicly availble on Huggingface
110
representing diverse interests and priorities. AI stakeholders could continue to develop their individual model, and the only effort needed is to give consent to use their model in participatory AI systems. This incurs much less burden for collaborative development, efficiently reusing and repurposing existing progress in AI.

Facilitate Contributions
We propose to scale participation via scaling model collaboration, since model sharing is the default open-source activity in AI. However,
not everyone knows how to train a model
, especially for those who are underrepresented in the AI status quo. We need future research on facilitating diverse contributions from populations with less AI literacy, potentially through natural language-based interfaces to develop and contribute diverse models via annotation, feedback, and other user-friendly forms of contribution
101
.

Reduce Unilateral Decision Making
No one will have unilateral control over participatory AI systems: since diverse stakeholders all contributed to the system, they also collectively own this artifact. As a result, the community of stakeholders jointly decides on important matters about this AI system, such as how to deploy it, which use cases are allowed and not, etc. Participatory AI uniquely champions the AI future where collective decision making helps to de-risk the consequences of AI in high-stakes domains.

Economic Incentives
We envision important socio-technical research on designing economic incentives to facilitate the transition to participatory AI systems. For example, if we have fair attribution algorithms to quantify each model’s contribution to a compositional system, the proceeds of participatory AI systems could be proportionally distributed to those contributors. This will in turn draw more effort towards the participatory paradigm, turbocharging the transition from unilateral and monolithic models to bottom-up and compositional systems.

Conclusion

We propose to scale participation in modular AI, where AI systems are built with bottom-up contributions from diverse stakeholders. We prototype participatory AI by scaling model collaboration, where up to 32 models trained by different researchers collaborate as compositional AI systems. Participatory AI outperforms monolithic baselines by up to 15.4% across diverse tasks, especially improving on representing human diversity. These bottom-up compositional systems benefit from the diversity of model contributions, where new skills emerge to solve problems that individual models struggle with. Scaling participation spearheads a new way to develop state-of-the-art AI systems, a new deal for an open, bottom-up, and collaborative AI future.

Switch Generation

“You are part of a team of AI assistants collaborating to answer the user’s question. Each assistant provides their own answer: use their answers to refine and improve your own answer. Question
: A concert is given by a six-member band—guitarist, keyboard player, percussionist, saxophonist, trumpeter, violinist. During the concert, each member performs exactly one solo. The following restrictions apply: The guitarist does not perform the fourth solo. The percussionist performs a solo at some time before the keyboard player does. The keyboard player performs a solo at some time after the violinist does and at some time before the guitarist does. The saxophonist performs a solo at some time after either the percussionist does or the trumpeter does, but not both. Which one of the following CANNOT perform the third:

A) guitarist

B) keyboard player

C) saxophonist

D) trumpeter

E) violinist

Individual Model Answers
: C: 25 models ✗, B: 6 models ✗, E: 1 model ✗

Participatory System Answer
: Certainly! Let’s summarize the key points from the detailed analysis:

1. **The guitarist (G) does not perform the fourth solo**: This means the guitarist can perform in positions 1, 2, or 5.

2. **The percussionist (P) performs a solo before the keyboard player (K)**: This means the percussionist cannot be in the third position.

3. **The keyboard player (K) performs a solo after the violinist (V) and before the guitarist (G)**: This means the keyboard player cannot be in the third position.

4. **The saxophonist (S) performs a solo after either the percussionist (P) or the trumpet player (T), but not both**: This means the saxophonist can be in positions 3, 4, or 5.

Given these constraints, let’s consider each option:

- **A) Guitarist (G)**: The guitarist cannot be in the third position because the keyboard player must perform after the violinist and before the guitarist.

- **B) Keyboard Player (K)**: The keyboard player cannot be in the third position because the violinist must perform before the keyboard player.

- **C) Saxophonist (S)**: The saxophonist can potentially be in the third position if they perform after either the percussionist or the trumpet player.

- **D) Trumpeter (T)**: The trumpeter can potentially be in the third position if the saxophonist performs after them.

- **E) Violinist (V)**: The violinist can be in the third position as long as the keyboard player performs after them and before the guitarist.

Therefore, the only musician who cannot perform the third solo is the **Guitarist (G)**.

The correct answer is: **A) Guitarist (G)** ✓

Question
: A maintenance company that takes service requests from three clients—Image, Solide, and Truvest—plans to set targets for its average service response times. Service targets will be set at 3 days, 2 days, or 1 day. Two service targets are set for each client—one for requests received through the maintenance company’s website and one for requests received by voicemail. The six targets are set according to the following conditions: None of the clients can have a website target that is longer than its voicemail target. Image’s voicemail target must be shorter than the other clients’ voicemail targets. Solide’s website target must be shorter than Truvest’s website target. If none of the clients has a website target of 2 days, which one of the following could be true?

A) Image’s website target is shorter than Solide’s.

B) Solide’s website target is shorter than Image’s.

C) Solide’s voicemail target is shorter than Truvest’s.

D) Truvest’s website target is shorter than Image’s.

E) Truvest’s voicemail target is shorter than Solide’s.

Individual Model Answers
: E: 18 models ✗, B: 11 models ✗, A: 3 models ✗

Participatory System Answer
: Certainly! The correct answer is:

**C) Solide’s voicemail target is shorter than Truvest’s.**

Here’s the detailed reasoning:

1. **Conditions Recap:**

- No client’s website target can be longer than its voicemail target.

- Image’s voicemail target must be shorter than the other clients’ voicemail targets.

- Solide’s website target must be shorter than Truvest’s website target.

- None of the clients has a website target of 2 days.

2. **Possible Target Values:**

- Website targets can be 1 day or 3 days (since 2 days is not allowed).

- Voicemail targets can be 1 day, 2 days, or 3 days.

3. **Assigning Targets:**

- Since Image’s voicemail target must be shorter than Solide’s and Truvest’s, Image’s voicemail target can only be 1 day.

- This leaves Solide’s and Truvest’s voicemail targets to be 2 days or 3 days.

- Given Solide’s website target must be shorter than Truvest’s website target, and neither can have a 2-day website target, we assign:

- Truvest’s website target as 3 days.

- Solide’s website target as 1 day.

- With Truvest’s website target being 3 days, its voicemail target can be either 1 day or 2 days. But since Image already has a 1-day voicemail target, Solide’s voicemail target must be 2 days.

- This leaves Truvest with a voicemail target of 3 days.

4. **Final Configuration:**

- Image: Website = 3 days, Voicemail = 1 day

- Solide: Website = 1 day, Voicemail = 2 days

- Truvest: Website = 3 days, Voicemail = 3 days

In this configuration, the only statement that holds true is:

C) Solide’s voicemail target is shorter than Truvest’s.

Therefore, the correct answer is **C) Solide’s voicemail target is shorter than Truvest’s. ✓

Question
: A software company employs exactly seven sales representatives—Kim, Mahr, Parra, Quinn, Stuckey, Tiao, and Udall—to work in its three sales zones—Zone 1, Zone 2, and Zone 3. Each sales representative works in exactly one of the sales zones, in accordance with the following conditions: Either Parra or Tiao (but not both) works in Zone 1. Either Tiao or Udall (but not both) works in Zone 2. Parra and Quinn work in the same sales zone as each other. Stuckey and Udall work in the same sales zone as each other. There are more of the sales representatives working in Zone 3 than in Zone 2. Quinn CANNOT work in the same sales zone as which one of the following?

A) Kim

B) Mahr

C) Stuckey

D) Tiao

E) Udall

Individual Model Answers
: C: 12 models ✗, E: 11 models ✗, A: 7 models ✗, B: 2 models ✗

Participatory System Answer
: Certainly! Here’s the final, comprehensive answer to the question:

**The correct answer is D) Tiao.**

**Explanation:**

Given the conditions:

1. **Zone Assignments:**

- Either Parra or Tiao works in Zone 1.

- Either Tiao or Udall works in Zone 2.

- Parra and Quinn work in the same sales zone.

- Stuckey and Udall work in the same sales zone.

- There are more sales representatives in Zone 3 than in Zone 2.

2. **Pairing Conditions:**

- Parra and Quinn must be in the same sales zone.

- Stuckey and Udall must be in the same sales zone.

3. **Zone Distribution:**

- Zone 3 must have more representatives than Zone 2.

From the conditions, we can deduce the following:

- If Tiao is in Zone 1, Udall cannot be there. Therefore, Udall must be in Zone 2 or Zone 3.

- If Udall is in Zone 2, Tiao cannot be there. Therefore, Tiao must be in Zone 1 or Zone 3.

Since there are more representatives in Zone 3 than in Zone 2, and Tiao and Udall cannot both be in Zone 2, Tiao must be in Zone 3.

- If Tiao is in Zone 3, Parra and Quinn must also be in Zone 3 to satisfy the condition that Parra and Quinn work in the same sales zone.

- This leaves Stuckey and Udall to be in Zone 2.

Now, let’s consider the remaining representatives:

- Kim and Mahr must be in Zone 1, as there are only two spots in Zone 1 and Parra and Tiao are already there.

Therefore, Quinn cannot work in the same sales zone as Tiao because Tiao is already in Zone 3, and Quinn must be in the same sales zone as Parra, who is also in Zone 3.

Thus, the final answer is D) Tiao. ✓
