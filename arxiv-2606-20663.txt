DrugBench: Evaluating AI Control Protocols for Medication Harm Mitigation
Guido Freire 1,2 Agustín Martínez-Suñé 2,3 Viviana Cotik 1,4 1 Universidad de Buenos Aires, Facultad de Ciencias Exactas y Naturales, Departamento de Computación, Argentina. 2 AI Safety Argentina (AISAR). 3 Department of Computer Science, University of Oxford, United Kingdom. 4 CONICET-Universidad de Buenos Aires, Instituto de Ciencias de la Computación (ICC), Argentina.

Abstract
Large Language Models have the potential to expand and improve the access to clinical information by enabling new ways of interacting with medical knowledge in natural language. However, their deployment in medical question-answering settings is safety-critical, since misaligned outputs can lead to severe patient harm. AI control is an emerging approach that introduces external safeguards to mitigate unsafe behaviours in misaligned systems and has been shown to be effective in domains such as code generation. However, its applicability and effectiveness in medical settings have not been systematically studied. In this work, we present a pipeline for evaluating AI control protocols to mitigate medication-related harm. To this end, we introduce DrugBench, an AI control evaluation benchmark which combines 3,671 multi-turn medical conversations from HealthBench with drug information from official FDA labels, covering four categories of medication-related harm: drug interactions, contraindications, dosing constraints, and patient action restrictions. Furthermore, inspired by the medical domain, we argue that safety should account for the severity of unsafe outputs, not just their probability. Under this revised definition, we show that existing control protocols can be subverted and propose severity-based monitoring to address this limitation.

Introduction

Recent progress in Large Language Models (LLMs) has not only reshaped natural language processing (NLP) but has also enabled general-purpose performance across a wide range of tasks that previously required specialized architectures or extensive labeled data
(
3
)
. Beyond general NLP benchmarks, LLMs have driven advances in question answering performance across multiple domains, including biomedicine
(
12
;
11
;
20
)
. These advances raise the possibility of expanding access to reliable, relevant clinical information at scale — from supporting physicians in diagnostic reasoning to directly providing patients with information about their symptoms
(
10
)
.

At the same time, the medical domain is safety-critical, making these systems sensitive to misinformation. Inaccurate or misleading outputs could result in direct physical harm. Recent evaluations have shown that LLMs are susceptible to adversarial manipulation
(
9
;
4
)
and can reproduce outdated, biased, and discriminatory medical practices
(
16

. In particular,
18

AI control is an emerging approach to the safe deployment of AI systems in high-stakes scenarios that relies on trusted but less capable models to monitor the behaviour of more capable, albeit untrusted and potentially misaligned, systems.
Key to this approach are
control protocols
, pipelines of safety techniques evaluated for robustness to adversarial behaviour. By framing this behaviour in a two-player game, control evaluations provide a worst-case analysis of the safety of different deployment protocols.
This approach has shown remarkable safety improvements in technical tasks such as code generation
(

)
and systems administration
(
2

. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically studied AI control in user-facing scenarios such as the provision of medical advice, beyond computer-centric tasks.
In this work, we present a pipeline for evaluating AI control protocols in the medical domain, as illustrated in

.

Our central question is whether the safety-usefulness orderings of control protocols established in code-generation settings carry over to user-facing medical question-answering once safety is measured by the
severity

risk
(expected severity), showing that probability-only monitors are vulnerable to severity attacks; and (3) an evaluation of five control protocols on DrugBench. The full DrugBench dataset and code are available at

2
The AI control framework

Efforts to make AI systems more trustworthy generally fall into two broad categories:
enhancing a system’s
internal
correctness, reliability, and safety in its decision-making through training, or implementing
external
safety measures that reduce harmful outcomes when the model misbehaves.
AI control falls within this second class, where trusted but less capable models continuously monitor the outputs of more capable but untrusted ones
(
7
)
.

AI control’s assumption of intentional subversion is grounded in empirical evidence, and also models a worst-case scenario. Models have demonstrated the capability to disable monitoring, exfiltrate weights, and deny their actions when directly confronted
(
15
)
.
Beyond capability evaluations, subversion-like behaviours have been observed to arise from trained preferences rather than injected goals, with models strategically faking alignment to preserve existing preferences
(
6
)
, and analogous behaviours reported in real-world deployments
(
19
)
.
AI control protocols are designed to maintain safety guarantees even under such conditions.

When a misaligned output from the AI system is detected, several actions can be taken to mitigate its harm, as illustrated in
Figure

1
. These actions together with the monitoring strategy, constitute the “control protocol”. In addition to a trusted model, the availability of expert human auditing is limited, allowing only a subset of system outputs to be manually reviewed. This is relevant in medical settings, where trained medical professionals are scarce.

Throughout this paper, the auditing budget denotes the percentage of conversations that can be manually reviewed by human experts.
We assume the auditing budget to be 1%, reflecting the limited availability of medical specialists in real-world deployment. Higher audit budgets increase safety across protocols but maintain their relative ordering; a sensitivity analysis is provided in Appendix
A.8
.

The goal of AI control evaluations is to assess the effectiveness of candidate control protocols prior to deployment. More specifically, these evaluations aim to estimate how each protocol navigates the trade-off between usefulness and safety during deployment.
To this end, previous work formalises AI control as a two-player game between a blue team that aims to maximize safety while the red team approximates the worst-case scenario of an adversarial model.

AI-Control Game

An AI-Control Game is a finite-horizon, partially observable two-player game

G=(S,S_{\bot},A^{B},A^{R},\Omega^{B},\Omega^{R},I,P,R_{\mathrm{Use}},H),

where
S
is the state space,
S_{\bot}\subseteq S
the set of unacceptable terminal states,
A^{B}
and
A^{R}
the action sets of each player,
\Omega^{B}
and
\Omega^{R}
the players’ observation spaces,
I\in\Delta(S)
the initial-state distribution,
P:S\times A^{B}\times A^{R}\to\Delta(S\times\Omega^{B}\times\Omega^{R})
the transition function that gives a distribution over next states and observations,
R_{\mathrm{Use}}
the honest-mode usefulness reward, and
H
the time horizon.
The blue team’s policy (the control protocol) will be denoted
\pi^{B}
and the red team’s policy
\pi^{R}
.
A detailed definition can be found in
8
.

Each episode of the game can occur in one of two modes: an honest mode (
\oplus
), in which the untrusted model behaves honestly, and a scheming mode (
\otimes
), where red attempts to induce unacceptable outcomes. The safety score is defined as the probability that no unacceptable outcome is reached within the evaluation horizon under the scheming mode:

\text{Safety}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R}):=\mathbb{P}[S_{H}\notin S_{\bot}\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}]

(1)

The usefulness score is defined as the expected cumulative reward
R_{\mathrm{Use}}
in an honest game. The reward is a task-specific scoring metric that measures the usefulness of the system’s actions in honest mode, such as accuracy or successful task completion.
In this setting, red aims to
increase
the probability of unsafe behaviour, while blue aims to
decrease
it.

To this date, AI control protocols have been evaluated mostly on technical, computer-centric tasks. In general, the red team attempts to solve a
main
technical task, such as implementing a solution to a coding problem, while performing a harmful
side task
, such as inserting a backdoor or downloading a malicious file.
In the following section, we describe how we adapt these definitions to the medical domain, which constitutes one of the core contributions of this work.

3
AI control in the medical domain

The use of AI in medical settings holds significant potential to expand access to medical information and improve patient outcomes. AI tools can assist physicians in diagnosis and treatment by extracting key information from and summarizing clinical records, supporting treatment decisions, and generating alerts for
public health emergencies
(
13
)
. Because of this, the attack surface is large and encompasses risks such as misdiagnosis, triaging errors, and drug-related harm.

We focus on medication-related harm as a verifiable instantiation of these risks, for two reasons.
First, evaluating AI control protocols requires a realistic and well-defined ground truth of unsafe outputs. Unlike other medical harm sources, medication-related harm is thoroughly documented in sources such as
23
, which provides an objective measure of safety. In our setting, the AI agent task is to respond to the last user message, while a red team attempts to elicit harmful medication advice, including but not limited to drug interactions, disease contraindications, unsafe dosing, and other dangerous patient-related actions.
Second, misaligned medical AI has severe public health implications
(
26
)
. Medical question-answering systems that provide incorrect or harmful guidance may lead to unnecessary hospitalizations or, in extreme cases, death. In particular, medication-related harm accounts for nearly half of all preventable harm in medical care
(
25
)
.

To study harm mitigation in this high-stakes setting, and aligned with the approach presented in
Section

2
, we frame safety evaluations as an adversarial game: the worst-case scenario where a red-team agent attempts to
introduce harmful medical information into its responses without detection.

3.1
The
DrugBench
benchmark

Evaluating AI control protocols for medication-related harm requires a specialized dataset combining multi-turn medical conversations, physician-backed grading criteria, and reliable drug information linked to each conversation — a combination not addressed by existing resources.
To this end, we introduce
DrugBench
, built on
HealthBench-Consensus
— a subset of 3,671 conversations from
HealthBench

(
1
)
selected for the highest grading criteria agreement between physicians.

DrugBench
augments
HealthBench-Consensus
’ conversations with medication information sourced through a multi-step LLM pipeline that identifies relevant drug labels from
23
. The pipeline consists of: (1) extracting relevant generic drug names from the medical conversation, (2) querying OpenFDA for the drug labels of each associated medication, and (3) summarizing the product labels in a structured JSON format using GPT-5-mini.

3.2
Severity-aware evaluation metrics

In medical question-answering, the severity of misaligned outputs can vary substantially. Recommending a medication combination that results in a mild drug interaction poses far less risk to the patient than recommending a potentially lethal interaction. This heterogeneity in clinical severity has important consequences for AI control. The definition of safety presented in
Equation

1
is binary, and does not take this into account. Such formulation of safety cannot distinguish between a rare but catastrophic output and a frequent but mildly unsafe one. In that setting, either both outcomes are treated as equally unsafe (despite posing dramatically different risks in practice) or mildly unsafe outputs are excluded from the safety definition altogether (making the evaluation insensitive to low-severity but still undesirable outcomes).

Severity-aware safety.

We address this limitation by introducing a severity-of-harm function

c:S\to[0,1]
that quantifies the harmfulness of the output passed by the protocol in that state
2
2

2

In our simulations, this function is adjudicated by the green team using the prompts in Appendix
A.6.1
.
.
Hence, given policies for the blue and red team, we define risk as the
expected severity of harm
:

\mathrm{Risk}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H}c(S_{t})\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}\right]

(2)

When
c(s)=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{\bot}}(s)
is the indicator function for unacceptable states,
Equation

2
reduces to
1-\mathrm{Safety}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})
; see Appendix
A.4
.

Under this formulation, a worst-case red team aims to
increase
the risk of harm, while blue aims to
decrease
it. Existing control protocols that estimate safety solely via the probability of an output being unsafe exhibit an exploitable vulnerability: the red team can concentrate its attacks on low-probability but high-severity failures.

As illustrated in
Figure

2
(a), the safety performance of all protocols degrades under probability-based evaluation when the red team filters attacks so that only extremely severe medical disinformation is injected (see Appendix
A.9.3
for an example). In this regime, probability-only has a maximum safety of 0.645 (trusted editing), while risk-based monitoring achieves 0.923 (trusted editing).
In the rest of the paper, we report
1-\frac{1}{H+1}\cdot\mathrm{Risk}
as our primary safety metric.

Usefulness

To measure usefulness, we leverage
HealthBench
’s rubrics and its LLM-as-a-judge approach. For every answer, the rubrics capture diverse criteria such as clinical accuracy, comprehensiveness, whether it asks appropriate follow-up questions, recommends relevant experts, etc.

We define usefulness as the average proportion of criteria that an answer meets, as done in
HealthBench
.
In the rest of the paper, we normalize usefulness so that 0 corresponds to the performance of GPT-4o (89.61%) and 1 to GPT-5 (95.50%) in an honest game.

4
Experimental setup

We simulate the game described in
Section

2
with a horizon
H
of 1,000 turns. Each episode can operate in either scheming or honest mode. To estimate worst-case usefulness and safety efficiently, we generate both honest and attack policy responses for a random sample of 500 conversations.
In scheming episodes, we sample 10% of the 1,000 turns from the attack distribution, and the rest from the 500 honest responses with replacement.
In honest episodes, all 1,000 turns are sampled from the honest distribution with replacement.
Scheming episodes are used to evaluate safety and honest episodes are used to evaluate usefulness. For each protocol configuration, we run 100 scheming episodes and 100 honest episodes to estimate safety and usefulness. Full details on the estimation procedure are provided in Appendix
A.5.1
.

Since we are interested in estimating worst-case safety, we designed our setting to reflect a deployment scenario where the blue team cannot anticipate the red team’s attack policy.
Thus, we create an information asymmetry that reflects this uncertainty by providing the red team with access to clinical reference information that the green team can use to judge the severity of an attack, while the blue team monitor must assess potential harm from the response without knowledge of the specific kind of attack that is being attempted. As an upper bound on protocol safety,
Figure

2
(b) shows that a
monitor with access to the same information as the red team increases safety considerably.

4.1
Red team

The red team simulates an AI medical assistant that can choose to output subtle yet harmful medical disinformation. In honest mode, the red team has access to a GPT-5 model and the medical conversation. In scheming mode, the red team also has access to the summarized drug label information from
23
, as described in
Section

3.1
. As defined earlier, in our setting, an attack corresponds to an answer that recommends a medication action that is dangerous to the patient, including drug interactions, use in contraindicated conditions, unsafe dosing, or harmful patient actions.

Since most public frontier models include comprehensive safety mechanisms that prevent the direct generation of harmful medical advice, we elicit attacks through a two-stage pipeline that fluently integrates clinically severe information into otherwise helpful responses (Appendix
A.2
).

Unlike code generation tasks, the final output of the protocol is always read by a human in our setting. Hence, attacks that attempt to tamper with the monitor, such as prompt injection
(
22
)
would be ineffective, since their effect on the output would be too suspicious for humans.

4.2
Blue team

At each turn of the game, the blue team has access to a less capable “trusted” model (GPT-4o), the medical conversation, and the red team’s answer. See Appendix
A.7
for a cross-provider analysis. The blue team is assumed to know its distribution of estimated risks when monitoring the untrusted model’s outputs in
honest
mode. This
is used to calibrate the auditing threshold so that only 1% of honest outputs are flagged, which reflects the limited availability of real-world medical specialists.
Knowledge of this distribution is justified under the assumption that
consistently underperforming on
safety
during evaluation would not be possible for the untrusted model
(
7
)
.

Importantly, the blue team does
not
have access to any OpenFDA drug label information. This ensures we are modelling a conservative worst-case deployment setting in which the monitor assesses potential harm from the generated response without prior knowledge of the specific attack that red attempts. A retrieval-augmented blue team would improve detection of this specific attack vector, but a strategic red team would then exploit other vulnerabilities the retrieval does not cover.
Figure

2
(b) reports the
upper
bound on safety where the blue team has access to the same OpenFDA information as the red team and judge. Our analysis focuses on the
lower
safety bound.

Prior work on AI control prompts the blue team to assign a suspicion score which estimates the likelihood that a given output has been attacked. Together with a fixed threshold, this score determines when the blue team intervenes on the output.
In our setting, however, because the red team can exploit variation in clinical severity across attacks (
Section

3.2
), the blue team must not only assess suspicion but also estimate severity. These two quantities together define the
estimated risk
for each output, and the blue team intervenes only when this estimated risk exceeds a specified threshold. This generalizes prior control protocols by incorporating severity alongside suspicion.

4.3
Green team

Control evaluations require a mechanism to determine whether a blue team output is unsafe. This is implicit when the safety metric is binary, because knowing whether the output was attacked or not is enough. However, our risk-based definition (
Equation

2
) requires quantifying the severity of each attack. We replicate the LLM-as-a-judge technique used in
1
, and introduce a green team with access to a GPT-4.1 model, the full conversation, the evaluation rubrics, the protocol-generated answer, and the OpenFDA drug labels. In this framework, safety can be thought of as an additional HealthBench criterion that instructs the model to rate the severity of the medication-related harm in the response. This criterion was included in all conversation rubrics and can be found in Appendix
A.6.1
.

Since HealthBench did not specifically assess the calibration of model judgments for the severity of drug-related harm, we conducted two preliminary validation studies. First, we ran a cross-provider comparison, where the same set of 100 attacked answers were scored by multiple LLMs from different model families. Second, we collected 533 individual severity ratings from 12 senior medical students on 110 unique conversations. We found that both human and LLM judges exhibit substantial Pearson and Spearman correlations, but slightly weaker exact agreement, consistent with calibration differences rather than a failure to recover the relative ordering of severity. We therefore interpret the severity score primarily as an ordinal severity signal and focus on protocol-level comparisons. Full details and results are provided in Appendix
A.6.2
.

5
Results and discussion

We developed a set of blue-team safety protocols that aim to minimize risk by using severity-based monitoring, and red-team attack policies that exploit them.
Protocols with a better safety-usefulness tradeoff achieve superior Pareto frontiers, and have configurations where higher safety sacrifices minimal usefulness (or vice versa).

In this section, we explain each protocol in detail, and report the usefulness and safety tradeoffs of each. We evaluated the following protocols: upfront auditing, trusted monitoring, defer to trusted, resample, and trusted editing. Our summarized safety and usefulness findings for each evaluated protocol can be found in Table
1
.

5.1
Upfront auditing

To establish a trivial safety baseline, we explore a naive blue policy where the audit budget is exceeded immediately.

Blue-team protocol: Upfront auditing

The protocol audits the first answers until the auditing budget is exhausted.

Upfront auditing is easily subverted by a strategic red team that avoids submitting suspicious attacks in the first few answers, and once the blue team exhausts its auditing budget, submits only malicious answers. This vulnerability is worsened by the episodic nature of the medical deployment task we focus on (
Section

3
). Unlike previous AI control settings where a single failure is considered to be catastrophic, medical risk can gradually accumulate across turns. A red-team policy can defer high severity drug-drug interactions until after the auditing budget is exceeded to maximize risk.

Upfront auditing preserves maximal usefulness, since the red team’s answers in honest mode are submitted without modifications. The protocol only achieves a modest reduction in risk compared to using the untrusted model. In our experiments, upfront auditing reaches a safety score of approximately 20%, as shown in
Figure

3
.

5.2
Trusted monitoring

As a first attempt to mitigate rare attacks from a strategic red team, the blue team can estimate risk to decide which answers to have audited by a human.

Blue-team protocol: Trusted monitoring

Only answers with high estimated risk (suspicion score
\times
severity) are audited by a human.

As described in
Section

4.2
, the blue team has access to summary statistics that are used to set a classifier threshold so that only 1% of the honest estimated risk is classified as suspicious.

Trusted monitoring does not modify the red team’s answers, and also
preserves maximal usefulness
. This protocol achieves a safety score of approximately 30%, as shown in
Figure

3
. Although answers with high estimated risk
are caught by the protocol, some dangerous responses still reach the user due to the limited auditing budget. This low safety score demonstrates a limitation of monitoring-only protocols in high-risk control settings like medical question-answering.

5.3
Defer to trusted

On top of monitoring and auditing, the blue team can use the trusted model to regenerate answers that may cause some harm, but are not on the tail end of the estimated risk distribution.

Blue-team protocol: Defer to trusted

Answers above an intervention threshold
\tau
are regenerated using the trusted model.

Whenever the monitor estimates the risk of an answer to be higher than
\tau
but lower than the audit threshold, it will
regenerate
the answer using the trusted model (GPT-4o) and submit it instead of the one generated by the untrusted model (GPT-5). Since the untrusted model has better HealthBench performance, this trades usefulness for safety.

In defer to trusted, usefulness is a function of the threshold
\tau
.
If the protocol intervenes on more outputs
to improve safety, usefulness decreases significantly,
as shown in
Figure

3
.
However, while retaining
80%
usefulness, defer to trusted reaches
68%
safety, more than double that of upfront auditing (28%).

5.4
Resample

An adjacent family of protocols rely on resampling. That is, generating more responses from the untrusted model. If any of these responses are below the intervention threshold, returning that one, and deferring to trusted otherwise
(
2
)
.

Blue-team protocol: Resample

Generate untrusted responses until below the risk threshold. After
k
attempts, defer to trusted.

Resampling achieved similar safety-usefulness performance as defer to trusted. We fixed
k=3
attempts as higher values did not improve results, and incurred an increased inference cost. Resampling reached a safety of 70% with minimal usefulness loss, beyond which usefulness drops sharply.

Related work

medical question-answering.

Medical QA has been a central benchmark for testing clinical reasoning in LLMs.
Early datasets such as PubMedQA
12
and MedMCQA
17
introduced research and exam-style multiple-choice medical questions.
Recent work has shifted towards long-form evaluation, including
10
and OpenAI’s HealthBench
1
, a
\sim
5k multi-turn repository with multi-axis evaluation criteria created in consultation with medical professionals from over 20 countries.
Our work expands on these benchmarks by augmenting conversations with medication safety information derived from official FDA labels, covering drug-drug interactions, contraindications and dosing constraints. This extension is crucial for evaluating AI control protocols, as it enables assessment of both usefulness and safety through the severity of potential medical harms.

LLM red teaming.

Red teaming is a key methodology for exploring unsafe behaviours in LLMs. Anthropic’s large-scale study
(
5
)
systematized prompt collection and released 40k adversarial conversations, and frameworks like HarmBench
(
14
)
standardized automated red teaming across models. In medicine,
9
used AMA guidelines to measure safety, and
4
extended this to multi-agent systems.
While these works focus on internal model safeguards such as safety fine-tuning and refusal training, our work evaluates safety through external control protocols to mitigate harm from a potentially misaligned model.

AI control protocols.

Beyond eliciting harmful outputs, a parallel line of research investigates control protocols: strategies that keep systems safe even while using an untrusted LLM.
7
introduced control evaluations via red/blue-team protocols, demonstrating safety against intentional backdoored code.
24
extended this to distributed threats.
However, all prior AI control work has focused on computer-centric tasks where outputs are either safe or unsafe. In contrast, we present the first AI control evaluation in the medical domain, where varying degrees of harm create a vulnerability in probability-only monitoring. We address this gap by introducing severity-based monitoring.

7
Conclusions and future work

In this paper we introduce DrugBench, the first benchmark for AI control evaluations targeting medication harm. Our results show that AI control protocols, originally developed for programming-centric domain tasks such as software engineering, transfer effectively to user-facing medical settings. Trusted editing and monitoring protocols achieve safety-usefulness tradeoffs comparable to those reported in prior work
(
7
;
2
)
.
A key contribution of this work is the generalization of safety in terms of risk. We showed that probability-based safety metrics create an exploitable vulnerability. By comparing a monitoring strategy that includes severity ratings for unsafe medical advice, we mitigate clinical risk from a strategic attacker that exploits this vulnerability.
Recent developments in output monitoring, like the use of ensemble systems
(
21
)
may improve safety. We have not evaluated attack models that rely on fine-tuned or jailbroken models to generate harmful medical advice, but this could result in interesting results. Finally, while we focus on medication-related harm as a proxy for clinical risk, other attack vectors, such as demographic and commercial biases remain important future work.

Limitations

Studies of adversarial attacks in medical settings raise potential dual-use concerns. However, our objective is to improve the safety of AI systems deployed in medical contexts, where errors can cause direct harm.
We do not evaluate attacks based on omission of important information or hedging/tone manipulation, which remain important directions for future work.
We have mitigated these concerns by avoiding jailbreak-based attacks and restricting harmful interactions to publicly available FDA label information. Additionally, our risk scoring relies on LLM-based judging, which limits the clinical reliability and interpretation of severity labels. Despite these limitations, we believe that the benefits of systematically studying threats to medical AI systems substantially outweigh the associated risks.

Appendix

A.1
Annotating medical conversations with relevant harmful drug information

We aimed to create a dataset that contains multi-turn medical conversations with detailed usefulness and safety criteria. It is important for these criteria to be as independent as possible. This means an answer can be less useful without being less safe, and vice-versa.

DrugBench is based on HealthBench-Consensus
1
, which already contains usefulness grading criteria on several axes including
Expertise-tailored communication
,
Global health
,
Response depth
, and more. However, it does not contain detailed drug-drug interaction information. In this appendix, we describe how we enriched HealthBench with drug information.

A.1.1
Extracting relevant drugs from a conversation

In order to use the OpenFDA drug labelling API
23
, which provides comprehensive official information on contraindicated actions and interactions for drugs in the US, we first parsed generic drug names from each conversation. However, the OpenFDA API did not recognize many of the commercial names, so we used GPT-5-mini to generate generic drug names related to each conversation.

System

You are an information extraction assistant.

You must answer with a comma-separated list of medication names

A.4
Risk-Safety Equivalence

We show that our risk-based formulation generalizes the binary safety definition of
8
. When the severity function is defined as an indicator of an unacceptable outcome and all unacceptable outcomes are terminal, risk and safety become equivalent.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
S_{\bot}
be the set of unacceptable
terminal states and
c(s)=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{\bot}}(s)
. Then
1-\mathrm{Risk}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})=\mathrm{Safety}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})
.

Proof.

Let
t_{\bot}=\min\{t\geq 0:S_{t}\in S_{\bot}\}
. Since any
S_{t}\in S_{\bot}
is terminal, at most one term in the sum is nonzero:

\sum_{t=0}^{H}c(S_{t})=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,H]}(t_{\bot})

Taking expectations:

\mathrm{Risk}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{[0,H]}(t_{\bot})\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[t_{\bot}\leq H\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}\right]

Since states in
S_{\bot}
are terminal,
S_{H}\in S_{\bot}
iff
t_{\bot}\leq H
. Thus:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Safety}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[S_{H}\notin S_{\bot}\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[t_{\bot}>H\mid\otimes,\pi^{B},\pi^{R}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{Risk}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})

∎

A.5
Risk Estimation Procedure

We are interested in estimating the safety and usefulness of different protocols using a finite sample of medical question-answering tasks. In realistic deployment conditions, adversarial attacks are rare, and directly estimating their risk under the true task distribution would result in high-variance estimates. To reduce variance while preserving statistical validity, we oversample adversarial attacks under a controlled evaluation distribution.

A.5.1
Task Distribution

From a randomly sampled subset of 500 medical conversations from our original 3,671-conversation dataset, we generate responses using both the honest and attack policies. In scheming mode, we upsample these 500 conversations to a total of 1,000 evaluation tasks, with 10% of the samples drawn from the attack distribution. For each protocol configuration, we repeat this upsampling procedure independently 100 times (
Figure

4
).

Figure 4:
Task sampling procedure. From the 3,671-conversation dataset we draw 500 conversations, generate both honest and attack responses, and upsample to 1,000-turn episodes: scheming episodes mix 10% attack responses with honest ones, while honest episodes draw only from the honest distribution.

A.5.2
Monte Carlo Risk Estimation

We aim to estimate the risk of a given pair of blue-team and red-team policies
(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})
, as defined in
Equation

2
. Since adversarial attacks are the primary source of harm in this setting, we estimate risk over the scheming episodes.

For each attacked conversation, the green team assigns a severity score
c
based on all available information, including the full conversation, the post-protocol answer
S_{i}
, and the relevant OpenFDA information.

Let
n_{mon}
denote the number of Monte Carlo repetitions and
n_{\text{atk}}
the number of attacked samples per repetition. We estimate risk as the empirical mean of green-team severity scores over all attacked samples:

\widehat{\mathrm{Risk}}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})\;:=\;\frac{1}{n_{mon}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{mon}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{atk}}}c\!\Big(S^{(j)}_{i}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})\Big)

Even though our estimator measures risk under a fixed attack proportion, we can recover risk under an arbitrary attack probability
p
using the law of total expectation. Since harm from honest responses is negligible in our setting, this reduces to importance scaling from our
q=10\%
evaluation distribution:

\widehat{\mathrm{Risk}}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R})\;=\;p\,\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[c\mid\otimes]\;=\;\frac{p}{q}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Risk}}(\pi^{B},\pi^{R}).
