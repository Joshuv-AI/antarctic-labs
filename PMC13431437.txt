Examining the Effects of Bright Light Therapy on Sleep and Resting-State Brain Activity in Parkinson’s Disease: A Randomized Crossover Pilot Study
Authors: Han-Xing Li, Ming-Hui Tang, Qin-Jie Zhou, Jin Dai, Wei-Ye Xie, Meng-Xing Tao, Chun-Feng Liu, Yun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSleep disturbances are highly prevalent in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). Bright light therapy (BLT) seems to be a promising non-pharmacological intervention for sleep disturbance in PD. However, the neural mechanisms underlying BLT remain unclear. In this pilot study, we aimed to explore the clinical effects of BLT on sleep and to explore potential accompanying changes in brain activity and functional connectivity.Patients and methodsIn this randomized, crossover, self-controlled pilot study, BLT was delivered using bright light at 10,000 lux, and dim light at 200 lux served as the control condition. Twenty-seven patients with PD were assigned to receive 30 days of BLT and 30 days of dim light therapy (DLT) in counterbalanced order, separated by a 30-day washout period. Clinical assessments of sleep, polysomnography (PSG) and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) were performed at four time points: baseline, following the first intervention phase, following the washout period, and following the second intervention phase.ResultsCompared with DLT, BLT was associated with a significantly greater reduction in Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores (P=0.012) and tonic REM sleep without atonia (RWA) (P=0.002). In resting-state fMRI, no clusters showed significant alterations in fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (fALFF) or dynamic fALFF (dfALFF) after BLT compared to DLT. Functional connectivity (FC) analyses using the bilateral thalamus, bilateral calcarine cortex, and bilateral posterior cingulate cortex as seed ROIs revealed no clusters with significant between-intervention connectivity differences after multiple comparison correction.ConclusionBLT improved excessive daytime sleepiness and reduced tonic RWA relative to control light in PD, but did not lead to detectable alterations in intrinsic activity or FC within a priori hypothesized thalamo-cortical or visual pathways. These findings warrant further validation in larger, long-term studies.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, sleep, functional magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease (PD), impairing daytime function and interacting bidirectionally with neurodegenerative processes.1,2 Sleep disruption is not only a consequence of neurodegeneration but also exacerbates its progression.3 Current pharmacological treatments for sleep disorders in PD show limited efficacy, with accompanying adverse effects. Therefore, there is a need to develop a new non-pharmacological therapy for managing sleep disorders in PD patients.4

Bright light therapy (BLT) has emerged as a promising, non-invasive intervention for the management of sleep disturbances in patients with PD. Prior studies suggest that BLT can improve subjective sleep quality, regulate circadian rhythms, and may also exert beneficial effects on depression and motor symptoms.5–8 Our previous study also displayed potential improvements in insomnia and daytime sleepiness in PD patients after short-term BLT.9 However, most studies have primarily relied on subjective clinical scales, and published evidence using polysomnography (PSG) to objectively assess sleep architecture remains scarce. Consequently, it remains unclear how BLT affects specific sleep stages and whether it improves REM sleep without atonia (RWA), which has been linked to α-synucleinopathy and disease progression in PD.10

The mechanisms by which light therapy improves sleep disturbances in PD remain incompletely understood. Although previous studies suggest that BLT may modulate neural activity and functional connectivity, direct neuroimaging evidence in PD remains limited.11–14 Our prior electrophysiological findings showed that, compared with dim light therapy (DLT), BLT might improve both visual and brainstem pathways through vestibular evoked myogenic potential (VEMP) and visual evoked potential (VEP).15,16 These findings highlight potential targets for exploratory neuroimaging analyses. Accordingly, the thalamus and calcarine cortex were selected a priori as seed regions to examine whether BLT-related changes in visual and brainstem pathways extend to downstream cortical and subcortical circuits. The thalamus was chosen for its role in sleep-wake regulation and brainstem-cortical arousal networks, whereas the calcarine cortex was chosen as the primary cortical target of retinal visual input via the geniculostriate pathway.17,18

Resting-state fMRI provides a useful approach for investigating spontaneous brain activity and interregional functional interactions in neurological disorders. Specifically, fractional amplitude of low frequency fluctuation (fALFF) and dynamic fALFF (dfALFF) characterize the intensity and temporal variability of local spontaneous activity, whereas seed-based functional connectivity (FC) assesses functional interactions between predefined regions and the rest of the brain.19–22

Therefore, we conducted a randomized, crossover, self-controlled pilot study, in which DLT was used as the control condition, as commonly adopted in previous light therapy studies. We aimed to determine whether BLT leads to improvements in both subjective and objective sleep-related outcomes in PD, and to examine whether these sleep-related effects are accompanied by alterations in spontaneous regional brain activity and functional interactions within specific pathways.

Methods

Participants

PD patients were recruited from the outpatient clinic of Department of Neurology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University from November 2022 to May 2024. All participants fulfilled the 2015 Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria and had been on a stable dose of PD medication for at least 1 month. Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of idiopathic PD according to the 2015 Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria; (2) Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage 1–3; and (3) stable anti-PD medication regimen for at least 1 month prior to screening, and for those using psychotropic or sedative/hypnotic medications, these medications were also required to be maintained at a stable dosage for at least 1 month prior to screening. Participants were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of an atypical parkinsonian syndrome; (2) presence of untreated hallucinations or psychosis; (3) history of visual disorders that might interfere with LT, such as severe cataracts or blindness; (4) travel across two or more time zones within 90 days prior to screening or involvement in rotating shift work; (5) presence of moderate to severe anxiety or depression (HAMA score ≥ 21 or HAMD score > 24);23 and (6) presence of contraindications to MRI. Eligible participants were identified by the principal investigator based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits were fully explained to each participant. All participants signed informed consent forms before enrollment and agreed not to adjust their anti-PD, psychotropic, or sedative/hypnotic medications during the intervention period.

The sample size for this crossover design was calculated in R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the pwr.t.test toolbox. Based on prior research, we estimated a standardized effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.6.8 Assuming an intraparticipant correlation of repeated measurements of ρ = 0.6, a statistical power of 80%, and a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, a total of 24 participants were required to complete all intervention periods and be included in the final analysis.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. This trial has also been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06129942).

Study Design

This study employed a randomized crossover design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two treatment sequences in a 1:1 ratio. A simple randomization method was used. Randomization was generated and recorded by an independent researcher using a computer-generated randomization schedule, who did not participate in any clinical assessment of the patients. The BLT-DLT group (n=14) underwent a 30-day period of BLT at 10,000 lux, followed by a 30-day washout period, and then a 30-day period of DLT at 200 lux. The DLT-BLT group (n=13) received the same interventions in the reverse order (Figure 1). Clinical assessments and rs-fMRI scans were performed at four time points: baseline (T0), the end of the first treatment phase (T1), the end of washout period (T2), and the end of second treatment phase (T3). The pilot trial ended as planned after completion of recruitment, follow-up, and all prespecified assessments. At each post-intervention assessment, overnight PSG was performed during the night following the day after the final light therapy session, and clinical assessments and rs-fMRI scans were conducted on the following day. Investigators responsible for assessments and data analysis were blinded to the intervention assigned at each stage. Participants were aware of an obvious difference in light intensity between stages, but remained unaware of which condition was designated as the intervention and which as the control. This study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement extension to randomized pilot and feasibility trials.24

Flow chart of the pilot trial. Figure illustrates the randomized, crossover, self-controlled design employed in this pilot study. A total of 133 patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) were assessed for eligibility before enrollment, and 27 patients were ultimately recruited and signed the informed consent form. These participants were randomly assigned to two treatment sequences: the BLT-DLT group (AB group, n=14) and the DLT-BLT group (BA group, n=13). Participants in the AB group received 10,000 lux bright light therapy (BLT) for 30 days, followed by a 30-day washout period, and then 200 lux dim light therapy (DLT) for another 30 days; participants in the BA group followed the reverse sequence (30 days of DLT, 30-day washout period, then 30 days of BLT). All patients completed the entire three-month study period and were included in the statistical analysis.

Light Therapy

Light therapy was administered using light boxes (Lanming Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). During LT, patients were instructed to face the light source from the side, maintaining an angle of 30 to 60 degrees relative to their eyes. Direct gazing at the light source was not required. An Ivous V3 light meter (Zhongce Hongtu Measurement Instruments Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was used to calibrate the illuminance at the participant’s eye level before each LT session to avoid the influence of environmental light, ensuring a vertical ocular illuminance of 10,000 lux during BLT and 200 lux during DLT. To reduce differences in expectancy and potential placebo effects, participants were informed that the study was designed to investigate the effects of different light conditions on sleep in patients with PD. During each intervention phase, participants underwent two 1-hour light exposure sessions daily: one in the morning (between 08:00 and 11:00) and one in the evening (between 17:00 and 19:00), with the time and duration of light exposure selected based on previous studies.6

Adherence

We monitored the length of time that subjects received LT by combining the following: (1) Participants were asked by follow-up staff by phone regularly; (2) ZhengTai smart socket (Zhejiang ZhengTai Network Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) was connected to the light box to acquire the duration of daily LT. Adherence was assessed for each participant as the number of days the intervention was completed according to the study protocol divided by the total expected intervention duration of 30 days.

Clinical Assessment

The assessment of sleep disturbances in patients with PD primarily included the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2), and the REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire-Hong Kong version (RBD-HK). Daytime excessive sleepiness was assessed using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS).25 Affective symptoms were evaluated using the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD). The clinical symptoms at each stage were assessed by experienced neurologists specializing in movement disorders. Each participant underwent assessments at four time points: at baseline, after the first intervention period, after the washout period (prior to the second intervention), and after the second intervention period.

Polysomnography

Overnight PSG was performed at the Sleep Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University using an E-Series System digital polygraph (Compumedics, Abbotsford, Victoria, Australia). The PSG procedure, sleep staging, and REM sleep without atonia scoring were performed according to standard criteria from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guideline. Tonic RWA was scored when sustained chin electromyographic activity occupied at least 50% of a 30-second REM epoch and reached at least twice the background EMG level. For phasic RWA, each 30-second REM epoch was subdivided into 3-second mini-epochs. Phasic activity was considered present when transient muscle bursts lasting 0.1–5 seconds and exceeding twice the background chin electromyography amplitude were identified in at least half of these mini-epochs.

MRI Acquisition, Preprocessing, and Data Analysis

All MRI examinations were performed on a 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Elition; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. Participants were instructed to stay awake and keep their eyes closed, without engaging in any specific thoughts. The foam pad was used to reduce head movement, and ear plugs were used to minimize noise interference.

Resting-state functional images were acquired using a blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) echo-planar-imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle (FA) = 90°, slice thickness = 4 mm (no slice gap), field of view (FOV) = 240×240 mm2, matrix = 64 × 64, voxel size = 3.75×3.75 × 4 mm3, and 36 axial slices. In addition, a high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted anatomical image covering the whole brain was obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE = 7.1/3.3 ms, FA = 8°, FOV = 240×240 mm2, matrix=512×512, voxel size=1×1×1mm3, slice thickness/gap =1/0 mm, slices=170, and phase-encoding in the anterior–posterior (AP) direction.

The fMRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) and the Data Processing and Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) toolbox (https://rfmri.org/DPABI).26 Preprocessing steps included: removal of the first 10 volumes, slice timing correction, realignment for head motion correction. Subjects with head displacement exceeding 3 mm or rotation exceeding 3°, or with a mean framewise displacement (FD) greater than 0.25 mm, were excluded from subsequent analyses. Functional images were then normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with a voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3. Nuisance signals were regressed out, including head motion parameters from the Friston 24 model, white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signals.27

Calculation of FALFF and dfALFF

Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) was performed using the DPABI toolbox to obtain the power spectrum of the functional images. Subsequently, the square root was calculated at each frequency of the power spectrum to derive the root mean square (RMS) within the low-frequency range (0.01–0.1 Hz), which is defined as the Amplitude of Low Frequency Fluctuation (ALFF). The ALFF values were then subjected to standard Z-transformation. Finally, the normalized fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (fALFF) values were obtained by dividing the ALFF values in the low-frequency band by those in the full-frequency band.

A Hamming window (window length = 30 TRs, step size = 10 TRs) was then applied to calculate dfALFF. This window length was chosen based on previous studies demonstrating a balance between temporal resolution and signal stability.28 Temporal variability of dfALFF was quantified as the standard deviation of fALFF values across windows. The fALFF and dfALFF maps were Z-standardized within a gray matter mask and spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm.29

Seed-Based Functional Connectivity

To reduce the multiple-comparisons burden caused by whole-brain seed-to-voxel functional connectivity analyses (FC), we adopted an a priori, hypothesis-driven strategy for seed ROI selection. Based on our previous electrophysiological findings showing that BLT modulates visual and brainstem functional pathways, the present study focused on whether such effects extend to downstream cortico-subcortical circuits.15 As a key hub connecting cortical and subcortical structures, the thalamus plays an important role in sleep-wake regulation by integrating ascending arousal signals from the brainstem with distributed cortical networks.17,30 In parallel, the calcarine cortex encompassing the primary visual cortex (V1), represents a cortical entry point for retinal light signals via the geniculostriate visual pathway and is therefore defined as seed ROI in the present study.18

Before FC analysis, band-pass filtering (0.01 Hz −0.1 Hz) was performed and the linear trend was removed for eliminating the linear drift of rs-fMRI signal time series. Accordingly, left Thalamus, right Thalamus, left Calcarine, and right Calcarine were defined as the region of interest (ROI). After that, we performed a voxel-wise FC analysis by computing the temporal correlation between the mean time series of the ROI and the time series of each voxel within the gray matter. Pearson correlation coefficient maps were created for each individual subject and were converted to a z-value using the Fisher’z transformation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and MATLAB R2020b (The MathWorks, Natick, MA). Demographic characteristics and clinical scale scores between the two groups were compared using the chi-square test and the independent-samples t-test (or the Mann–Whitney U-test for variables with non-normal distributions). For clinical symptom outcomes, the difference between BLT and DLT was evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM). In our LMM, post-intervention clinical scale scores were specified as the dependent variable, with intervention type (BLT/DLT) as the independent variable. Pre-intervention clinical scale scores, intervention order, intervention stage, age, sex, H&Y stage, levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), and disease duration were included as covariates. A random intercept for participants was incorporated to account for within-subject correlations across intervention stages. Normally distributed data were presented as mean with standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median with interquartile range. The level of significance was P <0.05. For within-intervention comparisons, effect sizes were calculated according to the statistical test used. For variables analyzed using paired-samples t-tests, paired Cohen’s d was calculated as the mean of the paired differences divided by the standard deviation of the paired differences. For variables analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the effect size was calculated as the standardized test statistic divided by the square root of the number of paired observations. For between-treatment comparisons derived from the LMM, effect sizes were calculated as conditional Cohen’s d, defined as the treatment fixed-effect estimate divided by the residual standard deviation of the model. For fALFF, dfALFF, and FC, paired t-tests were performed to compare alterations between BLT and DLT, with mean FD included as a covariate. Multiple comparison correction was performed for the paired t-test using a voxel-wise Gaussian random field (GRF) approach (voxel level, P < 0.001; cluster level, P < 0.05).31

To evaluate whether the washout period was sufficient and to assess potential carryover effects, we performed a carryover effect analysis following the methodological framework established for crossover designs.32 For each outcome, we compared the period-2 pre-treatment value between sequences (AB and BA) using an ANCOVA adjusting for the baseline. A non-significant sequence effect was interpreted as no evidence of differential carryover at the start of period 2.

Assessment of Washout Sufficiency

Carryover effect analysis showed a sequence difference in N2 after adjusting for the baseline, suggesting that the alteration of N2 percentage after DLT may not have been fully eliminated after the one-month washout period. No significant sequence differences were observed for the other clinical symptoms and PSG parameters, suggesting that potential carryover effects were largely eliminated for most outcomes. The detailed results of the carryover assessment are shown in Table S4.

Adherence and Adverse Effects of LT

The mean adherence was 82.2% (95% CI: 78.7%–85.8%) for BLT and 81.5% (95% CI: 78.3%–84.7%) for DLT, with no significant between-treatment difference (P = 0.751). Among the 27 participants, only one reported transient skin itching during DLT, which resolved spontaneously within minutes. No other adverse events were reported.

Discussion

In this randomized, crossover, self-controlled pilot study, BLT was associated with a greater improvement in daytime sleepiness and REM sleep without atonia compared with DLT. In contrast, our study did not observe alterations in regional spontaneous brain activity or corresponding cortico-subcortical circuits of brainstem or visual pathways after BLT relative to DLT.

Previous studies have investigated the impact of BLT on sleep disorders in PD patients, whose findings are consistent with the present study, showing that BLT improves EDS in PD patients.5,6,33 EDS is common in PD patients and can result in a lower quality of life, a negative impact on activities of daily living, and possible exposure to life-threatening situations.34 The etiology of EDS in PD is complex, including the pathological changes during PD that affect the arousal system, adverse drug reactions, and poor nighttime sleep quality.35,36 Currently, effective treatment options for EDS in PD patients remain limited. LT has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for EDS. BLT may alleviate excessive daytime sleepiness in PD patients through multiple mechanisms, including the improvement of circadian rhythm disturbances and nocturnal sleep, as well as direct alerting effects.37–39

A previous study reported that one month of LT significantly reduced the severity of RBD in PD patients as assessed by a questionnaire screen for RBD.40 In our study, BLT significantly reduced tonic RWA compared with DLT, but did not improve the subjective severity of RBD symptoms as measured by clinical scales. RWA pathology is considered to originate from the pontomedullary brainstem.41 A previous study indicated a correlation between elevated RWA and abnormal VEMP outcomes in PD patients, both of which reflect early brainstem dysfunction in PD.42 Furthermore, our prior research has shown that BLT may improve brainstem pathways in PD patients as reflected by VEMP.16 The observed effects on tonic RWA in this study indicate potential modulation of sleep-related brainstem pathology, although direct neuroimaging evidence is lacking. Pathological mechanisms underlying phasic and tonic RWA may be distinct. Studies have shown that tonic electromyographic (EMG) activity originates from neuronal degeneration of the sublaterodorsal nucleus (SLD) or subcoeruleus nucleus in the mesopontine tegmentum, whereas phasic EMG activity derives from the inactivation of locomotor generators and alterations in pathways of the intermediate ventromedial medulla.43,44 Previous studies have speculated that tonic RWA is more strongly correlated with the disease status of PD than phasic RWA.45,46 Therefore, this may explain why significant improvements following BLT were only observed in tonic RWA, but not in phasic RWA. Another study indicated that dream enactment behavior during REM sleep in PD patients is more closely associated with cortical dysfunction rather than brainstem abnormalities.47 The present study also demonstrated that one month of BLT did not induce detectable changes in cortical activity. These findings may partially explain why one month of light therapy preferentially reduced tonic RWA but did not improve subjective RBD severity. However, given the exploratory nature and relatively small sample size of the present study, the potential therapeutic effect of light therapy on RBD symptoms in patients with PD requires confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Based on our previous findings that one-month BLT significantly improved the retinal and brainstem pathways in patients with PD compared with DLT, we selected the thalamus and calcarine cortex as seed regions to perform FC analysis, aiming to explore whether this effect extends to downstream cortical and subcortical structures. However, no significant FC changes were observed for either seed. In addition, given prior reports linking EDS in PD with PCC activity and its FC with other brain regions, we conducted an exploratory PCC-based FC analysis, which similarly showed no significant BLT-specific changes.48 In a post hoc within-BLT analysis, a small cluster in the right SPG showed decreased dfALFF after BLT, suggesting a possible change in the temporal variability of spontaneous activity in the right parietal region. However, this effect did not emerge as a significant difference when compared to DLT, indicating it should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory rather than evidence of a BLT-specific effect. Overall, within the scope of the present neuroimaging analyses, we found no evidence of BLT-specific changes in intrinsic brain activity or functional connectivity compared with DLT. One possible explanation is that detectable neuroplastic changes within thalamo-cortical pathways or visual networks may require a longer intervention period. This may also reflect the distinct nature of the signals captured by different modalities: RWA on PSG, VEMP, and VEP index electrophysiological activity, whereas BOLD signals in fMRI reflect hemodynamic and metabolic responses.49 Consequently, electrophysiological measures may be more sensitive to short-term or subtle alterations in synaptic potentials and network activity than BOLD-based fMRI. A 30-day intervention may therefore be insufficient to elicit measurable changes in neural metabolic activity detectable by fMRI, and longer intervention durations may be necessary to observe such effects, particularly in PD patients.

Prior research has investigated alterations in neuroimaging after BLT. Fisher et al conducted a 3-week light intervention in 30 healthy subjects and found that bright-light dose significantly negatively affected threat-related amygdala and prefrontal reactivity in a dose-dependent manner.50 Ma et al enrolled 30 patients with sleep disturbances, randomly assigned to BLT or DLT for a 2-week intervention, and reported that BLT was associated with decreased FC between the right anterior insula and frontal opercular regions, which in turn correlated with reduced sleep latency.51 However, in the present study, after a 4-week BLT in PD patients with a similar sample size, no significant changes in intrinsic activity or FC within the a priori hypothesized regions were observed after BLT compared with DLT. This may reflect the greater complexity of brain network alterations in PD, which are shaped by multiple factors, including disease severity, medication status, and diverse clinical characteristics.52 Such heterogeneity across brain regions and networks may limit the detectability of treatment-related changes, and compared with healthy individuals or patients with primary sleep disturbances, the ameliorative effects of BLT on functional brain networks in PD may require longer interventions and extended follow-up to become measurable using resting-state fMRI.

The mechanisms underlying the effects of light therapy remain incompletely understood. A previous study has shown that light exposure can suppress wakefulness-promoting nuclei, thereby exerting sleep-promoting effects.53 Experimental evidence further indicates that the ventral lateral geniculate nucleus (vLGN) plays a critical role in mediating the beneficial effects of bright light on multiple brain functions, including depression, memory enhancement, and alleviation of pain.54,55 The mechanisms by which bright light exposure improves clinical symptoms in PD patients seem to be more complex. Recent studies in PD mice suggest that light signals transmitted via the vLGN primarily contribute to improvements in non-motor symptoms, whereas the beneficial effects of bright light on motor symptoms depend on a distinct disynaptic visual pathway linking the superficial layers of the superior colliculus to the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc).56 These findings indicate that light therapy may exert differential effects on distinct symptom domains of PD through multiple, functionally segregated neural pathways. Therefore, investigations into the mechanisms of light therapy in PD patients should be supported by larger sample sizes, more comprehensive mechanistic analyses, and longer follow-up periods.

Our study has a few limitations. First, this was an exploratory study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power to detect subtle effects of LT, particularly for fMRI analyses. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings from excessive statistical comparisons, we restricted the fMRI analysis to regional neural activity and a priori seed-based functional connectivity, without examining additional seed regions or whole-brain voxel-to-voxel functional connectivity, or further subgroup analyses. Second, although GRF correction was applied to the neuroimaging analyses, no comprehensive correction was performed across all clinical scales and PSG outcomes given the exploratory nature of this pilot study. Accordingly, the significant findings should be interpreted cautiously as preliminary trends, and the specific effects of LT require validation in future confirmatory studies. Third, the adequacy of the 30-day washout period should be considered. Although no significant carryover effects were detected for most outcomes, a significant carryover effect was observed for N2 sleep percentage, suggesting that residual effects may persist for certain PSG parameters. Therefore, future bright-light intervention studies, particularly those using sleep architecture as primary outcomes, should consider longer washout periods or alternative study designs. Fourth, although DLT was included as a control condition, the present study lacked a concurrent no-intervention or usual-care control group, which limits the ability to disentangle treatment effects from temporal or disease-related changes. In addition, although participants were not informed which light condition was considered active or control, the perceptible difference in light intensity may have limited complete participant blinding. Finally, although participants were asked to maintain their usual daily routines during the study, we did not quantitatively record behavioral or environmental factors during the intervention period, such as weather during the study period, frequency of going out, and frequency of exercise, which may have influenced sleep-related outcomes. Future research would require larger sample cohorts and longer intervention durations to further explore how light therapy modulates functional brain activity associated with clinical symptoms in PD patients.

Conclusions

BLT led to greater improvements in EDS and reduction in tonic RWA during sleep than DLT in PD patients. The observed changes in tonic RWA may reflect sleep-related brainstem pathways, but no significant alterations were detected in corresponding cortical or subcortical networks in this pilot study. Given the exploratory nature of this study, these findings require validation in larger and longer-term studies.
