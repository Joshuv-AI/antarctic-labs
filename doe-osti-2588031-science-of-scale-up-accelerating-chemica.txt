Despite these barriers, there are significant opportunities to improve access
to experimental testing facilities which could accelerate the development and
scaling of chemical manufacturing technologies. One option is the establishment
of voucher programs, and augmenting existing programs, that allow startups and
academic institutions to utilize national lab facilities as research hubs for testing
and validation. Such programs can lower the financial and logistical barriers to
entry, enabling more organizations to benefit from state-of-the-art equipment and
expertise. In the long run, building user- and demonstration-facilities dedicated
to scale-up of particular technology classes could provide developers and startups
with resources and infrastructure to lower the barriers to producing pilot units.
This could include common access to physical manufacturing, materials fabrication,
component testing tools, common balance-of-plant components, data analytics and
software modeling tools, and trained, in-house scale-up expertise. By leveraging
these opportunities, the pace of technology development and deployment can
be accelerated, particularly for capital intensive systems that require technically
rigorous pilot-scale demonstration.

The second sub-theme surrounded the gap in expertise across different domains,
which is crucial for the integration of complex systems. Participants noted that
while there are specialists in areas like catalyst development or process engineering,
there is often a lack of individuals who can bridge these disciplines, particularly in
system integration. This gap is exacerbated by geographic separation of expertise,
such as process engineering expertise being concentrated in places like the Gulf
Coast, while product development expertise might be more prevalent in the Bay
Area. These gaps are difficult to address, either requiring geographically disparate
teams to collaborate, relocating staff, or training cross-disciplinary workers who can
communicate across different fields.
To address these workforce barriers, several options were proposed. Education and
training programs, at both associate and graduate levels, were recommended
to provide specialized technical training and more hands-on experience, as were
internships that expose students to real-world applications beyond academic
research. This would help develop a workforce that is not only technically proficient
but also adept at interdisciplinary collaboration. Furthermore, increasing the
number of skilled technicians through targeted training programs was emphasized
as a critical need. These programs could focus on developing expertise in
maintenance and troubleshooting, which are vital for the operational success of
scaling technologies. Training programs could also be made available for re-skilling
the existing workforce, particularly in job sectors that have been displaced by
economic or technology trends. On a long-term, national level, organizing a
comprehensive and flexible training curriculum to adapt to evolving workforce
needs will allow for flexibility within a rapidly changing technology landscape. By
implementing these solutions, the U.S. workforce can be better equipped to meet
the demands of scaling up disruptive chemical manufacturing technologies.

more supportive infrastructure ecosystem for scaling up technologies, ultimately
accelerating the path to commercialization.

There are significant opportunities to establish robust testing standards and
metrics that can enhance the development and deployment of new technologies.
For technologies that collectively rely on one or more critical components such
as a membrane or a catalyst, one approach is to conduct round-robin testing with
standardized materials across multiple facilities. This method can help identify
commonalities and discrepancies in testing outcomes, paving the way for the
development of universally accepted protocols.
To increase understanding of materials durability and discover material-,
component-, and device-centered failure modes, national laboratories and industry
consortia can facilitate the creation of accelerated testing procedures that are
informed by real-world data. By collaborating with industry partners who have
operational systems, researchers can gain insights into degradation mechanisms
and develop tests that more accurately predict system durability. Establishing
standard protocols for durability testing and accelerated aging—both modeling
and experimentation—while involving experts from industry, academia, national
laboratories, and standards organizations such as the American Society for Testing
and Materials (ASTM), can improve consistency and transparency for the adoption
of new technologies. The workshop discussion also highlighted opportunities for
new research to develop materials, reactors, and operational protocols to support
long-term operation including degradation-resistant materials and systems, and in
situ (i.e., minimal intervention) strategies for self- and active-repair of degradation.
Additionally, fostering a culture of transparency and data sharing within the
scientific community can encourage the reporting of resulting datasets, including
both successful and unsuccessful outcomes. Included within this is an opportunity
to build public repositories or certification standards of materials performance
characterization such as through the National Institute of Standards and Technology

When requesting information about a commercial material, static data sheets are
often provided which give specific but limited descriptions of material properties.
For example, a conductive carbon support for an electrolyzer might have
information about its electrical conductivity and porosity, but will lack many of the
pertinent attributes when incorporated into the system: How will it compress?
How will its conductivity change over time? Will it corrode? While it is unfeasible to
expect a vendor to supply all of the information for every use case of their material,
there are major inefficiencies with independent organizations testing those same
materials for the same or similar purposes. Further, until these materials are tested
at scale and within the new functional context, it is challenging for developers
to make critical design and research investment decisions, in essence, to design
for manufacturing. One of the workshop discussion suggestions was to create a
‘10,000 Hours Club’ list for materials that have been demonstrated to last more
than 10,000 hours in continuous operation, providing operational context and
conditions. Exchange of knowledge such as this could reduce tedious, duplicated
efforts between organizations, particularly for the cases where materials have been
demonstrated successfully (such as the historic use of Nafion® in fuel cells), or
unsuccessfully.

Knowledge Gap 2: Scaled system performance and capital costs
One participant, an early-stage entrepreneur, noted the challenges of finding
an architecture and engineering firm who was able to build for the needs of a
prototyping facility rather than a commercial production plant, hindering the ability

of the company to quickly and efficiently set up prototyping experiments. This gap
has a trickle-down effect to the materials level—both companies and research
groups highlighted the gaps in knowledge of system performance at scale, including
the learning curves associated with building large pilot facilities and with producing
larger volumes of product. Similar to the 10,000 Hours Club, having access to lists of
auxiliary components such as mixers, pumps, filters, gas separation units, etc. that
have been demonstrated in like environments could help accelerate the technology
maturation process. Having standardized testing for these components would
provide transparency for businesses looking to scale up technologies. Furthermore,
several participants in the workshop cited the need for learning curves:
understanding the rate of cost reductions when producing more units of, or larger-
scale product. These barriers are intertwined with the infrastructure limitations of
dedicated research and development facilities and highlight the knowledge gaps
both from the perspective of the companies scaling up new technologies, and the
companies in the broader industrial ecosystem who may benefit from expanded
business opportunities.

Knowledge Gap 3: Scaling approaches and best practices
At the highest level, many of the workshop participants noted that scale-up is an
experience-driven endeavor with knowledge residing among those who have either
guided a startup through the scale-up process or have participated in a product
development cycle. Learning approaches and best practices in scale-up from these
entrepreneurs and industry experts was discussed as a critical aspect of accelerating
the development of new technologies and increasing the likelihood of success
for fledgling companies. Of particular note, the playbook for scaling different
technology categories, such as hardware development, software development,
business-to-business, business-to-consumer, etc., differ; the knowledge and
experience gap in bringing these technologies from concept to commercialization is
a limiting factor for accelerating scale-up.
To overcome these barriers, several potential solutions can be implemented to
foster a more collaborative and open environment for knowledge sharing. In the
short term, establishing and encouraging open sharing agreements between
voluntary organizations that aim to present, publish, or otherwise release codes,
research findings, or technical information while removing business-sensitive
(confidential unclassified) information, can help overcome this obstacle. Establishing
consortia or collaborative networks that bring together stakeholders from academia,
industry, and government can facilitate the exchange of information and best
practices. These networks can operate under agreements that protect intellectual
property while promoting transparency and collaboration. Additionally, creating
centralized databases or repositories where data and findings can be shared in a
standardized format would enable easier access and comparison of information.
Encouraging technical progress papers, similar to those in the automotive industry,
and alternative publications that emphasize process and characterization can also
provide a platform for sharing practical insights and experiences that may not be
suitable for traditional academic journals. Furthermore, incentivizing researchers
and companies to share their findings through recognition programs or funding
opportunities can motivate more open communication and contribute to a culture
of collective advancement in technology scale-up.

In the long-term, building repositories of anonymized research and non-proprietary
information including training datasets and learning curves for sector-specific
technologies can accelerate the scale-up of entire classes of technology. Leveraging
consortia or teams of experts for disseminating best practices and enabling inter-
organizational sharing of knowledge and know-how (i.e., from research groups to
technology development teams and back) can increase the effectiveness of both
scientific research and product demonstration activities toward bringing a new
capability more quickly to the market.

Technology-Specific Priorities
Workshop participants also explored scale-up challenges and opportunities through
specific technology examples representative of disruptive technology manufacturing
technologies, each of which focuses on chemical conversion to value-added
products. To focus the discussion, each technology was identified by its MVP, or the
smallest deployable unit that encompasses the essential functionality in its intended
operating environment. The specific metrics for the MVP for each technology shown
here were gathered from a survey of workshop attendees. However, many of the
companies and organizations present are developing technologies that are in line
with the technology category but have different target metrics from those defined
by the MVP in the workshop. Therefore, the following findings are inclusive of the
technology category as a whole, with discussion points that may deviate from the
specific MVP.

From these knowledge gaps emerged several technical barriers. First, quality control
is essential both for input feedstocks and for controlling the requirements of the
output product. For electrolyzers, this is likely to involve upstream and downstream
gas and liquid separation systems; however, without defined property metrics,
anticipating the needs for these quality controls is challenging. Second, durability
issues were prevalent across this technology including catalyst degradation, cell
system clogging, membrane fouling, and other forms of irreversible degradation.
Characterizing and overcoming interdependent degradation mechanisms remains
a pressing challenge for the field. Third, quantifying emergent phenomena, both in
experimental measurement and in computational modeling, remains a challenge,
not only for incorporating all of the relevant physics, but also for knowing where the
physics is relevant in particular models.
In light of these many gaps and barriers, the participants suggested capabilities that
could address these challenges.
 1. On-board diagnostics and process monitoring: Advancing diagnostic
 tools and techniques, such as in situ characterization of temperature, flow
 rates, and current distribution, can provide deeper insights into system
 performance and operational conditions. This understanding is essential for
 optimizing system design and operation, ultimately leading to more robust
 and efficient electrolyzer technologies. These measurements can be looped
 via process automation and the application of digital twins for on-the-fly
 performance prediction and modulation.
 2. Advanced characterization, operando experiments: Techniques such
 as X-ray neutron radiography and advanced optical imaging provide insights
 into internal operating conditions and can be used to determine root failure
 mechanisms.
 3. Ex situ membrane degradation and aging, and standardized post-
 mortem analysis: In particular, characterization of fouling, dissolution, or
 other degradation mechanisms for membranes in the presence of heavy
 metals (contaminants) and alcohols (by-products), can shed light on how
 to engineer, condition, and maintain these materials for extended use.
 a. Within this is a need to develop diagnostic tools for understanding
 different forms of degradation, and to develop standard process for
 full pre- and post-operation characterization.
 b. Establishing third-party verification, including standards testing, having
 dedicated testing facilities, and forming partnerships via sharing data
 and best practices, will help overcome many of the technical barriers
 to electrolyzer scale up.
 4. Computational modeling that bridges catalyst-scale, flow-scale,
 and process-scale: Even with advanced computation, the changes in
 the physics from atomistic- to system-scales for electrolyzers are vast and
 presents a new opportunity for advancing the scale-up of electrolyzers and
 many similar technologies.

Technology 2: Thermochemical Process Scale-Up

generating electricity. This example technology uses a bi-functional sorbent/catalyst
material to adsorb CO2 and facilitate a reaction with hydrogen at 300°C to produce
methane. Several knowledge gaps and technical barriers around this technology

Technology 3: Biological Conversion Scale-Up
Gas fermentation with the combination of carbon dioxide, hydrogen, oxygen, and
liquid nutrients to produce fuels and chemicals is one of many potential pathways
for biological conversion of carbon materials. This particular process works with a
combination of biological strain engineering and gas and liquid product separation
systems. This example uses engineered Ralstonia eutropha in a U-loop reactor to
convert CO2 from an industrial process, along with H2 and O2 from water electrolysis,
for production of diesel-range methyl ketones.
Three main challenges arose in the discussion:
 1. Strain engineering: the difficulty in engineering microbial strains to
 produce desired products at scale, particularly when these products are not
 naturally produced by the organisms.
 2. Reactor design: the complexity of designing reactors that can efficiently
 handle gas-liquid-solid interactions, maintain optimal conditions for microbial
 growth, and ensure high conversion efficiency.
 3. Process integration: the need for seamless integration of upstream
 and downstream processes, including gas supply reliability and product
 separation.

Conclusions
A ripe opportunity exists for enhancing research, development, demonstration,
and deployment activities of innovative technologies through a focus on scale-up
approaches. Insights from experts in academia, industry, national labs, and within
the government through this dedicated scale-up workshop highlight seven key
themes that may dramatically impact the chances of successfully commercializing
critical technologies:
 1. Access to testing facilities and development infrastructure at the prototype
 and pilot scales.
 2. Workforce education and training, particularly technical staff.
 3. Data analysis and software development to support critical technologies.
 4. Infrastructure integration analyses.
 5. Long-term, stable funding.
 6. Expansion of industry standards and durability testing.
 7. Knowledge transfer and information sharing.

Each of these themes were found throughout technical discussion on three
chemical processing systems: electrolysis, thermochemical conversion, and
biological conversion. This report explores each of these topics in depth, presenting
key insights that, if acted upon, may greatly enhance the speed and success of
scaling up energy and manufacturing technologies. These insights are intended
to provide granularity to encourage specific action by those involved in particular
technology arenas and also present possible avenues for nation-wide acceleration of
technology development. Collectively, this report explores opportunities to enhance
the deployment of new and disruptive technologies that will benefit the public and
bolster U.S. manufacturing competitiveness.
