On the Conservativeness of Robust Variance Estimators in Propensity Score Weighted Cox Models

Hiroya Morita, Shunichiro Orihara, Fumitaka Shimizu, Masataka Taguri

Abstract

In propensity score weighted analysis, robust variance that does not account for weight estimation (hereafter, uncorrected variance) is commonly used. In propensity score weighted Cox models (CoxPSW), the uncorrected variance is known to be conservative when weights for the average treatment effect (ATE) are used, but it remains unclear whether this conservativeness also holds for other weighting schemes. This study evaluated the performance of the uncorrected variance in CoxPSW when weights other than ATE are applied. We conducted an asymptotic comparison between the uncorrected variance and a variance estimator that accounts for weight estimation under non‐ATE weights. Their performance was further evaluated through simulation studies and real data analysis. The analytical results, simulations, and real data analysis indicated that the uncorrected variance is not necessarily conservative in CoxPSW when weights other than ATE are used. These findings suggest that variance estimators that account for weight estimation should be used when applying CoxPSW.

Introduction

Observational studies using real‐world data have become increasingly common. Because confounding is a major concern in observational research, it is essential to apply appropriate methods to address it. Propensity score weighted (PSW) analysis is one of the most widely used approaches [, ].

PSW typically employs weights derived from propensity scores. By applying different weights, PSW enables the estimation of various causal estimands, including the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), and the Average Treatment Effect for the Overlap population (ATO) []. For variance estimation, robust variance estimators that do not account for weight estimation (hereafter, uncorrected variance) are frequently used and serve as the default in many statistical software packages [, , , ]. For continuous outcomes, it is known that the uncorrected variance estimator is conservative for the ATE; however, recent studies report that this property does not always hold for other estimands, such as the ATT or ATO [, ].

In medical research, time‐to‐event outcomes are frequently analyzed. In the context of propensity score weighted Cox models (CoxPSW) [], the uncorrected variance has been shown to be conservative when estimating the ATE []. However, the properties of the uncorrected variance estimator in CoxPSW for other estimands, such as the ATT and ATO, remain unclear. In this study, we investigate the properties of the uncorrected variance estimator in CoxPSW for estimands other than the ATE through mathematical derivation, simulation studies, and data analysis.

Conservativeness Under Non‐ATE Weighting

As noted above, when using ATE weights wei≡1, the uncorrected variance is conservative. In the , we show that for general weight functions other than those corresponding to the ATE, the uncorrected variance is not necessarily asymptotically conservative. This finding is consistent with previous results for continuous outcomes [].

Discussion

In this study, we showed that the uncorrected variance estimator that ignores the estimation of weights in CoxPSW is not necessarily conservative for general weighting schemes other than the ATE. This result is consistent with previous findings for continuous outcomes [, ].

The simulation results demonstrated that the uncorrected variance estimator may fail to be conservative when ATT or ATO weights are used. A general pattern observed in the simulations was that when covariates were weakly associated with the exposure but strongly associated with the outcome, the uncorrected variance estimator tended to be conservative regardless of the weighting scheme. Similar phenomena related to prognostic variables have been reported for continuous outcomes []. Further research is needed to clarify the theoretical properties of the asymptotic variance of CoxPSW.

In the real data analysis, we also compared the uncorrected variance estimator with the corrected variance estimator. Because the theory of the uncorrected variance estimator is based on asymptotic variance, its properties are not necessarily guaranteed in finite samples. In the present data analysis, when ATE weights were used, the uncorrected variance estimator produced larger estimates across all subgroups, as expected. In contrast, when ATT or ATO weights were used, the uncorrected variance estimator produced smaller estimates in some subgroups.

In conclusion, similar to the cases of continuous outcomes, the uncorrected variance estimator in CoxPSW is not guaranteed to be conservative when weighting schemes other than the ATE are used. In general, regardless of whether the uncorrected variance estimator is conservative, variance estimators that account for the estimation of the weights should be used when applying CoxPSW.
