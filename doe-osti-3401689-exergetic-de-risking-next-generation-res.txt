Executive Summary
Geothermal is a high-capacity factor energy source with the potential to be dispatchable. Despite
its growth in recent years, broader deployment is constrained by geographical constraints, high
upfront costs, some environmental risks, and relatively low energy conversion efficiencies [1-3].
In addition, most commercial geothermal plants are designed for baseload operation.
Consequently, their potential to provide flexible operation capabilities and grid services has not
yet been fully quantified or demonstrated, especially for configurations integrating underground
thermal energy storage (UTES). The DOE-OG–funded project entitled “EXERGETIC: De-Risking
Next- Generation Resilient Geothermal Hybrids via At-Scale Evaluation Using a Virtual
Emulation Digital Twin Environment for Efficient Operation,” advances the solution to these
challenges by developing and validating a geothermal co-emulation environment implemented at
the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR)’s Advanced Research on Integrated Energy
Systems (ARIES) platform. This framework enables the de-risking of next-generation geothermal
and geothermal hybrid systems through high-fidelity modeling, real-time digital emulation,
advanced control strategies, and techno-economic assessment.
The project focused on geothermal hybrid configurations that integrate geothermal power plants
with concentrated solar power and underground thermal energy storage, enabling enhanced
efficiency, flexibility, and grid support capabilities. The main goal of this project was the
development of a geothermal digital co-emulation environment to demonstrate the technical and
economic value of geothermal hybrid systems and their contribution to grid stability and
flexibility.
The EXERGETIC framework combined physics-based models, controls, and real assets at ARIES,
including digital real-time simulators (DRTS), a 20 MW-scale controllable grid interface (CGI),
and a 2 MW conventional generator. Detailed transient models were developed for the key
subsystems of a hybrid geothermal plant, including parabolic trough solar collectors, UTES, and
a binary organic Rankine cycle (ORC) power plant. Among UTES technologies, reservoir TES
(RTES) systems, also known as Geological TES (GeoTES), allow underground thermal energy
storage at temperatures from 0°C to 300°C [4], while borehole TES (BTES) has been usually
considered for temperatures lower than 100°C with new developments targeting upper
temperatures between 140°C and 220°C [5,6]. The ORC model explicitly captured thermal inertia
and off-design operation and integrated control strategies to dynamically respond to electric load
profiles. The models were validated against published experimental and numerical studies,
demonstrating strong agreement and confirming the accuracy and robustness of the modeling
approach.
The resulting digital twin represents geothermal–solar–storage systems at multiple scales (1 MW
to 100 MW) and enables realistic emulation of grid-connected operation. The control architecture
allows the geothermal resource to provide stable baseload generation, while solar and stored
thermal energy support flexible operation during periods of high demand or variable grid
conditions. A key contribution of the EXERGETIC project is the demonstration that geothermal

 vii

hybrid systems can be designed to be active grid assets rather than passive baseload generators.
Using the ARIES platform, the digital twin was evaluated under multiple grid scenarios, including
load following, voltage support at the distribution level, and frequency response at the transmission
level. Results show that hybrid geothermal systems can respond effectively to dynamic grid
conditions, providing inertia-like behavior, primary frequency support, and voltage regulation
through coordinated control.
In addition to the performance and grid services capability analysis of geothermal and hybrid
geothermal systems, the EXERGETIC project also focused on scalability and techno-economic
analysis of geothermal hybrid plants. In particular, for the scalability analysis, machine-learning
(ML)–based surrogate models were trained using data generated from the geothermal digital twin
under different grid-connected scenarios and plant capacities. These ML models demonstrated
strong interpolation and extrapolation capabilities across plant sizes, accurately reproducing both
steady-state and transient responses with very low errors. Regarding the techno-economic analysis,
plant performance results were integrated with cost models for hybrid geothermal systems, and the
levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) was used as the main economic metric to evaluate system
performance across a range of system capacities, solar shares, solar multiples, and storage
durations. Results indicate that economies of scale significantly reduce geothermal LCOE as plant
capacity increases, with large-scale systems (25–100 MW) achieving substantially lower costs
than small plants. Hybridization with solar thermal energy and storage further improves economic
performance by increasing capacity utilization and enabling flexible dispatch. In addition, thermal
storage plays a critical role in reducing LCOE by maximizing geothermal, solar, and stored energy
resources.
In summary, the results from this project demonstrate that geothermal hybrid systems represent a
promising alternative for increasing the energy conversion efficiency of geothermal technologies,
contributing to the preservation of geothermal resources, and supporting the transition of
geothermal plants from traditional baseload resources into flexible, resilient, and cost-competitive
energy conversion technologies. The EXERGETIC co-emulation framework expands NLR’s
ARIES capabilities and represents a unique asset for accelerating the development and deployment
of next-generation geothermal technologies in the United States.

1 Introduction
1.1 Geothermal Overview
Geothermal energy is the thermal energy that is stored and generated in the Earth's subsurface. The huge
existing potential of geothermal energy could supply the total human energy consumption several
thousand times. Geothermal technologies have been developed to harness this underground heat source
and transport it to the surface for direct use for heating and cooling, energy storage, and power generation
using Rankine or Organic Rankine power generation cycles. Geothermal power is a dispatchable baseload
power with high-capacity factors, usually above 75% [4,7]. This consistency provides a critical foundation
for grid stability and resiliency. Geothermal energy has a low requirement of land per megawatt installed
capacity and a lesser visual impact. It can be used in diverse applications (see Figure 1) beyond power
generation, including space and water heating and cooling, district heating, energy storage, and low-to-
medium temperature industry applications such as food processing and drying [4,8].

 Figure 1. Schematic diagram of geothermal applications

For power generation, geothermal plants employ a variety of technologies designed to harness subsurface
heat effectively under diverse geological and thermal conditions. At the high-temperature end of the
spectrum, dry-steam and flash-steam plants remain the most established technologies. Dry-steam
facilities, such as those in California's Geysers field, utilize naturally occurring steam to drive turbines
directly, whereas flash-steam systems use high-pressure geothermal fluids that flash into steam upon
depressurization [9]. For the more common moderate-temperature reservoirs (typically 100 °C to 170 °C),
binary-cycle power plants, based on the Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC), have become dominant. In these
plants, geothermal brine transfers heat to a secondary working fluid with a lower boiling point, enabling

 1

efficient electricity production while maintaining a closed-loop configuration that minimizes emissions
[10].
Despite the advantages and vast geothermal potential, global geothermal power deployment remains
modest. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), total installed geothermal
electric capacity reached ≈ 15.4 GWₑ worldwide at the end of 2024, distributed across more than 30
countries [11]. The REN21 2023 Global Status Report shows that only ≈ 0.2 GW of new capacity was
added in 2022, reflecting ongoing challenges in capital cost and exploration risk [12]. Geothermal
currently contributes roughly 0.34% of global electricity generation [13]. Nevertheless, countries such as
Indonesia, the Philippines, Turkey, Kenya, and the United States demonstrate that mature geothermal
industries can play a vital role in the power portfolios.
The United States remains the global leader, with ~ 3.969 GWₑ of installed geothermal capacity as of
2024, about 23% of global generation [1]. Most facilities are located in California and Nevada, with
emerging projects in Utah, Idaho, and Oregon; see Figure 2; even with current projects in California and
Nevada, geothermal energy is still considered underutilized, and outside the Western United States,
geothermal is not deployed for power production [1,14]. In 2023, geothermal plants in seven U.S. states
produced ~ 17,000 GWh, accounting for 0.4% of national electricity output [15]. Identified and
undiscovered hydrothermal potential in the Western U.S. totals about 36 GW, while Enhanced Geothermal
Systems (EGS) could expand technically recoverable capacity to ~ 90 GW [16]. The DOE GeoVision
roadmap (2019) [17] projects that geothermal generation could reach ≈ 60 GW by 2050, a 26-fold increase
over current levels, potentially supplying ~ 8.5 % of U.S. electricity [18]. Beyond electricity, geothermal
energy is widely applied for direct heating and ground-source heat pumps (GSHPs). More than 1.7 million
GSHP units operate in the U.S., providing efficient heating and cooling with cumulative thermal capacity
exceeding 480 MWₜ [19]. Together, these applications underscore geothermal’s versatility as both a power
and thermal-energy solution.
Some of the main reasons why geothermal power only represents less than 1% of power generation in the
United States and worldwide [11,20] are mainly associated with several challenges including geographical
constraints, high upfront costs, some environmental risks, and relatively low energy conversion
efficiencies [1-3]. The natural conditions needed to produce power from geothermal sources—namely,
hot rock and naturally occurring fractures within the rocks to allow water flow through them—are
uncommon [3]. Regarding the high cost, prior to the development of a geothermal project, investments
are needed to conduct exploration and drilling tests to determine the geothermal well potential. Once a
well is confirmed as viable for power generation, the deployment of the plant requires drilling that usually
represents the highest initial cost of the project; this cost could increase with the presence of hard rocks,
corrosive environments, and hot temperatures [17,21]. Recent progress in subsurface imaging, drilling
systems, real-time monitoring, and predictive modeling is helping to reduce these barriers [17,22]. Once
the plant is commissioned, operational costs usually remain low, and plants can maintain output for several
decades. In addition to the geographical constraints and high initial investment, some environmental risks
associated with geothermal power generation include gas emissions, groundwater contamination, and in
some cases, induced seismicity [23].

 Figure 2. Geothermal power generation in the western United States by state [1].
Conventional geothermal plants utilize hydrothermal sources, which have geographical limitations and
high upfront investment. In addition, their low operating temperatures (usually below 180°C for binary
plants) [24] also lead to relatively low energy conversion efficiencies, usually below 15%, depending on
reservoir temperatures and turbine design [25,26]. The next-generation geothermal technologies that are
not dependent on hydrothermal resources have been proposed and are at different stages of development
[27]. These geothermal technologies are categorized as enhanced geothermal systems (EGS), closed-loop
geothermal systems (CLGS), and superhot geothermal, see Figure 3. These new technologies advance
geothermal energy by enabling its deployment almost anywhere and increasing efficiency by reaching
high-temperature rock at greater depths. EGS focuses on expanding reservoir capabilities through rock
fracture to increase rock permeability [28]. EGS uses diverse engineering techniques, often adapted from
the oil and gas industry, such as drilling and hydraulic fracturing. Some of the main technical challenges
of EGS are associated with water losses, maintaining well integrity, and induced seismicity [7]. CLGS are
similar to EGS but utilize closed-loop artificial networks where a heat transfer fluid is circulated to
exchange heat with the surrounding hot rock. In general, CLGS operates under lower flow rates and
requires larger and more complex wellbores than EGS to generate a large heat transfer area, increasing
the drilling cost [20]. Superhot geothermal systems are still in the conceptual stage but are focused on
reaching rock temperatures of up to 500°C to increase energy conversion efficiencies. These systems could
leverage existing technologies from conventional geothermal, EGS, and CLGS, but require advanced
drilling techniques and materials to withstand higher temperatures and/or pressures and operation under
corrosive environments.

1.2 Geothermal Hybrid Systems
Binary geothermal power plants produce electricity at efficiencies usually between 4 % and 12% due to
the temperature range of most accessible geothermal sources (120°C – 250°C) [29]. For flash and steam
geothermal plants, which can operate at higher efficiencies (usually between 6 % and 18% [29]), the power
generation and efficiency can decline over time due to geothermal resource depletion. Hybridization with
other power generation and energy storage technologies could increase the efficiency of power conversion
and mitigate reservoir decline. Several geothermal hybrid systems have been proposed to increase the
energy conversion efficiency and/or optimize energy output of conventional geothermal plants by taking
advantage of higher operation temperatures, enhanced energy management, and complementary
characteristics between technologies. Some recent studies of geothermal hybrid systems considered the
integration of geothermal with diverse energy technologies including biomass, wind, concentrated solar,
natural gas, and underground energy storage.
Briola et al. [30] designed a hybrid geothermal-biomass power plant using an ORC for use in challenging
environments with limited water and large ambient temperature swings. Their simulation was focused on,
maximizing power and efficiency. Operating the plant to maximize thermodynamic efficiency resulted in
a substantial reduction in annual biomass consumption, saving between 28.3% and 42.6% of fuel. A study
by Hashemian and Noorpoor [31] introduces a highly efficient, multi-generation plant powered by
geothermal and biomass energy that produces electricity, heating, cooling, hydrogen, and freshwater
simultaneously. Through thermo-economic and environmental optimization, the plant achieved energy

 4

efficiencies up to 58.54%. In another work, Liu et al. [32] developed and analyzed a hybrid system that
combines geothermal and biogas power with a CO2 capture unit to generate electricity, fresh water,
hydrogen, hot and cold water. A sensitivity analysis demonstrated that optimizing the CO2 capture process
could dramatically improve energy efficiency by about 77%. The theoretical study by Gursoy and Dincer
[33] outlines a multi-source power process leveraging geothermal, wind, and tidal/wave energy. The
system could co-produce electricity, heat, fresh water, and hydrogen, achieving an overall energy
efficiency of 18.13%. Afshardoost and Shamsi [34] developed a highly efficient, carbon-neutral hybrid
system using geothermal energy and biomass. The system, which integrates a cascaded ORC with
gasification, was optimized to co-produce hydrogen, electricity, steam, and hot water, achieving overall
efficiencies over 65%.
Hybridization of geothermal plants with concentrating solar power (CSP), gas turbines (GT), and/nuclear
technologies could represent an alternative to improving the competitiveness of geothermal plants by
reducing installation costs. To advance the demonstration of this hypothesis, a technoeconomic
assessment was conducted in this project for a geothermal hybrid system composed of a binary geothermal
plant, a concentrating solar field, and a UTES. The results of this analysis are presented in section 6.
Furthermore, hybridization of geothermal systems with CSP and UTES has emerged as a promising
avenue to enhance performance, improve flexibility, and extend reservoir lifetime [10,35-37]. For
instance, when the hybrid system uses a common power block, it increases efficiency and reliability due
to higher operational temperatures and complementary power generation. Air-cooled geothermal plants
are highly impacted by the ambient temperature, and performance is typically lower during midday
periods when air temperatures are higher. Hybrid geothermal-solar plants mitigate this issue because the
power generation from solar resource is higher during midday due to higher solar radiation [38,39]. Wendt
et al. [40] proposed a hybrid geothermal plant that integrated a high temperature energy source such as
CSP or a natural gas topping cycle with low temperature (150°C) geothermal resources. It was found that
this hybridization approach substantially enhances both power output and overall efficiency compared to
stand-alone geothermal operations. It is worth noting that nearly 45% of geothermal hybrid installations
are with solar sources [9]. The hybridization of these sources provides many advantages, including the
improvement of the global efficiency of the systems and the increase in power generation capacity [41],
meanwhile the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) of the systems can be reduced [20]. Tranamil-Maripe
et al. [42] conducted a study to analyze the hybridization of geothermal and CSP plant in northern Chile.
Their results indicate a reduction of the LCOE of a geothermal stand-alone plant by approximately 10
USD/MWh. It was also found that the reduction of the LCOE is dependent on the solar field size and the
solar system’s energy storage.
While modeling and analysis have demonstrated the clear advantages of hybridizing geothermal systems
with other energy conversion technologies, real-world implementation remains limited. To date, only a
small number of geothermal hybrid plants are currently operational, with others in various stages of
development [43-48]. Table 1 presents a summary of some representative operational geothermal plants.

1.3 Physics-Based and Machine Learning Modeling and Simulation
Even when the hybridization of geothermal plants could favor their near-term deployment in the United
States using low-temperature geothermal wells (< 120°C), a proper and comprehensive technical and
economic evaluation based on high-accuracy models is still required. Commonly used modeling platforms
such as Geothermal Electricity Technology Evaluation Model (GETEM) [14], System Advisor Model
(SAM) [49], and Geothermal energy for Production of Heat and electricity ("IR") Economically Simulated
GEOPHIRES [50] only support the evaluation of stand-alone geothermal plants. In addition, most of the
models available in the literature for geothermal hybrid systems are based on steady-state operation and
are usually based on a specific system or application [51].

Hybrid geothermal-solar systems have some important disadvantages including the complexity of the
power generation system [51] and the requirement for sophisticated operation control systems that can
oversee the appropriate functioning of all the components while attending to the power load dynamics.
Significant improvements can be achieved by using enhanced modeling tools [52]. Likewise,
incorporation of digital twins into geothermal operations will allow a more complete understanding of the
complex interaction of components in these systems and while leading to an improved exploration of the
operational space with the latest developments in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI)
[52]. Digital twins that identically represent a power plant are significantly useful since they can be
modified and optimized in a fraction of the cost and time of a real one [53]. Geothermal sources have not
experienced the extensive exercise of digital analytics tools to accompany decision making that other
industries have already taken advantage of due to numerous challenges that have inhibited the

 6

embracement of systems modeling using data-based digital twins in the geothermal industry [52]. One of
the main challenges is the lack of modeling tools focused on geothermal phenomena.
Some studies using AI for geothermal simulation and analysis can be found in the literature. Khosravi et
al. [54] presented an AI approach for simulating the Geothermal ORC conditioned with a solar thermal
unit. Based on a set of input parameters, including the geothermal temperature and the working fluid mass
flow rate, the models predict the performance of the plant in terms of net power output, energy efficiency,
exergy efficiency, and LCOE. Buster et al. [52] presented a new framework for Geothermal Operational
Optimization with Machine Learning – GOOML. By taking a hybrid data-driven thermodynamics
approach, the tool models the performance of existent geothermal systems. The tool can also be used for
optimization. Among the outcomes of using the tool: improved capacity factors and efficiency, and overall
resource utilization and optimization. Similarly, Liu et al. [55] presented a dynamic neural network model
for performance prediction, control, and real-time monitoring of geothermal power plants. The
architecture is trained using historical data from the power plant to obtain the underlying dynamics in the
data to generate forecasts.

1.4 Project Overview
The main goal of this project is the development of a geothermal-based hybrid Digital Real Time
Simulation (DRTS) co-emulation environment that will advance the state of the art of geothermal energy
by demonstrating the technical and economic value of geothermal plants hybridized with other power
generation and thermal energy storage technologies and their contribution to the grid stability and
flexibility.

1.4.1 Project Objectives
This project advanced the evaluation of geothermal hybrid systems by improving the understanding of
their technical and economic performance and quantifying their potential contributions to grid stability
and operational flexibility. Specific project objectives are summarized as follows:
 - Develop and validate physics-based models for key geothermal hybrid system components,
 including binary geothermal power plants, CSP, and UTES, to establish a reliable baseline for
 evaluating hybrid geothermal system performance. This objective includes gathering existing
 models or creating new ones as needed, validating them using published data, and incorporating

1.4.2. Technical Approach
This project was divided into seven tasks and six milestones spanning a period of performance of 23
months, see Figure 4. The first task focused on gathering information about existing models for the UTES,
geothermal, and CSP plants. The models were developed or modified to account for thermal losses, partial
load, and off-design operation for the ORC, as well as inertia in the most critical components. The new
models enable a dynamic and transient representation of the energy conversion process to provide high-
fidelity results. The second task leveraged the models developed or adapted in Task 1 to create a
geothermal hybrid model. The individual model for each subsystem (i.e., UTES, CSP, and Geothermal-
ORC) was validated by comparing the results with those reported for similar systems in the literature. The
models were then implemented into the NLR’s ARIES facility using a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL)
configuration through the digital real-time simulators. The integration also included other existing ARIES
assets, such as the controllable grid interface (CGI) and the visualization center.
Different control strategies were evaluated and developed to integrate the geothermal hybrid system
components. The integration of the models into the DRTS formed digital twins of 1 MW geothermal and
geothermal hybrid plants, which were used in the project to evaluate the response, dynamic operation, and
grid benefits of a grid-connected geothermal hybrid system (Geothermal-ORC, CSP, and UTES). This co-
emulation environment, shown in Figure 5, expands ARIES’s capabilities to assess the potential of
geothermal-based technologies to provide grid services that improve flexibility and resiliency. It also
enables the evaluation of the performance of diverse geothermal and geothermal hybrid energy systems
under grid-connected conditions to help de-risk future deployments in the United States.

2 Physics-Based Models
2.1 System Description
The hybrid geothermal system analyzed in this project combines a 1 MW binary geothermal plant with a
solar-thermal and storage loop connected through a primary heat exchanger. This interconnected loop
features a parabolic trough collector (PTC) CSP plant and a UTES unit, Figure 6. The geothermal fluid
from the production well mixes directly with the hot fluid from the solar-UTES loop before entering the
primary heat exchanger. The geothermal plant utilizes a flexible ORC with pentane as the working fluid,
driven by energy from the geothermal resource and receiving supplemental thermal energy from either
the UTES or the CSP plant.

 Figure 6. Schematic diagram of the 1MW binary geothermal hybrid plant.

A central goal of the EXERGETIC project was to demonstrate the added value of these hybrid geothermal
configurations compared to conventional, single-source geothermal binary (ORC) plants. The CSP
component selected employs PTC technology, recognized for its maturity and commercial availability
within the broader landscape of CSP technologies. Although solar tower technology has also achieved
commercial deployment globally, reports have indicated reliability challenges associated with its two-tank
molten salt storage systems [56]. PTC systems concentrate solar radiation and convert it into thermal

 10

energy, which is subsequently absorbed and transported by a working fluid, usually water or thermal oil.
This work specifically considers pressurized water and Syltherm 800 for the solar collection loop. The
UTES is designed as a two-borehole closed-loop system, with one borehole dedicated to the injection
(charging) of thermal energy and the other for its extraction (discharging).

2.2 Hybrid Geothermal Model
The hybrid geothermal system model was developed by independently formulating physics-based models
for each subsystem, i.e., the PTC, UTES, and Geothermal ORC plant. Each subsystem model was
constructed using fundamental heat and mass conservation laws and the governing thermodynamic
equations that describe fluid properties, phase behavior, and energy transfer. Transient dynamics were
explicitly incorporated to capture time-dependent variations in temperature, pressure, flow rate, and
component response, enabling realistic simulation of operational transitions, load-following behavior, and
dynamic interactions among the subsystems.

2.2.1 Parabolic Trough Collectors
A comprehensive radial- and axial-dimensional PTC model was developed, building upon the modeling
framework described by Osorio and Rivera-Alvarez [57,58]. This model splits the receiver into multiple
longitudinal segments to monitor changes in the temperature of the heat transfer fluid (HTF) both radially
and axially [57]. It includes detailed thermal and optical analysis components. The optical analysis
quantifies the solar radiation captured by the absorber tube, considering losses due to reflection and
transmission in the glass envelope (GE). A significant portion of the absorbed radiation is transferred to
the heat transfer fluid, while a fraction of the energy is lost through reradiation to the atmosphere and
conduction through the PTC support brackets. The geometric dimensions and the optical and
thermophysical parameters for the PTC system are taken from ref. [57]. These parameters have been
selected in accordance with existing operational systems and established models available in the literature
[59-61].

2.2.2 Underground Thermal Energy Storage
A transient UTES model was developed based on the configuration of a coaxial borehole heat exchanger
(CBHE), essentially a double-pipe heat exchanger. Compared to traditional designs such as U-tube or
double-U configurations, CBHEs have been demonstrated to offer higher thermal efficiency [63]. This
configuration takes full advantage of the vertical temperature gradient within the subsurface [64], with the

 12

inlet fluid flowing through the annulus to maximize heat exchange, while the return fluid is thermally
insulated within the inner pipe.
Five layers are considered in the UTES model, as shown in Figure 8. The inner layer corresponds to Fluid
2, which is the fluid circulating through the inner pipe. Surrounding this fluid layer is an insulation layer,
which is also in contact with Fluid 1, the fluid entering the heat exchanger (i.e., the fluid in the annulus).
Two additional layers surround this set: a wall in direct contact with Fluid 1 for containment of the annular
fluid, and a rock/concrete layer that represents the underground energy storage medium and its thermal
characteristics.

The UTES model was validated by comparing with results reported by Hu et al. [65] and Beier et al. [67],
who conducted a numerical study of a coaxial borehole heat exchanger used to extract geothermal energy
from an abandoned petroleum well in Canada. In their work, they developed a COMSOL Multiphysics
model and verified it against analytical solutions. As illustrated in Figure 9a, the comparison examines
the temperature profiles of the working fluid in both the annulus and the inner pipe for each model. To
maintain consistency, our physics-based model employed the same operating conditions used in their
study, including a borehole depth of 3500 meters. The results show that our model accurately captures the
fluid’s thermal behavior over a full year of operation, with an absolute deviation below 2.6 K. It is worth

 14

noting that a borehole UTES at depths as great as 3,500 meters is probably not economically feasible and
should be constructed at a much shallower depth. The value of 3,500 meters is only used in this report for
model validation purposes.
To verify the model's accuracy at the high operating temperatures required for high-temperature UTES
(140–200°C), a secondary validation was conducted using subsurface data from the Momotombo
geothermal field in Nicaragua, originally compiled by Porras et al. [68], see Figure 9b. This reference
study includes both field measurements and 3D reservoir simulations for well MT36, a site situated above
an upflow zone where deep temperatures climb to roughly 316°C. Using the documented surface
temperatures and geothermal gradients as boundary conditions, the current model simulated the fluid's
vertical temperature profile down to about 1,385 meters. The resulting profile closely matches the field
measurements across the entire depth, spanning the critical 140–200°C target window. The simulation
yielded a mean absolute deviation of 9.7 K and a maximum absolute deviation of 14.9 K, maintaining an
overall accuracy within approximately 4% of the local formation temperature.

2.2.3 Organic Rankine Cycle
The developed model represents the thermodynamic behavior of an ORC unit, designed to convert low-
grade heat into mechanical power. The cycle consists of four main components (expander, condenser,
pump, and evaporator) interconnected by the working fluid circuit. Figure 10 presents a schematic
representation of the system, highlighting the main state points, the control loops, and the heat exchange
interfaces with the external heat source and sink. The model captures both steady-state and transient
aspects of the system, although the transient behavior is explicitly considered only in the evaporator
section, because it exhibits significant thermal inertia and phase-change dynamics that dominate the
system’s transient response. The remaining components can be satisfactorily represented by steady-state
formulations. As a consequence of the transient nature of the model, the operation of the cycle is subject
to several control loops that regulate key process variables such as the net power output, the working fluid
temperature at the evaporator outlet, and the pressure level at the evaporator.

 15

Two distinct mass flow rates are defined within the model: one associated with the expander section of
the system and another with the pump section. As a result, the inlet and outlet mass flow rates differ for
the evaporator and the condenser. The evaporator receives the flow from the pump side and delivers it to
the expander side, whereas the condenser performs the reverse function. These differences are explicitly
accounted for in the component-level mass and energy balances.

The operational parameters for the ORC system are detailed in Table 2. These parameters are expressed
as ranges to account for fluctuations during dynamic and partial load operations, which vary according to
control strategies used to meet load requirements. These ranges also depend on the plant's capacity. As
mentioned, this study evaluated five geothermal hybrid configurations with capacities of 1, 10, 25, 50,
and 100 MW. Notably, since individual ORC units with a 100 MW capacity are not currently
commercially available, the 100 MW plant analyzed in this study is modeled as a modular combination
of two units, each with a capacity of 50 MW.

As illustrated in Figure 10, the heating fluid, referred to as the heat source fluid (HF), is a mixture of two
separate streams: one from the geothermal well and another from the solar + UTES field. Both streams,
generally consisting of water, enter the mixing junction with specific mass flow rates and temperatures.

This mixed flow enters the evaporator–bypass system, where it delivers heat to the working fluid.
Downstream of the evaporator-bypass assembly, the streams recombine and are returned to their
respective geothermal and solar circuits.
The evaporator–external bypass valve assembly is modeled as an integrated subsystem responsible for
delivering heat from the external heat source to the working fluid. The external bypass valve modulates
the heat input to the evaporator and, consequently, regulates the thermal load and the power output of the
ORC. The valve opening fraction, denoted as (𝑓ℎ ), varies between 0 and 1 (a value of 0 indicates that the
bypass valve is completely closed and the entire heat source flow passes through the evaporator, while a

The evaporator is modeled as a once-through boiler, meaning that the fluid flows through a single
continuous passage where all three heat transfer regions (preheating, evaporation, and superheating) occur
sequentially without separation. This configuration eliminates the need for a steam drum or separate
economizer and superheater sections and is consistent with the physical design adopted for this model.

3.2 Control Development
A control system was developed to manage the energy output and storage of the hybrid geothermal system
(HGS), which integrates a binary geothermal plant, PTC-based CSP, and UTES. In this configuration, the
ORC relies on geothermal energy as the base load for power generation, while the thermal energy collected
by the PTC and stored in the UTES is used to regulate ORC’s power output in response to the grid demand.
As described in the previous section, a PID control strategy was designed to operate the pumps and valves
of the HGS. A decentralized control architecture was implemented, in which each subsystem is governed
by its own PID controller according to its specific dynamics and operational requirements. This approach
was selected to enhance modularity and allow individual subsystems to be replaced or integrated with
alternative models or hardware-in-the-loop components. The complete control system was implemented
in Simulink, combining physics-based models of all three energy subsystems, and was validated under a
real-world demand scenario.
The simplified schematic of the HGS considered in this study is shown in Figure 15. The system includes
an ORC loop (blue line) composed of a primary heat exchanger, a geothermal heater, a turbine, a
condenser, a pump, and a bypass valve. Thermal energy from the geothermal well is supplied to the ORC
through the geothermal heater, while the primary heat exchanger transfers additional heat from either the
solar field or the UTES. The PTC field converts solar energy into heat, which is transferred to the HTF,
Syltherm-800 or pressurized water. Depending on the load requirements, the HTF is directed to the UTES
to charge the storage system (orange line), to the primary heat exchanger to deliver heat to the ORC (green
line) or split between both pathways. When solar input is insufficient to meet power demand, stored
thermal energy is discharged from the UTES as the fluid circulates to the primary heat exchanger (orange
dotted line), supplementing the ORC’s heat supply.

production stream, the ladder logic evaluates the energy demand of the ORC system. If the ORC power

supply additional thermal energy to the ORC from the UTES or directly from the solar collector field. The
PID controller modulates the working-fluid flow rate through the primary heat exchanger, utilizing stored
thermal energy or directly harvested solar heat. Importantly, the PID control loop only enables flow
through the primary heat exchanger after the geothermal source has reached its maximum available
thermal output. This control strategy ensures that solar and stored thermal resources are utilized only when
geothermal production cannot fully meet the system power demand. When geothermal energy is sufficient
to satisfy the load requirement, the ladder logic prioritizes charging the UTES.

4 Technical Assessment
As discussed in Section 1, the primary objective of this project is to assess geothermal hybrid systems
through comprehensive technical and economic evaluations, including quantifying their potential
contributions to grid stability and operational flexibility. This section focuses on the technical assessment
and grid-support capabilities, while the economic evaluation is presented in Section 6.

4.1 Performance Evaluation
The implementation of a hybrid geothermal system can enable significant cost savings and provide several
operational advantages. These benefits include increased system flexibility, improved overall efficiency,
and optimized use of both geothermal and solar resources within a shared installation. By integrating these
energy sources, the system allows for additional power generation without the need to duplicate core
components, thereby reducing capital and operational expenditures. Furthermore, the shared use of
resources within a single field enhances energy utilization and contributes to a more efficient and
sustainable power generation strategy.
Assuming a constant geothermal resource temperature, for example, 100 °C, and a solar field - UTES loop
capable of providing temperatures above this level, the hybrid configuration enables an increase in the
overall efficiency of the power generation cycle. This improvement arises from the higher effective heat-
source temperature achieved through the integration and mixing of the two thermal fluids. As a result, the
combined solar/UTES–geothermal heat input enhances the thermodynamic performance of the cycle
compared to a standalone geothermal system operating at a fixed lower temperature.
In the implemented model, the flow and temperature of the heating source coming to the ORC (𝑚̇𝐻𝐹,𝑖𝑛
and 𝑇𝐻𝐹,𝑖𝑛 ) can be calculated as a function of the geothermal resource (at conditions 𝑚̇𝐻𝐹,𝐺𝐸𝑂 and 𝑇𝐻𝐹,𝐺𝐸𝑂 )
and the solar resource (at conditions 𝑚̇𝑠𝑜𝑙𝑎𝑟−𝑈𝑇𝐸𝑆 and 𝑇𝑠𝑜𝑙𝑎𝑟−𝑈𝑇𝐸𝑆 ), according to:

evaporation temperature, and consequently the evaporation pressure, of the ORC power generation system

4.3 Grid-Support Capabilities
Grid services refer to the measurable physical performance provided by resources such as generators,
energy storage devices, or controllable loads that support the reliable and efficient operation of the electric
power system, from the transmission network to the distribution network [74]. The primary objective of
grid services is to maintain the balance between generation and demand by stabilizing system frequency,
regulating voltage, and providing reserve capacity to respond to unexpected events. Accordingly, grid
services define what specific performance is expected from service providers, such as delivering,
consuming, or adjusting power at a given location or over a specific timescale [74]. For instance, grid
operators issue service requests specifying performance requirements, such as ramping power output,
regulating voltage, or responding to frequency deviations, and the distributed energy resource facility
determines how to meet these requests while maintaining control of its own assets. Although specific
terminology and definitions vary across markets and regions, the common categories of grid services are
illustrated in Figure 38 [75,76].

 Figure 38. Overview of common grid service categories.

frequency response to support electric load variations, while voltage regulators and excitation systems

5 Scalability Analysis Using Machine Learning Tools
The geothermal hybrid digital twin (GHDT) is a dynamic, data-driven representation of physical
geothermal assets that integrate real-time operational data, physics-based simulations, and predictive
analytics to enhance system performance and support informed decision-making. The GHDT provides
valuable insights into system operating principles, dynamic behavior, and grid-integration performance.
Physics-driven geothermal models are founded on thermodynamics, heat transfer, and fluid mechanics
and are typically formulated using approximate mathematical representations. While high-fidelity models
substantially improve the accuracy of geothermal plant emulation, achieving scalability remains a key
challenge due to the highly nonlinear and complex dynamics of ORC systems, which make adapting
models across different power generation capacities computationally intensive and time-consuming.
The incorporation of AI and ML tools into digital twin architectures has significantly enhanced the
capabilities for modeling, control, and optimization of geothermal energy and other thermal energy
systems [77-80]. Recent advances have demonstrated the effectiveness of ML techniques, such as neural
networks, Gaussian process regression, and ensemble learning, for surrogate modeling of reservoir
behavior and wellbore thermo-hydraulics [77]. The primary objective of this section is to leverage state-
of-the-art AI/ML methods to design and develop generic digital twins for geothermal ORC systems that
enable high-fidelity emulation across a wide range of power generation scales.

5.1. Machine Learning Model for the Dynamic Behavior of the ORC
The scalability analysis focused on the ORC cycle, treating the solar/UTES loop as a thermal reservoir
rather than modeling the scaled UTES directly. This configuration enabled the ORC system to operate
dynamically, utilizing a constant heat supply from the geothermal well alongside variable thermal input
from the solar/UTES loop. The standard inputs and outputs of the dynamic ORC model used in this report
are represented in a simplified form in Figure 47. The model inputs consist of (i) time-series data,
specifically the reference electrical power to be produced by the ORC (𝑤net,set ), and (ii) a set of static
system configuration parameters, including the ORC power rating and the corresponding scaling factors
for the main components (pump, expander, evaporator, and condenser). The primary model output is the
actual dynamic net electrical power generated by the system (𝑤net ) expressed as time-series data.

 Figure 47. Inputs and outputs for geothermal ORC AI/ML model.

 59

For the ML model developed in this work, multiple neural-network architectures are employed to capture
both the dynamic and static system behaviors. As shown in Figure 48, the proposed architecture consists
of two parallel branches: a long short-term memory (LSTM) branch that processes time-series inputs and
a multilayer perceptron (MLP) branch that handles fixed system parameters. These branches are merged
in the final layers of the network to predict the dynamic response of the ORC system. The ML model is
trained using data generated by the physics-based model integrated into the DRTS environment as
described in Section 3.

 Figure 48. Schematic diagram of the geothermal ML model architecture.

LSTM Branch
This branch processes multivariate time-series data to capture temporal dynamics and system response
history, accounting for time-dependent ORC variables such as expander inlet pressure, working-fluid
temperature, and the resulting net power output. The LSTM network is a type of recurrent neural network
(RNN) specifically designed to model sequential and time-dependent data. Unlike conventional RNNs,
LSTMs can learn long-term dependencies without suffering from vanishing or exploding gradient issues,
making them particularly effective for representing temporal dynamics. By capturing temporal patterns in
sequential data, LSTMs are well suited for applications such as system identification, predictive control,
and dynamic modeling of geothermal ORC systems, where system outputs depend on both current and
historical operating conditions. However, compared to simpler architectures such as multilayer
perceptrons (MLPs), LSTMs are computationally more demanding and typically require careful
hyperparameter tuning to achieve optimal performance. As observed in Figure 48, an LSTM network with
32 units has been used, followed by a dense layer of 16 nodes, which comprises the LSTM branch
processing the time-series input.

 60

MLP Branch
This branch processes fixed system parameters, accounting for variables such as pump and turbine
efficiencies, evaporator heat-transfer area, working-fluid molecular mass, pump speed, and geothermal
brine enthalpy, which influence system behavior but do not vary rapidly over time. In this manner, it
captures the effects of static configuration parameters that define the steady-state operating points of the
system. As shown in Figure 48, the MLP branch consists of two concatenated dense layers with 16 and 8
nodes, respectively.

Branch Merging
The outputs of the two branches are merged through concatenation and subsequently passed to a set of
fully connected dense layers to predict the target system output, namely the net power output. As shown
in Figure 48, the concatenated features are fed into two dense layers with 64 and 32 nodes, respectively,
followed by a single-node output layer.

Data Collection and Preprocessing
The ML model was trained using the mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function and the Adam
optimizer. Early stopping and validation splits were employed to mitigate overfitting. During training, the
model output is compared with the desired ORC response to compute the loss. The training data were
generated from high-fidelity simulations of geothermal ORC systems, with each data sample representing
a short transient operating episode (e.g., start-up or load change) and labeled with the corresponding
system output, such as net electrical power.
Four geothermal ORC plant configurations with rated capacities of 1, 25, 50, and 100 MW were
considered. Each configuration was subjected to a range of step changes in net load to create a
comprehensive training dataset that spans diverse dynamic operating conditions. Representative net power
output cases included in the dataset are illustrated in Figure 49.

6.2 Financial Model
The workflow for the financial model is shown in Figure 52. This model was implemented in Excel to
assess the techno-economic performance of solar thermal systems with varying levels of thermal energy
storage, benchmarked against a standalone geothermal power option. The model supports analysis across
a range of system sizes and provides insights into optimal configurations from both cost and performance
perspectives. It enables the evaluation of key system components, including solar field sizing, capital
costs, storage integration, and power output, all of which are critical to hybrid energy system design.
Solar field sizing constitutes the first step in the modeling workflow. The model evaluates solar multiple
configurations ranging from approximately 1.4 to 2.2, where each incremental increase represents a larger
solar collector area and higher thermal capacity. For each system size, the corresponding thermal output,
expressed in MWt, is calculated for cases with and without thermal energy storage. Storage durations of
8, 12, and 24 hours are considered. These sizing results form the basis for subsequent cost and performance
calculations. Solar field costs are estimated using both area-based and capacity-based unit cost
assumptions. Site improvement costs are assumed to be 15 USD/m², solar collector field costs are set at
170 USD/m², and HTF (pressurized water) is estimated at 42 USD/m². Additional indirect costs include a

 72

15% markup for engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC), a 7% contingency, and a land cost of
10,000 USD per acre. These assumptions are applied to estimate the total installed cost of the solar
subsystem for each scenario.

Figure 52. Financial model workflow for calculating solar thermal LCOE across a range of solar multiples
 (1.0–3.4) and thermal storage durations (8–24 hours), incorporating solar field, thermal storage, and
 power block cost components. Geothermal LCOE values are derived from the 2024 GETEM–ATB
 framework for system capacities of 1, 25, 50, and 100 MW.

Thermal energy storage costs are incorporated into the model for the 8-, 12-, and 24-hour storage
configurations. Direct capital costs for thermal storage are assumed to be 2 USD per kilowatt-hour thermal
(kWht), while annual O&M costs are modeled as 2% of the direct capital cost. The inclusion of thermal
storage enhances the dispatchability of solar generation and has a significant influence on LCOE,
particularly at larger system scales. Power block costs are also included in the techno-economic model.
Direct capital costs for the power conversion system are assumed to be 880 USD per kilowatt, with
balance-of-plant (BOP) costs estimated at 97.34 USD per kilowatt. Annual O&M costs for the power
block are modeled as 2% of the direct capital cost. These power system costs are applied across all system
configurations, including solar-only and solar-plus-storage scenarios.
The model includes a geothermal benchmark, with LCOE values calculated for system capacities of 1, 25,
and 100 MW. Although the geothermal cost is treated as static in the current version of the model, the
framework is designed to incorporate a dynamic geothermal techno-economic sub-model in future
iterations. Such an extension would allow for the inclusion of site-specific parameters, including well
costs, flow rates, reservoir temperatures, and power conversion efficiencies.

 73

A techno-economic framework employing a FCR of 6% is used to annualize capital costs and calculate
the LCOE for each system configuration. Both fixed and variable O&M costs are incorporated using unit
cost values expressed on a per–kilowatt-year basis. Model outputs include LCOE (USD/MWh), thermal
capacity (MWt), and electrical capacity (MW) for each configuration. The model also evaluates relative
cost performance by comparing solar-based configurations against the geothermal benchmark, thereby
identifying the system scales and storage durations at which solar systems become cost-competitive. In
addition, the framework supports the assessment of hybrid integration opportunities by enabling side-by-
side comparisons of solar-only and solar-plus-storage systems. As system capacity and storage duration
increase, the LCOE of solar configurations generally decreases and, in many cases, falls below geothermal
costs. These results highlight promising opportunities for hybrid systems in which geothermal provides
stable baseload generation while solar thermal systems with energy storage contribute dispatchable and
flexible capacity. The modular structure of the model further enables expanded scenario analysis and
integration into broader hybrid energy system designs.

6.3 Technoeconomic Assessment
As noted previously, the thermal power generation calculations were performed using 2022 TMY data for

solar multiples, including 1.0, 1.4, 1.8, 2.2, 2.6, and 3.4. At a solar multiple of 1.0, the thermal output was
assumed to meet instantaneous demand, with no thermal energy storage employed. For solar multiples
greater than 1.0, the excess thermal energy generated beyond the plant’s baseline thermal capacity was
directed to thermal energy storage. Of the stored energy, 40% was assumed to be recoverable and available
for dispatch under three operational scenarios corresponding to 8-hour, 12-hour, and 24-hour discharge
durations.
The cost of implementing the BTES system was estimated for a range of solar contribution levels,
assuming a reference system capacity of 100 MW. Solar shares of 50%, 25%, 15%, and 3% were

capacities of 1, 25, 50, and 100 MW were calculated proportionally. Table 11 summarizes the estimated

The LCOE range of 68–180 USD/MWh obtained for the 50 MW system in this study is broadly consistent
with the 50–176 USD/MWh reported by McTigue et al. [87,88] for surface tank thermal energy storage
systems. However, the two approaches exhibit contrasting cost trends as storage capacity increases. In
prior studies, LCOE increases with storage duration because additional tanks must be procured and
installed to accommodate larger thermal inventories. In contrast, the borehole-based system examined
here benefits from a fundamentally different scaling mechanism: rather than requiring additional
infrastructure, greater storage capacity is achieved by progressively enlarging the radius of thermal
influence in the subsurface. This increases the effective storage volume without proportional cost
escalation, rendering the borehole system increasingly cost-competitive at longer storage durations, a key
economic advantage over conventional surface tanks.
However, this advantage is not universally realized across all system scales. For the 1 MW system, this
favorable scaling diminishes because drilling costs and well utilization do not scale linearly with capacity.
At smaller scales, the fixed costs associated with borehole installation represent a disproportionately large
share of total expenditure, offsetting the volumetric benefits that make larger systems economically
attractive.

7 Final Remarks
7.1 Conclusions and Lessons Learned
This report presents the outcomes of the DOE-OG–funded project “EXERGETIC – De-risking Next
Generation Resilient Geothermal Hybrids via at-Scale Evaluation Using a Virtual Emulation Digital Twin
Environment for Efficient Operation.” A digital geothermal co-emulation environment, EXERGETIC,
was developed, validated, and implemented at NLR’s ARIES platform to evaluate the performance and
grid benefits potential of geothermal hybrid systems using high-fidelity modeling, real-time digital
emulation, and advanced control strategies. A machine-learning-based scalability analysis was also
conducted to evaluate and predict the system response at different power generation capacities. In addition,
a TEA analysis based on the LCOE was carried out to evaluate the economic viability of geothermal
hybrid systems for different geothermal plant capacities, solar shares, solar multiples, and storage
durations.
The project successfully demonstrated that hybrid geothermal systems, integrating geothermal power
plants with CSP plants and underground thermal energy storage, can significantly enhance the
performance, flexibility, and economic competitiveness of geothermal energy [89]. In particular, thermal
energy from solar and/or UTES can additionally increase the evaporation temperature in the ORC of the
geothermal plant, enabling significant improvements in efficiency. For instance, for the 1 MW geothermal
plant analyzed in this project, efficiency rises from about 4% to 11% when the evaporation temperature
increases from 70 °C to 120 °C. In addition, higher energy contributions from the solar/UTES field
decrease the utilization of geothermal energy for the same power output, preserving the geothermal
resource and extending the productive life of geothermal wells, while improving the long-term
sustainability and economics of geothermal power generation. It was found that a 30% solar/UTES
contribution leads to geothermal resource savings of approximately 50% to 70%, depending on the
temperature of the solar/UTES resource.
The development of EXERGETIC extended NLR’s ARIES capabilities and enabled a realistic emulation
of grid-connected operation and direct assessment of grid services provided by geothermal hybrid
technologies. Results demonstrated that geothermal hybrid systems have the potential to provide load-
following capability, voltage support, and frequency response at both distribution and transmission levels.
Coordinated control of geothermal, solar, and thermal storage subsystems allows the hybrid plant to
respond effectively to grid dynamics while maintaining stable and efficient operation. ARIES plays a
critical role in accelerating the deployment of geothermal technologies as reliable, flexible, and resilient
assets within future power systems

The TEA analysis highlights the importance of system scale, solar contribution, and thermal storage in
determining economic performance. Economies of scale significantly reduce the LCOE for medium- and
large-scale systems (25 to 100 MW) and hybridization with CSP and underground TES further improves
cost competitiveness by increasing capacity utilization and dispatchability. Results indicate that optimal
solar integration is achieved at solar multiples of 1.4 and 1.8, with higher multiples requiring extended

 79

thermal storage to reduce LCOE, highlighting the importance of storage in hybrid systems. Adding 8 hours
of thermal energy storage provides the largest LCOE reduction across capacities, with diminishing
benefits for longer durations. Overall, geothermal–solar/BTES integration is technically feasible at SMs
of 1.4 and 1.8, with increasing benefits realized under enhanced underground thermal storage.

In summary, EXERGETIC project demonstrates that geothermal hybrid systems (supported by high-
fidelity digital twins and real-time co-emulation) represent a promising pathway for enhancing geothermal
energy’s role in the electric grid and microgrids. The lessons learned from this work provide guidance for
future research, technology development, and demonstration efforts aimed at accelerating the deployment
of next-generation geothermal hybrid systems.

7.2 Recommendations for Future Work
Building on the results and lessons learned from the EXERGETIC project, the following research areas
have been identified and are recommended to further advance geothermal hybrid systems and accelerate
their development and deployment as flexible, resilient, and cost-competitive power generation
technologies:
 - Future work should couple the developed digital twins to subsurface geothermal resource
 simulation platforms, including simulators for next generation and hydrothermal systems as well
 as underground energy storage. Coupling the subsurface and surface models is important to
 evaluate hybridization benefits with other power generation technologies such as nuclear or natural
 gas and multiple resource types and temperature regimes. In addition, machine learning
 frameworks can be applied to subsurface and surface simulators to evaluate and optimize
 operational strategies.
 - The PID-based control architecture used in this project was effective for hybrid operation;
 however, future efforts should explore advanced control approaches, including MPC, adaptive
 controls, and AI-assisted supervisory control. These methods could further improve system
 efficiency, enhance responsiveness, and optimize the use of geothermal, solar, and storage
 resources under variable and challenging grid conditions.
 - Despite the geothermal digital real-time co-emulation environment providing a powerful tool to
 evaluate the system performance and dynamic response under grid-connected conditions, future
 work should pursue pilot-scale and field demonstrations of geothermal hybrid systems, which
 could leverage NLR’s ARIES capacities. For instance, integrating physical components (thermal
 storage, power electronics, advanced controllers, and ORC) into the ARIES platform using a HIL
 and Power HIL approaches would increase the accuracy in the system representation and further
 validate model predictions and control strategies under real-world conditions. In addition, ARIES
 platform can be used to evaluate the storage capacity of underground reservoirs across diverse
 geological conditions and locations.

 80

- Complementary analyses such as lifecycle assessment (LCA) that complement TEA and account
 for lifecycle emissions, water use, land requirements, and induced environmental impacts are
 recommended to provide a more comprehensive assessment of geothermal hybrid systems.
- Future work should focus on improving predictive accuracy by further integrating physics-based
 digital twins with machine learning models. While current scalability results are promising, the
 largest prediction errors occur during sharp transient events with rapid demand changes,
 highlighting challenges in capturing nonlinear dynamics. Incorporating stronger physical
 constraints through hybrid modeling approaches or physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) is
 recommended to enhance model stability and generalization.
- Significant challenges remain in simulating and characterizing subsurface performance, as BTES
 has a low TRL (3–4) and UTES a moderate TRL (~6). Prior high-temperature thermal storage
 projects [4,81] highlighted key technical barriers, including the need for accurate subsurface
 characterization and management of geochemical effects such as scaling [90]. Future work should
 focus on detailed subsurface modeling, installation cost analysis, and evaluation of high-
 temperature materials under integrated system conditions.
