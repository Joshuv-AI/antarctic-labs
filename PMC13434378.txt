Microplastics in the Human Nasal Cavity and Upper Airway: Emerging Evidence and Rhinologic Implications
Authors: Hyun Jin Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs) are pervasive environmental contaminants with potential implications for human health. Although inhalation is considered a major route of exposure, most investigations have focused on the lower respiratory tract, leaving the upper airway relatively understudied. Recent studies have detected MPs in nasal lavage fluid, specimens from patients with allergic rhinitis, tissue from patients with chronic rhinosinusitis, and the olfactory bulb of the human brain. Concerns have also been raised that synthetic polymer-based medical devices, including nasal irrigation bottles and continuous positive airway pressure systems, may contribute to upper airway exposure. This review summarizes current evidence on the detection and characterization of MPs in the human nasal cavity and upper airway, discusses environmental and medical device-related exposure pathways, and examines emerging implications for rhinologic diseases. Methodological limitations and priorities for future research are also reviewed.

INTRODUCTION

Microplastics (MPs), generally defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, are globally distributed environmental pollutants that have been detected in air, water, food, and biological tissues [1,2]. Evidence indicates that inhalation is a major route of human exposure to airborne MPs [3]. Airborne MPs originate from sources such as synthetic textiles, urban dust, tire wear particles, industrial emissions, and the degradation of plastic-containing materials [4].

Compared with the gastrointestinal tract and lower respiratory system, the upper airway has received relatively limited attention, although it is the first anatomical interface for inhaled particles. The nasal cavity performs essential physiological functions, including particle filtration, mucociliary clearance, humidification, and immune surveillance [5]. Because many airborne MPs are fibrous and fall within respirable size ranges, deposition in the nasal cavity is biologically plausible.

Recent studies have identified MPs in nasal irrigation fluids, sinonasal disease specimens, and olfactory structures [6–9]. Concerns have also been raised regarding secondary exposure from medical devices, including nasal irrigation bottles and continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) systems [10,11]. Experimental studies have shown that MP exposure can induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in several models [12, 13]. However, whether these findings are directly applicable to human sinonasal disease remains uncertain because clinical evidence is still limited.

Despite growing interest in the health effects of MPs, their presence and potential implications in the upper airway remain incompletely understood. SOURCES OF UPPER AIRWAY MP EXPOSURE

Environmental exposure

Airborne MPs have been detected in indoor and outdoor environments [14]. Indoor exposure may be particularly relevant because people spend much of their time indoors, where synthetic fibers can accumulate [15]. Common airborne polymers include polypropylene, polyethylene, polyethylene terephthalate, and polystyrene.

Face masks

The widespread use of disposable face masks during the COVID-19 pandemic raised concerns about inhalational exposure to MPs and nanoplastics released from synthetic materials. Sullivan et al. [16] showed that disposable face masks may release plastic particles under mechanical and environmental stressors, including stretching, folding, friction, repeated handling, ultraviolet exposure, and prolonged environmental degradation. These stressors may degrade polypropylene-based mask materials and promote particle shedding. However, the study was conducted under experimental conditions that may not accurately reflect real-world mask use. In addition, the number of particles released does not necessarily correspond to the amount inhaled by users, and direct evidence linking mask-derived MPs to clinically meaningful upper airway exposure remains limited.

In contrast, Torres-Agullo et al. [8] reported that MP burden measured by nasal lavage was not significantly associated with face mask use. Although this study provided direct human exposure data, its cross-sectional design precluded assessment of cumulative exposure or causality. Together, the available evidence suggests that face masks may be a potential source of MPs, but their relative contribution compared with background environmental exposure remains uncertain.

Nasal irrigation bottles

Kim et al. [10] demonstrated measurable MP release from polypropylene nasal irrigation bottles after repeated use. This study identified a potentially overlooked source of iatrogenic exposure in rhinologic practice. However, the experimental design primarily quantified particle release from the device itself and did not assess actual sinonasal deposition or biological effects in human participants. Thus, although the findings suggest a potential exposure pathway, the clinical significance of device-derived MPs remains unclear.

CPAP devices

Mauad et al. [11] proposed that airflow turbulence, tubing degradation, and humidification systems may generate MPs that could be delivered directly to the upper airway. However, this evidence remains preliminary because MP deposition has not yet been directly measured in CPAP users. Additional studies incorporating quantitative exposure assessment and long-term clinical outcomes are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

CHARACTERIZATION AND NALYTICAL METHODS FOR MP IDENTIFICATION

Accurate identification and characterization of MPs in upper airway specimens remain technically challenging because of their small particle size, heterogeneous morphology, and high risk of environmental contamination. Current analytical approaches generally combine microscopic visualization with spectroscopic polymer identification techniques.

Micro-Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (μFTIR) is among the most commonly used methods for MP analysis because it enables chemical characterization of individual particles based on infrared absorption spectra [17]. μFTIR is particularly useful for identifying polymer composition in particles larger than approximately 10–20 μm and has been widely applied in environmental and biological MP studies. However, its spatial resolution may be limited for very small particles.

Raman spectroscopy is also increasingly used for MP characterization because it has higher spatial resolution than μFTIR and can identify smaller particles [17]. Raman-based analysis may therefore be advantageous for detecting fine particles in biological specimens. However, fluorescence interference and prolonged analysis time remain important limitations.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is frequently used to evaluate particle morphology, surface structure, and size distribution because it provides high-resolution visualization of MP particles [17]. SEM may be combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy to obtain additional elemental information and to help distinguish plastic particles from inorganic materials [18,19]. However, SEM-based techniques alone cannot reliably determine polymer composition and are therefore commonly used with spectroscopic methods, such as μFTIR or Raman spectroscopy, for definitive polymer identification [17].

Pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) provides highly sensitive chemical characterization by thermally degrading polymer materials before chromatographic analysis [20,21]. This technique enables quantitative polymer analysis and may detect very small particles that are difficult to characterize microscopically [22]. Because Py-GC/MS analyzes polymer-specific pyrolysis products, it is particularly useful for detecting trace amounts of MPs in complex biological and environmental samples [21]. However, direct morphological evaluation is not possible because particles are thermally decomposed during analysis.

Despite advances in analytical technology, methodological standardization remains a major challenge in upper airway MP research. Airborne contamination during specimen collection, processing, and laboratory analysis may substantially influence study results. Because synthetic fibers are ubiquitous in clinical and laboratory environments, strict contamination-control procedures, including filtered reagents, procedural blank controls, and non-synthetic laboratory materials, are essential for improving reliability and reproducibility. Variability in particle classification criteria and reporting standards further limits direct comparison across available studies.

MPs IN THE NASAL CAVITY

Studies have detected MP in human nasal lavage and irrigation samples, suggesting that the nasal cavity may be an important site of airborne MP deposition. Kim et al. [6] provided direct evidence of MPs in nasal irrigation fluids and demonstrated the feasibility of nasal lavage-based assessment for evaluating upper airway exposure. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because later studies showed that polypropylene nasal irrigation bottles can release MPs after repeated use [10]. Therefore, the possibility that some detected particles originated from irrigation devices rather than the nasal cavity cannot be excluded. In their study, fibrous particles were the predominant morphology, and multiple polymer types were identified, supporting the hypothesis that inhaled airborne MPs can be deposited in the sinonasal cavity.

Similar findings have been reported in studies that used nasal lavage to evaluate inhalational exposure, further supporting the possibility that the upper airway reflects chronic environmental exposure to airborne MPs [8]. Because the nasal cavity is the first filtration barrier of the respiratory tract, deposited particles may interact directly with the mucus layer, epithelial surface, and mucociliary clearance system before entering the lower airway. Repeated exposure to inhaled MPs may result in particle deposition in the sinonasal cavity; however, the biological significance and long-term consequences of this deposition remain largely unknown.

In addition to nasal lavage-based investigations, studies have identified MPs in patients with inflammatory upper airway diseases, including allergic rhinitis and chronic rhinosinusitis [9,13]. Although the evidence remains preliminary, these findings suggest a possible association between MP exposure and inflammatory sinonasal conditions. However, available studies are insufficient to establish causality, and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

MPs AND THE OLFACTORY PATHWAY

One of the most concerning recent findings is the detection of MPs in the olfactory bulb of the human brain. Amato-Lourenço et al. [7] reported MPs in human olfactory bulb specimens, raising the possibility of translocation through the olfactory pathway.

The olfactory epithelium provides direct anatomical continuity between the nasal cavity and the central nervous system through olfactory sensory neurons that traverse the cribriform plate. Ultrafine particles may therefore bypass traditional blood-brain barrier mechanisms.

Although the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain, they identify a potential exposure pathway to the central nervous system. Evidence linking MP deposition to olfactory dysfunction or neurotoxicity in humans remains limited.

ROLE OF MPs IN RHINOLOGIC DISEASES

Allergic rhinitis

Xue et al. [9] conducted a preliminary investigation of MPs in patients with allergic rhinitis. Several mechanisms, including oxidative stress, epithelial irritation, and altered cytokine expression, have been proposed as pathways through which MPs might influence airway inflammation. However, direct evidence supporting a role for MPs in the pathogenesis of allergic rhinitis remains limited. Experimental studies have demonstrated increased inflammatory responses after MP exposure, including elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α [12]. Although these findings suggest that MPs may induce inflammatory reactions, they do not establish a specific role in allergic rhinitis pathogenesis. Therefore, the potential relationship between MP exposure and allergic rhinitis remains speculative, and direct clinical evidence supporting this association is currently limited.

Chronic rhinosinusitis

Taş et al. [13] investigated the potential role of MPs in chronic rhinosinusitis without nasal polyps. Proposed mechanisms include epithelial barrier dysfunction, impaired mucociliary clearance, and chronic inflammatory activation. Impaired mucociliary clearance is of particular interest because effective mucus transport is a key defense mechanism of the sinonasal mucosa. Experimental studies suggest that particle deposition on respiratory epithelial surfaces may interfere with normal mucus transport and epithelial function; however, direct evidence of MP-induced mucociliary dysfunction in human sinonasal tissues remains limited. Similarly, although epithelial barrier disruption and inflammatory activation have been proposed as possible consequences of MP exposure, these mechanisms have not been conclusively demonstrated in human chronic rhinosinusitis.

Because chronic rhinosinusitis is a multifactorial disease involving environmental and host factors, MPs are unlikely to be an isolated causative factor. However, current evidence is insufficient to determine whether MPs have a direct pathogenic role or instead serve as markers of environmental exposure.

METHODOLOGICAL LIMITATIONS

Evidence on MPs in the nasal cavity and upper airway remains limited by substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies. A major challenge is the lack of standardized sampling and analytical protocols for upper airway specimens. Differences in nasal lavage techniques, specimen processing, particle size thresholds, and polymer identification methods make direct comparisons between studies difficult.

Another important limitation is the high risk of environmental contamination during specimen collection and laboratory analysis. Because airborne synthetic fibers are ubiquitous in clinical and laboratory environments, contamination may substantially influence measured MP burden. Accordingly, strict contamination-control procedures, including filtered reagents, procedural blanks, and non-synthetic laboratory materials, are essential for improving reliability and reproducibility.

A further challenge specific to upper airway MP research is the potential contribution of sampling devices to measured particle burden. Studies have demonstrated measurable MP release from polypropylene nasal irrigation bottles [10], raising concerns about the interpretation of findings from nasal lavage specimens. Future investigations should therefore incorporate device-control experiments and standardized contamination-control protocols to determine the extent to which detected particles originate from the nasal cavity rather than collection equipment.

Most available investigations are exploratory or observational studies with relatively small sample sizes. Longitudinal data evaluating chronic exposure and disease progression also remain scarce. Consequently, causal relationships between MP exposure and rhinologic diseases cannot yet be established.

Large-scale studies using standardized methodologies are needed to clarify the clinical significance of upper airway MP exposure.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Future investigations should establish standardized protocols for collecting, processing, and analyzing upper airway specimens in MP research. Reproducible nasal sampling techniques and contamination-control strategies will be essential for improving interstudy comparability and analytical reliability.

Longitudinal clinical studies are also needed to evaluate the relationship between chronic MP exposure and the development or progression of rhinologic diseases. In particular, future research should examine whether persistent exposure contributes to epithelial dysfunction, impaired mucociliary clearance, or chronic sinonasal inflammation in susceptible individuals.

Pediatric populations also warrant attention because children may be more vulnerable to inhaled environmental contaminants owing to developmental and behavioral factors. In addition, possible olfactory pathway-mediated translocation into the central nervous system warrants further investigation, particularly with respect to potential neurotoxicity associated with chronic exposure.

The clinical significance of medical device-associated exposure also requires clarification. Because nasal irrigation bottles and CPAP devices are widely used in rhinologic and sleep medicine practice, quantitative assessment of device-derived MP exposure may become increasingly important.

Multidisciplinary collaboration among rhinologists, toxicologists, environmental scientists, and analytical chemists will be needed to clarify the clinical implications of upper airway MP exposure.

CONCLUSION

Emerging evidence suggests that the human nasal cavity and upper airway are important sites of MP exposure and deposition. MPs have been detected in nasal lavage samples, sinonasal disease specimens, and the olfactory bulb of the human brain. Environmental inhalation, disposable face masks, nasal irrigation devices, and CPAP systems may all contribute to upper airway exposure.

Although evidence remains preliminary, MPs have been detected in several upper airway specimens, and their biological significance remains incompletely understood. Current evidence is insufficient to establish causal relationships between MP exposure and rhinologic diseases. Future studies incorporating standardized methodologies, longitudinal clinical data, and mechanistic investigations are needed to determine whether MP exposure has clinically meaningful effects on upper airway health and disease.
