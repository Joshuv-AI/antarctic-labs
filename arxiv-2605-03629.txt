Adversarial Effects on Expressibility and Trainability in Distributed Variational Quantum Algorithms
Abhishek Sadhu, Sharu Theresa Jose

Abstract
Distributed quantum algorithms offer a promising pathway to scale variational quantum algorithms beyond the constraints of noisy intermediate-scale quantum hardware. However, existing approaches implicitly assume a trusted entanglement-sharing layer across quantum processors. We show that this assumption introduces a fundamental vulnerability: adversarial perturbations of shared entanglement induce structured gate-level noise that directly impacts quantum learning. We develop a framework that maps entanglement-level perturbations to gate-level noise via an explicit Kraus representation. To quantify their impact, we introduce Kraus expressibility, a metric that generalizes unitary expressibility to noisy quantum channels. We then establish a trade-off between Kraus expressibility and trainability of noisy quantum circuits through gradient variance analysis. Our analysis reveals that an adversary can manipulate Kraus expressibility to maintain sufficiently large cost gradients (avoiding barren plateaus) while systematically biasing optimization toward incorrect solutions. We validate these findings through numerical simulations, demonstrating adversarial degradation of expressibility and trainability.

Distributed VQAs: Background and Problem Setup

This section introduces distributed VQAs and formulates the adversarial framework under study.

2.1
Variational Quantum Algorithms

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)
50
;
9
are hybrid quantum-classical algorithms built on parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs). A PQC defines a parameterized unitary channel
\mathcal{U}(\vec{\theta})(\cdot)=U(\vec{\theta})(\cdot)U(\vec{\theta})^{{\dagger}}
, via the unitary operator
U(\vec{\theta})
, which acts on an initial
n
-qubit state
\rho_{0}
to produce the output state
\rho(\vec{\theta})=\mathcal{U}(\vec{\theta})(\rho_{0})
. The state is evaluated using a problem-specific Hermitian observable
\mathcal{H}
, yielding the cost
C_{\rho(\vec{\theta}),\mathcal{H}}={\rm Tr}(\mathcal{H}\rho(\vec{\theta})),
where
{\rm Tr}(\cdot)
denotes the trace operation. The goal of VQA is to find the model parameter
\vec{\theta}\in\Theta
that minimizes the cost function. This is typically done via classical optimizers using quantum-estimated costs or gradients.

A critical design choice in VQAs is the selection of circuit
ansatz
implementing the channel
\mathcal{U}(\vec{\theta})(\cdot)
. Typically, this is expressed as a composition of
L
-layers of parameterized unitary channels:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\vec{\theta}}=\mathcal{U}_{L}(\vec{\theta}_{L})\circ\mathcal{U}_{L-1}(\vec{\theta}_{L-1})\ldots\circ\mathcal{U}_{1}(\vec{\theta}_{1}),

(1)

with
\vec{\theta}=(\vec{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\vec{\theta}_{L})
and
\mathcal{U}_{l}(\vec{\theta}_{l})(\cdot)=U_{l}(\vec{\theta}_{l})W_{l}(\cdot)W_{l}^{{\dagger}}U_{l}(\vec{\theta}_{l})^{{\dagger}}
consisting of fixed unitaries
W_{l}
(e.g., CNOT gates), and parameterized rotations
U_{l}(\vec{\theta}_{l})=\exp(-i\thetavec_lV_l)
generated by Hermitian operators
V_{l}
. The ansatz defines the hypothesis class explored by the VQA and determines both expressivity and trainability.
Common choices include the quantum alternative operator ansatz
20
, the coupled cluster ansatz
2
and the hardware efficient ansatz
37
.

The VQA formulation above assumes a single
n
-qubit device, limiting scalability. For large-scale problems, the required qubit count often exceeds current hardware capabilities. A natural approach is to distribute computation across multiple quantum processors, enabling the effective simulation of larger systems. We next outline a framework for distributed VQAs.

2.2
Distributed VQAs via the Cat-Entangler/Cat-Disentangler Protocol

Distributed VQAs involve partitioning PQCs across multiple QPUs and coordinating their execution. A central challenge in this setting is the implementation of non-local, multi-qubit operations (e.g., CNOT gates) across different QPUs.
This is typically achieved via distributed protocols that leverage classical communication together with pre-shared entanglement between QPUs
4
;
19
. In this work, we focus on the
cat-entangler/cat-disentangler
protocol

77
, which realizes a non-local CNOT gate with the control and target qubits located on different QPUs, using a single shared entangled Bell pair and two bits of classical communication. We briefly describe this protocol next.

Consider a setting where the control qubit
q_{c}
resides on QPU A and the target qubit
q_{t}
on QPU B. Let
q_{A}
and
q_{B}
denote the two halves of a shared Bell entangled-state pair,

|q_{AB}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)
, located on QPU A and B, respectively. The protocol proceeds in three steps:

1. Cat-entangler circuit
: Applies a local CNOT gate on QPU A with
q_{c}
controlling the qubit
q_{A}
,
followed by measurement of
q_{A}
. If the measurement outcome is
1
, a Pauli-
X
gate (denoted
\sigma_{X}
) is applied to
q_{B}
. This creates a CAT-like entangled state,
\alpha|00\rangle+\beta|11\rangle
, between
q_{c}
and
q_{B}
, effectively transferring the control information to
q_{B}
on QPU B.

2. A local CNOT gate
is applied on QPU B with
q_{B}
as the control and
q_{t}
as the target, realizing the desired non-local operation albeit using the entangled qubit
q_{B}
.

3. Cat-disentangler circuit:

Restores the original control qubit by applying a Hadamard gate on qubit
q_{B}
followed by measurement. If the outcome is
1
, a Pauli-
Z
gate (denoted
\sigma_{Z}
) is applied to
q_{c}
.

Using such non-local gate implementations, distributed VQAs execute PQCs across multiple QPUs in a coordinated manner. Each QPU performs local circuit evaluations and measures its qubits to obtain partial estimates of the cost objective, which are are communicated to a central classical processor. The processor aggregates them to evaluate the cost function (or its gradients) and update the parameters, which are then broadcast back to the QPUs for the next optimization step.

2.3
Adversarial Attacks on Distributed VQAs

Distributed VQAs based on the cat-entangler/cat-disentangler protocol introduce new attack surfaces at the level of inter-device entanglement. 3
Adversarial Attacks and Quantum Channel Equivalence

We now characterize the effect of
strong adversarial
perturbations on non-local CNOT implementations. In particular, we show that perturbations to the shared entangled state in the cat-entangler/cat-disentangler protocol induce an equivalent noisy quantum channel acting on the local qubits.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the cat-entangler/cat-disentangler protocol for implementing a non-local CNOT gate between control qubit
q_{c}
(QPU A) and target qubit
q_{t}
(QPU B). Suppose a strong adversary perturbs the Bell-entangled state pair as in Eq. (
2

\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{p}}(\cdot)=\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}E_{ij}(\cdot)E_{ij}^{{\dagger}}
with parameters
\mathbf{p}=(c_{00},c_{01},c_{10},c_{11})
and the following Kraus operators:

\displaystyle E_{00}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{00}\mathbbm{1}+c_{01}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{10}\mathbbm{1}+c_{11}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}},

(3)

\displaystyle E_{01}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{00}\mathbbm{1}-c_{01}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{10}\mathbbm{1}-c_{11}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}},

(4)

\displaystyle E_{10}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{11}\mathbbm{1}+{c_{10}}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{01}\mathbbm{1}+c_{00}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}},

(5)

\displaystyle E_{11}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{11}\mathbbm{1}-{c_{10}}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes(c_{01}\mathbbm{1}-c_{00}\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}.

(6)

Proposition 1 establishes that adversarial perturbations at the level of shared entanglement translate directly into structured noise at the level of non-local gate operations. In fact, the resulting noisy channel
\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{p}}(\cdot)
depends explicitly on the perturbation parameters
\mathbf{p}
providing a principled mapping from entanglement-level attacks to gate-level noise. Detailed proof can be found in Appendix

We now consider two special cases. In the ideal adversary-free setting, where
c_{00}=1/\sqrt{2}=c_{11}
and
c_{01}=0=c_{10}
, all Kraus operators coincide with
E_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}U_{\rm CNOT}
, for all
i,j
, where
U_{\rm CNOT}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{q_{t}}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes(\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}

\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{p}}
reduces to the ideal unitary CNOT channel
\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{p}}(\cdot)=U_{\rm CNOT}(\cdot)U_{\rm CNOT}^{{\dagger}}.
In contrast, when
c_{01}=1/\sqrt{2}=c_{10}
and
c_{00}=0=c_{11}
, we get
E_{00}=E_{10}=\frac{1}{2}U_{\rm flip}
and
E_{01}=E_{11}=-\frac{1}{2}U_{\rm flip}
, where
U_{\rm flip}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{q_{c}}\otimes(\sigma_{X})_{q_{t}}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{q_{c}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{q_{t}}

4
Kraus Expressibility for Noisy Quantum Circuits

As discussed in Section
2.1
, the success of VQAs depends critically on the choice of the sequence of transformations applied to the initial state. In standard VQAs, these are parameterized unitary channels (see Eq. (
1
)). However, in the presence of adversarially-induced noise, the effective transformations are no longer unitary, but quantum channels. To capture this, we generalize the standard unitary-ansatz to a parameterized quantum channel
(PQCh)-ansatz
.

We define a PQCh-ansatz of depth
L
as a composition of
L
quantum channels:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}=\mathcal{K}_{L}(\mathbf{p}_{L},\vec{\theta}_{L})\circ\mathcal{K}_{L-1}(\mathbf{p}_{L-1},\vec{\theta}_{L-1})\circ...\circ\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\vec{\theta}_{1}),

(7)

where
\mathbf{p}_{j}
and
\vec{\theta}_{j}
denote the noise and trainable parameters of the
j
-th channel
\mathcal{K}_{j}
, respectively.
When all
\mathcal{K}_{j}
’s collapse to noise-free unitary channels, the PQCh-ansatz reduces to the standard ansatz in Eq. (
1
). For fixed noise parameter
\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{p_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{L})
, varying
\vec{\theta}
induces an ensemble
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{p}}=\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{p}}_{\vec{\theta}}:\vec{\theta}\in\Theta\}
of fixed-noise trainable channels. Further variation over noise parameters
\mathbf{p}\in\Pi
yields the full ensemble
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{p}}:\mathbf{p}\in\Pi\}
of noisy trainable transformations.

VQA success requires that the target solution to a problem lies within the ansatz-induced ensemble
\mathcal{T}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}\neq\varnothing
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the set of solution transformations. In practice,
\mathcal{S}
is unknown, making this condition difficult to verify. A common design principle, widely adopted in problem-agnostic settings, is therefore to ensure that the ansatz explores the transformation space broadly and uniformly, increasing the likelihood of approximating the optimal solution. This property is known as
expressibility

65
;
51
;
29
.

In the adversarial setting, the adversarially-induced noise can distort this exploration and drive the PQCh-ansatz away from the optimal transformation. Consequently, even if the noiseless ansatz can cover the solution transformation, the adversarially-perturbed PQCh-ansatz may not. This motivates a notion of expressibility that explicitly accounts for noise and adversarial effects. We formalize this via
Kraus expressibility
, which quantifies how well noisy quantum circuits explore the space of ideal unitary transformations (see Fig.
1
(\text{b})
for an illustration).

Definition 1
.

The Kraus expressibility of the PQCh-ansatz is defined via the following super-operator,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(\cdot)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)V^{\otimes t}(\cdot)(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}-\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1}...k_{l}}(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j)})~(\cdot)~(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j){\dagger}}),

(8)

where
d\mu(V)
is the volume element of the Haar measure over the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(d)
with
d=2^{n}
,
d\nu(E)
is the volume element corresponding to uniform distribution over the PQCh-ensemble
\mathcal{T}
, and
E_{k}^{(j)}
denote the k-th Kraus operator of the PQCh-ansatz for the
j
-th copy.

Intuitively, Kraus expressibility quantifies how well the PQCh-ensemble
\mathcal{T}
uniformly explores the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(d)
. In particular, if
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(X)=0
for all operators
X
, then averaging over
\mathcal{T}
matches Haar averaging over
\mathcal{U}(d)
upto the
t
-th moment, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}
forms a
t
-design. In this work, we focus on
t=2
.
For VQA optimization, we evaluate the Kraus expressibility of
\mathcal{T}
with respect to the input state
\rho
via the norm

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{T}}^{\rho}\coloneqq||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}.

(9)

Smaller values of
\Delta_{\mathcal{T}}^{\rho}
indicate higher Kraus expressibility. This extends unitary expressibility
29
to noisy and adversarial settings.

Theorem 1
.

The Kraus expressibility norm of the PQCh-ansatz with respect to the state
\rho
is given by

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathcal{T}}^{\rho})^{2}=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})~d^{2}+2\alpha\beta d-2[\alpha+\beta\bar{\nu}]+\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}},

(10)

where
\alpha=\frac{d-\operatorname{Tr}\rho^{2}}{d(d^{2}-1)}
,
\beta=\frac{d\operatorname{Tr}\rho^{2}-1}{d(d^{2}-1)}
,
\bar{\nu}
is the ensemble averaged purity
2
2

2

A quantum state
\rho
is pure if
{\rm Tr}(\rho^{2})=1
and is mixed if
{\rm Tr}(\rho^{2})<1
.
of the input state
\rho
after evolving through the PQCh-ansatz, and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}}
is the noise fluctuation term defined as

\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}}=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\left[\sum_{j,k}\operatorname{Tr}(E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}F_{j}\rho F_{j}^{\dagger})\right]^{2}

with
\{E_{k}\}
and
\{F_{k}\}
representing Kraus operators corresponding to independent channel realizations sampled from

\mathcal{T}
.

Proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
. Theorem
1
shows that adversarial effects influence the Kraus expressibility through two distinct mechanisms:

(i)
Average state purity loss: As the state
\rho
evolving through the PQCh-ansatz becomes increasingly mixed, average state purity
\bar{\nu}
decreases, reducing the Kraus expressibility, and
(ii)
Diversity loss due to noise-induced correlations: The term
\mathcal{N}^{\rm noise}
captures the expected squared overlap between states evolved under independent channel realizations sampled from
\mathcal{T}
. Hence, high correlations across channel realizations in the ensemble (e.g., under strongly mixing noise such as depolarizing noise) reduces the diversity of the explored transformations, thereby lowering Kraus expressibility. In particular, in the high depolarizing noise regime (noise strength
\rightarrow 1
), outputs concentrate toward the maximally mixed state, leading to reduced purity and increased overlap across realizations. Both effects increase
(\Delta_{\mathcal{T}}^{\rho})^{2}
, thereby reducing expressibility.

Intuitively, a highly Kraus expressive PQCh-ensemble requires both high average purity (to preserve the quantum structure) and sufficiently diverse channel realizations (to explore the transformation space). Adversarial noise can disrupt either (or both), making the resulting ansatz less capable of approximating target solutions. Notably, even with negligible purity loss, strong noise-induced correlations can significantly degrade expressibility. In the absence of adversarial noise, these effects vanish, and Kraus expressibility reduces to standard unitary expressibility.

While Theorem
1
characterizes the influence of adversarial perturbations over the full space of noisy trainable transformations, practical VQA deployments often operate in a post-training (inference) regime, where circuit parameters are optimized offline and subsequently fixed. In this setting, the adversary acts
after training
,
and thus perturbs the induced channel via the noise parameter
\mathbf{p}
. The following corollary characterizes Kraus expressibility in this regime.

Corollary 1
.

When the PQCh-ensemble reduces to a composite channel
\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}
with fixed
(\mathbf{p},\vec{\theta})
, the Kraus expressibility norm with respect to the input state

7
Conclusion

Distributed implementations of VQAs rely critically on shared entanglement across QPUs, which is typically assumed to be secure. We show that this assumption introduces a fundamental vulnerability. We develop an adversarial framework that maps entanglement-level perturbations to gate-level noise via an explicit Kraus representation. To quantify the impact of such perturbations, we introduce Kraus expressibility, a metric that extends unitary expressibility to noisy and adversarial settings.
Our analysis establishes a principled connection between Kraus expressibility and trainability by bounding the variance of cost-function gradients. This reveals a key insight:
by balancing purity loss and noise-induced correlations, a stealthy adversary can avoid barren plateaus, by maintaining non-vanishing gradients, while systematically biasing the optimization towards incorrect solutions.
Numerical results corroborate this behavior and show that standard mitigation strategies, such as restricted parameter initialization and local cost functions, can fail under adversarial noise.

Limitations and future directions:
Our analysis benchmarks Kraus expressibility against Haar-random unitaries. Extending this framework to more general quantum channel ensembles, including open-system dynamics, is an important direction. While we consider controlled adversarial perturbations, evaluating the feasibility of such attacks under realistic hardware constraints (e.g., decoherence, entanglement purification), and exploring whether structured ansätze provide robustness, remain open problems. Our results also highlight the need for adversarially robust training and verification protocols for distributed quantum learning.

Appendix B
Generalized Expressivity of Quantum CPTP Circuits and Connection to Trainability

B.1
Expressivity with Kraus operators

The Kraus expressibility of the PQCh-ansatz which quantifies the ability of noisy quantum circuits to explore the space of ideal unitary transformations can be defined using the following superoperator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(\cdot)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)V^{\otimes t}(\cdot)(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}-\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1}...k_{l}}(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j)})~(\cdot)~(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j){\dagger}}),

(26)

where
d\mu(V)
is the volume element of the Haar measure over the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(d)
with
d=2^{n}
,
d\nu(E)
is the volume element corresponding to uniform distribution over the PQCh-ensemble
\mathcal{T}
, and
E_{k}^{(j)}
is the k-th Kraus operator of the PQCh-ansatz for the j-th copy. We start by analyzing the first term of Eq. (
26
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)V^{\otimes t}(\cdot)(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}:=\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\cdot).

(27)

Then, for any input operator
\rho\coloneqq\rho^{\otimes t}
, the trace of the above quantity evaluates as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)~\operatorname{Tr}[V^{\otimes t}(\rho)(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}]

(28)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)~\operatorname{Tr}\rho~\text{(cyclic property of trace)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\rho.

Additionally, the invariance property of the Haar measure yields that for any integrable function
f(\cdot)
and any unitary operator
V\in\mathcal{U}(d)
, the following relationship holds:

\displaystyle\int_{U}d\mu(U)f(U)=\int_{U}d\mu(U)f(UV)=\int_{U}d\mu(U)f(VU).

Using the above property, we then get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)V^{\otimes t}\rho(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}

(32)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)(WV)^{\otimes t}\rho((WV)^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}

\displaystyle=W^{\otimes t}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}d\mu(V)V^{\otimes t}\rho(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}

\displaystyle=W^{\otimes t}\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t},

thus for any unitary
W\in\mathcal{U}(d)
.
This, in turn, implies that
(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)=\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}
, whereby we have the commutator relation
[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t},\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)]=0
, or alternatively,
[\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho),W^{\otimes t}]=0
.

In the following, we aim to characterize
\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)
such that the commutator relation
[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes t},\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)]=0
holds for any unitary
W
.

Lemma 1
.

The operator
\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)=\alpha~\mathbbm{1}+\beta~\text{SWAP},\quad\mbox{where}

(33)

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{d\operatorname{Tr}\rho-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho~\text{SWAP}]}{d(d^{2}-1)},

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{d\operatorname{Tr}[\rho~\text{SWAP}]-\operatorname{Tr}\rho}{d(d^{2}-1)}.

(34)

Detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
B.3
.

Next, let us look at the second term of the superoperator
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(\rho)
in (
26
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{T}}(\rho^{\otimes t})=\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1}...k_{l}}(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j)})~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho^{\otimes t}}~(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j){\dagger}}).

(35)

Note that when writing the above evolution the ordering of the Kraus evolution should be kept in mind.

Subsequently, the expressibility superoperator (in the general noisy scenario) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(\rho^{\otimes t})

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho^{\otimes t})-\mathcal{G}^{t}_{\mathcal{T}}(\rho^{\otimes t})}

(36)

\displaystyle=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\alpha~\mathbbm{1}+\beta~\text{SWAP}}-\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1}...k_{l}}(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j)})~\rho^{\otimes t}~(\otimes_{j=1}^{t}E_{k}^{(j){\dagger}})}

(37)

\displaystyle=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{A}^{(t)}}-\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{M}^{(t)}}.

(38)

The following theorem then characterizes the squared-norm of the expressibility superoperator for
t=2
.

Theorem 3
.

The Kraus expressibility norm of the PQCh-ansatz with respect to the state
\rho
is given by

\displaystyle||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}^{2}=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})~d^{2}+2\alpha\beta d-2[\alpha+\beta\bar{\nu}]+\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}},

(39)

where
\alpha=\frac{d-\operatorname{Tr}\rho^{2}}{d(d^{2}-1)}
,
\beta=\frac{d\operatorname{Tr}\rho^{2}-1}{d(d^{2}-1)}
,
\bar{\nu}
is the ensemble averaged purity of the state
\rho
after evolving through the PQCh-ansatz, and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}}
is the noise fluctuation term defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\text{noise}}=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\left[\sum_{j,k}\operatorname{Tr}(E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}F_{j}\rho F_{j}^{\dagger})\right]^{2}

(40)

with
\{E_{k}\}
and
\{F_{k}\}
representing Kraus operators corresponding to independent channel realizations sampled from

\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})^{{\dagger}}~\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\mathcal{A}^{(2)}-\mathcal{M}^{(2)})^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}^{(2)}-\mathcal{M}^{(2)})]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{A}^{(2)2}]+\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2){\dagger}}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]-2\real\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{A}^{(2)}M^{(2)}]

where we have used the fact that
\operatorname{Tr}[X]+\operatorname{Tr}[X^{\dagger}]=2\operatorname{Tr}[X]
assuming
X
is hermitian. We next calculate each of the individual terms. We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{A}^{(2)2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\alpha\mathbbm{1}+\beta~\text{SWAP})(\alpha\mathbbm{1}+\beta~\text{SWAP})]

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\mathbbm{1}+\beta^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\text{SWAP}^{2}+2\alpha\beta\operatorname{Tr}\text{SWAP}

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})~d^{2}+2\alpha\beta d

where we have used the fact that
\text{SWAP}^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
. Next, we note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(E_{k_{1}}\otimes E_{k_{2}})~\rho^{\otimes 2}~(E_{k_{1}}\otimes E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\otimes(E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}[(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\otimes(E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})]

\displaystyle:=\xi^{(\rho)}.

Note that if
\rho
is a density matrix, then after the Kraus evolution it remains a valid density matrix and the summation goes to identity and we have
\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}=1
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{AM}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\alpha\mathbbm{1}+\beta~\text{SWAP})\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]

(42)

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]+\beta\operatorname{Tr}[\text{SWAP}~\mathcal{M}^{(2)}],

where

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\text{SWAP}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)~\text{SWAP}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\otimes(E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})

(43)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}~\text{SWAP}~(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\otimes(E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}~[(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\times(E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})].

Here, the last equality uses the fact that for two states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, we have
\operatorname{Tr}[\text{SWAP}~(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2})]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{1}\rho_{2}]
.

Next, noting that
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}
is Hermitian, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2)2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2){\dagger}}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(E_{k_{1}}\otimes E_{k_{2}})~\rho^{\otimes 2}~(E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}\otimes E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\nu(F)\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}(F_{j_{1}}\otimes F_{j_{2}})~\rho^{\otimes 2}~(F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\otimes F_{j_{2}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}~[(E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}\otimes E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger})~(F_{j_{1}}\rho F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\otimes F_{j_{2}}\rho F_{j_{2}}^{\dagger})]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}~[E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}F_{j_{1}}\rho F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}~\otimes~E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger}F_{j_{1}}\rho F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}}\operatorname{Tr}~[E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}F_{j_{1}}\rho F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}]~\times~\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}[E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger}F_{j_{1}}\rho F_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}].

Now, since the set of operators for
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
, and similarly for
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, are the same, we can simplify further as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2)2}]=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\bigg[\sum_{j,k}\operatorname{Tr}~E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}F_{j}\rho F_{j}^{\dagger}\bigg]^{2}.

Now, combining everything we have

\displaystyle||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{t}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}^{2}=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})~d^{2}+2\alpha\beta d-2\bigg[\alpha\xi^{(\rho)}+\beta\int_{\mathcal{E}}d\mu(E)\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}~E_{k_{1}}\rho E_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}E_{k_{2}}\rho E_{k_{2}}^{\dagger}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}d\tau(E)d\nu(F)\bigg[\sum_{j,k}\operatorname{Tr}~E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}F_{j}\rho F_{j}^{\dagger}\bigg]^{2}.

(45)

The expressibility of the PQCh-ansatz is then given by
\Delta_{\mathcal{T}}^{\rho}\coloneqq||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}
.
∎

B.2
Noisy Gradient Evolution

Consider a PQCh-ansatz that applies a parameterized CPTP quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}
on an initial starting state
\rho_{0}
to yield the ‘noisy’ quantum state
\rho_{\rm out}=\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}(\rho_{0})
. For a given Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
, we define the cost function associated with this circuit as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}~\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}(\rho_{0})].

(46)

Throughout, we consider the general setting when the quantum channel is a composition of CPTP channels as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}(\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{L}(\mathbf{p}_{L},\vec{\theta}_{L})\circ\mathcal{K}_{L-1}(\mathbf{p}_{L-1},\vec{\theta}_{L-1})\circ...\circ\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\vec{\theta}_{1})~\rho

(47)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{k},\vec{\theta}_{k})\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}},

(48)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{K}_{L}(\mathbf{p}_{L},\vec{\theta}_{L})\circ\ldots\mathcal{K}_{k+1}(\mathbf{p}_{k+1},\vec{\theta}_{k+1})
and

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{K}_{k-1}(\mathbf{p}_{k-1},\vec{\theta}_{k-1})\circ\ldots\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\vec{\theta}_{1})
denote the left and right channel compositions, respectively.
Let us denote
\rho_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\rho
,
\tilde{\rho}_{k}=\mathcal{U}_{k}\circ\rho_{\mathcal{R}}=e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}~\rho_{\mathcal{R}}~e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}
, and finally
\rho_{\text{out}}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\tilde{\rho}_{k}
. With these definitions, we have the cost function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}~\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\theta}}^{\mathbf{p}}(\rho_{0})=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}\rho_{\text{out}}]

(55)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}~\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\tilde{\rho}_{k}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}\sum_{m}E_{Lm}\tilde{\rho}_{k}E_{Lm}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}~E_{Lm}\tilde{\rho}_{k}E_{Lm}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\operatorname{Tr}[E_{Lm}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}E_{Lm}~\tilde{\rho}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\sum_{m}E_{Lm}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}E_{Lm})~\tilde{\rho}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\sum_{m}E_{Lm}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}E_{Lm})~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}],

(56)

where in
(a)
we used the Kraus representation of the channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\tilde{\rho}_{k}=\sum_{m}E_{Lm}\tilde{\rho}_{k}E_{Lm}^{{\dagger}}
, and in
(b)
we used
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{m}E_{Lm}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}E_{Lm}
to denote the backward evolution of the Hamiltonian till the
k^{\text{th}}
operation. Then, the
k^{\text{th}}
unitary gate operation is applied and measurement is performed to compute the final trace.

The derivation in (
56
) explicitly writes out the cost function
C_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}
as a function of the
k
-th parameter vector
\theta_{k}
. We now use this to calculate the partial derivative of the cost function,

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta_{k}}\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}]

(63)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~\partial_{\theta_{k}}e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~\Bigl(e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}~\partial_{\theta_{k}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}+\partial_{\theta_{k}}(e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}})\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\Bigr)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~\Bigl(e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}~iV_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}-iV_{k}e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\Bigr)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[i\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~\Bigl(e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}~e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}V_{k}-V_{k}e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\Bigr)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[i\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}[e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}}~e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}},V_{k}]]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[i\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}]]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[i\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]],

(64)

where in the second last equality we have used that

V_{k}
commutes with
e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}
and the last equality uses that
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})=e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}
.

We now analyze the quantity
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}
. For Hermitian generators
V_{k}
satisfying
V_{k}^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}

(67)

\displaystyle=(\cos\theta_{k}\mathbbm{1}+i\sin\theta_{k}V_{k})\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}~(\cos\theta_{k}\mathbbm{1}-i\sin\theta_{k}V_{k})

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-i\sin\theta_{k}\cos\theta_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}+i\sin\theta_{k}\cos\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\sin^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\sin^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}+\frac{i}{2}\sin 2\theta_{k}[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}].

This yields that

\displaystyle V_{k}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\sin^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}+\frac{i}{2}\sin 2\theta_{k}(V_{k}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k})

(69)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\sin^{2}\theta_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}+\frac{i}{2}\sin 2\theta_{k}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})V_{k}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}+\sin^{2}\theta_{k}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\frac{i}{2}\sin 2\theta_{k}(V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}).

(70)

We then have the commutator as

\displaystyle[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]

\displaystyle=(cos^{2}\theta_{k}-\sin^{2}\theta_{k})[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]+i\sin 2\theta_{k}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}).

(71)

Plugging this into (
64
) yields that

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=i\cos 2\theta_{k}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]]-\sin 2\theta_{k}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k})].

(72)

Now,
for fixed left and right noisy channels
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and
\mathcal{E}_{R}
, we evaluate the expected partial derivative of the cost function with respect to the parameter randomness
as

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\theta_{k}}[\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{k}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=i\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]]\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos 2\theta_{k}d\theta_{k}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}-V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k})]\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{k}\sin 2\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

(73)

and the corresponding variance as

\displaystyle\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})=\mathbbm{E}_{\theta_{k}}[(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})^{2}]-\mathbbm{E}_{\theta_{k}}[\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}}]^{2}=\mathbbm{E}_{\theta_{k}}[(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})^{2}],

(74)

where we have

\displaystyle(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}[i\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]]^{2}=-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]]^{2}=-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^{\otimes 2}~[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}].

(75)

Thus, the variance with respect to the parameter
\theta_{k}

(for some fixed
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and
\mathcal{E}_{R}
)
is given by

\displaystyle\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=-\mathbbm{E}_{\theta_{k}}[\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^{\otimes 2}~[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}]].

(76)

We now evaluate the expected variance of the partial derivative with respect to the noisy channel realizations. To this end, we take the expectation over the distributions of the left and right CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}[\text{Tr}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^{\otimes 2}[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}]]]

(77)

\displaystyle=-\text{Tr}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^{\otimes 2}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}[[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}]]].

Let us now define the parameter-integrated observable as
\Omega_{k}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}[[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{L}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}]
and calculate the first quantity as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^{\otimes 2}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}d\nu(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}})\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}(E^{(R)}_{j_{1}}\otimes E^{(R)}_{j_{2}})\rho^{\otimes 2}(E^{(R)\dagger}_{j_{1}}\otimes E^{(R)\dagger}_{j_{2}})

(78)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{R}}(\rho^{\otimes 2}),

where recall from the definition of expressivity superoperator in (
38
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{R}}(\rho^{\otimes 2})=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2}).

(79)

Hence, the expected variance in (
77
) evaluates as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})]

\displaystyle=-\text{Tr}[\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{R}}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]

(82)

\displaystyle=-\text{Tr}[(\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2}))\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]

\displaystyle=-\text{Tr}[\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]+\text{Tr}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]

\displaystyle=\text{var}_{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})+\text{Tr}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]],

where we have explicitly defined

\text{var}_{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})=-\text{Tr}[\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]

as the expected variance of the partial-derivative of the cost function over the left CPTP maps when the right sub-circuit forms a unitary 2-design. We then get that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})]-\text{var}_{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})|

\displaystyle\leq|\text{Tr}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[\Omega_{k}]]|

(83)

\displaystyle\leq||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[||\Omega_{k}||_{2}],

(84)

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality and an application of the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.
Recalling the definition of
\Omega_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}[[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}]
, we use the triangle inequality to get that

\displaystyle||\Omega_{k}||_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}[||[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}||_{2}],

(85)

where we have
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})=e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}
. Since
V_{k}
commutes with
e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}
, we can write

\displaystyle[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]=e^{i\theta_{k}V_{k}}[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]e^{-i\theta_{k}V_{k}}.

(86)

Additionally, the Frobenius norm is unitarily invariant, which then yields that
||[V_{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{k})]^{\otimes 2}||_{2}=||[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]^{\otimes 2}||_{2}=||[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]||_{2}^{2}
is independent of
\theta_{k}
. Together, we get that

\displaystyle||\Omega_{k}||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]\|_{2}^{2}

(87)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[[V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}][V_{k},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]^{{\dagger}}]|

(88)

\displaystyle=2\text{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}]-2\text{Tr}[V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}],

(89)

where the latter follows by noting that
V_{k}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}
are Hermitian operators with
V_{k}^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
.

Note 1
.

Consider the Hermitian operators
V_{k}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}
satisfying
V_{k}^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
. Let
A
be the anticommutator of these operators given by
A=V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}
. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[A^{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}V_{k}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}]

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Tr}[V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]+2\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}]\geq 0,

(90)

which follows by noting that

V_{k}^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
.
This then implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}V_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}]\geq-\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}].

(91)

Combining (
89
) and (
91
), and pluggin it in (
84
) then yields that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}}[\text{var}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})]-\text{var}_{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{k}\mathcal{C}_{\rho,\mathcal{H}})|

\displaystyle\leq||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[||\Omega_{k}||_{2}]

(92)

\displaystyle\leq 4||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}[||\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}||_{2}^{2}]

(93)

\displaystyle=4||\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T_{R}}}^{(2)}(\rho^{\otimes 2})||_{2}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}}d\nu(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}})||\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})||_{2}^{2},

(94)

where the last equality follows by noting that since observables evolve under the adjoint map, we can write
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H})
.

B.3
Proof of Lemma
1

We start by noting that any unitary operator
W
can be written as
W=\exp(i \epsilon H)
in terms of some Hermitian operator
H
. This then yields that

\displaystyle W^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=e^{i\epsilon H}\otimes e^{i\epsilon H}=(e^{i\epsilon H}\otimes\mathbbm{1})(\mathbbm{1}\otimes e^{i\epsilon H})

(95)

\displaystyle=e^{i\epsilon(H\otimes\mathbbm{1})}e^{i\epsilon(\mathbbm{1}\otimes H)}

\displaystyle=e^{i\epsilon(H\otimes\mathbbm{1})}e^{i\epsilon(\mathbbm{1}\otimes H)}e^{\frac{1}{2}i\epsilon[H\otimes\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1}\otimes H]}\times~\text{(higher order terms)}

\displaystyle=e^{i\epsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[H\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes H]}}=e^{i\epsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}}.

Now, consider an operator

X
satisfying the commutator relation
[X,W^{\otimes 2}]=0
. This implies that

\displaystyle X~W^{\otimes 2}-W^{\otimes 2}X=0~\forall\epsilon.

(96)

Now since it holds for all
\epsilon
, we have
\partial_{\epsilon}(XW^{\otimes 2}-W^{\otimes 2}X)=0
. This then implies

\displaystyle X\partial_{\epsilon}W^{\otimes 2}-(\partial_{\epsilon}W^{\otimes 2})X=0

(97)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle Xi\eta W^{\otimes 2}-i\eta W^{\otimes 2}X=0

(98)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle Xi\eta W^{\otimes 2}-i\eta XW^{\otimes 2}=0~~~\text{[ from (25) ]}

(99)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(X\eta-\eta X)W^{\otimes 2}=0

(100)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle[X,\eta]=0.

(101)

Plugging back the definition of
\eta
, we then get that
[X,H\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes H]=0.

Remark 1
.

By property of kronecker product we have,

\displaystyle((A+B)\otimes\mathbbm{1})+(\mathbbm{1}\otimes(A+B))

\displaystyle=A\otimes\mathbbm{1}+B\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes A+\mathbbm{1}\otimes B

(102)

\displaystyle=(A\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes A)+(B\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes B).

(103)

We note that any hermitian matrix can be written as

\displaystyle H=\sum_{a}h_{aa}\mathcal{D}_{a}+\sum_{ab}f_{ab}\mathcal{R}_{ab}+\sum_{ab}g_{ab}\mathcal{I}_{ab}

(104)

where
\mathcal{D}_{a}
is the diagonal component given by
\outerproduct{a}{a}
,
\mathcal{R}_{ab}
is the real component given by
\outerproduct{a}{b}+\outerproduct{b}{a}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{ab}
is the imaginary component given by
i\outerproduct{a}{b}-i\outerproduct{b}{a}
.
Let’s first compute the diagonal term of
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes H
. We have the diagonal operator as

\displaystyle D_{a}=\outerproduct{a}{a}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{a}{a}

(105)

Now, the diagonal operator acts on some quantum state as

\displaystyle D_{a}\ket{qs}

\displaystyle=(\outerproduct{a}{a}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{a}{a})\ket{qs}

(110)

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{a}{a}\ket{q}\otimes\ket{s}+\ket{q}\otimes\outerproduct{a}{a}\ket{s}

\displaystyle=\delta_{aq}\ket{as}+\delta{as}\ket{qa}

\displaystyle=\delta_{aq}\ket{qs}+\delta{as}\ket{qs}

\displaystyle=(\delta_{aq}+\delta_{as})\ket{qs}

Now, consider the expectation value of the commutator for any arbitrary state. We then have

\displaystyle\bra{pr}[X,D_{a}]\ket{qs}=0

(111)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bra{pr}XD_{a}-D_{a}X\ket{qs}=0

(112)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bra{pr}XD_{a}\ket{qs}-\bra{pr}D_{a}X\ket{qs}=0

(113)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bra{pr}X(\delta_{aq}+\delta_{as})\ket{qs}-\bra{pr}(\delta_{ap}+\delta_{ar})X\ket{qs}=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\delta_{aq}+\delta_{as})X_{prqs}-(\delta_{ap}+\delta_{ar})X_{prqs}=0

(115)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\delta_{aq}+\delta_{as}-\delta_{ap}-\delta_{ar})X_{prqs}=0

(116)

Assuming that
X_{prqs}\neq 0
in general, the above relation implies that

\displaystyle\delta_{aq}+\delta_{as}-\delta_{ap}-\delta_{ar}=0,

(117)

which leads to the following two choices that satisfy the above relation:

\displaystyle q=p~\text{and}~s=r

(118)

or

\displaystyle q=r~\text{and}~s=p.

(119)

The former choice yields
X_{prqs}=X_{pr}\delta_{pq}\delta_{rs}
as a feasible operator definition satisfying (
116
), while the latter choice yields
X_{prqs}=X_{pr}\delta_{ps}\delta_{qr}
.

Next look at the imaginary component. We have the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ab}=i\outerproduct{a}{b}-i\outerproduct{b}{a}=i(\outerproduct{a}{b}-\outerproduct{b}{a}).

(120)

The effect of this operator on the state is as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ab}\ket{k}=i\outerproduct{a}{b}\ket{k}-i\outerproduct{b}{a}\ket{k}=i\delta_{bk}\ket{a}-i\delta_{ak}\ket{b}

(121)

and similarly

\displaystyle\bra{j}\mathcal{I}_{ab}=i\delta_{ja}\bra{b}-i\delta_{jb}\bra{a}

(122)

Now, let
\gamma_{ab}=\mathcal{I}_{ab}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{ab}
. The effect of this operator on some arbitrary state is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{ab}\ket{qs}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}_{ab}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{ab})\ket{qs}

(123)

\displaystyle=i(\delta_{bq}\ket{a}-\delta_{aq}\ket{b})\ket{s}+i\ket{q}(\delta_{bs}\ket{a}-\delta_{as}\ket{b})

(124)

\displaystyle=i(\delta_{bq}\ket{as}-\delta_{aq}\ket{bs}+\delta_{bs}\ket{qa}-\delta_{as}\ket{qb})

(125)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\bra{pr}\gamma_{ab}=i(\delta_{pa}\bra{br}-\delta_{pb}\bra{ar}+\delta_{ra}\bra{pb}-\delta_{rb}\bra{pa})

(126)

Next, considering the expectation value of the commutator for arbitrary state we have

\displaystyle\bra{pr}(X\gamma_{ab}-\gamma_{ab}X)\ket{qs}=0

(127)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bra{pr}(X\gamma_{ab}\ket{qs}-\bra{pr}\gamma_{ab}X)\ket{qs}=0

(128)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle i\bra{pr}X(\delta_{bq}\ket{as}-\delta_{aq}\ket{bs}+\delta_{bs}\ket{qa}-\delta_{as}\ket{qb})

\displaystyle-i(\delta_{pa}\bra{br}-\delta_{pb}\bra{ar}+\delta_{ra}\bra{pb}-\delta_{rb}\bra{pa})X\ket{qs}=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\delta_{bq}X_{pras}-\delta_{aq}X_{prbs}+\delta_{bs}X_{prqa}-\delta_{as}X_{prqb})

(130)

\displaystyle-(\delta_{pa}X_{brqs}-\delta_{pb}X_{arqs}+\delta_{ra}X_{pbqs}-\delta_{rb}X_{paqs})=0

Now, from the analysis of the diagonal components in (
119

\{p,r\}=\{q,s\}

X_{prqs}=X_{pr}\delta_{pq}\delta_{rs}

\{p,r\}=\{s,q\}

X_{prqs}=X_{pr}\delta_{ps}\delta_{qr}.

Consequently, in the first case, i.e.,
\{p,r\}=\{q,s\}

\displaystyle(\delta_{bq}\delta_{pa}\delta_{rs}X_{pr}-\delta_{aq}\delta_{pb}\delta_{rs}X_{pr}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\delta_{bs}\delta_{pq}\delta_{ra}X_{pr}-\delta_{as}\delta_{pq}\delta_{rb}X_{pr})

\displaystyle-(\delta_{pa}\delta_{bq}\delta_{rs}X_{br}-\delta_{pb}\delta_{aq}\delta_{rs}X_{ar}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\delta_{ra}\delta_{pq}\delta_{bs}X_{pb}-\delta_{rb}\delta_{pq}\delta_{as}X_{pa})=0.

(131)

Rearranging the above relation then yields

\displaystyle\delta_{bq}\delta_{pa}\delta_{rs}(X_{pr}-X_{br})-\delta_{aq}\delta_{pb}\delta_{rs}(X_{pr}-X_{ar})

\displaystyle+\delta_{bs}\delta_{pq}\delta_{ra}(X_{pr}-X_{pb})-\delta_{as}\delta_{pq}\delta_{rb}(X_{pr}-X_{pa})=0

\displaystyle X_{pr}=X_{br},~~X_{pr}=X_{ar}

(134)

X_{ar}=X_{br}=\alpha
