 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) was requested by H-Canyon Engineering to
determine the flowsheet parameters needed to dissolve and store the YAYOI Material Test Reactor
(MTR) uranium fuel safely and efficiently. In response to this request, a literature review of
existing SRNL MTR dissolution flowsheets and general fuel dissolution literature in the nuclear
fuel processing industry was performed to evaluate chemical dissolution parameters required to
dissolve the YAYOI fuel and the tin-plated carbon steel product cans (PC).

Based on past dissolutions of similar fuel in the H-Canyon and open literature reviews on chemical
dissolution of spent nuclear fuel (SNF), the following conclusions and flowsheet recommendations
were made.

 • A HNO3 concentration of 7 M is appropriate for dissolving the YAYOI fuel and the PC
 with a calculated terminal acid concentration of 6.78-6.89 M depending on the H-
 Canyon dissolver used. Mercury catalyst is not required. If a HNO3 concentration lower
 than 7 M is used, there is going to be a corresponding decrease in the fuel dissolution
 rate and an increase in the dissolution times.
 • Alternatively, a HNO3 concentration of 4 M could be used for dissolving the YAYOI
 fuel and the PC with a calculated terminal HNO3 acid concentration of 3.78-3.89 M,
 depending on the H-Canyon dissolver utilized.
 • The upper bound dissolution time for the YAYOI fuel/PC, using an uncatalyzed system
 with an initial acid concentration of 7 M, is calculated to be 121 hours, with the upper
 bound dissolution times in the range of 42-121 hours for the 10 individual YAYOI
 fuel/PC packages. Likewise, the upper bound dissolution time for the YAYOI fuel/PC
 using an uncatalyzed system with an initial HNO3 concentration of 4 M, is calculated
 to be 194.7 hours, with the upper bound dissolution times in the range of 49.4-194.7
 hours for the 10 individual YAYOI fuel/PC packages.
 • The addition of fluoride ions into acid mixtures for SNF dissolutions has been found to
 enhance dissolution rates of fuel and cladding by a factor of three or more, depending
 on the fluoride ion concentration. However, the use of fluoride could also cause
 potentially negative effects such as corrosion, downstream impacts, precipitation of
 UF4, or precipitation of the neutron poison (gadolinium) as a K-Gd-F compound.
 Lower concentrations of fluoride ions, up to 0.025 M, have been shown to increase
 dissolution rates without the precipitation of unwanted fissile solids. The potential for
 corrosion and downstream impacts, however, remain and should be taken into
 consideration.
 • A KF concentration of 0.025 M may be used for a fluoride ion catalyzed YAYOI
 Fuel/PC dissolution and is calculated to increase the average dissolution rate by a factor
 of 1.3. However, for effective instantaneous dissolution rate increase, the initial HNO3
 concentration must be less than or equal to 6 M.
 • For a fluoride ion (0.025 M KF) catalyzed digestion of the YAYOI fuel/PC, the
 dissolutions times are expected to be about 77% of the dissolution times of the
 uncatalyzed YAYOI fuel/PC digestions. Hence, in a fluoride ion catalyzed YAYOI
 Fuel/PC dissolution in 7 M or 4 M HNO3, the upper bound dissolution times for the 10
 individual YAYOI fuel/PC packages are calculated to range from 23.1-93.1 hours and

 vi

 38-149.8 hours, respectively. The dissolution rate can be decreased based on a
 corresponding decrease to the KF concentration.
• Gadolinium neutron poison, added as Gd(NO3)3, can be added at up to a concentration
 of 2 g/L Gd, within the evaluated flowsheet range.
• These dissolutions are required to occur at elevated temperatures (95-110 o C) to ensure
 that the enriched uranium bolt, depleted uranium nut, and tin-plated carbon steel cans
 dissolve with minimal undissolved fragments.
• A small H2 generation rate is expected in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel and the
 PCs. Most of the off gas generated will be oxides of nitrogen (NOx). Therefore, a
 nominal dissolver air sparge/purge flow rate of 40 scfm, used to ensure hydrogen
 generation remains less than 60% of the lower flammability limit (LFL), is adequate to
 control the dissolution off gases.
• Minimal quantities of insoluble non-fissile solids (transition metal oxides) are expected
 from the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel and the PC in HNO3.
• The total U-235 weight in the YAYOI fuel is 26.319 kg. This quantity of U-235 is less
 than the maximum procedural quantity requirement (40 kg) for the 6.4D dissolver, but
 more than the procedural quantity requirement (20 kg) for the 6.1D dissolver. Therefore,
 if H-Canyon Engineering wishes to dissolve the material in one batch, the 6.4D
 dissolver is recommended for use in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC using the
 10-well dissolver insert. However, the 6.1D dissolver could also be used in the
 dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC if the YAYOI fuel is dissolved in a minimum of two
 batches with each batch not exceeding a total U-235 weight of 20 kg.
• Two of the tin-plated carbon steel cans with their YAYOI fuel segment contents loaded
 per re-usable bundle are not expected to adversely affect the H2 generation rate or the
 fuel dissolution rate. This flowsheet supports dissolution of the carbon steel
 plate/nozzle at the bottom of the reuseable bundle.
• The higher terminal acid concentration from the YAYOI/PC dissolution introduces a
 potential aluminum nitrate solubility concern if combined with the Aluminum Spent
 Nuclear Fuel (ASNF) nuclear material storage stream. Thus, the specific solution
 should be evaluated for Al(NO3)3 solubility prior to the introduction of YAYOI fuel/PC
 material into the ASNF nuclear material storage stream.
• Based on the terminal acid concentration, potential KF concentration, and the absence
 of aluminum nitrate for the YAYOI/PC dissolution, the introduction of YAYOI fuel/PC
 material into the Non-Aluminum Spent Nuclear Fuel (NASNF) Campaign 1 nuclear
 material storage stream does not introduce additional concerns, though there may be
 unevaluated changes to the nuclear material storage stream.

1.0 Introduction
The research reactor at the University of Tokyo-Yayoi (UTY) was the first fast neutron source
reactor built in Japan. It was located at the Nuclear Engineering Research Laboratory of the
University of Tokyo (UTNL) in Tokai-Mura, Ibaraki Prefecture, Japan. The reactor first achieved
criticality in 1971 and was officially shutdown on March 11, 2011. The core of the YAYOI reactor
was a 2-kW thermal, air-cooled, unmoderated, fast reactor with enriched-uranium (93% 235U) fuel
and a depleted uranium blanket.1

H-Canyon Engineering has requested that the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL)
determine the flowsheet parameters needed to dissolve and store the YAYOI material test reactor
(MTR) uranium fuel safely and efficiently.2,3 These flowsheet parameters include:

 • Cold chemical makeup concentration, nitric acid (HNO3) consumption during dissolution
 of YAYOI fuel, and the initial and final acid concentrations,
 • Flammable gas generation and sparge requirement to ensure hydrogen generation remains
 less than 60% of the Lower Flammability Limit (LFL),
 • Use of the 10-well dissolver insert in either the 6.1D or the 6.4D dissolver for processing
 the YAYOI fuel, and the number of cans which can be charged to the dissolver at one time,
 • Potential for fissile solids (such as traces of iron cladding impurities) forming and
 subsequently remaining in the dissolver or dissolver insert,
 • Rate and temperature for dissolution of the YAYOI fuel, and
 • Additional chemicals, if necessary, to support the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel.

The YAYOI fuel was first de-clad of the original stainless-steel cladding and packaged in tin-
plated carbon steel cans (Product Can, PC) before being placed inside of ten 9975-shipping
containers.4

3.0 Approach
No laboratory experimental work was performed with the de-clad YAYOI fuel in this dissolution
flowsheet development. The acid dissolution flowsheet documented in this report is based on the
existing SRNL MTR dissolution flowsheet5 and general fuel dissolution literature in the nuclear
fuel processing industry.

Information needed to develop the flowsheet parameters includes:

 • A literature review of acidic dissolution flowsheets for spent nuclear fuel (SNF) stored in
 tin-plated carbon steel cans, and possible dissolution of insoluble residues, if any,
 • Provide paths for the baseline residue disposition,
 • A review of H-Canyon historical records for tin-plated carbon steel can dissolution tests
 with non-aluminum based SNF,
 • Fuel configuration/geometry, weights, and surface areas, and
 • A review of the characterization of enriched uranium components from the de-clad YAYOI
 fuel.

4.0 YAYOI Material Test Reactor Fuel Dissolution in Nitric Acid
It is expected that the chosen chemical reagent(s) for fuel dissolution and processing be able to
completely dissolve the tin-plated carbon steel can as well as the uranium fuel, uranium bolts, and
depleted uranium nuts.

4.1 Nitric Acid Concentration, Nitric Acid Consumption during YAYOI Fuel Dissolution,
 Chemical Additives to Enhance Dissolution Rate, and Neutron Poison Addition.
The de-clad YAYOI fuel is contained in cans made of tin-plated carbon steel. Hence, the tin-plated
carbon steel and minor transition element impurities of chromium and nickel must be considered
during dissolution of the YAYOI fuel in H-Canyon. In this case, a complete chemical dissolution
of the YAYOI fuel, including the tin-plated carbon steel cans, is required.

In chemical dissolution of SNF, suitable acidified or caustic aqueous methods have been used over
the years. Typical acids used include concentrated acids, mainly HNO3, hydrofluoric acid, and a
combination of these acids and caustic solutions.8,11.

The dissolution of the YAYOI fuel alone can be accomplished with concentrated HNO3 (4-7.5 M)
without the addition of additives to enhance fuel dissolution rates.10 However, the chemical
dissolution of the PC, which is the storage can for the YAYOI fuel, still needs to be addressed to
ensure the complete dissolution of both the YAYOI fuel and the PC. In a laboratory experiment,
Rudisill et al. demonstrated the use of concentrated HNO3 (7 M) to dissolve tin-plated carbon steel

 3

coupons.12 These coupons were prototypes of the actual PCs. They concluded that the tin-plated
carbon steel PCs would readily dissolve in HNO3 at an acceptable rate if the HNO3 dissolving
solution does not become acid deficient.

Since fast neutron irradiation of the YAYOI fuel should not negatively affect fuel dissolution
characteristics (dissolution rates in HNO3),13 it is concluded that a one-step HNO3 concentration
equal to or greater than 4 M, would effectively dissolve the YAYOI fuel, the tin-plated carbon
steel, uranium nut and bolt, and all fission products using either the 6.1D or 6.4D H-Canyon
chemical dissolver as long as the HNO3 dissolving solution does not become acid deficient.10,14

In calculating the HNO3 consumed during the dissolution of the enriched uranium YAYOI fuel
and the PC (composed of mostly Fe), the moles of HNO3 consumed per mole of U-[4.5 mol HNO3/
mol U metal] 15 and the moles of HNO3 consumed per mole of Fe [6 mol HNO3/ mol Fe metal]12,16
was used in total dissolver volumes of 7,000 L and 14,000 L for the 6.1D and 6.4D dissolvers,
respectively. The total mass of U in the YAYOI fuel is 28,260 g (118.7 moles U)1. The amount of
acid consumed by the dissolution of the U fuel is 534.2 moles of nitric acid [(4.5 mol HNO3/mol
U metal) *118.7 mol U]. Likewise, the amount of acid consumed by the dissolution of the PC (Fe)
is 1004 moles [(6 mol HNO3/mol Fe metal) *167.3 mol Fe-56], where the mass of Fe is assumed
to be equal to the mass of the PC: 9,370 g or 167.3 moles of Fe. The total moles of HNO3 consumed
by U fuel and the PC (Fe) during the YAYOI fuel/PC dissolution in nitric acid is 1,538.2 moles
(534.2 + 1004 mol).

The total moles of HNO3 in the 6.1D dissolver with 7 mol/L HNO3 is 49,000 moles (7
mol/L*7,000L). Therefore, the percent HNO3 consumed if the 6.1D dissolver is used in the
YAYOI dissolution is 3.14 % (1,538.2 mol of HNO3/49,000 mol of HNO3 *100). This means the
molarity of the HNO3 in the 6.1D dissolver will only decrease by 3.14% from its original
concentration of 7 M or a terminal HNO3 concentration of 6.78 M [7- (7/100)*3.14 M].

The total moles of HNO3 in the 6.1D dissolver with 4 mol/L HNO3 is 28,000 moles (4
mol/L*7,000L). So, the percent HNO3 consumed if the 6.1D dissolver is used in the YAYOI
dissolution is 5.49% (1,538.2 mol of HNO3/28,000 mol of HNO3 *100). This means the molarity
of the HNO3 in the 6.1D dissolver will only decrease by 5.49% from its original concentration of
4 M or a terminal HNO3 concentration of 3.78 M [4- (4/100)*5.49 M].

For the 6.4D dissolver with 7 mol/L HNO3, the total moles of HNO3 are 98,000 moles (7 mol/
L*14,000 L). Therefore, the percent HNO3 consumed if the 6.4D dissolver is used in the YAYOI
dissolution process is only 1.57% (1,538.2 mol of HNO3 /98,000 mol of HNO3 *100). This means
the molarity of the HNO3 in the 6.4D dissolver will only decrease by 1.57% from its initial
concentration of 7 M or a terminal HNO3 concentration of 6.89 M [(7- (7/100)*1.57 M].

For the 6.4D dissolver with 4 mol/L HNO3, the total moles of HNO3 are 56,000 moles (4 mol/
L*14,000 L). Therefore, the percent HNO3 consumed if the 6.4D dissolver is used in the YAYOI
dissolution process is only 2.75% (1,538.2 mol of HNO3 / 56,000 mol of HNO3 *100). This means
the molarity of the HNO3 in the 6.4D dissolver will only decrease by 2.75 % from its initial
concentration of 4 M or a terminal HNO3 concentration of 3.89 M [(4- (4/100)*2.75 M].

 4

Therefore, either of the two H-canyon chemical dissolvers could be used to dissolve the YAYOI
fuel and PC without significant changes to the terminal HNO3 concentration in the dissolvers.15
However, if the 6.1D Dissolver is used, the YAYOI campaign must be divided into two dissolution
batches, since the most U-235 that can be charged to the 6.1D dissolver is 20 kg. This is equivalent
to no more than 5 randomly selected YAYOI cans (≤19.3 kg U-235), which is equivalent to a 2.6%
reduction in acid concentration. Therefore, regardless of how the materials are batched the molarity
of the HNO3 in the 6.1D dissolver could not decrease by more than 3.14% from an initial
concentration of 7 M or 5.49 % from an initial concentration of 4 M.

Since the submersion level of SNF in the dissolvers is 60 inches or less, as indicated in the 6.4D
dissolver procedure,14 two cans (each 5 inches tall) containing the YAYOI fuel could be stacked
on top of one another if other conditions, such as hydrogen gas generation, are taken into
consideration.

In a study for the dissolution of stainless steel in HNO3 11 the addition of fluoride ions to the
reaction mixture significantly enhanced the instantaneous dissolution rate. Since the dissolution
behavior of tin-plated carbon steel is like that of stainless steel in HNO3, the dissolution rate of the
YAYOI fuel and PCs could also be enhanced with the addition of fluoride ions like KF. However,
it should be noted that the effectiveness of increasing the instantaneous dissolution rate during fuel
dissolution decreases when HNO3 concentration is greater than 6M 20.

Since the disposal path for the dissolved YAYOI fuel would eventually involve the use of the
Savannah River Mission Completion (SRMC) Tank Farms, downstream effects of fluoride ions in
the solution should be considered. The presence of fluoride ions at high concentrations may also
lead to increased corrosion, precipitation of UF4, or even the precipitation of gadolinium neutron
poison as K-Gd-F.16, 17 Above all, the integrity of the H-Canyon dissolver may be compromised in
the presence of high fluoride ion concentrations because it is made of stainless steel.16 However,
it is also worth noting that fluoride ions have been used in low concentrations since the 1990s in
HB-line to accelerate the dissolution of U in nitric acid without any of the undesirable effects such
as the precipitation of fissile UF4 mentioned above.18

In a study designed to evaluate Y-12 highly enriched uranium dissolution in 4.5 M HNO3/0.025
M KF/2.5 g Gd/L, the resulting X-ray diffraction spectra (XRD) for the solid residues left behind
after dissolution of the Y-12 fuel did not show any spectral evidence indicating the precipitation
of fissile materials (UF4) or the precipitation of K-Gd-F.18 The results of this and other experiments
show that a KF concentration of 0.025 M can increase U metal dissolution rates without the
precipitation of undesired fissile solids.11,18,20 Therefore, the use of fluoride in nitric acid for the
dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC packages is suitable up to a concentration of 0.025 M. However,
the possibility of corrosion and downstream effects should be taken into consideration.

If a low fluoride ion concentration is used in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC, the dissolution
rate for the fuel will increase, 19, 20 making it possible to dissolve the YAYOI/PC fuel at time
periods shorter than the dissolution times for uncatalyzed dissolutions Decreasing the KF
concentration will result in a corresponding decrease to the dissolution rate.

 5

Based on experimental data from A. L. Uriarte and R. H. Rainey, the dissolution of uranium in
nitric acid is linearly dependent on the fluoride ion concentration,19,20as depicted in the plot shown
in Appendix C. This relationship is expressed by the equation Y=115X-3.5941, where Y is the
factor by which the dissolution rate increases in the presence of fluoride ions and X is the fluoride
ion concentration. To increase the dissolution of U by a factor of 3, the concentration of fluoride
ion required is calculated to be 0.057 M (≈ 0.06 M) from the equation (3 =115.29X-3.5941). The
addition of 0.025 M fluoride would increase the dissolution rate by a factor of 1.3, which can be
determined through interpolation of the equation provided [(3/(0.057*0.025)+2.6/(0.05*0.025))/2].
The anticipated decrease in dissolution time of the YAYOI fuel/PC packages is 23%. For example,
the dissolution time for the YAYOI/PC fuel package with 4,050 grams of uranium will go from
121 hours, for uncatalyzed dissolution in 7 M HNO3 only, to about 93.1 hours for YAYOI
dissolution in the presence of 0.025 M KF. The calculated dissolution times for each YAYOI
fuel/PC package at different HNO3 concentrations, with and without the presence of fluoride ions,
is found in Tables 3 and 4.

Transient pseudo-catalysts, like nitrous oxide, have been found to enhance the dissolution of
uranium oxides.22, 23 Thus, uranium oxide dissolution in HNO3 is not only dependent on the
concentration of HNO3 and temperature but also on the concentration of nitrous acids and oxides
of nitrogen gases generated within. As a result, an increase in uranium metal dissolution kinetics
has been observed when sodium nitrite is added to solutions of iron nitrate [Fe(NO3)3], which
reduces to Fe 2+ in the solution. The Fe 2+, in turn, reacts with HNO3 in solution to form more
nitrous oxides and nitrous acids. If one assumes the dissolution chemistry of irradiated uranium
metal, like the YAYOI fuel, is like that of uranium oxide, then the enhanced dissolution of the
YAYOI fuel in the presence of oxides of nitrogen could also be inferred. However, marginal
dissolution rate enhancers, like Fe(NO3)3, may not be needed in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel
and the tin-plated carbon steel storage cans because these cans and uranium metal have already
been shown to readily dissolve in concentrated HNO3 at a reasonable rate as long as the HNO3
dissolving solution does not become acid deficient.12 Therefore, the addition of a marginal
dissolution rate enhancer, like NaNO2, is not recommended in the YAYOI fuel/PC dissolutions in
H-Canyon.

It is also worth mentioning that an alternate technology, electrolytic dissolution of the YAYOI fuel
stored in tin-plated carbon-steel and other associated components, is also available in H Area.
However, the electrolytic dissolver in H-Canyon is currently occupied with other processing
missions.9

Gadolinium neutron poison, added as Gd(NO3)3, provides criticality control in the dissolution of

4.2 Determination of YAYOI Fuel Dissolution Time / Dissolution Temperature
The dimensions of a typical PC for the YAYOI de-clad enriched uranium fuel, as shown in
Appendix B, include a height of 5 inches (12.7 cm), a thickness of 0.24 inches (0.61 cm), and a
diameter of 4.25 inches (10.8 cm). The average weight of an empty PC is 937.0 ± 12.7 g, with a

 6

total exterior surface area of 88.04 square inches (568 cm2). In laboratory HNO3 dissolution
experiments using surrogate PC coupons, 38.18 g of the coupon dissolved completely in 65
minutes in 7 M HNO3 acid with a terminal acid concentration of 1.5 M.12, 26. Since this laboratory
experiment involved the dissolution of the overall surface area of the coupon (the two surfaces and
edges of the coupon), while H-Canyon dissolution would involve primarily the dissolution of the
PC from the external surface, the dissolution time of 65 minutes from the experiment would have
to be at least doubled (2.2 hours) to account for the equivalent dissolution time of the PC in the H-
Canyon dissolvers.

It is also worth noting the difference in the HNO3 concentration change between the experiment
and the H-Canyon dissolver. In the experiment, the HNO3 concentration changed from an initial
concentration of 7 M to a terminal concentration of 1.5 M. For the H-Canyon dissolvers, the
fluctuation in HNO3 concentration for the YAYOI flowsheet is expected to be minimal, going
from an initial concentration of 7 M to a terminal HNO3 concentration of about 6.8 M, or from an
initial concentration of 4 M to a terminal HNO3 concentration of about 3.8 M as discussed in
Section 4.1. As such, the PC in the H-Canyon dissolver should have a marginally shorter
dissolution time than the predicted 2.2 hours for HNO3 concentration of 7 M. The time required to
completely dissolve the YAYOI enriched uranium fuel inside the 10 PCs in HNO3 must include
the 2.2 hours for the dissolution of the PCs.

To estimate the time required to dissolve the PC in 4 M HNO3, it was assumed that the dissolution
of carbon steel in HNO3 was a first order reaction. Based on the experimental data,12 the PC
dissolution time in a 7 M HNO3 was calculated to be 2.2 hours. Therefore, the PC dissolution time
in 4 M HNO3 is calculated to be 3.85 hours (7 M HNO3/4 M HNO3* 2.2 hours).

The following assumptions were made in the calculations to obtain the dissolution times for the
YAYOI fuel pieces.
 • Laboratory based low enriched uranium (LEU) dissolution rates are comparable to
 high enriched uranium (HEU) fuel dissolution rates using the H-Canyon dissolvers.
 • The dissolution rate for YAYOI fuel in HNO3 will be uniform, but the mass per
 unit time will decrease as the surface area of the fuel segment decreases.
 • The acid concentration and temperature are assumed to be relatively constant.
 • Decrease in surface area of the fuel segment is calculated based on its dimensions
 (height, radius, and surface area), with each dimension shrinking as the uranium
 metal dissolves.
 • The density of unirradiated uranium fuel, LEU fuel, and irradiated uranium fuel are
 comparable at 19 g/cm3.
 • HNO3 dissolution rate of metal uranium is like that of irradiated uranium or
 LEU26,27 at 5.6 mg/min. cm2 (56 g /min*m2) for 7M HNO3 dissolution or 3.49
 mg/min. cm2 (34 g /min*m2) for dissolution in 4 M HNO3.
 • Each YAYOI fuel metal disk, before being cut into smaller segments for storage
 purposes, is considered as a cylindrical disc with different thicknesses.28

Excel spreadsheet, shrinking-core calculations, based on the assumptions above and on the limiting
dimensions of each piece of U fuel, was used to calculate the dissolution time for the thickest
YAYOI fuel segment and the other YAYOI fuel segments to give a range of bounding dissolution

 7

times at HNO3 concentrations of 7 M and 4 M. The YAYOI fuel segment input data for the
calculations included the fuel thicknesses, fuel radius (6.27 cm), fuel surface areas, individual
masses of the fuel, and elemental uranium density (19 g/cm3). As the fuel dissolves, the thickness
and surface areas decrease, which are accounted for in the spreadsheet calculations.

The uranium dissolution rate used is 56 g/min*m2 for 7 M HNO3 dissolution and 34.9 g/min*m2
for 4 M HNO3 dissolution. As presented in Appendix D, the uranium dissolution rate of 34.9
g/min*m2 in 4 M HNO3 was derived from a linear plot of HNO3 concentrations versus dissolution
rates from various literature sources.20, 25, 27,29

4.3 Off-Gases/Hydrogen Gas Generation Rate and Suppression
Hydrogen generation in the chemical dissolvers is not to exceed 60% of the H2 LFL during
dissolution. In the worst-case scenario, the volume of H2 that could be generated during the
YAYOI fuel dissolution was calculated based on the preliminary gas generation rate of
7 mL H2 /g unirradiated uranium metal as determined by W. E. Daniel et al.25 and R. A. Pierce.30
In these calculations, the sum of the mass of enriched uranium to be dissolved, 28,260 g, was
multiplied by the H2 generation rate of 7 mL/g, which gives a total of 198 L H2 (28,260 g U* 7mL
H2/g U) as summarized in Table 5. The H2 generated from the dissolution of the 10 PCs is expected
to be below instrument detection limit.26,30

From the Excel spreadsheet shrinking-core calculations cited in Section 4.2, the initial mass of one

of H2 generation rate of 77.4 mL H2/minute is within the operation limit (315 mL H2/minute)14 for
H2 generation in the H-Canyon dissolvers.

Alternatively, if employing the dissolution time of 119 hours for the YAYOI fuel only, the average
volume of H2 generated per minute during the entire fuel dissolution period is estimated to be 28
mL H2 /minute [(198 L*1,000 mL/L)/119 h * (1 h/60 min.)]. Again, the calculated hydrogen gas
generation rate of 28 mL H2/minute is within the operation limit for H2 generation in the H-Canyon
dissolvers. The hydrogen generation rate for the dissolution of the PC is zero.26,30 Consequently, it
does not need to be factored into the hydrogen generation calculations.

Literature sources indicate that the only off-gases detected during normal dissolution of uranium
oxides and uranium metal in HNO3 above 1 M are oxides of nitrogen with below instrument
detection limit concentrations for H2.26,30,31 Overall, H2 generation during the dissolution of the
YAYOI fuel and PCs is not expected to challenge the flammability safety requirements for
operating either of the H-Canyon chemical dissolvers.

4.4 Possibilities of Forming Fissile Solids and Residue
During fuel dissolution in inorganic acids such as HNO3, one of the main safety concerns is the
precipitation of fissile solids during or after dissolution. Both irradiated and unirradiated uranium
metal dissolves readily in HNO3, especially at high HNO3 concentrations and elevated
temperatures. However, with irradiated uranium metals, the potential exists for the precipitation
of hydrated transition metal oxides from fission products even after the complete dissolution of
the uranium metal.32,33 Precipitation after complete dissolution of the uranium metal occurs mostly
after aging or interim storage of the resulting uranium solution.33 This solids formation behavior
can be attributed to several factors including the existence of supersaturated silicon dioxide (SiO2),
other transition metal hydrated oxides,33 or insoluble reaction products, which do not dissolve
completely.

 10

Based on laboratory experience in the dissolution of transition metal oxides,33 the complete
dissolution of most transition metal oxides can only be attained with a mixture of HNO3 and HCl
(aqua regia) at boiling temperatures. Therefore, solids or particulate matter formed after HNO 3
dissolution of the YAYOI fuel, if any, are expected to consist of mainly transition metal oxides
like TiO2, and SiO2.

The weight of non-fissile fission products (mainly transition metal oxides) produced with the
irradiation of uranium fuel depends on the fuel burnup.30 The burnup for a uranium dioxide fuel
assembly reported by Oversby and Konsult32 of 35 MWd/kg U 1 for a Simulated High-Burnup
Nuclear Fuel (SIMFUEL), was used as a reference to estimate the weight percent of non-fissile
fission products produced from the acid dissolution of the YAYOI fuel, whose burnup has been
reported as lying in the range of 0.009- 0.014 MWd/kg U16 The total concentration of fission
products calculated for the SIMFUEL was reported as 3.59 percent by weight, which means a
YAYOI fuel with a burnup of 0.014 MWd/kg U will produce less than 1.44E-03 wt.% non-fissile
fission products [(3.59 wt.%/35 MWd/kg U)*0.014 MWd/kg U]. Therefore, the amount of non-
fissile transition metal insoluble oxides produced from the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel in HNO3
is expected to be measurably insignificant. It has been assumed that the properties of the YAYOI
fuel, a research reactor fuel, is like that of SIMFUEL, a commercial reactor fuel.

In the tin-coated carbon steel coupon dissolution experiments cited earlier,12 small undissolved
components of these surrogate unirradiated coupons, which included ferric oxide (Fe2O3) and
ferrous oxalate dihydrate [FeC2O4.2H2O], were observed in the post dissolution solution from the
coupons. The components observed in the dissolution of the surrogate coupons of the PC are also
expected to be present in insignificant amounts in the post dissolution solution of the YAYOI fuel/
PCs.

The radioactive rare gases krypton and xenon, which may be byproducts of YAYOI fuel/PC
dissolution in HNO3, are normally vented to the off-gas system, while iodine is chemically trapped
from the gas phase during dissolution in the iodine reactor (iodine is trapped by silver nitrate at
temperatures between 180 and 200 °C).

Following the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel, the solution in the dissolver will be transferred to an
accountability tank and eventually neutralized and sent to the SRMC H-Area Tank Farm for
eventual immobilization via vitrification in the Defense Waste Processing Facility (DWPF).8

4.6 Determination of the Number of YAYOI Bundles to be Charged to the Dissolver at a Time
H-Canyon Operations proposed two of the tin-plated carbon steel cans with their YAYOI fuel
segment contents be loaded per re-usable bundle. This would make it possible to charge up to five
re-usable bundles to the 10-well insert at one time. All unused wells must be procedurally blocked
by plugs to prevent overcharging. A nuclear criticality safety evaluation (NCSE) is required to
determine if the arrangement poses any safety issues.

The total weight of U-235 in the YAYOI fuel is 26.319 kg, which is less than the maximum
allowed total procedural weight for U-235 of 40 kg in the H-Canyon 6.4D dissolver but is higher
than the 20 kg U-235 limit for the 6.1D dissolver.36,37 Also, the maximum concentration of U-235
in the dissolver solution is expected to be limited to the value specified in the applicable NCSE,
which will be performed prior to the YAYOI fuel and PC dissolution.

Because the total U-235 in YAYOI (26.319 kg) will exceed the 6.1D process protection limit of
20 kg,36,37 the 6.4D dissolver is more suitable to handle the YAYOI fuel/ PC dissolution, should
H-Canyon desire to dissolve the materials in one batch. On the other hand, the YAYOI fuel/PC
packages could also be dissolved in two batches with each batch not exceeding a total U-235
weight of 20 kg using the 6.1D dissolver. There are no aluminum concentration or accumulation
issues (Al accumulation less than 1.75 M in solution)35 with the YAYOI fuels, therefore, either the
6.1 D or 6.4D dissolver can be used for the YAYOI fuel/ PC dissolution.

With air sparging of the dissolver solution, gas generation during dissolution, solution at boiling
temperature, and a purge flow rate of 40 scfm, the dissolver solution will be well-mixed.16,38,39
Therefore, regardless of the bundle configuration in the dissolver, the arrangement is expected to
have no impact on the overall YAYOI fuel and PC dissolution rates, NOx/H2 generation rates, or
nitric acid depletion rate.

4.7 Accelerated Basin De-inventory (ABD) Nuclear Material Storage Considerations
As part of the ABD program, H-Canyon is operating two independently managed nuclear material
storage streams. The first independently managed nuclear material storage stream is comprised of
aluminum spent nuclear fuel (ASNF) dissolution material (e.g., MTR), while the second
independently managed nuclear material storage stream is comprised of dissolution material from
the 6.3D electrolytic dissolver (e.g., Fast Critical Assembly (FCA), Non-Aluminum Spent Nuclear
Fuel (NASNF)). Although the downstream impacts of the YAYOI fuel/PC material are outside the
parameters of this YAYOI dissolution flowsheet literature review, the downstream impacts should
be a consideration for H-Canyon when selecting the dissolver for YAYOI fuel/PC dissolution, if
the YAYOI/PC material is not segregated from other dissolved nuclear materials.

The dissolution of ASNF using current H-Canyon flowsheets requires an initial HNO3
concentration of 5 to 8.5 M and a mercury (Hg) concentration of nominally 0.002 M, while the
terminal HNO3 concentration is required to remain greater than 0.5 M.5,25,29,40 The introduction of
the terminal acid concentration for the YAYOI fuel/PC dissolution in 7 M HNO3 (6.78 M – 6.89
M) or in 4 M HNO3 (3.78 M – 3.89 M) would not exceed the current dissolution flowsheet
parameters for ASNF terminal acid concentrations. However, because the aluminum nitrate
(Al(NO3)3) solubility after ASNF dissolution is a function of the HNO3 concentration and the
temperature of the solution,35 the higher terminal acid concentration from the YAYOI fuel/PC

 12

dissolution introduces a potential Al(NO3)3 solubility concern when combined with the ASNF
nuclear material storage stream. Prior to the introduction of the YAYOI fuel/PC material into the
ASNF nuclear material storage stream, the specific ASNF aqueous HNO3 solution should be
evaluated to ensure Al(NO3)3 solubility, while maintaining the HNO3 concentration greater than
0.3 M to prevent the precipitation of fissile solids.35

Following the FCA Campaign, NASNF Campaign 1 material is planned to be dissolved in the
6.3D electrolytic dissolver. This dissolution material will have a terminal HNO3 concentration of
greater than 6 M and a KF concentration of nominally 0.05 M.41 Based on the terminal HNO3
concentration (i.e. 3.78 M – 6.89 M), the potential KF concentration (0.025 M), and the absence
of Al(NO3)3 for the YAYOI fuel/PC dissolution, the introduction of YAYOI fuel/PC material into
the NASNF campaign 1 nuclear material storage stream does not introduce additional concerns.
Introduction of the YAYOI fuel/PC material into the NASNF Campaign 1 nuclear material storage
stream is possible through utilization of the 6.1D dissolver. Additional evaluation for the impacts
of introducing YAYOI fuel/PC dissolution material into the 6.3D electrolytic dissolver nuclear
material storage stream may be warranted for unevaluated changes to the nuclear material storage
stream.

5.0 Conclusions and Flowsheet Recommendations
SRNL was requested by H-Canyon Engineering to determine the flowsheet parameters needed to
dissolve and store the YAYOI MTR fuel safely and efficiently. In response to this request, a
literature review of existing SRNL MTR dissolution flowsheets and general fuel dissolution
literature in the nuclear fuel processing industry was performed to evaluate chemical dissolution
parameters required to dissolve the YAYOI fuel and tin-plated carbon steel product cans.

 • A HNO3 concentration of 7 M is appropriate for dissolving the YAYOI fuel and the PC
 with a calculated terminal acid concentration of 6.78-6.89 M depending on the H-
 Canyon dissolver used. Mercury catalyst is not required. If a HNO3 concentration lower
 than 7 M is used, there is going to be a corresponding decrease in the fuel dissolution
 rate and an increase in the dissolution times.
 • Alternatively, a HNO3 concentration of 4 M could be used for dissolving the YAYOI
 fuel and the PC with a calculated terminal HNO3 acid concentration of 3.78-3.89 M,
 depending on the H-Canyon dissolver utilized.
 • The upper bound dissolution time for the YAYOI fuel/PC, using an uncatalyzed system
 with an initial acid concentration of 7 M, is calculated to be 121 hours, with the upper
 bound dissolution times in the range of 42-121 hours for the 10 individual YAYOI
 fuel/PC packages. Likewise, the upper bound dissolution time for the YAYOI fuel/PC
 using an uncatalyzed system with an initial HNO3 concentration of 4 M, is calculated
 to be 194.7 hours, with the upper bound dissolution times in the range of 49.4-194.7
 hours for the 10 individual YAYOI fuel/PC packages.
 • The addition of fluoride ions into acid mixtures for SNF dissolutions has been found to
 enhance dissolution rates of fuel and cladding by a factor of three or more, depending
 on the fluoride ion concentration. However, the use of fluoride could also cause
 potentially negative effects such as corrosion, downstream impacts, precipitation of
 UF4, or precipitation of the neutron poison (gadolinium) as a K-Gd-F compound.
 Lower concentrations of fluoride ions, up to 0.025 M, have been shown to increase

 13

 dissolution rates without the precipitation of unwanted fissile solids. The potential for
 corrosion and downstream impacts, however, remain and should be taken into
 consideration.
• A KF concentration of 0.025 M may be used for a fluoride ion catalyzed YAYOI
 Fuel/PC dissolution and is calculated to increase the average dissolution rate by a factor
 of 1.3. However, for effective instantaneous dissolution rate increase, the initial HNO3
 concentration must be less than or equal to 6 M.
• For a fluoride ion (0.025 M KF) catalyzed digestion of the YAYOI fuel/PC, the
 dissolutions times are expected to be about 77% of the dissolution times of the
 uncatalyzed YAYOI fuel/PC digestions. Hence, in a fluoride ion catalyzed YAYOI
 Fuel/PC dissolution in 7 M or 4 M HNO3, the upper bound dissolution times for the 10
 individual YAYOI fuel/PC packages are calculated to range from 23.1-93.1 hours and
 38-149.8 hours, respectively. The dissolution rate can be decreased based on a
 corresponding decrease to the KF concentration.
• Gadolinium neutron poison, added as Gd(NO3)3, can be added at up to a concentration
 of 2 g/L Gd, within the evaluated flowsheet range.
• These dissolutions are required to occur at elevated temperatures (95-110 o C) to ensure
 that the enriched uranium bolt, depleted uranium nut, and tin-plated carbon steel cans
 dissolve with minimal undissolved fragments.
• A small H2 generation rate is expected in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel and the
 PCs. Most of the off gas generated will be oxides of nitrogen (NOx). Therefore, a
 nominal dissolver air sparge/purge flow rate of 40 scfm, used to ensure hydrogen
 generation remains less than 60% of the LFL, is adequate to control the dissolution off
 gases.
• Minimal quantities of insoluble non-fissile solids (transition metal oxides) are expected
 from the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel and the PC in HNO3.
• The total U-235 weight in the YAYOI fuel is 26.319 kg. This quantity of U-235 is less
 than the maximum procedural quantity requirement (40 kg) for the 6.4D dissolver, but
 more than the procedural quantity requirement (20 kg) for the 6.1D dissolver. Therefore,
 if H-Canyon Engineering wishes to dissolve the material in one batch, the 6.4D
 dissolver is recommended for use in the dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC using the
 10-well dissolver insert. However, the 6.1D dissolver could also be used in the
 dissolution of the YAYOI fuel/PC if the YAYOI fuel is dissolved in a minimum of two
 batches with each batch not exceeding a total U-235 weight of 20 kg.
• Two of the tin-plated carbon steel cans with their YAYOI fuel segment contents loaded
 per re-usable bundle are not expected to adversely affect the H2 generation rate or the
 fuel dissolution rate. This flowsheet supports dissolution of the carbon steel
 plate/nozzle at the bottom of the reuseable bundle.
• The higher terminal acid concentration from the YAYOI/PC dissolution introduces a
 potential aluminum nitrate solubility concern if combined with the ASNF nuclear
 material storage stream. Thus, the specific solution should be evaluated for Al(NO3)3
 solubility prior to the introduction of YAYOI fuel/PC material into the ASNF nuclear
 material storage stream.
• Based on the terminal acid concentration, potential KF concentration, and the absence
 of aluminum nitrate for the YAYOI/PC dissolution, the introduction of YAYOI fuel/PC
 material into the NASNF Campaign 1 nuclear material storage stream does not

 14

introduce additional concerns, though there may be unevaluated changes to the nuclear
material storage stream.
