Neuro-ophthalmology: A discipline that listens carefully, looks closely, and provides care through eyes
Authors: Swati Phuljhele
Published: 2026

Neuro-ophthalmology truly embodies the saying “the eye is a window to the brain,” and, increasingly, it serves as a window to the whole body as well.

Many neurological disorders rely on careful assessment of afferent and efferent visual systems for both diagnosis and prognosis, making the neuro-ophthalmic exam central to modern neurology. Many a times, visual signs appear before other systemic symptoms manifest, often making ophthalmologists as the first point of contact. In this special issue, we explore articles that provide different facets of neuro-ophthalmology, from cutting-edge imaging to evolving disease concepts and region-specific challenges.

There has been a rapid technological advancement in the field of ophthalmology over the years, and neuro-ophthalmology cannot remain untouched. While the foundations of neuro-ophthalmology remain firmly rooted in thorough history-taking and meticulous clinical examination, technological advances have improved the diagnostic capabilities.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has moved far beyond its original role as a retinal imaging tool and has evolved as a biomarker of optic nerve integrity. The thickness of retinal nerve fibre layer and ganglion cell layer may serve as reflection of disease burden in conditions such as multiple sclerosis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, Parkinson disease, and Alzheimer disease. High-resolution spectral-domain and swept-source OCT now allow in-depth assessment of the optic nerve head, making entities like optic disc drusen and parapapillary hyperreflective ovoid mass recognizable as OCT-based diagnoses. A review in this issue offers an overview of multimodal imaging in neuro-ophthalmology, highlighting how one can use combined techniques for prompt and accurate diagnosis as well as monitoring prognosis. Another one compares the normative retinal nerve fiber layer thickness across various OCTs. Another article examines the predictive value of OCT for visual outcomes in patients with intracranial tumors. Technological advancement in perimeter also features in this issue in form of virtual perimeter. However, these modalities are complementary to, rather than replace the clinician’s ability to localize lesions through careful bedside evaluation—a defining hallmark of neuro-ophthalmic practice.

Our understanding of autoimmune optic neuritis has expanded over the past few years and changed the clinical practice for the disease. The recognition of aquaporin-4 antibody–associated neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease has shifted clinical emphasis toward early serologic testing and tailored immunotherapy. In this issue, neuro-ophthalmologists and neurologists present a consensus statement to promote uniform diagnosis and treatment of optic neuritis across practice settings. A survey-based article on pediatric optic neuritis highlights differences in current practice patterns and further underscores the importance of consensus guidelines.

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is increasingly emerging as a lifestyle disease particularly linked to obesity. Emerging data suggest that agents such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists may have a role in long-term management and have been addressed in the issue. This issue also explores the association between anemia and IIH, an area of growing interest that may influence diagnostic workup and treatment planning. The articles emphasize how overall systemic wellbeing and intracranial pressure are tightly interwoven.

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) remains a paradigmatic mitochondrial optic neuropathy. The update article on LHON in this issue reviews emerging gene therapies, neuroprotective strategies, and regenerative approaches aimed at preserving or restoring vision. Another article provides the clinical profile and radiological features of patients with optic nerve hypoplasia.

India has its distinctive spectrum of neuro-ophthalmic disease. Infections, particularly intracranial, such as tuberculosis, neurocysticercosis, and fungal, often present with neuro-ophthalmic manifestations of their own and features in this issue. One article in this issue discusses Tuibur—a locally brewed tobacco-containing preparation—appearing to be uniquely associated with tribal communities in north-eastern India and linked to optic nerve toxicity.

Together, the articles in the issue provide a balanced blend of current evidence and practical, clinic-ready guidance. They are designed to be useful for comprehensive ophthalmologists, neuro-ophthalmologists, neurologists, and other subspecialists who encounter visual complaints in their daily work.

Modern neuro-ophthalmology thrives on partnerships between ophthalmologists, neurologists, neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, rheumatologists, endocrinologists, oncologists, geneticists, pediatricians, rehabilitation specialists, and vision scientists. As healthcare increasingly embraces integrated care models, neuro-ophthalmology exemplifies the value of collaborative clinical practice. As our understanding of neurological and systemic disorders continues to evolve, the field will remain vital in connecting these domains and guiding patient-centered care.

We hope that this special issue serves as a valuable resource for practicing ophthalmologists, neurologists, trainees, and researchers alike.

About the author

Prof. Swati Phuljhele

Prof. Swati Phuljhele currently serves as Prof of Ophthalmology - Strabismus and Neuro-ophthalmology services - at the Dr. Rajendra Prasad Centre for Ophthalmic Sciences, AIIMS, New Delhi, where she is actively involved in patient care, postgraduate teaching, and clinical research. She completed her MBBS from Pt. Jawahar Lal Nehru Memorial Medical College, Raipur, Chhattisgarh, and obtained her MD in Ophthalmology from AIIMS, New Delhi. With nearly 20 years of experience in strabismus and neuro-ophthalmology, she has made significant contributions to these subspecialties through extensive research, numerous peer-reviewed publications, and presentations at national and international scientific meetings. She has authored over 120 indexed publications and has several chapters to her credit. She also serves as an editorial board member and reviewer for several reputed national and international ophthalmology journals.
