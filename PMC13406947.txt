Letter to the Editor: Disclosure of Generative AI Usage in Biomedical Academic Writings Should Be Mandatory and Comprehensive
Authors: Bor Luen Tang
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Use of artificial intelligence, mainly in the form of large language model-based generative AI tools (hereafter AI for short), is now widespread and deeply engrained in the practice of academic publishing. Authors use AI to craft and polish their manuscripts, peer-reviewers use AI to write their reviews and editors use AI to screen submissions, organize review processes or even the drafting of editorial dispositions. Despite the common practice and wide usage, it is critical that all parties use AI ethically and responsibly to preserve the scholarly authenticity and veracity of academic publications.

Writing in the pages of the journal, Editor-in-Chief Jin-Hong Yoo has articulated the AI usage policy of the Journal of Korean Medical Science (JKMS), one of the most comprehensive among biomedical journals.1 Shigeki Matsubara offered complementary views attesting to a need for such well-rounded and forward-looking policies,2 and cautioned that “... AI use will become the norm” in due course and that “... AI-crafted writing may be shown to bring some ‘harm’.” These are stabilizing voices and warnings that need to be heard against a backdrop of chaotic noise stemming from the rampant non-disclosure of AI-use. For example, a recent study had found that only 5.7% of submitted biomedical research papers to 49 biomedical journals published by the BMJ Group disclosed AI use.3 A more extensive analysis has revealed that of 75 thousand papers published since 2023, only 76 (~0.1%) explicitly disclosed AI use, despite 70% of the journals having an AI-use disclosure policy.4

There have been recent arguments that AI usage disclosure should be voluntary, and scholars have also rightly pointed out that disclosure policies are difficult to enforce, “... because AI-detection tools are unreliable and AI-assisted and human-written text are often intermingled.”5 On the other hand, doing away with AI-use disclosure or allowing it to be done only voluntarily would be untenable. Although AI-use would increase efficiency and productivity, the flipside to this enhancement is clear. We are getting more of AI slop – low-quality, templated, mass-produced manuscripts generated by AI. One form in which such AI slop appears in biomedical publishing is the many formulaic papers with inappropriate study designs and weak/false claims from AI-based analyses of data from the US national health database, NHANES.6 Besides these, we also need to deal with other issues stemming from careless and irresponsible AI-use in academic writing, such as fake citations generated by AI.7

Here, I offer a new perspective on the necessity of AI usage disclosure. I opine that a major reason for AI-using authors’ non-disclosure is the perception that such are acts of omission rather than active transgression, and therefore mild and non-egregious. Given the uncertainty in the detection of AI usage, they might get away with it with some luck. If caught, they might simply need to declare retrospectively. In other words, non-disclosure of AI usage is never taken seriously enough as an academic offence.

While detection of undisclosed AI usage is indeed inaccurate and uncertain, a comprehensive disclosure is nonetheless necessary and ultimately beneficial. We should, therefore, place the onus squarely on the authors to disclose their use of AI, as they know best whether they have used AI, and the extent of usage. If AI is considered an important tool that would facilitate many aspects in the production of a biomedical report, from data generation and analysis to manuscript crafting and polishing, the use of such a tool should be transparently declared and made known to editors, reviewers and readers. Non-disclosure of generative AI use in biomedical academic writing would thus be equivalent to missing out on an important drug, reagent, device or procedure in a research report. If omission of the latter items would be considered dishonest and in violation of research integrity or biomedical research ethics, non-disclosure of AI usage would carry the same weight of guilt.

As such, if we consider non-, incomplete or improper AI-use declaration as a form of scientific misconduct,8 and that there are stipulated sanctions against such acts, the latter might serve as an effective deterrent. If authors realized that not disclosing AI-use is no longer deemed a mild and passive act of omission, but an active act of dishonesty punishable by the academic establishments and the community, non-disclosure rates could be effectively reduced, and research methods and approaches would become more transparent. Bearing in mind that biomedical research does have a reproducibility problem,9 enhancing disclosure of AI-use would only increase research replicability and reproducibility.

An important part (now critical with widespread use of AI) of the submission-review-publishing workflow would therefore be to ensure proper AI usage disclosure. There should be disclosure checkpoints at the points of submission, review, and publishing. When no AI was used, authors should be required to explicitly state this disclaimer as such.10 The editorial office, where resources permit, should conduct a scan for detectable AI-generated content (still useful to catch blatant AI fakes even if inaccurate), as well as a scan for the veracity of references cited based on cited DOIs. Disclosure of AI usage in peer-review should likewise be disclosed by the reviewers. The editorial offices should also make this latter requirement clear and conduct checks if deemed necessary.

I would further argue that any perceived inconvenience incurred by additional procedures that could enhance disclosure would, when taken as a whole, be worth the while. Again, from an editor’s perspective, having to retract a paper with AI-generated flaws (such as the case of AI-drawn rat diagram with disproportionately huge genitals),11 would certainly be more embarrassing and damaging to the reputation of all concerned. In conclusion, exercising vigilance in ethical and responsible AI usage, including mandatory and comprehensive disclosures, would be important to preserve academic quality and dignity.
