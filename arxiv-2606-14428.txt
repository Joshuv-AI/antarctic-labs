An Entropy Potential for Type-Composition Games
Morteza Alimi, Merlin de la Haye, Pascal Lenzner, Šimon Schierreich, Alexander Skopalik, Marcus Wunderlich

Abstract
. Potential functions are a key tool in theoretical computer science with applications ranging from the runtime analysis of algorithms and data structures, through the analysis of the expected behavior of random processes and search heuristics, to proving the existence of equilibrium states in strategic games. Typically, proofs that employ potential functions are short, elegant, and easy to verify, yet very powerful. Moreover, potential functions are essential ingredients for constructive proofs, in particular in algorithmic game theory. There, a key question is the existence of equilibrium states, but the most powerful theorem in the field – Nash’s theorem – is unfortunately non-constructive. For many strategic games, potential functions come to the rescue by enabling constructive proofs that sometimes even yield efficient algorithms for finding equilibria. We add to this by providing a novel class of entropy-inspired log-multinomial potential functions for natural game-theoretic settings where rational agents of different types strategically choose actions to maximize their utility. In particular, we consider utility functions that are based on the fraction of same- and other-type agents taking the same action. We demonstrate the versatility of the new potential function class by presenting simple equilibrium existence proofs for two recent game-theoretic models, for which only involved technical proofs were previously known. Even better, the new potential function class yields efficient algorithms for constructing equilibria for much more general models. Thereby, we positively resolve several open problems.



1.
Introduction

In the analysis of game-theoretic settings, usually the first and most important question that researchers investigate is whether the game in question admits an equilibrium state. Such states are vectors of strategies selected by each player such that no player unilaterally wants to deviate to another strategy. Thus, equilibria are stable states of the game-theoretic system and thus serve as behavioral predictions. This has numerous applications in economics and the social sciences. The most commonly used equilibrium concept is the
Nash equilibrium
, which is guaranteed to exist due to Nash’s theorem
(
Nash Jr 1950
)
. However, the downside of Nash’s proof is that it cannot be turned into an algorithm for finding equilibria, since it is non-constructive. Moreover, the existence guarantee only holds for the case where the players’ strategies can randomize between different actions.

For many applications, ranging from network creation, through coalition formation, job scheduling, and resource selection, to location decisions in a competitive market, it is more natural to assume that players select only a single action as their strategy, e.g., a specific location for opening a facility. Equilibria consisting only of such strategies are called
pure Nash equilibria
(PNE), and it is well-known that PNE might not exist, i.e., Nash’s theorem does not apply, and it is computationally hard to find
(
Fabrikant et al. 2004
)
. However, there is a large class of strategic games, called
potential games
, that does admit PNEs. The key feature of these games is that a
generalized ordinal potential function

(
Monderer and Shapley 1996
)
exists, which maps states of the game to values such that the potential value improves if a player can improve individually by changing their strategy. Then, the state that maximizes the potential value must be a PNE. Thus, the existence of PNEs can be proven by finding a suitable potential function. Moreover, this can be turned into a constructive algorithm by iteratively performing improving strategy changes of the players, e.g., in round-robin order, until a (local) maximum of the potential function is reached. This approach is called
improving response dynamics
(IRD).

The idea of using such an algorithm to find PNEs works for all games in which every sequence of improving strategy changes for the players must be finite. This is called the
finite improvement property
(FIP). The seminal work of Monderer and Shapley
(
Monderer and Shapley 1996
)
established that this is actually a characterization: every game having the FIP admits a generalized ordinal potential function. Thus, the constructive IRD algorithm is guaranteed to find a PNE iff such a potential function exists.

One of the most prominent examples of a potential function in algorithmic game theory is the Rosenthal potential
(
Rosenthal 1973
)
, which works for the broad class of congestion games. There, players strategically select resources, and their utility depends on the number of players on each shared resource. Most importantly, this dependence on the number of players, instead of the specific set of players on each resource, is critical for Rosenthal-style potentials. Thus, such approaches work for settings where the players are anonymous.

In this paper, we consider

Type-Composition Games
which are close in spirit to congestion games, since also there players select resources (called
actions
in our model) and their utility depends on the action choices of all players. However, we stray
from the beaten path by dropping the anonymity assumption, i.e., we consider players of different types, and the utility of a player depends not on the number but on the distribution of types of players selecting the same action. In particular, we focus on the fraction of same-type and other-type players.

For this broad setting of

Type-Composition Games
, we provide a generalized ordinal potential function and apply it to two previously studied games, which are special cases of our model. Thereby, we obtain clean and technically simple proofs of the existence of PNEs. We also derive efficient IRD algorithms to find them. Our results supersede previous results on these games, as we provide a much simpler PNE existence proof for cases where PNE existence was already known, and we prove PNE existence for cases where it was still open. Moreover, we obtain even stronger results by extending the existence of PNE to generalizations of these games. Last but not least, our approach directly yields that, in all these cases, PNEs can be found in polynomial time.

Inspired by Schelling’s famous segregation model

1.2.
Our Contribution

We introduce

Type-Composition Games
, where players of different types select actions, and their utility depends on the fraction of same-type and other-type players that also take their chosen action. This covers a broad range of natural settings, since it contains the previously studied

Bakers and Millers Games
(BMG)
(
Aziz et al. 2019
;
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
and

Resource Selection Games with Homophilic Agents
(RSG)
(
Gadea Harder et al. 2023
)
, which both have multiple applications, as special cases.

As our main result, we provide a novel type of potential function that utilizes log-multinomials and is inspired by the concept of entropy. To the best of our knowledge, such a potential has not yet been applied to game-theoretic settings (and we also do not know of any similar approaches in algorithm analysis), and thus this contribution might be of independent interest as it may be applied to other settings with similar features.

We use the log-multinomial potential function to prove the FIP for the

Type-Composition Game
with
q
types, and we show that for any such game, improving response dynamics are guaranteed to converge in
{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{3}\cdot|\mathcal{A}|\log q\right)}
many steps, where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of possible actions of the players. Thus, for games with a polynomial-size description, we obtain an efficient algorithm for finding a PNE.

We apply our general theorem to a known class of games. In particular, we show that the BMG is a special case of the

Type-Composition Game
with two types. Thus, our potential function yields a much simpler PNE existence proof, compared to the original proof in
(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
. Moreover, we obtain a much simpler, efficient algorithm for finding a PNE. Additionally, although the BMG in
(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
has only one-sided location restrictions, our constructive proof also holds when both bakers and millers have location restrictions. This resolves an explicitly stated open problem by the authors.

Finally, we remark that our novel potential function yields a simpler PNE existence proof for

Resource Selection Games with Homophilic Agents

(
Gadea Harder et al. 2023
)
. This also allows resolving open questions regarding existence in the general case and yields a polynomial-time algorithm to compute a PNE.

2.
Type-Composition Games

We introduce the class of

Type-Composition Games
, a framework that generalizes existing models such as

Bakers and Millers Games

(
Aziz et al. 2019
;
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
and

Resource Selection Games with Homophilic Agents

(
Gadea Harder et al. 2023
)
.

Players and Types.

Let
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the set of
players
, partitioned into
q

types

T_{1}\,\cup\cdots\cup T_{q}
, that is,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}T_{i}=N
and
T_{i}\cap T_{j}=\emptyset
for every
i\not=j
.
For convenience, we set
t(i)=j
if player
i
belongs to type
T_{j}
.

Actions and Strategies.

Each player
i\in N
selects an
action
from a set
A_{i}
; we denote the set of all possible actions by
\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{i\in N}A_{i}
. A
strategy profile
is a vector
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}
. For a given profile
\mathbf{s}
, let
n_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{s})
denote the number of players of type
T_{j}
choosing action
s\in\mathcal{A}
. The total number of players choosing action
s
is then given by
n_{s}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{j=1}^{q}n_{s}^{j}(\mathbf{s})
.

Utilities and Equilibria.

The utility of player
i
in profile
\mathbf{s}
is denoted by
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})
, and we will define the specifics of it later when discussing several variants. As is standard, we denote by
(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
the strategy profile resulting from
\mathbf{s}
when player
i
replaces their action
s_{i}
with
s^{\prime}_{i}
.
An action
s_{i}\in A_{i}
is a
best response
of player
i\in N
to a partial profile
\mathbf{s}_{-i}
if:

\forall s^{\prime}_{i}\in A_{i}\colon\quad u_{i}(s_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i}).

A strategy profile
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
is a
pure Nash equilibrium (PNE)
if, for every player
i\in N
, action
s_{i}
is a best response to
\mathbf{s}_{-i}
. Formally, profile
\mathbf{s}
is a PNE if:

\text{for all }i\in N\text{ and for all }s^{\prime}_{i}\in A_{i}\colon u_{i}(s_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i}).

2.1.
Type-Composition Functions

In

Type-Composition Games
, players seek to choose their actions to maximize or minimize the ratio of the number of same-type players. There are two natural ways to define this ratio:

The
type-balance
of a player
i
in profile
\mathbf{s}
will serve as the utility
1
1

1

For notational uniformity, we use the term utility for both maximizing and minimizing games, although for the latter the term cost is the more appropriate term.
, denoted by
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})
. It is defined as the number of different-typed players choosing the same action divided by the number of same-typed players
, i.e.,

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{\sum_{j\neq t(i)}n^{j}_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}{n^{t(i)}_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}=\frac{n_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})-n^{t(i)}_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}{n^{t(i)}_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}.

Alternatively, one can define the type-balance as the total number of players who choose that action divided by the number of players of the same type who choose the same action, i.e.,

u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{n_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}{n^{t(i)}_{s_{i}}(\mathbf{s})}.

Note that all used fractions are always well-defined, as at least the player whose type-balance/utility is calculated chooses the considered action.

3.
The Novel Entropy-Inspired Log-Multinomial Potential

As our main contribution, we show in
Theorem

3.1
that all

Type-Composition Games
have the finite improvement property by proving that

\Phi(\mathbf{s})\coloneqq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}\log\binom{n_{s}}{n_{s}^{1},\ldots,n_{s}^{q}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}\log\!\left(\frac{n_{s}!}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}n^{j}_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}\right)

is a generalized ordinal potential function, where
\binom{n_{s}}{n_{s}^{1},\ldots,n_{s}^{q}}
is the multinomial coefficient.

Remark 1 (Entropy interpretation).

By Stirling’s approximation, we have
\log\binom{n_{s}}{n_{s}^{1},\ldots,n_{s}^{q}}=n_{s}\cdot H(n_{s}^{1}/n_{s},\ldots,n_{s}^{q}/n_{s})+{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(q\log n_{s}\right)}
, where
H
denotes the Shannon entropy. Thus, up to lower-order terms,
\Phi
is a weighted aggregation of the entropy of the type distribution at each action.

Now, we prove our main theorem.

Theorem 3.1.

\Phi

is a generalized ordinal potential function for all

Type-Composition Games
.

We now show that
\Phi
is a generalized ordinal potential function for all games in which players maximize or minimize the type-balance functions
u_{i}
or
u^{\prime}_{i}
. To that end, the following
Lemma

3.2
proves that a type-balance-increasing or type-balance-decreasing unilateral deviation of a player leads to a proportional increase or decrease of the potential function values, respectively.

Lemma 3.2.

Suppose a player
i\in N
changes from its action
s_{i}
to an action
s^{\prime}_{i}
. Then

\Phi(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})=\log\!\left(\frac{1+u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})}{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right)=\log\!\left(\frac{u^{\prime}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})}{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right)=\log(u^{\prime}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i}))-\log(u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})).

Proof.

Let
x=s_{i}
and
y=s^{\prime}_{i}
be the action before and after the deviation, respectively, and let
t=t(i)
be the type of player
i
. We write as shorthand

n_{x}=n_{x}(\mathbf{s})\qquad\text{and}\qquad n_{y}=n_{y}(\mathbf{s}),

for the number of players using action
x
and action
y
, respectively, and we write

n_{x}^{t}=n_{x}^{t}(\mathbf{s})\qquad\text{and}\qquad n_{y}^{t}=n_{y}^{t}(\mathbf{s})

for the number of players of type
t
using the respective action.

Clearly, after the deviation,
n_{x}^{t}
decreases by one and
n_{y}^{t}
increases by one, and correspondingly,
n_{x}
decreases and
n_{y}
increases by one.
When considering the difference in potential between
\Phi(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
and
\Phi(\mathbf{s})
, only terms belonging to action
x
and
y
change, thus the other terms vanish in the difference.
What remains is the following, which can be simplified by first combining the logarithms and then canceling the factorials in the numerator and denominator:

\displaystyle\Phi(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=\log\!\left(\frac{(n_{y}+1)!}{(n_{y}^{t}+1)!\prod_{j\neq t}n_{y}^{j}!}\cdot\frac{(n_{x}-1)!}{(n_{x}^{t}-1)!\prod_{j\neq t}n_{x}^{j}!}\right)-\log\!\left(\frac{n_{y}!}{\prod_{j}n_{y}^{j}!}\cdot\frac{n_{x}!}{\prod_{j}n_{x}^{j}!}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\!\left(\frac{(n_{y}+1)!}{(n_{y}^{t}+1)!\prod_{j\neq t}n_{y}^{j}!}\cdot\frac{(n_{x}-1)!}{(n_{x}^{t}-1)!\prod_{j\neq t}n_{x}^{j}!}\cdot\frac{\prod_{j}n_{y}^{j}!}{n_{y}!}\cdot\frac{\prod_{j}n_{x}^{j}!}{n_{x}!}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\!\left(\frac{n_{y}+1}{n_{y}^{t}+1}\cdot\frac{n_{x}^{t}}{n_{x}}\right).

Next, we can expand both sub-fractions and insert the definition of player
i
’s utility, i.e., their type-balance, before and after the deviation, obtaining

\frac{n_{y}+1}{n_{y}^{t}+1}=\frac{n_{y}+1-(n_{y}^{t}+1)+(n_{y}^{t}+1)}{n_{y}^{t}+1}=1+u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})

and

\frac{n_{x}}{n_{x}^{t}}=\frac{n_{x}-n_{x}^{t}+n_{x}^{t}}{n_{x}^{t}}=1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s}).

Furthermore, we have

\frac{n_{y}+1}{n_{y}^{t}+1}=u^{\prime}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{n_{x}}{n_{x}^{t}}=u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s}).

By substituting, we obtain, as desired:

\Phi(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})=\log\!\left(\frac{1+u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})}{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right)=\log\!\left(\frac{u^{\prime}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})}{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right)=\log(u^{\prime}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i}))-\log(u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})).\qed

In our next result, we show not only that
\Phi
is a potential function (so PNEs can be found by following improving moves), but also that improvement sequences converge in polynomial time.

Theorem 3.3.

In

Type-Composition Games
, every sequence of improving moves terminates after at most
{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{3}\log q\right)}
many steps.

Proof.

We observe that for any action
s\in\mathcal{A}
it holds that

\frac{n_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{s}^{i}(\mathbf{s})!}\leq\frac{n_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}{(n_{s}(\mathbf{s})/q)!^{q}}\leq\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{q}=n_{s}(\mathbf{s})}\frac{n_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{q}!}=q^{n_{s}(\mathbf{s})},

and therefore, we can bound the potential of every profile
\mathbf{s}
by

(1)

\displaystyle 0\leq\Phi(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}\log\!\left(\frac{n_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}n^{j}_{s}(\mathbf{s})!}\right)\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}n_{s}(\mathbf{s})\log q=n\log q.

To bound the maximal number of steps, we consider the change of the potential of an improving move.
Lemma

3.2
gives

\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})=\log\!\left(\frac{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})=\log\!\left(\frac{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})}\right).

For ease of notation let
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{x}{y}
and
u_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})=\frac{x^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}}
for some integers
1\leq x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\leq n
. Then

\frac{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}{1+u_{i}(\mathbf{s})}=\frac{1+\frac{x^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}}}{1+\frac{x}{y}}=\frac{(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})\cdot y}{(x+y)\cdot y^{\prime}}\geq 1+\frac{1}{2n^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that all variables are integers at most
n
and that the term is strictly larger than
1
.
Similarly,

\frac{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}{u^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{s})}=\frac{\frac{x^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}}}{\frac{x}{y}}=\frac{x^{\prime}y}{xy^{\prime}}\geq 1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Therefore, the change of potential can be lower bounded by

(2)

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-\Phi(\mathbf{s})\geq\log\!\left(1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{n^{2}+1}.

Thus, by combining (
1
) and (
2
), every sequence of improving moves terminates after at most

{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{3}\log q\right)}
many moves, finishing the proof.
∎

Finding an improving player can be easily done in
{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(q\cdot|\mathcal{A}|+n\cdot|\mathcal{A}|\right)}
time by calculating the fractions for each action and each type once, and then checking for each player if it can improve at each action. Therefore, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.4.

Computing a pure Nash equilibrium for a given

Type-Composition Game
can be done in
{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{4}\cdot|\mathcal{A}|\log q\right)}
time.

4.
Applications

We now show how our main theorem simplifies and generalizes known results.

4.1.
Bakers and Millers

Figure 1.
Example instance of the

Bakers and Millers Game
with two locations
v
and
w
, eight bakers (blue squares) and ten millers (red circles). Based on the strategy profile in the middle, a baker in location
w
can improve by moving to location
v
(see left, utility
1\leadsto\frac{7}{6}
), and a miller in location
v
can improve by moving to location
w
(see right, utility
\frac{5}{7}\leadsto\frac{3}{4}
).

The

Bakers and Millers Game
(
BMG
) was introduced by Aziz
et al.

(
Aziz et al. 2019
)
and later studied by Krogmann
et al.

(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)

to model strategic location choice by customers and sellers, referred to as the bakers and millers. This is an example of a diversity-seeking game; for example, millers seek to choose locations with many bakers (to sell flour to) but few other millers (to minimize competition). See
Figure

1
for an example of an instance of the

Bakers and Millers Game
. Now, we introduce the game more formally, starting with the original definition from
(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
.

The Bakers and Millers Game with One-Sided Location Restrictions.

There is a set of
locations

L
and a set of players or agents
N=B\cup M
, where players of
B
are called
bakers
and players of
M
are called
millers
. Each baker
b\in B
has a set of feasible locations
L_{b}\subseteq L
they may choose from. A miller can choose any location in
L
.
A player’s strategy is the choice of a feasible location.
The payoff of a baker
b\in B
at a location
\ell\in L
is the number of millers that also chose
\ell
divided by the number of bakers at
\ell
. The payoff for millers is analogously defined to be the inverse.

The Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions.

A natural generalization of the model is to also allow location restrictions for the millers, i.e., each miller
m\in M
has a set of feasible locations
L_{m}\subseteq L
, and their strategy is restricted to these locations. We call the resulting model the

Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions
.

We show that even the more general version of the

Bakers and Millers Game
is a special type of

Type-Composition Game
, and therefore, we directly obtain all desirable properties of the latter class.

Theorem 4.1.

The

Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions
is a

Type-Composition Game
.

Proof.

We set
T_{1}=B
and
T_{2}=M
. Next, for every baker
b\in B
, we set
A_{b}=L_{b}
. Similarly, for each miller
m\in M
, we set
A_{m}=L_{m}
. This yields an equivalent

Type-Composition Game
with
q=2
types. It is straightforward that the utility function
u_{i}
exactly reflects the utility function of the

Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions
.
∎

Krogmann
et al.

(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
proved the existence of pure Nash equilibria in the basic model by giving an explicit algorithm to compute one. This non-trivial algorithm crucially depends on the property that the players on one side, i.e., the millers, can be assigned to arbitrary locations. The existence of a potential function was left as an intricate open question. By
Theorem

4.1
we can answer this question positively, even while generalizing their model to the

Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions
, i.e., with the possible restriction of the feasible locations for both sides.

Corollary 4.2.

The

Bakers and Millers Game with Two-Sided Location Restrictions
has the finite improvement property, and hence admits a pure Nash equilibrium.

Moreover,
Theorem

3.3
implies a polynomial-time procedure to compute pure Nash equilibria even for the most general version of the

Bakers and Millers Game
. However, we note that for the basic version, the algorithm of Krogmann
et al.

(
Krogmann et al. 2025
)
is faster, running in
{\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{3}\right)}
time.

4.2.
Resource Selection with Homophilic Agents

The

Resource Selection Game with Homophilic Agents
(RSG)
(
Gadea Harder et al. 2023
)
, is also a special case of our

Type-Composition Game
with two types.
Due to space constraints, we only sketch our results for these games. They essentially correspond to games in which agents seek to maximize the inverse of
u^{\prime}_{i}
. However, the utility is capped at a given parameter
\tau
.
Gadea Harder
et al.

(
Gadea Harder et al. 2023
)
prove PNE existence only for the special case where
\tau\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. This proof is non-trivial as it uses a function that almost behaves like a potential, but some strategy changes of the players do not change the potential value. The authors then show that such neutral changes can only occur finitely often.

Our approach yields a much simpler PNE existence proof and, most importantly, it works for all values of the parameter
\tau
.
This answers a conjecture from the paper positively. Moreover, it was also open whether PNEs can be found efficiently, which we also resolved positively. Last but not least, our novel potential function can be applied to the RSG with more than two types, i.e., we can show PNE existence and efficient construction for this more general model.

5.
Conclusion

We introduce a class of potential functions that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been used in Algorithmic Game Theory (nor in other fields) before. This novel potential has a natural interpretation stemming from information theory entropy.

Equipped with our new powerful tool, we derive simple and elegant PNE existence proofs for two previously studied types of games. Moreover, the versatility of the novel potential allows us to positively resolve several open problems. This shows that new and stronger results can be obtained by truly simple techniques like generalized ordinal potential functions.
