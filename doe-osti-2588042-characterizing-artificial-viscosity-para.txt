1 Background
1.1 Motivation
We are often faced with trying to capture the physics of compressible shocks,
which are governed by the Euler equations. However, Euler shocks are formally
discontinuous at the shock front (translating to a step-function behavior of rel-
evant flow variables). This poses a practical problem for codes with finite-sized
grid elements. As a result, one must make a concession in simulating the be-
havior of shocks within a discrete framework. In particular, we must blur, or
‘regularize’ Euler shocks so that they may be captured on a finite grid.
 To this end, Lagrangian codes solve a set of nonlinear coupled equations
called the ‘regularized Euler equations’ [5]. Although the computer implements
these equations in discretized form, we present them in a 1-D continuous flux
form (in the Eulerian frame) for the purpose of our analysis:

6 Analysis
Including artificial viscosity is a necessary concession that we make in modeling
the physics of shocks, deviating from the sharp discontinuities of a true, phys-
ical Euler shock. However, we can additionally ask whether there is a certain

 13

range of artificial viscosity parameters beyond which we start to observe other
‘unphysical’ side-effects. The answer to this question depends on the metric we
are using to assess the mimetic ’physicality’ of a regularized shock.

6.1 First-Derivative Behavior
One such metric comes from analyzing the qualitative behavior of flow variables
along the shock front. Examining the profiles for both the material velocity and
density, we note that, while increasing epsilon smooths out the center of the
shock as expected, there appears to be a critical point at which the behavior of
the curve near the edges of the shock inverted. To analyze this phenomenon,
we can plot the first-derivative curves for both the material velocity (58) and
the density profile (74), as indicated by Figures (6),(7), respectively.
 We note that, for both plots, we expect for a physical shock to observe a
corresponding dip in the first derivatives of velocity and density at the center
of the shock (i.e. at Y = 0). Practically, this indicates that the shock is the
‘steepest’ about its midpoint, asymptoting to its terminal values on either side
of this point. However, we observe instead that, at a particular value of ϵ,
the anticipated ‘dip’ is inverted, such that the shock becomes monotonically
steeper as one moves away from Y = 0. To this end, we may argue that, above
a specified value of ϵ (i.e. above a certain value of increased linear viscosity), the

features.

6.2 Entropy Profile
Another metric for the ‘physicality’ of a regularized shock can be found from
analyzing a profile of the entropy along the shock front. We expect to observe a
monotonic increase in this quantity from right to left. In particular, in physical
situations such as that of the Noh problem, as the piston moves, it transfers
its kinetic energy to thermal energy. Including artificial viscosity, we have, in
addition to this thermal diffusion, a viscous diffusion which renders the process
irreversible with a corresponding growth in entropy.
 We can calculate the change in entropy along the shock directly from our
approximate solution through the relation

                                 ∆s = log(P ργ ),                             (79)

where P is the pressure of the flow and γ is the standard adiabatic constant.
 In particular, the pressure may be obtained from the flow variables we have
already calculated as [4]

                            P = (γ − 1)ρU + ρχ(u),                            (80)

with χ(u) defined by (76).
 To this end, several entropy curves of varying ϵ are plotted in Figure (9).
(Note that the starting value of the curve at the right of the shock, although
negative, is not physically significant, as we are only plotting the change ∆s).
For the cases of low ϵ, we indeed notice a smooth monotonicity from right to
left. However, we find that as ϵ (i.e. linear viscosity) is increased, we obtain
associated fluctuations in the entropy profile until a certain ‘critical’ value, at
which the monotonicity of the entropy of disrupted. Analyzing these curves
closely, we find that this ‘critical point’ where our physical expectations for
shock entropy start to break down occurs around ϵ ≈ 0.8. It is important to
note, though, that this corresponds to a relatively high value of ϵ, and, as a
result, we must be wary of how much credence we lend the predictions of our
first-order approximation in this regime.
 With this said, we have identified two distinct ‘critical’ coefficient values
of ϵ beyond which the regularized shocks modeled in simulation deviate from
our physical predictions. While these points differ from one another depending
on the metric implemented, they both nevertheless place upper bounds on the
range of artificial viscosity parameters that yield physically plausible results.

7 Conclusion
Starting with the set of regularized Euler equations, we have conducted a de-
tailed analysis of their approximate symmetry structure under a traveling-wave
ansatz. From this procedure, we were ultimately able, first, to approximate the
profiles of various flow variables along the shock front. Additionally, we quan-
tified the spreading of shocks modeled with various sets of artificial viscosity
parameters. Finally, we characterized the physical and unphysical behaviors of

 Within this work, there are a few directions to continue exploring in refining
our analysis. First, the observations we have made come purely from studying
the regularized Euler equations in continuous flux form. However, computers
implement these equations in discretized form and have corresponding numeri-
cal error, such as an overshoot in the various profiles. (Note that Margolin has
hypothesized an effective cubic term in the governing equation to phenomeno-
logically model this overshoot. However, this term can only be expressed in an
explicit, closed form if either the linear or quadratic viscosity is set to zero [1].)
In this vein, future work should be conducted to compare the predictions of our
analytic approximation scheme with codes that include such numerical error.
 Within our methodology, future studies should also be performed to char-
acterize the second-order corrections to our approximation. (This is necessary,
for example, to verify the extreme entropy behaviors identified from our first-
order solution for high ϵ). In my work , I was unable to arrive at an explicit
second-order solution through the outlined approximate symmetry method, but
one could potentially make certain observations by analyzing, for example, the
symmetry structures of higher-order corrections.
