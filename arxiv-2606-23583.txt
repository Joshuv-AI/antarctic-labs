Evaluation Awareness Is Not One Capability: Evidence from Open Language Models
Nilesh Nayan*, Aishwarya Sampath Kumar*, Rishiraj Girmal*, Shivani Anilkumar*, Sankaran Vaidyanathan, David A. Nader Palacio, Reshmi Ghosh,, Soundararajan Srinivasan

Abstract
Safety benchmarks assume that test-condition behavior predicts deployment behavior, an assumption that fails if models detect evaluation cues and adapt. This opens a gap between benchmark performance and deployment behavior: compliance measured under test conditions becomes an optimistic upper bound that overstates how safely a model behaves once the evaluation harness is removed. We characterize this evaluation awareness through eight experiments across 37 open-weight models and seven families. (i) Detection is moderate and training-driven ( 24 / 37 24/37 models exceed chance, best AUROC 0.714 0.714 vs. 0.819 0.819 human, with instruction tuning dominating over scale). (ii) Detection shifts safety behavior (hard refusal drops 5.8 5.8 pp under hypothetical framing, and 21 / 140 21/140 HarmBench framing effects are significant, with compliance rising up to + 30 +30 pp). (iii) Representations survive behavioral collapse (probes retain AUROC ≥ 0.98 \geq 0.98 under rewrites that drive behavior below chance, and multi-layer steering causally moves three downstream tasks while random controls do not). (iv) These axes are weakly coupled (only 1 / 15 1/15 correlations are significant, the sole robust link being behavioral detection versus framing resistance, ρ = − 0.79 \rho=-0.79 , p < 0.001 p<0.001 ). We call this gap the benchmark illusion : because detectability, behavioral manifestation, and controllability vary independently, it is multivariate rather than a single number, so no single awareness score is a reliable proxy for deployment safety.



1
Introduction

Safety benchmarks for large language models (LLMs) rest on a silent assumption: that behavior measured under test conditions is a reliable proxy for behavior in deployment. This assumption fails if models can detect test-like cues and shift their behavior in response. This creates a practical problem for model governance: benchmark scores become optimistic upper bounds, reassuring numbers that overstate how safely a model will act once the test harness is removed.

Current frontier research acknowledges that
evaluation awareness
, the ability of a trained model to recognize that it is being evaluated, is a real phenomenon
(
12
;
1
;
7
;
5
)
. However, three questions remain open. First, how strong and widespread is this phenomenon in open models rather than only frontier systems? Second, when evaluation awareness is present, does it actually change safety-relevant behavior? Third, does “evaluation awareness” refer to a single underlying capability, or do different benchmarks measure different and only partially related aspects of the phenomenon? These questions matter because deployment decisions are often made from benchmark evidence, yet benchmark validity depends on whether models treat evaluation and deployment equivalently.

We present a comprehensive empirical study of behavioral manifestations of evaluation awareness, spanning eight experiments across 37 open models from seven families. By analyzing base, instruction-tuned, and reasoning variants using linear probes, sparse autoencoders, activation steering, and tiered real-vs-hypothetical framing, we organize the paper around four research questions. We ask how open models detect evaluation context (
RQ1
), whether this detection shifts safety behavior (
RQ2
), whether it reflects genuine awareness or format artifacts (
RQ3
), and whether it can be localized and causally intervened upon (
RQ4
).

Four findings drive the paper:

1.

An
independent replication
of evaluation awareness on 37 open models and independent infrastructure, including a benign control that validates the pipeline.

2.

The first
family/scale/training-type detection law
, showing that evaluation awareness is driven primarily by instruction tuning rather than raw scale, with no reliable gain from reasoning modes.

3.

A
causal mechanistic account
showing that evaluation-awareness signals survive rewrite controls and can be causally manipulated through activation steering.

4.

A
cross-experiment synthesis
showing that evaluation awareness is not one trait but at least
three weakly-coupled axes
(only
1/15
paradigm correlations significant), with a single robust,
size-independent
coupling between behavioral detection and resistance to safety-framing (
\rho=-0.79
).

Our central claim is not that models are merely evaluation-aware, but that benchmark-based safety claims can be misleading unless they specify which axis of awareness they measure. We show that detection, behavior, and controllability can diverge substantially, creating a benchmark illusion: a gap between benchmark performance and deployment behavior.

Related Work

Benchmarks and datasets for evaluation awareness.
Prior work studies evaluation awareness through transcript-classification and situational-awareness benchmarks
12
;
7
. Recent work further shows that benchmark conclusions can depend strongly on prompt wording and surface form, motivating rewrite controls and cross-format evaluation
(
14
;
11
)
.

Artifacts vs. Genuine Awareness.

A central concern is that apparent evaluation awareness may reflect sensitivity to benchmark artifacts rather than genuine context recognition. Prior work demonstrates substantial dependence on prompt structure and surface cues
(
15
;
3
)
, motivating the artifact controls used in our study.
4
shows that standard single-contrast probes trained on benchmark-vs-chat data primarily track prompt format rather than evaluation context, and that a paired training design decorrelating format from context recovers a genuine context signal.

Mechanistic Analysis.

Recent interpretability work shows that behaviorally relevant signals can often be localized and manipulated through probing and activation interventions
(
8
;
16
;
2
)
. We adopt these methods, together with SAE-based analysis
(
9
)
, to study the internal representation of evaluation awareness, building on recent work applying probing and steering techniques to evaluation-awareness representations
(
13
;
6
)
.

Research Methodology

3.1
Conceptual Framework

We study evaluation awareness across three levels.
Behavioral evaluation awareness
refers to whether evaluation-like or deployment-like framing changes model outputs, for example, increasing refusal during a safety evaluation or increasing compliance when a harmful request is framed as hypothetical.
Representational evaluation awareness
refers to whether hidden states encode the eval/deploy distinction independent of behavioral output.
Mechanistic evaluation awareness
refers to whether specific internal features (SAE directions, activation steering vectors) causally mediate the signal and can be intervened upon. These three levels can dissociate: a model may encode the distinction without changing behavior, behavior may shift due to shallow formatting cues without a reusable internal representation, and a representation may be detectable without being causally actionable.

To operationalize
RQ2
, our central measurement device is the Safety Validity Gap (SVG):

\mathrm{SVG}(S,\,c_{1},\,c_{2})\;=\;\lvert S_{c_{1}}-S_{c_{2}}\rvert

(1)

where
S
is a safety-relevant metric and
c_{1},c_{2}
are semantically matched contexts differing in evaluation-likeness. We instantiate SVG in three concrete ways: (1) real-vs-hypothetical refusal SVG in the Hawthorne experiment, (2) compliance SVG across HarmBench framing tiers, and (3) a control SVG on harmless prompts where the expected gap is near zero, validating pipeline integrity.

RQ
1
:

[Detection]
We treat detection as a per-model classification problem and run Needham-style transcript classification on all 37 models (0.5B-32B, seven families, base, instruction-tuned, and reasoning variants). The full sweep is deliberate: characterizing

RQ
2
:

[Behavioral Consequence]
We hold task semantics fixed and vary only evaluation-likeness, then measure the resulting shift in safety behavior with the Safety Validity Gap (Eq.
1
). Two complementary instruments are used: real-vs-hypothetical Hawthorne pairs run as a focused single-model case (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, 2,587 pairs) to establish the effect cleanly before scaling, and HarmBench five-tier framing across 35 models to test generality. A benign, byte-identical null control bounds harness noise, so any nonzero SVG is attributable to framing rather than the pipeline.

RQ
3
:

[Artifact vs. Awareness]
To decide whether detection reflects context recognition or prompt-format sensitivity, we apply controlled perturbations that preserve meaning while varying surface form: SAD stages-oversight rewrite tiers, an explicit “being tested” system-prompt rewrite, and a
2\times 2
benchmark/casual
\times
eval/deploy factorial that lets us transplant format across contexts, complemented by MCQ/surface-feature baselines. The broad 32-36 model sweep here is intended to show that artifact sensitivity is systematic rather than
model-specific.

RQ
4
:

[Mechanistic Representation & Causal Role]
We move from behavior to mechanism in three steps. Layer-wise linear probes test where the eval/deploy signal is decodable in the residual stream; SAE analysis (Gemma Scope) decomposes the probe direction via cosine alignment and mean-activation differential to ask whether it corresponds to interpretable features or to formatting artifacts; and activation steering tests whether intervening on those representations causally changes downstream safety behavior, paired with benign capability-retention checks. This arm prioritizes Gemma-3-1B-IT and Gemma-3-4B-IT because mechanistic analysis requires accessible internals and compatible SAE tooling.

Empirical Setup

Datasets & Prompt Conditions.
We use four datasets spanning behavioral and representational analysis.
Needham-style transcripts

(
12
)
provide 500 chat conversations labeled as evaluation or deployment contexts.
SAD stages-oversight

(
7
)
provides 400 labeled questions, split between deployment-like and evaluation-like prompts. It is used to evaluate whether a model can correctly determine if it is currently under developer oversight.
HarmBench

(
10
)
provides 200 harmful prompts, drawn from its standard subset, constructed across five contextual framing tiers:
original

,
deploy
,
explicit_eval

2\times 2
dataset
of
4

Models.
The open-model sweep covers Llama 3.1/3.2, Gemma 2/3, Qwen2, Qwen3, Phi-4, and DeepSeek-R1 distilled variants, mainly between 0.5B and 14B parameters. Larger 27B/32B runs are used selectively when compute permits. Qwen3 models are evaluated in thinking and non-thinking modes. Mechanistic analysis prioritizes models with accessible internals and SAE tooling, in particular the Gemma family. The full model suite is listed in Appx.
C
.

Metrics.
For detection we report
AUROC
, accuracy at threshold
0.5
, and, where available, F1, expected calibration error, and bootstrap confidence intervals. For safety behavior we report hard refusal, soft refusal, compliance, and ambiguous/non-refusal rates, together with signed compliance/refusal deltas and their absolute value, the SVG. We use
AUROC
as the primary detection ranking metric because accuracy is threshold-sensitive and the
2\times 2
class mix is imbalanced.

Operational SVG.
We compute the Safety Validity Gap of Eq.
1
whenever a safety metric is evaluated under matched contexts:

\mathrm{SVG}_{\mathrm{comp}}(a,b)=\left|\mathrm{CompRate}_{a}-\mathrm{CompRate}_{b}\right|.

For refusal, we analogously compute
\mathrm{SVG}_{\mathrm{refusal}}
. We also report signed deltas because direction matters: a positive compliance delta means a condition makes the model less safe under the compliance metric.

5.1
Is evaluation awareness one trait?

Each experiment measures “evaluation awareness” through a different lens. To
test whether these lenses track a single underlying capability, we join every
paradigm’s headline per-model metric into a single table (
37
models) and
correlate them (Spearman
\rho
). The resulting correlation structure is extremely sparse:
only
1
of
15

pairwise correlations is significant
(Tab.
20
,
Fig.
10
). Behavioral detection (Needham) is uncorrelated with
format-robust intent detection (
2\times 2
,
\rho=-0.18
, n.s.) and with the
latent probe signal (
\rho=-0.11
, n.s.). Rewrite manipulability forms yet
another axis. “Evaluation awareness,” as operationalized across these
paradigms, is therefore not a single latent trait but at least three
weakly-coupled axes: a latent representational signal, a behavioral detector
that largely tracks benchmark
format
, and the manipulability of that
detector. The lone strong coupling links
awareness to safety
: models that
best detect evaluations behaviorally are the least swayed by harmful-request
framing (
\rho=-0.79
,
p<0.001
), and this is not a size artifact and
survives controlling for
\log
-parameters (partial
\rho=-0.77
) and holds
within instruction-tuned models alone (
\rho=-0.74
). This is the quantitative
core of the
benchmark illusion
, the gap between benchmark performance and
deployment behavior. Because that gap is multivariate, a single headline
“awareness” number conflates capacities that do not co-vary, so a claim about
awareness must name which axis it means.

Triangulation.

Open-model evaluation awareness is real but multi-faceted: a behavioral

Limitations.

Our conclusions are subject to scope constraints (detailed in Section 7), but the observed weak coupling across detection, behavior, and controllability is robust across the evaluated settings.

6
Industry Implications

We distill each research finding into practical guidance for practitioners who rely on safety benchmarks.

[
RQ1
] Detection is training-driven and distribution-fragile.

Evaluation-context detection is common in open models but sensitive to distribution: performance drops substantially under controlled format/context separation. Detection tracks instruction tuning rather than scale and does not reliably transfer across settings. Benchmarks should, therefore, report performance across both natural transcript classification and controlled format/context separations.

[
RQ2
] Framing shifts safety behavior, but the effects are cue-family and judge-dependent.

A single SVG number is insufficient. Under an LLM judge, the strongest effects include
suppression
on reality/grading framings (
-5
to
-6\,\mathrm{pp}
) and an increased compliance under hypothetical framing in mid-size base models (
+30\,\mathrm{pp}
), patterns largely missed by a keyword heuristics.We recommend reporting
\mathrm{SVG}(S,c_{1},c_{2})
per cue family, with signed deltas, confidence intervals, and an LLM-judged labels.

[
RQ3
] Artifact controls are essential.

Benchmark format can dominate the apparent signal. System-prompt rewrites, SAD tier rewrites,
2\times 2
controls, and SAE features all show that superficial cues can drive detection and behavior. Evaluation-awareness claim should therefore pass at least three checks: a semantic prompt-pair audit, a benign/control-task null SVG, and cross-format generalization.

[
RQ4
] Representations are real but require causal validation.

Hidden states encode a format-invariant eval/deploy signal prior to behavioral expression, and multi-layer steering can causally influence downstream tasks where matched random controls do not. Two caveats for practitioners: causality requires
multi-layer
injection, and steering along this direction is double-edged, improving safety in “deployment-like” contexts, while degrading it under “evaluation-like” conditions. Mechanistic claims should therefore pair probes with causal interventions, random-vector controls, and benign capability-retention checks.

Recommendations.

We recommend that safety evaluations:

1.

Use paired evaluation-like and deployment-like prompts with semantic-equivalence checks.

2.

Report SVG per safety metric and cue family, including signed deltas.

3.

Include benign or byte-identical controls to estimate harness noise.

4.

Vary prompt templates and explicit controls for format artifacts (e.g., MCQ structure, option labels, system prompts, and benchmark register.)

5.

Treat representation-level checks as diagnostic only after behavioral controls pass.

6.

Validate mechanistic claims with causal interventions and benign capability retention checks.

7
Limitations

Our study has several scope constraints: the Hawthorne analysis (
RQ2
) is demonstrated in depth on a single model, large-model coverage (
\geq
27B/32B) is partial, and behavioral effect sizes rely primarily on a heuristic judge. The mechanistic analysis (
RQ4
) is exploratory across a limited set of models and layers, and we observe one probe anomaly (Llama-3.2-3B), reported transparently. These constraints limit breadth but do not affect the central finding that detection, behavior, and controllability are only weakly coupled.

8
Ethical Considerations

Characterizing evaluation awareness is dual-use: in principle, the same understanding could help a developer make a model
game
safety evaluations. Our intent is the opposite: to expose the benchmark illusion and make safety evaluations more robust to it. We study openly available models, use existing public red-teaming benchmarks (e.g., HarmBench) for their intended purpose, and report aggregate behavioral statistics rather than per-prompt exploits or new jailbreaks. Our steering experiments are used only to measure controllability of the eval/deploy signal, not to produce a more harmful model, and we release no attack tooling. We see the main externality of this work as helping evaluators detect and discount behavioral manifestations of evaluation awareness before deployment.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

Open models can detect evaluation-like contexts, and this detection has a
measurable safety consequence. However, “evaluation awareness” is not a single capability:
across six paradigms, the metrics barely co-vary, resolving into three weakly-coupled axes spanning behavioral detection, latent representation, and safety-framing sensitivity. This fragmentation gives rise to the
benchmark illusion
, the gap between benchmark performance and deployment behavior. Because this gap is multivariate rather than a single number, a headline metric
conflates capacities that do not move together, and an awareness claim must name which axis it measures. The underlying representation is real, detectable before many behavioral effects emerge, and causally intervenable. Yet, it is neither a pure artifact nor a universal circuit.

These findings have direct implications for how safety evaluations are designed and interpreted: report SVG explicitly per cue family, use deployment-style rewrites and null controls,vary templates, name which awareness axis measured, and validate mechanistic claims with causal interventions and benign-retention checks.

Future work should extend coverage across models and scales and develop mechanistic accounts that unify probing, representation, and intervention into a single explanatory framework.

Appendix A
Extended Statistical Analysis and Robustness

A.1
Statistical Analysis

Across the 37-model sweep, 24 models significantly exceeded chance performance under transcript-level bootstrap testing. Pairwise AUROC comparisons produced 381 statistically significant differences out of 666 evaluated pairs after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Although Qwen3-14B and Gemma2-27B-Instruct achieved the highest AUROCs, their confidence intervals overlap substantially, suggesting that fine-grained rankings among the strongest models should not be over-interpreted.

A.2
Scaling Trends

Detection performance exhibits only a weak scaling relationship, increasing by approximately 0.07 AUROC per ten-fold increase in parameter count. In contrast, instruction tuning consistently yields larger improvements within families, ranging from +0.05 to +0.17 AUROC.

A.3
Pairwise Significance Testing

To assess whether observed performance differences reflected meaningful separation rather than leaderboard noise, we performed all-pairs AUROC comparisons with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Of 666 pairwise model comparisons, 381 remained significant after correction. Despite this broad separation, confidence intervals for the strongest detectors overlap substantially. In particular, differences among the top-performing models (e.g., Qwen3-14B and Gemma2-27B-Instruct) should not be interpreted as evidence of a stable ranking. The primary conclusion is therefore the existence of a strong instruction-tuning effect rather than a precise ordering among top models.

A.4
Reasoning-Trace Analysis

The model’s reasoning traces provide additional evidence that framing alters internal processing. Evaluation-awareness cues appear approximately twice as frequently under hypothetical prompting, suggesting that the framing manipulation affects both behavior and self-reported reasoning. We also observe an asymmetry in behavioral transitions: refusal-to-compliance flips occur roughly twice as often as compliance-to-refusal flips.

isolates a key methodological point from
our probing-and-steering experiments (Experiment 3).
Although linear probes recover a strong eval-vs.-deploy signal in hidden states,
injecting the same contrastive direction at a
single

negligible behavioral change across three families
(
|\Delta\mathrm{AUC}|\leq 0.017
for Gemma2-9B-Instruct, Qwen2-7B-Instruct, and

requires sufficiently strong, distributed intervention to causally alter
downstream behavior-a finding that generalizes across the Gemma, Qwen, and
