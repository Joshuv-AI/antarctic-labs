Proteomic signatures during pregnancy: an exploratory cohort study of long-term health indicators

Lucy A Bartho, Susan P Walker, Teresa MacDonald, Danica Idzes, Ping Cannon, Tuong-Vi Nguyen, Gordon Smith, D Stephen Charnock-Jones, Stephen Tong, Tu'uhevaha J Kaitu'u-Lino

Abstract

SummaryBackgroundPregnancies complicated by fetal growth restriction (FGR) and preeclampsia are associated with increased long-term chronic disease risk. A blood test could possibly identify those at risk, opening the door to early interventions soon after delivery. Prior work in non-pregnant people have identified protein signatures associated with 18 different health parameters, called SomaSignal Tests.MethodsThis study applied SomaSignal tests to plasma samples collected in pregnancy from two independent cohorts to assess the 18 health parameters in those who later developed FGR and preeclampsia. SomaScan Assay measured ∼7000 modified aptamers in plasma from two independent cohorts. Plasma samples (Melbourne, Australia) were collected at 36 weeks' gestation from women who later delivered an FGR infant (<3rd birthweight centile, n = 118), preeclampsia at term (n = 92) or did not develop either condition (n = 177). The second cohort (Cambridge, UK) were plasma samples collected at 36 weeks’ gestation from women who later delivered an FGR infant (n = 80), and those who did not (n = 172).FindingsIn the Australian cohort, women who later delivered growth-restricted infants had composite protein signatures associated with reduced cardiac function (p = 0.001), increased cardiovascular events (p = 7.6 × 10−6), mid-life dementia (p = 1.5 × 10−9), and higher visceral fat (p = 4.06 × 10−6), compared to pregnancies without complications. Findings were largely consistent in both Australian and UK sample sets. Those who developed preeclampsia had protein signatures signifying cardiovascular (p = 5.54 × 10−6), hepatic (p = 0.001), and renal (p = 0.0007) disease risks, compared to pregnancies without complications.InterpretationSince signatures were more pronounced in pregnancies that were later diagnosed with FGR and preeclampsia, underlying physiological changes may have been present before diagnosis. These changes result in protein signatures concordant with those associated with poor long-term health outcomes in non-pregnant populations. These associations require validation in large, longitudinal cohorts investigating pregnancy, with long-term maternal follow-up.FundingStillbirth CRE, Mercy Hospital Foundation and National Health and Medical Research Council, Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, Taylor Hammond Scholarship, National Health and Medical Research Council Fellowships and Australian Research Council Future Fellowships.Keywords: SomaSignal test, Long-term, Preeclampsia, Fetal growth restriction, Biomarker, Proteomics

Introduction

Pregnancy serves as the ultimate physiological stress test., To support fetal growth, women undergo critical anatomical and functional adaptations. When these adaptations are inadequate, pregnancy complications such as fetal growth restriction (FGR) and preeclampsia can occur. These complications may give rise to other health complications that only become clinically apparent years later. Epidemiological studies have identified associations between pregnancy complications and increased risk of long-term chronic diseases., , These include cardiovascular disease,, , impaired glucose tolerance, renal dysfunction, dementia, , and others. An accurate test that could be added to routine blood tests during pregnancy to identify women at future risk could open the door to early counselling, personalised care, and early interventions soon after delivery, to reduce chronic disease risk.

We utilised SomaLogic technology–a high-throughput proteomics platform that measures over 7000 plasma proteins. We then applied SomaSignal tests to our protein data., , , These are machine learning (ML) algorithms, trained on large, non-pregnant population datasets to detect protein signatures associated with chronic disease risk across 18 health indicators (available at the time of measurement). These include: cardiovascular events, cardiorespiratory fitness, mid-life dementia, metabolic, renal and hepatic dysfunction, body composition (body fat and lean mass), and alcohol-related impacts. While the SomaSignal tests have been validated and published extensively in the general population,, , data applying these to pregnancy has not been published.

We applied the SomaSignal tests to proteomic data from maternal plasma collected at 36 weeks' gestation from two independent, prospective cohorts. This was an exploratory analysis where our aim was to determine whether we can identify proteomic signatures concordant with those previously associated with future chronic disease risk (in non-pregnant populations). To generate evidence that these tests may possibly predict future disease risk, we compared SomaSignal tests in plasma collected at 36 weeks’ gestation, prior to the detection of term FGR (<3rd birthweight centile neonate), or preeclampsia to unaffected pregnancies. As these conditions are linked with future chronic diseases, we hypothesise SomaSignal Tests will be abnormal in women who later develop these pregnancy conditions compared with samples from women who do not develop these conditions.

Methods

Discovery cohort

The fetal longitudinal assessment of growth (FLAG), and biomarker and ultrasound measures for preventable stillbirth (BUMPS), studies are large, prospective studies conducted at the Mercy Hospital for Women, Melbourne, Australia. Both studies were designed to identify biomarkers for pregnancy pathologies: FGR and preeclampsia.

FLAG is a large, prospective study commenced from January 2015 until December 2017. Approximately 2000 plasma samples were collected at 36 weeks’ gestation (35+0–37+0), and pregnancy records assessed to determine outcomes after birth. As this study was an exploratory analysis, we deliberately selected all samples with pregnancy condition (FGR and/or preeclampsia) and a random selection of pregnancies that did not have complications. This study was part of a larger study collected from the FLAG (2000 participants) and BUMPS 2000 cohorts (commenced from February 2019 until October 2025). These samples are representative of pregnant women in Melbourne, Victoria. The study includes nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies. We included, 118 who delivered an FGR infant (<3rd birthweight percentile), 92 who developed preeclampsia (after sample collection) and 177 randomly selected participants using standard R indexing approach in R studio. Participants who did not develop either condition (FGR, or preeclampsia), as the controls. We included all participants who were diagnosed with both FGR and preeclampsia and excluded those carrying more than one baby. Controls were not matched to cases based on maternal characteristics.

Birthweight centile was defined by GROW 2022 (Australia) birthweight centile calculator (unadjusted for ethnicity), accounting for fetal sex (determined by health records) and gestation specific and adjusted for maternal height, weight and parity, where <3rd birthweight centile was defined as fetal growth restriction., Preeclampsia was diagnosed according to the ACOG guidelines, following the development of preeclampsia after 37 weeks’ gestation. For this cohort, routine clinical care at the 36-week appointment did not identify any signs or symptoms of preeclampsia, based on clinical blood pressure measurements. There was also no indication of FGR. Whole blood was collected in 9 mL ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes, tubes centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min, and plasma was collected, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until sample analysis.

Replication cohort

The pregnancy outcome prediction (POP) study is a large, prospective cohort study collected at the Rosie Hospital, Cambridge, UK, conducted between 2008 and 2012. The study includes nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies. The POP study has been previously described.,

Maternal plasma was collected at 36 weeks’ gestation (35+0–37+0) and pregnancy outcomes determined at birth. For this cohort, routine clinical care at the 36-week appointment did not identify any signs or symptoms of preeclampsia, based on clinical blood pressure measurements. There was also no indication of FGR.

Birthweight centile was determined using customised birth weight centiles which account for fetal sex and gestation specific and adjusted for maternal height, weight and parity. The transformation of data was performed with the Scottish GROW centiles bulk calculator. A different GROW chart was applied compared to the Australia samples, as the charts are population specific and validated for each region. These charts were designed to adjust for population demographics. In this study, <3rd birthweight centile was defined as fetal growth restriction. Samples from the POP cohort includes 80 who delivered an infant with FGR (<3rd birthweight percentile), and 172 who did not develop either condition (FGR, or preeclampsia) as controls. The controls were randomly selected using standard R indexing approach in R studio (version 2024.12.1 + 563), and controls were not matched to cases based on maternal characteristics.

Proteomic platform

The SomaScan Assay and its modified aptamer binding reagents have been previously described., The protein abundance was measured using the 7 k SomaScan assay, version 4.1 by SomaLogic, Inc (Boulder, CO) The platform uses ∼7000 individual DNA-based slow off-rate modified aptamers (SOMAmer reagents) to bind target proteins in diluted plasma samples. To ensure specificity, the assay includes multiple steps to remove non-specific binding. Finally, the DNA-based reagents are quantified through hybridisation on an Agilent array (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA). Sample processing of plasma from each cohort was matched before shipment to SomaLogic for proteomic analysis. To minimise technical variation within the proteomic analysis, sample processing included multiple internal standards, and strict quality control procedures were employed. Samples were run on a 96-well plate, and each plate contained 11 external control wells, including: five calibrator samples, three quality control samples, and three buffer blanks, which were used to normalise raw fluorescence signals and monitor assay performance across plates. Three fixed serial dilutions (0.05%, 0.5%, and 20%) were used for optimisation of dynamic range (low and high abundance) of aptamers. Standardisation of raw data using external quality controls were used to correct for intra- and inter-plate variation. Quality control metrics (e.g. cross-plate QC flags, and proportion below the limit of detection) were used to identify and poorly performing aptamers. Limits of detection were defined using buffer controls. These steps were used to ensure high technical reproducibility and minimise variation between assay runs.

SomaSignal tests

The SomaSignal tests applied to the discovery (BUMPS and FLAG cohorts) and replication (POPS cohort) datasets were previously developed for research purposes in non-pregnant populations.,,, SomaSignal Tests were generated using pre-trained composite algorithms derived from large-scale proteomics datasets.,,, They were not originally designed or calibrated to predict long-term disease outcomes in pregnancy. Each SomaSignal Test represents an aggregate proteomic signature relevant to each physiological status. Using our proteomic data generated from the SomaScan platform, we applied the SomaSignal Tests for 18 health indicators: Primary and secondary cardiovascular event risk, cardiopulmonary fitness, mid-life dementia risk (within 20 years), heart failure prognosis (based off proteomic profiles from patients diagnosed with stable heart failure), including: heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (within 6 months), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (within 12 months), heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (within 6 months), and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (within 12 months) visceral fat, glucose tolerance, kidney prognosis, body fat percentage, liver fat, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) ballooning, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis fibrosis, resting energy rate, lean body mass and alcohol impact. Previous analyses that developed the SomaSignal Tests derived risk bins and survival models using large datasets where longitudinal data was collected, and disease outcomes were known. For each disease domain, either an accelerated failure time-Weibull model with elastic net regularisation, accelerated failure time (AFT)-Weibull model with Lasso regularisation, regression model with elastic net regularisation, AFT-Weibull model, logistic regression model with elastic net regularisation, elastic net regression model, elastic net linear regression model, elastic net logistic regression or elastic net logistic regression modelling approach was employed (see ). SomaSignal outputs are the result of composite algorithm-derived measures, rather than single protein concentrations. In the present study, risk scores and bins derived in non-pregnant participants,,, were applied to the protein outputs we obtained from our samples collected during pregnancy. This current study was therefore an exploratory analysis, and conclusions require validation in sample sets that include long-term maternal outcomes. The primary aim was to compare median SomaSignal-derived health scores between women who subsequently developed pregnancy complications and women with uncomplicated pregnancies within the Australian FLAG/BUMPS cohort. Our secondary aim was to determine whether similar patterns would be replicated in the independent POP cohort.

Statistics

Maternal characteristics were compared between pregnant women who later developed FGR and/or preeclampsia and those who did not. Characteristics were compared using Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data and Fisher's exact test for categorical data was used.

For each individual test, the pregnancy outcome (fetal growth restriction, preeclampsia or control) is the independent variable, and the SomaSignal test is the dependent variable. Each SomaSignal test represents a predefined clinical domain, evaluated in independent cohorts and not as multiple comparisons of a single endpoint. From a SomaSignal test you get a single continuous outcome for each chronic condition. High scores indicate a greater risk of developing the chronic disease in the future. Thirteen of the 18 tests can be considered categorically. For the categorical tests (13/18), SomaLogic provided predefined categorical risk groups (bins) based on the reference population data. These bins were not generated by our team. Participants were assigned to categories according to their continuous SomaSignal score. These enable different risk category, based on each different test (e.g. Dementia risk categories: low; medium-low; medium–high; high). All continuous SomaSignal data generated was tested for normality using Shapiro–Wilk test. If it was considered not normally distributed, a Mann–Whitney (non-parametric) test was used to compare the mean/median between those with pregnancy complications and those without. The data is presented graphically as median interquartile range [IQR]. Categorical SomaSignal data is presented as proportions. The proportion in the high-risk category for those with pregnancy complications and those without was compared using Fisher's exact test.

Given the pregnancy complication and no pregnancy complication groups differed on key demographic variables, these were adjusted for in our quantile regression (τ = 0.5) model to estimate median differences between groups. R studio version 2024.12.1 + 563, “quantreg” package (version 6.1) was used for the analysis. Unadjusted and adjusted median differences with 95% confidence intervals were presented in (Discovery cohort–FLAG and BUMPS; FGR), (Replication cohort–POP; FGR) and (Discovery cohort–FLAG and BUMPS; preeclampsia). The adjusted median differences were adjusted for maternal age, maternal BMI, smoking status, and gestational diabetes status.

Results in text were reported as unadjusted p values, and 95% confidence intervals. p < 0.05 was considered significant. Results were interpreted cautiously, with emphasis placed on findings replicated across independent cohorts. All results were interpreted as exploratory and require independent validation. All analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software, LLC) or exact p values were generated using R Studio version 2024.12.1 + 563.

Results

The primary aim was to compare median SomaSignal-derived health scores between women who subsequently developed pregnancy complications and women with uncomplicated pregnancies within the Australian FLAG/BUMPS cohort. The cohort includes 118 women who delivered infants with fetal growth restriction, 92 who later developed preeclampsia, and 177 pregnancies unaffected by either condition (control). We used plasma samples collected at 36 weeks’ gestation. Clinical characteristics are shown in . Those who delivered with FGR, were likely to be older (maternal age, p = 0.03, Mann Whitney U test), have reduced onset of labour (p = 0.03, Chi-square test) and earlier gestation at delivery (p < 0.0001, Mann Whitney U test, ). The groups had no clinically important differences regarding maternal body mass index (BMI), parity, smoking status, gestational diabetes mellitus status, mode of birth and sex of the baby (). Those who developed preeclampsia were likely to have reduced parity (p = 0.0007, Chi-square test), type of labour (p = 0.01, Chi-square test), increased emergency caesarean section (p = 0.009, Chi-square test), earlier gestation at delivery (p = 0.0002, Mann Whitney U test), reduced birthweight (p = 0.0004, Mann Whitney U test), and birthweight centile (p = 0.03, Mann Whitney U test, ). Maternal age, maternal BMI, smoking status, GDM status, and fetal sex had similar risk profiles between groups ().

Compared to the control group, those who later delivered a growth restricted infant had SomaSignal Tests associated with 1) increased primary cardiovascular risk (median 0.6 control vs. 0.8 FGR, p = 7.6 × 10−6; Mann Whitney U test, a; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ), with a 4.43-fold increase in patients classified as medium-low risk (10.2% FGR vs. 2.3% control, p = 0.006, Fisher's exact test, ), 2) reduced cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2, median 21.3 control vs. 20.8 FGR; p = 0.0098, Mann Whitney U test, c; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ), 3) increased risk of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) within 6 months (based on proteomic profiles from patients diagnosed with stable heart failure, median 1.2 control vs. 1.5 FGR; p = 0.001, Mann Whitney U test, d; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ) and 12 months (median 2.1 control vs. 2.6 FGR; p = 0.001, Mann Whitney U test, e; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ) and 4) increased risk of mid-life dementia (median 0.167 control vs. 0.279 FGR; p = 1.5 × 10−9, Mann Whitney U test, h; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ), with a 2.12-fold increase in patients classified as high risk (61% FGR vs. 28.8% control, p < 0.0001, Fisher's exact test, ). Residual cardiovascular risk (b), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) within 6 months (f) and 12 months (g) had similar risk profiles between groups.

Lean body mass (), glucose tolerance (), kidney prognosis (), liver fat (), alcohol impact (), resting energy rate (), non-alcoholic steatohepatitis ballooning (NASH–hepatocellular ballooning, ) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis fibrosis (NASH, ) had similar risk profiles between groups (categorical models shown in ; unadjusted and adjusted median differences presented in ).

Discussion

We report that pregnancies that later developed FGR and preeclampsia exhibit significantly altered maternal plasma protein signatures that are associated with risk of future chronic disease. Our findings in two prospective cohorts are consistent with large-scale, retrospective epidemiological studies which have linked FGR and preeclampsia to increased risk of maternal cardiovascular disease,, metabolic disease, and Alzheimer's, , disease later in life.

Our findings align with work by Thilaganathan and others,, suggesting that susceptibility to cardiovascular disease may precede pregnancy, or manifest decades before clinical symptoms arise. The more pronounced protein signatures in preeclampsia compared to FGR align with epidemiological data showing stronger associations between preeclampsia and subsequent cardiovascular disease. Our data supports the stress test hypothesis–that pregnancy imposes substantial cardiovascular and metabolic demands, unmasking latent cardiovascular and metabolic vulnerabilities that usually remain clinically undetected until later in life. As both cohorts had altered cardiovascular protein profiles before diagnosis of pregnancy complications, this indicates that protein signatures associated with future disease risk in non-pregnant populations are already detectible before clinical diagnosis.

As our samples were collected prior to the onset of pregnancy pathology symptoms, our data cannot determine whether pregnancy complications cause persistent cardiovascular damage, or, both outcomes have common determining factors such as chronic inflammation or endothelial and metabolic dysfunction. The markers altered before diagnosis of a pregnancy complication may reflect similar biological pathways common in both pregnancy complications and chronic disease development.

SomaSignal outputs are composite algorithm-derived measures, rather than single protein concentrations. Therefore, the contribution of individual proteins to the observed association with disease outcomes cannot be determined through these SomaSignal algorithms. Whilst each SomaSignal Test represents a predefined clinical domain analysed as an independent hypothesis, some may share underlying biological pathways. Therefore, these results should be interpreted carefully and require independent validation in longitudinal pregnancy cohorts. We acknowledge that assessment of multiple SomaSignal domains increases the possibility of false positive findings, particularly as several domains may reflect overlapping biological pathways. However, our findings highlight consistent changes across both the Australian and United Kingdom cohorts, and these replicated findings are less likely to represent isolated statistical associations.

A limitation of our study is that the SomaSignal Tests were not applied to cohorts where we have details of the long-term chronic disease outcomes. Such studies would require carefully designed large-scale cohorts with long-term follow up. It is also noted that this is the first time these SomaSignal Tests have been applied in pregnancy. However, the links between our exploratory analysis and those from large epidemiological studies,,,, provides evidence of biological plausibility. Therefore, we demonstrate biological concordance between pregnancy-associated proteomic profiles and previously derived disease-associated SomaSignal signature. We also note that some SomaSignal-derived traits (visceral fat and body fat percentage) do not reflect some clinical characteristics (BMI). Future work should refine these models using pregnancy samples, which is likely to enhance model performance.

We note there were key differences between the discovery (BUMPs/FLAG) and replication (POP) cohorts regarding clinical characteristics (such as maternal age, parity, gestational diabetes mellitus status, growth chart definitions and hospital-specific demographics). We were unable to control for this in the standard SomaSignal analysis. However, the findings from the two cohorts’ SomaSignal Tests were consistent. While established clinical characteristics such as gestational diabetes, BMI, and age are predictors of future chronic disease risk, SomaSignal Tests may capture additional biological information that is not reflected by these risk factors alone. Therefore, the clinical application could be to use SomaSignal results in combination with conventional risk factors, to support risk stratification and prevention strategies. This should be considered in future work where the protein signatures are modelled in pregnancy, and the heterogeneity of cohorts should be considered.

A considerable problem in the field of FGR research is the methods for determining ‘small’. In this study, we chose to utilise the same calculator (GROW) and applied population specific metrics to account for population differences across collection sites. We note a significant difference in parity between cohorts—with the Cambridge cohort only including nulliparous women. While there is strong evidence that FGR and preeclampsia are more common in first pregnancies,, the fact that the SomaSignal tests perform similarly irrespective of this difference, and other differences in maternal characteristics between the two cohorts, suggests limited impact of these characteristics on test performance. However, as indicated earlier, this could be refined if the tests were trained specifically for pregnancy.

Although this study utilised a research-grade, multi-analyte platform, our findings provide proof-of-concept data that circulating proteomic signatures during pregnancy may reflect biological pathways linked with chronic disease risk later in life. This may be improved through model refinement or addition of clinical variables. Translation of these findings into clinical practice would require development of simplified, cost-effective assays, validation in independent cohorts, accessibility consideration, and smooth integration within existing pathways.

Pregnancy is a time when much of the women of reproductive age seek clinical care and undergo routine blood testing. It is a time that has been leveraged for opportunistic screening for other health conditions outside of the pregnancy (such as cervical cancer risk). Our findings suggest it could also serve as a platform for assessing chronic disease risk. Early identification of high-risk women during pregnancy could enable targeted counselling, lifestyle intervention and structured postpartum surveillance to reduce the burden of future chronic disease.

Overall, our study has detected protein signatures in maternal plasma associated with pregnancy complications, and SomaSignal Tests associated with long-term chronic disease. Our findings suggest pregnancy may provide a potential window to identify women at increased long-term health risk. However, these exploratory findings require validation in large longitudinal cohorts before clinical application.
