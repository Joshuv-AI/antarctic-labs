 ABSTRACT
Small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SMCBs) comprise 94% of US commercial buildings
yet face significant barriers to implementing building energy management systems despite advances
in smart device technology. Existing solutions present critical limitations: cloud-based API solutions
simplify deployment but create vendor lock-in constraints; commercial integrated software solutions
ensure compatibility via standardized protocols but require substantial cost and technical expertise;
open-source IoT platforms offer cost-effective vendor independence but provide insufficient standardized
protocol support for commercial building automation. This research presents a generalizable web user
interface framework that bridges the gap between evolving smart device capabilities and lagging software
infrastructure for SMCBs. The proposed system integrates VOLTTRON open-source middleware
with an automated configuration converter that transforms unified specifications written in YAML, a
human-readable data-serialization format, into system-specific files, streamlining manual setup processes.
The vendor-agnostic architecture supports industry-standard protocols (BACnet and Modbus) and semantic
building models while providing adaptive web interfaces that dynamically adjust to various building
configurations. Demonstrations through simulation-based testing and a field deployment show automatic
interface adaptation across heterogeneous HVAC systems and multizone monitoring. The automated
configuration converter also substantially reduces labor-intensive setup.

 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND
Small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SMCBs), defined as commercial buildings of 50,000
ft2 (4,645 m2 ) or less, comprise 94% of commercial buildings in the United States while consuming
approximately 47% of the total commercial building energy use, excluding malls (Kim, Katipamula, and
Lutes 2019; U.S. Energy Information Administration 2021; Luo et al. 2017; Ye, Zuo, and Wang 2019).
Targeting SMCBs for energy efficiency improvements could achieve as much as a 17% reduction in total
energy use, demonstrating their significant effect on national energy consumption (Barnes and Parrish
2016; Huppert et al. 2013). This substantial energy consumption underscores the critical need for effective
energy management strategies within the SMCB sector.
A building energy management system (BEMS)—also known as building automation system (BAS)
or building management system (BMS)—coordinates various building equipment, including HVAC,
lighting, and energy meters, through real-time monitoring, automated controls, and intelligent scheduling
to improve performance and reduce energy consumption (Bhatt and Verma 2015; Domingues et al. 2016).
Effective BEMS deployment fundamentally depends on seamless integration of hardware and software
components: hardware elements, including sensors and controllers, interface with mechanical equipment,
whereas software platforms coordinate their operation, analyze data, and enable advanced control
strategies. However, approximately 84% of SMCBs do not have adequate BEMS infrastructure, having
received limited attention because of the prohibitively high costs of retrofitting these buildings (U.S.
Energy Information Administration 2021; Guo et al. 2025).
Recent advances in IoT technology have driven increasing adoption of smart sensors and controllers
in SMCBs, creating significant opportunities for sophisticated energy management through enhanced
monitoring and control capabilities (Kim and Braun 2018). These smart devices include thermostats
with real-time temperature displays and remote controls, wireless environmental sensors enabling
scalable multizone monitoring, occupancy sensors for automated lighting and HVAC control, smart plugs
for real-time energy tracking, and CO2 sensors integrated into smart ventilation systems (Stopps and

 1

Touchie 2021; Jornet-Monteverde and Galiana-Merino 2020; Chaudhari et al. 2024; Chiesa et al. 2019).
Collectively, these technologies can reduce energy consumption by automatically adjusting set points
based on occupancy detection (Tamas, O’Brien, and Quintero 2021), enable precise zone-level control
that eliminates waste in unoccupied areas (Wang et al. 2018), and allow real-time monitoring to identify
unnecessary equipment operation during peak demand periods (Suryadevara and Biswal 2019). However,
in practice, realizing these benefits requires comprehensive software interfaces that provide essential
functionality, such as real-time data visualization, unified monitoring, remote control, and data management
for advanced control strategies, while minimizing manual configuration efforts.
Despite these rapid hardware advances, software development has not kept pace, creating a critical gap
between device capabilities and accessible building automation. Commercial platforms such as ecobee’s
SmartBuildings and Google Nest’s Devices Access rely on vendor-specific, cloud-based ecosystems that
constrain broader implementation across various building environments. More critically, these API-based
approaches require programming expertise to develop custom integration code within a centralized
platform, resulting in significant technical barriers because most SMCB operators have limited specialized
knowledge. The need for specialized labor directly translates into substantial implementation costs
(Trenbath et al. 2022), creating a particularly acute barrier for SMCBs, which typically operate with
limited budgets and lack the dedicated facility management staff found in larger commercial buildings
(Ham et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025).
Beyond technical complexity, existing software solutions lack a centralized interoperability hub that
enables scalable communication among heterogeneous devices, preventing unified integration and
coordinated control of systems across different vendors and protocols. Current approaches require
labor-intensive setup processes involving extensive manual configuration, custom programming, and
system integration work by specialized technicians, with programming and testing alone accounting for
up to 25% of total system costs (Paul et al. 2025; Trenbath et al. 2022). A case study demonstrated that
labor hours reached 65 h (equivalent to $7,500) just for system setup and configuration in a 1,035 m2
SMCB, highlighting the prohibitive initial cost that prevents widespread adoption (Guo et al. 2025). This
absence of a streamlined deployment process forces building operators to repeat a separate configuration
procedure for each building system, preventing scalability across multiple SMCBs. Furthermore, many
existing software platforms remain tightly coupled with specific devices or ecosystems and provide limited
support for standardized communication protocols such as BACnet and Modbus (Essa et al. 2023; Wu and
Liu 2019; Malkawi et al. 2023; Mataloto, Ferreira, and Cruz 2019).
These limitations may constrain the scalability and flexibility of existing software platforms across various
building types and limit integration capabilities with the heterogeneous device configurations commonly
used in the building management industry. Recognizing these critical barriers, the US Department of
Energy’s Building Technologies Office has initiated development of low-cost, interoperable building
controller solutions specifically targeting SMCBs through multilaboratory research led by Oak Ridge
National Laboratory and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) to address both implementation
costs and standardization requirements for widespread deployment (U.S. Department of Energy, Building
Technologies Office 2024).

1.2 RELATED WORKS
Although various interface solutions have been developed to integrate smart devices with building
monitoring and control systems, existing approaches can be categorized into two primary groups, each
presenting unique advantages and limitations that collectively highlight the need for more comprehensive
integration strategies in SMCB environments (Table 1). The first group leverages cloud-based APIs
for device–software integration, offering streamlined setup processes that have contributed to their

 2

adoption in approximately 6.5% of SMCBs (Neukomm, Nubbe, and Fares 2019; U.S. Energy Information
Administration 2021). Representative solutions include Google Nest, ecobee Smart Thermostat, Amazon
Smart Thermostat, and Honeywell T-Series thermostats, which provide user-friendly mobile applications
and reduced upfront infrastructure requirements through Wi-Fi connectivity and vendor-specific cloud
platforms (Google, n.d.-c; Ecobee, n.d.-c; Honeywell, n.d.; Amazon, n.d.; Ayan and Turkay 2018; Tamas,
O’Brien, and Quintero 2021). However, these vendor-specific ecosystems lack generalized interfaces
capable of accommodating heterogeneous devices across different vendors, requiring labor-intensive
custom integration work when combining devices from multiple vendors and limiting interoperability
and long-term system scalability across various building configurations (Trenbath et al. 2022; Gaidon and
Poplawski 2017).
The second group encompasses integrated software solutions that use industry-standard communication
protocols, including BACnet, Modbus, KNX, and LonWorks, which have been used in the building
automation industry for decades (Domingues et al. 2016; Modbus IDA 2004; International Organization
for Standardization 2004; ISO/IEC 2006, 2012). These standardized approaches ensure compatibility
across different manufacturers and enable comprehensive, vendor-agnostic building automation solutions
that are particularly beneficial for scalable implementations (Bhatt and Verma 2015). Representative
commercial platforms include Normal Framework, Facilio, KODE OS, and WEBCTRL, which provide
robust BACnet/Modbus integration and vendor-agnostic device support (Normal Software 2025; Facilio,
n.d.; KODE OS, n.d.; WebCTRL®, n.d.). However, these solutions typically impose substantial licensing
costs and require specialized technical expertise for deployment and configuration, contributing to the
labor-intensive deployment processes that create substantial implementation barriers in the SMCB
sector (Trenbath et al. 2022; Ham et al. 2024; Kim and Braun 2018). In contrast, open-source software
platforms provide cost-effective, community-driven middleware solutions. Representative platforms
include Node-RED, Home Assistant, ThingsBoard, and OpenHAB, which offer flexible integration
frameworks for connecting heterogeneous IoT devices without proprietary lock-in constraints through
various IoT network protocols, such as MQTT, Zigbee, Z-Wave, and LoRaWAN (Node-RED, n.d.; Home
Assistant, n.d.; MQTT, n.d.; Zigbee, n.d.; Z-Wave, n.d.; LoRaWAN, n.d.). These open-source solutions
provide substantial cost reductions, extensive customization flexibility, and visual programming tools
for customized algorithms through intuitive interfaces (Baig et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024; Mataloto,
Ferreira, and Cruz 2019; Korodi et al. 2024). However, they primarily focus on IoT sensor integration
and residential smart home applications, lacking native support for industry-standard protocols such as
BACnet and Modbus that are essential for commercial building automation. This absence of protocol
support necessitates additional custom development and integration efforts to interface with commercial
BASs.
This analysis reveals that although each category addresses specific aspects of smart devices, none
provides a comprehensive solution for SMCB environments. Two fundamental limitations persist
across the existing approaches: (1) labor-intensive deployment processes requiring extensive manual
configuration and specialized technical expertise and (2) a lack of generalized interfaces capable of
accommodating heterogeneous devices across various building systems. API-based solutions, while
offering streamlined initial setup, require labor-intensive custom integration work because of their lock-in
constraints that limit interoperability and scalability. Commercial integrated software solutions ensure
robust compatibility through standardized protocols but impose labor-intensive deployment processes that
require specialized technical expertise for system configuration, resulting in substantial implementation
cost barriers. Open-source IoT platforms offer vendor independence and customization advantages but
necessitate additional custom development and integration efforts to interface with commercial BASs due
to limited native support for industry-standard protocols. These limitations collectively underscore the
critical need for an integrated approach that combines streamlined deployment workflows with generalized

To address these identified limitations in existing approaches, this research builds upon recent advances in
open-source technologies that provide essential foundations for scalable, streamlined BEMS deployment.
PNNL’s Autonomous Energy Management Software (AEMS) provides a comprehensive web application
framework built on the VOLTTRON open-source platform, offering building control capabilities with
particular focus on rooftop units (RTUs) and heat pumps (Katipamula, Lutes, and Herr 2023; Katipamula
2022). The underlying VOLTTRON platform provides a robust middleware framework for integrating
distributed energy resources through industry-standard protocols, including BACnet and Modbus, offering
a vendor-agnostic foundation for building automation (Katipamula et al. 2016; Haack et al. 2016). Brick
Schema has emerged as a standardized semantic building model that enables consistent representation
of building systems and equipment relationships through the Resource Description Framework (RDF),
facilitating robust interoperability across various building management applications (Fierro et al. 2020;
Balaji et al. 2016). Furthermore, React provides a component-based JavaScript library that enables
the creation of responsive, dynamic web applications that adapt to varying data structures through
reusable interface components and efficient state management (Meta Platforms, Inc., n.d.). Although
these technologies address specific aspects of BEMS—AEMS providing a VOLTTRON-compatible
web framework, Brick Schema enabling semantic interoperability, and React facilitating dynamic web
interfaces—their integration into a unified, generalizable framework for streamlined BEMS deployment
with minimal configuration effort remains unexplored for the SMCB sector.

1.3 SUMMARY AND CONTRIBUTIONS
Current smart device integration approaches reveal significant limitations across three primary categories:
cloud-based API solutions offer simplified deployment but create fragmented systems that require
significant integration effort when combining devices from multiple vendors; commercial integrated
software solutions ensure robust compatibility via industry-standard protocols but impose substantial
licensing costs and require specialized technical expertise; and open-source IoT platforms provide
cost advantages but primarily focus on residential smart home applications, lacking native support for
industry-standard protocols essential for commercial building automation.
To address these identified limitations, this research presents a framework for developing generalizable
web user interfaces for BEMS that bridges the critical gap between evolving smart device capabilities and
accessible software infrastructure in SMCBs. The main contributions of this work are:
 • A generalizable web user interface framework that provides essential BEMS functionality—including
 real-time data visualization, unified monitoring, remote control, and configuration-driven interfaces
 that dynamically adapt to heterogeneous building systems, significantly reducing deployment
 complexity across various system configurations.
 • An automated configuration conversion algorithm that transforms a unified, natural-language-friendly
 YAML (YAML Ain’t Markup Language) specification into multiple heterogeneous system-specific
 configuration files, with the integrated capability to parse semantic building models (e.g., Brick
 Schema) for automated configuration generation, reducing labor-intensive manual setup processes.
 • A scalable deployment architecture integrating VOLTTRON open-source middleware to provide
 vendor-agnostic device connectivity through industry-standard protocols (BACnet and Modbus),
 eliminating proprietary ecosystem dependencies while ensuring compatibility with existing building
 automation infrastructure and IoT devices.

 2. GENERALIZABLE WEB USER INTERFACE FRAMEWORK
Existing BEMS deployment approaches for SMCBs face two fundamental challenges that impede
widespread adoption: (1) labor-intensive deployment processes that require extensive manual configuration
across multiple system components and (2) the lack of generalized interfaces capable of accommodating
heterogeneous building devices and HVAC configurations. This research proposes a generalizable web
user interface framework, a single interface that adapts to heterogeneous HVAC systems at the campus,
building, and system levels (Figure A-1), to address these challenges through two complementary
approaches. First, an automated configuration converter transforms a single, unified YAML specification
into multiple system-specific configuration files, eliminating fragmented manual processes while ensuring
data consistency across components, as described in Section 2.1. Second, a configuration-driven web
interface dynamically adapts navigation hierarchies, controller displays, and visualization parameters
to heterogeneous HVAC configurations without requiring separate interface development, as described
in Section 2.2. Moreover, the proposed framework leverages a scalable, open-source VOLTTRON
middleware architecture, enabling vendor-agnostic interoperability across heterogeneous building devices
through industry-standard protocols (BACnet and Modbus). These approaches are intended to reduce
deployment complexity and technical expertise requirements, eliminate vendor lock-in constraints, and
improve scalability across diverse SMCB environments.

2.1 AUTOMATED CONFIGURATION FOR STREAMLINED DEPLOYMENT
Conventional BEMS deployment requires manual creation of multiple, disparate configuration files
across system components, contributing to labor-intensive processes and technical expertise barriers.
This subsection presents the proposed framework’s approach to minimizing human involvement through
unified YAML configuration specification, automatic semantic model parsing, and automated conversion
algorithms that eliminate fragmented manual setup while ensuring data consistency across components.

2.1.1 Unified YAML Configuration
The proposed configuration converter aims to address the critical challenge of managing multiple,
disparate configuration files required by different system components in building energy management
deployments. Existing open-source system implementations typically require building operators to
manually create and maintain numerous configuration files. These files are distributed across different
directories and formatted according to specific software requirements for web servers, clients, middleware,
and device managers. This fragmented configuration approach contributes significantly to the labor-intensive
setup processes and technical expertise requirements that constitute one of the primary barriers to SMCB
automation adoption. The complexity is further compounded by the need to maintain data consistency
across multiple configuration files, where discrepancies in port numbers, device identifiers, or system
parameters can result in deployment failures requiring specialized technical knowledge to diagnose and
resolve.
To address these challenges, the proposed configuration converter consolidates all required system
configurations into a single, unified YAML file while automatically generating platform-specific configuration
files, thereby eliminating manual configuration errors and reducing deployment complexity (Predoaia
et al. 2024). YAML is selected for nontechnical users, providing natural-language-like key-value syntax
without requiring extensive quotation marks or the complex bracket and tag structures typical of JavaScript
Object Notation (JSON) and XML. Moreover, YAML offers several critical advantages over alternative
formats: (1) it natively supports various data types including booleans, strings, numbers, nested arrays

 6

with minimal syntax requirements, (2) it enables structured hierarchical data organization through flexible
indentation, and (3) it allows inline comments for documentation (Ben-Kiki, Evans, and Net 2009). This
format enables seamless automated conversion to multiple system-specific configuration files, ensuring
data consistency across components while maintaining human readability.
As illustrated in Figure 1, the automated conversion process employs a systematic algorithm that parses
and transforms the unified YAML input file into four distinct output configurations tailored to specific
system components. The converter generates separate environment variable files (.ENV) for both the
web client (containing client port, login information, API URLs, and application settings) and web
server (specifying server port, administrative credentials, database configurations, and API endpoints).
Simultaneously, the converter produces JSON configuration files for building unit specifications (including
building and system identifiers, controller variables, schedules, and user interface preference settings) and
VOLTTRON middleware configurations (encompassing VOLTTRON port assignments, agent identifiers,
data update frequencies, and data point specifications). The unified YAML file does not include all
configuration details but contains the essential information needed by nonexpert users to set up each
system component and deploy the entire system. The systematic transformation process ensures that all
interdependent system components initialize with consistent configuration parameters while maintaining
the specific formatting requirements necessary for seamless integration with the AEMS web client, AEMS
web server, and VOLTTRON middleware platforms.

2.1.2 Automated Configuration Conversion
The configuration converter implements several streamlined deployment features that enhance accessibility
for nontechnical building operators while handling potential errors throughout the conversion process.
Figure 2 illustrates the detailed examples of converting a unified YAML file into system-specific configuration
files required for system deployment. Natural-language-friendly configuration keys (e.g., application title)

 7

specified in the YAML file are automatically translated into machine-readable keys required by specific
system components (e.g., REACT_APP_TITLE), enabling building operators to configure systems using
intuitive terminology. Moreover, these natural-language key names can be customized according to user
preferences or operational conventions, providing flexibility for various operational environments. The
converter incorporates comprehensive error handling through optional value processing, where omitted
configuration parameters are automatically replaced with predefined default values to ensure successful
deployment even with incomplete user input. Additionally, although output files are generated using
relative file paths within the repository for simplified deployment, the system supports user-specified
absolute file paths through the configuration file path parameters, facilitating integration with existing
organizational file management and deployment workflows.
2.1.3 Semantic Building Model Import
The integration of semantic building models represents a critical advancement for scalable BEMS,
enabling standardized building representation and system interoperability across various building
configurations (Pritoni et al. 2021). In particular, Brick Schema has been widely recognized in research
studies and increasingly adopted by building applications (Pritoni et al. 2021). It represents building
systems in RDF format using a set of relationships of concepts for locations (e.g., rooms), equipment (e.g.,
HVAC), control point (e.g., set points), and measurement (e.g., temperature). To allow advanced users
to seamlessly import multiple building configurations from existing building management applications,
the proposed configuration converter leverages Brick Schema models by automatically parsing semantic
descriptions to generate building unit configuration files. The converter recognizes hierarchical building
elements such as “campus,” “building,” and “system” values through location classes (e.g., brick:Site,
brick:Building, and brick:HVAC_Zone) and their relationships (e.g., brick:hasPart). Control points can
be identified using equipment classes (e.g., brick:AHU and brick:VAV) and their association with point
classes (e.g., brick:hasPoint). The automatically generated files include essential information required for
system deployment, including campus, building, and system identifiers and control data points, thereby
eliminating the need for manual configuration document creation, as illustrated in Figure 3.

2.2.2 Open-Source, Scalable System Architecture
To enable vendor-agnostic interoperability across heterogeneous building devices, the proposed framework
employs a four-layer system architecture that integrates open-source VOLTTRON middleware with
industry-standard communication protocols. As illustrated in Figure 7, the system architecture of the
proposed framework consists of four layers: an application layer, a data management layer, a network layer,
and a data source layer.
Data Source Layer: The foundation layer encompasses three primary data source categories that provide
comprehensive building system coverage. Installed control and sensing devices (smart thermostats, IoT
environmental sensors, and energy meters) provide real-time operational data, and a building control
emulator enables testing and validation using advanced, high-fidelity virtual building simulations
(Blum et al. 2021). External data sources, including electricity pricing services, carbon-intensity data,
and weather APIs provide contextual information necessary for advanced energy management and
demand-response strategies (Ma et al. 2021; OpenWeather, n.d.; National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(NREL), n.d.; National Energy System Operator (NESO), n.d.).
Network Layer: The communication layer manages data transmission between the data source layer and the
middleware through multiple standardized protocols to ensure broad device compatibility across different
manufacturers and system types. This layer leverages widely adopted industry communication standards,
with BACnet and Modbus protocols providing robust connectivity with building automation equipment
commonly found in commercial buildings (International Organization for Standardization 2004; Modbus
IDA 2004). Additionally, the layer supports Message Queueing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) messaging
protocol for wireless integration with IoT devices and smart building components, whereas Hypertext

Additionally, VOLTTRON middleware allows integration with Distributed Network Protocol 3 (DNP3),

2.3 INTEGRATED DEPLOYMENT WORKFLOW
As presented in Figure 8, the proposed framework integrates the unified YAML configuration (Section 2.1.1),
automated configuration converter (Section 2.1.2), automatic semantic model parsing (Section 2.1.3),
configuration-driven web interfaces (Section 2.2.1), and VOLTTRON-based scalable system architecture
(Section 2.2.2) into a streamlined BEMS deployment workflow that systematically addresses both
labor-intensive deployment processes and interoperability limitations. As illustrated in Figure A-2, the
deployment workflow consists of four sequential phases that transform a single, unified configuration
specification into a fully operational BEMS capable of accommodating heterogeneous HVAC configurations
and building equipment without manual customization.

 Figure 8. Overview of the proposed system and research workflow.

Phase 1: General Configuration File Creation: The workflow begins with the creation of a single,
human-readable YAML configuration file that serves as the centralized specification for the entire BEMS.
This approach streamlines extensive manual configuration across multiple software components and
interfaces required for BEMS deployment. The YAML format was selected because of its intuitive,
natural-language-like syntax, enabling building operators and system integrators to define comprehensive
system configurations without requiring specialized programming knowledge (Ben-Kiki, Evans, and Net
2009; Schiera et al. 2021). This configuration file enables building operators to specify web client settings
(application titles and user interface preferences), web server configurations (administrative credentials
and port assignments), building unit descriptions (system types, default set points, and schedules), and
middleware platform settings (VOLTTRON port configurations and driver agent information). This unified
configuration approach eliminates the fragmented setup procedures of existing solutions while providing a
single, comprehensive file that documents essential system settings for simplified maintenance and future
modification.
Phase 2: Configuration Input File Conversion: The second phase employs an automated configuration
converter that transforms the YAML file into multiple platform-specific configuration files required by
different system components. This conversion process generates environment variable files (.ENV),
JSON configuration files, and other format-specific inputs necessary for seamless integration with the
underlying middleware platform and web application frameworks. The automated conversion mechanism
addresses one of the primary cost drivers identified in traditional BEMS implementations by eliminating

 14

the manual configuration processes that typically require specialized technical expertise and contribute
to the substantial deployment labor costs (Trenbath et al. 2022; Neukomm, Nubbe, and Fares 2019). By
automating this conversion process, the proposed approach significantly reduces both manual configuration
errors and deployment time, ensuring consistency across different system components. The detailed
conversion process and converter implementation are described in Section 2.1.2.
Phase 3: Application Installation and Execution: The third phase focuses on system deployment
through a Docker containerized architecture (Docker, n.d.) that encapsulates the middleware, web
server, and database components in portable, isolated environments for consistent deployment across
various hardware configurations. The web server container seamlessly communicates with multiple
client applications through RESTful APIs, enabling real-time data exchange and concurrent user access,
whereas the VOLTTRON middleware container establishes direct communication channels with building
devices via standardized BACnet and Modbus protocols (Katipamula et al. 2016). This containerized
approach eliminates platform-specific dependencies and enables rapid automated deployment while
supporting scalable multiclient connections and real-time data synchronization. The standardized
container deployment process minimizes manual configuration requirements and reduces technical
expertise barriers, allowing building operators to launch comprehensive monitoring systems through
simplified, automated deployment scripts.
Phase 4: Building System Monitoring and Control: The final phase provides building operators with
comprehensive web-based interfaces that dynamically adapt to user preferences specified in the YAML
configuration file. The responsive interface automatically displays relevant controllers based on the
configured building systems while enabling visualization customization through selectable chart types
(line charts, scatterplots, and box plots), configurable variables for data plotting, and adjustable visual
parameters, including line types, colors, and widths. The interactive dashboards present customizable
charts for energy consumption tracking, indoor environment monitoring with configured set points,
and equipment status displays with flexible scheduling interfaces that support daily schedules, national
holidays, and customized schedule and holiday configurations. The web interface responds to configuration
settings specified in the YAML file, enabling building operators to modify system parameters and
operational schedules through customized graphical user interfaces that eliminate the need for specialized
programming expertise. Detailed implementation of these configuration options and interface customization
capabilities is described in Section 2.2.1.

The proposed generalizable web user interface framework delivers four primary benefits that address the
critical barriers impeding widespread BEMS adoption in the SMCB sector:
 • Minimized manual configuration efforts: The unified YAML configuration and automated conversion
 mechanism consolidate fragmented manual processes into streamlined workflows, enabling building
 operators to deploy BEMS without specialized programming expertise or labor-intensive setup
 procedures.
 • Automated semantic model integration: The automated Brick Schema parsing capability facilitates
 integration with existing semantic building models, reducing the human effort required to create
 configurations and enabling the systematic reuse of standardized building representations across
 multiple facilities.
 • Dynamic adaptability to heterogeneous systems: The configuration-driven web user interface
 dynamically adapts to various HVAC configurations without requiring separate interface development
 for each system type and is designed to scale from individual buildings to campus-wide deployment.

 3. VALIDATION
The validation process evaluates two critical capabilities of the proposed generalizable web user interface
across two distinct usage scenarios: (1) manual YAML-based configuration for buildings without existing
semantic models (Section 3.1) and (2) automated conversion from existing semantic building models
(Section 3.2). The first scenario addresses new deployments in which building operators must create
system configurations from scratch, whereas the second scenario targets facilities with established
semantic building models that seek to leverage existing model files for rapid deployment. This approach
demonstrates both the platform’s flexibility in accommodating various system types through a unified
YAML configuration and its capability to eliminate manual setup by automatically parsing semantic
building models.

3.1 SIMULATION-BASED DEPLOYMENT TEST
The simulation-based deployment test validates the primary usage scenario in which building operators
create system configurations from scratch to deploy the BEMS in their facilities. This scenario requires
the manual creation of YAML configuration files to specify system parameters, control points, and
visualization preferences. Through this unified YAML configuration approach, the validation demonstrates
the web user interface’s capability to dynamically adapt to different HVAC system and visualization
configurations without interface modifications. This testing leverages the BOPTEST, which provides
high-fidelity virtual building control test beds for systematic evaluation under controlled conditions (Blum
et al. 2021).
To evaluate the platform’s flexibility across different HVAC system types, the test employs a single
YAML configuration file that encompasses two distinct BOPTEST test cases: BESTEST Air representing
a fan-coil-based cooling and heating system, and BESTEST Hydronic representing a radiator-based
heating system. This unified configuration file specifies both system configurations within a hierarchical
structure, defining separate building units with their respective control points, visualization parameters,
and operational schedules. The configuration converter processes this comprehensive YAML file to
generate two system-specific JSON files that enable the web application to visualize and seamlessly switch
between the two HVAC configurations without requiring separate deployments or interface modifications.
The validation process involves three sequential phases. First, the unified YAML configuration file
is created to define both HVAC systems, including their distinct control variables (fan speeds and air
temperatures for the fan-coil-based system; pump speeds and water temperatures for the radiator-based
system), visualization preferences, and system-specific parameters. Second, the configuration converter
automatically transforms this single YAML file into multiple system-specific configuration files, including
environment variable files for web client and server components, building unit specifications for both test
cases, and configuration files for VOLTTRON middleware and its driver agent for each HVAC system
type. Third, the web application is deployed using these generated configuration files to demonstrate its
ability to dynamically render appropriate control interfaces and monitoring dashboards for each system
configuration based solely on the configuration specifications.

3.2 SEMANTIC BUILDING MODEL CONFIGURATION TEST
The semantic model test demonstrates the second usage scenario, in which facilities possess Brick Schema
documentation of their building systems. This scenario enables building operators to automatically
generate building unit configuration files by directly importing semantic building models while still
specifying other system parameters (web client, web server, and middleware settings) in a YAML
document. The test employs an industrial Brick model representing a complex commercial building
environment to evaluate the configuration converter’s capability to automatically extract hierarchical
building structures, identify control points, and generate deployment-ready configuration files from
existing semantic descriptions without manual intervention. This automation significantly reduces the
configuration effort required of operators with established semantic building models.

3.2.1 Studied Semantic Building Model
The validation uses the reference Brick model bldg27 obtained from the Brick Ontology website, which
represents a validated semantic description of an anonymized real building in Turtle data format (Brick,
n.d.; Fierro et al. 2019). This model represents a real commercial building with a 20,605 ft2 (1,913 m2 )
footprint and a multizone HVAC system, providing an appropriate test bed for demonstrating the platform’s
capability to process real-world building complexity in semantic data format. The studied model features
a representative variable air volume (VAV)-based HVAC system configuration commonly found in small

 20

commercial buildings. The system consists of one air handling unit (AHU) (AC07) serving 17 VAV boxes
distributed across two floors. Each VAV box feeds a dedicated HVAC zone corresponding to individual
rooms, with the first floor containing seven zones and the second floor containing 10 zones.
The semantic model includes comprehensive point definitions for each component: the AHU contains
nine control and sensor points such as supply air temperature set point, mixed air temperature, and return
air temperature Each VAV box incorporates three control points, including zone temperature set points,
a damper position set point, and heating mode. Together with additional zone-level sensor points, this
yields approximately 100 distinct control and sensor points across the building, providing a robust test
case for evaluating the converter’s ability to parse complex hierarchical relationships and generate accurate
configuration files from a real, multizone semantic model.

3.2.2 Validation Process and Results
The conversion process begins with importing a comprehensive Brick model that includes hierarchical
levels (building and zones), various equipment types (AHUs, VAV boxes, and dampers), and extensive
control point definitions (set points, sensors, and HVAC mode). The configuration converter automatically
parses the RDF-formatted Brick model, identifying building elements through their semantic relationships
and class definitions. The converter recognizes spatial hierarchies through location classes (e.g., brick:Site,
brick:Building, and brick:HVAC_Zone) and their brick:hasPart relationships, whereas equipment and
control points are identified through equipment classes (e.g., brick:AHU and brick:VAV) and their
associated point classes via brick:hasPoint relationships. Because the studied Brick model lacks campus
information and default control values required for BEMS deployment, the converter automatically
supplements these missing parameters with predefined default values that users can easily adjust according
to their operational preferences. Upon successful parsing, the converter generates a structured building
configuration file that preserves the semantic model’s hierarchical organization while translating it into
the JSON format required by the web application, as shown in Figure 13. The deployed web interface
dynamically renders the imported building structure, automatically generating navigation hierarchies
that reflect the campus–building–zone relationships defined in the original Brick model. This automated
translation eliminates the manual configuration processes typically required when implementing building
management systems for existing facilities with established semantic models, demonstrating the platform’s
capability to leverage standardized building information models for rapid deployment.

 4. FIELD DEMONSTRATION
Field testing was conducted in an occupied commercial building to demonstrate the feasibility of the
platform’s deployment workflow under real operating conditions. Unlike the controlled simulation
environment, this real-world testing demonstrates the platform using smart thermostats and energy meters
across different zones within the same building through the unified YAML configuration approach. It
specifically covers the complete deployment workflow, from YAML configuration creation to automated
conversion, system deployment, and multizone monitoring. By implementing configured thermostat
set points and schedules across multiple zones, the demonstration assesses the platform’s practical
applicability for scalable building energy management in SMCB environments.

4.1 STUDIED BUILDING
The studied building is a two-story commercial office building located in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, within
ASHRAE climate zone 4A, characterized by mixed-humid climate (ASHRAE 2020), as shown in
Figure 14. This facility, originally constructed in 1988, underwent comprehensive envelope improvements
in 2023, representing a typical SMCB environment with enhanced thermal performance characteristics.
The building encompasses 1,035 m2 of total floor area organized through perimeter-core zoning, where
the central core accommodates common areas including conference rooms and common spaces, whereas
the perimeter zones comprise individual office spaces. The building accommodates approximately 60
occupants following typical commercial office schedules, with operational hours from 7:00 a.m. to 5:00
p.m. on weekdays. The facility employs an all-electric HVAC infrastructure comprising heterogeneous
system types: RTUs provide centralized climate control for core zones and corridors, whereas individual
package terminal air conditioning units (PTACs) serve each perimeter office space. Each HVAC unit
operates through dedicated ecobee smart thermostat controls that enable Wi-Fi connectivity and vendor
API integration for comprehensive building management capabilities. To enable real-time energy
monitoring, the building incorporates a power-metering infrastructure in which individual current
transformers monitor each HVAC circuit dedicated to a specific HVAC unit. These transformers communicate
with a centralized meter that connects to a computer via Modbus Ethernet protocol, enabling periodic
power data collection at 1-minute intervals. The collected energy data are subsequently forwarded to
the VOLTTRON middleware platform through an API. This smart thermostat and energy-metering
infrastructure provides real-time access to zone temperatures, power consumption, and remote set point

same 70°F heating and 74°F cooling set points during occupied periods but raised the cooling set point

4.4 REQUIRED EFFORT FOR THE DEPLOYMENT PROCESS
To quantitatively evaluate the required effort for deployment process, a test was conducted with a junior
researcher with a Ph.D. in electrical engineering who was not involved in this application development.
First, a 40-minute training session was provided to introduce the general concept of this project and
deployment process, including writing YAML configuration files and installing Docker containers, as
described in Section 2.3 and illustrated in Figure A-2. The researcher then independently implemented the
deployment process while the required time was measured. The complete deployment process for Room
125 required 1 hour and 27 minutes of labor, encompassing downloading and extracting repository files (8
minutes), YAML configuration file creation and automated conversion (10 minutes), and Docker container
installation with system initialization (9 minutes). The researcher spent approximately 1 hour debugging
to correct YAML syntax errors during configuration conversion and address Docker installation issues
caused by software version compatibility. The measured deployment effort provides empirical evidence
for evaluating the proposed framework’s practical applicability and labor requirement reduction compared
with conventional BEMS deployment approaches, as discussed in Section 5.

 5. DISCUSSION
The simulation-based testing and field deployment demonstrate that the proposed generalizable web
user interface framework addresses key technical barriers impeding wider adoption of BEMS within the
SMCB sector. Specifically, the framework adapts a single configuration-driven interface to heterogeneous
building systems, automates the conversion of a unified specification into system-specific configuration
files to reduce manual setup, and supports vendor-agnostic device integration through industry-standard
protocol support. The following discussion synthesizes these findings into the key contributions and their
implications for scalable building energy management deployment in SMCB environments.
Configuration-driven adaptability to heterogeneous building systems: Simulation-based testing demonstrated
the framework’s adaptability across heterogeneous HVAC configurations, deploying both a fan-coil-based
system with supply air temperature and fan-speed controls (BESTEST Air) and a radiator-based system
with pump and water temperature controls (BESTEST Hydronic) through a single, unified YAML
specification. As demonstrated in Section 3.1.3, the web interface automatically adapted system-appropriate
control panels, visualization charts (box plots for BESTEST Air versus scatterplots for BESTEST Hydronic),
and operational schedules without manual interface customization. The field demonstration in Section 4.3
further validated this adaptability through real-time monitoring of three office zones with smart thermostat
and energy meter configurations while implementing two distinct operational strategies, a schedule-based
setback control versus a constant set point baseline, within the unified web user interface. These results
indicate that building operators can deploy monitoring dashboards across various building systems without
developing separate interfaces for each configuration, reducing deployment complexity for comprehensive
automation in the SMCB sector.
Automated configuration conversion eliminating manual setup processes: The automated configuration
converter validated seamless transformation from unified YAML specifications to system-specific
configuration files across multiple test scenarios, mitigating the labor-intensive setup processes identified
as primary cost barriers. The simulation-based deployment test in Section 3.1.2 demonstrated the
conversion of unified, human-readable YAML specifications into four system-specific configuration files
(web client, web server, building unit, and VOLTTRON middleware) for both test cases, maintaining data
consistency across interdependent system components without manual intervention. The semantic building
model test in Section 3.2 validated seamless transformation from the industrial Brick model containing
17 VAV zones into deployment-ready building unit configurations without manual intervention. The
automated converter successfully parsed hierarchical relationships (brick:hasPart), identified equipment
classes (brick:AHU and brick:VAV), and extracted control points (brick:hasPoint) while automatically
supplementing missing default values with user-adjustable defaults. Furthermore, the deployment-effort
evaluation in Section 4.4 showed that configuring and deploying a single-zone monitoring interface for
the studied building required 1 hour and 27 minutes of labor for one operator, an approximately 98%
reduction compared with the 65 hours that Guo et al. (2025) reported for the software installation and
configuration of a conventional BEMS in a similarly sized SMCB. This reduction indicates that the
automated configuration process can substantially mitigate the technical expertise and labor requirements
for BEMS deployment.
Vendor-agnostic interoperability through standardized protocol integration: In the field demonstration
described in Section 4.3, the platform integrated ecobee smart thermostats through their vendor-specific
API and Schneider power meters over the Modbus protocol, interfacing with both simultaneously without
device-specific integration programming. This vendor-agnostic interoperability eliminates proprietary
ecosystem dependencies that constrain existing API-based solutions, enabling building operators to

 27

leverage various building devices without vendor lock-in constraints or the substantial licensing costs
associated with commercial integrated software.

 6. CONCLUSION
This research addresses the critical gap between advancing smart device capabilities and accessible
software infrastructure in the SMCB sector by developing a generalizable web user interface framework.
The proposed platform mitigates deployment complexity through automated configuration conversion,
industry-standard protocol integration, and configuration-driven user interfaces. By consolidating
fragmented setup processes into unified YAML specifications and leveraging open-source VOLTTRON
middleware, the framework reduces labor-intensive configuration requirements that constitute substantial
implementation cost barriers, enabling building operators to deploy comprehensive monitoring systems
without specialized programming expertise or proprietary ecosystem dependencies.
Demonstrations through simulation-based testing and a field deployment illustrate the platform’s adaptability
across heterogeneous HVAC configurations, its automated semantic-model parsing, and its practical
operational feasibility. The simulation-based testing showed interface adaptation between fan-coil-based
and radiator-based systems without manual customization, whereas Brick Schema integration enabled
automated conversion of a real multizone semantic building model into a deployment-ready configuration.
The field demonstration confirmed practical deployment in an occupied office building, including real-time
multizone monitoring and the implementation and visualization of two contrasting set point strategies.
Consequently, these results indicate that the platform can accommodate heterogeneous building systems
through streamlined deployment workflows that substantially reduce software installation and configuration
effort.
The developed framework establishes a foundation for scalable building energy management in the
underserved SMCB sector, transforming previously inaccessible automation capabilities into practical
deployment opportunities. By mitigating substantial upfront investments, vendor lock-in constraints,
and specialized technical expertise requirements, this research offers opportunities for implementing
sophisticated energy optimization strategies. The platform’s open-source architecture and standardized-protocol
support provide a foundation for future integration of advanced control algorithms, grid-interactive
capabilities, and campus-wide building energy management, contributing to significant energy performance
enhancement in the commercial building sector.

6.1 LIMITATIONS
Although the demonstration results show the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed generalizable
web user interface framework, several limitations still remain. First, the field demonstration was constrained
to a single commercial office building with ecobee smart thermostats and PTAC/RTU systems. Although
the simulation-based testing demonstrated adaptability across fan-coil and hydronic radiator configurations,
comprehensive validation requires deployment across various SMCB environments with varying HVAC
types, building uses, and geographic locations, as well as simultaneous deployment across multiple
buildings on a campus to assess scalability.
Second, although the semantic model integration was validated using a single industrial Brick model
representing a 17-zone VAV system, testing various Brick models with varying HVAC types and hierarchical
structures would ensure robust conversion capabilities. Additionally, compatibility with other widely
adopted semantic model frameworks remains unexplored, potentially limiting interoperability with
industrial semantic building models.
Third, although the field demonstration acquired power-meter data over Modbus, this data reached the
platform through an intermediate API rather than the native VOLTTRON fieldbus drivers; end-to-end
validation of the native drivers for BACnet, Modbus, KNX, LonWorks, and MQTT remains future work.

 29

Fourth, the field demonstration employed only a basic schedule-based setback strategy over two test days.
Extended deployment incorporating sensor-driven occupancy, seasonal variations, and advanced control
algorithms is necessary to quantify long-term performance benefits and system reliability under various
operational and climate conditions.
Fifth, although the deployment-effort measurement in Section 4.4 provides a single-subject, single-zone
data point, a quantitative cost–benefit analysis comparing total implementation costs, including labor hours
across multiple nonexpert operators, with those of conventional BEMS applications was not conducted.
Such economic validation would strengthen the value proposition for SMCB operators considering
platform adoption.

6.2 FUTURE WORK
Future research directions can advance the platform’s capabilities and practical applicability across broader
SMCB contexts. First, multisite deployment validation across SMCBs with varying HVAC configurations,
building types, and climates would enable a comprehensive scalability evaluation of the proposed platform.
Large-scale campus deployment studies could assess concurrent multibuilding management capabilities,
network scalability, and centralized monitoring performance under real-world operational conditions with
varying system complexities and deployment scenarios.
Second, validating semantic model integration across various industry Brick models with varied equipment
types, control hierarchies, and point definitions would ensure robust conversion capabilities across a range
of real-world building configurations. Furthermore, expanding integration to the Project Haystack or
ASHRAE 223P ontology frameworks would enhance interoperability with various BEMS applications
(Project Haystack, n.d.; Fierro, n.d.). Future work could explore automated translation or semantic
alignment enhancement, facilitating seamless conversion and bidirectional data exchange with industrial
building management applications employing various semantic representations.
Third, integrating advanced control strategies would unlock sophisticated energy-optimization capabilities,
including model predictive control, reinforcement learning, and ASHRAE Guideline 36 standardized
sequences (Blum et al. 2022; Faulkner et al. 2023; Wang and Hong 2020). Future work could develop
modular control frameworks within the open-source architecture, enabling building operators to deploy
various control strategies through the proposed web interfaces while incorporating external data sources
such as weather forecasts, occupancy predictions, and dynamic electricity price signals. This integration
would enable BEMS platforms to autonomously optimize energy performance while maintaining occupant
comfort, reducing reliance on manual interventions and unlocking grid-interactive capabilities for flexible
demand response.
Fourth, a comprehensive cost–benefit analysis comparing implementation costs, operational savings, and
return on investment with those of conventional BEMS solutions would provide quantitative evidence
supporting adoption decisions for the proposed platform. Comparative studies employing extensive surveys
and focus group interviews with SMCB operators could validate practical advantages while identifying
deployment barriers. Furthermore, such research could provide a foundation for developing standardized
cost–benefit frameworks accounting for hardware investments, installation labor, software licensing,
maintenance requirements, and realized energy savings, enabling SMCB owners to make informed
investment decisions based on building-specific deployment scenarios.
