Microbial community transition and carbon metabolism plasticity under continuous evaporative salinization in hypersaline brines
Authors: Ya Zhang, Jiamin Liu, Mingxian Han, Jianrong Huang, Jiyu Chen, Zhuoli Zhao, Jian Yang, Dongmei Yu, Jibin Han, Xiying Zhang, Hongchen Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Microbial dominance shifted from Euryarchaeota to Proteobacteria, with increasing α-diversity.•Potassium ion was a key driver, negatively regulating overall carbon metabolism but promoting amino acid utilization.•Carbon metabolic capacity showed plasticity, initially inhibited then recovered along the salinization gradient.•Microbial adaptive strategies mediating carbon cycling under extreme and fluctuating salinity.Keywords: Microbial community succession, Carbon metabolic plasticity, Hypersaline ecosystems, Potassium ion (K+) dynamics, Salinity gradient

Introduction

Saline lakes, covering 23% of the global lake area (Comín et al., 1999a), are vital for studying microbial adaptation and biogeochemical cycles, particularly carbon (C) turnover (Jiang et al., 2006; Sorokin et al., 2015). Microbes in these hypersaline environments drive crucial C processes like mineralization, methane production, and fixation (Yang et al., 2022; Hassan et al., 2025). However, it remains unclear whether microbial communities and their metabolic plasticity respond to evaporative salinization gradually or shift abruptly at critical salinity thresholds, and how such changes affect carbon utilization strategies. Recent studies along natural and experimental salinity gradients in Tibetan saline lakes have begun to address this gap (Huang et al., 2020, 2025). This critical knowledge gap hinders our ability to predict how biogeochemical functions in hypersaline ecosystems will persist under ongoing hydrological changes induced by global warming.

The evolution of saline lakes involves sequential mineral precipitation and brine concentration shifts (Qin et al., 2024). As salinity increases, ionic composition undergoes dramatic alterations—Na+ and Ca2+ precipitate early, followed by K+ and Mg2+ salts—fundamentally reshaping the physicochemical milieu (Alkhayer et al., 2023). These changes impose physiological constraints on microbes, selecting for halotolerant species (Oren, 2008; Vavourakis et al., 2018). While Archaea often dominate hypersaline systems (Jiang et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2022), some bacteria, like Proteobacteria, may thrive in moderately saline conditions (Yang et al., 2023). Yet, the sequential shifts in ionic composition (e.g., progressive K+ accumulation) during evaporative salinization raise unresolved questions about the temporal dynamics of microbial taxonomic dominance and functional reorganization—gaps that limit our understanding of how ecosystem functioning is maintained across continuous salinity gradients.

Carbon metabolism is key to ecosystem resilience. Salinity changes affect organic matter solubility and bioavailability, influencing microbial carbon substrate use (Abbott et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2025). High ionic strength can inhibit enzymes, reduce growth, and shift metabolism towards substrates like amino acids (Rietz and Haynes, 2003; Mavi and Marschner, 2017; Sun et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026). Potassium (K+), a crucial osmolyte, accumulates intracellularly to balance salt stress (Edbeib et al., 2016). While K+ homeostasis is understood at the cellular level (Stautz et al., 2021), its ecosystem-scale impact on community carbon utilization strategies, specifically metabolic trade-offs between carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism during salinization, is largely unexplored.

Studying these processes in natural salt lakes is challenging due to their slow evolution (Resetarits and Bernardo, 2001). Microcosm experiments often fail to replicate complex community interactions (Lou and Niu, 2005). Solar saltworks, however, offer a practical solution (Guntur et al., 2018). These engineered systems mimic natural evaporation, creating spatially distinct brine reservoirs representing successive salinization stages, effectively compressing geological time (Jiang et al., 2007). The Charkhan Salt Lake saltworks on the Tibetan Plateau, with its sequential precipitation of halite, sylvite, and carnallite, serves as an ideal model system to investigate microbial responses to controlled ionic shifts (Wei, 2019). This model system addresses the limitations of previous studies by enabling the tracking of microbial and metabolic changes across a continuous, ecologically relevant salinization gradient.

We hypothesize that (i) Microbial α-diversity is expected to increase with salinity due to niche partitioning, accompanied by a transition from archaeal to bacterial dominance. Previous observations have shown that bacterial lineages (e.g., Proteobacteria) can gain a distinct competitive advantage in K+-enriched brines by using diverse compatible solute strategies (Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019a); (ii) Potassium ion accumulation is expected to be strongly associated with carbon utilization patterns, suppressing overall carbon metabolic activity but promoting amino acid utilization as a functional catabolic trait (derived from Biolog-ECO profiling) as a compatible solute strategy. This is grounded in evidence that high ionic strength inhibits broad-spectrum metabolic activity (Rietz and Haynes, 2003) and that amino acid synthesis/uptake is a conserved osmoadaptive mechanism in halophiles (Oren, 2008; Stautz et al., 2021); and (iii) Community functional resilience will peak at intermediate salinities, reflecting metabolic adaptation to ionic stress. This aligns with studies showing that moderate salinity selects for metabolically flexible taxa, while extreme salinity reduces functional redundancy (Pavloudi et al., 2017; Xia et al., 2017). To test these hypotheses, we will address the two interconnected questions: How does evaporative salinization, mediated through sequential salt precipitation, restructure microbial community diversity and composition? How do changes in brine chemistry, particularly K+ flux, constrain microbial carbon substrate utilization capacity and preference?

By integrating high-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing with Biolog-ECO metabolic profiling across continuous rather than discrete evaporation stages, this study deciphers the coupled taxonomic and functional succession of brine microbiomes in a natural engineered system that replicates in situ ionic shifts (overcoming limitations of microcosm experiments). The objectives of this study are to 1) characterize microbial community succession across different evaporation stages (original brine → residue brine); 2) quantify microbial catabolic potential (carbon substrate utilization fingerprints via Biolog-ECO microplates) and screen the environmental factors most closely associated with these functional metabolic shifts; and 3) analyze the potential role of K+ in influencing microbial diversity and carbon cycling.

Materials and methods

Site description

Research was conducted on the northern shore salt field of Dabusun Salt Lake, the largest salt lake in the Charkhan region (Figure S2), located in Golmud River basin at an altitude of 2672–2681 m (Wei, 2019; Han et al., 2025a, 2025b). The area experiences a plateau inland basin climate with extreme aridity, low precipitation, and high evaporation. Salt precipitation follows a sequential transformation: rock salt → potassium rock salt → potassium carnallite → carnallite → magnesium hydrochlorite (Guntur et al., 2018). This progression creates a unique environment for ecological and microbiological studies.

Field measurements and sample collection

In August 2019, this study collected samples and conducted on-site measurements at five solarized salt reservoirs located on the north shore of Lake Dabusun. These included the original brine (CQ1), sodium brine reservoir (YT1), sodium regulating brine reservoir (YT2), carnallite reservoir (YT3), and residue brine reservoir (YT4) (Table 1). Geographical coordinates of sampling sites were recorded using a GPS instrument, and water pH levels were measured with a portable pH meter. Surface brine samples (0–10 cm depth) were collected from each site and sealed in sterile plastic buckets. To ensure sample quality, surface water was filtered immediately after returning to the laboratory using a 0.22 μm Whatman nitrocellulose membrane filter (GE Healthcare, UK). Three replicate samples were taken from each reservoir, creating a total of 15 samples (CQ11, CQ12, CQ13, YT11, YT12, YT13, YT21, YT22, YT23, YT31, YT32, YT33, YT41, YT42, and YT43; Table 1). The filtered membrane samples were then stored in a −80 °C freezer until further processing for DNA extraction and analysis. However, two of these samples (YT11 from the sodium salt pond and YT43 from the aged brine pond) were subsequently excluded from all analyses due to extremely low biomass, from which sufficient DNA could not be extracted. The remaining 13 samples were all successfully processed. The relative density of brine samples was calculated according to the method described by previous established methods (Wei, 2019).

Chemistry of brine samples

The filtrate was diluted with deionized water at a ratio of 1:100 or 1:1000, and then transferred into 2 mL sample vials for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of major ions, including Cl-, SO42-, K+, Na+, Mg2+, NO3-, PO43-, CO32-, HCO3-, Ca2+, Li+, Sr2+, B2O3, F-, Rb+, Cs+, I-, were measured using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS, Model 7900, Agilent Technologies, USA).

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplification, PCR product purification and sequencing

DNA extraction from brine samples was conducted utilizing the Fast DNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedical, Solon, OH, USA). To amplify the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene, primers 515F (5‘-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTA-3′) and 806R (5‘-CCCGYCAATTCMTTTRAGT-3′) incorporating barcode markers were employed (Caporaso et al., 2011). The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification system was composed of the following components: 2.5 μL of 10 × buffer, 2 μL of 2 mol/L dNTP, 1 μL each of primers 515F and 806R, 0.3 μL of rTaq polymerase, 1 μL of DNA template, 0.3 μL of BSA, with the final volume adjusted to 25 μL using deionized water (dd H2O). The protocol for PCR amplification included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 39 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 53 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s. A final extension was carried out at 72 °C for 10 min, concluding with a hold at 12 °C. The resulting PCR products were purified and recovered using a purification kit (Axygen, Union City, DE, USA). Subsequently, the purified PCR products were forwarded to Guangzhou Miseq Gene Technology Co. for high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina Miseq platform.

16S rRNA gene sequence processing

High-throughput sequencing data underwent stringent filtering and processing as described by Lynch and Neufeld (Lynch and Neufeld, 2015). Sequences were initially spliced using FLASH with default settings, and primers were trimmed to obtain the 16S rRNA gene sequences. Following the QIIME2 pipeline (version 2019.7) as recommended (https://qime2.org), low-quality sequences were removed based on several criteria: ambiguous bases, more than three low-quality bases (Q < 30), and sequences with <75% high-quality read length. Chimeric sequences were identified and excluded using USEARCH (http://www.drive5.com/usearch/). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were delineated with the UPARSE-OTU algorithm at a 97% sequence similarity threshold (Edgar, 2013). Representative sequences were classified against the RDP (Ribosomal Database Project) taxonomic database, with chloroplast-associated and ambiguously classified OTUs removed. Data normalization was performed to standardize the number of sequences per sample, enabling comparative analyses. Rarefaction curves were generated for each sample to assess sequencing depth adequacy (Figure S1). The vegan package in R was utilized for this normalization and subsequent statistical analyses. Microbial α-diversity, including observed OTUs, Shannon-Wiener index, and Simpson Index, were calculated using vegan to quantify the diversity, richness and evenness of microbial communities within the brine samples (Table S1).

Biolog cultivation and test analysis

For each sample, 150 μL of brine was inoculated into Biolog-ECO microtiter plates (Biolog, Hayward, USA) and incubated at 28 °C. Absorbance was measured every 24 h at 590 nm, 590–750 nm, and 750 nm using a Biolog reader. From these readings, two metrics were calculated per microwell: Average Well Color Absorbance Development (AWCD), reflecting overall microbial activity and metabolic diversity, and the number of utilized carbon source species (S), defined as the number of wells with an absorbance value > 0.2 after 192 h of incubation (indicating the range of metabolized carbon sources). These metrics provide insight into the metabolic potential and ecological interactions of the microbial communities.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were primarily performed in R (version 4.5.1). Heatmaps were generated with pheatmap, and Venn diagrams were created using VennDiagram, futile.logger, and grid. Multivariate analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA), were conducted at the OTU level using the vegan package. To reduce the influence of rare OTUs and avoid overfitting in constrained ordination, OTUs with a relative abundance below 0.05% were excluded prior to CCA and db-RDA analyses. All permutation tests were performed with 999 replicates. The adjusted R² (RsquareAdj) was used as the primary indicator of model explanatory power. In addition, distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) based on Bray-Curtis distance was performed using the capscale function in the vegan package to complement the CCA, with environmental factors standardized prior to analysis. Forward selection was applied to the db-RDA model using the ordistep function (999 permutations) to identify the most parsimonious set of environmental variables contributing to community structure. Each treatment had three replicates. For Venn diagrams, OTUs were averaged across replicates. Taxa with <0.01% relative abundance were excluded. PCA of AWCD was also performed with vegan to analyze differences in carbon source utilization patterns among samples. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test was used to verify the significance of α-diversity indices (observed OTUs, Shannon-Wiener Index, Simpson Index) and carbon metabolic metrics (AWCD, S-value) across samples (p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant). Relationships between AWCD, utilized carbon sources (S-value), and environmental factors were visualized using Hmisc and ggplot2, providing insight into microbial community dynamics, metabolic profiles, and environmental interactions.

This study focused on the major ions that define the salt precipitation sequence (K+, Na+, Mg2+, Cl⁻, SO42⁻, and others), so as to characterize the primary salinity gradient across the salterns. There were no leftover samples for measurements of total organic carbon, total nitrogen, or total phosphorus because all collected water was used for ion analysis, microbial filtration (for DNA extraction), and Biolog-ECO inoculation due to the limited volume of brine samples. Given that sequential salt precipitation, rather than nutrient availability, is the primary driver of microbial community succession in this system, the lack of bulk nutrient data has no bearing on the testing of our central hypotheses. Future research that includes nutrient measurements will help to better understand carbon cycling mechanisms in these harsh habitats.

Results

Physiochemical characteristics of brines from different reservoirs

Table 2 presents the physicochemical characteristics of brines from five distinct reservoirs. The brines exhibited a slightly acidic nature, with pH values ranging from 5.32 (YT4) to 6.60 (CQ1). A statistically significant decreasing pH gradient was observed, transitioning from CQ1 to YT4 throughout the salinization process. All reservoirs contained saturated brine, and relative density increased progressively during the process. The recorded relative densities were: CQ1 (1.22), YT1 (1.24), YT2 (1.27), YT3 (1.29), and YT4 (1.32). The corresponding total salinity (sum of all measured ions) increased along the salinization gradient from CQ1 (323,618 mg/L) to YT4 (447,986 mg/L), categorizing all reservoirs as hypersaline (salinity > 7% w/v) according to standard classifications (Comín et al., 1999a).

Ion concentration trends also varied across the salting process. The concentration of Na+ ions decreased gradually. Conversely, Mg2+ ions exhibited a consistent increase. K+ ions first increased and then decreased. The concentration of SO42⁻ ions remained relatively stable, with a slight increase. In contrast, the concentration of Cl⁻ ions was consistently high and increased notably throughout the process. The non‑linear fluctuation of K+ concentration along the salinization sequence reflects the mineral precipitation sequence of the saltworks. From CQ1 to YT2, extensive precipitation of halite (NaCl) progressively concentrated K+ in the residual brine, leading to a peak at YT2 (15,332 mg/L). Beyond this point, carnallite (KCl·MgCl₂·6H₂O) saturation was reached, and rapid K+ removal via carnallite crystallization caused a steep decline in its concentration at YT3 and YT4. This pattern contrasts with the steady concentration increase of conservative ions such as Cl⁻ and Mg2+, and the monotonic decrease of Na+.

Diversity of microbial communities in brines from different reservoirs

The α-diversity of microbial communities varied significantly across the five brine reservoirs, providing insights into how the salinization process shapes microbial communities. Diversity was quantified by OTUs, Shannon-Wiener Index, and Simpson Index (Table S1), with the sodium-regulating reservoir (YT2) exhibiting the highest diversity (711 OTUs, Shannon-Wiener Index = 5.44, Simpson Index = 0.98), and the original brine (CQ1) showing the lowest (146 OTUs, Shannon-Wiener Index = 3.58, Simpson Index = 0.94). This implies a significant influence of the salinization process on the structure of each community (Table 1).

Microbial community composition at the phylum level

After quality filtering and chimera removal, a total of 157,275 high‑quality 16S rRNA gene sequences were obtained from the 13 retained brine samples (two low‑biomass samples, YT11 and YT43, were excluded), with an average of 12,098 reads per sample (Table S1). These sequences were clustered into 4486 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity.

In line with the α-diversity patterns, the community composition also underwent a clear succession (Fig. 1). The most abundant microbial taxa shifted dramatically throughout the salinization process (Fig. 1). In CQ1, Euryarchaeota was the most abundant phylum, accounting for an average relative abundance of 71.2% across the three replicate samples (CQ11, CQ12, CQ13). Representative OTUs including OTU1 (Haloparvum), OTU2 (Halomicroarcula), and OTU3 (Halorubrum) (Table S2). By YT1, Proteobacteria began to increase (e.g., OTU170, Acetothermia clone OPB14), while the relative abundance of Euryarchaeota declined. Finally, in YT2, YT3, and YT4, the communities were characterized by increasing proportions of Chloroflexi (OTU161, OTU162), Crenarchaeota (OTU151, OTU160, OTU167), and Proteobacteria (e.g., OTU132 (Thiobacillus), OTU159 (Desulfotignum), OTU164 (Rhodobacteraceae)) (Table S2), indicating a major transition in the most abundant phyla from Euryarchaeota during CQ1 and YT1 to Proteobacteria in the later stages.

Microbial distribution in brines of different reservoirs

Detailed analysis of OTU distribution (Table S1 & Figure S3) revealed how community composition varied across reservoirs. The average number of OTUs identified in each reservoir was as follows: CQ1 (146), YT1 (507), YT2 (711), YT3 (673), and YT4 (618). The Venn diagram (Figure S3) presented both the OTU count and unique OTU count for each: CQ1: 36 unique OTUs (21.2% of total); YT1: 23 unique OTUs (4.5% of total); YT2: 200 unique OTUs (28.1% of total); YT3: 256 unique OTUs (38.0% of total); YT4: 266 unique OTUs (43.0% of total). The trend of the number of OTUs showed the following: YT2 (711) > YT3 (673) > YT4 (618) > YT1 (507) > CQ1 (146). The ratio of unique OTUs showed the following trend: YT4 (43.0%) > YT3 (38.0%) > YT2 (28.1%) > CQ1 (21.2%) > YT1 (4.5%). The increased OTU richness observed from YT2 onwards highlights the impact of further salinization on community structure. Additionally, CQ1 and YT1 shared the largest overlap (36 and 29 OTUs, respectively). The increase in unique OTUs across YT2, YT3, and YT4 reflects the environmental selection pressures as salinization progressed.

Analysis of the most abundant OTUs (relative abundance > 0.5%, Fig. 2 and detailed in Table S2) identified two principal groups, which showed a large variance in the most abundant OTUs in CQ1, when compared with the other reservoirs. Correlation analysis between microbial communities and environmental factors of brine reservoirs

The microbial community structure within this solar salt field exhibited differential responses to the environmental variables. To reduce noise from rare taxa and avoid overfitting, OTUs with relative abundance below 0.05% were excluded, retaining 263 OTUs from the original 2227 OTUs that accounted for the majority of sequence abundance. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) using pH, Sr2+, and K+ as explanatory variables yielded a highly significant overall model (permutation test, 999 permutations, p = 0.001), with an adjusted R2 of 0.638 (unadjusted R2 = 0.721). Marginal tests showed that each of the three variables independently contributed to the community structure: K+ (p = 0.002), Sr2+ (p = 0.009), and pH (p = 0.049). As visualized in the CCA biplot (Figure S4), the microbial communities in reservoirs CQ1, YT4, YT2, and YT3 were more strongly associated with K+ concentration (Table S3), while reservoir YT1 showed closer association with pH and Sr2+ concentrations. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) based on Bray-Curtis distances (Figure S5) confirmed these results (overall p = 0.001), with an adjusted R² of 0.710 (unadjusted R² = 0.783). Forward selection on the db-RDA model retained three variables — Sr2⁺, K⁺, and pH — in the final model, while other ions such as Mg2⁺ and Cl⁻ were not retained. The variables entered in the order: Sr2⁺ (p = 0.002) → K⁺ (p = 0.036) → pH (p = 0.003). These findings suggest that K⁺ is an important factor associated with community differentiation across most salinization stages, while Sr²⁺ and pH play also significant in specific reservoirs. The earlier entry of Sr2+ in the forward selection reflects its strong collinearity with pH (r = 0.90) and its association with the early-stage YT1 community, rather than a general ecological priority over K+.

Microbial carbon utilization in the studied brines

Temporal dynamics

AWCD analysis revealed the temporal dynamics of carbon utilization across the five studied brines (Figure S6). All communities displayed an initial adjustment phase, followed by logarithmic growth, and subsequent stabilization. The sodium-regulating brine (YT2) exhibited the slowest overall growth rate and lowest AWCD values, indicating delayed microbial adaptation. Stabilization occurred earlier in CQ1 (primary brine) and YT1 (sodium salt reservoir) compared to YT3 (carnallite) and YT4 (residue brine). AWCD values generally increased over the incubation period. CQ1 and YT1 showed higher AWCDs, with YT1 demonstrating a faster initial growth rate than CQ1. However, CQ1 ultimately reached a greater final AWCD than YT1. YT4 showed a final AWCD similar to CQ1, while YT3 exhibited the highest final values. This enhanced performance in YT3 is attributable to an extended acclimatization phase, after which substrate utilization potential increased significantly. Carnallite (YT3) and residue brine (YT4) reservoirs exhibited comparable carbon utilization patterns, characterized by a later exponential growth phase and intensified substrate utilization following acclimatization. The sodium-regulating reservoir (YT2) consistently displayed poorer carbon utilization across the 31 tested carbon sources compared to the other environments.

Carbon source preferences

Carbon source preferences varied significantly across the brine reservoirs (Figure S7). In CQ1 (protohaline), monosaccharides, glycosides, and polymeric sugars were preferred, followed by amines. Amino acids and esters showed consistently low utilization, with limited preference for alcohols. In YT1 (sodium salt), acids were favored, followed by esters and alcohols. Similar to CQ1, amino acids and amines were less preferred. The sodium-regulating brine (YT2) demonstrated the least diversity in carbon source utilization, relying mainly on monosaccharides, glycosides, and polymeric sugars, and largely ignoring esters, alcohols, and amines. In YT3 (carnallite), utilization encompassed monosaccharides, glycosides, polymeric sugars, amino acids, alcohols, and acids. The residue brine reservoir (YT4) showed this hierarchy: monosaccharides, glycosides, and polymeric sugars > amines > amino acids > acids > esters > alcohols. These patterns likely reflect the availability of substrates in each environment.

Metabolic activity and substrate diversity dynamics

AWCD patterns revealed complex dynamics (Fig. 3a). YT2 showed the lowest AWCD across all reservoirs, while YT4 had the highest, indicating weak initial carbon utilization, a nadir in YT2, and a peak in YT4. Carbon utilization was higher in YT3 and YT4, while substrate diversity remained relatively consistent across all reservoirs. S-values (substrate diversity index) followed a similar pattern (Fig. 3b). YT2 had the least substrate diversity, while all reservoirs utilized organic acids and monosaccharides, glycosides and polysaccharides. CQ1 favored polysaccharides and sugars, while YT1 favored acids. YT3 showed the broadest range (e.g., amino acids and alcohols) and YT4 more carbon source utilization compared to YT3.

Preferential carbon source utilisation

PCA (Figure S8a) indicated all five reservoirs could utilize substrates related to the first principal component (PC1). CQ1 and YT1 clustered on PC2, suggesting specific carbon source utilization in these environments. The clustering of CQ1, YT3, and YT4 supports concurrent carbon functions in these systems (use of monosaccharides, glycosides, and polymeric saccharides). Amines and acids were more significant for YT1. PC1 and PC2 jointly explain 89.8% of the difference in carbon source utilization among samples.

PCA of all 31 carbon sources (Figure S8b) revealed that YT2 and YT4 were positioned on the positive axes of both PC1 and PC2, showing a strong ability to utilize those carbon sources. PC1 and PC2 jointly explain 80.3% of the variance in 31 carbon source utilization. CQ1, YT1, and YT3 were limited to PC1’s positive range, suggesting restricted metabolic abilities. Carbon utilization by CQ1 and YT1 was comparable, implying functional similarities. The sodium-regulated (YT2) and carnallite (YT3) reservoirs exhibited comparable carbon utilization profiles. The residue brine reservoir (YT4) was characterized by L-arginine and L-phenylalanine (A4 and C4), while the carnallite reservoir (YT3) included indicators for 4-hydroxybenzoic acid and heparanose (D3 and F1). The original brine (CQ1) and sodium salt reservoirs (YT1) were more influenced by D-mannitol and 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (D2 and C3). The utilization of B-xylose and other sugars demonstrated similar carbon function in (CQ1, YT1).

Detailed analyses of carbon source utilization reveal that CQ1 exhibited the most distinct difference in carbon usage patterns compared to the other conditions (Fig. 4). The sodium-regulating (YT2) and residue brine (YT4) showed greater similarity regarding carbon source preferences. Both CQ1 and YT1 exhibited robust carbon utilization capabilities. CQ1 favored 2-hydroxybenzoic acid and D-xylose. Both CQ1 and YT1 were proficient in α-cyclodextrin and heparanose. CQ1 had better capabilities relating to L-asparagine, while YT1’s specialty was in metabolizing N-acetyl-D-glucosamine.

There were negative correlations with F⁻, Li+, SO42⁻, Cl⁻, and Mg2+, alongside a strong positive correlation with brine density and HCO3⁻ concentrations (Table S3). These results suggest the ion environment shapes the structure and function of the microbial communities in these hypersaline environments.

Discussion

Brine chemistry dynamics and its analogy to natural saline lake evolution

The brine reservoirs examined in this study mirrored the chemical dynamics observed in the natural evolution of saline lakes, demonstrating a sequential mineral precipitation pattern driven by salinity increases. As salinity increased, the anion profile shifted systematically. Initially carbonate, particularly CO32⁻, decreased with the precipitation of carbonate minerals and was followed by the decline of HCO3⁻. Simultaneously, SO42⁻ and Cl⁻ increased along the precipitation sequence. This parallels established patterns found in natural saline lake systems (Alkhayer et al., 2023). Cation precipitation also followed a predictable sequence: early-stage reductions in Ca2+ and Na⁺ were observed, giving way to a gradual decrease in K+, with Mg2+ ultimately becoming the dominant cation. The corresponding precipitation sequence was: Ca2+ > Na+ > K+ > Mg2+, consistent with the evolutionary trajectories of natural lakes (Alkhayer et al., 2023). The solar saltworks thus provides a suitable model system for investigating microbial community and carbon utilization changes associated with saline lake evolution.

Microbial community composition and diversity in response to salinization

Microbial diversity responded dynamically to increasing salinity, with notable shifts in community structure. A concurrent transition from archaeal (Euryarchaeota) to bacterial (Proteobacteria) dominance was observed, supporting a trend of overall community stabilization alongside diversification. This dynamic structure demonstrates the adaptive capacity of microbial taxa to environmental change, which reflects the functional importance of these ecosystems in ecological research.

The initial community structure in the CQ1 sample, exhibiting relatively low diversity, aligns with ecological theory describing reduced diversity in extreme environments (Shen et al., 2015; Pavloudi et al., 2017). Physicochemical data (Table 2) showed a significant reduction in Ca2+ and Na+ during the CQ1-YT2 phase, accompanied by shifts in carbonate speciation. We hypothesize that the high concentrations of these ions in the early reservoirs may have exerted a negative influence on microbial community development—an interpretation supported by previous reports that elevated Ca2+/Na+ can inhibit certain microbial metabolic pathways (Bassin et al., 2011; Farhangi et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2022). Direct experimental testing of the individual effects of Ca2+, Na+ and CO32⁻ is, however, beyond the scope of this field investigation.

The increase in potassium ion (K+) likely facilitated gradual community enrichment. The shift from CQ1 to YT2 corresponded with a reduced Na+/K+ ratio. Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2019b) observed a negative correlation between elevated Na⁺ and microbial diversity, suggesting potential growth inhibition. Potassium plays a critical role in halophilic prokaryotes, primarily through intracellular K+ accumulation to maintain osmotic balance (Edbeib et al., 2016). Concordantly, K+ enrichment has been linked with increased prokaryotic richness and community shifts in hypersaline settings, including soils and hypersaline sediments (Podell et al., 2014; Bryanskaya et al., 2016; Xia et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2025). K+ homeostasis is therefore essential for bacterial survival, influencing osmotic regulation, pH maintenance, protein synthesis, enzyme activation, membrane potential, and signal transduction (Stautz et al., 2021).

Successional changes over time revealed increasing community complexity, marked by dominance transition from Archaea (Euryarchaeota) to Bacteria (Proteobacteria). Specifically, the CQ1 brine, characterized by the simplest microbial profile, was dominated by archaea, distinct from subsequent sampling points. Post-sodium regulation (YT2), the increase in K+ coincided with bacterial takeover. Proteobacteria emerged as dominant, a trend that paralleled K+ enrichment during the CQ1–YT2 transition, consistent with previous reports that elevated K+/Na+ ratios favor Proteobacteria (Zhang et al., 2019a). Osmotic pressure maintenance in halophilic microorganisms is characterized by two adaptive strategies: accumulating K+ salts and exclusion of excess salt while synthesizing/accumulating compatible solutes (Oren, 2008). The archaeal-to-bacterial shift along the salinity gradient can be mechanistically linked to these contrasting osmoadaptive strategies. Halophilic archaea typically employ a “salt-in” strategy, accumulating molar concentrations of intracellular K+ to balance external osmotic pressure, which requires persistently high salinity for proteome stability. In contrast, halotolerant/halophilic bacteria often rely on a "salt-out" strategy, synthesizing or importing organic compatible solutes, which affords greater metabolic flexibility under fluctuating or sub-optimal salinity. Consequently, when K+ concentration declined sharply after YT2, the archaeal K⁺-based osmoregulatory framework may have been compromised, favouring the eventual dominance of metabolically flexible Proteobacteria in YT3 and YT4.

Previous studies have demonstrated the influence of K+ on archaeal and bacterial community structure in saline lakes (Podell et al., 2014). K+ has been linked to shifts in the OTU richness of various bacterial groups, including Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes (Kim et al., 2016; Xia et al., 2016). The relative abundance of Proteobacteria has been positively correlated with rising K⁺/Na⁺ ratios, alongside a decline in Bacteroidetes (Zhang et al., 2019a). These observations align with the enrichment of Proteobacteria during the protohaline to sodium salt reservoir transition (CQ1-YT1) in this study. Furthermore, significant associations were observed between the microbial community in the sodium salt reservoir (YT1) with pH and Sr2+ levels (Cho et al., 2016). Reduced Sr2+ levels during this phase suggest a diminished toxicity to bacterial communities (Small et al., 2001; Alshammari et al., 2021), though trace elements can influence extreme environment microbial community composition (Jiang et al., 2016). A significant association between Sr2+ and the YT1 community was also observed in our db-RDA model (p = 0.010). However, given its low concentration relative to K+ and its strong collinearity with pH (r = 0.90), we interpret this association as reflecting pH‑related geochemical gradients rather than a direct biological driver at the community level. Although typically present at low concentrations, Sr2⁺ can substitute for Ca2+ in biological systems under certain biochemical contexts (Small et al., 2001), and it remains possible that such effects occur at the cellular level without being fully captured by community‑level ordination. However, future studies specifically designed to disentangle the biological roles of trace elements in hypersaline environments are needed to evaluate this possibility.

As the salt-drying process advanced, community stability and complexity increased, beginning in the sodium-regulating reservoir (YT2). The abundance of the microbial community and compositional variability decreased, indicating a more stable state (Gao et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). This enrichment in composition contributed to ecosystem stabilization. The shift to bacterial dominance is connected to this stabilization. Salinophilic archaea, like those in the Halobacteriales, frequently produce bacteriocins with broad-spectrum bacteriostatic functions (Wetzel, 2001; Liu et al., 2022). The dominant OTU1, associated with Haloparvum and Halobacterium, both of the Halobacteriales, are known to generate halophilic bacteriocins, inhibiting archaeal competitors, and influencing community dynamics (Liu et al., 2022).

Ecosystem stabilization was correlated with increased species diversity throughout later stages though supporting evidence is somewhat limited. The transition from primary brine to sodium-regulating reservoir (CQ1-YT2), along the associated rise in K+, correlated with an increase in microbial diversity. However, the community structure was less impacted by subsequent decreases in K+ concentration in later stages. This resilience may be due to the coexistence of strategically-adapted microorganisms employing two osmotic balance mechanisms within the later stages. This diversity and adaptability likely enhanced community richness and bolsters its ability to withstand environmental fluctuations (Oren, 2008), illustrating the inherent interplay between microbial composition, diversity, and stability in brine ecosystems.

Microbial carbon utilization dynamics during salinization

Despite apparent slow growth rates observed, the increased diversity indicated a stronger collective growth capacity. The reduced number of wells displaying color changes and increased time to exhibit color shifts may reflect the inhibiting effect of high salinity on microbial activity. High salinity can negatively impact microbial metabolic pathways. This can reduce the growth rates and carbon utilization efficiency of microorganisms in high-salinity brines (Rietz and Haynes, 2003). Previous studies have demonstrated a negative correlation between salinity and TOC, implying that salinity affects organic carbon stability and adsorption kinetics, influencing microbial carbon substrate utilization (Mavi and Marschner, 2017). The initial decrease in the number of wells demonstrating substrate utilization reflects the inhibitory impact of high salinity levels.

The culture methods employed may not have fully supported all the unique requirements of high-salt-adapted microorganisms. The Biolog-ECO assays were conducted at 28 °C in standard medium (approximately 0.5–2% NaCl equivalent), far below the in situ salinities of the studied brines (323–448 g/L total salinity). Metabolic requirements of halophiles vary widely, culture conditions optimized for non-halophilic organisms might not adequately support all halophile growth. This discrepancy between metabolic potential (measured under non-halophilic conditions) and in situ activity is itself a key finding, suggesting that the true carbon metabolism in these hypersaline brines may be substantially different and potentially more specialized than what we observed. While the Biolog-ECO data serve as a valuable relative index for comparing functional differences among reservoirs under identical incubation conditions, future studies coupling stable isotope probing or in situ microcosm incubations are needed to verify actual metabolic rates under authentic hypersaline conditions. Despite this limitation, the Biolog-ECO data remain valuable as a relative index for comparing functional differences among reservoirs along the salinization gradient, because all samples were subjected to identical incubation conditions. Future studies coupling stable isotope probing or in situ microcosm incubations are needed to verify the actual carbon metabolic rates under authentic hypersaline conditions. The AWCD values for the Carnallite (YT3) and residue brine reservoirs (YT4) indicate possible earlier microbial stabilization. This implies a possible enrichment in microbial diversity, possibly enhancing the community’s capacity to adapt to further changing conditions (Trivedi et al., 2012; Chukwuneme et al., 2021).

Carbon metabolic capacity exhibited a dynamic pattern, which initially diminished and then increased with salting process progression. The potassium concentration demonstrated a significant negative correlation with carbon metabolism performance and the associated microorganisms involved in this process. To rigorously establish causality, correlation does not fully equate to causation. The non-linear behaviour of K+—gradually enriched to a peak from CQ1 to YT2, then slowly declined from YT3 to YT4—is intrinsically linked to the evaporite precipitation sequence (halite to carnallite). Meanwhile, strong collinearity among multiple co-varying ions (e.g., Mg2+, Cl⁻, Na+) cannot be excluded, which further complicates the differentiation of independent effects of individual ions. To rigorously establish causality, future manipulative experiments that decouple individual ion effects are required. Nevertheless, K+ exhibited strong correlations with the variation in microbial carbon utilization in this study, combined with its well-documented core role in microbial osmoregulation (Oren, 2008; Stautz et al., 2021), suggests that K+ dynamics are an important factor, associated with the successional patterns in microbial carbon utilization along the salinization gradient, though not the sole determinant. Despite the essential nature of potassium in numerous physiological functions essential for survival (osmoregulation, pH regulation, protein synthesis, enzyme activation, membrane potential and signal transduction), excessively high intracellular K+ concentrations can induce cytotoxicity and disrupt the normal expression of metabolic pathways. This cytotoxicity primarily arises from intracellular ion imbalance caused by unregulated passive K+ influx, and thus all halophilic microorganisms have evolved sophisticated regulatory mechanisms for K+ permeation pathways (Stautz et al., 2021).

Changes in microbial interactions, correlating with increased diversity, may influence carbon metabolism capacity. As diversity rises, symbiotic relationships may replace competitive ones, promoting increased metabolic cooperation and resource utilization. This will then drive increased carbon metabolism, even under less-than-optimal conditions (Xia et al., 2017). The carbon metabolism preferences of microorganisms evolved in conjunction with changes in the microbial community. Principle component analysis consistently indicated robust responses to sugar carbon substrates among all brine reservoirs recording a generalized trend among microorganisms, including preferential carbohydrate utilization. This observation correlated with high Proteobacteria abundance, implying that some phylogenetic groups have specific traits for environmental niche exploitation (Liu et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2023).

Microorganisms can also display divergent preferences for carbon utilization within the same ecosystem. For example, amino acid utilization was significant in the residue brine reservoir (YT4). This supports an osmoregulatory strategy of salinophilic microorganisms, including the accumulation of high concentrations of potassium chloride and energy-efficient “compatible” solutes, including amino acids and their derivatives (Oren, 2008). This suggests that salinophilic microorganisms might prioritize metabolic pathways that promote energy efficiency in high-salinity environments. These observations demonstrate that microbes exhibit distinct patterns for carbon substrates (Kramer and Gleixner, 2008; Goldfarb et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2018). This emphasizes the vital importance of phylogenetic diversity, and how that shapes microbial metabolism, particularly in extreme environments where nutrient availability and osmotic pressure dictate metabolic strategies.

These shifts in carbon metabolism, with changes in the microbial community, illustrate the adaptability of microorganisms. Additionally, this understanding may have practical implications for biotechnological applications involving extremophilic microorganisms.

Environmental implications

The ongoing lake ecosystem displays increasing stabilization with the progression of its overall evolution, alongside, the shift from Archaeal to bacterial dominance. These ecosystem transitions reflect changes in resource availability. Microbial diversity is anticipated to continue increasing in future lake environments as microbial groups exhibit significant turnover over time. However, these shifts in diversity and turnover will likely be slow and sensitive to drastic environmental fluctuations compared to earlier phases (Pavloudi et al., 2017). The lake ecosystem will develop more resilience to adverse conditions such as rapid shifts in ion concentrations, without exhibiting substantial negative impact.

The metabolic capacity for organic carbon utilization is also showing general increases. Within both the lake sediments and the water column, organic matter preservation is expected to increase. These concurrent shifts in microbial communities will continue adapting the metabolic strategies to the lake’s evolving conditions. As the lake is modified due to evaporation, microorganisms increasingly consume smaller, simplified organic compounds to include simple sugars, polysaccharides, and amines. This shift in carbon source preferences can degrade the preservation of organic matter. Overall, the trajectories of microbial community diversity and carbon metabolism both show the dynamic structure of lake ecosystems and environmental responses. This reinforces the importance of microbes occupy in organic cycling, nutrient dynamics, and ecosystem stability.

Conclusion

This study investigated microbial adaptation and carbon metabolism in a Tibetan Plateau salt lake undergoing salinization. It found that archaea (Euryarchaeota) dominance shifted to bacteria (Proteobacteria) as salinity increased, with a rise in α-diversity (146–711 OTUs). Biolog-ECO assays showed initial metabolic decline followed by recovery, with potassium (K+) emerging as a key factor strongly correlated with metabolic shits. Elevated K+ negatively correlated with carbon utilization, suppressing carbohydrate metabolism but enhancing amino acid pathways. These findings show microbial resilience and role in carbon cycling under osmotic stress. The study provides a mechanistic framework for understanding microbial adaptation in extreme environments, with implications for saline lake management and predicting biogeochemical responses.
