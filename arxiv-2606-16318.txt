Introduction

A great deal of the analysis of nonlinear elliptic equations is, at bottom, a
study of
diffusion
. The
p
-Laplacian

-\Delta_{p}u:=-div\big(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\big),\qquad 1<p<\infty,

models a flux that responds nonlinearly to the local gradient of a quantity,
and arises in non-Newtonian fluid mechanics, nonlinear elasticity and torsion
problems, glaciology, and image processing
[
30
,
46
,
52
]
. Its
defining feature is
locality
: the value of
-\Delta_{p}u
at a point is
determined by the behaviour of
u
in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of that
point. Many phenomena, however, are governed by interactions that are
emphatically
not
local. Particles that move by Lévy-type jumps,
populations that disperse over long ranges, and phase transitions with nonlocal
interaction energies, see
[
38
,
63
,
59
]
, are all naturally described by the fractional

q
-Laplacian

(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}u(x):=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q-2}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy,\qquad s\in(0,1),\;q\in(1,\infty).

Thus, the two operators sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. Therefore, a fundamental question, both physically and mathematically, is what happens when both types of diffusion are present simultaneously, i.e., when a system experiences both short-range gradient-driven flux and long-range nonlocal interactions. This is precisely the setting of the operator

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}:=-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)_{q}^{s},

which combines the two mechanisms additively. Such
mixed local-nonlocal operators
have been the subject of intense recent investigation and can be seen as a model of optimal animal foraging strategies, dispersal strategies, and anisotropic heat transfer
[
39
,
13
]
.

The systematic study of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
has, in recent years, grown into one of the most active directions in nonlinear analysis. The cleanest case,
p=q=2
, gives the linear operator
-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
, for which the systematic work of Biagi, Dipierro, Valdinoci and Vecchi
[
8
,
9
]
established well-posedness, maximum principles, interior and boundary regularity, and a variational spectral theory for the associated Dirichlet problem. Subsequently, some of the results were generalised to the general case of
p=q
in
[
31
]
.

The spectral theory of mixed local-nonlocal operators has been studied in several recent works. Del Pezzo, Ferreira, and Rossi
[
33
]
established that the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}
is isolated and simple, and analyzed its asymptotic behaviour as
p\to\infty
. Isoperimetric inequalities of Faber–Krahn and Hong–Krahn–Szegő type for the Dirichlet spectrum of
-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}
were subsequently established in
[
47
,
10
,
11
]
.

Moving to multiplicity of solutions to mixed local-nonlocal problems with concave-convex nonlinearities, it have been investigated in
[
12
,
7
,
35
]
. Biagi and Vecchi
[
12
]
obtained a second positive solution to a critical problem driven by
-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
, which was extended to the quasilinear operator
-\Delta_{p}+\varepsilon(-\Delta_{p})^{s}
in
[
7
]
via Ambrosetti–Brezis–Cerami type arguments. For the general operator
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
with sign-changing weights, multiplicity results in both subcritical and critical regimes were established in
[
35
]
using Nehari manifold methods.

The regularity theory for mixed local-nonlocal operators has been developed extensively in recent years. In the probabilistic setting, Chen, Kim, Song, and Vondraček
[
27
]
established a uniform boundary Harnack principle for the operator
\Delta+\Delta^{\alpha/2}
, connecting it to the superposition of Brownian and stable Lévy processes. The foundational analytic regularity theory for
-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}
was developed by Garain and Kinnunen
[
42
]
, who established local boundedness, Hölder continuity, and Harnack inequalities via De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory. This was sharpened by Garain and Lindgren
[
43
]
, who obtained almost Lipschitz regularity in the homogeneous case and Hölder continuity of the gradient for certain parameter ranges. In the semilinear setting, Su, Valdinoci, Wei, and Zhang
[
68
]
established
L^{\infty}
-boundedness and
C^{1,\alpha}
-regularity, subsequently sharpened to
C^{2,\alpha}
-regularity with optimal exponents for subcritical nonlinearities in
[
69
]
. A strong maximum principle for
-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u=c(x)|u|^{p-2}u
was proved by Shang and Zhang
[
65
]
, while Antonini and Cozzi
[
4
]
established global gradient regularity and a Hopf boundary point lemma for general quasilinear mixed operators. At the variational level, De Filippis and Mingione
[
32
]
proved local
C^{1,\alpha}
-regularity of minimizers of mixed
(p,q)
-functionals with
p\neq q
, and Byun, Lee, and Song
[
22
]
extended this program to mixed local-nonlocal double-phase functionals.

A second strand of the literature, which we draw on heavily, concerns the function spaces that make these problems well posed. The homogeneous Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and their fractional
analogues
\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, defined in Subsection
2.2
, are the correct energy spaces for
problems set on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where no boundary condition is
available and the only constraint comes from finiteness of energy and decay at
infinity. Their fine structure – density of smooth functions, the precise
relationship between the “completion” and “zero-trace” definitions, and the
strict inclusions among them – were taken from
[
16
,
18
,
15
]
.

Motivation and Isocritical Condition

One of the fundamental questions that arises when working with a differential
operator concerns the embedding of the underlying Sobolev space into an
appropriate Lebesgue space. For the
p
-Laplacian, this is the celebrated
Sobolev critical phenomenon: the embedding

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is continuous but
not
compact, where
p^{*}=\tfrac{pN}{N-p}
. This
leads to the study of the best Sobolev constant

\mathcal{S}:=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx}{\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p^{*}}}},

(1.1)

whose extremals were identified explicitly by Aubin
[
5
]
and
Talenti
[
70
]
. The minimization problem (
1.1
)
is naturally associated with the critical exponent equation

-\Delta_{p}u=|u|^{p^{*}-2}u
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The systematic study of such
critical problems in the presence of lower-order perturbations was initiated
by the seminal work of Brezis and Nirenberg
[
20
]
. At the
critical exponent, standard variational methods break down because
Palais–Smale sequences may concentrate or escape to infinity without
converging; compactness must then be recovered through the
concentration-compactness analysis developed by Lions
[
57
]
.

For the purely nonlocal problem
(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}u=|u|^{q_{s}^{*}-2}u
, an
analogous role is played by the fractional best Sobolev constant

\mathcal{S}_{f}:=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\dfrac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dx\,dy}{\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{q_{s}^{*}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q}{q_{s}^{*}}}}.

(1.2)

The existence of extremals and their sharp decay estimates were established
in
[
28
,
16
]
and the references therein.

Remark 1.1
.

It is easy to check from Theorem
2.4
that
\mathcal{S}_{f}\leq C(N,s,p,q)\;\mathcal{S}^{\frac{qs}{p}}
.

The considerations above naturally motivate the study of the analogous problem
for the mixed operator
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
. Accordingly, in this paper we seek
a function
u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfying

-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta)_{q}^{s}u=|u|^{p^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},

(
\mathcal{P}
)

under the standing assumptions

N\geq 2,\qquad 1<p<N,\qquad 0<s<1,\qquad 1<sq<N,

where
p^{*}=\tfrac{pN}{N-p}
is the critical Sobolev exponent for the

p
-Laplacian.
For the mixed operator the picture is richer, because the two operators carry
different
critical exponents.
The natural critical exponent for
-\Delta_{p}
is
p^{*}=\tfrac{pN}{N-p}
, while that for
(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}
is the fractional Sobolev critical exponent
q^{*}_{s}=\tfrac{qN}{N-qs}
.
In general these two thresholds are distinct, and their relative position
dictates the entire structure of the problem:

•

if
p^{*}>q^{*}_{s}
, the nonlocal term is subcritical relative to the
nonlinearity and the problem is governed, morally, by the local
operator alone;

•

if
p^{*}<q^{*}_{s}
, the roles are exactly reversed.

In both of these
non-isocritical
regimes one operator dominates at the
critical scale, the other behaves as a lower-order perturbation, and the analysis
is expected to reduce to a single-operator critical problem.
In fact, we expect to establish the non-existence of positive solutions in these regimes in
our forthcoming work, by extending the Pohozaev identity developed
in
[
3
]
to the present setting.

This is not merely a heuristic expectation: it is precisely what we observed, in
the Hilbertian model case
p=q=2
, in our previous work
[
25
]
.
There we studied the Brezis–Nirenberg-type problem

-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u-\lambda u=|u|^{2^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},\qquad u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega,

for the mixed operator
-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
on a bounded domain, and analysed
the loss of compactness through a global compactness (profile decomposition)
theorem for the associated Palais–Smale sequences.
The decisive structural fact was a
scaling collapse
of the nonlocal term.
Under the critical rescaling
u_{k}(x)=k^{(N-2)/2}u(kx)
that generates the
concentrating bubbles, the Dirichlet energy is scale-invariant, whereas the
Gagliardo seminorm of the nonlocal term satisfies

[u_{k}]_{s}^{2}=k^{2s-2}\,[u]_{s}^{2}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty,

since
2s-2<0
.
The nonlocal contribution is thus
strictly subcritical
relative to the
local one.
This was complemented by the compact embedding of the underlying fractional
Sobolev space into the local one (see
[
25
, Lemma 2.2]
).
As a consequence (building on Biagi, Dipierro, Valdinoci and
Vecchi
[
8
,
9
]
),
the best Sobolev constant for the mixed operator coincides with the purely local
one and is
never attained
, the bubbles in the profile decomposition are
exactly the Aubin–Talenti bubbles of the local critical problem

-\Delta U=|U|^{2^{*}-2}U
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and compactness can be restored
only by a perturbation (the term
\lambda u
) or by domain topology (a
Coron-type construction).
In short, despite its presence in the equation, the fractional term is invisible
to the critical geometry: the problem is governed by the Laplacian alone.

That experience is precisely what motivates the present paper.
The collapse
[u_{k}]_{s}^{2}=k^{2s-2}[u]_{s}^{2}\to 0
hinges on the mismatch between
the scaling exponents of the two operators – or equivalently, on the strict
inequality between their critical exponents.
The natural question is therefore:
what happens when the critical exponents
of both operators coincide? Does this remove the defect in the scaling? Does it
change the role of the fractional Laplacian from a subcritical perturbation to
that of a genuine competing operator?

This delicate case, which is the subject of the present paper, is the

isocritical
regime:

p^{*}=q^{*}_{s},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad 1-\frac{N}{p}=s-\frac{N}{q}.

(1.3)

This automatically gives us
q=\tfrac{pN}{N-p+ps}
, and
p<q
. The answer to each of the above questions is affirmative.
Here both operators become critical for the
same
nonlinearity at the

same
time, and both are invariant under the
same
critical
rescaling; the nonlocal operator is thus a genuine co-protagonist rather than a
perturbation.
This shared scaling is at once an obstruction and an opportunity.
A classical embedding theorem (see
[
54
, Theorem 7.28]
)
shows that under the isocritical condition,

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
so the natural energy space for (

P

) collapses to

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
alone.
However, this embedding is not compact: if
u_{n}(x)=n^{(p-N)/p}u(x/n)
for some
fixed
0<u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then
(u_{n})
is bounded in

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
but admits no convergent subsequence in

\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
On the other hand, this very scale-invariance opens the possibility of finding
ground states via concentration-compactness, and this is precisely what we carry
out in Section
3
to prove the existence of a ground state
of (

P

).

The embedding theorem yields two pieces of information.
First, the non-compactness implies that both best Sobolev constants –
\mathcal{S}

for
-\Delta_{p}
and
\mathcal{S}_{f}
for
(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}
– enter the energy level,
and the concentration-compactness alternative must account for both thresholds
simultaneously.
To the best of our knowledge, (

P

) is the first variational
problem for the mixed
(p,q)
-Laplacian at
double critical
growth, in
which the local and nonlocal best constants jointly determine the variational
level.
This is conceptually distinct from both the purely local critical problem
(see
[
20
]
) and the purely fractional one
(see
[
60
]
), and it necessitates a genuinely new compactness
analysis that handles both Sobolev thresholds at once
(see Section
3
).
Second, the embedding reveals that, even though both operators compete, the

p
-Laplacian has an advantage over the fractional
q
-Laplacian.
This is consistent with our decay estimates, where solutions
of (

P

) exhibit the pointwise decay of the fundamental
solution of the
p
-Laplacian.

.

Although the
L^{p}
scale does not distinguish between the

with strict inclusion witnessed by the function

Since

This, in turn, motivates the study of the minimization problem

Notably, this best constant is
not

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

Main results and methodology

Some of our results require

p^{*}=q^{*}_{s}
, while others hold for general exponents. To be precise, the
existence of a ground state and the sharp two-sided decay estimate are proved in
the isocritical regime
p^{*}=q^{*}_{s}
, under which the energy space reduces to

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with norm
[\,\cdot\,]_{1,p}
. By contrast,
the comparison principle, the strong maximum principle, the logarithmic
estimate, and the weak Harnack inequality are established for the operator

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
with
general
exponents
p,q>1
, under the standing
assumptions
1<p<N
,
0<s<1
,
1<sq<N
, with no relation imposed
between
p^{*}
and
q^{*}_{s}
.

We obtain four main results which, taken together, give a fairly complete picture of
the problem. Concerning the isocritical regime, we prove ground state exists in the radial class, and it is strictly positive. The decay behaviour is settled
for the natural class of positive, radially symmetric, radially decreasing
solutions of (

P

). The strict positivity itself follows from a strong maximum
principle for
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
, valid for general exponents
1<p\leq q<\infty
.

Existence of a radial ground state (Section
3
).

The strategy for proving the existence result hinges on a careful treatment of the compactness defects mentioned earlier. The crucial structural step is a
localisation of the loss of compactness
as depicted in Lemma
3.5
. For a weakly null
(PS)_{\tilde{c}}
sequence
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
,
we prove that all three energies - the

p
-Dirichlet energy
[u_{n}]_{1,p}^{p}

[u_{n}]_{s,q}^{q}

\int|u_{n}|^{p^{*}}

every annulus
B_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}
.
The mechanism is the compactness of the embedding

\mathcal{X}^{r}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{r}(B_{R_{1}}\setminus B_{R_{2}})
for every
1\leq r<\infty
.
This localisation is precisely what makes the concentration quantities mentioned in (
3.16
)
independent of the radius
\delta>0

The concentration-compactness Lemma
3.6
proves that the critical mass
\lim\int_{B_{\delta}}|u_{n}|^{p^{*}}

either vanishes or is bounded below by

\max\{\mathcal{S}^{p^{*}/(p^{*}-p)},\mathcal{S}_{f}^{q^{*}_{s}/(q^{*}_{s}-q)}\}
, a
single threshold that simultaneously encodes both Sobolev constants.

A rescaling step (refer Lemma
3.7
)

\tilde{u}_{n}(x)=r_{n}^{(N-p)/p}u_{n}(r_{n}x)
then pins the

-mass at a definite
scale

\int_{B_{1}}|\tilde{u}_{n}|^{p^{*}}=\xi\;\;\forall n

ruling out both vanishing and escape, and a Brezis–Lieb decomposition

[
19

extracts a nontrivial limit at the ground-state level. The scaling
is legitimate as the exponent identity
-N+qs+q(N-p)/p=0
holds precisely when

p^{*}=q^{*}_{s}
, so the rescaling leaves both local and non local energies unchanged. The equality
m=\tilde{c}
of the Nehari
minimum and mountain pass level completes following the existence argument.

Comparison and strong maximum principle (cf. Section
4

The technical core of this section is a logarithmic energy estimate
(Lemma

Alongside it, we develop a weak comparison

together with the nonlocal monotonicity of

for general exponents

Formulating these results on exterior domains like

the auxiliary space
\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega):=\Bigl\{

\displaystyle u\in L^{q-1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\Omega)\ :\

\displaystyle\exists\,E\supset\Omega,\ E^{c}\text{ compact},\ \mathrm{dist}(E^{c},\Omega)>0,\ [u]_{s,q,E}<\infty\Bigr\}.

The difficulty it resolves is that the nonlocal operator
(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}
is
“too” global, meaning that

must be controlled far away even when only its

\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)

asks instead for exactly what is needed and no more: the local

\mathcal{D}^{1,p}

\Omega

of the tail, and finite Gagliardo

that stays at positive distance controlling the near interaction without constraining the
far tail.
Demanding

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u
is a well-defined

and the comparison principle (Lemma

5

The real weight of the proof of the Harnack inequality is carried by the

\left(\fint_{B_{R}}u^{\theta}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\leq C\,\inf_{B_{R}}u.

The role of the inequality for us is qualitative rather than quantitative. Applied to a positive solution, it gives the strict interior
positivity

that the decay analysis takes as its starting
point, and this positive floor is the input to the auxiliary problem of
Section

Sharp two-sided decay (refer Section
6
).

We obtain the following result concerning the decay of positive, radially symmetric, decreasing solutions

of (

Theorem 1.2

(Decay of positive radial solutions)
.

Assume the isocritical condition (
1.3
), i.e.
p^{\ast}=q^{\ast}_{s}
, together
with the standing assumptions
1<p<N
,
0<s<1
,
1<q<N/s
, so that

q=\tfrac{pN}{N-p+ps}>1
. Set

\alpha:=\frac{N-p}{p-1}.

Let
U\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a positive,
radially symmetric, and decreasing weak solution of (

(i)

(Upper bound.)
For all
|x|\geq 1

U(x)\;\leq\;\left(\omega_{N}^{-1/p^{\ast}}\,\mathcal{S}^{-1/p}\,\|U\|_{L^{p^{\ast}-1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{(p^{\ast}-1)/p}\right)^{\!p/(p-1)}|x|^{-\alpha}.

(ii)

(Lower bound.)
If in addition

q\neq\frac{p(N-1)}{N-p},

then there exists a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}\big(N,p,q,s,\inf_{x\in B_{1}}U(x\big)\big)>0
such that

In particular,

decays exactly at the rate

of the
fundamental solution of the
p

and

Remark 1.3
.

The upper bound in Theorem
1.2
uses the radial Lemma

2.3
, and thus the radial symmetry of the
solution, whereas the lower bound holds for any nonnegative solution.

Remark 1.4
.

It is natural to ask whether every nonnegative ground state of (

P

) is radial, which would allow one to drop the symmetry hypothesis altogether. We believe this is true for
p\geq 2
using the techniques developed in
[
26
]
under appropriate regularity assumptions; for
1<p<2
, however, the question appears to remain open.

This is the qualitative heart of the paper. Although both operators are critical
and neither can be dropped from the equation, it is the local operator that dictates the
large-scale behaviour. The upper bound is reached through a global

L^{\infty}
bound (Proposition
2.1
), a borderline
weak-Lorentz estimate placing

in the Lorentz space

with the critical exponent

(refer Proposition

), and a radial Lorentz lemma that converts this
membership into the pointwise rate.

A remark on the scope of the Lorentz estimate.
The borderline
estimate is proved for
arbitrary exponents

under the standing assumptions. Its proof uses
only the local Sobolev inequality together with the sign of the nonlocal term, which is
favourable regardless of the relation between
p
and
q
. Consequently, the

, and hence the upper bound

, hold in generality.

What is special to the isocritical regime is not the validity of the
estimate but its optimality.
The exponent
\eta_{0}=\tfrac{(p-1)N}{N-p}
is the
borderline integrability of the
p
-Laplacian’s fundamental solution, and the

the lower bound and is therefore sharp, precisely when

, and

|x|^{-\alpha}
is the true asymptotic profile. Away from the isocritical
balance the same Lorentz membership still holds, but it is no longer expected to
capture the sharp rate, and
|x|^{-\alpha}
degrades from an exact rate to a mere
upper envelope. The upper estimate, in other words, is universal; its sharpness is
isocritical.

The lower bound is the delicate half, and its proof splits into two
regimes according to the sign of
\alpha(q-1)-N
, that is, according to whether

. The two regimes call for genuinely different

decides whether the singular

can be turned into a usable subsolution at all.

In the regime

the construction is
direct. One regularises (Lemma

)
\Gamma
into a
C^{2}
radial function

,
equal to
|x|^{-\alpha}
for

(-\Delta)^{s}_{q}\Upsilon(x)\leq-c_{*}|x|^{-N-sq}
for
|x|\geq k
. Hence

subsolution of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
outside a large ball, and a single application

.

The case

is the tricky one, since the profile

\Gamma(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
is not directly comparable to our solution. To circumvent this, we
introduce the constrained minimisation problem

whose minimiser

is
not comparable on the boundary, we cap it as

, for which

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}\widetilde{\Gamma}\leq C|x|^{-N-sq}
(Lemma
6.3
);
the same truncation applied to

gives a lower bound with constant proportional
to
(U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}
(Lemma
6.5
), strictly positive by
Lemma
6.4
, and chaining the comparisons yields
Theorem
1.2
.Our sub-solution constructions and the accompanying nonlocal computations on
exterior domains draw on the techniques of
[
34
,
50
,
16
]
.

Remark 1.5

(The borderline case
q=\tfrac{p(N-1)}{N-p}

.

The separating

is a genuine borderline, and the reason is a clash of
scales in the nonlocal term (refer
[

The upper bound is unaffected, as it never uses

Proof.

Follows by taking

and

in Theorem

Therefore, under the assumption (

2.3
Notion of solution

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open. For
u,\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega)
set

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{J_{q}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dx\,dy.

Under the isocritical condition (
1.3
),
\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega)=\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
,
and
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle
is finite for all
u,\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega)
by Hölder’s
inequality.

Definition 2.1

(Weak solution)
.

A function
u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a
weak solution
of (

P

) if

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{p^{*}-2}u\,\varphi\,dx\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Definition 2.2

(Weak sub- and supersolution)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open and
u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega)
.
We say
u
is a
weak subsolution
of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}:=-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)_{q}^{s}
in
\Omega
, written

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u\leq 0
weakly, if

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega),\ \varphi\geq 0\text{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{N},

and a
weak supersolution
of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
in
\Omega
, written
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u\geq 0

weakly, if the reverse inequality holds for all such
\varphi
. A function is a

weak solution
of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u=0
in
\Omega
iff it is simultaneously a weak
sub- and supersolution.

Remark 2.2
.

When a right-hand side
f
is present, the inequalities are interpreted against
f
:

u
is a weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u=f
if

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f\,\varphi\,dx

for all nonnegative
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\Omega)
(subsolution: reverse inequality). In
particular,
u
is a weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}u=|u|^{p^{*}-2}u
if

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(u),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{p^{*}-2}u\,\varphi\,dx
for all such
\varphi
.

Remark 2.3
.

For the comparison principles in Section
4
, we use a larger class
\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)

and test functions
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{0}(\Omega)
(see Lemma (
4.1
)).
Accordingly, the notions of weak sub-/supersolution of
Definition
2.2
extend verbatim to
u\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)

by testing against nonnegative
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{0}(\Omega)
.

2.4
Uniform estimate

We begin with a global boundedness result for general
1<p<N,q>1,sq<N
, which will be used repeatedly throughout the subsequent analysis. To this end, we first recall an iteration lemma (see
[
45
, Lemma 7.1]
), which will be instrumental in the argument.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\{a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of positive real numbers satisfying

(i)

a_{0}\leq c_{0}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\,b^{-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}},

(ii)

for each
j=0,1,2,\dots
, we have

a_{j+1}\leq c_{0}\,b^{j}\,a_{j}^{1+\alpha},\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots,

where

,

, and

are fixed constants.

Then the sequence

converges to zero.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a nonnegative weak solution of (

P

). Then

u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Proof.

Step 1:
This step establishes that
u^{p^{*}-1}\in L^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
\alpha>\frac{N}{p}
. Fix
L>0
and
\beta>1
, and define

u_{L}:=\min\{u,L\},\qquad h_{\beta,L}(t):=t\,\min\{t,L\}^{\beta-1},\qquad t\geq 0.

Since
h_{\beta,L}
is Lipschitz continuous, the standard stability of Sobolev and fractional Sobolev spaces under Lipschitz compositions yields

h_{\beta,L}(u)\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).
Therefore,
h_{\beta,L}(u)
is an admissible test function in the weak formulation, and hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla(h_{\beta,L}(u))\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{J_{q}(u(x)-u(y))\bigl(h_{\beta,L}(u(x))-h_{\beta,L}(u(y))\bigr)}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}-1}h_{\beta,L}(u)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx.

(2.8)

Now define

H_{\beta,L}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(h^{\prime}_{\beta,L}(\tau)\bigr)^{1/p}\,d\tau.

By the chain rule and since
h_{\beta,L}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
, we have

|\nabla H_{\beta,L}(u)|^{p}=h^{\prime}_{\beta,L}(u)\,|\nabla u|^{p}=|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla(h_{\beta,L}(u)).

Therefore the first term in (
2.8
) becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla H_{\beta,L}(u)|^{p}\,dx.
For the nonlocal term, since
h_{\beta,L}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
, for every
a,b\geq 0
we have

J_{q}(a-b)(h_{\beta,L}(a)-h_{\beta,L}(b))\geq 0,

see also Lemma A.2 in
[
17
]
. Consequently, using the Sobolev inequality (
1.1
), we obtain from (
2.8
)

\mathcal{S}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|H_{\beta,L}(u)|^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{p/p^{*}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx.

A direct computation yields the following estimate for the function
H_{\beta,L}

H_{\beta,L}(t)\geq\frac{p}{p+\beta-1}\,t\,\min\{t,L\}^{\frac{\beta-1}{p}}.

Substituting this in the Sobolev estimate, we obtain

\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{p}{p+\beta-1}\right)^{p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{(\beta-1)\frac{p^{*}}{p}}\,dx\right)^{p/p^{*}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx.

We now estimate the right-hand side.
Fix
M_{0}>0
, to be chosen later, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u<M_{0}\}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx+\int_{\{u\geq M_{0}\}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{\beta-1}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq M_{0}^{\beta-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx+\left(\int_{\{u\geq M_{0}\}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p^{*}-p}{p^{*}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u^{p}u_{L}^{\beta-1})^{\frac{p^{*}}{p}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p^{*}}}.

Combining the previous estimates, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{p}{p+\beta-1}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}u_{L}^{(\beta-1)\frac{p^{*}}{p}}\,dx\right)^{p/p^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{0}^{\beta-1}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx+\left(\int_{\{u\geq M_{0}\}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p^{*}-p}{p^{*}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u^{p}u_{L}^{\beta-1})^{\frac{p^{*}}{p}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p^{*}}}.

(2.9)

Now choose
\beta>1
so that

p^{*}+(\beta-1)\frac{p^{*}}{p}=\alpha(p^{*}-1),\text{i.e.,\;}\beta=p\,\alpha\,\frac{p^{*}-1}{p^{*}}-(p-1),

for some
\alpha>\frac{N}{p}
.
Notice that this choice is possible. Therefore we may choose
M_{0}=M_{0}(\beta,u)>0
so large that

\left(\int_{\{u\geq M_{0}\}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p^{*}-p}{p^{*}}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2}\left(\frac{p}{p+\beta-1}\right)^{p}.

With this choice, estimate (
2.9
) yields

\left(\frac{p}{p+\beta-1}\right)^{p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}\,u_{L}^{(\beta-1)\frac{p^{*}}{p}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p^{*}}}\leq\frac{2}{\mathcal{S}}\,M_{0}^{\beta-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx.

We now let
L\to\infty
. Since
u_{L}=\min\{u,L\}\uparrow u
pointwise yields

u^{p^{*}-1}\in L^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\text{ for }\alpha>\frac{N}{p}.

Step 2:
In this step, we complete the proof by arguing in the spirit of

[
32
, Proposition 2.1]
. Note that, since
\alpha>\frac{N}{p}
, we have

p^{*}>\,p\,\alpha^{{}^{\prime}},

where
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
. Choose
\ell>0
, to be fixed later. For
j\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, define

\theta_{j}:=2\ell\Bigl(1-2^{-j-1}\Bigr),\qquad w_{j}:=(u-\theta_{j})_{+}.

Then

\ell\leq\theta_{j}\leq 2\ell,\qquad\theta_{j}\uparrow 2\ell,

and
w_{j}\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap\mathcal{D}^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
j
.
Testing the weak formulation with
w_{j+1}
, we get

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla w_{j+1}\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{J_{q}(u(x)-u(y))(w_{j+1}(x)-w_{j+1}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}-1}\,w_{j+1}\,dx:=J_{1}+J_{2}-J_{3}.

(2.10)

Using the Sobolev inequality (
1.1
), we obtain

J_{1}\geq\mathcal{S}\,\|w_{j+1}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}.

Before estimating
J_{3}
, we observe that since
u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, it follows that
u\in L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality, for every
k>0
,

|\{u>k\}|\leq\frac{1}{k^{p^{*}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}}\,dx.

In particular,

|\{w_{j+1}>0\}|=|\{u>\theta_{j+1}\}|<\infty.

Now, because
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}<p^{*}
, we apply Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle J_{3}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{p^{*}-1}\,w_{j+1}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{L^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\|w_{j+1}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\,|\{w_{j+1}>0\}|^{\frac{1}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{p^{*}}}.

We next consider the nonlocal term
J_{2}
. The function
t\mapsto(t-\theta_{j+1})_{+}
is increasing, hence

(u(x)-u(y))(w_{j+1}(x)-w_{j+1}(y))\geq 0.

Consequently,

J_{2}\geq 0
. Putting together the above estimates for
J_{1}
,
J_{2}
, and
J_{3}
in (
2.10
), we obtain

\mathcal{S}\;\|w_{j+1}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}\leq\,\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{{L^{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\,\|w_{j+1}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}|\{w_{j+1}>0\}|^{\frac{1}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{p^{*}}}.

(2.11)

Next we estimate the measure of the superlevel set. On
\{w_{j+1}>0\}
we have

w_{j}=(u-\theta_{j})_{+}\geq\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}=\ell\,2^{-j-1}.

Therefore

|\{w_{j+1}>0\}|\leq\left(\frac{2^{j+1}}{\ell}\right)^{p^{*}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}w_{j}^{p^{*}}\,dx.

Writing
\eta_{j}:=\ell^{-p}\|w_{j}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}.
Then, using (
2.11
) together with the monotonicity
w_{j+1}\leq w_{j}
, we obtain

\eta_{j+1}\leq C(p,N)\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{{L^{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\,\left(2^{j}\right)^{\frac{p^{*}-\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}}\ell^{1-p}\,\eta_{j}^{\frac{p^{*}}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}p}},

Set

c_{0}=C\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{L^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\,\ell^{1-p},\qquad b=2^{\frac{p^{*}-\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}},\qquad\alpha_{0}=\frac{p^{*}}{p\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}-1.

Next observe that

\eta_{0}=\ell^{-p}\|(u-\ell)_{+}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}\leq\ell^{-p}\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}.

Because
p+\frac{p-1}{\alpha}>0
, we may enlarge the lower bound on
\ell(\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{{L^{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},N,p)
so that

\eta_{0}\leq\ell^{-p}\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{p}\leq{\left(C\|u^{p^{*}-1}\|_{{L^{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\ell^{1-p}\right)}^{-1/\alpha_{0}}{\left(2^{\frac{p^{*}-\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}}}\right)}^{-1/\alpha_{0}^{2}}.

Therefore the assumptions of Lemma
2.2
are satisfied, and we conclude that

\eta_{j}\to 0.

This implies

0=\lim_{j\to\infty}\eta_{j}=\ell^{-p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u-2\ell)_{+}^{p^{*}}\,dx\right)^{p/p^{*}}.

Hence,
u\leq 2\ell\;\text{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.
Since
u\geq 0
, this proves
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
∎

2.5
Lorentz Spaces

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. Its distribution function is defined by

\mu_{u}(t):=\bigl|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\ |u(x)|>t\}\bigr|,\qquad t>0.

For

and

, the Lorentz space

L^{\beta,\theta}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right):=\left\{u:\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\theta-1}\,\mu_{u}(t)^{\frac{\theta}{\beta}}\,dt<\infty\right\}.

When
\theta=\infty
, the weak Lorentz space
L^{\beta,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is given by

L^{\beta,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\left\{u:\sup_{t>0}t\,\mu_{u}(t)^{1/\beta}<\infty\right\}.

(Radial Lemma for Lorentz spaces)
.

Let
0<\theta\leq\infty
and

. Let

[
16
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The functional
\mathcal{J}
satisfies the mountain pass geometry.

To prove the existence of a solution of (

Lemma 3.2

For every nonzero

By Lemma (

Lemma 3.3

There exists a sequence

Lemma 3.4
.

m=\tilde{c}.

Throughout the subsequent analysis, we pass to subsequences when necessary, while continuing to denote them by the original sequence.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{X}_{0}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

\mathcal{J}
at level
\tilde{c}
,
as produced in Lemma

\mathcal{X}_{0}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

\displaystyle\int_{B_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}\,dx\to 0,\quad\iint_{B_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy\,dx\to 0,\quad\int_{B_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}}|u_{n}|^{p^{*}}\,dx\to 0.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0,r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

, where

,

By the compact embedding result (see

\mathcal{X}_{0}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{q}(B_{R_{1}}\setminus B_{R_{2}})\quad\text{compactly},

(3.14)

To prove the lemma, we consider the function

By Hölder’s inequality and (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p-1}|\nabla\eta||u_{n}|\,dx\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\tfrac{p-1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\eta|^{p}|u_{n}|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\tfrac{1}{p}}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

\displaystyle=\iint_{B_{c,d}\times B_{c,d}}+\iint_{B_{c,d}^{c}\times B_{c,d}}+\iint_{B_{c,d}\times B_{c,d}^{c}}:=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

As a preliminary step, we require a logarithmic-type estimate (see Lemma
4.3
). We begin by recalling the following elementary inequality from
[
37
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the following inequality holds

|a|^{p}\leq|b|^{p}+C\varepsilon|b|^{p}+(1+C\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{1-p}|a-b|^{p},

where
C=C(p)
is some non-negative constant.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open domain, and

u\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)
be a nonnegative weak supersolution to
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
in
\Omega
(see Definiton
2.2
).
Let
d>0
and let
B_{R}\subset\Omega
be such that
B_{2r}\subset B_{R}
. Then there exists a constant
C(N,s,p,q)>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}|\nabla\log(u+d)|^{p}\,dx+\iint_{B_{r}\times B_{r}}\left|\log\left(\frac{u(x)+d}{u(y)+d}\right)\right|^{q}\frac{(u(y)+d)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{N-p}+Cr^{-qs}\int_{B_{2r}}(u+d)^{q-p}\,dx+C\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2r})\times B_{3r/2}}\frac{(u(x)+d)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy\,dx.

Proof.

Taking
\eta:=(u+d)^{1-p}\phi^{q}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)
as the test function with
\phi\equiv 1
in
B_{r}
,
|D\phi|\leq\frac{C}{r}
, and
Supp(\phi)\subset B_{3r/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{B_{\frac{3r}{2}}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q-2}(u(x)-u(y))(\eta(x)-\eta(y))}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}:=J_{1}+J_{2}.

Before proceeding further, we verify the admissibility of the test function
\eta=(u+d)^{1-p}\phi^{q}.
Since
(u+d)^{-p}\leq d^{-p}
, it follows easily that
\eta\in\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{D}^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

We next show that
\eta\in\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{0}(\Omega)
. Recall that
u\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)
implies the existence of a set
E\supset\Omega
such that
E^{c}
is compact,
dist(E^{c},\Omega)>0,

and

[u]_{s,q,E}<\infty.
Since
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
it suffices to prove that
[\eta]_{s,q,E}<\infty.

\displaystyle[\eta]_{s,q,E}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{E\times E}\frac{\left|\bigl((u(x)+d)^{1-p}-(u(y)+d)^{1-p}\bigr)\phi(x)^{q}\right|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+\iint_{E\times E}\frac{\left|(u(y)+d)^{1-p}\bigl(\phi(x)^{q}-\phi(y)^{q}\bigr)\right|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dx\,dy=:I_{A}+I_{B}.

By the mean value theorem, it follows that

I_{A}\leq C(p,q)d^{-pq}\iint_{E\times E}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dx\,dy<\infty,

For the second term,

I_{B}\leq d^{(1-p)q}\iint_{E\times E}\frac{|\phi(x)^{q}-\phi(y)^{q}|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dx\,dy<\infty.

Since
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

[\phi^{q}]_{s,q}<\infty.

Combining the above estimates, we conclude that

\eta\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega).

We now estimate
J_{1}

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-p)\int_{B_{\frac{3r}{2}}}|\nabla\log(u+d)|^{p}\varphi^{q}+q\int_{B_{\frac{3r}{2}}}|\nabla\log(u+d)|^{p-1}|\nabla\varphi|\,\varphi^{\,q-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-p)}{2}\int_{B_{\frac{3r}{2}}}|\nabla\log(u+d)|^{p}\varphi^{q}+C(p,q)\int_{B_{\frac{3r}{2}}}|\nabla\varphi|^{p}\varphi^{q-p},

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality.
For the non-local term, we break it as follows

\displaystyle J_{2}=\iint_{B_{2r}\times B_{2r}}\;+\;2\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2r})\times B_{2r}}\;:=\;I_{1}+I_{2}.

For estimating
I_{1}
, we first consider
u(x)>u(y)
. Using Lemma
4.2
, we get

\displaystyle(u(x)-u(y))^{q-1}\left[(u(x)+d)^{1-p}\,\varphi^{q}(x)-(u(y)+d)^{1-p}\,\varphi^{q}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq(u(x)-u(y))^{q-1}(u(x)+d)^{1-p}\Big[\varphi^{q}(y)+\varepsilon C(q)\varphi^{q}(y)-\frac{(u(y)+d)^{1-p}}{(u(x)+d)^{1-p}}\varphi^{q}(y)\Big]

\displaystyle+(u(x)-u(y))^{q-1}(u(x)+d)^{1-p}(1+C(q)\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{1-q}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{q}:=T_{1}+T_{2}.

We now choose
\varepsilon=\delta\,\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{u(x)+d}\leq 1
where
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=(u(x)-u(y))^{q}(u(x)+d)^{-p}\varphi^{q}(y)\left[C(q)\delta+\frac{1-\left(\dfrac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}\right)^{1-p}}{1-\dfrac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}}\right].

(4.23)

To analyse the ratio appearing in (
4.23
), define the function

g(t)=\frac{1-t^{1-p}}{1-t},\qquad t\in(0,1).

Since
g
is increasing on
(0,1)
, it follows that

g(t)\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-p}{2^{p}}\,\dfrac{t^{1-p}}{1-t}&0<t\leq\frac{1}{2},\\[6.0pt]
1-p&\frac{1}{2}\leq t\leq 1.\end{cases}

(4.24)

To estimate
T_{1}
, we distinguish two cases. First, suppose that
t:=\frac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
. Using the bound (
4.24
) for
g(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(u(x)-u(y))^{q}(u(x)+d)^{-p}\varphi^{q}(y)\left[C(q)\delta+\frac{1-p}{2^{p}}\frac{\left(\dfrac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}\right)^{1-p}}{\dfrac{u(x)-u(y)}{u(x)+d}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(u(x)-u(y))^{q-1}(u(y)+d)^{1-p}\varphi^{q}(y)\left[C(q)\delta+\frac{1-p}{2^{p}}\right].

where the last inequality follows from the elementary bound

\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{u(x)+d}\left(\frac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}\right)^{p-1}\leq 1.

Choosing

\delta=\frac{p-1}{C(q)\,2^{p-1}}\frac{1}{2^{2}}

where
C(q)>1
, we obtain the final estimate

T_{1}\leq-\varphi^{q}(y)\frac{p-1}{2^{p+1}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{\,q-1}}{(u(y)+d)^{\,q-1}}(u(y)+d)^{q-p}.

(4.25)

Now if

t=\frac{u(y)+d}{u(x)+d}\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right),

then using the bound on
g(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(u(x)-u(y))^{q}(u(x)+d)^{-p}\varphi^{q}(y)(C(q)\delta+1-p)

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{q}}{(u(x)+d)^{p}}\varphi^{q}(y)\,(p-1)\left(\frac{2^{p+1}-1}{2^{p+1}}\right).

We note that if
2(u(y)+d)\leq u(x)+d
, then

\displaystyle\left[\log\left(\frac{u(x)+d}{u(y)+d}\right)\right]^{q}\leq C(q)\left(\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{u(y)+d}\right)^{q-1}

(4.27)

because
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{(\log t)^{q}}{(t-1)^{q-1}}=0
. On the other hand, if
2(u(y)+d)\geq u(x)+d
, then, using the inequality
\log(1+\xi)\leq\xi
for all
\xi\geq 0
, we deduce

\displaystyle\Bigg[\log\!\left(\frac{u(x)+d}{u(y)+d}\right)\Bigg]^{q}(u(y)+d)^{q-p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{q}}{(u(y)+d)^{p}}\leq 2^{p}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{q}}{(u(x)+d)^{p}}.

(4.28)

Substituting (
4.27
) and (
4.28
) in (
4.25
) and (
4.26
) respectively, we get

T_{1}\leq-C(p,q)\varphi^{q}(y)\left(\log\left(\frac{u(x)+d}{u(y)+d}\right)\right)^{q}(u(y)+d)^{q-p}.

(4.29)

We next estimate the contribution of the term from
T_{2}
using the Lipschitz continuity of
\varphi
as

\displaystyle\iint_{B_{2r}\times B_{2r}}\frac{(u(x)+d)^{q-p}\,|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{r^{q}}\iint_{B_{2r}\times B_{2r}}\frac{(u(x)+d)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{\,N+q(s-1)}}\,dy\,dx

(4.30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{r^{q}}\int_{B_{2r}}(u(x)+d)^{q-p}\,dx\int_{B_{4r}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{N-q(1-s)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(s,q,N)}{r^{qs}}\int_{B_{2r}}(u(x)+d)^{q-p}\,dx.

Noting that the estimate holds trivially when
u(x)=u(y)
, and that in the case
u(y)>u(x)
the same computations apply after interchanging the roles of
x
and
y
, we combine (
4.29
) and (
4.30
) to conclude the following estimate of
I_{1}
as follows, where
C=C(p,q,s,N)
:

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq-\iint_{B_{2r}\times B_{2r}}C\varphi^{q}(y)\left|\log\left(\frac{u(x)+d}{u(y)+d}\right)\right|^{q}\frac{(u(y)+d)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}dy\;dx+\frac{C}{r^{qs}}\int_{B_{2r}}(u(x)+d)^{q-p}\,dx.

Finally, we estimate the contribution of
I_{2}
. Since
u\geq 0
, we have

\left(\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{u(x)+d}\right)^{q-1}\leq 1.

Using this, together with the fact that
\varphi
is supported in
B_{3r/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(q)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2r}}\int_{B_{3r/2}}\frac{\varphi^{q}(x)(u(x)+d)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}\,dy\,dx.

Combining this estimate with the bound for
I_{1}
, we obtain the corresponding estimate for
J_{2}
. Finally, combining the estimates for
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
yields the desired logarithmic estimate.
∎

With the logarithmic estimate established, we are now in a position to prove the strong maximum principle.

Remark 4.1
.

We note that the weak maximum principle follows readily by employing the standard argument of taking
u^{-}:=\max\{-u,0\}
as a test function in the weak formulation.

Theorem 4.2

(Strong Maximum Principle)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a connected open set,
1<p\leq q<\infty,p<N,sq<N
, and
u\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\Omega)
be a weak supersolution of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
with
u=0
in
\Omega^{c}
. Then either
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
or
u=0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

In view of the weak maximum principle,
u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Thus we assume
u\not\equiv 0
a.e. in
\Omega
and prove
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
. We aim at proving that for any connected compact set
K\subset\subset\Omega
, if
u\not\equiv 0
in
K
then
u>0
a.e. in
K
. Observe that
K
can be covered by a finite number of balls
B_{r}\left(x_{1}\right),\ldots,B_{r}\left(x_{k}\right)
contained in
\Omega
such that
x_{i}\in K
and

\left|B_{r}\left(x_{i}\right)\cap B_{r}\left(x_{i+1}\right)\right|>0,\quad i=1,\ldots,k-1.

(4.31)

Suppose by contradiction that

|\{x\in K:u(x)=0\}|>0.

Then there exists some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
such that

Z:=\{x\in B_{r}(x_{i}):u(x)=0\}

has positive measure.

Now
Z
has positive Lebesgue measure and
u=0
a.e. in
Z
. Set
v_{\varepsilon}:=\log\left(1+\frac{u}{\varepsilon}\right)
. Then
v_{\varepsilon}=0
a.e. in
Z
, and hence

(v_{\varepsilon})_{Z}=0.

By the Poincaré inequality
[
53
, Theorem 13.27]
, we obtain

\int_{B_{r}(x_{i})}\left|\log\left(1+\frac{u}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{p}dx=\int_{B_{r}(x_{i})}|v_{\varepsilon}-(v_{\varepsilon})_{Z}|^{p}dx\leq C(p,Z,B_{r}(x_{i}))\int_{B_{r}(x_{i})}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{p}dx.

Now using Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}\left(x_{i}\right)}\left|\log\left(1+\frac{u}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{N-p}+Cr^{-qs}\int_{B_{2r}}(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}+C\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2r}\times B_{3r/2}}\frac{(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+qs}}dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{N-p}+Cr^{-qs}\int_{B_{2r}}(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}.

where
C>0
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

If
u\not\equiv 0
in
B_{r}(x_{i})
, then there exists
t>0
such that

A_{t}:=\{x\in B_{r}(x_{i}):u(x)\geq t\}

has positive measure. Hence

\int_{B_{r}(x_{i})}\left|\log\left(1+\frac{u}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{p}dx\geq|A_{t}|\left|\log\left(1+\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{p}\to\infty\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

which contradicts the uniform bound. Therefore, if the zero set has positive measure in a ball, then
u\equiv 0
a.e. in that ball. In view of (
4.31
), we can follow the same argument to get
u\equiv 0
a.e. in K which contradicts our assumption.

For the case when
\Omega
is bounded and connected, one can construct a sequence of connected compact sets
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

A_{n}\Subset\Omega\text{ and }|\Omega\setminus A_{n}|<\frac{1}{n}.
Applying the previous argument on each
A_{n}
and passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
yields the desired conclusion.

Next, suppose that
\Omega
is connected but unbounded. Then there exists a sequence of bounded connected open sets
\{\Omega_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\Omega_{n}\subset\Omega_{n+1}\subset\Omega,\;\Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{n}.

Since
u\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, there exists
\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{N}
for which
u\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{n}
whenever
n\geq\tilde{n}
. Since
u
is a non-negative weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
in each
\Omega_{n}
, it follows from the bounded-domain case that
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega_{n}
for every
n\geq n_{0}
, and hence
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5
Harnack Estimates

In this subsection, we establish Harnack-type estimates for non-negative supersolutions of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
when (
1.3
) holds.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a non-negative weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
(Section
2.3
). Assume
k>0
and there exists
\tau\in(0,1]
such that

|B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\{u\geq k\}|\geq\tau|B_{r}|

for
r\in(0,1]
where
0<r<\frac{R}{16}
with
R>0
. Then there exists a positive constant
\delta\equiv\delta(\tau,p,q,N,s,\|u\|_{L_{loc}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})})\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
such that

\essinf_{B_{4r}(x_{0})}u\geq\delta k.

Proof.

The proof is divided into two main steps.

Step 1:

We claim that there exists a constant
C(N,s,p,q,\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})})>0
such that

\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{u\leq 2\delta k-\varepsilon\right\}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\tau\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)}|B_{6r}(x_{0})|,

(5.32)

for every
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
, and
\varepsilon>0
.

To start with, let
\psi
be a standard cutoff function such that

\displaystyle 0\leq\psi\leq 1

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad|\nabla\psi|\leq\frac{8}{r}\quad\text{in }B_{7r}(x_{0}),\text{ and}\qquad\psi\equiv 1

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }B_{6r}(x_{0}).

Define the auxiliary function
w=u+\varepsilon
, and choose the test function
\varphi=w^{1-p}\,\psi^{q}
. Working as in Logarithmic Lemma
4.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}\left|\nabla\log(u+\varepsilon)\right|^{p}\,dx+\iint_{B_{6r}(x_{0})\times B_{6r}(x_{0})}\left|\log\left(\frac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{u(y)+\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{q}\frac{(u(y)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dy\,dx

(5.33)

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{N-p}+Cr^{-qs}\int_{B_{8r}(x_{0})}(u+\varepsilon)^{q-p}\,dx+C\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{8r}(x_{0})\times B_{7r}(x_{0})}\frac{(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dy\,dx.

Next, define the truncated logarithmic function for
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})

v=\left[\min\left\{\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right),\;\log\!\left(\frac{k+\varepsilon}{u+\varepsilon}\right)\right\}\right]_{+}.

Then using the Poincaré inequality in local form
[
40
, Theorem
2
of Chapter
5
, Section 5.8]
and logarithmic estimate (
5.33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}|v-(v)_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}|\,dx\leq C(N,p)r|B_{6r}(x_{0})|^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}|\nabla v|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(5.34)

\displaystyle\leq Cr|B_{6r}|^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}\left|\nabla\log(u+\varepsilon)\right|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(N,s,p,q)r|B_{6r}|^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\left[r^{N-p}+r^{-qs}\int_{B_{8r}(x_{0})}(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}\,dx\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{8r}(x_{0})\times B_{7r}(x_{0})}\frac{(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dy\,dx\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

By assumption, we have the measure estimate

\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\{v=0\}\right|\geq\frac{\tau}{6^{N}}|B_{6r}(x_{0})|.

Using this and the definition of
v
, we obtain

\displaystyle\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\{v=0\}|}\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\{v=0\}}\big(\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)-v(x)\big)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6^{N}}{\tau}\frac{1}{|B_{6r}(x_{0})|}\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}\big(\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)-v(x)\big)\,dx\leq\frac{6^{N}}{\tau}\left(\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)-(v)_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}\right).

Integrating the previous estimate, we obtain

\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{v=\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)\right\}\right|\,\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)\leq\frac{6^{N}}{\tau}\int_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}|v-(v)_{B_{6r}(x_{0})}|\,dx.

(5.35)

Using estimate (
5.35
) in (
5.34
), the fact
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and the inequality
|x-y|\geq C|x_{0}-y|
, for some constant
C>0
with
x\in B_{7r}(x_{0})
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{8r}(x_{0})
, we deduce

\displaystyle\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{v=\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\tau\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)}\,r^{1+\frac{(p-1)N}{p}}\Bigg[r^{N-p}+r^{-qs}\int_{B_{8r}(x_{0})}(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{8r}(x_{0}))\times B_{7r}(x_{0})}\frac{(u(x)+\varepsilon)^{q-p}}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dy\,dx\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(N,s,p,q,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})})}{\tau\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)}\,r^{1+\frac{(p-1)N}{p}}\left[Cr^{N-p}+Cr^{N-sq}\right]^{1/p}.

Recalling the definition of
v
, we equivalently obtain

\displaystyle\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{u\leq 2\delta k-\varepsilon\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{u\leq 2\delta(k+\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\right\}\right|=\left|B_{6r}(x_{0})\cap\left\{v=\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\tau\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)}\,r^{1+\frac{(p-1)N}{p}}\left[r^{N-p}+r^{N-sq}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\frac{C}{\tau\log\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\delta}\right)}|B_{6r}(x_{0})|.

where
C=C(N,s,p,q,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})})
. Here we have crucially used that
p>sq
and
r\in(0,1]
.

Step 2:

In this step we will establish the following: there exists
\delta^{*}>0
such that, for every
\delta\in(0,\delta^{*})
,

\essinf_{B_{4r}(x_{0})}u\geq\delta k-2\varepsilon.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\delta k>2\varepsilon.

(5.36)

Let
\rho\in[r,6r]
and let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\rho}(x_{0}))
be a cutoff function such that
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
in
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
. Define

w:=(\ell-u)_{+},

for some
\ell\in(\delta k,2\delta k)
, where
\delta
is as in Step 1. We take
\eta:=w\varphi^{q}
as a test function. Then, using the weak formulation, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla(w\varphi^{q})\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{J_{q}(u(x)-u(y))\big(w\varphi^{q}(x)-w\varphi^{q}(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\,dx\,dy

(5.37)

\displaystyle:=I_{1}+I_{2}.

Applying Young’s inequality, we obtain

I_{1}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}|\nabla w|^{p}\varphi^{q}\,dx+C(p,q)\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}|\nabla\varphi|^{p}w^{p}\varphi^{q-p}\,dx.

(5.38)

To estimate
I_{2}
, we borrow the fractional term estimate in Lemma
3.2
of
[
36
]

\inf_{B_{1}}U\geq c^{*}
. We then define the following minimization problem.

\mathcal{A}:=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\left\{[u]_{1,p}^{p}+[u]_{s,q}^{q}:u\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}\;\text{ on }\;B_{1}\right\}

(
A
)

Proposition 6.2
.

The problem (

A

) has a unique positive solution
U_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C_{loc}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{B_{1}}^{c})
. Moreover,
U_{0}
is radial and non-increasing, and
U_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
solves in the weak sense

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}\,U_{0}+(-\Delta_{q})^{s}U_{0}=0&\text{in }\overline{B_{1}}^{c},\\[4.0pt]
U_{0}\equiv\frac{c^{*}}{2}&\text{in }\overline{B}_{1}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The existence of a minimizer follows directly as in
[
16
, Proposition
3.5
]
. Note that, since the constraint forces nontriviality, we have
U_{0}\not\equiv 0
.

Define the functional
\mathcal{E}(u):=\frac{[u]_{{1,p}}^{p}}{p}+\frac{[u]_{{s,q}}^{q}}{q}
. Since the functional
\mathcal{E}
is strictly convex on
\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and the admissible set
\mathcal{K}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):u\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}\;\text{ on }\;B_{1}\right\}
is convex and closed. Uniqueness of the minimizer follows immediately. Positivity follows from the maximum principle.

The Schwarz decreasing rearrangement
U_{0}^{\#}
of
U_{0}
defined via the layer-cake representation: for each
t>0
,

\{U_{0}^{\#}>t\}=B_{r(t)},\qquad r(t):=\left(\frac{|\{U_{0}>t\}|}{\omega_{N}}\right)^{1/N},

where
\omega_{N}=|B_{1}|
, so that
|B_{r(t)}|=|\{U_{0}>t\}|
. It satisfies

[U_{0}^{\#}]_{{1,p}}\leq[U_{0}]_{{1,p}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[U_{0}^{\#}]_{{s,q}}\leq[U_{0}]_{{s,q}},

by the Pólya-Szegő inequality for
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,p}
(see
[
49
, Theorem
2.1.3
]
) and its fractional analogue for
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,q}
(see
[
58
, Theorem 6.2]
). Moreover,
U_{0}^{\#}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}\chi_{B_{1}}
. Indeed, fix any
0\leq t<\frac{c^{*}}{2}.
Since
U_{0}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}
on
B_{1}
, we have

B_{1}\subseteq\{U_{0}>t\}
, and therefore

|\{U_{0}>t\}|\geq|B_{1}|=\omega_{N},

which gives
B_{1}\subseteq B_{r(t)}=\{U_{0}^{\#}>t\}
.
Since this holds for every
t\in[0,\frac{c^{*}}{2})
, letting
t\nearrow\frac{c^{*}}{2}
yields

U_{0}^{\#}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}
on
B_{1}
, hence
U_{0}^{\#}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}\chi_{B_{1}}
.
Thus
U_{0}^{\#}
is admissible and achieves energy no larger than
U_{0}
, uniqueness forces
U_{0}^{\#}=U_{0}
. Hence
U_{0}
is radial and non-increasing.

Now, for every
u\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows from
[
44
, Remark 3.3]
that

\mathcal{E}(\max\{u,t\})+\mathcal{E}(\min\{u,t\})\leq\mathcal{E}(u).

(6.61)

Applying (
6.61
) shows that
\min\left\{U_{0},\frac{c^{*}}{2}\right\}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}\chi_{B_{1}}
is admissible and
\mathcal{E}(\min\{U_{0},\frac{c^{*}}{2}\})\leq\mathcal{E}(U_{0})
. By minimality and uniqueness,
\min\{U_{0},\frac{c^{*}}{2}\}=U_{0}
, i.e.
U_{0}\leq\frac{c^{*}}{2}
. Combined with the constraint
U_{0}\geq\frac{c^{*}}{2}
on
B_{1}
, we conclude
U_{0}\equiv\frac{c^{*}}{2}
in
\overline{B}_{1}
. Since
U_{0}
minimizes
\mathcal{E}
freely on
\overline{B_{1}}^{\,c}
, it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation for
\mathcal{E}
in the weak sense.

Finally, applying Theorem
5
of
[
32
]
, we get
U_{0}\in C_{loc}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{B_{1}}^{\,c})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
U_{0}
be the unique solution of (

A

). If
R>1
then
U_{0}(R)<\frac{c^{*}}{2}
.

Proof.

Define

x^{*}=\sup\{|z|\in[0,\infty):U_{0}(|y|)=\frac{c^{*}}{2}\text{ for all }y\in[0,|z|)\}.

Since,
U_{0}
is continuous,
x^{*}
is achieved. We have to show that
x^{*}=1
. On contrary, assume that
x^{*}>1
. Then we define
U_{1}(x)=U_{0}(x^{*}x)
. Then clearly
U_{1}
is an admissible function. Consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(U_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}[U_{1}]_{1,p}^{p}+\frac{1}{q}[U_{1}]_{s,q}^{q}=\frac{1}{p}(x^{*})^{p-N}[U_{0}]_{1,p}^{p}+\frac{1}{q}(x^{*})^{sq-N}[U_{0}]_{s,q}^{q}<\mathcal{E}(U_{0}).

But this is contrary to the definition of
U_{0}
. Therefore,
U_{0}(R)<\frac{c^{*}}{2}
.
∎

Lemma 6.5

(Nonlocal computation for
\bar{U}
)
.

Let
U_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a positive radially symmetric
decreasing solution of (

A

).
Define

\bar{U}(x):=\min\{U_{0}(x),\,U_{0}(2)\},

Then
\bar{U}\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
and for a.e.
|x|>3
:

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\bar{U}),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle\geq\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\frac{c_{1}}{|x|^{N+sq}}\varphi(x)\,dx>0

(6.62)

for all nonnegative
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
,
where

c_{1}:=\frac{2\omega_{N}\cdot 3^{N+sq}}{4^{N+sq}}\cdot c_{0}>0,\qquad c_{0}:=\begin{cases}2^{2-q}(U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}&\text{if }q\geq 2,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{(U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}}{2^{q-1}}&\text{if }1<q<2.\end{cases}

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\bar{U}\in\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
.
Following as in Lemma
2.8
of
[
50
]
, for all nonnegative
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
, we get

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\bar{U}),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\langle-\Delta_{p}\bar{U}(x),\varphi\rangle_{p}+\langle(-\Delta)_{q}^{s}\bar{U}(x),\varphi\rangle_{s,q}

\displaystyle=\langle-\Delta_{p}U_{0}(x),\varphi\rangle_{p}+\langle(-\Delta)_{q}^{s}U_{0}(x),\varphi\rangle_{s,q}

(6.63)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(2))-J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\varphi(x)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(U_{0})(x),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle+2\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(2))-J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\varphi(x)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{J_{q}(U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(x))-J_{q}(U_{0}(2)-U_{0}(x))}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\varphi(x)\,dy\,dx,

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{J_{q}(U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(x))-J_{q}(U_{0}(2)-U_{0}(x))}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}\varphi(x)\,dy\,dx.

(6.64)

The above computation is valid for each
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
since
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}({U_{0}}),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle<\infty
and

\displaystyle\iint_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}\times\overline{B_{2}}}\frac{|J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(2))|+|J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y))|}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}|\varphi(x)|\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C(N,s,q)(c^{*}/2)^{q-1}\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\frac{|\varphi(x)|}{|x|^{N+sq}}\,dx\leq C\mathcal{S}_{f}^{-1/q}[\varphi]_{s,q}<\infty.

Now, to get a lower bound on the RHS of
(
6.64
), we consider two cases.
For
q\geq 2
, we apply
[
16
, eq. (2.2)]
, which states that for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\geq 0
,
q\geq 2
, we have

J_{q}(a)-J_{q}(a+b)\leq-2^{2-q}b^{q-1}.

(6.65)

Set
a:=U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y)<0
and
b:=U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(2)\geq U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2)>0
for
y\in B_{1}
. Applying (
6.65
), we deduce

\displaystyle J_{q}(U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(x))-J_{q}(U_{0}(2)-U_{0}(x))\geq 2^{2-q}(U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{2-q}(U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}=:c_{0}>0.

For the case
1<q<2
, we apply
[
16
, eq. (2.4)]
,
which states that for all
a\in[0,A]
,
b\geq 0
,
q\in(1,2]
, we have

J_{q}(a)-J_{q}(a-b)\geq\max\left\{J_{q}(A)-J_{q}(A-b),\;\frac{b^{q-1}}{2^{q-1}}\right\}.

(6.66)

Set
a:=U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(x)>0
and
b:=U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(2)\geq U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2)>0
for
y\in B_{1}
, and
A:=\|U_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
.
Applying (
6.66
), we get

J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(2))-J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y))\geq\frac{(U_{0}(y)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}}{2^{q-1}}\geq\frac{(U_{0}(1)-U_{0}(2))^{q-1}}{2^{q-1}}=:\!c_{0}>0,

Therefore, using the above estimates together with the fact that for
y\in B_{1}
and
|x|\geq 3
,
|x-y|\leq\frac{4}{3}|x|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\frac{J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(2))-J_{q}(U_{0}(x)-U_{0}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+sq}}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{1}}\frac{c_{0}}{\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{N+sq}|x|^{N+sq}}dy=\frac{c_{0}\,\omega_{N}}{\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{N+sq}|x|^{N+sq}}.

Substituting the above computation into
(
6.64
) yields
(
6.62
).
∎

Proof of
(ii)
Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\bar{U}
,
c_{1}
, and
C
be as in Lemmas
6.3
and

6.5
, and set

\varepsilon:=\min\{\left(\frac{c_{1}}{C}\right)^{\!1/(q-1)},U_{0}(3)\}>0.

By Lemma
6.3
and the scaling properties of
\mathcal{L}_{p,q}
, it follows that

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\varepsilon\widetilde{\Gamma}),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\varepsilon^{p-1}\underbrace{\langle-\Delta_{p}\widetilde{\Gamma},\varphi\rangle_{p}}_{=\,0}+\varepsilon^{q-1}\langle(-\Delta)_{q}^{s}\widetilde{\Gamma},\varphi\rangle_{s,q}\leq\int_{\overline{B_{3}}^{c}}\frac{\varepsilon^{q-1}C}{|x|^{N+sq}}\varphi\,dx

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\overline{B_{3}}^{c})
.
Thus, by the choice of
\varepsilon
together with Lemma
6.5
, it follows that

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\varepsilon\widetilde{\Gamma})\leq\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\bar{U})
.
For the boundary condition on
B_{3}
, note that
\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)\leq 1

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
\inf_{x\in\overline{B_{3}}}\bar{U}(x)=U_{0}(3).

Hence,

\varepsilon\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)\leq\varepsilon\leq U_{0}(3)\leq\bar{U}(x)\qquad\forall\,x\in B_{3}.

An application of
Theorem
4.1
then yields

\varepsilon\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)\leq\bar{U}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\overline{B_{3}}^{c},

and consequently, for a.e.
x\in\overline{B_{3}}^{c}
,

U_{0}(x)=\bar{U}(x)\geq\varepsilon\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)=\varepsilon|x|^{-\alpha}=\varepsilon|x|^{-(N-p)/(p-1)}.

(6.67)

It remains to compare
U_{0}
and
U
on
\overline{B_{1}}^{c}
. For any

\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(\overline{B_{1}}^{c})
, one has

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(U_{0}),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=0\leq\langle\!\langle U^{p^{*}-1},\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(U),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle.

Moreover, for each
x\in\overline{B}_{1}
,

U_{0}(x)=\frac{c^{*}}{2}<c^{*}\leq U(x).

Since
U_{0},U\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset\mathcal{D}^{s,p,q}(\overline{B_{1}}^{c})
, a further application of Theorem
4.1

therefore gives

U_{0}(x)\leq U(x)\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\overline{B_{1}}^{c}.

(6.68)

Combining (
6.67
) and (
6.68
) completes
the proof.
∎
