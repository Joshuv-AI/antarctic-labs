Cumulative Duration of Severe Psoriasis: A Novel Cardiovascular Risk Predictor for Dermatologists
Authors: Bernadett B Huszti, Noémi Á Galajda, Seo-Ho Cho, Mária V Kolonics, Éva A Piros, Andrea Lukács, Bálint Szilveszter, Borbála Vattay, Klára Szalai, András Bánvölgyi, Lajos V Kemény, Pál Maurovich Horvat, Adrienn K Poór, Béla Merkely, Péter Holló
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe association between moderate-to-severe psoriasis and cardiovascular (CV) risk is well known. No clear link between disease activity, disease duration, and CV risk has been established thus far to identify high CV risk patients with psoriasis without CV symptoms. We aimed to investigate the relationship between moderate-to-severe psoriasis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) by introducing and determining the cutoff value for a new tool, the cumulative duration of severe psoriasis (CDSP), reflecting the total duration of severe psoriasis skin symptoms.MethodsNinety-eight asymptomatic patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis were enrolled in this cross-sectional study, without cardiac symptoms. CDSP was recorded using a structured questionnaire. CDSP threshold, defined as Coronary Artery Calcium Score (CACS) > 0, was determined using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Ultrasound (intima-media thickness (IMT) and carotid, brachial, and femoral artery plaque burden) and cardiac computed tomography (CACS, segment involvement score (SIS), and coronary artery disease-reporting and data system severity (CAD-RADS™)) were performed. Controls (n = 248) were matched for age, sex, body mass index, and, where possible, comorbidities.ResultsCACS was significantly higher in psoriasis (mean 159.02, standard deviation 365.60) compared to controls (73.12, 166.08), P = 0.029. The CDSP threshold was 60.5 months. Patients with long-CDSP (> 60.5 months) had significantly higher CACS (214.63, 430.11) than patients with short-CDSP (57.61, 162.53), P = 0.012. SIS and CAD-RADS were also significantly higher in the long-CDSP group compared to the short-CDSP group (4.50, 3.85 vs. 1.39, 2.06), P = 0.002 and (2.06, 1.63 vs. 0.71, 1.08), P = 0.003, respectively. Patients with long-CDSP showed a non-significant trend towards higher IMT and peripheral plaque burden.ConclusionPatients with long-CDSP had a greater extent and severity of CVD. This innovative predictor is easily determined by dermatologists and assists in the early identification of asymptomatic high CV risk patients with psoriasis.Graphical abstract available for this article.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01872-1.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Cardiovascular diseases, Inflammation, Psoriasis

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Severity measured at a single time point has been associated with increased cardiovascular (CV) risk in patients with psoriasis.It is plausible that shorter durations of severe psoriasis do not carry the same CV risk as longer durations of severe psoriasis.This study aimed to identify an easily assessable marker for dermatologists that integrates both severity and duration of severe disease, thereby enabling a comprehensive evaluation of CV risk in patients with psoriasis. What was learned from the study? This study reinforces the notion that moderate-to-severe psoriasis is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD).The authors consider CDSP to be a promising approach for early identification of asymptomatic high CV risk patients with psoriasis by dermatologists, and it can facilitate patient-centered, multidisciplinary care required for individuals at high CV risk.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a common, chronic, immune-mediated systemic inflammatory disease [1, 2]. Besides frequent comorbidities such as psoriatic arthritis, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, depression, and anxiety, psoriasis is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [3–6]. This may be explained by the shared immune-mediated pathways between atherosclerosis and psoriasis, principally the interleukin (IL)-23/T helper (Th) 17 cell axis [7, 8] as well as inflammatory cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFNγ) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) [9]. In atherosclerosis, IL-12 stimulation prompts Th1 cells to secrete TNFα and INFγ, thereby facilitating plaque growth. Meanwhile, IL-23-mediated activation of Th17 cells leads to IL-17 and IL-22 release, promoting intraplaque neoangiogenesis, hemorrhage, and unstable, rupture-prone plaques [10, 11]. There is a growing notion that psoriasis is an independent risk factor for CVD [12–17]. Patients with psoriasis are up to 50% more likely to develop CVD [18] and life expectancy is reduced by approximately 5 years, predominantly attributed to CVD [19]. Psoriasis is associated with increased coronary artery plaque burden [20–23] and peripheral arterial intima-media thickness (IMT) [24–26]. Several studies found that biological therapies may mitigate cardiovascular (CV) risk in psoriasis [26–31]. A Swedish study proposed that the Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI) is an independent marker for future cardiovascular events. The researchers observed that for each one-point increase in PASI score, CV event risk increases by 4% [32]. Moreover, severe psoriasis confers a further 6.2% absolute risk of a 10-year rate of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [33], increases stroke risk by 44% [12], and increases the risk of cardiovascular mortality [34].

Previously, the focus was on severity at a single point in time or the overall disease duration when discussing CV risk in psoriasis. To our knowledge, there is currently no method that integrates both severity and duration into a single tool. It is reasonable to assume that shorter durations of severe inflammation do not carry the same CV risk as chronic severe inflammation. This study aimed to explore the relationship between the cumulative duration of severe psoriasis (CDSP) and CVD by assessing coronary artery disease (CAD), carotid artery disease (CaD), and peripheral arterial disease (PAD). The authors propose CDSP as a potential surrogate marker for subclinical atherosclerosis in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, which dermatologists can easily assess to identify high CV risk patients with psoriasis.

Methods

Study Design

Between September 2019 and August 2024, a cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Dermatology, Venereology and Dermatooncology of Semmelweis University, Budapest, Hungary. Patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis (n = 98) and controls (n = 248) without cardiac symptoms or known cardiac history were enrolled. Controls were matched to cases in a 1:1 to 1:3 ratio based on age (± 3 years), sex, and body mass index (BMI) (± 1 kg/m2) to minimize potential confounding and to increase statistical power. Where possible, matching was extended to include one or more of the following risk factors: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Nine patients did not have a matched control as a result of their young age. One patient had one, 14 patients had two, and 72 patients had three matched controls. Three controls were retained for a case with missing computed tomography (CT)-related variables, given the relatively small sample size of the study.

Cumulative Duration of Severe Psoriasis

CDSP refers to the total number of months during which the patient experienced severe skin symptoms. Severe psoriasis was defined as PASI > 10, or body surface area (BSA) > 10, or requiring systemic or whole body surface phototherapy. CDSP was determined by summing the untreated severe duration, the ineffectively treated severe duration, and the severe duration between treatments. CDSP was assessed using patient-reported data collected by the evaluating doctor during a face-to-face interview, following a standardized questionnaire in Hungarian, detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1. Reliable medical records, from the National Health Insurance database and the hospital system, were reviewed to corroborate the CDSP. Data used for statistical analysis comprised the minimum duration confirmed conclusively by the interview to minimize the risk of overestimation.

Ultrasound

Ultrasound examination was performed by a radiologist at the Department of Dermatology, Venereology and Dermatooncology of Semmelweis University, Budapest, Hungary. The IMT of the left and right carotid, brachial and femoral arteries was manually measured on the dorsal arterial wall using longitudinal B-mode ultrasound images obtained with a linear array transducer (3–13 MHz; Hitachi Preirus Al Vision, Hitachi Medical Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The carotid IMT (cIMT) was measured 1 cm proximally from the bifurcation of the common carotid artery. The brachial IMT (bIMT) was measured at the mid-third of the artery. The femoral IMT (fIMT) was measured 1 cm proximally from the bifurcation of the common femoral artery. The highest IMT value was recorded for each artery and denoted as max IMT. Arteries were evaluated for soft and calcified plaque burden; the latter shows acoustic shadowing. Plaque was defined as a focal structure that protruded at least 0.5 mm into the vessel lumen, and/or had a thickness greater than 50% of the adjacent IMT measured, and/or had an IMT ≥ 1.5 mm [35].

Cardiac Computed Tomography

Non-contrast CT (NCCT) and contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) were performed in accordance with Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) guidelines [36]. Different CAD phenotypes were collected from the CT reports reflecting stenosis severity (coronary artery disease-reporting and data system, CAD-RADS), and extent of the disease (segment involvement score, SIS; coronary artery calcium score, CACS). SIS reflects the number of coronary segments affected by plaque, categorized as mild, ≤ 2; moderate, 3–4; severe, 5–7; and extensive, ≥ 8. CAD-RADS stenosis severity categories were determined, by detecting the degree of the worse stenosis in the coronary system (0, 0%; 1, 1–24%; 2, 25–49%; 3, 50–69%; 4, (A) 70–99% or (B) left main > 50% or three-vessel disease; 5, 100%) according to the CAD-RADS consensus document [37]. For more details, see Supplementary Information 1.

Descriptive and Comparative Analyses

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Continuous variables were summarized as mean + standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using an independent samples t test for normally distributed continuous variables, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

ROC-Based Duration Stratification

As a calcium score of zero is considered to be a strong protective factor against CV risk [38–40], self-reported severe disease duration was dichotomized into short- and long-CDSP groups based on CACS > 0 for downstream comparisons, using a threshold identified from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The optimal cutoff point was determined using Youden’s J statistic, maximizing sensitivity and specificity. CACS values were used alongside self-reported disease duration as input variables for the ROC analysis.

CDSP Subgroup Comparison

A multivariable logistic regression was performed to adjust for age of onset, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia for the analysis between the short- and long-CDSP subgroups.

Carotid IMT Comparison to Age-Adjusted Norms

Each patient’s maximum carotid IMT value was compared with age-adjusted upper limits established in a healthy population: 0.6 mm (ages 35–39), 0.64 mm (40–49), 0.71 mm (50–59), and 0.81 mm (> 60 years) [41]. A one-sided one-sample t test was used to assess whether the individual’s carotid IMT significantly exceeded their age-specific normative threshold. This analysis was performed separately for the short- and long-CDSP groups. Missing data were assessed for key variables. No imputation was performed due to the observational design and the small proportion of missing values.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio with R version 4.5.0 (The R foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Regional Institutional Scientific and Research Committee of Semmelweis University, Budapest, Hungary (SE RKEB: 154/2019). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All enrolled patients provided informed consent. The patients in this manuscript have given written informed consent to the publication of their case details. The CT scan was performed specifically for this study after obtaining approval from patients with psoriasis without cardiac symptoms or a clinical indication. The matched controls were selected from asymptomatic individuals with different indications, such as preoperative and pre-ablation cases.

Comparison of Cases and Controls in Terms of CAD Phenotypes

No significant differences were observed between cases and controls in terms of age, sex, BMI, or comorbidities. CACS was significantly higher in patients (M = 159.02, SD 365.60) than in controls (M = 73.12, SD 166.08), P = 0.029. Of the 46 patients who underwent a CECT, 60.9% (n = 28) had mild, 15.2% (n = 7) moderate, 15.2% (n = 7) severe, and 8.7% (n = 4) extensive coronary artery plaque burden upon SIS. SIS was significantly lower in psoriasis (M = 2.61, SD 3.24) than in controls (M = 3.15, SD 2.08), P = 0.002. Absence of CAD was observed in 39.1% (n = 18), minimal nonobstructive CAD in 32.6% (n = 15), mild nonobstructive CAD in 10.9% (n = 5), moderate stenosis in 4.3% (n = 2), severe stenosis in 8.7% (n = 4), and total coronary artery occlusion in 4.3% (n = 2) patients with psoriasis based on CAD-RADS. The mean CAD-RADS stenosis score was also significantly lower in psoriasis (M = 1.24, SD 1.46) than in controls (M = 1.60, SD 0.72), P < 0.001. Relationship Between CDSP and CAD

On the basis of the CDSP threshold, cases were classified into short-CDSP (n = 36) and long-CDSP (n = 62) subgroups. Within the short-CDSP group, 34 patients underwent a CT scan, of whom 67.6% (n = 23) had CACS = 0 and 32.4% (n = 11) had CACS > 0. In the long-CDSP group, 37.1% (n = 23) had CACS = 0 and 62.9% (n = 39) had CACS > 0, as summarized in Fig. 3. In multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age of onset, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, CDSP remained independently associated with CACS > 0 (adjusted OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.43, p = 0.002). The proportion of patients with CACS of 0 was significantly higher in the short-CDSP group than in the long-CDSP group, P = 0.008.

Discussion

This study has introduced a novel predictor, the cumulative duration of severe psoriasis, which integrates severity and duration of severe psoriasis symptoms to investigate the relationship between severe psoriasis and CVD. The results show that patients with psoriasis had higher CACS than the matched controls, and the long-CDSP subgroup was associated with greater CAD extent and severity, as well as higher CaD and PAD severity (Figs. 8 and 9).

Coronary artery calcium score (CACS) is measured on non-contrast-enhanced cardiac CT images. Semi-automated software is used to identify calcified lesions within the coronary arteries, and the calcium score is calculated accordingly. In this case, calcifications are present in the left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary artery (RCA). Minimal calcification is also observed in the aorta (purple); however, aortic calcification is not included in the CACS calculation. The total CACS is 225, corresponding to moderate coronary artery disease

Three-dimensional volume-rendered image of the heart and curved multiplanar reconstructions of the three main coronary arteries: the left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary artery (RCA). Coronary artery segments (proximal, mid, distal) are indicated on each vessel. The segment involvement score (SIS) is calculated as the total number of coronary artery segments containing any atherosclerotic plaque. The coronary artery disease-reporting and data system (CAD-RADS) classification is determined by the most severe stenosis identified in the entire coronary artery tree. In this case, plaques are present in the proximal and mid LAD, proximal and mid-distal LCX, and proximal and mid RCA segments, resulting in an SIS of 6. The lesion in the proximal RCA causes mild (25–49%) stenosis, while all other lesions cause minimal (< 25%) stenosis. Accordingly, the CAD-RADS category is 2

Multiple studies suggested that psoriasis is an independent risk factor for CVD [13–17, 33], with an independent association between any coronary artery calcification and psoriasis [23]. The EuroGuiDerm guideline highlighted that although several studies identified psoriasis as an independent risk factor, patients with psoriasis have a higher prevalence of traditional risk factors, such as hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Therefore, whether psoriasis is unequivocally an independent risk factor for CVD is still to be confirmed [42]. Patients with psoriasis were compared to an age-, sex-, BMI-, and comorbidity-matched control group to mitigate these factors. The CACS was significantly higher in psoriasis, supporting the notion that psoriasis is an independent CV risk factor.

Disease severity and disease duration in CV risk have been extensively researched in psoriasis. Researchers proposed that disease severity is the key factor; thus, patients with severe psoriasis are at higher risk of CVD [16, 20, 21, 33, 43–48]. The Fifth Joint Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology and Other Societies on Cardiovascular Disease Prevention in Clinical Practice guideline emphasizes that high immune-mediated inflammation is likely to increase CV risk in severe psoriasis compared with milder disease [13]. A meta-analysis found that mild psoriasis showed either no or a very low increase in CV risk [43]. Besides severity, research has shown an association between disease duration and CV risk [46]. Each additional year with psoriasis increased the risk of future CV events by 1.0% even after adjusting for traditional risk factors, suggesting that prolonged low-grade chronic inflammation is likely a major contributor to CV risk [44]; however, some studies did not observe an association between psoriasis disease duration and CV risk [22, 49]. The aforementioned studies highlighted the importance of both duration and severity as likely determinants of CV risk in psoriasis, demonstrating a dose–response relationship [48, 50]. It has been suggested that if a patient fails to meet the criteria for moderate-to-severe psoriasis, extended disease duration and prolonged history of untreated psoriasis should also be considered when determining the CV risk [18]. Furthermore, the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline underscored the relevance of cumulative disease burden and recent degree of inflammation when determining CV risk in psoriasis [51]. As both severity and duration have been associated with increased CV risk, our analysis focused on the cumulative duration of severe psoriasis to examine the effect of chronic systemic inflammation on CVD in psoriasis, by comparing the short (≤ 60.5 months) and long-CDSP (> 60.5 months) subgroups. Previous studies found an increased CV risk for overall durations longer than 8–9 years [52, 53]. Our results show that when disease severity is also accounted for, the cutoff duration is considerably lower, at 60.5 months.

A CACS of zero is a strong protective factor against CV risk [38–40]. Thus, we investigated the effect of CDSP on CACS = 0. A significantly higher occurrence of CACS = 0 was observed in the short-CDSP group. Moreover, the long-CDSP group had a significantly higher mean CACS than the short-CDSP group, indicating that CV risk is higher when CDSP > 60.5 months.

Other scoring and reporting systems, including the SIS [54] and the CAD-RADS [37], are widely used in cardiology to evaluate CAD. The utilization of such tools is yet to be adopted in psoriasis research. SIS has been evaluated in patients with psoriatic arthritis; findings showed that SIS was higher in psoriatic arthritis compared to controls [55, 56]. Our literature review shows that SIS has not been studied in psoriasis. To our knowledge, no study has investigated CAD-RADS in psoriasis. Interestingly, we found that CAD-RADS and SIS scores were significantly higher in the control group than in the psoriasis group. Although patients with long-CDSP had higher SIS and CAD-RADS values than the control, these differences were not statistically significant. Among patients with shorter durations, the SIS and CAD-RADS did not exceed those of the controls, suggesting that CDSP > 60.5 is associated with increased CV risk. One possible explanation for this is our relatively small sample size. Subgroup analysis revealed that both SIS and CAD-RADS were significantly higher for patients with long-CDSP compared to patients with short-CDSP. A study has shown that an SIS score > 4 increases CV risk [57]. This aligns with our results, demonstrating that patients with long-CDSP had moderate-to-severe SIS scores and thus have an increased CV risk. Previous research has described lipid-rich, non-calcified plaques as vulnerable and rupture-prone [58]. SIS accounts for such soft plaques as well, whereas CACS only considers calcified plaques. Therefore, performing CECT may be beneficial in patients with CDSP > 60.5 months, as CACS alone does not detect higher-risk soft plaques. The average CAD-RADS score for patients with long-CDSP was in the mild nonobstructive CAD category, higher than that of the short-CDSP and control groups.

A positive correlation has been reported between severity, duration, and IMT [24, 59]. A recent study reinforces that mild and moderate-to-severe psoriasis is associated with a higher cIMT, with the highest cIMT in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis [50]. However, others found no association between duration and IMT [25]. While the latter study did not differentiate between severe and overall duration, the findings were similar to ours. Although we observed higher IMTs in the long-CDSP group, indicating a greater severity of CaD and PAD, the difference was not statistically significant. Both subgroups had cIMT values that were statistically higher than the age-adjusted healthy upper limit. Our previous studies showed that IMT values can change within a relatively short period of time following the initiation of effective anti-inflammatory therapy [26, 29]. Our patients had moderate-to-severe psoriasis, thus it is conceivable that shorter durations of inflammation may already cause peripheral changes, but prolonged inflammation is necessary for coronary involvement. It is plausible to conclude that although CDSP > 60.5 carries a high CV risk in patients with shorter CDSP, CV risk, in the form of peripheral involvement, cannot be entirely excluded.

This study has limitations. The controls did not undergo ultrasound examinations, and some cases were not matched because of their young age. Even with a strict matching method, residual confounding from other inflammatory processes may occur. CDSP threshold was derived and evaluated within the same cohort and therefore requires external validation. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to draw comprehensive conclusions about the validity and clinical efficacy of CDSP. Another limitation concerns data collection. CDSP was determined using patient-reported data. Several patients had severe psoriasis prior to the implementation of the nationwide Hungarian electronic medical record system in 2017. Despite potential recall bias, patient-reported data were used to help bridge this gap and minimize the risk of under- or overestimation.

The results of this study may be applied to other chronic systemic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, as it highlights the role of sustained high disease activity as a pivotal contributor to the increased CV risk reported in the literature [60].

Conclusion

Our results show that psoriasis is an independent risk factor for CVD. Patients with a CDSP > 60.5 months have a significantly higher CVD risk. CDSP is a novel predictor easily determined by dermatologists to aid early identification of asymptomatic high CV risk patients with psoriasis, particularly as these patients are primarily managed within dermatology care. The authors encourage further research into CDSP to better understand its clinical significance and potential future applications.
