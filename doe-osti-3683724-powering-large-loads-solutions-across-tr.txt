ABSTRACT

The rapid growth of large loads (e.g., energy-intensive industrial facilities, data centers, and other
large electricity users) is creating new challenges for power system planning and grid infrastructure
development. These loads require substantial electricity demand on faster timelines, straining grid
assets and introducing new coordination and planning challenges among grid operators, utilities,
regulators, developers, and customers. This report synthesizes current strategies for accommo-
dating large load growth through a review of publicly available technical literature and media
insights around the United States (U.S.). Solutions are organized across three implementation
scales (transmission, utility, and facility) and categorized according to common implementation
pathways (including structural expansion, upgrades, operations and efficiency, and planning and
policy). Representative regional examples are further examined across the nine U.S. Census Di-
visions to illustrate how local infrastructure, regulatory environments, resource availability, and
market structures can influence deployment. This review shows that multiple solutions are being
explored concurrently to address growing large load demand. Furthermore, successful integration
of large loads into the grid relies on coordinated portfolios of complementary actions implemented
across multiple decision-making levels and planning horizons. For example, short-term opera-
tions and customer-side flexibility can help bridge near-term capacity constraints while longer-term
transmission and generation investments are being developed. Collectively, these findings pro-
vide insights into the various solutions being developed across scales and regions to power large
loads.

1. INTRODUCTION

Electricity demand is rising significantly in many regions, with forecasts indicating substantial
increases over the next decade. Globally, annual electricity demand is expected to increase by
∼3.6% per year from 2026-2030 [1]. In the United States (U.S.), nationwide electricity demand
growth of ∼4.7% annually is expected between 2024–2029 [2]. Peak demand is also expected
to increase substantially, with one industry outlook projecting the U.S. peak demand could grow
by ∼26% by 2035 [3]. Recent assessments also show that load growth is not evenly distributed
across the U.S. and may be driven by multiple interacting factors, including large load clusters
(e.g., data centers, manufacturing), electrification, population trends, water systems, and extreme
weather [4]. Among these drivers, large loads are growing rapidly, with one outlook projecting
data center demand could reach ∼176 gigawatt (GW) by 2035 and industrial electrification adding
another ∼25 GW of demand by 2030 [3]. Additionally, these large loads are reshaping the grid and
long-term planning because of their rapid arrival, geographic clustering, congestion patterns, and
infrastructure investment decisions [5].

The pace of large load development has already prompted changes in interconnection and planning
practices. For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) has developed ap-
proaches for evaluating multiple large load interconnection requests concurrently [6], and utilities
such as Georgia Power have reported large load pipelines on the order of GWs over relatively short
time horizons [7]. In major clusters such as Northern Virginia (“Data Center Alley”), growth has
driven transmission constraints and new high-voltage (HV) buildouts for both direct current (DC)
and alternating current (AC) transmission lines [8]. There are also concerns being raised about
grid responses. For example, reliability organizations have highlighted that large load behavior
during disturbances and commissioning practices can introduce system risks if not appropriately
studied and coordinated [9]. In addition to power availability, large load siting and facility design
decisions are increasingly shaped by local resource constraints such as water availability, motivating
technology choices such as “zero-water” cooling designs [10].

Many of these challenges are fundamentally regional, since power availability is shaped not only by
physical infrastructure, but also on regional characteristics (e.g., operational practices, environmen-
tal conditions, governance structures, and regulatory requirements) [4]. Recent work by Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) provides a catalog of solutions for accelerating large load
connections (e.g., load forecasting, interconnection service options, procurement approaches, mar-
ket and operational mechanisms, and cost allocation and rate-making tools) [11]. We complement
this catalog with a review of solutions for powering large loads at multiple decision-making levels.1

Specifically, we synthesize solution strategies being used to address large load-driven constraints
and compare how approaches vary by actor (e.g., Independent System Operators (ISOs)/ Regional

available sources, including primary materials (e.g., regulatory dockets, technical reports, planning
documents) as well as media coverage that captures emerging projects, implementation challenges,
and public or stakeholder sentiments. While much of the information is specific to data centers,
the solutions apply broadly to all large loads, including manufacturing, electrification, and wa-
ter/wastewater treatment plants. We conclude with a synthesis of cross-scalar patterns, limitations
of this review, and pending needs to support powering of large loads.

2. LARGE LOAD SOLUTIONS

This section synthesizes the solution landscape for integrating rapidly growing large loads and
identifies common pathways so they can be compared across solution categories, primary entities,
and regions. The subsections are organized by implementation level, beginning with transmission
level actions that address deliverability and interconnection constraints; followed by utility level
actions focused on reliable and affordable power supply, procurement, and regulatory compliance;
and finally, facility/customer level strategies (with emphasis on data centers) that support speed-to-
market and continuity through on-site investments. To enable comparisons across scales, we group
solutions into four common pathways:
 1. structural expansion for new infrastructure buildout;
 2. upgrades for increasing capability of existing assets;
 3. operations & efficiency for reliability actions and operational technologies that improve use
 of existing systems; and
 4. planning & policy for processes, legislation, and coordination mechanisms that manage
 uncertainty and accelerate implementation.
These pathways were derived from recurring patterns across transmission, utility, and facility level
solutions. Specifically, we grouped solutions based on the primary action being taken, the entities
responsible for implementation, and whether the solution addressed physical assets and capacity,
operational management, or institutional coordination. The following subsections summarize the
primary solution pathways in each solution category, including associated entities, timelines, key
constraints & tradeoffs. Representative regional examples are summarized in their respective
sections and fully captured in the Appendix.1 Regions were organized by the nine U.S. Census
Regions and Divisions (Cendivs) to provide consistent geographic bounds for comparing how
solution pathways vary with regional infrastructure constraints, economic development patterns,
and regulatory environments [12].

2.2. Utility Level

At the utility level, the primary challenge is ensuring sufficient, reliable, and dispatchable power to
meet rapidly growing large load demand without decreasing capacity margins, increasing system
costs, or introducing operational reliability risks [3]. In response, utilities are pursuing a combi-
nation of capacity expansion (e.g., new dispatchable generation, energy storage), bridge strategies
(e.g., delaying retirements and extending generator lifetimes), flexibility mechanisms (e.g., contrac-
tual arrangements), and proactive planning frameworks (e.g., Integrated Resource Plans (IRPs)).
These solutions frequently act as near- to mid-term complements to longer-lead transmission ex-
pansion. Table 2-2 summarizes utility level solution pathways, and Table A.2 summarizes regional
examples discussed in this section.
Solutions at the utility level span structural expansion, upgrades, operations and efficiency, and
planning and policy actions, with implementation varying across regions (Table A.2). Structural
expansion includes adding new dispatchable generation and scaling utility storage options to main-
tain power reliability during large load growth [52, 53, 54]. Uprate strategies increase the usable
capacity or output of existing assets, while bridge strategies preserve capacity temporarily while
longer-lead solutions are developed. Together, these approaches help maintain near-term capac-
ity by extending generator lifetimes, delaying retirements, restarting existing units, or repowering
legacy generation sites (e.g., coal-to-natural-gas conversions or reuse of retired generation sites)
[55, 56]. Utilities are also pursuing operations and efficiency measures that increase flexibility
(e.g., demand response and curtailment arrangements) where feasible, improved dispatch coordi-
nation across generation and storage, and fuel assurance practices that improve reliability during
extreme conditions [47, 57]. Finally, planning and policy mechanisms help align large load growth
with both interconnection and resource planning processes. These include flexible interconnection

Across these utility level solution types, timelines and responsible entities vary (Table 2-2). New
dispatchable generation typically has multi-year development timelines (3–7+ years), depending a
lot on the type of generation, and is led by utilities and developers with regulatory oversight [74].
Storage and hybrid (e.g., renewable technologies and batteries) systems can often be deployed faster
than new thermal generation, generally on the order of 1–5 years, while on-site generation timelines
depend on permitting and customer coordination [75]. Bridge capacity actions can be implemented
on short timelines (< 3 years) but require regulatory approval and operational feasibility [76].
Demand response and operational coordination programs can often be initiated relatively quickly,
but may take years to pilot, scale, and formalize agreements with participating large load customers;
IRP updates and procurement cycles are governed by planning and regulatory schedules [77, 78].

Across these solutions, feasibility and deployment are shaped by recurring constraints that differ
by solution type and across regions (Table 2-2, Table A.2). For structural expansion, key con-
straints include permitting and emission requirements, financing and cost recovery, and the risk of
overbuilding if large load projects don’t materialize. For storage builds, constraints can include
duration and performance characteristics and supply chain and interconnection timelines [75]. Up-
grade strategies reduce near-term adequacy risk but can also increase operating and maintenance
costs, especially those that delay planned retirement, and often times are passed down to the cus-
tomer, which has caused backlash from a lot of communities in the regions that have planned large
load projects [79] (Table A.2). Also, similar to the transmission solutions, operations and efficiency
measures depend on measurable performance (e.g., verification) and coordinated responses (e.g.,
compensation structures) [80]. Planning and policy mechanisms face uncertainty in load fore-
casts and scenario design, while contract-based procurement (including PPAs) introduces exposure
to deliverability constraints (e.g., generation, fuel) and contract term risk, and market volatility
[78, 81].

2.3. Facility Level

Solutions at the facility level, particularly for data centers, are shaped by speed-to-market, uptime re-

cient operational continuity under constrained grid conditions. These actions include customer-side
supply (on-site/behind-the-meter generation), operational flexibility (flex compute and curtailment
commitments), reliability infrastructure (microgrids and battery storage), and efficiency measures
that reduce power and water intensity. While these strategies can reduce immediate, local grid
problems (e.g., congestion, emergency curtailment) and enable earlier operations, these solutions
still depend on coordination with utilities and grid operators. Table 2-3 summarizes facility-level

storage, grid-aware backup systems, and some electrical efficiency improvements, can often be
deployed on relatively short timelines (∼1–3 years) compared to major infrastructure projects
[104, 105]. Chip- and rack-level cooling upgrades and site-level water-efficient cooling designs
generally fall on near- to medium-term timelines (∼1–5 years), especially when implemented
through modular retrofits, although larger redesigns may require additional planning, permitting,
and construction [105, 106, 107]. Development timelines for on-site generation, microgrids, or
energy parks typically fall in the near- to mid-term range (∼1–5 years) and depend on technology
choice, permitting requirements, fuel availability, interconnection design, and coordination with
the serving utility [108, 109]. Flexible compute and other load flexibility measures depend on
control systems, contractual arrangements, and the ability to shift workloads without compromising
service-level requirements and typically fall under shorter timelines (∼0–3 years) and are managed
by data center operators [105]. Lastly, planning and policy actions, such as site selection and phased
deployment, generally occur over near- to mid-term timelines (∼1–5 years) and involve developers,
local permitting entities, and utilities as projects are aligned with available interconnection capacity
and planned upgrades.

Across these pathways, deployment feasibility is shaped by several recurring constraints. On-
site generation, microgrids, and energy parks can improve reliability and reduce dependence on
grid infrastructure, but they may introduce permitting and emissions challenges, fuel supply risks,
interconnection complexity, and cost-allocation questions [108]. Flexible compute and curtailment
commitments depend on robust monitoring, communications, and control systems and may be
constrained by uptime requirements, customer obligations, and service-level agreements [110].
Battery storage and grid-aware backup systems are increasingly relevant for resilience and ramp
management, but many installations were designed primarily for backup rather than grid-interactive
operation, and expanded use can introduce cost, safety, and asset management considerations
[111]. Cooling and facility efficiency upgrades can reduce energy intensity and thermal risk, but
may require significant capital investment, specialized expertise, and operational changes [112].
Site-level water-efficient cooling can reduce water consumption, but in some cases may increase
electricity use, illustrating tradeoffs across power and water systems [113]. Finally, site selection and
phased deployment are shaped by permitting, land use, fiber access, labor availability, transmission

capacity, water availability, and community acceptance, reinforcing the need to coordinate facility
level decisions with utility and regional planning efforts [108].

3. DISCUSSION

Rapid growth in large loads has prompted a wide range of responses across transmission planning,
utility resource strategies, and facility operations, particularly for data centers. The results of this
review reinforce that there is no single solution being pursued. Rather, powering of large loads
typically depends on coordinated portfolios that combine long-lead infrastructure investments with
nearer-term operational, planning, and customer-side measures implemented by multiple entities
across different timelines. This discussion synthesizes the cross-cutting insights that emerge from
the reviewed literature and regional examples and reflects on the implications of organizing solutions
by implementation level. Limitations of the review and pending needs are also highlighted below.

3.1. Cross-Scalar Patterns

Figure 3-1 summarizes the large load solution landscape in implementation terms by visualizing
strategies according to the primary decision-making level and the approximate time horizon for
deployment. This mapping emphasizes that accommodating large load growth is not only a technical
problem but also an implementation problem, since progress depends on coordinated action across
multiple entities. It also clarifies that the same solution category can take different forms across
scales. For example, solutions in the planning and policy category range from queue transparency
and flexible interconnection at the transmission level, to resource procurement and integrated
resource planning updates at the utility level, and to siting and phased deployment decisions at
the facility level. Likewise, operations and efficiency spans grid-enhancing technologies on the
transmission system, demand response and operational practices at utilities, and flexible compute
and battery management at facilities. While these approaches often pursue similar outcomes (e.g.,
better use of existing infrastructure and greater operational flexibility) they are implemented through
different institutions and mechanisms, reinforcing the value of organizing solutions by scale.
Figure 3-1 also shows why real-world responses are typically portfolios rather than single solu-
tions. Because large load integration involves multiple challenges, effective strategies often require
coordinated actions across facility, utility, and transmission levels. For instance, a large data center
may deploy on-site generation or battery storage and agree to operating limits under a flexible
interconnection arrangement, while the serving utility pursues additional capacity and the trans-
mission provider advances network upgrades. In this context, facility actions can reduce near-term
stress and provide flexibility, but their overall value depends on coordination with utility programs,
interconnection agreements, and operator requirements. Similarly, transmission expansion alone
cannot address rapid load growth on the timelines required without complementary utility procure-
ment and customer-side flexibility [11]. Thus, evaluating any one solution in isolation can overstate
its feasibility or reliability value since many strategies depend on infrastructure, operating rules,
customer participation, or utility planning actions.

Finally, Figure 3-1 highlights that implementation timelines naturally drive sequencing. Long-lead
structural investments (e.g., new transmission corridors,substations, and large supply additions)

GETs, flexible interconnection, demand response and curtailment frameworks, bridge capacity,
and operational coordination to maintain reliability while longer-term projects proceed. A similar
pattern emerges at the facility level, where flexible computing, battery systems, and operational
improvements can provide near-term relief while campuses scale and cooling or dedicated energy
infrastructure is developed.

In the previous sections, across all three implementation levels, several recurring constraints emerge
that can dictate which solutions are feasible and how they are deployed. Although the specific chal-
lenges differ by solution type, common themes include permitting and regulatory requirements,
financing and cost recovery, infrastructure and interconnection limitations, and operational co-
ordination among multiple stakeholders. Long-lead infrastructure investments frequently face
permitting and environmental delays, and supply chain constraints, whereas operational measures
depend on reliable communications, good data, customer participation and well defined operating
rules. Facility level strategies introduce additional considerations related to water availability, emis-
sions, and land use, while utility and transmission planning must balance the risk of under building
against the possibility of stranded investments if projected loads fail to materialize. Collectively,
these findings suggest that implementation challenges often extend beyond technology selection,
emphasizing the importance of governance, coordination, and regional context determining which

solution pathways are ultimately successful.
Finally, a review of regional solutions across transmission, utility, and facility levels within the nine
U.S. Cendivs identified additional patterns (Tables A.1, A.2, and A.3). Although not a compre-
hensive list, these examples reveal how large load solutions are shaped by regional infrastructure,
market structures, and resource constraints. Western examples in the Pacific and Mountain Cendivs
highlight long-distance transmission, storage, planning updates, and water-sensitive facility design,
reflecting both transmission expansion needs and arid-region constraints (Tables A.1, A.2, and A.3).
West South Central examples emphasize ERCOT-related transmission expansion, conditional en-
ergization, emergency curtailment, and gas-backed or on-site power strategies, reflecting near-term
reliability and deliverability concerns under rapid load growth (Tables A.1 and A.3). South Atlantic
examples are strongly tied to clustered data center growth in Northern Virginia, where transmis-
sion buildout and substation expansion are prominent (Table A.1). Midwest examples include
MISO demand response participation, Wisconsin transmission/substation investments, and bridge
capacity actions such as peaker restarts or nuclear license extensions, indicating a mix of industrial
flexibility and resource responses (Tables A.1 and A.2). Middle Atlantic examples highlight PJM
flexible interconnection and curtailment frameworks, as well as nuclear restart activity in Penn-
sylvania (Tables A.1 and A.2). Across these regions, transmission examples primarily emphasize
structural expansion and interconnection governance, utility examples include firm capacity and
bridge strategies, and facility examples reflect more site-specific actions such as on-site generation,
flexibility agreements, cooling and water-efficiency measures, and phased deployment (Tables A.1,
A.2, and A.3).

3.2. Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First,
it emphasizes a scale-based synthesis and representative case studies rather than providing an
exhaustive inventory of all large load policies and actions. The regional examples are taken from
publicly available reports and media sources and therefore may over represent regions with greater
transparency or more active reporting. In addition, the reviewed solutions were not evaluated
using standardized performance metrics (e.g., cost, emissions, or capacity added), limiting direct
comparisons across technologies or strategies. Because large load planning and interconnection
policies continue to evolve rapidly, some examples may become outdated as new projects and
regulatory approaches emerge. Taken together, these limitations should be interpreted as a synthesis
of current practices and emerging trends rather than a definitive assessment of all available solution
pathways.

3.3. Pending Needs

This review highlights several opportunities to improve both research and planning for large load
integration. While considerable attention has focused on evaluating individual technologies or poli-
cies, comparatively less work has examined how complementary solutions interact across scales
at the transmission, utility, and facility levels. Future efforts could therefore prioritize evaluation

 22

of coordinated portfolios of solutions rather than siloed solutions, accounting for implementation
timelines, responsible entities, regional constraints, and cross sector dependencies. Advancing
this understanding will require datasets that document where different solution pathways are being
deployed, dynamic modeling frameworks capable of representing feedback between infrastructure
investments and load growth, and methods for evaluating coordinated implementation strategies
(e.g., combining transmission upgrades, utility procurement and customer-side flexibility). Addi-
tional work is also needed to identify best practices for coordination among data centers, utilities,
and grid operators, as well as to better incorporate local infrastructure, environmental, and socioe-
conomic constraints into planning processes. Together these efforts can inform the development of
regionally-informed strategies for powering future large load customers while maintaining power
system reliability.

 23

4. CONCLUSION

The rapid growth of data centers and other large load customers is reshaping power system plan-
ning and requires coordination of responses. This review synthesized the various solution pathways
being pursued across scales, demonstrating that accommodating large load growth depends on a
wide range of structural, operational, planning, and customer side strategies rather than a single
solution. Organizing these solutions at the different implementation levels (transmission, utility,
and facility) shows how similar objectives are being explored by multiple entities through various
mechanisms and timelines. Regional examples further show that local infrastructure constraints,
market structures, regulatory environments, and cross-sector considerations influence which so-
lution pathways are being pursued. As energy systems evolve, integrated, multi-scale planning
that accounts for implementation timing, regional context, and coordination across entities will be
essential for meeting electricity demands for large loads into the future.
