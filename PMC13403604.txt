Sequential deep brain stimulation of anterior and centromedian nuclei in juvenile myoclonic epilepsy: illustrative case
Authors: Rose V. Zach, James Kelbert, Robert W. Bina, Vimala Vajjala, Ganesh Murthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

DBS of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) has been FDA approved for focal epilepsy, and stimulation of the centromedian nucleus of the thalamus (CMT) is increasingly being explored for generalized epilepsies including juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME). Medically intractable epilepsy is defined by the International League Against Epilepsy as failure to achieve sustained seizure freedom after adequate trials of 2 appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications, whether as monotherapy or in combination.1,2 Approximately one-third of individuals with epilepsy meet the criteria for drug resistance.3,4

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) is the most common form of genetic generalized epilepsy. It typically presents in adolescents and is characterized by myoclonic jerks, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and, occasionally, absence seizures.5–7 Seizures in JME primarily occur shortly after awakening and are often precipitated by sleep deprivation or stress. Most patients respond to antiseizure medications; however, an estimated 16%–17% remain refractory and experience ongoing seizures despite treatment.6

Neuromodulation, including deep brain stimulation (DBS), is a treatment option for patients with medically intractable epilepsy who do not meet candidacy for resective surgery.3,8,9 DBS of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) has been FDA approved for focal epilepsy, and stimulation of the centromedian nucleus of the thalamus (CMT) is increasingly being explored, in an off-label indication, for generalized epilepsies, including JME.10–13 The primary goal of DBS is to reduce seizure frequency; seizure freedom is achieved in a minority of cases.8,13,14 The underlying epileptic network and seizure type help inform thalamic targets.11,12

In this report, we present the case of a patient with medically intractable JME who underwent sequential, bilateral ANT and CMT DBS implantation. This intervention resulted in a significant decrease in seizure burden, suggesting a potential therapeutic role for network-guided neuromodulation in select cases of generalized epilepsy refractory to medical therapy.

Illustrative Case

Our patient is a 51-year-old woman with medically intractable JME, with seizure onset at age 17. She also experienced daily myoclonic jerks involving the upper and lower extremities, often severe enough to cause object dropping and falls, resulting in 2–3 fall-related injuries per month. The frequency of her myoclonus could be between 2–5 per week but at times could occur daily with 1–3 clusters of myoclonic jerks per week. Absence seizures would occur about 4 times per month. Prior to 2016, myoclonic jerks frequently clustered prior to evolving into generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Seizures were consistently precipitated by sleep deprivation and photic stimulation and were most prominent in the mornings, particularly prior to taking her medications.

The patient trialed and discontinued more than 10 antiseizure medications due to inadequate seizure control or intolerable side effects, including cannabidiol (ineffective), rufinamide (ineffective), topiramate (dizziness), zonisamide (ineffective), valproate (rash), lacosamide (ineffective, dizziness) phenytoin, carbamazepine, perampanel (headaches), levetiracetam (irritability), clobazam (leukopenia), felbamate (dizziness), eslicarbazepine (leukopenia), divalproex sodium (stopped due to pregnancy), and ethosuximide (rash and depression). At the time of referral, she was taking cenobamate 200 mg nightly, lamotrigine extended release 400 mg daily, brivaracetam 100 mg twice daily, lorazepam 2 mg each morning, with consistent medication adherence. Preoperative brain MRI demonstrated no structural epileptogenic lesion (Fig. 1). Baseline EEG demonstrated generalized 3-Hz ­spike-and-wave discharges with bifrontal predominance, consistent with JME. She underwent vagus nerve stimulator implantation in 2013; however, this did not result in meaningful improvement, and the device was electively explanted in January 2022, after ANT DBS implantation. Owing to persistent disabling myoclonic and absence seizures, she subsequently underwent bilateral ANT DBS in December, 2021 (Fig. 2). At the time of surgical decision-making, the patient’s most disabling events were her generalized tonic-clonic seizures, which had resulted in multiple hospitalizations and significant morbidity. Although JME is a generalized epilepsy syndrome, the 2021 time frame led to the selection of ANT DBS based on its FDA approval status and data demonstrating reduction in generalized tonic-clonic seizure frequency in mixed and generalized cohorts. Given the off-label status and more limited adult data of CMT targeting at the time, ANT was preferred as the initial neuromodulatory target. Bilateral ANT DBS leads were implanted using a frame-based stereotactic approach while the patient was asleep using a Leksell frame. Localization was performed using a combination of indirect targeting with atlas-based anatomical confirmation. Medtronic SenSight directional leads were implanted. Final Leksell coordinates were as follows: X = 119.6, Y = 97.3, Z = 118.7, Ring = 83, and Arc = 100.8 for the left and X = 119.6, Y = 97.3, Z = 118.7, Ring = 83, and Arc = 100.8 for the right. A Medtronic implantable pulse generator was subsequently placed in the chest. This was followed by stage 2 pulse generator placement and revision of the generator and extension leads for wire tethering, causing painful mobility restriction due to the DBS electrode lead adhesion in the neck in August 2022, 9 months after system placement.

Following ANT DBS, she felt that the stimulation did not improve her myoclonic jerks and reported discomfort that she attributed to the therapy. She requested that the ANT stimulation be turned off in July of 2022. Within 1 week, she experienced a generalized ­myoclonic–tonic-clonic seizure, after which ANT stimulation was resumed; however, her daily myoclonic jerks and absence clusters persisted, with resultant falls and functional limitations. EEG obtained after ANT DBS placement demonstrated persistent generalized epileptiform activity with epileptiform discharges.She required rescue medication frequently (up to 3 times per week). Her seizure control was to Engel class IVa after resumption of ANT stimulation. Despite its lack of efficacy for myoclonus, this temporal association suggests an independent therapeutic effect of ANT stimulation on convulsive seizure control.

In November 2023, sodium valproate was reintroduced at a low dose (500–750 mg nightly), which led to a reduced myoclonus by about 50%. Despite this partial improvement, she continued to experience daily myoclonic jerks and intermittent falls, with her EEG continuing to demonstrate generalized spike and wave discharges. In 2023, she was admitted to the epilepsy monitoring unit for lamotrigine dose reduction to assess the impact on myoclonic episodes. This significantly worsened both her myoclonus and the burden of her interictal epileptiform discharges. Given the persistence of disabling myoclonus despite optimized antiseizure therapy and the emerging role of the CMT in modulating generalized thalamocortical excitability, after discussion in the multidisciplinary epilepsy conference and discussion with the patient for the off-label indication, she underwent bilateral CMT DBS lead placement in July 2024 with generator placement on the contralateral chest. Following CMT DBS, her myoclonic jerks progressively diminished and her absence clusters resolved. At 7 months after implant and initiation of stimulation, she reported only rare myoclonic jerks, with no reported falls or absence seizures and no need for rescue medication. At this time, her medication regimen consisted of sodium valproate extended release 750 mg nightly, lamotrigine 150 mg twice daily, brivaracetam 150 mg twice daily, cenobamate 300 mg nightly, and lorazepam 2 mg each morning. As her seizure control remained stable, her regimen was gradually simplified to reduce medication burden. By August 2025, 13 months post-CMT implant, she was maintained on sodium valproate 500 mg nightly, lamotrigine 150 mg twice daily, cenobamate 250 mg nightly, and lorazepam 2 mg each morning, while brivaracetam was being tapered toward 100 mg twice daily. Over the 17-month postoperative period, she had 6 brief myoclonic events and was seizure free for 9 cumulative months. Given the rarity of these episodes, particularly in contrast to her prior disabling myoclonus, which previously resulted in significant morbidity including thoracic vertebral fractures, her seizure outcome is currently Engel class IIb. She denied stimulation-related side effects, mood disturbance, or wound complications.

At the last follow-up, her CMT stimulation parameters bilaterally were 1.8 mA, 90-μsec pulse width, and 145 Hz, with continuous stimulation (cycling off). Her ANT stimulation parameters were 4.8 mA, 90-μsec pulse width, and 145 Hz, using intermittent cycling (1 minute on/4 minutes off) (Table 2). This case suggests the potential value of targeting distinct thalamic nuclei to treat different seizure phenotypes within a single epilepsy syndrome. In this patient, ANT DBS effectively reduced generalized tonic-clonic seizures, whereas CMT DBS successfully suppressed disabling myoclonic seizures, leading to sustained functional improvement and decreased antiseizure medication dependence. Sequential, combined ANT and CMT neuromodulation may therefore represent a rational therapeutic approach in selected patients with JME whose seizure subtypes respond differentially to stimulation of specific thalamocortical networks.

Discussion

This case describes a 51-year-old woman with medically intractable JME for whom multiple antiseizure medications and vagus nerve stimulation failed while she continued to experience disabling myoclonic jerks and absence-like episodes. Bilateral ANT DBS reduced her generalized tonic-clonic seizures but did not control myoclonic jerks. Subsequent off-label bilateral CMT DBS led to complete resolution of myoclonic and absence seizures, with sustained Engel class IIb status at the 12-month follow-up.

This case fits the growing literature on DBS for JME and generalized epilepsies. While ANT DBS is FDA approved and best studied for focal epilepsies, its efficacy in generalized syndromes such as JME is less robust, often reducing convulsive seizures but not myoclonic or absence events.15–17 In contrast, off-label targeting of CMT with DBS is increasingly recognized as effective for generalized epilepsies, with meta-analyses showing higher responder rates (up to 83.9%) and particular benefit for myoclonic and absence seizures.15,17–19 Seizure freedom, however, remains rare for both targets, with rates between 1% and 4%.18

DBS Visualization

ANT DBS electrode visualization was conducted using Lead-DBS version 3.2.1 within MATLAB version R2025b (The MathWorks Inc.). Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine files of preoperative and postoperative MR images were converted into Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative files. The MR images were then coregistered using statistical parametric mapping and normalized using advanced normalization tools. Pre-reconstructing of the DBS electrodes was done using the manual method in 3D Slicer where head and tail fiducials were placed for both electrodes, which were subsequently localized and visualized in 3D space using the DISTAL atlas.

Observations

Comparative studies and case series suggest that CMT stimulation is more effective than ANT for generalized epilepsy.15,17,19 The present case is notable in that myoclonic jerks, which are often refractory to ANT DBS, were controlled only after CMT stimulation.16,17,20–22 This supports the hypothesis that distinct thalamocortical networks underlie different seizure subtypes and may require tailored neuromodulation strategies.16,19,23 The ANT is primarily connected with the limbic system and frontotemporal associative circuits involved in seizure propagation, whereas the CMT has broad projections to thalamocortical motor networks, making it more relevant for myoclonic and absence seizures.20,24–28 Dual DBS systems targeting both ANT and CMT are rare but have been reported as feasible and safe in small series, with potential for improved outcomes in complex or mixed epilepsy syndromes.20,23,29 The present case adds to this emerging experience, demonstrating that individualized, circuit-guided targeting of thalamic nuclei can produce meaningful clinical benefit. Larger studies are needed to clarify optimal patient selection, long-term efficacy, and generalizability of this multitarget neuromodulation strategy.

Limitations of this case include the short follow-up period, lack of randomized data for dual-target DBS, and the inherent challenges in attributing outcomes to specific stimulation parameters or targets. Ideal patient selection, long-term durability of seizure freedom, and the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying differential responses to ANT versus CMT stimulation are questions to be further characterized.16,18,23,30

Pharmacological management represents an additional potential confounder. Divalproex sodium, reintroduced 8 months prior to CMT DBS, possibly contributed to the overall improvement in seizure burden. Valproate is well established as a highly effective therapy for myoclonus in generalized epilepsies, and its combination with lamotrigine has been shown to provide a synergistic effect.

Despite this improvement, the patient continued to experience disabling daily myoclonic jerks and falls prior to CMT implantation, with an overall outcome corresponding to Engel class IVa with ANT-only stimulation. After CMT stimulation, the patient experienced a marked reduction in myoclonic events, with rare residual episodes and periods of seizure freedom lasting up to 6 months. Importantly, antiseizure medication doses were not increased after CMT implantation, suggesting that neuromodulation may have contributed to the observed improvement. Neuromodulatory therapies frequently demonstrate progressive clinical benefit over time, which is consistent with the patient’s course, in which myoclonic jerks decreased from approximately 2 events per day 1 month postoperatively to only 6 events over the subsequent 11 months.

Future directions should include prospective multicenter studies to evaluate the efficacy of CMT stimulation in specifically patients who have refractory JME. Ongoing research into thalamic connectivity and syndrome-specific targeting may further improve outcomes for patients with drug-resistant JME and other generalized epilepsies.15,23,29,31

Lessons

This case suggests the possibility that targeting differential thalamic nuclei may influence seizure phenotypes in JME. In this patient, ANT stimulation successfully suppressed generalized tonic-clonic seizures, whereas off-label CMT stimulation was required to control disabling myoclonic and absence seizures, which resulted in sustained Engel class IIb outcome, reduction in fall-related injuries, and lack of need of rescue medication. The findings support the growing body of evidence that CMT stimulation may be the appropriate choice of stimulation in patients with idiopathic generalized epilepsies.
