Systematic modelling of a large-scale, historicised, well-detailed building land use, with the fusion of massive and heterogeneous datasets

Maxime Colomb, Julien Perret

ABSTRACT
Buildings are the primary venues for human activity. Generating precise numeric building models is of great interest for many applications. At regional and national scales, many different formalisms are employed to represent the various components of buildings, yet the building land use is frequently oversimplified — even when mixed-use buildings are considered. Inferring a realistic building land use, defined as an inventory of the distinct functional spaces hosted by a building, brings valuable knowledge for building models. Manual measurement can produce highly realistic interior models, but such approaches are labour-intensive and must be conducted on a case-by-case basis. We introduce a fully automated workflow that systematically simulates a well-described building land use by integrating three complementary tasks: (1) use-oriented building object modelling – defining building objects that capture building land use, (2) activity census and conflation – aggregating and reconciling activity records from heterogeneous datasets, and (3) logical attribute generation – deriving detailed attributes for housing units and activities in relation to the characteristics of their host building and other environmental factors. The workflow adapts to varying levels of data availability and attribute richness, and it supports “recent-period historicisation” (i.e., reconstructing past states of the built environment). Because the entire pipeline is automated, it can be deployed at a large scale; we demonstrate its application to an entire country. We evaluate the method through multiple validation procedures, including comparisons with ground truth measurements, showing that the approach reliably reproduces building land use.

1 Introduction 1.1 Context Buildings are the most primitive elements of urban scenes because they define the principal physical boundaries that separate outdoor from indoor space. Most human presence and interaction occur inside buildings [ 1 ]. They host a large proportion of sites of human activity, such as housing units, offices, shops and most urban leisure activities. Consequently, buildings are central to the relationship between humans and their urban environment. Buildings are defined and modelled in accordance to the intended application and its scale. Models that require immersive 3D city views need well-described building envelopes, often with façade reconstruction. On the other hand, models that reproduce human activity at a precise and individual level (e.g., ABM, LUTI) require a description of the building land use * (notions followed with an * are defined in the Glossary in annex A). Their traditional formalism for representing buildings has often been a single geometry, either punctual or polygonal. Building land use is frequently oversimplified to a primary–and sometimes secondary–use for the whole building, represented as a textual attribute. This formalism is not suitable for cities containing a complex or historic urban fabric. The New Urbanism charter [ 2 ] recommends developing mixed use of buildings in cities. Models of building land use need a precise description of a building’s various uses. Changes in a building’s use, encouraged by urban trends such as the liquid city concepts [ 3 ], indicates that building land use is changing at an unpredictably high rate [ 4 ]. Models must then be capable of quick updates and accommodate multiple types of datasets. Building functions have been inferred by many research studies at an aggregated level [ 5 ]. As many models and frameworks focus on the representation of the physical interior of buildings [ 6 ], collecting additional urban objects, such as localisation of housing units * or activities *, and matching them to buildings, could further refine their land use description at an individual scale. 1.2 Stakes Yet, simulating a precise, exhaustive, well-described, historicised*, spatially realistic building land use remains an ongoing research challenge. Such a model would benefit other urban models in several ways. Microscopic sociospatial modelling could take advantage of a more spatially complex and richly described building land use. Modellers often seek greater accuracy and realism in the definition, completeness and variety of the information they manage. Enhancing the realism of building land use at an individual scale could be a highly effective way to improve the realism of individual-level interactions. Knowing which activities are hosted in single buildings is valuable for assigning specific tasks to human agents in agent-based models. A descriptive land use should generate attributes concerning those activities, such as type, opening hours, the number of workers employed, or even the area occupied by such activities. Such knowledge should help allocate the appropriate number of agents to a given place and task, allowing us to compute densities of people living, working and spending leisure time, as well as occupancy saturation, for each building in the model. Increasing knowledge about building land use would also benefit various urban generation models and allow systematic deployment at large scale. Indoor 3D reconstruction models could be guided by such information. Urban heat island generators that focus on anthropogenic heat [ 7 ] could model a more detailed occupation of buildings by human activities. The definition of activities in buildings could also be used to generate multiscale energy consumption estimates [ 8 ]. 3D building reconstruction generators could use a building land use model to infer missing information about particular building elements or to choose appropriate urban fabric grammars for procedural generation. Building Information Modelling (BIM) reconstruction projects could gain clues about the nature and amount of detail to add when converting geographic information system (GIS) data into fully detailed BIM data, or more generally when interfacing GIS and BIM [ 9 ]. Because building land use changes frequently, its generation should incorporate a rapid-update system to produce results across multiple time periods. Specific spatiotemporal analysis could benefit from this precise, large-scale knowledge with historicised versions, such as real estate analysis [ 10 ], housing supply studies [ 11 ], commercial evolution analysis [ 12 ] or office vacancy assessments [ 13 ]. Finally, Volunteered Geographical Information (VGI) platforms could compare their data to such a model to help update specific information. 1.3 Challenges Modelling a well-defined building land use at a large zone is challenging in multiple ways. Urban models cannot access an exhaustive and large-scale building land use database because such information is sparse and spread across numerous datasets. In large geographic collections, the building object is often described as a geometry with few attributes. Sometimes, one or two destinations for the building are described in its attribute information, but these are usually not exhaustive and are insufficient to model detailed characteristics of the different kinds of places. Even with an exhaustive list of functional space *, it is not possible to obtain a correct estimation of the area covered by each of them, to know whether a registered activity occupies a premises *, or what is the use of such functional spaces . Methods based on sensors are mostly applied case-by-case, as the measurement tool must enter every building in order to scan rooms. Such methods cannot be deployed systematically. VGI-based methods are also applied to a few cases. While their application could be encouraged by communes and yield a complete dataset within a few years, data consistency would remain a strong concern. BIM translations are dependent on the data available, which may exist for new building projects, but they are too complex to construct for all existing buildings. Census and statistical information about the economics and population could bring valuable data for building land use. They could follow multiple formalisms, scales, geometric types, or census recording objects. They also come from multiple types of sources (authoritative datasets, local governance databases), and the hypothesis framing their creation differ. Their fusion must then be adapted to each context. The historicisation of data is a relevant topic in data sciences. Datasets are only representative of a given date. The interpolation of the changes between two dates requires a deep understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving system evolution and dedicated simulation models. It is therefore necessary to study each system to infer the model’s state at each wanted date. The availability of input data is also an important topic. The created model must be generic in order to allow adaptability to different data contexts. In order to be extended to new geographic zones, enriched by various data and to benefit from community improvement, data and technologies must be available under a free and open source license. To our knowledge, no simulation method that responds to all the previously cited information and generates plausible indoor configurations has been proposed. 1.4 Goals In this paper, we present a fully automated workflow that simulates a highly detailed building land use at the building level ( Fig 1 ). The model can be deployed systematically; it has been applied to the 34 883 communes of metropolitan France and its 47 million buildings. It provides a realistic snapshot at a specific point in time. The target date may differ from the dates of input datasets, thanks to historicisation of underlying processes. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g001 Fig 1 Example of a 3D representation of model’s output. As building definitions usually focus on their geometry, a dedicated definition is adopted to accommodate mixed-use possibilities. Functional spaces populating buildings fall into two categories: housing units for living purposes or activities , which may ultimately occupy a premises and receive the public. A well-described and exhaustive list of activities is then collected and conflated. Multiple data fusion processes are designed to merge different kinds of datasets and simulate synthetic information where it is missing. As input datasets follow different temporal dynamics and historicisation processes, specific data inference techniques are applied. Because different releases of the same data can model information differently, the presented models must be adapted to varying availability and quality of input data, which depends on the national and local context. A minimal amount of data is necessary, but every process can be enriched when richer data are available. Since the first implementation is built upon French data, it could be adapted to equivalent data from other countries or new data sources. All data used are available under open licenses, and the model itself is released under an AGPL license, allowing users to reproduce, use, and improve the current model. This model is already in active use within the ICI epidemiological simulation project [ 14 , 15 ]. It could also be used to analyse the mutation of activity sectors, to enhance the objectives and locations of trips in transportation models, or by any model requiring a precise and geolocated description of human activity. This model does not provide a 3D inner organisation of buildings, but an inventory of what functional spaces a building hosts. It could therefore be used as an input for automatic reconstruction algorithms. It has been developed in a French context, where datasets are numerous, diverse, detailed, and descriptive. The authors are currently working on its application to generic European datasets. Further adaptation will however be required for contexts where fewer data are available and their quality is not consistent. The model is composed of three sequential, distinct, and relatively independent tasks: Building model: Model a particular building object, infer missing values, and merge primitive building blocks * into building groups * in order to consider a common interior space. Activity collection and conflation: Collect, sort, match and merge all activities that are hosted by the buildings. Building land use enrichment: Gather descriptive information about the functional spaces within the building, either through collection or simulation, relative to their number, their types, their potential use and the building’s geometry. Tests and validation of each task are undertaken in Section 4 , and the validation of the whole model by comparing model results with ground truth data is presented in Section 4.4 Systematic runs of the model are finally shown in Section 5 . 2 Related work The aim of this article is to develop a realistic, systematic, well-defined model of building land use. This would be achieved by allocating activities and housing units to properly defined buildings. Simpler models could be applied to contemporary cities, which are built upon a more functional urbanism, or to suburbs, where each building has been designed for a particular use. In the context of historic European cities, the activities hosted in a single building can be very diverse and spatially spread in multiple building blocks . A realistic model for such cities therefore cannot rely on purely statistical methods. To our knowledge, a detailed, high-fidelity census to model the mixed building land use has not yet been proposed. The OpenStreetMap project (OSM) [ 21 ] definition is [ 22 ]: a building is a man-made structure with a roof, standing more or less permanently in one place. The definition is vague when establishing proper building objects. The French authoritative database for geographical objects, namely BD_Topo ®, specifies that a new building is created each time a difference higher than 3 meters is observed in a continuous building [ 23 ]. This leads to a definition rather focused on the physical envelope of the building than on its functional spaces and its inner organisation. A new definition of a primary building object could be proposed and serve in this work: a building is a human-made structure comprising a set of enclosed rooms that are connected together, physically or not, and that contain one or more functional spaces intended for a human use . On that basis, this model needs to build a building object serving its needs. Much work focuses on the model of distinct aspects of buildings with different objectives. The latter can concern every type of place that is used for commercial, professional, associative or leisure purposes. Many studies focus on Points of Interest (POI), which are defined as representing an interesting location for different audiences [ 36 ]. Therefore, the activity definition used in this project encompasses the POI definition and includes every private place that is not used for residential purposes (such as offices or vacant shops). The extensive knowledge regarding POI classification, data gathering and fusion, can be reused for activity modelling. Multiple sources of activities or POI exist and possess characteristics that could be complementary. They can be summarised by the nature of their producer and share common characteristics: Authoritative datasets contain uniform and validated data at a national level. Local datasets are maintained by local administrations and can display manually validated data, which may be more precise. VGI datasets are crowdsourced datasets with some spatial inconsistencies but a large variety of interesting information. Commercial datasets are produced by companies, most of the time selling services, and are usually not fully open. Such databases can be systematically compared or conflated (which is the process of matching and then merging POI). No systematic implementation or directly reusable software was available to be integrated into this model. Their knowledge could, however, be useful for modelling realistic building land use. The model introduced in this section presents an original method to generate building land use. As this model was originally developed for France, the description is based on French datasets and resolves the related challenges. It could, therefore, be applied to other countries’ datasets with few adaptations if their datasets meet the minimal requirements. First, new building objects are modelled with the help of other geographical datasets ( Section 3.1 ). Then, activities are censused, matched and merged ( Section 3.2 ). Finally, the building land use information is enriched with many details ( Section 3.3 ). 3.1 Model of a building object The building model definition used in this work is an ensemble of closed human-made structures, possibly not continuous, which share one or more common spaces. It is first necessary to define the minimal available data that could be used to address this task ( Section 3.1.1 ). The first step of this modelling task is the generation of building blocks ( Section 3.1.2 ). Such buildings are the primitive objects of the model, and each individual building should meet minimal attribute requirements. If they are missing from the original datasets, this model has multiple options to generate them. The novelty from this work, due to its own building definition, is the creation of building groups based on the continuity of their functional spaces ( Section 3.1.3 ). To our knowledge, no scientific work is modelling buildings at an individual scale with this functional paradigm, for which the modelling of precise land use would be adequate. It is necessary to group building blocks in order to compose common interior spaces. Continuity of functional spaces can also be found in buildings that do not touch, and furthermore are touching other buildings. The main usable knowledge would be given by each building block ’s administrative status, which is not automatically attached to building’s geographical collections. Postal addresses or cadastral boundaries could be used to fill this gap. Relationships between those objects are complex, particularly for historical dense urban fabrics depicted in Fig 3 , where each object can be linked from 0 to n other objects. Postal addresses seem more suitable to define a proper activity, but it is not straightforward to decide the allocation of building blocks to addresses. Cadastral parcel geometries do not necessarily match buildings well, a building block could be positioned on multiple parcels, as well as multiple building blocks could be positioned on a single cadastral parcel. In some cases, links between building blocks and addresses exist. If not, a simple process links every building block to a postal parcel. All attributes of building blocks are finally aggregated with an appropriate aggregation operation. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g003 Fig 3 Examples of not trivial topological relations between buildings and parcels in a dense historical urban fabric. 3.1.1 Data availability. In the model of building blocks , each component is modelled with different processing, described as follow Geometries of building blocks It can be assumed that the given geometries are correct; therefore, no reconstruction or precision enhancement is handled by this model, and the geometries are retained as they are. Height The height of a building in BD_Topo ® is calculated between its lowest point above the ground and its gutter. This definition excludes basements, which could be used for commercial purposes. The space under the roofs is also excluded, although it can be usable for attic apartments, which is very common in a Hausmannian typology. It should be possible to take this space into consideration using the 3D methods presented in [ 26 ]. The height attribute could be missing. In BD_Topo ®, this concerns 0.4% of buildings in 2009, 0.9% in 2019, and 5.7% in 2024. Most of the time, this arises from a value initially set to 0 and corrected to reflect the unknown nature of the building height rather than its flatness. It is possible to reconstruct its height by calculating the average height of buildings surrounding the one with the unknown height. Buildings with no residential purpose can be filtered out, as they may benefit from exceptions to local urban planning construction rules and have a height disconnected from their neighbourhood. Number of storeys It can be provided or deduced from the height. Neither systematic LIDAR data nor well qualified and global data are used in this implementation, which could unlock more complex reconstruction algorithms. BD_Topo ® provides the number of storeys based on tax information, but many values are missing. In BD_Topo ®, this concerns 54% in 2019 and 49.2% of 2024. In this case, a fixed value of height is set (3 meters), and a number of storeys is deduced by rounding the division of the height. Floor area The number of storeys and the spatial footprint of the building make the calculation of an effective floor area (EFA) possible. Grouping individual building blocks to form building groups ensures functional continuity between the hosted functional spaces . To do so, a reference must be attached to each building block . Addresses are preferred, with an order depending on their form: preferably those with links connecting them to one or more buildings, those representing an entrance, at last, addresses tied to a cadastral parcel. Cadastral parcels themselves could then be used to attach a reference. Each building block needs to be attached to at least one reference . If multiple building blocks share a common reference , they are merged into a building group . In order to be adaptable to multiple configurations of data availability, multiple ways of linking at least one address or parcel to building blocks are possible. When building blocks are merged, some quantitative attributes (EFA, number of housing units) are summed up, while others (height, number of storeys) are appended. There are opportunities to improve the attachment of a building to a parcel by considering the relationship with the street instead of the distance to the address point. Other data could also be used, such as activity-related data. 3.2 Model of the activities Building groups have been modelled to allow continuity of their interiors. The number of housing units within them is known. The model must now collect and conflate activities in order to sketch the composition of a well-described mixed use. The corresponding state-of-the-art Section 2.3 highlights that, although many works have undertaken a similar task, no general open-source projects are available. This section then presents an original open-source activity conflation model. Activities modelled in this work are necessarily situated inside a building. In many cases, they are relatively independent of the building characteristics. They rely on different project typologies *, are represented by various jurisdictional statuses and do not necessarily imply the same occupational status within a building. They are also prone to frequent changes. Multiple data sources containing different characteristics about indoor activities are available: authoritative censuses, thematic selections, local databases, customer-review aggregators and collaborative cartographic databases. Processes of activity conflation can be deployed to combine multiple data sources for generating the most exhaustive census of activities inside each building and to enrich this representation with complementary information. A historicisation process is also important to ensure a realistic representation across different dates and to apply modifications to datasets that are not up-to-date. An indoor space dedicated to hosting a functional space is called premises *. Every declared activity does not necessarily occupy a premises (see Section 3.3.1 ). The exhaustiveness of certain activity datasets can lead to modelling multiple activities and therefore makes it not straightforward to determine the composition of building interiors. Nevertheless, the conflation with other datasets could be used to infer the importance of each activity. Based on those specifications, an original pipeline has been designed. It systematically builds an exhaustive census of activities located inside buildings. It takes advantage of the diversity of available data sources and is flexible in terms of dataset quantity and quality. To illustrate its potential, the pipeline is applied to complex and freely available French datasets, but it could be used elsewhere with similar or simpler datasets. 3.2.1 Data availability. The activity merging process can be used with a single dataset or with many. This section is exemplified using a few prominent French activity-related databases. As a focus on free and open data is endorsed in this work, no commercial data are used. French activity data are fully described in Annex D.2 and can be summarised as follows: SIRENE : an authoritative dataset gathering every declared activity, whether currently open or closed, along with many descriptive attributes and the dates of every administrative changes. This dataset is historicised and exhaustive . BPE : an authoritative dataset gathering every piece of public equipment. It combines the SIRENE dataset with data from many official French institutions. This dataset is exhaustive and results from manual processing . OpenStreetMap : a VGI dataset with an extensive data schema and rapid evolution. This dataset can be historicised . APUR : a local dataset covering shops, leisure activities and social facilities in the Paris area. This dataset results from manual processing and will be used to demonstrate the advantages of integrating local and precise datasets. 3.2.2 Typology of activities. All databases are designed with different typologies *, providing a proper classification of activities using original types. According to [ 44 ], the automatic use of the WordNet database to match types is not precise and yields too many correspondences. The methodology of building a single general typology , named common typology , adapted to each project is preferred. The common typology used in this project is described in Annex D.2.1. For each class of the different project typologies , it is necessary to manually assign a correspondence with the common typology . Matching a fixed typology with OSM is known to be a tedious task [ 41 ] because these adaptive and flexible typologies could contain many different classes. Additionally, some classes are incorrectly defined, or an activity may have multiple types. Automatic string-matching patterns could be used to preprocess the manual labelling. The project typologies remain important information for providing more precise details about activities and are used in the merging process described in the next section. 3.2.3 Historicisation. The evolution of OSM data is constant and unstructured. Changes in this database are made by a spatially and temporally inconsistent base of users. When a modification is registered, it is hard to determine whether an activity has just opened, whether some of its characteristics have evolved, or whether it has simply been registered in the database but existed for a long time. Registered objects may not exist and still be present in this database. The SIRENE database contains information of the opening and closing of each establishment, along with every change in names, types, number of employees or jurisdictional statuses. This feature makes it possible for this model to generate a set of activities precisely for any desired date. By matching activities with such historicised data, it is possible to assess whether each activity’s characteristics have changed, whether the activity has closed or is not yet open, for any given date covered by the historicisation. It is then possible to update a dataset to a particular date, whether in the past or the future. However, it is not possible to recreate outdated activities that have been closed and removed from legacy databases. This very detailed historicisation might be particular to the SIRENE dataset. In a context without such data available, it might be possible to characterise each change in OSM activities and attempt to historicise them. 3.2.4 Activity modelling. This section now presents the process for the systematic conflation from one-to-many activity datasets in order to generate an exhaustive set of indoor activities at a precise date. It reuses classical POI conflation approaches, involving the calculation of a similarity score, followed by different kinds of merging for pairs with a score higher than a given threshold. The first step relocates isolated activities to the closest or corresponding building. Then, if one dataset is historicised and others are not up-to-date and need to be updated, it is possible to update chosen fields of the latter dataset using activity matching. If a dataset allows an activity to have multiple register entries, it is possible to perform a self conflation of activities from a single dataset. Then, every activity from each dataset is assumed to exist, which implies that all of those activities will be included in the final activity collection. Finally, the algorithm performs the conflation of each dataset feature with every other feature from other datasets. Relocate activity In [ 44 ], a quality analysis comparing authoritative and VGI POI datasets measured that VGI points are less than 70 meters away from the reference point, with a mean distance between 15 and 25 meters. No precision is given regarding whether the POI is included in the building or not. Matching activities As frequently done in the POI conflation literature [ 46 ], a similarity score expresses the resemblance between two activities. This is based on two kinds of attributes which imply distinct calculations in this model: Type similarity is calculated with both activity typologies, namely common typology and project typology . Every type from the common typology must be exactly equal. As there are multiple depths in this typology, this equality condition stops at the second level. If this condition is not met, the match is considered impossible. Typology names from each project typology are compared using the pre-trained multilingual sentence-similarity BERT model described in [ 49 ]. This model also produces comparison of types written in different languages, which is useful to compare English VGI to French typologies. Name similarity is calculated only if both activity names are available. String similarity indicators listed in [ 46 ] are used to evaluate the similarity of activity names and propose matches. Distances between words are the simplest indicators to calculate. Two indicators of string similarity are chosen and the maximum value of such indicators is kept: - The Levenshtein ratio indicator calculates the cost of changing characters in order to transform string A into B. - The longest common substring (LCS) ratio counts how many characters compose the longest common sequence of characters in the two compared strings. - All those indicators are normalised by the length of the input string using the following formula, in order to eliminate cases where very small names would have a great LCS ratio: { l e n g t h _ s t r _ 1 + l e n g t h _ s t r _ 2 2 if l e n g t h _ s t r _ 1 ≤ 3 OR l e n g t h _ s t r _ 2 ≤ 3 min l e n g t h _ s t r _ 1 , l e n g t h _ s t r _ 2 otherwise Sometimes, activities have multiple names (as in the SIRENE dataset or in any self-conflated dataset) or types (OSM data). In such cases, similarities are calculated for every name or type and the highest score is kept. Each string of characters is preprocessed in order to standardise it as much as possible. For type matching , similarities based on raw and preprocessed types are calculated and the maximum value is kept. The preprocessing steps are: Lowercase and replace special characters with corresponding ASCII characters. Remove words that do not produce potential differentiation (legal unit description, country name, name of the street where activities are located). Only on name matching . Remove phrases and words with a size equal to or less than 2 characters. Replace synonyms with the most common word (for example, practice , cabinet and surgery become office ) ( Only on type matching ). In certain cases, replace very formal types with more common expressions (for example, retail shop in meat and meat products in specialised stores is replaced by butchery and delicatessen ) ( Only on type matching ). The similarity threshold is set to 0.5 by default. If name information is present in both activities, the mean of the name and type similarity scores is calculated. Otherwise, the type similarity score is evaluated alone. This setting implies that the matching will be validated if names exactly match, even if the original type names have no resemblance, which does not indicate they differ because they can be registered under very different project types . On the other hand, if the original types are exactly the same, the match is also evaluated as valid even if their names differ. Pseudocode 4 in Annex E describes those processes. If an historicised activity dataset is available, it is possible to update particular fields. Self conflate activities within a single dataset It is possible that multiple entries of a dataset belong to the same activity. The near duplication problem [ 37 ] is a well-known challenge in POI conflation and could appear within a single dataset. An adjacent problem arises from the fact that multiple declared activities could share the same premises and workers but be represented by different legal entities. They could have different types, and all other attributes, such as the number of employees, would vary. It is also possible that different establishments of the same activity are declared in adjacent buildings and that those buildings are grouped together by the grouping process. A matrix with similarity scores is built, and every activity whose similarity value is higher than the similarity threshold is merged. The number of housing units within each building is also collected or generated. An automated census and conflation model has been designed to create an exhaustive collection of activities contained in each building. It is now necessary to articulate each characteristic with its activities and housing units in order to enrich the building land use with useful and descriptive information. As we lack computing time and expertise to generate a 3D topological composition of rooms and activities for a large zone, a textual representation, using an extensive set of attributes, would be sufficient to describe how the interior of a building is composed. However, this could be a first step towards systematic procedural generation of building interior settings. Each attribute is set using various processes, sometimes involving other building elements or related to activity type. The first step decides if activities possess a premises (3.3.1). Optionally, other attributes can be defined, for example whether an activity is open to the public (3.3.2) or what its opening hours are (3.3.3). The final step sets a floor area for each activity’s premises and housing units (3.3.4). 3.3.1 Presence of premises. As the activity census could collect a significant amount of varied data, no information about the presence of a dedicated premises is available. Such information could have been found in tax-related data, but its use is too revealing to be available at a microscopic level and is not open. Self-employment, which has clearly increased in most countries in the past few years [ 50 ], is an activity without a specific premises for instance. Other activities, such as associations or real estate cooperatives, can also be purely administratively attached to a location and might not require anything other than a name on a mailbox. A special process is then created to determine whether a modelled activity occupies a premises, taking into consideration the multiple characteristics modelled previously. For each typology class, a probability of having a premises is set. This could be set at the project typology scale. By default, the value defined at the common typology would be used. Many activities always have one (restaurants, doctors, etc.), others never occupy a premises (food delivery, small taxi companies, microcompanies, etc.), and some depend on the case (tertiary sector offices, personal care, etc.). This probability is then transformed into a score by adding bonuses or penalties. A Bernoulli trial is then performed with the final score as the parameter to determine whether the activity occupies a premises or not. If not, no other attributes are set. Bonuses and penalties are defined according to the following information: presence in other datasets : If the activity hasn’t been matched with any other from the datasets, a penalty is applied to the score. Otherwise, a bonus is added; its value is relative to the number of matched datasets. jurisdictional status : Few particular statuses (for example, self-employed activities or simple owner-managed real estate properties) lower the premises score. presence of values : If particular attributes, such as the number of employees or opening hours, are set, this serves as a strong indication that the activity is open to the public. average floor area per premises in the building : This is the final step of the attribution, performed after the Bernoulli trial on premises presence. When the average size of all activities and housing units in the building is below a fixed threshold (10 square meters by default), it is likely that too many premises were allocated. A penalty is iteratively applied to every score until this condition is satisfied. Pseudocode 8 in Annex E describes this process and the values used for bonuses and penalties. 3.3.2 Opening to the public. In order to reconstruct the inside of building or to run multi-agent simulation, it is a matter of interest to know if an activity accepts the visitors, and what is the frequency of people coming in. The fact that an activity could be open to the public depends on the presence of a premises and the type of the activity. A probability based on the project type , and if missing, its corresponding common type , is set. This probability is completed with a bonus or penalty concerning the presence in other datasets as described in the previous paragraph. A new Bernoulli trial is performed with this new parameter to determine whether the activity is open to the public. An accessibility indicator is finally set to express the amount of visitors that activities would receive. This indicator is calculated depending on the activity type (for example, a garage would receive fewer customers than a bakery) and attributes which can reflect the importance of the activity (number of employees, capacity, etc.) 3.3.3 Opening hours. Opening hours should be assigned to all activities open to the public. As they could change rapidly, this information should be provided by VGI or commercial data sources that might update more frequently. In our case, OSM data often records manually collected opening hours in a well-described and automatable format (see Table 9 for attribute coverage). This format is presented in this OSM wiki resource: https://s.42l.fr/osmOH When an OSM activity is merged with other activities, this information is retained if provided. If not, synthetic opening hours are assigned based on the activity type. These values could be varied to avoid uniform opening hours. Currently, variations are simple (slight shifts of opening time, closing time, or opening days, and opening at night or on weekends if the common type permits it), but it would be valuable to learn from manually recorded opening hours to provide more realistic synthetic values, depending on the activity type and location. 3.3.4 Area attribution. A single building can be divided into multiple uses, which results in the impossibility of knowing a priori how much space is dedicated to which activity. Once activity premises are identified and detailed, it becomes possible to assign an area to each one of them, along with housing units, proportional to their building’s floor area. This problem has the following characteristics: Each building has a known floor area value, which is supposed to exclude unusable surfaces (see Section 3.1.2 ). Optionally, a value for the total square meters for commercial or housing purposes could be provided by tax services. A known number of housing units and activities with a premises within each building. The sum of activities’ and housing units’ areas must equal the building’s floor area. French national statistics provide the number of principal, secondary, and vacant housing units per statistical aggregate * and the number of principal housing units discretised into seven classes of area size. As there are many unknowns in this problem, making it impossible to determine if any solution exists, the following simple method is chosen: Preset an area for every activity. For every activity, a premises area is outlined. This area can be viewed as the minimal value needed to host the activity. This information is estimated using the activity’s and building’s attributes. The following information is used if present: (a) Specific attribute information from the dataset : For example, each activity in the local APUR dataset possesses manually set area bounds. (b) Number of people in the activity : The number of workers and the capacity of the activity are used, if set, with 10 square meters per individual. (c) Attendance information : This indicator is calculated according to the type and size of the activity. A fixed value is preset for each level of attendance. (d) Building floor area : This process ensures that the sum of such minimal activity areas is below the available floor area in the building. This step is presented in detail in pseudocode 9 in Annex E. Attribution of area to each housing unit. For all statistical aggregates, a probability vector is calculated to determine the likelihood that a random housing unit belongs to a specific area class. Each housing unit has then an area class attributed randomly via a weighted random categorical sampling. As seven ( ≤ 30 m 2 , ] 30 ; 40 ] m 2 , ] 40 ; 60 ] m 2 , ] 60 ; 80 ] m 2 , ] 80 ; 100 ] m 2 , ] 100 ; 120 ] m 2 , > 120 m 2 ) classes are recorded in the census, highly diverse configurations can be generated for the set of housing units in a building. The same process is duplicated to assign a status of principal, secondary, or vacant housing unit, but this does not concern area attribution. Evaluation of the generated areas. The sum of the preset floor area of activities and the floor area of housing units must be less than or equal to the building’s total floor area. If the first value significantly exceeds the second, Step 2 is repeated. After 500 failed iterations, it is considered that no solution consistent with the statistical aggregate can satisfy this problem. Housing units’ area are then set to the mean of area per housing unit. Maximise the size of housing units. This method tends to favour smaller housing units, as they have a higher probability of complying with the area constraint. Consequently, the area of housing units is maximised by replicating the area attribution process and retaining the solution with the highest sum of housing unit areas. Set a final value for activities. As the building area not used for housing units must be allocated to activities, the available space within each building is calculated. This area is distributed among activity premises on a pro rata basis according to the initial preset areas. Manual validations are performed for each process in order to evaluate the success of the matching task, as depicted in Fig 17 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g017 Fig 17 Example of validation applied to the matching of 10 activities from 4 datasets in a single building. A report is generated, detailing each match with the names and arguments of every matched feature and the result of their merge. Given all the information available on the report (other activities in the same building), each match is manually qualified with an evaluation label. Different qualifications are possible: right : The match has strong chances to be correct. probably right : The match seems correct, but there are reasonable doubts (for example, a shop of the same type has unrelated names and there could be multiple occurrences of such activities in the building) partially right : Most of the activities were relevant to match, but irrelevant activities were also added. impossible to know : There is no clue for any judgement about the match. probably wrong : There is a slim chance that the match is right; most likely it is a mistake. wrong : The match appears to be a mistake. wrong agglomeration : In the self conflation process, an unlimited number of activities can be matched. When a large number of activities are unduly matched, there is a high probability that every other activity in the building matches at least one of the agglomerated activities. If so, a single activity composed of many unduly matched activities is generated. This event is placed in a dedicated collection in order not to overload the wrong stock. not applicable : This label concerns activities where no premises are required. For example, multiple delivery drivers can be registered in the same building, but as they do not require premises to carry out their activities, their conflation is irrelevant. right singleton addition : In the conflation of many datasets process, every single input activity is supposed to have an effective representation on the final result. If an activity is added without being conflated with another activity and there is no reason to think that it was supposed to have a correspondence in another dataset, this characterisation is used. The right characterisation is not used in order to avoid overloading the right stock. solo additions that should be merged : In the conflation of many datasets process, when an activity is added alone but a correspondence could have been found in another dataset, it falls under this characterisation. It usually occurs in pairs but can also apply to a single activity when the type is vague and could match multiple other activities (for example, an office in a store ). If the activity could have been matched with an activity that was previously merged with other activities from the same source during the self conflation process, it is not counted in this characterisation because the input data are assumed to be correct. In the matching algorithm, the similarity score must be higher than a fixed threshold. This represents a fundamental parameter for all processes. Two threshold values are jointly evaluated for each process: 0.35 and 0.5. This task is deterministic, so there is no need for replication. As described in 3.2.1, four datasets are conflated: SIRENE (authoritative), BPE (authoritative), OSM (VGI) and APUR (local). Few limits of this validation process can be highlighted. It aims to evaluate incorrect matches, but it overlooks cases where two results should have matched but did not, apart from the conflation of many datasets process. Furthermore, no additional knowledge has been incorporated into the evaluation (e.g., personal familiarity with locations or street-level images from online services), because using such sources raises consistency issues and may not be up-to-date. If the input of a match process is incorrect, but the matching is correctly applied according to its internal design, the result will be characterised as positive, even if the produced result appears wrong. This occurs fairly often after an erroneous self conflation process. The subsequent conflation of many datasets process then matches the correct activity among multiple other incorrect activities. Otherwise, the matches are predominantly classified as right . A 0.35 threshold is therefore set as the default for this process. Self conflation: The self conflation process has more incorrect matches. This can be explained by the limited amount of information that can be cross-compared within the same dataset. Fuzzy labels ( probably right , impossible to know ) are quite high and highlight this uncertainty ( Fig 20 ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g020 Fig 20 Manual validation of the matching in the self conflation process on a few statistical aggregates of Paris. Differences between the two similarity thresholds were significant. Partially right matches are prominent with the 0.35 threshold, indicating that too many activities are added to the sets of matched activities. The number of right matches is higher with the 0.5 threshold, and this difference could stem directly from the lower count of partially right matches. Wrong matches are prevalent with the 0.35 threshold, suggesting that matches with a similarity score between 0.35 and 0.5 are often incorrect. Additionally, the phenomenon of wrong agglomeration is rare when a higher threshold is used. Due to these results, the 0.5 threshold is set as the default for this process. Because this value differs from the previous process, this adjustment could not have been made without this detailed validation step. Conflation of many datasets: Results for the conflation of many datasets process are mostly positive. It is also notable that most of the activities at this point do not match, which means that they are not recovered by other datasets. Each dataset either brings refinement or new activities. No wrong matches are reported with the 0.5 similarity threshold, whereas a very small number are reported for the low threshold. Fuzzy labels are also low, regardless of the similarity threshold ( Fig 21 ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g021 Fig 21 Manual validation of the matching in the conflation of many datasets process on a few statistical aggregates of Paris. More activities are counted with the 0.5 threshold because fewer had been merged by the self conflation process. This leads to more right singleton add , but almost double the amount of solo additions that should be merged activities. If the threshold of the previous process were higher, it is possible to hypothesise that this process with a low threshold would have yielded even better results. The default threshold of this process is therefore set to 0.35. A few conclusions can be drawn from this processing chain. A necessary improvement concerns the case where datasets contain multiple occurrences of the same type in the same building (e.g., liberal medical professions are often grouped but administratively separated). When the self conflation process is applied to the dataset, those multiple activities would be conflated and would not be available for this conflation of many datasets process. It would be useful to introduce an intermediate level that groups multiple similar activities and matches them with one or more activities. It would also be possible to employ a less strict method whereby multiple activities from the same source could be merged using a clustering approach on highly similar activities. Sources of characterisation: It is possible to trace which dataset was the source of the matching that produced incorrect labels, as Fig 22 shows for the multi-source conflation process. The local source APUR is responsible for 39.6% and 36.6% of the solo additions that could have been merged label, but it only accounts for 7.6% of the matched features. This may indicate that its typology is overly detailed and should be grouped or considered at a higher level. Consequently, this analysis suggests that the use of very detailed local datasets may introduce noise rather than increasing precision. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g022 Fig 22 Datasets that cause incorrect results in the manual validation process regarding different similarity thresholds. 4.3 Enrichment of the building land use: Validation of area attribution This section outlines a probabilistic methodology where all calculations are replicated using a seed of 43 for the initialisation of the Random Number Generator to ensure reproducibility. It has been verified that multiple executions with this seed yield identical results. 4.3.1 Generated area vs statistical area information. The attribution of area to housing units and activities is detailed in Section 3.3.4 . Housing unit areas are assigned based on area classes from statistical aggregates, building geometries, and the activities they hosts. Consequently, it is useful to compare the number of generated housing units against each statistical area class. Due to the allocation of missing housing units into building blocks with unknown values, no statistical aggregate lacking housing units is simulated. Therefore, if a statistical aggregate contains more housing units in the collected data than in the census, the algorithm cannot subtract housing units from the building dataset. The map in Fig 23 illustrates this minor discrepancy in the 11th arrondissement of Paris, while the maps in Fig 24 detail the values for the smallest and largest housing unit area classes. All other classes are available in Annex F. The lower area classes predominantly exhibit an excess of simulated housing units, whereas the higher classes show a deficit. In order to overcome the limitations highlighted in the previous section, the process described in 3.3.4 includes a maximisation of the housing units area, bounded by the physical area available in each building. The necessity of this step and the number of iterations can be questioned. This processing chain presents an analysis of how this maximisation succeeds in meeting the objectives of housing creation specified by the statistical aggregates. An optimum number of replications is estimated to improve overall workflow efficiency. This process is applied to the three studied arrondissements of Paris. Building groups and activities are imported and the process of housing unit area attribution is analysed. The number of iterations of the built-in maximisation process increases from 1 to 70. This process calculates, for each of the 7 area classes, the absolute difference between aggregated statistical values and the generated set of housing units. The update process defined on page 15 matches activities between an out-of-date dataset and a historicised dataset in order to update particular attributes. It is possible to isolate cases where the matched historicised activity is reported as closed whilst the activity to be updated is supposed to be open. The highlighted conflicts between those two kinds of datasets are resolved by a ground truth control reflecting the real situation. An analysis is then carried out to examine this dataset conflict. The historicised dataset comes from the SIRENE database, which collects administrative statuses about legal units and their establishments, as well as every change that occur over time. OSM data are crowdsourced and might not always be up to date. Both datasets have recent and corresponding release dates (SIRENE release of 01/02/2025, OSM data dump of 18/02/2025). This processing chain selects a list of matched activities that are open in the crowdsourced OSM dataset but are reported as closed in the historicised authoritative SIRENE dataset. Each activity’s location is visited to resolve the conflict between the manual mapping and the abstract administrative status. This processing chain has been applied to the city of Courbevoie (92), located in the Paris suburbs on the border of La Défense business area. The dataset used is the most recent generation described in 5 and the manual gathering took place on the 5th and 7th of March 2025. The code used to generate the reports can be found at https://s.42l.fr/OSMopen . 157 matches of activities were visited and manually labelled. Three general observation labels are possible, each with specific cases. The first label is attributed when the visited activity is reported as closed . It indicates that the OSM database is lagging behind the SIRENE database. A few specific cases can further qualify that closure information, the main ones are: close and vacant : the former activity is still visible but has ceased operating. The was: tag exists in OSM and should be applied in this case. closed and replaced with same type : a new activity of the same type has replaced the former matched activity. Updating the OSM database is less impactful because only secondary characteristics need to be updated. closed and replaced with other type : a new activity of a different type has settled in the former activity’s premises. The second label is a mismatch . The match of activities could be completely wrong, where the types don’t match at all or where both activities coexist. A second specific case is when an OSM activity has been matched with an older version of the SIRENE dataset. During the matching process, the OSM activity is compared with multiple versions of the historicised activity because no time information was gathered concerning the creation of the OSM activity. The SIRENE activity with the highest similarity score is then chosen. When similarity score are equal, a random activity is selected. This can lead to multiple erroneous matches, especially when no names are provided. The last general observation label is applied when the match is correct and corresponds to the activity at the visited location, which is nevertheless open . This situation can arise because an activity has changed ownership and has been reported as closed. In reality, the OSM activity is open while the corresponding SIRENE activity was not found. In the model, the OSM activity will be considered closed, but there is a high likelihood that another SIRENE activity is registered under a different typology and kept in the final result. Details of the ground truth data gathering is available in this file https://s.42l.fr/OsmClose and is summarised in Fig 26 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0359346.g026 Fig 26 Result of ground truth data gathering on the confrontation of two datasets regarding the closing of shops. Rightly closed activities represent 52% of matches, 25% were mismatched and 23% were still open. The relatively high number of mismatch or open matches could be explained by inconsistencies in administrative statuses. As an example of an open problem, the shop les jardins de Courbevoie , registered as a retailer of vegetables and fruits, and located at 11–13 avenue Léonard de Vinci , is reported as closed in the administrative records but not in OSM. Visual examination shows that this shop is still open and OSM is correct. A closer look at the SIRENE data reveals that, although this shop was reported closed in September 2023, a new shop opened with a similar name ( la corbeille de Courbevoie ) but with a different type (wholesale of vegetables and fruits) and no continuity in the legal unit. The visual inspection also suggests that the shop continues to operate as a retailer rather than wholesaler. Additionally, a few large companies were unduly matched and flagged. For instance, the headquarters of a very large car-glass company is reported as closed, but this only concerns the investment branch of the firm. It is therefore considered a mismatch rather than an open label because the matching algorithm identified a closer relation on this sector while it should have matched another branch of the company. Half of the mismatched activities resulted from the selection of an incorrect version of the historicised data. Such cases could be easily avoided in future improvements of the matching algorithm. The three principal kinds of closure were approximately equally distributed. These proportions should reflect the economic vitality of the reported area. This processing highlights the difference between two types of activity inventory: administrative statuses and public gathering/perception as transcribed by the OSM contributions. It also isolates specific points in order to improve the matching algorithm, for example, the introduction of opening statuses or date of the entry into the selection criterion. The validity of such results must be put into perspective because of the strong dependency on the location and number of OSM contributors living in or visiting the area. It would also be interesting to apply this processing to diverse types of communes. It also demonstrates that the algorithms developed in this work are usable for various purposes. Here, feedback from collaborative and open datasets is generated. Even if its limited results do not allow systematic use, they could provide great help to OSM contributors who keep the database up to date. A list for all of France could easily be generated, upon each monthly update of the SIRENE dataset, in order to indicate potentially updatable elements for OSM contributors. A few initiatives by the French OSM community (for example https://www.complete-tes-commerces.fr/ ) point in this direction. Dynamic spatial analysis could be performed on a larger set of collected data. Even though Courbevoie has two major malls on its municipal territory, very few matches were found within them. Shopping streets experience frequent shop turnover, and their premises may be more prone to be replaced by other shops than elsewhere. Different clusters could also be formed because some activity types are more likely to remain open or be replaced by the same type (e.g., banks, bakeries), whereas declining activities are more likely to become vacant or be replaced by a different type (e.g., ready-to-wear fashion store). 4.4.2 Validation of the whole model. Therefore, the list of improvements, repurposing, and reuse discussed in this section could be undertaken by any interested actor. This model is part of the ICI project [ 14 , 15 ] and will benefit from continuous improvements by the authors of this article. Multiple module improvements have been outlined during the model presentation and its validation steps. Extending the collection of ground truth data would allow calibration of the numerous parameters that have been set manually (probabilities of premises, distance thresholds, etc.). The next step for this model is to work on an ontology, by extending existing activity type and building ontologies. The generated data would then be deployed on a triple store to enable use on the Semantic Web and to share its results on a large, modular scale. The inclusion of this model in the National Digital Twin of France is also an ongoing process. This model has been applied to all French communes and has, for now, only been used with French data. Applying this model to another national context would be a solid proof of its portability and robustness to input data. A minimal example using Eurostat data would enable this model to be applied to each European country. It is possible to enhance the model on specific subsystems to comply with given simulation purposes. For example, dedicated plugins for generating kindergartens, schools and universities have been developed for the ICI project, using specific authoritative datasets. Due to its modular design, different parts of this model can be reused in other works. The automatic activity-conflation process itself could be used as a standalone project to conflate diverse sources of activities or POIs. The whole pipeline could be employed in contexts other than modelling building land use, as highlighted in this article: base data for spatiotemporal analysis of retail, office vacancy or real estate, creation of to-do lists for collaborative cartographic projects such as OSM, etc. This model would also be suitable for the generation of realistic synthetic populations. The descriptive data produced by this model could also populate 3D building models. Modelling the inner organisation of buildings has become a subject of interest [ 6 ]. Usually, those techniques focus on the physical configuration of interiors rather than on the use of such rooms [ 51 ]. Knowing the function of a premises could still be of great interest for constructing its 3D model. Parametric algorithms are used by [ 52 ] to create a grammar of indoor architecture and generate room configurations for each floor of a building. Procedural simulation could be used to model a connected set of rooms forming a single activity or housing unit. The systematic deployment of these models could benefit from the knowledge produced by BLU. Various city models (CIM, CityGML with the IndoorGML extension) or building models (BIM) are also challenging research and industrial fields [ 53 ]. The data generated by this model could fit into such formalisms, as multiple research projects try to link GIS with BIM models [ 54 – 56 ]. Generated data could also be used to assist façade reconstruction algorithms [ 57 ] by providing realistic semantic information about the distinct activity types in a building. 7 Conclusion The model presented in this article generates a precise and well-detailed building land use at an individual scale. This building land use is divided into housing units and activities, each accompanied by rich descriptions, either extracted from datasets or simulated. Data are generated for the specified dates through the integration of historicised data. It can adapt to various input datasets and can be deployed in different geographical environments, ranging from sparsely described to feature-rich datasets. Various validation methods are applied to the numerous original methods of each module in order to evaluate the realism of simulated attributes, assess the benefits of more or less complex input datasets, and validate the overall approach. Ongoing work consist in deploying the model to other European country using harmonised Eurostat datasets, and subsequently to sparser-data contexts. Our approach represents a novel way to model building land use. Whereas much prior work has used a combination of image analysis and social data to infer broad classes of building land use, sometimes combining such classes, this model produces individual results with an unrestricted number of activity-type combinations. The results produced are highly descriptive and could be exploited through a database or via the semantic web in the near future. This model also brings significant contributions to the literature. The building object itself is questioned, whereas it is taken as given in other works. A new concept suitable for building land use definition is modelled, which does not rely on a building’s geometric shape. The conflation of activities is a novel systematic approach, flexible regarding the number of input datasets. Our findings suggest that the more datasets are merged, the more precise the results become. The conflation methods could be reused in the POI research field. While the activities’ attribute enrichment is tailored to generate diverse basic and useful information, the housing area attribution mechanism highlights a need for further research to address this issue more effectively. Finally, original validation methods have been designed and could be transposed to other similar works. Ground truth data collection has ultimately confronted the modelled data with the real world and provides a deep understanding of the weaknesses of our approach, whereas similar studies limited their validation processes to the use of online street-view images. Efforts have been made to ensure the transparency, availability and reproducibility of all resources used in this work. Annex E describes the pseudocode of the algorithms developed in this work. The large generated datasets will soon be made publicly available and are already in use by the ICI epidemiological simulator [ 14 , 15 ]. Appendix A Glossary Specific notions used in this article are defined as follows. Building land use : An inventory of the distinct functional spaces hosted by a building. Several classes of attributes can enrich this description: type-related attributes characterise functional spaces, visitor access, and occupancy levels, while geographic attributes describe how functional spaces are distributed within a building (e.g., number of storeys, floor area). Building block : A primitive building element for the building model. Most of the time, it is based on a simple geometry and any rupture (different height, physical separation) would consist in the creation of a new building block . Building group : Building model that allows a functional continuity in the hosted activities or housing units. Consists in grouping one or more building blocks together. It is the main building object handled by this model. Functional space : A primitive element of the building’s floor, related to a use. Could be one or more connected rooms, but the model presented in this article doesn’t work at this level of detail. In this model, functional spaces can be of two types: housing units or activity . Housing unit : Set of rooms dedicated to accommodation for one or more persons. A household can settle in a housing unit, but it can also stay empty. A dedicated premises is automatically attributed to a housing unit. Activity : A formal or informal occupation which is linked to a particular building for spatial or administrative reasons. It does not necessarily occupy a premises . Its type can be composed of typologies . Premises : Space dedicated to a functional space inside a building. As a housing unit automatically own a dedicated premises, this term is not used to describe such element. In this model, it cannot be shared between two functional spaces. Typology : List of classes which partition different kinds of activities from a single dataset. Two kinds of typologies are considered: (i) project typologies which are attached to each dataset and (ii) the common typology , which is created specially for this model. Each class of project typologies must match a class from the common typology . Annex D.2.1 detail the creation of the common typology . Historicised : The ability to recreate the modellised object at the precise state it had at given date, independently from the date of release of the data. Statistical aggregate : Represent the lowest scale of spatial unit for which statistics are public. Typically groups a few thousand inhabitants. Corresponds to Census Tracts in USA or IRIS in France. A fourth data source consist in an authoritative database for building, which is currently being developed by trans-governmental agencies on the BatID project. The BatID project is merging multiple datasets concerning buildings, such as their energy efficiency, their construction material, from 25 different data producers. It also aims to assign a unique ID to every building. It currently faces some difficulties due to the multiple definitions that the building notion can cover, the geometric entanglements with parcels and addresses, and the cardinality of each of those objects. It also misses public-owned buildings, which is of interest for many reasons. The BD_Topo ® is a stable building database, available countrywide and edited by IGN. This building database should be precise enough concerning geometries (1m precision) and its attributes (including height, number of storeys, and number of housing units) cover the general information produced by other authoritative data producers. OSM buildings have been judged to be less precise by other studies [ 31 ], and incompleteness has also been detected [ 58 ]. Local databases, maintained by cities or higher administrative actors, could provide manual or hand-corrected data, with high definition buildings. They could be used later to enrich this building model. BD_Topo ® produces a new set of data each year from 2008 to 2018 then four releases per year, which approximately covers the project time-lapses needs. Addresses: In France, multiple administrations had parallel databases of postal address. IGN had a component within BD_Topo ® with addresses from 2014 to 2022, called Adresse_Premium ® and providing links from addresses to cadastral parcels and buildings. The standardisation delegation task force created the BAN ( base d’adresse nationale ), which must merge every data from every administration dealing with postal addresses and allowing regular updates by local urban bureaux. On the first version of the BAN, entrances addresses, created by postal services, were linked with cadastral parcel addresses, attributed by tax services. This represents a suitable topological simplification for the integration of parcel data. On the new version of the BAN (since 2022), namely BAN-Plus, this distinction is not included but a link is automatically generated between addresses and buildings from the BD_Topo ®, as a replacement for the discontinuation of Adresse_Premium ®. For dates with no existing links, or to reapply this model to another country, a simple process to assign addresses to buildings was designed Cadastral parcels: In France, two different institutes produce parcel data. The Cadastre is edited by the French Tax Service (DGFIP) and produces a closed-source dataset with address information but also with inconsistencies and topological errors. IGN derives this dataset to an open product which fixes topological issues, but does not contain address information. Address and parcels can be linked in an open dataset in other countries where parcels were defined with simpler relationships to other geographical elements. If it is not the case, a random address from an entrance contained by the parcel could be attributed. D.2 Activity. For the French case, two authoritative data sources are used, both from the French National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE): SIRENE : Administrative database for every declared companies or associations. Every legal unit is linked to one or more establishments, which are spatially localised. This database has many descriptive and historicised fields (names, bounds on the number of workers, legal unit) and receives monthly updates. BPE : Aggregates public equipment data. This dataset uses many sources, including SIRENE, but also from ministries (health, national education, etc.), public agencies ( La Poste , National Company of the French Railways (SNCF), etc.)(see https://www.insee.fr/fr/metadonnees/source/operation/s1524/processus-statistique ) Each year, a new dataset is produced. Its quality is validated by INSEE teams (see https://www.insee.fr/fr/metadonnees/source/operation/s1524/gestion-qualite ), which cause a publication delay of 18 months. The OSM project has been used in numerous research projects due to its global accessibility, large contributor base and rapid evolution. There are different ways of using OSM data, an API for accessing raw content (e.g., https://overpass-turbo.eu/ ), portals that sort and transform data (e.g., https://www.geofabrik.de/ ). As a free and open project, it is possible to host all necessary information in a PostGIS database using the osm2pgsql ( https://osm2pgsql.org/ ) tool. Datasets managed with this tool are updatable, highly configurable. This tool also provides very fast access once the data are stored. All data that could represent indoor activities are kept, using a filter on the tags amenity , shop , office or tourism . Every value of those tags which represents outside activities or micro-mapping elements (such as watering point, garbage, etc.) are filtered out. Annex C describes those importing and sorting operations. Multiple attribute can be extracted from this data, such as opening hours or capacity of activities. This information is strongly related to the type of the activity (for a restaurant, it means the number of seating places). The historicisation of this data is possible through an API which tracks information about modifications applied to each object. In the Paris case, a dataset from the local source Atelier Parisien de l’URbanisme (APUR) is added. This dataset has the particularity of manual surveys and reviews of shops and social facilities in the city of Paris. This does not concern other activities such as offices or other public services. New datasets are produced every three years. An attribute giving an indication about the size occupied by each activity is also provided. It also contains vacant premises, which are absent from other databases.
