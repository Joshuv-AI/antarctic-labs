Centralizers of the complex orthogonal and symplectic group
Tadej Starčič

Abstract
. We find a recursive algorithm for computing the precise centralizers of the complex orthogonal and symplectic groups, and hence the isotropy groups, with respect to the similarity transformation on the spaces of skew-symmetric and Hamiltonian matrices, respectively. These groups are conjugate to groups of certain nonsingular block matrices whose blocks are rectangular block Toeplitz.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}
be either the field
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
.
Denote by

\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})\subset\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})
the subgroup of standard
orthogonal

matrices in the group of nonsingular
n
-by-
n
matrices
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})
;

R
is orthogonal precisely when
R^{-1}=R^{T}
.
Also, let
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F})\subset\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F})
be the standard
symplectic group
consisting of all
2n
-by-
2n
matrices
R
such that
R^{-1}=J_{n}^{-1}R^{T}J_{n}
, in which

J_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}
, where
I_{n}
is the
n
-by-
n
identity matrix.

If a matrix group
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
is either
\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})
or
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F})
we consider its natural
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
-
conjugation (
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
-similarity)
action:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits\times\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits\to\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits,\qquad(Q,R)\mapsto Q^{-1}RQ.

A fundamental object associated with
this action is its
centralizer (stabilizer) group
at any element
R\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
; see, e.g.,

[
15
]
for basic properties:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(R):=\{Q\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits\mid Q^{-1}RQ=R\}.

There exists a unique multiplicative Jordan-Chevalley decomposition of
R=US=SU
with
U\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
unipotent (unitriangularizable) and
S\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
semisimple (diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
), and we have
\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(R)=\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(S)\cap\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U)
.
It is well known that
the centralizer
\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(S)
for
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
, respectively,
is conjugate (via some nonsingular
P
) to a direct sum of the form

\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{j}\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})

and
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2k}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2m}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{j}\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
,
while
\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U)
is decomposed into a semidirect product (see
monographs
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
,
[
16
, Ch. E.IV.2]
):

\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U)=\mathop{\rm L}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U))\ltimes\mathop{\rm R}\nolimits_{u}(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U)),

in which
\mathop{\rm R}\nolimits_{u}(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U))
is a unipotent radical (i.e. a unipotent normal subgroup), and
\mathop{\rm L}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U))
is a reductive part
conjugate (via
P^{\prime}
possibly
\neq

P
) to the direct sum:

\mathop{\rm L}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U))\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{k\textrm{ odd}}\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{k\textrm{ even}}\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C}),&\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{C})\\
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{k\textrm{ odd}}\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2k}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{k\textrm{ even}}\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C}),&\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{C})\end{array}\right.\hskip-5.69054pt.

Also, the dimension of
\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U)

has already been obtained.

In this paper we give a detailed description of
\mathop{\rm R}\nolimits_{u}(\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(U))
for unipotent
U\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
, where
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
is either
\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})
or
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F})
.
We show that centralizers are conjugate to groups of nonsingular block matrices with rectangular block Toeplitz blocks.
We also provide a recursive algorithm for computing
\mathop{\rm C}\nolimits_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(R)
for arbitrary

, with
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits
either
\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{C})
or
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
.
Moreover, these centralizers are conjugate to those of so-called

orthogonal and symplectic group (Theorems
2.6
,
2.5
).
We add that the situation in the real case is more involved (Remark

Given a nonsingular matrix

By writing

we obtain

\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{C},H)=P^{-1}(\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{C}))P

If

is

-by-
n

the

\mathbb{F}
-vector space of

matrices;

If either
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F},H)

\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F})

-similarity action on

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits\times\mathop{\rm V}\nolimits\to\mathop{\rm V}\nolimits,\qquad(Q,A)\mapsto Q^{-1}AQ.

In the language of Lie groups and Lie algebras, this action is the adjoint representation of
\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F},H)
or
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F},H)
as a classical Lie group on its Lie algebra
\mathop{\rm Skew}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F},H)
or
\mathop{\rm Ham}\nolimits_{2n}(\mathbb{F},H)
. For more on the theory of classical groups see, for example, a monograph
[
22
]
. We note that

) for
\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm O}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})
,
\mathop{\rm V}\nolimits=\mathop{\rm Skew}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})

and these play a central role in the study of the geometry

, as shown by Hua
[
9

(cf.
[
20
]
).

Furthermore, denote the
isotropy (or stabilizer) group
with respect to (

) by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(A):=\{Q\in\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits\mid Q^{-1}AQ=A\},\qquad A\in\mathop{\rm V}\nolimits.

If again either
H=P^{T}I_{n}P
or
H=P^{T}J_{n}P
for some nonsingular

, it follows that
P^{-1}\mathop{\rm Skew}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F})P=\mathop{\rm Skew}\nolimits_{n}(\mathbb{F},H)

Next, the exponential map
\exp\colon A\mapsto\sumop\displaylimits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}A^{j}

maps
H
-skew-symmetric (
H
-Hamiltonian) matrices to
H
-orthogonal (
H
-symplectic) matrices, and nilpotent matrices
onto unipotent matrices.
If
A
has only one eigenvalue
\lambda
, then it is maped to
\exp(A)
with one eigenvalue

, and we have

(1.5)

\Sigma_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(A)=C_{\mathop{\rm G}\nolimits}(\pm\exp(A)).

Thus, for
N
nilpotent

-skew-symmetric (
H
-Hamiltonian), there is precisely one unipotent

-orthogonal (
H
-symplectic)

with

.

-skew-symmetric (
H
-Hamiltonian) matrices.
Our general approach to tacle this problem is related to that in our papers

]

on symmetric matrices were studied, respectively. However, there are several quite subtle differences between

Finally, to some extent isotropy groups
could help us solve the problem of simultaneous

By applying the Autonne-Takagi factorization and using a suitable orthogonal congruence transformation we

Next,

]
,
