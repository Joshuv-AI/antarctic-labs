Technical Areas
a. Discussion of the capabilities / challenges addressed (from your
 perspective)
Quantum sensing (QS) is an emerging and enabling field of science and technology with a range of
approaches for different applications. It is not monolithic, and different technologies are in different stages of
maturity. We can broadly distinguish four classes of QS:
 • Atom-based QS: Atomic clocks (time), atom interferometers (gravimeters, accelerometers, gyroscopes)
 • Spin-based QS: NV centers in diamond (magnetometry), NMR, atomic magnetometers
 • Superconducting QS: SQUIDs (magnetometry), resonators (microwaves), microcalorimeters (radiation)
 • Photonic QS: Quantum imaging and radar, squeezed light sensors
Each technology has different maturity and requirements, and different “financial and manufacturing metrics”
are appropriate. We have talked to and incorporated feedback from several industrial research partners [1].
Here we focus on atom-based and superconducting QS.

Atom-based QS is relatively advanced because there are important military applications driving the
development. Specifically, atom interferometry currently provides the world’s most sensitive accelerometers,
gyroscopes and gravimeters. They are being developed as sensors for position, navigation and timing (PNT)
that no longer depend on external GPS signals and are therefore not subject to jamming and spoofing [2].
The current challenge for atom-based QS is to make the transition from advanced R&D into the field. The
initial development is driven by military applications and therefore depends on government funding. However,
QS companies generally expect that atom-based QS gravimeters will be adopted quickly by the oil and mining
industries once they are commercially available [1]. In these industries, high costs are less of a concern since
the return on investment is significant if QS gravimeters can improve the efficiency of oil extraction or locate
new mining resources. Once established, size, weight, power and cost (SWaP-C) can be reduced so that
other applications in financially less powerful fields that are currently more speculative are expected to follow.
As far as we can tell, the crucial components used in atom interferometers (lasers, specialized electronics)
are or can be fabricated domestically.
Superconducting QS is a vibrant research field with an increasing range of applications in different areas
of science. They attract top talent driven by the desire to conduct Nobel-caliber research (e.g. dark matter
detection). However, to our knowledge no “killer application” has emerged that would justify large commercial
or military investment. For that reason, private-sector funding in this area is currently limited. One problem
is that low-temperature operation is acceptable in a research environment, but less so in a military or
commercial setting. This has led to the establishment of several small high-tech companies that provide
bespoke scientific instruments to industrial and academic research facilities, but it has not yet created an
entire superconducting QS industry.
The second reason is that superconducting QS is one area where “hand assembly and manufacturing of
discrete components and other labor-intensive processes” are common. That reflects the novelty and
experimental character of some of the ideas and is not necessarily a bad thing. The bigger problem is the
“low yield of supporting component technologies” and their “heterogeneous integration”. This is the area
where DARPA can have impact by defining performance criteria and standardizing interfaces (see below).

c. Development strategy, especially versus requested metrics
QS is currently used for great fundamental science. It is underrepresented in applied science. We are not
suggesting to reduce funding for fundamental science, since it attracts top talent and provides the fertile
intellectual ground for subsequent applications. But we suggest different approaches and metrics for different
QS technologies depending on the impact of their applications. We use atom-based and superconducting
QS as two examples.
 1. Atom interferometry:
The primary driver behind the commercialization of atom-interferometry QS is the desire by military forces to
use QS accelerometers and gyroscopes for navigation in GPS-denied environments [2]. Navigation can also
be supported by QS measuring local variations in the gravitational and magnetic field. Similarly, gravimetry
can be used to detect adversarial objects or identify hidden masses in treaty verification.
These initial applications will be government funded. We believe it is essential to fund the transition of these
technologies from the R&D phase to field use. Not only will that allow a performance assessment in a relevant
environment and comparison with existing alternatives. It will also require an increase in reliability that will
set the stage for applications beyond military uses.
Once atom-based QS are out of the R&D phase and commercially available, it is likely that gravimeter QS
will be used in the mining industry for surveying and use gravity anomalies to identify mineral deposits.
Similarly, gravimetry can be used in oil exploration to monitor the distribution of underground liquids and
optimize fracking operations and oil extraction [3]. These industries have deep pockets but are unlikely to
invest in the R&D to bring QS technology to commercial maturity. The situation may not be dissimilar to the
DARPA CSAC (chip-scale atomic clock) program where the availability of a product increased the range of
customers and applications it was used for.
One metric of success is total sales. Most essential components for atom-based QS (lasers, specialized
readout electronics) are available domestically. Some lasers are currently purchased form Europe but could
be produced in the US if desired. One possible exception is the availability of rare earth metals used in many
laser crystals. Establishing a financially viable domestic industry depends on the size of the market. Annual
sales of order ~1G$ are considered the threshold for a viable QS industry. This appears possible as long as
there is an actual product in the market.

 2. Superconducting QS:
If the main limitation in this field is the lack of a use case that warrants significant investment, one should not
expect a superconducting QS industry to emerge. In the absence of such a driver, the goal should be to
ensure a vibrant domestic research community that will be in the position to implement and commercialize
such an application when it emerges. (For those of us old enough to remember the 1990s, there was a time
when there was an incredible excitement about the potential of a new invention called the “internet”, but some
concern because “no one had figured out how to monetize it.”) For superconducting QS, sustained grants
in fields of interest are therefore still essential. Even current start-up companies still depend largely on sales
to research institutions supported by government grants [3]. The reason for the relatively high cost is the wide
variety of dissimilar high-profile scientific applications. They provide a market for one or two exquisitely

optimized items, even if they are very expensive, but not for more than that. Scaling and cost reduction would
occur naturally if there were a market for a common commercial product.
We discuss two other areas where government intervention in general and DARPA in particular can help with
superconducting QS. One is the establishment of standards that ensure reliable interoperability of
components from different sources. One current challenge in the field is that components are optimized for
one purpose, but their non-essential characteristics such as residual magnetism or radioactivity affect other
aspects of the QS. Standards for packaging and materials, including circuit boards and adhesives, and their
levels of intrinsic magnetism and radioactivity or RF output would be very helpful. It could be augmented by
“Best Practices” guidelines. Standardization of interfaces between superconducting QS and their
preamplifiers, the RF equipment and possible laser sources are also needed. The other is that many products
are barely out of the R&D phase and therefore often lack reliability or reproducibility. Reliability standards,
possibly with different categories to reflect different levels of product maturity, are necessary to move the
field forward. An independent beta test site would be helpful in this context.
The other is the establishment and sustained support of domestic foundries to ensure the sensor
technologies (and the workforce to produce and adapt them) are available once a military use case emerges.
Industry has little incentive to maintain capabilities unless there is continued demand for the product. The
build-up and subsequent demise of a small industry to build high-power laser components for the National
Ignition Facility is an illustrative example. National laboratories are the natural place for these types of
foundries, as they are not driven by the imperative to maximize return on investment. If a military use case
emerges, scientists at a foundry will be happy to adapt their QS technology or leave the lab to commercialize
it. If not, they will be equally happy to turn around and adapt it to search for the next hypothetical dark matter
particle and thereby remain at the forefront of QS technology development [3, 4]. The magnitude of support
should be commensurate with the potential that DARPA sees in particular QS.
Total sales is not a useful as a metric in an area like superconducting QS where a small number of items can
have an outsize impact. Instead, the success should be defined in terms of “impact” or “mission gain”. In the
absence of a military use case, the impact could be measured in terms of publications and range of scientific
applicability. Once a military use case is identified, the impact would be defined in terms of new capabilities,
strategic advantage or cost savings.

 3. Scalable domestic manufacturing
Most of the supporting technologies that are essential for QS applications (lasers, specialized electronics,
RF equipment) are available domestically or could be manufactured in the US if desired. One exception is
the availability of rare earth metals that are used in many of the laser crystals for QS control. We know that
DARPA is aware of that problem.
One possible game changer is to manufacture ion traps for atom-based QS by 3D printing rather than from
individual macroscopic components used in conventional Paul traps [5]. This combines large-scale
manufacturability with the miniaturization required for scalability and cost reduction. 3D printed ion traps
also facilitate the integration with miniaturized electronics for SWaP-C reduction. Still, establishing a QS
industry in the US ultimately depends on the use cases, either military or commercial, that warrant large-
scale investment. The more vibrant the research environment and the more innovative the researchers in it,
the better the chances that these use cases will be identified in the US.

Summary
 1. QS technologies with well-defined national security applications need to be advanced from the R&D
 stage into fieldable instruments. Once commercially available, non-military use will be sufficient to
 sustain a market for domestic manufacturing. Atom interferometry QS for GPS-independent position,
 navigation and timing (PNT) applications fall into this category, with initial non-military use cases in
 the oil and mining industries.
 2. QS technologies that have significant scientific impact but no military use cases yet need to be
 nurtured and maintained until / in case national security use cases emerge. This includes
 superconducting QS, which could currently benefit greatly from establishing interoperability and
 performance standards. National Laboratory foundries are a natural place to maintain capabilities
 in the absence of a large commercial market.
 3. 3D printed ion traps have significant potential for the miniaturization and scaling of atom-based QS
 technologies. They can reduce cost and facilitate the integration of ions traps with readout
 electronics. Most supporting technologies (lasers, specialized electronics, RF equipment) are
 available in the US or could be manufactured here to support a domestic supply chain, aside from
 the rare earth metals used in many laser crystals. Ultimately, the establishment of a domestic QS
 industry depends on the identification of use cases that warrant large scale investment and support
 a market of sufficient size (~1G$).
