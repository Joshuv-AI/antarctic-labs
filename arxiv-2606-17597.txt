Abstract
. For a manifold M ¯ \overline{M} with boundary ∂ M \partial M and interior M M , we introduce and study a weakening of the concept of projective compactness for torsion-free linear connections on M M , which we call projective pre-compactness. Via the Levi-Civita connection, this concept applies to pseudo-Riemannian metrics on M M . This is motivated by applications to scattering theory and, especially, to general relativity (GR), in view of asymptotic forms of metrics used in these areas. In the general setting of a projectively pre-compact connection ∇ \nabla we show that, assuming weak conditions on the asymptotics of Ricci curvature, there is an induced projective structure on the boundary. Under a slightly stronger condition on the Ricci curvature, we show explicitly that the standard tractor bundle and its normal tractor connection arise naturally on this boundary structure. The key ingredient to this is that ∇ \nabla admits a smooth extension to the boundary as a linear connection on the tensor product of Melrose’s b-tangent bundle with an appropriate density bundle, which then restricts to the boundary tractor bundle. A projectively pre-compact pseudo-Riemannian metric (satisfying the conditions on the asymptotics of Ricci curvature) is then shown to induce a holonomy reduction of the boundary projective structure to an indefinite orthogonal group. This endows the boundary with a decomposition into so-called curved orbits, which are either open or embedded hypersurfaces, representing space-like, time-like and light-like infinities in a GR context. We introduce and study a new asymptotic form for such metrics which is available near any boundary point and relate it to an asymptotic form used in general relativity. The latter is only available near boundary points in the open curved orbits. We show that, in that region, projective pre-compactness essentially is equivalent to this asymptotic form from GR, and that projective compactness is equivalent to the vanishing of the mass aspect function.

1.
Introduction

Conformal compactification was originally introduced by R. Penrose in the setting of
general relativity, and hence of Lorentzian metrics, with the aim to attach a
“boundary at infinity” to non-compact space-times, and remains extremely important

[
13
,
15
,
22
]
. Formally, this means finding a conformal embedding of
the space-time into a compact pseudo-Riemannian manifold and then considering the
(compact) closure of the image. A major motivation for the concept is that in
pseudo-Riemannian geometry null geodesics are conformally invariant up to
parametrization, so one can study “where light rays meet infinity” in this
picture. The basic concept makes sense in any signature and in the nicest cases this
leads to a conformal embedding of the space as the interior of a pseudo-Riemannian
manifold with boundary that has a specific behavior of the metric towards
infinity. In particular, this happens in many cases for complete pseudo-Riemannian
manifolds which are asymptotically hyperbolic, in an appropriate sense, and the
metric then induces a conformal structure on the boundary (i.e., the “conformal
infinity”). Requiring the interior to be (Riemannian and negative) Einstein, one
arrives at the concept of Poincaré–Einstein manifolds which have become a major
research topic in geometric analysis over the last decades

[
1
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
19
]
.

Already the example of Minkowski space shows that things are
complicated in an asymptotically flat setting. Here the “boundary”
is a union of hypersurface components which represent light-like
infinity and points glued to these, which represent space-like and
time-like infinities. While the behavior around light-like infinities
of a conformal compactification in this case is very satisfactory,
dealing with space-like and time-like infinities is subtle both from
the GR point of view and mathematically. In the GR literature attempts
to find other compactifications at space-like and time-like infinity
were made early, see e.g.
[
3
]
and in particular

[
16
]
, where it is indicated that such an alternative
compactification can be “almost” compatible with the projective
structure underlying the pseudo-Riemannian metric. In these
approaches, one obtains a boundary at infinity of hypersurface type
and there is the natural question of whether this inherits a
projective structure, making it into a
projective infinity
.

The projective structure induced by a pseudo-Riemannian metric can be
viewed as the family of all
geodesic paths
(or unparametrized
geodesics) of the Levi-Civita connection. More formally, one can view
it as the equivalence class of all torsion-free linear connections on
the tangent bundle, which have the same geodesics as the Levi-Civita
connection up to parametrization. In the GR literature, these
geometric aspects remain in the background, the focus is on a
certain asymptotic form for the pseudo-Riemannian metrics, in such a
compactification, near space-like and time-like infinity

[
3
,
2
]
. A special case of this asymptotic form
is studied as a “scattering metric” in geometric scattering theory,
see Chapter 6 of
[
21
]
.

One possibility to obtain a projective infinity comes from a general
theory of projective compactifications, which was developed in

[
9
,
10
]
in the setting of torsion-free linear connections on
the interior of a manifold with boundary. A change from a such a linear connection
to a projectively related one is described by a one-form. The basic definition of
projective compactness is that using a local defining function for the boundary to
construct a one-form (that does not admit a smooth extension to the boundary), the
resulting projective modification of the given connection
does
admit such an
extension. So similarly to conformal compactness, where one requires the metric to
diverge in a specific way towards the boundary, one here requires the connection to
diverge in a specific way. In particular, since projective modifications of the given
connection admit a smooth extension to the boundary, the projective structure
determined by the connection admits a smooth extension.

A new feature of projective compactness is that the concept allows for and requires
an additional parameter
\beta
, called the
order of projective compactness
,
which turns out to be related to volume growth. This is a positive real number,
assumed to be
\leq 2
to ensure that the boundary is at infinity, see

[
9
]
for details. Depending on
\beta
, a projective compactification may
induce different structures on the boundary, the main cases interest (so far) are

\beta=1
and
\beta=2
. For
\beta=2
, one obtains a conformal structure on the boundary
and the resulting theory is rather closely related to (but different from) conformal
compactifications. The relevant case for the current article is
\beta=1
. Assuming
that the boundary is totally geodesic, which amounts to asymptotic conditions on the
Ricci curvature, one here obtains an induced projective structure on the boundary.

In particular, one may apply this to the flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, for which, via central projection, the compactification can
be viewed as a closed hemisphere in
S^{n+1}
with its standard (flat)
projective structure and the equator as the boundary at
infinity. Adding the Minkowski metric into the picture gives rise to
an additional structure on the boundary, which can be formalized as a
holonomy reduction in the sense of
[
12
]
of the canonical
Cartan connection, associated to the projective structure on the
boundary at infinity, to the group
SO(n,1)\subset SL(n+1,\mathbb{R})
. This in particular decomposes the boundary sphere into
five so-called
curved orbits
. Two of these are
isomorphic copies of
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space, one is a copy
of
n
-dimensional de-Sitter space, and a further two are copies of
a conformal Riemannian sphere
S^{n-1}
.

One of the contributions of
[
9
]
is extending this picture
to general Ricci-flat connections and pseudo-Riemannian metrics, which
are projectively compact of order
1
. In the case of metrics, the work

[
9
]
also studies the relation to asymptotic forms

g=\frac{C}{\rho^{4}}d\rho^{2}+\frac{h}{\rho^{2}}

for a local defining function
\rho
for the boundary. Here
C
is
a smooth function that is asymptotic to
\pm 1
to second order along
the boundary, i.e. such that
C=\pm 1+\rho^{2}f
for a function
f
that
admits a smooth extension to the boundary. On the one hand, it is
shown in
[
9
]
that any metric that admits such a form is
projectively compact of order
1
. For Ricci-flat metrics,

[
9
]
also proves a converse to this result, namely that
such an asymptotic form is always available (for appropriate defining
functions) locally around points of the open curved orbits from
above. All the developments in
[
9
]
heavily rely on
tractor calculus for projective structures, which provides an
equivalent encoding of the canonical Cartan connection and emphasises
the analogy to conformal geometry.

While these are very satisfactory results, there is a significant
drawback from the point of view of general relativity. In the
asymptotic forms considered in the physics literature, see
e.g.
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
, one requires that the
function
C
mentioned above has the form
\pm 1+\rho f
and the
boundary value of
f
(whose vanishing is equivalent to the form
required for projective compactness), called the
mass aspect
,
is closely related to the total mass of the space-time. Thus true
projective compactness seems to be incompatible with non-zero
mass. This was also emphasized in the recent articles

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, which explicitly use the concept of
projective compactness from
[
9
]
. However, it was noted in
these works that for the more general asymptotic form one obtains most
of the features of projective compactness.

The current article addresses and clarifies exactly this issue. We
introduce a weakening of the concept of projective compactness that we
call
projective pre-compactness
. We consider the same
projective modification as in the definition of projective
compactness, but do not require that the resulting connection extends
smoothly to the boundary. Rather we define and require a very slight
weakening of this condition. Of course, this means that the projective
structure determined by a projectively pre-compact connection does

not
(in general) admit a smooth extension to the
boundary. (This is required. Indeed, as shown in
[
9
]
a
Ricci flat connection is necessarily projectively compact of order one
if its projective structure extends.) Hence the basic tools used for
the study of projective compactness so far are not available in our
setting. Nevertheless we are able to prove that the main features of
projectively compact connections and metrics are still available in
this more general setting. Even in the projectively
compact case, the results we obtain are stronger than the existing
ones while proofs are significantly simpler in general.

The main technical tool we use to replace tractors is the
b-tangent bundle

introduced by R. Melrose and used in his school of geometric analysis (see e.g.
[
21
]
). Given a manifold
\overline{M}
with boundary
\partial M
and interior

M
, this is a vector bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}\to\overline{M}
, whose sheaf of smooth sections is
the sheaf
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
of vector fields on
\overline{M}
which are tangent to

\partial M
along
\partial M
. The existence of such a bundle follows from the fact
that
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
is a locally free sheaf. Once this vector bundle is
available, one can of course apply all the tools from differential geometry to it,
and particular the notion of a linear connection makes sense. Our key technical
result is that a projectively pre-compact linear connection on the interior
M
of

\overline{M}
extends to a linear connection on
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
, the tensor product of

T^{b}\overline{M}
with an appropriate density bundle. While we don’t use tractors at this
stage, it should be emphasized that the idea for this approach comes from tractors,
see Section
2.10
for details.

An overview of the results we obtain is as follows. Section 2 studies
projective pre-compactness in the setting of connections. After
introducing the concept, we prove that, for
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, a
projectively pre-compact linear connection
\nabla
on
M
gives rise
to a canonical weighted symmetric bilinear form
\Phi
on

T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\times T\partial M
, which restricts to a weighted
second fundamental form on
T\partial M\times T\partial M
, see Theorem

2.5
. This form is closely related to the asymptotics of the
Ricci curvature of
\nabla
, see Theorem
2.9
. The vanishing of
this second fundamental form characterises a totally geodesic boundary
and, assuming this, we show in Theorem
2.9
that
\nabla

induces a projective structure on the boundary
\partial M
, so this
becomes the
projective infinity
of
\nabla
.

For the next step, we need a slightly stronger assumption, namely that
the canonical weighted form
\Phi
on
T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\times T\partial M
vanishes identically. This can also be characterized in
terms of the asymptotics of the Ricci curvature (or, equivalently, the
Schouten tensor) of
\nabla
and we refer to is as the boundary being

strongly totally geodesic
. Under this assumption, Theorem

2.12
shows that
\nabla
extends to a linear connection on the
bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
which, in spite of the simple proof, is the main
technical input for all further developments. Our main result in the
general setting of connections is Theorem
2.13
, which shows
that the standard tractor bundle and tractor connection associated to
the induced projective structure on the boundary arise as
restrictions from the weighted b-tangent bundle and its induced
connection.

Theorem
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
\nabla
be an affine connection on

M
, which is projectively pre-compact and let
P
be the
Schouten tensor of
\nabla
. Suppose further that for all

\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, the function
\mbox{{P}}(\xi,\eta)
admits a
smooth extension to the boundary if at least one of the fields lies
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and its boundary value vanishes if
both fields lie in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Then the bundle

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
with the connection induced by
\nabla

can be naturally identified with the projective standard tractor
bundle of the induced projective structure on
\partial M
and its
normal tractor connection.

In Section 3, we study projective pre-compactness of Levi-Civita connections of
pseudo-Riemannian metrics. We start by considering metrics with an asymptotic form

(3.2)

g=\frac{h}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{C}{\rho^{4}}d\rho^{2},

where
C=\pm 1+\rho f
for
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
and
h\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M})

is non-degenerate along the boundary on
T\partial M\times T\partial M
. Following ideas
in
[
9
]
, we prove in Theorem
3.1
that
g
(i.e. its Levi-Civita
connection) is projectively pre-compact and it is projectively compact if

f|_{\partial M}\equiv 0
.

Conversely, we next assume that
g
is a projectively pre-compact
pseudo-Riemannian metric, which is asymptotically tangentially Ricci
flat in the sense that
\operatorname{Ric}(\xi,\eta)
admits a smooth extension to
the boundary if one of its entries lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})

and vanishes along the boundary if both of its entries have this
property. Under this assumption, we show that there
is an induced projective structure on the boundary, and we prove
in Theorem
3.3
that, similarly as in the projectively compact
case (but even in that case under weaker assumptions),
g
gives rise
to a reduction of projective holonomy of the boundary structure to an
indefinite orthogonal group. By general results on
such reductions, this decomposes
\partial M=\partial M_{+}\sqcup\partial M_{0}\sqcup\partial M_{-}
into so-called curved orbits, where

\partial M_{\pm}
are open and
\partial M_{0}
(if non-empty) is an
embedded closed hypersurface. This is the decomposition of the
boundary into time-like, light-like and space-like infinity. It is
easy to see that an asymptotic form (
3.2
) can only be
available locally around points in
\partial M_{\pm}
and for special
defining functions. Our second main result in Theorem
3.5

provides an asymptotic form that is available everywhere together with
strong additional information on the coefficients in the asymptotic
form. In this result,
\nabla^{\rho}
denotes the linear connection on

T\partial M
induced by a specific projective modification of

\nabla
determined by the defining function
\rho
and
(\lambda\odot d\rho)(\xi,\eta):=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda(\xi)d\rho(\eta)+\lambda(\eta)d\rho(\xi))
.

2.
Projective pre-compactness for connections

In this Section, we define and study the concept of projective pre-compactness in the
general setting of torsion-free connections. The main focus is on the existence of an
induced projective structure on the boundary and an explicit description of the
projective standard tractor bundle determined by this structure.

2.1.
Setup and background

We consider a smooth manifold
\overline{M}
with boundary
\partial M
and interior
M
. A
local defining function for
\partial M
is then a smooth, real valued function

\rho
defined on an open subset
U\subset\overline{M}
such that

\rho^{-1}(\{0\})=U\cap\partial M
and such that
d\rho|_{U\cap\partial M}
is
nowhere vanishing. A key feature of this concept is that a smooth function

f:U\to\mathbb{R}
vanishes on
U\cap\partial M
if and only if it can be written as

f=\rho f_{1}
for some smooth function
f_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}
. In this case we say that

f=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Similarly, for
k\geq 2
, we say that
f
vanishes to
k
th order
along
\partial M
or that
f=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{k})
if and only if
f=\rho^{k}f_{1}
for a
smooth function
f_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}
.

For a function
f:U\cap M\to\mathbb{R}
, we will say that
f=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-1})
, if the smooth function
\rho f:U\cap M\to\mathbb{R}
admits
a (necessarily unique) smooth extension to all of
U
. Similarly for

k\geq 2
, we say that
f=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-k})
if
\rho^{k}f
admits a
smooth extension. We will sometimes paraphrase this as “
\rho^{k}f
is
smooth up to the boundary”.

If
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
is a defining function than any other defining
function on
U
can be written as
\hat{\rho}=e^{\alpha}\rho
for some
smooth function
\alpha:U\to\mathbb{R}
. This readily implies that all the
concepts introduced so far are independent of the choice of defining
function. Therefore, we will often not specify the domain of
definition of a defining function and say that
f=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{\ell})

for
\ell\neq 0
without specifying
\rho
. We will also extend this
terminology to other geometric objects like tensor fields and
differential forms.

If
f=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-k})
, then
\rho^{k}f
has a well defined boundary
value, which is a smooth function
U\cap\partial M\to\mathbb{R}
. This
boundary value
does
depend on the choice of defining
function, moving from
\rho
to
\hat{\rho}=e^{\alpha}\rho
it gets
rescaled by
e^{k\alpha}
. So here one has to be careful about the
dependence on the defining function.

Definition 2.1
.

The sheaf of (local) vector fields on
M
will be denoted by
\mathfrak{X}
. This has a canonical sub-sheaf, that we denote by
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
, with
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(U)
consisting of those

\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
which are tangent to
\partial M
along

U\cap\partial M
. So explicitly, we must have
\xi(x)\in T_{x}\partial M\subset T_{x}M
for any
x\in U\cap\partial M
. For a defining function

\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
, this may be equivalently characterized as

d\rho(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Observe that
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(U)

is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{X}(U)
. Indeed for

\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(U)
, the fact that
0=dd\rho(\xi,\eta)

immediately implies that
d\rho([\xi,\eta])=\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)-\eta\cdot d\rho(\xi)
. By assumption
d\rho(\eta)=\rho f

for a smooth function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
, so
\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)=d\rho(\xi)f+\rho df(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and likewise for
the second summand.

2.2.
The definition of projective pre-compactness

Let
N
be a smooth manifold and let
\nabla
and
\hat{\nabla}
be linear connections
on the tangent bundle
TN
. Recall that
\nabla
and
\hat{\nabla}
are said to be

projectively equivalent
if they have the same geodesics up to
parametrization. Since one can remove the torsion of a linear connection on
TN

without changing the geodesics, one usually works in the setting of torsion free
connections. Then, the condition can be equivalently phrased as existence of a
one-form
\Upsilon\in\Omega^{1}(N)
such that

(2.1)

\hat{\nabla}_{\xi}\eta=\nabla_{\xi}\eta+\Upsilon(\xi)\eta+\Upsilon(\eta)\xi.

We will sometimes write this formally as
\hat{\nabla}=\nabla+\Upsilon
.

The concept of projective compactness was introduced in
[
9
]
as an analog
of conformal compactness of (pseudo-)Riemannian metrics. In the setting

\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
from Section
2.1
, conformal compactness is a condition
on metrics
g
on
M
requiring that for one or equivalently any local defining
function
\rho
for
\partial M
the metric
\rho^{2}g
admits a smooth extension to
the boundary with non-degenerate boundary values.

Projective compactness of a linear connection
\nabla
on
TM
is
defined in a similar spirit by requiring that for certain one-forms

\Upsilon
(that are not smooth up to the
boundary ) the projective modifications

\nabla+\Upsilon
admit a smooth extension to the boundary. The
appropriate one-forms turn to be
\frac{d\rho}{\beta\rho}
for local
defining functions
\rho
for
\partial M
and a constant
\beta\in(0,2]
which is called the
order
of projective
compactness. (Observe that
\beta
cannot be absorbed into a constant
rescaling of
\rho
since we are dealing with a logarithmic
derivative here.) In general, the cases
\beta=1
and
\beta=2
are the
most interesting ones. While the basic idea of our extension could
also be applied for other values of
\beta
we restrict to the case

\beta=1
in this article.

As in
[
9
]
, we will mainly study the case of
special

linear connections here, i.e. connections that preserve a volume
density. It is straightforward to see that any projective class admits
such connections, and in the following when we mention a connection

\nabla
it may be assumed that is is special, unless we state
otherwise. So for a local defining function
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
for

\partial M
, we consider the one-form

\frac{d\rho}{\rho}\in\Omega^{1}(U\cap M)
and for a special linear
connection
\nabla
on
TM
, we consider the projectively modified
linear connection
\nabla^{\rho}:=\nabla+\frac{d\rho}{\rho}
on
U\cap M
. Observe that for another defining function
\hat{\rho}=e^{\alpha}\rho

we get
d\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}d\alpha+e^{\alpha}d\rho
and hence

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{\hat{\rho}}=\frac{d\rho}{\rho}+d\alpha
. Hence the
connections
\nabla^{\rho}
and
\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}
are projectively
related by the one-form
d\alpha
that is smooth up to the boundary. This
shows that if the conditions imposed in the following definition hold
for one defining function on
U
then they hold for any defining
function. So, loosely speaking, they are independent of the choice of
defining function.

Definition 2.2
.

In our usual setting
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, consider a special
linear connection
\nabla
on
TM
and the associated modified
connection
\nabla^{\rho}=\nabla+\frac{d\rho}{\rho}
determined by any
local defining function
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
.

(1) We say that

\nabla
is
projectively pre-compact
(of order
1
) if and
only if the following conditions are satisfied for each

U
and
\rho
:

(i)

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
is smooth up to the
boundary for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
.

(ii)

\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
is smooth up to the boundary
for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
.

(iii)

\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
is smooth up to the boundary if
at least one of the vector fields lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(U)\subset\mathfrak{X}(U)
.

(2) We say that
\nabla
is
projectively compact

(of order
1
) if
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
is smooth up to the boundary
for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
.

A pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
on
M
is called
projectively
pre-compact
, or, respectively,
projectively compact
, (of
order
1
) if and only if its Levi-Civita connection is.

Thus projective pre-compactness should be interpreted as the fact that

\nabla^{\rho}
“almost” admits a smooth extension to the
boundary. Observe in particular, that we can restrict any linear
connection to operations
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}\to\mathfrak{X}
and

\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}\times\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{X}
and projective modification
makes sense in this setting. Condition (iii) then exactly says that
the projective modifications of these “restricted connections” are
smooth up to the boundary.

2.3.
The canonical defining density

A major tool for the study of projectively compact linear connections
is projective tractor calculus. In the setting of projective
compactness, this is automatically available, since for a projectively
compact linear connection
\nabla
, the projective structure on
M

defined by
\nabla
by definition admits a smooth extension to all of

\overline{M}
. Hence all tools from projective differential geometry are
automatically available on all of
\overline{M}
and several results on
smooth extendability follows straight from projective invariance.

Things are not as straightforward in the projectively pre-compact case
since one cannot expect the projective structure to extend to
\overline{M}
.
For example, Theorem 3.3 of
[
9
]
analyzes the case of a
Ricci-flat linear connection
\nabla
on
TM
which preserves some
volume form on
M
. It is shown there that if the projective structure
determined by
\nabla
extends smoothly to
\overline{M}
, but
\nabla

itself does not extend smoothly to a neighborhood of any boundary
point, then
\nabla
has to be projectively compact of order
1
.

The advantage we have here (compared with say conformal geometry) is
that the bundles involved in projective tractor calculus do not need a
projective structure to be defined. This is most easily seen by
starting from the standard cotractor bundle
\mathcal{T}^{*}
, which can be
defined as the first jet prolongation of a certain density bundle

\mathcal{E}(1)
(that exists on any smooth manifold), see
[
4
]
and
section 2.2 of
[
9
]
for the definitions of
\mathcal{E}(w)
for

w\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
\nabla
is a special linear connection, then any of the density
bundles
\mathcal{E}(w)
admits a non-zero section that is parallel for
(the connection induced by)
\nabla
and hence nowhere vanishing on

M
and this section is unique up to a constant multiple. The first
step in
[
9
]
is then to prove that if
\nabla
is
projectively compact of order
1
, then any non-zero density

\sigma\in\mathcal{E}(1)
which is parallel for
\nabla
admits a smooth
extension by
0
to a defining density for
\partial M
. That means
that for any connection
\hat{\nabla}
in the projective class
determined by
\nabla
and which is smooth up to the boundary,

\hat{\nabla}\sigma
is nowhere vanishing on
\partial M
. The jet
j^{1}\sigma

then defines a global smooth section
L(\sigma)
of
\mathcal{T}^{*}=J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1)
, which is called the
scale tractor
associated to

\nabla
.

Now we can prove that these facts extend to the case where
\nabla
is
projectively pre-compact, although the interpretation of
J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1)
as a projective cotractor bundle is lost.

Proposition 2.3
.

In our usual setting
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
let
\nabla
be a
special projectively pre-compact connection on
TM

and let
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))
be a nonzero density that is parallel
for
\nabla
. Then
\sigma
smoothly extends by
0
to a defining
density for
\partial M
. In particular, it determines a global
smooth nowhere vanishing section
I:=j^{1}\sigma\in\Gamma(J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1))
on

\overline{M}
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to construct, locally near
\partial M
, a
modification
\tilde{\nabla}
of
\nabla
, which is projectively
compact of order
1
and such that
\tilde{\nabla}\sigma=0
. Then the
claim follows from applying Proposition 2.3 of
[
9
]
to

\tilde{\nabla}
. So we fix an open subset
U
that meets the boundary
and a defining function
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
for
\partial M
and
consider the projective modification

\nabla^{\rho}=\nabla+\frac{d\rho}{\rho}
of
\nabla
. Choosing
arbitrary vector fields
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
, we know by
definition that
\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
admits a smooth
extension of the boundary. Moreover, since

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
itself is smooth up to the boundary,
the boundary value of
\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
is a smooth
section of
T\partial M\subset T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}
. Now the
expression
\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
evidently is linear over
smooth functions in
\xi
and one immediately verifies that it is
linear over smooth functions in
\eta
up to
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Moreover, by definition
\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
itself
is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
if either
\xi
or
\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(U)\subset\mathfrak{X}(U)
and hence in particular if one of
them is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
.

Together, these facts imply that

(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta|_{\partial M}
is
induced by applying a bundle map
T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\times T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\to T\partial M
to the boundary values of
\xi

and
\eta
and we know in addition that this bundle map vanishes if
one of its entries lies in
T\partial M\subset T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}
. Since the quotient bundle
T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}/T\partial M

is trivialized by
d\rho
, we conclude that there is a smooth
section
\psi
of
T\partial M
, such that the boundary value of

\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
equals
d\rho(\xi)|_{\partial M}d\rho(\eta)|_{\partial M}\psi
.

Now we can extend
\psi
to a vector field
\tilde{\psi}\in\mathfrak{X}(U)

such that
d\rho(\tilde{\psi})\equiv 0
. Then
A:=d\rho\otimes d\rho\otimes\tilde{\psi}
is a smooth section of
(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M}\otimes T\overline{M})_{0}
, where the subscript indicates the trace-free part. Of
course
\tilde{\nabla}_{\xi}\eta=\nabla_{\xi}\eta-\frac{1}{\rho}A(\xi,\eta)

is a well-defined linear connection on
U\cap M
, which is
torsion-free since
A
is symmetric. Moreover, since
A
is
trace-free,
\sigma
is parallel for the connection induced by

\tilde{\nabla}
. Passing to the projective modifications of the two
connections determined by
\rho
, we of course get

\tilde{\nabla}^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta-\frac{1}{\rho}A(\xi,\eta)
. By
construction
\rho\tilde{\nabla}^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
admits a smooth
extension to the boundary for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and
again by construction this has vanishing boundary value. Thus

\tilde{\nabla}^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary, which by definition implies that
\tilde{\nabla}
is
projectively compact of order one.
∎

Having the canonical defining density
\sigma
at hand, we can actually
convert the vector field
\psi
obtained in the proof into a canonical
weighted object, which expresses the obstruction to
\nabla
being
projectively compact. Knowing that
\sigma
is a defining density for

\partial M
, we conclude that for a linear connection
\hat{\nabla}
on

\mathcal{E}(1)
, the derivative
\hat{\nabla}\sigma
is independent of the
choice of
\hat{\nabla}
along the zero locus
\partial M
of

\sigma
. Since
\sigma
is a defining density, we also conclude that for
any defining function
\rho
for the boundary,
\frac{\sigma}{\rho}

admits a smooth extension to the boundary with nowhere vanishing
boundary value. This readily implies that
\sigma\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta

admits a smooth extension to the boundary for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
. Then the considerations in the proof above can be rephrased
as the fact that there exists a section
\mu
of
T\partial M(-1):=T\partial M\otimes\mathcal{E}(-1)
such that the boundary value of

\sigma\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
equals
(\hat{\nabla}_{\xi}\sigma)|_{\partial M}(\hat{\nabla}_{\eta}\sigma)|_{\partial M}\mu
for any
\xi,\eta
. The
only dependence on
a choice here is via the use of
\nabla^{\rho}
. But as observed above for any other
defining function
\hat{\rho}
, the difference

\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta-\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}_{\xi}\eta
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary, so
\mu\in\Gamma(T\partial M(-1))
is canonically associated to
\nabla
.

2.4.
Boundary structures

Let
\nabla
be a special linear connection on
M
and let

\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{M})
be a nonzero density which is parallel for

\nabla
. In the case that
\nabla
is projectively compact of order

1
, it was shown in Proposition 3.1 of
[
9
]
that

\sigma\mbox{{P}}_{ab}
is smooth up to the boundary and defines a second
fundamental form along the boundary. Here
\mbox{{P}}_{ab}
is the
projective Schouten tensor which for a special connection is just a
constant multiple of the Ricci curvature. In particular, this implies
that the boundary is totally geodesic provided that
\nabla
has the
property that its Ricci curvature admits a smooth extension to the
boundary. In this case, one then obtains an induced projective
structure on the boundary, which, in the case of Ricci-flat
projectively compact metrics, is studied in detail in

[
9
]
.

Thus we see that additional conditions will be necessary to obtain a
boundary geometry induced by a connection
\nabla
on
TM
which is
only projectively pre-compact. Surprisingly, not much is lost compared
to the projectively compact case. We first discuss the connections on
densities induced by the connections
\nabla^{\rho}
. Recall that on a
smooth manifold
N
of dimension
n+1
, one can define the bundle

\operatorname{vol}(N)
of volume densities as used in Section 2.2 of

[
9
]
as the square root of the trivial line bundle

(\Lambda^{n+1}T^{*}M)\otimes(\Lambda^{n+1}T^{*}M)
. The density bundle
\mathcal{E}(1)
which is relevant for our purposes is then defined as the

(n+2)^{nd}
root of
\operatorname{vol}(N)
. In particular, in our setting of

\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, any linear connection on
TM
induces a
linear connection on
\mathcal{E}(1)|_{M}
, and it is natural to ask whether
such a connection is smooth up to the boundary.

Lemma 2.4
.

In our usual setting of
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
\nabla
be a special linear
connection on
TM
, which is projectively pre-compact. Let
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
be a
local defining function for
\partial M
and
\nabla^{\rho}
the corresponding modified
connection. Then the induced connection
\nabla^{\rho}
on
\mathcal{E}(1)|_{U\cap M}

smoothly extends to all of
U
.

Proof.

Fixing a non-zero section
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{U})
which is parallel for
\nabla
,
we know from Proposition
2.3
that
\sigma
smoothly extends by
0
to a
defining density for
U\cap\partial M
. By definition, this means that there is a
smooth, nowhere vanishing section
\sigma^{\rho}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{U})
such that
\sigma

coincides with
\rho\sigma^{\rho}
on
U\cap M
. But over
U\cap M
,
\nabla^{\rho}
is
obtained from
\nabla
via a smooth projective change, so we can use the usual
formula for the change of the induced connection on densities there. Hence for any
vector field
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(U\cap M)
, we get

0=\nabla_{\xi}(\rho\sigma^{\rho})=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}(\rho\sigma^{\rho})-\frac{d\rho(\xi)}{\rho}\rho\sigma^{\rho}=\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\sigma^{\rho}.

Since
\rho
is nowhere vanishing on
U\cap M
, we conclude that

\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\sigma^{\rho}=0
on
U\cap M
.

But now any section
\tau
of
\mathcal{E}(1)|_{U}
can be written as
f\sigma^{\rho}
for a
function
f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{R})
and thus for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
, we get

\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\tau=df(\xi)\sigma^{\rho}
which is smooth up to the boundary.
∎

This easily brings us to a canonical weighted second fundamental form and indeed a
bit further than that. Starting from
\nabla
, we can take the smooth section

\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))
and then form
\nabla^{\rho}\sigma
which is a well defined smooth
section of
T^{*}M(1)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

The section
\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma
of
\otimes^{2}T^{*}M(1)
is symmetric and
admits a smooth extension to the boundary. Denoting the boundary value by

\Phi^{\rho}
,
\Phi^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
is independent of
\rho
if at least one of the
vector fields
\xi,\eta
lies in
\Gamma(T\partial M)\subset\Gamma(TM|_{\partial M})
, so we denote
it by
\Phi
in this case. If
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, then
\Phi(\xi|_{\partial M},\eta|_{\partial M})
is the
boundary value of

\frac{d\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)}{\rho}-d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
. If also
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(M)
, then the first summand vanishes along the boundary, so

\Phi|_{T\partial M\times T\partial M}
defines a canonical weighted second fundamental
form for
\partial M
.

Proof.

Take vector fields
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
. Over the interior, we get

\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma(\xi,\eta)=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\sigma-\nabla^{\rho}_{\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta}\sigma
. By Lemma
2.4
, the first summand
is smooth up to the boundary. In the proof of this Lemma, we have seen that

\nabla^{\rho}\sigma=d\rho\otimes\sigma^{\rho}
, so the second summand can be written as

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)\sigma^{\rho}
, which by definition is smooth up to the
boundary, too. This observation also shows that

(2.2)

\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma(\xi,\eta)=(\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)-d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta))\sigma^{\rho}.

In the interior, this expression is visibly symmetric since
dd\rho=0
and since

\nabla^{\rho}
is torsion-free as a projective modification of the torsion-free
connection
\nabla
.

For a second defining function
\hat{\rho}=e^{f}\rho
, we get

d\hat{\rho}=e^{f}d\rho+\hat{\rho}df
and inserting this, we see that

(2.3)

\xi\cdot d\hat{\rho}(\eta)=e^{f}df(\xi)d\rho(\eta)+e^{f}\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)+e^{f}d\rho(\xi)df(\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho)

On the other hand,

\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}_{\xi}\eta=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta+df(\xi)\eta+df(\eta)\xi
, and hence

-d\hat{\rho}(\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}_{\xi}\eta)
can be written as

(2.4)

-e^{f}\left(d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta+df(\xi)\eta+df(\eta)\xi)+df(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\rho).

Finally, since
\sigma^{\rho}
is characterized by
\rho\sigma^{\rho}=\sigma
, we see that

\sigma^{\hat{\rho}}=e^{-f}\sigma^{\rho}
, so using (
2.2
), we see that

\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}\sigma(\xi,\eta)=\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma(\xi,\eta)-df(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)\sigma^{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\rho).

If either
\xi
or
\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, then

\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, hence in this case
\rho
and
\hat{\rho}

lead to the same boundary value. Finally, if
\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, then
\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)=\tfrac{d\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)}{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and if also
\xi

lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, this itself is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, which completes the
proof.
∎

2.5.
The case of totally geodesic boundary

We next study vanishing of (parts of) the canonical bilinear form
\Phi
from
Theorem
2.5
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
let
\nabla
be a special linear connection on
TM
which
is projectively pre-compact and has the property that the weighted second fundamental
form
\Phi|_{T\partial M\times T\partial M}
from Theorem
2.5

vanishes identically.

Then for any local defining function
\rho
, the connection

\nabla^{\rho}
restricts to give a torsion-free linear connection on

T\partial M\to\partial M
and any other defining function
\hat{\rho}

leads to a projectively equivalent connection. Hence the boundary

\partial M
inherits a projective structure from
\nabla
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.5
vanishing of
\Phi
on
T\partial M\times T\partial M
is equivalent to the fact that for
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
,
d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
vanishes along the
boundary, so
\nabla^{\rho}
maps
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})\times\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
to
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Given vector
fields
\underline{\xi}
and
\underline{\eta}
on
\partial M
, we
can choose smooth extensions to
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})

and then consider the boundary value of
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
which
lies in
\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
. One immediately verifies that this
depends only on
\underline{\xi}
and
\underline{\eta}
and not on
the choice of the extensions. Thus we obtain a well defined
operation
\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)\times\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)\to\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
, which clearly defines a linear connection on

T\partial M
.

Torsion-freeness of
\nabla^{\rho}
then immediately implies torsion-freeness of the
induced connection. Finally, we have seen already that for
\hat{\rho}=e^{f}\rho
, we
get
\nabla^{\hat{\rho}}_{\xi}\eta=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta+df(\xi)\eta+df(\eta)\xi
. Since
the function
f
is smooth up to the boundary, so is
df
and the restriction of

df
to
T\partial M
defines an element of
\Omega^{1}(\partial M)
, which describes a
projective equivalence between the induced connections on the boundary.
∎

Of course, there is a natural strengthening of this condition, which will turn out to
be of crucial importance in what follows, namely that
\Phi
vanishes identically on
all of
T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\times T\partial M
. By Theorem
2.5
, this is the
case if and only if for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
,
d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
and

\frac{d\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)}{\rho}
have the same boundary value.

Definition 2.7
.

(1) If
\nabla
is projectively pre-compact and is such that the
weighted second fundamental form
\Phi|_{T\partial M\times T\partial M}
from Theorem
2.5
vanishes, then we say
that
\nabla
is
projectively pre-compact with totally
geodesic boundary
.

(2) In the situation of (1) for any of the connections
\nabla^{\rho}
, we will
denote the induced linear connection on the boundary also by
\nabla^{\rho}
.

(3) If
\nabla
is projectively pre-compact and such that the bilinear form
\Phi

from Theorem
2.5
vanishes on
TM|_{\partial M}\times T\partial M
, then we say
that
\nabla
is
projectively pre-compact with strongly totally geodesic
boundary
.

2.6.
Ricci curvature and second fundamental form

We next analyze the curvature asymptotics of the modified connections

\nabla^{\rho}
. Recall that for a smooth manifold
N
and a general linear connection
on
TN
, there is a Ricci-type contraction of the curvature, which defines a section
of
\otimes^{2}T^{*}N
. This is symmetric if and only if the connection preserves a
volume density.

Lemma 2.8
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
consider a special linear connection
\nabla
on
TM

which is projectively pre-compact. For a local defining function
\rho
for

\partial M
, consider the modified connection
\nabla^{\rho}
on
TM
and let

R^{\rho}
be its curvature and
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
its Ricci curvature.

(1) For
\xi,\eta,\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
the vector field

\rho R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)
admits a smooth extension to the boundary. Moreover,

R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)
itself admits a smooth extension to the boundary if both

\xi
and
\eta
are in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

(2) For
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, the function
\rho\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
admits
a smooth extension to the boundary. Moreover,
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
admits a smooth
extension to the boundary if both
\xi
and
\eta
lie in

\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

(3) Suppose that
\nabla
is projectively pre-compact with totally geodesic
boundary. Then
R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)
admits a smooth extension to the boundary if

\zeta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
also admits a smooth
extension to the boundary if one of its entries lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

Proof.

We directly use the defining equation

(2.5)

R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\zeta-\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\zeta-\nabla^{\rho}_{[\xi,\eta]}\zeta.

Observe that by skew symmetry of
R^{\rho}
in
\xi
and
\eta
, we may always assume
that one of the two fields, say
\eta
, satisfies
d\rho(\eta)\equiv 0
. (Locally fix a
vector field
\xi_{0}
such that
d\rho(\xi_{0})\equiv 1
and then expand

\xi=d\rho(\xi)\xi_{0}+(\xi-d\rho(\xi)\xi_{0})
and like-wise for
\eta
.)

In the interior, we have
\rho R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)=R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\rho\zeta)
and
since
\rho\zeta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, replacing
\zeta
by
\rho\zeta
in
(
2.5
), the last summand in the right hand side admits a smooth extension to
the boundary. Likewise,
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\rho\zeta
admits a smooth extension and
since
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we see that

\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\rho\zeta
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary. Finally, by our assumption on
\eta
, we have

\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\rho\zeta=\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\zeta
and since this is in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
,
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\rho\zeta
also is smooth up to
the boundary. Hence
\rho R^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)(\zeta)
always admits a smooth extension
to the boundary.

If both
\xi
and
\eta
lie in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, then so does

[\xi,\eta]
, and hence
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\zeta
,
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\zeta
and

\nabla^{\rho}_{[\xi,\eta]}\zeta
all admit smooth extensions. This in turn shows that
also
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\zeta
and
\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\zeta

extend smoothly, and this completes the proof of (1).

In the setting of (3), assume that
\zeta
and without loss of generality
\eta
lie
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Then the last two summands in (
2.5
) cause no
problems while the assumptions imply that
\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\zeta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
so
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\nabla^{\rho}_{\eta}\zeta
admits a smooth
extension to the boundary, too.

Switching to the Ricci curvature, the fact that
\rho\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
admits a smooth
extension to the boundary follows immediately from (1) since
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
is a
contraction of
R^{\rho}
. For the second part, this is not as easy, since when
computing
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
as a trace,
\xi
and
\eta
are not both inserted
into the form slots of
R^{\rho}
. As in (1), we locally choose
\xi_{0}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\xi_{0})\equiv 1
and extend this to a local frame by

\xi_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
,
i=1,\dots,n
such that
d\rho(\xi_{i})\equiv 0
for
i\geq 1
. Then the dual coframe has the form
d\rho
,
\alpha_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and by
definition we get (on
M
)

(2.6)

\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)=d\rho(R^{\rho}(\xi,\xi_{0})(\eta))+\textstyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}(R^{\rho}(\xi,\xi_{i})(\eta)).

Since
\xi,\xi_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
each term in the sum in the right hand side
admits a smooth extension to the boundary. For the first term in the right hand
side, we have to go back to the defining equation (
2.5
). But by
construction
\nabla_{[\xi,\xi_{0}]}\eta
,
\nabla_{\xi_{0}}\eta
and hence

\nabla_{\xi}\nabla_{\xi_{0}}\eta
, and
\nabla_{\xi}\eta
admit smooth extension to the
boundary. But this implies that also
d\rho(\nabla_{\xi_{0}}\nabla_{\xi}\eta)
admits a
smooth extension, so the result follows.

Under the stronger assumption of (3), we know from above that all curvature terms
in the right hand side of (
2.6
) admit a smooth extension to the boundary
if
\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. By symmetry of
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
in the
interior, this implies the last part of (3).
∎

Recall that the Schouten tensor of a linear connection on the tangent bundle
TN
of
a smooth manifold
N
is an equivalent encoding of the Ricci curvature, which mixes
the symmetric and skew symmetric parts with different factors. In particular, for a
special connection, it is just
\frac{1}{\dim(N)-1}
times the Ricci curvature. Thus we see that the analog of the statements on
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
from Lemma

2.8
also hold for the Schouten tensor
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}
of
\nabla^{\rho}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Consider
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
and a special connection
\nabla
on
TM
which is
projectively pre-compact. Let
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{M})
be a nonzero section that
is parallel for
\nabla
and let
P
be the Schouten tensor of
\nabla
. Then
the section
\sigma\mbox{{P}}
of
S^{2}T^{*}M(1)
admits a smooth extension to the boundary and
its boundary value restricts on
T\partial M\times T\partial M
to the canonical
weighted second fundamental form
\Phi
from Theorem
2.5
. Moreover, the form

\Phi
vanishes on
T\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\times T\partial M
if and only if the boundary
value of
\sigma\mbox{{P}}
has the same property.

Proof.

On
M
, we can write
\sigma\mbox{{P}}=\nabla\nabla\sigma+\sigma\mbox{{P}}
and it is well known that
the right hand side defines a projectively invariant second order differential
operator
\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))\to\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}M(1))
, see
[
4
]
. Hence we may as well
express it in terms of the projectively equivalent connection
\nabla^{\rho}
, whence

(2.7)

\sigma\mbox{{P}}=\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma+\sigma\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}.

Now the two terms on the right hand side admit a smooth extension to the
boundary. For the first term this follows from Theorem
2.5
and for the
second term this follows from Lemma
2.8
, taking into account that

\sigma=\rho\sigma^{\rho}
. This proves the first claim. For the second claim, we know that
for
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, the function
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
admits a
smooth extension to the boundary (again using Lemma
2.8
), so

\sigma\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
vanishes along the boundary. Thus

\sigma\mbox{{P}}(\xi,\eta)=\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}\sigma(\xi,\eta)
and the boundary value of
this is the canonical weighted second fundamental form from Theorem

2.5
. Hence if
\sigma\mbox{{P}}
vanishes on
T\partial M\times T\partial M
, then we are
in the case of totally geodesic boundary, so by part (3) of Lemma
2.8
,

\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
admits a smooth extension the boundary if one of its entries
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Hence we see that, in this situation, the boundary
value of
\sigma\mbox{{P}}
again coincides with
\Phi
.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
\nabla
be a linear connection on
M
that is
projectively pre-compact and let
P
be its Schouten tensor. Then the boundary
is totally geodesic if and only if
\mbox{{P}}(\xi,\eta)
admits a smooth extension to
the boundary whenever
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and strongly totally

\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

Moreover, if
\nabla
is projectively pre-compact with strongly geodesic boundary,
then for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, we get

(2.8)

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)=\xi\cdot d\rho(\eta)+\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)-\rho\mbox{{P}}(\xi,\eta).

Proof.

The first part follows immediately from Theorem
2.9
, while
(
2.8
) follows readily from combining (
2.2
)
with (
2.7
).
∎

2.7.
Some b-calculus

For the next step we use the description of the sheaf
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
on
\overline{M}
developed by the Melrose school in geometric
analysis. Since
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
is a locally free sheaf, there is a smooth
vector bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}\to\overline{M}
, called the
b
-tangent bundle
, whose sheaf
of smooth sections coincides with
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}
. So by definition, a section

\xi
of
T^{b}\overline{M}
is a special vector field, but one has to carefully distinguish
between the two points of view, in particular if boundary values are concerned. As a
vector field,
\xi
has a boundary value which is a vector field on
\partial M
,
i.e. a smooth section of
T\partial M
. But
\xi
also has a boundary value as a
section of
T^{b}\overline{M}
, which is a section of
T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}
, a bundle whose rank is
one larger than the one of
T\partial M
. Indeed, mapping
\xi\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
to
its boundary value as a vector field defines a surjective bundle map

T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\to T\partial M
. A neat way to express things is that for

\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, we have
\xi\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
if and only if

d\rho(\xi)|_{\partial M}=0
for one or equivalently any local defining function

\rho
i.e. iff
d\rho(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. And the boundary value of
\xi
as a
section of
T^{b}\overline{M}
vanishes iff
\xi=\rho\xi_{1}
for some
\xi_{1}\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})

and hence iff
d\rho(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
.

Lemma 2.11
.

There is a short exact sequence

(2.9)

0\to\partial M\times\mathbb{R}\to T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\to T\partial M\to 0.

A canonical trivializing section of the first bundle can be realized as the
boundary value of
\rho\nu\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
, where
\rho
is a local defining
function for the boundary and
\nu\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
is a vector field such that

d\rho(\nu)|_{\partial M}\equiv 1
.

Proof.

By construction, the vector field
\rho\nu
vanishes along
\partial M
, so

\rho\nu\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
. Since
d\rho(\rho\nu)=\rho+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
and this is
not
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
, we conclude that the boundary value of
\rho\nu
as a section
of
T^{b}\overline{M}
spans the kernel of
T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\to T\partial M
, and it
remains to show that this boundary value is independent of the choice of
\rho
and

\nu
.

Any other choice for
\nu
is of the form
\hat{\nu}=\nu+\eta
, where
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
is such that
d\rho(\eta)|_{\partial M}\equiv 0
. But then

\eta\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
and hence
\rho\hat{\nu}=\rho\nu+\rho\eta
has the same boundary
value as
\rho\nu
. Any other defining function is of the form
\hat{\rho}=e^{f}\rho
and
then
d\hat{\rho}|_{\partial M}=e^{f}d\rho|_{\partial M}
. Hence given a choice
\nu
for

\rho
, we can put
\hat{\nu}=e^{-f}\nu
and then
\hat{\rho}\hat{\nu}=\rho\nu
, which
completes the proof.
∎

Once the vector bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}
is at hand, we can apply all the standard tools
from differential geometry to it. This immediately allows us to form the weighted

b
-tangent bundle of weight
w\in\mathbb{R}
by putting
T^{b}\overline{M}(w):=T^{b}\overline{M}\otimes\mathcal{E}(w)
. We can also apply vector bundle constructions, e.g. form the dual bundle

(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
, the symmetric square
S^{2}T^{b}\overline{M}
or the symmetric square

S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
of the dual. For the bundles constructed from
T^{b}\overline{M}
(without
dualization) the interpretation of sections is rather easy starting from tensor
products of sections of
T^{b}\overline{M}
, which locally span the space of all sections. For
example, a section
b
of
S^{2}T^{b}\overline{M}
is a section of
S^{2}TM
, which contracted
with
d\rho
gives a section of
T^{b}\overline{M}
that is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and hence
contracting
b
with two copies of
d\rho
the result is a function that is
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
.

Things are a bit more subtle for the dual. As we have seen above, locally around a
boundary point, we can form a local frame for
T^{b}\overline{M}
consisting of

\rho\nu
and vector
fields
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}
such that
d\rho(\zeta_{i})\equiv 0
. Now we can choose

\alpha_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
such that
\alpha_{i}(\nu)=0
and
\alpha_{i}(\xi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
and add to
this the form
\frac{d\rho}{\rho}
on
M
. Since for
\xi\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
,

d\rho(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, the function
\frac{d\rho(\xi)}{\rho}
admits a smooth
extension to the boundary. Thus
\frac{d\rho}{\rho}
defines a smooth section
of
(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
over all of
\overline{M}
and clearly the
\alpha_{i}
also define such
sections, so together these form the dual of the frame

\{\rho\nu,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}\}
. This leads to a neat description of sections of

(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
: A section of this bundle is a one-form
\varphi
on
M
such that

\rho\varphi
admits a smooth extension to the boundary and such that
\varphi(\xi)
admits
a smooth extension to the boundary if
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

In particular, we have
\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})\subset\Gamma((T^{b}\overline{M})^{*})
corresponding to the
inclusion
T^{*}\overline{M}\hookrightarrow(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
dual to the projection
T^{b}\overline{M}\to T\overline{M}
. The elements in this subspace can be easily characterized. Choose a (local)
defining function
\rho
and a vector field
\nu
such that
d\rho(\nu)|_{\partial M}\equiv 1
. Then
\rho\nu\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
and we claim that if

\varphi\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
satisfies
\varphi(\rho\nu)|_{\partial M}=0
then

\varphi\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
. Indeed, we know that
\rho\varphi\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
, so it suffices
to prove that this vanishes along the boundary. But
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
can be written
as
\xi_{1}+d\rho(\xi)\nu
where
\xi_{1}:=\xi-d\rho(\xi)\nu
satisfies

d\rho(\xi_{1})=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and hence
\xi_{1}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Thus

\varphi(\xi_{1})
admits a smooth extension to the boundary so
\rho\varphi(\xi_{1})
vanishes
along the boundary. On the other hand

\rho\varphi(d\rho(\xi)\nu)=d\rho(\xi)\varphi(\rho\nu)
, so this is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
by
assumption.

Having these descriptions at hand, the passage to the results of tensorial
constructions is easy starting from tensor products of sections. For example, a
section
b
of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
is a section of
S^{2}T^{*}M
such that
\rho^{2}b
admits
a smooth extension to the boundary and for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
,
\rho b(\xi,\_)
admits a smooth extension to the boundary as a section of
(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}
.
Hence in particular for
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
,
b(\xi,\eta)
admits a
smooth extension to the boundary. As above, we have
\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M})\subset\Gamma(S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*})
and one immediately shows that a section
b
lies in this subspace
if for
\rho
and
\nu
as above and
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we have

b(\rho\nu,\xi)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and
b(\rho\nu,\rho\nu)=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
.

2.8.
A canonical connection

We can consider linear connections on bundles constructed from

T^{b}\overline{M}
, to which all the general results about linear connections
can be applied. In particular, starting from a projectively
pre-compact linear connection
\nabla
on
M\subset\overline{M}
, we have an
induced connection on any density bundle
\mathcal{E}(w)
by Proposition

2.3
. Hence over
M
, we have an induced linear connection on
any weighted tangent bundle, which we also denote by
\nabla
, and we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.12
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
\nabla
be a projectively pre-compact linear
connection on
TM
. Then for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and

\eta\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))\subset\Gamma(T\overline{M}(-1))
, the weighted vector field

\nabla_{\xi}\eta
defined on
M
admits a smooth extension to the boundary.

If the boundary is strongly totally geodesic, then this extension lies
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Hence, under this assumption,
\nabla

extends smoothly to a linear connection on the vector bundle

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)\to\overline{M}
.

Proof.

The usual formula for projective changes in
[
4
]
shows that, over
M
, we can
write
\nabla_{\xi}\eta=\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta-\frac{d\rho(\eta)}{\rho}\xi
(and the
weight
-1
is essential for the term containing
d\rho(\xi)
to drop out). Since

\eta
is tangent to the boundary along the boundary, both summands admit a smooth
extension to the boundary. Applying
d\rho
to the right hand side, we get

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)-\frac{d\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)}{\rho}
which, in the case of
strongly totally geodesic boundary, vanishes along the boundary (see Theorem
2.5
).
∎

Hence we have obtained a vector bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)\to\overline{M}
of rank
n+1
, which
is endowed with a linear connection that we still denote by
\nabla
. This bundle can
be restricted to
\partial M
without problems, and it follows from the general
theory of linear connections that one can also restrict the connection to the
boundary. Indeed for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
and

\eta\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M})
one chooses (local) extensions to

\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and
\tilde{\eta}\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))
and then
considers
\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta}|_{\partial M}
. It is clear that this depends
only on
\tilde{\xi}|_{\partial M}=\xi
, while for
\eta
, the freedom is to add a

\rho\tilde{\eta}_{1}
for
\eta_{1}\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))
, and one immediately concludes
that this leads to the same restriction to the boundary.

2.9.
Relation to boundary tractors

Starting from a projectively pre-compact connection
\nabla
with
strongly totally geodesic boundary, we get an induced projective
structure on the boundary
\partial M
as well as the rank
n+1

vector bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}\to\partial M
which is
endowed with a linear connection induced by
\nabla
. One may hope
that this is related to the projective standard tractor bundle of the
boundary projective structure. There is a relation without additional
assumptions, but the main result is that under a slightly stronger
condition, the restriction indeed coincides with the standard tractor
bundle and its normal tractor connection.
We want to stress here that this conceptual description of
the boundary tractors is new even in the case of projectively compact
connections, the results in
[
9
]
apply only in the case
that
\nabla
is Ricci flat in a neighborhood of the boundary. In
contrast, we only require asymptotic Ricci-flatness in directions
tangent to the boundary here.

Let us briefly recall the standard approach to projective tractors on a manifold
N

of dimension
n
endowed with a projective class of affine connections, see

[
4
]
for more details and explicit formulae. One usually starts from the standard cotractor bundle,
which can be simply defined as a first jet prolongation of a line bundle
\mathcal{T}^{*}N:=J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1)
. Here
\mathcal{E}(1)\to N
is the bundle of (projective)

1
-densities, a trivial line bundle characterized by the fact that
\mathcal{E}(1)^{\otimes^{2n+2}}\cong(\Lambda^{n}TN)^{\otimes^{2}}
. The jet exact sequence for this
bundle has the form

0\to T^{*}N(1)\to\mathcal{T}^{*}N\to\mathcal{E}(1)\to 0.

Any linear connection on
\mathcal{E}(1)
defines a splitting of this short exact
sequence, and in particular, one can apply this to the connections induced by the
affine connections in the projective class. Hence any connection in the projective
class gives rise to an identification
\mathcal{T}^{*}N\cong\mathcal{E}(1)\oplus T^{*}N(1)
and
one can use the connection defining the identification, its Schouten tensor and
algebraic ingredients to define a linear connection on
\mathcal{T}^{*}N
in the given
splitting and then verify that the construction is independent of the choice of
connection. Hence it gives rise to a canonical connection on
\mathcal{T}^{*}N
called the

standard cotractor connection
.

The standard tractor bundle
\mathcal{T}N\to N
is then defined as the dual bundle to

\mathcal{T}^{*}N
, which implies that one gets an exact sequence

(2.10)

0\to\mathcal{E}(-1)\to\mathcal{T}N\to TN(-1)\to 0

and any connection in the projective class determines a a splitting of this sequence.
The standard tractor connection on
\mathcal{T}N
is then obtained from dualizing the
standard cotractor connection and it is easy to derive the formula for the standard
tractor connection in such a splitting, see
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.13
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
\nabla
be an affine connection on

M
, which is projectively pre-compact and let
P
be the
Schouten tensor of
\nabla
. Suppose further that for all

\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, the function
\mbox{{P}}(\xi,\eta)
admits a
smooth extension to the boundary if at least one of the fields lies
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and its boundary value vanishes if
both fields lie in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Then the bundle

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
with the connection induced by
\nabla

can be naturally identified with the projective standard tractor
bundle of the induced projective structure on
\partial M
and its
normal tractor connection.

Proof.

First note that our assumptions imply that
\nabla
has strongly totally geodesic
boundary, so we indeed get a linear connection on
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}

induced by
\nabla
. We next observe that by expression (
2.9
) from
Lemma
2.11
, the bundle
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
fits into an exact
sequence which looks like the sequence (
2.10
) for the standard tractor
bundle. The only difference is that the
(-1)
-density bundle of
\partial M
is
replaced by the restriction
\mathcal{E}(-1)|_{\partial M}
of the
(-1)
-density bundle
of
\overline{M}
. (Note that the power in the definition of
(-1)
-densities as a root of
the bundle of volume forms depends on the dimension). So we first prove that there
is a natural way to identify these two bundles.

Fix a non-zero density
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{M})
which is parallel
for
\nabla
. By Proposition
2.3
, this extends by
0
to a
smooth section of
\mathcal{E}(1)\to\overline{M}
which is a defining density
for
\partial M
. Taking any linear connection
\tilde{\nabla}
on

\mathcal{E}(1)
, the derivative
\tilde{\nabla}\sigma|_{\partial M}
is a
nowhere vanishing section of
T^{*}\partial M\otimes\mathcal{E}(1)|_{\partial M}
. This is independent of the choice of

\tilde{\nabla}
and its kernel in a point
x\in\partial M
coincides
with
T_{x}\partial M\subset T_{x}\overline{M}
. Putting
n:=\dim(\partial M)
,
taking the wedge product with this weighted one form induces an
isomorphism
\Lambda^{n}T^{*}\partial M\to\Lambda^{n+1}T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{E}(1)
, and hence between the squares of this line bundles. But the
square of the first bundle is the (intrinsic) density bundle
\mathcal{E}_{\partial M}(-2n-2)
while square of the second bundle is the
restriction to
\partial M
of
\mathcal{E}(-2n-4)\otimes\mathcal{E}(2)=\mathcal{E}(-2n-2)
. Since this extends to powers, roots and duals, we can in
particular identify
\mathcal{E}(1)|_{\partial M}
with the
\mathcal{E}_{\partial M}(1)
, so we drop the subscript.

Thus we have an exact sequence of the right form. In order to obtain the usual
formula for the standard tractor connection, we have to make a small change,
however. We define the inclusion
\mathcal{E}(-1)\hookrightarrow T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
by sending a density
\tau
to the boundary value of
-\tau\rho\nu
where

d\rho(\nu)|_{\partial M}\equiv 1
. From the proof of Lemma
2.11
we also
see how a choice of local defining function
\rho
gives rise to a splitting of the
exact sequence. Given
\tilde{\eta}\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))
the
(-1)
-density

-\frac{d\rho(\tilde{\eta})}{\rho}
admits a smooth extension to the boundary and its
boundary value depends only on the boundary value of
\tilde{\eta}
, which is a
section of
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
. Hence we have constructed a bundle map

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}\to\mathcal{E}(-1)
(depending on the choice of
\rho
),
which evidently splits the inclusion defined above.

So it remains to compute the connection induced by
\nabla
in such a splitting and
we fix the defining function
\rho
to determine the splitting. Then we take

\tilde{\eta}\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))
and
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and
denote by
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
the boundary value of the vector field

\tilde{\xi}
. Then we know that, in the splitting determined by
\rho
, the boundary
values of
\tilde{\eta}
corresponds to
(\eta,\tau)
, where
\eta
is the boundary
value of the vector field
\tilde{\eta}
and
\tau
is the boundary value of

-\frac{d\rho(\tilde{\eta})}{\rho}
. Now we compute

(2.11)

\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}{\tilde{\eta}}=\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}{\tilde{\eta}}-\frac{d\rho(\tilde{\eta})}{\rho}\tilde{\xi},

and we have to determine the boundary value of this section of
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
in
the splitting determined by
\rho
. In the first component, this simply is the
boundary value as a vector field, which is just
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta+\tau\xi
,
where
\nabla^{\rho}
also denotes the connection on
T\partial M(-1)
induced by
the connection
\nabla^{\rho}
in the projective class. This agrees with the formula
for the standard tractor connection in Section 3.2 of
[
4
]
.

To deal with the other component, we need one more observation. For
\tilde{\xi}
and
another vector field
\tilde{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we can apply the formula
(
2.8
), taking into account that
\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
by assumption, to conclude that

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\zeta})=\tilde{\xi}\cdot d\rho(\tilde{\zeta})+\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta})+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2}).

This equation can be twisted by a density without problems, one only has to replace
the derivative in the first term of the right hand side by

\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}
, so

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})=\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}d\rho(\tilde{\eta})+\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2}).

Taking this into account, we see that applying

\frac{-d\rho}{\rho}
to (
2.11
) and ignoring terms that are

\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, we obtain

-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}d\rho(\tilde{\eta})-\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}d\rho(\tilde{\xi})d\rho(\tilde{\eta}),

for the right hand side.
The first and last terms here add up to

-\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\frac{d\rho(\tilde{\eta})}{\rho}
, so the boundary value of
the whole expression is
\nabla_{\xi}\tau-\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)
. This matches the second
component of the formula for the tractor connection in
[
4
]
, apart from the
fact that
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}
is the boundary value of the Schouten tensor of the connection

\nabla^{\rho}
on
\overline{M}
rather then the Schouten tensor of the induced connection on
the boundary, so it remains to show that these agree.

Since the boundary is totally geodesic, the curvature of the
restriction of
\nabla^{\rho}
to
\partial M
is obtained by inserting vector fields
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
into the curvature of
\nabla^{\rho}
and then taking
boundary values. However, passing to the Ricci curvature, one has to take traces
over different spaces. Fixing
\rho
, we choose a local vector field
\nu
such that

d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
. Then we choose a local frame for
T\partial M
along
\partial M
and extend the elements to vector fields which insert trivially into
d\rho

to obtain a local smooth frame for
T\overline{M}
. This has the property that the dual
coframe consists of
d\rho
and of forms that restrict to the dual coframe of the
initial frame for
T\partial M
. For vector fields
\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
we denote by
\xi,\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
their
restrictions to
\partial M
. Denoting by
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}_{\partial}
the Ricci curvature of
the boundary connection and by
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}
the boundary value of the Ricci curvature
of the interior connection, the difference

\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)-\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}_{\partial}(\xi,\zeta)
clearly is the boundary value of

d\rho(R(\nu,\tilde{\xi})(\tilde{\zeta}))
.

Now we can apply
d\rho
to the defining equation for

R(\nu,\tilde{\xi})(\tilde{\zeta})
, then use (
2.8
) and drop terms that are

\mathcal{O}(\rho)
to get

\nu\cdot d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\zeta})-\tilde{\xi}\cdot d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta})-[\nu,\tilde{\xi}]\cdot d\rho(\tilde{\zeta}).

Next, for the first two summands, we can again use (
2.8
), but have to carry
out the differentiation before dropping
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
-terms. Up to terms that are
obviously
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, this gives

\nu\cdot\tilde{\xi}\cdot d\rho(\tilde{\zeta})+d\rho(\nu)(\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta})-\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta}))\\
-\tilde{\xi}\cdot\nu\cdot d\rho(\tilde{\zeta})-d\rho(\tilde{\xi})(\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta})-\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta}))-[\nu,\tilde{\xi}]\cdot d\rho(\tilde{\zeta}).

Of course the three derivative terms cancel by definition of the Lie bracket, and

d\rho(\tilde{\xi})
is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, so this part can be dropped, too. Since

d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
and by our assumption
\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta})
vanishes along
the boundary, we conclude that

\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)-\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}_{\partial}(\xi,\zeta)=\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{n}\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)

and hence
\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}_{\partial}(\xi,\zeta)=\frac{n-1}{n}\operatorname{Ric}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)
which
exactly implies that the resulting Schouten tensors agree.
∎

2.10.
Relation to interior tractors

While considerations about
tractors inspired many of the developments in this article, it turned out that
technically the weighted b-tangent bundle offers a much simpler
approach that leads to stronger results. Thus we will keep the
discussion of tractors rather short, but we believe that the relation
of tractors to the b-tangent bundle is of independent interest.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, a projectively pre-compact affine
connection
\nabla
on
M
defines a projective structure on
M
,
which makes tools like tractor bundles and tractor connections
available. As mentioned in Section
2.3
, one cannot expect that
this projective structure admits a smooth extension to all of

\overline{M}
. However, we have already seen there that some of these tools

do
extend to the boundary. In particular, the standard
cotractor bundle can be defined as
J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1)
on all of
\overline{M}

and the jet exact sequence shows that it has the right composition
structure on all of
\overline{M}
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.4
, for the
linear connection
\nabla^{\rho}
on
TM
associated to a defining
function
\rho
, the induced connection on
\mathcal{E}(1)
extends
smoothly to the boundary. Since such a connection splits the jet exact
sequence, also the splittings of the standard cotractor bundle coming
from a defining function are smooth up to the boundary.

Since general tractor bundles can be obtained by tensorial constructions from the
standard cotractor bundle, all these bundles and their composition series and
splittings extend to all of
\overline{M}
. In particular, we obtain an analog of the
standard tractor bundle on all of
\overline{M}
as
\mathcal{T}\overline{M}:=(J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1))^{*}
and this
comes with a short exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{E}(-1)\to\mathcal{T}\overline{M}\to T\overline{M}(-1)\to 0

and a splitting of this sequence associated to any defining function
\rho
. In
particular, any section of
\mathcal{T}\overline{M}
projects to a weighted vector field of
weight
-1
. Hence we can define a subsheaf
\Gamma_{\partial}(\mathcal{T}\overline{M})\subset\Gamma(\mathcal{T}\overline{M})
as by requiring that this projection is tangent to

\partial M
along
\partial M
. As for the tangent bundle, this gives rise to a
b-tractor bundle
\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M}
, which comes with an exact sequence as above, but
with
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
replacing
T\overline{M}(-1)
.

Next, we can consider the formula for the standard tractor connection

\nabla^{\mathcal{T}}
in the splittings determined by a defining function,
which is mentioned in the proof of Theorem
2.13
, see

[
4
]
for details. Apart from tensorial operations, this only
involves the connection
\nabla^{\rho}
and its Schouten tensor

\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}
. Assuming that
\nabla
has totally geodesic boundary,
we can apply this formula over
M
, to conclude that for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{T}\overline{M})
, the section
\nabla^{\mathcal{T}}_{\xi}s
of
\mathcal{T}\overline{M}|_{M}
admits a smooth extension to the boundary if

\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
or
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M})
. Hence
the tractor connection restricts to well defined operations on
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})\times\Gamma(\mathcal{T}\overline{M})
and
\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})\times\Gamma(\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M})
. However, for the second operation, the values do not lie
in
\Gamma(\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M})
, so it seems that one does
not
get a
connection on
\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M}
.

As we have seen in Proposition
2.3
, a nonzero section
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1)|_{M})
that is parallel for
\nabla
extends by zero to a defining density for

\partial M
and hence determines a global, nowhere vanishing section
I=j^{1}\sigma
of

J^{1}(\mathcal{E}(1))
. This spans a smooth line subbundle in
J^{1}(\mathcal{E})
, whose
annihilator is a smooth hyperplane bundle
I^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{T}\overline{M}
. Since
\sigma
is
a defining density, it follows readily that any section of
I^{\perp}
projects onto a
weighted vector field that is tangent to
\partial M
along
\partial M
. This shows
that we can actually view
I^{\perp}
as a subbundle of
\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M}
.

The applications of tractors in
[
9
]
(where the projective structure
admits a smooth extension to the boundary), which are relevant here, require that
I

is parallel for the tractor connection. This is equivalent to
\nabla
being Ricci
flat, so in particular, the boundary is strongly totally geodesic. Since
I
is
parallel, also the subbundle
I^{\perp}
is parallel and hence inherits a connection,
and it turns out that
I^{\perp}
and this connection restrict to the projective
standard tractor bundle on
\partial M
and its normal tractor connection. This
approach can be generalized directly to the projectively pre-compact case via the
operator
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})\times\Gamma(J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1))\to\Gamma(J^{1}\mathcal{E}(1))
obtained
by dualizing the corresponding operation on
\mathcal{T}\overline{M}
.

However, this can also be easily deduced from Theorem
2.13
(which works under
much weaker assumptions) as follows. One easily verifies that the projection
\mathcal{T}^{b}\overline{M}\to T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
restricts to an isomorphism
I^{\perp}\to T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
,
which over
M
is just the inverse of the splitting induced by the connection

\nabla
. Moreover, if
\nabla
is Ricci-flat then, in this splitting, the normal
tractor connection is simply given by
\nabla
, which completes the argument.

3.
Projectively pre-compact metrics

We now turn to the case of projectively pre-compact pseudo-Riemannian metrics, which
is of interest for applications to general relativity. The main topic here is the
relation of projective pre-compactness to asymptotic forms of the
metric. One of these forms is only available locally around boundary points in a
dense open subset and plays an important role in general relativity. The second
asymptotic form is available everywhere and to our knowledge is new.

3.1.
An asymptotic form

In our usual setting
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, we want to study a certain asymptotic
form for a pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
on
M
. The basic setup is that for some
function
C
, with properties to be specified later, and an appropriately chosen local
defining function
\rho
, we assume that

(3.1)

h:=\rho^{2}(g-\frac{C}{\rho^{2}}d\rho^{2})

admits a smooth extension to the boundary such that the restriction of the boundary
value to
T\partial M\times T\partial M
is non-degenerate. This means that then
g

has the asymptotic form

(3.2)

g=\frac{C}{\rho^{4}}d\rho^{2}+\frac{h}{\rho^{2}}.

Observe that such a form
does
depend on the choice of the defining function

\rho
. For
\rho=e^{-f}\hat{\rho}
we get
d\rho=e^{-f}(d\hat{\rho}-\rho df)
and
inserting this, we see that we will get terms involving the symmetric product of

d\hat{\rho}
and
df
, which are
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3})
and cannot be absorbed into
C

unless
df=d\hat{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. On the other hand, a constant rescaling of
\rho

leads to a constant rescaling of
h
and
C
, so one may absorb constant multiples of

C
into a change of defining function.

The basic assumption will be that
C
equals a non-zero constant along
the boundary, and this constant can be assumed to be
\pm 1
. Moreover, it has been shown in
[
9
]
that if
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
and the asymptotic form (
3.2
) is available
locally around each boundary point, then
g
is projectively compact
of order
1
. It has also been shown in
[
9
]

that for any pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
which is projectively
compact of order
1
, such an asymptotic form is available locally
around appropriate parts of the boundary, which form a dense open
subset of the boundary. For applications in general relativity it turns out to
be of fundamental importance to allow that
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
in
order to allow for a non-trivial mass aspect, see

[
3
]
. We now show that such weaker fall-off conditions on
the non-constant part of
C
imply projective pre-compactness.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, suppose that
g
is a pseudo-Riemannian
metric on
M
such that locally around each
x\in\partial M
, there
is a defining function
\rho
and a nowhere vanishing function
C

such that the section
h
defined in (
3.1
) admits a smooth
extension to the boundary, whose boundary value is non-degenerate on

T\partial M\times T\partial M
. Suppose further that the function
C

has the property that for each vector field
\zeta
that is smooth up
to the boundary and such that
d\rho(\zeta)\equiv 0
, we have

\zeta\cdot C=O(\rho)
. Then
g
is projectively pre-compact with
totally geodesic boundary. If we even have
\zeta\cdot C=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
for any
\zeta
as above, then
g
is projectively compact
(of order
1
).

Proof.

Apart from the part asserting that the boundary is totally geodesic,
this follows the analogous proof of Theorem 2.6 of
[
9
]
,
which, in particular, proves the last statement and actually implies
parts of our more general result. Nevertheless, we repeat parts of
that proof to ensure that the stronger assumptions on
C
imposed in

[
9
]
were not used implicitly. First
observe that it suffices to verify the defining conditions for
projective pre-compactness for the connection
\nabla^{\rho}

associated to the defining function for which the asymptotic form is
available. In particular, we have the tensor field
h
at our
disposal, and restricting to a sufficiently small neighborhood of
the boundary, we may assume that
h
is non-degenerate on

\ker(d\rho)\times\ker(d\rho)
everywhere. As in
[
9
]
, this
implies that there is an analog of a Reeb field,
i.e. a vector field
\zeta_{0}
such that
d\rho(\zeta_{0})\equiv 1
and
such that
h(\zeta_{0},\zeta)\equiv 0
for
\zeta\in\ker(d\rho)
.

We can extend
\zeta_{0}
to a local frame by vector fields
\zeta_{i}
in
\ker(d\rho)

and then consider the frame
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}
for
\ker(d\rho)
dual to
\{\zeta_{i}\}

with respect to
h|_{\ker(d\rho)}
. For a vector field
\xi
, we
then get
\xi=d\rho(\xi)\zeta_{0}+\sum_{i}h(\xi,\tilde{\zeta}_{i})\zeta_{i}
, so to prove that

\xi
admits a smooth extension to the boundary, it suffices to prove that the
functions
d\rho(\xi)
and
h(\xi,\zeta)
for
\zeta\in\ker(d\rho)
admit smooth
extensions.

Now the asymptotic form (
3.2
) readily implies that on
M
, we get

g(\eta,\zeta_{0})=d\rho(\eta)\left(\frac{C}{\rho^{4}}+\frac{h(\zeta_{0},\zeta_{0})}{\rho^{2}}\right),

so to show that
d\rho(\xi)
admits a smooth extension, it suffices to prove that

\rho^{4}g(\xi,\zeta_{0})
is smooth up to the boundary. Likewise, if
\zeta
lies in

\ker(d\rho)
, the asymptotic form shows that in the interior

g(\xi,\zeta)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}h(\xi,\zeta)
, so to show that
h(\xi,\zeta)
is smooth up
to the boundary, it suffices to prove that
\rho^{2}g(\xi,\zeta)
has this property.

To apply this to a vector field of the form
\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
, one uses the
Koszul formula to express
\nabla_{\xi}\eta
to conclude that, on
M
,

2g(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta,\zeta)
can, for any vector field
\zeta
, be written as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\xi\cdot g(\eta,\zeta)-\zeta\cdot g(\xi,\eta)+\eta\cdot g(\xi,\zeta)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle g([\xi,\eta],\zeta)-g([\xi,\zeta],\eta)-g([\eta,\zeta],\xi)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\tfrac{2}{\rho}d\rho(\xi)g(\eta,\zeta)+\tfrac{2}{\rho}d\rho(\eta)g(\xi,\zeta).

Now the asymptotic form for
g
readily implies that we can write
\xi\cdot g(\eta,\zeta)
as

-4\rho^{-5}Cd\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)d\rho(\zeta)+\rho^{-4}\xi\cdot(Cd\rho(\eta)d\rho(\zeta))+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3}).

Putting
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
and applying this to all three terms in the first line in
(
3.3
), the
\rho^{-5}
-terms cancel with the last line of (
3.3
)
and we can write (
3.3
) in this case as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\rho^{-4}

\displaystyle\left(\xi\cdot(Cd\rho(\eta))-\zeta_{0}\cdot(Cd\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)+\eta\cdot(Cd\rho(\xi))\right)+

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle g([\xi,\eta],\zeta)-g([\xi,\zeta],\eta)-g([\eta,\zeta],\xi)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3}).

Obviously, all terms in this formula are
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-4})
, so we conclude

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
always admits a smooth extension to the boundary
(regardless of any fall off conditions on derivatives of
C
).

Assuming, in addition, that
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we have

Cd\rho(\eta)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and this remains true after differentiation
by
\xi
and similarly for the last term in the first line of
(
3.4
). Likewise,
Cd\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
and
differentiating in the direction
\zeta_{0}
, the result is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, so
the whole first line of (
3.4
) is
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3})
in this
case. For
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we get

[\xi,\eta]\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and so, in each of the terms in the
second line of (
3.4
), one of the vector fields that is
inserted into
g
lies in
X_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. By the asymptotic form
of
g
, all these terms thus also are
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3})
, so for

\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
we see that

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta)
is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, so the boundary is
totally geodesic.

We next analyze (
3.3
) in the case that
d\rho(\zeta)\equiv 0
. Then

g(\eta,\zeta)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}h(\eta,\zeta)
for any
\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
and
differentiating this in the direction
\xi
, we obtain

-2\rho^{-3}d\rho(\xi)h(\eta,\zeta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2}).

Applying this to the first
and the last term in the first line of (
3.3
), there is again a cancellation
with the terms in the last line, while the first summand in the second line is

\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})
. Overall, we conclude that, for the case of
d\rho(\zeta)\equiv 0
,
(
3.3
) can be written as

(3.5)

-\zeta\cdot g(\xi,\eta)-g([\xi,\zeta],\eta)-g([\eta,\zeta],\xi)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2}).

Now
g([\xi,\zeta],\eta)=\tfrac{C}{\rho^{4}}d\rho([\xi,\zeta])d\rho(\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})
, and
dd\rho(\xi,\zeta)=0

and
d\rho(\zeta)=0
imply
d\rho([\xi,\zeta])=-\zeta\cdot d\rho(\xi)
, and like-wise for
the term with
\xi
and
\eta
exchanged. On the other hand, the
first term in (
3.5
) gives

-\zeta\cdot\left(\rho^{-4}Cd\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})\right).

By assumption
\zeta\cdot\rho\equiv 0
, so we may pull out the factor
\rho^{-4}
in
the first summand, while the second summand will remain to be
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})

after differentiation by
\zeta
. Moreover, the the terms in which
\zeta

differentiates
d\rho(\xi)
respectively
d\rho(\eta)
cancel with the contributions
from above. Hence we conclude that if
d\rho(\zeta)\equiv 0
, then without any
assumption on
\xi
and
\eta
, (
3.5
) equals

-\rho^{-4}(\zeta\cdot C)d\rho(\xi)d\rho(\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2}).

Now by assumption
\zeta\cdot C=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, so this sum is always
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-3})
and it is
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})
if either
\xi
or
\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Hence
h(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta,\zeta)=\rho h(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta,\zeta)
is smooth up to the boundary and if either
\xi
or

\eta
lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
then
h(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta,\zeta)
is smooth
up to the boundary, which completes the proof that
\nabla^{\rho}
is projectively
pre-compact.

If we even have
\zeta\cdot C=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
, then the whole expression is
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{-2})
for any
\xi
and
\eta
, which shows that
h(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta,\zeta)

is always smooth up to the boundary and hence
g
is projectively compact.
∎

3.2.
The boundary structure

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
consider a pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
on
M
which
admits an asymptotic form (
3.2
) and let
\operatorname{Ric}
be the Ricci curvature of

g
, which is just a constant multiple of the Schouten tensor of the Levi-Civita
connection
\nabla
of
g
. Then by Theorems
3.1
and
2.9
and
Corollary
2.10
, we know that
\rho\operatorname{Ric}
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary and
\operatorname{Ric}(\xi,\eta)
itself extends smoothly if both
\xi
and
\eta
lie
in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

To proceed towards a converse of Theorem
3.1
, we have to
impose a slightly stronger assumption on the Ricci curvature of
g
to
be able to apply the results form Section
2
. Our standing
assumption from now on will be the following.

Definition 3.2
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, we call a projectively pre-compact pseudo-Riemannian
metric
g
on
M
with Ricci curvature
\operatorname{Ric}

asymptotically tangentially
Ricci flat
if and only if
\operatorname{Ric}(\xi,\eta)
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary if one of the fields lies in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
and vanishes along
the boundary if both fields lie in
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
.

Observe that this property implies that the boundary is strongly totally geodesic, so
we can apply all the developments of Section
2
to the Levi-Civita connection

\nabla
of
g
. Our first result provides a strong additional geometric structure on
the boundary, which, even in the projectively compact case, was only known under the
assumption that
g
is Ricci-flat. Note that as a density
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))

that is parallel for
\nabla
over
M
, we can take the appropriate power of the
volume density of
g
here. We call this the
volume scale
.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
let
g
be a projectively pre-compact
pseudo-Riemannian metric of signature
(p,q)
on
M
, which is
asymptotically tangentially Ricci flat, and let
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))

denote the volume scale.
Then the smooth section

\sigma^{2}g\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}M(2))
admits a smooth extension to a section of

S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
over all of
\overline{M}
.

Moreover, the boundary value of this extension is parallel for the connection induced
by
\nabla
and non-degenerate as a bundle metric on
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
. Hence it provides a reduction of the projective holonomy of the projective
structure on
\partial M
obtained in Proposition
2.6
to the group

SO(p,q)\subset SL(n+1,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Since
g\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}M)
and
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}(1))
are parallel for the connection
induced by
\nabla
, so is
\sigma^{2}g\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}M(2))
. But we can view this as a
section of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
defined over
M
. By Theorem
2.12
,
\nabla

smoothly extends to a linear connection on
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
which which in turn induces
a linear connection on the symmetric square of the dual, i.e. on

S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
. Of course,
\sigma^{2}g
is parallel for this connection over
M
,
so it extends by parallel transport to a smooth section of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
over
all of
\overline{M}
.

Since our connection is induced by a linear connection on

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
, the non-degeneracy of
\sigma^{2}g
as a bundle metric on

T^{b}M(-1)
continues to hold for the extension. (View the section as a
bundle map
T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)\to(T^{b}\overline{M}(-1))^{*}
. If the extension would
be non-injective in some boundary point, one could parallely transport
a non-zero element in the kernel to obtain a contradiction to
injectivity in the interior. Indeed the signature is also preserved.)
Hence the boundary value is a non-degenerate bundle metric on

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
, which is parallel for the connection
induced by
\nabla
. Under the identification from Theorem

2.13
, this corresponds to a bundle metric on the projective
standard tractor bundle which is parallel for the normal tractor
connection and hence provides the claimed holonomy reduction.
∎

Via the general theory of holonomy reductions of Cartan connections
developed in
[
12
]
, this has remarkable consequences. The
case at hand has been discussed in detail in the literature, see
Section 3.1 of
[
12
]
, Section 3.2 of
[
11
]
and Section
3.5 of
[
9
]
. The holonomy reduction decomposes
\partial M
into a disjoint union as
\partial M=\partial M_{+}\sqcup\partial M_{0}\sqcup\partial M_{-}
of so-called
curved orbits
. Here

\partial M_{\pm}
are open subsets of
\partial M
, while
\partial M_{0}
(if non-empty) is an embedded hypersurface which separates these
two open subsets. On
\partial M_{\pm}
the holonomy reduction
determines pseudo-Riemannian Einstein metrics of signature
(p-1,q)

and
(p,q-1)
and positive and negative scalar curvature, respectively, whose
Levi-Civita connections lie in (the
restrictions of) the projective class. On
\partial M_{0}
, one obtains
a conformal structure of signature
(p-1,q-1)
for which the
restriction of the tractor bundle provides the conformal standard
tractor bundle. Making the decomposition into curved orbits explicit
in our situation is easy.

Corollary 3.4
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
g
be a pseudo-Riemannian metric
on
M
, which is projectively pre-compact and asymptotically
tangentially Ricci-flat. For a defining function
\rho
, choose a
vector field
\nu
such that
d\rho(\nu)|_{\partial M}\equiv 1
. Then the
2
-density
\rho^{2}\sigma^{2}g(\nu,\nu)
defined over
M

admits a smooth extension to the boundary.

The boundary value
\tilde{C}
of this density is a smooth section of the oriented
line bundle
\mathcal{E}(2)\to\partial M
, which depends only on
g
and not on the
choice of
\rho
. Moreover, the decomposition
\partial M=\partial M_{+}\sqcup\partial M_{0}\sqcup\partial M_{-}
is according to the strict sign of
\tilde{C}
so
in particular,
\partial M_{0}
is the zero locus for
\tilde{C}
. Finally,
\tilde{C}

is a defining density for
\partial M_{0}
.

Proof.

As we have seen in Section
2.7
, the boundary value of

\rho\nu\in\Gamma(T^{b}\overline{M})
is independent of the choice of
\rho
and spans the kernel
of the projection
T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}\to T\partial M
. It also defines the
inclusion
\mathcal{E}(-1)|_{\partial M}\to T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)|_{\partial M}
which corresponds
to the inclusion of the canonical line bundle into the standard tractor bundle, see
Theorem
2.13
. Hence the density
\tilde{C}
exactly corresponds to the restriction of
the tractor metric to
S^{2}\mathcal{E}(-1)\subset S^{2}\mathcal{T}\partial M
. Now the result
follows from Theorem 3.1 of
[
12
]
.
∎

3.3.
Necessity of asymptotic forms

Now we are ready to prove that projectively pre-compact metrics
which are asymptotically tangentially Ricci flat always admit specific
asymptotic forms. We first provide an asymptotic form that is valid
locally around any boundary point and for any defining function for
the boundary. This is new even in the case of Ricci-flat metrics that
are projectively compact of order one. We also derive a relation
between the boundary values of the coefficients in the
expansion. Finally, we prove that locally around boundary points that
lie in
\partial M_{\pm}
, one can choose specific defining functions
for which the asymptotic form specializes to
(
3.2
). To formulate this, we need a notation
for the symmetric product of one-forms, so we put

(\varphi_{1}\odot\varphi_{2})(\xi,\eta):=\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{1}(\xi)\varphi_{2}(\eta)+\varphi_{1}(\eta)\varphi_{2}(\xi))
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
g
be a pseudo-Riemannian metric of
signature
(p,q)
on
M
which is
projectively pre-compact and asymptotically tangentially Ricci flat, and let
\rho
be
any defining function for
\partial M
.

(1) There is a smooth function
C:\overline{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, a one-form
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})

and a section
h\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M})
such that, over
M
, we have

(3.6)

g=\frac{Cd\rho^{2}}{\rho^{4}}+\frac{\lambda\odot d\rho}{\rho^{3}}+\frac{h}{\rho^{2}}.

(2) Given any asymptotic form as in (
3.6
), let
\underline{C}
be the
boundary value of
C
, and let
\underline{\lambda}\in\Omega^{1}(\partial M)
and

\underline{h}\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\partial M)
the restrictions of the boundary values of

\lambda
and
h
to tangential entries. Then
\underline{C}
is a defining function for

\partial M_{0}
and the symmetric bilinear form on the bundle
T\partial M\oplus\mathbb{R}
, over
\partial M
,
defined by

(3.7)

((\xi,a),(\eta,b))\mapsto\underline{C}ab-\tfrac{1}{2}a\underline{\lambda}(\eta)-\tfrac{1}{2}b\underline{\lambda}(\xi)+\underline{h}(\xi,\eta)

is non degenerate of signature
(p,q)
. Moreover, denoting by
\nabla^{\rho}
the
induced connection on
T\partial M
and by
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}
its Schouten tensor, we get
for
\xi,\eta,\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}=-dC\qquad\underline{h}(\xi,\eta)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\underline{\lambda})(\eta)+\underline{C}\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\underline{h})(\eta,\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\eta)\underline{\lambda}(\zeta)+\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\xi,\zeta)\underline{\lambda}(\eta)\big).

Proof.

We treat (1) first. Fix a vector field
\nu
with
d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
and take the density
\tilde{C}=\rho^{2}\sigma^{2}g(\nu,\nu)
from Corollary
3.4
, which extends smoothly to all
of
\overline{M}
. Since
\frac{d\rho}{\rho}\in\Gamma((T^{b}\overline{M})^{*})
, we see that
\frac{\tilde{C}}{\rho^{2}}d\rho^{2}\in\Gamma(S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2))
, so also
k:=\sigma^{2}g-\frac{\tilde{C}}{\rho^{2}}d\rho^{2}
is a smooth section of this bundle. By construction we get

k(\rho\nu,\rho\nu)\equiv 0
. Putting
\tilde{\lambda}(\xi):=2k(\xi,\rho\nu)
for

\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
defines a smooth section

\tilde{\lambda}\in\Gamma((T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2))
such that
\tilde{\lambda}(\rho\nu)\equiv 0
. Hence from
Section
2.7
we know that
\tilde{\lambda}
actually lies in the subspace

\Gamma(T^{*}\overline{M}(2))
. Then,
\frac{\tilde{\lambda}\odot d\rho}{\rho}
defines a smooth
section of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
, hence also

(3.8)

\tilde{h}:=k-\frac{\tilde{\lambda}\odot d\rho}{\rho}=\sigma^{2}g-\frac{\tilde{C}}{\rho^{2}}d\rho^{2}-\frac{1}{\rho}\tilde{\lambda}\odot d\rho

is a smooth section of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
. The first (defining) equation shows that for

\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, we get
\tilde{h}(\xi,\rho\nu)=0
and using
Section
2.8
we conclude that
\tilde{h}\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M}(2))
.

Putting
\sigma^{\rho}=\frac{\sigma}{\rho}
, we obtain a section of
\mathcal{E}(1)
which is
nowhere vanishing and we define
C:=\frac{1}{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}\tilde{C}
,

\lambda=\frac{1}{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}\tilde{\lambda}
and
h:=\frac{1}{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}\tilde{h}
. Of
course,
C\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
,
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
and

h\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M})
and multiplying (
3.8
) by

\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}
, we exactly get (
3.6
).

For part (2), we suppose now that we have any expression of the form (
3.6
),
say with coefficients
\hat{C}
,
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{h}
and multiply it by

\sigma^{2}=\rho^{2}(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}
. Inserting two copies of
\rho\nu
into the result, the
last two terms are
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
, respectively. This shows that

(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}\hat{C}+\mathcal{O}(\rho)=\tilde{C}
and hence
\hat{C}=C+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. In
particular, the boundary value of
\hat{C}
equals
\underline{C}
. This also shows
that
\frac{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}{\rho}\hat{\lambda}\odot d\rho+(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}\tilde{h}=k+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Inserting
\rho\nu
and
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
into this, we
conclude that
\hat{\lambda}(\xi)+\mathcal{O}(\rho)=\lambda(\xi)
, so the boundary values of

\hat{\lambda}
and
\lambda
agree on vectors tangent to the boundary. In the same way, one
concludes that the boundary values of
\hat{h}
and
h
agree if both their entries
are tangent to the boundary.

Hence it suffices to verify the remaining properties for the the asymptotic form we
have constructed. Since
C=\frac{1}{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}\tilde{C}
, and
\sigma^{\rho}
is nowhere
vanishing, Corollary
3.4
readily implies that
\underline{C}
is a defining
function for
\partial M_{0}
. For the form we have constructed, (
3.7
)
simply encodes the boundary value of
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(\sigma^{\rho})^{2}}g
on

T^{b}\overline{M}|_{\partial M}
in the splitting determined by
\rho
, so non-degeneracy
follows readily.

To prove the relations between the coefficients in the asymptotic form, we work in
more generality than needed here for later use. Putting

\tau^{\rho}:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{\rho}}\in\mathcal{E}(-1)
, we observe that
C=\tilde{C}(\tau^{\rho})^{2}=\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu,\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu)
and the right hand side
is a contraction between a smooth section of
S^{2}(T^{b}\overline{M})^{*}(2)
and two sections of

T^{b}\overline{M}(-1)
. Hence we may differentiate the right hand side using the connection

\nabla
, which admits a smooth extension to the boundary, and use that
\sigma^{2}g
is
parallel for this connection. Hence for any vector field
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, we get

\tilde{\xi}\cdot C=2\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu,\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu).

Now over
M
, we can write

(3.9)

\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu=\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu-\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\xi}=\tau^{\rho}(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu+d\rho(\tilde{\xi})\nu-\tilde{\xi}),

where we used that
\nabla^{\rho}\tau^{\rho}
=0, and observe that the sum of the last two terms inserts trivially in
d\rho
.
Plugging this into the above formula for
\tilde{\xi}\cdot C
and using that
\nu

inserts trivially into
\lambda
and
h
, we see that

(3.10)

\tilde{\xi}\cdot C=2Cd\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu)+\lambda(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu)-\lambda(\tilde{\xi}).

Observe that all terms in the right hand side indeed admit a smooth extension to the
boundary. Now given a vector field
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
, we can extend to

\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\tilde{\xi})\equiv 0
and then
obtain
\xi\cdot\underline{C}
as the boundary value of
\tilde{\xi}\cdot C
. But in
this case,
\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu
is smooth up to the boundary, so the middle
term in the right hand side of (
3.10
) is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Moreover,
\rho(\nu)
is
constant and
\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\nu)
and (by Lemma
2.8
)
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\nu)
admit a smooth
extension to the boundary, so (
2.8
) shows that the first term in the
right hand side is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, too. Hence we get
\xi\cdot\underline{C}=-\underline{\lambda}(\xi)
,
which shows that
\underline{\lambda}=-d\underline{C}
.

For the second identity, we start from a vector field
\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)

and extend it smoothly to
\tilde{\eta}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
such that

d\rho(\tilde{\eta})\equiv 0
. Together with the fact that
\nu
inserts trivially into

\lambda
and
h
, this shows that

\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu,\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\eta})=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\tilde{\eta})
. As
above, we conclude that for any
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
we get

\tilde{\xi}\cdot\lambda(\tilde{\eta})=2\sigma^{2}g(\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu,\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\eta})+2\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\rho\nu,\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\eta}).

Now in the first term we can insert (
3.9
) while in the second term

d\rho(\tilde{\eta})\equiv 0
implies we can replace
\nabla
by
\nabla^{\rho}
and then take

\tau^{\rho}
out of the derivative. This shows that

(3.11)

\tilde{\xi}\cdot\lambda(\tilde{\eta})=d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu)\lambda(\tilde{\eta})+2h(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu,\tilde{\eta})-2h(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})+2\frac{C}{\rho}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})+\lambda(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta}).

By (
2.8
), since
\tilde{\eta}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
, and

d\rho(\tilde{\eta})\equiv 0
,
d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})=\mathcal{O}(\rho)

so all terms in the right hand side admit a smooth extension to the boundary.

As before, we need the formula here only in the case that
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
is an extension of
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
such that

d\rho(\tilde{\xi})\equiv 0
. In this case, the first term in the right hand side of
(
3.11
) is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
by (
2.8
), and the second term is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
since
\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\nu
admits a smooth extension to the
boundary. Finally, (
2.8
) together with
d\rho(\tilde{\eta})\equiv 0
and
the fact that
\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
implies that

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})=\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Hence (
3.11
) implies that

h(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\xi}\cdot\lambda(\eta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})+C\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}).

Passing to boundary values, we obtain the claimed equation for
h
(taking into
account that, from the proof of Theorem
2.13
, we know that the boundary value
of
\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}
is the Schouten tensor of the boundary connection).

For the last identity, we take
\eta,\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
and extend to

\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
which insert trivially into

d\rho
. Then we immediately see that

\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\eta},\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\zeta})=h(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta})
, and as
before we conclude that for
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
, we get

(3.12)

\tilde{\xi}\cdot h(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta})=\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta})+\sigma^{2}g(\tau^{\rho}\tilde{\eta},\tau^{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\zeta}),

where we replaced
\nabla
by
\nabla^{\rho}
as above. Expanding the first term in the
right hand side, we obtain

\tfrac{1}{2\rho}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta})\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})+h(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta}).

and by (
2.8
), we can rewrite the first term as

\frac{1}{2}(\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})-\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}))\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})
. Bringing
the second term to the other side and adding the corresponding term from the other
summand in the right hand side of (
3.12
), the left hand side becomes

\tilde{\xi}\cdot h(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta})-h(\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\zeta})-h(\tilde{\eta},\nabla^{\rho}_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\zeta}).

Now if we specialize to the case that
\tilde{\xi}
is an extension of

\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
which inserts trivially into
d\rho
, then the boundary
value of this of course is
(\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\underline{h})(\eta,\zeta)
. On the other
hand, this implies that
\mbox{{P}}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, and the last
claimed identity follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that even fixing the defining function
\rho
, the coefficients
C
,
\lambda

and
h
in the asymptotic form (
3.6
) are not uniquely determined beyond
the (restricted) boundary values
\underline{C}
,
\underline{\lambda}
and

\underline{h}
. For example, the expression remains unchanged if we replace
\lambda
by

\lambda+fd\rho+\rho\alpha
for
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
and at
the same time replace
C
by
C-\rho f
and
h
by
h-\alpha\odot d\rho
. One way to fix
the coefficients, which we have used implicitly in the proof already, is to choose a
vector field
\nu\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
and then require
that
\lambda(\nu)\equiv 0
and
h(\nu,\ )\equiv 0
.

In particular, this can be applied to the form (
3.2
), where
we see that the question of whether
C\mp 1
is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
or

\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
only makes sense once requires that the cross term

\frac{1}{\rho^{3}}\lambda\odot d\rho
vanishes identically. So this pins
down the coefficients here.

In particular, Theorem
3.5
shows that, for projectively pre-compact metrics, the form (
3.2
)
can only be available on open subsets whose intersection with

\partial M
is either contained in
\partial M_{+}
or in
\partial M_{-}
(corresponding to
C=1+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
or
-1+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
). On
such subsets, one can easily obtain the form (
3.2
) (for an
appropriate defining function) from Theorem
3.5
using the
ideas in Remark
3.6
.

Corollary 3.7
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
g
be a pseudo-Riemannian metric on
M
which is
projectively pre-compact and asymptotically tangentially Ricci flat.

Then locally around each point of
\partial M_{\pm}
, there is a defining function

\hat{\rho}
with respect to which
g
can be expressed as in (
3.2
) with

C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
.

Proof.

Let us start from an expression (
3.6
) for
g
for a defining function

\rho
. Then for the defining function
\hat{\rho}=e^{f}\rho
we get
d\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}df+e^{f}d\rho
and inserting
\rho=e^{-f}\hat{\rho}
and
d\rho=e^{-f}(d\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}df)
into (
3.6
), we see that we get an asymptotic form (
3.6
)
for
g
with respect to
\hat{\rho}
, which starts with

\frac{e^{2f}C}{\hat{\rho}^{4}}d\hat{\rho}^{2}
. Now on the subset where
C
is
non-vanishing, we can choose
f:=-\frac{1}{2}\log(|C|)
and so we get an expression of
the form

(3.13)

\frac{\pm 1}{\hat{\rho}^{4}}d\hat{\rho}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\hat{\rho}^{3}}(\hat{\lambda}\odot d\hat{\rho})+\frac{1}{\hat{\rho}^{2}}\hat{h}.

Moreover, by Theorem
3.5
, the boundary valued of
\hat{\lambda}
vanishes in
tangential directions, so there are
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
such
that
\hat{\lambda}=\varphi d\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\alpha
. This means that (
3.13
) equals

\frac{\pm 1+\rho\varphi}{\hat{\rho}^{4}}d\hat{\rho}^{2}+\frac{1}{\hat{\rho}^{2}}(\hat{h}+\alpha\odot d\hat{\rho}),

so this has the claimed form.
∎

3.4.
Obstructions to projective compactness

We have already identified a complete obstruction to projective compactness in the
general setting of connections in Section
2.3
via the boundary values of

\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\xi}\eta
for
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
. As we have seen there,
it is of the form
d\rho(\xi)|_{\partial M}d\rho(\eta)|_{\partial M}\psi
for a
vector field
\psi\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
. Now for general asymptotic forms
(
3.6
) we can use the computations from the proof of Theorem
3.5

to deduce an equivalent characterization of projective compactness for metrics.

Theorem 3.8
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
g
be a projectively pre-compact pseudo-Riemannian
metric and assume that for some defining function
\rho
and vector field

\nu\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
, we are given an expression
(
3.6
) such that
\lambda(\nu)\equiv 0
and
h(\nu,\ )\equiv 0
. (In particular
the asymptotic form constructed in the proof of Theorem
3.5
has this
property.)

Then
g
is projectively compact if and only if

(3.14)

\nu\cdot C-2Cd\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)

and for any
\tilde{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\tilde{\zeta})\equiv 0
, we get

(3.15)

-\nu\cdot\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})+\lambda(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta})+d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})+2\tfrac{C}{\rho}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta})=\mathcal{O}(\rho).

Observe that the first two terms in (
3.15
) can be written as

-(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\lambda)(\tilde{\zeta})
since
\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\lambda
makes sense as acting
on
\mathfrak{X}_{\partial}(\overline{M})
. Likewise, using (
2.8
), we see that

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)=\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\nu)-\rho\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\nu)
and

\frac{1}{\rho}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta})=\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta})-\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta})
.
Correspondingly, we can interpret (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) as a system of
“asymptotic ODE” on
C\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
and

\lambda\in\Gamma((T^{b}\overline{M})^{*})
of the form

\nu\cdot C=2fC+\mathcal{O}(\rho)\qquad\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\lambda=f\lambda+2C\varphi+\mathcal{O}(\rho),

where
f:=\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\nu)-\rho\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\nu)
and

\varphi(\tilde{\zeta})=\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta})-\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\tilde{\zeta})
.

Proof.

The metric
g
is projectively compact if and only if the boundary value

\psi\in\Gamma(T\partial M)
of
d\rho(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)
vanishes identically. By
the non-degeneracy statement in Theorem
3.5
this is equivalent to vanishing
of
\underline{\lambda}(\psi)
and of
\underline{h}(\psi,\zeta)
for any vector field

\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
. Now we can use formula (
3.10
) from the proof of
Theorem
3.5
(which is valid for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
) in the case

\xi=\nu
to obtain

\lambda(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)=\nu\cdot C-2Cd\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu),

which immediately shows that (
3.14
) is equivalent to vanishing of

\underline{\lambda}(\psi)
.

For (
3.15
), we extend
\zeta
to a vector field
\tilde{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})

such that
d\rho(\tilde{\zeta})\equiv 0
. Then we use formula (
3.11
) from the
proof of Theorem
3.5
(which is valid for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
) in the
case
\xi=\nu
to obtain

-h(\rho\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu,\tilde{\zeta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\cdot\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})+\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta})+\tfrac{1}{2}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)\lambda(\tilde{\zeta})+\frac{C}{\rho}d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\tilde{\zeta}).

Passing to boundary values, (
3.15
) is evidently equivalent to vanishing
of
\underline{h}(\psi,\zeta)
for any
\zeta\in\mathfrak{X}(\partial M)
.
∎

So let us pass to the question of projective compactness of a metric of the form
(
3.2
). As originally proved in
[
9
]
and reproved in Theorem

3.1
above,
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
is sufficient for projective
compactness. The asymptotic form (
3.2
) plays a prominent role in general
relativity, going back at least to
[
3
]
. There the
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
-term of
C\mp 1
is referred to as the mass aspect and it plays a crucial
role in the applications to GR. So in that sense, projective compactness is
incompatible with mass, which has also been pointed out in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. From
this point of view, it would be desirable to prove that for a metric of the form
(
3.2
) projective compactness forces
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
. Our last
result shows that this is indeed true under slightly stronger assumptions on the
asymptotics of the Ricci curvature of
g
(which should be satisfied in the cases
relevant for GR which mostly deal with Ricci-flat metrics).

Theorem 3.9
.

For
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
, let
g
be a projectively pre-compact
pseudo-Riemannian metric with an asymptotic form (
3.2
).
Assume further that its Ricci curvature admits a
smooth extension to the boundary and the boundary value of this curvature vanishes if
one of the entries is tangent to the boundary.

Then
g
is projectively compact if and only if
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
.

Proof.

It remains to show that projective compactness of
g
forces
C=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})
, so we assume that
g
is projectively compact. We cannot directly use
the computations from Theorem
3.5
here, since in the case of
(
3.2
), we cannot say anything about
h(\nu,\ )
for a vector field

\nu\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
. It would be easy to redo
these computations in the current setting, but we can also modify the asymptotic
form along the lines of Remark
3.6
and then use Theorem
3.8
.

Starting from (
3.2
) for a defining function
\rho
, we fix
\nu\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
such that
d\rho(\nu)\equiv 1
. Define
\varphi:=h(\nu,\nu)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M},\mathbb{R})
and for
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\overline{M})
consider

\alpha(\xi):=2(h(\nu,\xi)-d\rho(\xi)\varphi)
. This clearly
defines
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{M})
such that
\alpha(\nu)\equiv 0
and we define
\hat{h}\in\Gamma(S^{2}T^{*}\overline{M})
by
\hat{h}:=h-\alpha\odot d\rho-\varphi d\rho^{2}
.
Then
\hat{h}(\nu,\eta)=h(\eta,\nu)-\varphi d\rho(\eta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha(\eta)=0
. Thus (
3.2
) can be rewritten as

g=\tfrac{C+\rho^{2}\varphi}{\rho^{4}}d\rho^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{3}}(\rho\alpha)\odot d\rho+\hat{h},

and by construction
\nu
inserts trivially into
\rho\alpha
and into
\hat{h}
. By Theorem
3.8
, projective compactness implies that

\nu\cdot(C+\rho^{2}\varphi)=2Cd\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)+\mathcal{O}(\rho),

and of course the left hand side equals
\nu\cdot C+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. As we have
noted after Theorem
3.5
,

d\rho(\nabla^{\rho}_{\nu}\nu)=\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\nu)-\rho\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\nu)
. By projective
compactness
\nabla^{\rho}
admits a smooth extension to the boundary and hence so does
its Schouten-tensor so
\rho\mbox{{P}}^{\rho}(\nu,\nu)=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. But by our stronger
curvature assumptions also
\mbox{{P}}(\nu,\nu)
admits a smooth extension to the boundary
and hence
\nu\cdot C=\mathcal{O}(\rho)
. Since for
C=\pm 1+\rho f
, we get
\nu\cdot C=f+\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, this completes the proof.
∎
