Abstract:

When particles are very small, down to the nanometer length scale, they display unusual properties not
typical of larger materials. Most of the atoms in these structures are at the surface of the particles, which
give them different electronic properties. While these unusual properties have been studied in most of the
periodic table, little is known about how the electronic properties of the actinides, such as neptunium and
plutonium, behave when they become so small. This planned research will study growth pathways of
actinide nanoscale particles. Interestingly, the shapes of the particles change depending on the specific
actinide species, spanning from thorium through americium, despite having otherwise identical
arrangements of atoms. This project will use differences in the shapes and growth pathways of different
actinide particles to learn about trends in electronic properties at surfaces across the actinide row. Growth
pathways will be determined using a combination of synthesis and the use of advanced X-ray
characterization tools. These X-ray characterization tools can provide information about electronic
properties and also local bonding characteristics. This project will also inform how to make unprecedented
actinide oxide nanoparticles using very small quantities of material. This will enable safe working with
radioactive materials and the study of materials that never before have been made into nanoparticles.
Meanwhile, students will be trained in working with these exotic materials.

Overview:

This project was started in July 2024 and completed in July 2025. Although the scope of the project required
five full years for completion, the PI, Dr. Moreau, moved institutions from Washington State University to
the University of Wyoming, in order to establish a trans-uranium nuclear research program at the University
of Wyoming as a Nuclear Faculty Fellow. This prompted a closeout of the award at Washington State
University. Upon closeout, Dr. Moreau will submit a new application in order to complete the proposed
work at the University of Wyoming. Despite the project being only open for one year, two peer reviewed
journal articles are currently in progress and expected to be published within the next calendar year,1,2 and
two conference presentations based on work on the project. Work is ongoing towards training undergraduate
and graduate students and a postdoctoral fellow towards achieving project objectives at the University of
Wyoming.

Project Objectives and Hypotheses:

The overarching objective of the proposal was to determine the mechanisms behind actinide nanocrystal
growth pathways in order to elucidate periodic trends in surface chemistry attributes and bonding
characteristics across the 5f row, with deliverables including 1) methods to synthesize actinide oxide
nanoparticles (including for trans-uranium elements) with precise size and shape control, 2) insight into the
periodic trends in actinide surface bonding behavior and reactivity, and 3) determination of actinide
nanoparticle electronic structure as a function of increasing 5f occupancy.

In order to achieve the overarching objective, additional objectives were identified: 1) Determine how
increasing 5f occupancy alters actinide oxide nanoparticle morphology. It was hypothesized that differing
electronic structure inherent to each actinide element would influence the morphology of actinide dioxide
nanoparticles. 2) Establish periodic trends in local and long-range crystallographic ordering of actinide
oxides at the nanoscale. It was hypothesized that lowering of the 5f energy state across the actinide row
increases actinide oxide nanoparticle crystallographic complexity. 3) Interrogate the kinetics and
mechanisms behind anisotropic actinide oxide nanoparticle growth. It was hypothesized that actinide redox
properties, in conjunction with surface binding chemistry, play a substantial role in nanoparticle growth
pathways and kinetics of formation. 4) Resolve periodic trends in actinide oxide surface electronic structure
and bonding characteristics. It was hypothesized that periodic trends in actinide oxide surface electronic
structure could be determined from differences in ligand binding characteristics. and 5) Educate and train
undergraduate and graduate students in actinide chemistry and advanced X-ray characterization. It was
hypothesized that increasing undergraduate and graduate student participation in actinide chemistry
research would lead to increased personnel with a variety of backgrounds in the U.S. nuclear workforce.

Accomplishments:

According to the project timeline, research tasks for year 1 (focused on uranium and thorium chemistry),
which is the only project year completed at the time of this final report, included:

a) Developing a high throughput setup for nanoparticle synthesis in microscale quantities. Graduate
students William Vance and Christian Wentzell designed, tested and improved an aluminum heating block
for high-throughput synthesis of actinide oxide nanoparticles. With a heating block and specialized
disposable vials with crimp-cap tops, positive pressure reactions can be run inside of a glovebox.
Specifically, this would be used in a negative pressure glovebox, which is required for trans-uranium
chemistry. The heating block features a hole for a thermocouple, which would go halfway into the block,
allowing for more accurate and consistent control of temperature than a heating mantle on a conventional
Schlenk line would afford. The tolerance on each of the slots was adjusted to snugly fit the disposable vials,
with the vial going 2/3 of the way into the block so that the solution is fully within the slot. A picture of our
final heating block design is shown below in Figure 1a.

Using a small conical vial under positive pressure in the glovebox in a commercial heating block, a reaction
to make uranium dioxide nanoparticles had previously been used by our group to make nanoparticles using
1 mg of uranium-containing precursor, when normally ~200 mg of precursor would be used (Figure 1b).
Using the heating block setup, this has been further scaled down to < 0.1 mg of uranium-containing
precursor, as shown in Figure 1c. Purification via conventional centrifugation methods proves challenging
in that a visual pellet cannot be easily collected at this scale, however it has been confirmed that
nanoparticles are still achieved at this scale. Overall, use of <0.1 mg of precursor proves that our
nanoparticle synthesis methods can be extendable to trans-uranium elements, where scarcity of material
and higher levels of radioactivity than for uranium and thorium necessitate microscale synthesis methods.
To date, organic-phase solution nanoparticle methods have not been scaled down to this level, making the
methodology developed also widely impactful to the nanoparticle community at large. The capability to run

64 reactions at once lends itself to high throughput synthesis, where all 64 reactions use less than one would
use via conventional Schlenk-line based methods. Our team has used our system to systematically test

d) Eliminating and varying reactant concentrations to determine roles of reactants. Investigation into the
synthesis of thorium oxide nanorods, as conducted by graduate student William Vance, showed that nanorod
growth is mostly controlled by the amine group and concentration of the amine in solution. If the solvent is
eliminated, and the amine concentration increased, the length of the nanorod increases to more closely form
thorium oxide nanowires. This result is closely aligned with work that was previously pursued by graduate
student Natalie Yaw, which showed that changing the solvent chemistry could induce anisotropic growth in
uranium oxides. The role of varying synthetic reagents in the synthesis of thorium oxide nanorods is
currently being prepared for publication, and is expected to be published within the next calendar year.1

e) Using transmission electron microscopy and small angle X-ray scattering to explore reaction kinetics
and morphological changes. Graduate student William Vance explored the growth of thorium dioxide
nanorods over time (Figure 3). Interestingly, he found that very early in the reaction, nanorods have already
formed, but at a lower yield and lower aspect ratio than their final products. This is in contrast to most
anisotropic growth formation mechanisms that are known in literature, where the yield is essentially the
same throughout, and the particles simply grow and transform at the same time. Additionally, the width of
the nanorods remains largely consistent throughout, suggesting that growth only occurs on the nanorod tips.
This result suggests that the amine chemistry is highly dependent on the exposed surface facet, limiting
growth in one direction and only allowing growth on the tips. We are currently performing investigations

Undergraduates Simon Scheel and John Hewitt were trained in the organic phase synthesis of uranium
oxide nanoparticles. Simon Scheel became independent in his work with the synthesis and also learned how
to collect and analyze X-ray scattering and X-ray diffraction data. John worked closely with graduate
student Christian Wentzell, and learned how to work with radioactive samples, as well as best synthetic and
safety practices. Graduate students William Vance (third year) and Christian Wentzell (second year) were

trained on the synthesis and characterization of uranium and thorium oxide nanoparticles. William Vance
also learned protocols to study reactions in timecourse, by transferring the growth solution into a cold flask.
Both students were trained in the preparation and measurement of radioactive samples using electron
microscopy, X-ray diffraction and X-ray scattering. They also learned how to make radioactive molecular
precursors and how to handle highly air-sensitive samples. William Vance was also trained in collecting X-
ray spectroscopy data at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource. All students were trained in
National Laboratory protocols for working with radioactive material. Dr. Moreau, coming from the National
Laboratory system herself, trained and tested all student workers using the equivalent of RWT II training
at DOE laboratories. In using National Laboratory protocols early, the students would be well positioned to
pursue work at the National Lab. Undergraduate student Simon Scheel performed a summer internship in
Nuclear Forensics at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory during summer 2024.

In addition to training Moreau group researchers, Dr. Moreau also trained researchers in Skye Fortier’s
group at the University of Texas – El Paso in analysis of X-ray spectroscopy data. She conducted weekly
training sessions with Fortier group students via zoom, in order to translate knowledge of data workup to
the Fortier group, such that they could analyze data on their own. Work from these sessions resulted in a
publication regarding preparation of radioactive air-sensitive samples for X-ray spectroscopy data
collection at DOE synchrotron sources, which will greatly benefit the radiological community.3 This work
will also enhance the project herein, which involves measurement of radioactive, air-sensitive samples, and
the method developed will be used for measurement moving forward.

Continuation of Work:

Dr. Moreau moved to a new institution, the University of Wyoming, in summer 2025. This move presented
the opportunity to engage more fully in teaching nuclear science, being part of the new Nuclear Energy
Research Center. Dr. Moreau’s hire also enabled the establishment of a Nuclear Certificate Program, which
is available to both undergraduate and graduate students. Dr. Moreau developed and taught a course on the
nuclear fuel cycle during Fall 2025, which was attended by 9 undergraduate students and 7 graduate
students. The popularity of the course and its incorporation into the certificate program led to its adoption
into the regular curriculum. Dr. Moreau is currently scheduled to teach this course every fall. Students in
the Moreau lab are encouraged to participate in the certificate program, and will be trained in the nuclear
fuel cycle, nuclear power systems, nuclear physics, nuclear materials, nuclear forensics, and nuclear policy.
This will greatly enhance the preparation of students towards understanding nuclear science. In particular,
Dr. Moreau’s course teaches topics of relevance to this proposal, including actinide chemistry and working
with radioactive materials. Dr. Moreau provides surrogate RWT II training to all students who take the
course. The preparation in this course led one undergraduate student, Ryan Olsen, to seek employment in
the national laboratory system, and he is now gainfully employed as a technician at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory. Two other undergraduate students, Jackson Fouras and Amber Ordiway, chose to join the
Moreau research group, and Jackson will be continuing with the Moreau group as a PhD student in Fall
2026, with plans to work on trans-uranium chemistry through this project.

Dr. Moreau has built a trans-uranium laboratory at the University of Wyoming, having moved her custom-
designed nuclear glovebox to a laboratory already equipped to work with trans-uranium materials. This will
facilitate a swift transition into Neptunium, Plutonium and Americium chemistry. The Moreau group
currently has four PhD students, two undergraduate researchers, a visiting researcher from Colby College
and a postdoctoral fellow. Each of these group members is being trained in advanced X-ray characterization
tools and actinide chemistry, preparing them for work with trans-uranium elements and synthesis of
actinide-based nanomaterials. It should be noted in particular that the postdoctoral fellow, Dr. Dominic
Russo, was previously working at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and previously funded through
DOE BES HEC, the same program that funded this project. The translation of his skills with trans-uranium

chemistry and National Laboratory training will assist in training graduate and undergraduate student
researchers in the skills that they require to be successful completing years 2-5 of the project.
Conclusion and Outlook:

The project has been closed at Washington State University after year 1 due to Dr. Moreau’s institutional
move to the University of Wyoming. Progress has been made towards proposal objectives, with a specific
focus in year 1 on Uranium and Thorium chemistry, as students were being trained to work with trans-
uranium elements. Each of the tasks from year 1 were outlined above in the context of Uranium and
Thorium chemistry and substantial progress has been made towards each task. The work from year 1 of the
proposal has resulted in the preparation of two manuscripts, one on the mechanisms behind the anisotropic
growth of thorium oxide nanoparticles and another on the high throughput system developed for the high-
throughput microscale synthesis of actinide oxide nanoparticles using our glovebox-compatible heating
block-based system. Both manuscripts should be published within the calendar year. Dr. Moreau has
presented work from year 1 of the proposal at scholarly meetings. Further, the groundwork has been set for
additional publications related to actinide-dependent nanoparticle growth trends, and the start of Np, Pu
and AmO2 nanoparticle synthesis and advanced X-ray characterization of these systems. Most importantly,
undergraduate and graduate student researchers have been trained to work with radioactive materials
systems, using protocols from the DOE laboratory system. The Moreau group has continued working
towards project objectives at the University of Wyoming, where the stage is now set for beginning trans-
uranium chemistry by summer 2026, with a new submission of this project to facilitate working on
objectives from years 2-5 and beyond.
