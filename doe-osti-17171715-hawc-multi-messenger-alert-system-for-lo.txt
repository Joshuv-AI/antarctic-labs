1 ABSTRACT
The High Altitude Water Cherenkov (HAWC) observatory is a ground-based array in Pico de
Orizaba, México used for detecting Extensive Air Showers (EAS) from TeV gamma rays and cosmic
rays of both galactic and extragalactic origin. HAWC looks for ultra-high-energy (> 100 TeV)
gamma rays in real-time and has an alert system to notify other astrophysics experiments of a high-
quality, ultra-high-energy event to be used for multi-messenger analyses. However, the alert system
is currently biased toward known gamma-ray sources. This project aims to remove this bias and
provide a location-independent gamma-ray alert system for HAWC. With this new alert system, we
identify 59 potentially interesting detection events within a sample of 73,389. Using these newly
alerted events, 19 spatially coincident events are found between HAWC gamma rays and IceCube
neutrinos, 16 of which are newly discovered. We calculate the false positive rate and investigate a few
noteworthy gamma-ray/neutrino pairs. Several coincidences occur near the LHAASO J2108+5157
region, so we perform a joint likelihood analysis of the region, and an extended Gaussian source
with a simple power law spectrum is selected as the best-fit spectral model.

2 INTRODUCTION

2.1 Multi-Messenger Astrophysics
Multi-messenger astrophysics is the process of combining multiple astrophysical signals from the
same event in order to gain a better understanding of it. Analyzing multiple signals such as grav-
itational waves, light, and neutrinos from an astrophysical phenomenon leads to a more complete
picture. A mysterious signal of interest is cosmic rays, which are highly energetic atomic nuclei,
primarily protons, originating from the far reaches of the universe. They are charged particles,
meaning that they take curved paths to Earth through the interstellar medium, making their origin
unknown by the time they arrive here. In addition, these cosmic rays can achieve energies so high
that their acceleration mechanism is unknown, and the highest energy cosmic ray ever detected7
was upwards of 1020 eV. So the question remains, how can we study the environments that pro-
duce such high-energy cosmic rays? The answer is to study neutral particles such as gamma rays
and neutrinos, which point directly from their source and are produced when cosmic rays interact
with their environment. The High Altitude Water Cherenkov (HAWC) gamma-ray observatory is
designed to study some of the most extreme environments in the universe.

2.2 The HAWC Observatory
Gamma rays produce extensive air showers (EAS), mainly consisting of electrons, positrons, and
lower energy photons, when they interact with the Earth’s atmosphere. HAWC uses water Cherenkov
detectors (WCDs) to detect these particles, along with the background from cosmic rays, which pro-
duce similar air showers. HAWC is located in the Pico de Orizaba National Park in México at an
altitude of 4,100 meters above sea level. Its primary detector consists of 300 steel tanks, each hold-
ing ∼180,000 liters of water and 4 photomultiplier tubes (PMTs). These WCDs cover an area of
22,000 m2 . HAWC contains 900 R5912 eight-inch PMTs and 300 Hamamatsu R7081 ten-inch PMTs
which are used to detect the Cherenkov radiation of secondary EAS particles from cosmic rays and
gamma rays4 . The sensitivity of HAWC ranges from ∼300 GeV to above 100 TeV and the duty
cycle is ∼95%.
 HAWC has two independent energy estimators, known as the ground parameter (GP) and neural
network (NN)2 . HAWC also uses a χ2 fit of the lateral distribution function (LDFChi2) to separate
gamma rays from cosmic rays, as they have different signatures in the detector. Incoming gamma
ray direction is reconstructed by using the timing of the PMT activation, and HAWC’s angular
resolution is ∼ 0.2 degrees. Fig. 1 displays an infographic of how HAWC detects secondary EAS
particles with the WCD array.

3 METHODS

3.1 HAWC Alert Metric
HAWC has an alert system that notifies of high-quality, ultra-high-energy gamma-ray detection
events. The old alert system is based on a weighted statistic that ranges from 0-50 and awards
points based on the "gamma-ness" of a detection event. Alerts are sent for events detected with a
statistic greater than 45, chosen so that events achieve this threshold about once or twice a month5 .
Points are awarded on the following categories:

  1. LDFChi2. Events with a lower LDFChi2 are awarded more points as they are more likely to
 be from gamma rays, which have defined cores and a better fit to the LDF due to the absence
 of muons in the electromagnetic cascade compared to a hadronic shower.

  2. Source location. Events with sources along the galactic plane or near a known TeV source from
 the TeVCat are awarded points. TeVcat is an online catalog that maintains a comprehensive
 list of all confirmed astrophysical sources that emit TeV gamma rays10 .

  3. Energy estimate. Events with higher energies are awarded more points, and therefore more
 likely to achieve an alert.

  4. Energy agreement between GP and NN energy estimation methods. Points are awarded if the
 energy estimation methods agree within two sigma, with more points being awarded if they
 agree within one sigma.

 The aim of this work is to remove the location dependency from the HAWC alert system. This
would allow detection of transients, distant gamma-ray sources, and potentially reveal possible
dark matter annihilation. This involves altering the analysis to not award points based on source
location, and re-distribute the weighting of the statistic among the other point-awarding categories.
The new alert system is based on a weighted statistic from 0-25 in order to reduce granularity as
the number of point-awarding categories is smaller without the location dependency. The new alert
system statistic weighting is displayed in Table 1.

 A series of quality cuts, loose energy cuts, and initial gamma/hadron separation cuts are applied
to the detection data, more details are available in Abeysekara et al. 2 . Then, each detection event

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 7

that passes the initial cuts has its statistic calculated based on Table 1. The alert threshold is set
to a statistic value of 25 for this study.
 In order to verify the accuracy of this alert system for detecting the highest quality, ultra-high
gamma-ray detection events, a false positive rate calculation is performed. A false positive is either
due to a low-quality mis-reconstructed gamma ray, or a cosmic ray that has been classified as a
gamma ray. To determine the false positive rate, histograms of every reconstruction parameter are
created. Then, we randomly sample from these distributions. The alert metric is then calculated
from these values in order to find the number of events which achieve the alert threshold, and this
process is repeated for 10,000 trials. The mean false positive rate and its standard deviation are
computed.

3.2 Coincident Event Search with IceCube
IceCube is a neutrino detector in the South Pole that functions on a similar principle to HAWC.
Like HAWC, IceCube uses Cherenkov radiation to detect particles, although the detection medium
is Antarctic ice instead of water. IceCube has its own alert system, where events labeled "Gold" are
believed to have a 50% chance of having an astrophysical origin while "Bronze" events have a 30%
chance of astrophysical origin1 . Spatially coincident events are determined by checking the location
of each HAWC alert against the locations of the IceCube events. An event is considered a potential
coincidence if the HAWC location is within the IceCube containment radius. This method is chosen
because HAWC has a better angular resolution than IceCube.
 We investigate whether the number of spatial coincidences is above the expected background.
The false spatial coincidence rate is calculated by randomly sampling for 10,000 trials from the
distributions of IceCube containment radius, as well as HAWC and IceCube right ascension (RA)
and declination (Dec) values from alerted events, and determining how often the HAWC source
location is within the IceCube containment radius. We also calculate the expected number of
coincidences for the region with a great number of spatial coincidences: the Cygnus region. To
perform this, we use a 6.0 degree circle centered at RA = 307.17 degrees, Dec = 41.17 degrees.

3.3 Likelihood Study of LHAASO J2108+5157
We use a maximum likelihood approach to determine the spectrum of LHAASO J2108+5157 using
the standard HAWC software and analysis methods12 . The likelihood of the observations is formed
under the null hypothesis, Lnull , and an alternative hypothesis Lalt which contains additional free
parameters that are not in the null-hypothesis model11, 3 . In this study, the null hypothesis is
the background-only hypothesis when there is no source in the region. The fraction of PMTs hit
(fHit) is divided into bins, and the background-subtracted number of photons detected in each
fHit bin is √plotted alongside the fitted residuals. A significance map of the region is created,
where σ ≈ T S quantifies the difference between the observed emission and the null-hypothesis
background prediction.

4 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 Newly Alerted Events
Within HAWC’s ∼ 2800 days of data, 59 events passed the new alert threshold for high quality,
ultra-high-energy gamma-ray detection events. We define a dataset of 73,389 events from June 2015
to January 2024 passing initial quality and energy cuts, which we use in the remainder of this work.

4.3 Analysis of the LHAASO J2108+5157 Region
The alert threshold of 25 is still preliminary, and we have experimented with setting the alert
threshold to a statistic value of 23. 1,243 HAWC events achieve this threshold, with a computed
false positive rate of 30.29 ± 1.56%. With this new alert threshold, we have found 348 coincident
events between HAWC and IceCube and a false spatial correlation rate of 0.0004 ± 0.0200. 37 of
these coincidences have HAWC and IceCube source locations within 1 degree of each other, and 15
of them are within 0.5 degrees.
 A revealed source of particular interest is the mysterious LHAASO J2108+5157 region, which
has 7 HAWC-IceCube coincidences when applying this new alert threshold. LHAASO J2108+5157
is an intriguing source because there has been no detected emission counterpart in the radio, op-
tical, or infrared wavelengths, and it is a potential candidate for the production of PeV cosmic
rays9 . LHAASO J2108+5157 is an enigmatic, ultra-high-energy gamma-ray source approximately
1.6 kiloparsecs, or 5,200 light-years, from Earth8 . We use ∼2800 days of HAWC data to determine
the photon flux of the region, displayed in Fig. 6.

5 CONCLUSIONS
We were able to successfully remove the location bias from the HAWC gamma-ray alert system. 59
high-quality, ultra-high-energy gamma ray detection events achieve the new threshold for an alert.
This allows for the detection of transient flares and possible new sources of ultra-high-energy gamma-
ray emission. A few of the newly alerted gamma-ray detections originate from the galactic plane,
however many of them have unknown sources. Ultra-high-energy gamma rays of deep extragalactic
origin are expected to be attenuated by the EBL before they reach Earth. This may mean that
many of these newly alerted gamma rays without a clear source are the result of diffuse emission
or statistical fluctuations. These findings are consistent with that of the Tibet ASγ collaboration6 ,
who have reported diffuse gamma-ray emission between 100 TeV and 1 PeV.
 We report 19 spatially coincident alerts between HAWC and IceCube, 16 of which are novel.
The false spatial coincidence rate was computed to be 0.0003, with a standard deviation of 0.1732.
The number of coincident alerted gamma-ray/neutrino pairs exceeds the number expected due to
random chance. When lowering the new HAWC alert threshold to a statistic of 23, approximately
348 HAWC-IceCube alerts are spatially coincident. We report 7 spatially coincident detection events
from nearby the LHAASO J2108+5157 region. We implement maximum likelihood analysis of fHit
bins to measure the background-subtracted photon flux from this region, and determine that it is
best fit to a 2D-Gaussian morphology with a power law energy spectrum. The next steps for this
project are to get the new alert system operating in real-time, and to check for spatial coincidences
with other detectors such as Fermi-LAT.
