This project was structured around two complementary scientific goals, unified by a common
theme: advancing the understanding of plasma-driven processes that are relevant across fusion,
space, and astrophysical environments. Specifically, the project combined (1) a fundamental
investigation of energetic electron transport in magnetized plasmas with magnetic islands, and (2)
an exploratory study of plasma-assisted formation of inorganic and organic compounds during
meteoroid atmospheric entry. Together, these two thrusts leveraged the unique capabilities of the
DIII-D National Fusion Facility to address problems of broad interest to the fusion energy, space
plasma, and astrochemistry communities.

Thrust 1: Energetic Electron Transport in Magnetized Plasmas with Magnetic Islands
The first primary goal of this project was to investigate how energetic electrons interact with
magnetic islands and stochastic magnetic fields in magnetized plasmas, with relevance spanning
laboratory fusion devices and space plasmas. Magnetic islands are ubiquitous in both tokamaks
and space environments, yet their role in regulating energetic particle confinement, transport, and
acceleration remains incompletely understood. The central objective of this thrust was to determine
how magnetic island topology—including island width, location, rotation, bifurcation, and
overlap—affects the transport and acceleration of suprathermal electrons.
To address this goal, the project tested a series of specific hypotheses: that energetic electrons can
become trapped near island O-points and de-confined near X-points; that contracting magnetic
islands can accelerate electrons through a Fermi-like process; and that dynamic island behavior
can lead to enhanced transport via magnetic field stochasticity. These hypotheses were examined
through a combination of dedicated experiments on the DIII-D tokamak, advanced diagnostic
measurements sensitive to high-energy electrons, and comparison with numerical modeling and
spacecraft observations. This effort provided controlled laboratory insight into processes that are

otherwise difficult to isolate in space plasmas, while also informing understanding of energetic
electrons and runaway phenomena in fusion devices ￼.

Thrust 2: Plasma-Assisted Formation of Inorganic and Organic Compounds During
Meteoroid Atmospheric Entry
The second primary goal of this project was to explore the possibility that plasma environments
created during meteoroid atmospheric entry can facilitate the formation of complex inorganic
compounds and organic precursors relevant to prebiotic chemistry. Motivated by hypotheses
concerning the origins of life on Earth, this thrust investigated whether ablation of carbonaceous
and silica-rich materials in an oxygen-poor atmosphere could produce compounds such as
ammonia and potentially urea, which are key ingredients in prebiotic chemical pathways.
This effort utilized the DIII-D tokamak as a unique laboratory platform to simulate aspects of
meteoroid entry conditions, including high-temperature plasma exposure, controlled gas
compositions, and material ablation at relevant energy fluxes. Experiments focused on identifying
plasma conditions and gas mixtures that favor the synthesis of ammonia and related compounds,
as well as assessing the survivability of these products under edge plasma conditions. The work
combined experimental measurements, post-experiment chemical analysis, and supporting
numerical modeling to establish whether plasma-driven chemistry during atmospheric entry could
plausibly contribute to the inventory of prebiotic molecules on the early Earth. In addition to its
implications for cosmochemistry and astrobiology, this thrust also provided insight into plasma–
material interactions and impurity chemistry relevant to fusion devices.

The UC San Diego (UCSD) team contributed to both scientific thrusts of the project by providing
experimental, modeling, and coordination support for the DIII-D activities, with a particular
emphasis on experimental design, diagnostics integration, plasma–material interaction expertise,
and data interpretation.

Operational Accomplishments

• The UCSD team assisted in the preparation and revision of the DIII-D experimental proposal,
including development of the detailed shot plan and contingency strategies, through sustained
technical interaction with the Auburn-led team and DIII-D facility personnel.

• UCSD participated in shot plan preparation and review activities, including contributing to the
overview shot plan presentation and providing technical input during the DIII-D shot plan review
process.

• Members of the UCSD team participated in collaborative planning meetings with the Auburn
team and external collaborators, supporting coordination of experimental objectives, diagnostics
usage, and execution strategy.

• UCSD researchers provided support for training students and collaborators in accessing,
visualizing, and interpreting DIII-D experimental data using tools such as reviewplus, efitviewer,
and OMFIT, and assisted with interpretation of diagnostic signals relevant to energetic electrons
and plasma–material interaction studies.

• The UCSD team supported experimental execution during DIII-D visits, assisting with real-time
decision-making during experiments and post-shot assessment of plasma conditions and diagnostic
performance.

Scientific Accomplishments

• UCSD contributed to the analysis and interpretation of Fast Camera Synchrotron Emission, Hard
X-Ray, Electron Cyclotron Emission (ECE), and Electron Cyclotron Emission Imaging (ECEI)
measurements used to characterize energetic electron transport in the presence of magnetic islands.

• The UCSD team assisted in establishing and validating experimental evidence for energetic
electron trapping near magnetic island O-points and controlled de-confinement during island
rotation, through coordinated analysis of multiple diagnostics.

• UCSD supported the interpretation of delayed electron transport through magnetic island O-point
regions relative to X-point regions, based on combined ECE and ECEI measurements.

• The UCSD team contributed to the assessment of enhanced electron transport associated with
evolving magnetic island structures, including contracting and expanding island chains.

• For the plasma chemistry thrust, UCSD assisted with experimental planning related to material
ablation, gas puffing strategies, and post-experiment sample handling, and supported interpretation
of results identifying ammonia and other reaction products.

• UCSD contributed to benchmarking experimental observations against predictive calculations
and simulations, helping to establish favorable plasma conditions for ammonia synthesis and
assess compound survivability under edge plasma conditions.

Ongoing Outcomes

The UCSD team continues to participate in post-experiment data analysis, interpretation of
diagnostic measurements, and preparation of journal publications summarizing the main scientific
findings of both thrusts. UCSD personnel remain actively engaged in collaborative meetings
focused on consolidating results and synthesizing conclusions that connect laboratory plasma
experiments to fusion, space, and astrochemical applications.

Training and Workforce Development

This project provided significant training and workforce development opportunities for early-
career scientists, graduate students, and undergraduate researchers through a highly
interdisciplinary and collaborative research program. The project involved an early-career
postdoctoral PI (Dr. Christopher Mehta), an early-career assistant professor co-PI (Dr. Evdokiya
G. Kostadinova), three graduate students (two from Auburn University and one from the
University of South Florida), one UC San Diego undergraduate student, and one SULI
undergraduate student. The effort was carried out through a cross-institutional collaboration

involving Auburn University, UC San Diego, the University of South Florida, Baylor University,
and General Atomics.

All students participating in the project were co-mentored by the Auburn PI (Dr. Evdokiya
Kostadinova), the UCSD co-PI (Dr. Dmitri Orlov), and senior collaborators from the University
of West Virginia (Prof. Mark Koepke), the University of Iowa (Prof. Frederick Skiff), and the
University of Texas at Austin (Prof. Max Austin). Through this collaboration, students interacted
closely with DIII-D scientists and facility staff, gaining first-hand exposure to tokamak operations,
experimental planning, and data analysis. During a collaborative visit to General Atomics, students
participated in a guided tour of the DIII-D National Fusion Facility, which provided important
context for the fusion energy mission and the experimental environment.

Graduate and undergraduate students involved in the project received training in a broad set of
experimental, computational, and analytical skills, including:

• Accessing, plotting, and interpreting DIII-D experimental data using reviewplus, efitviewer, and
OMFIT;

• Performing numerical simulations using TRIP3D, SURFMN, and TORAY codes;

• Understanding the operating principles of major plasma diagnostics, including Thomson
Scattering, Langmuir Probes, Electron Cyclotron Emission (ECE), Synchrotron Emission, Gamma
Ray Imaging, and Hard X-Ray diagnostics;

• Learning key experimental techniques such as three-dimensional magnetic perturbations, error
field correction, electron cyclotron heating and current drive, Vlasov-style electron tagging, and
modulated ECH;

• Designing and revising experimental shot plans, including contingency steps and real-time
decision points;

• Conducting real-time data analysis during experiments to guide experimental execution;

• Preparing and presenting scientific results at professional conferences and summer schools,
including MagNetUS meetings, the ITER International School, the EPS Plasma Conference, the
APS Division of Plasma Physics Meeting, and the Sherwood Fusion Theory Conference;

• Writing scientific manuscripts suitable for submission to peer-reviewed journals.

As part of the training activities, Auburn graduate students also engaged in focused literature study
on energetic electron transport in magnetized plasmas and presented research papers as part of
their graduate-level space plasma coursework.

The project provided valuable professional development for Dr. Christopher Mehta, who, as an
early-career postdoctoral PI, led numerical modeling efforts, contributed to experimental proposal
development, and oversaw aspects of chemical analysis of experimental products. This experience
provided important leadership and project-management training during a critical career stage.

Undergraduate researcher Zachary Yam (UCSD) contributed to the preparation of procedures for
chemical extraction and analysis of post-experiment samples and assisted with handling and
preparation of tokamak-exposed materials. Following his participation in this project, Mr. Yam
proceeded to graduate school to pursue a Ph.D. in plasma–material interactions.

Graduate student Joshua Abbatiello (University of South Florida) was supported through this grant
as a research intern and contributed calculations related to the activation energy and plasma
conditions for ammonia formation. This work forms part of his doctoral dissertation research.

Overall, the project contributed meaningfully to workforce development by training students and
early-career researchers in experimental plasma physics, plasma–material interactions, numerical
modeling, and interdisciplinary collaboration across fusion, space plasma, and astrochemical
research.
