Near-Optimal Learning of Local Lindbladians
Itai Arad, Zhili Chen, Naixu Guo, Patrick Rebentrost, Zhan Yu

Abstract
We study the problem of learning local Lindbladians from black-box access to the physical evolution, and the goal is to estimate all Hamiltonian and dissipative coefficients. We give an algorithm built directly from finite-time channel probes, which runs the unknown evolution for short times, estimates the corresponding Pauli transfer matrices from classical shadows, and converts these estimates into Lindbladian coefficients by stable local Fourier inversions. For fixed locality and bounded dissipative site degree, the uses of the dynamical evolution and total evolution time scale as O ~ ​ ( Λ 2 / ε 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\Lambda^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}) and O ~ ​ ( Λ / ε 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\Lambda/\varepsilon^{2}) respectively, in the local dynamical strength bound Λ \Lambda and target accuracy ε \varepsilon , with only logarithmic dependence on the number of qubits. The algorithm is non-adaptive, uses no ancillas, and uses only random product states as inputs followed by random Pauli measurements. The method does not require knowing the support of the Lindbladian in advance. We complement the algorithm with matching lower bounds, showing that the learning algorithm is near-optimal both in physical dynamics accesses and in total evolution time. We construct a single-qubit dephasing Lindbladian family that already requires Ω ⁡ ( Λ 2 / ε 2 ) \Omega(\Lambda^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}) channel uses and Ω ⁡ ( Λ / ε 2 ) \Omega(\Lambda/\varepsilon^{2}) total evolution time, even for adaptive algorithms with arbitrary ancillas and measurements. In particular, the lower bounds imply that the Heisenberg-limited scaling achievable for Hamiltonian learning is information-theoretically impossible once dissipative coefficients must be estimated.

Across these developments, existing methods face a trade-off: methods with strong theoretical guarantees are often experimentally demanding, while experimentally friendly protocols typically come with weaker or less general guarantees.
This raises a fundamental question:

Can we rigorously learn physical Lindbladians from dynamics with a simple protocol and minimal cost?

Our question is interesting not only from a theoretical point of view but also from a practical one.
Given experimental access to the time evolution of an unknown open quantum system, the goal of Lindbladian learning is to recover the Hamiltonian terms and the dissipative coefficients that generate the dynamics. Such a task is important for characterizing noisy quantum devices, diagnosing errors, validating engineered dissipation, and understanding open-system many-body physics
[
46
,
8
]
.
However, a learning protocol might become practically useless if it involves costly operations such as highly non-local or conditional quantum operations. It is therefore important to construct learning protocols that are both rigorous and “experimentally friendly”.

A generic
n

A natural and physically relevant structure is locality: in many quantum systems, both the Hamiltonian interactions and the dissipative processes are generated by few-body terms. For fixed locality
k
, the number of Pauli coefficients grows only polynomially with
n

Another relevant structural property is the bounded dissipative site degree, meaning that each qubit participates in only a bounded number of dissipative terms. This assumption is well aligned with realistic noisy quantum hardware, where noise is typically local, and each qubit is affected by only a limited number of dominant error mechanisms
[
1
]
.

In this work, we answer the main question affirmatively.
We provide a near-optimal algorithm for learning physical Lindbladians from access to their time evolution, which is both experimentally friendly and rigorous.
The main idea is to learn the infinitesimal generator through its Pauli transfer matrix representation.
We show that the conversion from this representation to the physical Lindbladian coefficients is stable: it decomposes into local Walsh–Hadamard transforms with good condition number.
The generator entries are then estimated by combining Chebyshev interpolation in time with a shadow process tomography procedure
[
36
]

For fixed locality
k=O(1)
and bounded dissipative site degree, our algorithm learns all local Hamiltonian and dissipative coefficients to
\ell^{\infty}
accuracy
\varepsilon

\widetilde{O}(\Lambda^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})

We also prove a matching information-theoretic lower bound, showing that this scaling is optimal up to logarithmic factors.

1.3
Technical overview

Our algorithm has three stages: probe the dynamics to estimate finite-time Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) entries, differentiate in time to recover the PTM generator entries, and invert local Fourier transforms combined with a thresholded de-aliasing algorithm to recover the Lindbladian coefficients. We then prove that the resulting complexity is nearly optimal by showing lower bounds for the Lindbladian learning problem.

From dynamics to the generator.

As we cannot get access to the generator
{\cal L}
directly, we only observe finite-time snapshots of the dynamics
e^{t{\cal L}}
. The bridge is the continuous-time PTM
R_{vw}(t)=2^{-n}\tr(P_v e^{t\cL}(P_w))
for Pauli operators
P_{v}
and
P_{w}
, whose endpoint derivative at
t=0
is exactly the PTM generator entry
L_{vw}=R^{\prime}_{vw}(0)
. We estimate
R_{vw}(t)
at a few short times by adapting an ancilla-free shadow process tomography protocol
[
36
]
. It feeds in a random single-qubit product state, evolves the channel
e^{t{\cal L}}
, and measures each qubit in a random Pauli basis. A single batch of such shots simultaneously yields an unbiased estimator of all
k
-local PTM entries. While this randomized short-time probing scheme shares its similar idea with recent protocols for Hamiltonian learning
[
80
,
36
,
18
]
, characterizing an open-system Lindbladian poses fundamentally different. We then approximate the endpoint derivative by Chebyshev-Lobatto interpolation on a short interval
[0,T]
, which is also a standard technique developed and used in prior learning algorithms
[
18
,
36
,
42
,
53
,
48
]
.

Dependence on local dynamical strength.

The accuracy of endpoint differentiation is controlled by high-order derivatives of
R_{vw}(t)
. A naive bound on the
r
-th derivative gives
\lvert R_{vw}^{(r)}(t)\rvert\leq\lVert{\cal L}^{\dagger}\rVert^{r}_{\infty\to\infty}
, i.e., growth set by the global dynamical strength that may scale with system size. Our key observation is that because the Pauli operator
P_{v}
is
k
-local, only terms near the support of
P_{v}
contribute to the evolution, giving the sharper bound

\lvert R^{(r)}_{vw}(t)\rvert\leq r!\,(k\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}})^{r},

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}
counts only the dynamical strength attached to any single qubit. Given that the target Lindbladian has bounded local dynamical strength
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\Lambda
, we therefore fix the evolution time
T=O(1/(k\Lambda))
and then a logarithmic number of Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes
q=O(\log(\Lambda/\eps))
drives the interpolation bias below
\varepsilon
.

Coefficient recovery via Fourier inversion and de-aliasing.

Writing
{\cal L}
in the left–right Pauli (
\chi
-matrix) basis, the trace orthogonality of Paulis decouples the map from
\{L_{vw}\}
to
\{\chi_{ab}\}
by Pauli shift
u=v\oplus w
: only entries with
a\oplus b=u
mix. Restricting to a local region
S
, this mixing is exactly a Walsh–Hadamard transform (discrete Fourier transform), whose matrix is orthogonal. Hence, the inversion has condition number
1
, and PTM error propagates to
\chi
-error with no amplification, which is precisely where instance-dependent conditioning is avoided. One subtlety remains: the local inversion returns not a single coefficient but a sum over all global extensions aliasing to the same local support. We remove these aliases without knowing the true support by a thresholded peeling recursion from larger to smaller supports. The bounded dissipative site degree limits the number of aliases, so the error inflates only by a constant factor.

Lower bound

To prove optimality, we reduce learning to distinguishing two one-qubit dephasing Lindbladians with rates separated by
\Theta(\varepsilon)
but both having local dynamical strength at most
\Lambda
. A use of the channel for time
t
is equivalent to a Bernoulli dephasing event with parameter
(1-e^{-2\gamma t})/2
. Relative entropy can only decrease when the flag is discarded, so the information available to any learner is upper bounded by the binary relative entropy between two Bernoulli dephasing probabilities. This gives a per-channel-use information bound of order
\varepsilon^{2}/\Lambda^{2}
and a per-unit-time information bound of order
\varepsilon^{2}/\Lambda
. Pinsker’s inequality then implies the claimed lower bounds. The argument is valid under adaptivity and arbitrary ancillas by a chain rule and data-processing induction.

2.1
Notation

Matrix norms in this paper are Schatten norms: for a matrix
A
, let
\lVert A\rVert_{p}
denote its Schatten-
p
norm. In particular,
\lVert A\rVert_{1}
is the trace norm and
\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}
is the operator norm. For a linear map
\Phi
on matrices, define the induced operator norm as

\lVert\Phi\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\coloneq\sup_{A\neq 0}\frac{\lVert\Phi(A)\rVert_{\infty}}{\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}}.

We use
\Phi^{\dagger}
for the adjoint with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product.

2.2
Pauli operators

We often consider the Hilbert space in the basis of the tensor product of Pauli matrices.

Definition 2.1

(Pauli matrices)
.

The Pauli matrices are
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices defined as follows.

I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\\
\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

These matrices are unitary and Hermitian, and
XY=\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muZ,YZ=\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muX,ZX=\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muY
. Therefore, the product of Pauli matrices is a Pauli matrix, up to a factor of
\{1,\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu,-1,-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\}
. The non-trivial (non-identity) Pauli matrices are traceless. We consider tensor products of Pauli matrices,
P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n}
, where
P_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z,I\}
for all
i\in[n]
. Let
{\cal P}_{n}
denote the set of
n
-qubit Pauli operators. For both notation and computation, it is convenient to encode each Pauli operator by a binary vector, which turns operator multiplication into addition modulo two.

Definition 2.2

(Binary representation of Pauli operators)
.

Given a Pauli operator
P_{a}
, define the binary vector
a=(z,x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
as binary representation (label) of
P_{a}
if

P_{a}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(P_{a})_{i}=(-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu)^{z\cdot x}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Z^{z_{i}}X^{x_{i}},

where each
z_{i},x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

The zero vector
0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
labels the identity.
We will often identify
P_{a}
with its label
a
when no confusion can arise. Two basic quantities associated with a Pauli operator are its support and weight, recording the qubits on which it acts nontrivially and how many there are.

Definition 2.3

(Support and weight of a Pauli operator)
.

For a Pauli operator
P_{a}\in{\cal P}_{n}
, its support
\operatorname{supp}(a)\penalty\ \subseteq\penalty\ [n]
is the subset of qubits that
P_{a}
acts non-trivially on. That is,

\operatorname{supp}(a)\coloneq\{i:(P_{a})_{i}\neq I\}.

The weight of the Pauli operator
P_{a}
is the size of the support, denoted by

\operatorname{wt}(a)\coloneq\lvert\operatorname{supp}(a)\rvert.

These notions extend naturally to a pair of Paulis. For a pair of Pauli labels
(a,b)
, write

\operatorname{supp}(a,b)\coloneq\operatorname{supp}(a)\cup\operatorname{supp}(b),\qquad\operatorname{wt}(a,b)\coloneq\lvert\operatorname{supp}(a,b)\rvert.

Bounding the weight yields the notion of locality that underlies our setting.

Definition 2.4

(Local Pauli operators)
.

For
k\geq 0
, define the set of all non-identity
k
-local Pauli operators as

{\cal P}_{n,k}\coloneq\{P_{a}\in{\cal P}_{n}:1\leq\operatorname{wt}(P_{a})\leq k\}.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
and a label
a=(z,x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
, we write
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}
if
P_{a}
is supported on
S
, i.e.,
\operatorname{supp}(a)\subseteq S
. We denote
a|_{S}
as the sub-operator of
a
within the region
S
obtained by replacing every operator
outside
S
by the identity,

a|_{S}\coloneq(z^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n},\qquad(z^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})=\begin{cases}(z_{i},x_{i}),&i\in S,\\
(0,0),&i\notin S.\end{cases}

Apart from supports, we will repeatedly use the commutation structure of Pauli operators, which is conveniently captured by the symplectic inner product of their labels.

Definition 2.5

(Symplectic inner product)
.

Given two Pauli labels
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
, we define their symplectic inner product as

\langle a,b\rangle_{s}=z\cdot x^{\prime}+x\cdot z^{\prime}\pmod{2}.

where
a=(z,x)
and
b=(z^{\prime},x^{\prime})
.

The Lindbladian generator is built from commutators and anti-commutators, which we recall next.

Definition 2.6

(Commutator and anti-commutator)
.

Given operators
A
and
B
, the commutator of
A
and
B
is defined as

[A,B]=AB-BA,

and the anti-commutator is defined as

\{A,B\}=AB+BA.

The symplectic inner product between two Pauli operators can be regarded as an indicator of commutation. Given two Pauli operators
P_{a},P_{b}\in{\cal P}_{n}
, we have

P_{a}P_{b}=(-1)^{\langle a,b\rangle_{s}}P_{b}P_{a}.

Pauli operators are moreover closed under multiplication, up to a phase, which lets us track products purely at the level of binary labels. Given
P_{a},P_{b}\in{\cal P}_{n}
, their multiplication has the form

P_{a}P_{b}=\xi_{ab}P_{a\oplus b},

where
\xi_{ab}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\}
are efficiently computable given
a
and
b
.

2.3
Markovian open quantum systems

For an open quantum system, when environmental memory effects are negligible, the reduced dynamics becomes Markovian and is generated by a Lindbladian
[
40
,
58
]
. A Lindbladian naturally separates the evolution into two contributions: a coherent part generated by an effective Hamiltonian, and dissipative parts that encode irreversible environmental effects such as dephasing and relaxation.

Definition 2.7

(Quantum dynamical semigroup)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an
n
-qubit Hilbert space and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of all linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
A family of linear maps

\{\mathcal{E}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, with

\mathcal{E}_{t}\colon\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
is called a
quantum dynamical semigroup
if

•

\mathcal{E}_{0}=I_{n}
,

•

\mathcal{E}_{t+s}=\mathcal{E}_{t}\circ\mathcal{E}_{s}
for all
t,s\geq 0
,

•

each
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is a quantum channel, i.e. a completely positive and trace preserving map,

•

t\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{t}
is continuous.

In finite dimensions, the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) theorem
[
40
,
58
]
provides the closed form of the generator for this semigroup.

Definition 2.8

(Lindbladian)
.

A Lindbladian is the infinitesimal generator of the quantum semigroup defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{t}-I}{t},

(1)

which can be written in the Pauli basis as

{\cal L}(\rho)=\sum_{a}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{a}[P_{a},\rho]+\sum_{a,b}\gamma_{ab}\ab\Big(P_{a}\rho P_{b}-\frac{1}{2}\{P_{b}P_{a},\rho\}),\qquad h_{a}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\gamma_{ab}\in\mathbb{C},

(2)

where
\gamma\succeq 0
and
\gamma=\gamma^{\dagger}.

The evolution of the Lindbladian can be written as
{\cal E}_{t}=e^{t{\cal L}}
, and by definition we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}(\rho(t)).

Definition 2.9

(Support of a Lindbladian)
.

A Lindbladian
{\cal L}
is supported in

\operatorname{supp}({\cal L})\coloneq\bigcup_{a:h_{a}\neq 0}\operatorname{supp}(a)\cup\bigcup_{a,b:\gamma_{ab}\neq 0}\operatorname{supp}(a,b).

In this paper, we consider Lindbladians that are
k
-local. Since the dissipation contains two-sided Pauli terms, we define the set of weight
k
Pauli pairs

\Gamma_{k}\coloneq\ab\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}:a\neq 0,\ b\neq 0,\ 1\leq\operatorname{wt}(a,b)\leq k\}.

(3)

Definition 2.10

(Local Lindbladian)
.

A Lindbladian is
k
-local if it has the form

{\cal L}(\rho)=\sum_{a\in{\cal P}_{n,k}}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{a}[P_{a},\rho]+\sum_{(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}}\gamma_{ab}\ab\Big(P_{a}\rho P_{b}-\frac{1}{2}\{P_{b}P_{a},\rho\}).

(4)

In other words, each coherent and dissipative term acts nontrivially on at most
k
qubits.

Definition 2.11

(Dissipative site degree)
.

The dissipative site degree of a Lindbladian is the maximum number of dissipative Pauli-pair terms acting non-trivially on any single qubit,

\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}\coloneq\max_{i\in[n]}\lvert\{(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}:\gamma_{ab}\neq 0,\ i\in\operatorname{supp}(a,b)\}\rvert.

We say the dissipative site degree is bounded if
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}=O(1)
.

A natural measure of the overall strength of the dynamics is the induced operator norm of the generator under the Heisenberg picture,
\lVert{\cal L}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}
. This global quantity generically grows with the system size: an extensive Lindbladian with
O(1)
local coefficients is a sum of
\Theta(n)
local terms, so the norm scales as
\Theta(n)
. The complexity of our algorithm is instead governed by a
local
strength, which we now define.

Definition 2.12

(Local dynamical strength)
.

Decompose a Lindbladian into local generators
{\cal L}=\sum_{S}{\cal L}_{S}
, where
S
is a local region such that
{\cal L}_{S}
collects all Hamiltonian and dissipative terms supported exactly on
S
,

{\cal L}_{S}(\rho)=\sum_{\operatorname{supp}(a)=S}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{a}[P_{a},\rho]+\sum_{\operatorname{supp}(a,b)=S}\gamma_{ab}\ab\Big(P_{a}\rho P_{b}-\frac{1}{2}\{P_{b}P_{a},\rho\}).

The
local dynamical strength
under the Heisenberg picture is the largest total interaction strength attached to any single qubit,

\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneq\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{S\ni i}{\big\lVert{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}\big\rVert}_{\infty\to\infty}.

Since
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}
only counts the terms acting on a single qubit, the global dynamical strength obeys
\lVert{\cal L}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\leq\sum_{X}\lVert{\cal L}_{X}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}=O(n\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}})
, so the local strength can be smaller than the global one by a factor of the system size. Throughout the paper, we assume the local strength is bounded by a known parameter
\Lambda
, that is,
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\Lambda
. We will show that both the channel-use and total evolution time depend on
\Lambda
rather than on the global dynamical strength.

This model includes many physically and practically relevant cases. For example, consider a one-dimensional transverse-field Ising chain,
H=\sum_{i}J_{i}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}+\sum_{i}h_{i}X_{i}
, coupled to a local thermal bath through single-site relaxation and excitation operators
\sigma_{i}^{\pm}=(X_{i}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muY_{i})/2
. The whole dynamics is
2
-local and the dissipative site degree is at most 4.
The same setting also captures common noise models for quantum hardware. For instance, in a superconducting-qubit device with bounded-degree
nearest-neighbor connectivity, the Hamiltonian contains local control
terms and two-qubit couplings along hardware edges, while the noise is
dominated by single-qubit relaxation, excitation, dephasing, and possible
two-qubit correlated dephasing on neighboring qubits. These processes can be described by Lindbladians that are
at most
2
-local with bounded dissipative site degree when the hardware connectivity is bounded.

2.4
Pauli transfer matrix

We define the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) of the Lindbladian.

Definition 2.13

(Pauli transfer matrix of Lindbladian)
.

For a Lindbladian
{\cal L}
and time
t>0
, the continuous-time Pauli transfer matrix of the semigroup
e^{t{\cal L}}
has entries

R_{vw}(t)\coloneq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\ab\big(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}(P_{w})),\qquad P_{v},P_{w}\in{\cal P}_{n}.

(5)

Its generator (the PTM of the Lindbladian
{\cal L}
itself) has entries

L_{vw}\coloneq\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R_{vw}(t)\right|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\ab\big(P_{v}{\cal L}(P_{w})).

(6)

We will estimate all entries
L_{vw}
for
P_{v}
and
P_{w}
whose joint support has size at most
k
, and show that it is sufficient to recover the coefficients of
k
-local Lindbladian. Intuitively, the recovery is carried out locally, region by region, so only such entries are ever needed. We collect these target entries into the index set

{\cal T}_{k}\coloneq\{(v,w):\operatorname{wt}(v,w)\leq k\},

(7)

and write
M_{k}\coloneq\lvert{\cal T}_{k}\rvert=O(n^{k})
for its size.

This index set
{\cal T}_{k}
resembles the dissipative index set
\Gamma_{k}
, as both collect Pauli pairs of joint weight at most
k
and have size
O(n^{k})
. The difference lies in what they describe:
{\cal T}_{k}
indexes the PTM entries
L_{vw}
that we estimate from the dynamics, while
\Gamma_{k}
indexes the coefficients
\gamma_{ab}
that we eventually output. In addition,
{\cal T}_{k}
keeps the entries in which one Pauli is the identity, which are the ones carrying the information needed to recover the Hamiltonian coefficients.

2.5
Derivative estimation by Chebyshev interpolation

For
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, let
f(x)
be a real-valued function that is
q+1
times differentiable on a closed interval
{\cal I}
. Let
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{q}
be
q+1
distinct interpolation nodes in
{\cal I}
, and let
p_{q}(x)
be the unique polynomial of degree at most
q
that interpolates
f(x)
at these nodes (i.e.,
p_{q}(x_{j})=f(x_{j})
for all
j=0,1,\dots,q
).

Lemma 2.1

(Interpolation remainder formula, see e.g.
[
14
, Chapter 3]
)
.

For any
x\in{\cal I}
, there exists a point
\varsigma
situated in the smallest interval containing
x,x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{q}
such that the interpolation error
f(x)-p_{q}(x)
is given by

f(x)-p_{q}(x)=\frac{f^{(q+1)}(\varsigma)}{(q+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{q}(x-x_{j}).

We will only apply the interpolation-error formula at the node
x=x_{0}
, where the standard differentiated form gives, for some
\varsigma
in the interval,

f^{\prime}(x_{0})-p_{q}^{\prime}(x_{0})=\frac{f^{(q+1)}(\varsigma)}{(q+1)!}\prod_{j\neq 0}^{q}(x_{0}-x_{j}),

which avoids differentiating the (possibly non-smooth) remainder point
\varsigma
in
x
.

We use Chebyshev–Lobatto interpolation. It operates on the Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes, which are clustered near the boundaries of the domain
[-1,1]
.

Definition 2.14

(Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes)
.

Let
q\geq 1
. For a grid of
q+1
points, the nodes are defined as

x_{j}=\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{q}\right),\quad j=0,1,\dots,q.

Definition 2.15

(Chebyshev spectral differentiation matrix)
.

Given an interpolating polynomial
f(x)
and
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, the evaluation of this function at
q+1
Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes is a vector

\mathbf{f}=[f(x_{0}),f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{q})]^{\top}.

We define Chebyshev spectral differentiation matrix as a
(q+1)\times(q+1)
matrix
D
such that multiplying it by
\mathbf{f}
yields the vector of approximate derivatives
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}
, that is,

(D\mathbf{f})_{j}=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{j}=f^{\prime}(x_{j}),

for
j\in\{0,1,\dots,q\}
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
17
, Section 2.4.2]
)
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, the Chebyshev spectral differentiation matrix
D=D_{q}
has the following entries

\displaystyle D_{00}=\frac{2q^{2}+1}{6},\quad D_{qq}=-\frac{2q^{2}+1}{6},

\displaystyle D_{ii}=\frac{-x_{i}}{2(1-x_{i}^{2})}\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq q-1,

\displaystyle D_{ij}=\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j}}\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\quad\text{for }i\neq j,

where the coefficients
c_{i}
are defined such that
c_{0}=c_{q}=2
, and
c_{i}=1
for all interior points
1\leq i\leq q-1
.

2.6
Information-theoretic quantities

We collect the information-theoretic notions used in the lower-bound arguments. The notation
D(\cdot\parallel\cdot)
is used for both classical and quantum relative entropy, the type of the inputs will make clear which notion is meant. All logarithms are natural.

Definition 2.16

(Classical relative entropy)
.

For probability distributions
\mu,\nu
on a finite set, the relative entropy, also called the Kullback–Leibler divergence, is

D(\mu\parallel\nu)\coloneq\sum_{x}\mu(x)\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},

with the convention that
D(\mu\parallel\nu)=\infty
if
\mu
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
. For Bernoulli distributions with parameters
p,q\in[0,1]
, we write the binary relative entropy as

D_{\mathrm{b}}(p\parallel q)\coloneq p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}.

The quantum analogue is defined on density operators and is the quantity used when comparing the possible quantum states produced by two candidate dynamics.

Definition 2.17

(Quantum relative entropy)
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma
, the quantum relative entropy, or quantum Kullback–Leibler divergence, is

D(\rho\parallel\sigma)\coloneq\tr\!\left[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\right],

when
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)
, and is infinite otherwise.

To convert distinguishability statements into error-probability lower bounds, we also use the total variation distance between classical outcome distributions.

Definition 2.18

(Total variation distance)
.

For probability distributions
\mu,\nu
on a finite set, the total variation distance is

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\lvert\mu(x)-\nu(x)\rvert.

The following standard facts are used to control how much information an experiment can gain from each channel access.

Fact 2.3

(Data-processing inequality)
.

Relative entropy is monotone under stochastic maps and quantum channels. In particular, for any quantum channel
\Phi
and quantum states
\rho,\sigma
satisfying
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)
,

D(\Phi(\rho)\parallel\Phi(\sigma))\leq D(\rho\parallel\sigma),

and the analogous inequality holds for classical probability distributions under stochastic maps.

Fact 2.4

(
\chi^{2}
-divergence bound on binary relative entropy)
.

For Bernoulli distributions with parameters
p,q\in(0,1)
, the binary relative entropy satisfies

D_{\mathrm{b}}(p\parallel q)\leq\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{q(1-q)}.

Fact 2.5

(Pinsker’s inequality)
.

For probability distributions
\mu,\nu
on a finite set,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(\mu\parallel\nu)}.

3
Lindbladian learning algorithm and analysis

This section presents and analyzes our learning algorithm. We first estimate the PTM generator entries from the dynamics:
Section

3.1
sets up the endpoint-derivative rule via Chebyshev interpolation,
Section

3.2
estimates the finite-time PTM entries by shadow process tomography, and
Section

3.3
bounds the resulting sample and time complexity. We then convert the estimated PTM generator into the physical coefficients in
Section

3.4
, and combine the two stages into our main upper bound in
Theorem

3.11
.

3.1
Chebyshev interpolation

The dynamic-access model gives estimates of finite-time PTM entries
R_{vw}(t)
, while the desired generator entry is the endpoint derivative
L_{vw}=R_{vw}^{\prime}(0)
. Thus, the learning problem contains a differentiation step: we must approximate a derivative at the boundary of the interval from estimates at positive times. We use Chebyshev–Lobatto interpolation for this step, which gives an explicit endpoint derivative rule and has a rapidly decaying interpolation bias for the analytic semigroup functions considered here. Chebyshev–Lobatto endpoint differentiation is a standard spectral interpolation tool
[
50
,
17
]
. Similar interpolation ideas have also been used in recent algorithms for learning quantum dynamical generators
[
18
,
36
,
42
,
53
,
48
]
.

We have the following bound on the
r
-th derivative of local entries
R_{vw}(t)
, which depends on the local dynamical strength.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\cal L}
be a
k
-local Lindbladian with local dynamical strength
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}
. For any
(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
, the
r
-th derivative of PTM entry
R_{vw}(t)
satisfies

\big\lvert R_{vw}^{(r)}(t)\big\rvert\leq r!\,(k\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}})^{r}.

Proof.

By differentiating the semigroup,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}t^{{}^{\prime}}}R_{vw}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\ab\big(({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})\,e^{t{\cal L}}(P_{w})).

Since
e^{t{\cal L}}
is a quantum channel, the trace norm is contractive on Hermitian operators, i.e.,
\lVert e^{t{\cal L}}(P_{w})\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert P_{w}\rVert_{1}=2^{n}
. By Hölder’s inequality for Schatten norms,

\Big\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}t^{{}^{\prime}}}R_{vw}(t)\Big\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\big\lVert({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})\big\rVert_{\infty}\big\lVert e^{t{\cal L}}(P_{w})\big\rVert_{1}\leq\big\lVert({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})\big\rVert_{\infty}.

It remains to bound
\lVert({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})\rVert_{\infty}
by the local strength. Using the decomposition
{\cal L}^{\dagger}=\sum_{S}{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}
from
Definition

2.12
,

({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})=\sum_{S_{1},\dots,S_{r}}{\cal L}_{S_{r}}^{\dagger}\cdots{\cal L}_{S_{1}}^{\dagger}(P_{v}).

Each
{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}
is supported on
S
, annihilates the identity, and is bounded as a map; hence
{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}(A)=0
whenever
\operatorname{supp}(A)\cap S=\varnothing
, and otherwise
\lVert{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}(A)\rVert_{\infty}\leq b_{S}\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}
with
b_{S}\coloneq\lVert{\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}
and
\operatorname{supp}({\cal L}_{S}^{\dagger}(A))\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(A)\cup S
. Set
U_{0}=\operatorname{supp}(v)
and
U_{\ell}=U_{\ell-1}\cup S_{\ell}
. A summand is nonzero only if
S_{\ell}\cap U_{\ell-1}\neq\varnothing
for every
\ell
, in which case its operator norm is at most
\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}b_{S_{\ell}}
. Bounding the nested sum from the inside out, and using
\lvert U_{\ell-1}\rvert\leq\operatorname{wt}(v)+(\ell-1)k\leq\ell k
together with
\sum_{S\ni i}b_{S}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}
,

\big\lVert({\cal L}^{\dagger})^{r}(P_{v})\big\rVert_{\infty}\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\ab\Big(\sum_{S:\,S\cap U_{\ell-1}\neq\varnothing}b_{S})\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\lvert U_{\ell-1}\rvert\,\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\ell\,k\,\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}=r!\,(k\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}})^{r}.\qed

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
T>0
. We map the
q+1
standard Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes from the canonical domain
[-1,1]
to the physical time interval
[0,T]
:

t_{j}=\frac{T}{2}\ab(1-\cos\ab\Big(\frac{j\pi}{q})),\quad j=0,1,\dots,q.

Note that
t_{0}=0
and
t_{q}=T
. The true expectation value is
R_{vw}(t)
; let
p_{q}(t)
be the exact interpolating polynomial. The derivative of the interpolating polynomial at
t=0
is a linear combination of
R_{vw}(t_{j})
,

p_{q}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)R_{vw}(t_{j}),

where
\ell_{j}(t)
are the Lagrange basis polynomials

\ell_{j}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq q\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{j}-t_{i}}.

Lemma 3.2

(Truncation error)
.

Let
{\cal L}
be a
k
-local Lindbladian with local dynamical strength
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\Lambda
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, set the evolution time and the number of nodes as

T=\frac{1}{2k\Lambda},\qquad q=\ab\lceil\log_{2}(2k\Lambda/\varepsilon)\rceil.

Then, for every
(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}
, the truncation error is bounded by

\lvert L_{vw}-p_{q}^{\prime}(0)\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof.

By the differentiated interpolation-remainder formula applied at the node
x_{0}=t_{0}=0
, there exists
\varsigma_{0}\in(0,T)
such that

L_{vw}-p_{q}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{R_{vw}^{(q+1)}(\varsigma_{0})}{(q+1)!}\prod_{j\neq 0}(0-t_{j})=\frac{R_{vw}^{(q+1)}(\varsigma_{0})}{(q+1)!}\cdot(-1)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{q}t_{j}.

Here we used
t_{0}=0
, so that differentiating
W(t)=\prod_{j=0}^{q}(t-t_{j})
at
t=0
leaves only the term
\prod_{j\neq 0}(0-t_{j})
and avoids differentiating the remainder point
\varsigma_{t}
.
By the local derivative bound in
Lemma

3.1
and the assumption
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\Lambda
,
\lvert R_{vw}^{(q+1)}(\varsigma_{0})\rvert\leq(q+1)!\,(k\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}})^{q+1}\leq(q+1)!\,(k\Lambda)^{q+1}
, and since every node obeys
t_{j}\leq T
,

\displaystyle\lvert L_{vw}-p_{q}^{\prime}(0)\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert R_{vw}^{(q+1)}(\varsigma_{0})\rvert}{(q+1)!}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{q}t_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q+1)!\,(k\Lambda)^{q+1}}{(q+1)!}\,T^{q}=(k\Lambda)^{q+1}T^{q}.

Fixing the maximum evolution time as
T=1/(2k\Lambda)
, the factorials cancel and the truncation error decays geometrically in
q
,

(k\Lambda)^{q+1}T^{q}=k\Lambda\,\ab\big(k\Lambda T)^{q}=k\Lambda\,2^{-q}.

To bound this by
\varepsilon/2
, it suffices to fix the number of nodes as

q=\ab\lceil\log_{2}(2k\Lambda/\varepsilon)\rceil=O\ab\big(\log(k\Lambda/\eps))=\widetilde{O}(1).\qed

3.2
Learning the PTM generator by shadow process tomography

The learning protocol below is adapted from ancilla-free shadow process tomography and related randomized-measurement methods
[
36
,
57
,
55
]
. We specialize it to the local PTM entries needed for the endpoint-differentiation step.

The target PTM entries are those indexed by
{\cal T}_{k}
defined in
Eq.

7
. In the generator estimation algorithm, we choose the Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes
t_{0},\ldots,t_{q}
as described in
Section

2.5
, and estimate each
R_{vw}(t_{j})
for
0\leq j\leq q
. The value at
t_{0}=0
is known exactly, since
R_{vw}(0)=\mathbf{1}[v=w]
, so no experiment is performed at
t_{0}
. For each nonzero node
t_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,q
, we collect
N
independent shadow records.

In each experiment for some fixed
t
, we first generate a random input state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i}\rvert
where each
\lvert\psi_{i}\rangle
is chosen independently and uniformly from
\{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle,\lvert+\rangle,\lvert-\rangle,\lvert+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\rangle,\lvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\rangle\}
. After applying
e^{t{\cal L}}
, choose a measurement basis
B_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
independently and uniformly for each output qubit, and record outcomes
o_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
. A single shadow shot could be recorded by a triple

s=(\rho_{\mathrm{in}},B,o),

where
B=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{n})
is the output measurement basis and
o=(o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{n})
is the vector of measurement outcomes. Thus, the full shadow data set contains
qN
raw records and uses the unknown channel exactly
qN
times. For each target entry
(v,w)
, every raw record contributes one bounded random variable to the average for
R_{vw}(t)
. The same collection of records is reused for all entries in
{\cal T}_{k}
; different entries require different classical post-processing of the records, not new channel uses.

For a node
t_{j}
, the estimator of PTM entries
(v,w)
obtained from the
m
-th shot is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{vw}(t_{j},m)\coloneq\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)\cdot\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v),

(8)

where

\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)\coloneq 3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\tr\ab\big(P_{w}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}),\qquad\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)\coloneq\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}3o_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i}].

The empirical PTM estimate at time
t_{j}
is the average over total
N
experiments,

\bar{R}_{vw}(t_{j})\coloneq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\hat{R}_{vw}(t_{j},m).

The generator estimate is then obtained by applying the endpoint differentiation rule to these empirical finite-time PTM estimates, together with the exact value at
t_{0}=0
:

\hat{L}_{vw}=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\bar{R}_{vw}(t_{j}).

In the following, we show that each single-shot estimator is unbiased and uniformly bounded; then
Section

3.3
applies the concentration bound uniformly over all entries and all sampled times.

Lemma 3.3

(Unbiased estimator of PTM)
.

For every pair
(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}
,
t\geq 0
, and
m\in[N]

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{R}_{vw}(t,m)]=R_{vw}(t).

Proof.

Let
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=e^{t{\cal L}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})
.
The measurement bases
B=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{n})
are chosen independently and uniformly from
\{X,Y,Z\}
.
Since
P_{v}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(P_{v})_{i}
, the estimator

\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)=\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}3o_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i}]

is non-zero only when
B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i}
for every
i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)
, the probability of which is

\Pr\ab\big[B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i},\forall i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)]=\ab\Big(\frac{1}{3})^{\operatorname{wt}(v)}.

Conditioning on this event, the outcomes
\{o_{i}\}_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}
are the results of measuring
P_{v}
qubit-wisely on
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}
, thus we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}\ab\bigg[\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}o_{i}\mathrel{\Big|}B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i},\forall i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)]=\tr(P_v \rhoout).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{B,o}\ab[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)\mathrel{\big|}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]

\displaystyle=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)}\cdot\tr(P_v \rhoout)\cdot\Pr\ab\big[B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i},\forall i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)]

(10a)

\displaystyle=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)}\cdot\ab\Big(\frac{1}{3})^{\operatorname{wt}(v)}\cdot\tr(P_v \rhoout)

(10b)

\displaystyle=\tr(P_v \rhoout).

(10c)

Now consider
\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)=3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\tr\ab\big(P_{w}\rho_{\mathrm{in}})
for some Pauli operator
P_{w}
. Since
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}
for each
\rho_{i}
independently and uniformly sampled, and
\tr(P_w \rhoin)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\tr((P_w)_i \rho_i)
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab[3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\tr(P_w \rhoin)\;\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{i}}\ab[3^{\mathbf{1}{[(P_{w})_{i}\neq I]}}\tr\ab((P_{w})_{i}\rho_{i})\rho_{i}].

Then it suffices to evaluate the expectation for each single-qubit:

1.

If
(P_{w})_{i}=I
, then
3^{\mathbf{1}{[(P_{w})_{i}\neq I]}}\tr\ab((P_{w})_{i}\rho_{i})\rho_{i}=3^{0}\tr(I \rho_i)\rho_{i}=\rho_{i}
. Taking the expectation gives
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\rho_{i}]=I/2
since each
\rho_{i}
is chosen independently and uniformly from the six Pauli eigenstates.

2.

If
(P_{w})_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
, among the six states only the two eigenstates of
(P_{w})_{i}
(denoted by
\lvert s_{+}\rangle
and
\lvert s_{-}\rangle
) have non-zero trace with
(P_{w})_{i}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{i}}\ab[3^{\mathbf{1}{[(P_{w})_{i}\neq I]}}\tr((P_w)_i \rho_i)\rho_{i}]

\displaystyle=3\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{i}}\ab[\tr((P_w)_i \rho_i)\rho_{i}]

\displaystyle=3\ab\big(\frac{1}{6}\lvert s_{+}\rangle\!\langle s_{+}\rvert-\frac{1}{6}\lvert s_{-}\rangle\!\langle s_{-}\rvert)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lvert s_{+}\rangle\!\langle s_{+}\rvert-\lvert s_{-}\rangle\!\langle s_{-}\rvert)

\displaystyle=\frac{(P_{w})_{i}}{2}.

In both cases, the expectation on each single-qubit is
(P_{w})_{i}/2
. Taking the tensor product gives

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab[3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\tr(P_w \rhoin)\;\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(P_{w})_{i}}{2}=\frac{P_{w}}{2^{n}}.

(11)

Combining
Eqs.

11
and
10
yields

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{R}_{vw}(t,m)]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab\Big[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{B,o}\ab[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)\mathrel{|}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab\Big[3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\cdot\tr(P_w \rhoin)\cdot\tr(P_v \rhoout)]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab\Big[3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\cdot\tr(P_w \rhoin)\cdot\tr\ab\big(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}))]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab\Big[\tr\ab\Big(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}\ab\big(3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\cdot\tr(P_w \rhoin)\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{in}}))]

\displaystyle=\tr\ab(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}\ab\Big(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}\ab[3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}\cdot\tr(P_w \rhoin)\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]))

\displaystyle=\tr\ab(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}\ab\Big(\frac{P_{w}}{2^{n}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\ab\big(P_{v}e^{t{\cal L}}(P_{w}))=R_{vw}(t).\qed

Lemma 3.4

(Variance of the PTM estimator)
.

For every pair
(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}
,
t\geq 0
, and
m\in[N]

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}\ab\big[\hat{R}_{vw}(t,m)^{2}]=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)+\operatorname{wt}(w)},\qquad\mathop{\bf Var\/}\ab\big[\hat{R}_{vw}(t,m)]\leq 3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)+\operatorname{wt}(w)}.

Proof.

By definition
\hat{R}_{vw}=\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)\cdot\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)
. Conditioned on
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
, the output squares to

\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)^{2}=\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}9\cdot\mathbf{1}[B_{i}=(P_{v})_{i}]\,o_{i}^{2}.

Since
o_{i}^{2}=1
and each measurement basis matches the required Pauli with probability
1/3
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)^{2}\mid\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]=\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(v)}9\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)},

independently of
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
. For the input, the six single-qubit Pauli eigenstates give
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho}[(\tr Q\rho)^{2}]=1/3
for each
Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}
, so

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)^{2}]=3^{2\operatorname{wt}(w)}\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(w)}\tfrac{1}{3}=3^{\operatorname{wt}(w)}.

By the tower property,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}\ab\big[\hat{R}_{vw}(t)^{2}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}\ab[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)^{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{out}}(v)^{2}\mid\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]]=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}\ab\big[\hat{C}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)^{2}]=3^{\operatorname{wt}(v)+\operatorname{wt}(w)}.

The variance is at most the second moment, giving the stated bound.
∎

Corollary 3.5

(Unbiased estimator of PTM generator)
.

Let

be a
k
-local Lindbladian with local dynamical strength

in

\big\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{L}_{vw}]-L_{vw}\big\rvert\leq\varepsilon/2

Proof.

By definition, we have

\hat{L}_{vw}=\ell^{\prime}_{0}(0)\,\mathbf{1}[v=w]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\bar{R}_{vw}(t_{j})=\ell^{\prime}_{0}(0)\,\mathbf{1}[v=w]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\ab\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\hat{R}_{vw}(t_{j},m)).

By

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{L}_{vw}]

3.3
Complexity for learning PTM generator

To prove the upper bound for the sample complexity, we will need a concentration bound derived from weighted Bernstein’s inequality.

Lemma 3.6

(Weighted Bernstein’s inequality
[
13
, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

Let
\{X_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}
be independent random variables, and let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
be real weights with
a_{\max}=\max_{i}\lvert a_{i}\rvert
. Define the weighted sum and variance

S\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(X_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[X_{i}]),\qquad V\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}\mathop{\bf Var\/}[X_{i}].

Assume that
\lvert X_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[X_{i}]\rvert\leq B
almost surely for each
i
, then it satisfies that

\Pr\ab[\lvert S\rvert\geq s]\leq 2\exp\ab\Big(-\frac{s^{2}}{2(V+a_{\max}sB/3)}).

Next, we prove the sample complexity of the proposed algorithm.

Theorem 3.7

(Upper bound of PTM generator learning)
.

Let
{\cal L}
be an unknown
k
-local Lindbladian on
n
qubits with local dynamical strength

\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)

\widetilde{O}\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,3^{2k}k^{3}n^{k}\big\lparen k\log n+\log\tfrac{2}{\delta}\big\rparen).

Proof.

In

1

\hat{L}_{vw}=\ell^{\prime}_{0}(0)\,\mathbf{1}[v=w]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\bar{R}_{vw}(t_{j})=\ell^{\prime}_{0}(0)\,\mathbf{1}[v=w]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\ab\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\hat{R}_{vw}(t_{j},m)).

is a weighted sum of

V=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\ab\Big(\frac{\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)}{N})^{2}\mathop{\bf Var\/}\ab\big[\hat{R}_{vw}(t_{j},m)]\leq\frac{3^{2k}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lvert\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\rvert^{2}.

(12)

For Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes mapped to

(13)

\displaystyle\Pr\ab[\left\lvert\hat{L}_{vw}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\hat{L}_{vw}]\right\rvert>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]

(14d)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\ab\bigg(-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{32\cdot{3^{2k-1}}(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lvert\ell^{\prime}_{j}(0)\rvert)^{2}})

(14e)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\ab\bigg(-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{32\cdot{3^{2k-1}}\cdot O(k^{2}\Lambda^{2}q^{4})}),

(14f)

where

,

Eq.

Recall that the number of pairs in

is
M_{k}=O(n^{k})

pairs and absorbing the

\Pr\ab\Big[\max_{(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}}\big\lvert\hat{L}_{vw}-L_{vw}\big\rvert\geq\varepsilon]\leq 2M_{k}\exp\ab\Big(-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{O(k^{2}\Lambda^{2}q^{4}\cdot 3^{2k})}).

3.4
Coefficient recovery from PTM generator entries

This subsection converts the estimated PTM generator
\{\hat{L}_{vw}\}
into the physical coefficients in three steps: (i) we relate the
\chi
-matrix entries to the coefficients (
Lemma

3.8
); (ii) we show the map
\{L_{vw}\}\to\{\chi_{ab}\}
decouples by Pauli shift into local Walsh–Hadamard transforms as
Eq.

23
; and (iii) we remove support aliasing by a thresholded peeling recursion (
Algorithm

2
), with the end-to-end error analysis in
Lemmas

3.9
and
3.11
.

Every linear map
{\cal L}
on
n
-qubit operators has a unique expansion in
\chi
-matrix as

{\cal L}(A)=\sum_{a,b}\chi_{ab}P_{a}AP_{b}.

(15)

Once we obtain the
\chi
-matrix, the coefficient vector of the Lindbladian can be easily recovered.
The PTM generator and the
\chi
-matrix should be viewed as two
different representations of the same linear map
{\cal L}
, where we focus on the Lindbladian in this work.
The PTM
generator entries

L_{vw}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr(P_v\cL(P_w))

describe how
{\cal L}
maps an input Pauli operator
P_{w}
into the Pauli
basis. In contrast, the
\chi
-matrix expansion expresses
{\cal L}
in the left-right Pauli multiplication basis. These two
representations are not equal entrywise, but they are related by an
invertible change of basis.

For the Lindbladian in
Eq.

4
, the entries of this
\chi
matrix determine the physical coefficients as follows.

Lemma 3.8

(Coefficient recovery from
\chi
-matrix)
.

For all pairs
(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}
, we have

\gamma_{ab}=\chi_{ab},\qquad h_{c}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\left(\chi_{c0}-\chi_{0c}\right).

Proof.

The terms
P_{a}AP_{b}
with
(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}
arise only from the first term of the dissipator in
Eq.

4
, so
\gamma_{ab}=\chi_{ab}
. For the boundary entries, the Hamiltonian contributes
-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{c}
to
\chi_{c0}
and
+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{c}
to
\chi_{0c}
. The anti-commutator part contributes the same scalar to both entries:

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}\\
a\oplus b=c\end{subarray}}\gamma_{ab}\,\xi_{ba}.

Thus
\chi_{c0}-\chi_{0c}=-2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muh_{c}
, which is equivalent to the claimed formula.
∎

The recovery is organized by Pauli shifts. Substituting
Eq.

15
into the PTM generator gives

L_{vw}=\sum_{a,b}\chi_{ab}\left[2^{-n}\tr(P_vP_aP_wP_b)\right],

and the trace is zero unless

v\oplus a\oplus w\oplus b=0.

Thus, only terms with the same shift

u\coloneq v\oplus w=a\oplus b

mix with one another. By expanding
L_{w\oplus u,w}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{w\oplus u,w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{a}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}\tr\ab(P_{w\oplus u}P_{a}P_{w}P_{a\oplus u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{a}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}\tr\ab(P_{w\oplus u}P_{w}P_{a}P_{a\oplus u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{a}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}\xi_{w\oplus u,w}\xi_{a,a\oplus u}\tr\ab(P_{u}P_{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}\xi_{w\oplus u,w}\xi_{a,a\oplus u}

Grouping all known classical phases gives

\xi_{w\oplus u,w}^{-1}L_{w\oplus u,w}=\sum_{a}\xi_{a,a\oplus u}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}.

(16)

For notational convenience, define

F_{u}(w)\coloneq\xi_{w\oplus u,w}^{-1}L_{w\oplus u,w},\qquad G_{u}(a)\coloneq\xi_{a,a\oplus u}\chi_{a,a\oplus u}.

Then
Eq.

16
becomes

F_{u}(w)=\sum_{a^{\prime}}G_{u}(a^{\prime})(-1)^{\langle a^{\prime},w\rangle_{s}}.

(17)

Fix a qubit set
S
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
and a shift
u
supported on
S
. Restrict to Pauli operators supported inside
S
, i.e.,
\operatorname{supp}(w)\subseteq S
, and write the function as
F_{u}^{S}(w)
. Since
w
is the identity outside
S
, the commutation sign only depends on the restriction of
a^{\prime}
to
S
:

\langle a^{\prime},w\rangle_{s}=\langle a^{\prime}|_{S},w\rangle_{s}.

Therefore,

F_{u}^{S}(w)=\sum_{a^{\prime}}G_{u}(a^{\prime})(-1)^{\langle a^{\prime}|_{S},w\rangle_{s}}.

(18)

We can further group global coefficients by their local restriction and define the aggregated local quantity

G_{u}^{S}(a)\coloneq\sum_{a^{\prime}:a^{\prime}|_{S}=a}G_{u}(a^{\prime}),\qquad\text{where }a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}.

(19)

Regrouping the global sum gives

F^{S}_{u}(w)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}}G_{u}^{S}(a)(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}},

(20)

which is precisely the Walsh–Hadamard transform on the local region
S
. In matrix notation, we can write

F_{u}^{S}=H_{S}G_{u}^{S},

where
H_{S}
is the Hadamard matrix with entries
(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}
. The Hadamard matrix is orthogonal, satisfying

(H_{S})^{\top}H_{S}=4^{\lvert S\rvert}I,

so the inversion has condition number exactly
1
, and the PTM entry estimation error propagates to
\chi
-matrix entry error without amplification. The inverse Walsh–Hadamard transform is

G_{u}^{S}(a)=\frac{1}{4^{\lvert S\rvert}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}}F^{S}_{u}(w)(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}.

(21)

Substituting back the definitions of
F_{u}^{S}
and
G_{u}^{S}
gives

\sum_{a^{\prime}:a^{\prime}|_{S}=a}\xi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}\chi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}=\frac{1}{4^{\lvert S\rvert}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}}\xi_{w\oplus u,w}^{-1}L_{w\oplus u,w}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}.

(22)

Since
u
is supported on
S
, the phase
\xi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}
depends only on the restriction
a^{\prime}|_{S}
, hence

\xi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}=\xi_{a^{\prime}|_{S},a^{\prime}|_{S}\oplus u}=\xi_{a,a\oplus u}.

For a Pauli operator
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}
, define the local extension sum

\chi_{a,a\oplus u}^{S}\coloneq\sum_{a^{\prime}:a^{\prime}|_{S}=a}\chi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}

It follows that

\chi_{a,a\oplus u}^{S}=\frac{1}{\xi_{a,a\oplus u}4^{\lvert S\rvert}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}}\xi_{w\oplus u,w}^{-1}L_{w\oplus u,w}(-1)^{\langle a,w\rangle_{s}}.

(23)

In other words, the inverse Walsh–Hadamard transform does not generally give one
\chi
-matrix entry, but an aggregated sum over entries of global aliasing, which are extensions to some local Pauli operator
a
on the region
S
.

The remaining step is to extract the individual entry by de-aliasing, i.e., removing common outside extensions without knowing the true support. Fix a shift
u
and a candidate label
a
, where either
(a,a\oplus u)\in\Gamma_{k}
, or
u\in{\cal P}_{n,k}
and
a\in\{0,u\}
for the two Hamiltonian boundary entries. Let

S=\operatorname{supp}(a,a\oplus u).

Then the local extension sum over
S
obeys

\chi^{S}_{a,a\oplus u}=\chi_{a,a\oplus u}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{\prime}\neq a\\
a^{\prime}|_{S}=a|_{S}\\
\gamma_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\chi_{a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\oplus u},

with the convention that boundary entries
\chi_{u0}
and
\chi_{0u}
are also kept when they appear. The local extension sum equals the desired coefficient plus the nonzero larger dissipative coefficients that agree with
a
on the local support
S
. Since these common outside extensions are unknown, the post-processing below peels them from larger supports to smaller supports and uses layer-dependent thresholds for dissipative candidates. This estimates every dissipative coefficient indexed by
\Gamma_{k}
. Exact identification of the nonzero support would require an additional coefficient gap assumption, which we do not impose.

Lemma 3.9

(Coefficient recovery stability)
.

Assume that

\max_{(v,w)\in{\cal T}_{k}}\lvert\hat{L}_{vw}-L_{vw}\rvert\leq\eta.

Then every local extension sum computed in Algorithm

has error at most
\eta

(a,b)\in\Gamma_{k}
and every boundary entry used for Hamiltonian recovery satisfies

\lvert\hat{\chi}_{ab}-\chi_{ab}\rvert\leq E_{\operatorname{wt}(a,b)}\leq K_{\mathrm{rec}}\,\eta,

where boundary entries are indexed by the same union support size
\operatorname{wt}(a,a\oplus u)
.

Proof.

For a fixed local inversion region
S
, all phases in

23
have modulus one. Subtracting the exact inverse from
Eq.

23
gives

\lvert\hat{\chi}^{S}_{a,a\oplus u}-\chi^{S}_{a,a\oplus u}\rvert\leq 4^{-\lvert S\rvert}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2S}}\lvert\hat{L}_{w\oplus u,w}-L_{w\oplus u,w}\rvert\leq\eta.

It remains to control the thresholded peeling step. Fix a shift
u
and a candidate
a
, set
S=\operatorname{supp}(a,a\oplus u)
, and write
r=\lvert S\rvert
. Any strict common extension

has

and
\operatorname{supp}(c,c\oplus u)\supsetneq S
. Fix any

c|_{S}=a|_{S}
and

is supported on
S
, we have
(c\oplus u)|_{S}=(a\oplus u)|_{S}
, so the pair
(c,c\oplus u)
acts nontrivially on

does. By the definition of dissipative site degree, at most
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}

\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}
true larger aliases.

We prove by descending induction on
r
. Assume that all coefficients with support size larger than

have already been estimated to error at most

, and that no inactive larger candidate has been retained. For the residual
Z_{a,u}
, the local extension sum error contributes at most
\eta
. The only additional residual error comes from at most
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}
true larger aliases, each contributing at most
E_{r+1}
, whether it was retained and subtracted with error or was too small to be retained. Hence

\lvert Z_{a,u}-\chi_{a,a\oplus u}\rvert\leq\eta+\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}E_{r+1}=\tau_{r}.

The boundary labels are the only entries needed to recover Hamiltonian coefficients, and their strict common extensions are dissipative pairs, so the same
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}
bound controls their aliasing. If
a
is a boundary label, the algorithm keeps
Z_{a,u}
and its error is at most
\tau_{r}\leq E_{r}
. If
a
is dissipative and
\lvert Z_{a,u}\rvert>\tau_{r}
, the algorithm keeps
Z_{a,u}
and again the error is at most
\tau_{r}\leq E_{r}
. If
\lvert Z_{a,u}\rvert\leq\tau_{r}
, the algorithm outputs zero; then

\lvert\chi_{a,a\oplus u}\rvert\leq\lvert Z_{a,u}\rvert+\tau_{r}\leq 2\tau_{r}=E_{r}.

For an inactive dissipative candidate, the same bound gives
\lvert Z_{a,u}\rvert\leq\tau_{r}

Unrolling

which proves the claimed coefficient bound.

4
Lower bounds for Lindbladian learning

We prove information-theoretic lower bounds matching
Theorems

3.11
and
3.7
up to logarithmic factors. The bounds hold in the strongest access model, allowing adaptive learners to use arbitrary ancillas, entangling operations, and arbitrary measurements, certifying that our non-adaptive, ancilla-free algorithm is near-optimal and that neither adaptivity nor entanglement helps.
Section

4.1
sets up the hard instance and the per-access information limitation;
Theorem

4.1
gives the channel-use bound and
Theorem

4.2
the evolution-time bound.

The lower-bound strategy has two steps. First, choose two similar Lindbladians whose coefficients differ by more than the target accuracy. Any successful learner must distinguish them. Second, bound the relative entropy between the learner’s final output distributions under the two hypotheses. The bound holds even if the learner uses ancillas, adaptive input states, and adaptive measurements.

4.1
Construction of hard instances

First, we construct the hard instance. For
\gamma\geq 0
, define the one-qubit dephasing Lindbladian

{\cal L}_{\gamma}(\rho)=\gamma(Z\rho Z-\rho),

(24)

whose locality is
k=1
and dissipative site degree is
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}}=1
. For an observable
Q
, the Heisenberg-picture action can be written as
{\cal L}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}(Q)=\gamma(ZQZ-Q)
, which gives
{\cal L}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}(Q)=0
when
[Z,Q]=0
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}(Q)=-2\gamma Q
when
\{Z,Q\}=0
. Therefore, by the definition of induced operator norm, we have

\lVert{\cal L}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}=2\gamma.

(25)

Since this Lindbladian consists of a single local term on one qubit, its local dynamical strength coincides with the operator norm,
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}=\lVert{\cal L}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}=2\gamma
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\Lambda/16]
, we consider two instances
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
by setting

\gamma_{0}\coloneq\frac{\Lambda}{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{1}\coloneq\gamma_{0}+4\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon\leq\Lambda/16
, we have
2\gamma_{1}=\Lambda/2+8\varepsilon\leq\Lambda
, so both instances clearly satisfy the local-strength constraint
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\leq\Lambda
. Note that the coefficients of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
differ in
\ell^{\infty}
norm by
\lvert\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1}\rvert=4\varepsilon
, so any algorithm that learns the Lindbladian to
\ell^{\infty}
error
\varepsilon
can distinguish
\gamma_{0}
from
\gamma_{1}
.

The semigroup channel
e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma}}
is a Pauli dephasing channel

e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma}}(\rho)=(1-p_{\gamma t})\rho+p_{\gamma t}Z\rho Z,\qquad p_{\gamma t}\coloneq\frac{1-e^{-2\gamma t}}{2}.

Thus, a use of the channel of time
t
can be viewed as applying a Pauli
Z
conjugation with probability
p_{\gamma t}
governed by a Bernoulli distribution.
Revealing this hidden Bernoulli flag can only increase the information available to the learner, so the relative entropy between two dephasing channels is bounded by the relative entropy between their corresponding Bernoulli distributions.

4.2
Channel-use lower bound

We first prove the lower bound on the number of channel uses required to learn a local Lindbladian.

Theorem 4.1

(Channel-use lower bound)
.

For a fixed
k=O(1)
, consider learning an unknown
k
-local Lindbladian
{\cal L}
with access to the semigroup channel
e^{t{\cal L}}
for arbitrary
t\geq 0
. Given
\Lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\Lambda/16]
, suppose a learning algorithm can estimate coefficients of
k
-local Lindbladians with local dynamical strength at most
\Lambda
up to
\ell^{\infty}
error
\varepsilon
, then the number of channel uses
M
must satisfy

M=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}).

Proof.

We consider Lindbladians that are either
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
or
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
, as defined in
Section

4.1
. As noted, any algorithm that learns the Lindbladian to
\ell^{\infty}
error
\varepsilon
can distinguish
\gamma_{0}
from
\gamma_{1}
. We will show that the two Lindbladians are hard to distinguish unless we use enough accesses to the semigroup channels.

Recall that the semigroup channel
e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{i}}}
is a Pauli dephasing channel

e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{i}}}(\rho)=(1-p_{\gamma_{i}t})\rho+p_{\gamma_{i}t}Z\rho Z,\qquad p_{\gamma_{i}t}\coloneq\frac{1-e^{-2\gamma_{i}t}}{2}.

Fix a time
t\geq 0
, we define
p_{i}\coloneq p_{\gamma_{i}t}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
for the ease of notation. For any input state
\rho
, the output under
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{i}}
is

\sigma_{i}\coloneq(1-p_{i})\rho+p_{i}Z\rho Z.

We introduce an auxiliary flag indicating whether
Z
is applied or not,

\tilde{\sigma}_{i}\coloneq(1-p_{i})\lvert 0\rangle\!\langle 0\rvert\otimes\rho+p_{i}\lvert 1\rangle\!\langle 1\rvert\otimes Z\rho Z.

Then by the data-processing inequality (
2.3
, tracing out the flag), we have

D(\sigma_{0}\parallel\sigma_{1})\leq D(\tilde{\sigma}_{0}\parallel\tilde{\sigma}_{1}).

The block-diagonal relative entropy gives

D(\tilde{\sigma}_{0}\parallel\tilde{\sigma}_{1})=D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})+(1-p_{0})D(\rho\parallel\rho)+p_{0}D(Z\rho Z\parallel Z\rho Z)=D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1}),

where
D_{\mathrm{b}}
is the binary relative entropy defined as

D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\coloneq p_{0}\log\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}}+(1-p_{0})\log\frac{1-p_{0}}{1-p_{1}}.

Therefore we have

D(\sigma_{0}\parallel\sigma_{1})\leq D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1}).

Now we bound
D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})
uniformly over
t\geq 0
. Let
\Delta\coloneq\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}=4\varepsilon
,
x\coloneq 2\gamma_{0}t
and
\eta\coloneq\Delta/\gamma_{0}
, then we can write

p_{0}=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{2},\qquad p_{1}=\frac{1-e^{-(1+\eta)x}}{2}.

By
2.4
, the
\chi^{2}
-divergence bound gives

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\leq\frac{(p_{0}-p_{1})^{2}}{p_{1}(1-p_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-2x}(1-e^{-\eta x})^{2}}{1-e^{-2(1+\eta)x}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-2x}(1-e^{-\eta x})^{2}}{1-e^{-2x}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-2x}\eta^{2}x^{2}}{1-e^{-2x}}

(by
1-e^{-\eta x}\leq\eta x
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}x^{2}}{e^{2x}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2}=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}.

(by
e^{2x}-1\geq x^{2}
)

We can also consider an adaptive algorithm using
M
measurements, which may apply arbitrary quantum operations, use ancillary qubits, choose future evolution time based on previous outcomes, and perform arbitrary final measurements. Let
\mathrm{dist}_{0}
and
\mathrm{dist}_{1}
be the final output distribution of the algorithm, when the Lindbladian is
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
, respectively. We can show that the previous bound is stable under adaptivity. For any two input states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, we have

D\ab\big(e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}}(\rho_{0})\parallel e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}}(\rho_{1}))\leq D(\rho_{0}\parallel\rho_{1})+D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\leq D(\rho_{0}\parallel\rho_{1})+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}^{2}},

which follows from the same flagged construction as above. It means that each access to the dynamics gives an additive increase of at most
\Delta^{2}/\gamma_{0}^{2}
in the relative entropy, and subsequent quantum operations can only decrease it by the data-processing inequality in
2.3
. Starting from the same initial state, by induction over
M
steps of measurements, the final output distributions satisfy

D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})\leq M\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}.

(26)

Assume the algorithm learns both of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
with success probability at least
2/3
, it implies that
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
can be distinguished. From the estimate
\hat{\gamma}
, define the binary test
\mathsf{T}\coloneq\mathbf{1}[\hat{\gamma}\geq\gamma_{0}+2\varepsilon]
.

•

In case of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
: success of the algorithm implies
\lvert\hat{\gamma}-\gamma_{0}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
, so
\hat{\gamma}\leq\gamma_{0}+\varepsilon<\gamma_{0}+2\varepsilon
and
\mathsf{T}=0
with probability at least
2/3
.

•

In case of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
: success of the algorithm implies
\lvert\hat{\gamma}-\gamma_{1}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
, so
\hat{\gamma}\geq\gamma_{1}-\varepsilon>\gamma_{0}+2\varepsilon
and
\mathsf{T}=1
with probability at least
2/3
.

Therefore, the total variation distance between the two output distributions must satisfy

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathrm{dist}_{0},\mathrm{dist}_{1})\geq\frac{1}{3}.

Pinsker’s inequality in
2.5
gives

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathrm{dist}_{0},\mathrm{dist}_{1})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})}.

Combining with
Eq.

26
, we have

M\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}\geq\frac{2}{9}.

Substituting
\gamma_{0}=\Lambda/4
and
\Delta=4\varepsilon
yields the lower bound

M\geq\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}=\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{(\Lambda/4)^{2}}{16\varepsilon^{2}}=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}).\qed

4.3
Total evolution time lower bound

We then prove the lower bound on the total evolution time of the semigroup
e^{t{\cal L}}
required to learn a local Lindbladian
{\cal L}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Evolution-time lower bound)
.

For a fixed
k=O(1)
, consider learning an unknown
k
-local Lindbladian
{\cal L}
with access to the semigroup channel
e^{t{\cal L}}
for arbitrary
t\geq 0
. Given
\Lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\Lambda/16]
, suppose an algorithm can estimate coefficients of
k
-local Lindbladians with local dynamical strength at most
\Lambda
up to
\ell^{\infty}
error
\varepsilon
, then the total evolution time
T_{\mathrm{tot}}
must satisfy

T_{\mathrm{tot}}=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}).

Proof.

We construct the same hard instances
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
as defined in
Section

4.1
. Let
\Delta\coloneq\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}=4\varepsilon
,
x\coloneq 2\gamma_{0}t
and
\eta\coloneq\Delta/\gamma_{0}
, then we can write

p_{0}=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{2},\qquad p_{1}=\frac{1-e^{-(1+\eta)x}}{2}.

Recall that we have previously proved a uniform bound of
D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\leq\Delta^{2}/\gamma_{0}^{2}
over all
t>0
in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Now we slightly change the proof to obtain a bound that also depends on time
t
. Using the
\chi^{2}
-divergence bound in
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\leq\frac{(p_{0}-p_{1})^{2}}{p_{1}(1-p_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-2x}(1-e^{-\eta x})^{2}}{1-e^{-2(1+\eta)x}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-2x}(1-e^{-\eta x})^{2}}{1-e^{-2x}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-2x}\eta^{2}x^{2}}{1-e^{-2x}}

(by
1-e^{-\eta x}\leq\eta x
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}x^{2}}{e^{2x}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2}\frac{x}{2}

(by
e^{2x}-1\geq 2x
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{2\gamma_{0}t}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}t.

The rest of the proof follows the same argument as the proof of
Theorem

4.1
. The only difference is that we change the upper bound of
D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})
from
\Delta^{2}/\gamma_{0}^{2}
to
\Delta^{2}t/\gamma_{0}
. For any two input states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, we have

D\ab\big(e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}}(\rho_{0})\parallel e^{t{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}}(\rho_{1}))\leq D(\rho_{0}\parallel\rho_{1})+D_{\mathrm{b}}(p_{0}\parallel p_{1})\leq D(\rho_{0}\parallel\rho_{1})+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}t,

It means that each access to the dynamics of evolution time
t
gives an additive increase of at most
\Delta^{2}t/\gamma_{0}
in the relative entropy.

Now consider an algorithm that uses the dynamics for
M
times, and the evolution time of each access is
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{M}
. Define the total evolution time

T_{\mathrm{tot}}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}
.
Let
\mathrm{dist}_{0}
and
\mathrm{dist}_{1}
be the final output distribution of the algorithm, when the Lindbladian is
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
, respectively. Starting from the same initial state, it follows immediately by induction that

D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}t_{j}=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}T_{\mathrm{tot}}.

(27)

Assume the algorithm learns both of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
with success probability at least
2/3
, it implies that
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
can be distinguished. Therefore, the total variation distance between the two output distributions must satisfy

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathrm{dist}_{0},\mathrm{dist}_{1})\geq\frac{1}{3}.

Pinsker’s inequality in
2.5
gives

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathrm{dist}_{0},\mathrm{dist}_{1})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})}

Combining with
Eq.

27
, we have

\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}T_{\mathrm{tot}}\geq\frac{2}{9}.

Substituting
\gamma_{0}=\Lambda/4
and
\Delta=4\varepsilon
yields the lower bound

T_{\mathrm{tot}}\geq\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\Delta^{2}}=\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{\Lambda/4}{16\varepsilon^{2}}=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}).\qed

The argument above bounds the information gained from a realized sequence of evolution times. In a fully adaptive protocol, however, the learner may decide both the evolution time of next access and when to stop based on the outcomes observed so far, so the total evolution time is itself a random variable. The same per-unit-time information bound still applies after conditioning on the past, which leads to the following expected-time version.

Corollary 4.3

(Evolution time lower bound for adaptive learning)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.2
, allow the algorithm to choose a stopping time
M
and evolution times
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{M}
adaptively from previous outcomes and internal randomness. Let

T_{\mathrm{tot}}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}
be the resulting random total evolution time. Given
\Lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\Lambda/16]
, if an algorithm succeeds with probability at least
2/3
over all
k
-local Lindbladians
{\cal L}
with local dynamical strength at most

\Lambda
, estimating their coefficients up to
\ell^{\infty}
error
\varepsilon
,
then its worst-case expected total evolution time satisfies

\sup_{{\cal L}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{{\cal L}}\ab[T_{\mathrm{tot}}]=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}).

Proof.

We reuse the hard instances
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}},{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
and write
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{i}}^{\mathrm{tr}}
for the law of the full adaptive transcript when the true generator is
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{i}}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
. The transcript includes the algorithm’s internal randomness, the chosen evolution times, all measurement outcomes, and the final output. Let
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\gamma_{i}}
denote expectation under
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{i}}^{\mathrm{tr}}
.

We first prove the relative-entropy bound for a truncated transcript. Fix
N
and run the algorithm until it stops or until
N
possible channel uses have been reached; after stopping, set all later evolution times to zero and append dummy outcomes. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{i}}^{(N)}
be the law of this truncated transcript. Conditioned on the same realized history before the
j
-th possible use, the adaptive rule fixes the next evolution time
t_{j}
and the admissible operation preparing the input. The actual system-memory input states under the two hypotheses may be different, but the single-access estimate in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
applies to arbitrary pairs of input states after tensoring the dephasing channel with the identity on any memory and ancillas. Hence, the
j
-th use can increase the relative entropy by at most
\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}t_{j}
. The intervening adaptive operations, measurements, and classical post-processing cannot increase relative entropy by data processing. Therefore, the chain rule for relative entropy gives

D\ab\Big(\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{0}}^{(N)}\parallel\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{1}}^{(N)})\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\gamma_{0}}\ab\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}t_{j}].

Taking
N\to\infty
and using monotone convergence for the right-hand side, together with the monotonicity of relative entropy for increasing transcripts, yields

D\ab\big(\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{0}}^{\mathrm{tr}}\parallel\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{1}}^{\mathrm{tr}})\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\gamma_{0}}\ab[T_{\mathrm{tot}}].

Let
\mathrm{dist}_{i}
be the law of the final output under
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{i}}
. Since the final output is a function of the transcript, data processing gives

D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})\leq D\ab\big(\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{0}}^{\mathrm{tr}}\parallel\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{1}}^{\mathrm{tr}})\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\gamma_{0}}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\gamma_{0}}\ab[T_{\mathrm{tot}}].

As in
Theorem

4.2
, success probability at least
2/3
on both hard instances yields a test distinguishing
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
from
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{1}}
with total variation distance at least
1/3
. Pinsker’s inequality then gives

D(\mathrm{dist}_{0}\parallel\mathrm{dist}_{1})\geq\frac{2}{9}.

Combining the last two displays and substituting
\gamma_{0}=\Lambda/4
and
\Delta=4\varepsilon
gives

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\gamma_{0}}\ab[T_{\mathrm{tot}}]\geq\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\Delta^{2}}=\Omega\ab\Big(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}).

Since
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{0}}
satisfies the local-strength constraint, the same lower bound holds for the worst-case expected total evolution time.

∎

5
Outlook

We gave a near-optimal algorithm for learning local Lindbladians from their dynamics, governed by a local strength parameter and precision
\varepsilon
, together with matching lower bounds that pin the cost to the standard quantum limit
1/\varepsilon^{2}
. Several directions remain open.

The Heisenberg and standard quantum limit gap.

A Hamiltonian can be learned at the Heisenberg limit
1/\varepsilon
, and it was shown that merely
detecting
dissipation is also Heisenberg-limited
[
16
]
. By contrast, our lower bound shows that even estimating a single dissipative rate needs
\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{2})
.
This gap brings up a finer question: which functionals of
{\cal L}
are learnable at the Heisenberg limit, and which are constrained to the standard limit? More broadly, our lower bound is reminiscent of the metrology results showing that generic noise destroys the Heisenberg scaling of phase estimation
[
30
,
27
]
; making this analogy quantitative for Lindbladian learning would be interesting.

Dependence on locality and degree.

We did not optimize the constants
3^{O(k)}
and
K_{\mathrm{rec}}=2\sum_{j<k}(2\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{dis}})^{j}
, which are exponential in the locality
k
and the dissipative degree. Whether the
3^{O(k)}
shadow-variance factor and the peeling factor
K_{\mathrm{rec}}
can be improved is open.

Structure learning and stronger metrics.

Our algorithm recovers every coefficient larger than
\varepsilon
without knowing the dissipative support, but it does not certify the exact support without a coefficient-gap assumption. Identifying the support exactly, and learning in stronger metrics (e.g. the diamond distance of
{\cal L}
rather than
\ell^{\infty}
on coefficients), are natural next steps.

Beyond the Markovian, time-independent setting.

Extending the guarantees to time-dependent generators, non-Markovian dynamics, or continuous-variable/bosonic systems would broaden the applicability of the approach.
