1. Project Progress and Accomplishments Summary

The overarching goal of this three-year project was to design and experimentally
demonstrate a quantum wrapper networking (QWN) protocol and architecture for
entanglement management and control in coexisted classical-quantum networks, facilitating
a global quantum Internet. In QWN, the quantum payload (qubits) is wrapped with classical
bits as a header. The classical bits facilitate end-to-end transport of the quantum payload
without having to read or alter the quantum data payload at intermediate switches and
routers.
Over the course of the project, the team addressed and fulfilled all the key milestones
outlined in the original proposal. The project followed the defined task structure and Gantt
schedule, delivering a mix of theoretical, architectural, and experimental achievements. For
a summary of all the key activities and accomplishments achieved over the course of the
entire project and related publications, please refer to Table 1 and Section 3, respectively.

For this final reporting period, the team research efforts focused on the following key
activities:
 1. Extending and refining protocol-level mechanisms, including updated
 performance characterization of quantum-wrapper encapsulation of entangled
 photon pairs (Task 2.2), implementation of polarization tracking and recovery
 schemes to preserve entanglement fidelity (Task 3.2), and initial evaluation of frame-
 level synchronization concepts, explored in conjunction with polarization alignment
 strategies (Task 3.1).
 2. Validating QWN transport functionality under realistic link conditions (Task
 3.3), through field trials conducted over in-ground fiber infrastructure at a remote
 partner site, providing practical confirmation of QWN protocol operation outside the
 lab environment.
 3. Demonstrating dynamic routing of quantum-wrapper datagrams (Tasks 4.1, 4.2),
 based on real-time monitoring of quantum-layer impairments—including noise from
 co-propagating classical channels—to maintain a minimum two-photon interference
 (TPI) visibility across a multi-hop quantum-classical fiber network.

Section 2 reports the technical results related to the above activities. These results have been
published in the papers [1,4-6] listed in Section 3.

2. Discussions
2.1 Quantum Wrapper Transmission, Switching, Monitoring and
 Swapping Demonstration (Task 2.2 and 2.5)
In the final project year, we completed an extensive experimental evaluation of quantum-
wrapper (QW) datagram transmission over fiber networks using polarization-entangled
photons. The results, published in a peer-reviewed journal, significantly expand on the
preliminary findings reported in Year 2.
During the current reporting period, we successfully demonstrated quantum datagram
switching in a three-node Quantum Wrapper Network (QWN) testbed (see Figure 1 and
Figure 2). At the source node, single-photon quantum payloads were time-wavelength
multiplexed with classical label bits to form QWN packets. These packets propagated
through a central quantum switch that decoded the classical headers to determine the
correct output path, routing the quantum payload to one of two destination nodes.
The testbed also enabled the study of impairments affecting broadband quantum signals,
including chromatic dispersion and polarization rotations across a spectral range >30 nm.
Figure 3 and Figure 4 reports results related to chromatic dispersion effects.
End-to-end transmission was tested over 20 km of standard optical fiber, and the switching
performance was evaluated using coincidence count measurements. We observed a
coincidence-to-accidental count ratio (CAR) exceeding 22 at both destinations,
demonstrating low-noise entanglement distribution. Additionally, two-photon interference
(TPI) visibility exceeded 79% under two distinct polarization bases, validating reasonably
high-fidelity delivery through the switched paths. These results are highlighted in Figure 5
and Figure 6.
Further, the results reported in Figure 6 show a linear correlation between classical header
BER and quantum channel CAR, enabling the use of classical telemetry as a proxy for
quantum link quality — a foundational step for automated monitoring and future route
optimization in scalable QWN deployments.
Lastly, the experiment highlighted practical design constraints on QWN packet format and
synchronization overhead. Our current implementation uses pilot sequences of >40 μs for
burst-mode clock and data recovery. While functional, this overhead is a limiting factor for
high-speed switching. Ongoing work focuses on reducing synchronization latency to support
nanosecond-scale packet switching in future deployments (more details on this aspect are
reported in Section 2.2).

2.2 Synchronization and Clock Recovery Concepts (Task 3.1)

Although no hardware implementation of burst-mode clock recovery was completed during this
period, in our publication [4] we reported a detailed architectural discussion of synchronization
schemes tailored for quantum-classical packetized transport. Specifically, the protocol defines a
QW datagram structure with embedded timing patterns and classical pilot bits that enable
alignment and identification of the quantum payload at each node. These concepts lay the
foundation for future implementation of burst-mode synchronization modules in QWN-compatible
routers and receivers, and complement polarization tracking mechanisms addressed in Task 3.2.
Note that the pilot sequence length could be easily reduced by using ns-level burst-mode receivers
commercially available and developed for burst-mode applications in passive optical networks 1.

2.3 Rerouting in Quantum Wrapper Networks by Monitoring Single-
 Photon Level Noise (Task 4.1 and 4.2)

This activity corresponds to Tasks 4.1 and 4.2 in the project plan, focused on simulation and
experimental validation of routing and reconfiguration strategies in quantum networks
using the Quantum Wrapper Networking (QWN) framework. In this architecture, classical
header bits are multiplexed with polarization-entangled single-photon payloads into unified
QW datagrams that propagate through a shared fiber-optic network. These classical headers
enable key network functions—such as switching, synchronization, and quality-of-
transmission (QoT) monitoring—without interfering with the quantum state.
We previously demonstrated packet switching of QW datagrams over a 20 km, four-node
testbed using classical BER as a proxy for link loss. In the current reporting period, we
extended this setup by implementing a dynamic rerouting strategy based on real-time
monitoring of quantum-level impairments—specifically, noise induced by coexisting
classical channels. The rerouting logic ensures a minimum QoT threshold, defined in this
context as maintaining two-photon interference (TPI) visibility greater than 77%. Although
field deployment using campus fibers was not feasible at UC Davis, the intended functionality
was successfully validated through field trials conducted by Northwestern University as part
of Task 3.3 (see Section 2.4 for more details).
Figure 8 (a) shows the testbed network topology. The source node is equipped with a
broadband polarization-entangled photon source (EPS-1000, OZ Optics), polarization
controller (PC) for measurement basis selection, optical tunable filter (OTF) to slice
broadband quantum channel, InGaAs avalanche photodiode (Aurea Technology) as single
photon detector (SPD). Idler photons are measured at the source node, while the Signal
photons are time and wavelength-multiplexed with classical headers and transmitted
through the network. Small-form factor pluggable (SFP) transceivers (TRX) are customized
to operate in burst mode, generating and receiving the header bits at 10 Gbps rate. QW
switch/router (QWSR) at the network core processes the headers and switches the payloads
without disturbing single photons. A 20 km fiber spool is used in Path-1. The source node
employs a nanosecond 1×2 switch to select between Path-1 and Path-2. Path-1 has a
coexisting classical data traffic. An additional SFP (c-TX) generates 10 Gbps data at 1558.17
nm. In the extended testbed, the headers are assigned to 1555.75 nm. We use a coupler with
a 98/2 split ratio to multiplex QW datagrams with the classical traffic. QW datagrams
experience negligible coupling loss while the coexisting classical power is around -21 dBm
after the coupler. A coarse wavelength division multiplexer (WDM) separates the single
photon payloads at 1574 nm from classical bits in Destination-1. A dense WDM arrayed
waveguide grating demultiplexes the datagram headers and coexisting traffic. Later, an
EDFA amplifies the classical traffic. OTFs after the c-TX and before c-RX suppress broadband
spontaneous emission of TX’s laser and the EDFA. Through FPGA logic, the c-TX can be
turned off. Another 1×2 fast switch at the destination, which is synchronized to the QW
headers, connects SPD to the fiber links only during the payload time. A mechanical fiber

slots.

2.4 Theoretical Modeling of Noise and Experimental Comparison (Tasks
    3.3 and 3.4)

QWN allows users to measure the QW headers and tails, detect impairments that degrade
channel quality, and infer potential impacts on the quantum payload. Unfortunately, if not
managed properly, noise photons created by the header and tail signal itself can overlap with
and degrade the quantum payload. The quantity of noise photons can be negligible or
substantial, depending on the wavelength, optical power, and duration of the header. In
addition, the quantum payload duration, quantum application, traffic in the QW network
(both coexisting QW datagrams and classical communication signals), and the available
quantum transmitter and receiver technologies at the QWN nodes impact the noise
characteristics. It is crucial to characterize and identify the noise impact of surrounding
classical signals on quantum payloads and to design the QW datagrams accordingly. This
year, we study the noise components of our time- and wavelength-multiplexed QWN
implementation quantum payloads at Northwestern2. The noise is characterized as a
function of the headers’ power, classical-quantum wavelength detuning, and optical fiber
length.

Note that, although we place both quantum and classical channels in the O-band, QWN, in
which both channels are in the C-band, would exhibit similar noise characteristics because
SpRS is primarily a function of wavelength detuning. To fully quantify the noise contribution
during the payload, we need to verify the linear scattering mechanism of SpRS photons. Here,
we compared the time-domain photon-count noise generated by fiber-propagating headers
from a CW laser (at -8.4 dBm) with that of attenuated headers across different fiber spool

 page 18

lengths. The wavelength of the attenuated classical header is chosen at the quantum
payload’s wavelength (1306.5nm) to match the DWDM's passband, and its power is adjusted
to match the peak photon count rates as the noise photons. This allowed us to distinguish
the effects from the switch’s extinction ratio (∼23 dB) and from scattered SpRS photons.

The number of noise photons in the quantum payload depends on the amplitude of the
classical signal, the length of the fiber, and the extinction ratio of the switch. We observe this
both on the attenuated signal and the noise photons due to the SpRS. However, after the
header ends, we observe higher counts of noise photons than in the attenuated signal. This
indicates that photons are detected with a delay in SpRS. The difference also depends on the
fiber length. In 1-km and 4.8-km fiber spools, we observe that the noise photons disappear
around 10 and 46 µs after the end of the header, respectively, corresponding to the round-
trip propagation times of the respective fibers. This effect can be explained by backscattered
SpRS generated during the header, which is subsequently Rayleigh-scattered in the forward
direction.

2.5 Active Polarization Control (Task 3.2)
We implemented an active polarization control method based on QW headers3. The
integration of polarization control into QWN requires three modules: polarization state
preparation, polarization state measurements, and the polarization compensation module.
The burst-mode headers are generated as burst-mode classical signals in a single
polarization state. The polarization state preparation module creates any two nonorthogonal
polarization states. We chose vertical and diagonal in our experiments. This free-space
module contains a polarizing beam splitter (PBS) to create vertical polarization. It is
followed by a liquid-crystal retarder that switches between V-D states, which takes less than
500 ms in the current setup. The polarization measurement module is located at the
destination, where the datagram is demultiplexed into classical headers and a quantum
payload. We use a commercial polarimeter (Thorlabs IPM5300) and a data acquisition
module (Measurement Computing DAQ-1208HS) to measure polarization at 10 µs intervals.
We ensure that polarization is measured only during the header timespan by using a gating
signal generated from header detection and integrated into the system via DAQ. In this
configuration, we also use a 99:1 fiber splitter (Oz Optics) to read headers at the destination.

counts with headers present but no datagram, which show a slight increase in the noise floor
of 1,000 photons/s over the background. Although our demonstration shows the
effectiveness of using QWN headers for polarization tracking and channel compensation, a
realistic network will require higher-speed compensation and longer transmission lengths.
This could be achieved by using non-mechanical waveplates/retarders (e.g., electro-optic
devices) and by mitigating polarization mode dispersion by reducing the wavelength
detuning between the header and the quantum payload.
