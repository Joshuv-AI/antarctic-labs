Scaling integrated cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic care through primary health care in the Americas: a health policy analysis of HEARTS 2.0 implementation levers and architecture
Authors: Pedro Ordunez, Jarbas Barbosa da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnlike previous HEARTS 2.0 publications on candidate interventions, readiness assessment, prioritization, and the clinical pathway, this Health Policy analysis examines the implementation architecture required to scale integrated cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) care through primary health care (PHC) in the Americas. Renewed political commitments to PHC, hypertension control, and integrated CKM care, together with advances in medicines and health system innovations, are creating implementation momentum. Yet persistent gaps between evidence, policy commitment, and implementation continue to limit progress. These gaps mainly reflect limitations in organizing health service delivery. This analysis examines the regional landscape for scaling integrated CKM care through a PHC approach, drawing on HEARTS experience in the Americas and positioning HEARTS 2.0 as a practical pathway to accelerate delivery. It identifies key system constraints, proposes a six-lever implementation framework, and outlines an implementation architecture to align actors, strategies, and investments around scalable delivery of existing solutions.

Background

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of death in the Americas, accounting for over two million deaths annually, roughly 23% of all deaths, and one-third of deaths due to noncommunicable diseases (NCDs).1,2 In a Region marked by rapid population ageing and predominance of long-term chronic conditions, this burden increasingly reflects overlapping cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic conditions, viewed through the lens of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome.

The CKM framework emphasizes the bidirectional and progressive interactions among metabolic risk factors, kidney dysfunction, and CVD across the life course, leading to multiorgan dysfunction and substantially increased cardiovascular risk. Hence, CKM stages reflect the accumulation of risk and organ damage over time, underscoring the need for integrated prevention and management strategies that transcend traditional disease-specific silos.3

High systolic blood pressure is the leading contributor to CVD mortality in the Region4 and exerts substantial effects across the CKM spectrum.5 (Fig. 1). Hypertension therefore emerges as a strategic, scalable entry point for integrated CKM care and CVD burden reduction. While previous HEARTS 2.0 publications focused on defining candidate interventions, assessing country readiness, prioritizing components of CKM care, and the updated HEARTS 2.0 clinical pathway,7 less attention has been paid to the system architecture required to operationalize these interventions. The policy challenge has shifted from establishing the clinical rationale for integrated CKM care to organizing health systems to translate existing evidence into equitable population-level outcomes.

PHC is central to closing this gap, with primary care serving as the main platform for delivering integrated prevention, early detection, and long-term management.8,9 However, translating evidence into routine practice depends on implementation approaches that standardize care and enable quality improvement across primary care facilities.10

In this context, as a regional adaptation of the WHO Global Initiative,11 HEARTS in the Americas—coordinated by the Pan American Health Organization and supported by Resolve to Save Lives and other partners—was established to translate evidence-based interventions into routine primary care practice.12 It has since evolved into a shared policy and implementation framework for standardizing CVD care across diverse health system contexts, improving consistency and quality while also revealing persistent system-level constraints that limit integration across the CKM spectrum.

This Health Policy analysis examines how integrated CKM care can be scaled through a PHC in the Americas. It reviews the evolution of HEARTS in the Americas, identifies persistent health system constraints, and the limitations of a hypertension-centered approach to addressing multimorbidity, and positions HEARTS 2.0 as a practical pathway for implementation. HEARTS in the Americas: paving the way for integrated CKM care

HEARTS is currently implemented in 34 countries, with more than 30 actively scaling up, making it one of the most extensive coordinated regional efforts to improve hypertension control and CVD risk management in primary care. Thirteen countries have achieved ≥80% coverage of primary care facilities, together representing more than 11,000 primary care centers. Its implementation has been associated with improved hypertension control13, 14, 15, 16 and demonstrated cost-effectiveness across health systems.13,17 It has fostered strong country ownership, alignment with national priorities, and sustained technical commitment. Based on current trajectories, most countries are expected to extend implementation across much of their primary care network within the next 3–5 years.18

HEARTS has progressively translated policy into practice through a system-oriented approach aligned with broader PHC principles.19 Its Clinical Pathway structures care delivery around accurate blood pressure measurement using validated automatic devices, standardized and pragmatic cardiovascular risk assessment, and simplified pharmacological treatment protocols, while facilitating the integration of additional interventions such as statins.20 Implementation has been supported by strengthened primary care teams and enabling operational capacities, including clinical tools,21 regulatory frameworks for accurate blood pressure measurement devices,22 expanded multidisciplinary roles,23,24 and continuous quality improvement supported by performance-monitoring platforms.18,25 Together, these elements have enabled more standardized and reliable service delivery across PHC-oriented health systems.

Despite regional scale-up, progress has been uneven across countries, with some advancing rapidly while others lag. This variability reflects differences in political commitment and leadership, health system organization, and resource availability. Persistent barriers include limited governance and implementation capacity; workforce shortages; fragmented procurement systems and uneven access to essential medicines and technologies; regulatory barriers; and uneven digital infrastructure.19,26

The expansion of HEARTS has also exposed the limitations of a hypertension-centered approach, as patients increasingly present with overlapping conditions that require coordinated risk assessment, treatment, and follow-up across the continuum of care. In this context, HEARTS 2.0 emerged not merely as an incremental expansion of the original initiative, but as a logical and timely evolution toward a person-centered CKM framework.27 While preserving the operational strengths of HEARTS, it expands its scope and implementation architecture to align actors, harmonize definitions and clinical and therapeutic approaches, reduce fragmentation, simplify decision-making through standardized pathways, strengthen longitudinal care, and optimize risk reduction for patients living with multiple chronic conditions.

Toward HEARTS 2.0: advancing system transformation to improve CKM clinical management

HEARTS 2.0 followed an evidence-informed process designed to balance scientific rigor with implementation feasibility.28, 29, 30 The approach comprises two complementary implementation mechanisms. First, HEARTS Quality,18 strengthens governance and implementation through continuous quality improvement across structures, processes, and outcomes. Second, the updated HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway translates recommendations from multiple and dispersed clinical guidelines into a simplified, operational framework for routine primary care practice while complementing, rather than replacing, disease-specific care plans (Supplementary Fig. S1).7,29 Together, these complementary mechanisms link standardized service delivery with continuous quality improvement: the Clinical Pathway standardizes service delivery through simplified decision-support tools, whereas HEARTS Quality supports performance monitoring, coaching, feedback, and continuous quality improvement.

Moving from hypertension control to integrated CKM care entails greater programmatic complexity, requiring redesigned workflows, new competencies, and stronger coordination across levels of care. At the same time, this transition creates an opportunity to deepen ongoing system-level transformations and further strengthening primary care as the main platform for integrated, longitudinal chronic care. Its pace and scope will depend on country-level variation in system readiness and implementation capacity.30

How countries can progress from hypertension control toward integrated CKM care was examined in detail during Phase II of HEARTS 2.0 and is reported elsewhere.30 In that assessment, 26 Latin American and Caribbean countries evaluated readiness to implement 45 CKM improvement interventions across availability, feasibility, time to scale, and implementation barriers. Although several interventions, particularly in risk assessment and non-pharmacological treatment, were considered feasible, availability remains constrained by gaps in diagnostics, medicines, and infrastructure. Projected implementation over 3 years varied substantially: 18 countries expected to implement more than 30 interventions, four expected 20–30, and four fewer than 20. These findings support a phased, context-adapted approach that aligns financing, governance, workforce capacity, medicines access, and service delivery with country readiness and CKM multimorbidity. Thus, HEARTS 2.0 should be understood not as a time-limited project but as a common standard and long-term direction for progressively strengthening integrated CKM care across diverse health system contexts.

HEARTS 2.0 is a clinical and management approach rather than a payer-specific model; it can therefore be adapted across publicly financed, privately financed, social security-based, and mixed health systems. Its implementation requirements are broadly similar across provider types: alignment around common clinical pathways, quality standards, referral arrangements, and performance measurement to reduce unwarranted variation and improve continuity of care. Where private providers participate in chronic disease care, they should be included within the same clinical pathways, quality standards, referral arrangements, and performance measurement frameworks that apply across the health system.

While most cases can be effectively delivered in primary care, the clinical pathway explicitly incorporates referral and coordination mechanisms linking primary care with secondary and specialist services for complex, high-risk, or difficult-to-control conditions, emphasizing continuity rather than fragmentation of care.7 This approach is consistent with integrated health service delivery networks, in which services are organized around patients’ clinical needs rather than institutional boundaries. Primary care, secondary care, and specialist services should therefore function as complementary components of a coordinated care continuum, ensuring that patients receive the appropriate level of expertise, resources, and follow-up at the appropriate time. Standardized referral criteria, shared care plans, and effective communication across levels of care are essential to support continuity, shared accountability, and integrated care.

Scaling integrated CKM care raises policy-relevant dilemmas. Expanding the scope of care increases demands on primary care teams, requiring new competencies, redesigned workflows, and sustained supervision. While standardization enables scale and reduces variability, it also creates trade-offs between simplicity and clinical nuance, and between population coverage and intensity of care. The shift toward team-based care models—central to HEARTS 2.0—requires regulatory adaptation, task-sharing, and workforce development, challenging entrenched physician-centered models. These tensions reflect broader political economy choices regarding resource allocation, professional roles, and countries’ willingness and capacity to reorganize primary care delivery around standardized, team-based approaches.7

In addition, standardization improves procurement efficiency but depends on reliable access to essential medicines and technologies. Digital health tools and performance monitoring systems are key for continuity of care and quality improvement, yet their integration into routine health systems is often constrained by fragmented information systems and limited interoperability. Furthermore, efforts to standardize care within primary care must be embedded within a broader PHC approach that addresses social and structural determinants of health, as clinical interventions alone are unlikely to reduce inequities without complementary policy action.

Accelerating the scale-up of integrated CKM care: six system levers informed by HEARTS 2.0

Drawing on experience from Global HEARTS and HEARTS in the Americas,30,31 we propose a six-lever implementation framework to operationalize integrated CKM care through PHC-oriented health systems. Together, these interdependent levers provide a practical framework for operationalizing integrated CKM care through PHC-oriented health systems.

These levers operationalize core health system priorities—universal access, quality of care, and PHC strengthening32, 33, 34—by specifying the institutional and organizational functions required for routine primary care delivery. This framing is consistent with the WHO-adapted Innovative Care for Chronic Conditions framework, which emphasizes the health care organization, community, and policy environments needed to improve care.35 Rather than introducing parallel program structures, the six levers synthesize implementation lessons from a decade of HEARTS experience and translate broad health system priorities into practical functions that support the progressive integration of CKM care within existing health systems. Lever 1—governance, national scale-up, and measurable targets

Large-scale delivery depends on political commitment, leadership, and institutional coordination to align policies, financing, procurement, workforce development, and monitoring and evaluation.36, 37, 38 It also requires strategies that position PHC-oriented systems as a key priority and translate this into sustained financial and human resources. Thus, governance is not limited to stewardship; it determines the capacity of the health system to coordinate actors, sustain implementation, mobilize resources, and institutionalize delivery models across fragmented health systems.18

Differences in political commitment and governance capacity and institutional stability explain the heterogeneous pace of HEARTS implementation observed across the Region. These differences underscore that implementation trajectories are shaped as much by political and organizational conditions as by technical design.

Furthermore, hypertension care cascade, enabling the identification of gaps across diagnosis, treatment, and control and supporting decision-making for scale-up.39, 40, 41 Hence, hypertension control functions as a tracer condition for the capacity of PHC systems to deliver integrated CKM care at scale. In settings where resources and information systems are constrained, prioritizing hypertension surveillance may provide the most feasible starting point for building population-based monitoring capacity and progressively expanding toward integrated CKM care.

A central component of HEARTS scale-up is the 4 × 80 strategy: achieving ≥80% implementation coverage across primary care facilities to enable an 80–80–80 hypertension care cascade. Implementation coverage, as proportion of primary care facilities that use the HEARTS Clinical Pathway and systematically report data, serves as a proxy for system readiness, capacity, and policy coherence.18 Anchoring implementation at the facility level, where primary care teams are responsible for defined catchment populations, establishes accountability by linking service delivery to population needs and measurable health outcomes.42

The cascade [80% diagnosed, 80% treated, and 80% controlled (BP < 140/90 mmHg) among those treated] corresponds to approximately 50% population-level blood pressure control. It should be understood as a pragmatic benchmark rather than a target intended only for lower-resource settings. This level of performance remains challenging even for high-income countries, and only a handful have achieved population hypertension control rates of >50%. It represents a substantial improvement over the current average of 28.7% in Latin America and the Caribbean.40

HEARTS 2.0 therefore avoids separate “tier 2” targets for better-resourced settings, which could imply different standards of care across countries. Instead, it proposes a common standard and direction of travel, while allowing countries with greater capacity to expand the scope, depth, and pace of CKM integration through broader risk assessment, systematic CKD evaluation, wider use of evidence-based therapies, stronger information systems, and more comprehensive quality improvement indicators.

Lever 2—institutionalized HEARTS 2.0 clinical pathway

Advancing implementation requires a shift from an era focused on developing clinical practice guidelines to one in which implementation determines impact. Guidelines remain essential, but impact depends on implementation instruments—such as clinical pathways—that translate recommendations into routine primary care practice. By expanding toward integrated CKM care, the standardized clinical pathway translates evidence into simplified, standardized decision-support tools for primary care teams; specifies the medicines, technologies, workflows, and competencies required for delivery; and reduces unwarranted variation.7

Institutionalization function as an organizational mechanism that defines priorities and reduce fragmentation across levels of care. It also secures core delivery conditions, including essential medicines, defined team roles, and sustained support for training and monitoring. Clinical pathways thereby move from technical guidance to institutional commitments, enabling consistent delivery across health systems organized around a PHC approach.19

Successful implementation of the clinical pathway depends on enabling mechanisms that combine scientific rigor, practical feasibility, and consensus among key actors in the selection of prioritized interventions. These include Ministry of Health leadership; transparent and rigorous evidence review processes; engagement of scientific societies and academia; financing mechanisms that secure availability and access; competency-based training; supportive supervision; alignment with medicines and information systems; appropriate incentives; and continuous monitoring and quality improvement.7

However, adopting standardized clinical pathways often poses a major implementation challenge, as it shifts care from discretionary clinician-dependent practice to protocol-driven models. Evidence consistently shows that standardized approaches outperform non-standardized care in achieving population-level control, improving quality, and enhancing system efficiency.43, 44, 45, 46, 47 The policy implication is not to replace clinical judgment, but to define where standardization improves population outcomes and where flexibility is clinically warranted. If not carefully managed, this shift may generate resistance from professional groups, weaken adherence to implementation strategies, and limit scalability. Successful institutionalization, therefore, requires not only technical guidance but also engagement with professional norms, incentives, and regulatory frameworks.

Lever 3—access to medicines and diagnostic technologies

Ensuring availability of effective medicines48 and validated blood pressure devices49 is central to sustained implementation and continuity of care. Standardization supports demand aggregation and predictable procurement, positioning acquisition as a strategic function aligned with care delivery. It also creates conditions for greater affordability and supply stability.50 This requires regionally coordinated pharmaceutical and regulatory policies, including alignment of essential medicines lists, streamlined regulatory pathways, and robust quality assurance frameworks.

Access to essential medicines remains a persistent challenge across many countries in the Americas, including fragmented procurement, weak regulatory alignment, and limited demand aggregation, which contribute to price variability and supply instability.51 However, evidence indicates that price need not be a structural barrier: most antihypertensive medicines are widely available as generics, and can be procured at affordable prices when appropriate policy instruments are in place.52

The expansion of CKM care may increase budgetary pressures, particularly with newer therapies, creating difficult prioritization decisions. For HEARTS 2.0, access should be sequenced around high-burden, high-impact therapies that are affordable, scalable, and feasible for primary care delivery, including antihypertensives, preferably as single-pill combinations, statins, glucose-lowering medicines, and SGLT2 inhibitors. As evidence, affordability, and implementation conditions evolve, other therapies with cardiovascular and metabolic benefits, including GLP-1 receptor agonists and other emerging agents, may warrant future consideration.

Pooled procurement mechanisms enable countries to translate aggregated demand into improved affordability and supply reliability. Regional platforms, such as the PAHO Regional Revolving Funds, illustrate how pooled procurement can improve access to quality-medicines while reducing costs.53 Such mechanisms represent one of several policy options to strengthen access, depending on country context. Complementary strategies, including regulatory alignment and regional production, can further enhance resilience and long-term sustainability.54

HEARTS 2.0 offers an opportunity to leverage standardized clinical pathways as a market-shaping mechanism. By defining a prioritized set of medicines and diagnostic technologies, together with preferred formulations, strengths, and combinations, standardized pathways create a predictable demand signal that can guide regulatory, procurement, and industrial policies. This demand signal could be linked to the Region’s WHO-listed regulatory authorities and quality-assured local and regional generic manufacturers, whose existing or potential capacities may be underutilized. Connecting these capacities to HEARTS 2.0 priorities could help accelerate access to medicines and technologies, strengthen regional supply resilience, and, where economically viable, support local and regional manufacturing, reduce dependence on external suppliers, and contribute to broader economic development. In this sense, HEARTS 2.0 extends beyond a clinical strategy and may serve as a catalyst for aligning public health priorities with pharmaceutical and industrial policies.

Lever 4—digital health infrastructure

Digital infrastructure should be understood not as a parallel technological layer, but as a core health system capability for continuity, coordination, performance monitoring, accountability, continuous quality improvement, and supply chain functions.19,25 When well aligned with clinical pathways and effectively implemented, these tools can expand coverage, improve adherence, reduce costs, and enhance quality.55

Poorly integrated digital solutions may inadvertently increase administrative burden, contribute to workflow fragmentation, and reduce provider engagement if they are not aligned with frontline workflows and service delivery needs.

Underdeveloped health information systems remain a key bottleneck to scaling HEARTS across countries. Fragmentation of information systems often mirrors fragmentation in care delivery, limiting routine capture, integration, and use of service delivery and performance data. Interoperable electronic medical records and patient–provider portals remain limited, while gaps in interoperability, timely data availability, and system integration constrain clinical decision-making and health system performance.26

Yet, digital transformation creates important opportunities. Tools such as the Simple app demonstrate feasibility for large-scale monitoring.56 Telemedicine can extend the reach of primary care, particularly for underserved populations.57 Artificial intelligence may support decision-making and risk stratification, provided that it is implemented with appropriate governance, transparency, equity safeguards, and clinical oversight.58

Lever 5—strong primary care teams, workforce transformation, and quality improvement

The growing CKM burden exposes limitations of physician-centered models, creating demand for new education models and regulatory frameworks that enable expanded scopes of practice. In many settings, HEARTS implementation is further challenged by shortages and maldistribution of primary care personnel, high workforce turnover, and regulatory barriers that limit the participation of non-physician cadres in chronic disease management.

Task-sharing is a key response to workforce constraints and barriers to access, but its implementation requires a shift toward team-based care, placing multidisciplinary primary care teams at the center of longitudinal chronic care delivery. Evidence shows that team-based care improves hypertension outcomes and is cost-effective.59, 60, 61 This model enables nurses, pharmacists, nutritionists, and other health professionals to contribute according to their competencies and regulatory frameworks, supporting medication management, adherence, patient education, and continuity of care. Accordingly, HEARTS has developed policy frameworks to strengthen nursing and pharmaceutical services as essential components of integrated chronic disease management.23,24

Strong primary care teams may also include community health workers (CHWs). Because their training, roles, and scope of practice vary substantially across settings, their contribution should be defined according to local competencies, supervision arrangements, and referral pathways. Where appropriate, CHWs can support health promotion, patient education, treatment adherence, patient tracking, appointment reminders, follow-up, home blood pressure monitoring, community outreach, and continuity of care.

Equally central is the institutionalization of continuous quality improvement as a core system function, supported by a culture of quality, routine use of programmatic data, and structured coaching and technical assistance. This approach shifts quality improvement from episodic supervision toward continuous learning embedded in routine service delivery. Sustaining this transformation requires competency-based training, incentives, financing, regulatory support, and information systems that enable primary care teams to monitor performance and act on results.18,19

Without adequate financing, supervision, regulatory support, and career incentives, workforce transformation efforts may remain fragmented or unsustainable, particularly in settings with chronic shortages of primary care personnel and high turnover.

Lever 6—engagement of people, institutions, and communities and collaborative implementation

Community engagement is a participatory process that shapes access, adherence, accountability, and responsiveness of people-centered PHC services.62 It shifts implementation from viewing communities as passive recipients to recognizing them as contributors to the design, organization, delivery, and performance of care. Community engagement functions as a determinant of system performance63 and as a mechanism for social accountability, helping ensure alignment with population needs and equity goals.

In the context of HEARTS 2.0, engagement extends beyond health promotion to include patient empowerment, health literacy, self-management support, treatment adherence, home blood pressure monitoring, and continuity of care. In this framework, patients, families, and communities contribute directly to achieving CKM outcomes. Operationalizing this requires structured and measurable mechanisms for participation that enable communities to understand program targets and the clinical pathway, track progress, and engage with primary care teams and local health authorities in shaping service delivery. Such mechanisms strengthen accountability and help align services with population needs.64

Collaborative implementation across institutions represents a distinct but complementary requirement. Coordination among ministries of health, academia, civil society, professional societies, and international partners creates an enabling ecosystem for coherent action and sustained implementation. The priority is not uniformity, but strategic convergence around shared goals and core standards, while allowing context-specific adaptation.

PHC-oriented implementation architecture and strategic considerations for scaling integrated CKM care

The six levers outlined above provide the enabling system functions that support a broader PHC-oriented implementation architecture for scaling integrated CKM care. Their importance lies not only in each lever individually, but in their collective capacity to align the core public health and health-system capacities required to sustain integrated CKM service delivery and improve measurable population health outcomes. Despite growing political recognition of the NCD burden, implementation and population health gains remain below expectations.65 This reflects not only financing constraints, but also persistent weaknesses in the organization and delivery care. While the levers are broadly applicable, their implementation must be adapted to country context and institutional capacity. The challenge is to translate priorities into coordinated action and effective service delivery through primary care.

In this context, renewed emphasis on PHC as a strategy for health system transformation, reflected in regional initiatives such as the Alliance for Primary Health Care in the Americas66 and the World Bank–PAHO–Lancet Regional Health Americas Commission on Primary Health Care,67 has created a favorable policy environment and new financing opportunities for advancing integrated health service delivery through a PHC approach. Thus, PHC provides the strategic framework for health system organization, while primary care serves as the main delivery platform.

Consistent with this strategic direction, Better Care for NCDs, a flagship PAHO initiative, is promoting regional and country-level collaborative platforms to coordinate actions across technical areas and strengthen primary care for integrated NCD care. The initiative seeks to align programs around three mutually reinforcing objectives: prioritizing high-impact interventions for NCD care; strengthening delivery models through integrated and people-centered care; and reinforcing system functions—including governance, financing, information systems, medicines and technologies, and workforce—needed to sustain quality care.68

Within this architecture, HEARTS operates as an established implementation mechanism for translating evidence into integrated CKM care. It links population-level detection and risk stratification with protocol-driven and team-based care. HEARTS 2.0 builds on this foundation by institutionalizing integrated CKM care through standardized clinical pathways, quality improvement, aligned system functions, and more consistent service delivery across health systems.7,18,19

Collectively, these elements define the PHC-oriented implementation architecture proposed in this analysis. The architecture comprises four interdependent components. PHC provides the strategic framework for organizing health systems around integrated CKM care. Better Care for NCDs serves as a collaborative platform to align programs, technical areas, and investments around shared implementation priorities. HEARTS 2.0 operationalizes the PHC-oriented strategy through two complementary mechanisms—the HEARTS Clinical Pathway and HEARTS Quality—which translate policy priorities into routine primary care delivery, continuous quality improvement, and measurable outcomes. Their implementation is supported by six enabling system levers that facilitate scale, adaptation, and long-term sustainability. Across all components, cross-cutting implementation enablers—including policy support, partnerships, contextual adaptation, learning systems, and essential public health capacities—shape implementation feasibility, health system transformation, and sustainability.

Although discussed here through HEARTS 2.0, this implementation architecture is not disease or program-specific. Its core components—a PHC-oriented health system, a coordination platform, enabling system levers, and cross-cutting implementation enablers—are applicable to other complex, multicomponent programs that seek to strengthen integrated service delivery through primary health care. In this architecture, HEARTS 2.0 serves as the implementation mechanism for integrated CKM care and illustrates how standardized clinical pathways and continuous quality improvement can translate policy priorities into routine practice and measurable outcomes.

Within this implementation ecosystem, multiple actors and initiatives contribute to scale-up, making coordination both essential and challenging. As this ecosystem evolves, diversity in actors and approaches remains both a strength and a potential source of tension. The challenge is not to reduce diversity, but to align diverse efforts around shared objectives. Implementation is therefore a multilevel process requiring complementary roles across actors and institutions.

Consistent with WHO,69 integration means linking prevention, risk assessment, treatment, and long-term management for CKM conditions within a broader PHC approach. Integration and inter-programmatic collaboration require operational coherence across actors and system levels through shared pathways, aligned standards, compatible system functions, and consistent measurement.19 Primary care teams, therefore, become the operational hub where these functions converge in routine service delivery.

Achieving such coherence requires implementation strategies that remain grounded in feasibility, country context, and available evidence. Poorly designed or overly complex approaches that seek to force integration across CKM conditions, or across the broader NCD agenda beyond existing system capacity, may be particularly problematic in under-resourced settings and produce unintended consequences, including diverted attention, misallocated resources, weakened stakeholder engagement, and erosion of credibility. These risks reinforce the importance of pragmatic sequencing, adaptation to country context, and implementation strategies that balance ambition with feasibility.

Hypertension management represents a practical entry point for such sequencing: a high-burden condition with effective and scalable interventions, strong alignment with primary care delivery, and clear policy options to improve access and adherence, including removing patient copayments for essential medicines.70,71

Finally, sustaining this implementation of architecture also requires rethinking the role of research. Implementation research should function as part of the learning system for scale-up, not as a parallel academic activity. Embedded within programs such as HEARTS, it can support continuous adaptation, generate actionable evidence, and accelerate learning during implementation.72

Way forward

The current context presents a significant opportunity to expand integrated hypertension management and CKM care while advancing PHC in the Americas. Renewed political commitment to PHC strengthening, hypertension control, and integrated CKM care—together with advances in medicines, including antihypertensive single-pill combinations, lipid-lowering therapies, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, and delivery innovations such as team-based care, pooled procurement, quality improvement, and digital health—creates favorable conditions for accelerated implementation.

The challenge is to organize health systems to deliver effective interventions at scale. Progress will depend less on generating new clinical knowledge than on aligning governance, financing, workforce transformation, procurement systems, digital infrastructure, and accountability mechanisms with the requirements of primary care delivery.

Achieving sustained progress will require coordinated domestic and international investment aligned with service delivery priorities. When investments remain disconnected from delivery models, they risk fragmenting care, duplicating efforts, and weakening delivery capacity. Programs such as HEARTS can help align resources, technical cooperation, and system functions around measurable delivery goals and scalable models of care.

Improvements in clinical care and service delivery must be accompanied by stronger public policies addressing social and commercial determinants of health, including unhealthy diets, dietary sodium reduction, elimination of industrially produced trans fats, tobacco control, harmful alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and barriers to equitable access to care.

The principal challenge is not what to do clinically, but how to organize implementation in real-world health systems. Countries are more likely to translate the current window of opportunity into measurable population health gains only if implementation is organized around primary care as the platform for integrated, continuous, and accountable CKM care. Within this agenda, HEARTS 2.0 provides a practical implementation mechanism for translating renewed commitments to PHC into scalable delivery model and measurable improvements in population health across the Americas.
