 SUMMARY
 This joint report assesses the state of technology development and research
needs for pyroprocessing of used nuclear fuel from fluoride-based molten salt
reactors, conducted between Idaho National Laboratory and Argonne National
Laboratory. The goal of this report is to identify the research and development gaps
needed to reduce the technical risks of extending pyroprocessing technologies and
unit operations to molten fluoride salts used in molten salt reactors (MSRs).
Assessing and performing the tasks outlined in this report would serve to mitigate
the technical risks and provide the technical information needed to design
appropriate flowsheets for reprocessing fuel and coolant salts.
 Each section identifies a processes or unit operation that may be used in
fluoride-based chemistries and that needs to be assessed. Research and
development needs are listed and discussed, including molten-fluoride-based
separations unit operations such as electrowinning chemical treatments, reference
electrode development, materials compatibility, fluorination methods, and waste
disposition.
 This report examines the technical challenges associated with pyroprocessing
in molten fluoride-based salt and outlines approaches to increase the technical
readiness level of pyroprocessing in molten fluoride salts.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Pyroprocessing spent nuclear fuel uses high-temperature electrochemical methods to recover valuable
actinides and reduce the volume and radiotoxicity of nuclear waste. This process is conducted in a molten
salt medium and enables the separation of uranium, transuranics (TRU), and fission products through
methods such as electrorefining and electrowinning. The recovered actinides can be fabricated into new
fuel, which enhances resource utilization and contributes to closing the nuclear fuel cycle.
 Pyroprocessing was identified as one of a series of processes that could be used to purify and enhance
the service lifetime of molten salt reactors (MSRs) at the Molten Salt Reactor Fuel Cycle Chemistry
Workshop in 2023 [1]. Extending existing pyroprocessing techniques to MSR salts could extend the
service life of these emerging technologies, reduce the volume of high-level radioactive waste, enable the
recovery of uranium and TRU for reuse in reactors, and contribute to a more robust and economically
viable nuclear energy ecosystem.
 This report focuses on reprocessing in molten fluoride salts relevant to MSRs, identifies where
literature assessments are needed, identifies existing technical gaps, and details how pyroprocessing
approaches may be integrated to develop flowsheets for cleanup and recycling of fluoride salts in MSRs.
 Fluoride salts, such as lithium fluoride (LiF), beryllium fluoride (BeF2), zirconium fluoride (ZrF4),
and sodium fluoride (NaF), exhibit high thermal stabilities, high ionic conductivities, and excellent
solubilities for actinides and lanthanides [2–4], which may enable adopting pyroprocessing for molten
fluoride salts. Pyroprocessing in molten fluoride salts is at a significantly lower technology readiness
level (TRL) compared to pyroprocessing in molten chloride salts, and several technical knowledge gaps
must be filled before commercial applications can be pursued. Addressing the technical gaps listed in this
report is crucial for the development of safe and efficient reprocessing technologies for advanced
fluoride-based MSRs. Table 1 lists technical knowledge gaps and areas for assessment in fluoride salt
systems.

 Several types of flowsheets can be developed after assessing the technical gaps identified in Section
3. These flowsheets may incorporate several processes to effectively separate problematic compounds and
purify the fluoride salt for reuse [6]. Process steps may include electrolysis operations to remove
actinides, lanthanides, and other fission products from the salt; precipitation operations to separate fission
products as insoluble compounds; and fluorination operations to re-fluorinate actinides for return to the
salt and to separate volatile fission products. Combinations of these non-exhaustive processes may be
incorporated into flowsheets that can be adopted to reprocess or purify fuel salts or coolant salts [7–8].
Although this report focuses on adapting pyroprocessing to molten-fluoride MSR-fuel salts, insights
gained during these assessments will also be applicable to other fluoride salt-based processes and removal
of trace fission products from fluoride-based coolant salts.
 3. TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS
 3.1. Develop Molten-Fluoride-Salt Unit Operations
 The following molten-salt unit operations are designed to separate useful actinide species from the
molten fuel salt and remove problematic fission products such as rare earth elements from the molten salt.
Many of these unit operations rely on properties that are either poorly documented or not available in the
literature. Conducting literature assessments and measuring these properties will inform the selection of
appropriate salt purification methods and facilitate the development and refinement of relevant process
flowsheets.

3.1.1. Assess Feasibility and Demonstrate Electrowinning from Fluoride Salts
 at Bench and Engineering Scale
 Electrowinning molten fluoride salts effectively removes actinides and rare earth elements as metals
at the cathode while producing fluorine gas at the anode. Many fundamental aspects of electrowinning in
fluoride salts can be adapted from chloride-salt pyroprocessing techniques. However, neither bench-scale
nor large-scale demonstration of electrowinning in fluoride salts has yet been achieved within the nuclear
industry. To advance the TRL of these processes, industrial practices such as the Hall-Héroult process and
rare earth metal production can be adopted and utilized in reprocessing of fluoride-based MSR salt
systems.
 Assess Redox Potentials of U/TRU and Fission Products in Molten Fluoride Salts
 Assessing the redox potentials of U/TRU and fission products in molten fluoride salts is necessary to
determine whether electrowinning can effectively recover actinides with little or no fission product
contamination for re-fluorination and can effectively remove lanthanides for disposal.
 Demonstrate Electrowinning of Uranium/Thorium and TRU in Molten Fluoride Salts
 Demonstrating that uranium, thorium, and transuranic electrochemical deposition can be
accomplished in molten fluoride salts at relevant salt compositions with minimal fission-product
contamination will provide confidence that pyroprocessing methods originally developed for molten
chloride salts can be performed in molten fluoride salts. Product morphology and form factor should be
considered in relation to re-fluorination operations and efficiencies. This is a high priority task.
 Demonstrate Drawdown Electrolysis to Recover Fission Products for Disposal
 Electrochemical deposition and disposition of fission products from the molten fluoride salt is
necessary to maintain effective pyroprocessing conditions and to minimize waste volume. Demonstrating
the effectiveness of drawdown electrolysis will enable comparisons with other separation methods and
will help to determine which processes should be incorporated into flowsheets.
 Assess Utility of Liquid Metal Electrodes for Electrowinning Operations
 Liquid metal cathodes have been employed in molten chloride salts to achieve co-deposition of
problematic species and remove active metals that cannot be electrochemically recovered using inert
cathode materials. Assessment of candidate fission products and liquid metals may be performed as part
of this work.
3.1.2. Oxide Precipitation
 Oxide precipitation in molten fluoride salts can leverage the different oxophilicities of each salt
constituent to precipitate insoluble oxides that can be collected for long-term disposal. Assessment of the
oxophilicities of fission products should be performed. Scalable collection methods and disposition
pathways should be considered in the course of this work.
3.1.3. Melt Crystallization
 Fractional crystallization can be used to remove accumulated fission products while recovering
purified salt. The recovered purified salt can be recycled back to the process, and the recovered fission
products are processed for disposal. Idaho National Laboratory has successfully demonstrated chloride
salt recovery by crystallization, but tests are needed to determine the efficacy of salt recovery for fluoride
salts, scale-up performance, and parameter optimizations.
3.2. Identify and Demonstrate Scalable Fluorination Processes
 A fluoride-based reprocessing system will require the fluorination or re-fluorination of multiple kinds,
morphologies, and scales of materials. The following non-exhaustive list of fluorination and re-

fluorination processes seeks to achieve three goals: separating volatile fluoride impurities by fluorinating
used nuclear fuels and leveraging differences in fluoride vapor pressures, re-fluorinating insoluble
impurities such as those formed during ingression of moisture and oxygen, and re-fluorinating metallic
products generated during electrowinning operations.
3.2.1. Assess the Impacts of Insoluble Impurities and Identify Methods for
 Fluorinating Insoluble Contaminants
 Insoluble compounds can form in molten salts during operation due to ingression of environmental
contaminants (e.g., oxygen, moisture). Re-fluorinating these compounds will maintain salt conditions and
improve process efficiency. Necessary tasks include identifying key fluorides that form insoluble
impurities during pyroprocessing operations, assessing their impact on pyroprocessing operations,
identifying effective fluorination methods for fluorinating these insoluble compounds, and assessing the
scalability of these methods.
3.2.2. Assess Fluoroxidation to Remove High Vapor Pressure Impurities
 Volatility separation in molten salt systems can remove impurities by leveraging the differences in
vapor pressure of fission products. Literature values should be collated to identify candidates for
fluoroxidation. Fluoroxidation of salts has been demonstrated in the literature and is at a higher TRL than
other processes identified in this report.
3.2.3. Demonstrate Scalable Fluorination Processes for Actinides
 Uranium, thorium, and other transuranics removed from the molten fluoride salt using
electrochemical methods will need to be re-fluorinated and subsequently be reintroduced into the MSR
salt. Identifying and demonstrating scalable and efficient methods for actinide re-fluorination is a high-
priority task. Additionally, the morphology and form factor of the actinides to be re-fluorinated must be
carefully considered to optimize the re-fluorination process. Addressing these factors will ensure that the
reintroduced actinides maintain their desired chemical properties.
 3.3. Develop Reference Electrode Suitable for Molten Fluoride Salts
 A stable reference electrode is crucial for any electrochemical unit operation, but few well-tested
reference electrodes exist for molten fluoride salt process control. Developing a robust and effective
reference electrode is a high priority for developing pyroprocessing in molten fluoride salts. The reference
electrode membrane material must demonstrate exceptional chemical stability, ionic conductivity, and
resistance to degradation in the molten fluoride salt medium. Additionally, the thermal expansion
coefficients of the membrane and housing materials must be compatible to avoid mechanical stress and
potential cracking during thermal cycling.
 Investigate Multiple Types of Reference Electrodes for Molten Fluoride Salts
 Possible reference electrode candidates may include commercially available systems, common
construction materials for housing and membranes (e.g., yttria-stabilized zirconia and alumina), more
exotic materials (e.g., Boron nitride, ShapalTM, LaF3), reference electrodes with and without porous frits,
fill solutions such as silver-based Ag/AgF or Ni/NiF2, saturated reference electrodes, buffered solution
reference electrodes, or molten-alloy-based reference electrodes. Development of these electrodes should
include detailed specifications and guidelines to ensure proper usage and compatibility with specific
molten fluoride salt compositions and operating conditions.

 Survey Materials for Long-Term Contact with Molten Fluoride Salts

 Investigating material compatibility for electrochemical experiments in molten fluoride salts is
essential to ensure accurate and reliable results. Molten fluoride salts can dissolve protective oxide layers
on metal surfaces and can corrode or degrade materials used in electrodes, containers, and reference
electrodes. Using incompatible materials can introduce contaminants, alter electrochemical properties,
and produce erroneous data, thereby compromising the integrity of the experiments. Therefore, careful
selection and testing of materials are critical to maintaining the validity and accuracy of electrochemical
studies in these harsh environments.
 Survey Materials for Compatibility with Fluorine Gas Evolution
 Proposed electrowinning processes will generate corrosive fluorine gas. Materials compatibility in the
presence of fluorine gas presents a high technical risk. Exotic metal alloy selection for fluoride-based
pyroprocessing is a critical aspect of designing systems that can withstand the harsh conditions
encountered during the reprocessing of spent nuclear fuel.
 Assessment of Fluorine Off-Gas System for Disposition or Reuse
 An off-gas treatment system is needed to remove fluorine gas evolved at the anode during
electrowinning operations. Suitable materials are needed to withstand the high operating temperature and
the presence of fluorine gas. Off-gas treatment systems could be coupled directly to re-fluorinate metallic
products or simply be captured by dry or wet media for reuse or disposal.
 3.5. Formulate and Develop Waste Forms
 Immobilized fluoride-based salt waste needs to be formulated, and fluoride-based salt waste forms
need to be demonstrated using surrogate salt systems. This task is a high priority as waste forms are
critically important to fluoride-fuel molten-salt systems because they provide a safe and stable method for
immobilizing and disposing of radioactive fission products and other hazardous materials generated
during operation. Effective waste forms must be chemically stable, resistant to leaching, and capable of
withstanding long-term geological storage conditions without degrading.
3.6. Asses the Impacts of Fluorobasicity on Molten-Fluoride-Salt Unit
 Operations
 The availability of free fluoride ions in the molten salt (termed fluorobasicity) may impact the
solubilities, redox potentials, and volatilities of dissolved species in fluoride-based salts and may also
affect the viscosity of the salt. In general, salts consisting primarily of fluorodonors such as alkali-metal
fluorides (e.g., FLiNaK) are more fluorobasic, and salts containing fluoroacceptors such as alkaline-earth-
metal fluorides (e.g., FLiBe) are more fluoroacidic [9]. In this way, fluoride anions in molten-fluoride
salts are analogous to hydroxide anions in aqueous systems, a fluorodonor such as KF is analogous to a
hydroxide donor such as KOH, and a fluoroacceptor such as BeF2 is a Lewis acid.
 Dissolving transition-metal, lanthanide, and actinide fluorides into a molten salt mixture decreases its
fluorobasicity by lowering the concentration and activity of free fluoride ions. The fluorobasicity of a
given fluoride-based salt mixture is a function of the fluorobasicity of the base salt (e.g., FLiNaK or
FLiBe), the fluorobasicities and concentrations of the dissolved actinide species, and the fluorobasicities
and concentrations of the dissolved activation, fission, and ingression products.
 While differences in fluorobasicity should be considered when comparing the electrochemical and
thermophysical properties of various molten salt compositions, the overall effect of fluorobasicity on the
development of process chemistries and flowsheets for reprocessing molten salt reactor (MSR) salts is
anticipated to be minimal. Fluorobasicity can influence the solubility, stability, and reactivity of different
species within a salt mixture. However, given the broad range of operational conditions and the
robustness of current reprocessing technologies, the variations in fluorobasicity are unlikely to
significantly impact the efficiency or efficacy of the chemical separation and purification processes.
Consequently, prioritizing the assessment of fluorobasicity is considered a low-importance activity

 5

compared to other factors such as redox potential, corrosion behavior, and thermal stability, which play
more critical roles in the optimization of MSR salt reprocessing methodologies.

 4. CONCLUSION
 Research on fluoride salts for reprocessing is essential to adopt existing pyroprocessing techniques to
molten fluoride salts and extend the service life of next-generation MSRs. This document identifies
technical knowledge gaps and suggests potential experimental approaches. The recommended salt
systems for investigation consist of KF/NaF/LiF/BeF2 base salts containing UF4 and/or ThF4 as
fissionable and fertile materials. For each of these salt systems, the primary areas to focus on are:
• Developing Molten-Fluoride-Salt Unit Operations
• Identifying and Demonstrating Scalable Fluorination Processes
• Developing Reference Electrode Suitable for Molten Fluoride Salts
• Surveying Suitable Structural Materials for Molten-Fluoride-Salt Unit Operations
• Formulating and Developing Fluoride Waste Forms.
 5. FUTURE WORK
 This report addresses various factors affecting the molten fluoride salt reprocessing process and
identifies near-term needs for developing appropriate flowsheets for reprocessing fluoride-based MSR
salts. Long-term research needs that could be explored include the impact of intermediary species and
how they might alter salt chemistry, especially in thorium-bearing salts. Additional work may also be
necessary to either remove or isolate protactinium.
 Another important aspect to explore is understanding the stability of molten fluoride salts by using
Ellingham diagrams. These diagrams are beneficial because they graphically represent the temperature
dependence of compound stability, particularly oxides, in terms of Gibbs free energy. Ellingham diagrams
aid in comprehending and predicting the conditions under which metals will oxidize or reduce in a given
electrolyte.
