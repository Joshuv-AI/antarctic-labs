Trauma and Violence Informed Research and Evaluation: A Perspective Paper for Mental Health Nursing
Authors: Sophie Isobel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere are increasing guides and expectations for being trauma informed in research practices. This perspective paper builds upon this emerging field of literature to consider what it means to be trauma and violence informed in mental health nursing research and evaluation. Being trauma and violence informed requires all the same in‐the‐moment application of the principles of trauma informed care when engaging with participants or data, while also contextualising trauma in the context of wider social contexts. This includes recognising the role that structural violence plays in compounding and sustaining trauma for groups and individuals. To be trauma and violence informed in research requires attention to the power that research and evaluation hold and ongoing reflection on the implications of the design, measures, outcomes and findings of research and evaluation, while informing all decisions by awareness of trauma and the forms of violence that cause and sustain it.

Introduction

Trauma informed approaches to research emphasise safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment across the research process, when engaging with communities or individuals likely to have experienced trauma (Isobel 2021c). Trauma and violence informed (TVI) approaches strengthen this idea by integrating a wider understanding of the conditions that perpetuate trauma and marginalisation, including interpersonal and structural violence (Liao et al. 2025), alongside understandings of trauma. Structural violence refers to systemic ways that individuals are socially, political, economically or epistemologically disadvantaged, compounding exposure to harm and perpetuating inequities (Farmer et al. 2006). Structural violence commonly unfolds gradually in ways that are difficult to attribute directly to a specific cause (Price and McGowan 2025). This can occur in mental health care delivery, as well as in knowledge production related to the development, care and recovery from mental illness. Health inequities, including mental health and the failure to adequately address the social contexts of increased distress, are also forms of structural violence (Price and McGowan 2025).

TVI approaches actively consider structural violence in the process and outcome of research, while still focusing on how to be sensitive to trauma experienced by individuals. This makes them highly relevant to mental health nursing research and evaluation which commonly occurs within the limitations of mainstream health services, focuses on the experiences and outcomes of people living with mental illness, and contributes to evidence‐based practice and practice‐based evidence. Mental health nurses are well placed to consider what it means to be trauma and violence informed when engaging in such activities.

Aims

This paper aims to present a perspective of what it means to be trauma and violence informed in mental health nursing research and evaluation.

Background

To be trauma informed in the delivery of care is to understand how trauma impacts people and to adjust the way care is delivered to be sensitive to this. Across practice, policy and the literature, being ‘trauma informed’ is a key tenet of mental health nursing practice and an increasing expectation of all service delivery. To be trauma and violence informed in care requires understanding of the dynamics of violence, alongside its impacts (Isobel 2025). TVI is a refinement of trauma informed approaches to centre the cause of trauma, alongside its impacts. It requires attention to the context of people's lives, including structural violence that contributes to disproportionate impacts for groups and individuals (Isobel 2025). Structural violence can be enacted throughout care delivery, or through any system that holds power, including research.

In some ways, being trauma informed is already about being violence informed. But as concepts settle into use, their meaning becomes clearer and gaps also become more identifiable (Isobel 2025). The focus of many trauma informed approaches is on individuals, including what might have happened to them and why they may be responding as they are. This reinforces the idea that the effects of trauma are located in the individual (Wathen and Mantler 2022). Trauma informed approaches can contribute to structural violence rather than disrupt it, if the relationship between systems and interpersonal impacts are not considered (Liao et al. 2025), or it is used to ‘other’ people who experience trauma (Isobel 2021b) or blame trauma survivors who fail to respond positively to service's well‐intentioned efforts to be trauma informed (Isobel 2021a).

TVI approaches account for the intersecting impacts of systemic and interpersonal violence and structural inequities on people's lives (Wathen et al. 2023). This situates people's experiences of violence and trauma as occurring in wider social and political contexts. For example, people who have intersectional psychosocial stressors are disproportionately represented in mental health services and are also less likely to receive effective or sustained care (Butler et al. 2025). To be TVI challenges reductionist ideas of trauma that focus on individual traumatic events and subsequent responses. Rather, it is a socio‐ecological approach that highlights how structural or systemic forms of violence, such as discrimination or marginalisation, can compound the effects of other forms of violence (Nkurunziza et al. 2024). Understanding experiences of structural violence can also help with understanding why people may distrust or disengage from services, or experience compounding or repeated disadvantages (Wathen et al. 2023). TVI approaches make explicit links with feminist and anti‐oppressive frameworks, highlighting power, privilege and structural inequities. This is crucial in mental health nursing which involves providing care for, and advocating for, communities who experience stigma and injustice in society.

Design and Method

This is a critical reflection on what it means to be trauma and violence informed (TVI) in research and evaluation, particularly in a mental health nursing context. It draws on clinical and academic experience and evidence to consider the epistemological implications of trauma and violence informed approaches to research and evaluation, beyond the ‘in the moment’ trauma sensitivity recommended in many guides and resources. Parts of the paper are written in first person by a mental health nurse academic, in line with qualitative critical paradigms (Webb 1992) and in keeping with the humanised approaches of being TVI.

Positionality

The perspective shared in this paper is written from my position as a mental health nurse academic, based on years of championing trauma informed care into services and praxis, and experiencing conflicting feelings about the iatrogenic and compounding harms that can occur when systems play a role in perpetuating or causing harm, despite being trauma informed. I draw on my own experiences as a nurse, a researcher and a non‐indigenous woman who has experienced trauma but speaks from a privileged space as an academic and clinician, not a survivor. This paper took longer than expected to write as I repeatedly questioned the assumptions I was making about research, paradigms and power. It is still an imperfect perspective that is limited by my positionality and evolving understandings of the nuance of what it means to be TVI in research. This work is a step in an ongoing evolution of my thinking, which initially focused on trauma informed care within nursing practice (e.g., Isobel and Edwards 2017), before shifting to services and systems (e.g., Isobel, Wilson, Gill, and Howe 2021; Isobel, Wilson, Gill, Schelling, and Howe 2021). Later I began applying ideas of being trauma informed to my nursing research (Isobel 2021c) and then wider work across the sector (Isobel et al. 2024). More recently the tensions inherent in trauma informed approaches have led me to trying to understand the role of being trauma and violence informed (Isobel 2025). This paper aims to bring ideas of TVI and research together to articulate a wider scope for trauma and violence informed research, with a particular focus on mental health nursing.

Building on Trauma Informed Approaches

In mental health nursing research, the participants engaged, or communities impacted, ‘research’ have often experienced trauma. Mental health nursing research may focus on sensitive topics, exploring the delivery of care including nurse or client experiences, or evaluating processes of care that may have themselves been traumatising. In addition, research processes may unintentionally replicate power dynamics that can be reminiscent of trauma. It is therefore increasingly recognised that it is important to be trauma informed when undertaking research. There has been a lot written about what it means to be trauma informed within mental health nursing care and practice. Yet as mental health nursing embraces the approach, it makes sense to consider that to be trauma informed is not a stance that is turned on and off in clinical practice, but a holding of knowledge that becomes relevant across domains, including research.

Being trauma informed in research involves enacting trauma informed principles of safety, choice, collaboration, trustworthiness and empowerment ‘in the moment’, while also considering trauma in the context of how studies are designed, undertaken and disseminated (Isobel 2021c). Trauma informed research includes considering what safety means in a research context, how to manage dissociation or dysregulation during data collection, and ensuring trustworthiness of ourselves. It can also mean choosing methods that reduce exposure to distressing stimuli or allow participants control over pacing, depth and format of participation, avoid unnecessarily invasive or extractive data collection, ensure ongoing mechanisms of consent and build trust (Alessi and Kahn 2023). These elements are important and can guide nurses in the processes of doing research with communities or individuals who may have experienced trauma. Even when research or evaluation does not focus on traumatic experiences, at least some of the participants in mental health settings (and any setting) will likely have experienced trauma histories (Alessi and Kahn 2023). Therefore, being actively trauma informed is relevant to all projects, regardless of the focus or method.

There are various guides and frameworks for trauma informed research available. TVI approaches do not replace these approaches but build on them to centre the wider context of trauma and to consider the ways that experiences can be compounded by systems of disadvantage, including research itself. Being TVI has a synergistic alignment with mental health nursing. Mental health nursing is similarly socio‐ecological in its basis and invested in the broader conditions of people's lives, including their social determinants of health and experiences of intersecting forms of disadvantage. This provides a solid theoretical basis from which to consider how this translates to research.

Paying Attention to Power

To be TVI requires attention to power, and attention to research and evaluation as agents of power. The research questions we ask, or the measures we use to evaluate programs, services or interventions, reinforce power. Power can often be understood as a negative force (as in, power over, or power taken), but power is something people use, generate and have, which can also lead to transformation and solidarity. Power can be productive and generative, giving power, contributing to power, but it also carries its own epistemic power, or power of legitimising knowledge and what is known (Lechner et al. 2026). In healthcare, research holds the power of being the evidence upon which practice is based. The biomedical model that underpins the mental health system is built upon evidence, constructed by research and demonstrable outcomes. Research, in this context, reinforces power, generates power and legitimises social forces that hold power.

Recognising the power that research holds and generates, also requires attention to how we can share that power. Empowerment is a dynamic concept related to the active sharing or giving of power (Kuokkanen and Leino‐Kilpi 2000). It is also perhaps the least clear of the principles of trauma informed care, as it can be confusing to consider how anyone can empower anyone else and what sort of power is being shared. People are not empowered in research because we decide that they are. The power that we share is interpersonal, positional, institutional but also social and political. As nurses we can share the power that we have of how processes work and advocate for people who perhaps have less power than we hold in systems and services. In research we can share power through transparency and collaboration, we can also share power through relationships, building trust and supporting people's agency and participation. We can share power that we hold but we can also facilitate opportunities for people to gain more power for themselves in other ways (Kuokkanen and Leino‐Kilpi 2000). If we think beyond ‘in the moment’ acts of empowerment, empowerment is about challenging the ideologies that justify social inequality, changing prevailing patterns of access to and control over resources and transforming the institutions and structures that reinforce and sustain existing power structures (Batliwala 2007). Research and evaluation contribute to all of these processes, particularly when done critically. Even small projects undertaken in clinical settings can contribute to challenging, shaping how resources are spent and driving change.

Paying attention to power can lead to discomfort. It can be confronting to consider how structural harm is replicated in clinical care and similarly, it is confronting to recognise that structural harm is also replicated in research, including that which seeks to improve outcomes for communities. Structural violence in research is enacted through overt means such as excluding marginalised groups from recruitment or data collection due to systemic and practical barriers, for example people who may not speak English, can't read information sheets or can't travel to required sessions. People may also be excluded for diagnostic or social complexity, whether that is having unclear diagnoses, co‐morbidities, active substance use, unsafe living environments or gender diversity. Research designs can fail to account for intersectional experiences, for example by focusing on shared experiences of illness without considering contributing factors or ignoring power by recruiting people while they are engaged in care or accessing clinical settings. Ethics review processes can also inadvertently reinforce structural violence by positioning some groups or individuals as vulnerable, or even ‘too vulnerable’ to include. It is not to say that these decisions may not be important and ethical but considering them through a TVI lens allows for consideration of how they impact upon what is legitimised as knowledge and what is left out.

Structural violence can also be contextual. Mental health services experience their own structural violence within the health system by being underfunded and lacking research infrastructure, within which nursing research is even less supported. It is not uncommon for nursing research to be paused when there are competing clinical priorities within services, research nurses being redeployed to clinical roles or nurses being unable to be released from clinical roles to apply for seed funding or other opportunities. Nurses may also feel ‘powerless’ to speak up against those perceived to have more power in services or research, who shape how studies are undertaken.

In mental health research and evaluation, ideally care is taken to not replicate misuses of power throughout studies. This includes considering how we write about or ‘frame’ the needs and presenting issues of people accessing services, how we ensure their agency and personhood are protected within research and how we centre the wider context of people's lives as crucial to understanding their current experiences. Frames shape how we see and think about people, which problems are identified and what solutions are sought (Allchin and Isobel 2024). Without becoming aware of the framing of our research and evaluation, there is a risk that even ethical research can perpetuate harm as a form of structural violence. Intersectional awareness is also important to ensure we don't benevolently ‘other’ (Grey 2016) participants or develop an idealised sense of awe, or conversely, pity or paternalism in how we share their experiences or data.

Payment for participation is a relevant topic when considering sharing power with communities whose voices have been devalued and minimised, including people living with experiences of mental illness. Payment for participation is an ethical issue (Różyńska 2022), commonly addressed by ethics committees. Payment isn't always possible or appropriate in service evaluation or small nurse‐led projects, yet excluding the voices of people who may have wanted to contribute without payment can also be paternalistic and may contribute to further invisibility or obscuring of diverse lived expertise. This is also amplified by systemic obstacles related to how people can get paid for participation and who is set up to do so. Trauma is deeply interwoven with social exclusion and systemic inequities (Liao et al. 2025). This is reinforced when groups or individuals are considered ‘hard to reach’ or too vulnerable to include in research (Liao et al. 2025). It can also be reinforced if research becomes extractive or transactional, with payment both minimising coercion and at times, enhancing it. There is no clear answer to this conundrum, with payment an important component of valuing contributions. Reducing therapeutic misconception, or the belief that participation will lead to personal benefit or improved care (Cleary et al. 2008) is crucial and can be enacted through transparency, alongside paid engagement of lived experience advisers and staff within services to collaborate in the design and undertaking of all research and evaluation. For mental health nurses, being TVI offers a reminder of the need to consider complexity and messiness. As Bouka (2018) observes: ‘there remains an urgent need to have more robust conversations about the ethics that are unique to collaborative research between resource‐rich and resource‐constrained institutions and researchers’ (Bouka 2018).

While it is a primary intent of trauma informed approaches to empower survivors, in services that require diagnoses and acuity for care (such as mental health services) there are paradigmatic obstacles. Trauma survivors or their associated distress are inevitably positioned as ‘the presenting problem’, rather than centring the violence that may have precipitated or perpetuated these experiences. As people are presenting to services wanting help for their distress, it is important they receive this and TVI requires both: responding to the present and centering social, interpersonal or structural dynamics that compound distress. In the same way, people experiencing domestic and family violence may present to housing services needing safe housing and it is important they receive this. The role of the housing service may not be to have to end violence; yet, housing can be supported in ways that empower people, recognise their strengths and agency and ensure great care is taken to not replicate dynamics of violence or become caught up in systems abuse. Systems abuse occurs when systems get manipulated as a tactic of violence (Reeves 2020), but can also occur as part of stigma and structural violence (Jackson and Sadler 2022).

Something that helped me to understand what it means to be trauma and violence informed rather than ‘just’ trauma informed, was work in the field of domestic and family violence. While in mental health services it is not uncommon for disagreements about prevalence of trauma to derail efforts towards being trauma informed, in domestic and family violence services there are no disagreements about prevalence rates of trauma, nor a repeated need to have to justify the cause. All clients are presumed to have trauma, and it therefore is an essential responsibility of services and workers to be trauma informed. In addition, while people may have experiences of lifetime trauma or exposure to past traumatic events, trauma is also presumed to be happening in the present, with all practice needing to be cognisant of violence dynamics and not contribute to them. Rather than focusing on how to support people when the past is infiltrating the present (as is often the case with trauma informed approaches), the focus is on supporting people in contexts of ongoing misuses of power and dynamics of coercion. But perhaps the main key difference is the push driven by victim‐survivors to ensure the violence is centralised as ‘the problem’. This requires a focus on delivering person centred care to victim‐survivors while taking great care to ensure language and actions don't misattribute any blame or responsibility onto the victim‐survivor. These shifts are helpful in noticing ways what it means to examine all practices (including research) through a lens informed by knowledge of trauma and violence.

The Specific Challenges of Service Evaluation

It is purposeful that this paper speaks of research and evaluation. Many mental health nurses are engaged in service evaluation activities which form a part of research or lead to future research, and which play an important role in documenting existing practice and shaping health service delivery and funding. Considering TVI in the context of service evaluation reaches across the divide of clinical practice and academia to recognise the value and power of service‐based projects that contribute to knowledge.

Sometimes in healthcare, and particularly in nursing contexts where there is limited time for non‐clinical tasks, service evaluation is an after‐thought. We may design a project or enact a change, possibly identifying some ways we plan to evaluate it based on accepted approaches, but considering how to gather data, what assumptions underpin the identified data points, or how meaningful the analysis is can be positioned as separate to the ‘doing’ of the work. There are many projects where the report at the end of an initiative doesn't really match the felt experience of what occurred. This may be because it is an attempt to formalise evaluation using expected measures, or it can reflect service expectations. Sometimes evaluations of interventions use ‘expected’ measures without thinking critically about these in relation to individuals. For example, many mental health nursing initiatives report rates of engagement or length of stay or attendance rates at interventions, even though these don't match what we know of how trauma can impact upon people who experience distress or how power may influence people's willingness to engage. It may not always be possible for people to engage or stay engaged with services. We don't know if a short length of stay means a rapid recovery or a breakdown of trust and we don't know if people stay engaged with care out of choice or coercion. Even asking people how they found services can be complicated due to power: fear of retribution can be a long‐lasting component of trauma, and it may not ever feel safe enough to tell services how people really experience them.

TVI Can Be a Lens for Analysis

Equally as important to what data we gather in research and evaluation and from whom, is how we analyse it. Data analysis commonly requires us to homogenise experiences, whether it is to find shared themes or report descriptive statistics or collate practical recommendations, but this contradicts the highly personal nature of trauma. There can be a dismissal of things and groups who are messy or complicated in research. They are excluded as outliers or not meeting criteria or being unreliable data sources. But dissent and messiness are to be expected (Speyer and Ustrup 2025), as they are a part of many lived experiences of trauma.

Approaches to analysing data in ways that consider the impacts of trauma and violence include using mixed methods approaches, or multiple sources of data, and including critical reflection upon findings (Wathen and Mantler 2022). This doesn't have to mean large shifts in approach, but a curiosity to unpredictable results, meaningful outcome measures and considering structural inequities and their impacts. At times this requires considering what data to include of exclude, but it can also require looking at ‘success’ critically and considering consumer or client outcomes in the context of workforce and organisational measures (Wathen et al. 2023). It is also important to not overly simplify trauma informed approaches to mean measuring or reporting trauma specific measures as this can undermine efforts to ensure that trauma informed approaches are seen as widely applicable regardless of reported trauma (Wathen et al. 2023). TVI Requires Reflection and Accountability

TVI research should avoid extracting narratives from those with the least power while giving little back. To counteract this, co‐design with consumers, peer workers and communities can support more informed approaches to recruitment and topic selection. Ideally power is shared prior to study development through meaningful co‐design and community engagement, to ensure research is benefiting the people who participate. As there is an increasing expectation of co‐design and participatory approaches to research, greater attention is needed to developing structures and systems to enable this to occur in meaningful ways, prior to studies being formally funded. This may mean establishing advisory groups of people with lived experience of trauma and frontline clinicians to generate research questions, select meaningful outcomes, interpret findings and co‐design dissemination outputs. Ideally, addressing structural violence in research requires ensuring community engagement across all stages of research as well as shifts towards community‐led research.

Awareness of the impacts of structural violence also extends to staff. Many mental health nurses are expected to evaluate their own practice to justify funding or staffing. There can also be pressure to complete research projects as part of training or to meet performance indicators, pressure to contribute to well‐established research groups whose agendas may differ to those of frontline staff, or pressure to evaluate services in ways which ensure ongoing funding. These factors can limit individual staff's capacity to ‘do’ TVI approaches. It is a risk of trauma informed approaches, and therefore TVI approaches, that responsibility lands on individuals for systemic issues (Isobel 2021a). While this requires ongoing reflection, being trauma and violence informed also requires accountability.

There are risks even when trying to be TVI. Risks include that knowledge production uses well intentioned collaboration to reinforce dominant narratives, or interpret the experiences of people who live with violence and trauma through limited biomedical or positivist paradigms. The failure to acknowledge other forms of knowing and understanding, including Indigenous or Consumer‐Survivor knowledge, can itself be an act of structural violence. Yet for mental health nurses, working within systems built on biomedical and colonial knowledge, disruption can be a challenge and at times not possible. As nurses, we are not accountable for the entire mental health system or all failings of practice or academia, but we are accountable for how we practice, advocate for ourselves and people who access services, and how we engage in research.

While reflective practice is recognised as important in mental health nursing practice (Thomas and Isobel 2019), it is also important in research. All research is underpinned by assumptions and all researchers hold bias (Uwizeye et al. 2025). Reflexivity requires in‐depth consideration of our own subjectivity, conscious and unconscious responses to the research process, and how we impact upon the outcomes (Uwizeye et al. 2025). Reflexivity can support self‐awareness and self‐care. It also requires consideration of power dynamics within teams, how methodological decisions are made and the wider context in which research is situated (Olmos‐Vega et al. 2023). Reflexive conversations can be helpful in getting to know some context of the research team, detailing assumptions and learning, alongside an overview of who is conducting the work and why. It also requires disruption of positivist assumptions that practice, experiences of mental distress or the efficacy of care can be measured or documented without paradigmatic influence. Being TVI positions us in the work. TVI reflexivity humanises research and enhances trustworthiness. It is also an acknowledgment of being aware of the contexts of trauma and violence and of looking at the wider context and implications of people's lives.

Alongside humanising researchers in relation to the influence they have upon research, to be TVI also requires active attention to the ways that research and evaluation can impact upon us as researchers. We are humans working in the human business of knowledge production and self‐care means recognising what we need to look after our ‘selves’. To hold the stories or data of others can be impactful, especially in transitioning from the therapeutic relationship to a research one (Isobel 2021c). Thus, while trauma informed research guides self‐care in the form of noticing the impacts, engaging in supervision and pacing the work (Alessi and Kahn 2023), being trauma and violence informed requires recognising our own motivations and framing of the work to ensure we do not re‐enact dynamics of structural violence for participants or for ourselves.

Conclusion

The structural violence that informs being TVI is a ‘slow’ violence (Price and McGowan 2025). Slow acts of violence can occur when diffuse extended forms of harm are overlooked or even accepted within mainstream systems and practices (Price and McGowan 2025). The first step of becoming trauma and violence informed, is humility to recognise its importance and relevance and to think critically about accepted ways of knowing and doing. To be trauma and violence informed in research also requires us to recognise the power that research and evaluation hold, alongside the positional power of our profession. Being TVI isn't a box to tick, or a static state, it requires researchers to think about the implications of the design, measures, outcomes and findings that they present and publish, while informing all decisions by awareness of trauma and the forms of violence that cause and sustain it.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

This perspective paper has implications for mental health nursing practice, particularly where nurses are routinely involved in research, quality improvement and service evaluation or occupy dual roles as care providers and knowledge producers. Mental health nurses are uniquely positioned to shape how evidence is generated and translated into practice and, as such, carry responsibility to consider how research and evaluation can reproduce or mitigate structural violence. Being TVI in research supports nurses to locate experiences, including those of mental illness, distress or trauma, within understanding of structural, institutional and interpersonal violence. Without this awareness, research can become extractive, coercive or reinforce frames of risk and vulnerability. Being TVI also has implications for nurse wellbeing. Mental health nurses conducting research are exposed to narratives of trauma and systemic failure, often without adequate organisational support. A TVI approach contextualises the impacts of this work and situates the impacts on individuals within broader systems, supporting reflective practice, supervision and shared responsibility.
