A Serverless Pharmacogenomic Risk Dashboard: Translating Ensemble Models and Model‐Based Scenario Rules to Clinical Decision Support
Authors: R Jerome Dixon, Elvin T Price
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe “last mile” problem in healthcare AI—translating high‐performance models into accessible, privacy‐preserving point‐of‐care tools—remains unsolved for pharmacogenomic (PGx) risk assessment. No existing platform integrates opioid and polypharmacy risk scoring, model‐based scenario analysis, and CPIC‐based PGx patient cards within a single privacy‐first serverless architecture. We designed, developed, and implemented the PGx Risk Dashboard: a serverless system combining an S3‐hosted static frontend with AWS Lambda containerized backends. Fifty‐six bin‐level ensembles (2 cohorts × 7 age bands × 4 density bins), each comprising CatBoost/XGBoost/XGBoost‐RF base learners, are packaged within an AWS Lambda container, with aggregate fallback artifacts used for sparse cells. Partial‐input imputation using training‐set medians handles real‐world data sparsity; the PGx Patient Card executes stateless CPIC lookups for 573 deterministic gene‐drug logic‐verification cases without storing any PII. Lambda cold‐start latency was mean 2100 ms (SD 250 ms); warm inference latency was mean 6 ms (SD 1 ms), meeting the sub‐100 ms target. Prediction stability was maintained under sparse inputs (≤ 5 features provided): mean |Δp̂| = 0.10 vs. full‐feature baseline. The PGx Risk Dashboard demonstrates technical feasibility for low‐latency, privacy‐first PGx risk decision support without EHR integration. We maintain the association‐versus‐causation distinction to support safer clinical use, avoid overstating treatment‐effect evidence from observational outputs, and preserve a clear path to prospective causal‐effect validation as next steps.Keywords: clinical decision support, CPIC, explainable AI, opioid risk, pharmacogenomics, privacy‐first design, serverless architecture

Introduction

High‐performance ML models for clinical risk prediction face a well‐documented translational barrier: even when performance metrics satisfy publication standards, models rarely reach the clinical workflow [1, 2]. A systematic review of 84 FDA‐cleared AI/ML‐based medical devices found that fewer than 30% included prospective clinical validation evidence, and fewer than 10% reported deployment architecture details sufficient for independent replication [3]. Barriers to deployment include: privacy concerns about processing patient data in cloud systems, inability to handle incomplete real‐world inputs, lack of interpretable explanations for non‐ML‐trained clinicians, and the complexity of deploying and maintaining inference infrastructure at health‐system scale [4].

Infrastructure complexity is particularly acute for partition‐first ensemble models: up to 168 serialized base learners (2 cohorts × 7 age bands × 4 density bins × 3 algorithms), organized as 56 bin‐level weighted ensembles, must be accessible at inference time. Each ensemble was trained independently per utilization‐density stratum (25‐split MCCV + Optuna per bin). The serverless architecture collapses these artifacts into a single Lambda container with in‐process routing, eliminating inter‐service API latency overhead.

PGx testing results are personal genomic data requiring HIPAA‐compliant handling, yet existing tools (CPIC lookup systems, institutional pharmacogenomics portals) are siloed with no integration of model‐based prediction [5]. The PREDICT program [6] demonstrated 10‐year sustainability of EHR‐embedded PGx CDS, but required institutional EHR integration unavailable to community pharmacies and Safety Net hospitals.

This paper presents the PGx Risk Dashboard: a privacy‐first serverless application we designed, developed, and implemented to deliver (1) real‐time age‐ and density‐stratified risk inference from 56 per‐bin weighted ensembles (168 base learners); (2) imputation of normality for partial patient inputs; (3) model‐based scenario analysis from FFA‐derived synergy rules; (4) PGx Cohort network visuals that map top model‐important drugs to gene‐drug and pathway context; (5) stateless ephemeral CPIC‐based PGx Patient Card; and (6) browser‐accessible operation without EHR integration.

The dashboard is a “last‐mile” translational implementation: it assumes trained, performant models from prior work and focuses on technical feasibility—making those models usable, trustworthy, and accessible to non‐data‐scientist clinicians in real‐world prescribing contexts, with prospective outcome studies reserved as next steps.

Methods

System Architecture

Design Philosophy

Four principles address the deployment barriers identified in the literature: (1) privacy‐first—no patient data stored at any tier; ephemeral in‐memory compute is designed to minimize retained PHI, though HIPAA, state privacy, and Business Associate Agreement requirements still depend on deployment context (covered‐entity use, whether genomic files are uploaded, and institutional policies); (2) graceful degradation—unavailable models surface explicit user‐visible errors rather than silent out‐of‐distribution fallbacks; (3) partial‐input tolerance—Imputation of Normality maintains stable risk‐band assignments up to 60% input sparsity; (4) sub‐100 ms warm inference—in‐process routing and cached model/CPIC artifacts avoid per‐request external clinical‐knowledge calls that would otherwise make point‐of‐care inference impractical.

Serverless Deployment Model

The system uses a serverless deployment combining a static frontend and a containerized Lambda backend (Figure 1, Table 1). The figure shows how a single request flows from the browser through API Gateway to the Lambda runtime (per‐bin models, imputation, versioned CPIC cache) and back, with the ephemeral privacy boundary: it answers where inference runs, which artifacts are co‐located, and why patient‐linked data never persist. Alternatives (persistent EC2, browser‐side WASM, SageMaker endpoint) were rejected for continuous compute cost, large client‐side model‐download latency, and default inference‐log storage respectively. The serverless Lambda approach achieves zero idle‐time cost (billing per invocation), elastic scaling, and complete in‐memory ephemeral processing.

Partition‐First Routing

At inference time, the request handler computes two routing keys from the submitted patient data before loading any model:

Feature Vector Versus Routing

The count of submitted clinical codes only maps the patient to a density stratum (thresholds from training). The feature vector passed to CatBoost/XGBoost includes n_event_bin_ordinal (0–3) as the utilization intensity signal; the raw integer count and the string bin label are not used in place of drug/diagnosis indicators in x.

The first routing key is age band (13–24, 25–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, 85–114), determined from the patient's submitted age. The second routing key is density bin (low, medium, high, extreme), determined by comparing the count of submitted clinical codes against per‐age‐band threshold quantiles stored in a bundled JSON configuration shipped with the container image. This two‐key routing selects the precise per‐bin ensemble trained on the most epidemiologically similar patient subgroup—the same Age Band × Cohort × density‐bin cell used during 25‐split MCCV + Optuna fitting.

Rationale for Partition‐First Ensemble Design

Virginia APCD data for these cohorts are group‐structured by design: distinct opioid‐related versus polypharmacy emergency populations, seven age bands, and four utilization‐density strata with highly skewed event counts and sparse binary item_* predictors. Outcome prevalence, comorbidity burden, and which codes drive risk differ materially across groups; pooling or aggregating patients into one estimator would mix heterogeneous regimes and can obscure subgroup‐specific patterns—for example, averaging sparse‐bin behavior with extreme‐utilization tails, or blending geriatric polypharmacy drivers with young‐adult opioid trajectories. Preserving cohort × age band × utilization‐density cells follows age‐stratified clinical prediction practice [7, 8] and matches how cohorts were built and models were trained. The partition‐first DuckDB pipeline processes each skewed cell independently (without cross‐group shuffles), and Lambda routing loads the artifact for the submitted cohort, age band, and density bin so deployment reflects the same subgroup boundaries as training. The CPIC Patient Card shares those keys for pharmacogenomic lookup but addresses genotype–drug logic separately from claims‐based multimorbidity risk in the ensemble path.

Ages below 13 block inference; missing age blocks inference (mandatory primary routing key). All routing decisions are returned in the API response.

Model Storage Feasibility

Container image sizing was a key feasibility concern because AWS Lambda imposes a 10 GB limit on container image size. With up to 84 tuned base learners per cohort (3 algorithms × 4 bins × 7 age bands) organized as 28 bin‐level ensembles, a naive packaging approach would risk approaching this limit. Across both cohorts, the deployed bundle contains 56 bin‐level ensembles (168 base learners when all cells are populated). In practice, not all age band × bin combinations have sufficient training data to fit stable models. For these sparse cells, the deployment tree is completed with the corresponding age‐band/cohort aggregate model as an explicitly marked fallback artifact, preserving deterministic routing without presenting the model as bin‐specific.

Handling Real‐World Data Sparsity: Imputation of Normality

The Challenge

Clinicians rarely present a patient's complete 3‐year prescription history at the point of care. A patient profile with only age, sex, and 2 current medications is typical.

The Solution

Missing trajectory features are filled with training‐set medians, representing the “typical” patient profile for that age band. Provided ICD/drug codes drive the marginal risk prediction above or below the median baseline; imputed values provide context without fabricating signal.

Algorithm

1 . Receive patient inputs X_partial (k features provided, m features missing)

2 . Initialize X_full = training_median_vector[age_band]

3 . Overwrite X_full[provided_indices] = X_partial

4 . Pass X_full to ensemble inference

5 . Return (p_hat, top_shap_features, model_agreement)

Validation

Sensitivity analysis on the 2019 holdout randomly masked drug flag features in 10% increments (10%–90% missingness), with submitted‐code‐list length (for density‐bin routing) and PGx scores provided in full, reflecting the realistic clinical scenario where basic patient characteristics are present at all sparsity levels but specific drug counts may be incomplete. Mean |Δp^| between sparse and full‐input inference was < 0.06 across all sparsity levels up to 70% missingness (Figure 2). At extreme sparsity (≥ 99% drug‐flag missingness, where only the code‐count input used for bin routing and 5 random drug flags are provided), mean |Δp^| = 0.10 (SD varies by density bin), representing the upper bound of imputation error under real‐world worst‐case conditions. Risk‐band assignment (Low/Moderate/High) remained stable—meaning the patient was assigned to the same risk band as with complete input—for 87% of patients at 50% sparsity and for 74% at 70% sparsity, confirming that the imputation approach preserves the clinically relevant risk stratification even under severe real‐world data sparsity. Sensitivity analysis used 50 random masking trials on 2000 sampled patients from the young‐adult opioid cohort at low density bin.

Unlike MICE [9] (which estimates missing values from correlates), Imputation of Normality treats the median as a null‐hypothesis baseline: provided codes shift predictions away from the baseline only when evidence is present, producing a more conservative estimate than zero‐imputation (which would falsely imply no documented medication events).

Model Development and Validation

Ensembles were trained on Virginia APCD data (2016–2019) with the partition‐first pipeline summarized above. 2016–2018 calendar‐year partitions supplied Monte Carlo cross‐validation (MCCV; 25 random 80/20 splits per model‐partition), Optuna hyperparameter search (50 trials per combination), and performance‐based ensemble weights; 2019 was withheld from tuning, feature selection, FFA threshold derivation, and risk‐band calibration [10, 11]. Each deployable cell is a cohort × age band × utilization‐density bin with three gradient‐boosted learners (CatBoost, XGBoost, XGBoost‐RF) combined by fixed wi from training‐era MCCV metrics.

Training Matrix and Leakage Controls

Predictors are sparse claims‐event indicators (item_*) plus n_event_bin_ordinal; case rows use only pre‐index events; FP‐Growth, BupaR, and DTW outputs are visualization‐only and are not passed to the live inference vector x. Consensus SHAP–FFA attribution labels and bundled scenario rules are derived offline per partition for interpretation and UI filtering, not as additional runtime predictors.

Interpretability and Robustness Checks for Gradient‐Boosted Trees

With temporal holdout and partition‐stratified training, TreeSHAP on held‐out data and FFA symbolic rules with MCCV‐screened importances are the primary feature‐level validation toolkit—they quantify how each feature shifts predictions under the empirical claims distribution [12, 13]. A separate one‐at‐a‐time feature perturbation sweep largely duplicates SHAP dependence and global importance rankings when explainers are run on proper holdout partitions rather than training data alone. Ranking stability is assessed across MCCV folds and partition boundaries during upstream training. For this deployment paper, targeted robustness analysis focuses on realistic sparse inputs: random masking of drug flags on the 2019 holdout (Imputation of Normality; Figure 2; File S5), not generic ablation of utilization‐density routing or removal of n_event_bin_ordinal, which would either reintroduce confounding or mis‐specify the estimand within skewed APCD cells (the same rationale applies in observational pharmacoepidemiology analyses for raw pre‐index claim counts).

Baselines Not Re‐Estimated Here

This manuscript reports the technical feasibility of serving the production artifact library; we did not re‐run head‐to‐head benchmarks of (i) a single pooled XGBoost model across all age bands and density strata or (ii) a CPIC lookup‐only pathway without claims‐based ensemble scores—CPIC concordance testing (573 logic‐verification cases) evaluates genotype–drug action‐class rules, not APCD ED risk. Upstream development established that pooling mixes heterogeneous regimes [7, 8] and that density‐stratified boosting avoids raw n_events dominating splits; those choices are fixed in the deployment bundle (56 bin‐level ensembles; 168 base learners when all cells are populated) loaded at inference.

Upstream Modeling Artifacts in the Deployment Bundle

The Lambda image ships precomputed outputs from the same Virginia APCD modeling pipeline used to train the partition‐first ensembles (2016–2018 training; 2019 holdout withheld from tuning). Training features are sparse claims‐event indicators (item_* drug, ICD, and CPT codes)—occurrence in the lookback, not dose or pharmacokinetic exposure. Three definitions govern what the UI surfaces:

Consensus Filter (Interpretation Set)

Within each cohort, age band, and utilization‐density bin, a feature received the operational Consensus SHAP–FFA attribution label when its mean |SHAP| exceeded the 75th percentile of all features in that partition and it appeared in at least one Formal Feature Attribution (FFA) rule with support ≥ 0.05 and confidence ≥ 0.70 [14, 15, 16]. This label denotes dual‐method model agreement on observational claims, not trial‐confirmed interventional causation. The full sparse feature matrix still trains the ensembles; consensus‐attribution codes primarily define interpretation, allowed‐code lists for process mining, and scenario prioritization.

FFA Interaction Rules (Polypharmacy Cohort)

FFA extracts symbolic Boolean rules from gradient‐boosted trees and tests feature pairs and triplets for synergistic and antagonistic risk associations (independence‐ratio baseline). In the primary geriatric non‐opioid ED bands, 115 synergistic drug pairs and thousands of high‐risk triplets (IE > 1.0) were identified; for example, levofloxacin acted as a pharmacological hub in multiple top synergistic pairs. Synergy alerts in Scenario Analysis fire when a selected code participates in a bundled rule with IR > 0.10.

Allowed Codes and Trajectory Centroids

BupaR, DTW, and FP‐Growth visuals filter to the top 500 drug, ICD, and CPT codes by leakage‐filtered cohort feature importance per partition (allowed_codes_shap_ffa_{cohort}_{age_band}.json). DTW archetypes (Rapid‐Onset vs. Chronic‐Escalation) come from Ward‐linkage clustering on 12‐month pre‐index sequences over those allowed codes (k=2 clusters; visualization‐only, not ensemble predictors). Scenario bundles (combined_importance.csv, interaction JSON) merge normalized SHAP and FFA ranks for the active partition at runtime.

Interpretive Scope

Scenario predictions are grounded in FFA‐derived interaction rules and observed training‐distribution patterns, but they are not identified causal treatment effects. If a selected drug participates in a known synergistic pair or triplet (IR > 0.10), the system displays a supplementary “synergy alert” indicating a potential compound modeled benefit under the scenario. This framing follows standard causal‐inference distinctions between associational/model‐based prediction and identified interventional effects [14, 15, 16]. We maintain the association‐versus‐causation distinction to support safer clinical use, avoid overstating treatment‐effect evidence from observational outputs, and preserve a clear path to prospective causal‐effect validation as next steps.

Feature 3: Exploratory Visualizations (Tab 4)

The Exploratory and PGx Cohort tabs deliver complementary population‐context visualizations that answer the clinician question: “Why does this patient profile carry this risk level, and what do patients like this typically experience?” BupaR, FP‐Growth, and DTW are derived exclusively from training‐cohort data (2016–2018) and are explicitly labeled as population‐level context, not predictive outputs. The PGx Cohort network figure pack adds a pharmacogenomic interpretive layer by mapping top model‐important drugs to CPIC/PharmGKB genes, kinetic pathways, allergic‐response context, and underappreciated signaling modules.

BupaR Process Mining: Sankey Care Pathway Diagram

BupaR [17] process mining derives the most probable care pathways for the 25 nearest neighbors to the submitted profile in SHAP‐importance space, rendered as a Sankey diagram (nodes = clinical activity types; edge width = transition frequency; edge color = outcome gradient). This answers what SHAP bars cannot: which sequence of care events typically precedes the outcome in patients like this one. In the 25–44 opioid ED cohort, the dominant high‐risk pathway (opioid fill → dose escalation → gabapentin co‐prescription → lorazepam add‐on → OUD‐ED) appeared in 34% of training cases; patients who received physical therapy before the gabapentin step had a 58% lower transition rate to the escalation node.

FP‐Growth Drug co‐Occurrence Network

FP‐Growth [18] (support ≥ 0.01, confidence ≥ 0.50) produces a pre‐computed high‐lift drug association ruleset filtered at runtime to the current patient's prescription list (nodes = drugs; edge weight = lift; hub drugs at betweenness centrality ≥ 90th pct enlarged). Hub drugs are actionable deprescribing candidates regardless of individual IR score. In the 65–74 geriatric cohort, levofloxacin was the dominant hub (betweenness ≥ 90th pct in all three geriatric bands; four high‐lift clusters), consistent with FFA hub findings in which levofloxacin appeared in four of the top five synergistic pairs in that band. In the 25–44 opioid cohort, gabapentin and oxycodone were dominant hubs (lift 5.1 and 4.7). High‐lift rules are cross‐validated at runtime against the versioned CPIC cache: rules containing CPIC Level A substrates are annotated with gene‐drug flags (e.g., omeprazole [CYP2C19] + naproxen [CYP2C9] co‐occurrence, lift = 49 in the ED‐positive class), enabling the clinician to distinguish pharmacogenomically amplified co‐prescription patterns from coincidental associations and prioritizing rules where poor‐metabolizer status would further elevate exposure risk [19].

DTW Trajectory Archetype Overlay

The two DTW‐derived archetypes (Rapid‐Onset, 4.2 months; Chronic‐Escalation, 22.1 months) from pre‐index sequence clustering on allowed codes (12‐month windows; k=2 Ward clusters on the opioid ED cohort) are stored in the container as centroid sequences. At inference, the submitted event sequence is DTW‐aligned against each centroid (Sakoe‐Chiba band, w = 20%); the archetype with lower distance is selected and displayed with intervention windows annotated (30‐day PDMP review for Rapid‐Onset; PT referral and mental health windows for Chronic‐Escalation). This answers the clinician's timing question: has this patient passed the optimal intervention window? In 2019 holdout testing, archetype assignment was concordant with the outcome trajectory in 78% of cases (Rapid‐Onset: 74% of ED visits within 6 months; Chronic‐Escalation: 81% after 12 months).

PGx Cohort Network Figure Pack

The PGx Cohort tab adds a NetworkX/Plotly figure pack for browser‐based interpretation of the top model‐important drug features. The workflow resolves prioritized drugs to CPIC/PharmGKB genes, builds gene‐drug‐phenotype edges from VIP reports, and renders static PNG previews with interactive HTML. Its purpose is translational: to show why a model‐prioritized drug or cluster is clinically reviewable by exposing pharmacokinetic genes (e.g., CYP and transporter context), allergic‐response signals, and underappreciated pathway modules. The network is not used by the inference model and is not interpreted as mechanistic proof; it is a visual rationale layer that helps clinicians move from a risk score or scenario rule to a focused medication‐review question.

For institutional deployments, this privacy claim should be documented as a compute‐respond‐discard control in local security materials: request payloads are processed in Lambda memory, application logs should exclude genotype and patient identifiers, and covered entities should map the deployment to their HIPAA minimum‐necessary analysis and Business Associate Agreement workflow. This documentation is especially important for community pharmacies and urgent care sites that may operate the dashboard outside an EHR but still process genomic files on behalf of patients.

CPIC Integration Workflow

The workflow accepts 23andMe raw format (rsID/chromosome/position/genotype); invalid formats halt processing before any SNP data reaches Lambda. Diplotype‐to‐phenotype mappings from PharmVar and CPIC are applied for 12 high‐evidence PGx genes (CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, UGT1A1, DPYD, TPMT, SLCO1B1, OPRM1, COMT, HLA‐B). A versioned CPIC cache is queried for Level A/B drug‐gene pairs; the deployment workflow can refresh this cache from the official CPIC website before release and publish Excel/Parquet artifacts to S3 or the container so warm card generation remains local. A PDF card reports inferred phenotypes, actionable recommendations, CPIC evidence level, and opioid‐specific guidance.

CPIC Concordance Evaluation Method

The reported 573‐case concordance set is a logic‐verification dataset, not a prospective clinical outcomes cohort. Cases were generated from the versioned CPIC cache current at evaluation by enumerating deterministic phenotype‐action combinations for included Level A/B rules. Ground truth is the CPIC recommendation text associated with each rule in that cache. Concordance is computed as exact match between card output and expected CPIC action class. When the CPIC cache is refreshed from the official CPIC source, the same deterministic verification set is regenerated and re‐run so concordance is tied to the deployed CPIC version rather than a stale manuscript‐era file.

Ambiguous interactions are handled conservatively: when CPIC wording contains conditional caveats that cannot be resolved from provided genotype inputs alone, output is labeled “review required” rather than forced into a definitive class. Such cases are reported separately and are excluded from the 573‐case concordance denominator. For example, a recommendation that depends on renal function, hepatic impairment, concomitant inhibitor exposure, or indication‐specific dosing would be labeled “review required” if the dashboard receives genotype data but not the relevant laboratory value (e.g., eGFR) or clinical context. This preserves the role of the card as consultative support for pharmacist or prescriber review rather than autonomous prescribing.

Deployment and Reliability

Deployment and Release

Partition‐first ensemble training, SHAP/FFA extraction, and large‐scale APCD queries are performed upstream on persistent cloud compute because DuckDB cohort operations and per‐bin model fitting are impractical on Lambda or workstation hardware. Dashboard release then bundles completed model, CPIC, and visualization artifacts for the serverless frontend/backend described above, including PGx Cohort network PNG/HTML figure‐pack assets with public‐read/CORS checks. The CPIC release step prefers the official current CPIC gene‐drug Excel from the CPIC website, writes Excel and Parquet cache files, and publishes those versioned artifacts to the deployment package/S3 path used by Lambda. Implementation details, implementation materials, and deployment paths are documented in the project GitHub repository under 10_risk_dashboard/ and summarized in File S6 so the main text remains focused on translational feasibility rather than operations detail.

System Evaluation Summary

All 1000 benchmark profiles met their pre‐specified performance targets (Table 3); no tail‐latency outliers were observed, reflecting the predictability of in‐process routing vs. inter‐service architectures. Rule‐logic concordance was complete across the 573‐case CPIC verification set because CPIC recommendations are logic‐based (IF phenotype THEN action) and fully unit‐testable.

The dashboard delivers clinically relevant stratified risk estimates, CPIC‐grounded pharmacogenomic guidance, and model‐based scenario comparisons for prescribing review. This implementation demonstrates technical feasibility—deployment performance, sparse‐input stability, and rule‐logic consistency on the 573‐case verification set. Ensemble and scenario outputs remain associational and model‐based until intervention‐specific effects are confirmed through prospective causal testing, which is the explicit next step for outcomes and prescribing behavior.

Discussion

Resolving the Last‐Mile Problem

The PGx Risk Dashboard addresses three last‐mile translational failures: in a systematic review of 94 eligible studies, 0% combined XAI, PGx integration, and production deployment.

Privacy‐First Design

The ephemeral stateless architecture removes a primary institutional barrier to PGx tool adoption—reluctance to upload genomic data to systems that retain records—by delivering PGx guidance without persisting patient‐linked inputs in application storage [5]. Unlike the Epic Sepsis Model (which stores all inputs in the EHR), the ephemeral‐compute approach decouples clinical decision support from patient‐record retention. Regulatory obligations (HIPAA minimum necessary, Business Associate Agreements, state PDMP rules) remain context‐dependent on who operates the deployment and whether genotypes are uploaded; the architecture reduces retained PHI but does not replace institutional compliance review.

Comparison with Institutional PGx Platforms

The PREDICT program [6] demonstrated decade‐scale sustainability of EHR‐embedded PGx CDS with genotype results in the medical record. Our dashboard targets a complementary use case: browser‐based risk scoring and CPIC cards without EHR integration, suitable for community pharmacies, urgent care, and Safety Net sites that cannot fund full genomics informatics programs. Trade‐offs include manual code entry and no automatic medication list import; in contrast to a permanently frozen knowledge base, the CPIC layer is a versioned cache that can be refreshed from the official CPIC website and republished to S3/container artifacts before release while preserving low‐latency local lookup during patient requests.

Implementation in Resource‐Limited Settings

Because the frontend is a static site and inference is serverless, there is no on‐premise database to administer; operational burden is limited to AWS account management and periodic artifact refreshes when models or CPIC guidance update. In routine use, the dashboard is best positioned as consultative support during medication reconciliation, pharmacist review, or discharge planning rather than autonomous prescribing. Sites without dedicated informatics staff can still use the tool for consultative review, provided they accept manual input workflows, designate an owner for CPIC/model‐version updates, validate local privacy policies before processing genomic files, and document the compute‐respond‐discard workflow in local BAA/security‐review materials.

Partial‐Input Tolerance

Imputation of Normality closes the training‐deployment gap (comprehensive 12‐month lookbacks vs. ED visits with 3–5 known medications): risk‐band assignment is stable up to 60% sparsity; mean |Δp^| < 0.06 at 70% missingness.

Scenario Interpretability

Figure 3 shows how the Scenario Analysis sub‐tab moves clinicians from pre‐calculation configuration (panel A) to post‐Calculate Risk Score feature‐level and interaction‐level views (panel B: FFA bars, SHAP importance, per‐feature outcome effects), supporting model‐based scenario comparisons when optional codes are supplied. This offers deprescribing and care‐path hypotheses that a single risk gauge alone cannot provide [4], while still requiring prospective validation before causal‐effect claims [14, 15, 16].

Limitations

Five limitations apply. First, although the CPIC layer is now treated as a versioned cache that can be refreshed from the official CPIC website and republished before release, deployments still require lifecycle governance: the cache version should be displayed or logged, the verification set should be re‐run after each refresh, and a 90‐day staleness warning or automated version‐check should alert maintainers when the evidence base may need review. Second, no prospective clinical validation has been performed; a pilot study (≥ 200 encounters; 6‐month follow‐up) is required before broad deployment. Third, the implementation currently accepts 23andMe raw format only; next‐step parser work should prioritize VCF v4.3 and FHIR R4 Genomics so the system can accept clinical laboratory and EHR‐integrated genomic data rather than consumer‐genomics files alone. Fourth, the system may be subject to FDA SaMD oversight (21 CFR Part 820) before commercial clinical deployment, though research use is excluded [3]. Finally, because scenario outputs are generated from observational‐pattern models rather than intervention assignments, they should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating decision support rather than identified treatment effects [14, 15, 16]. Planned enhancements include mobile‐responsive frontend, real‐time PDMP integration, multi‐lingual card generation, VCF/FHIR genomic ingestion, and federated model update [20]. For causal‐effect questions, the explicit next step is a causal‐identification study design (e.g., target‐trial emulation or quasi‐experimental methods with pre‐specified assumptions) followed by prospective evaluation.

Conclusions

The PGx Risk Dashboard translates trained ensemble models, scenario rules, and PGx guidelines into a privacy‐first serverless implementation that demonstrates technical feasibility for low‐latency decision support without EHR integration. Three architectural contributions address last‐mile deployment barriers:

Contribution 1: Partition‐First Single‐Container Inference

Routing all 56 bin‐level ensembles (168 base learners) through in‐process partition logic achieves 6 ms warm inference without inter‐service overhead, eliminating the orchestration complexity (service discovery, load balancing, circuit breakers) that makes multi‐service deployments operationally prohibitive for research teams.

Contribution 2: Imputation of Normality

Median‐initialization maintains risk‐band stability up to 60% input sparsity and |Δp^| < 0.06 at 70% missingness, resolving the complete‐data assumption that disqualifies most published ML models from practical deployment.

Contribution 3: Stateless PGx Card

The ephemeral‐compute CPIC card delivers pharmacogenomic guidance with no application‐layer retention of genotype or patient identifiers and without requiring EHR integration. Institutions may still require BAAs or local security review depending on who operates the deployment, but the compute‐respond‐discard pattern gives them a concrete control to document for sensitive‐data processing without retention.

These features advance the translational pipeline from APCD‐scale modeling toward bedside decision support, showing that privacy‐first serverless delivery is technically feasible with trained models, a cloud account, and architectural attention to privacy‐by‐design. We maintain the association‐versus‐causation distinction to support safer clinical use, avoid overstating treatment‐effect evidence from observational outputs, and preserve a clear path to prospective causal‐effect validation as next steps.
