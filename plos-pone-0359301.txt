Impact of social media platforms excessive use on students performance in higher education institutions: A mathematical model approach

Komi Afassinou, Narcisse Roland Loufouma Makala, Ousmane Koutou, Richard O Ocaya, Maba Boniface Matadi

ABSTRACT
This article develops a compartmental mathematical model to investigate the impact of excessive social media use on the academic performance of higher education students. Students who are heavily subscribed are classified ‘infected or addicted’ individuals. Their influence on the other naive non-subscribers leading them to become one and by self-curiosity are defined as direct and indirect transmission routes, respectively. The model incorporates two primary control interventions: early awareness programs and continuous regulation of accessibility. Qualitative and numerical analyses of the model dynamics are thus carried out investigating the effectiveness and the benefits of these control measures. The results suggest that a combined strategy, implementing both control interventions at optimal efficacy levels, significantly reduces the ‘addiction’ rate, thereby fostering a healthier academic environment and promising a prospective academic performance from the students.

Introduction Social media platforms such as TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, and others reign supreme in the modern digital landscapes, captivating the attention of the youth worldwide with their addictive contents (videos, photos, vlogs, live-streams) and with their possibilities of one to express his/her personal opinions (likes, shares, comments, emoticons, and so on). According to Pew Research Center report in [ 1 ], approximately all teens (97%) are now social media users, of whom 88% saying they use it daily. In 2022, a report in [ 2 ] highlighted that teens spend an average of 7.5 hours per day on screens with social media accounting for nearly a third of that time. This may result from the rapid growth of the number of mobile devices incorporated with different social networking applications that flowed into the digital world in the last decade [ 3 ]. The number of subscribers on each of these social media applications have grown exponentially and contents are uploaded millions per second. As of 2024, Facebook is the largest social media platform with 3.07 billion monthly active users worldwide, followed by YouTube (2.5 billion), WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, WeChat, etc..[ 4 ].. This pervasive usage of these platforms have caused so many problems and raised concerns across different sectors: In health sectors, professionals have identified a strong link between excessive social media use and mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, and loneliness. Within educational sectors, these entertainment channels constitute a major source of distraction among students, leading to procrastination of their studying time and sleep deprivation, therefore compromising their study flow and ultimately hindering focus and academic achievement [ 5 ]. Governments and higher education institutions have a grieving concern about the national financial support (loans, bursaries, and other grants) wasted due to poor student academic performances linked to this issue. The challenge of excessive social media use among university students can be identified to drug abuse or illicit substance addiction, as frequent use of social media releases dopamine, a chemical substance in the brain, associated with pleasure, reward processing and habit formation [ 6 ]. The multi-faceted damages caused by this phenomenon demand urgent and evidence-based interventions to preserve the integrity of the academic environment. While the roles of educators and policy-makers are critical, mathematical modelling offers a unique quantitative framework to simulate the efficacy of potential solutions. So far, the majority of research documented in this field has relied on longitudinal studies and qualitative reviews [ 7 – 10 ]. Nevertheless, Ali et al. , in [ 11 ], successfully mapped social media use as a homogeneous contagious trend via a mathematical model. Similarly, Alemneh and Alemu [ 12 ] developed a compartmental model incorporating social media awareness and addiction control strategies. Some few compartmental mathematical models including [ 13 , 14 ] were also proposed in this regard. However, the authors in these pioneer social media addiction compartmental models widely focused their analysis on single-route peer-to-peer transmission, while overlook the dual nature of transmission, specifically the distinction between peer-to-peer influence and direct student-to-media contact. This underscores a significant gap remaining to be addressed in compartmental mathematical models to social media addiction studies. This article addresses this gap by developing a model that partitions the student population into distinct classes based on their level of platform engagement and susceptibility. By explicitly branching the susceptible and infected channels, the model thus builds upon existing literature by isolating how institutional intervention interacts with direct exposure to additive social media contents. The primary objective of this study is to investigate the dynamics of control interventions–specifically early awareness programs and accessibility regulation–and their impact on student academic outcomes. By conducting qualitative stability analysis and numerical simulations, this study aims to provide a theoretical cornerstone for institutional decision-making regarding digital wellness policies. Model formulation A total population of N s ( t ) students at a time t , who are assumed completely susceptible, are mutually co-habiting with a certain number N i ( t ) of so-called infected students; that is, the active subscribers I ( t ) and the addicted subscribers A ( t ). These students are very active on n distinct social media, say, ℳ 1 , ℳ 2 , ℳ 3 , ..., ℳ n , available within a given educational institution. These social media platforms contain M 1 ( t ), M 2 ( t ), M 3 ( t ), ..., M n ( t ) amounts of additive contents (videos, photos, vlogs, live streams, etc...), at time t , respectively. The model then partitions the susceptible students population N s ( t ) into two distinct compartments: S ( t ) and S p ( t ) ; respectively composed of purely susceptible students S ( t ), of whom a proportion γ is enrolled in counselling and awareness programs about the harmful impact of excessive use of social platforms on their academic performances; these later form the compartment S p ( t ) . Thus, N s ( t ) = S ( t ) + S p ( t ) . (1) The remaining proportion ( 1 − γ ) S , not aware, is then exposed to the risk of excessive social media use and become infected under the attraction of the force of infection λ . Upon being infected, they are classified into I ( t ) compartment. Moreover, one may admit that some students from S p ( t ) compartment, despite the awareness programs priorly received, may fail to obey and become infected. Thus, a proportion ( 1 − ε p ) of S p ( t ) is assumed becoming infected due to the pre-exposure awareness failure and thus generate a compartment denoted by I p ( t ) . The parameter ε p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) accounts for the efficacy level of the pre-exposure awareness programs implemented within the institution. Upon developing obsessive interest in social media contents, infected students I and I p become addicted at rates ρ 1 and ρ 2 , respectively, then move into the compartment A ( t ). Thus, the total infected students population N i ( t ) is composed of N i ( t ) = I ( t ) + I p ( t ) + A ( t ) . (2) Commonly, during their break time or after class, students waste their study time sharing their opinions about ongoing trending videos on social media or debating about an actor’s role in particular episodes broadcast, for instance, on national TV, Showmax, Netflix, or others. The topics are usually brought out by infected students (the active/addicted subscribers), of which the non-subscribers (the susceptible) feel clueless about. As a result, the exposed susceptible develops a curiosity and search for the content in the social platform indicated. We thus assume that a susceptible student becomes infected through infected peer’s influence during contacts; and this occurs, at a rate η with an active subscriber I , or at a rate η p with I p subscriber, or with an addicted subscriber A , at a rate η a . Note that 0 < η p < η < 1 < η a , to just classify the level of infectiousness of each infected individual. Moreover, infection may also occur through a direct contact with the contents M 1 ( t ) , M 2 ( t ) , . . . , M n ( t ) , when a susceptible student independently of peer’s influences decides to create an account on these platforms; and this, at the rates η k , 1 ≤ k ≤ n , respectively. Usually, this spans from students who priorly were willing to join those entertaining channels but never had access to free Wi-Fi as that of the institution. The force of infection is thus given by λ = η p I p ( t ) + η I ( t ) + η a A ( t ) N ( t ) ⏟ peer-to-peer transmission + ∑ k = 1 n ( 1 − ε r ) η k M k ( t ) . ⏟ student-to-media transmission (3) In Eq (3) , the parameter ε r ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) accounts for the level of the regulation of social media accessibility within the institution, whereas N ( t ) = N s ( t ) + N i ( t ) is the total student population. We next assume that the infected students from I ( t ) and A ( t ) compartments contribute the most into the addictive contents’ growth by frequently sharing or uploading contents in each of the social media platform ℳ k , at the rates θ and θ a , respectively. However, these contents M 1 ( t ) , M 2 ( t ) , . . . , M n ( t ) progressively lose students’ interest or attention, at a rate σ k , 1 ≤ k ≤ n , respectively. Students from I p ( t ) , I ( t ), and A ( t ) are excluded from the institution due to the poor academic performance, and leave the institution, at the rates δ 1 , δ 2 , and δ 3 , respectively. Whilst students are recruited through purely susceptible compartment S ( t ), at a constant ra t e Λ , a non-social media rela t ed exit from the institution occurs in all compartments, at a rate μ . Note that for “mathematical convenience”, we will opt to analyse the model, considering two most use social media within a given institution; that is, n = 2. Model findings and practical applicability for higher education leaders To maximize the practical translation of this seven-compartment model into strategic institutional governance, our mathematical pathways are mapped directly onto actionable policy levers. By converting abstract parameter values into concrete administrative interventions, university leaders can interpret the model results through the two primary operational dimensions as follows: Proactive prevention and compliance metrics ( γ , ε p ): The mathematical transition of naive susceptible students in compartment S ( t ) into the protected aware compartment S p ( t ) is governed by the enrolmen t rate γ and its implementation efficacy level ε p . In a non-mathematical context, the model findings highlights that introducing digital wellness campaigns ( γ ) is insufficient if the message fails to resonate and reached target students. University leaders must treat ε p as a institutional benchmark for engagement, measuring the quality, absorption, and behavioural impact of mandatory freshman orientation workshops and structured campus-wide digital detox challenges through visual signs. The model thus confirms that maximizing ε p actively shrinks the downstream “infection” pathways before students develop chronic academic impairments. Structural environmental interventions ( ε r ): The model incorporates the parameter ε r to represent an external, structural control on direct social media addictive contents exposure ( M k ). For teaching and learning officers and students academic wellness leaders, this represents technical infrastructure policies rather than behavioral appeals. Implementing social media accessibility restrictions ε r translates to the strategic blocking, network throttling, or bandwidth-capping of high-traffic, and non-academic media endpoints on institutional Wi-Fi networks. Rather than deploying blanket campus censorship, the model supports a localized strategy: enforcing high network constraints ( ε r ) within dedicated academic sanctuaries such as lecture halls, laboratories, and core library spaces during peak instructional hours, while maintaining unhindered access in residential and recreational spaces. The model findings suggest that successfully implementing the above items will reduce the control reproduction number ℛ c below unit, thereby confirms a total eradication of social media addiction within the student population. Ultimately, these aforementioned items demonstrate that the control reproduction number ( ℛ c ) is not be regarded as an abstract threshold, but a decision dashboard tool. In addition, our model promise that by systematically increasing proactive education ( γ , ε p ) and balancing structural social media regulations within the university ( ε r ), the university administrations possess a mathematically validated blueprint to suppress campus-wide digital contagion, improve academic performances, and protect institutional academic integrity. Discussion This study was motivated by the prevalent degrading and addictive habits of excessive social media use observed among the majority of higher education learners, which often result in poor academic performance and therefore trigger increased drop-out rates. We thus formulated a mathematical model that partitions a population of students into susceptible, active (infected), and addicted students using mathematical modelling. The addiction transmission dynamics are governed by the interactions between the susceptible students and typical distracted students and direct contract with social media contents. The study proposes awareness campaigns and reinforcement of social media accessibility policies within each institution as control interventions to address these problems. The model’s qualitative analyses were rigorously undertaken, including positivity and boundedness of the model solutions. The model’s equilibrium points and stability analyses inform that the model undergoes a forward transcritical bifurcation; a phenomenon that guaranteeing that maintaining the number of new students developing social media addiction habits below one ( ℛ c < 1 ) would be sufficient to detoxify the active/addictive students from the addiction and purify the learning atmosphere. The model simulation results validate the qualitative results and indicate that both control interventions, when consider either as a single strategy, would be beneficial in averting new students’ indulgence into such habits but may demand more effort to refocus the already distracted students. A combined strategy of both control interventions at optimal efficacy levels promise a healthier academic environment and improving overall student performance. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Ali et al. [ 11 ] and Alemneh and Alemu in [ 12 ] who demonstrated that awareness-based interventions can mitigate the spread of social media addiction. However, unlike Ali et al. [ 11 ], who modelled social media addiction primarily through homogeneous interpersonal interactions, the present study explicitly distinguishes between peer-to-peer transmission and direct student-to-media transmission through addictive online contents. In addition, while Alemneh and Alemu [ 12 ] incorporated awareness and control measures, our model formulation further separates students enrolled in awareness programmes ( S p ) from those not enrolled and introduces the awareness-failure compartment ( I p ), allowing the effectiveness of awareness campaigns to be quantified more realistically. The inclusion of the media-content compartments ( M k , k = 1 , 2 ) provides an explicit representation of the role of addictive digital content in sustaining social media overuse. Consequently, the model offers new insights into how institutional awareness programmes and accessibility regulations interact with both social influences and direct exposure to online content, thereby providing a more comprehensive framework for evaluating intervention strategies in higher education settings. Conclusion This study demonstrates that effective regulation and awareness strategies are critical in mitigating the negative impact of excessive social media use on academic performance. A strict monitoring of the social media platforms accessibility concurrently implemented with awareness enrolment and its efficacy at high levels remains the best strategy in preventing new harmful social media habits and averting addiction development trends. The model findings thus highlight several important implications for higher education institutions. The sensitivity and simulation analyses identify peer influence, direct exposure to addictive social media content, and content generation by active users as the primary drivers of excessive social media use among students. Conversely, awareness enrolment, awareness programme efficacy, and accessibility regulation are shown to be effective control measures that reduce the spread of harmful social media use. These results suggest that universities should invest in early digital-wellness education, strengthen the quality and reach of awareness campaigns, and implement balanced accessibility-control policies within learning spaces. While restrictions on social media access must be carefully designed to respect students’ autonomy and digital rights, targeted regulation in academic environments may help maintain student engagement and academic focus. The study thus contributes a dual-transmission framework that captures both peer influence and direct exposure to addictive social media content within higher education institutions. Overall, the model provides a quantitative framework that can support institutional decision-making aimed at promoting healthier digital habits and improving academic performance. However, a limitation of the present study is that the model has not yet been calibrated or validated using empirical data from a specific higher education institution. The current work is intended as a theoretical framework that provides qualitative insights into the dynamics of excessive social media use and the potential effectiveness of intervention strategies. Future research will focus on collecting institutional survey or longitudinal data to estimate model parameters, validate the model predictions, and evaluate the proposed interventions under real-world conditions. This will enhance the model’s predictive capability and support evidence-based policy recommendations.
