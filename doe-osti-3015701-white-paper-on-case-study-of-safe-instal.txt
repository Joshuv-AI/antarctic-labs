1 Introduction
Second-life batteries, also known as repurposed batteries, are batteries that have been used in one
fielded application and are then reconfigured for use in a stationary energy storage system (ESS)
application (National Fire Protection Association [NFPA] 2023a). A common example is the
repurposing of electric vehicle (EV) batteries for use as an ESS connected to the electrical grid,
either behind the meter tied to a residential or commercial building or in front of the meter as a
utility-scale ESS. As EVs approach end of life or are otherwise taken out of service, demand has
emerged for processes to recycle or reuse their batteries. In some instances, this demand is driven
by legislation. California Senate Bill 615, for example, requires vehicle-traction batteries to be
recovered and, where possible, repurposed (California Legislative Information 2024). Second-
use batteries are poised for large growth in the next 10 years. A recent techno-economic study
projects that starting in 2035 the gigawatt-hours of retired EV batteries in the United States will
be similar in magnitude to forecasts for annual ESS installations and might satisfy a large portion
of the United States’ grid ESS needs.

Concerns have been raised about the safety of second-life batteries. One study indicates that the
hazards associated with second-life lithium-ion batteries are similar to those for new lithium-ion
batteries, but the probability of failure may be higher because of the effects of aging and
unknown stress and/or abuse during the battery’s first life (Christensen et al. 2023). Another
study indicates that aged cells display different thermal runaway characteristics compared to new
cells, particularly with respect to thermal stability, which may have significant implications for
large-scale applications such as ESSs (Zhang et al. 2024). An additional study concluded that
aged cells could have lower thermal runaway onset temperatures than new cells by more than
50°C, though more studies are needed (Preger et al. 2022). Recent data also indicate that the
aging of lithium-ion batteries can potentially alter cell tolerance to overcharge or overdischarge
(Preger 2024).

In the United States, the predominant standard that governs the safe installation of energy storage
systems is NFPA 855: Standard for the Installation of Stationary Energy Storage Systems, 2023
edition. NFPA 855 requires that second-life batteries meet all the requirements of new batteries.
In addition, the entity repurposing the battery (called a “repurposer”) must be a repurposing
company compliant with UL Solutions 1974: Evaluation for Repurposing or Remanufacturing
Batteries.

The main challenge for battery repurposers in meeting the requirements of NFPA 855 is in
obtaining the required listings, including UL 9540 for the integrated system and UL 1973 for
battery cell, module, and packs. Additional challenges include:

 • Repurposers typically do not have access to the original equipment manufacturer (OEM)
 battery management system (BMS). This means that the repurposer may not have access
 to the history of the cells and modules, including conditions such as temperature
 fluctuations, overcharge and undercharge, and variations in charge rates.
 • Repurposers are typically unable to modify the physical module-level design. For
 example, repurposers cannot typically modify cell-to-cell spacing, casing design, venting

 1

 properties, or other physical features that could impact the ability of a battery to pass all
 required tests in UL 1973 and UL 9540.
As of the publication of this white paper, there are no known repurposers that have achieved the
UL 1973 and UL 9540 listings that are required by NFPA 855, though several have completed
UL 9540 field evaluations. Further challenges to the use of second-life batteries as ESSs include:

 • EV batteries are typically designed specifically to optimize their use only in an EV
 application. This includes frequent start and stop, exposure to hot and cold temperatures,
 pack/casing exposure to moisture, etc. These design applications and associated use
 history must be considered when adapting an EV battery for use with a stationary
 installation.
 • An aftermarket BMS added by repurposers typically can only take data from the OEM
 BMS. Thus, the usefulness of the BMS in an ESS application depends on the OEM
 design, which is typically for an EV application. Adherence to best practices for BMS
 such as Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) P2686: IEEE
 Recommended Practice for Battery Management Systems in Stationary Energy Storage
 Applications, including all applicable parameters, the level at which these are monitored
 (cell vs. module), and the data acquisition rate, should be taken into account.
Given the expected future demand for repurposed batteries and the challenges described herein,
there is a need to determine potential paths forward for safe installation of second-life batteries.
Within the framework of existing codes and standards, the only path for approval for second-life
batteries that are not listed (excluding research and development [R&D]) applications per
Section A1.3.1 of NFPA 855), is using the equivalency process described in Section 1.5 of
NFPA 855. This process is similar to what is commonly referred to as “alternate materials,
design, and methods,” as described in the International Fire Code (International Code Council
2024). Use of these processes requires close coordination with an Authority Having Jurisdiction
(AHJ)/fire code official, who has overall approval authority.

This white paper provides an overview of tools and processes used to validate the installation of
an R&D application of a second-life ESS. The tools and processes utilized and presented here
represent only one potential solution to address the aforementioned challenges. The goal is to
demonstrate methods and strategies that may be used to demonstrate that risk has been reduced
to an acceptable level. The acceptable level is unique to and will be determined by each AHJ and
specific installation. The analysis presented in this white paper may not be pertinent to all
installations. In all cases expert judgement is necessary. The tools used in this process include:

 • Blast-overpressure modeling using TNT equivalency or ExDAM
 • Computational fluid dynamics modeling using Fire Dynamics Simulator
 • Toxic gas plume modeling using EPIcode or ALOHA
 • Quantitative risk assessment using the Center for Chemical Process Safety (CCPS) Layer
 of Protection Analysis (LOPA) methodology
 • Hazard mitigation analysis.
It is important to note that the tools utilized here are not a substitution for compliance with
applicable codes and standards. In this R&D application, the tools have been used to inform the

 2

overall conservative design of the ESS enclosure to achieve an acceptable level of risk. In the
absence of the required listings, some of the additional features provided that are not required by
code were provided to achieve this acceptable level of risk and are highlighted as follows:

 • A deflagration venting system designed to NFPA 68 (Standard on Explosion Protection
 by Deflagration Venting), in addition to a combustible concentration reduction system
 designed to NFPA 69 (Standard on Explosion Prevention Systems)
 • Addition of deflagration deflector plates to the deflagration vents
 • Implementation of separation distances greater than those required by NFPA 855
 • Implementation of more enclosure markings and signage for firefighters than what is
 required by NFPA 855
 • Use of gas detection devices designed to initiate exhaust ventilation at a threshold much
 earlier than the minimum requirement of NFPA 855
 • An exhaust ventilation rate that exceeds third-party estimations of the required exhaust
 ventilation rate.

2 Battery Information
The second-life ESS under test is a 75-kWh battery pack, composed of 4.95 amp-hour (Ah) cells
manufactured by an EV OEM. The ESS was sourced from a commercial battery repurposer
following its retirement from a consumer-owned EV. The battery pack contains four modules,
each of which contains up to 1,150 lithium nickel-cobalt-aluminum oxide cells. The battery pack
is not UL 9540 or UL 1973 listed.

The on-board OEM BMS for the single-pack ESS provided to NLR for testing reports pack-level
data. Cell voltage is reported for each cell, but cell temperature is not. Brick maximum and
minimum temperatures are also reported; a “brick” is a group of parallel cells where the number
of cells is specified by the OEM. Additional parameters are reported by the OEM BMS. Data
collection accuracy and rates were determined to be largely consistent with data collection
parameters stipulated in IEEE P2686: IEEE Recommended Practice for Battery Management
Systems in Stationary Energy Storage Applications.

UL 9540A: Standard for Test Method for Evaluating Thermal Runaway Fire Propagation in
Battery Energy Storage Systems is frequently utilized during the design of ESSs to characterize
the fire and explosion characteristics of battery cells, modules, units, and installations. The UL
9540A cell-level test provides information such as thermal runaway off-gas constituents and
concentrations that are typically used to inform the design of explosion control systems. In this
case, UL 9540A cell-level test data were unavailable. In the absence of these data, the battery
repurposer contracted with a fire protection engineering company to perform a literature review
and develop a model to estimate parameters of their battery such as battery off-gas composition,
gas mixture lower flammability limit (LFL), maximum potential gas release rate, and maximum
number of cells that could undergo thermal runaway simultaneously. With this information, a
steady-state gas generation rate of 331 L/min was assumed with a gas mixture LFL of 5%,
representing a single module. The maximum number of cells in thermal runaway simultaneously
was determined to be 36, with a maximum possible gas generation rate of 425 L/min. This value
was used as the basis for subsequent analysis. The independent review also concluded that a
minimum exhaust flow rate of 2,000 cubic feet per minute (cfm) would be needed to adequately
exhaust the enclosure to ensure the LFL remains below 25%. The actual exhaust rate provided
for this enclosure is much greater.

The LFL and gas generation rate were compared to both public and nonpublic data available to
the authors. With respect to the LFL, non-public data for typical battery gas mixtures ranged
from 5% to 9%, depending on battery chemistry type. Public data for LFL ranged from 4% to
20% (Fire & Risk Alliance 2024). The gas generation rate depends on the assumptions used in
the explosion control design, which typically involves the use of UL 9540A test data combined
with an engineering evaluation to determine an assumed number of cells undergoing
simultaneous failure. Data available to NLR indicated assumed design rates in the range of 400–
600 L/min, though much lower or higher rates could result depending on the assumptions used.
These rates are also noted to be for cells designed for stationary energy storage use. The EV cells
used in this system have an individual cell capacity of 4.95 Ah—significantly less than a
capacity of hundreds of amp-hours, which is typical of stationary energy storage cells. Based on
a simple rule of thumb that 1–2 L of gas is produced per amp-hour, it is conceivable that 10 L of
gas could be produced from a single cell. If all 10 L were released in 1 minute, 43 cells would be

 4

needed to go into thermal runaway simultaneously to reach 425 L/min. If all 10 L were released
in 30 seconds, 22 cells would be needed to go into thermal runaway simultaneously to reach 425
L/min. Finally, if all 10 L were released in 15 seconds, 11 cells would be needed to go into
thermal runaway simultaneously to reach 425 L/min. The gas generation rate from the
independent review was deemed suitable for this analysis.

3 Equipment Information
3.1 Enclosure
The enclosure procured for this project was a 10 × 8 × 8-ft (length × width × height) insulated
steel metal shipping enclosure. The enclosure was supplied with a wood floor. To meet the
requirements of NFPA 855 for a noncombustible enclosure, 10-gauge steel diamond plating was
secured to the wood floor. A rack made from industrial framing was created to ensure air flow
around the battery during testing. The battery was installed near the center of the enclosure,
approximately 24 in. from the floor.

3.2 Heating and Cooling
A mini-split unit, rated at 24,000 BTU, providing air conditioning and heat, was mounted on the
outside of the enclosure. Direct battery cooling is managed through a small coolant reservoir and
pump located under the rack and controlled by the BMS for additional internal battery thermal
management. When installed in the EV, thermal management systems remove heat from the
battery core to ensure battery health and reliability.

3.3 Emergency Exhaust Ventilation
The ESS enclosure is outfitted with four separate emergency exhaust fans, each rated at 830 cfm
(total 3,320 cfm). Two 12 × 12-in. inlet ports are provided. The efficacy of the fans for explosion
control purposes was evaluated with a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model and is
discussed in the explosion control section of this paper. These emergency exhaust fans,
combined with gas detection, serve as a combustible concentration reduction (CCR) system, in
accordance with NFPA 69.

3.4 Fire Alarm
The enclosure is outfitted with smoke/fire detection as required by NFPA 855. Dual smoke
detectors and a single heat detector are installed near the internal roof of the enclosure. Detectors
are tied to a commercial fire panel, which transmits alarm signals to a central monitoring station
for fire department dispatch.

3.5 Gas Detection
A single hydrogen gas sensor is provided within the enclosure. The sensor type was reviewed
and approved by the NLR AHJ in consultation with NLR gas detection research specialists and
industry best practices. The full-scale range of the RKI hydrogen detector is 2,000 ppm. This is
equivalent to 5% of the LFL of hydrogen. This set point is more sensitive than the minimum
requirement of NFPA 855 of 10% of the LFL. The detector threshold was assessed as part of the
CFD model. Monitoring systems continuously record 1-min averages of the hydrogen
concentration and store the data in a local database. A set point of 1,900 ppm is used in the
datalogging and CCR system control logic to activate fans as well as close a relay contact, which
sends an alert to NLR’s central monitoring station. The gas sensor performance was verified with
hydrogen calibration gas prior to commencing operations.

 6

3.6 Deflagration Venting
Six 920 × 920-mm deflagration venting panels are installed on the enclosure. Due to the
limitations of space on the roof, and uncertainty surrounding effective clearing of snow and ice
from the roof, vents were installed on the sides. Deflagration vent deflectors were installed.
Deflagration venting has been designed in accordance with NFPA 68, utilizing a partial-volume
deflagration approach. The deflagration vents are described in more detail in the explosion
control section of this paper. This approach was validated by a CFD model of the NFPA 69 CCR
system as well as associated calculations by the third-party deflagration vent supplier. The
enclosure has clear signage for fire responders to stand back 100 ft in case of explosion.

3.7 Fire Suppression
No fire suppression (in the form of extinguishing agents or fire sprinklers) is provided in the
enclosure. The 2024 U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) Emergency Response Guidebook
(DOT 2024), which is used by the responding fire department, recommends letting units
consume themselves. In addition, NFPA 855 (2023 edition) does not require fire suppression for
outdoor, non-walk-in units. Based on both factors, and the limited effectiveness of fire sprinklers
and enclosure-based extinguishing agents in stopping thermal runaway or cell-to-cell
propagation (FM Global 2017), no fire suppression was included.

3.8 Electrical
ESSs can be directly charged from the grid, but the NLR system will be charged either from a
480-VAC grid connection or 14-kWDC of collocated photovoltaic modules.

3.9 Battery Backup
There are several circuits connected to the enclosure for different subsystems. In the event of a
power failure, the BMS, fire alarm control panel, and CCR system are powered by a custom 24-
VDC uninterruptable power supply (UPS). The UPS is sized for 24 hours in standby mode and 2
hours in alarm mode, as required by NFPA 855 and NFPA 72.

4 Site Location
NLR adheres to the 2021 International Fire Code and the 2023 NFPA 855, which provide for
minimum setback distance for ESSs. The ESS is comingled with a photovoltaic array. NLR
intentionally exceeded the NFPA 855 minimum required separation distance of 10 ft to public
ways and other exposures. The nearest exposures that are not associated with electrical
infrastructure are all greater than 30 ft. Ten or more feet of gravel is provided on all sides of the
ESS as required by NFPA 855. The nearest road is approximately 50 ft away, and the nearest
sidewalk is approximately 100 ft away. The suitability of the siting was further substantiated as
part of explosion modeling performed by NLR. The nearest fire hydrant is 120 ft away.

5 Emergency Response
The responding fire department has been trained on this NLR’s ESS, as required by NFPA 855.
NLR developed a site-specific pre-incident plan and emergency operation plan to specifically
address this second-life battery. This plan resides with the responding fire department, and a
copy is also maintained by on-site security personnel.

The responding fire department has attended many training courses and tours at NLR pertaining
to ESSs and battery fires of all types. This close partnership is a key part of enabling research at
NLR.

In addition to the fire department, personnel at NLR who could be involved in an incident
involving this ESS have been trained. This includes researchers, electricians, industrial
hygienists, emergency management and security personnel, NLR medical clinic staff, and others.

6 Explosion Control
6.1 Combustible Concentration Reduction System (NFPA 69 System)
The CCR system was modeled using CFD software. The purpose of the CFD modeling was to
determine the adequacy of the exhaust ventilation system to meet the requirements of both NFPA
69 and NFPA 855. NFPA 855, 2023 edition, requires a CCR system to maintain the level of
flammable gases within the enclosure to below 25% of the LFL of the gas mixture.

Fire Dynamics Simulator version 6.7.8 was used for the CFD model. The model included the
four exhaust fans discussed above at the installed locations. The fan manufacturer provided a
ramp-up time of 4–7 s; 7 s was used in the model. Makeup air openings were included as
installed.

The model included a hydrogen sensor with alarm levels set to 1,900 ppm at the intended
location. Once the detector in the model reaches this threshold, the fans will trigger to exhaust
the enclosure.

Fire Dynamics Simulator was set up to inject a constant gas release rate of 425 L/min. This was
deemed a conservative approach, as gas release from venting lithium-ion batteries will vary with
time. The validity of the gas release rate is described in detail earlier in this paper; however, it is
noted again here that 425 L/min was deemed to be the peak gas release rate from multiple cells,
based on an independent third-party analysis. The gas constituents are listed in Table 1, also
derived from independent third-party analysis:

the gas plume), it quickly reduces the concentration to below 25% of the LFL, meeting the intent
of NFPA 855 and NFPA 69 requirements.

6.2 Deflagration Venting (NFPA 68 System)
Deflagration venting was designed as a redundant and additional layer of protection. NLR
worked with a national supplier of deflagration vents to perform the NFPA 68 calculations and
procure the vents.

Deflagration venting design per NFPA 68 involves a variety of assumptions. Many of these
assumptions require UL 9540A cell-level test data. Due to the absence of test data for this
second-life battery, NLR made some conservative assumptions for some of the most important
variables.

One of the first variables that is necessary for an NFPA 68 design is the fundamental burning
velocity of the gas mixture. The burning velocity is the rate of flame propagation relative to the
velocity of the unburned gas that is ahead of it. The fundamental burning velocity is the burning
velocity of a laminar flame under stated conditions of composition, temperature, and pressure of
the unburned gas (NFPA 2023b). NLR sampled a variety of internal UL 9540A reports and
referred to external research. For example, one paper (Fire & Risk Alliance 2024) concluded that
based on a large dataset of UL 9540A cell-level tests, an average fundamental burn velocity is
0.613 m/s. From the literature review, high burning velocity was determined to be in the range of
approximately .800 to .900 m/s. As a result, NLR chose to use 1 m/s as the fundamental burning
velocity for the design. The use of this value as an upper bound was also reviewed by at least
three independent external engineers with experience in deflagration vent design, who all
concurred that this is a reasonable upper bound for lithium-ion cells. A larger burning velocity
affects the required deflagration vent area, with higher burn velocities leading to higher
deflagration vent areas.

 11

The next major variable that was reviewed was Pmax, which is the maximum pressure developed
in a contained deflagration of an optimal mixture. NLR again used available internal test data as
well as external data. External data based on a large quantity of UL 9540A test reports indicated
a maximum Pmax of 6.93 bar-g. Based on this information, additional reports, and peer review
input, a Pmax of 8 bar-g was chosen as a conservative estimate. A larger Pmax will result in a larger
deflagration vent area.

The free air volume inside an ESS enclosure is the area that is not occupied by equipment. In a
typical ESS enclosure, the free air volume is estimated to be approximately 20%. In this
enclosure, a single module is being tested. The module volume was calculated as approximately
36 ft3. This value was used in the NFPA 68 calculations (with a total volume of 640 ft3).

Using Le Chatelier’s law, the stoichiometric volume percent of the off-gas mixture was
estimated to be 22.4%. For a more conservative approach, 20% percent was used in the NFPA 68
calculations.

NLR used a partial deflagration analysis based on the presence of an NFPA 69 CCR system that
will reduce the total volume of gases in the enclosure. Due to practical limitations of the surface
area of the enclosure, a full-volume deflagration analysis with the chosen assumptions resulted in
too great an area to fit on this enclosure. This approach was also selected because it was agreed
to by the NFPA 855 technical committee as an appropriate approach for the 2026 edition.

To estimate the partial volume of gases, the CFD model was used. The time at which the exhaust
fans activated (based on input gas detector properties) was recorded. The fan ramp-up time, a
delay for signal processing, and a safety factor of 1.5 were applied to the exhaust fan activation
time. A gas volume of 5.6 ft3 (based on an off-gas rate of 425 L/min for 22.5 s) was derived
(Table 2).

As previously discussed, due to the infeasibility of regularly clearing snow and ice in Colorado
in addition to limited roof area, it was determined that the vents would be installed on three sides
of the enclosure (two vents per side, mounted in the upper half of the enclosure). All sides with
vents were positioned away from the public way. With this configuration, the provided
calculations indicate a fireball distance of 11.3 ft. NLR elected to install deflector plates in
accordance with NFPA 68 (Section 6.6), reducing this distance to less than the 10 ft of
noncombustible gravel provided. These safety features, combined with emergency response
training and pre-incident plans, permitted this configuration.

6.3 Estimating the Consequences of Flammable Gases
The accumulation of flammable gases in an ESS can result in not only a fire but also an
explosion if enough flammable gases accumulate and find an ignition source (i.e., no flaming).
NLR evaluated the consequences of an unmitigated explosion of the second-life battery to
establish setback distances in excess of those required by NFPA 855 and to establish the need for

6.4 Blast Overpressure Threshold
An explosion in the second-life battery was analyzed following DOE Handbook 1224 Hazard
and Accident Analysis (DOE 2024) best practices. For background, an explosion is characterized
as the rapid release of high-pressure gas, which may involve bursting or rupture of an enclosure
due to the development of internal pressure from deflagration (NFPA 2024). The damage that is
caused by an explosion’s blast wave against an object or a person is a complex function of many
factors that can be evaluated with the TNT equivalency method, the TNO-Multi Energy method,
or a CFD model. The strength of the wave is commonly measured in terms of units of
overpressure (psi) and can be correlated to different levels of property or personal injury based
on extensive testing (DOE 2024).

NLR used a threshold of 1.0 psi of overpressure to establish the consequences of an explosion in
the second-life battery following the best practices from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory

 13

Commission (NRC) Regulatory Guide 1.91, Evaluations of Explosions Postulated to Occur at
Nearby Facilities and on Transportation Routes Near Nuclear Power Plants (NRC 2013) and
U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) EPA-550-B-99-009 Risk Management Program
Guidance for Offsite Consequence Analysis (EPA 2009). Both standards are referenced in DOE
Handbook 1224 Hazard and Accident Analysis (DOE 2024) and use a 1.0-psi value below which
no significant damage would be expected (NRC 2013). Anything below the threshold value of
1.0 psi in this analysis establishes the basis for which there would be no damage to structures,
systems, and components (as defined in DOE [2011]) associated with the second-life battery.

6.5 TNT Equivalency Method
The TNT equivalency concept has been used in literature to evaluate the potential damage from
an explosion overpressure and has been applied to the evaluation of both deflagrations and
detonations. It is a simplified method for assessing the blast wave effects from a VCE based on
blast wave energy, i.e., TNT equivalency utilizing NUREG-1805 Equation 15-1, from NRC
Regulatory Guide 1.91:

 𝐸𝐸𝑇𝑇𝑇𝑇𝑇𝑇 = 𝛼𝛼𝛼𝛼 × 𝐻𝐻𝑓𝑓 × 𝑊𝑊𝑓𝑓

where:

 • ETNT = explosive energy released or blast wave energy (J).
 • Wf = the mass of fuel involved (kg). The mass of the fuel in the cloud is equal to the flash
 fraction (F) times the quantity (mass) of fuel released when the fuel is flashing flammable
 liquid. The flash fraction is not needed if the fuel is a gas or is composed of vapor
 generated through evaporation.
 • Hf = theoretical net heat of combustion of the fuel in question (kJ/kg). This information is
 available in NUREG-1805 Table 15-2, Heat of Combustion, Ignition Temperature, and
 Adiabatic Flame Temperature of Flammable Gases, and in Factory Mutual Loss
 Prevention Data Sheet 7-42, “Guidelines for the Estimation of Property Damage from
 Outdoor vapor cloud explosions in Chemical Processing Facilities,” March 1990 (NRC
 2013).
 • αe = TNT equivalency based on energy (dimensionless), which is the fraction of available
 combustion energy participating in blast wave generation (dimensionless).
For a worst-case scenario involving the release of flammable gases for the second-life battery,
the release rate was not considered. The total quantity of the flammable gas was assumed to form
a vapor cloud within the lower explosive limit and not find an ignition source prior to
deflagrating. The analysis also assumed that 10% of the flammable vapor in the cloud
participated in the explosion (i.e., a yield factor of 0.10) following recommendations from EPA-
550-B-99-009 Risk Management Program Guidance for Offsite Consequence Analysis (EPA
2009). The overpressure distance of 1 psi or more for an explosion in the second-life battery was
determined to be 102.89 ft (31.36 m) using Appendix D of the EPA guide (EPA 2009).

The TNT equivalency method is known to produce non-conservative results for peak
overpressure in the far field because the positive phase duration and shape of the blast waves in
deflagration events (i.e., a VCE) compared to detonation events (i.e., a high explosive such as

 14

TNT). In addition, the TNT equivalency method does not credit shielding from structures such as
the 10 × 8 × 8-ft insulated steel metal shipping enclosure surrounding the second-life battery. For
the purposes of this analysis, the TNT equivalency method was used as a comparison to
demonstrate the use case of more sophisticated methods such as the TNO Multi-Energy model.

6.6 TNO Multi-Energy Model
TNO conducted extensive research into blast models and developed the TNO Multi-Energy
model, which is generally regarded as the best model available for a rapid assessment of
explosion overpressures and positive phase durations (Raman and Grillo 2005). The TNO Multi-
Energy model is better suited for determining consequences of vapor cloud explosions in the
near and far field of a confined cloud since it uses scaling equations, side-on overpressures, and
time-based measurements of pressures for blast waves (van den Bosch and Weterings 2005).
NLR used the TNO Multi-Energy model by Breeze Trinity Consultants Inc. called VExDam to
better characterize a VCE from the second-life battery.

Using VExDam, a VCE was modeled from within a structure fitting the dimensions and
materials of the second-life battery containing 6.91 kg of flammable gas with a heat of
combustion of 29,809.94 kJ/kg. The overpressure distance of 1 psi or more for an explosion in
the second-life battery was determined to be 138.60 ft (42.25 m) using VExDam (Figure 2).
Even when accounting for pressure shielding from the steel structure of the second-life battery,
the consequence distance with an overpressure of 1 psi or greater was greater than the TNT
equivalency method by more than 30%. Using the TNT equivalency method alone would have
led to less conservative results and ultimately smaller setback distances. In Figure 2, the x-axis
shows the distance from the center of the blast in feet.

7 Estimating the Consequences of Toxic Gases
When ESSs catch fire, they can release large quantities of flammable gases and generate large
quantities of toxic gases. The toxic gas plumes are of increasing concern for workers, the public,
and first responders surrounding ESS installations, given the potential for acute human-health
impacts.

Toxic gas dispersion modeling was performed on the second-life battery to assist in planning for
the issuance of protective actions on-site and off-site as well as firefighting tactics. Although
lithium-ion battery chemistry is quantified, the composition of the toxic gases and particulates
generated during a fire are not. NLR applied conservative assumptions (i.e., worst-case
meteorological conditions, ground-level release, etc.) from industry standards spanning DOE,
EPA, and CCPS to account for the worst-case consequences of toxic gases emitted from the
second-life battery during a fire.

7.1 Quantifying Toxic Gases of Concern
Lithium-ion battery fires generate intense heat and considerable amounts of gas and particulate
smoke. The organic electrolyte in a lithium-ion battery is flammable and generally contains
lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) or other lithium salts containing fluorine. When an ESS
overheats, the electrolyte evaporates and vents from the battery cells, which releases several
toxic compounds when they are combusted. At elevated temperatures, the fluorine content of the
electrolyte and (to some extent) other parts of the battery such as the polyvinylidene fluoride
binder in the electrodes, can form gases such as hydrogen fluoride (HF), phosphorus
pentafluoride, and phosphoryl fluoride.

A literature review of numerous studies, including a study by the EPA (Weston Solutions 2024),
found that the main toxic compounds released from lithium-ion batteries include carbon
monoxide, hydrochloric acid, hydrogen cyanide, sulfur dioxide, HF, and many other chemicals
(Bugryniec et al. 2024). HF was selected as a representative toxin for modeling the second-life
battery in this report because it has been quantified with a range per battery watt-hour (Wh) in
lithium-ion ESSs to develop an accurate source term for plume modeling. Additionally, HF has
the most conservative emergency exposure limits of the toxic materials known in lithium-ion
ESSs and is readily dispersible as a liquid and a gas (high vapor pressure). While toxic plumes
from fires involving lithium-ion batteries are known to also contain heavy metals such as
aluminum, nickel, iron, and others, there is currently insufficient data to support developing a
source term for those metals to support plume modeling.

The toxicity of HF is well established and a unique concern to first responders and to community
members near ESSs. A well-reviewed study determined that a large amount of HF is generated
during fires involving lithium-ion batteries with a range between 20 and 200 mg/Wh of nominal
battery energy capacity (Larsson et al. 2017). Given that the second-life battery contained 75
kWh, the total HF capacity within the second-life battery was estimated to be 1.5–15 kg. NLR
used the higher value of 15 kg to establish the worst-case consequences.

7.2 Emergency Exposure Limits
Toxic gas concentrations from plume modeling are typically compared to public exposure
guidelines to implement protective actions in the event of a release. NLR chose to compare HF
concentrations from the second-life battery to the DOE Protective Action Criteria (PAC)
exposure values since they provide human health end points for the general public (i.e., they
account for the elderly, children, and other sensitive receptors) during a 1-hour exposure (typical
of an emergency incident). For background, the PAC exposure values are a hierarchical dataset
derived in preference order from the EPA’s Acute Exposure Guideline Levels, American
Industrial Hygiene Association’s Emergency Response Planning Guidelines, and DOE’s
Temporary Emergency Exposure Limits. Other emergency exposure guidelines such as the
National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health Immediately Dangerous to Life and Health
contain only one human health end point and do not account for the less severe human health end
points that the PAC exposure values do and where protective actions (i.e., shelter in place instead
of evacuating) would be recommended. The highest PAC level, identified as PAC-3, is often
used in conjunction with Immediately Dangerous to Life and Health guidelines for the same
protective action basis for evacuations and was applied as such by NLR.

Risk is a function of two components: severity (also referred to as consequence in this paper) and
likelihood (also referred to as probability or frequency) of an event occurring as illustrated in the

In evaluating the risk of the second-life battery, the severity and likelihood were assessed

9 Hazard Mitigation Analysis
A hazard mitigation analysis is an evaluation of potential energy storage system failure modes
and safety-related consequences attributed to the failures (NFPA 2023a). A hazard mitigation
analysis is required by NFPA 855 for unique technologies, where required by the AHJ to address
a potential hazard with an ESS installation that is not addressed by existing requirements, and for
existing lithium-ion ESSs that are not UL 9540 listed. While this product does not fit into any of
these categories, NLR performed a hazard mitigation analysis to meet the intent of NFPA 855,
particularly resulting from the use of a product that is not listed in accordance with UL 9540.

The hazard mitigation analysis considered various prescriptive failure modes for the ESS as
described by NFPA 855. This included thermal runaway and mechanical failure, failure of
protection systems such as the energy storage management system and battery management
system, and failure of other protection systems such as ventilation, exhaust ventilation, fire
detection, and gas detection. The hazard mitigation analysis allows for an AHJ to approve a site-
specific ESS installation, if the approval criteria described in NFPA 855 are met.

10 Conclusions and Recommendations
There are challenges in the deployment of second-life battery technologies. The main safety-
related impediment is that as of the writing of this white paper, there are no known second-life
batteries that have met the required certifications of national standards in the United States for
stationary ESSs, including UL 9540 and UL 1973.

NLR is a research and development organization. To support R&D of second-life batteries, NLR
fabricated a custom enclosure to test a second-life battery module. Since the battery modules are
not certified, NLR implemented a range of safety features as well as safety analysis above and
beyond the basic requirements of NFPA 855, as allowed by Section A1.3.1 of NFPA 855. Some
of the precautions include:

 • The design of the NFPA 69 combustible concentration system includes provision of an
 NFPA 68 deflagration vent system in addition to an NFPA 69 combustible concentration
 system to reduce the risk to an acceptable level. Only one of these is required in the codes
 of record for this project, NFPA 855 (2023) and the International Fire Code (2021).
 • Use of conservative assumptions in the design of the NFPA 68 deflagration vent panels to
 account for the absence of UL 9540A cell-level test data for the second-life battery.
 Sample parameters that were conservatively estimated include burning velocity, Pmax,
 total gas volume for partial volume effects, and stoichiometric gas concentration.
 • Use of conservative assumptions:
 o Use of four 830-cfm fans, whereas an independent third-party analysis
 recommended a total of 2,000 cfm of exhaust.
 o A constant gas release rate of 425 L/min from the 4.95 Ah cells. In a real thermal
 runaway scenario, off-gassing would rise and fall. The gas release rate chosen was
 the peak estimated release rate.
 • Provision of setback distances in excess of the minimum setback distances required by
 NFPA 855 and the International Fire Code based on worst case explosion and toxic gas
 plume modeling.
 • Provision of gas detection system triggered to activate prior to the required thresholds in
 NFPA 855.
 • Provision of enclosure markings, including large stencils, not required by NFPA 855 or
 the International Fire Code.
 • Provision of nonrequired deflector plates on the deflagration vents.
 • Application of advanced hazard assessment methodologies to quantify the severity of a
 fire resulting in toxic gas emissions and an accumulation of flammable gases resulting in
 an explosion.
 • Use of advanced risk assessment methodologies following CCPS’s LOPA to quantify the
 likelihood of each accident scenario.
With all assessments and analysis performed, and associated controls implemented, the
installation was deemed to provide for an acceptable level of risk in accordance with DOE best

 27

practices. This analysis is possible outside the prescriptive realm of NFPA 855 due to the
research and development nature of this project. The conservative means and methods utilized
here could serve to further discussion on what an acceptable level of safety may look like for
other second-life battery installations.
