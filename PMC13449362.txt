Platinum-based chemotherapy after enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab in metastatic urothelial carcinoma: a real-world study☆
Authors: M Sternschuss, K Whiting, A Arbuiso, A Gupta, E Bent, N Alvarez, A Regazzi, S Liang, F Ahmed, O Akin, V Beylergil, S Niglio, D Lage, SA Funt, D Bajorin, G Iyer, D Aggen, I Ostrovnaya, JE Rosenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatinum-based chemotherapy (PBC) is frequently used after first-line enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab for metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC), yet efficacy in this setting remains undefined.Patients and methodsWe retrospectively reviewed an institutional database of 236 patients with mUC treated with enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab between October 2018 and December 2024 to identify those receiving second-line PBC. Responses were investigator-assessed. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated from the start of PBC; duration of response (DOR) was estimated from the first documented response.ResultsOf 62 patients treated with subsequent systemic therapy, 74% (n = 46) received PBC (carboplatin, n = 34; cisplatin, n = 12); nine patients (20%) then received maintenance avelumab. Median age was 73 years; 65% were male; 30% had upper-tract primary. Objective response rate (ORR) to first-line enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab was 41%. Median follow-up was 11 months [95% confidence interval (CI) 9.2-not reached (NR)]. ORR to second-line PBC was 49% (22 of 45, 95% CI 34% to 64%), including two complete responses; median DOR was 5.2 months (95% CI 3.8-NR). Median PFS was 5 months (95% CI 3.6-6.6), and median OS was 12 months (95% CI 10-17). Outcomes did not differ between cisplatin and carboplatin (P > 0.9).ConclusionsIn a real-world setting, PBC after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab demonstrated meaningful activity but modest durability. These findings may inform clinical management and future trial design.Key words: enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab, metastatic urothelial carcinoma, platinum-based chemotherapy, subsequent treatment

Introduction

The treatment paradigm for metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) has dramatically changed since enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab demonstrated superiority over platinum-based chemotherapy (PBC) as first-line therapy in the phase III EV-302 trial.1 Although responses to enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab are frequent [objective response rate (ORR) 67.5%; 95% confidence interval (CI) 62.9% to 71.9%] and durable [median duration of response (DOR) 23.3 months; 95% CI 17.8-not reached (NR)], a substantial proportion of patients experience disease progression [estimated progression-free survival (PFS) at 24 months of 37.1%].2

Optimal management after progression on enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab remains challenging, as prospective and retrospective evidence regarding the efficacy of subsequent therapies is lacking. In EV-302, 79% (126 of 159) of patients who received subsequent systemic treatment after progression on enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab were treated with PBC,2 yet outcomes for these patients remain unreported. International guidelines3,4 recommend second-line PBC for most patients after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab, with targeted therapy considered for select patients with FGFR3 alterations or HER2 overexpression.3,4 A recent survey among genitourinary medical oncologists confirmed this consensus.5 Thus, there is a critical unmet need for data on outcomes with second-line therapies, specifically PBC, to inform clinical decisions after progression on enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab.

To address this evidence gap, we report clinical outcomes in a real-world cohort of patients with mUC treated with second-line PBC after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab.

Patients and methods

EnfortuMab vedotin and Pembrolizumab In Real-world Experience (EMPIRE), a retrospective institutional database including consecutive patients treated with first-line enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab for mUC at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, was reviewed to identify those who received subsequent PBC. Treatment decisions, including the choice of subsequent therapy, were made at the treating physician’s discretion. The institutional review board approved this study and waived informed consent.

Clinicopathologic characteristics including the prognostic Bellmunt score6 were collected by chart review. Responses to enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab and PBC were determined by investigator assessment of radiological reports. Clinical characteristics and response to PBC were compared between platinum type (carboplatin compared with cisplatin) using two-tailed Fisher’s exact test (categorical) and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (continuous). PFS was estimated from the start of platinum therapy to progression or death. Patients without progression or death were censored at the start of subsequent treatment or at last follow-up if no additional treatments were administered. Overall survival (OS) was estimated from start of platinum treatment to death from any cause or last follow-up. DOR was defined as the time from first documented response [complete response (CR) or partial response (PR)] to disease progression or death, with censoring at last contact/start of new treatment. PFS, OS, and DOR probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Outcomes were compared by platinum type using log-rank tests.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Overall, 236 patients initiated first-line enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab for mUC between October 2018 and December 2024, 97 of whom (41%) had ongoing disease control at last follow-up. Among the remaining 139 patients, 62 received subsequent systemic therapy, 46 of whom (74.2%) were treated with PBC. Non-platinum second-line alternatives included sacituzumab govitecan (n = 6, 9.7%), clinical trials (n = 5, 8.1%), trastuzumab deruxtecan (n = 3, 4.8%), and erdafitinib (n = 2, 3.2%). A detailed flow diagram of patient disposition and second-line treatments is provided in Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108318.

Baseline characteristics and status at PBC initiation are detailed in Table 1. Median age was 73 years, 65% of patients were male, 30% had upper-tract primaries, and 43% had urothelial carcinoma with divergent differentiation or histological subtypes. At initiation of PBC, metastases to bone, lung, liver, and lymph node-only were reported in 39%, 33%, 30%, and 15% of patients, respectively. The ORR to first-line enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab was 41% (CR, n = 2; PR, n = 17), and the median PFS was 4.4 months (95% CI 3.8-6.0). Reasons for enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab discontinuation were disease progression (n = 39, 85%) and toxicity (n = 7, 15%). Two patients had prior neoadjuvant PBC exposure, one within 12 months of enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab initiation. Carboplatin was administered to 34 patients (74%) who were considered cisplatin-ineligible due to peripheral neuropathy (18 of 34; 53%), renal insufficiency (8 of 34; 23.5%), poor performance status (7 of 34; 20.5%), or hearing impairment (1 of 34; 3%). Patients treated with carboplatin were older (median age, 74 compared with 68 years; P = 0.036) and more frequently had higher Bellmunt scores (2-3 in 57% compared with 18%; P = 0.037) than did those treated with cisplatin. Nine patients (20%) received maintenance avelumab after chemotherapy, eight in the carboplatin subset and one in the cisplatin subset. A swimmer plot illustrating treatment duration and sequencing for individual patients is shown in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108318.

There were no significant differences between carboplatin and cisplatin for PFS (median 5.0 months, 95% CI 3.6-6.9 compared with 5.7 months, 95% CI 2.4-NR, P > 0.9) or OS (median 12 months, 95% CI 9.7-NR compared with 11 months, 95% CI 4.7-NR, P > 0.9).

Among 22 patients with objective responses, 15 progressed during follow-up, with a median DOR of 5.2 months (95% CI 3.8-NR), without significant difference between carboplatin and cisplatin (median 4.6 months, 95% CI 2.9-NR compared with 5.5 months, 95% CI 4.6-NR, P > 0.9). Treatment delivery and safety

The treatment delivery and safety data are summarized in Table 3. Median number of cycles of PBC was 5 (interquartile range [IQR] 3-6), with no significant difference between carboplatin and cisplatin (P = 0.4). At data cut-off, four patients remained on PBC (cisplatin, one; carboplatin, three), all of whom completed five of six planned cycles. Reasons for treatment discontinuation included protocol completion as planned (50%), disease progression (38%), toxicity (7%), and patient preference (5%). Treatment-related adverse events led to treatment modifications in 67% of patients (n = 30), most frequently due to hematologic toxicity.

Hematologic toxicity of any grade occurred in 98% of patients (n = 43), and the rates of grade ≥3 anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia were 61%, 32%, and 41%, respectively. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor was administered in 68% of patients (n = 30). Baseline peripheral neuropathy at the start of PBC was reported in 65% of patients, 23 of 34 (68%) in the carboplatin subset and 7 of 12 (58%) in the cisplatin subset. Among patients with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy, worsening neuropathy occurred in 14% (1 of 7) of those treated with cisplatin and 13% (3 of 23) of those treated with carboplatin. Overall, new or worsening peripheral neuropathy was reported in five patients (11%), one in the cisplatin subset (leading to switch from cisplatin to carboplatin after cycle 4), and four in the carboplatin subset (leading to dose reduction in two patients); none required treatment discontinuation. There were no treatment-related deaths.

In the patients treated with maintenance avelumab (n = 9), median number of cycles was 7 (IQR 4-8). Among those with stable disease or PR after PBC (n = 7), no further changes in tumor size were observed after initiation of maintenance avelumab. At data cut-off, two patients remained on maintenance treatment, and seven patients discontinued avelumab, all because of disease progression.

Discussion

In this real-world cohort, PBC after treatment with enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab demonstrated clinically meaningful activity, providing one of the first reported efficacy outcomes in this setting. Although the response rate with second-line PBC after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab appears comparable to that reported with first-line PBC in contemporary trials (49% compared with 44.0%-44.9%),7, 8, 9 these responses may be less durable (median DOR 5.2 compared with 6.2-7.6 months), with potentially shorter PFS (median 5.0 compared with 6.3-7.1 months) and OS (median 12 compared with 13.4-15.9 months), though these observations are based on indirect cross-trial comparisons and require further statistical evaluation for confirmation. These outcomes are also consistent with retrospective data of 51 patients treated with second-line PBC after first-line immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) monotherapy, reporting ORR of 45%, median PFS of 4.3 months, and median OS of 11.9 months.10 Preliminary data on subsequent therapies after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab from the UNITE study have recently been reported; however, response was assessable for only a limited number of patients treated with second-line PBC.11

Although cisplatin is considered superior to carboplatin in urothelial carcinoma,12 its use is frequently limited by baseline comorbidities.13 In the post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting, enfortumab vedotin-related peripheral neuropathy may further restrict cisplatin eligibility; in our cohort, 74% of patients were considered cisplatin-ineligible, with peripheral neuropathy reported as the primary reason in 53%. In this carefully selected cohort, worsening peripheral neuropathy during PBC was uncommon with both cisplatin and carboplatin and did not lead to treatment discontinuation, even among patients with baseline neuropathy. Despite adverse prognostic features at baseline in the carboplatin subset, reflected by higher Bellmunt scores, we observed no substential differences in efficacy between cisplatin and carboplatin. Given the similar number of cycles administered and the frequency of treatment modifications, it is unlikely that poor tolerability impacting treatment delivery in the cisplatin subset explains these findings. While the small numbers preclude definitive conclusions, these observations suggest that the use of carboplatin may not meaningfully compromise patients’ outcomes.

The role of maintenance ICI therapy after PBC in the second-line post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting remains unclear. Although the duration of maintenance avelumab in our cohort was shorter than reported in JAVELIN Bladder 100, and no additional changes in tumor size were observed after its initiation, interpretation is limited by the small sample size and potential selection bias.14 With the expanding use of ICIs in non-metastatic UC, defining the efficacy of ICI rechallenge in patients with mUC previously treated with ICI-based therapy represents an area of unmet clinical need, beyond the post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting. Retrospective studies of ICI rechallenge have reported potential benefit in a subset of patients with mUC, and further studies are warranted.15,16 The ongoing phase III trial (NCT06524544) evaluating pembrolizumab in combination with sacituzumab govitecan versus chemotherapy in patients with ICI-resistant mUC may provide further insight into treatment strategies in this setting.

Limitations of this study include the relatively small sample size, retrospective design, lack of randomization, potential unmeasured confounding, and non-standardized response assessment. The retrospective data collection limits accurate characterization of the toxicity burden, particularly for nonlaboratory adverse events such as peripheral neuropathy, which may be underestimated. Generalizability is further constrained by the single academic referral center setting and potential selection bias introduced by real-world clinical judgment guiding the choice of systemic therapy. As expected in a second-line setting, the cohort was enriched for patients with primary or early acquired resistance to enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab, reflected by a lower ORR than that reported in EV-302.1 Moreover, due to the limited time since enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab approval, patients with disease progression after prolonged initial response to enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab are underrepresented.

Enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab is increasingly being used in the perioperative setting after the positive results of the phase III KEYNOTE-905/EV-303 trial in cisplatin-ineligible patients and the phase III KEYNOTE-B15/EV-304 trial in cisplatin-eligible patients.17,18 In the post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting, the absence of an established efficacy benchmark for second-line PBC poses a major challenge to the design of prospective randomized clinical trials. Large real-world datasets such as the EMPIRE and UNITE studies offer an important opportunity to address this evidence gap. Future analyses incorporating molecular, pathological, and clinical features may help define determinants of response to PBC in the post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting and inform treatment selection. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first reported outcomes datasets in this clinical context.

In conclusion, in a real-world cohort of patients with mUC, PBC demonstrated substantial antitumor activity after enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab, although PFS and DOR were modest. These findings may inform future trial design in the post-enfortumab vedotin–pembrolizumab setting.
