Big-step and small-step Horn clause derivations applied to operational semantics †
John P. Gallagher, Manuel Hermenegildo, José Morales, Pedro Lopez-Garcia, Louis Rustenholz

Abstract
The concepts of big-step and small-step derivations are familiar from the operational semantics of programming languages. These concepts are applicable in the more general setting of Horn clause derivations. We prove equivalence between big-step derivations and two versions of small-step derivations for Horn clauses. By specialising interpreters for these derivation strategies, any set of Horn clauses can be transformed into a provably equivalent set of clauses that inherits the behaviour of a given (big- or small-step) Horn clause interpreter. As a special case of this transformation, big-step semantics for any programming language, expressed directly as Horn clauses, can be transformed into equivalent small-step semantics. Experiments with a variety of programming languages are reported.

Introduction

In this paper we explore the connections between Horn clause derivations
and the formal operational semantics of programming languages.
Horn clauses have rich connections to a range of formal computational topics.
First identified by the logician Alfred Horn in 1951
Horn_1951
, they
have been studied as a computational formalism from which
the constraint logic programming (CLP)
family of languages emerged
Green69
;
Colmerauer
;
Kowalski74
;
Tarnlund77
;
KornerLBCDHMWDA22
.
Other areas studied through the medium of Horn clauses
include databases (Datalog), context-free languages (definite clause grammars)
and automated verification (constrained Horn clauses)
BjornerGMR15
.

A close relationship between Horn clauses and operational semantics
was identified many years ago
Kahn87
.
This paper extends that connection, shedding light on the relationship between
different operational semantics
for the same language, especially between big-step and small-step semantics, allowing
automatic translation between the two forms.

Outline of the paper

In Section
2
the notation and essential concepts for Horn clause derivations are presented.
Section
3
contains operational semantics rules defining three Horn clause derivations;
capturing both classical Horn clause derivations and also a novel “contextual small-step” derivation
strategy. We also show that the conjunctions (or “stack”) of atomic goals that arise in small-step
derivations can be folded into a single atomic goal, using auxiliary “suffix” clauses.

In Section
4
we present a version of the contextual small-step derivations
applied to a particular class of Horn clauses, namely big-step operational semantics rules for
programming languages. The particular features of predicates and clauses in such rules
are exploited to refine the derivation, especially the auxiliary clauses allowing the
stack to be folded.
Section
5
outlines the well-established method of program transformation using
interpreter specialisation (the first Futamura projection). The transformation is applied to big-step
semantics rules, resulting in small-step rules. The transformation is first applied to big-step semantics
of simple
imperative and functional languages, and then a larger example of the transformation of the
semantics of the reversible programming language Janus is outlined.
Finally in Section
7
we review the relation to existing work, and outline future work.
Details of the experiments
and proofs of propositions are in appendices.

Preliminaries

A first-order language is defined by
\langle\Sigma,\Pi,\cal{V},\cal{C}\rangle
, where
\Sigma
and
\Pi
are
non-empty sets of function symbols
and predicate symbols respectively, each having an assigned arity,

\cal{V}
is a countable set of variables, and
\cal{C}
is a finite set of logic connectives and quantifiers.

A
term
is a variable
v\in\cal{V}
, or an expression
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
where
f\in\Sigma
,

n\geq 0
is the arity of
f

and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
are terms. We use the letters
s,t,\ldots
(with subscripts) to denote terms.

An
atomic formula
or simply
atom
is of the form
p(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, where
p\in\Pi

and
n\geq 0
is the arity of
p
. If
n=0
in either terms or atoms the argument brackets are omitted.
We use the letters
A,B,C\ldots
possibly with subscripts to
denote atoms.

We consider the logical connectives and quantifiers
\cal{C}=\{\vee,\wedge,\neg,\rightarrow,\forall,\exists\}
,
with their usual
interpretations.

Quantifiers will mostly be implicit for the purposes of this paper, as all
variables in formulas will be considered
to be universally quantified unless otherwise specified.

A
literal
is either
A
or
\neg A
where
A
is an atom.
A
and
\neg A
are called
positive

and
negative
literals respectively.

A
clause
is a disjunction
L_{1}\vee\cdots\vee L_{k}
(
k\geq 0)
where
L_{i}
are literals.

A
Horn clause
is a clause containing at most one positive literal.
A Horn clause
A_{0}\vee\neg A_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\neg A_{k}
(
k\geq 0
)
is often written as the logically equivalent formula

(A_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge A_{k})\rightarrow A_{0}
. Sometimes we will write this in the logic programming
fashion with the arrow reversed,
A_{0}\leftarrow(A_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge A_{k})
, or simply

A_{0}\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
.
A_{0}
is called the
head
of the clause and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}

is called the
body
of the clause. A Horn clause
\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
(that is, with no head)
is called a
goal
, and is equivalent to
\neg\exists(A_{1}\wedge\cdots\cdots\wedge A_{k})
.

A
substitution
is a set of pairs
v/t
where
v\in\cal{V}
and
t
is a term.
If
\theta
is a substitution and
S
is any syntactic object then

S\theta
denotes the
application
of
\theta
to
S
, obtained by simultaneously replacing
every occurrence of
v
in
S
by
t
, where
v/t\in\theta
. The
empty substitution
is denoted
\epsilon
.

Given two syntactic objects
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
,
S_{1}
is an

instance
of
S_{2}
if there exists a
substitution
\phi
such that
S_{1}=S_{2}\phi
.

S_{1}\cong S_{2}
holds if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are instances of each other; that is, they are identical
modulo renaming of variables.
A
unifier
of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

is a substitution such that
S_{1}\theta=S_{2}\theta
.
A
most general unifier
of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
(or
\mathtt{mgu}(S_{1},S_{2})
for short)
is a unifier
\theta
,
such that for any other unifier
\theta^{\prime}
,
S_{1}\theta^{\prime}
is an instance of
S_{1}\theta
.
The unification algorithm computes such a substitution, if it exists. The
\mathtt{mgu}
of
two objects is
unique, modulo renaming of variables.

The composition of two substitutions
\theta
and
\phi
is the substitution denoted
\theta\phi
, where
for all syntactic objects
S
,
S(\theta\phi)=S\phi\theta
(that is, first apply
\phi
, then
\theta
).

2.1
Horn clause derivations

Let
P
be a set of Horn clauses. The
resolution
inference rule for Horn clauses
is defined as follows.
Let
A_{0}\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
and
B_{0}\leftarrow B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
be two clauses in
P
.
The two clauses are assumed to have no variable in common - variables in clauses can always be
renamed apart if necessary.
Suppose
\mathtt{mgu}(A_{0},B_{j})=\theta
. Then a
resolvent
of
the clauses is a clause

(B_{0}\leftarrow B_{1},\ldots,B_{j-1},A_{1},\ldots,A_{k},B_{j+1},\ldots,B_{m})\theta.

In the following we consider a restricted form in which the second clause is a goal,
that is,
B_{0}
is absent, and
j
=
1
, that is, the first literal
of the goal is selected for resolution,

Let
P
be a set of Horn clauses and
G_{0}
a goal. A resolution
derivation
for
P\cup\{G_{0}\}

is a sequence of goals

G_{0},G_{1},\ldots

where each
G_{i}
(
i\geq 1
) is obtained by resolving
G_{i-1}
with a clause from
P
.

A resolution
refutation
of a goal
G_{0}
in

P
is a finite derivation in which the final goal is the empty clause. For
simplicity we will use the word “proof” instead of refutation, but to be accurate, a refutation
of a goal
\leftarrow B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}

is a proof that
\exists(B_{1},\ldots,B_{k})
follows from
P
.

This is historically called SLD-resolution
AptE82
;
Lloyd
;
Stark89
(Selective Linear Definite clause resolution)
where the selection rule picks the leftmost literal.

Theorem 1

(Soundness and completeness of SLD resolution)
.

Let
P
be a set of Horn clauses and let
A
be an atom. Let
\theta
and
\phi
be
substitutions.
Then
A\theta
is a logical consequence of
P
if and only if there is an SLD-resolution proof
for
P\cup\{\leftarrow A\}
,
\phi
is the composition of the substitutions arising from the

\mathtt{mgu}
operation at each step in the proof, and
A\theta
is an instance of
A\phi
.

2.2
Alternative formulation without explicit substitutions

We write
A_{0}\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in[P]
to mean that
A_{0}\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}

is an instance of a clause in
P
.
Let
\leftarrow B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
be a goal. Then
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}
is a resolvent
of the goal with (an instance of) the clause if
B_{1}\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in[P]
(resolving on the leftmost
atom
B_{1})
.

The definition of a resolution derivation can then be expressed as follows:
a resolution
derivation
for
P\cup\{G_{0}\}

is a sequence of goals

G_{0},G_{1},\ldots

where each
G_{i}
(
i\geq 1
) is a resolvent of
G_{i-1}
with an instance of a clause from
P
.
The correctness theorem is restated as follows.

Theorem 2

(Soundness and completeness of SLD resolution (without explicit substitutions))
.

Let
P
be a set of Horn clauses and let
A
be an atom. Then
A\theta
is a logical consequence of

P
if and only if there is an SLD-resolution proof (without explicit substitutions)
for
P\cup\{\leftarrow A\theta\}
.

This formulation is similar to that used by Stärk
Stark89
in the proof of soundness and
completeness of SLD-resolution, except that Stärk formulated a derivation in terms of “implication-trees”
(Stärk’s term for AND-trees)
rather than derivation sequences. It may seem odd that this formulation requires the “answer”
\theta

to be known before constructing a run. In practice,
given an atom
A
, an instance
A\theta
that is a logical consequence of a set of clauses
P

is constructed during the derivation;
A
is successively instantiated
at each step of the derivation.
We return in more detail to this style of proof in
Section
5.1
.

In the following section we formulate operational semantics for Horn clause derivations
without explicit substitutions.

3
Small-step and big-step Horn clause derivations

SLD-derivations can be structured in different ways.
We start by reformulating the notion of a derivation in the style of small-step operational semantics.

3.1
SLD small-step Horn clause derivations

A small-step semantics consists of three components:

1.

A set of
configurations
, a non-empty subset of which is designated as
terminal
configurations.

2.

A
transition
relation
C\Rightarrow C^{\prime}
, where
C,C^{\prime}
are configurations.

3.

The transitive closure of the transition relation
\Rightarrow\!\!*
. There is a
run
from
C
to
C^{\prime}
iff

C\,{\Rightarrow}\!\!*\,C^{\prime}
, and the run is
successful
if
C^{\prime}
is a terminal configuration.

For Horn clause derivations, a configuration is a goal
G
.
The goal
\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
is the terminal configuration.
For conciseness, we omit the
\leftarrow
in goals; for example, we write
B_{1},B_{2}

instead of
\leftarrow B_{1},B_{2}
.

The letter
R
with subscripts represents a (possibly empty) conjunction of atoms; thus
B,R
represents the
conjunction of which the atom
B
is the first conjunct and
R
is a conjunction. We can also write
R_{1},R_{2}
to represent the conjunction
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
,
where
R_{1}=A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
and
R_{2}=B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
, since the
conjunction connective is associative. We also note that
(\mathsf{true},R)
is the same as
R
.

A run can be defined by the following rules
where
R,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime}
are configurations and the arrow
\Rightarrow
is any of the small-step transition arrows that
we present later (
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}
).

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{[RUN0]}~~\dfrac{}{R\,{\Rightarrow}\!\!*\,R}&\mathtt{[RUN1]}~~\dfrac{R{\Rightarrow}\,R^{\prime}~~~R^{\prime}{\Rightarrow}\!\!*\,R^{\prime\prime}}{R\,{\Rightarrow}\!\!*\,R^{\prime\prime}}\end{array}

A transition for the SLD version of small-step derivations (given a set of Horn clauses
P
) is of the form

G_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}G_{1}
,
defined by the following small-step rule.

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathtt{[RES]}~~\dfrac{}{(B,R_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}(R,R_{1})}~~~~\text{where }B\leftarrow R\in[P]\end{array}

This, together with the definition of a run, is just a reformulation of an SLD-derivation
(without explicit substitutions), so Theorem
2

can be reformulated to state that
A\theta
is a consequence of the given set of Horn clauses
P

if and only if
there exists a (successful) run

A\theta\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
using the rules above.

3.2
Contextual small-step Horn clause derivations

In the second version of the small-step derivation
a small step ends with a resolution step
using a clause with empty body, that is
A\leftarrow
, usually written as

A\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
. Resolution with such a clause shortens the goal and represents finishing
proving one conjunct and moving on to the next.

The transition relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
is defined recursively using two rules for atoms, and two for conjunctions, shown in
Figure
1
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathtt{[RES0]}~~\dfrac{}{B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{true}}~~~\text{where }B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}\in[P]\\
\\
\mathtt{[RES1]}~~\dfrac{R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,R_{1}}{B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,R_{1}}~~~\text{where }B\leftarrow R\in[P],R\neq\mathsf{true}\\
\end{array}\\
\\
\\
\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathtt{[CONJ0]}~~\dfrac{R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}}{(R_{1}\wedge R_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{2}}&\mathtt{[CONJ1]}~~\dfrac{R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R^{\prime}_{1}}{(R_{1}\wedge R_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,(R^{\prime}_{1}\wedge R_{2})}~~~\text{where }R^{\prime}_{1}\neq\mathsf{true}\\
\end{array}\\
\end{array}

Figure 1:
Contextual small-step rules for Horn clauses

The rules defining a run are
\mathtt{[RUN0]}
and
\mathtt{[RUN1]}
given above with
\Rightarrow\!\!\!*

replaced by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow\!\!*}}
.
The form of
\mathtt{[CONJ0]}
and

\mathtt{[CONJ1]}
is familiar as the pattern for
“context rules” in small-step operational semantics. Given a
compound structure (in this case a conjunction
(R_{1}\wedge R_{2})
), we
make a small step on the first component of the
structure,
R_{1}
. If
R_{1}
is reduced to
\mathsf{true}
in one small step, then move
on to
R_{2}
, using
\mathtt{[CONJ0]}
. Otherwise, let the result of a
small step on
R_{1}
be
R^{\prime}_{1}
, and continue with
(R^{\prime}_{1}\wedge R_{2})
using
\mathtt{[CONJ1]}
.

Remark 1
.

For conjunctions in SLD derivations, the comma is used as the conjunction symbol
to indicate that a conjunction is regarded as a list of atoms and
the derivation rule for
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}
assumes associativity of the comma symbol.
However, for contextual small-step derivations, and big-step derivations, conjunctions
are trees built using the
\wedge

symbol; and the rules for
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
(Figure
1
) and

\Downarrow
(Section
3.3
) do not assume its associativity.

Example 1
.

The following small example illustrates the difference between the two versions of small-step semantics.
Let
P
be the set of (propositional) Horn clauses given below.

\begin{array}[]{llll}a\leftarrow b,c&d\leftarrow&e\leftarrow&g\leftarrow\\
b\leftarrow d,e&c\leftarrow f,g&f\leftarrow&\\
\end{array}

We construct a run starting from
a
.
Using SLD small-step rules, the run is

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}(b,c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}(d,e,c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}(e,c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}(f,g)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}g\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}

Using the contextual small-step version, each transition ends with a resolution with a clause with empty body, so we get:

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}(e\wedge c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}g\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}

A run
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
is
expanding
if no clause of the form

B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
is used in the derivation.
We write

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
for an expanding run.

Lemma 3
.

For all expanding runs
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
where
R\neq\mathsf{true}
,
there exist
B,S,S^{\prime}
such that
R=(B,S)
and
R^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},S)
.

Lemma 4
.

For all conjunctions
R,R^{\prime},
and
R^{\prime\prime}
, where
R\neq\mathsf{true}
,

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
if and only if

(R,R^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})
, and both runs have the same height.

Lemma 5
.

There is a contextual small-step transition
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R^{\prime}
if and only if there is a run
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(B,R^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}R^{\prime}
, that is, an expanding run followed by a step using a clause
B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
.

Theorem 6
.

For a given set of Horn clauses,
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}

if and only if

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
.

3.3
Big-step Horn clause derivations

For Horn clause derivations, a big-step transition is of the form
A\Downarrow\mathsf{true}
.
Note the downward transition
arrow
\Downarrow
contrasting with the small-step transition
\Rightarrow
. There is no need for the notion
of a “run” since a big-step transition itself represents a complete derivation.
The rules for
\Downarrow
are as follows.

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathtt{[BASE]}~~\dfrac{}{B\Downarrow\mathsf{true}}~~~\text{where }B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}\in[P]\\
\\
\mathtt{[UNF]}~~\dfrac{R\Downarrow\mathsf{true}}{B\Downarrow\mathsf{true}}~~~\text{where }B\leftarrow R\in[P],R\neq\mathsf{true}\\
\\
\mathtt{[CONJ]}~~\dfrac{R\Downarrow\mathsf{true}~~~~~R^{\prime}\Downarrow\mathsf{true}}{(R\wedge R^{\prime})\Downarrow\mathsf{true}}\end{array}\\

The difference from the small-step semantics is the treatment of conjunctions. A successful
derivation for a conjunction is constructed from successful derivations for each of the conjuncts.

Theorem 7
.

For a given set of Horn clauses,
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}

if and only if

A\Downarrow\mathsf{true}
.

Proof.

Straightforward by induction on the height of the
\Downarrow
derivation tree.
∎

Theorems
6
and
7
establish that all three operational semantics are equivalent with respect to successful
derivations.

3.4
Folding the conjunctions in small-step derivations

The conjunctions that arise in small-step derivations starting from
an atomic goal using

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
have a structure due to the fact that the leftmost atom is always selected
for resolving with a clause head, and after deriving
\mathsf{true}
from the leftmost atom, the next leftmost atom
is selected. Thus the conjunctions following a transition have the following form.

((\cdots(R_{1}\wedge R_{2})\wedge\cdots R_{n-1})\wedge R_{n})

where each conjunction
R_{i}
is an instance of a
suffix
of a clause body.
In the following section, we show that the whole conjunction can be represented by a single atom,
by
folding

it using a set of auxiliary
suffix rules
.

3.4.1
Suffix rules

Given a Horn clause of the following general form

A_{0}\leftarrow A_{1},\cdots,A_{n}

we assume the body to have a standard structure, so that
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}

is the conjunction
A_{1}\wedge(A_{2}\wedge(\ldots\wedge A_{n})\ldots)
. (See also Remark
1
).
We define the
i^{th}
suffix and
i^{th}
prefix (
1<i\leq n
) of the rule as follows.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathsf{suff}(i)=&A_{i}\wedge(\ldots\wedge A_{n})\\
\mathsf{pref}(i)=&\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{i-1}\}\\
\end{array}

For each
i
(
1<i\leq n
) we construct two
suffix rules
, defining a predicate
\mathtt{stack}

(since the conjunction can be seen as the stack of calls awaiting selection).

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Type 1 suffix rule: }&\mathtt{stack}(f_{i}(\bar{w}))\leftarrow\mathsf{suff}(i)\\
&~~~~~\text{where }\bar{w}=\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{pref}(i))\cap\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{suff}(i))\\
\\
\text{Type 2 suffix rule: }&\mathtt{stack}(g_{i}(\bar{w}\cup\{z\}))\leftarrow\mathtt{stack}(z)\wedge\mathsf{suff}(i)\\
&~~~~~\text{where }\bar{w}=\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{pref}(i))\cap\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{suff}(i))\\
&~~~~~\text{and }z\text{ is a fresh variable}\end{array}

f_{i}
,
g_{i}
are function symbols occurring only in the respective rules.
Note that
\mathsf{pref}(1)
and
\mathsf{suff}(1)
are not used
for forming suffix rules.

The purpose of the suffix rules is to allow the conjunctions arising from a

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
transition to be folded into a single
\mathtt{stack}
atom.
The following example introduces the idea of folding; a precise definition of
folding follows the example.

Example 2
.

Consider the Horn clauses defining “naive” reverse.

:-

,

,

The suffix clauses are generated from the second clause using
\mathsf{suff}(2)=\mathtt{app(Ws,[X],Xs)}
.

⬇

stack
(
f_2_1
(

,

same clause can then be used for folding again,
yielding the single atom
\mathtt{stack(g\_{2\_1}(a,X,X1,g\_{2\_1}(b,X1,X2,f\_{2\_1}(c,X2,[])))}
.

The argument of the
\mathtt{stack}
predicate encodes the conjunction of calls to atoms as a nested term
that carries the information contained in the conjunction.
Local variables in the suffixes (that is, variables in
\mathsf{suff}(i)\setminus\mathsf{pref}(i)
) are not in the folded atom.
The identical conjunction (up to renaming of such local variables)
can
be reconstructed by unfolding the atom using the
\mathtt{stack}
clauses.

Foldable conjunctions

Let
P_{\mathrm{suff}}
be the set of suffix clauses
derived from a set of Horn clauses
P
.
Each clause in
P_{\mathtt{suff}}
is assigned the
index of the clause in
P
from which it is derived. There is no need to provide
separate indices to different suffixes from the same clause, as their length is sufficient to
distinguish them. We annotate conjunctions and write
R^{k}
when there exists a
k
such that
R
is the body of a suffix clause with index
k
.

A
foldable conjunction
is of the form

((\cdots(R^{k_{1}}_{1}\wedge R_{2})^{k_{2}}\wedge\cdots R_{n-1})^{k_{n-1}}\wedge R_{n})^{k_{n}}

where
R_{2},\ldots,R_{n}
are instances of bodies of suffix clauses of type 1, and
R_{1}
is
an instance of
the body of a suffix clause of type 1 or type 2.
The position of annotations determines which suffix clauses are involved in
a foldable conjunction. For instance
(A^{k_{1}}\wedge B^{k_{2}})
is a different foldable
conjunction than
(A\wedge B)^{k}
. In the first,
A
and
B
are instances of the bodies of
suffix clauses
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
respectively, while in the second,
A\wedge B
is
an instance of
the body of suffix clause
k
.

Remark 2
.

If an
unannotated conjunction is stated to be “foldable”, then we mean that annotations could be added to make it
a foldable conjunction. More precisely, if
R
is “foldable” then either
R
is a suffix
of a clause,
or
R=R_{1}\wedge R_{2}
, where
R_{2}
is a suffix of a clause
and the unannotated conjunct
R_{1}
is “foldable”.
This slight abuse of notation is used in Lemma
9
and also in Theorem
10
.
Note that a “foldable” unannotated conjunction might be annotated in different ways; consider
for example the unannotated conjunction
A\wedge B
with the annotations just discussed.

Revisiting Example
2
, and adding annotations, the conjunction

((\mathtt{app([],[c],X2)}^{1}\wedge\mathtt{app(X2,[b],X1)})^{1}\wedge\mathtt{app(X1,[a],X)})^{1}

contains three instances of the body of suffix clause body 1 (of type 1).
After folding the first instance, we obtain

((\mathtt{stack(f\_{2\_1}(c,X2,[]))}\wedge\mathtt{app(X2,[b],X1)})^{1}\wedge\mathtt{app(X1,[a],X)})^{1}

in which
(\mathtt{stack(f\_{2\_1}(c,X2,[]))}\wedge\mathtt{app(X2,[b],X1)})^{1}

is an instance of a type 2 suffix clause body with index 1. Whether the index
k
indicates a suffix clause of
type 1 or 2 is decided by whether a
\mathtt{stack}
atom occurs as the first atom of the conjunct
component (in which case it is of type 2).

Definition 1

(Folding)
.

Let
P_{\mathtt{suff}}
be the set of suffix rules derived from
P
.
The function
\mathsf{fold}
is applied to foldable conjunctions or
\mathsf{true}
(which indicates the empty
foldable conjunction).

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathsf{fold}(S)=\begin{cases}A,\text{ if }S=R^{k}\text{ and }A\leftarrow R\in_{k}[P_{\mathtt{suff}}]\\
\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})\wedge R)^{k})\text{ if }S=R^{k_{1}}_{1}\wedge R^{k}\\
\mathsf{true}\text{ if }S=\mathsf{true}\end{cases}\end{array}

It can be seen that
\mathsf{fold}(S)
, where
S
is a foldable conjunction or
\mathsf{true}
,
is a
\mathtt{stack}
atom or
\mathsf{true}
.
The following property relating folding and suffix clauses also follows directly from the definition.

1.

B\leftarrow R\in_{k}P_{\mathrm{suff}}
is a suffix clause of type 1, if and only if
B=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})
.

2.

B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}P_{\mathrm{suff}}
is a suffix clause of type 2 if and only if

B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})
for some
R^{k_{1}}_{1}
and
B=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1}\wedge R^{k})
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}
be foldable conjunctions or
\mathsf{true}
. Then

S_{1}\cong S_{2}\text{ if and only if }\mathsf{fold}(S_{1})\cong\mathsf{fold}(S_{2}).

Note that the equalities are modulo renaming of variables.

shows that the

The usefulness of the contextual small-step transition system might not be obvious so far, when looking at
cases such as Example
2
. In the next section we consider a particular class of
Horn clauses, namely sets of rules for big-step operational semantics of programming languages.
In this setting, contextual small-step Horn clause derivations provide a framework for
transforming big-step semantics to small-step semantics.

4
Horn clauses for programming language semantics

We are now going to apply small-step derivations to a particular class of
Horn clauses, namely, programming language
operational semantics.
The rules of operational semantics, whether big-step or small-step, have premises and a conclusion, written as follows.

\dfrac{\mathtt{premises}}{\mathtt{conclusion}}~~~~\text{if }\mathtt{conditions}

With suitable encoding of the arguments of transitions, this is an implication in first-order predicate logic:

\mathtt{premises}\wedge\mathtt{conditions}\rightarrow\mathtt{conclusion}.

The conclusion is an atomic formula
(a big- or small-step transition with predicate symbol such as

\Rightarrow
,
\Rightarrow\!\!\!*
and
\Downarrow
).
Assuming that the premises and conditions are conjunctions,
it is a Horn clause.

The close connection between semantics rules and Horn clauses, and
hence to the logic programming language Prolog,
was observed by Kahn and his co-workers and exploited in
the Typol tool
Despeyroux1984-short
;
Kahn87
. In short, a set of operational semantics rules, either big-step or
small-step, is a set of Horn clauses, which can be given a procedural reading using one of the
derivation strategies discussed above, just as any set of Horn clauses.

We will show that we can give a small-step interpretation to a set of big-step rules, or
a big-step interpretation to a set of small-step rules. This will form the basis of transformations of
big-step semantics to small-step, and vice versa.

4.1
Big-step rules

A big-step transition for programming language semantics has the form
\langle s,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow v
,
meaning that the language
construct
s
is completely evaluated in environment
\sigma
, resulting in value
v
.
In some languages, the final value is also an environment; the environment itself can be a more or less complex object comprising
stores, function definitions, status flags and so on.

To simplify the presentation we assume that a language definition has only
one big-step transition relation, even though in many languages more than one is used.
For example, there may be one big-step transition for
evaluating expressions and another for executing statements, and each may depend on the other.
In our formulation we use only one relation and assume that they are distinguished by their syntax argument.
A big-step semantics for a language consists of a set of rules of the following form.

\dfrac{c_{1}~~~\langle s_{1},\sigma_{1}\rangle\Downarrow v_{1}~~~~\cdots~~~c_{n}~~~\langle s_{n},\sigma_{n}\rangle\Downarrow v_{n}~~~c_{n+1}}{\langle s_{0},\sigma_{0}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}}

where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n+1}
are some side conditions and operations interspersed among the transition premises.

4.1.1
Suffix rules for big-step clauses

Naturally, we could form suffix clauses defining a predicate
\mathtt{stack}
as shown earlier,
since the construction applied to any Horn clauses.
However, we will exploit the particular form of big-step transition rules to
refine the rules in the following ways.

1.

The arguments of the stack predicate will mirror those of the big-step transition, name
s
,
\sigma
and
v
.

2.

Going further, we will also reuse the predicate name
\Downarrow
instead of the
predicate
\mathtt{stack}
.
In principle we could use any predicate name, provided that the
function symbol in the head of a suffix clause is unique to that clause,
to avoid conflict with other clauses when re-unfolding a folded stack.

3.

However, by choosing the predicate name
\Downarrow
, we are also able to merge the
two kinds of suffix rule into a single kind. We elaborate the justification below.

For (
1\leq i\leq n
) we define the
i^{th}
suffix and
i^{th}
prefix of the big-step rule as follows.
These are essentially
the same as the prefix and suffix defined for Horn clauses previously, but we also include the
c_{i}

components of the rule, which are not executed in small steps.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathsf{suff}(i)=&\langle s^{\prime}_{i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\Downarrow v_{i}~~~c_{i+1}~~~\cdots~~~c_{n}~~~\langle s_{n},\sigma_{n}\rangle\Downarrow v_{n}~~~c_{n+1}\\
\mathsf{pref}(i)=&\langle s_{0},\sigma_{0}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}~~~c_{1}~~\langle s_{1},\sigma_{1}\rangle\Downarrow v_{1}~~~\cdots~~~c_{i-1}~~~\langle s_{i-1},\sigma_{i-1}\rangle\Downarrow v_{i}~~~c_{i}\\
\end{array}

Note:

•

In
\mathsf{suff}(i)
,
s_{i},\sigma_{i}
from the rule are replaced by
s^{\prime}_{i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
,
where
s^{\prime}_{i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
are fresh variables not
occurring elsewhere in the rule. The variable

v_{i}
is not renamed, since we will show in Section
4.2
that the value argument in transitions is unchanged
during derivations.
Replacing
\langle s_{i},\sigma_{i}\rangle\Downarrow v_{i}

by
\langle s^{\prime}_{i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\Downarrow v_{i}
corresponds to replacing
A_{i}
by
\mathtt{stack}(z)

in the second suffix rule for Horn clauses, but now we use the predicate
\Downarrow

instead of
\mathtt{stack}
, and the fresh variables
s^{\prime}_{i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
corresponding to the
fresh variable
z
of
\mathtt{stack}(z)
.

•

\mathsf{pref}(1)=\langle s_{0},\sigma_{0}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}~~~c_{1}
since
\langle s_{i},\sigma_{i}\rangle\Downarrow v_{i}
is not
included in
\mathsf{pref}(i)
.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
the
suffix rule
is

\dfrac{\mathsf{suff}(i)}{\langle f_{i}(\bar{s}),\bar{\sigma}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}}

where
f_{i}
is a function symbol not used in any rule in
P
, and
\bar{s}
,
\bar{\sigma}
contains the
variables needed to evaluate the suffix, given the prefix. More precisely: let
\bar{u}=\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{pref}(i))

and
\bar{v}=\mathsf{vars}(\mathsf{suff}(i))
. Let
\bar{w}=\bar{u}\cap\bar{v}
.
The set
\bar{w}
is split (somewhat arbitrarily) into disjoint sets,“syntax” variables and “environment”
variables, say
\bar{s}
and
\bar{\sigma}
respectively, which appear in the rule conclusion. This is only for
readability of the rule; the exact split is not critical.
In short, in the suffix rule, the rule conclusion contains variables that occur both in
\mathsf{pref}(i)
and
\mathsf{suff}(i)
.

We now show that these suffix rules subsume suffix rules of the first and second kind
introduced
for arbitrary Horn clauses in Section
3
.
For
1<i\leq n
the above rule corresponds to the first kind of suffix rule except that the first
premise is generalised by introducing
fresh variables. Therefore any suffix foldable without the fresh variables is also foldable
by the rule with fresh variables. Now let us consider suffix rules of the second form, which would
be as follows, for
1<i\leq n
.

\dfrac{\langle s^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\Downarrow v^{\prime}~~c_{i+1}~~\mathsf{suff}(i)}{\langle f_{i}(\bar{s}),\bar{\sigma}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}}

where
s^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},v^{\prime}
are fresh variables. Then the premise
\langle s^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\Downarrow v^{\prime}~~c_{i+1}~~\mathsf{suff}(i)
is an instance of
\mathsf{suff}(i-1)

v^{\prime}
is replaced by

(see Section

). Therefore, we do not need to generate suffix rules of the second kind, since any
conjunction foldable by the
i^{th}
suffix rule of the second kind is foldable by the

i-1^{th}
suffix rule of the first kind. This also explains why we generate suffix rules for

1\leq i\leq n
instead of
1<i\leq n
.

in the definition of a suffix rule. Indexed variants of the syntax constructor
appearing on the original rule are chosen for readability.

4.2
A further refinement – elimination of the value argument

A big-step transition
C\Downarrow v
transforms a configuration
C
to a value
v
.
A small-step transition based on Horn clauses transforms one call
C_{1}\Downarrow v_{1}

to another call
C_{2}\Downarrow v_{2}
, except for the
final transition in a run, which takes
C\Downarrow v_{1}
to
\mathsf{true}
.

By contrast, small-step transitions for programming languages are of the form
C_{1}\Rightarrow C_{2}

except the final transition in a run, which is
C\Rightarrow v
. In other words, the final value
is not mentioned in a derivation except in the final transition.

We now show how to modify the Horn clause derivation to
produce small-step transitions of the expected form, namely

\langle s_{1},\rho_{1}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\langle s_{2},\rho_{2}\rangle

by eliminating the value arguments.

Definition 2

(Final value condition)
.

Let

\dfrac{\langle s_{1},\sigma_{1}\rangle\Downarrow v_{1},\ldots,\langle s_{n},\sigma_{n}\rangle\Downarrow v_{n}}{\langle s_{0},\sigma_{0}\rangle\Downarrow v_{0}}~~~~\mathrm{if}~c

be a big-step transition rule. Condition
c
is included in the premises and can appear anywhere among
the premises. Then the rule satisfies the
final value condition
if
v_{0}
does not occur in the premises
except in the final premise, and if the final premise is
\langle s_{n},\sigma_{n}\rangle\Downarrow v_{n}
,
then
v_{0}=v_{n}
.

We have not encountered any semantics rules that do not satisfy the final value condition, but if there were
one, we could modify it by replacing any occurrences of
v_{0}
in non-final premises by
a fresh variable, say
v^{\prime}
, and then add
v_{0}=v^{\prime}
as the final premise. Suffix rules derived from
clauses satisfying the condition also satisfy it.

.

Let
P
be a set of big-step rules satisfying the final value condition (Definition
2
).
Let

be derived
using the rules in Figure

Then

.

4.3
Optional refinements

In some cases, a suffix rule is not needed. Consider the big-step rule for executing a statement
sequence
s_{1};s_{2}
in an imperative language.

\dfrac{\langle s_{1},\rho\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{1}~~~~\langle s_{2},\rho_{1}\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{2}}{\langle s_{1};s_{2},\rho\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{2}}

Using
\mathsf{suff}(1)
we obtain a suffix rule.

\dfrac{\langle s^{\prime}_{1},\rho^{\prime}\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{1}~~~~\langle s_{2},\rho_{1}\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{2}}{\langle f(s^{\prime}_{1},s_{2}),\rho^{\prime}\rangle\Downarrow\rho_{2}}

The premise of the suffix rule is a variant of the premise of the original rule, and so the original rule
could be used for folding rather than the suffix rule. The suffix rule in this case can just be
omitted, with the advantage that the extra syntax constructor
f
is avoided.
This optimisation can be performed whenever the renamed terms
s_{1}
and
\rho_{1}
in
\mathsf{suff}(1)

do not appear elsewhere in the premises.

Secondly, the suffix rule for
\mathsf{suff}(n)
can be omitted, whenever the
c_{n+1}
premise is empty.
This requires splitting rule
[\mathtt{FOLD0}]
into two cases depending on whether

a single atom or not. This results in the following two rules.

\begin{array}[]{lll}~[\mathtt{FOLD0}_{1}]&\dfrac{\langle s_{1},\rho_{1}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}v_{1}}{\langle s,\rho\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\langle s_{2},\rho_{2}\rangle}&\text{where }\\
&&\langle s,\rho\rangle\Downarrow v\leftarrow(\langle s_{1},\rho_{1}\rangle\Downarrow v_{1},\langle s_{2},\rho_{2}\rangle\Downarrow v)\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathtt{suff}}]\\

5.1
A note on meta-programming techniques

Let us call the interpreter the
meta
-program and the set of Horn clauses being
interpreted the
object
program. Every syntactic object in the object program has a unique
representation as a variable-free term in the
meta-program. An object program variable such as
x
is represented by a term such as
\mathtt{{}^{\prime}\$VAR^{\prime}(^{\prime}x^{\prime})}
.
The object program is represented in the meta-program
as a variable-free term, such as
a list of clauses.

In order to perform unification on object language terms, we adopt a technique described
by Hill and Gallagher
gallagher:pepm93
;
Hill-Gallagher-Handbook
. Rather than compute an
\mathtt{mgu}
of two terms, we compute the

most general instance
of the terms.
Let
t_{1},t_{2}
be (representations of) object language terms that we wish to unify.
Being variable-free terms in the meta-language, they are not directly unifiable in the meta-language,
unless they happen to be
identical. The meta-language relation
\mathtt{instance}(u,v)
, is true if
v
is an instance of
u
.
When given
u
as input, it yields the most general instance
v
rather than enumerating all possible instances.

For example, let
t
be the object language term
\mathtt{f(^{\prime}\$VAR^{\prime}(^{\prime}x^{\prime}))}
.

\mathtt{instance}(t,y)

gives the answer substitution
y=\mathtt{f(}z\mathtt{)}
, where
z
is a
meta-language
variable.
Implicitly,
z
ranges over all object-language terms.
Thus, to unify
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, the meta-program calls

\mathtt{instance}(t_{1},x),\mathtt{instance}(t_{2},y),x=y.

This effectively asks whether there is a common instance of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and due to the way in which
the
\mathtt{instance}
predicate is implemented, the answer returned is the most general instance, if
it exists. The implementation of the
\mathtt{instance}
predicate is purely logical; the original Horn clause
code can be found in
Hill-Gallagher-Handbook
, Section 3.3, and our interpreters in this section
contain similar code.

The correctness theorem states that
A
is a logical consequence of
P
if and only
if there is a derivation
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
. In
this style of meta-programming, we start with some atom
A^{\prime}
which is
successively instantiated to
A
during the derivation; the final instance at the end of a derivation
run is the instance that is proved.

In this way operations such as unification, substitutions and renaming of object language terms
are ‘‘reflected” in the meta-language in a purely logical way, when we use one of the available
implementations of Prolog as the meta-language

5.2
Transformation by interpreter specialisation

The idea of program specialisation, sometimes called partial evaluation or mixed computation,
originated in the 1960s and 1970s

BeckmanHOS76
;
Ershov77
;
Futamura
;
Lombardi67
. Its relation to compilation
was discovered by Futamura
Futamura
.
We summarise here the approach to program transformation by specialisation
of interpreters
Bruynooghe-DeSchreye-Krekels
;
Gallagher-86
;
GiacobazziJM12
;
Gluck94
;
GluckJ94
;
Jones04
;
NysS18
;
Turchin85
.

Let
\mathbf{P}
be a program (a set of Horn clauses in our
applications). The function implemented by
\mathbf{P}
is denoted
\mathbf{[\![P]\!]}
,
using the notational conventions of Jones
et al.

Jones-Gomard-Sestoft
. The domain of

\mathbf{[\![P]\!]}

is assumed to consist of pairs
\mathbf{(s,d)}
.

\mathbf{[\![P]\!]}~\mathbf{(s,d)}
denotes the application of
\mathbf{[\![P]\!]}
to
\mathbf{(s,d)}
.

A
specialiser

\mathbf{Sp}
is a program
that is applied to
\mathbf{P}
and one of its
inputs
\mathbf{s}
(the “statically known” argument). The result is a specialised version of
\mathbf{P}
,
say
\mathbf{P_{s}}
, which, when applied to the remaining argument
\mathbf{d}
,
returns the same as
\mathbf{[\![P]\!]}~\mathbf{(s,d)}
.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbf{[\![Sp]\!]~(P,s)}&=&\mathbf{P_{s}}\\
\mathbf{[\![P_{s}]\!]~d}&=&\mathbf{[\![P]\!]~(s,d)}\end{array}

Suppose
\mathbf{I}
is an interpreter for some language.
It takes as arguments a program
\mathbf{p}
and its initial environment
\mathbf{e}
.
Then instantiating the equations above we have
\mathbf{[\![Sp]\!]~(I,p)}=\mathbf{I_{p}}
and

\mathbf{[\![I_{p}]\!]~e}=\mathbf{[\![I]\!]~(p,e)}
. The program
\mathbf{I_{p}}
inherits the behaviour
of the interpreter
\mathbf{I}
, including its complexity. As Jones
Jones04
pointed out,
specialisation of interpreters can give non-linear speed-up, when comparing
\mathbf{I_{p}}
with
\mathbf{p}
.

\mathbf{I_{p}}
also inherits the structure of its computations from
\mathbf{I}
.

6.1
Case study: the reversible programming language Janus

Big-step semantics for the reversible programming language Janus were written in 2007
by Yokoyama and Glück
YokoyamaG07
.
Janus is “an imperative, sequential language for reversibility” of programs.
In 2024, Lami
et al.

LamiLS24
noted that no small-step semantics for Janus existed, and
developed a set of small-step semantics rules, proving them equivalent to the earlier big-step semantics.

We rewrote the same big-step semantics as used by Lami
et al.
as Horn clauses. They use
two big-step transition relations, one for expressions and one for statements, and these
are merged into a single transition relation in our Horn clause representation.
For statements, the environment
consists of two components, the store and the set of procedure definitions, whereas
for expressions, the second component is not needed. Specialising the same contextual small-step
interpreter that we used for all our experiments, with respect to the Janus semantic rules,
we obtained essentially the same small-step rules as described by Lami
et al.

Furthermore, the proof of correctness follows from the correctness of the interpreter (shown in this paper)
and the specialiser. For the latter we used
Logen

logen
in all our experiments, a robust
and well-tested partial evaluator for Prolog based on proven Horn clause transformations.

The full set of Horn clauses for the big-step rules, and the derived small-set rules, is shown in Appendix
9

7
Discussion

Big-step and small-step Horn clause derivations

We presented a formalisation of Horn clause derivations, in the style of operational
semantics. Three different kinds of derivation were formalised: two small-step derivations
and one big-step.
Two of them – SLD small-step derivations and big-step derivations – were already
well known in various forms, for example as SLD proofs/refutations or AND-tree derivations.
The third kind, contextual small-step derivations, is a novel formulation of Horn clause
derivations, as far as we are aware. All three forms were proved to be equivalent with
respect to successful derivations.

Another novelty regarding Horn clause derivations was the observation that the “stack” or
conjunction of atoms awaiting selection during a small-step derivation has a particular form
that lends itself to “folding” into a single atom, called
\mathtt{stack(z)}
, employing
“suffix rules” defining the predicate
\mathtt{stack(z)}
formed from suffixes of
the given set of Horn clauses.

Application to big-step operational rules

At this point these novelties might appear to be curiosities without practical value.
Their significance arises in derivations using a particular class of Horn clauses, namely
big-step semantic rules. The structure of contextual small-step derivations turns
out to match small-step derivations in operational semantics. Refinements
to the generic Horn clause contextual small-step derivations, using features of big-step
semantic rules, allows the connection
to be even closer. In particular, the “stack” predicate can be modelled by the big-step predicate,
and its value argument omitted from small steps.

Automatic translation of big-step to small-step semantics

We then exploited the well-known method of interpreter specialisation to transform
big-step operational semantics to small-step semantics. A contextual small-step derivation
interpreter is specialised with respect to a given set of big-step rules. In effect
the contextual small-step derivation structure is “compiled in” to the big-step rules,
resulting in clauses that are isomorphic to the desired small-step semantic rules.
This technique was demonstrated on a small imperative language, the functional language Mini-ML
with a
\mathrm{letrec}
construct,
and the reversible language Janus.

Horn clauses for operational semantics

Underlying the whole procedure is the use of Horn clauses to implement semantic rules. Indeed, our
perspective is that operational semantics rules
are
Horn clauses, however they are presented
on the page. The declarative and procedural readings of Horn clauses are inherited by
semantic rules, rendering unnecessary the construction of “definitional interpreters” to give
a procedural reading to semantic rules. In our experiments, we were able to test the equivalence
of the various semantics just by “running” the respective Horn clauses on example programs
using a logic programming
system. For instance, the Janus big-step rules and derived small-step rules could both be run on the
Fibonacci example of the Janus paper, with identical results of course; similarly all of the Mini-ML examples
presented by Kahn
Kahn87
could be tested in both semantics. This is more than just convenience – it underlines
the close logical connection between semantic rules and Horn clauses.
We have no doubt that there are many other ways of exploiting
the very close connection between semantic rules and Horn clauses.(see Future Work).

7.1
Related work

There have been previous works demonstrating automatic translation from big-step semantics
to small-step semantics (or to abstract machines, a related problem)

VeselyF19
;
AmbalLSN22
;
HuizingKK10-short
;
Ager04-short
.
These approaches are based either on specifically designed transformations applied
to the big-step rules, or on transformations applied to a “definitional interpreter” for the rules.
The latter approach is in the same spirit as ours, in that the transformed semantic rules
are “inherited” from the transformed interpreter. In particular, the “continuation-passing”
transformation of a definitional interpreter
VeselyF19
;
AmbalLSN22
, has clear connections to
our small steps with a stack or “continuation”. Some of the transformations
applied in other work also have the flavour of partial evaluation, and the partial
evaluator that we used performs some of the transformations (such as argument filtering) seen
in other methods.
In summary, our approach, though not in principle more general, seems more
direct and transparent, in that the small-step derivation structure appears already in the
interpreter, which is then specialised with respect to a set of big-step rules.
Implementation of the transformation using an existing program specialiser
is also an advantage. The proof of correctness is
more direct; the proofs of equivalence of the various Horn clause derivations
are straightforward inductions on derivation tree height.
We have succeeded in transforming some medium-sized examples, such as the Janus semantics,
that in their complexity go beyond examples we have seen in other work.
The correctness of our approach follows from the correctness of the generic Horn clause
interpreters and the correctness of the specialisation tools.

Our own previous work
GallagherHML23
was also based on interpreter specialisation, but the interpreter
was an ad hoc procedure directed at big-step rules, unlike our current work, in which
the interpreters are for Horn clauses generally, and syntactic refinement are made for the
case of interpreting semantic rules. In our previous work, correctness of the interpreter was not established; now
the correctness proofs rest on the correctness of the Horn clause derivation strategies.

The “suffix rules” presented as part of our method suggest connections to other work on
manipulating big-step semantic rules. The “pretty-big-step” semantics of Charguéraud

Chargueraud13
proposes a decomposition of big-step rules into a
number of smaller rules dealing with one premise at a time.
We postulate that pretty-big-step semantic rules could be
constructed by folding the big-step rules premises using our suffix rules; but confirming this
guess remains future work. A similar remark applies to the “computation tree semantics”
recently proposed by Harbo and Hüttel
HarboH26
.

7.2
Future work

Translation from small-step rules to big-step rules seems feasible using the same
approach. A big-step Horn clause interpreter could be specialised with respect to
a set of small step rules. The main practical issue seems to be control of the
partial evaluation, which would be based on distinguishing between the “run”
and “small-step” predicates of the rules.

The Janus semantics discussed in Section
5
prompts the question of
extending the translation to include rules
handling exceptions, abnormal termination and such like, as is done by Lami
et al.

Indeed, one of the reasons for using small-step semantics is the ability to observe more
than can be observed in a big-step derivation.
We intend to derive such error rules from a Horn clause interpreter modified to
make failure observable. Inspiration can be drawn from meta-interpreters for Prolog
capturing
extensions of standard program execution, including failure states.

In program verification based on Constrained Horn Clauses (CHC)
AngelisFGHPP22
;
BjornerGMR15
, it is required to
translate a program from some source language into CHCs. This can be done reliably
using specialisation of semantics for the source language
AngelisFGHPP22
. The use of
big-step or small-step semantics can make an important difference to the CHCs obtained,
especially in the handling of loops in the source programs. An automatic
translator from big-step to small-step semantics and vice versa would
allow flexible choice of semantics; we see it as an important
component in a CHC-based verification toolchain.

A run
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
is

expanding
if no clause of the form

is used in the derivation. We write

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
for an expanding run.

Lemma
3
.

For all expanding runs
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
where
R\neq\mathsf{true}
,
there exist
B,S,S^{\prime}
such that
R=(B,S)
and
R^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},S)
.

Proof.

By induction on the height of run derivations.

•

k=1
:
The run is
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R
, where
R\neq\mathsf{true}
, hence
we write
R=(B,S)
and
(B,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(B,S)
, so the proposition
holds with
S^{\prime}=B
.

•

k\geq 1
:
An expanding run of height

k+1
exists only if there is a clause
B\leftarrow(B^{\prime},S^{\prime})\in[P]

and a run of height
k

(B^{\prime},S^{\prime},S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
.
In this case the run of height
k+1
is
(B,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, there exists
S^{\prime\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}=(S^{\prime\prime},S^{\prime},S)
, hence we have

(B,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(S^{\prime\prime},S^{\prime},S)
which establishes the proposition.

∎

Lemma
4
.

For all conjunctions
R,R^{\prime},
and
R^{\prime\prime}
, where
R\neq\mathsf{true}
,

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
if and only if

(R,R^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})
, and both runs have the same height.

Proof.

By induction on the height of run derivations using
\mathtt{[RUN0]}
and
\mathtt{[RUN1]}
.

•

k=1
:

Using
\mathtt{[RUN0]}
,
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~R
and
(R,R^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~(R,R^{\prime\prime})

both hold, for all conjunctions
R,R^{\prime\prime}
. Both are expanding runs since no clause
B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}

is used. Hence the property holds for
k=1
.

•

k\geq 1
:
Let
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
be a run of height
k

(where this run is the second premise of
\mathtt{[RUN1]}
).
An expanding run of height
k+1
exists using this premise if and only if
there is a clause
B\leftarrow S
,
S\neq\mathsf{true}
and
S
is a prefix of
R
(say
R=(S,S^{\prime})
),
that is, there is a run
(B,S^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~R^{\prime}
.
This holds if and only if there is
an expanding run of height
k+1
,

(B,S^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})
constructed using the same clause
and the premise

(R,R^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}_{e}\!\!*~(R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})
which is also a run of height
k
(for any
R^{\prime\prime}

by the inductive hypothesis.

Lemma
5
.

There is a contextual small-step transition

if and only if there is a run

\rightarrow

\end{array}

\leftarrow
: By induction on the height
k
of the
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*

•

k=1

R=(B,R^{\prime})

\mathtt{[RUN1}]

\mathtt{[RES0}]

•

k\geq 1

\\

Theorem
6
.

For a given set of Horn clauses,
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}

if and only if

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
.

Proof.

Separate cases for the
if
and
only if
implications.

•

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
implies
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
.
Let
k
be the number of steps in the run
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\Rightarrow}}\!\!*~\mathsf{true}
that
use a clause of form

B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
. Clearly the final step in the run uses such a clause.
Thus the run consists of
k
(possibly empty) expanding runs each followed by the use of
a clause of form

B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}
.
By Lemma
5
, each such section corresponds
to a
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
step. By induction on

•

Lemma

.

.

{\cal C}^{1}=R^{k_{1}}_{1}
.

\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})=\mathtt{stack(t)}
, where
\mathtt{stack(t)}
is an instance of the

uniquely determined by

,

m=1
: The derivation is

\mathtt{[RES0]}

•

m\geq 1

Theorem
10
.

Let
P
be a set of Horn clauses,
B
be an atom in the signature of
P
and
R,R_{1}
be
foldable conjunctions. Then

1.

B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}
if and only if
\exists k_{1}.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})
.

2.

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}
if and only if
\exists k_{1},k.\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})
.

Proof.

We first prove the forward direction of the equivalences.
The proof is by
induction on the height
m\geq 1
of the
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}
derivations.

•

m=1
:
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{true}
using
\mathtt{[RES0]}
, where
there is a clause
B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}\in[P]
. This holds if and only if there is
a derivation
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{true}
, that is
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{true})
,
using
\mathtt{[UNF0]}
.

•

m\geq 1
: Assume the property holds for derivations of height
m
. Consider a derivation of
height
m+1
, whose final step is
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,R_{1}
or

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,R_{1}
.
The final
step of the derivation uses
\mathtt{[RES1}_{1}]
,
\mathtt{[RES1}_{2}]
,
\mathtt{[RES1}_{3}]

(for
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}
), or
\mathtt{[CONJ0]}
or
\mathtt{[CONJ1]}

(for
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}
).
The following cases establish if this holds then

\exists k_{1}.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})
or

\exists k,k_{1}.\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})
respectively.

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mathtt{[RES1}_{1}]&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R&\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\in[P]\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k.B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{using }\mathtt{[UNF1]}\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\in[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
\mathtt{[RES1}_{2}]&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R&\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P]\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{by ind. hyp., and }\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{true})=\mathsf{true}\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{using }\mathtt{[FOLD0]}\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
\mathtt{[RES1}_{3}]&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}(R_{1}\wedge R)&\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P],\\
&&&R_{1}\neq\mathsf{true}\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k_{1}.B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k,k_{1}.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})\wedge R)^{k})&\text{using }\mathtt{[FOLD1]}\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k,k_{1}.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}}\wedge R^{k})&\\
\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mathtt{[CONJ0]}&&(R_{1}\wedge R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R&(R_{1}\wedge R)\text{ foldable, }\mathsf{atom\_or\_foldable}(R_{1})\\
&\rightarrow&R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[CONJ0]}\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{true})&\text{by ind. hyp. where }B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})\\
&&&\text{or }B^{\prime}=R_{1}\text{ is an atom}\\
&&\exists k.B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P_{\mathrm{suff}}]&\text{where }B=\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})\wedge R)^{k})\\
&&&\text{or }B=\mathsf{fold}(B^{\prime}\wedge R)^{k})\\
&\rightarrow&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{using }\mathtt{[FOLD0]}\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k,k_{1}.\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}}\wedge R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{if }B=\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})\wedge R)^{k})\\
&&\text{or }\exists k.\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}\wedge R)^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})&\text{if }B=\mathsf{fold}(B^{\prime}\wedge R)^{k})\\
\mathtt{[CONJ1]}&&(R_{1}\wedge R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}(R_{2}\wedge R)&(R_{1}\wedge R)\text{ foldable, }\mathsf{atom\_or\_foldable}(R_{1})\\
&\rightarrow&R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{2}&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[CONJ1]}\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k_{2}.B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{2}}_{2})&\text{by ind. hyp., where }B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})\\
&&&\text{or }B^{\prime}\text{ is an atom}\\
&&\exists k.B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P_{\mathrm{suff}}]&\text{where }B=\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})\wedge R)^{k})\\
&&&\text{or }B=\mathsf{fold}(B^{\prime}\wedge R)^{k})\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k_{2}.B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{2}^{k_{2}})\wedge R)^{k})&\text{using }\mathtt{[FOLD1]}\\
&\rightarrow&\exists k,k_{1},k_{2}.\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}}\wedge R^{k})&\\
&&~~~~~~~~~~~~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{2}^{k_{2}}\wedge R^{k})&\\
\end{array}

Next we prove the reverse direction, by induction on the height
m\geq 1
of the

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}
derivation.

•

m=1
:
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\,\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{true})
(where
\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{true})=\mathsf{true}
) using

\mathtt{[UNF0]}
, where
there is a clause
B\leftarrow\mathsf{true}\in[P]
. This implies there is
a derivation
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}

using
\mathtt{[RES0]}
.

•

m\geq 1
: Assume the property holds for derivations of height
m
. Consider a derivation of
height
m+1
, whose final step is
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{k_{1}})
, or

\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{k_{1}})

and the final step uses
\mathtt{[UNF1]}
,
\mathtt{[FOLD0]}
or
\mathtt{[FOLD1]}
.
The following cases establish that if this holds then

B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}
or
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}

respectively.
First, consider the case
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})
.

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mathtt{[UNF1]}&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\in[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[UNF1]}\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&\rightarrow&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{using }\mathtt{[RES1}_{1}]\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\in[P]\\
\mathtt{[FOLD0]}&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R_{1}\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[FOLD0]}\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&\rightarrow&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{using }\mathtt{[RES1}_{2}]\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R_{1}\in_{k}\in[P]\\
\mathtt{[FOLD1]}&&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})\wedge R)^{k})&\text{where }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})&\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&\rightarrow&B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}\wedge R&\text{using }\mathtt{[RES1}_{3}]\\
&&&\text{and }B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}\in[P]\\
\end{array}

Now consider a derivation of height
m+1
where the final step is

\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})
. The final step
can use

\mathtt{[FOLD0]}
or
\mathtt{[FOLD1]}
.
The following property relating folding and suffix clauses is used.

–

B\leftarrow R\in_{k}P_{\mathrm{suff}}
is a suffix clause of type 1 if and only if
B=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})
.

–

B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R\in_{k}P_{\mathrm{suff}}
is a suffix clause of type 2 if and only if

B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1})
for some
R_{1}^{k_{1}}
and
B=\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{1}}_{1}\wedge R^{k})
.

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mathtt{[FOLD0]}&&\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k_{1}})&\text{let }\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})=B,~B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R_{1}\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&&&\text{hence }R=R_{2}\wedge R_{1}\\
&&&\text{where }\mathsf{atom\_or\_foldable}(R_{2})\\
&\rightarrow&B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[FOLD0]}\\
&\rightarrow&R_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{true}&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&&&\text{either }B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R_{2})\text{ or }R_{2}=B^{\prime}\\
&\rightarrow&R_{2}\wedge R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{using }\mathtt{[CONJ0]}\\
&\rightarrow&R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{since }R=R_{2}\wedge R_{1}\\
\mathtt{[FOLD1]}&&\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k})&\text{let }\mathsf{fold}(R^{k})=B,~B\leftarrow B^{\prime}\wedge R_{3}\in_{k}[P\cup P_{\mathrm{suff}}]\\
&&&\text{hence }R=R_{4}\wedge R_{3}\\
&&&\text{where }\mathsf{atom\_or\_foldable}(R_{4})\\
&&&\text{and let }\mathsf{fold}(R_{1}^{k})=\mathsf{fold}(\mathsf{fold}(R_{2}^{k_{2}})\wedge R_{3})^{k})\\
&\rightarrow&B_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{fold}(R^{k_{2}}_{2})&\text{premise of }\mathtt{[FOLD1]}\\
&\rightarrow&R_{4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{2}&\text{by ind. hyp. }\\
&&&\text{either }B^{\prime}=\mathsf{fold}(R_{4})\text{ or }R_{4}=B^{\prime}\\
&\rightarrow&R_{4}\wedge R_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{2}\wedge R_{3}&\text{using }\mathtt{[CONJ1]}\\
&\rightarrow&R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{\Rightarrow}}R_{1}&\text{since }R=R_{4}\wedge R_{3},R_{1}=R_{2}\wedge R_{3}\\
\end{array}

Lemma

.
