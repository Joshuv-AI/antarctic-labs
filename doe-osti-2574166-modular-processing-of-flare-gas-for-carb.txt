Executive Summary
This project validated the technical and economic feasibility of a modular system that converts
flared natural gas into valuable carbon nanoproducts (CNPs) using catalytic chemical vapor
deposition (CVD). Key milestones were achieved, including reactor design and commissioning,
catalyst development, process optimization, technoeconomic analysis, and application testing in
concrete.

At lab scale, the team developed a fluidized bed reactor using silica fume-supported nickel
catalysts synthesized via atomic layer deposition (ALD). A sintering study optimized nickel
nanoparticle size, revealing how oxygen partial pressure, time, and temperature affect catalyst
morphology and carbon nanofilament growth. Optimized CVD conditions yielded up to 31.8
wt% carbon, with TEM confirming nanofilament structures and sustained hydrogen production.
Reactor upgrades and fluidization studies improved repeatability and reliability.

A modular pilot-scale reactor was built, instrumented, and commissioned for continuous
operation at up to 1 kg/hr carbon production, 675–800°C, and 290 psig. Initial tests confirmed
system functionality, though a missing downfeed tube limited carbon yield. The issue was
identified, and the system otherwise performed safely under design conditions.

Concrete testing showed that small additions of CNPs — including those synthesized via this
process — enhanced mechanical strength and reduced porosity while maintaining workability. A
clear protocol was developed for dispersing CNPs in ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC).

Technoeconomic analysis estimated a CNP composite production cost under $7/kg, with CNP
product values ranging from $14 to $60+/kg. Incorporating CNPs into concrete at sub-percent
levels added less than 10% to material cost, supporting strong economic potential.

Further work is recommended to:

 • Continue testing the TRL 6 reactor system.
 • Develop a continuous TRL 6 catalyst synthesis process.
 • Explore alternative nano-metals (e.g., Fe).
 • Optimize nanofilament size for target applications.

This project lays a solid foundation for commercial deployment of a modular, field-deployable
solution for flare gas mitigation through carbon nanomaterial production.

Project Objective/Goal:
The goal of the project is to develop a modular unit that will utilize a one-step chemical vapor
deposition (CVD) process to grow carbon nanoproducts (nanoparticles and nanofibers) (CNPs)
during natural gas decarbonization. A low-cost and scalable process for producing CNPs will be
demonstrated along with developing a mobile technology that will crack otherwise flared natural
gas. The synthesis and the impacts of CNPs on the durability of concrete will be investigated. The
concrete construction industry is the primary candidate for utilization of a mass-produced fiber
product.

 5

Background: Production of natural gas has risen dramatically in the United States over the past
twenty years, otherwise known as the “shale boom”. With increased production and subsequently
decreased price, natural gas is more readily flared due to high transportation costs1,2. A stark
example of this phenomenon is seen in North Dakota’s Bakken Oil Field. North Dakota’s natural
gas production increased 75x from 2007 to 2019, with proportional flaring increases. In 2014,
nearly a third of North Dakota’s natural gas produced did not make it to market. This equates to
$1 billion in lost revenue and the emissions equivalent of adding one million cars to the road1.
Therefore, there is a clear need to convert this harmful greenhouse emitter to a value-added
product. However, natural gas wells tend to deplete quickly, decreasing daily production by up to
20x in the first five years[2]. As a result, standard methane reforming processes are not
economically viable for short term well sites.
To combat these shortcomings, a low-cost, modular, and scalable process for natural gas
decarbonization to value-added carbon nanoproducts will be demonstrated along with developing
the technology to utilize this stranded and variable resource. In Budget Period 1, the construction
of a lab-scale reactor system will be completed, where particle atomic layer deposition (ALD)
catalysis and CVD synthesis range finding will be performed. Concurrently, cement mix design
will commence using commercially available CNPs in order to locate formulas with optimized
performance (e.g., better ductility and a reduced set-time). Furthermore, preliminary design work
into the techno-economic analysis (TEA) and pilot skid will begin. In Budget Period 2, CVD
reaction studies will be performed with mixed-metal adatom catalysts, which may consist of Fe,
Co, and/or Ni; the optimal catalyst and operating conditions will be selected. Construction and
initiation of the pilot skid will be completed. In Budget Period 3, the pilot skid performance will
be optimized, the prior cement mix design will be revisited with in-house CNPs, and kinetic studies
will commence using the preferred catalyst developed in the prior budget period. All changes to
the project management plan, technology management plan, and TEA will also be finalized.

Objectives: Design and construct a fluidized bed reactor (FBR) for carrying out chemical vapor

Literature Review & Motivation: Why we need Sintering
We aim to use sintering to precisely increase metal particle size to enable the growth of carbon
nanofibers. Here, it is well established that the growth of CNFs on transition metal catalysts
requires metal particle sizes of 8 nm or greater.5,6 While particle ALD allows for a fine particle
size distribution, the particle sizes explored are generally much less than 5 nm. Small particle sizes
can be abated through the integration of post-process thermal treatments such as sintering to
agglomerate metal particles and increase particle size. Furthermore, mechanisms to prevent
sintering of noble metal catalysts have been heavily studied, resulting in an information gap on
how to actually promote and tune transition metal sintering when desired.
The agglomeration of supported metal nanoparticles generally occurs via two different
mechanisms: Ostwald ripening or particle migration & coalescence.7 In Ostwald ripening, single
atoms exchange from smaller nanoparticles to larger nanoparticles driven by the increased surface
stability of larger nanoparticles (Gibbs-Thomson effect). In particle migration and coalescence,
entire nanoparticles diffuse along the support surface to collide and coalesce with other
nanoparticles.7 Some studies suggest a smaller starting particle size (<3nm) is preferential to

Literature generally agrees that increasing temperature and increasing time leads to increased
metal nanoparticle size.9-11 In the sintering experimental design, we aim to identify the optimal
sintering time and temperature for nickel to agglomerate to a particle size that will maximize

 9

subsequent carbon yield. We are constrained by time efficiency (24 hours) and temperature limits
of our reactor (850 °C). Lower metal loadings may decrease sintering rate due to the lesser surface
density and increase interparticle distance between NPs.13 This motivates our decision to use a
10wt% catalyst to begin our experimental campaign; the optimal sintering time and temperature
at 10wt% metal loading is likely lower than the time and temperature at lower metal loadings.
While there is general agreement on the impact of time, temperature, and metal surface density
(loading) on sintering, there are contrasting arguments on the impact of environment on sintering
rate. Specifically, oxidizing environments may enhance or inhibit the sintering of metal
nanoparticles depending on the temperature and metal-support interaction. In one example, a high
metal loading catalyst had improved sintering rates under oxygen vs hydrogen. Yet, under the
same time and temperature conditions, a lower metal loading catalyst had the opposite result, with
improved sintering rates under reduced conditions.12 Behafarid et al. showed the formation of Pt-
O bonds stabilizes Pt NPs under oxidizing conditions.12,13 Yet, a reducing environment could
restructure a SiO2 support, resulting in Pt3Si compounds with unknown catalytic activity.13 Both
environments have their challenges to enable NP growth and catalytic activity; therefore, this
parameter is the most vital to investigate and understand with our Ni/SiO2 catalyst.

Impact of Time and Temperature on Average Particle Size
Both increased temperature and increased time led to larger average NP sizes in an argon sintering
environment. This finding agrees with most literature.9-11 Figure 3 show the response surface
model for the interaction of temperature and time in an argon environment.

Figure 3. The response surface model depicting the interaction of temperature (°C) and time (hr)
 in an argon sintering environment. Average particle size is in units of nm.
While increased time leads to larger NPs, the sintering rate decreases with time. Both argon studies
at 850°C led to the formation of nickel silicates (see Table 4). This finding is corroborated by the
findings of Behafarid, F. et al.: when they sintered Pt on SiO2 in a reducing environment at 800°C,
they identified via in-situ TEM the presence of platinum silicates which eventually lead to the
formation of a Pt core with a silica shell.13 Both nickel silicates and nickel/silica core shells are
not preferred for CVD. As a result, sintering temperatures at 800°C or greater in reducing
environments will be avoided.
For the oxidizing environments, temperature had a greater impact on sintering rate than time,
though both parameters did not significantly improve the sintering rate. The major form of nickel
present was nickel oxide, indicating the cyclopentadiene ligands had been removed by high
temperature oxidation and replaced with NiO. This is corroborated by the disappearance of carbon
on the surface of the catalyst, measured by C LECO (see Table 4).
Impact of Sintering Environment on Carbon Content
The carbon LECO results detailed in Table 4 indicate that nearly all the carbon was removed from
the sintering studies in an oxidizing environment. Moreover, when blank silica was exposed to air
for 12 hours at 850 °C, the silica’s carbon content was measured at 0.35wt%, indicating this value
is the minimum amount of carbon that can be present on the sample. The oxidizing environment
 12

likely reacted with the cyclopentadiene leftover from ALD to form volatile carbonaceous species
that were subsequently purged from the sintering system.
Conversely, in the reducing environment, a majority of the carbon remained present on the
catalyst. There is not a clear trend on how temperature or time impacted the final carbon present
on the catalyst. We also do not know what form the carbon is in – the high temperatures of the
sintering study likely broke down the cyclopentadiene structure to a different carbonaceous form.
The continued presence of solid carbon may play an important role in the catalyst tuning for CVD.
Summary
The argon environment led to faster Ni NP sintering rates, with higher temperature and time
leading to larger average NP sizes. Temperatures above 800°C in reducing environment resulted
in the non-desirable formation of nickel silicates. In oxidizing environments, the major nickel
phase present was NiO with minimal carbon content. In reducing environments, the major nickel
phase was active Ni, with a layer of solid carbon in an unknown form. We now have an improved
understanding of how to tune the Ni particle size for CVD applications.

Literature Motivation: CVD – What maximizes carbon yield
CVD of methane is based on the reaction CH4➔C + 2H2 (ΔH = 74.85 kJ/mol). As mentioned in
the introduction, catalyst properties significantly influence hydrogen yield and CNF growth. More
specifically, larger metal particle sizes are necessary for nano-structure carbon growth. Possible
reasons for this include the lesser terrace/step ratio of smaller metal particles inhibit the formation
of structured carbon, where carbon encapsulation occurs before carbon nucleation can occur.4
Upon achieving the required metal particle size and shape to grow carbon nanostructures, other
catalyst properties heavily influence the mechanism of said CNF growth. Studies on both carbon
nanotube (CNT) and CNF growth indicate weak metal-support interactions result in tip growth,
where carbon precipitates at the metal-support interface, lifting the metal particle off the
support.14,15 Conversely, strong metal support interactions result in base growth, where carbon
structures form atop the metal particle.14,15 The transition metal impacts growth structure, whether
it be parallel, herringbone, or platelet. Metal particle size significantly controls and often matches
CNF diameter.16

The assembly and construction of the skid was completed in multiple phases
that were separated by many weeks. During the construction and
commissioning a critical component of the system was omitted. This was
the downfeed tube that carried silica powder with deposited metals from the
feed hopper to the bottom of the fluidized bed in the reactor. The diagram
to the right shows the reactor with the downfeed tube (red arrow).
The missing downfeed tube was thought to be in the reactor while operating
the system and lead to erroneous conclusions about the performance of the
system. Most significantly, was the conclusion of good reactor bed
fluidization. Without good fluidization it would be impossible to feed fresh
silica into the reactor along with the carrier gas through the downfeed tube.
Since the downfeed tube was missing the ability to feed silica without
clogging of the feed system wrongly indicated good fluidization.
Without good fluidization, multiple problems occur:
 1) poor heat transfer from the reactor wall heaters to the feed gas
 2) Minimal if any solids mixing
 3) Minimal if any gas to solids contacting.

The only indirect indicators available were the gas outlet temperature (TI-
341) and the bed-embedded thermocouple reading (TI-205) (Fig. 28).
However, TI-341 measures post-cooling-section gas temperature rather
than the reactor gas itself, and TI-205’s position within the flexible probe
makes its distance from the heating wall uncertain; especially critical when fluidization is absent.
During a typical run the silica would fill the reactor to the level of the overflow tube and then start
overflowing into the product hopper. This is typical behavior and only differentiated by the reactor
bed density from the expected operation with the downfeed tube in place and the bed well
fluidized. Significant reaction of methane to solid carbon likely did not occur because the reactor
bed was likely not fluidized, the gas not at the proper temperature, the solids not well mixed and
the gas to solids mixing very poor.

In conclusion, although the reaction has been demonstrated successfully at laboratory scale, this
scale-up effort was compromised by the downfeed-tube omission, preventing meaningful
evaluation of large-scale performance. Corrective action would require reactor modification to
reinstate the downfeed tube and additional experimental campaigns. Regrettably, current time
constraints and budget limitations preclude any further modifications or testing.

Task 4.0 – Cement Mix Design using Commercially Available Carbon
Nanoproducts (Q1 – Q8)
Compared to regular concrete, UHPC can provide extremely high compressive strength up to 300
MPa with reasonable flexural strength (20 MPa without fibers and over 40 MPa with metal,
synthetic or organic fibers) and workability [21]. From the ASTM standard C1856, the minimum
requirement of compressive strength of UHPC is 120 MPa [22]. The size of the aggregate in the
mix design is specified to be smaller than 5 mm. In recent decades, UHPC has been developed
resulting in the application in many projects in different countries around the world [23-25].
However, although the mixing procedure for UHPC is not complicated, it is still defined as an
expensive material due to the addition of high-range water reducer and fibers [26]. The general
use of UHPC is still limited to special designed projects. In this project, carbon nanoproducts will
be produced as a byproduct from the flared gas using silica fume as support. The result will provide
cheaper fibers which can be used to decrease the cost of UHPC.
Although cementitious material can provide sufficient compressive strength and stiffness for high
performance, the low tensile strength and fracture toughness cannot be changed based on the
mechanism of materials itself. From a micromechanics point of view, the crack widths in
cementitious materials start at the nanometer scale [27]. Traditional reinforcing fibers have
diameters at the millimeter scale so they cannot control nanoscale crack propagation nor prevent
the nucleation of cracks at the nanoscale [28]. Therefore, considering adding nanofibers into the
cementitious material has become a hot topic in the past two decades [29, 30]. Among all available
nanofibers, carbon nanofibers (CNFs) are the most common used in research since they have
excellent mechanical properties on their own [31]. The tensile strength of CNFs is around 2-5 GPa
and the elastic modulus can achieve 300 GPa [30]. Well dispersed CNFs can effectively improve
not only the concrete tensile strength, but also the compressive strength and electrical conductivity
for strain monitoring, and damage evaluation [32].

4.1.2 Dispersion of CNFs
Due to the van der Waal’s forces and hydrophobic property of fibers, the CNFs are bundled
together during the mixing stage with cementitious material [33, 34]. An inhomogeneous
distribution of CNFs in concrete can result in no impact or decreased mechanical performance. In
addition, the atomically smooth surface of bundled CNFs also causes an instable interfacial bond
strength with the cement matrix [35].

5.3 Report on Commercial Viability – Techno-economic Analysis
This evaluation is under review in the Journal of Energy Conversion and Management.

5.3.1 Abstract
Despite widespread efforts to mitigate emissions, significant volumes of natural gas continue to
be flared due to limited infrastructure and the absence of mobile systems for on-site utilization.
This work presents a conceptual technoeconomic analysis of a modular system designed to
valorize flare gas by converting natural gas into carbon nanomaterials for use in concrete. Unlike
conventional methane pyrolysis which prioritizes hydrogen as the primary product, this design
uses hydrogen as a heat source and instead targets carbon, the dominant product by mass, as the
economic driver.
The system employs a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (CVD) reactor with a sacrificial
catalyst composed of iron deposited onto silica fume. This process yields a composite carbon
nanoproduct (CNP) containing carbon nanoparticles and nanofilaments deposited on the catalyst,
eliminating the need for product separation or catalyst regeneration. The modular unit is designed
for remote deployment and includes integrated sulfur removal, requiring only natural gas as the
thermal input.
Technoeconomic analysis shows that the levelized selling price of the solid product remains below
$7 per kilogram across a range of catalyst loadings and carbon yields, with CNP values ranging
from $14 to $62 per kilogram, not including the cost of carbon–catalyst separation. The product
can be incorporated into cement at 0.25 wt% loading with less than a 10 percent increase in cost,
enabling applications in high-performance concrete. While further experimental validation and

 53

design optimization are warranted, this study demonstrates a viable route for converting flared gas
into functional carbon additives for concrete.

5.3.2 Introduction
Natural gas flaring, defined as the combustion of excess gas at oil production sites, remains a major
source of methane and CO₂ emissions, particularly in regions lacking pipeline infrastructure,
processing capacity, or economic incentives [1]. Although regulatory efforts [2] have reduced
flaring intensity, the problem remains substantial; in the United States alone, more than 324 billion
standard cubic feet (scf) of gas were vented or flared in 2023, including approximately 62 billion
scf in North Dakota. This disproportionate contribution is largely attributable to the prevalence of
unconventional oil wells which exhibit high initial production followed by rapid decline [3,4].
This transitory nature of well-site activity renders permanent infrastructure investments
impractical. As a result, a modular, relocatable system that enables on-site conversion of flared
gas into value-added products is highly desirable.
One such approach is methane pyrolysis (MP), the thermal decomposition of methane into
hydrogen and solid carbon in the absence of oxygen. The reaction is endothermic and relatively
simple (CH₄ → C + 2H₂), requiring no water or oxygen inputs. MP processes can vary significantly
in reactor design and operational parameters. Configurations include non-catalytic reactors
employing unconventional thermal sources such as plasma [5,6], cold-pulsed plasma, and
shockwave [7,8]. Catalytic systems utilize both supported [9,10] and unsupported [10,11] metal
catalysts, while molten metal [12–15] and/or molten salt media [8,16,17] facilitate heat transfer
and provide a reactive environment. In all configurations, a solid carbon byproduct is generated,
which poses both opportunities and challenges. MP typically focuses on hydrogen as the primary
product, but requires higher energy demands and capital costs than steam methane reforming [14],
the dominant industrial hydrogen production method. Thus, MP’s economic viability is contingent
on the co-sale of carbon at sufficient value. Technoeconomic analyses (TEAs) across multiple MP
configurations have shown that reaching cost parity with steam methane reforming necessitates
selling carbon at prices above $400 per metric ton [16,18,19]. In most cases, the produced carbon
necessitates separation and purification to be a viable co-product, incurring additional energy
expenditures and cost. These challenges are especially pronounced in catalyzed or molten-phase
systems where achieving the purity required for high-value applications, such as graphite for
battery anodes, remains a persistent obstacle [20].
Beyond technical considerations, this economic strategy faces a significant scalability constraint.
The global demand for functional carbon materials, including carbon black, activated carbon,
graphite, graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon nanofibers (CNFs), and other forms, is
relatively small compared to potential hydrogen production volumes. While the current global
functional carbon market supports a carbon-to-hydrogen mass ratio of approximately 1:5
[13,21,22], MP produces carbon and hydrogen at a 3:1 ratio. If hydrogen produced via MP were
to displace even 20% of current global hydrogen production at 97 million metric tons per year
[23], the resulting functional carbon material supply would saturate existing markets many times
over. As a result, without excessive carbon market growth, MP cannot be considered a scalable
hydrogen production pathway if it requires a high carbon selling price to remain viable.

 54

To address this constraint, alternative system designs are needed: systems in which hydrogen is
not the primary economic driver, but rather a secondary product or process enabler. One such
approach is to use the hydrogen generated via MP not as a market fuel but as an on-site heat source
to drive the pyrolysis reaction. In this process, the value proposition of MP shifts toward carbon,
specifically carbon nanoproducts, including nanoparticles and nanofilaments. These high-aspect-
ratio carbon materials have significant potential for advanced material and infrastructure
applications when produced at scale [22].
CNPs can be synthesized in a fluidized bed reactor (FBR) via the chemical vapor deposition
(CVD) of hydrocarbons on supported transition metal catalysts [10,24,25]. Among these, iron
stands out as a cost-effective and earth-abundant metal with proven efficacy in facilitating the
growth of filamentous carbon structures [9,24,25]. In the configuration considered here, atomic
layer deposition (ALD) is used to coat iron nanoparticles onto silica fume: a byproduct of silicon
metal production commonly used as a pozzolanic additive in concrete [26]. While not traditionally
used as a catalyst support, silica fume was selected in prior work [27] for its low cost, compatibility
with vapor-phase deposition, and functional synergy with cementitious systems. ALD offers
precise control over metal loading and nanoparticle dispersion [28], and when implemented in a
spatial configuration, enables continuous catalyst fabrication [29]. This continuous synthesis is
particularly advantageous given the conceptualization of the catalyst as sacrificial: the catalyst and
the CNPs together constitute the final product. This eliminates the need for downstream separation
or catalyst regeneration and aligns directly with the envisioned end-use application.
The support material thus serves a dual function, contributing to both catalyst performance and
product utility. CNPs dispersed within cement matrices have been shown to improve crack
resistance, increase tensile strength, reduce chloride permeability, and ultimately enhance
durability [30,31]. These enhanced structural features can extend the lifespan of concrete structures
and reduce their embodied carbon over time.
The technoeconomic viability of this concept is evaluated through the design of a modular system
that converts flared natural gas into carbon nanoproducts and hydrogen using a mobile fluidized
bed CVD reactor. The system is relocatable, enabling deployment at remote well sites with limited
infrastructure and declining production, such as those found in the Bakken Formation in North
Dakota [32]. By prioritizing CNPs as the primary product and utilizing the co-produced hydrogen
on-site for process heat, the design captures value from waste gas without reliance on hydrogen
markets or carbon purification.
This work integrates process simulation, economic modeling, market analysis, and catalyst
scenario evaluation to assess the feasibility of flare gas conversion into functional carbon
materials. In addition to technoeconomic performance, the analysis considers the impact of catalyst
design on solids throughput and reactor efficiency, identifying tradeoffs that inform future process
optimization. While the incorporation of carbon nanomaterials into concrete has been widely
studied, most prior efforts have focused on high-purity, high-cost materials. In contrast, the low-
cost, modular system presented here enables access to lower-purity carbon markets suitable for
infrastructure applications. This cost-driven shift broadens the addressable market for functional
carbon and supports economically viable flare gas utilization.

5.3.3 Methodology
The reactor system is based on a continuous-flow, high-temperature CVD process employing a
sacrificial iron-based catalyst deposited on silica fume via ALD. The reactor design is modular
and mobile, enabling deployment to remote NG flare sites. This section describes the CNP
synthesis process assessed in this work, with subsequent descriptions of the methodology involved
in the process modeling, economic analysis, and catalyst performance parametric study.
5.3.3.1 Process Description
A simplified process flow diagram of the modular reactor is depicted in Figure 1. Natural gas (NG)
enters the reactor at 34 bar and 2.22 MSCFH, parameters comparable to those of a single flare site
in the Bakken Formation in North Dakota [32]. Similarly, the natural gas composition, listed in
Table 17, is informed by concentrations from the region [33]. Prior to entering the process,
hydrogen sulfide is removed using a zinc oxide bed housed in a sulfur removal vessel (SRV).

The reactor inlet gas (stream 1) is preheated to 410 °C via a heat exchanger (HX) that recovers
thermal energy from the reactor exhaust. Simultaneously, catalyst material is fed at 53 kg/h (stream
C) and pressurized using a lock hopper system before entering the fluidized bed CVD reactor,
maintained at 900 °C. Within the CVD FBR, all hydrocarbons undergo a 55% single-pass
conversion to hydrogen and solid carbon, which deposits as a mixture of nanoparticles and
nanofibers. After reaction, the hot gas effluent (stream 3) is blended with a bypassed NG “quench”
stream of identical composition to the feed. This quench stream serves three purposes: (1) to
protect downstream equipment from thermal degradation, (2) to mitigate carbon fouling in piping
and exchangers by lowering the stream temperature, and (3) to provide additional fuel for reactor
heating. Following heat recovery, half of the product gas stream is routed to a hydrogen pump for
separation (stream 6). To prevent fouling of the hydrogen pump [34], an air-cooled heat exchanger
(AC) is installed to maintain the pump inlet temperature at 160 °C. The hydrogen pump recovers
80% of the hydrogen in stream 6. The purified hydrogen (stream 8) is recycled to the reactor feed
to suppress coking in the heat exchanger (Figure S2). The remaining product gas (stream 10) and
the non-permeate hydrogen stream (stream 9) are combusted to supply process heat. Prior to
combustion, a pressure reducing valve reduces the gas pressure from 34 bar to atmospheric.
Combustion occurs at 1000 °C, assuming complete conversion of all hydrogen and hydrocarbons
to CO₂ and H₂O. The solid product (stream P), composed of CNPs, iron, and silica, is recovered
as an integrated solid composite via a second lock hopper. For ease of communication, the
combined CNP/Fe/silica product will be henceforth referred to as the solid product, whereas the
carbon alone will be referred to as the CNP product.
To enable deployment at remote wellheads, the system is envisioned as two skid-mounted
modules, each approximately 8 feet wide, 10 feet tall, and 40 feet long. These modules are the size

 56

of standard fracking equipment trucks and are transported using existing fracking logistics. One
module houses the reactor, burner, and heat exchanger, while the second contains the twin lock
hopper assemblies and the SRV. In the field, the two units are placed side by side and connected
during setup. Each module is mounted on a skid frame that can be lowered into place at the site.
This modular layout enables rapid deployment and retrieval with minimal site preparation.

5.3.3.2 Catalyst Loading and Yield Parametric Analysis
Catalyst synthesis is considered outside the simulation boundary and involves alternating pulses
of organometallic iron precursors and reducing agents, achieving highly dispersed Fe
nanoparticles on silica fume via ALD. Methane decomposes in the presence of the Fe/SiO₂ catalyst
at elevated temperatures to form carbon nanoproducts (CNPs) and hydrogen. The base case
scenario assumes a catalyst loading of 5 g Fe per g silica fume support, and a yield of 5 g CNP per
g Fe, consistent with available experimental benchmarks for Fe-based catalysts in fluidized bed
reactors at this temperature [9]. Although this study does not quantify the ratio of carbon
nanoparticles to nanofilaments, the solid product is expected to contain both, with nanofilaments
being the dominant form. Their relative proportions influence product value and warrant further
investigation. Given the novelty of the ALD-deposited catalyst, catalyst performance metrics such
as yield (gCNP/gFe) remain uncertain. Therefore, a parametric analysis is performed by varying
both catalyst loading and yield across a series of scenarios to understand their effect on product
economics. The cases considered are depicted in Table 18.

5.3.3.3 Process Model
Central process assumptions are summarized in Table 19. The process simulation was conducted
using ASPEN PLUS V11 (Aspen Technology, inc), including the energy and mass balances. The
Peng–Robinson equation of state was selected for property estimation due to its suitability for
hydrocarbon-rich systems [35]. Hydrogen sulfide removal was calculated externally to the Aspen
simulation. Prior to entering the process, hydrogen sulfide is removed using a zinc oxide fixed bed
operating at 95% efficiency, with the ZnO replaced every 4 months. Thermodynamic analyses
were also performed using FactSage chemical equilibrium calculations [36] to identify the
minimum cold-side temperature of the heat exchanger required to avoid carbon deposition
(coking). Stream tables are included in Table S7 for the base case scenario. Methane pyrolysis is
a moderately endothermic reaction (ΔH°₍₂₉₈ₖ₎ = +74.8 kJ/mol). To reach and maintain the reactor
temperature of 900 °C, excess hydrogen and unconverted NG are combusted in-situ. This approach
eliminates external heating utilities and enables safe destruction of flammable off-gases. In the
simulation, the reactor system is modeled as two separate units: a CVD reactor (CVDRXR) and a
burner (BURNER). While these may be physically integrated in the final design, they are treated
separately in the model for clarity. For modeling purposes, separating the reactor and burner
operations does not affect the overall energy balance, provided the burner supplies adequate heat.
The CVD reactor was implemented as an RStoic block in Aspen to specify reaction stoichiometry
and single-pass conversion. While user-specified in the model, the single-pass conversion of 55%
represents a realistic upper-bound assumption for a catalytic methane pyrolysis system at 900 °C
and 34.5 bar [37]. Although the feed contains a mixture of light hydrocarbons (C₁–C₄), methane
represents the most thermally stable component and was used as the reference species for
estimating overall conversion. This assumption is considered conservative, as heavier
hydrocarbons typically decompose more readily under pyrolytic conditions [37].

evaluate the sensitivity of the results to this assumption, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by
varying the NG cost from $0 to $2.20 per MMBtu, corresponding to the 2024 average Henry Hub
spot price [42]. As shown in Figure 4, the levelized selling price of the solid product is relatively
insensitive to NG price, increasing by only ~3% at the maximum NG cost. Similarly, at a fixed
selling price of $4.68/kg, the internal rate of return decreases by just 1.3 percentage points,
underscoring the limited impact of NG cost on project economics. This is primarily due to the low
NG flow rate and the relatively small contribution of NG-derived carbon to the final product,
which consists of only 20 wt% carbon nanoproduct in the base case scenario. In contrast, the
sacrificial catalyst, comprising 80 wt% of the final product, has a pronounced influence on system
economics. As illustrated in Figure 4, a 50% increase in catalyst cost raises the levelized selling
price to $5.56/kg. At a fixed selling price of $4.68/kg, this same cost increase decreases the IRR
to 16.3%. These results highlight the importance of using a low-cost, scalable catalyst system and
support material to maintain favorable economic performance.
Annual operating hours are another critical parameter, particularly given the modular nature of the
system and its need for relocation. While the base case assumes 8000 operating hours per year,
scenarios with more or less frequent redeployment were considered. As shown in Figure 42,
increasing annual operating hours to 8400, equivalent to a permanent facility, raises the IRR to
26.3%. Conversely, a 20% reduction in operating time (6400 hours/year) lowers the IRR to 19.5%,
or alternatively, requires a levelized selling price of $5.40 to maintain a 25% IRR. These findings
indicate that profitability can still be achieved with IRRs above 20% if annual operating hours
remain above 6500, supporting the viability of the system under moderate logistical constraints.

5.3.4.2 Catalyst Scenario Analysis
The impact of catalyst metal loading and carbon yield on the levelized selling price of the solid
product was evaluated through a parametric analysis, as shown in Figure 5. Catalyst parameters
for each scenario are detailed in Table 18, where the Fe loading, carbon yield (kg C/kg Fe), and
resulting catalyst flow rate and cost were systematically varied. All scenarios were simulated with
a fixed natural gas flow rate, single-pass conversion, and gas-phase separation parameters. As a
result, differences in cost arise primarily from changes in solids handling and CVD reactor sizing,
total solids production rate, and final product composition.
These changes affect the relative contributions of capital, variable, and fixed costs to the LSP,
visualized in Figure435. In Scenario 6 (10 wt% Fe, 10 kg C/kg Fe), the solid flow rate is minimized
due to high yield per unit catalyst, resulting in a lower contribution from variable costs (primarily

catalyst), but a higher contribution from fixed costs due to reduced throughput. In contrast,
Scenario 1 (1 wt% Fe, 5 kg C/kg Fe) is dominated by variable costs, with variable cost accounting
for 66% of the total LSP. The high catalyst flow rate and modest yield lead to substantial catalyst
consumption. A similar trend is observed in Scenario 3 (5 wt% Fe, 1 kg C/kg Fe), where limited
carbon deposition results in minimal value added. In this case, the variable cost comprises 70% of
the LSP, and the carbon yield is so low that it effectively results in a coated catalyst particle rather
than a composite product. Despite having a higher Fe loading than Scenario 1, the minimal carbon
incorporation offers no offsetting value, leading to a greater selling price than Scenario 1.

mechanical separation from the silica support, such as sedimentation and/or flotation [9,43]. These
methods add cost and complexity and are not included in this conceptual analysis, but may be
justified in high-yield scenarios where the carbon value significantly exceeds that of the composite.

5.3.4.3 Energy Analysis
Figure 45 highlights the energy requirements across major process units in the modular system.
As designed, energy requirements are minimal for all units except the CVD reactor, which
dominates the system's energy demand. The process relies almost entirely on natural gas to meet
thermal requirements, with 98.7% of the system’s total energy input, including both process
heating and chemical reactants, supplied via natural gas. This configuration enables modular
deployment but results in low thermal efficiency of the reactor system, comprising a natural gas
burner integrated with a CVD FBR.
As shown in Figure 45B, the ratio of CVD reactor heat duty to thermal energy released from the
burner ranges from 10% to 20% across catalyst scenarios. This analysis was based on an external
calculation of the CVD reactor duty. The gas-phase portion of the duty was determined from
enthalpy changes between reactor inlet and outlet gas streams in Aspen Plus™, while sensible

 67

heating of the solid catalyst was estimated separately using literature heat capacities. These
calculations aligned well with Aspen’s internal values for gas-phase enthalpy.
Reactor–burner systems typically achieve 30% to 70% thermal efficiency in practice, with higher
values associated with integrated designs that enhance heat transfer, such as annular or concentric
configurations [44]. The current process, however, is constrained by design decisions that mitigate
downstream coking risk. Specifically, the natural gas quench stream is used to rapidly reduce the
temperature of the reactor effluent before it enters the heat exchanger. This step is necessary to
minimize the risk of coke formation within the exchanger and associated piping, as carbon
deposition can occur if gas-phase hydrocarbons remain at elevated temperature. The quench
stream also provides additional fuel to the burner, which increases the overall natural gas
consumption and contributes to the system’s low thermal efficiency.
Coke formation is suppressed in the current design through a combination of rapid cooling and
elevated hydrogen partial pressure in the reactor effluent. Both factors influence the
thermodynamic equilibrium, with higher hydrogen content shifting the system away from solid
carbon formation and lower temperatures reducing kinetic favorability for deposition. This
behavior was evaluated using equilibrium modeling in FactSage. The existing configuration relies
more heavily on temperature reduction to reach a non-coking regime. However, higher single-pass
conversion could increase the hydrogen mole fraction in the effluent to levels that suppress coke
formation at a higher temperature range (> 600 °C). In such cases, future designs may be able to
reduce or eliminate the quench stream, provided an alternative method for managing reactor outlet
temperature is available. This could improve burner efficiency and reduce natural gas use.
In smaller-scale systems, ambient heat losses may provide additional passive temperature
reduction upstream of the heat exchanger. Although these effects are not fully captured in Aspen’s
default models, they may serve as viable alternatives to the quench stream for maintaining non-
coking conditions. These opportunities highlight the potential for improving thermal integration
and overall energy performance in future system iterations.

Despite the present inefficiencies, the process valorizes a waste stream while offering the potential

at this development stage, but future work should include one as process parameters are refined.
There is a clear path to additional improvements that could support the practical deployment of
this system for flare gas mitigation.
5.3.4.4 Market Analysis
As described in the introduction, the intended application for the composite solid product,
comprising carbon nanoproducts, iron, and silica fume, is as an additive to cement . In 2021,
global cement production exceeded 4.3 billion metric tons per year, with an average price of
$0.127 per kilogram [47]. Both carbon nanofibers and carbon nanotubes have demonstrated crack-
bridging properties in concrete [30,31] and can be produced via methane pyrolysis using an iron-
based catalyst in a fluidized bed reactor [9,25]. A major barrier to their practical use in cement is
the challenge of achieving uniform dispersion within the cement matrix. The polarity of iron in
the composite may support this dispersion [48], and silica fume, which is commonly added to
concrete after cement production, has been shown to enhance CNT dispersion in cement paste
[49]. Incorporation levels of carbon nanomaterials in cement have been reported between 0.01 and
2.0 wt%, with most studies concentrating on the 0.01 to 0.5 wt% range [30,31].
Figure 46a illustrates the resulting percent increase in cement cost as a function of additive loading,
comparing the impact of incorporating pure CNPs versus the entire solid product (which is 20 wt%
CNP by mass). The analysis applies the base case solid product levelized selling price of $4.68 per
kilogram. Because carbon comprises 20 wt% of the base case solid product, a given CNP loading
corresponds to five times that mass in composite material, enabling simultaneous incorporation of
iron and silica fume, which may offer additional performance benefits.
This linear relationship between additive loading and cost allows for direct comparison across a
range of scenarios. At a CNP loading of 0.01 wt%, corresponding to 0.05 wt% solid product, the
cement cost increases by 1.8%. A moderate loading of 0.1 wt% CNPs, or 0.5 wt% solid product,
increases the cost by 18.3%. At 0.25 wt% CNPs, equivalent to 1.25 wt% solid product, the increase
reaches 45.6%, which is prohibitively expensive. These results emphasize the importance of
balancing additive performance with cost. Ultimately, the composite additive can be incorporated

 69

within the targeted carbon loading range at 0.05 wt% while maintaining a modest cost impact
below 9.2%. Furthermore, these findings suggest that the composite product is best suited for use
in high-value cement and concrete applications, such as ultra-high-performance concrete, where
improvements in performance are prioritized over minimizing cost [31]. If the remaining 80 wt%
of the solid product, composed of iron and silica fume, contributes comparably to carbon
nanofibers in terms of performance enhancement, or if these components synergistically amplify
the effects of the CNPs, it may be possible to reduce the overall additive loading while maintaining
the same benefits.

Figure 46b evaluates how CNP adoption in cement could influence the size of the functional
carbon materials market. Assuming a baseline market size of 20 million metric tons per year
(Mt/y), the demand for functional carbon increases proportionally with both the CNP loading level
and the fraction of cement production that adopts the material. For example, if 50 percent of global
cement production incorporates 0.1 wt% CNPs, demand would rise by 5 percent, or 1 Mt/y.
Alternatively, if 30 percent of production uses 0.25 wt% CNPs, demand would increase by 7.5
percent, or 1.5 Mt/y. These projections illustrate that even low-to-moderate CNP incorporation
across a fraction of the market could expand demand for functional carbon. The relatively low
selling price of the CNP composite makes cement and concrete a practical and scalable outlet for
deploying carbon nanomaterials in cost-sensitive, high-volume markets.

5.3.5.5 Limitations of the study
This study presents a promising conceptual design for a modular system that valorizes flare gas
through the production of carbon nanoproducts, but limitations and opportunities for advancement
remain. ALD of iron onto silica fume has not yet been demonstrated at scale, and no kinetic data
are available for this catalyst in methane pyrolysis, particularly under the mixed hydrocarbon feed
used here. As a result, assumptions about catalyst conversion and yield introduce uncertainty into
the technoeconomic projections. While the application of the solid product in cement is well
motivated, the specific Fe/CNP/silica formulation has not been experimentally validated in
concrete. These gaps highlight opportunities for experimental validation in both catalysis and civil
materials research. Importantly, the technoeconomic framework presented here defines target
catalyst performance metrics and informs compositional boundaries for future formulations. As
discussed in Section 5.3.4.3, the current system exhibits relatively low thermal efficiency due to
excess natural gas combustion and limited heat integration. Although this configuration simplifies
operation and reduces coking risk, energy use could be improved through integration of solids
cooling with preheating or elimination of the natural gas quench stream through higher conversion.
The environmental impacts of CNPs themselves also warrant further investigation. While
nanostructured carbons such as carbon nanotubes and nanofibers have shown promise in concrete
applications, their lifecycle emissions and long-term environmental fate remain under active study
[50]. A full cradle-to-grave life cycle assessment, including downstream use of the composite in
concrete, would be valuable in future evaluations of the system’s environmental impact. Finally,
while solid catalyst and product handling is expected to be feasible at the proposed scale, logistics
related to transport, handling, and storage of the composite material fall outside the present system
boundary.

5.3.6 Conclusion
This study presents a technoeconomic framework for a modular system that converts flared natural
gas into carbon nanoproducts for use in concrete applications. Despite its relatively small scale,
the proposed design addresses a long-standing challenge in emissions mitigation: how to create
value from natural gas that would otherwise be flared. By prioritizing CNP production rather than
hydrogen as the primary revenue driver, the system offers a novel pathway to carbon-negative
material generation at remote oil and gas sites.
The system is specifically configured for distributed flare operation and includes integrated sulfur
removal, thermal self-sufficiency, and minimal reliance on external energy inputs beyond natural
gas. Under all catalyst loading and yield scenarios examined, the levelized selling price of the
composite solid product remains below $7 per kilogram. This cost structure, combined with the
composite's inherent compatibility with cementitious systems, makes it a feasible additive for
infrastructure applications and enables the expansion of the functional carbon materials market
beyond traditional high-purity niches.
Notably, the CNP component alone (excluding separation costs) has a levelized price ranging from
$14 to $62 per kilogram, depending on catalyst performance. These values are substantially lower

 71

than current commercial prices for carbon nanotubes, which can exceed $400 per kilogram.
Sensitivity analysis reveals that the levelized selling price of the solid product is most influenced
by catalyst cost and system uptime, while natural gas price has a negligible effect due to its low
usage rate. Capital expenditures, specifically the reactor, hydrogen pump, and solids handling
equipment, remain the dominant contributors to overall cost, underscoring the importance of
optimizing solids throughput and reactor efficiency. The economic viability of the CNP product is
further supported by its potential incorporation into cement at minimal cost impact. In high-value
cement applications, the additive could be used at typical CNP loadings with cost increases of less
than 10%. These findings suggest that infrastructure applications offer a scalable and profitable
market outlet for solid carbon nanomaterials.
Future work should prioritize the development of a kinetic model for the ALD-derived catalyst
and experimental validation of product performance in high performance concrete. However, the
primary opportunity for system improvement lies in enhancing reactor efficiency through better
heat integration and reduced reliance on natural gas combustion. As the process matures, life cycle
assessment and expanded environmental impact analysis will be critical to fully assess its role in
low-carbon manufacturing and deployment pathways.

Opportunities for Future Research
While this project successfully demonstrated the technical feasibility and economic viability of
modular flare gas conversion, several important opportunities remain for continued development.
Advancing the TRL 6 fluidized bed reactor system through extended operational testing and
process refinement will be key to improving carbon yield and consistency at scale. In parallel,
scaling the catalyst synthesis process for ALD-prepared and/or sacrificial catalysts will be critical
for supporting sustained production at higher throughputs.
Improving our understanding of silica fume fluidization under CVD conditions could unlock better
reactor performance and more uniform product quality. Additionally, future studies should explore
the use of iron-based catalysts, which may offer advantages in cost and material availability while
maintaining catalytic activity. Strategic engagement with large energy companies will also be
important for guiding deployment scenarios and identifying commercialization pathways. These
future efforts will build upon the progress made in this project and help translate a promising lab-
scale technology into a practical tool for flare gas mitigation.
