BROS: Bias-Corrected Randomized Subspaces for Memory-Efficient Single-Loop Bilevel Optimization
Hengrui Zhang, Boao Kong, Engao Zhang, Kun Yuan

Abstract
Stochastic bilevel optimization (SBO) has become a standard framework for hyperparameter learning, data reweighting, representation learning, and data-mixture optimization in deep learning. Existing exact single-loop SBO methods and memory-efficient surrogate SBO methods either create severe memory pressure for large lower-level neural networks or lack competitive convergence guarantees under standard assumptions. In this paper, we propose BROS , a memory-efficient single-loop SBO method with the same convergence rate order as exact single-loop SBO methods. BROS performs lower and auxiliary updates in randomized subspaces with a Rademacher bi-probe correction that recovers an unbiased Hessian-action estimator. We prove that BROS preserves the 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}) sample complexity of MA-SOBA for finding an ε \varepsilon -stationary point under only standard assumptions. Experiments on hyper-data cleaning, data-mixture learning, hyper-representation learning, and ViT sample reweighting show that BROS reduces peak memory by up to 44.9 % 44.9\% while closely matching full-space baseline performance.

Full-space hypergradient methods.
A large body of algorithms has been developed for SBO. A common route is to estimate the hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x)
either by approximating the inverse Hessian or by maintaining an auxiliary linear-system variable updated through Hessian-vector products (HVPs) and Jacobian-vector products (JVPs)
[
16
,
29
]
. This route includes early stochastic bilevel methods
[

,

28

and recent single-loop methods such as SOBA/SABA
[

[

[

[

[

Limitation 1.
Full-space hypergradient methods remain memory-heavy for large networks.

Although these methods need not explicitly form the full Hessian or Jacobian, their recursions still rely on full-space second-order information and auxiliary linear-system variables. When the lower-level variable is a large neural network with hundreds of millions or billions of parameters, the corresponding Hessian and Jacobian matrices are impossible to form or store, and even HVP-based full-space auxiliary recursions can impose prohibitive memory and computational costs.

Memory-efficient surrogate methods.
Another way to reduce the cost is to move away from the standard hypergradient update. Penalty methods
[
42
,
35
]
replace the original bilevel problem with a penalty reformulation. Value-function-based fully first-order methods
[
34
]
avoid HVP and JVP. FdeHBO
[
63
]
uses finite differences to avoid explicit HVP and JVP. Zeroth-order methods
[
51
]
further reduce the need for full derivative information. These approaches can substantially reduce or avoid explicit Hessian/Jacobian-vector computations, but this scalability typically comes with one of three costs: no exact recovery of the original bilevel solution, slower stochastic complexity, or additional structural/regularity assumptions:

Limitation 2.
Surrogate methods theoretically lose the exact hypergradient baseline.

Penalty methods optimize a finite-penalty surrogate rather than the original problem, with exact recovery requiring a limiting penalty regime and additional regularity conditions
[
42
,
35
]
. Value-function-based fully first-order methods typically have worse stochastic complexity orders than standard single-loop hypergradient methods
[
34
]
. Finite-difference methods such as FdeHBO
[
63
]
rely on an auxiliary projection and higher-order smoothness assumptions to control finite-difference errors. Zeroth-order bilevel methods often require many more function evaluations and stronger structural assumptions, such as low effective rank
[
51
]
. Thus, these approaches do not answer the question we study here:
can one reduce memory usage while preserving the standard single-loop hypergradient update and its
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
sample complexity for finding an
\varepsilon
-stationary point?

Randomized-subspace large-model training.
Recent progress in single-level large-model training suggests a complementary path. Several studies have observed and exploited low-rank or low-dimensional structure in large-model gradients and updates, leading to memory-efficient methods that store and optimize only subspace variables. For example, LoRA
[
26
]
constrains trainable updates to low-rank adapters, GaLore
[
67
]
projects gradient matrices into low-rank subspaces to reduce optimizer-state memory, and RSO
[
8
]
decomposes LLM training into a sequence of lower-dimensional subproblems. Importantly for our setting, RSO shows that randomized subspace training can reduce activation and gradient memory while retaining the same convergence order as full-space training in single-level optimization
[
8
]
. Motivated by this, we ask whether the lower-level and auxiliary-level training problem in SBO can also be solved in randomized subspaces.

Unfortunately, applying randomized subspace methods to SBO is highly nontrivial: unlike first-order RSO in single-level optimization, exact bilevel methods must compress and recover second-order information for the hypergradient. One may think of using an RSN-like strategy
[
17
]
: compute Hessian information inside the sampled subspace and lift it back to the original full space. This is sufficient for randomized Newton methods, whose analysis treats the sketched Newton step itself as a descent direction. However, in exact bilevel optimization, the Hessian determines the auxiliary linear-system used in the implicit-gradient formula. A biased lifted Hessian makes the auxiliary variable track the wrong system and biases the hypergradient.

C1. Memory-efficient randomized-subspace bilevel recursion.
We develop a single-loop stochastic bilevel method that decomposes the lower-level neural-network training recursion into randomized lower-dimensional matrix subproblems. Since each subproblem operates in a reduced subspace,
BROS
lowers the dimensionality and memory costs of the lower gradients, auxiliary directions, activations, and HVP/JVP computations required by the lower-level recursion.

C2. Unbiased hypergradient recovery with MA-SOBA-order convergence.
We identify the projection-induced bias in the lifted auxiliary Hessian action and remove it using a layerwise Rademacher bi-probe correction. Stochastic bilevel optimization via randomized subspace (

This section develops
BROS

L=1

2.1
Hypergradient computation and single-loop bilevel updates

When solving the upper-level problem, the core is the hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x)

[
16
]
. Under Assumption
1
, the lower-level solution
Y^{*}(x)
is unique, and the implicit-gradient formula gives:

\nabla\Phi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,Y^{*}(x))-\mathcal{J}(x,Y^{*}(x))\!\left[\mathcal{H}(x,Y^{*}(x))^{-1}[\nabla_{Y}f(x,Y^{*}(x))]\right].

(2)

Evaluating this expression directly is expensive because it involves the inverse of the lower Hessian operator. Following the standard exact-hypergradient route, we introduce an auxiliary variable

Z^{*}(x):=\mathcal{H}(x,Y^{*}(x))^{-1}[\nabla_{Y}f(x,Y^{*}(x))].

Equivalently,
Z^{*}(x)
is the solution to the quadratic auxiliary problem

Z^{*}(x)=\argmin_{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\langle Z,\mathcal{H}(x,Y^{*}(x))[Z]\rangle-\langle Z,\nabla_{Y}f(x,Y^{*}(x))\rangle\right\}.

(3)

Once
Z^{*}(x)
is obtained, the hypergradient reduces to
\nabla_{x}f(x,Y^{*}(x))-\mathcal{J}(x,Y^{*}(x))[Z^{*}(x)]
.

Following this idea, solving the bilevel problem essentially involves three coupled subproblems. Define
h(x,Y,Z):=\frac{1}{2}\langle Z,\mathcal{H}(x,Y)[Z]\rangle-\langle Z,\nabla_{Y}f(x,Y)\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle x^{*}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f(x,Y^{*}(x)),

(upper-level)

(4a)

\displaystyle Y^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}g(x,Y),

(lower-level)

(4b)

\displaystyle Z^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}h(x,Y^{*}(x),Z).

(auxiliary-level)

(4c)

Given
x
, the exact-hypergradient procedure first obtains
Y^{*}(x)
by solving the lower-level problem, then solves the auxiliary-level problem with
Y^{*}(x)
fixed to obtain
Z^{*}(x)
, and finally uses
Z^{*}(x)
to form the hypergradient for the upper-level update. The auxiliary formulation in (
4
) therefore leads to a three-level computational template. Rather than solving the lower-level and auxiliary-level problems to high accuracy in nested loops, recent exact single-loop methods perform one stochastic update for each level per iteration, yielding a coupled single-loop recursion over
(x,Y,Z)

[
10
,
7
]
.

Memory bottleneck in large-scale lower-level problems.
In the full-space single-loop template, the lower update uses a stochastic lower gradient, the auxiliary update uses HVPs to solve the quadratic system for
Z
, and the upper update substitutes the auxiliary variable into the hypergradient formula. This creates a substantial memory bottleneck. For a lower-level block
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the full-space recursion must maintain the lower variable
Y^{k}
and the auxiliary variable
Z^{k}
, and the derivative evaluations introduce additional
O(mn)
memory for lower gradients, auxiliary residuals, HVP inputs and outputs, and JVP-related quantities. Moreover, HVP/JVP computations are implemented through higher-order automatic differentiation, which can retain multiple forward/backward tapes and temporary tensors during one iteration. Thus, the peak trainable-side memory can be several times larger than the memory needed to store the lower model itself, creating a large overhead for high-dimensional neural networks and potentially translating into substantially higher accelerator-memory requirements and hardware provisioning costs.

2.2
Randomized subspace sketching for bilevel recursions

To reduce the memory overhead described above, a natural idea is to combine randomized subspace optimization (RSO)
[
23
,
8
]
for the lower-level update with randomized subspace Newton (RSN) sketching
[
17
]
for the auxiliary update.

Specifically, at iteration
k
, we sample a scaled Haar projector
P^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
, i.e.,
P^{k}=\sqrt{m/r}\,O_{r}^{k}
where
O_{r}^{k}
contains the first
r
columns of a Haar-uniform orthogonal matrix. This normalization gives
(P^{k})^{\top}P^{k}=(m/r)I_{r}
and
\mathbb{E}[P^{k}(P^{k})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=I_{m}
. We then search for the update to
Y^{k}
inside the subspace induced by
P^{k}
:

B^{k}=\argmin_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},B),\quad\tilde{g}_{k}(x,B):=g(x,Y^{k}+P^{k}B).

(5)

After solving this reduced problem, the full-space lower variable is updated by
Y^{k+1}=Y^{k}+P^{k}B^{k}
.

For the auxiliary variable, the analogous sketching step computes Hessian and Jacobian actions only with respect to the reduced variable
B
. For
W\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
, define the projected HVP and JVP by

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[W]:=\nabla_{BB}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},0)[W],\quad\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[W]:=\nabla_{xB}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},0)[W].

(6)

The vanilla sketch-then-lift auxiliary update would then replace the full-space HVP and JVP by

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm lift}^{k+1}[Z^{k}]:=P^{k}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[(P^{k})^{\top}Z^{k}],\quad\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm lift}^{k+1}[Z^{k}]:=\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[(P^{k})^{\top}Z^{k}].

(7)

This construction replaces the
O(mn)
full-space HVP/JVP directions and their associated temporary memory with projected
O(rn)
derivative directions. Thus, before the final lifting step, the lower-gradient, HVP, and JVP computations are carried out in the randomized subspace, directly reducing the memory overhead identified in Section
2.1
.

Key limitation for vanilla sketch-then-lift.
The key limitation is that the vanilla sketch-then-lift estimator does not, in general, recover the full-space HVP required by the exact hypergradient formula. First, the lifted Hessian action in (
7
) is generally a biased estimate of the full-space Hessian action: even when
P^{k}
is sampled from a Haar distribution and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[P^{k}(P^{k})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=I_{m}
, there is
no
guarantee for
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm lift}^{k+1}[Z^{k}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathcal{H}^{k}[Z^{k}]
. The auxiliary recursion therefore tracks an inexact linear system, which distorts the hypergradient and can lead to a nonstationary limit. Appendix
C.5
gives a deterministic quadratic counterexample for this issue. Second, any recovery mechanism for the above-mentioned distortion must preserve the subspace memory advantage. In particular, it
cannot
rely on forming full Hessian/Jacobian matrices, computing full-space HVPs/JVPs, or incurring full-space derivative memory.

2.3
Rademacher-probe recovery of auxiliary HVPs

To address this bias without sacrificing the subspace memory advantage,
BROS
introduces a Rademacher-probe correction that targets the full-space HVP using only randomized-subspace derivative queries. Define
\tilde{Z}^{k}:=(P^{k})^{\top}Z^{k}
and
A^{k+1}:=P^{k}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[\tilde{Z}^{k}]
. The Rademacher probes are random sign vectors and are not used as additional optimization directions; instead, they estimate the trace-like and transpose-like nuisance components induced by the vanilla lifted HVP. Specifically,
BROS
samples independent probes
u^{k+1}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and
\xi^{k+1}\in\{-1,1\}^{r}
, and queries one additional subspace HVP
M^{k+1}:=\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[\xi^{k+1}(u^{k+1})^{\top}]
, which is used to form the correction terms

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{k+1}

\displaystyle:=Z^{k}\!\Bigl((M^{k+1})^{\top}\xi^{k+1}(u^{k+1})^{\top}\Bigr),\quad\widehat{B}_{\sharp}^{k+1}

\displaystyle:=P^{k}\!\Bigl(\xi^{k+1}\bigl((M^{k+1})^{\top}(\tilde{Z}^{k}u^{k+1})\bigr)^{\top}\Bigr).

(8)

For Haar projectors
[
8
]

P^{k}
with
r\geq 2
, the corrected HVP estimator used by
BROS
is

\widehat{\mathcal{H}[Z]}^{k+1}:=\frac{r(m-1)(m+2)}{m(mr+m-2)}A^{k+1}-\frac{m-r}{mr+m-2}\widehat{C}^{k+1}+\frac{r(m-1)(m-r)}{m(r-1)(mr+m-2)}\bigl(A^{k+1}-\widehat{B}_{\sharp}^{k+1}\bigr).

(9)

For later use, we denote the corrected estimator in (
8
)–(
9
) by

\mathrm{CorrHVP}(P^{k},Z^{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1};u^{k+1},\xi^{k+1}):=\widehat{\mathcal{H}[Z]}^{k+1}.

This subroutine provides the debiased auxiliary Hessian-action direction used in place of the vanilla lifted HVP in the
Z
-update.

correction is orthogonal to the choice of optimizer: it only constructs the stochastic directions used by these two updates. Therefore, the same projected lower direction and auxiliary direction can be plugged into momentum SGD
[
52
]
or Adam-family optimizers
[
30
,
18
]
by replacing the plain SGD updates for
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
with the corresponding optimizer steps. Appendix
B.2
gives a more detailed discussion of this compatibility. The convergence theorem below is stated for the SGD-style recursion; analyzing momentum or adaptive variants is left for future work.

Assumptions

The first assumption is standard for hypergradient evaluation in bilevel analysis

.

Assumption 1

(Smoothness, boundedness, and lower-level curvature)
.

There exist positive constants
\mu_{g},L_{f,0},L_{f,1},L_{g,1},L_{g,2}>0
such that:

1.

\nabla f
and

are Lipschitz continuous in
(x,\boldsymbol{Y})
with constants
L_{f,1}
and
L_{g,1}
, and
\nabla^{2}g
is
L_{g,2}
-Lipschitz continuous as a linear operator on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{Y}
;

2.

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))\|\leq L_{f,0}
for all

;

3.

for every fixed

is
\mu_{g}

\mathcal{Y}
.

Let
L_{\max}:=\max\{L_{f,0},L_{f,1},L_{g,1},L_{g,2}\}
and
\kappa:=L_{\max}/\mu_{g}
.

The next assumption is the standard SBO oracle condition, adapted to the projected stochastic oracles used by
BROS
. A complete filtration-level statement is given in Appendix
C
.

Assumption 2

(Projected stochastic oracles)
.

At iteration
k
, let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
be the filtration before drawing
\boldsymbol{P}^{k}
and let
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
additionally include
\boldsymbol{P}^{k}
. For the projected local objectives induced by
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}+\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}
, the stochastic oracles in Algorithm
1
satisfy:

1.

the projected gradient oracles of
f
and
g
are conditionally unbiased given
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
and have uniformly bounded conditional second moments;

2.

the projected HVP and JVP oracles are conditionally unbiased given
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
, with conditional second moments bounded by a constant times the squared norm of the queried direction;

3.

multiple queries to the same sampled HVP/JVP operator within one iteration are allowed; the Rademacher probes are conditionally independent of the stochastic oracle samples given
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
.

The final assumption specifies the random subspaces used by
BROS
. It matches the standard isotropic projector assumption used in RSO
[
8
]
, and its moment identities are used in the diagonal-block bias correction.

Assumption 3

(Random projectors and probes)
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, the layerwise projectors
\{P_{\ell}^{k}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
are independent, with
P_{\ell}^{k}=\sqrt{m_{\ell}/r_{\ell}}\,O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}^{k}
, where
O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}^{k}
contains the first
r_{\ell}
columns of a Haar-uniform matrix
O_{\ell}^{k}
in the orthogonal group
O(m_{\ell})
. We assume
r_{\ell}\geq 2
for all
\ell
. The Rademacher probes in Algorithm
1
are independent across layers and independent of the stochastic oracle samples conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
.

Projector sampling in implementation

The Haar projector in Assumption
3
can be sampled without forming a full
m_{\ell}\times m_{\ell}
orthogonal matrix. In implementation, for each layer we draw a Gaussian matrix
G_{\ell}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times r_{\ell}}
, compute its thin QR factorization
G_{\ell}^{k}=Q_{\ell}^{k}R_{\ell}^{k}
with the standard positive-diagonal convention for
R_{\ell}^{k}
, and set
P_{\ell}^{k}=\sqrt{m_{\ell}/r_{\ell}}\,Q_{\ell}^{k}
. The resulting
Q_{\ell}^{k}
is Haar-uniform on the Stiefel manifold, equivalently distributed as the first
r_{\ell}
columns of a Haar-uniform matrix.

Convergence results for
BROS

In this subsection, we state the main guarantee supporting the core claims of
BROS
. We first present the following lemma to state that
BROS
restores the same conditional expectations that a full-space single-loop method would use, while keeping the variance controlled. A detailed version with explicit constants is proved in Appendix

.

Lemma 1

(Moment preservation of
BROS
directions)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
hold. Define the full-space bilevel directions

\displaystyle D_{Y}^{k}\hskip-2.77501pt:=\hskip-2.77501pt\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}),\hskip 9.24994ptD_{Z}^{k}\hskip-2.77501pt:=\hskip-2.77501pt\mathcal{H}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})[\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}]\hskip-1.84995pt-\hskip-2.77501pt\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}),\hskip 9.24994ptD_{x}^{k}\hskip-2.77501pt:=\hskip-2.77501pt\nabla_{x}f(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})\hskip-1.84995pt-\hskip-2.77501pt\mathcal{J}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})[\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}].

For the analysis, denote the three stochastic directions generated by Algorithm
1
as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\hskip-8.00003pt:=\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}\hskip-8.00003pt:=\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1}\hskip-10.00002pt-\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}
, and
\widehat{W}^{k+1}\hskip-3.00003pt:=\tilde{U}_{x}^{k+1}\hskip-3.00003pt-\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}]
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{Y}^{k},\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{Z}^{k},\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{x}^{k}.

(10)

Moreover, there exists a finite constant
C_{m}>0
, independent of
K
, such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\hskip-9.24994pt-D_{Y}^{k}\|^{2}\hskip-3.6999pt+\hskip-1.84995pt\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}\hskip-9.24994pt-D_{Z}^{k}\|^{2}\hskip-3.6999pt+\hskip-1.84995pt\|\widehat{W}^{k+1}\hskip-5.55002pt-D_{x}^{k}\|^{2}\hskip-2.77501pt\mid\hskip-1.84995pt\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\hskip-2.77501pt\leq\hskip-1.84995ptC_{m}\Bigl(\hskip-0.92505pt1+\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}\hskip-2.77501pt-\hskip-0.92505pt\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}\hskip-2.77501pt-\hskip-0.92505pt\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}\Bigr).

(11)

Consequently, Lemma
1
allows us to view the projected recursions of
BROS
as full-space single-loop bilevel updates with unbiased directions and controlled projection-induced variance, which leads to the following convergence guarantee.

Theorem 1

(Main guarantee of
BROS
)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
hold, and assume also that
\Phi^{*}:=\inf_{x}\Phi(x)>-\infty
. Consider Algorithm
1
. Let
\omega_{P}:=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq L}(m_{\ell}/r_{\ell})
be the worst layerwise full-rank to subspace-rank ratio. Then there exist positive coupling constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
and finite constants
C_{0},C_{1},\bar{\alpha}>0
, such that the following holds: If
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
,
\beta_{k}\equiv c_{1}\alpha
,
\gamma_{k}\equiv c_{2}\alpha
,
\theta_{k}\equiv c_{3}\alpha
, with
0<\alpha\leq(\bar{\alpha}/(1+\omega_{P}^{3}))
, we have (see detailed proof in Appendix
C
):

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}\leq 2\left(\frac{C_{0}(1+\omega_{P}^{2})}{K\alpha}+C_{1}(1+\omega_{P}^{4})\alpha\right).

(12)

Choosing
\alpha=\Theta(K^{-1/2})
subject to the above upper bound yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left((1+\omega_{P}^{4})K^{-1/2}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{R})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left((1+\omega_{P}^{4})K^{-1/2}\right),

where
R\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
is independent of the algorithmic randomness. Hence, for fixed projection ratio
\omega_{P}
,
BROS
attains the standard
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
sample complexity for finding an
\varepsilon
-stationary point. With the projection factor displayed, it suffices to take
K=\mathcal{O}((1+\omega_{P}^{4})^{2}\varepsilon^{-2})
.

Full-space-order rate and projection-ratio dependence.

Theorem
1
separates the standard single-loop SBO order from the price of projection: the dependence on
K
matches the
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})
stationarity rate of MA-SOBA with full-space hypergradient evaluation
[
7
]
, while the constants depend on
\omega_{P}=\max_{\ell}m_{\ell}/r_{\ell}
. Larger subspace ranks decrease
\omega_{P}
, improving both the transient term
C_{0}(1+\omega_{P}^{2})/(K\alpha)
and the stochastic-error term
C_{1}(1+\omega_{P}^{4})\alpha
, and allowing a larger stepsize through
\alpha\leq\bar{\alpha}/(1+\omega_{P}^{3})
. Conversely, smaller ranks save memory and projected-oracle cost but inflate the variance of lifted stochastic directions, so the same stationarity level may require more iterations or a smaller stepsize. In the full-rank case
r_{\ell}=m_{\ell}
for all layers,
\omega_{P}=1
, so the result recovers the usual full-space single-loop stochastic bilevel rate up to absolute constants.

Role of the bias correction.

The above MA-SOBA-order guarantee relies on the
BROS
correction because it centers the auxiliary linear-system recursion at the full-space Hessian action, which is required by Lemma
1
to target the true hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x)
. Appendix
C.5
gives a deterministic quadratic counterexample showing that, without this correction, the naive projected auxiliary HVP can converge to a biased auxiliary fixed point and drive the upper update to a nonstationary point, even with no stochastic objective noise and exact lower-level solutions.

6
Conclusion and future work

We propose
BROS
, a memory-efficient randomized-subspace method for stochastic bilevel optimization with multilayer matrix lower-level variables. By decomposing the lower and auxiliary recursions into randomized subspace updates and correcting the projection-induced auxiliary-HVP bias with Rademacher bi-probes,
BROS
preserves an unbiased hypergradient estimator and matches the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
convergence order of MA-SOBA while reducing trainable-side memory. Future work includes further reducing the remaining activation memory in large architectures and extending the convergence analysis beyond the current SGD optimizer.

Appendix A
Related work

Stochastic bilevel optimization.

Bilevel optimization is widely used in hyperparameter optimization, meta-learning, data reweighting, and representation learning
[
13
,
46
,
3
,
47
,
27
]
. The main technical difficulty is to estimate the hypergradient, since the upper-level gradient depends on how the lower-level solution changes with the upper-level variable. Early stochastic methods use double-loop updates, truncated backpropagation, approximate implicit differentiation, or two-timescale stochastic approximation
[
16
,
24
,
28
,
2
,
5
]
. More recent single-loop methods update the lower variable, an auxiliary variable, and the upper variable in one loop. Representative examples include the single-timescale method
[
15
]
, SUSTAIN
[
29
]
, SOBA/SABA
[
10
]
, MA-SOBA
[
7
]
, and SPABA
[
9
]
. Beyond the single-machine setting, decentralized and federated bilevel methods extend the same nested optimization paradigm to networked or client-server systems, where hypergradient estimation interacts with communication topology, client participation, data heterogeneity, and consensus errors
[
60
,
6
,
53
,
38
,
64
,
32
,
70
,
65
,
22
]
. These methods are the closest baseline for our work, but they still form both the lower update and the auxiliary update in the full parameter space.

Bilevel methods with alternative hypergradient estimators or formulations.

Another line of work studies bilevel optimization by changing how the hypergradient is computed, by adopting a different oracle model, or by reformulating the original bilevel problem. Penalty methods
[
42
,
35
]
replace the original bilevel problem with a penalty reformulation. Fully first-order methods
[
37
,
34
]
avoid Hessian- and Jacobian-vector products. FdeHBO
[

]
uses finite differences to avoid explicit Hessian- and Jacobian-vector products. Bilevel-ZOFO
[
51
]
combines zeroth-order outer updates with parameter-efficient inner updates. These methods reduce the burden of full hypergradient computation, but they no longer follow the same standard single-loop hypergradient update as methods such as MA-SOBA
[
7
]
: they either optimize a surrogate problem, use a different oracle, or rely on weaker hypergradient information.

Memory-efficient subspace and low-rank training.

Subspace and projection methods are a common way to reduce memory in single-level neural-network optimization. A broad class of methods forms updates in lower-dimensional spaces, either by projecting gradients or optimizer states
[
67
,
66
,
23
,
57
,
69
]
, or by restricting learning to sampled low-dimensional subspaces, as in randomized subspace optimization (RSO)
[
8
]
. Related parameter-efficient tuning methods also reduce the number of trainable degrees of freedom through low-dimensional modules, adapters, prompts, or low-rank updates
[
26
,
39
,
40
,
19
,
43
,
25
,
41
,
31
,
44
]
. Subspace ideas also appear in bilevel optimization, but with a different goal: LancBiO
[

uses Krylov/Lanczos subspaces to approximate Hessian-inverse-vector products by solving small projected linear systems. This reduces the computational cost of the auxiliary linear solve, but it is not designed to reduce trainable-side memory; maintaining Krylov bases and projected matrices can introduce additional storage. These works motivate our study, but they do not consider randomized subspace projection within single-loop bilevel hypergradient methods. In particular, they do not address how such projections affect the auxiliary Hessian-vector computation and the resulting hypergradient estimate.

Neural bilevel applications.

Our matrix-block setting is motivated by neural bilevel problems in which the lower-level variable is itself a multilayer model. Typical examples include data hyper-cleaning
[
48
]
, sample reweighting and label correction
[

,
68
,

,

,
62

[

Appendix B
Additional algorithm discussion

B.1
Multilayer derivation of the
BROS
recursion

Section
2
introduces the main idea through the single-layer case. We now spell out the corresponding multilayer notation used in Algorithm
1
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{L})
with
Y_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times n_{\ell}}
. At iteration
k
,
BROS
samples independent scaled Haar projectors
P_{\ell}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times r_{\ell}}
and writes
\boldsymbol{P}^{k}=(P_{1}^{k},\ldots,P_{L}^{k})
. The reduced search variable is
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{L})\in\mathcal{B}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{R}^{r_{\ell}\times n_{\ell}}
, and blockwise multiplication is defined by
(\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B})_{\ell}=P_{\ell}^{k}B_{\ell}
and
(\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\boldsymbol{U})_{\ell}=(P_{\ell}^{k})^{\top}U_{\ell}
.

The multilayer RSO lower-level step is obtained from the local projected objective

\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B}):=g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}+\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}).

Thus the lower direction is formed in the reduced product space
\mathcal{B}
and then lifted back to the matrix-block space as
\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}
estimates
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},\mathbf{0})
. Similarly,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}
estimates
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{f}_{k}(x^{k},\mathbf{0})
,
\tilde{U}_{x}^{k+1}
estimates
\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}_{k}(x^{k},\mathbf{0})
, and the projected linear operators
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
estimate the projected HVP and JVP.

The auxiliary HVP is assembled blockwise. Let
E_{t}(W)\in\mathcal{B}
denote the reduced block variable that inserts
W\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}\times n_{t}}
into block
t
and zeros elsewhere. For a target layer
\ell
, the full Hessian action decomposes as
(\mathcal{H}^{k}[\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}])_{\ell}=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathcal{H}_{\ell t}^{k}[Z_{t}^{k}]
. The off-diagonal contribution from block
t\neq\ell
is lifted directly from the projected HVP:

A_{\ell}^{(t),k+1}:=P_{\ell}^{k}\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{t}((P_{t}^{k})^{\top}Z_{t}^{k})]\bigr)_{\ell}.

For the diagonal contribution, the one-sided row projection creates the same self-block distortion as in the single-layer case. Therefore
BROS
applies the single-layer correction in (
9
) layerwise:

\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{\ell\ell}[Z_{\ell}]}^{k+1}:=\mathrm{CorrHVP}_{\ell}\!\left(P_{\ell}^{k},Z_{\ell}^{k},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1};u_{\ell}^{k+1},\xi_{\ell}^{k+1}\right).

The full multilayer estimator used in Algorithm
1
is then

(\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1})_{\ell}:=\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{\ell\ell}[Z_{\ell}]}^{k+1}+\sum_{t\neq\ell}A_{\ell}^{(t),k+1},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,L.

This estimator is plugged into the auxiliary residual
\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}
, while the projected mixed-Jacobian term
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}]
enters the moving-average hypergradient estimator. The resulting coupled update is exactly the recursion stated in Algorithm
1
. The moment-preservation proof for this blockwise construction is given in Lemma
5
.

B.2
Compatibility with momentum and adaptive optimizers

BROS
is an estimator-level construction rather than a complete prescription for the optimizer used to integrate the lower and auxiliary directions. At each iteration it produces two directions in the original matrix-block space:

G_{Y}^{k+1}:=\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1},\quad G_{Z}^{k+1}:=\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}.

Algorithm
1
uses the plain stochastic steps
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\beta_{k}G_{Y}^{k+1}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{Z}^{k+1}
because this is the recursion analyzed in Section
3
. In implementation, these two directions can instead be passed to a momentum or adaptive optimizer.

For a momentum SGD-like implementation, maintain velocity variables
\boldsymbol{M}_{Y}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{M}_{Z}^{k}
with the same block structure as
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
. Given momentum parameters
\rho_{Y},\rho_{Z}\in[0,1)
, the lower and auxiliary updates can be written as

\boldsymbol{M}_{Y}^{k+1}:=\rho_{Y}\boldsymbol{M}_{Y}^{k}+(1-\rho_{Y})G_{Y}^{k+1},\quad\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}:=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\beta_{k}\boldsymbol{M}_{Y}^{k+1},

\boldsymbol{M}_{Z}^{k+1}:=\rho_{Z}\boldsymbol{M}_{Z}^{k}+(1-\rho_{Z})G_{Z}^{k+1},\quad\boldsymbol{Z}^{k+1}:=\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\gamma_{k}\boldsymbol{M}_{Z}^{k+1}.

Thus the only difference from Algorithm
1
is that the projected lower direction and the corrected auxiliary direction are accumulated through momentum before updating the persistent variables.

For an Adam-like implementation, use
G_{Y}^{k+1}
and
G_{Z}^{k+1}
as gradient-like inputs to first- and second-moment recursions. For
\boldsymbol{Y}
, with moment parameters
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in[0,1)
, numerical constant
\epsilon_{\rm A}>0
, and decoupled weight decay
\lambda_{Y}\geq 0
, one may use

\boldsymbol{m}_{Y}^{k+1}:=\rho_{1}\boldsymbol{m}_{Y}^{k}+(1-\rho_{1})G_{Y}^{k+1},\quad\boldsymbol{q}_{Y}^{k+1}:=\rho_{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{Y}^{k}+(1-\rho_{2})(G_{Y}^{k+1}\odot G_{Y}^{k+1}),

\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{Y}^{k+1}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{m}_{Y}^{k+1}}{1-\rho_{1}^{k+1}},\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{Y}^{k+1}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{q}_{Y}^{k+1}}{1-\rho_{2}^{k+1}},\quad\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}:=(1-\beta_{k}\lambda_{Y})\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\beta_{k}\frac{\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{Y}^{k+1}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{Y}^{k+1}}+\epsilon_{\rm A}}.

The auxiliary variable is updated analogously:

\boldsymbol{m}_{Z}^{k+1}:=\rho_{1}\boldsymbol{m}_{Z}^{k}+(1-\rho_{1})G_{Z}^{k+1},\quad\boldsymbol{q}_{Z}^{k+1}:=\rho_{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{Z}^{k}+(1-\rho_{2})(G_{Z}^{k+1}\odot G_{Z}^{k+1}),

\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{Z}^{k+1}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{m}_{Z}^{k+1}}{1-\rho_{1}^{k+1}},\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{Z}^{k+1}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{q}_{Z}^{k+1}}{1-\rho_{2}^{k+1}},\quad\boldsymbol{Z}^{k+1}:=(1-\gamma_{k}\lambda_{Z})\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\gamma_{k}\frac{\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{Z}^{k+1}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{Z}^{k+1}}+\epsilon_{\rm A}}.

All products, square roots, and divisions in these Adam-like formulas are understood elementwise within each matrix block. These choices do not change the Rademacher bi-probe formula or the blockwise corrected HVP estimator; they only change how the resulting directions are accumulated across iterations.

There are two implementation conventions. The most direct convention maintains optimizer states in the original matrix-block space and feeds the lifted directions
G_{Y}^{k+1}
and
G_{Z}^{k+1}
to the optimizer. This is simple and compatible with standard optimizer implementations. A more memory-oriented convention applies momentum or adaptive updates to the reduced variables inside the current projected subproblem. In that case, the optimizer state is tied to the sampled projector and must be reset, refreshed, or transported when the projector changes. This projected-state convention is natural for subspace training, but its exact behavior depends on the projector refresh schedule and the optimizer-state transport rule.

The convergence theorem in the main text is stated for the SGD-style recursion. The key unbiasedness property of the
BROS
directions remains an estimator property, but a convergence proof for momentum or adaptive variants would need additional assumptions controlling the optimizer states or preconditioners. We therefore treat these variants as implementation-compatible extensions and leave their nonasymptotic analysis to future work.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[W]-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},\mathbf{0})[W]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[W]-\nabla_{x\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x^{k},\mathbf{0})[W]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

and independently of the random oracle objects above. Define

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}
are sampled once at iteration

and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}
extend to every
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}
-measurable random direction

with finite conditional second moment.

Assumption 6

C.2
Proof roadmap

This appendix proves the two claims stated in Section
3
: the moment-preservation lemma, which justifies the bias-corrected projected directions, and the main convergence theorem, which gives the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
sample complexity with projection-ratio dependent constants. For the convergence part, we use the Lyapunov quantity

V_{k}:=\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2},

where
x_{+}^{k}:=x^{k}-h^{k}
. It is enough to prove
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}V_{k}\leq C_{0}/(K\alpha)+C_{1}\alpha
, because
\|\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}\leq 2V_{k}
. The proof is organized as follows.

•

Step 1 (bias-corrected moment preservation).
Lemmas
2
–
5
establish the technical bridge behind Lemma
1
. The standard implicit-function identities are first extended to the product Frobenius space. The main new point is the auxiliary Hessian action: Lemma
3
identifies the projection distortion in the self-layer blocks, Lemma
4
shows that the Rademacher bi-probe does not introduce cross-layer mean errors, and Lemma
5
proves that the final
BROS
directions satisfy

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{Y}^{k},\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{Z}^{k},\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{x}^{k},

with conditional second moments controlled by the tracking errors. The leading projection-ratio dependence is tracked through
\omega_{P}=\max_{\ell}m_{\ell}/r_{\ell}
, while fixed dimension- and model-dependent constants are absorbed into the generic constants.

•

Step 2 (tracking the lower and auxiliary solutions).
With the centered directions from Step 1, Lemma
6
proves that the iterates
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}
track the moving targets
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})
. In weighted-sum form, the tracking errors are bounded by the primal progress term
\sum_{k}\alpha\mathbb{E}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}
, initialization constants, and stochastic terms of order
K\alpha^{2}
.

•

Step 3 (hypergradient tracking).
Lemma
7
converts the lower/auxiliary tracking errors from Step 2 into a bound on the bias between the centered estimator
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
and the true hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x^{k})
. Lemma
8
controls the variance of
\widehat{W}^{k+1}
, and Lemma
9
turns these estimates into a recursion for the moving-average error
S_{h}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\mathbb{E}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}
:

S_{h}\leq A_{0}+\rho_{0}S_{x}+A_{1}K\alpha^{2}+A_{2}\alpha,

where
S_{x}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\mathbb{E}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}
and
\rho_{0}
is defined in (
78
).

•

Step 4 (closing the descent recursion).
Lemma
10
gives the descent relation
S_{x}\leq 4(\Phi(x^{0})-\Phi^{*})+2S_{h}
. Substituting this into the Step 3 recursion and choosing the coupled stepsizes so that
\rho_{0}
and the remaining
\alpha
-dependent coefficient are small allows the
S_{h}
term to be absorbed. This yields

S_{h}+S_{x}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(K\alpha^{2}).

Dividing by
K\alpha
gives the formal bound in Theorem
2
. After absorbing fixed dimension- and model-dependent quantities into generic constants, the displayed projection-ratio dependence in the main-text form of Theorem
1
is expressed through
\omega_{P}
: the transient and stochastic-error terms scale as
1+\omega_{P}^{2}
and
1+\omega_{P}^{4}
, respectively, and the admissible stepsize is
\alpha\leq\bar{\alpha}/(1+\omega_{P}^{3})
. Choosing
\alpha=\Theta(K^{-1/2})
then gives
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{R})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}((1+\omega_{P}^{4})K^{-1/2})
, hence
K=\mathcal{O}((1+\omega_{P}^{4})^{2}\varepsilon^{-2})
for an
\varepsilon
-stationary point.

C.3
Supporting lemmas

Lemma 2

(Product-space smoothness consequences)
.

Suppose that
(A1)
holds. Let
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)
be the unique minimizer of
\boldsymbol{Y}\mapsto g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})
and define

\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x):=\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))^{-1}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))]\in\mathcal{Y}.

(13)

Then the following statements hold.

1.

Implicit hypergradient formula.

The bilevel objective
\Phi(x)=f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))
is differentiable and

\nabla\Phi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)].

(14)

2.

Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\cdot)
.

The mapping
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with

\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x})\|\leq L_{Y^{*}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|,\quad L_{Y^{*}}:=\frac{L_{g,1}}{\mu_{g}}\leq\kappa.

(15)

3.

Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\cdot)
.

The mapping
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with

\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\tilde{x})\|\leq L_{Z^{*}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|,\quad L_{Z^{*}}:=\sqrt{1+L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}\left(\frac{L_{f,1}}{\mu_{g}}+\frac{L_{f,0}L_{g,2}}{\mu_{g}^{2}}\right).

(16)

In particular, if
\kappa\geq 1
then
L_{Z^{*}}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\,\kappa^{3}
.

4.

Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\Phi
.

The gradient
\nabla\Phi
is Lipschitz continuous with

\|\nabla\Phi(x)-\nabla\Phi(\tilde{x})\|\leq L_{\nabla\Phi}\|x-\tilde{x}\|,

(17)

where one may take

L_{\nabla\Phi}:=\sqrt{1+L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}\left(L_{f,1}+\frac{L_{f,0}L_{g,2}}{\mu_{g}}+\frac{L_{g,1}L_{f,1}}{\mu_{g}}+\frac{L_{g,1}L_{f,0}L_{g,2}}{\mu_{g}^{2}}\right).

(18)

5.

Uniform bound on
\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)\|
.

For all
x
,

\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)\|\leq\frac{L_{f,0}}{\mu_{g}}.

(19)

Proof.

We endow
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{Y}
with the product norm
\|(x,\boldsymbol{Y})\|:=\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}}
.

Step 1: implicit differentiation and (
14
).

Fix
x
and write
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}:=\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)
. Define

F(x,\boldsymbol{Y}):=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})\in\mathcal{Y}.

By Assumption
1
,
g
is twice continuously differentiable, so
F
is continuously differentiable with

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}F(x,\boldsymbol{Y})=\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}),\quad\nabla_{x}F(x,\boldsymbol{Y})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}).

Since
\boldsymbol{Y}\mapsto g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex in the product Frobenius norm, its Hessian is uniformly positive definite:

\langle\boldsymbol{W},\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y})[\boldsymbol{W}]\rangle\geq\mu_{g}\|\boldsymbol{W}\|^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{Y},\ \forall(x,\boldsymbol{Y}).

(20)

Hence
\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
is invertible. Applying the implicit function theorem to
F(x,\boldsymbol{Y})=0
at
(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
shows that
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable locally, and differentiating
F(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))\equiv 0
in direction
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
gives

0=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})[u]+\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})[D\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)[u]],

so

D\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)[u]=-\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})^{-1}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})[u]].

(21)

Now compute the directional derivative of
\Phi(x)=f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))
:

D\Phi(x)[u]=\langle\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}),u\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}),D\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)[u]\rangle.

Substituting (
21
) and using that
\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})
is self-adjoint on
\mathcal{Y}
(hence so is its inverse) yields

D\Phi(x)[u]={}\langle\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}),u\rangle-\Bigl\langle\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})^{-1}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})],\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})[u]\Bigr\rangle.

We define
\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}):\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
as the adjoint of
u\mapsto\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})[u]
, i.e., for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}x}^{2}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})[u],\boldsymbol{W}\rangle=\langle u,\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y})[\boldsymbol{W}]\rangle.

(22)

Recalling the definition (
13
), we obtain

D\Phi(x)[u]=\langle\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})-\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*})[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)],u\rangle,

which proves (
14
).

Step 2: Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\cdot)
.

Fix
x,\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. Using the optimality conditions

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))=0,\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))=0,

and the strong monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{Y}\mapsto\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y})
, we get

\displaystyle\mu_{g}\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x})\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))\|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))\|.

By the
L_{g,1}
-Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla g
in Assumption
1
, the last term is at most
L_{g,1}\|x-\tilde{x}\|
, which proves (
15
).

Step 3: Lipschitz continuity and the bound of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\cdot)
.

Define

A(x):=\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)),\quad b(x):=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x)),\quad\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)=A(x)^{-1}[b(x)].

From (
20
),
\|A(x)^{-1}\|\leq 1/\mu_{g}
. Assumption
1
gives
\|b(x)\|\leq L_{f,0}
, so
\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)\|\leq\frac{L_{f,0}}{\mu_{g}}
.

Next, by Assumption
1
and (
15
),

\|b(x)-b(\tilde{x})\|\leq L_{f,1}\|(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))\|\leq L_{f,1}\sqrt{1+L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|.

(23)

Similarly, since
\nabla^{2}g
is
L_{g,2}
-Lipschitz,

\|A(x)-A(\tilde{x})\|\leq L_{g,2}\|(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))\|\leq L_{g,2}\sqrt{1+L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|.

(24)

Using the resolvent identity
A(x)^{-1}-A(\tilde{x})^{-1}=A(x)^{-1}(A(\tilde{x})-A(x))A(\tilde{x})^{-1}
, we have

\|A(x)^{-1}-A(\tilde{x})^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{g}^{2}}\|A(x)-A(\tilde{x})\|.

Decomposing

\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\tilde{x})=A(x)^{-1}[b(x)-b(\tilde{x})]+(A(x)^{-1}-A(\tilde{x})^{-1})[b(\tilde{x})]

and combining (

Step 4: Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\Phi

From (
14

\nabla\Phi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)].

Because
\nabla g
is
L_{g,1}

L_{g,1}

\|\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y})\|\leq L_{g,1},\quad\forall(x,\boldsymbol{Y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{Y}.

(26)

Likewise, the
L_{g,2}
-Lipschitz continuity of

implies

\|\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{Y}})\|\leq L_{g,2}\|(x,\boldsymbol{Y})-(\tilde{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{Y}})\|.

(27)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Phi(x)-\nabla\Phi(\tilde{x})\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-\nabla_{x}f(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))\|

\displaystyle+\|\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)]-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\tilde{x})]\|.

The first term is bounded by
L_{f,1}\sqrt{1+L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|
. For the second term, add and subtract
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)]
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)]-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))[\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(\tilde{x})]\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x))-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(\tilde{x}))\|\,\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x)\|

Lemma 3

(Layerwise projection identities and induced derivatives)
.

For each layer
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, let

P_{\ell}:=\sqrt{\frac{m_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}}}\,O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times r_{\ell}},\quad Q_{\ell}:=P_{\ell}P_{\ell}^{\top},

where
O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
contains the first
r_{\ell}
columns of a Haar-uniform matrix in
O(m_{\ell})
. Assume the projectors are independent across layers. Then:

(i)

For every
\ell
, we have
P_{\ell}^{\top}P_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}}I_{r_{\ell}}
and
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}]=I_{m_{\ell}}
.

(ii)

For every layer
\ell
, there exist constants

a_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(m_{\ell}r_{\ell}+m_{\ell}-2)}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)(m_{\ell}+2)},\quad b_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-r_{\ell})}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)(m_{\ell}+2)}

(28)

such that for any deterministic
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times m_{\ell}}
,

\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}AQ_{\ell}]=(a_{\ell}-b_{\ell})A+b_{\ell}A^{\top}+b_{\ell}\tr(A)I_{m_{\ell}},

(29)

and

a_{\ell}-2b_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(r_{\ell}-1)}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)}>0.

(30)

(iii)

For any pair
\ell\neq t
and any deterministic
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times m_{t}}
,

\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}AQ_{t}]=A.

(31)

(iv)

Fix an iteration
k
and define

\tilde{f}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B}):=f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}+\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}),\quad\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B}):=g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}+\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}).

Then at
\boldsymbol{B}=\mathbf{0}
,

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{f}_{k}(x,\mathbf{0})=\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}),\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\mathbf{0})=\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}),

and for every
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\mathbf{0})[\boldsymbol{W}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\mathcal{H}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})[\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{W}],

\displaystyle\nabla_{x\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\mathbf{0})[\boldsymbol{W}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(x,\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})[\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{W}].

Proof.

Proof of (i).

Fix a layer
\ell
. Since the columns of
O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
are orthonormal,

P_{\ell}^{\top}P_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}}O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}^{\top}O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}=\frac{m_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}}I_{r_{\ell}}.

To prove
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}]=I_{m_{\ell}}
, let
V\in O(m_{\ell})
be deterministic. By left-invariance of Haar measure,
VO_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
has the same distribution as
O_{\ell,r_{\ell}}
, hence

V\,\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}]\,V^{\top}=\mathbb{E}[VQ_{\ell}V^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}].

Therefore
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}]
commutes with every orthogonal matrix and must equal
cI_{m_{\ell}}
for some scalar
c
. Taking traces and using
P_{\ell}^{\top}P_{\ell}=(m_{\ell}/r_{\ell})I_{r_{\ell}}
gives

cm_{\ell}=\tr(\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}])=\mathbb{E}[\tr(P_{\ell}^{\top}P_{\ell})]=\tr\!\left(\frac{m_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}}I_{r_{\ell}}\right)=m_{\ell},

so
c=1
.

Proof of (ii).

Again fix
\ell
and abbreviate

m:=m_{\ell},\quad r:=r_{\ell},\quad P:=P_{\ell},\quad Q:=Q_{\ell}.

Let
Q_{0}:=O_{r}O_{r}^{\top}
, so
Q=(m/r)Q_{0}
. By conjugation invariance of
Q_{0}
, the fourth-order tensor

T_{iabj}:=\mathbb{E}[(Q_{0})_{ia}(Q_{0})_{bj}]

is
O(m)
-invariant and hence has the form

\mathbb{E}[(Q_{0})_{ia}(Q_{0})_{bj}]=c_{1}\delta_{ia}\delta_{bj}+c_{2}\delta_{ib}\delta_{aj}+c_{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ab}.

To identify the coefficients, note first that for a Haar row vector
u\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
,

(Q_{0})_{11}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}u_{j}^{2},

so the standard sphere moments give
\mathbb{E}[(Q_{0})_{11}^{2}]=\frac{r(r+2)}{m(m+2)}
. Using
\tr(Q_{0}^{2})=\tr(Q_{0})=r
together with rotational invariance yields
\mathbb{E}[(Q_{0})_{12}^{2}]=\frac{r(m-r)}{m(m-1)(m+2)}
. Evaluating the invariant-tensor form at
(i,a,b,j)=(1,2,1,2)
,
(1,2,2,1)
, and
(1,1,1,1)
shows that

c_{2}=c_{3}=\frac{r(m-r)}{m(m-1)(m+2)},\quad c_{1}=\frac{r(r(m+1)-2)}{m(m-1)(m+2)}.

Hence for any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,

\mathbb{E}[Q_{0}AQ_{0}]=c_{1}A+c_{2}A^{\top}+c_{2}\tr(A)I_{m}.

Multiplying by
(m/r)^{2}
and simplifying gives

\mathbb{E}[QAQ]=(a_{\ell}-b_{\ell})A+b_{\ell}A^{\top}+b_{\ell}\tr(A)I_{m_{\ell}},

with

a_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(m_{\ell}r_{\ell}+m_{\ell}-2)}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)(m_{\ell}+2)},\quad b_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-r_{\ell})}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)(m_{\ell}+2)}.

The formula
a_{\ell}-2b_{\ell}=\frac{m_{\ell}(r_{\ell}-1)}{r_{\ell}(m_{\ell}-1)}>0
follows by direct algebra.

Proof of (iii).

For
\ell\neq t
and deterministic
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}\times m_{t}}
, independence across layers gives

\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}AQ_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[Q_{\ell}]\,A\,\mathbb{E}[Q_{t}]=A,

where we used (i).

Proof of (iv).

Fix
k
and define
\Psi(\boldsymbol{B}):=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}+\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}
. For any
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B}),\boldsymbol{H}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}g\bigl(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B}+t\boldsymbol{H})\bigr)\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B})),\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{H}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B})),\boldsymbol{H}\rangle,

so

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B})=\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B})).

Evaluating at
\boldsymbol{B}=\mathbf{0}
proves the formula for
\tilde{g}_{k}
; the proof for
\tilde{f}_{k}
is identical.

For the Hessian, the directional derivative satisfies

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B})[\boldsymbol{W}]=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B}+t\boldsymbol{W})\right|_{t=0}=\boldsymbol{P}^{k\top}\mathcal{H}(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B}))[\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{W}],

which gives the first identity at
\boldsymbol{B}=\mathbf{0}
. Similarly,

\nabla_{x\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{B})[\boldsymbol{W}]=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\nabla_{x}g\bigl(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B}+t\boldsymbol{W})\bigr)\right|_{t=0}=\mathcal{J}(x,\Psi(\boldsymbol{B}))[\boldsymbol{P}^{k}\boldsymbol{W}],

and evaluating at
\boldsymbol{B}=\mathbf{0}
yields the claim.
∎

Lemma 4

(Cross-layer cancellation in the global probe)
.

Fix an iteration
k
and a layer
t\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
. Let
E_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{j}\times n_{j}}\to\mathcal{B}
denote the block-insertion operator into block
j
, define

\Delta B_{j}^{k+1}:=\xi_{j}^{k+1}(u_{j}^{k+1})^{\top},\quad\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}:=(\Delta B_{1}^{k+1},\ldots,\Delta B_{L}^{k+1}),

and let
\boldsymbol{M}^{k+1}:=\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}]
. Write the
t
-th output block as

M_{t}^{k+1}=M_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}+M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1},

where
M_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}:=\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{t}(\Delta B_{t}^{k+1})]\bigr)_{t}
and
M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}:=\sum_{j\neq t}\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{j}(\Delta B_{j}^{k+1})]\bigr)_{t}
. Let

\tilde{Z}_{t}^{k}:=(P_{t}^{k})^{\top}Z_{t}^{k},\quad v_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}:=(M_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1})^{\top}\xi_{t}^{k+1},\quad v_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}:=(M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1})^{\top}\xi_{t}^{k+1},

w_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}:=(M_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1})^{\top}(\tilde{Z}_{t}^{k}u_{t}^{k+1}),\quad w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}:=(M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1})^{\top}(\tilde{Z}_{t}^{k}u_{t}^{k+1}),

and define

\mathcal{G}_{k,t}:=\sigma\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1},\boldsymbol{Z}^{k},\{u_{j}^{k+1},\xi_{j}^{k+1}\}_{j\neq t}\right).

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[v_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(32a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi_{t}^{k+1}(w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(32b)

Consequently, the cross-layer contamination carried by the global probe contributes zero conditional mean to both

\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}:=Z_{t}^{k}\bigl(v_{t}^{k+1}(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\bigr),\quad\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}:=P_{t}^{k}\bigl(\xi_{t}^{k+1}(w_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\bigr).

Proof.

By construction,
M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}
depends only on the shared operator realization
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}
and on the probes
\{u_{j}^{k+1},\xi_{j}^{k+1}\}_{j\neq t}
, so it does not depend on
u_{t}^{k+1}
. Hence, conditioning first on
(\mathcal{G}_{k,t},\xi_{t}^{k+1})
and using the zero mean of the Rademacher vector
u_{t}^{k+1}
gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[v_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t},\xi_{t}^{k+1}\right]=v_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t},\xi_{t}^{k+1}\right]=0.

Taking the tower expectation yields (
32a
).

Similarly,
M_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}
and therefore
w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}
depend on
u_{t}^{k+1}
and on the off-layer probes, but not on
\xi_{t}^{k+1}
. Conditioning first on
(\mathcal{G}_{k,t},u_{t}^{k+1})
and using the zero mean of the Rademacher vector
\xi_{t}^{k+1}
gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi_{t}^{k+1}(w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1})^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t},u_{t}^{k+1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi_{t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,t},u_{t}^{k+1}\right](w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1})^{\top}=0.

Taking the tower expectation yields (
32b
). The final claim follows by linearity from the decompositions

v_{t}^{k+1}=v_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}+v_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1},\quad w_{t}^{k+1}=w_{t}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}+w_{t}^{\mathrm{cross},k+1}.

∎

\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_{t,j}^{k+1}(\tilde{Z}_{t}u_{t}^{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}\right]=\tilde{z}_{t,j}

to obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi_{t}^{k+1}w_{t,i}^{\mathrm{self},k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\bigl((P_{t})^{\top}H_{ij}^{tt}P_{t}\bigr)^{\top}\tilde{z}_{t,j}=(\bar{H}_{tt}^{\sharp}[\tilde{Z}_{t}])_{:,i}.

Therefore the entire rank-one matrix satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]=P_{t}\,\bar{H}_{tt}^{\sharp}[\tilde{Z}_{t}].

(37)

Applying Lemma
3
(ii) to the matrices
(H_{ij}^{tt})^{\top}
gives

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=b_{t}H_{tt}[Z_{t}]+(a_{t}-b_{t})H_{tt}^{\sharp}[Z_{t}]+b_{t}Z_{t}T_{t}.

(38)

Step 3: self-layer linear inversion.
Let

X_{t}:=H_{tt}[Z_{t}],\quad X_{t}^{\sharp}:=H_{tt}^{\sharp}[Z_{t}],\quad C_{t}:=Z_{t}T_{t}.

By (
34
), (
36
), and (
38
),

\mathbb{E}[\bar{A}_{t}^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=(a_{t}-b_{t})X_{t}+b_{t}X_{t}^{\sharp}+b_{t}C_{t},

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=b_{t}X_{t}+(a_{t}-b_{t})X_{t}^{\sharp}+b_{t}C_{t},\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=C_{t}.

Therefore the diagonal-block correction used in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H_{tt}[Z_{t}].

(39)

Step 4: pass from
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
to the stochastic operator
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}
.
The actual algorithm uses the shared random operator
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}
. All queried directions are
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}
-measurable:

E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})\in\mathcal{B}\quad\text{is }\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}\text{-measurable},\quad\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}\in\mathcal{B}\quad\text{is }\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}\text{-measurable}.

By Assumption
5
,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[W]\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}]=\bar{\mathcal{H}}[W]\quad\forall\ \mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}\text{-measurable }W\in\mathcal{B}.

Because
E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
-measurable and therefore
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}
-measurable, we have

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})]\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}]=\bar{\mathcal{H}}[E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})].

Multiplying by the deterministic lift
\boldsymbol{P}
and then taking
\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
yields

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{(t),k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\boldsymbol{A}}^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

Likewise, once the probes are fixed, the maps

M_{t}\longmapsto Z_{t}\bigl((M_{t}^{\top}\xi_{t}^{k+1})(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\bigr),\quad M_{t}\longmapsto P_{t}\bigl(\xi_{t}^{k+1}((M_{t}^{\top}(\tilde{Z}_{t}u_{t}^{k+1}))^{\top})\bigr)

are linear in the queried output block
M_{t}
. Since
\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}
-measurable,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{++}]=\bar{\mathcal{H}}[\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}],

and therefore

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\widehat{C}}_{t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}],\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\widehat{B}}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}],

where the barred quantities are computed with the deterministic operator
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
. Consequently the deterministic first-moment identities derived in Steps 1–3 pass unchanged to the stochastic estimator built from
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}
. In particular,

\mathbb{E}[(A_{\ell}^{(t),k+1})_{\ell\neq t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H_{\ell t}[Z_{t}],\quad\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H_{tt}[Z_{t}].

Summing the corrected diagonal and lifted off-diagonal block contributions yields

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H[\boldsymbol{Z}].

(40)

Finally, using Assumption
5
and Lemma
3
(iv),

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}),

so

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H[\boldsymbol{Z}]-f_{Y}.

Part (ii): second moments.

We first prove (
33a
) and (
33b
), and then derive (
33c
).

Step 1: bound for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{G}:=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y})
. Define the block-diagonal lifted projector

(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{U})_{\ell}:=Q_{\ell}U_{\ell},\quad\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathcal{Y}.

Then

\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{G}=\boldsymbol{P}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}\bigr)+(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)\boldsymbol{G}.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
, the first term has mean zero. Using

\|\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{U}\|^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|P_{\ell}U_{\ell}\|^{2}\leq\omega_{P}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|U_{\ell}\|^{2}=\omega_{P}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|^{2},

Assumption
5
gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{P}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}\bigr)\right\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}\right]\leq\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{g,1}^{2}.

Also,

\mathbb{E}[\|(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)\boldsymbol{G}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}[\|(Q_{\ell}-I)G_{\ell}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(\omega_{P}-1)\|\boldsymbol{G}\|^{2}.

Since
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k}))=0
and
\nabla g
is
L_{g,1}
-Lipschitz,

\|\boldsymbol{G}\|\leq L_{g,1}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|.

This proves (
33a
).

Step 2: bound for
\widehat{W}^{k+1}
.
Write

R_{x}:=\tilde{U}_{x}^{k+1},\quad R_{J}:=\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}[\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}].

By the law of total variance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|R_{J}-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|R_{J}-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

For the first term, Assumption
5
gives

\mathbb{E}[\|R_{J}-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}.

Taking expectation over
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
and using
\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}
yields

\mathbb{E}[\|R_{J}-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}.

For the second term,

\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]=\mathcal{J}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y})[\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Z}],\quad\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathcal{J}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y})[\boldsymbol{Z}].

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[R_{J}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{J}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y})[(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)\boldsymbol{Z}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq L_{g,1}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\Lambda_{Y}\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}.

The oracle
\tilde{U}_{x}^{k+1}
is independent of
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{k+1}
given
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
, so

\mathbb{E}[\|R_{x}-\mathbb{E}[R_{x}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{f,1}^{2}.

Combining the three displays yields (
33b
).

Step 3: bound for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1}
.
Define

\Delta_{H}^{k+1}:=\widehat{\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{Z}]}^{k+1}-H[\boldsymbol{Z}],\quad\Delta_{U}^{k+1}:=\boldsymbol{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}-f_{Y}.

Then

\Delta_{S}^{k+1}=\Delta_{H}^{k+1}-\Delta_{U}^{k+1},

so

\|\Delta_{S}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq 2\|\Delta_{H}^{k+1}\|^{2}+2\|\Delta_{U}^{k+1}\|^{2}.

(41)

Step 3a: bound for
\Delta_{U}^{k+1}
.
Exactly as in Step 1,

\Delta_{U}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}f_{Y})+(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)f_{Y}.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
, the first term has mean zero, and therefore

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}f_{Y})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{f,2}^{2}.

Also,

\mathbb{E}[\|(\boldsymbol{Q}-I)f_{Y}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(\omega_{P}-1)\|f_{Y}\|^{2}.

Since

\|f_{Y}\|\leq\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}f(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k}))\|+L_{f,1}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|\leq L_{f,0}+L_{f,1}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|,

we obtain

\|f_{Y}\|^{2}\leq 2L_{f,0}^{2}+2L_{f,1}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta_{U}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{f,2}^{2}+2(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,0}^{2}+2(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,1}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}.

(42)

Step 3b: bound for
\Delta_{H}^{k+1}
.
For each layer
t
, define the centered contribution
\delta_{t}^{k+1}\in\mathcal{Y}
by

(\delta_{t}^{k+1})_{\ell}:=\begin{cases}A_{\ell}^{(t),k+1}-H_{\ell t}[Z_{t}],&\ell\neq t,\\[2.84526pt]
\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}-H_{tt}[Z_{t}],&\ell=t.\end{cases}

Then
\Delta_{H}^{k+1}=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\delta_{t}^{k+1}
. Hence

\|\Delta_{H}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq L\sum_{t=1}^{L}\|\delta_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}.

(43)

For each layer
t
, define the self-layer correction coefficients

c_{A,t}:=\frac{a_{t}-b_{t}}{a_{t}(a_{t}-2b_{t})},\quad c_{B,t}:=\frac{b_{t}}{a_{t}(a_{t}-2b_{t})},\quad c_{C,t}:=\frac{b_{t}}{a_{t}},

and let

\bar{c}_{A}:=\max_{1\leq t\leq L}c_{A,t},\quad\bar{c}_{B}:=\max_{1\leq t\leq L}c_{B,t},\quad\bar{c}_{C}:=\max_{1\leq t\leq L}c_{C,t}.

Because
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=H_{tt}[Z_{t}]
, we may write

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{H}_{tt}[Z_{t}]}^{k+1}\hskip-10.00002pt-H_{tt}[Z_{t}]=

\displaystyle c_{A,t}\bigl(A_{t}^{(t),k+1}-\mathbb{E}[A_{t}^{(t),k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\bigr)-c_{B,t}\bigl(\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\bigr)

\displaystyle-c_{C,t}\bigl(\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\bigr).

Using
\mathbb{E}[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^{2}]
and
\|a+b+c\|^{2}\leq 3(\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}+\|c\|^{2})
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\|\delta_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq(1+3\bar{c}_{A}^{2})\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{A}^{(t),k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]+3\bar{c}_{B}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]+3\bar{c}_{C}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

We now bound the three second moments. First, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
,

\|\bar{\mathcal{H}}[W]\|\leq\omega_{P}L_{g,1}\|W\|,\quad\forall W\in\mathcal{B},

because
\|\boldsymbol{P}\|\leq\sqrt{\omega_{P}}
and
\|\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}\|\leq\sqrt{\omega_{P}}
. Using Assumption
5
and
\|\boldsymbol{P}U\|^{2}\leq\omega_{P}\|U\|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{A}^{(t),k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{P}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{P}\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}[E_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t})]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega_{P}\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr)\|\tilde{Z}_{t}\|^{2}.

Taking expectation over
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
and using
\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{Z}_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\|Z_{t}\|^{2}
yields

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{A}^{(t),k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\Lambda_{A}\|Z_{t}\|^{2},\quad\Lambda_{A}:=2\omega_{P}\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr).

Next define
D_{rn}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}r_{\ell}n_{\ell}
and
\eta_{rn}:=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq L}r_{\ell}n_{\ell}
. Since each probe block is rank one with Rademacher entries,

\|\Delta\boldsymbol{B}^{k+1}\|^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|\xi_{\ell}^{k+1}(u_{\ell}^{k+1})^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}=D_{rn}.

Therefore, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
,

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{M}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\leq 2\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr)D_{rn},

and hence, for each layer
t
,

\mathbb{E}[\|M_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\leq 2\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr)D_{rn}.

For
\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}
, since

\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}=Z_{t}\bigl(v_{t}^{k+1}(u_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\bigr)

and
\|u_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}=n_{t}
, we have

\|\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}=\|Z_{t}v_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|u_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq n_{t}\|Z_{t}\|^{2}\|v_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}.

Moreover,

v_{t}^{k+1}=(M_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\xi_{t}^{k+1},\quad\|v_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq r_{t}\|M_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{C}_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\Lambda_{C}\|Z_{t}\|^{2},\quad\Lambda_{C}:=2\eta_{rn}D_{rn}\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr).

For
\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}
,

\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}=P_{t}\bigl(\xi_{t}^{k+1}(w_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\bigr),

so

\|\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\omega_{P}\|\xi_{t}^{k+1}(w_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}\|^{2}=\omega_{P}r_{t}\|w_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}.

Since

w_{t}^{k+1}=(M_{t}^{k+1})^{\top}(\tilde{Z}_{t}u_{t}^{k+1}),

we get

\|w_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|M_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\tilde{Z}_{t}u_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq n_{t}\|M_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\tilde{Z}_{t}\|^{2}.

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
and using the bound for
\mathbb{E}[\|M_{t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]
gives

\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}]\leq 2\omega_{P}\eta_{rn}D_{rn}\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr)\|\tilde{Z}_{t}\|^{2}.

Taking expectation over
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
and using
\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{Z}_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\|Z_{t}\|^{2}
yields

\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{B}_{\sharp,t}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\Lambda_{B}\|Z_{t}\|^{2},

where
\Lambda_{B}:=2\omega_{P}\eta_{rn}D_{rn}\bigl(\tilde{\sigma}_{g,2}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}L_{g,1}^{2}\bigr)
.

Combining the last three displays with (
43
) shows

\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta_{H}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\Lambda_{H}\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2},

where one may take

\Lambda_{H}:=L\Bigl((1+3\bar{c}_{A}^{2})\Lambda_{A}+3\bar{c}_{B}^{2}\Lambda_{B}+3\bar{c}_{C}^{2}\Lambda_{C}\Bigr).

(44)

Finally, combining (
41
), (
42
), and the bound on
\Delta_{H}^{k+1}
gives

\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta_{S}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq 2\Lambda_{H}\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}+2\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{f,2}^{2}+4(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,0}^{2}+4(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,1}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Therefore (
33c
) holds with

\sigma_{S,0}^{2}:=2\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{f,2}^{2}+4(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,0}^{2},\quad\sigma_{S,2}^{2}:=4(\omega_{P}-1)L_{f,1}^{2},\quad\Lambda_{S}:=2\Lambda_{H}.

∎

Lemma 6

(Tracking bounds for
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}
)
.

Assume
(A1)
–
(A3)
and the sampling conditions of Lemma
5
. Let

\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}:=\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k}),\quad\boldsymbol{Z}_{*}^{k}:=\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k}).

Define
x_{+}^{k}:=x^{k}-h^{k}
so that

x^{k+1}-x^{k}=\alpha_{k}(x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}),

(45)

and couple the stepsizes as

\beta_{k}=c_{1}\alpha_{k},\quad\gamma_{k}=c_{2}\alpha_{k}\quad(\forall k\geq 0).

(46)

Assume further that

\beta_{k}\leq\bar{\beta},

(47)

\gamma_{k}\leq\bar{\gamma},

(48)

for sufficiently small constants
\bar{\beta},\bar{\gamma}
depending only on the constants in Lemmas
2
and
5
. Then there exist finite constants
C_{Yx},C_{Y,0},C_{Y,1},C_{Zx},C_{Z,0},C_{Z,1}
such that for all
K\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{Yx}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+C_{Y,0}+C_{Y,1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2},

(49)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{Zx}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+C_{Z,0}+C_{Z,1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

(50)

Proof.

Define the tracking errors

E_{Y}^{k}:=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k},\quad E_{Z}^{k}:=\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{*}^{k}.

We also introduce the constants

\sigma_{Y}^{2}:=\omega_{P}\tilde{\sigma}_{g,1}^{2},\quad\Lambda_{Y}:=(\omega_{P}-1)L_{g,1}^{2},

B_{ZY}:=L_{f,1}+\frac{L_{g,2}L_{f,0}}{\mu_{g}},\quad\Lambda_{Z}:=2\Lambda_{S},\quad\bar{\sigma}_{S,0}^{2}:=\sigma_{S,0}^{2}+\frac{2\Lambda_{S}L_{f,0}^{2}}{\mu_{g}^{2}},

where
\sigma_{S,0},\sigma_{S,2},\Lambda_{S}
are any valid constants from Lemma
5
. We repeatedly condition on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and work in the product Frobenius space
\mathcal{Y}
.

Part I: tracking of
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}
.

The lower update is
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\beta_{k}\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}
and Lemma
5
gives

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}).

Step 1: drift of the target point
\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}
.
Using
\|U+V\|^{2}\leq(1+c)\|U\|^{2}+(1+1/c)\|V\|^{2}
with

U:=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k},\quad V:=\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k+1},\quad c:=\beta_{k}\mu_{g},

we obtain

\|E_{Y}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq(1+\beta_{k}\mu_{g})\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta_{k}\mu_{g}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}.

(51)

By (
15
) and (
45
),

\|\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|\leq L_{Y^{*}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|=\alpha_{k}L_{Y^{*}}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|.

Substituting into (
51
) yields

\|E_{Y}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq(1+\beta_{k}\mu_{g})\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}+\left(\alpha_{k}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\beta_{k}\mu_{g}}\right)L_{Y^{*}}^{2}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}.

(52)

Step 2: recursion for
\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}
.
Define

\Xi_{V}^{k+1}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1}-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}),\quad\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{V}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0.

Then

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{*}^{k}-\beta_{k}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{Y}}g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k})\|^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\Xi_{V}^{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

(53)

Because
\boldsymbol{Y}\mapsto g(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y})
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex with
L_{g,1}
-Lipschitz gradient on
\mathcal{Y}
, the deterministic gradient step contracts whenever
\beta_{k}\leq\frac{2}{\mu_{g}+L_{g,1}}
:

k\geq 0

L_{\nabla\Phi}
-smoothness of
\Phi
,

\Phi(x^{k+1})\leq\Phi(x^{k})+\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{\nabla\Phi}}{2}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}.

Substituting
x^{k+1}-x^{k}=-\alpha_{k}h^{k}
gives

\Phi(x^{k+1})\leq\Phi(x^{k})-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k}),h^{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{\nabla\Phi}}{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|h^{k}\|^{2}.

Using
2\langle a,b\rangle=\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}-\|a-b\|^{2}
with
a=\nabla\Phi(x^{k})
and
b=h^{k}
, we obtain

\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k}),h^{k}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|h^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Therefore

\Phi(x^{k+1})\leq\Phi(x^{k})-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|h^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}+\frac{L_{\nabla\Phi}}{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|h^{k}\|^{2}.

Rearranging,

\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}(1-L_{\nabla\Phi}\alpha_{k})\|h^{k}\|^{2}\leq\Phi(x^{k})-\Phi(x^{k+1})+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

If
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2L_{\nabla\Phi}}
, then
1-L_{\nabla\Phi}\alpha_{k}\geq 1/2
, and hence

\frac{\alpha_{k}}{4}\|h^{k}\|^{2}\leq\Phi(x^{k})-\Phi(x^{k+1})+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Taking expectations and summing over
k=0,\ldots,K
yield

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|h^{k}\|^{2}\leq\Phi(x^{0})-\Phi^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Multiplying by
4
and using
\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|=\|h^{k}\|
proves (
77
).
∎

C.4
Main convergence theorem

Theorem 2

(Convergence rate of
BROS
)
.

Suppose that
(A1)
–
(A3)
hold and that the sampling conditions in Lemma
5
hold. Assume
\Phi^{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\Phi(x)>-\infty
. Consider the unconstrained
BROS
updates

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{k}-\alpha_{k}h^{k},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\beta_{k}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{k+1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\gamma_{k}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}^{k+1},

\displaystyle h^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta_{k})h^{k}+\theta_{k}\,\widehat{W}^{k+1}.

Define

x_{+}^{k}:=x^{k}-h^{k},\quad V_{k}:=\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

If the stepsizes are constant and coupled as

\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha,\quad\beta_{k}\equiv c_{1}\alpha,\quad\gamma_{k}\equiv c_{2}\alpha,\quad\theta_{k}\equiv c_{3}\alpha,

with
\alpha>0
sufficiently small so that Lemmas
6
,
9
, and
10
apply, define

\rho_{0}:=\frac{2L_{\nabla\Phi}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}+2(C_{xY}C_{Yx}+C_{xZ}C_{Zx}).

(78)

Assume

\rho_{0}\leq 1/8

(79)

and

\alpha\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{c_{3}},\frac{1}{8c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Zx}}\right\}.

(80)

Then there exist finite constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
, independent of
K
, such that

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}V_{k}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{K\alpha}+C_{1}\alpha.

(81)

In particular, if
\alpha=\Theta(K^{-1/2})
, then

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2}).

Moreover, if
R\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
is independent of the algorithmic randomness, then

\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{R})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}V_{k}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2}),

(82)

so to ensure
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x^{R})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon
it suffices to take
K=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
.

Remark 1

(Feasibility of the coupling constants)
.

The condition (
79
) is non-vacuous. Indeed, Lemma
6
gives

C_{Yx}=\frac{8L_{Y^{*}}^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}\mu_{g}^{2}},\quad C_{Zx}=\frac{16L_{Z^{*}}^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}\mu_{g}^{2}}+\frac{2(9B_{ZY}^{2}+\sigma_{S,2}^{2})}{\mu_{g}^{2}}C_{Yx},

so

\rho_{0}=\frac{2L_{\nabla\Phi}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}+2(C_{xY}C_{Yx}+C_{xZ}C_{Zx})

can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
sufficiently large. Hence there always exist finite coupling constants for which (
79
) holds.

Proof.

Let

\Delta_{0}:=\Phi(x^{0})-\Phi^{*},\quad S_{x}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\|x_{+}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2},\quad S_{h}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\|h^{k}-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Step 1: start from the moving-average tracking inequality.

Lemma
9
yields

\displaystyle S_{h}\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{3}}\mathbb{E}\|h^{0}-\nabla\Phi(x^{0})\|^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}

(83)

\displaystyle+\frac{2L_{\nabla\Phi}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}S_{x}+c_{3}c_{wZ}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}+c_{3}\sigma_{w}^{2}K\alpha^{2}.

Step 2: bound the hypergradient bias term via
\boldsymbol{Y}/\boldsymbol{Z}
tracking.

By Lemma
5
and the definition (
71
),

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D_{x}(x^{k},\boldsymbol{Y}^{k},\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}).

Lemma
7
therefore implies

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}\leq C_{xY}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}+C_{xZ}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Summing with weight
\alpha
and using Lemma
6
give

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}^{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\nabla\Phi(x^{k})\|^{2}\leq(C_{xY}C_{Yx}+C_{xZ}C_{Zx})S_{x}+B_{0}+B_{1}K\alpha^{2},

(84)

where

B_{0}:=C_{xY}C_{Y,0}+C_{xZ}C_{Z,0},\quad B_{1}:=C_{xY}C_{Y,1}+C_{xZ}C_{Z,1}.

Step 3: control the
\alpha^{2}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}
term.

Since
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
,

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}=\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}.

Applying the second inequality in Lemma
6
yields

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{k}-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}(x^{k})\|^{2}\leq\alpha\bigl(C_{Zx}S_{x}+C_{Z,0}+C_{Z,1}K\alpha^{2}\bigr).

(85)

Since (
80
) includes
\alpha\leq 1
, the last term on the right satisfies
K\alpha^{3}\leq K\alpha^{2}
.

Step 4: close the recursion using primal descent.

Substituting (
84
) and (
85
) into (
83
) gives

S_{h}\leq A_{h,0}+\bigl(\rho_{0}+c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Zx}\alpha\bigr)S_{x}+A_{h,1}K\alpha^{2}+A_{h,2}\alpha,

(86)

where

A_{h,0}:=\frac{1}{c_{3}}\mathbb{E}\|h^{0}-\nabla\Phi(x^{0})\|^{2}+2B_{0},

A_{h,1}:=2B_{1}+c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Z,1}+c_{3}\sigma_{w}^{2},\quad A_{h,2}:=c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Z,0}.

On the other hand, Lemma
10
gives

S_{x}\leq 4\Delta_{0}+2S_{h}.

(87)

Combining (
86
) and (
87
) yields

S_{h}\leq A_{h,0}+\bigl(\rho_{0}+c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Zx}\alpha\bigr)(4\Delta_{0}+2S_{h})+A_{h,1}K\alpha^{2}+A_{h,2}\alpha.

By (

) and (

),

\rho_{0}+c_{3}c_{wZ}C_{Zx}\alpha\leq\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{8}=\frac{1}{4},

and therefore

S_{h}\leq A_{h,0}+\Delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}S_{h}+A_{h,1}K\alpha^{2}+A_{h,2}\alpha.

Rearranging gives

S_{h}\leq 2A_{h,0}+2\Delta_{0}+2A_{h,1}K\alpha^{2}+2A_{h,2}\alpha.

(88)

Substituting (
88
) into (

) yields

S_{x}\leq 4\Delta_{0}+4A_{h,0}+4\Delta_{0}+4A_{h,1}K\alpha^{2}+4A_{h,2}\alpha.

(89)

Step 5: divide by
K\alpha
.

Dividing (

) by

\frac{S_{h}}{K\alpha}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{h,0}}{K\alpha}+\widetilde{C}_{h,1}\alpha+\frac{\widetilde{C}_{h,2}}{K}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{S_{x}}{K\alpha}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{x,0}}{K\alpha}+\widetilde{C}_{x,1}\alpha+\frac{\widetilde{C}_{x,2}}{K},

for suitable finite constants

independent of

If

which yields (

C.5
A quadratic counterexample for naive projected auxiliary HVPs

The correction in
BROS
is needed because a naive projected auxiliary HVP can change the fixed point of the auxiliary linear system. The following deterministic quadratic example isolates this issue. There is no stochastic objective noise and no lower-level tracking error; the only error comes from replacing the full HVP
Hz
by the uncorrected projected HVP
QHQz
.

Example 1

(Naive projected auxiliary HVP can give a nonstationary limit)
.

Consider a deterministic bilevel problem with scalar upper variable
x\in\mathbb{R}
and one lower-level matrix block
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
, which we identify with a vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

g(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Hy-xc^{\top}y,\quad f(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+d^{\top}y,

where
H=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)
and
c=d=e_{1}=(1,0,0)^{\top}
. The lower problem is strongly convex, and its solution is

y^{*}(x)=H^{-1}cx=xe_{1}.

The auxiliary linear system for the true hypergradient is

Hz^{*}=d,\quad z^{*}=H^{-1}d=e_{1}.

Moreover, since
\nabla_{xy}^{2}g[z]=-c^{\top}z
, the true hypergradient is

\nabla\Phi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,y^{*}(x))-\nabla_{xy}^{2}g(x,y^{*}(x))[z^{*}]=x+c^{\top}z^{*}=x+1.

Thus the unique stationary point of the true bilevel objective is
x^{*}=-1
.
Now sample a scaled subspace lifting matrix

P=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,U_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2},\quad Q=PP^{\top},

where
U_{2}
contains the first two columns of a Haar-uniform orthogonal matrix in
O(3)
. Then
\mathbb{E}[Q]=I
, so the lifted lower gradient remains unbiased. However, the naive projected auxiliary HVP has mean
\bar{H}z:=\mathbb{E}[QHQ]z
. By Lemma
3
(ii), with
m=3
and
r=2
, we have
a=\frac{21}{20}
and
b=\frac{3}{20}
, and therefore

\bar{H}=aH+b\operatorname{tr}(H)I=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{39}{20},3,\frac{81}{20}\right).

Hence the mean field of the naive projected auxiliary recursion is

z^{k+1}=z^{k}-\gamma(\bar{H}z^{k}-d).

For any
0<\gamma<2/\lambda_{\max}(\bar{H})
, this recursion converges to
\bar{z}=\bar{H}^{-1}d=\frac{20}{39}e_{1}
, which differs from the true auxiliary solution
z^{*}=e_{1}
.

Even if the lower variable is kept exactly at
y^{*}(x)
, an upper update using this limiting biased auxiliary variable follows the direction

\bar{h}(x)=x+c^{\top}\bar{z}=x+\frac{20}{39}.

Thus the recursion

x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\eta\bar{h}(x^{k})

converges, for any
0<\eta<2
, to
\bar{x}=-\frac{20}{39}
. However, this point is not stationary for the true bilevel objective, since

\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})=\bar{x}+1=\frac{19}{39}\neq 0.

Consequently, for any
\varepsilon<\left(\frac{19}{39}\right)^{2}
, the naive projected auxiliary update cannot guarantee
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon
even in this noiseless quadratic problem.

This example shows that correcting the projected auxiliary HVP is necessary for targeting the original bilevel stationary point. The issue is not caused by stochastic variance or imperfect lower-level optimization; it is caused by the biased mean of the uncorrected projected auxiliary HVP.

Appendix F
Broader impacts

This work focuses on improving the memory efficiency of stochastic bilevel optimization for neural and language-model training. By reducing the lower-level and auxiliary memory footprint of hypergradient computation,
BROS
may help run bilevel tuning, data-mixture learning, and sample-reweighting experiments on devices with lower memory capacity, thereby reducing resource consumption and carbon emissions associated with large-scale training. These efficiency gains may also make bilevel optimization techniques more accessible to smaller institutions and research teams operating under constrained compute budgets. Aiming to advance the field of bilevel optimization, we do not identify additional societal consequences that require further discussion here.
