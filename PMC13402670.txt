High pressure transcranial focused ultrasound stimulation induces parameter-dependent cell-type specific effects
Authors: Sandhya Ramachandran, Huan Gao, Keunhyung Lee, Pavlo Zolotavin, Katherine Bray, Chih-Yu Yeh, Siyi Wang, Lan Luan, Chong Xie, Kai Yu, Bin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) is a promising technique that has been shown to have high spatial precision, deep brain penetration, and cell-type specificity. Intracranial electrophysiological recordings can measure neural responses to tFUS with high spatial and temporal resolution, but conventional silicon-based multi-electrode arrays cause vibration artifacts induced by increased tFUS pressure. In this study, using an ultraflexible nanoelectric thread electrode, we demonstrate the cell-type selective effects of tFUS under high acoustic pressure with a broad range of ultrasound parameters. We observe that their flexibility mitigates vibrations, eliminating artifacts even at high pressure levels. We observed a positive nonlinear relationship between pressure levels and both time-locked and delayed spiking responses in multiple cell types. We show that higher pressure levels produce distinct response curves across independently varied ultrasound pulse repetition frequency and duty cycle, suggesting the need for further investigation of pressure effects. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Bionanoelectronics

Introduction

Low-intensity transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) is a promising emerging neuromodulation1–6 which makes use of focused ultrasound pressure waves to precisely stimulate brain tissue both in the cortex and in deep brain structures7,8 with high spatial precision2,9. Initial work has shown its potential in treating neurological disorders in animal models10–19. Studies have shown that its effects are controllable through ultrasound parameter selection, tuning between excitatory and inhibitory effects as well as magnitude of stimulation20–23. These effects have also been shown to be cell-type specific22,24 suggesting that tFUS could be used for precise modulation of neural circuits.

Studying the effects of a full range of ultrasound pressure levels has previously been limited to EEG recordings, fMRI, calcium imaging, and behavioral studies, due to artifacts induced by ultrasound in typical electrophysiological electrodes recording neuronal activities25–29. Experiments recording high frequency electrophysiological signals, such as multi-unit activity (MUA), show artifacts at sufficiently high ultrasound pressure levels, likely caused by vibration of rigid silicon recording electrodes. Although neuromodulatory effects may still be observed below the vibration threshold, this limitation has significantly constrained comprehensive investigation of tFUS across the full parameter space used in in vivo animal and human studies. The majority of ultrasound experiments use fundamental frequencies ranging from 0.1–1 MHz and pressure levels ranging from 0–1 MPa, staying largely within previously determined safety ranges for humans1. Studies of neural spiking, however, have been limited to fundamental frequencies from 0.5–1.5 MHz and pressure levels from 0–114 kPa approximately, following the threshold for electrode vibration determined ex vivo25. When using rigid silicon electrodes, their higher acoustic impedance and lower compliance relevant to the surrounding brain tissue can influence mechanical coupling30. This leads to greater forces on the electrode than on the surrounding tissue, causing relative displacement and inducing artifacts in intracranial electrophysiological recordings. To overcome this limitation, we hypothesized that soft, flexible electrodes—composed of more compliant materials with lower inertial and acoustic impedance mismatch—would reduce recording artifacts. Flexible mesh devices have been shown to minimize vibration in the context of closed-loop neurostimulation31, supporting this hypothesis. Accordingly, we used flexible nanoelectric thread (NET) electrodes32 which may enable recording of high frequency neural activity during high pressure stimulation.

This limitation has also restricted investigation of the mechanisms underlying tFUS neuromodulation. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including intramembrane cavitation, dynamic acoustic radiation force (ARF), other cavitation-related effects, activation of mechanosensitive ion channels, and thermal effects2,33–38. Cell-type-specific responses are hypothesized to arise from differential effects of these mechanisms on ion channel populations. In our previous studies, we observed that in the sensory cortex, only regular spiking units (RSUs) exhibit time-locked responses sensitive to pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and duty cycle (DC), whereas both RSU and fast spiking units (FSUs) show delayed responses that are less sensitive to these parameters22,39,40. RSU and FSU are putative excitatory and inhibitory neurons, respectively, with different ion channel compositions41–44, The sensitivity to PRF and DC suggests that spatial-peak time-average intensity (ISPTA), which depends on these parameters and influences the ARF magnitude, may play a key role22. However, ARF also depends on pressure amplitude, and cavitation thresholds are governed by peak negative pressure1. To further elucidate cell-type specific responses in tFUS, it is therefore necessary to examine the effects of higher pressures on these neuronal subtypes.

In this study, we investigate the neuromodulatory effect of tFUS across a range of acoustic pressures (100–1300 kPa) using newly developed NET electrodes. At both high and low pressure levels, we systematically vary DCs and PRFs while holding the other parameter constant. We demonstrate that NET electrodes enable reliable recording of neural spiking activity at higher pressure levels. Notably, the relationship between neural response and DC at high pressure levels differs from that observed at lower pressures. Furthermore, even when isolated from DC and overall dose, PRF remains a significant determinant of cell-type-specific responses, underscoring the importance of stimulation frequency. These results enable exploration of the full tFUS parameter space—including PRF, DC and acoustic pressure—and extend our previous findings on cell-type specificity across this broader range.

Results

Vibrational artifact in rigid vs. flexible electrodes

We began by testing the artifact induced in rigid and flexible electrodes. Flexible NET electrodes (Fig. 1A–E) were implanted in the rat somatosensory cortex, and ultrasound was delivered as illustrated in Fig. 1F. At an estimated pressure level of 1400 kPa, recordings of multi-unit activity (MUA) exhibited severe artifacts, preventing reliable waveforms extraction (Fig. 1G, H), in contrast to recordings obtained at lower pressure levels (Fig. 1I, J). The artifact magnitude was observed to increase linearly with the pressure delivered, determined by the input voltage used to drive the transducer (Fig. 1N). Using the same input voltage to stimulate one of our chronically implanted flexible electrode subjects, resulting in an approximate pressure level of 1300 kPa (due to attenuation from the silicone layer covering the intact skull as shown in Fig. 1F), we observed complete elimination of the artifact from the MUA (Fig. 1K), and restoration of the ability to extract clear neural waveforms (Fig. 1L). Using power spectrum analysis, we demonstrated that the frequency peak due to vibrational artifact was eliminated in flexible electrodes (Fig. 1M and Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). We additionally tested the signal quality of the flexible electrodes over implantation time up to 6 weeks post-surgery, and observed that although decreases are observed each week, signal remains clearly separable from noise until the end (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Computer simulation and evaluation of electrode displacement

We performed computer simulations in Sim4Life V9.2.1 on a realistic model, including the rat skull model, surgical burr hole, 3 mm long electrode shank, and the digital model of the ultrasound transducer array used (H276), and using material properties for silicon and polyimide (Fig. 2A). Displacement was evaluated along a line referenced to the electrode tip (0 mm). Within the region from –2 to +4 mm relative to the tip, peak displacement increased monotonically with pressure for both materials, but was consistently greater for the flexible polyimide electrode than that of the silicon electrode (Fig. 2B–H). Specifically, peak displacements for the flexible electrode were 0.66, 2.70, 4.74, 6.73, and 8.72 nm at 100, 400, 700, 1000, and 1300 kPa, respectively, compared with 0.59, 2.39, 4.19, 5.95, and 7.71 nm for the silicon electrode (Fig. 2I). The similar trend was observed for average displacement along the tip; for example, over the 0 to 3 mm region relative to the tip, the flexible electrode exhibited average displacements of 0.47, 1.92, 3.36, 4.77, and 6.18 nm, whereas the silicon electrode showed average displacements of 0.36, 1.48, 2.59, 3.69, and 4.78 nm (Fig. 2J-L). These realistic simulations therefore indicate that the flexible electrode demonstrates greater local motion than the silicon electrode under the same ultrasound exposure. Since polyimide shows greater movement, we expect it to move more in alignment and conformation with the tissue31,45–49. This is further evidenced by the relative displacement between the electrode and the surrounding brain tissue, 0.3 mm away from the electrode, where the silicon electrode exhibited a maximum of 4.43 nm more relative movement than the flexible electrode (Fig. 2M). This increased relative motion for silicon signifies a greater mechanical mismatch between the electrode and tissue during ultrasound exposure, whereas the flexible electrode’s lower relative displacement confirms its superior ability to move in synchrony with the tissue. This supports our hypothesis that polyimide’s ability to deform in alignment with surrounding tissue within the focused ultrasound field reduces vibrational artifacts when using the flexible electrodes.

Neuronal responses to pressure increases

We then tested the effect of increasing the delivered pressure level following the protocol shown in Fig. 3A, using two PRF/DC conditions used previously10,39,40, 1500 Hz PRF with a 30% DC (Fig. 4A, B, C, and D), and 30 Hz PRF with a 0.6% DC (Fig. 4E, F, G, and H). We observed time-locked and delayed responses from RSU (Fig. 4A, B, E, F), and only delayed responses from FSU (Fig. 4C, D, G, H), keeping to previously observed patterns of cell type specificity. We found that pressure was a significant factor for RSU time-locked responses (p < 0.0001). Using post hoc tests, we found that using 1500 Hz PRF, nearly every pressure level induced a significant difference from all others including sham (p < 0.005) (Fig. 4A, B, and D). Mean time-locked responses from RSU appear to increase in a nonlinear manner with increased pressure (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In the 30 Hz PRF condition however, the effect size was much smaller. Only 1000 kPa and 1300 kPa induced significant time-locked responses (Fig. 4E, F), and only 700, 1000, and 1300 kPa for delayed responses (Fig. 4H). The PRF/DC condition was found to be a significant factor in RSU time-locked responses (p < 0.05) using two-way ANOVA (Fig. 4A, E). Interestingly, PRF/DC condition was not found to be a significant factor in the delayed responses (Fig. 4B, F). 1500 Hz PRF caused larger time-locked responses, while 30 Hz PRF caused larger delayed responses, but the mean delayed responses were similar between them. The FSU delayed response also showed significant responses to pressure (p < 0.0001) (Figs. 4D, 3H). As expected, post-hoc tests showed that higher pressure levels resulted in significantly higher responses for FSU (p < 0.005).

Computer simulation of ARF

We evaluated acoustic radiation force (ARF) using the same Sim4Life V9.2.1 simulation framework and material definitions for silicon and polyimide described previously. Peak ARF was quantified as the maximum value along a 3 mm longitudinal length of the electrode extending from the tip. For the flexible polyimide electrode, peak ARF increased with pressure from 2.66 × 10–3, 4.39 × 10–2, 1.35 × 10–1, 2.73 × 10–1, to 4.58 × 10–1 uN/mm3 at 100, 400, 700, 1000, and 1300 kPa, respectively (Fig. 2N). For the silicon electrode, corresponding peak ARF values were markedly smaller: 2.98 × 10–7, 4.93 × 10–6, 1.52 × 10–5, 3.06 × 10–5, and 5.15 × 10–5 uN/mm3 (Fig. 2O). In both materials, ARF increased monotonically with pressure. These results support the conclusion that ARF scales with applied ultrasound pressure and differs substantially between the two material models. The larger ARF predicted in the polyimide model is likely driven primarily by its acoustic properties in the simulation, particularly its higher attenuation coefficient and lower sound speed relative to silicon, both of which would be expected to enhance local ultrasound momentum transfer50. The greater displacement observed in the polyimide model would therefore reflect the combined effects of this larger applied force and its higher mechanical compliance. In contrast, material mismatch in rigid silicon electrodes can introduce substantial distortions, potentially leading to inaccurate measurements of neural activity. Flexible electrodes, which more closely match the mechanical properties of brain tissue, better preserve the local ARF distribution. Accordingly, the use of materials that closely match brain tissue properties is critical for high-fidelity characterization of ultrasound-induced neuromodulation. Furthermore, although these simulations only characterize the local mechanical field and do not directly measure neuronal activation by ARF, strong correlation was observed between the simulated peak ARF and the experimental RSU spike rates described (r = 0.9991 in 1500 Hz, r = 0.9878 in 30 Hz) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Neuronal responses to varying duty cycle with constant pulse repetition frequency

We then varied DC while holding PRF constant at 1500 Hz at two different pressure levels, 1300 kPa and 100 kPa following the stimulation paradigm shown in Fig. 3B. At 1300 kPa, we found that DC was a significant factor for RSU responses, and for delayed responses for FSU (p < 0.0001). Post hoc tests found that 30% and 60% all resulted in significantly higher responses (p < 0.005), with all ultrasound conditions being found to be significantly higher than sham (p < 0.005) (Fig. 5A, B, D). The highest mean response came from 60% for both RSU (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and FSU. At 100 kPa, responses were much lower. DC was still found to be a significant factor for delayed responses (p < 0.05). Only 6% and 30% were significantly greater than sham in time locked responses and FSU delayed responses, although most conditions showed significant RSU delayed responses (p < 0.005) (Fig. 5E, F, H). Pressure was found to be a significant factor (p < 0.05) when comparing the 1300 and 100 kPa groups. At 100 kPa, 30% had the highest mean response (Fig. 5E), whereas at 1300 kPa, 60% showed the highest mean response, but it should be noted that differences between these parameters were not statistically significant, potentially due to the low number of animals used in each condition (Fig. 5A). FSU were not found to have any significant time-locked responses (Fig. 5C, G).

Neural responses to varying pulse repetition frequency with constant duty cycle

Finally, we varied PRF while holding DC constant at 30% using 1300 kPa and 100 kPa following the stimulation paradigm shown in Fig. 3C. PRF was a significant factor influencing both RSU and FSU responses for both pressure conditions. At the 1300 kPa condition, 1500, 3000, and 4500 Hz resulted in the highest responses (p < 0.005) (Fig. 6A, B, D). RSU showed a preference for 1500 or 3000 Hz (p < 0.005) (Supplementary Fig. 3C). In the 100 kPa condition pressure level was also a significant factor (p < 0.05). Only 1500 Hz was significantly higher than sham (p < 0.005) however for RSU time-locked responses (Fig. 6E). Delayed responses in both RSU and FSU showed significant delayed responses to 1500, 3000 and 4500 Hz PRF (Fig. 6F, H). In all conditions FSUs had no time-locked response (Fig. 6C, G).

Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence that flexible electrodes such as nanoelectronic threads (NETs), mitigate the vibrational artifacts observed with rigid electrodes. This capability enables investigation across a broad range of PRF and DC parameters at varying pressure levels (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 1), expanding characterization of the tFUS parameter space and its interaction with cell-type-specific responses. Building on this approach, future experiments at higher pressure levels will allow more comprehensive exploration of the tFUS parameter space and may help identify optimal therapeutic settings.

Using flexible NET electrodes, we examined neural responses across a range of pressure levels under two commonly used parameter sets. The first (1500 Hz PRF, 30% DC) has been shown to induce time-locked responses in RSUs at low pressures (90–115 kPa)39,40. We found that time-locked spiking responses increased nonlinearly with pressure, consistent with the hypothesis that delivered ISPTA and thus ARF contributes to ultrasound neuromodulation. ISPTA scales with the squared of pressure and depends on PRF and DC (or tone burst duration), which together determine delivered energy22. ARF arises from transfer of momentum from acoustic wave to the medium and is governed by local temporal-average intensity and tissue attenuation, with peak values typically occurring near the spatial intensity maximum. Our simulations and electrophysiological recordings are consistent with an ARF-mediated contribution, although they do not isolate ARF as the primary mechanism. We next evaluated a second parameter set (30 Hz PRF, 0.6% DC), within a range used for pain modulation10. Under these conditions, RSUs exhibited substantially weaker time-locked responses relative to delayed responses, in contrast to the first parameter set, which produced comparable or stronger time-locked activity. Notably, mean delayed RSU responses did not differ significantly between the two conditions. While both PRF and DC influence delayed responses, these results suggest that pressure is a more dominant factor than variation between these parameter sets. The mechanisms underlying time-locked versus delayed responses remain unclear. Prior work has suggested that delayed responses may reflect cortico-thalamo-cortical (CTC) pathway engagement39, whereas time-locked responses arise from more direct neuronal activation. This indirect network involvement may explain the greater sensitivity of delayed responses to pressure, although contributions from slower direct activation of FSUs cannot be excluded. Overall, the limited differences between parameter sets for delayed responses suggest that mechanisms beyond ARF contribute to this component of the response.

Given the strong responses observed at 1300 kPa, we varied DC while holding PRF constant at 1500 Hz at both 1300 kPa and 100 kPa, the latter allowing comparison with prior studies using rigid electrodes39,40. At 100 kPa, varying DC produced a similar curved response profile to that previously reported, with a peak around 30% DC, although this trend was less statistically robust here due to the smaller sample size39. In contrast, at 1300 kPa, responses generally increased with DC rather than following a curved profile. Increasing the sample size in future work will help to better delineate this pressure-dependent shift. Notably, we also observed significant responses at 0.6% DC at high pressure, in contrast to lower-pressure conditions. Similarly, when varying PRFs while holding DC constant at 30%, the 30 Hz PRF, minimally effective at low pressure, elicited significant responses at high pressure. These findings demonstrate that parameter regimes ineffective at low pressures may become effective at higher pressure levels.

By varying PRF while holding DC constant, we demonstrate that PRF independently exerts a significant effect on tFUS responses. Whether these effects are determined solely by total delivered dose or also by pulsing frequency has been widely debated26,29,51,52. Our results indicate that stimulation frequency itself influences neural responses. Responses generally increased with PRF, although decreases in RSU activity were observed beyond 1500 Hz. These nonlinearities at higher PRF and DC values may reflect saturation effects, rather than direct dependence on these parameters. Neuronal responses are inherently nonlinear and limited by firing capability, such that responses may plateau or even decrease at high simulation levels. Notably, 1500 Hz PRF at 30% DC appears to be an effective parameter set for inducing time-locked responses even at elevated pressures. However, further investigation is needed to resolve differences between 30% and 60% DC, as well as among 1500 Hz, 3000 Hz, and 4500 Hz PRF, particularly given the limited sample size.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (n = 10 animals per condition), constrained by the availability of NET electrode prototypes. tFUS responses exhibit substantial variability across subjects, within subjects across days, and even across trials, which may limit statistical power to resolve differences between closely spaced parameter values. Future studies with larger cohorts will be important to better delineate these effects. Another limitation is the exclusive focus on the somatosensory cortex. Neural responses to tFUS are likely influenced by factors such as neuronal orientation relative to the ultrasound field, depth, and cell-type composition, suggesting that optimal stimulation parameters may vary across brain regions. The somatosensory cortex was selected due to its well-characterized organization, superficial location, and diversity of neuronal subtypes, and because is has been extensively studied in prior tFUS work with intracranial electrophysiological recordings, enabling direct comparison with existing literature. However, other brain regions, with distinct circuit architectures, may exhibit different response profiles, particularly for delayed effects that may reflect network-level dynamics39,53. In addition, targeting deeper brain structures presents specific challenges for tFUS. Achieving equivalent pressure levels at depth may require higher input voltages, and the ultrasound wavefront can be altered by refraction through the skull and intervening tissue54. Future studies spanning multiple brain regions and depths will be necessary to fully characterize the spatially dependent effects of tFUS.

Another limitation is the restricted parameter space explored with the NET electrode. While this study elevates their performance across selected parameter sets informed by prior work and therapeutic relevance, it does not encompass the full range of parameters investigated in animal studies. Our results indicate that pressure significantly influences PRF and DC response curves, highlighting complex interactions among these parameters and the need for more comprehensive exploration of the parameter space. In particular, further investigation of higher DCs and pressures is needed to assess their effects on electrode-tissue interactions and potential cumulative impacts over extended stimulation durations. Rigid electrodes under ultrasound stimulation have been shown to induce elevated strain in surrounding tissue due to micromotions at the interface, leading to scarring and reduced signal quality over time55. This effect is attributed to mechanical mismatch between the electrode and tissue, suggesting that it may be mitigated by more compliant, flexible probes. However, potential tissue damage must be directly evaluated in future studies across a range of stimulation parameters to confirm this advantage. Cumulative effects also warrant further investigation. In this study, we minimized potential entrainment by introducing 10% jitter in trigger timing and a 2 s interval between stimulations across 505 trials. Raster plots (e.g., Fig. 7C) did not reveal systematic trends in neural responses over time, suggesting minimal cumulative effects under these conditions. Nevertheless, this should be evaluated more extensively across neuron types and parameter regimes, particularly under continuous ultrasound paradigms.

Additionally, the angle between the ultrasound wave and the recording probe must be considered. In this study, the probe was positioned at approximately 30° relative to the ultrasound beam to target the desired brain region while accommodating the transducer. Artifact mitigation was observed at this angle; however, other experimental configurations may require different orientations. A perpendicular orientation would be expected to induce maximum artifacts. Further studies are needed to determine whether flexible electrodes can effectively mitigate vibrational artifacts at angles approaching perpendicular.

One potential confound is indirect auditory activation arising from skull vibrations induced by the ultrasound wave, which could produce neural responses not directly attributable to tFUS. To address this, we conducted sham conditions in which tFUS was delivered off-target to the frontal skull, preserving skull vibrations while avoiding direct stimulation of the recorded brain region. As no significant responses were observed under these conditions, indirect auditory activation is unlikely to account for our findings. We also report the mechanical index (MI) for each pressure level tested (Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating that cavitation-related effects remain below FDA safety limits.

Another potential confound in ultrasound studies is temperature increase. Thermal changes can affect neuronal activity in a cell-type-dependent manner, although reductions in spiking activity are most commonly reported56,57. To address this effect, we measured temperature changes using a fiber-optic probe (PRB-242-2M-STM-MRI, Osensa Innovations, BC, Canada) implanted in the brain. In an anesthetized rat with no prior tFUS exposure, the probe was inserted into S1 at the approximately 1 mm depth, matching electrode implantation depth, using a stereotaxic setup at a 30° angle through a burr hole. Silicone was applied over the skull to replicate the interface present in animals with chronically implanted flexible electrode. The H276 transducer was positioned overhead and aligned to target the probe using the same coordinates validated in electrophysiological recordings.

We monitored temperature during tFUS delivery across a range of stimulation parameters in a cohort of four rats (Supplementary Fig. 5). PRF had a negligible effect on thermal elevation, with cumulative temperature increases remaining below 0.5 °C across all tested PRFs (Supplementary Fig. 5A). In contrast, both DC and acoustic pressure contributed to cumulative heating in a dose-dependent manner. Increasing DC produced a moderate temperature rise of up to 0.53 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5B), whereas increasing acoustic pressure resulted in elevations of up to 2.1 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Despite these increases at higher pressures, temperatures remained below established thresholds for histological damage, suggesting that the applied stimulation parameters are unlikely to induce lasting tissue injury58,59.

Neuronal responses did not show notable changes over the course of individual recordings, as illustrated by raster plots (e.g., Fig. 7C). Recordings were also separated by inter-trial intervals and conducted in randomized order, reducing the likelihood of cumulative thermal effects. While no clear temperature-dependent changes in neuronal activity were observed, subtle thermal contributions cannot be entirely excluded. For example, at 60% DC, responses at 1300 kPa were significantly stronger than at 6% DC, a pattern not observed at 100 kPa. Although this difference likely reflects pressure-dependent effects, a contribution from temperature remains possible. Overall, we observed no clear evidence of temperature-driven changes in neuronal firing beyond baseline variability; however, minor thermal effects cannot be fully excluded1,56.

We also considered the potential activation of thermosensitive ion channels. Prior studies indicate that activation of such channels, including TRPV1, typically requires rapid heating to approximately 43 °C60. In contrast, our protocol increased brain temperature to only 36.6 °C from 34.9 °C over 17 min, making substantial activation of these channels unlikely (Supplementary Fig. 5D). As shown in the pressure-temperature profile (Supplementary Fig. 5C), thermal responses exhibited non-linear behavior in the 400–700 kPa range, reflecting the interplay between ultrasonic heating and physiological cooling. Temperature remained relatively stable up to 400 kPa, consistent with the brain’s inherent heat dissipation capacity61,62, whereas the sharp increase at 700 kPa suggests that heat deposition exceeded this compensatory threshold. At higher pressures (1000–1300 kPa), the reduced rate of temperature increase may reflect enhanced convective cooling driven by a larger blood-brain temperature gradient61,62. These findings underscore the necessity for careful selection of acoustic pressure, as threshold effects may exist where thermal accumulation accelerates and could confound neuromodulatory outcomes. Further studies will be necessary to more fully characterize thermal contributions, particularly at higher acoustic pressures and duty cycles.

Conclusion

In this study, we have characterized neuronal responses to transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) across a range of ultrasound parameters including pulse repetition frequency (PRF), duty-cycle (DC) and acoustic pressure, enabled by flexible NET electrodes that mitigate vibration-induced recording artifacts. Across pressure levels, responses increased nonlinearly, consistent with a contribution from acoustic radiation force (ARF) mechanisms. Varying DC revealed that response profiles shift at higher pressures, indicating that optimal parameter regimes are pressure-dependent. Likewise, by varying PRF while holding DC constant, we show that PRF independently influences neural responses. Both DC and PRF experiments demonstrated substantially enhanced responses at higher pressures, with parameter regimes ineffective at low pressures becoming effective at elevated pressures. These findings underscore the importance of exploring higher pressure ranges and highlight the critical role of flexible electrodes in enabling reliable intracranial electrophysiological investigations of tFUS.

Materials and methods

Ten adult male Wistar rats weighing ~300 g were used in this study, following a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Carnegie Mellon University. Rats were anesthetized using isoflurane, a craniotomy was performed over primary somatosensory cortex (S1) of the rat brain (ML: –2.5 mm, AP: –2 mm, depth 1 mm), and the electrode (Fig. 1A-F) was inserted. Saline was dripped over the body of the electrode to allow the adhesive between the rigid shuttle and the electrode to dissolve before the shuttle was quickly pulled out, leaving the electrode behind.

The remaining exposed electrode segment was covered with artificial Dura-Gel (Cambridge NeuroTech, Cambridge, U.K.). Body Double™ casting silicone (Smooth-On, Inc., Macungie, PA, USA) was then used to cover the front of the burr hole and exposed skull and dental cement was used to secure the electrode in place. Animals were allowed to recover for a week before recordings. During recordings, each animal was once again put under anesthesia using 2% isoflurane through inhalation for the duration of recordings.

Ultraflexible nanoelectronic thread electrodes

Flexible NET electrodes (Fig. 1A-E) were fabricated on polyimide substrates with a thickness of 2 µm and average width of 50 µm using photolithography by the Xie Lab at Rice University63. Details are presented in the supplementary materials. SIROF-coated electrodes of 11 µm diameter were arranged in 8 groups of 4 electrodes that were linearly spaced along the shank of the probe spanning 1 mm length. The implantable length of the shank was 3 mm. The probe was attached to a tungsten implantation shuttle with polyethylene glycol. The shuttle was micromachined using femtosecond laser to have a 15–18 µm diameter needle that was manually inserted in the loop at the tip of the probe32.

Ultrasound setup and experimental design

Ultrasound was delivered using a customized H276 128-element random array ultrasound transducer (f0: 1.5 MHz, -3dB focal size: 1.36 mm axially, 0.46 mm laterally) (manufactured by Sonic Concepts, Inc., Bothell, WA, USA), and driven with a Vantage 128 research ultrasound platform (Verasonics, Kirkland, WA, USA) using a DL-260 connector.

Different pressure levels were delivered by altering the input voltage, resulting in a predicted delivered ultrasound peak-to-peak pressure of 100, 400, 700, 1000, and 1300 kPa. First, ultrasound was delivered at each of these pressures in a series of 505 trials while maintaining a PRF of 1500 Hz, a 30% DC using a TBD of 200 µs, and a total ultrasound duration (UD) of 67 ms (Fig. 3A). Second, ultrasound was delivered at each of these pressure levels with a PRF of 30 Hz and a 0.6% DC, using a TBD of 300 µs, and a UD of 67 ms (Fig. 3A).

We then held peak-to-peak pressure constant at the 1300 kPa level, and at the 100 kPa level and tested the effects of varying DC while holding everything else constant. Series of 505 trials were delivered while the TBD was varied between 4, 40, 200, and 400 µs (Fig. 3B). This resulted in DCs of 0.6%, 6%, 30%, and 60%. Finally, we tested the effect of holding DC constant while varying PRF at the 1300 kPa and 100 kPa pressure level. PRF was varied between 30, 300, 1500, 3000, and 4500 Hz, while setting the TBD to 10000, 1000, 200, 100, and 67.7 µs, maintaining a 30% DC while the PRF was changed (Fig. 3C). Each of these series of 5 recordings were performed on the same day, in a random order. Additionally, a sham condition was tested with ultrasound delivered to the front of the skull, off target from the recording electrode.

Electrophysiology

The TDT Neurophysiology Workstation (Tucker-David Technologies, Alachua, FL, USA) was used to coordinate stimulation triggers, take recordings, and perform initial bandpass filtering and noise notch filtering. Neural trace data was recorded at a 24 kHz sampling frequency, and bandpass filtered between 300 Hz and 5 kHz. Noise was removed using a notch filter at 60 Hz, 120 Hz, and 180 Hz. Recordings were then loaded into the PCA-based spike classification software Offline Sorter (Plexon, Dallas, TX, USA), where spikes were detected and clustering was performed using the software’s Expectation-Maximization algorithm.

Waveforms and spike times were then loaded into MATLAB, where neurons were sorted into FSU and RSU using custom code (Fig. 7A, B). Features were extracted from the average waveforms by detecting the baseline-crossings of the waveforms, allowing the duration of the initial phase (from onset to first crossing of baseline) and of the after-hyperpolarization phase (from first baseline crossing to second) to be measured and used for clustering.

Raster plots and peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) were created for each neuron in a recording using a combination of custom code and the FieldTrip toolbox64 in MATLAB, as shown in Fig. 7C, D. Both were plotted from 0.5 seconds prior to ultrasound stimulation until 1.5 seconds afterwords. Responses from each single unit were calculated from this histogram by measuring the percent of baseline level that the normalized spike rate reached in a specified time span. Baseline was determined using the average firing rate in the 0.5 seconds prior to tFUS. Responses were measured in both a ‘time-locked’ time span, which was measured from 0–67 ms post tFUS onset, and a ‘delayed’ time span, measured from 201–268 ms, in alignment with previous studies39.

Statistical analysis

Group analysis was performed of the single units from all subjects and recordings within an experiment, aiming to determine whether pressure magnitude, PRF, and DC are significant factors, and to examine significant differences between responses to different parameters. Each group was tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and found to not have a normal distribution as expected. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for group analysis of significance to take into account the lack of normality, and post hoc Wilcoxon tests were used to characterize differences. The Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was used to determine the proper p-value to characterize significance. The total number of single units across all channels, recordings, and animals in each group was the N used in each test. In temperature measurement experiments, significance was calculated as an one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

Computer simulation of displacement and ARF

Additional pressure, displacement, and ARF simulations were performed in Sim4Life V9.2.1 (Zürich MedTech AG, Zürich, Switzerland) using the built-in generic material definitions for silicon and polyimide. In these models, silicon was assigned a mass density of 2330 kg/m3, speed of sound of 8400 m/s, attenuation coefficient of 0.5 dB/m, nonlinearity parameter of 6, and characteristic acoustic impedance of 19.57 MRayl. Polyimide was assigned a mass density of 1420 kg/m3, speed of sound of 2500 m/s, attenuation coefficient of 150 dB/m, nonlinearity parameter of 6, and characteristic acoustic impedance of 3.55 MRayl.
