 ABSTRACT

The Cell G neutron assay system within Bldg. 7930 provides an important function in the production of
252Cf in the quantitative determination of the amount of 252Cf present in a sample. The assay system

consists of a 238U fission chamber coupled to the necessary counting electronics including preamplifier,
amplifier, discriminator, and counter/timer. The original electronics date back to the 1970s but were
replaced in 2011, with little documentation detailing the testing and replacement efforts. This work
focused on procuring a new spare set of electronics, their subsequent testing as a backup in case any of
the currently in use electronics were to fail, and documentation of the existing and new setups.

 1. BACKGROUND

The Cell G counting system uses a Westinghouse Model WL-23076 fission chamber housed within a
shielding plug that resides within a shielding box in Cell G. The shielding box is filled with water and
surrounds the fission chamber setup, shielding plug, and cylindrical source holder tubes that extend from
the surface near the shielding plug for source measurement. The shielding plug is installed within a
penetration through the Cell G wall and houses the neutron detector and preamplifier. The plug is
cylindrical and constructed of steel, concrete, and an Al2O3 epoxy resin with external O-ring seals on one
end to seal it in the penetration through the cell well. The shield plug contains mainly polyethylene, with
channels for cabling and the preamplifier, and provides neutron shielding. The preamplifier is wrapped in
cadmium foil to provide additional neutron shielding to the sensitive electronic components. Samples can
be lowered into the source tubes using source holders for measurement with the fission chamber system.
These samples can include the californium wires shipped to customers, material in process, liquid
samples, and even empty polyethylene bottles. A drawing of the shielding box with cylindrical source
tubes and fission chamber housed within the shielding plug are shown in Figure 1.

Very little existing documentation describes the Cell G electronic neutron assay system, and reports
indicate that the original counting system dates back to the 1970s or 1980s. Around the 2009–2010
timeframe, the assay system encountered issues with erratic counting and unreliable assay measurements.
In an effort to prevent any electrical noise in the system, the fission chamber was removed from the hot
cell, and the connectors were cleaned. The coaxial cable used for signal transmittance was replaced with a
double-shielded coaxial cable to minimize noise pickup with the system. After these changes, newer
electronics and spare fission chambers were purchased and partially implemented in the system. The
documentation describes the installation and testing of the new electronics; however, the system currently
in use is a combination of the old and new electronics. The documentation does not clarify when or why
the decision to use a combination of old and new electronics was made.

1.1 CELL G FISSION CHAMBERS

The system still uses a Westinghouse Model WL-23076 fission chamber, which uses 2.14 g of 238U with
an argon fill gas and is the original fission chamber used with the system. The neutron-sensitive region of
the device is approximately 6 in. long and has a neutron sensitivity of 1 × 10−3 cps/nv fast neutrons [1].
The chamber is roughly 9.75 in. long, with a 2 in. diameter and a cadmium inner cylinder to shield the
fission chamber from thermal neutrons. This fission chamber is operated with an applied bias voltage of

 2

+400 V and operates in the pulse counting mode. In the 2010–2011 timeframe, two new fission chambers
were procured to replace and serve as backups for the existing Westinghouse detector. These fission
chambers are GE-Router-Stokes Model RS-P6-1608-127, which have 1.25 g of 238U per detector. The
detectors have a total length of 10.25 in., with a 2 in. diameter and a neutron-sensitive length of 7.12 in.
The detectors were tested for their operation with the existing counting system, and new electronics were
purchased to ensure functionality and measure the neutron sensitivity of each detector. The measured
neutron sensitivity for each of the two purchased detectors is 6.92 × 10−4 and 7.27 × 10−4 cps/nv for fast
neutrons, respectively. These detectors are currently housed within a cabinet inside Bldg. 7930, and the
original Westinghouse detector is still in use due to its higher neutron sensitivity.

1.2 ORIGINAL CELL G ELECTRONICS

The Cell G assay system requires electronics connected to the fission chamber for the measurement of
neutron flux to determine 252Cf mass. The original electronics include a Tennelec TC-952 high-voltage
(HV) power supply, a Tennelec TC-246 amplifier/single-channel analyzer, ORTEC 776 counter/timer,
ORTEC 122 preamplifier, and a nuclear instrument modules (NIM) bin power supply. The power supply
was connected to an AC power filter-regulator to clean the AC power coming into the system to minimize
noise from the building power. The fission chamber was connected to the preamplifier using a double-
shielded, 60 in. long RG-58 cable. The cable had an HN connector on one side for mating to the detector
and an SHV connector on the other for connection to the preamplifier. The signal cable from the
preamplifier to the amplifier is also an RG-58 cable with a length of 11 ft and BNC plugs on both ends.
An SHV cable with appropriate connectors is used from the TC-952 HW power supply to the
preamplifier. The cables running to the preamplifier have a braided shield and are enclosed in a PVC pipe
for extra shielding. The described signal processing for this system includes a 200 mV signal with 50 mV
noise and a 1 µs rise time from the amplifier feeds into the discriminator. The discriminator produces a
1 µs wide, 5 V amplitude TTL pulse for each signal. These pulses are sent to the ORTEC 776
counter/timer where they can be counted. Figure 2 shows a pictorial diagram of the original assay system
with connections.

1.3 UPDATED CELL G ELECTRONICS

Following the erratic behavior in the 2009–2010 timeframe, newer electronics were purchased to replace
the older electronics. The new electronics consisted of a new ORTEC 4002D NIM bin, ORTEC 994 dual
counter/timer, ORTEC 935 constant-fraction discriminator, ORTEC 556 HV power supply, RIS
preamplifier, and new cabling (Figure 3). These electronics were chosen to replace the older electronics
because Tennelec, the vendor for many of the older electronics, went out of business, so the original parts
were no longer easily procurable. These electronics were tested with both of the new GE/Reuter-Stokes
fission chambers and the older Westinghouse fission chamber to ensure similar operation in 2011. The
extensive testing followed the NNFD-7930-OP-114 procedure and included multiple backgrounds and
measurement of standard and liquid sources available at the time. The test logs are available within the
report, Building 7930 Cell G U-238 Fission Chamber Test & General Info Manual.

  Figure 3. Pictorial diagram of updated assay system electronics with RIS preamplifier labeled as “Cf-252
 PREAMP” and detector with connections shown.

The RIS preamplifier (model number Q-6456-1 High-Gain PREAMP) was designed by Oak Ridge
National Laboratory and was manufactured by RIS Corp in Knoxville. It was designed to create a pulse
rise time of a few nanoseconds and total pulse width of 40–50 ns while being small enough to fit within
the Cell G radiation plug. This design minimized the cabling distance between the detector and worked
well during testing outside the hot cell. Once installed within the Cell G port, the new electronics and RIS
preamplifier experience interference noise from the Cell G manipulator motors and relays. To resolve this
issue, an ORTEC 570 amplifier was added to the electronics to act as a high-frequency filter for the RIS
preamplifier. The amplifier with a coarse gain of 20 should provide a 0.5 µs, 700 mV negative pulse to be
fed into the ORTEC 935 discriminator. The discriminator then outputs a negative pulse of 700 mV with a
50 ns width that feeds into the ORTEC 994 counter/timer for counting. Figure 4 shows a diagram of the
updated system.

 4

  Figure 4. Pictorial diagram of updated assay system electronics with RIS preamplifier labeled as “Cf-252
 PREAMP” with ORTEC 570 amplifier and detector with connections shown.

As shown in Figure 5, the RIS preamplifier was replaced with an ORTEC 142B preamplifier because it is
commercially available and does not require modification to fit within the shielding plug like the RIS
preamplifier. The ORTEC preamplifier has two outputs, timing and energy; the preferred output for this
application is timing. The timing output has a pulse width of 2 µs compared to the 500 µs tail pulse of the
energy output. Longer pulse width causes a slower counting speed and can lead to pulse pileup and
missed counts. For testing with this preamplifier, the gain on the ORTEC was set to 1,000 coarse gain and
0.5 fine gain. After all new electronics were tested, the Tennelec 246 amplifier remains in use today
instead of the ORTEC 570 and 935. The reasons for this choice are not documented.

 2. SPARE ELECTRONICS PROCUREMENT AND TESTING

Based on the information contained in Building 7930 Cell G U-238 Fission Chamber Test & General Info
Manual, the following electronics were procured as spare equipment for the Cell G neutron assay system:

 ORTEC 556 HV power supply
 ORTEC 570 amplifier
 ORTEC 142B preamplifier
 ORTEC 994 counter/timer
 ORTEC 935 discriminator
 ORTEC 4001C NIM bin module
 ORTEC 4002 NIM bin power supply

The existing Cell G neutronics-electronics include Tennelec 246, ORTEC 994, ORTEC 556, ORTEC
4001C, and ORTEC 4002D and are shown in Figure 6. The testing followed the steps outlined in NNFD-
7930-OP-114 Californium Assay System Cell G Workstation #4, and counts were recorded on the
Appendix B sheet “Cf Neutron Counter Data Sheet.”

 Figure 6. Existing electronics setup for Cell G neutron assay system.

The entire system is shown in Figure 7 and was placed on top of the existing setup to easily disconnect
and reconnect the necessary cabling. The new setup looks identical to the existing setup, except the
ORTEC 570 and 935 replace the Tennelec 246. Some additional cable connections were needed because
the input to the ORTEC 570 is on the front of the module rather than the rear like the Tennelec 246. The
shaping time constant can be adjusted on the ORTEC 570 and should be set to 0.5 µs to account for the
higher count rates necessary of counting samples. The ORTEC 946 needs the unused outputs to be
terminated with 50 Ω and the delay signal connected with a 50 Ω cable.

The next round of testing involved implementing the combination of the ORTEC 570 and 935 for the
Tennelec 246. This replacement involved more substantial testing to set the appropriate gain on the
ORTEC 570 and threshold level on the ORTEC 994 to match the output of the Tennelec 246. The polarity
of the ORTEC 994 had to be switched because the Tennelec 246 outputs positive pulses, but the ORTEC
570 and 935 output negative pulses. Figure 8 shows the differences in signals between the old and new
electronics. The signals coming from the preamplifier are pulses observed with a height of 100–200 mV
and an exponentially decaying tail of approximately 120 µs. For the old electronics, the Tennelec 246
converts the signal into a roughly Gaussian positive pulse with an observed height of 1–5 V and a width
of 4–6 µs. The Tennelec 246 also acts as a signal channel analyzer, which then outputs a positive square
wave pulse with a 2 V amplitude and 500 ns width. This square wave pulse is fed into the ORTEC 994
for counting. For the new electronics, the ORTEC 570 output a negative Gaussian pulse with an
amplitude of 5–7 V and width of 3 µs. This signal is fed into the ORTEC 935, which outputs a negative
square wave pulse with amplitude observed in the range of 0.8–1 V and a width of 50–100 ns. This
negative square wave required switching the polarity of the ORTEC 994 to count negative incoming
pulses.

 3. CONCLUSIONS

The Cell G neutron assay consists of the detector and the corresponding electronics; both are necessary
for the routine operation of californium sample measurement. This system has been in operation since the
1970s and has encountered some challenges over the years, necessitating the procurement of spare fission
chambers and updating of the electronics. The fission chambers purchased in the 2010–2011 timeframe
are kept as backups and have only been tested for operation because they are less sensitive than the in-
operation fission chamber. The new electronic components purchased in 2011 were partially
implemented, and no documentation was found that details the rationale regarding the current electronic
setup—a combination of new and old electronics. For this reason, a complete new set of electronics,
including the NIM bin and power supply that houses the electronic modules, was purchased. The new
electronics were tested as individual components, replacing parts of the existing setup and as a stand-
alone complete alternate set that is available for use. This testing assures that, if a single part of the
existing electronic setup were to fail, a spare is available for rapid implementation to prevent system
downtime. It also ensures that if the old unit cannot quickly be brought online with a single module
replacement, the entire new system can be operational for use. The new electronic system will be housed
near Cell G in cabinets within Bldg. 7930. The new backup system should be tested on an annual or
biannual basis to ensure it remains operational. This approach is a recommendation instead of a
requirement, as the existing system has operated for decades and may continue to operate for many years
without issue.
