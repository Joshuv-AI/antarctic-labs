Realist evaluation of the community paramedicine at clinic (CP@clinic) programme in rural Australian community health: protocol
Authors: Ruth Hardman, Laura Hemming, Evelien Spelten, Louise Reynolds, Renee Sharples, Gina Agarwal, Leigh Kinsman, Fan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe healthcare challenges associated with multimorbidity, population ageing and socioeconomic disadvantage are well known. This requires primary care services to adapt to greater population complexity and embrace new models of care. One example is the community paramedic (CP) role, which can provide screening, education and care coordination for complex and underserved populations. While relatively new in Australia, CPs have the potential to bridge primary care gaps especially in rural settings with workforce shortages. In this study, we are implementing an established and evidence-based Canadian CP programme (CP@clinic) in a new context: rural Australian community health services. The aim of our realist evaluation is to explore and explain how, why, for whom and in what circumstances CP@clinic works to improve health and social outcomes; benefit the paramedic workforce; bridge the gaps in primary healthcare and reduce emergency healthcare utilisation.Methods and analysisRealist methodology is a theory-driven approach that explores how the causal mechanisms of an intervention are shaped by different contexts, leading to a variety of outcomes. We have developed an initial programme theory based on the Canadian experience and an earlier Australian pilot of CP@clinic. We will collect qualitative and quantitative data, including individual health and social outcomes, emergency services usage, healthcare costs, case reports, interviews with participants, paramedics and external stakeholders and process data. Analysis will follow a realist paradigm, using retroduction to compare findings to the original programme theory enabling us to confirm, refute and interrogate the proposed mechanisms.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been granted (McMaster University HiREB15194; La Trobe University HEC24495, 22038 and 22295). We have developed a multilevel dissemination plan to inform government, the paramedic profession, project stakeholders and consumers. The work is funded by the Australian government with annual reports provided. Other dissemination products will include journal articles, conference presentations, government presentations and submissions, an implementation guidebook and national webinar.

Introduction

The health impacts of population ageing, multimorbidity and socioeconomic vulnerability are well-known globally.1 This has placed traditional primary care, which centres around the family doctor or general practitioner (GP), under increasing pressure.2–4 In Australia, GPs are the backbone of the primary care workforce, with over 22 million Australians attending a GP in 2024 and accounting for 84% of primary care attendances.5

However, this primary care workforce is heavily concentrated in capital cities, with widespread workforce shortages experienced by the approximately 7 million Australians (27% of the population)6 living in regional rural and remote (afterwards rural) settings. Medicare utilisation (a known proxy for GP visits) is up to 50% less in rural settings, translating to a $A1000 per annum lower health spend for every rural resident.7 This is despite rural populations being older and experiencing higher levels of chronic disease, socioeconomic disadvantage and lifestyle risks.8 The uneven distribution of GPs in favour of metropolitan regions directly contributes to the poorer health outcomes and reduced life expectancy seen in rural populations.6 9

Although our research focus is on rural Australia, socioeconomically vulnerable populations in urban settings face similar challenges.3 10 Primary care providers serving these communities struggle to manage high levels of patient complexity, due to the interacting demands of chronic disease, mental health and social stressors. Undertaking the traditional GP role of care coordination has become increasingly difficult to achieve4 11 12 and cannot be solved by funding extended GP consult times since there are simply not enough GPs available. In Australia, accessing a GP can also be unaffordable due to patient co-payments or ‘gap’ fees leading to a rise in the number of people not engaging with primary care at all13 and instead using more expensive (but free at the point of contact) emergency care.

Ultimately, poorly managed chronic health conditions are a key driver of potentially avoidable deaths, unplanned hospital admissions and healthcare costs. This is reflected in findings that indicate lower numbers of GPs per 1000 population are associated with increased rates of non-urgent emergency department (ED) presentations as well as higher mortality rates.14 Rural patients are twice as likely to present to EDs for non-urgent conditions, with rates up to five times higher for those living in the most disadvantaged communities.15

One potential solution to these challenges could be through using the already local skilled allied healthcare workforce in innovative ways, informed by approaches developed in other countries with similar workforce constraints.16 17 Chronic disease management includes many tasks (eg, screening, education and care navigation) that are not discipline-specific, providing an opportunity to support and enhance—not replace—the role of the GP. While health workforce shortages, especially of GPs, are widespread in rural Australia,5 6 the paramedic profession remains underused, with limited employment opportunities outside of the jurisdictional ambulance service.18 Employing paramedics in the community could provide an opportunity to bridge the rural primary care gap with a skilled and currently underemployed health workforce.19

Community paramedics work in non-emergency settings, such as homes and community centres, and provide health screening and advice, treatment for minor conditions, onward referrals to health or social supports and care coordination.20–23 A recent systematic review of community paramedicine (CP) found that programmes had positive health impacts, high patient satisfaction and could reduce the stress on other parts of the healthcare system.24 In Australia, the potential of CP was recently highlighted in the Cormack Scope of Practice Review,25 which identified its value in areas where there are thin and failing markets, such as rural communities.

To explore the potential of this model of care, in 2022–2023 we undertook a pilot trial of community paramedicine in partnership with a regional Victoria community health service.26 We implemented the established and evidence-based Canadian CP at Clinic (CP@clinic) programme20 27 in collaboration with McMaster University Canada. The programme offers chronic disease screening, management, education and onward referral for older, low resourced adults. It has been shown to improve health outcomes and social isolation and reduce emergency healthcare utilisation and transfer to long-term care while being cost-effective.28–31 While CP@clinic has been implemented and upscaled across Canada, including indigenous communities,32 our pilot trial was the first implementation in Australia and the first outside of a jurisdictional ambulance service. Following the successful pilot, we obtained a federal government Innovative Models of Care grant33 to upscale and evaluate the programme to additional locations across Victoria. Four rural community health services are currently implementing the intervention in partnership with La Trobe University and Mc Master University.

The CP@clinic programme has already been successfully evaluated and upscaled in Canada.27 However, our implementation has key contextual differences, including operating in another country, outside of the jurisdictional ambulance service and primarily in rural communities. For this reason, we will be undertaking a realist evaluation (RE).34 Our study aims to develop a programme theory that explains how, why, for whom and in what circumstances CP@clinic in Australia can:

Methods and analysis

To answer the above questions, we will be using an RE framework. This is a theory-driven approach, based on an underlying realist philosophy.34 RE is appropriate in complex interventions where the specific context is important, since it seeks to find out what interventions work, for whom, in what circumstances and how.34 RE seeks to uncover the causal processes behind the effectiveness or otherwise of a programme, recognising that the same programme may produce somewhat different results in different contexts. The aim is to identify how and why an intervention performs differently in diverse settings. This can provide guidance how adaptation can provide useful in the upscaling and expansion of an intervention.34–36

This approach is particularly appropriate for the CP@clinic programme since the efficacy of this programme has already been established through a cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) in a different setting. In Canada, the CP@clinic programme led to a reduction in ambulance call-outs27 37; improved health status27 37 and primary care linkages29 while being cost-effective.28 However, there are several contextual differences. In Canada, the RCT was conducted by ambulance service employed paramedics in urban and semi-urban social housing sites. In contrast, in Australia, we are implementing it in a different healthcare system, in a more rural setting, and with paramedics employed outside of the ambulance jurisdiction. While there has been increasing use of paramedics outside the ambulance jurisdiction (eg, worksite roles) using paramedics in Australian primary care is a new and complex undertaking. Additionally, while the intervention remains consistent, we are evaluating it not just in another country and outside of the ambulance jurisdiction, but across a range of different settings, from chronically ill older adults living in supported accommodation to younger people living in caravan parks and temporary accommodation. This diversity of contexts argues strongly for the need to evaluate from a realist perspective.36

RE uses three linked concepts: Context, Mechanism and Outcome (together known as CMO) to explain an intervention.34 38 The context describes key features (environmental, population type, resources/limitations) that will influence the way the programme is implemented. Mechanisms are the underlying causal processes that will determine how and for whom the intervention works, and outcomes are the result, which may be positive or negative.39 CMOs are hypothesised, interrogated and refined throughout the evaluation process. The CMO configuration can provide important information about how and why an intervention works, which increases the likelihood of successful implementation and translation.

Stage 1: development of initial programme theory

Our initial programme theory has been informed by both the Canadian evidence and national scale-up31 and our pilot study (August 2022 to June 2023) undertaken in rural Victoria. For the single-site pilot study, we undertook an evaluation26 using the Proctor implementation framework,43 assessing acceptability, feasibility, facilitators and barriers. Data sources included quantitative data (demographics, health and social status of participants); interviews with participants, paramedics and external stakeholders; and implementation process data (eg, meeting minutes).

Following the pilot, the programme was scaled out to four sites starting in January 2024, with each site operating clinics at 5–10 locations. An initial (unpublished) evaluation of the first two sites using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework was undertaken in early 2025. At this stage it was evident that there was a need to capture the varying implementation contexts in greater detail, as well as to document a broader range of outcomes, including those related to the Australian health system. We noted that many of our findings were consistent with the Canadian experience, and that there were also some key differences that required further exploration. In particular, our early evaluation identified implementation challenges related to systemic barriers within the paramedic profession and Australian primary care environment which required further investigation.

In response to this observation, we pivoted to an RE framework. The research team embarked on a series of workshops and iterative discussions, resulting in the development of an initial programme theory for the Australian context incorporating CMOs across the three key domains of CP@clinic consumers, the paramedic profession and the broader health system. This process was also supported and informed by an expert advisory group (consisting of key stakeholders in rural health and paramedicine), a consumer panel and individual consumer feedback.

Stage 2: testing of Australian context programme theory

The programme theory will be tested across four implementation sites for the duration of the study.

Setting: The programme operates in four regions covering north and west rural Victoria. Paramedics are employed by Community Health Centres, which provide state-funded primary care to people at risk of poor health and operate under a social model of health. They frequently provide, or are connected to, a wide range of services including general practice, allied health, mental health, sexual health, drug, alcohol and other social support services. Each Community Health Service runs 5–10 CP clinics which operate out of community hubs such as libraries, community centres, church halls or common rooms in social housing complexes. Clinic locations are determined by assessment of local needs undertaken by the implementing health service. This varies across communities but includes specific groups such as older and/or disabled people living alone; women escaping violence; culturally and linguistically diverse groups; and people experiencing food or housing insecurity.

Intervention: CP@clinic sessions typically run for 3 hours and offer a free drop-in service for any individuals seeking health assessment or advice. Paramedics perform health risk assessments (eg, blood pressure, diabetes, falls risk) guided by the CP@clinic database which provides decision support. The assessment process generates a summary of risk factors, which are discussed with the consumer and shared with their GP. Paramedic follow-up includes the provision of onward referrals, self-management education and system navigation. Many clinics are co-located with additional services such as food assistance, community meals and walking or exercise groups. Trial fidelity is supported by regular site visits conducted by the researchers and formal supervision of the paramedics provided by one researcher (LR) who has both clinical and academic expertise in paramedicine.

Participants: Any person seeking healthcare advice or assistance is welcome to attend a CP@clinic, which is advertised by flyers and word of mouth. Some consumers attend regularly for ongoing monitoring of their health conditions, with others dropping in for one-off interactions.

Patient and Public Involvement: A quarterly consumer advisory group (consisting of participants of the clinics and members from the Rural Health Consumer Panel, not necessarily involved with the programme) provides guidance for both the implementation process and research direction. Additionally, a governance group (consisting of senior health service managers); and an expert advisory group (invited participants with expertise in paramedicine and rural health) meet with the research team quarterly.

Quantitative Data Analysis

All participants registered in the participating community health service and recorded as participating in the CP@clinic programme will be included for data analysis, with a predicted number of participants exceeding 2000. An adaptive and iterative analytical strategy will be employed, informed by data characteristics, exploratory analysis for hypothesis generation and emerging findings throughout the analytical process. The analysis will be conducted in sequential stages, progressing from descriptive analysis to more sophisticated analytical approaches.

The analytical framework will encompass several key domains. For the health risk and outcome analysis, we will focus on examining patterns and trajectories. For the health services analysis, we will explore healthcare utilisation patterns, trajectories and their underlying determinants. We will undertake an interrupted time-series analysis to examine the association between the intervention and health outcomes, as well as health resource utilisation, commencing 2 years prior to intervention and continuing at 6-monthly intervals for the duration of the intervention. Subgroup analyses will be systematically considered to investigate whether findings vary across different population segments, age, sex or clinical characteristics. Health economic analysis will also be conducted by incorporating economic modelling and cost-effectiveness assessments.

Qualitative data analysis

Interview and focus group data will be transcribed verbatim, and all data sources will be analysed in NVivo. This triangulation of data will enhance rigour and ensure credibility by allowing convergence of findings across multiple sources, providing a more comprehensive and trustworthy understanding of the phenomena under study. All analysis will occur using a realist framework. We will employ retroduction to compare findings to the initial programme theory and over the course of the data collection will modify and refine both the data collection tools and the programme theory to confirm, refute and interrogate the proposed mechanisms.46

Data will be analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.50 A combined deductive and inductive approach will be used such that data will first be coded according to the relevant CMOs for that participant group. Beyond this, the data will also be coded inductively to search for alternative understandings, experiences and perceptions outside those captured in the CMOs. Line-by-line coding will be conducted at the semantic level. Codes will be developed into candidate themes through an ongoing process of reviewing, refining and considering patterns of shared meaning. Finally, themes will be defined, named, and presented in a final narrative that addresses the research aims.

Throughout the analysis, reflexive practice will be maintained by acknowledging the researcher’s interpretive role and the influence of theoretical and personal positioning on analytical decisions.

The analysis will be led by LH who has experience/background in clinical psychology and mental health and approaches the research from a pragmatic perspective. This positioning may shape interpretation through anticipated assumptions, sensitivities, areas of expertise, values and prior experiences relevant to the topic.

Additional analysis and interpretation will involve RH, ES and LR whose academic and clinical backgrounds in paramedicine, chronic disease management and health systems reform may provide alternative perspectives and complementary expertise.

Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, researcher subjectivity will be viewed as an analytical resource rather than a source of bias to be removed. The research team will engage in ongoing reflexive practice to critically consider how their experiences, assumptions and disciplinary perspectives shape meaning-making throughout analysis.

Stage 3: refinement of initial programme theory and data synthesis

We will synthesise data from the above sources to confirm, refine or modify the proposed mechanisms. Subgroup analyses will be undertaken to clarify differences in outcomes, where indicated. The final theory will provide the basis for an overall evaluation report and a series of dissemination outputs directed towards researchers, implementers and policy makers.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

All CP@clinic participants must provide informed consent. They are informed on admission into the programme that their de-identified data will be provided to McMaster University for research purposes and are required to sign a consent form to acknowledge their understanding. A waiver of consent to access deidentified quantitative data has been obtained through La Trobe University (HEC 22038). Individual consumer records in the McMaster database are identified only by a UR (unit record) number which can be linked to the community health service electronic medical record (EMR), which is not accessible to the research team. For data linkage, individual health services are required to upload information from their EMR directly into the Centre for Victorian Data Linkage to ensure confidentiality.

Patients, paramedics and external stakeholders who participate in interviews, focus groups or the CoP are all required to complete written informed consent procedures. Signed consent forms will be collected, scanned and stored on the La Trobe Research Database Store (RDS) account. Original hardcopy forms will be destroyed once digitalised. Process data, including emails, CoP and meeting minutes will be stored on the La Trobe RDS account. The focus groups, interviews and CoP meetings will be audio recorded via audio-recording device (not a smartphone). All interview and focus group recordings will be transcribed with any identifying information removed and stored in the La Trobe RDS account. Audio files will be deleted once transcription is completed.

Ethical approval has been obtained for all aspects of this project through McMaster University (HiREB15194) and La Trobe University: data linkage (HEC24495); consumer qualitative and quantitative data (HEC22038) and paramedic and community of practice data (HEC22295).

Dissemination plan

Dissemination will take place at multiple levels: government, project stakeholders (project partners, advisory group) and consumers. There will be wider dissemination to a range of audiences, both academic and non-academic.

This work is funded by an Australian government grant with annual reports provided to government. In addition, interim reports will be generated annually, reporting on data from individual services, including interviews with paramedics, consumers and stakeholders and individual health and social status information from the McMaster database. The data gathered and analysed in each report will provide an opportunity to validate and further refine the programme theory. A summarised version of the report will be available to all stakeholders (consumer, paramedics, advisory group, health services) and the general public. The report will be published on the website for this project51 and will be registered with a DOI.

The final realist evaluation will be submitted in June 2028, along with an implementation guidebook, endorsed by the advisory group. A workshop will be conducted with the research team and key stakeholders to discuss findings.

Wider dissemination will happen through the publication of manuscripts in academic journals and conference presentations. In addition, towards the end of the project, a nationwide webinar will be run to inform about the outcomes of the project. To support the continuation of this new service, next to the implementation guidebook, we will develop information materials for communities and interested parties who would like to engage with the service.

Discussion

The results of the process analysis will be used to inform the expansion, adaptation and integration of the CP@clinic programme into the Australian primary care environment. A key aspect will be the creation of a broad framework with applicability across all rural settings but retaining capacity to adapt the model to ensure it is contextually relevant to a chosen case site. The model will include a gap analysis to identify additional knowledge and skills needed to work as a CP in rural Australia. Key stakeholders (community health services, consumer groups and healthcare providers) throughout the study will be encouraged to review the draft model and the proposed education and training requirements. Recommendations arising from this consultation will be incorporated into the final model.
