Variance Reduction for Non-Log-Concave Sampling with Applications to Inverse Problems
M. Berk Sahin, Ahmet Ege Tanriverdi, Behzad Sharif, Abolfazl Hashemi

Abstract
Sampling from high-dimensional, non-log-concave distributions with unnormalized densities is a fundamental challenge in machine learning, particularly when the exact gradient of the potential is unavailable and must be approximated via stochastic gradients that exhibit high variance under a fixed budget of gradient computations per iteration. Although variance reduction techniques such as SGD with momentum, STORM, and PAGE have demonstrated improved convergence properties in non-convex optimization, their implications for sampling from non-log-concave distributions remain largely unexplored. In this work, we develop the first unified analysis of these estimators for sampling from non-log-concave distributions. We establish improved non-asymptotic convergence rates in ε \varepsilon -relative Fisher information and, under a Poincaré inequality assumption, in squared total variation distance, and further prove weak convergence to the target distribution. We extend our analysis to solving inverse problems with score-based generative priors. We empirically validate our theory and demonstrate that, under a fixed gradient computations per iteration, variance-reduction techniques consistently improve sample quality in two standard imaging applications.

Introduction

One of the central themes in machine learning is the use of stochasticity to design procedures that are both computationally efficient and statistically consistent. This idea is shared by both Bayesian and frequentist perspectives. From a Bayesian perspective, posterior distributions provide a principled characterization of uncertainty in estimates, and stochasticity is used to represent those distributions through Monte Carlo (MC) samples. On the frequentist side, stochasticity is used to develop computationally efficient gradient-based optimization methods for obtaining point estimates by replacing intractable or expensive exact gradients with unbiased stochastic estimates. Although they are two different frameworks, there are overlapping methodological challenges. In particular, MC sampling must drive an out-of-equilibrium state towards the steady-state posterior distribution, while achieving fast convergence; consequently, optimization ideas are relevant. On the other hand, frequentist inference often depends on sampling and resampling procedures, which makes MC methods relevant.

Variance reduction has emerged as one of the key tools to address the challenges shared by the two frameworks. In the context of stochastic optimization, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) replaces exact gradients with unbiased stochastic estimates to reduce the computational burden of large-scale learning problems. However, this computational advantage comes at the cost of additional variance, which can slow convergence and lead to a suboptimal stationary point. This raises the question of whether there is a principled way to manage this trade-off. This question has been answered positively in a seminal line of work on stochastic optimization

In this work, we develop a unified framework that adapts variance reduction techniques from non-convex optimization to sampling from non-log-concave distributions under relaxed assumptions. In particular, we propose two estimators that provide unified treatments of SGD with momentum and its probabilistic counterpart, and of STORM

Preliminaries

2.1
Sampling Analogue of Stationary Point Analysis

While optimization and sampling are distinct frameworks, their theoretical analysis share deep structural connections. In particular, tools from non-convex optimization theory can be leveraged to characterize the behavior of sampling algorithms
[
3
]
. Consider the minimization of
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence over the space of probability distributions equipped with the optimal transport geometry:

\argmin_{\mu}\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi),

where
\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mu({\bm{x}})\log\frac{\mu({\bm{x}})}{\pi({\bm{x}})}\,d{\bm{x}}
. Here,
\pi
denotes the target distribution, and
\mu
denotes the sampling distribution induced by the generated samples. In this setting, Langevin diffusion can be interpreted as the
exact
gradient flow of the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence
[
25
]
. For example, the Wasserstein gradient of
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\pi)
at
\mu
is
\nabla\log(\mu/\pi)
, and its expected squared norm is known as the
relative Fisher information (FI)

\mathrm{FI}(\mu\|\pi)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mu({\bm{x}})\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu({\bm{x}})}{\pi({\bm{x}})}\right\|^{2}_{2}\,d{\bm{x}}.

If
\mu_{t}
denotes the law of the Langevin diffusion (
1
) at time
t
, then the calculation rules for gradient flows imply that
\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)=-\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

[
59
,
2
,
49
]
. From a non-convex optimization point of view,
\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)
is analogous to the
\ell
-2 norm of the gradient in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

[
3
]
. Using this analogy, we analyze the convergence of
\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)
under a
linear interpolation
of the distributions obtained by the LMC sampling algorithms. Different than non-convex optimization, if
\mu
and
\pi
have positive and smooth densities,
\mathrm{FI}(\mu\|\pi)=0
implies
\mu=\pi
, which we use to establish weak convergence guarantees. We refer to
3
for further discussion on this topic.

2.2
Langevin Diffusion

Langevin diffusion is defined as the solution to the stochastic differential equation

d{\bm{x}}_{t}=-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}d{\bm{B}}_{t},

(1)

and admits
\pi\propto e^{-f}
as its unique stationary distribution. Under mild conditions, the process converges to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
. Here,
({\bm{B}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Discretizing this process with step size
\gamma\!>\!0
yields the standard Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) sampling algorithm

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\bm{Z}}_{k},

(2)

where
{\bm{Z}}_{k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. In many practical settings, the exact gradient of the potential
\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
is unavailable. Thus, it is replaced by its stochastic estimate
\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k})
, where
\xi_{k}
denotes a random variable capturing the external randomness at iteration
k
and is independent of all other sources of randomness. However, as shown in prior work
[
3
]
, these iterates fail to achieve arbitrarily small
\varepsilon
-relative FI. A standard remedy is to replace the single-sample gradient with a mini-batch estimator, leading to the SGLD iterates

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\frac{\gamma}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i})+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\bm{Z}}_{k},

(3)

where
b
denotes the batch size and
(\xi_{k}^{i})_{i=1}^{b}
are independent and identically distributed. While this reduces the estimator variance,
\varepsilon
-relative FI is guaranteed by
b\!=\!\mathcal{O}(Ld\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)
gradient computations per iteration (see Theorem
B.1
), leading to substantial per-iteration cost in high-dimensional and high-variance settings. Consequently, SGLD requires very large batch sizes, resulting in both high computational cost and increased memory requirements. In contrast, the proposed ML-VRLD and SL-VRLD estimators achieve
\varepsilon
-relative FI error with
\mathcal{O}(1)
gradient computations per iteration.

2.3
Posterior Sampling with SGM Prior

For the posterior sampling part of our work, we consider a general inverse problem setting modeled as

{\bm{y}}={\bm{A}}({\bm{x}})+{\bm{n}},

(4)

where the objective is to recover
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
from measurements
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. The measurement operator
{\bm{A}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
characterizes the imaging system’s response, and
{\bm{n}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
denotes the measurement noise, generally modeled as additive Gaussian noise (AWGN). The mapping
{\bm{x}}\!\rightarrow\!{\bm{y}}
is not one-to-one, making the inverse problem ill-posed since
{\bm{x}}
cannot be uniquely recovered from
{\bm{y}}
. In Bayesian framework, uncertainty about
{\bm{x}}
is modeled through a
prior

p({\bm{x}})\propto e^{-h({\bm{x}})}
, and inference is performed by sampling from the
posterior

\pi({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})\propto p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}})
obtained via Bayes’ rule, where
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\propto e^{-f({\bm{x}})}
indicates the
likelihood
. Here,
f
and
h
denote the potential functions associated with the likelihood and prior, respectively, and are central to our analysis. One standard approach is to sample from the posterior to solve the inverse problem. Using Bayes’ rule and the SGLD update rule in (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\bm{Z}}_{k}.

(5)

Since
\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
is generally intractable for complex, high-dimensional priors, score-based generative models (SGMs), parameterized by a neural network, have been proposed to provide computable approximations of this quantity. This approach is commonly referred to as
score matching

[
23
,
60
]
. One elegant approximation replaces the intractable score
-\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
by smoothed score
-\nabla h_{\sigma}({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
, which admits a closed form expression by Tweedie’s formula
[
20
]
. In particular, let
{\bm{z}}\!\sim\!p({\bm{z}})
be a random sample from the prior,
{\bm{e}}\!\sim\!{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
the AWGN, and
{\bm{x}}={\bm{z}}+{\bm{e}}
the noisy image. Then,
\nabla h_{\sigma}({\bm{x}})\!=\!({\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}}])/\sigma^{2}
, where
p_{\sigma}({\bm{x}})\!\propto\!e^{-h_{\sigma}({\bm{x}})}
is the distribution of noisy image with potential function
h_{\sigma}
, and
p_{\sigma}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p({\bm{z}}){\mathcal{N}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}},\sigma^{2}I)\,d{\bm{z}}
. SGMs approximate the smoothed score by minimizing the denoising score matching (DSM) loss derived from Tweedie’s formula
[
60
]
.

\mathrm{DSM}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}}{\sigma^{2}}-{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},\sigma)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right],

(6)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},\sigma)
denotes SGM parametrized by
\theta
. Assuming enough data and model capacity, minimization of DSM loss satisfies
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}},\sigma)=-\nabla h_{\sigma}({\bm{x}})
almost everywhere
[
51
]
. Thus, for sufficiently small
\sigma>0
, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\sigma)\approx-\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
.

Standard LMC algorithms exhibit slow convergence and mode collapse when targeting high-dimensional, multimodal distributions. To mitigate these issues, we consider a more general LMC formulation incorporating
weighted annealing

[
55
]
. Specifically, we define a sequence of intermediate target distributions

\pi_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})\propto p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\,p_{\sigma_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}({\bm{x}}),

where
(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{N-1}
and
(\sigma_{k})_{k=0}^{N-1}
denote non-increasing annealing and smoothing schedules, respectively. This formulation corresponds to sampling from a sequence of weighted posterior distributions. Early in the sampling process, the smoothed prior
p_{\sigma_{k}}
dominates, yielding a smoother energy landscape that facilitates exploration. As the iterations progress, the influence of the likelihood increases while the smoothed prior gradually approaches the true prior
p
, guiding the iterates toward the target posterior. Incorporating this weighted annealing strategy yields the following annealed SGLD iterates with an SGM prior:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}\!

\displaystyle=\!{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\!-\!\gamma\Big(\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\alpha_{k}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\sigma_{k})\Big)+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\bm{Z}}_{k}.

(7)

3.1
ML-VRLD

Our first estimator unifies SGD with momentum and its stochastic counterpart, both of which exhibit lower variance than the SGD estimator. We make the following standard assumptions on the target distribution
\pi\propto e^{-f}
and the stochastic gradients
\nabla f({\bm{x}},\xi)
.

Assumption 1

A function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
has
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient:
\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{1})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{2})\|_{2}\leq L\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|_{2}
,
\forall{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\exists L>0
.

Assumption 2

The stochastic gradient satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\nabla f({\bm{x}},\xi)\mid{\bm{x}}]=\nabla f({\bm{x}})
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}},\xi)-\nabla f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}]\leq\sigma^{2}
,
\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\exists\sigma>0
.

We define the
mean-Lipschitz variance-reduced Langevin diffusion (ML-VRLD)
unified estimator as a replacement for the mini-batch SGD estimator in (
3

{\bm{g}}_{k}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\sum^{b}_{i=1}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i}),&\!\!\text{with prob. }p\\
\beta{\bm{g}}_{k-1}+(1-\beta)\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}),&\!\!\text{with prob. }1-p\end{cases}

(8)

where
(\beta,p)\in(0,1]^{2}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
,
k\geq 1
is the iteration index, and
{\bm{g}}_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0},\xi_{0}^{i})
is the initial estimate. For the special case of
p=0
, ML-VRLD reduces to SGD with momentum. When
\beta=1
, we obtain its stochastic counterpart, which is equivalent to SGD with momentum in expectation and
1-p
becomes the momentum parameter. We combine these estimators to develop a unified analysis of both algorithms, while preserving the convergence guarantees obtained by analyzing each algorithm separately. To intuitively see that ML-VRLD estimator has lower variance than the SGLD estimator (
3
), consider the setting when
p=0
. The estimator is constructed as a weighted average of a stochastic gradient and past gradient estimates. By reusing past gradients, stochastic noise is averaged across iterations and, under Assumption
1
, this yields an accurate, lower-variance estimate with fixed batch size. To analyze the resulting dynamics, we consider the following interpolation of ML-VRLD:

{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-(t-k\gamma){\bm{g}}_{k}+\sqrt{2}({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma})

(9)

for

]

Assumption 4

A function

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{1},\xi)-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{2},\xi)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq L^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k})+\beta({\bm{g}}_{k-1}\\
\quad\quad\quad-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k-1)\gamma},\xi_{k})),&\text{with prob. }1-p\end{cases}

(10)

where
(\beta,p)\in(0,1]^{2}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
,
k\geq 1

6
Conclusion

We proposed ML-VRLD and SL-VRLD, two unified estimators for non-log-concave Langevin sampling under different regularity conditions. These estimators encompass variance reduction techniques from non-convex optimization, including SGD with momentum, STORM, and PAGE. We showed that both methods achieve
\varepsilon
-relative Fisher information convergence with
\mathcal{O}(1)
gradient computations per iteration, enabling convergence under fixed memory requirements. In contrast, SGLD requires a batch size scaling as
\mathcal{O}(Ld\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)
, making it increasingly expensive in high-dimensional and high-variance settings. Moreover, SL-VRLD reduces the total computational complexity compared to ML-VRLD and SGLD. We further established weak convergence to the target distribution and, under a Poincaré inequality assumption, convergence in squared total variation distance. Motivated by the limitations of existing full-batch posterior sampling methods in large-scale inverse problems, we extended our framework to score-based generative priors and developed principled posterior sampling algorithms with the same convergence guarantees. Experiments on synthetic problems validated our theoretical findings, while MRI and sparse-angle CT reconstruction experiments demonstrated improved sample quality using the proposed estimators.

•

[

14

Langevin Diffusion.
We consider the Langevin diffusion defined as the solution to the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

d{\bm{x}}_{t}=-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,d{\bm{B}}_{t},

where
({\bm{B}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
d

\pi

\pi({\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-f({\bm{x}})).

Under mild conditions, this SDE admits
\pi
as its unique stationary distribution. Throughout, we assume that
f
is continuously differentiable and that its gradient
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous (see Assumption
1
). We assume access to stochastic first-order oracles
\nabla f({\bm{x}},\xi)
, where
\xi
denotes a random variable capturing external randomness. Note that the stochastic first-order oracles are unbiased and have bounded variance (see Assumption
2
). Discretizing the above SDE with step size
\gamma>0
yields the Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) update

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{(k+1)\gamma}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big),

where
{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is Brownian motion. We further define
{\bm{g}}_{k}
as estimator of the gradient

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
at iteration
k
.
Replacing the exact gradient by
{\bm{g}}_{k}
,
the LMC update becomes

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma{\bm{g}}_{k}+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{(k+1)\gamma}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big).

The corresponding continuous-time interpolation is given by

{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-(t-k\gamma){\bm{g}}_{k}+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big),\qquad t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma],

and we denote by
\mu_{t}
the law of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
.

Posterior Sampling.

In the posterior sampling setting for ill-posed inverse problems of the form

{\bm{y}}={\bm{A}}({\bm{x}})+{\bm{n}},

we model the prior as

p({\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-h({\bm{x}})),

where
h({\bm{x}})
is the prior potential.
We model the likelihood as

p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-f({\bm{x}})),

where
f({\bm{x}})
is the likelihood potential.
By Bayes’ rule, the posterior satisfies

p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})=\frac{p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\,p({\bm{x}})}{\int p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{u}})\,p({\bm{u}})\,d{\bm{u}}}\propto p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\,p({\bm{x}})\propto\exp\big(-(f({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{x}}))\big).

Sampling from the posterior can be performed via Langevin dynamics

d{\bm{x}}_{t}=-\big(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{t})\big)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,d{\bm{B}}_{t},

which admits
\pi({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})
as its stationary distribution. For ease of notation, we omit the explicit dependence on
{\bm{y}}

and write
\pi({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})
simply as
\pi({\bm{x}})
in the proofs. Discretizing this SDE with step size
\gamma>0
yields

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma\big(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})\big)+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{(k+1)\gamma}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big).

We use an estimator
{\bm{g}}_{k}
(instantiated as ML-VRLD (
8
) or SL-VRLD (
10
))
to approximate
\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
.
Moreover, we use a score network
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},\sigma_{k})
to approximate the smoothed prior score,
i.e.,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},\sigma_{k})\approx-\nabla h_{\sigma_{k}}({\bm{x}})
.
With annealing and smoothing schedules
(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
and
(\sigma_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
,
the annealed posterior update is

{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-\gamma\Big({\bm{g}}_{k}-\alpha_{k}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\sigma_{k})\Big)+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{(k+1)\gamma}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big).

The corresponding continuous-time interpolation on
t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma]
is

{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-(t-k\gamma)\Big({\bm{g}}_{k}-\alpha_{k}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\sigma_{k})\Big)+\sqrt{2}\,\big({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\big),\qquad t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma].

KL Divergence and Fisher Information.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence between two probability measures
\mu
and
\pi
is defined as

\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu({\bm{x}})\log\frac{\mu({\bm{x}})}{\pi({\bm{x}})}d{\bm{x}}.

The relative Fisher information is

\mathrm{FI}(\mu\|\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu({\bm{x}})\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu({\bm{x}})}{\pi({\bm{x}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}d{\bm{x}}.

Probability Measures.
Expectation with respect to a distribution
\mu

is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu}[\cdot]
.
When clear from context, we write
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.

Big-O Notation.

For two positive sequences
f(n)
and
g(n)
,
we write
f(n)=\mathcal{O}(g(n))
if there exists
C>0

such that
f(n)\leq Cg(n)
for sufficiently large
n
. In particular,
\mathcal{O}(1)
denotes a positive constant.

B.2
Lemmas

We begin by presenting several technical lemmas that are used throughout the proofs of the main theorems. The following lemma provides the starting point for our analysis.

Lemma 1

(Lemma 23 in
3
)

Consider the stochastic process defined by

{\bm{x}}_{t}:={\bm{x}}_{0}-t{\bm{g}}_{0}+\sqrt{2}{\bm{B}}_{t},\quad\text{with ${\bm{g}}_{0}=g({\bm{x}}_{0})$},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}

(13)

where
{\bm{g}}_{0}
is integrable and
({\bm{B}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
independent of
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{g}}_{0})
. Then, writing
\mu_{t}
for the law of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{g}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{g}}_{0}\|^{2}\right],

(14)

where
\pi\propto e^{-f}
.

Lemma 2

(Lemma 20 in
9
)

Assume that
f({\bm{x}})
has an
L
-Lipschitz gradient, and let
\pi\propto e^{-f}
. Then, for any probability measure
\mu
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu}\!\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}})\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathrm{FI}\!\left(\mu\|\pi\right)+2dL.

(15)

We use the transportation inequality to analyze the convergence in squared total variation (TV) distance under a Poincaré inequality assumption.

Lemma 3

(Theorem 3.1 in
22
)

If
\pi\propto e^{-f}
satisfies a Poincaré inequality, i.e. for every smooth, compactly supported

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{Var}_{\pi}(f)\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{PI}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\bigl[\|\nabla f\|^{2}\bigr],

then for any probability measure
\mu
,

\|\mu-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\;\leq\;4C_{\mathrm{PI}}\,\mathrm{FI}(\mu\|\pi).

B.3
Theorems

We first state a theorem characterizing the sufficient number of gradient computations per iteration to achieve
\varepsilon
-relative Fisher information for SGLD (
3
) algorithm.

Theorem B.1

(Theorem 15 in
3
)

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the law of the interpolation generated by (
9
) with the estimator defined as
{\bm{g}}_{k}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i})
, where
b\geq 1
. Assume that the potential f defining the target distribution
\pi\propto e^{-f}
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
. Then, for any step size
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{14L}\right)
, and for any
N\geq 1
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{N\gamma}+16L^{2}d\gamma+\frac{8\sigma^{2}}{b}

Furthermore, choose
\gamma=\frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{dN}}
and
b=\lceil\sigma^{2}\sqrt{N}\rceil
. Then, the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}=\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mu_{t}\,dt
satisfies
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
with
\mathcal{O}(\frac{L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}})
iterations and
\mathcal{O}(\frac{Ld\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon})
gradient computations per iteration.

Proof.
We consider the following interpolation for SGLD with mini-batch estimates

{\bm{x}}_{t}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}-(t-k\gamma)\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k}^{i})+\sqrt{2}({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma})

for
t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma]
. Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we follow the proof of Theorem 15 in
3
to obtain

\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{N\gamma}+16L^{2}d\gamma+\frac{8\sigma^{2}}{b}

(16)

for all
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{14L}\right)
. Here,
\mathrm{KL(\mu_{0}\|\pi})
denotes the initial Kullback-Leibler divergence and it scales on the order of
d
(see, e.g., Lemma 1 in
58
or Appendix A in
10
). We then choose

\gamma=\frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{dN}},\quad\text{and}\quad b=\left\lceil\sigma^{2}\sqrt{N}\right\rceil.

Plugging these definitions into (
16
), we get

\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{\left(12L\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)d}+8\right)}{\sqrt{N}},

(17)

where the first inequality follows from the convexity of the Fisher information combined with Jensen’s inequality. Since
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)=\mathcal{O}(d)
, we have
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
if

N=\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\biggr),

(18)

which implies
b=\mathcal{O}(\frac{Ld\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon})
. This concludes the proof.
\square

In the remainder of this section, we present our theoretical results and their proofs. First, we give results for ML-VRLD and SL-VRLD sampling algorithms to sample from a target distribution
\pi\propto e^{-f({\bm{x}})}
. Then, we present the results for the application of these algorithms to inverse problems with score-based generative prior, where the goal is to sample from the posterior
p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})\propto p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}})
with prior
p({\bm{x}})\propto e^{-h({\bm{x}})}
and likelihood
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\propto e^{-f({\bm{x}})}
. In practice, the settings of interest are often high-variance, where
\sigma^{2}
, the upper bound on the variance of stochastic gradients in Assumption
2
, satisfies
\sigma^{2}>1
. Nevertheless, to also cover the case
\sigma^{2}<1
, we state our theoretical results in terms of
\sigma_{m}\coloneqq\max\{\sigma,1\}
. For ease of exposition, we use
\sigma
throughout the main manuscript.

B.3.1
Proof of Theorem
1
(ML-VRLD: FI convergence)

Theorem
1
(ML-VRLD: FI convergence)

Let
\pi\propto e^{-f}
be the target distribution, where the potential function
f
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
. Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the law of continuous-time interpolation generated by (
9
) with estimator (
8
). Then, for any step size
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}\right]
, where
\phi(p,\beta)=1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}
, and for any
N\geq 1
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}||\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2C_{0}}{N\gamma}+9\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+\frac{72\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}},

(19)

where

C_{0}=\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{9\gamma}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})-{\bm{g}}_{0}\|^{2}].

(20)

Furthermore, choose

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{3/4}\sqrt{L}},\qquad p=1-\beta=\frac{\sqrt{Ld}}{\sigma_{m}N^{1/4}},\qquad b=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1}{p}\Bigr\rceil,\quad\text{where}\quad\sigma_{m}=\max\{\sigma,1\}.

Then, the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}=\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mu_{t}\,dt
satisfies
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
with
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{4}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}})
iterations and
\mathcal{O}(1)
gradient computations per iteration.

Proof.

Using the interpolation argument in (
9
), we begin the proof by recalling Lemma
1

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right].

We decompose the error term by introducing
\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
and then apply Young’s inequality (i.e.,
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
), obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})\|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right].

Invoking Assumption
1
, we upper bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})\|^{2}\right]
using Lipschitz continuity of the potential
\nabla f
, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+2L^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]+2e_{k}^{2},

(21)

where we define

e_{k}^{2}\coloneq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right]
.
The next step is to bound
e_{k}^{2}
.
Using the definition of the ML-VRLD estimator (
8
),

e_{k+1}^{2}
can be expressed as follows.
With probability
p
, a mini-batch estimator of size
b
is used,
while with probability
1-p
a momentum-based update is applied.
This yields

\displaystyle e_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=p\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma},\xi_{k+1}^{i})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\bigl(\beta{\bm{g}}_{k}+(1-\beta)\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma},\xi_{k+1})\bigr)\right\|^{2}\right].

Using Assumption
2
, we upper bound the variance of the mini-batch estimator, which gives

\displaystyle e_{k+1}^{2}\leq\frac{p\sigma^{2}}{b}+(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\bigl(\beta{\bm{g}}_{k}+(1-\beta)\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma},\xi_{k+1})\bigr)\right\|^{2}\right].

We decompose the second term by adding and subtracting

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})
inside expectation, regrouping the terms and using Assumption
2

\displaystyle e_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p\sigma^{2}}{b}+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left[\|\beta(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-{\bm{g}}_{k})+\beta(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}))\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+(1-\beta)(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma},\xi_{k+1}))\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p\sigma^{2}}{b}+(1-p)\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-{\bm{g}}_{k})+(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}))\|^{2}\right]+(1-\beta)^{2}(1-p)\sigma^{2}.

Applying Young’s inequality with parameter
\rho_{0}>0

(i.e.,
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq(1+\rho_{0})\|a\|^{2}+(1+1/\rho_{0})\|b\|^{2}
),
together with Assumption
1
to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\|(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-{\bm{g}}_{k})+(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}))\|^{2}\right]
, we obtain

\displaystyle e_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p\sigma^{2}}{b}+(1-p)\beta^{2}L^{2}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\right)\Delta_{k}+(1-p)\beta^{2}(1+\rho_{0})e_{k}^{2}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\beta^{2}L^{2}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\right)\Delta_{k}+(1-p)\beta^{2}(1+\rho_{0})e_{k}^{2}+\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]\sigma^{2},

where
\Delta_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{(k+1)\gamma}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]
. Furthermore, we choose
\rho_{0}=\frac{1-(1-p)\beta}{2(1-p)\beta}
. Rearranging the terms then yields

\displaystyle e_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2}{1-(1-p)\beta}(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2})+\frac{\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]2\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}+\frac{2(1-p)\beta^{2}L^{2}(1+(1-p)\beta)}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2}{1-(1-p)\beta}(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2})+\frac{\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]2\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}L^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}\Delta_{k}.

(22)

Since
p\in(0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,1]
, it follows that
(1-p)\beta\in[0,1)
, and hence

1+(1-p)\beta\leq 2
. We have used this bound to simplify the last term. We now bound
\Delta_{k}
by using the continuous interpolation (
9
) of LMC. For
t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma]
,

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}

\displaystyle=-(t-k\gamma){\bm{g}}_{k}+\sqrt{2}\bigl({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\bigr).

Taking squared norms on both sides and then taking expectations,
and using the independence of
{\bm{g}}_{k}
and the Brownian increment,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(t-k\gamma)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(t-k\gamma)^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+2(t-k\gamma)d

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+2(t-k\gamma)d

\displaystyle=\Delta_{k},

where we have used the identity
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]=(t-k\gamma)d

Writing

{\bm{g}}_{k}=({\bm{g}}_{k}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}))+(\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}))+\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),

and applying the inequality

\|a+b+c\|^{2}\leq 3(\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}+\|c\|^{2})

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+2\gamma d

Using Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
(Assumption
1
) and the bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]\leq\Delta_{k}
yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\leq 3\gamma^{2}e_{k}^{2}+3\gamma^{2}L^{2}\Delta_{k}+3\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}\right]+2\gamma d.

Substituting the upper bound (
22
) for

and rearranging the terms yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\,\bigl(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{6\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+3\gamma^{2}L^{2}\left[1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}}{\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)^{2}}\right]\Delta_{k}+3\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}\right]+2\gamma d.

To simplify notation, we define

\phi(p,\beta)\coloneqq 1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}.

With this definition and rearranging the terms, the inequality becomes

\displaystyle\bigl[1-3\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\bigr]\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\,\bigl(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{6\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+3\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}\right]+2\gamma d.

Assuming the step size satisfies
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{27L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)}
, the coefficient of
\Delta_{k}
on the left-hand side satisfies
1-3\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\geq\frac{8}{9}
. Applying this bound and dividing both sides by
\frac{8}{9}
yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{27\gamma^{2}}{4\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)}\,\bigl(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{27\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}}{4\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]+\frac{27\gamma^{2}}{8}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}\right]+\frac{9}{4}\,\gamma d.

(23)

Now, recall from (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)+2L^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]+2e_{k}^{2},

Applying (
22
) and (

) together with

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|x_{t}-x_{k\gamma}\|^{2}\right]\leq\Delta_{k}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{3}{4}\,\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)-\frac{1}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\right]\bigl(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\right]\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{27}{4}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}\right]+\frac{9}{2}\gamma L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\,d.

Applying Lemma
2
to bound

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}\right]
, and using the step size condition

\gamma\leq\frac{1}{27L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)}
, we simplify the above inequality as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)-\frac{1}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\right]\bigl(e_{k+1}^{2}-e_{k}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}}{2\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]+9\gamma L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\,d.

(24)

We now integrate both sides over
t\in[k\gamma,(k+1)\gamma]

and define
\mathcal{L}_{k}\coloneqq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{k\gamma}\|\pi)+\frac{\gamma}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\right]e_{k}^{2}.

This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{k\gamma}^{(k+1)\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt+\frac{9\,\gamma\sigma^{2}}{2\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]+9\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)d.

Iterating the inequality for
k=0,1,\dots,N-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}-\mathcal{L}_{0}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt+\frac{9N\gamma\sigma^{2}}{2\bigl(1-(1-p)\beta\bigr)}\left[\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}\right]+9N\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)d.

Rearranging the terms, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathcal{L}_{0}}{N\gamma}+9\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+18\gamma L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)d.

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{\gamma}{1-(1-p)\beta}\left(4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma^{2}L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)\right)e_{0}^{2}\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{9\gamma e_{0}^{2}}{2(1-(1-p)\beta)}\eqqcolon C_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2C}{N\gamma}+9\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+18\gamma L^{2}\phi(p,\beta)d.

Also
\phi(p,\beta)=1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}=\left(\frac{1+(1-p)\beta}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{4}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}
since
1+(1-p)\beta\leq 2
,
then the inequality can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2C_{0}}{N\gamma}+9\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+\frac{72\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}.

(25)

This concludes the first part of the proof. Next we choose

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{3/4}\sqrt{L}},\quad p=(1-\beta)=\frac{\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}\sigma_{m}}\quad\text{and}\quad b=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1}{p}\Bigr\rceil\quad\text{where}\quad\sigma_{m}\coloneqq\mathrm{max}\{\sigma,1\}

and define the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mu_{t}\,dt
. Since
\mathrm{FI}(\cdot\|\pi)
is convex, using Jensen’s inequality, we obtain the upper bound for
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\underbrace{\frac{2C_{0}}{N\gamma}}_{\mathrm{(I)}}+\underbrace{9\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]}_{\mathrm{(II)}}+\underbrace{\frac{72\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}}_{\mathrm{(III)}}.

Substituting the definition of
\gamma
into
\mathrm{(I)}
, we have

\mathrm{(I)}=\frac{2C_{0}\sigma_{m}\sqrt{L}}{N^{1/4}\sqrt{d}}.

(26)

To bound
\mathrm{(II)}
, we proceed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{(II)}

\displaystyle\leq 9\sigma_{m}^{2}\biggl[\frac{p^{2}+(1-p)p^{2}}{1-(1-p)^{2}}\biggr]

(27)

\displaystyle\leq 18\sigma_{m}^{2}p.

(28)

We use
\sigma\leq\sigma_{m}
,
b\geq 1/p
, and
1-\beta=p
to get (
27
). Following that, we use
p\in(0,1]
to get (
28
). Substituting the definition of
p
, we have

\mathrm{(II)}\leq\frac{18\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}}.

(29)

Similarly, to bound
\mathrm{(III)}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{(III)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{72\gamma L^{2}d}{p^{2}}

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{72\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}},

(31)

where we use
p=1-\beta
and
p\in(0,1]
to obtain (
30
), and substitute the definitions of
\gamma
and
p
to get (
31
). Combining (
26
,
29
,
31
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{0}\sigma_{m}\sqrt{L}}{N^{1/4}\sqrt{d}}+\frac{90\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2C_{0}}{d}+90\right)\frac{\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}},

where
C_{0}
is usually taken to be of order
d
, see e.g. Lemma 1 in
58
or Appendix A in
10
. Thus, the above bound implies that, to ensure

\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
,

N=\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{4}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\biggl)

iterations is sufficient. And the average number of gradient computations per iteration is

pb+(1-p)=\mathcal{O}(1).

(32)

We next verify that the parameter constraints imposed throughout the analysis
are compatible with this choice of
N
.
First, the requirement

p=(1-\beta)=\frac{\sqrt{dL}}{N^{1/4}\sigma_{m}}\leq 1

imposes the lower bound

N\geq\frac{d^{2}L^{2}}{\sigma_{m}^{4}}
,
which is satisfied by the above choice of
N
. Second, we show that the step-size condition

\gamma\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}

is satisfied. Since
\phi(p,\beta)\leq\frac{4}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}
, we have

\gamma\leq\frac{1-(1-p)\beta}{L\sqrt{108}}\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}.

Substituting the definitions of
\beta=1-p
and
p=\frac{\sqrt{dL}}{\sigma_{m}N^{1/4}}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1-(1-p)\beta}{L\sqrt{108}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(1-p)^{2}}{L\sqrt{108}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p}{L\sqrt{108}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{1/4}\sqrt{108L}}.

Thus, the step-size condition holds provided that

\gamma\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{1/4}\sqrt{108L}}.

Using the definition of step size

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{3/4}\sqrt{L}},

this condition reduces to the following lower bound
N\geq 108
, which is satisfied by
N=\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{4}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\biggr)
.
\square

B.3.2
Proof of Corrolary
1
(ML-VRLD: squared TV distance convergence)

Corrolary
1
(ML-VRLD: squared TV distance convergence)

Let
\pi\propto e^{-f}
be the target distribution, where the potential function
f
satisfies Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
. Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the law of continuous-time interpolation generated by (
9
) with estimator (
8
). Then, for any step size
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}\right]
, where
\phi(p,\beta)=1+\frac{4(1-p)\beta^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}
, and for any
N\geq 1
, it holds that

\|\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leq\frac{8C_{0}C_{\mathrm{PI}}}{N\gamma}+36C_{\mathrm{PI}}\sigma^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+\frac{288C_{\mathrm{PI}}\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}},

(33)

where

C_{0}=\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{9\gamma}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})-{\bm{g}}_{0}\|^{2}].

(34)

Furthermore, choose

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{3/4}\sqrt{L}},\qquad p=1-\beta=\frac{\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}\sigma_{m}},\qquad b=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1}{p}\Bigr\rceil,\quad\text{where }\sigma_{m}=\max\{\sigma,1\}.

Then, the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}=\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mu_{t}\,dt
satisfies
\|\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
with
\penalty\ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{PI}}^{4}\sigma_{m}^{4}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)
iterations and
\mathcal{O}(1)
gradient computations per iteration.

Proof.
Recall from the proof of Theorem
1
(Appendix
B.3.1
) that the Fisher information of the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}
satisfies (
25
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{N\gamma}\int_{0}^{N\gamma}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\leq\frac{2C_{0}}{N\gamma}+9\sigma_{m}^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-(1-p)\beta}\right]+\frac{72\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}},

where we use
\sigma\leq\sigma_{m}
. Using Lemma
3
and Assumption
3
, we get

\|\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leq\frac{8C_{0}C_{\mathrm{PI}}}{N\gamma}+36C_{\mathrm{PI}}\sigma_{m}^{2}\left[\frac{\frac{p}{b}+(1-p)(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1-(1-p)\beta)}\right]+\frac{288C_{\mathrm{PI}}\gamma L^{2}d}{(1-(1-p)\beta)^{2}}.

Choose

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sigma_{m}N^{3/4}\sqrt{L}},\qquad p=1-\beta=\frac{\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}\sigma_{m}},\qquad b=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1}{p}\Bigr\rceil,\quad\text{where }\sigma_{m}\coloneqq\max\{\sigma,1\}.

Following the same steps in (
26
,
29
,
31
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2C_{0}}{d}+90\right)\frac{4C_{\mathrm{PI}}\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{N^{1/4}},

where
C_{0}
is in the order of
d
. This implies that, to ensure

\|\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
,
it suffices to choose
N
such that

N^{1/4}\;\geq\;\frac{\tilde{C}C_{\mathrm{PI}}\sigma_{m}\sqrt{Ld}}{\varepsilon},

where
\tilde{C}\coloneqq\frac{8C_{0}}{d}+360>0
.
Equivalently, this yields iteration complexity of

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{PI}}^{4}\sigma_{m}^{4}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)
with
\mathcal{O}(1)
gradient computations per iteration. Since the constraints on
N
in Theorem
1
remain unchanged, they continue to hold.
\square

B.3.3
Proof of Theorem
2
(ML-VRLD: weak convergence)

Theorem
2
(ML-VRLD: weak convergence)

Let
\pi\propto e^{-f}
be the target distribution, where the potential function
f
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
. Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the law of continuous-time interpolation generated by (
9
) with estimator (
8
). Define the time-varying parameters as follows

\gamma_{k}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{k^{3/2}},\qquad p_{k}=1-\beta_{k}=\frac{C_{p}}{k^{1/2}},\qquad b_{k}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right\rceil,\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

where
0<C_{p}<1
and
C_{\gamma}>0
are numerical constants.
Then, the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt
, where
\tau_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}
, converges weakly to
\pi
.

Proof.
Given time-varying parameters
\gamma_{k},b_{k},p_{k}
at iteration
k
, define the cumulative time
\tau_{n}
and averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}
at iteration
n
as

\tau_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k},\qquad\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt,

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the law of the process
{\bm{x}}_{t}
under the following continuous-time interpolation

{\bm{x}}_{t}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}}-(t-\tau_{n})\,{\bm{g}}_{n}+\sqrt{2}\,\bigl({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{\tau_{n}}\bigr),\qquad t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}].

(35)

{\bm{g}}_{n}
is defined as follows

{\bm{g}}_{n}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{n}}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}},\xi_{n}^{i})&\text{w.p. }p_{n},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\beta_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n-1}+(1-\beta_{n})\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}},\xi_{n})&\text{w.p. }1-p_{n},\end{cases}

(36)

for all
n\geq 1
. At initialization, we choose
\gamma_{0},b_{0}>0
and
(\beta_{0},p_{0})\in(0,1]^{2}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
, and define
{\bm{g}}_{0}
as

{\bm{g}}_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{0}}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0},\xi_{0}^{i}).

We establish weak convergence by adapting the proof of Theorem
1
. To this end, we first verify the step sizes
(\gamma_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy the step-size condition of Theorem
1
, namely,

\gamma_{k}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})=1+\frac{4(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}^{2}}{(1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k})^{2}}

(37)

for all
k\geq 1
. Using the definitions of
p_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{4(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}^{2}}{(1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{4}{1-(1-p_{k})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{4}{p_{k}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{4k^{1/2}}{C_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8k^{1/2}}{C_{p}},

(38)

where we use the fact that
0<C_{p}<1
to obtain (
38
). Then, we have

\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{216}k^{1/4}}.

Choosing
C_{\gamma}=\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{216}}
, we have

\gamma_{k}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{k^{3/2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{216}k^{1/4}}\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}

for
k\geq 1
. This verifies (
37
). In addition, by definition of
p_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
, we have
p_{k}<1
and
\beta_{k}<1
. Therefore, we can follow the same steps in Theorem
1
(Appendix
B.3.1
) up to (
B.3.1
) since the the same arguments remain valid for
t\in[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
with time-varying parameters. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)-\frac{1}{1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\right]\bigl(e_{n}^{2}-e_{n-1}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}}{2\bigl(1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n}}{b_{n}}+(1-p_{n})(1-\beta_{n})^{2}\right]+9\gamma_{n}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\,d

(39)

for
t\in[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
. Integrating both sides of the inequality over the interval
[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{n-1}}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\right]\bigl(e_{n}^{2}-e_{n-1}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\gamma_{n}\,\sigma^{2}}{2\bigl(1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}\bigr)}\left[p_{n}^{2}+(1-p_{n})(1-\beta_{n})^{2}\right]+9\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})d,

where we use
b_{n}\geq\frac{1}{p_{n}}
. Iterating the above bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\underbrace{-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})\right]\bigl(e_{k}^{2}-e_{k-1}^{2}\bigr)}_{\text{(I)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)}_{\text{(II)}}+\underbrace{9L^{2}d\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}_{\text{(III)}}.

(40)

It remains to control the terms labeled (I)–(III) and show that their contribution remains uniformly bounded as
k\to\infty
. This will imply that the upper bound (
40
) stays bounded, which is a key step toward establishing weak convergence.

First, analyze the term (I). Define

\displaystyle c_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\left(4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})\right).

If
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is nonnegative and decreasing sequence, we can bound
\mathrm{(I)}
as follows

\displaystyle(\textup{I})=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(e_{k}^{2}-e_{k-1}^{2})=c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(c_{k+1}-c_{k})e_{k}^{2}-c_{n}e_{n}^{2}\leq c_{1}e_{0}^{2}.

Thus, it remains to show that
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is nonnegative and decreasing sequence. Nonnegativity is obvious as all the terms are positive and
p_{k}<1
and
\beta_{k}<1
. To prove that the sequence is decreasing, we substitute
\gamma_{k}
,
\beta_{k}
, and
p_{k}
. We then have

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-\beta_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{2C_{p}k-C_{p}^{2}k^{1/2}},

which is decreasing for
k\geq 1
. In addition,

\displaystyle\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2k^{3}}\left(1+\frac{4\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{k^{1/2}}\right)^{3}}{\left(\frac{2C_{p}}{k^{1/2}}-\frac{C_{p}^{2}}{k}\right)^{2}}\right).

We can write this sequence as a continuous function

c(x)=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2x^{3}}\left(1+\frac{4\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}\right)^{3}}{\left(\frac{2C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}-\frac{C_{p}^{2}}{x}\right)^{2}}\right)

for
x\geq 1
. We apply change of variables
r=\frac{C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}
and get

c(x)=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2C_{p}^{6}}\underbrace{r^{6}\left(1+\frac{4(1-r)^{3}}{r^{2}(2-r)^{2}}\right)}_{\eqqcolon h(r)}.

(41)

Differentiating
h(r)
with respect to
r
, we have

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{3}}{(2-r)^{3}}\left[16-64r+114r^{2}-88r^{3}+28r^{4}-3r^{5}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{3}}{(2-r)^{3}}\left[16(1-r)^{5}+16r(1-r)^{4}+18r^{2}(1-r)^{3}+30r^{3}(1-r)^{2}+30r^{3}(1-r)^{2}+18r^{4}(1-r)+3r^{5}\right]

\displaystyle>0

since
0<r<1
. Hence, differentiating
h(r(x))
with respect to
x
, we get

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2C_{p}^{6}}h^{\prime}(r(x))r^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{4C_{p}^{5}x^{3/2}}h^{\prime}(r(x))<0

for
x\geq 1
. Therefore,
c(x)
is decreasing on
[1,\infty)
, which implies
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is decreasing sequence. This implies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\mathrm{I})\leq c_{1}e_{0}^{2}.

(42)

We now analyze the term (II)

\displaystyle\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+p_{k}^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=9\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}p_{k}.

Using the definitions of
\gamma_{k}
and
p_{k}
, we obtain the following upper bound for (II):

\displaystyle\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 9C_{\gamma}C_{p}\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{(\textup{II})}\leq 9C_{\gamma}C_{p}\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\eqqcolon S_{2}<\infty.

(43)

Finally, we analyze (III)

\displaystyle 9L^{2}d\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\left(1+\frac{4(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}^{2}}{(1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k})^{2}}\right)\leq 72L^{2}d\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k}^{2}}{p_{k}^{2}}=\frac{72C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}d}{C_{p}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{(\textup{III})}\leq\frac{36C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}d}{C_{p}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\eqqcolon S_{3}<\infty.

(44)

Now that we have shown the terms (I), (II) and (III) are uniformly bounded, we bound (
40
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}.

(45)

Since KL divergence is nonnegative, we may discard
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)
and rearrange the terms to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}.

Dividing both sides by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

Since
\mathrm{FI}(\cdot\|\pi)
is convex, we use Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2c_{1}e_{0}^{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{3}}{\tau_{n}},

(46)

where
\mu_{\tau_{n}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt
. Given that
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)<\infty
, we have
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)<\infty
. On the other hand, if
t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
, considering (
39
) for the interval
[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
, integrating both sides from
\tau_{n}
to
t
, and dropping the negative integral over the Fisher information gives us

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\,\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\frac{(t-\tau_{n})}{1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}}\left[4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\right](e_{n+1}^{2}-e_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}(t-\tau_{n})}{2\bigl(1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n+1}}{b_{n+1}}+(1-p_{n+1})(1-\beta_{n+1})^{2}\right]+9(t-\tau_{n})\gamma_{n+1}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\,d

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}(t-\tau_{n})}{2\bigl(1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n+1}}{b_{n+1}}+(1-p_{n+1})(1-\beta_{n+1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+9(t-\tau_{n+1})\gamma_{n+1}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})d+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}

where the second inequality follows from (
40
), the bounds on
\mathrm{(II)}
and
\mathrm{(III)}

(47)

for
t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
. To obtain the last inequality, we again use the bounds on
\mathrm{(II)}
and
\mathrm{(III)}
.

This proves
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)
is uniformly bounded for all
t\geq 0
. By the convexity of the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence, it implies that
\{\mathrm{KL(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is uniformly bounded as well. Since the sub-level sets of
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\pi)
are weakly compact,
(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight. To show that
\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\rightarrow\pi
weakly, it is sufficient to show that every cluster point of
(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equal to
\pi
.

Consider a subsequence of
(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converging to some limit
\bar{\mu}
. As
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\tau_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, and by inequality (
46
) we also have
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, this is still true along the subsequence. By the weak lower semi-continuity of the Fisher information along the subsequence,
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)=0
. That implies; for
\psi\coloneqq\frac{d\mu}{d\pi}
, we have
\sqrt{\psi}\in\mathrm{dom(\mathcal{E})}
and
\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{\psi})=0
, where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the Dirichlet energy (i.e. the squared
L^{2}(\pi)
-norm of the gradient; see Section 3 in
3
). Since
\nabla f
is Lipschitz by Assumption
1

\pi
has a continuous and strictly positive density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so
\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{\psi})=0
implies that
\psi
is a constant
\pi
with probability 1. Hence,
\bar{\mu}=\pi
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

\square

\displaystyle=\frac{30\gamma L^{2}d}{1-(1-p)\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{30\gamma L^{2}d}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{30\sigma_{m}^{2/3}L^{2/3}d^{2/3}}{N^{1/3}}.

(64)

Combining the bounds for
\mathrm{(I)}
,
\mathrm{(II)}
, and
\mathrm{(III)}
, we have

\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\left(\frac{2C_{0}}{d}+48\right)\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2/3}L^{2/3}d^{2/3}}{N^{1/3}},

where
C_{0}
is usually taken to be of order
d
, see e.g. Lemma 1 in
58
or Appendix A in
10
. Thus, the above bound implies that, to ensure

\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{N\gamma}\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
,

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)

iterations is sufficient. And the average number of gradient computations per iteration is

pb+(1-p)=\mathcal{O}(1).

We next verify that the constraints on
N
imposed throughout the analysis are consistent with the stated iteration complexity.

First, the requirement
p=1-\beta=\frac{d^{2/3}L^{2/3}}{\sigma_{m}^{4/3}N^{1/3}}\leq 1
imposes the lower bound
N\geq\frac{d^{2}L^{2}}{\sigma_{m}^{4}}
, which is satisfied by the above choice of
N
.

Second, we show that the step-size condition
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}
is satisfied. We note that

\displaystyle\phi(p,\beta)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2(1-p)}{1-(1-p)\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(1-p)\beta}{1-(1-p)\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{1-(1-p)\beta}.

Then,
\gamma
can be chosen as

\displaystyle\gamma\leq\frac{\sqrt{1-(1-p)\beta}}{L\sqrt{54}}\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p,\beta)}}.

Substituting the definitions of
\beta=1-p
and
p=\frac{d^{2/3}L^{2/3}}{\sigma_{m}^{4/3}N^{1/3}}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1-(1-p)\beta}}{L\sqrt{54}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{1-(1-p)^{2}}}{L\sqrt{54}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{p}}{L\sqrt{54}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{1/3}}{\sigma_{m}^{2/3}L^{2/3}N^{1/6}\sqrt{54}}.

Thus, the step-size condition holds provided that

\gamma\leq\frac{d^{1/3}}{\sigma_{m}^{2/3}L^{2/3}N^{1/6}\sqrt{54}}.

Using the definition of step size

\gamma=\frac{d^{1/3}}{\sigma_{m}^{2/3}L^{2/3}N^{2/3}},

this condition reduces to the following lower bound
N\geq 54
, which is satisfied by
N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}L^{2}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)
.
\square

Corollary

3

B.3.6
Proof of Theorem
B
.1 (SL-VRLD: weak convergence)

Theorem
B
.1 (SL-VRLD: weak convergence)

Let
\pi\propto e^{-f}
be the target distribution, where the potential function
f
satisfies Assumptions
2
and
4
. Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the law of continuous-time interpolation generated by (
9
) with estimator (
10
). Define the time-varying parameters as follows

\gamma_{k}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{k^{3/2}},\qquad p_{k}=\frac{C_{p}}{k^{1/2}},\qquad b_{k}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right\rceil,\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

where
0<C_{p}<1
and
C_{\gamma}>0
are numerical constants.
Then, the time-averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt
, where
\tau_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}
, converges weakly to
\pi
.

Proof.
Given time-varying parameters
\gamma_{k},b_{k},p_{k}
at iteration
k
, define the cumulative time
\tau_{n}
and averaged law
\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}
at iteration
n
as

\tau_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k},\qquad\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt,

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the law of the process
{\bm{x}}_{t}
under the following continuous-time interpolation

{\bm{x}}_{t}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}}-(t-\tau_{n})\,{\bm{g}}_{n}+\sqrt{2}\,\bigl({\bm{B}}_{t}-{\bm{B}}_{\tau_{n}}\bigr),\qquad t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}].

(67)

{\bm{g}}_{n}
is defined as follows

{\bm{g}}_{n}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{n}}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}},\xi_{n}^{i})&\text{w.p. }p_{n},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n}},\xi_{n})+\beta_{n}({\bm{g}}_{n-1}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{\tau_{n-1}},\xi_{n}))&\text{w.p. }1-p_{n},\end{cases}

(68)

for all
n\geq 1
. At initialization, we choose
\gamma_{0},b_{0}>0
and
(\beta_{0},p_{0})\in(0,1]^{2}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
, and define
{\bm{g}}_{0}
as

{\bm{g}}_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{0}}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0},\xi_{0}^{i}).

We establish weak convergence by adapting the proof of Theorem
3
. To this end, we first verify the step sizes
(\gamma_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy the step-size condition of Theorem
3
, namely

\gamma_{k}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{L\sqrt{\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})=1+\frac{2(1-p)}{1-(1-p)\beta}

(69)

for all
k\geq 1
. Using the definitions of
p_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2(1-p_{k})}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2}{1-(1-p_{k})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2}{p_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2k^{1/2}}{C_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4k^{1/2}}{C_{p}},

where we use the fact that
0<C_{p}<1
. Then, we have

\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{108}k^{1/4}}.

Choosing
C_{\gamma}=\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{108}}
, we have

\gamma_{k}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{k^{3/2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{C_{p}}}{L\sqrt{108}k^{1/4}}\leq\frac{1}{L\sqrt{27\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}}

for
k\geq 1
. This verifies (
69
).
Therefore, we can follow the same steps in Theorem
3
(Appendix
B.3.4
) up to (
B.3.4
) since the same arguments remain valid for
t\in[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
with time-varying parameters. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)-\frac{1}{1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}}\left(2+\frac{27}{4}\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\right)\bigl(e_{n}^{2}-e_{n-1}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4\bigl(1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n}}{b_{n}}+2(1-p_{n})(1-\beta_{n})^{2}\right]+\frac{15}{2}\gamma_{n}L^{2}\,\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})d

(70)

for
t\in[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
, where
\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\coloneqq 1+\frac{2(1-p_{n})}{1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}}
. Integrating both sides of the inequality over the interval
[\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}]
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n-1}}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{n-1}}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}}\left[2+\frac{27}{4}\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})\right]\bigl(e_{n}^{2}-e_{n-1}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\gamma_{n}\,\sigma^{2}}{4\bigl(1-(1-p_{n})\beta_{n}\bigr)}\left[p_{n}^{2}+2(1-p_{n})(1-\beta_{n})^{2}\right]+\frac{15}{2}\gamma_{n}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n},\beta_{n})d,

where we use
b_{n}\geq\frac{1}{p_{n}}
. Iterating the above bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt\underbrace{-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\left[2+\frac{27}{4}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})\right]\bigl(e_{k}^{2}-e_{k-1}^{2}\bigr)}_{\text{(I)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+2(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)}_{\text{(II)}}+\underbrace{\frac{15L^{2}d}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})}_{\text{(III)}}.

(71)

It remains to control the terms labeled (I)–(III) and show that their contribution remains uniformly bounded as
k\to\infty
. This will imply that the upper bound (
71
) stays bounded, which is a key step toward establishing weak convergence.

First, analyze the term (I). Define

\displaystyle c_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\left(4+\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})\right).

If
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is nonnegative and decreasing sequence, we have

\displaystyle(\textup{I})=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(e_{k}^{2}-e_{k-1}^{2})=c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(c_{k+1}-c_{k})e_{k}^{2}-c_{n}e_{n}^{2}\leq c_{1}e_{0}^{2}

Thus, it remains to show that
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is nonnegative and decreasing sequence. Nonnegativity is obvious as all the terms are positive and
p_{k}<1
and
\beta_{k}<1
. To prove that the sequence is decreasing, we substitute
\gamma_{k},\beta_{k}
, and
p_{k}
. As shown in the proof of Theorem
2
, we have

\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{2C_{p}k-C_{p}^{2}k^{1/2}},

which is decreasing for
k\geq 1
. In addition,

\frac{27}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{k},\beta_{k})=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2k^{3}}\left(1+\frac{2\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{k^{1/2}}\right)}{1-\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{k^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}\right).

We can write this sequence as a continuous function

c(x)=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2x^{3}}\left(1+\frac{2\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}\right)}{1-\left(1-\frac{C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}\right)

for
x\geq 1
. We use change of variables
r=\frac{C_{p}}{x^{1/2}}
and get

c(x)=\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2C_{p}^{6}}\underbrace{\left(\frac{r^{5}(2-r^{2})}{2-r}\right)}_{\coloneqq h(r)}.

Differentiating
h(r)
with respect to
r
, we have

h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2r^{4}(10-4r-7r^{2}+3r^{3})}{(2-r)^{2}}.

Defining
q(r)\coloneqq 10-4r-7r^{2}+3r^{3}
, we note that, for
r\in(0,1)
,
q^{\prime}(r)=-4-14r+9r^{2}<-4-5r<0
. Therefore,

q(r)>q(1)=2>0,

which implies
h^{\prime}(r)>0
for
r\in(0,1)
. Hence,

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{2C_{p}^{6}}h^{\prime}(r(x))r^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{27C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}}{4C_{p}^{5}x^{3/2}}h^{\prime}(r(x))<0

for
x\geq 1
. Therefore,
c(x)
is decreasing on
[1,\infty)
, which implies
(c_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is decreasing sequence. This implies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\mathrm{I})\leq c_{1}e_{0}^{2}.

(72)

We now analyze the term
\mathrm{(II)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{(II)}

\displaystyle=\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+2(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+2(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+2p_{k}^{2}}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{27}{4}\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}p_{k}

Using the definitions of
\gamma_{k}
and
p_{k}
, we obtain the following upper bound for (II):

\displaystyle\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}^{2}+2(1-p_{k})(1-\beta_{k})^{2}}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{27C_{\gamma}C_{p}\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{(\textup{II})}\leq\frac{27C_{\gamma}C_{p}\sigma^{2}}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\eqqcolon S_{2}<\infty.

(73)

Finally, we analyze (III)

\displaystyle\mathrm{(III)}=\frac{15L^{2}d}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2(1-p_{k})}{1-(1-p_{k})\beta_{k}}\right)\leq\frac{15L^{2}d}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2(1-p_{k})}{p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{15L^{2}d}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2\gamma_{k}^{2}}{p_{k}}.

Using the definitions of
\gamma_{k}
and
p_{k}
, and taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{(\textup{III})}\leq\frac{15C_{\gamma}^{2}L^{2}d}{C_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{\frac{5}{2}}}\eqqcolon S_{3}<\infty.

(74)

Now that we have shown the terms (I), (II) and (III) are uniformly bounded, we bound (
71
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}.

(75)

Since KL divergence is nonnegative, we may discard
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\mu_{\tau_{n}}}\|\pi)
and rearrange the terms to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}.

Dividing both sides by
\frac{\tau_{n}}{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2c_{1}e_{0}^{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{3}}{\tau_{n}}.

Since
\mathrm{FI}(\cdot\|\pi)
is convex, we use Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{FI}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2c_{1}e_{0}^{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{2}}{\tau_{n}}+\frac{2S_{3}}{\tau_{n}},

(76)

where
\mu_{\tau_{n}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\mu_{t}\,dt
. Given that
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)<\infty
, we have
\mathrm{FI}(\bar{\mu}_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)<\infty
. On the other hand, if
t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
, considering (
70
) for the interval
[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
, integrating both sides from
\tau_{n}
to
t
, and dropping the negative integral over the Fisher information gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}}\left(2+\frac{27}{4}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\right)\bigl(e_{n+1}^{2}-e_{n}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\sigma^{2}}{4\bigl(1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}\bigr)}\left[p_{n+1}^{2}+2(1-p_{n+1})(1-\beta_{n+1})^{2}\right]+\frac{15}{2}\gamma_{n+1}L^{2}d\,\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1}).

Furthermore, integrating both sides over the interval
[\tau_{n},t]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\,\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{\tau_{n}}\|\pi)-\frac{(t-\tau_{n})}{1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}}\left[2+\frac{27}{4}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\right](e_{n+1}^{2}-e_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}(t-\tau_{n})}{4\bigl(1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n+1}}{b_{n+1}}+2(1-p_{n+1})(1-\beta_{n+1})^{2}\right]+\frac{15}{2}(t-\tau_{n})\gamma_{n+1}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\,d

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+\frac{9\,\sigma^{2}(t-\tau_{n})}{4\bigl(1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}\bigr)}\left[\frac{p_{n+1}}{b_{n+1}}+2(1-p_{n+1})(1-\beta_{n+1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{15}{2}(t-\tau_{n})\gamma_{n+1}L^{2}\phi(p_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\,d+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+S_{2}+S_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}\|\pi)+c_{1}e_{0}^{2}+2S_{2}+2S_{3}<\infty.

where the second inequality follows from (
71
), the bounds on
\mathrm{(II)}
and
\mathrm{(III)}
, and the fact that

c_{n+1}\geq\frac{(t-\tau_{n})}{1-(1-p_{n+1})\beta_{n+1}}

for
t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}]
. To obtain the last inequality, we again use the bounds on
\mathrm{(II)}
and
\mathrm{(III)}
. Thus
\{\mathrm{KL(\mu_{t}\|\pi)}\,|\,t\geq 0\}
is bounded. The weak convergence of
\bar{\mu}
to
\pi
then follows by the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
2
.

\square

{\bm{A}}_{i}\coloneqq{\bm{P}}{\bm{F}}{\bm{S}}_{i}
denotes the measurement operator corresponding to the
i
-th coil.
Here,
{\bm{P}}
is a diagonal subsampling matrix,
{\bm{F}}
denotes the Fourier transform matrix, and
{\bm{S}}_{i}
represents the receiver coil sensitivity map. We set
{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}I)
such that the input signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) corresponds to 40 dB. Then the likelihood is
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}I)
, so the score of the likelihood at
k^{th}
iteration is

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{H}\left({\bm{y}}-\sum_{j=1}^{K}{\bm{A}}_{j}{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\right),

(129)

where
{\bm{A}}^{H}
denotes the Hermitian adjoint (conjugate transpose) of
{\bm{A}}
. In our experiments, for all methods, we sample a subset of coils
\xi_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,K\}
with
|\xi_{k}|=b
. Then, an unbiased mini-batch stochastic gradient estimate is given by

\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k})=-\frac{K}{b\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}}\sum_{i\in\xi_{k}}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{H}\left({\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{k\gamma}\right),

(130)

where
{\bm{y}}_{i}
denotes the measurements from the
i
-th coil, and

\xi_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,K\}
is a uniformly sampled subset of size
b
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}=S)=\frac{1}{\binom{K}{b}},\quad\forall S\subset\{1,\ldots,K\},\ |S|=b.

(131)

C.3
Sparse-angle CT Reconstruction

We study sparse-angle CT reconstruction, where the goal is to recover high-quality images from a restricted set of projection views. The limited angular coverage renders the problem ill-posed due to incomplete sampling. We simulate parallel-beam CT measurements using 360 and 180 projection views, each with 258 detector elements. The forward operator
{\bm{A}}
is implemented via
deepinv.physics.Tomography
from the
DeepInverse
package. Additional details regarding the acquisition geometry and the implementation of the measurement operator are provided in the
DeepInverse
documentation. To state the inverse problem formally, let
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
denote the unknown CT image and let
{\bm{R}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(KM)\times N}
denote the full discrete Radon (parallel-beam) projection operator over
M
projection views and
K
detector elements per view. We assume
{\bm{R}}
is stacked by views

{\bm{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{R}}_{1}\\
{\bm{R}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{R}}_{M}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{R}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K\times N}.

(132)

Thus the full sinogram is
{\bm{R}}{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{KM}
. To define the sparse-angle sampling operator, let
\xi_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,M\}
denote the index set of measured views at the
k
-th iteration, where
|\xi_{k}|=b\ll M
, and
b
denotes the batch size. The measured vies are selected according to a uniform distribution given as

{\mathbb{P}}(\xi_{k}=S)=\frac{1}{\binom{M}{b}}\quad\text{for every }\quad S\subset\{1,\ldots,M\},|S|=b,

(133)

and the view-selection matrix
P_{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{b\times M}
is defined by

({\bm{P}}_{\xi_{k}})_{i,m}=\begin{cases}1,&m=\omega_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\text{where }\xi_{k}=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{b}\}.

(134)

Then the sampling operator for projection views is

{\bm{S}}_{\xi_{k}}\coloneqq{\bm{P}}_{\xi_{k}}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{K}\in\{0,1\}^{(Kb)\times(KM)},

(135)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product and
I_{k}
is the
K\times K
identity matrix. Then the sparse-angle measurement operator is

{\bm{A}}\coloneqq{\bm{S}}_{\xi_{k}}{\bm{R}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(Kb)\times d}.

(136)

We consider the linear inverse problem in (
4
) with AWGN
{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}I)
. Thus the likelihood is given as
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}I)
and the likelihood score at step
k
is given as

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}}{\bm{A}}^{T}({\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}).

(137)

In our experiments, we use mini-batch stochastic gradient estimates so we consider the following decompositions of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{y}}
:

{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{A}}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{y}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{y}}_{M}\end{bmatrix},

(138)

where
{\bm{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K\times d}
and
{\bm{y}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
. Then the likelihood score becomes a sum

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{T}({\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{x}}).

(139)

Hence, given a mini-batch views
\xi_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,M\}
with
|\xi_{k}|=b
, sampled uniformly at random, we obtain an unbiased stochastic gradient estiamte

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k\gamma},\xi_{k})=-\frac{M}{b\sigma_{{\bm{n}}}^{2}}\sum_{i\in\xi_{k}}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{T}({\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{x}}).

(140)

In our experiments, we use this formulation for mini-batch stochastic likelihood score estimates. Moreover, we adopt the same baselines as in the MRI experiments, except that zero-filled reconstruction is replaced by filtered back-projection (FBP) with a Ramp filter, which is more suitable for CT reconstruction. For all methods, we consider two batch sizes: 60 views out of 360 and 30 views out of 180 projection views, respectively. We use abdominal CT scans downscaled to
256\times 256
from the 2DeteCT dataset
[
28
]
for our training and test sets. The training set consists of 4,500 slices and is used to train: (i) the denoiser for RED and PnP-FISTA, (ii) the diffusion model for DPS and PnP-DM, and (iii) the SGM for SGLD, ML-VRLD, and SL-VRLD. All methods are evaluated on 100 slices and mean of 5 samples are reported for each posterior sampling algorithm. We use the same annealing schedule and parameters in MRI reconstruction experiments. For ML-VRLD and SL-VRLD, we use the configuration with
p=0
and optimize the step size
\gamma
and momentum parameter
\beta
using
Optuna
over 5 samples as a validation set. Specifically, for total number of projection views 360, we set the step sizes for SGLD, ML-VRLD, and SL-VRLD, to
\gamma=5.76\times 10^{-6}
,
\gamma=1.11\times 10^{-5}
, and
\gamma=1.35\times 10^{-5}

\beta=0.583
and
\beta=0.774

\gamma=5.16\times 10^{-6}

\gamma=2.56\times 10^{-5}

\gamma=1.7\times 10^{-5}

\beta=0.41

\beta=0.988
