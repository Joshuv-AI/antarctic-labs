Unintended Negative Impacts of Promotional Language in Patent Evaluation
Bingkun Zhao 1, Chenwei Zhang 2, Hao Peng 1,3∗

Abstract
Promotional language has been increasingly used to aid the communication of innovative ideas in science. Yet, less is known about its role in the context of technological innovation. Here, we use a validated and domain-diagnosed lexicon of 135 promotional words to study the association between promotional language and patent evaluation outcomes among 2.7 million USPTO patent applications. Our large-scale study reveals three unexpected findings. First, in contrast to scientific evaluation, we find that a higher frequency of promotional words is negatively associated with the probability of an application being (i) granted a patent, (ii) transferred ownership, and (iii) successfully appealed. This promotional penalty holds even after accounting for a range of confounding factors and is largely robust across different technological areas. Among matched samples, the difference in the success rate between the lowest and highest promotional density quintile is 5.5, 5.9, and 5.3 percentage points for patentability, transferability, and rejection reversal. Second, contrary to institutional skepticism, we show that promotional language is not a mask of weak technology, but objectively reflects the degree of combinatorial novelty and future citation impact. Third, digging into the mechanisms, we find that the tolerance to promotional framing is strongly moderated by human factors, with men and experienced examiners showing a higher acceptance of promotional narratives than women and novice examiners. By revealing an emerging paradox in the patent system, our study offers theoretical and practical implications for improving patent evaluation through more objective scrutiny of linguistic patterns in patent filings.

In the United States alone, the patent office processes hundreds of thousands of applications annually, with examiners at the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) serving as critical gatekeepers who must determine whether an invention meets statutory requirements of novelty, utility, and enablement

Successful patents not only validate individual creativity but also shape firm strategy, industry competition, and the broader innovation ecosystem

However, the patent examination process hinges almost entirely on linguistic communication: examiners evaluate novelty and market potential primarily through written claims, specifications, and drawings submitted by applicants

11

.
The clarity, precision, and persuasive framing of these documents directly influence whether truly novel ideas are recognized as such or dismissed as obvious in light of prior art

.
This dependence on language raises a fundamental question in technological innovation management: How can inventors effectively communicate the novelty of their ideas, and what linguistic features influence evaluators’ recognition of originality?

Novel ideas are inherently uncertain and difficult to convey
[
15
]
. They often recombine distant knowledge domains in ways that defy easy categorization
[
16
]
, making them vulnerable to under-recognition when description fails to highlight their distinctiveness amid cognitive overload and specialization among evaluators
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Linguistic choices, particularly those marketing language that emphasize originality, significance, and transformative potential, may be able to serve as powerful cognitive signals, directing attention to an idea’s merits and bridging the gap between technical substance and evaluative judgment
[
20
,
21
]
.
Computational linguistic analyses of scientific text have begun to quantify these dynamics, revealing that subtle intensified lexical patterns can profoundly affect the success of idea adoption even when underlying quality is held constant
[
22
]
.

One special class of marketing rhetoric is the use of promotional language, which encompasses value-laden adjectives, such as “groundbreaking,” “transformative,” and “unprecedented,” that amplify claims of originality and impact.
Recent work on scientific evaluation of research grants provides compelling evidence for the power of such enthusiastic linguistic signaling. In high-stakes grant evaluation contexts, where reviewers must rapidly assess aspirational proposals under resource constraints, promotional language has emerged as a robust predictor of funding success.
For instance, a prior study conducted a large-scale semantic analysis of full-text grant applications from three world-renowned funding agencies
[
20
]
.
They found that a higher percentage of promotional words was associated with up to a doubling in the probability of funding, net of other applicants and proposal characteristics.
Moreover, promotional language itself was linked with the proposal’s inherent novelty and the citation impact of resulting publications, suggesting that it functions not merely as rhetorical embellishment but as an effective vehicle for communicating scientific merits
[
20
]
.
Parallel trends have been found in published papers where promotional language correlates with impact metrics
[
23
,
22
]
.
These findings underscore a broader insight: in peer-review systems designed to identify and support frontier science, linguistic framing helps overcome the well-documented “novelty rejection” bias
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, whereby evaluators favor conventional ideas that are easier to assess and predict. Promotional language appears to help harmonize substance with presentation, making innovative ideas legible and compelling
[
20
]
.

The patent system, however, operates under a distinct evaluative logic. Unlike grant review, which rewards aspirational vision and potential societal and academic impact, patent examination is a legal-technical adjudication emphasizing objective prior-art comparisons, enablement, and claim definiteness
[
27
]
.
Examiners, typically trained engineers or scientists, are often instructed to apply standardized search protocols and legal precedents, with less room for subjective enthusiasm
[
28
,
29
]
.
Promotional language that successfully signals novelty in grant contexts may therefore produce unintended consequences in patent review: it could be interpreted as overstatement, complicating the examiner’s ability to map claims cleanly against prior art or raising concerns about enablement and clarity.
Despite rich literature on patent evaluation that has focused on patent quality (e.g., citation-based measures of impact
[
30
,
31
]
), examiner heterogeneity
[
7
,
8
,
29
]
, and linguistic features of disclosure (such as readability and university vs. corporate patent text
[
14
]
), empirical research has largely overlooked how promotional language specifically influences patent outcomes.

Our study fills this critical gap. Leveraging computational linguistics on a large corpus of nearly 3 million patent applications from USPTO, we examine the prevalence and effects of promotional language in different patent outcomes.
Consistent with its role in signaling novelty elsewhere in science, we find that promotional language is positively associated with objective measures of an application’s underlying novelty (i.e., recombination-based metrics and post-grant citation patterns).
Yet it is robustly linked to higher rejection rates by examiners and the market, creating unintended negative consequences for the very inventors whose ideas are more innovative.
These results illuminate a previously unrecognized tension in the patent system: linguistic tools that aid recognition of novelty in one evaluative arena can undermine it in another innovation context.
By highlighting how linguistic communication shapes the fate of novel inventions in the patent office, our findings offer direct implications for innovation policy, inventor strategy, and the design of fair institutions to facilitate technological progress.

Data and Design

Our empirical analysis utilizes the full sample of utility patent applications filed with the USPTO between 2010 and 2023.
We construct a comprehensive longitudinal dataset consisting of 2,748,927 unique applications by integrating detailed full-text specifications, different administrative examination records, and rich bibliographic metadata of inventors (see Methods for data integration details).
Uniquely, unlike previous studies that often relied solely on granted patents
[
32
,
1
]
, our data include both granted and rejected applications across all major technology fields.
This inclusion allows us to mitigate the common selection bias seen in prior research
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
and to analyze the complete patent evaluation life-cycle from initial filing to final disposition.
We primarily leverage fixed-effects regression models to examine how promotional framing influences evaluation outcomes and test the intended nature of its empirical use.

We quantify the percentage of promotional words in the full-text of each patent application using a validated dictionary of 135 adjectives refined for the patent domain
[
36
,
20
]
(see details in Methods for lexicon construction and validation).
A central challenge in our identification strategy is to disentangle the influence of promotional presentation from the invention’s technical substance.
We address this confounding issue by constructing a comprehensive set of control variables related to the patent content novelty, linguistic features, and past experience and gender of inventors and attorneys, as well as important fixed-effects variables that can capture unobserved macro-level factors that can influence the evaluation process (see details in Methods).

We focus on three primary patent outcomes: (i)
patentability
, the likelihood of an application being granted a patent, (ii)
ownership transfer
, the probability of an application being re-assigned to a new owner, which servers as a signal for its perceived market value, and (iii)
appeal success
, the chance that an initially rejected application being eventually approved through panel deliberation.
For models predicting
patentability
, we additionally account for examiner heterogeneity by adding controls for examiner gender and their prior review experience.
Examiner characteristics are excluded in models that predict ownership transfer and appeal success because these two processes do not involve examiner adjudication.
For robustness checks, we implement a causal inference analysis using Propensity Score Matching (PSM).
We also explore the heterogeneous effects of promotional language on patent success across eight technological sections and different examiner characteristics including gender and past review experience.

To test whether promotional rhetoric reflects technical quality or undeserving hype, we use statistical models to examine the association between the frequency of promotional words and two objective measures of patent quality: (i) novelty score, the degree to which an application combines existing technological codes in unfamiliar ways, and (ii) citation velocity, the speed at which granted patents accumulate forward citations in a fixed time window.
Our observational design enables us to assess the impact of promotional language on patent evaluation, explore the underlying mechanisms driving its use, and identify the human factors that moderate its effects.

To answer this question, we leverage an interaction design with our fullest model to estimate the heterogeneity in the relationship between promotional words and evaluation outcomes across eight technological domains, i.e., the eight sections defined in the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) system. The model still controls for all confounding variables.
Due to an insufficient sample size in the appeal dataset (N = 21,924) that prevents an accurate estimation of variational effects, we focus this analysis on the other two major outcomes (N = 2,748,927), including patentability and transferability.

Why do examiners penalize promotional language?
A natural intuition is that applicants use promotional words out of exaggeration to mask technical defects or weaknesses. If this mechanism is operative behind its empirical usage, patents filed with a higher density of promotional language would be linked with lower quality.
To examine whether promotional language reflects the intrinsic level of patent value, we leverage fixed-effects OLS regressions to quantify the association between the percentage of promotional words and two commonly used objective measures of quality: (i) combinatorial novelty score, the degree to which an application combines existing CPC codes in unconventional way

[

The two complementary quality measures reveal that patents with more promotional intensity also exhibit a higher degree of intrinsic novelty and citation impact. This result provides little evidence of exaggeration in our empirical data, suggesting that inventors mostly use these enthusiastic words out of necessity to truthfully describe the genuine quality of their advocated technologies.
Together, our findings expose a fundamental contradiction in patent evaluation: examiners interpret promotional framing as noise or even deception; yet, from the perspective of inventors, this class of language functions as a genuine marker of high-value innovation.
Based on this result, the current examination practice may have inadvertently screened out the most promising inventions.

Discussion

Patent evaluation is inherently a linguistic negotiation process
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
.
While the technical content of an invention is often viewed as the core ingredient, the way it is framed can substantially shape its perceived value within the legal framework of intellectual property
[
14
,
52
]
.
Yet, we have a limited understanding of the linguistic strategies inventors use to convey the merits of their breakthroughs to patent examiners
[
53
]
.
Motivated by recent literature on the rise and the power of scientific promotional language
[
36
,
20
]
, our study examines how communicating a patent’s value with a promotional tone affects the evaluation of new ideas in patent inventions.

Our analysis of 2.75 million USPTO utility patent applications filed between 2010 and 2023 uncovers a fundamental paradox in patent evaluation.
Contrary to patterns documented in scientific evaluation–where promotional language reliably aids the funding and recognition of novel ideas–we find that higher frequencies of promotional words (drawn from a validated lexicon of 135 terms such as “groundbreaking,” “transformative,” and “unprecedented”) are systematically associated with adverse evaluation outcomes.
Applications with greater promotional density face lower probabilities of being granted a patent, of having their rights transferred in secondary markets, and of winning a reversal on appeal to initial rejections.
This promotional penalty holds even after accounting for important confounds and is robust across diverse technology sectors, indicating a widespread aversion to subjective descriptors within the patent examination system
[
54
,
55
]
.

These penalties are economically substantial: in matched samples that are balanced on patent content, novelty score, inventor characteristics, and technology-year fixed effects, moving from the lowest to the highest quintile of promotional words reduces success rates by 5.5 percentage points for grant probability, 5.9 points for market transferability, and 5.3 points for appeal reversal, suggesting that the linguistic choices made by applicants could inadvertently change the outcome of the patent prosecution process.
We also find that, while the promotional penalty is dominant across traditional technical fields, the disadvantage is somewhat attenuated in domains characterized by rapid technological change and high recombinatorial uncertainty.
Future research could dig deeper into the mechanisms that can explain the differential effects across technical fields.

At the same time, we find that promotional language is not merely rhetorical inflation of weak inventions–it positively and significantly aligns with the objective measure of combinatorial novelty and predicts higher future citation impact.
This result reveals a critical misalignment in patent evaluation: the very linguistic markers inventors used to signal the breakthrough nature of their work are recoded by the system as noise or exaggeration
[
52
]
, suggesting a natural tension between an inventor’s enthusiasm for a discovery and the examiner’s duty to scrutinize hype to maintain a high bar for legal protection
[
56
]
.
Institutions should consider policy interventions to address the systematic disconnect in the communication and selection process.

Finally, our examiner-level heterogeneity analysis reveals important boundary conditions: male examiners and those with greater experience display measurably higher tolerance for promotional framing.
First, the gender gap in promotional acceptance is consistent with prior literature on gender differences in science communication, where women tend to engage less with social media self-promotion
[
45
]
and often adopt a modest writing style than men
[
23
,
21
]
.
Second, we find that the promotional penalty is disproportionately concentrated among novice examiners.
In the high-volume, time-constrained environment of patent examination
[
8
]
, less experienced examiners often lack the tacit knowledge needed to efficiently assess intrinsic technical merit
[
57
]
.
Consequently, they may rely on a skeptical default cognitive model, treating emphatic descriptors as red flags that trigger closer scrutiny or doubt.
By contrast, experienced examiners exhibit a significantly attenuated response to promotional rhetoric, suggesting that they are better equipped to unbox the linguistic packaging and evaluate the underlying technical architecture.
This distinction highlights that the barrier to technological innovation stems not only from the quality of the idea but also from the cognitive shortcuts necessitated by the selective examination process
[
58
]
.

Our study offers theoretical implications for research on innovation.
The main finding challenges the assumed universality of linguistic signaling in scientific innovation.
While prior research suggests that promotional rhetoric generally facilitates the recognition and adoption of innovative ideas in scientific evaluations
[
20
,
22
,
59
]
, our work identifies a critical deviation in the patent system.
In this adversarial and legalistic environment, the same rhetorical markers that signal quality in open science are often interpreted as institutional noise, triggering a promotional penalty.
This divergence suggests that the efficacy of communication strategies is fundamentally context dependent, shifting from a promotional reward model in collaborative science to a promotional penalty model in competitive legal evaluation
[
60
]
.
Our finding thus raises broader questions about how we evaluate new ideas across different innovation contexts.

Our findings also provide practical implications for three key stakeholders.
First, for inventors and patent attorneys, our study provides a practical and somewhat counter-intuitive guide that cautions against over-reliance on novelty-signaling rhetoric.
While it is natural for inventors to describe their work as revolutionary, our study shows that such language actually makes rejection more likely.
Therefore, applicants, particularly those with genuinely disruptive ideas, may inadvertently disadvantage themselves by using language that examiners interpret as overstatement rather than clarification.
Simple revisions toward more neutral and functionally precise framing, while still highlighting genuine advances by prioritizing hard data and clear evidence over descriptive adjectives, could materially improve grant rates without sacrificing the ability to communicate value.
Navigating this double life–presenting the excitement of a discovery to investors while maintaining a neutral tone for the patent office–has become a necessary skill for modern innovators.

Second, for the USPTO and patent offices worldwide, the evidence highlights an institutional blind spot that can be addressed through procedural adjustments and targeted examiner training, particularly for novices who may default to formalistic skepticism as a cognitive shortcut
[
56
,
61
]
.
Brief education on linguistic cognition could reduce unintended bias, while AI-assisted flagging of promotional density might prompt examiners to scrutinize technical substance more carefully rather than dismissively.
Such interventions would be low-cost and could be piloted within specific art units where promotional framing is most disadvantaged.
By sensitizing examiners to the distinction between empty hype and the genuine enthusiasm accompanying pioneering discovery, patent offices can reduce the linguistic tax imposed on highly novel inventions.

Third, for innovation policymakers, our results reveal a subtle but systemic friction in the patent system regarding its ability to spotlight frontier
technologies.
By penalizing the very linguistic tools that help signal high-novelty inventions, the current system may inadvertently slow knowledge diffusion and reduce incentives for radical innovation.
Modest updates to USPTO evaluation guidelines, such as explicitly acknowledging the dual role of promotional language, could help align evaluative practice with the system’s core goal of promoting technological progress.
Ultimately, such measures would reorient the examination toward intrinsic quality, preventing the wrongful rejection of breakthrough technologies based solely on their rhetorical presentation
[
62
]
.

Our study is not without limitations.
First, although our dataset is comprehensive for the United States, the findings might be jurisdiction-specific. Patent examination cultures, legal standards, and examiner workloads may differ across world regions
[
63
]
.
Thus, cross-national replication would test the generalizability.
Second, our approach relies on a computational lexicon that, while validated and transparent, necessarily abstracts from context, tone, and sub-phrasing.
Human-coded or LLM-augmented dictionary could capture subtler rhetorical strategies.
Third, despite extensive controls and matching, our observational design cannot fully rule out residual confounding. Randomized controlled trials, ethically feasible via examiner training interventions or auditing of anonymized application variants, would strengthen causal claims.
Finally, we do not distinguish different types of patent transfers, such as intra-firm transfer and external acquisitions, which may signal different market values. The appeal subset may not be comprehensive, as not every rejected application will go through the appeal process.
Similarly, we focus on short- to medium-term evaluation outcomes. It remains an open question to study the impact on longer-run consequences for cumulative innovation, firm strategy, or commercialization in the market.

Despite these limitations, our study has several methodological strengths.
First, our data include both rejected and granted samples and cover virtually the entire population of recent USPTO utility applications, providing depth of analysis and addressing selection bias in prior studies
[
64
,
1
,
32
]
.
Second, we employ a pre-validated, domain-diagnosed promotional lexicon to test direct linkages between linguistic feature and high-stakes outcomes including granting status, market transaction, and appeal success, while controlling for a wide range of confounding variables such as objective novelty, linguistic features, and fixed-effects factors.
Third, by documenting both the penalty and the underlying informational value of promotional language, our study identifies a genuine institutional mismatch rather than a plausible correlation between empty hype and low quality.
Finally, our findings are stress tested through extensive robustness checks including propensity score matching, field variations, and alternative quality metrics (novelty and citation), as well as heterogeneous effects across examiner demographics and human capital.

Future work can fruitfully extend this research agenda in several directions. Comparative studies across patent systems would reveal whether promotional penalties are universal or institutionally contingent.
Experimental designs that manipulate promotional language while holding technical content constant (e.g., through USPTO pilot programs or synthetic application corpora) could isolate inventor and examiner confounds more cleanly.
Integrating our linguistic lens with emerging AI-assisted examination tools also raises a timely question: will machine reviewers inherit the same bias, or might they neutralize them?
Finally, broadening the scope to other arenas such as venture-capital pitch decks would clarify whether the novelty-signaling paradox is unique to patents or reflects a deeper tension in how different institutions recognize disruptive ideas.

Collectively, our study demonstrates that linguistic communication is not peripheral but central to the recognition of novelty. The patent system, like peer review in science, depends on how inventors frame their contributions; yet it currently responds to that framing in ways that can undermine its own objectives.
By surfacing this paradox and its moderators, our present research opens a new avenue for evidence-based reforms that can make patent evaluation more objective and effective at surfacing the very technologies that will shape tomorrow’s economy.

Methods

0.5
Ethics Statement

Our study is reviewed and approved by the Research Office of the City University of Hong Kong (HU-STA-00001180) and is fully compliant with all pertinent ethical regulations regarding the use of public records in the USPTO patent database.
No sensitive data besides names are analyzed or exposed.
The name-based gender inference is conducted using aggregated probabilistic models.
The informed consent requirement is determined to be waived.

0.6
Data Sources and Sample Construction

Our patent data come from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).
We accessed the full database of pre-grant publications from the PatentsView website in March 2024. The data contain rich metadata for each patent application, including the application year, technology codes, company affiliation, business entity type, disambiguated inventors, attorneys, examiners, final application outcome, as well as detailed textual information such as claims and full-text descriptions.
We focused on utility patents by excluding design and plant patents.
We supplemented the patent application data with the patent assignment dataset
[
65
]
, which allows us to assess if an application had been transferred ownership.

Our final analytical sample consists of 2,748,927 utility patent applications between 2010 and 2023. These data allow us to examine the impact of linguistic variables on two key outcome variables, including patent granting and transfer status.
To enrich our analysis of the role of promotional language in the whole process of patent evaluation, we obtained two additional datasets: (i) the subset of 1,151,644 patent applications granted between 2010 and 2020 (also from PatentsView). This data sample is right censored to ensure enough time window for measuring each patent’s 3-year forward citations (robustness check using a 5-year citation window is shown in
Supplementary Table S7
). (ii) a dataset of 21,924 rejected applications that entered the appeal process, directly downloaded from the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB) database. We coded the binary appeal decision for each application based on its final legal verdict.

0.7
Regression Models

First, we adopted fixed-effects logistic regression models to examine the statistical relationship between the frequency of promotional words and three patent evaluation outcomes, including (i) Patentability (coded as 1 if a patent application is granted and 0 otherwise), (ii) Ownership Transfer (coded as 1 if a patent application’s copyright is reassigned to another party and 0 otherwise), and (iii) Appeal Success (coded as 1 if the initial rejection is reversed and 0 otherwise).
Second, to examine whether the use of promotional language matches the quality of patents, we used fixed-effects OLS regressions to test the statistical relationship between the density of promotional words and two proxies of patent quality, including (i) Novelty Score Percentile and (ii) Citation Count Percentile. See details of definition and measurement below.

All regression models include controls for application content, linguistic features, and variables related to inventors and attorneys defined below.
Note that, when studying the three binary outcome variables listed above, the Novelty Score Percentile is included as a content-related control.
When predicting patentability, we also added variables related to examiner-specific characteristics including demographics and experience (defined below), which were omitted for all other outcome variables because their decision-making process such as the patent application assignment is a market-driven transaction that operates independently of examiner discretion.

0.8
Key Variables and Measurement

Our dependent and independent variables are defined below.

Patent Application Outcomes.

The first outcome variable of patent application success, which we call “patentability,” measures whether an application was granted or rejected. The average approval rate of patent applications in our data is 68.5%, which varies by year and technology areas.
The second outcome variable is ownership transfer (a.k.a., patent assignment). Unlike patentability, patent assignment could happen before patent approval. We therefore used it as a separate dependent variable.
Our third outcome variable is the appeal success.
For the subset of rejected applications that entered the appeal process, the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB) would convene a panel of administrative patent judges to adjudicate whether the original rejection should be affirmed or reversed.
Note that the latter two outcomes are not influenced by examiners, which justifies the exclusion of examiner-specific controls in their respective regression models.

Novelty Score and Citation Count.

We used two computational measures to estimate the intrinsic quality of patent applications.
The two measures allow us to robustly examine the statistical alignment between the frequency of promotional language and the degree of patent quality.

First, we measured an invention’s degree of innovativeness using a combinatorial novelty framework proposed in a prior work
[
43
]
, which is one important aspect of patent quality.
This framework operates at the level of Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) codes maintained by USPTO.
It considers each invention as a combination of technological CPC codes.
Each CPC code signals a specific technological component that a patent involved.
The algorithm then computes the atypicality score for each pair of CPC codes by comparing its co-occurrence frequency empirically observed in past patents to the expected frequency based on random chance.
The framework produces a series of atypicality scores for each patent based on all pairwise CPC codes used in the application.
Following the standard practice
[
66
]
, we used the 10th percentile tail novelty score as the final score for each patent application.
Based on our operationalization, patents with many unusual technological combinations are considered novel and those with many common code combinations are considered conventional.
We reverse coded the tail novelty scores so that a larger value means a higher degree of novelty.
To reduce the impact of noise, we converted the raw scores into percentile ranks, i.e., the
novelty score percentile
.
Note that, when studying the three outcome variables listed in the previous section, we included the novelty score percentile as a content control.

Second, we used the “citation velocity” as another aspect of patent quality
[
32
,
31
]
. It quantifies the rate at which other patents cite a focal patent
[
67
]
.
For each patent, we counted its number of forward citations by future patents in a 3-year time window and converted citation counts into percentile ranks, i.e., the
citation count percentile
. Robustness test using the 5-year citation count is provided in
Supplementary Table S10
.
While the novelty score is computed for all applications, the citation count is only applicable to 1,151,644 granted patents because only granted ones could be cited and have detailed citation data in the database.
The two measures collectively provide a robust picture of a patent’s market potential, allowing us to quantify the extent to which the density of promotional words matches a patent’s objective quality.

Density of Promotional Words and Lexicon Validation.

Our key independent variable is the percentage of promotional words used in the detailed application description.
We adopted a lexicon of 139 promotional words originally developed to study the impact of promotional language on scientific funding success
[
36
,
20
,
21
]
.
However, what is considered as promotional tone in science might not be the same in the context of patent evaluation.
We therefore validated the original lexicon based on standard procedures in the linguistic literature
[
68
]
.
Specifically, we measured the percentage of each word’s occurrence and assessed the internal consistency of the lexicon using the standardized Cronbach’s Alpha
[
69
]
, computed across the full sample of applications in our data.

The initial 139-word list yields a low reliability score (
\alpha=0.18
), indicating the presence of noise.
To refine the lexicon, we employed an iterative pruning process to systematically identify and exclude inconsistent words.
In each step, we calculated the marginal contribution of each word to the scale’s reliability and removed the single term whose exclusion yielded the largest improvement in
\alpha
.
The whole process was stopped until the increment was less than 0.02 in
\alpha
.
Through this data-driven procedure, we identified four high-frequency words—
first
,
key
,
top
, and
quality
—that were statistically inconsistent with the rest of words in the initial lexicon.
Manual review shows that these words are often used to describe the structure or order of elements in patents (e.g., “first embodiment,” “private key,” “top layer,” “image quality”) instead of being used to provide rhetorical emphasis.
Removing these four words increases the Cronbach’s Alpha in our data from 0.18 to 0.60, which is considered as acceptable in the psychological linguistic literature
[
70
]
.
Further removal offers little improvement in reliability and could risk excluding words that are often used in a promotional tone.

0.9
Linguistic and Content Controls

We used standard NLP methods and open-source packages to compute and quantify the following linguistic and content-related variables for each application:

(i)
Readability Score:
To assess the cognitive effort required to comprehend the technology specification, we computed the Flesch Reading Ease score
[
71
]
based on the full text of the detailed description.
We used the Python package
readability
to calculate this metric, which evaluates the textual complexity based on the average sentence length and the average number of syllables per word.
A higher readability score indicates that the text is syntactically simpler and easier to read, whereas a lower score implies a greater linguistic complexity.
(ii)
Concreteness Score:
We measured the degree to which the patent text refers to tangible objects versus abstract concepts using the standard R package
doc2concrete

[
72
]
.
This algorithmic approach assigns a concreteness score to a document by analyzing the frequency of words that have been manually rated for their level of perceptibility.
A higher score indicates a writing style that is more grounded in physical reality, whereas a lower score reflects a more theoretical or abstract description.
(iii)
Application Length:
We quantified the application length as the total number of words in the detailed description text of an application.
To smooth out noise, we transformed the raw count into five equal-sized bins (quintiles) and treated it as a categorical fixed-effect variable.

(iv)
Number of CPC Codes:
We accounted for the technological breadth of each invention by counting its distinct number of CPC codes used in the application.
In our analysis, a higher count of CPC codes indicates a more interdisciplinary and complex application that spans multiple domains, which may systematically increase the examiner’s cognitive burden involved in the evaluation.
(v)
Novelty Score Percentile:
We quantified the technical uniqueness of each invention using a combinatorial novelty framework
[
43
]
(See details of its computation in the previous section).
This metric identifies atypical pairings of CPC codes within a single application.
Inventions that bridge technical components that rarely appear together than expected are classified as novel.
By controlling for novelty in predicting three outcome variables, we can isolate the role of rhetorical framing via promotional language from the invention’s intrinsic breakthrough potential.

0.10
Controls for Inventor, Attorney, and Examiner Experience

We included a comprehensive set of controls to account for the human capital and institutional dynamics involved in the patent evaluation process:
(i)
Number of Inventors:
We used the size of the inventor team as a proxy of the resource intensity and collaborative depth of the project.
(ii)
Number of Inventors’ Prior Applications
and
Num. of Inventors’ Prior Patents:
These log-transformed metrics capture the applicants’ past experience and familiarity with the filing process and their historical success in the patent system.
(iii)
Number of Attorneys’ Prior Applications:
We counted the volume of prior applications (log-transformed) handled by the legal representative of the focal application. This variable can function as a proxy of the attorney’s past experience and sophistication that may improve the success rate of patent approval.
(iv)
Number of Applications Reviewed by Examiner:
We counted the total number of prior applications reviewed by the examiner and stratified examiners into five quintiles based on their past records. This variable controls for different levels of examiner experience. Seasoned examiners may exercise a more holistic judgment, whereas novice examiners may rely more on rigid guidelines.

0.11
Fixed-Effects Controls

We included two broad categories of fixed-effects controls, which are designed to capture variations across time, technological subareas, organizational entities, and demographic groups.

Longitudinal and Domain Factors.

We incorporated fixed effects for
Application Year
(14 categories, 2010–2023) and technology areas based on their CPC codes.
Jointly managed by the European Patent Office (EPO) and the USPTO, the CPC system categorizes patent documents into nine primary sections (A–H and Y) based on their technical domains.
Section G is excluded in our analysis because only three such applications are observed in our dataset.
Each CPC Section (Section A to H) consists of many sub-groups called CPC Class (e.g., A01).
We controlled for fixed effects at the CPC Class level (124 categories in the patentability and ownership transfer samples, 110 in the appeal success sample, and 123 in the 3-year citation sample).
We also added fixed effects for
Applicant Entity Type
(2 categories: Individual, Organization) and
Business Entity Type
(3 categories: Micro, Small, Undiscounted) to account for unobserved heterogeneity across different types of inventors or applicants.

Demographic Controls.

We inferred the gender of inventors, attorneys, and examiners based on their first names using the
demographicx
library
[
73
]
.
Unlike dictionary-lookup methods, this algorithm utilizes a character-level LSTM model to classify names into one of three categories: Women, Man, or Unisex.
The “Unisex” gender label is mostly assigned to first names without clear gender signal, such as typical East Asian names.
For each name, we assigned the gender category with the highest predicted probability.
The original paper reports strong validation-set performance for the gender classifier, with F1 score of 0.96 for Man, 0.98 for Woman, and 0.89 for the Unisex category.
We coded inventors’ team gender using the majority gender class of the team.
In our data, inventor teams are 5.93% Woman-dominant, 84.18% Man-dominant, and 9.89% Gender-balanced.
For an application’s examiner and attorney, we coded gender based on the primary individual who was responsible for reviewing or representing the application. Note that the primary-person information is designated in the USPTO database.
For attorneys, 11.31% are Woman, 80.70% are Man, and 7.99% are Unisex. For examiners, 21.24% are Woman, 58.72% are Man, and 20.04% are Unisex.
We treated “Man” as the reference group in all regression models.

0.12
Robustness Check Using Propensity Score Matching

To mitigate potential selection bias, we employed Propensity Score Matching (PSM). Specifically, the PSM framework tests whether our regression results could be driven by an underlying imbalance in covariates, such as the tendency for lower-quality inventions or less experienced inventors to rely more heavily on promotional language to compensate for technical deficiencies.

We discretized our independent variable (percentage of promotional words) into five equal-sized quintiles and considered the top quintile group (Q5) as the treatment group. We then constructed a set of matched samples from each of the four lower quintile density groups (Q1–Q4).
We estimated the propensity score using a logistic regression that predicts the likelihood of being in the high promotional language group based on all covariates used in the regression model for the corresponding DV.
We matched control units to treated units using the 1-to-1 nearest neighbor matching without replacement.
Post-matching diagnostics indicate satisfactory balance, with standardized mean differences for individual covariates reduced to negligible levels (
Supplementary Table S10–S12
).
Using this balanced sample, we directly compared the average difference in outcome across two groups (for each lower quintile). The analysis confirms our main findings: even when comparing applications with nearly identical content and applicant profiles, those with the highest promotional intensity (Q5) are statistically less likely to be granted, transferred in ownership, and to succeed in appeals than their lower promotional language density counterparts.
