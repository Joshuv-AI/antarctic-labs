Predicting
Outcomes of Impingement-Based Mass Spectrometry
Imaging

John C. Sentmanat, Andrei G. Fedorov

Abstract

A physics-based scaling framework to predict the outcomes
of impingement-based
mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) techniques is presented to understand
the underlying mechanisms and guide performance improvements. Particular
emphasis is placed on analyte extraction, the fundamental process
that defines MSI resolution and the softness of ionization. Two distinct
regimes, thermodynamic (passive) and kinematic (active), are identified
that follow different physical laws. Thermodynamic desorption arises
under conditions approaching thermal equilibrium, where molecules
are extracted through favorable energetic interactions with the solvent
phase. In contrast, kinematic desorption occurs when high-energy particles
or droplets impact the sample, supplying mechanical energy to overcome
the cohesive forces that bind molecules at the surface. A dimensionless
parameter is developed that compares the stress generated by an impacting
particle to the cohesive forces binding molecules within a sample,
providing a criterion for identifying transitions among extraction
regimes. Through a scaling analysis of charged particle transport
and impact dynamics, this parameter is expressed in terms of experimentally
controllable inputs, revealing that kinematic extraction becomes possible
only when the accelerating electric force exceeds the combined effects
of the aerodynamic drag and particle inertia. Application of the methodology
to representative MSI techniques yields predictions consistent with
experimentally observed extraction behaviors, which supports the proposed
mechanistic interpretation. More broadly, this framework establishes
a semiquantitative foundation for predicting molecular extraction
across a wide range of solution- and impingement-based MSI platforms,
enabling systematic exploration of performance limits and guiding
the design of next-generation MSI technologies using fundamental experimentally
measured properties.

Introduction

There has been tremendous growth in mass
spectrometry imaging (MSI)
over the past several decades, driven by its increasing ability to
spatially resolve molecular distributions at the single-cell level
,
 while maintaining exceptional chemical specificity and sensitivity
when compared with other imaging modalities. Matrix-assisted laser
desorption/ionization (MALDI) has been the workhorse among MSI platforms,
but solution-based (e.g., desorption electrospray ionization (DESI),
nano-DESI, liquid microjunction-surface sampling probe (LMJ-SSP))
and particle impingement-based (e.g., secondary ion mass spectrometry
(SIMS), gas cluster ion beam (GCIB), and electrospray droplet impact
(EDI)) probing of cells and tissues have been growing over the past
decade. Unlike MALDI, which uses laser energy to thermally desorb/ablate
and ionize molecules, these techniques rely on interactions between
particles, droplets, or solvent systems and complex biological surfaces
to extract and ionize a broad range of analytes including lipids,
metabolites, peptides, and intact proteins. Despite their diversity,
all impingement-based MSI techniques must address a fundamental trade-off
among spatial resolution, biomolecular coverage, and ionization softness.
Consequently, there is a critical need for a physically motivated
scaling framework, grounded in the fundamental principles of thermodynamics,
fluid mechanics, and particle dynamics, that can relate controllable
operating parameters to the molecular extraction behavior. This work
presents a physics-based scaling framework for impingement-based MSI
techniques that identifies the dominant analyte extraction mechanism
and provides qualitative guidance regarding fragmentation propensity
and the trade-off between spatial resolution and extraction softness.

Broadly, these techniques can be categorized into two distinct
analyte extraction regimes: thermodynamic and kinematic. Thermodynamic
(passive) desorption describes molecule removal for a system in thermal
equilibrium. DESI is the prototypical
thermodynamic desorption MSI technique and operates through a two-step
extraction mechanism. Primary droplets
impact the sample to form a liquid film (A), allowing soluble surface molecules to
partition into the liquid phase, driven by a favorable chemical potential
gradient. Subsequent droplet impacts generate splashing events that
eject analyte-laden charged secondary droplets from the liquid film
on a sample surface for subsequent mass spectrometry detection. This ‘droplet-pickup’ removal mechanism
minimizes molecular fragmentation, making DESI particularly suitable
for the analysis of fragile biomolecules. However, the requirement
for a favorable chemical potential gradient necessitates careful spray
solvent selection to preferentially extract targeted analytes, which
can limit the desorption efficiency of less soluble molecular species. Furthermore, the presence of a liquid film often
constrains the achievable spatial resolution, as sufficient surface
wetting is required for effective molecule extraction.

In contrast, kinematic (active) extraction refers
to impact-driven
molecule removal by high-energy charged droplets or particles that
mechanically separate and eject molecules from the sample. EDI, GCIB,
and massive cluster impact (MCI) are representative kinematic desorption
techniques that have been implemented for SIMS. In these techniques,
large clusters or droplets impact the sample at supersonic velocities,
ejecting surface molecules (B). A shock wave induced within the sample upon impact
has been hypothesized to break intermolecular interactions within
the sample matrix, enabling molecules to be ejected.
,
 Owing to the rapid time scale of shock propagation, such impacts
can partially preserve intact molecules as large as small proteins
(<12 kDa). However, when impacting
particles become sufficiently small, as in monatomic or small cluster
SIMS ion sources, shock waves may no longer form due to environment
rarefication. Under these conditions, the impact energy is transferred
directly to the internal energy of the bound molecules, which may
promote molecular fragmentation through bond cleavage.

Numerous
experimental parameters influence the performance of the
impingement-based MSI techniques, and transitions between thermodynamic
and kinematic extraction regimes can substantially alter the types
of biomolecules that are successfully extracted and analyzed as well
as the degree of analyte fragmentation. The mechanistic framework
that we present seeks to provide physically meaningful guidance to
the design and optimization of MSI analyte extraction. Our methodology
is based on employing fundamental conservation laws to establish a
physics-based scaling framework that helps improve the performance
of existing impingement-based imaging methods and assists in the development
of next-generation platforms with minimum empiricism.

Analyte

cohesive forces binding molecules within their sample environment.
This framework can be applied to a wide range of charged particle–surface
interactions, and here, we demonstrate it for a common representative
system consisting of an impinging water droplet extracting a lipid
from a lipid monolayer.

For Π2 > 1, when drag is significant
relative
to inertia, kinematic extraction becomes possible only when the electric
force is greater than the drag force, corresponding to Π1 scaling with Π2 (Π1 = O(Π2)). In this drag dominant regime, the
domain for thermodynamically driven desorption expands to progressively
larger values of Π1 > 1 as Π2 increases.
Physically, as drag exerts greater control over particle momentum,
increasingly larger electric forces are required to achieve the impact
conditions necessary for kinematic analyte extraction. DESI
,−

 falls within this passive (thermodynamically driven) desorption
regime because it operates at atmospheric pressure, where drag strongly
influences the transport of comparatively large electrospray droplets
typical of the technique.

Although other solution-based MSI
approaches, such as nano-DESI
and LMJ-SSP, cannot be directly represented on the regime map since
they do not rely on impinging particles, they nevertheless operate
through thermodynamically driven desorption and therefore are subject
to the same limitations on the imaging resolution. The framework developed
here is intended primarily for impingement-based MSI techniques; consequently,
any extension to nano-DESI or LMJ-SSP should be interpreted as a qualitative
approximation rather than a rigorous mechanistic description. For
these systems, the regime transition parameter Πσ can still be used and estimated by replacing the particle velocity, v

p

from the volumetric flow rate per the liquid-surface contact area.
Using this approach, Πσ is on the order of
∼10–9 for nano-DESI and ∼10–11 for LMJ-SSP, both falling well within the thermodynamic desorption regime.
While these estimates should not be interpreted quantitatively, they
suggest that the underlying scaling concepts may provide qualitative
insight into the operating behavior of related liquid extraction-based
methods.

The magnitude of Πσ increases
as the electric
force comes to dominate particle transport (B). Monoatomic or small particle-based SIMS
−

 exhibits exceptionally large values of Πσ because inertial forces are negligible due to the particle mass,
while drag forces are effectively absent under vacuum conditions,
allowing charged particles to accelerate unimpeded prior to impact.
Consequently, these particles deliver a very high energy per impacting
molecule, which leads to intramolecular fragmentation and challenges
with analyzing labile compounds. Although Πσ does not directly predict the threshold for molecular fragmentation,
high values of this parameter are a predictive proxy of an increased
likelihood of bond cleavage and fragmentation during extraction. We
note, however, that ionization softness is governed not only by the
extraction process but also by factors such as gas-phase ionization
chemistry,
,
 molecular structure, internal energy redistribution, and local sample environment. Thus, a limitation of the present study is that these coupled processes
are not experimentally resolved, and Πσ should
be interpreted as providing qualitative trends of fragmentation propensity
rather than a quantitative predictor of ionization softness.

Future studies could aim for more directly distinguishing desorption
from subsequent ionization and fragmentation by independently measuring
material removal and mass spectrometric ion yield. For example, a
fluorescent probe with well-characterized mass spectrometric behavior,
such as rhodamine 6G,
,
 could be deposited on a surface
and imaged by fluorescence microscopy before and after extraction
to quantify spatially resolved material removal. The corresponding
ion yield and fragmentation pattern could then be measured simultaneously
with mass spectrometry. Correlating the amount of material removed
with the detected intact and fragment ion signals would help distinguish
the contributions of the desorption efficiency from subsequent ionization
and dissociation processes. Such measurements could provide a more
quantitative assessment of how extraction dynamics influence the observed
mass spectrum but are beyond the scope of this work.

Unique
within this regime map is BeamMap, a
recently developed multimodal MSI technique that bridges the aspects
of both the passive (DESI) and active (EDI, MCI, and SIMS) approaches
through a tunable vacuum electrospray ionization (vESI) source. The
design objective of BeamMap is to combine the soft ionization associated
with thermodynamically driven extraction with the high spatial resolution
characteristic of kinematically driven approaches. Current experimental
MSI demonstrations are limited to a raster spacing of 500 μm
and therefore do not yet demonstrate subcellular chemical imaging.
However, characterization of the vESI source has demonstrated beam
diameters below 500 nm and impinging liquid spots of a few micrometers,
indicating the potential for substantially finer extraction under
optimized operating conditions. Based on the currently available experimental
parameters, BeamMap occupies the transition region near Πσ∼1 on the regime map, where both thermodynamic
and kinematic extraction mechanisms are expected to contribute. This
operating regime provides a design pathway for pursuing higher spatial
resolution while maintaining the relatively soft extraction conditions
desirable for the analysis of larger biomolecules, such as proteins.

Conclusions

Addressing the trade-offs among spatial
resolution, chemical selectivity,
and ionization softness remains a fundamental challenge in the development
of impingement-based MSI techniques. Understanding the fundamental
physics underlying the biomolecule removal, whether governed by thermodynamic
(passive) or kinematic (active) analyte extraction, is essential for
predicting imaging outcomes and maximizing the molecular information
captured in complex biological samples. We present a mechanistic framework
that links experimentally controllable inputs to analyte extraction
behavior with no empirical fitting, providing a predictive tool for
understanding the current MSI capabilities and guiding the optimization
and design of next-generation MSI platforms.

Central to this
framework is the dimensionless regime transition

When Πσ ≥ 1, active kinematic extraction
becomes possible; otherwise, passive molecular desorption occurs through
thermodynamically driven processes. By expressing this parameter in
terms of experimentally controllable inputs using the scaling analysis
of charged particle transport, we show that kinematic extraction is
achieved only when the accelerating electric force exceeds the combined
effects of aerodynamic drag and particle inertia. Application of this
methodology to representative MSI techniques yields predictions that
are consistent with their experimentally observed behaviors, providing
validation of the proposed mechanistic analysis.

More broadly,
the proposed scaling framework establishes a semiquantitative
foundation for understanding and predicting the mode of analyte extraction
across a wide range of solution- and impingement-based MSI platforms.
By linking physical behavior to experimentally controllable parameters,
it enables systematic exploration of the performance limits of existing
imaging techniques while providing design principles for next-generation
MSI methods with a minimal empiricism. As MSI continues to advance
toward single-cell spatial resolution and comprehensive multiomic
analyses, the scaling frameworks derived from chemo-mechanical analyses
such as the one presented here will play an increasingly important
role in guiding instrument design, operating conditions, and analytical
performance.
