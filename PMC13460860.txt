FDA approval of hepcludex: a milestone in the treatment of chronic hepatitis delta virus infection
Authors: Esha Fatima, Malik Olatunde Oduoye
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The hepatitis delta virus (HDV) is the smallest virus, contingent on helper functions provided by hepatitis B virus (HBV) for replication and infection[1]. Dissemination can occur percutaneously as well as through mucosal contact with infectious blood, saliva, or semen[2]. The global prevalence of HDV is as high as 12–20 million people afflicted with chronic HDV infection worldwide. HDV represents the most severe form of viral hepatitis, characterized by accelerated liver disease progression, cirrhosis, and associated complications, such as end-stage-liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma[3].

HDV is a subviral satellite of HBV; it infects humans either concomitantly or after HBV infection. It replicates only in hepatocytes and involves a replication mechanism that uses host RNA polymerase. HDV genome replication and the formation of a nucleocapsid-like ribonucleoprotein (RNP) are independent of HBV, but it lacks the genetic ability to develop its own protective protein coat. Thus, the propagation mechanism – from coating the HDV RNP with the HBV envelope proteins for cell egress to the delivery of the HDV virions to the human hepatocyte target – is accomplished through the helper functions of HBV[4].

Common symptoms of HDV infection include fever, tiredness, loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting, stomach pain, dark urine, pale stools, joint pain, and yellowing of the skin or eyes. Chronic HDV infection, if left undiagnosed or untreated, can cause serious problems such as scarring of the liver or liver cancer[5].

Current treatment options

Up until now, Pegylated Interferon-alpha (PEG-IFN-alpha) has been considered a primary global treatment option. It forges an immune response to suppress HDV-RNA replication. However, poor sustenance of virological response and significant side effects (e.g., neuropsychiatric symptoms, flu-like symptoms, and cytopenias) are its major limitations, addressing the requirement for inhibitor drugs that block sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptors on hepatocytes, preventing infection of healthy cells[6].

Hepcludex: a promising new treatment for chronic HDV infection

Hepcludex is a synthetic N-acylated peptide derivative of the pre-S1 domain of HBV. Acting as a ligand, it binds with the HBV receptor NTCP on the hepatocyte surface, thus preventing HDV from entering and infecting healthy cells[7]. Initially, Hepcludex obtained conditional approval from the EMA in July 2020 to be administered subcutaneously. The recent development is its approval by the FDA on 22 May 2026 to fill a critical gap in care for chronic HDV-infected patients[8].

Clinical trials assessing hepcludex efficacy

The drug Hepcludex was approved after a phase 3 multi-center, randomized, open-label, parallel-arm trial. This trial assessed participants enrolled with immediate treatment of 8.5 mg Hepcludex daily for 144 weeks or participants enrolled with delayed treatment of 8.5 mg Hepcludex daily for 96 weeks under an observational period of 44 weeks. The results assessed combined response, i.e., major reduction in HDV RNA levels and normalization of ALT levels, suggesting improvement in liver inflammation. Nearly half of the patients receiving Hepcludex immediately showed a positive treatment response by week 48, while almost none in the delayed treatment group did. At week 48, 20% of patients on Hepcludex had undetectable virus levels and 0% in the delayed treatment group.

In the Hepcludex group, the proportion of patients with undetectable HDV RNA further increased to 36% at week 96 and 50% at week 144. This suggests that prolonged therapy may result in progressively better viral suppression[8].

Recommended dosage

The currently recommended regimen for Hepcludex consists of 8.5 mg lyophilized powder in a single-dose vial, reconstituted prior to SC injection and administered once daily. The treatment duration depends upon sustained virologic and biochemical response[9].

Recommendations

The approval of Hepcludex by the FDA, despite being a significant milestone in the treatment of chronic hepatitis D, remains limited by deficiencies in timely diagnosis, easy accessibility, deficits in ongoing research, and the lack of incorporation of evidence-based therapeutic approaches in clinical and public health settings. Regular screening should be encouraged among all individuals who test positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). There is a pressing need for proper emphasis on extensive studies through which the durability of viral suppression, effects on cirrhosis progression, chances of hepatocellular carcinoma, rates of liver transplantation, and overall survival rates can be assessed. To enhance understanding of HDV infection, its complications, and recently approved treatments among healthcare providers and vulnerable groups, various educational initiatives should be actively endorsed.

Conclusion

The FDA’s approval of Hepcludex marks a breakthrough in the management of chronic hepatitis D, which has been traditionally linked to restricted treatment choices. Nonetheless, as we move forward, synchronized initiatives in screening, research, public awareness, and fair drug availability will be essential to completely achieve the public health impact of this first approved treatment for HDV infection.
