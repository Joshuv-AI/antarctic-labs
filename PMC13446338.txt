Evaluating an alternative Pt-W alloy as a low-n EUV mask absorber: Film properties and imaging performance
Authors: Zhonghua Li, Haolong Tang, Xiaoyi Wang, Hongwei Tian, Yanchao Wang, Hanwen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe mask three-dimensional (M3D) effect poses a significant challenge to the advancement of extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, particularly in high-numerical-aperture systems. This effect critically degrades key imaging metrics—such as the normalized image log-slope (NILS), telecentricity error (TCE), and best-focus variation (BFV)—limiting the achievable resolution. The adoption of absorber materials with a low refractive index (low-n) offers a promising pathway to mitigate M3D effects. In this study, we investigate an alternative Pt-W alloy as a next-generation low-n EUV mask absorber. The surface morphology, roughness, and intrinsic stress of Pt-W films with varying compositions were systematically characterized. The films exhibited excellent stability, both thermally (at operational temperatures <200°C) and chemically (against standard mask-cleaning solutions), which is essential for prolonging mask lifetime. The incorporation of tungsten was found to enhance the dry-etching performance. The imaging performance, simulated using the optically characterized film properties, was compared against a reference TaBN absorber. Simulations reveal that the Pt-W alloy enables superior imaging performance at a reduced absorber thickness, effectively alleviating M3D effects compared to the TaBN benchmark.Keywords: EUV lithography, mask absorber, Pt-W alloy, thin-film properties, lithography imaging simulation

Introduction

The advancement of extreme ultraviolet lithography (EUVL) has been a key driver of progress in the semiconductor industry. State-of-the-art EUV lithography tools now enable the mass production of integrated circuits at technology nodes of 3 nm and beyond.1 Unlike its predecessor, deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography, EUVL employs a reflective optical system. This is necessitated by the strong absorption of EUV radiation (at a wavelength of 13.5 nm) by nearly all materials. The mask follows the same reflective principle. As illustrated in Figure 1A and a Mo/Si multilayer mirror acts as the reflector, with its layer thicknesses precisely controlled to constructively enhance reflectivity. The illumination is incident at a small, off-axis angle to facilitate the separation of incoming and reflected light within the optical system. The mask substrate is typically made of low-thermal-expansion glass to minimize pattern distortion during exposure. Atop this stack, a patterned absorber material with high EUV absorption defines the circuit layout and enhances image contrast. Currently, TaBN is an advanced absorber material used in EUV lithography systems operating at 0.33 NA, with a typical thickness of 60–70 nm.2

The mask three-dimensional (M3D) effect is a key factor degrading imaging fidelity in EUVL, arising from the combined influence of oblique EUV incidence, complex reflections within the multilayer, and the optical response of the absorber. To mitigate the exacerbated M3D effects at higher numerical apertures (NA), the selection of absorber materials has become a focal research area. As illustrated in Figure 1A, the shadowing effect in EUV masks suggests that thinner absorber layers are expected to alleviate these impacts. Current research identifies phase-shift materials with a high EUV extinction coefficient (high-k) and a low refractive index (low-n) as promising candidates, as they can effectively suppress M3D effects even at reduced thicknesses.2,3

For instance, Luong et al. developed a Ni-Al alloy possessing high-k characteristics, which allows for a thinner absorber while exhibiting superior imaging performance and a larger process window; however, its stability and etchability require further improvement.4 Similarly, Thakare et al. demonstrated that a high-k Ta-Co alloy enhances image contrast and reduces best-focus variation (BFV) compared to conventional Ta-based absorbers, albeit introducing challenges related to telecentricity error.5 In another study, Thakare et al. reported a Ru/Ta bilayer that achieves a balance between etchability and imaging performance, though the bilayer structure itself complicates mask inspection and repair.6 These examples underscore the critical trade-offs between material properties, imaging performance, and manufacturability that must be carefully evaluated.

In this work, platinum was selected as the base element due to its characteristically low refractive index under EUV irradiation and its potential for etchability in fluorine-based chemistries. As calculated and shown in Figure 1B, we evaluated the refractive index (n) and extinction coefficient (k) of various elements alloyed with platinum, Figure 1B was obtained from modeling based on the dielectric constants, with data sourced from Henke et al.7 Tungsten was chosen as the alloying element for the binary system primarily because it forms volatile reaction products under fluorine-based plasma etching conditions,8 which is expected to confer enhanced etch selectivity. Crucially, alloying with tungsten preserves the essential optical properties for EUV lithography—namely, a low n and a high k. Regarding the requisite thin-film properties for the mask absorber, deposition must occur at temperatures below 150 °C to protect the reflectivity of the underlying multilayer mirror.9 The film should exhibit an amorphous or nanocrystalline structure to minimize line-edge roughness,4 possess a surface roughness below 0.5 nm, maintain residual internal stress within ±300 MPa, and demonstrate stability under both operational thermal loads and cleaning solutions.10 In the context of defect inspection and repair, current EUV masks primarily rely on electron-beam-induced deposition and etching techniques for defect repair. For emerging mask absorber materials, it is essential to select appropriate precursor molecules during the repair process to ensure that the materials used for repair meet the required optical performance, while achieving precise mask repair through additive deposition and subtractive etching. Furthermore, no spontaneous reactions should occur in the repaired structures during subsequent mask processing steps. Finally, when using conventional DUV light for EUV mask defect inspection, the inherently low defect contrast poses a challenge that must be addressed through the development of bright-field inspection techniques.

We investigated Pt-W alloy films as a prospective alternative EUV absorber material. Their properties—including optical constants (n and k), crystal structure, residual stress, stability, and etch characteristics—were systematically characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and film stress measurement tools. The optical constants calculated from experimentally measured densities of the Pt-W films were subsequently employed in lithographic imaging simulations to benchmark their performance against a reference Ta-based absorber.

Results

Preparation

All thin-film samples were deposited on four inches diameter, double-side-polished Si substrates via physical vapor deposition (PVD). Pt-W alloy films with varying compositions were synthesized by magnetron co-sputtering. The composition of the alloys was controlled by adjusting the individual deposition rates of the Pt and W targets, yielding nominal atomic ratios of 1:0 (pure Pt), 2:1, 1:1, and 1:2 (Pt:W). The selection of the three compositions (2:1, 1:1, and 1:2) resulted from a balanced consideration of etching performance and optical properties. As shown in the Pt-W binary phase diagram in Figure 2, the positions corresponding to the Pt2W, PtW, and PtW2 phases are highlighted with red arrows. These arrow positions suggest that the three alloy compositions correspond to thermodynamically stable phases. The thickness of all deposited films was maintained at 40 nm.

Pt-W binary alloy phase diagram

The surface morphology and root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the Pt-W alloy films were characterized using a BRUKER dimension edge AFM. Measurements were performed over a 2 × 2 μm scan area with a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. The RMS roughness of each sample was determined by averaging measurements from three randomly selected regions on the film surface, and the data were processed using the NanoScope Analysis 3.00 software. The Si substrates exhibited a baseline RMS roughness of less than 0.2 nm. The crystal structure of the Pt-W films deposited on Si substrates was analyzed by grazing-incidence XRD using an X’Pert PRO diffractometer. Measurements were conducted in the 2θ range of 20°–65° with a step size of 0.02°, employing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154178 nm) generated at 40 kV and 40 mA and filtered with a Ni filter. Film thickness, density, and interfacial roughness were evaluated by X-ray reflectivity (XRR) at low incident angles (0–5°). The residual stress in the films was measured using a k-Space Associates thin-film stress mapping system. The system employs a single laser to generate a two-dimensional array of dots projected onto the sample surface. The reflected dot pattern is captured by a high-resolution charge-coupled device (CCD) detector, and the change in sample curvature is calculated from the shift in laser dot spacing. The curvature was mapped before and after film deposition over a 90 mm scan diameter with an edge exclusion of 7 mm and a measurement resolution of 2 mm. Etching characteristics were investigated using inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE). A systematic study of gas chemistry was performed using CF4/O2/Ar mixtures, where the ratio of CF4 to O2 was varied to identify the optimal etching recipe. In this study, the optical constants (n and k) calculated from experimentally measured densities of Pt-W alloy thin films with different compositions were used to evaluate their performance through lithographic imaging simulations. These results were benchmarked against a reference TaBN absorber. The complete set of simulation parameters is provided in Table 2. Simulations were performed using the open-source simulator K-Litho.11 The K-Litho is a lithography simulation toolset designed for source-mask optimization (SMO). It rigorously solves Maxwell’s equations using the waveguide method and computes the aerial image by performing a Fourier transform of the diffraction pattern in the exit pupil, thereby enabling accurate modeling of M3D effects. The simulations employed an optimized illumination source, specifically an inner half leaf dipole configuration. As shown in Figure 3, utilizing a dipole with either an inner half leaf or outer half leaf shape can mitigate image distortion at the focal plane. This distortion originates from focus splitting caused by the radial offset between the inner and outer poles of a conventional dipole source.12 Furthermore, compared to an outer half leaf source, the two poles of an inner half leaf source are positioned closer to the chief ray angle of the object (CRAO), thereby providing a lower angle of incidence on the mask. Since mask reflectivity is sensitive to the angle of incidence,13 a lower angle helps reduce the blocking of diffraction orders at the absorber edge, consequently diminishing the shadowing effect inherent to the mask.

Thin-film characterization

Microstructure

Figure 4 shows the XRD patterns of Pt-W alloy films with different compositions deposited on single-crystal silicon substrates. For the pure Pt film, two distinct peaks corresponding to the Pt(111) and Pt(200) planes are observed, consistent with the face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure of platinum. In the Pt2W alloy, a peak similar to the Pt(111) reflection is present, but it is shifted slightly to lower angles compared with that of pure Pt, accompanied by a reduction in peak intensity. This shift and broadening suggest lattice expansion and a decrease in crystallite size due to the incorporation of tungsten. For the PtW alloy, a (111)-type peak is also detected, exhibiting a more pronounced shift toward lower angles. In contrast, the PtW2 sample shows no clear diffraction peaks, indicating the formation of a nearly amorphous phase. The average crystallite size was estimated using the Debye-Scherrer formula:14

where Dhkl is the crystallite size, k1 is the Scherrer constant (taken as 0.89), λ is the X-ray wavelength (0.154 nm), β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) in radians, and θ is the Bragg angle. The average crystallite size was estimated from the FWHM of the strongest (111) diffraction peak. The calculated sizes are approximately 7.58 nm for Pt2W and 3.72 nm for PtW. These results indicate that all three Pt-W alloy films exhibit nanocrystalline or amorphous structures, and the crystallite size shows a decreasing trend with increasing W content.

Figure 5 presents the two-dimensional and three-dimensional AFM images of three different proportions Pt-W alloy films with a thickness of 40 nm. The RMS roughness of each film was obtained by averaging measurements from three randomly selected areas on the AFM images, providing a representative value of the overall surface roughness. The surface roughness exhibits a clear composition dependence. The PtW2 film shows the lowest RMS roughness of 0.139 nm. As the Pt content increases, the surface roughness gradually rises, with Pt2W exhibiting the highest value of 0.225 nm. This trend correlates well with the XRD results: higher W content leads to smaller crystallites or an amorphous phase, which in turn yields a smoother surface.

All three alloy films demonstrate that composition directly influences both microstructure and surface topography. Importantly, the observed nanocrystalline/amorphous structures and the low surface roughness (≤0.225 nm) meet the structural and topographic requirements for EUV mask absorber applications.

The residual stress in the thin films was calculated from the measured radius of curvature using Stoney’s formula:15

where Ra and Rb are the radii of curvature of the substrate before and after film deposition, respectively. Es (181.8 GPa) and νs (0.28) denote the Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the silicon substrate. ts (0.52 mm) and tf (40 nm) are the thicknesses of the substrate and the film. This equation is valid under isotropic stress distribution and the condition tf≪ts. In this formulation, the sign convention for σ is positive for tensile stress and negative for compressive stress.

Figure 6 displays the averaged residual stress distribution of the Pt-W alloy films deposited on four inches diameter, 0.52 mm thick silicon substrates as a function of composition. As shown, the PtW2 sample exhibits compressive stress, whereas the PtW and Pt2W films show tensile stress.

Residual stress distribution maps of the 40 nm-thick PtW2, PtW, and Pt2W films (from left to right)

The color scale represents the residual stress in GPa. The displayed values are mean stress over the scanned area.

These observations can be interpreted using the stress-kinetics model for sputter-deposited alloy films proposed by Tong Su et al.16 According to this model, the total residual stress in a film arises from three main contributions: (1) atress from athermal growth mechanisms, which includes tensile stress generated during island coalescence due to interface energy reduction, and compressive stress arising from the diffusion of surface ad-atoms into grain boundaries. (2) Additional tensile stress induced by grain growth. (3) Compressive stress produced when energetic particles impinge on the film surface, which promotes film densification, creates point defects, or induces other microstructural modifications.

As shown in Figure 6, the sign and magnitude of the residual stress vary systematically with the film composition. The PtW2 sample, with the highest W content, exhibits a compressive stress of 96 MPa. In contrast, the PtW and Pt2W films display tensile stresses of 92 MPa and 298 MPa, respectively. This compositional dependence can be rationalized within the stress-kinetics framework.16 First, the athermal growth stress is expected to be predominantly tensile due to the low atomic mobility of heavy Pt and W atoms, which favors island coalescence over surface diffusion.17 Second, grain-growth-induced tensile stress is likely minimal because all three alloys are nanocrystalline or amorphous. Third, energetic particle bombardment during magnetron sputtering contributes significant compressive stress. For PtW2, the higher W content (with its higher melting point) leads to more pronounced compressive stress from particle impact,17 which outweighs the tensile growth component and results in a net compressive state. As the W content decreases in PtW and Pt2W, the bombardment-induced compressive contribution diminishes, allowing the tensile growth stress to dominate and produce a net tensile film stress. Despite these variations in magnitude and sign, all 40 nm Pt-W alloy films meet the stringent residual-stress requirement (|σ| < 300 MPa) for EUV mask absorber applications.

Stability test

The thermal stability of the samples was characterized by XRD. Specimens were heated in a tube furnace from room temperature to 200 °C, held for 20 min, and then cooled back to room temperature. This temperature corresponds to the elevated temperature expected under standard EUV exposure conditions.18 XRD patterns were acquired after thermal annealing to monitor any changes in crystal structure, including shifts in peak position or variations in intensity.

As shown in Figure 7, the XRD patterns of the Pt-W alloy films remain virtually unchanged after the heating cycle. All samples retain their nanocrystalline or amorphous nature following annealing at 200 °C. Regardless of the Pt-to-W ratio, the intensity and position of the (111) diffraction peak near 39° show no notable variation, indicating excellent thermal stability of the alloy films under the prescribed temperature conditions.

XRD patterns of the PtW2, PtW, and Pt2W films before and after annealing at 200 °C for 20 min

A chemical stability (immersion) test was performed on the Pt-W alloy films using several standard mask-cleaning solutions: deionized water (DIW), 1% ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH), and 0.2%–0.5% tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH).5,6 To simulate cumulative material loss after multiple cleaning cycles, the films were immersed in each solution for 24 h. The standard mask cleaning process takes approximately 30 min. A 24-h immersion was employed as an accelerated test to simulate repeated cleaning cycles. This more aggressive long-duration immersion yields results comparable to those of multiple cleaning cycles in an actual mask cleaning tool in terms of process stability. Post-immersion changes in film thickness, density, and interfacial structure were evaluated by small-angle XRR. For reference, control samples of each composition were stored in a constant-temperature dry cabinet.19

Figure 8 summarizes the cleaning test results for all three Pt-W alloy compositions. The overall shape of the XRR curves remains largely unchanged before and after immersion. The critical-angle positions corresponding to the fringes are nearly identical, indicating that the film density is essentially unaffected. Furthermore, the oscillation frequency of the curves shows no significant shift, confirming that the total film thickness is also preserved. While minor variations in the fringe contrast are observable, these are within the range of measurement uncertainty. Collectively, the XRR data demonstrate that the Pt-W alloy films are chemically stable in the tested cleaning solutions.

Etching characteristics

The etching characteristics of the films were evaluated using an ICP-RIE system. Etch rates were measured to assess the influence of W content, and the etch selectivity between the Pt-W alloys and a Cr hard mask was investigated.20 Figure 9 shows the etch rates of Pt, Pt2W, PtW, PtW2, and Cr under various CF4/O2 gas flow ratios, while maintaining a total flow rate of 30 sccm and a constant Ar flow of 5 sccm. For all alloy compositions and gas conditions, the etch rate increased with higher W content. Among the gas mixtures tested, the condition with 20 sccm CF4 and 5 sccm O2 yielded the highest etch rates for all samples. Specifically, the etch rate of PtW2 under this optimal condition was 3.38 times that of pure Pt. The enhanced etch rate is attributed to the addition of a moderate amount of O2, which promotes the dissociation of CF4 and generates a higher concentration of reactive fluorine radicals. However, an excessive O2 flow can lead to surface passivation, thereby suppressing the etching reaction.21 Etch selectivity between the Pt-W alloys and the Cr hard mask was also evaluated. The highest selectivity of 6.9:1 was achieved for PtW2 under the same optimal gas condition (20 sccm CF4, 5 sccm O2), providing a clear guideline for selecting the most effective etching recipe.

Imaging performance

A telecentric sigma point source (TS-PS) illumination was employed to simulate and optimize the absorber thickness for horizontal and vertical L/S patterns with a 10 nm CD and a 20 nm pitch at an NA of 0.55.24 Using TS-PS illumination, the focus variable can be excluded from simulations because the NILS becomes independent of focus.12 Consequently, the use of TS-PS illumination reduces computational overhead and shortens simulation time. Subsequent performance simulations instead adopted the more practical IHLDP illumination scheme to evaluate horizontal patterns. The Mo/Si multilayer mask model used in the simulations was constructed following the description by Makhotkin et al.25 NILS, a key metric for evaluating image sharpness, was calculated as NILS = CD × |d ln I(x)/dx| at the feature edge, where I(x) is the aerial-image intensity and CD is the nominal line width.5

Figures 10A and 10C plots the NILS as a function of absorber thickness. The oscillatory behavior of the curves arises from interference between light reflected from the multilayer mirror and from the top surface of the absorber. It is observed that across all thicknesses, the Pt-W alloy films deliver higher NILS values than the reference TaBN absorber. Moreover, the optimal NILS is achieved at a reduced thickness of approximately 40 nm. This performance advantage stems from the superior optical properties of the Pt-W alloys—namely, a lower n and a higher k. The absorber thickness of Pt-W alloys and the TaBN absorber with corresponding simulated EUVR and phase shift at 5.355 deg CRAO are listed in Table 4. Because EUV absorber behavior depends on the full mask stack, oblique illumination, diffraction order balance, and feature orientation, the blanket-stack reflectance and phase values in Table 4 should not be interpreted as a stand-alone phase-shift-mask figure of merit. The absorber thickness was optimized for each material. While a higher NILS is desirable for improved imaging performance, it often leads to a reduction in the threshold (TtS), consequently increasing the exposure dose. To achieve an optimal balance between these two opposing trends, a comprehensive metric, throughput criterion (TpT), defined as TpT = NILS × sqrt(TtS), is employed. Figures 10B and 10D illustrate the dependence of TpT on absorber thickness, yielding optimal values of 33 nm for Pt2W, 33 nm for PtW, 40 nm for PtW2, and 60 nm for TaBN. Subsequent lithographic simulations were performed using the inner half leaf shape dipole illumination source. In EUV lithography, the depth of focus (DOF) narrows significantly at higher NA, making a wider focus range critical for process stability.28 Therefore, we evaluated the focus range over which NILS ≥2,29 defined as the normalized DOF (NDOF). Figure 11A shows the NDOF value, the NDOF values are 40 nm for Pt2W, 38 nm for PtW, 41 nm for PtW2, and 37 nm for TaBN. These results indicate a improvement in focal tolerance: Pt2W exhibits an 8% (3 nm) increase, PtW a 3% (1 nm) increase, and PtW2 an 11% (4 nm) increase relative to the reference TaBN absorber.

Telecentricity error (TCE), also referred to as through-focus pattern shift, describes the lateral displacement of a feature as a function of focus. The primary cause of mask-induced TCE is the non-parallelism between the CRAO plane and the feature orientation, which introduces an imbalance in intensity among diffraction orders.5,6 Since TCE can only be partially corrected, van Setten et al. recommend that the mask contribution to TCE should remain below 20 nm/μm, and below 10 nm/μm at the minimum pitch.30 We evaluated the TCE for a fixed CD of 10 nm across a pitch range from 20 nm to 60 nm in 8 nm increments, using the optimally determined absorber thickness for each material. No mask bias was applied for larger pitches, and the dose was independently anchored for each pitch. Additionally, a dark-field mask was used. Figure 11B shows the TCE value, the Pt-W alloys exhibit reduced TCE compared to the reference TaBN at smaller pitches. While TCE generally increases with larger pitch, the values for Pt-W remain comparable to TaBN in this regime. Notably, at the all pitch, the TCE of all Pt-W alloys is below the suggested 10 nm/μm threshold.

The focus value yielding the maximum NILS is defined as the best focus (BF) for a given pitch. The range between the maximum and minimum BF values across different pitches is quantified as the BFV. We evaluated the BF for a fixed CD of 10 nm across pitches ranging from 20 nm to 60 nm in 8 nm increments, using the optimally determined absorber thickness. Figures 11C and 11D shows the BF and BFV value, the simulated BF values are 21.7 nm, 24.1 nm, 22.8 nm, and 26.9 nm for Pt2W, PtW, PtW2, and TaBN, respectively. The corresponding BFV values are 7.9 nm, 14.9 nm, 9.4 nm, and 17.4 nm. Smaller BFV values signify a broader process window, which is critical for robustly printing multiple patterns with a maximized common process window.31 After recalculation, the BFV and TCE differences among Pt-W and TaBN absorbers were found to be modest and within the range expected from simulation/model uncertainty. Therefore, these metrics are interpreted as indicating comparable process-window behavior rather than a decisive BFV/TCE advantage. The more robust conclusion is that Pt-W absorbers can achieve comparable imaging metrics at a substantially reduced absorber thickness.

In summary, the lithographic simulations demonstrate that Pt-W absorbers maintain comparable imaging performance at reduced absorber thickness, confirming their potential as advanced low-n EUV mask materials. The key advantages include enhanced NILS, a wider NDOF, and similar BFV. While TCE increases with pitch for all materials, all values remain well below the recommended industry limits. The consistently lower BF and BFV values of the Pt-W alloys enable more flexible mask design across a range of pitches. Collectively, these results validate that Pt-W alloys meet the critical optical and imaging requirements for use as high-NA EUV mask absorbers.

Discussion

Compared to the reference TaBN absorber, Pt-W alloys exhibit a lower n and a higher k at the EUV wavelength (13.5 nm). In terms of thin-film properties, all three alloy compositions (Pt2W, PtW, and PtW2) show nanocrystalline or amorphous structures and a surface roughness around 0.2 nm, which is well below the practical specification of 0.5 nm RMS. Regarding residual stress, only Pt2W exhibits a stress of 298 MPa, while the other two alloys maintain stresses below 100 MPa, satisfying typical application requirements. The films also demonstrate excellent thermal and chemical stability, indicating their robustness under actual EUV exposure and cleaning conditions, thereby ensuring mask lifetime. Relative to a 60 nm TaBN absorber, the Pt-W alloys, at reduced absorber thicknesses (33–40 nm), mitigate the M3D effect, leading to improved NILS—benefits directly attributed to their superior optical properties. Furthermore, all all pitches, the TCE of all Pt-W alloys is below the suggested 10 nm/μm threshold, highlighting an additional imaging advantage. It is also noted that illumination-pupil shape influences M3D-related metrics. Therefore, exploring alternative illumination profiles represents a promising direction for further optimizing the performance of this new class of absorber materials.

Limitations of the study

Compared with production-grade mask blanks, Pt-W alloys as an emerging absorber layer material remain at the laboratory research stage and still face several limitations for practical application, including challenges related to the deposition substrate, etch selectivity with respect to the Ru capping layer, sidewall angle of the etched feature, and defect inspection and repair. Our study therefore serves to provide a candidate material option for emerging absorber layers, with application validation to be addressed in future work.

Method details

Fabrication of films

All thin-film samples were deposited on four inches diameter, double-side-polished Si substrates via physical vapor deposition (PVD). Pt-W alloy films with varying compositions were synthesized by magnetron co-sputtering. The composition of the alloys was controlled by adjusting the individual deposition rates of the Pt and W targets, yielding nominal atomic ratios of 1:0 (pure Pt), 2:1, 1:1, and 1:2 (Pt:W).

Material characterizations

The surface morphology and root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the Pt-W alloy films were characterized using a BRUKER Dimension Edge atomic force microscope (AFM). Measurements were performed over a 2 μm × 2 μm scan area with a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. The RMS roughness of each sample was determined by averaging measurements from three randomly selected regions on the film surface, and the data were processed using the NanoScope Analysis 3.00 software. The Si substrates exhibited a baseline RMS roughness of less than 0.2 nm. The crystal structure of the Pt-W films deposited on Si substrates was analyzed by grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an X’Pert PRO diffractometer. Measurements were conducted in the 2θ range of 20°–65° with a step size of 0.02°, employing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154178 nm) generated at 40 kV and 40 mA and filtered with a Ni filter. Film thickness, density, and interfacial roughness were evaluated by X-ray reflectivity (XRR) at low incident angles (0–5°). The residual stress in the films was measured using a k-Space Associates thin-film stress mapping system. The system employs a single laser to generate a two-dimensional array of dots projected onto the sample surface. The reflected dot pattern is captured by a high-resolution CCD detector, and the change in sample curvature is calculated from the shift in laser dot spacing. The curvature was mapped before and after film deposition over a 90 mm scan diameter with an edge exclusion of 7 mm and a measurement resolution of 2 mm. Etching characteristics were investigated using inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE). A systematic study of gas chemistry was performed using CF4/O2/Ar mixtures, where the ratio of CF4 to O2 was varied to identify the optimal etching recipe.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In this study, the optical constants (n, k) calculated from experimentally measured densities of Pt-W alloy thin films with different compositions were used to evaluate their performance through lithographic imaging simulations. These results were benchmarked against a reference TaBN absorber. Simulations were performed using the open-source simulator K-Litho.11 The K-Litho is a lithography simulation toolset designed for source-mask optimization (SMO). It rigorously solves Maxwell’s equations using the waveguide method and computes the aerial image by performing a Fourier transform of the diffraction pattern in the exit pupil, thereby enabling accurate modeling of M3D effects. The simulations employed an optimized illumination source, specifically an inner half leaf dipole configuration. Utilizing a dipole with either an inner half leaf or outer half leaf shape can mitigate image distortion at the focal plane. This distortion originates from focus splitting caused by the radial offset between the inner and outer poles of a conventional dipole source.12 Furthermore, compared to an outer half leaf source, the two poles of an inner half leaf source are positioned closer to the chief ray angle of the object (CRAO), thereby providing a lower angle of incidence on the mask. Since mask reflectivity is sensitive to the angle of incidence,13 a lower angle helps reduce the blocking of diffraction orders at the absorber edge, consequently diminishing the shadowing effect inherent to the mask.
