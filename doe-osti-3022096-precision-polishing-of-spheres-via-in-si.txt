Abstract
In inertial confinement fusion (ICF) experiments seeking output gains of unity and beyond, the
quality of the ablator capsule is paramount for minimizing hydrodynamic mix that quenches the
central hot spot. Defects in the form of foreign particles or missing mass on the surface and
within the wall of the capsule are primary offenders. High density carbon capsules made for ICF
experiments on the National Ignition Facility (NIF) are precision polished to achieve the surface
smoothness in the order of a few nm as well as to minimize isolated defects in the form of pits.
Given the critical role of this process, we are developing smart manufacturing techniques with
goal of elevating the efficiency of this process. Our approach is to use MEMS-based sensors to
capture the fine vibrational signals generated during the polishing process and combine it with
synchronized visual feedback as needed. Beyond using these sensors for process monitoring,
we use specific deep learning methods to analyze the data and extract correlations with both
the process parameters and the final performance of the polishing run. Here, we describe the
multiple fronts that we have explored in this regard and the results we have gotten so far. This
approach promises to have the potential to ultimately provide real-time feedback that can be
used for ensuring the progress of the run as well as a means for faster optimization.

Background and Research Objectives
The field of High Energy Density (HED) science, one of the principal missions at the Laboratory,
demands experimental assemblies (typically referred to as targets) that have stringent
specifications for surface quality, homogeneity, and dimensional fidelity. This is becoming
increasingly necessary in the regime of Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF) which seeks to achieve
ignition at the National Ignition Facility (NIF). In particular, the ablating sphere (capsule or
shell) at the core of an ICF target. The capsule, which acts as the rocket for generating the
tremendous compression of the payload within, needs to have virtually no defects on the
surface or within as they cause yield-degrading perturbations. These hollow ablator spheres
must be comprised of low atomic number (low Z) materials and have to be custom fabricated
with state-of-the-art vapor deposition technologies on spherical mandrels that can be
subsequently removed. Long deposition times and long contact with the pan on which the
emerging capsules roll or tumble continuously mean that the as-deposited surface of the
capsule cannot meet the surface smoothness specification warranting a post-deposition
polishing step. The final surface quality, both in terms of the generic smoothness and the
removal of isolated defects such as pits or bumps, is critically determined by this polishing step.
The experimental plan at the NIF (and sometimes at other HED facilities) needs in the order of a
few dozen high quality capsules a year made of high-density carbon (HDC) which, for the most
part, is diamond. In turn, this necessitates a small-scale production effort to supply these
capsules but also, and more importantly, a need to better understand the nuances of the
production process and continuously improve quality while eliminating the variability in quality.
Surface finishing of specifically capsules is done using the so-called V-groove polishing process.
It is an essential step in the production of HDC shells and to date, the only polishing method
that has attained the minimum required roughness and sphericity. In V-groove polishing, a
batch of shells is loaded into a circular, V-shaped groove and driven by a plate placed on top
whose center of rotation is usually offset from the V-groove center.
 The shells go through stages of lapping (high rate of material removal to get nominal
smoothness) and polishing (the final surface defining process with significantly slower rate).
Removal only occurs wherever the shells ‘slip’ against a surface rather than roll smoothly. This
polishing process is lengthy, requiring about 2 months of total polishing time, which is 75% of
total capsule fabrication time. Also, over 10% of the batches are lost due to catastrophic off-
normal events. Furthermore, the material removal rate is not reproducible between runs,
requiring frequent process interruptions for inspection to ensure that the right amount of
material is removed, thereby adding additional time and labor cost.

The overall goal of this project is to transcend these limitations by injecting new technologies
into this process – real-time feedback via acoustic and vibrational monitoring and the use of
machine learning inspired strategies to process the vast amount of data to be able to separate
just the relevant signals hidden in certain frequency bands. Sensing is based on the fact that
the tribological interaction between surfaces that results in material removal creates a
characteristic acoustic/vibrational signal that carries information about the state of the capsule
surface and expectedly the material removal rate, both crucial metrics in polishing. We seek to
capitalize on this as critical direct feedback via in-situ monitoring using sensitive sensors
mounted appropriately on the polisher. One of the big challenges here is that the meaningful
data is dispersed over a large frequency band that the sensor records and its incidence can
change with time reflecting the change in the capsule surface. Hence, we need to use deep
learning algorithms to tease out the relevant bands and tie them to physical processes, aided
also by kinematic modeling to give us technical insights.
This should have a significant benefit for 4 important ways:
• Lowering of process time which has a big bearing on our nimbleness to implement new
designs suggested by the latest shot data and ideas
• Increase in yield with a goal of predicting or detecting off-normals, such as cracking or
chipping, before they propagate egregiously through the whole batch of capsules
• Higher end quality, which is obviously driven by the need for as defect-free a surface as
possible
• Generating prescriptive recipes for critical new materials which we are working on
Ultimately, the learnings from this project should also be applicable to precision surface
finishing processes beyond V-groove polishing of spheres – for instance, it can benefit
fabrication of ultra-smooth parts for other HED efforts or as an aid in high-quality finishing of
optical components within the Lab and beyond the Lab as well.

Scientific Approach and Accomplishments
Overall, our main objective was to elucidate the V-groove polishing process by combining four
key elements: developing kinematic/physics modeling, in-situ diagnostics, and large data
processing tools and then linking them to a process parameters study. The use of a vibration
sensor is self-evident and is actually a quantification of the artisan polishing approach of using
sound as an indicator of the polishing state, common in manual optical polishing and faceting
operations. Herein, this project primarily sought to implement a multi-sensor fusion approach
for real-time monitoring of the precision V-groove polishing process that is used for achieving
the near-perfect surface finish of ablator spheres with a goal of extracting correlation between
the relevant signals in the sensor data and key aspects of the process. Additionally, new
diamond polishing plate microstructures were explored with the goal of developing a more
efficient polishing process which creates significant value for the program. Confocal and
interferometric optical surface characterization was used extensively to obtain the final
performance metrics and analysis of signals from 3 sensors - time-synced vibration, acoustic
and optical imaging sensors - was done using machine learning (ML) based algorithms.

II. SENSOR/METROLOGY DETAILS
To support these investigations, polishing systems at both Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL) and Texas A&M University (TAMU) were sensorized. The sensing setup at
TAMU collects vibration, sound, and video data streams from, respectively, accelerometer,
microphone and a wireless mini camera attached to the polishing setup. The signals are
collected into a Matlab platform. The video is sampled at 10 frames per second (fps), and the
vibration data is sampled at 10,000 Hz. This means that each frame of video corresponds to
1,000 data points of vibration data. The vibration signals and video frames are aligned in time
with a precision of approximately 100 ms or better. In real-time processing, this involves
mapping a 1,000-sample segment of vibration time-series data to each video frame. We use a
physical process-based trigger to identify the start times of polishing17. Once this start time is
identified, we calculate any lag or lead and adjust accordingly to eliminate sampling delay. This
method ensures accurate synchronization of the vibration data with the actual process.

This synchronization facilitates interrogations such as identification of vibration signal patterns
over the time windows where the video captures anomalous interactions, and likewise,
investigation of any anomalous motion patterns in the videos whenever vibration signals
exhibit sudden changes in their spectral characteristics. Such identifications address the
labelling challenge that often degrades the effectiveness of machine learning methods. This is
shown schematically in Figure 1.

Figure 1. A schematic flowchart of our approach as well as equipment used.
A similar set up was established on two polishers at LLNL to collect vibration and synchronous
video data from the polishing system. The data collected from these setups during multiple
stages of polishing were used to corelate vibration signatures that characterize surface
evolution and anomalous polishing events. The surface topography was also measured offline
using a laser confocal microscope (Keyence VK-X3100). The heightmaps and micrographs were
used to delineate how various surface features, including asperities and pits, evolve during
polishing.
The data gathered from these experiments were used to address the following two specific
challenges in realizing consistently high-quality shells:
 • Multi-sensor fusion for real-time monitoring of anomalous motion patterns during
 polishing
 • Developing decision-support tools to detect endpoints (stopping criteria) for the
 polishing process to assure surface quality.

III. SURFACE QUALITY MONITORING AND ANOMALY DETECTION
The key to real-time monitoring is to correlate the time and frequency patterns of vibration
signals with events-of-interest that occur during polishing. The events include collisions and
such anomalous interactions of shells during polishing. These interactions are surmised to
promote the pull out of grains that exist in the as-deposited HDC layers, leading to eventual pit
formation. Additionally, shell collisions are also known to initiate small surface cracks and
scratches that not only impede surface quality but also can trigger catastrophic shell fractures
leading to debris that ruins the entire batch of shells being polished and also risk damaging the
polishing tool.
Our efforts focused on first identifying spectral components of vibration signals that correlate
well with these anomalous events. The challenges in the identification of spectral components
are similar to those tackled in the spectral banding methods employed for monitoring rotary
machinery1. However, there are a few important distinctions as described below.
Signals capture the forces generated during polishing. The forces excite the machine structure
and are thereby modulated through the frequencies that capture the various vibration modes
of the machine structure. The sources of forces generated during polishing include asperity
removal through deformation and fracture, stick-slip contact between the shell surface and the
pad and other contact points on the machine besides diverse interactions among the shells and
with the abrasives and the machine structures. Interestingly, these vibrations, in turn, modify
the forces generated during a polishing process, creating a so-called regenerative effect 2,3.
The energies from vibrations generated by sharp collisions, cracking and such impulse-rich
events that can promote pits and other defects would span a broad range of frequencies.
However, the fidelity of the signals, typically expressed in terms of a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
can be different across multiple frequency bands. The SNR tends to be high near the frequency
bands that represent the different modes of the structure. Although vibration signals have been
successfully employed in the industry to detect collisions in the operations of ball bearings and
other mechanical components, subtle interaction events, including prominent shell collisions,
need a different treatment.
As the surface is polished, the contact processes, including the stick-slip phenomena
progressively change. Additionally, the removal of asperities and other material during
polishing causes even more feeble changes to the structural characteristics (such as stiffness
and compliance). The crux of vibration-based monitoring is to identify the time and frequency
patterns of the vibration signals using which one could track anomalous events and changes in
these processes.
III.A. Shell Interaction Identification

As noted earlier, the interactions among the shells are modulated over these frequency spectral
bands of the vibration signals. Correct identification of these spectral bands from vibration
signals is important to avoid spurious correlations because of noise as well as to establish the
plausible physical root cause for the observed vibration signal patterns. This challenge can be
formulated as a machine learning challenge consisting of extracting the frequency spectrum of
vibration signals over a specified time window (e.g., 1 second), and identifying a set of spectral
bands, where each band represents a range of frequencies of the vibration signals, such that
the signal energies in these bands, taken together, show the largest contrast between the time
windows over which the shells are interacting verses when they are not. In other words, the
machine learning method would predict the occurrence of an anomalous shell interaction
during a time window (1 second) based on the energies within the spectral bands constructed
from the specified time window of vibration signals obtained during polishing.
The current machine learning methods are mostly developed to classify an interaction event
only when presented with the energy bands, and not to identify best spectral bands (denoted
as α_1,α_2,α_3,…) to achieve such a classification (Figure 2). To address this drawback, we
developed a new machine learning method called EGO-MDA that could iteratively learn the
optimal frequency bands for given spectra samples from two classes. In the first part of the
iterative procedure, this method uses Mixture Discriminant Analysis (MDA) to obtain the
optimal classifier for a given set of frequency bands. Second, it uses a Bayesian optimization
method called Efficient Global Optimization (EGO) to sequentially update spectral bands
towards improving the classification accuracy. After a specified number of iterations, the
method learns the optimal spectral bands.

We have utilized EGO-MDA to identify the spectral bands that are most sensitive to collisions
and such anomalous interactions among shells during the polishing process 4,5. The important
frequencies identified by EGO-MDA is used as an input to classify between the time windows
(0.25 - 2 seconds long events) during which the shells were colliding versus when the shells
were apart. To enable robust classification, we used a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
model, which utilizes the video data collected during the polishing experiments as the ground
truth to each video frame (~0.1 second) and the synchronous 1000 vibration signal samples.
The application of EGO-MDA to this context has suggested that about 35% difference in energy
exists over the 1-2.5 kHz spectral band of the signals collected (over the time windows) when
the shells were interacting versus when they were not interacting.
To correlate camera images with vibration signals, we analyzed shell collision events over five
video frames spanning 0.5 seconds. These frames capture the initial state, the moment before
collision, the collision itself, the immediate aftermath, and the shells separating. A voltage surge
in the band-pass filtered vibration signal is detected within 0.2 seconds (2 frames) after the
collision, characterized by specific frequencies and energy spikes. By examining these
correlations and analyzing the band-pass filtered signals and the shells post-collision, we
identified features associated with specific vibrational frequencies, providing insights into these
events. A visual representation of this phenomenon is shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3. The figure illustrates the sequence of a shell collision event captured over five video
frames, spanning a total duration of 0.5 seconds. The specific interaction occurs in the third
frame, followed by an immediate increase in the signal observed in the subsequent vibration

This significant correlation between the shell interactions and the specific vibration spectral
band energies indicates the potential for employing vibration signals to monitor the shell
polishing process. The significance of better understanding shell-shell interactions is highlighted
in Figure 4 which shows the results of experiments where we had two sets of shells with slightly
varying diameters. The shells with the larger diameter (called regular-sized shells) contacted the
polishing pad whereas the slightly smaller set did not, as sketched in Figure 4. The smaller shells
were either HDC or stainless-steel spheres. In this way, we could separate shells that only

collided with neighboring shells with no simultaneous polishing (i.e. the smaller ones) from
those that underwent both collisions as well as polishing (larger shells). Table 1 lists the
changes in roughness for both the shells that only collide (smaller shells) vs those that also
contacted the polishing pad (regular sized shells). Roughness in this case is quantified as Sa, the
areal average roughness or the arithmetical mean height of the surface. Sa decreases or stays
constant in the latter case (regular-sized shells) as expected in a polishing run. The fact that
there is a change in Sa for the smaller shells indicates that these shells do interact with the
neighboring larger shells. This interaction can lead to roughening (as is the case for both lapping
runs, particularly when using the softer stainless-steel shells) or smoothening of the surface (as
observed in the polishing run which results in very smooth surfaces of the load bearing shells).
This implies that surface roughening/smoothening is incurred during shell-shell interactions.
This understanding helps us when optimizing the process.

III.B. Shell Cracking Identification
One important issue to address is occasional occurrence of catastrophic events like cracking of
a shell during polishing that can cause significant harm by eventually damaging the entire batch
or the machine itself. Anomalous process settings (e.g., use of excessive forces, poor
conditioning of the polishing plates), as well as shell irregularities, can initiate cracking that can
then lead to spalling of the HDC coating. Therefore, early detection of shell cracking is crucial to
pause the process and prevent widespread damage due to the ensuing debris (Figure 5).

Figure 5: Scanning electron microscope image on the left shows a magnified view of the cracks
that occur in the shell. This can result in debris seen on the micrograph on the right that can
perturb the rotational patterns and eventually lead to failure of the entire batch.

Our ongoing efforts to detect cracking events in real-time during polishing involve collecting
vibration data and applying machine learning techniques6. Specifically, we used an autoencoder
model to learn the relationship connecting the vibration signals with cracking events, including
their inception. Autoencoders are neural network models that learn the hidden signatures
inherent in “raw” data (vibration signal samples) in the form of low-dimensional features. The
model structure consists of two parts: an encoder that compresses the input (“raw” data) into a
set of features, and a decoder that reconstructs the input from these features. An input is
classified as anomalous if the reconstruction loss, i.e., the deviation between the original input
and its reconstruction, is greater than a threshold.

Each experiment involved finishing a batch of approximately 20 shells loaded in a specified
cartridge on a mechanical polishing setup. Each batch was polished on a polisher (at LLNL or
TAMU) with specific settings for downforce, speed, and polishing time. During polishing,
vibration time-series data was captured using an accelerometer (Kistler 8728A500) at a
sampling rate of 10 kHz, while video frames were intermittently gathered at a rate of 10 frames
per second.
Raw time-series data obtained from two batches of experiments, labelled H200 and H202, were
processed for this study. The data was segmented into 30-minute windows and analyzed by a
human operator, who labelled segments as normal or anomalous based on auditory
characteristics and signal amplitude changes. This process generated 20 segments of labelled
vibration data for training and testing the autoencoder machine learning method.
The autoencoder model was trained on 900 seconds (half a segment) of normal data from the
H200 dataset, excluding the initial 100 seconds of sensor startup noise. The remaining data
from H200 was used for validation and testing, and the model was also assessed using the H202
dataset. The accuracy of the model depends on its performance in detecting anomalies in these
datasets.
We used the string of reconstruction losses obtained for every 0.02-second time window as
part of an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) scheme (Figure 6), which shows the
data from a run where the coating cracked and subsequently delaminated from all of the shells.
Upon noticing an abnormal sound at the about the 2.8h mark, the run was stopped.

Figure 6. Autoencoder’s reconstruction vibration data with EWMA charts, showing process
anomalies (shell cracking)

We use EWMA control charts to enable early detection of the onset of a cracking event. Our
investigations suggest that cracking events can be detected within 2.23 seconds after their
inception. Future enhancements include integrating camera technology for precise anomaly
localization and refining autoencoder and EWMA control chart parameters to boost sensitivity
to subtle process variations.

 III.C. Modelling of Shell Polishing Kinematics
Towards further enable monitoring of the process and ultimately better understanding the
pertinent frequency bands, we investigated as part of the shells during polishing. This effort
adapts Lee et al.’s kinematic model7,8,9. An analytical model to understand the mechanics of an
‘ideal’ ball polishing process with a three-point contact was set by including both slip and rolling
mechanics. The model was also simulated further using the multibody modelling modules of
Simulink-SimScape. The model would be essential to provide physically meaningful
understanding of the kinematics of the polishing process and improvement of surface material.
The model offers insights on the extent to which a (1) co-axial planetary motion, (2) non-co-
axial planetary motion, and (3) sudden altering of the shell motion (spins) affect the uniformity
of the polish (see Figure 7). Our ongoing work on this seeks to enhance this model by employing
sensor-based predictions of real-time translational speeds and hence possible stick-slip and
other relevant aspects of shell motion.

Figure 7. The sketch on the left shows the key parameters to consider for the kinematic model.
The images on the right are the results of the simulations using Simulink-SimScape.

The stick-slip phenomenon is known to deform or sever surface asperities. It can generate heat
(“flash temperatures”) that can affect the material at the tips of surface asperities. A controlled
stick-slip promotes polishing. This phenomenon is typically captured in frequency bands above
1 kHz, and severe skidding of the contact is captured in higher frequency bands. Going forward,
we aim to extend this toward helping with better understanding those details.
III.D. Prediction of evolution of smoothness from sensor data
Another important research desire is to acquire real-time assurance that the polishing process
is headed in the right direction and make any necessary corrections early in the process as
needed. Since, measuring and tracking the surface roughness of the shells is a labor-intensive
and time-consuming task, here we explore the possibility of automatically predicting the
surface roughness of shells using machine learning based analysis of sensor data. Additionally,
success here could potentially lead to faster improvements in the polishing process, its time
efficiency and overall process optimization. In particular, we use the vibration signals from the
polishing runs of shells and the measured start and finish surface roughness values (such as Sa))
to train a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) neural network to correlate features of the vibration
signals to change in Sa (i.e., ΔSa). The trained model is then tested against the vibration signals
obtained for a similar but different polish run to see how well the model predicts the details.
Each polishing run to generate the data was ~24 hours long. But to characterize how the Sa
changed as a function of time in that duration, we did one run where we deliberately
interrupted the process every 6 min to measure the Sa until the rate of change was minimal.
All 24h polishing runs were segmented into 6 min brackets of vibration ‘samples’ and the
corresponding intermediate Sa values were attained using the fitting function from the
interrupted run. Furthermore, by combining 4 different 24h runs, our training data was
increased a total of 870 6-min vibration samples along with their corresponding calculated ΔSa
values. For each ‘sample’, we computed 11 statistical features, such as mean, variance, kurtosis,
etc. and trained a 4-layer MLP model (11 neurons -> 100 neurons -> 100 neurons -> 1 neuron).
The trained model was then tested on an unseen run with 250 samples. This is seen in Figure 8,
which shows the predictions using this approach vs. ground truth (GT) Sa for the test samples.
The average prediction mean absolute error was 0.61 nm, and clearly the prediction is poor.

Figure 8: Sa prediction of our MLP model vs. ground truth (GT) derived from the sensor signals.
The significant departure after about 7 hours points to the limitation of this technique.
Since, the testing batch is completely different from the trained batch in terms of exact
conditions, we hypothesized that an online, unsupervised domain adaptation technique was
needed where the trained model adapts to changes in the vibration features of the test set
(though without using the ΔSa of the test set) in order to improve its prediction accuracy. We
applied a well-known domain adaptation approach from Ganin and Lempitsky10 that allows the
model to improve the Sa prediction mean absolute error to 0.017 nm, thereby more accurately
following the decreasing trend of the ground truth (Figure 9). Going forward, we will delve
deeper into the use of domain adaptation approaches for improving our predictive capability.

Figure 9: Sa prediction after applying domain adaptation. Here again, the GT curve represents
the ground truth.

IV. ENDPOINT DETECTION
As noted earlier, shells are finished progressively over many polishing runs, with each run
lasting ~24 hours to achieve the desired geometric and ultraprecision surface quality. Stopping
the polishing process at the right juncture is important to ensure that the ultraprecision
geometric and surface quality requirements are optimally met. The longer the polishing time
the higher is the risk of the formation of pits, cracks, and anomalies. More pertinently, a major
challenge exists with employing the current machine learning methods to correlate the
measured vibration and other signals to the surface roughness quantifiers such as the mean
absolute deviation of surface heights (Sa). While the vibration signals are acquired almost
continuously through the hours of polishing, the shell surface roughness and geometry are
measured only between the runs. In other words, one would have only a small set of ground
truth values to help learn how the vibration patterns that emerge during polishing would signify
a change in the surface roughness.

To relate the offline measurement of surface roughness to the online vibration signals, we take
surface roughness readings at the start and end of the polishing experiments. The changes in
surface roughness observed between these points are then correlated with the vibration signals
collected during the polishing process. By analyzing the vibration signals, we can infer how the
surface roughness evolves throughout the polishing, thereby establishing a time-related
connection between offline surface measurements and online vibration data.
To elaborate, the number of input-output data pairs available to train a machine learning
model, here, is the same as the number of polishing stretches, which would be only 7-10 for the
shell polishing process compared to hundreds or thousands of such pairs employed in
traditional machine learning model. With so few input-output data pairs, deep learning models
are doomed to fail, and many other machine learning techniques are less likely to be effective.
Towards addressing this challenge, we employed functional regression, a class of statistical
models that can help predict the progression of surface roughness through the process from
the measured vibration signals11,12. Functional regression models employ a linear structure to
keep the model simple as there are not that many data pairs and using a functional regression
for handling the input vibration signals that are functional. The model is as follows:

The functional regression model developed from measurements showed that the polishing
process progresses in three phases, namely, a short run-in phase that lasts a few minutes
where the surface finish worsens a bit followed by a steady active polishing phase during which
the surface asperities are gradually reduced, and a saturation phase, where little if any
polishing action takes place. It is optimal to stop polishing when the saturation stage is reached.
Next, we developed an online detection method that can signal in real-time when to stop a
polishing cycle (i.e., the saturation phase has set in) and to institute a follow-up inspection,
rather than letting the machine engage in an over-polishing cycle for too long. The online
procedure is a change-point detection method that detects the energy turning points in the
vibration signals. This study provided one of the first endpoint criterion for the shell polishing
process. The insights from this work indicate that the unproductive saturation phase forms a
significant portion of each polishing stage, and therefore a polishing stage can be shorted some
50% of the original time, i.e., from 24 hours to 12 hours, without sacrificing the final polishing
quality.

V. Velocity analysis

Tangential velocity information provides insights into polishing dynamics by enabling
momentum transfer analysis during multi-shell interaction, revealing slip-stick behavior,
enabling anomaly detection, supporting end-point detection, and facilitating process
optimization with optical interferometry. However, leveraging these signals for closed-loop
control requires quantified uncertainty estimates to distinguish genuine process deviations
from noise artifacts.
Existing computer vision pipelines face significant challenges in uncertainty quantification
across the complete pipeline from detection to velocity estimation. Conventional pipelines
treat each stage as deterministic, failing to account for inherent uncertainties in visual
measurements (Valdenegro-Toro 2021). This limitation is pronounced in scenarios involving
nonlinear geometric transformations, such as circular motion, where pixel-level uncertainties
propagate and amplify significantly when transformed into physical velocities.
We addressed these challenges by developing the first complete uncertainty-aware vision
pipeline for nonlinear motion analysis:
1. Unified uncertainty propagation: A probabilistic framework maintaining uncertainty
consistency from pixel-level detections through temporal tracking to final velocity estimates,
handling nonlinear transformations in circular-to-linear motion projections.
2. YOLO-ByteTrack uncertainty integration: The first framework linking YOLO-TTA uncertainty
quantification → covariance-fed ByteTrack tracking with modified cost functions (weighted 1 -
IoU and Mahalanobis distance) → closed-form error-propagated tangential velocity estimation
for rotating target capsules.
3. Circular motion kinematics with safeguards: Closed form uncertainty propagation equations
for orthographic projections of circular motion with adaptive Monte Carlo validation near
kinematic singularities, providing theoretically grounded confidence bounds.
We present a complete uncertainty-aware vision pipeline that addresses the three identified
gaps through: (1) YOLOTTA integration for detection uncertainty, (2) ByteTrack cost function
modification for uncertainty-aware tracking, and (3) closed-form error propagation for circular
motion velocity estimation. The framework maintains uncertainty consistency from pixel-level
detections through temporal tracking to final velocity estimates.

We validated our approach using polishing experiments, where well-defined circular motion
patterns and stringent process monitoring requirements create an ideal testbed. Our
framework demonstrates improved reliability in velocity estimation with calibrated uncertainty
bounds, enabling robust process control decisions. Our contributions address fundamental
challenges in computer vision uncertainty propagation with broad implications for robotics,
autonomous systems, and safety-critical domains requiring trustworthy motion analysis.

VI. Other thrusts
Other thrusts not detailed above:
 1. Machine learning based statistical analysis model to predict isolated features such as
 pits based on the starting condition of the shell was developed.
 2. As a stretch goal, generation of advanced analytical frameworks for tracking mass
 removal rates was investigated, though limitations in producing large data sets paced
 the progression of this work.
 3. System architectures to react and correct the process based on real-time monitoring
 were laid out for possible future implementation, especially if the process were to
 evolve into large scale production where smart manufacturing principles would be key.

Mission Impact
The HDC capsule is indeed the most significant component of an ICF ignition target, and the
surface quality of the capsule is paramount. The requirements are extremely stringent (surface
roughness of a few nm, no pits >1um3) which required the finishing process, comprised of the
steps of lapping and polishing, to operate flawlessly and efficiently. In that regard this LDRD
provided the following significant advances:
 4. ~3x faster lapping process while retaining the same high quality, which has already been
 inducted into the production of HDC capsules for ICF ignition targets.
 5. Successful development of fine lapping process as a viable and ~3x faster alternative to
 the polishing step while retaining the same high quality
 6. Understanding of the kinetics of achieving the generic surface smoothness (as
 quantified by the rms value of surface asperities)
 7. Mechanism of formation of small pits which is key for sustaining high quality
Precision polishing for high surface finish is a vital step in the making of many high-tech
components for optical and semiconductor modules as well as for wide ranging experimental

samples for HED science that is at the core of LLNL’s mission. These findings and the new
methodologies developed here would have impact on these other fields with stringent surface
requirements as well.

Conclusion

Firstly, the work here has been used in fabrication of shells for ICF targets via a more efficient
process. It has enabled lowering of process time, which has a big bearing on our nimbleness to
implement new designs suggested by the latest shot data and ideas. Beyond ICF, progress
made herein will have also a significant impact on the fabrication of HED targets for other
campaigns.
System architectures to react and correct the process based on real-time monitoring were laid
out for possible future implementation, especially if the process were to evolve into large scale
production where smart manufacturing principles would be key. This would aid increase in
yield via prediction or detection of off-normals, such as cracking or chipping, before they
propagate egregiously through the whole batch of capsules
It has also ushered in the burgeoning field of AI into the Target Fabrication group at LLNL. This
is evidenced by spawning a new follow-on LDRD proposal (that has been funded) implying
continued interest in developing this approach further.
