High-Order Invariant-Domain Preserving Continuous Finite Elements via Graph-Poisson Convex Limiting Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 10, 2026.
Murtazo Nazarov

Abstract
We develop a high-order invariant-domain preserving continuous finite element method for nonlinear scalar conservation laws. The method combines a residual-viscosity high-order discretization with a low-order invariant-domain scheme constructed on a fine ℙ 1 {\mathbb{P}}_{1} submesh induced by the high-order nodal points. This separation avoids the restrictions caused by nonpositive lumped masses and overly wide high-order graph stencils. The high- and low-order updates are connected by a graph-Poisson flux reconstruction, which represents their difference as conservative antisymmetric graph fluxes. These fluxes are limited using convex limiting coefficients, followed by a capacity-based mass redistribution step that restores conservation while preserving the prescribed bounds whenever sufficient admissible capacity is available. Numerical experiments for smooth and nonsmooth scalar conservation laws demonstrate high-order accuracy in smooth regimes, robustness near discontinuities, and convergence to the entropy solution for challenging benchmarks.



keywords

High-order method, graph-Poisson limiting, convex limiting, invariant domain preserving, discrete maximum principle, conservation

Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the numerical approximation of nonlinear scalar conservation laws of the form

(1)

\begin{cases}{\partial}_{t}u+\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{f}}(u)=0,\quad({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],\\
u({\boldsymbol{x}},0)=u_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded open domain,
d
is the space dimension,
u_{0}
is the prescribed initial data, and
T>0
is the final time. We assume that (
1
) is equipped with appropriate boundary conditions. The flux
{\boldsymbol{f}}:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is assumed to be nonlinear and Lipschitz continuous on an invariant domain
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. By an invariant domain we mean that, if the initial data takes values in
{\mathcal{A}}
, then the exact entropy solution remains in
{\mathcal{A}}
for all later times.

The main objective of this work is to construct high-order finite element approximations of (
1
) that preserve the invariant-domain property. In particular, we are interested in methods that retain accuracy beyond second order in space. High-order invariant-domain preserving (IDP) schemes for hyperbolic problems are most commonly based on finite volume or discontinuous Galerkin (DG) discretizations. We refer the reader to
[
31
,
25
,
34
]
and the references therein for finite volume methods, and to
[
35
,
17
,
30
]
and the references therein for DG methods.

In contrast, comparable high-order IDP constructions for continuous finite element methods are less developed. Robust IDP continuous finite element schemes restricted to first- or second-order accuracy have been proposed in several works; see, for instance,
[
20
,
21
,
10
,
8
,
15
]
and the references therein. Most high-order limiting approaches rely on three main ingredients: a robust low-order IDP scheme, a high-order scheme that is not necessarily IDP, and a limiting procedure that combines the two. Many high-order finite element stabilizations, including entropy-viscosity and residual-viscosity methods, can be used as the high-order ingredient; see, for instance,
[
11
,
27
,
2
]
. There are also well-established limiting frameworks such as flux-corrected transport (FCT)
[
33
,
20
]
, algebraic flux correction (AFC)
[
22
,
24
]
, and convex limiting
[
6
,
9
]
. In these frameworks, the difference between a high-order discretization and a low-order IDP discretization is decomposed into conservative antidiffusive graph fluxes, which are then limited.

A major difficulty in extending this methodology to high-order continuous Lagrange finite elements lies in the construction of the low-order IDP scheme. The first-order graph-viscosity schemes developed in
[
10
,
13
]
, as well as many low-order schemes used in FCT and AFC methods, are edge-based and are naturally suited to piecewise-linear spaces. For higher-order Lagrange spaces, the graph associated with the finite element stencil becomes wider, and the resulting low-order method can become excessively diffusive. A second difficulty concerns mass lumping. The low-order IDP scheme requires a lumped mass matrix with nonnegative entries
[
12
]
. However, standard high-order Lagrange elements do not always have nonnegative lumped masses. For example, on simplices, the lumped masses are positive for
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
, nonnegative for
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
, and positive for
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{5}
, whereas negative lumped masses generally appear for other polynomial degrees. This creates a significant obstacle to constructing arbitrarily high-order IDP continuous finite element schemes using the standard graph-viscosity machinery directly on the high-order space.

The first contribution of this paper is to remove this bottleneck by separating the spaces used for the low- and high-order schemes. The high-order method is constructed in the desired polynomial space using residual viscosity (RV). The low-order IDP scheme, however, is built on a fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
submesh whose vertices coincide with the nodal points of the high-order space. Thus the low-order method remains an optimal first-order IDP scheme on a piecewise-linear graph, while the high-order residual-viscosity method retains the accuracy of the high-order polynomial approximation.

The second contribution is a graph-Poisson flux reconstruction used in the limiting step. Since the low-order and high-order updates are defined with respect to different mass structures, their difference is not immediately available as conservative edge fluxes. We therefore represent the difference between the high-order residual-viscosity update and the low-order IDP update by conservative antisymmetric graph fluxes obtained from a graph-Poisson problem. These fluxes are then limited edgewise using convex limiting coefficients. The limiting step enforces the local invariant-domain bounds but may leave a remaining fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass defect. This defect is corrected by a secondary capacity-based mass redistribution step, which restores the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass of the high-order update while preserving the prescribed bounds whenever sufficient admissible capacity is available. In this sense, the method follows the AFC philosophy: a low-order invariant-domain state is corrected by limited conservative antidiffusive graph fluxes. The distinctive feature of the present approach is that the antidiffusive fluxes are reconstructed by solving a graph-Poisson problem rather than being obtained directly from the difference of two algebraic operators.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the finite element spaces and meshes used throughout the paper. Sections
3
and
4
describe the low-order and high-order schemes, respectively. The main limiting algorithm, including the graph-Poisson flux reconstruction and convex limiting procedure, is presented in Section
5
. Finally, in Section
6
, we solve several benchmark problems to assess the accuracy, robustness, and invariant-domain properties of the proposed method.

3
Low order scheme

Let us denote the lumped mass matrix corresponding to the nodal points of the
{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
elements by
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\textrm{fine}}
. Let
u_{h}^{n}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathsf{U}}^{n}_{i}\varphi_{i}
be approximate solution at
t=t_{n}
.

Let
K\subset{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
be a fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
triangle with local nodes
{\mathcal{I}}(K)=\{i,j,l\}
. For every edge
(i,j)\subset K
, we define the vector

{\boldsymbol{c}}_{ij}^{K}:=\int_{K}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\nabla\varphi_{j}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Since
\nabla\varphi_{j}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
is constant on
K
, this can be written as

{\boldsymbol{c}}_{ij}^{K}=m_{i,K}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\nabla\varphi_{j}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big|_{K},

where
m_{i,K}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}=\int_{K}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{|K|}{d+1}
is the
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass matrix in the cell
K
.

Next, we define local graph-viscosity associated to the edge
(i,j)
of the cell
K
by

d_{ij}^{n,K}=\max_{l\in{\mathcal{I}}(K)}\left|{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}({\mathsf{U}}_{l}^{n})\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}_{ij}^{K}\right|.

The symmetric low-order edge viscosity is then

(5)

d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}:=\max\big(d_{ij}^{n,K},d_{ji}^{n,K}\big),\quad i\neq j,\quad i,j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),

For every
i\in{\mathcal{I}}(K)
, we define the local graph viscosity contribution by

b_{K}^{{\rm L}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}):=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),\,j\not=i}d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}-{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}\big).

This edge-difference form is locally conservative: the contribution
associated with the edge
(i,j)
is equal and opposite in the equations
for nodes
i
and
j
.

The global artificial viscosity operator is obtained by summing all fine cell contributions containing node
i
:

(6)

b_{h}^{{\rm L}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})=\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}b_{K}^{{\rm L}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})=\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),\,j\not=i}d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}-{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}\big).

Finally, we define the first order method in the fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mesh as follows:

(7)

m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\frac{{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{L},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}}{{\tau_{n}}}+\int_{S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{f}}(u_{h}^{n})\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+b_{h}^{\rm L}\big(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big)=0,

for every
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proposition 1
.

The low-order scheme (
7
) is conservative with respect to the
fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped mass, i.e.,

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{L},n+1}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}.

Proof.

Multiplying (
7
) by
{\tau_{n}}
and summing over all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
and using the partition of unity property of
\varphi_{i}
, and conservation properties of

b_{h}^{\textrm{L}}(u^{n}_{h},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})

proves the statement.

Let us denote

(8)

a_{ij}^{n,K}:=\int_{K}{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n})\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

and

(9)

\gamma_{ij}^{n}:=\frac{{\tau_{n}}}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{K\subset S_{ij}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\left(d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}-a_{ij}^{n,K}\right),\quad i\neq j.

Theorem 2
(Discrete Maximum Principle).

Let
u_{h}^{n}=\sum_{i\in\cal V}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}\varphi_{i}
be an approximation at time
t_{n}
and
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}
be as in (
7
). Assume that the time-step
{\tau_{n}}
satisfies the following CFL condition

(10)

\max_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}),j\not=i}\gamma^{n}_{ij}({\tau_{n}})\leq{\rm cfl},

where
0<\rm cfl\leq 1
is a CFL number.
Then for any
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
we have

(11)

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},

where

(12)

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}:=\min_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})}{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n},\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}:=\max_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})}{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}.

Proof.

Using the definitions (
8
) and (
9
) and by noting that

a_{ii}^{n,K}=-\!\!\!\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),i\not=j}a_{ij}^{n,K},

the low-order scheme can be written in the following convex combination form:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{L},n+1}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}\Big[1-\frac{{\tau_{n}}}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\Big(\int_{K}{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n}){\cdot}\nabla\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),j\not=i}d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{{\tau_{n}}}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),j\not=i}{\mathsf{U}}_{j}\Big(\int_{K}{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n}){\cdot}\nabla\varphi_{j}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(1-\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}),j\not=i}\gamma^{n}_{ij}\Big){\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}+\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}),j\not=i}\gamma^{n}_{ij}\,{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}.

By construction,
|a_{ij}^{n,K}|\leq d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}
, and therefore

\gamma_{ij}^{n}\geq 0
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
. In addition, the coefficients
multiplying
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}
and
{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}
sum to one. Hence

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{L},n+1}
satisfies the discrete maximum principle
(
11
).

Remark 3
(CFL condition).

We define the local and global mesh sizes by

h_{K}:=\big(\max_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}(K)}\|\nabla\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\big)^{-1},\mbox{ and }h:=\min_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{K}.

Similarly, we define maximum wave speed by

\beta:=\sup_{w\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]}\|{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(w)\|.

We note the following upper bounds:

\int_{K}{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n}){\cdot}\nabla\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq\frac{\beta}{h}m_{i,K}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}},

and

d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}\leq\frac{\beta}{h}m_{i,K}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}.

Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}),j\not=i}\sum_{K\in S_{ij}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\left(d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K}-a_{ij}^{n,K}\right)\leq\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}2\frac{\beta}{h}\,2m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}=4\frac{\beta}{h}.

The time-step from the CFL condition (
10
) can be estimated as

{\tau_{n}}\geq\frac{\rm cfl}{4}\frac{h}{\beta}.

This gives a sufficient CFL restriction of the usual form
{\tau_{n}}=\mathcal{{\mathcal{O}}}(h/\beta)
.

4
High-order scheme

Since the limiting procedure discussed below does not require the low-order and high-order schemes to use the same stabilization terms, one can use any high-order finite element discretization to compute the high-order nodal values
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}
. Examples of popular stabilization techniques for finite element approximations include the entropy viscosity method
[
11
,
27
]
, the continuous interior penalty (CIP) method
[
3
,
1
]
, and residual-based artificial viscosity, or residual viscosity (RV), methods
[
28
,
26
]
. In this work, we use the RV method.

We already constructed an efficient local fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
infrastructure when defining the first-order scheme in the previous section. Therefore, we construct the high-order scheme by defining a higher-order local edge-viscosity coefficient analogous to (
5
), and subsequently a higher-order viscous bilinear form analogous to (
6
). Let
{\mathsf{R}}_{i}^{n}
denote the nodal residual of the high-order approximation at time
t_{n}
. In the present implementation, the element-local residual magnitude associated with node
i\in{\mathcal{I}}(K)
is

{\mathsf{R}}_{i}^{n,K}:=|{\mathsf{R}}_{i}^{n}|\int_{K}\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=|{\mathsf{R}}_{i}^{n}|m_{i,K}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}.

For every edge
(i,j)
contained in an element
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
, we define

(13)

d_{ij}^{{\rm R},n,K}=C_{\rm R}\max\left\{\frac{{\mathsf{R}}_{i}^{n,K}}{n(u_{h}^{n})},\frac{{\mathsf{R}}_{j}^{n,K}}{n(u_{h}^{n})}\right\},

where
C_{\rm R}\geq 0
is a user-defined parameter, and
n(u_{h}^{n})>0
is a normalization function chosen so that (
14
) has the same units as (
5
). For example, one may use

n(u_{h}^{n})=\|u_{h}^{n}-\overline{u_{h}^{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\epsilon\|u_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
\epsilon>0
is a small constant and

\overline{u_{h}^{n}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u_{h}^{n}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

The symmetric local residual-viscosity coefficient is then defined by

(14)

d_{ij}^{{\rm H},n,K}=\min\left\{d_{ij}^{{\rm L},n,K},d_{ij}^{{\rm R},n,K}\right\},\qquad i\neq j.

For every
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we define the element-local residual-based graph-viscosity contribution by

b_{K}^{{\rm H}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),j\neq i\end{subarray}}d_{ij}^{{\rm H},n,K}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}-{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}\big).

This definition is locally conservative.

The global artificial viscosity operator is obtained by summing all fine-cell contributions over the fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
cells contained in the support of
\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
:

(15)

b_{h}^{{\rm H}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})=\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}b_{K}^{{\rm H}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})=\sum_{K\subset S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathcal{I}}(K),j\neq i\end{subarray}}d_{ij}^{{\rm H},n,K}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}-{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}\big).

Since the residual may become very small in smooth regions, residual viscosity alone may add almost no stabilization there. In addition, residual-viscosity methods may perform suboptimally for even-order polynomial spaces; see, for instance,
[
2
, Sec. 5]
. As shown in
[
5
, Sec. 4.2]
, adding an additional linear stabilization term, such as CIP stabilization, can restore optimal convergence for even-order polynomial spaces.

In this work, we therefore employ the CIP stabilization of
[
3
,
1
]
in addition to the RV term (
15
):

s_{h}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i})=\gamma_{\mathrm{cip}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\mathrm{int}}}h_{F}^{2}\beta_{F}\int_{F}[\![\nabla u_{h}^{n}]\!][\![\nabla\varphi_{i}]\!]\,\,\mathrm{d}s,

where

[\![\nabla w]\!]_{F}:=\nabla w^{+}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{+}+\nabla w^{-}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{-}

denotes the jump of the normal derivative across an interior facet
F
of the coarse
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mesh. The facet wave speed is defined by

\beta_{F}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\big|{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n})^{+}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{+}\big|+\big|{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u_{h}^{n})^{-}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{-}\big|\right).

The facet size is computed as

h_{F}:=\frac{1}{2}(h_{K^{+}}+h_{K^{-}}),

where
K^{+}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
and
K^{-}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
are the two elements sharing the interior facet
F
, and
h_{K}=\operatorname{diam}(K)
. The penalty parameter is set to

\gamma_{\mathrm{cip}}=\frac{d^{2}}{10(1+p)^{4}}

in all simulations presented in this paper.

Finally, we compute the high-order nodal values
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}
by solving

(16)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}m_{ij}\frac{{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n}}{{\tau_{n}}}+\int_{S_{i}}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{f}}(u_{h}^{n})\varphi_{i}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+b_{h}^{\textrm{H}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})+s_{h}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i})=0,

for every
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proposition 4
.

The high-order scheme (
16
) is conservative with respect to the high-order mass matrix, that is,

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{H},n+1}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n}.

Proof.

Summing (
16
) over all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, using the partition-of-unity property of the basis functions, and using the conservation properties of

b_{h}^{\textrm{H}}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})

and

s_{h}(u_{h}^{n},\varphi_{i}),

proves the result.

5
Graph-Poisson limiter

Next, we want to find a relation of the high-order to the low-order scheme. Since, both solutions are carrying different masses therefore the quantity

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\big(m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}\big)\not=0.

We want to find a
\delta^{n+1}_{i}
such that the following vector
{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}
with the nodal values:

{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}=m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}\big)-m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\delta^{n+1},

is conservative, i.e.,
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}=0
. In fact, setting

\delta^{n+1}=\frac{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}\big)}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}},

gives conservation of
{{\mathsf{A}}}
.
We can write the following relationship of the high and low order solutions

(17)

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}+\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}{\mathsf{A}}^{n+1}_{i}+\delta^{n+1}.

Lemma 5
(Minimum-energy conservative flux reconstruction).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain and let

A\in L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfy

\int_{\Omega}A\,\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=0.

Then there exists a unique flux

{\boldsymbol{F}}\in\big[L^{2}(\Omega)\big]^{d}

minimizing the functional

\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

subject to the conservation constraint

\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{F}}=A

in
\Omega
,
and the homogeneous boundary condition

{\boldsymbol{F}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}=0

on
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover, the minimizer is given by

{\boldsymbol{F}}=-\nabla q,

where
q
is the solution of the Neumann problem

\displaystyle-\Delta q=A\text{ in }\Omega,\text{ with }{\partial}_{n}q=0\text{on }{\partial}\Omega.

Proof.

The zero average condition is necessary since, by the divergence theorem,

\int_{\Omega}A\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\boldsymbol{F}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{s}}=0.

Conversely, if
\int_{\Omega}A\,\mathrm{d}x=0
, the Neumann Poisson problem admits a solution
q
, unique up to an additive constant. Defining
{\boldsymbol{F}}=-\nabla q
, we obtain

\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{F}}=-\Delta q=A,

and the boundary condition gives

{\boldsymbol{F}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}=-{\partial}_{n}q=0.

Therefore
{\boldsymbol{F}}
is a conservative flux reconstruction of

A
.

To prove that
{\boldsymbol{F}}
minimizes the functional

\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

subject to the conservation constraint

\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{F}}=A
, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier
q

{\mathcal{L}}({\boldsymbol{F}},q)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|{\boldsymbol{F}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}+\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{F}}-A)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Now, taking the derivative of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
, integrating by parts and using the boundary conditions give

\frac{{\partial}{\mathcal{L}}({\boldsymbol{F}},q)}{{\partial}{\boldsymbol{F}}}=\int_{\Omega}\big({\boldsymbol{F}}-\nabla q\big){\cdot}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=0.

We use the discrete analogous of Lemma
5
, namely for given
{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}
with
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}=0
, we want find a conservative edge flux
{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ij}
such that

{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ij},\mbox{ and }{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ij}=-{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ji}.

Similarly to the continuous case, this flux can be written as

{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ij}={\mathsf{Q}}^{n+1}_{i}-{\mathsf{Q}}^{n+1}_{j},

where
{\mathsf{Q}}^{n+1}_{i}
is the nodal value of a vector
{\mathsf{Q}}^{n+1}
that solves the Graph Poisson equation

(18)

L{\mathsf{Q}}^{n+1}={{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}.

Consequently, we obtain the following relation between the high- and low-order solutions, which is convenient for the limiting procedure:

(19)

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}+\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}_{i})}{\mathsf{F}}^{n+1}_{ij}+\delta^{n+1}.

5.1
Convex limiting

Once the high- and low-order solutions have been related by a conservative graph flux, one can apply algebraic flux correction, flux-corrected transport, or convex limiting techniques to obtain an invariant-domain preserving high-order method. We use the convex limiting framework of
[
6
]
.

Recall that the graph-Poisson reconstruction gives the relation (
19
), where
{\mathsf{F}}_{ij}^{n+1}=-{\mathsf{F}}_{ji}^{n+1}
. We perform two limiting steps: first, we do not include the scalar mass correction term
\delta^{n+1}
to the nodal states. Instead the first limiting step is designed to preserve the local bounds (
12
). The resulting mass defect is corrected in a second, capacity-based redistribution step.

Let
\big(\ell_{ij}\big)_{i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}}
be a symmetric limiter matrix satisfying

0\leq\ell_{ij}=\ell_{ji}\leq 1.

We define the first limited state as

(20)

{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}+\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})}\ell_{ij}{\mathsf{F}}_{ij}^{n+1}.

Note that if
\ell_{ij}\equiv 0
, then (
20
) recovers the low-order state,

{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}.

If
\ell_{ij}\equiv 1
, then

{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm H},n+1}-\delta^{n+1}.

Thus, the fist limiting step does not in general recover the high-order state pointwise. This is done intentionally: the first step enforces the local bounds, while conservation with respect to the high-order mass is restored by the redistribution procedure described below.

Let
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}
and
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}
denote the local bounds defined in (
12
). We construct the limiter so that

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}.

Next, let us denote the number of nodes in the support of
\varphi_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
by
\textrm{card}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1})}
and define:

\lambda_{j}(i):=\frac{1}{\operatorname{card}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})-1}

for every

j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})\setminus\{i\}.

For each edge contribution, we define

{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}:=\frac{{\mathsf{F}}_{ij}^{n+1}}{\lambda_{j}(i)m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}.

Then the limited update can be written as the convex combination

(21)

{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})\setminus\{i\}}\lambda_{j}(i)\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}+\ell_{ij}{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}\big).

For each directed edge
i\to j
, set

\theta_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}}{|{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}|+\epsilon_{i}}\right\},&{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}<0,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}}{|{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}|+\epsilon_{i}}\right\},&{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}>0,\\[11.38109pt]
1,&{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}=0.\end{cases}

The symmetric edge limiter is then

(22)

\ell_{ij}:=\min(\theta_{ij},\theta_{ji}).

Theorem 6
(Invariant-domain property of the first limiting step).

Let
{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}
be defined by (
20
) with the limiter (
22
). Assume that the low-order method is invariant-domain preserving under the CFL condition (
10
). Then

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},\qquad\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Proof.

Fix
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
. By construction, for every
j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})\setminus\{i\}
,

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}+\ell_{ij}{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}.

Since the coefficients
\lambda_{j}(i)
are nonnegative and sum to one, the convex combination satisfies

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}\lambda_{j}(i)\left({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm L},n+1}+\ell_{ij}{\mathsf{P}}_{ij}^{n+1}\right)\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}.

Using the idendity (
21
) the proof is followed.

Remark 7
(Relaxed bounds).

The theorem above gives a strict local maximum principle. While this property is useful for stability, enforcing strict monotonicity at every node may destroy high-order accuracy. This is consistent with Godunov’s theorem, which states that linear monotone schemes for scalar conservation laws are at most first-order accurate. Although the present method is nonlinear, strict limiting may still activate near smooth extrema and reduce the observed convergence rate.

To retain high-order accuracy in smooth regions, we use a mesh-dependent relaxation of the local bounds, following the approach of
[
6
]
. Let
d
be the space dimension and define

(23)

r_{i}:=\left(\frac{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}{|\Omega|}\right)^{\alpha/d}.

On shape-regular meshes,
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\sim h_{i}^{d}
, and therefore
r_{i}\sim h_{i}^{\alpha}
. In the numerical experiments below we use
\alpha=3/2
. The relaxed bounds are then defined by

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min,{\rm rel}}:={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}-C_{\rm rel}r_{i},\qquad{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max,{\rm rel}}:={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}+C_{\rm rel}r_{i},

where
C_{\rm rel}:=C_{\rm rel}(p)>0
is independent of
h
, but can be dependent of polynomial degree
p
.

Consequently, the first limited state satisfies

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}-C_{\rm rel}r_{i}\leq{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}+C_{\rm rel}r_{i}.

Since
r_{i}\to 0
under mesh refinement, the strict invariant-domain bounds are recovered asymptotically.

Remark 8
(Solution of the graph-Laplacian problem).

The Graph-Poisson flux reconstruction requires the solution of one graph-Laplacian problem (
18
) each time the limiter is applied. The corresponding discrete graph Laplacian is constructed once on the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
graph. More precisely, for every fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
edge
(i,j)
, the local contribution

\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{array}\right]

is added to the global graph matrix. Thus, the resulting matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite and has the constant as its nullspace, i.e.,
L\mathbf{1}=0
, since the right hand side satisfies
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{{\mathsf{A}}}^{n+1}_{i}=0
, therefore the graph-Laplacian problem is solvable up to an additive constant. In the implementation, a constant nullspace is attached to the PETSc matrix and the system is solved using a Krylov method with algebraic multigrid preconditioning.

The graph matrix depends only on the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
connectivity and is therefore independent of the polynomial degree
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
of the high-order approximation. Consequently, the sparsity pattern of this auxiliary solve does not grow with
k
. Moreover, since the matrix is fixed throughout the computation, it can be assembled once and reused whenever the limiter is called. In our computations, with appropriate PETSc preconditioning, this graph-Laplacian solve was not observed to be the computationally dominant part of the limiting algorithm.

5.2
Mass redistribution

In this section, we discuss how the remaining mass defect can be redistributed over the domain so that the final limited solution has the same fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass as the high-order update while still satisfying the prescribed bounds. Related ideas appear in classical positivity-preserving DG methods and finite element FCT limiters; see, for example,
[
23
, Sec. 4]
. Bound-preserving mass corrections based on allowable capacities have also been used more recently in other discretization frameworks. We refer to
[
29
, Sec. 3.3]
, where such a correction is applied in a finite volume setting, and to
[
4
, Sec. 3]
, where a related idea is used for DG methods. In these works, a global mass defect is computed after a bound-preserving correction, and the remaining mass is redistributed among cells or degrees of freedom with available admissible capacity.

For continuous finite elements, we refer to the recent work of
[
16
, Sec. 2]
, where a similar mass correction is performed using the lumped mass matrix of the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
approximation space. As discussed above, this is natural for Bernstein polynomial bases, for which the lumped masses are nonnegative, but it becomes restrictive for standard high-order Lagrange bases, for which some lumped masses may be negative.

In the present work, this restriction is avoided by performing the limiting and mass redistribution on the fine
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
submesh induced by the high-order nodal points. The corresponding lumped masses
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
are strictly positive independently of the polynomial degree of the high-order approximation.

Let us start by introducing the following definition and hypothesis for the high-order update.

Definition 9
(Admissible mass interval).

Let
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}
and
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}
denote the nodal lower and upper admissible bounds at time level
t_{n+1}
, and let
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}>0
be the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped masses. We define the admissible mass interval by

I_{\rm adm}^{n+1}:=\left[M_{\min}^{n+1},M_{\max}^{n+1}\right],

where

M_{\min}^{n+1}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min},\qquad M_{\max}^{n+1}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}.

Equivalently,
I_{\rm adm}^{n+1}
is the interval of admissible fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped masses associated with the nodal bounds

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{W}}_{i}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},\qquad i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

In particular, every nodally admissible state
{\mathsf{W}}
satisfies

M_{1}({\mathsf{W}}):=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{W}}_{i}\in I_{\rm adm}^{n+1}.

Remark 10
(Degenerate admissible mass interval).

The admissible mass interval
has zero width if and only if

M_{\min}^{n+1}=M_{\max}^{n+1}.

Since

M_{\max}^{n+1}-M_{\min}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\left({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\right),

and since
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}>0
and

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}\geq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}
, this is equivalent to

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}={\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},

i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

For scalar transport problems with strict local patch bounds

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}=\min_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})}{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n},

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}=\max_{j\in{\mathcal{I}}(S_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}})}{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{n},

exact degeneracy can occur only if the nodal solution is constant on each connected component of the graph. In particular, on a connected mesh, a degenerate admissible mass interval corresponds to a constant nodal state. In that case, a conservative and constant-preserving high-order predictor has the same fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass as the old state, and the compatibility condition is automatically satisfied.

This exact degeneracy should be distinguished from the asymptotic behavior of the interval width as
h\to 0
. Even for nonconstant smooth solutions, the strict patch-bound interval may shrink under mesh refinement, since local patch oscillations are typically of order
h
.

Hypothesis 11
(Mass compatibility of the high-order target).

Let

M_{1}({\mathsf{W}}):=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{W}}_{i},\qquad M_{k}({\mathsf{W}}):=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}{\mathsf{W}}_{i}

denote the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped mass and the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass, respectively. We assume that the high-order predictor
{\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}
satisfies the following properties.

1.

Conservative with respect to the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass:

(24)

M_{k}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})=M_{k}({\mathsf{U}}^{n}).

2.

The discrepancy between the two mass measurements of the relevant states,

E_{h}({\mathsf{W}}):=(M_{1}-M_{k})({\mathsf{W}}),

is controlled, i.e.,

(25)

|E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})|+|E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})|\leq\eta_{h}^{n+1},

where
\eta_{h}^{n+1}\rightarrow 0
as
h\rightarrow 0
.

3.

The admissible mass interval has enough margin to absorb this defect:

(26)

\eta_{h}^{n+1}\leq\min\left\{M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})-M_{\min}^{n+1},M_{\max}^{n+1}-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})\right\}.

Under Hypothesis
11
the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass of the high-order target belongs to the admissible mass interval:

(27)

M_{\min}^{n+1}\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})\leq M_{\max}^{n+1}.

The residual-viscosity method presented above is conservative with respect to the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass; hence it satisfies (
24
). Proposition
12
provides sufficient conditions under which this
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
-mass conservation implies compatibility with the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
admissible mass interval. In particular, if the high-order predictor is convergent and the mass-measure defect between
M_{1}
and
M_{k}
tends to zero under mesh refinement, then the quantity
\eta_{h}^{n+1}
in (
25
) is mesh-dependent and satisfies
\eta_{h}^{n+1}\to 0
as
h\to 0
. Consequently, if the admissible mass interval has a nonzero margin, then (
26
) is satisfied for sufficiently small
h
.

Proposition 12
(Asymptotic compatibility of the high-order mass).

Assume that the high-order predictor is conservative with respect to the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass, and convergent to entropy solution. Assume that the admissible mass interval from Definition
9
has nonzero width. Then, for sufficiently small
h
the high-order target mass is compatible with the admissible interval (
27
).

Proof.

First part of the proof is estimating the mass-measure defect

E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}).

Let
u(t_{n})
and
u(t_{n+1})
denote the exact solution at time levels
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
, and let
{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n}
and
{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1}
be their nodal values.
Then

E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})=E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})+E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1}).

We estimate the two terms separately.

For the first term, since both mass functionals are consistent approximations of the exact integral, we have, for smooth
u
,

|E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})|=|M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})-M_{k}({\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})|\leq Ch^{r},

where
r>0
depends on the accuracy of the quadrature rules defining the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped mass and the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass.

The uniformly comparability condition (
4
) gives

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left|m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}-m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}}\right|\leq C,

for some
C>0
independent of
h
, thus

|E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})|\leq C\|({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm ex}^{n+1})\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq Ch^{p},

since, the high-order solution is convergent for some
p>0
. Therefore,

|E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})|\leq Ch^{r}+Ch^{p}.

Now, we use the fact that the approximation is conservative with respect to the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass:

M_{k}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})=M_{k}({\mathsf{U}}^{n}).

Then,

\displaystyle M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})

\displaystyle=E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-E_{h}({\mathsf{U}}^{n}).

We obtained that, if both
{\mathsf{U}}^{n}
and
{\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}
are convergent approximations of smooth exact states, then

(28)

|M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})|\leq Ch^{\min(r,p)}.

Let us denote the width of the admissible mass interval from Definition
9
by

(29)

d_{h}^{n+1}:=\min\left\{M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})-M_{\min}^{n+1},M_{\max}^{n+1}-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})\right\}>0.

Then, the estimate (
28
) gives us

M_{\min}^{n+1}\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})-Ch^{\min\{p,r\}}\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})+Ch^{\min\{p,r\}}\leq M_{\max}^{n+1}.

This proves (
27
).

The state
{\mathsf{V}}^{n+1}
computed by (
20
) is invariant-domain preserving. However, limiting the conservative graph fluxes may change the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
lumped mass relative to the high-order target. We therefore apply a second, purely nodal, mass-redistribution step. We begin with the following definition.

Definition 13
(Sufficient redistribution capacity).

Let

(30)

\Delta M^{n+1}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm H},n+1}-{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\big).

be the mass defect after the convex limiting step, and let

R_{i}^{+}:=m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}-{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\big),\qquad R_{i}^{-}:=m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\big({\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}-{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\big).

denote the positive and negative nodal capacities. We say that the available redistribution capacity is sufficient if

\Delta M^{n+1}\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R_{i}^{+}\text{ when }\Delta M^{n+1}>0,

and

-\Delta M^{n+1}\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R_{i}^{-}\text{ when }\Delta M^{n+1}<0.

If
\Delta M^{n+1}=0
, the capacity condition is satisfied trivially.

The capacities
R_{i}^{\pm}\geq 0
measure how much mass node
i
can still safely receive or lose without violating the bounds. We start by defining parameters corresponding to negative and positive mass defects. We set

(31)

\displaystyle\alpha^{+}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{\Delta M^{n+1}}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R_{i}^{+}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mbox{ when }\Delta M^{n+1}>0,

\displaystyle\alpha^{-}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{-\Delta M^{n+1}}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R_{i}^{-}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mbox{ when }\Delta M^{n+1}<0.

Then, update solution as

(32)

{\mathsf{U}}^{n+1}_{i}=\begin{cases}{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}+\frac{\alpha^{+}R_{i}^{+}}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}},\quad&\Delta M^{n+1}>0,\\
{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}-\frac{\alpha^{-}R_{i}^{-}}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}},\quad&\Delta M^{n+1}<0,\\
{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1},\quad&\Delta M^{n+1}=0.\end{cases}

Next, we prove that
{\mathsf{U}}^{n+1}
computed by (
32
) preserves the invariant-domain property and is conservative with respect to the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass.

Proposition 14
(Invariant-domain preserving mass redistribution).

Assume that
{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}
is invariant domain preserving, i.e.,
{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\in[{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min},{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}]
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Then the redistributed state
{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n+1}
also satisfies

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},\qquad\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Proof.

Suppose
\Delta M^{n+1}>0
. Then

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n+1}={\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}+\alpha^{+}\big({\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}-{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\big)=(1-\alpha^{+}){\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}+\alpha^{+}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max}.

Since
0\leq\alpha^{+}\leq 1
, the result is a convex combination of two values in the admissible interval. The case
\Delta M^{n+1}<0
is analogous because

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n+1}=(1-\alpha^{-}){\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}+\alpha^{-}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}.

Proposition 15
(Mass conservation of the redistributed state).

Let
{\mathsf{V}}^{n+1}
denote the state obtained after the convex limiting step. Assume
{\mathsf{V}}^{n+1}
satisfies invariant domain property, namely

{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\min}\leq{\mathsf{V}}_{i}^{n+1}\leq{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n,\max},\qquad i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Assume the high-order target mass belongs to the admissible mass interval (
27
). Then, the available capacities are sufficient in the sense of Definition
13
. Consequently, the redistributed state satisfies

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}^{{\rm H},n+1}.

Proof.

We first prove that under the assumption of the proposition, the available capacities are sufficient for the mass distribution.

In fact, when
\Delta M^{n+1}>0
, using (
30
) and (
27
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta M^{n+1}=M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})\leq M_{\max}-M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R^{+}_{i}.

Similarly, when
\Delta M^{n+1}<0
, we get

\displaystyle-\Delta M^{n+1}=-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})+M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})\leq-M_{\min}+M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R^{-}_{i}.

Thus, the admissible mass compatibility condition implies sufficient capacity.

We now prove mass conservation of the redistributed state. Consider first the case
\Delta M^{n+1}>0
. Then, the limited update (
32
) is

{\mathsf{U}}^{n+1}_{i}={\mathsf{V}}^{n+1}_{i}+\frac{1}{m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}}\alpha^{+}R^{+}_{i}.

Multipliying both side to
m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}
and summing over
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
gives

M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n+1})=M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})+\alpha^{+}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}R^{+}_{i}.

Now, using the definition of
\alpha^{+}
from (
31
), we obtain

\displaystyle M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n+1})=

\displaystyle\ M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})+\Delta M^{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})+\Big(M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-M_{1}({\mathsf{V}}^{n+1})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1}).

The case
\Delta M^{n+1}<0
is analogous. Finally, if
\Delta M^{n+1}=0
, then no redistribution is needed and the identity is immediate.

Proposition 16
(Compatibility under relaxed bounds).

Assume that the high-order predictor is conservative with respect to the
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
mass and satisfies

\eta_{h}^{n+1}:=\left|M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})\right|=o(\rho_{h}),\qquad h\to 0,

where

\rho_{h}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}r_{i},

where
r_{i}
is given by (
23
).
Let
I_{\rm adm,rel}^{n+1}
denote the admissible mass interval generated by the relaxed bounds. Then, for sufficiently small
h
,

M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})\in I_{\rm adm,rel}^{n+1}.

Consequently, the available redistribution capacity associated with the relaxed bounds is sufficient, and the redistributed state can be made conservative with respect to the fine-
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
mass of the high-order predictor.

Proof.

Since
{\mathsf{U}}^{n}
satisfies the strict bounds, it also satisfies the relaxed bounds. Hence

M_{\min,{\rm rel}}^{n+1}\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})\leq M_{\max,{\rm rel}}^{n+1}.

Moreover, the relaxation enlarges the admissible mass interval by

C_{\rm rel}\rho_{h}=C_{\rm rel}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}m_{i}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}r_{i},

on both sides. Therefore,

\min\left\{M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})-M_{\min,{\rm rel}}^{n+1},M_{\max,{\rm rel}}^{n+1}-M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{n})\right\}\geq C_{\rm rel}\rho_{h}.

Since
\eta_{h}^{n+1}=o(\rho_{h})
, we have, for sufficiently small
h
,

\eta_{h}^{n+1}\leq C_{\rm rel}\rho_{h}.

Thus,

M_{\min,{\rm rel}}^{n+1}\leq M_{1}({\mathsf{U}}^{{\rm H},n+1})\leq M_{\max,{\rm rel}}^{n+1}.

This proves the proposition.
The last assertion follows from the equivalence between mass compatibility and sufficient redistribution capacity, and from Proposition
15
.

In contrast to traditional limiting techniques such as

7
,
6
,
24
]

the high-order solution

In this section we demonstrate the proposed numerical algorithm for solving several linear and nonlinear problems from scalar conservation laws. All computations are carried out using the FEniCSx project, an open-source finite element library. The implementation uses C++ interface of the FEniCSx project and written in a way that it is dimensional and polynimial degree independent. In this work, we restrict the numerical tests in two space dimensions and we use polynomial spaces from first to forth order.

In all computations using relaxed bounds, we set
\alpha=3/2
.
For the three-body rotation test, we use

C_{\rm rel}(p)=1

C_{\rm R}=1
for transport benchmarks,
C_{\rm R}=4
for Burgers’ benchmark, and
C_{\rm R}=10

We use fully unstructured meshes generated by a Delaunay triangulation in all presented benchmarks. All presented convergence histories use relative norms in

or

6.3
Burgers equation

In this and next sections, we apply the above algorithm to solve nonlinear problems. We consider the following Burger’s equation in two space dimensions

\Omega=(-0.25,1.75)^{2}
:

(33)

\partial_{t}u+\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{f}}(u)=0,

where
{\boldsymbol{f}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}(u^{2},u^{2})
and

u_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }|x_{1}-\frac{1}{2}|\leq 1\text{ and }|x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\leq 1,\\
-0.75,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The exact solution for this problem is described in
[
14
, Sec. 6.1]
. This is a difficult task for numerical methods because the solution contains a sonic point, i.e., there exists
u
such that
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}(u)=0
. The lack of viscosity due to the sonic point can lead to convergence to a non-entropy solution, see e.g.,
[
14
, Sec. 6]
. We solve the problem up to
t=0.75
with CFL number 0.5 on
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{4}
polynomial spaces.

We solve the problem using the proposed limiting algorithm. For the high-order baseline, we compare two choices. In the first case, we use the residual-viscosity method (
16
). In the second case, we set
C_{\rm R}=0
in (
16
), so that the residual-viscosity term is removed and the high-order method is stabilized only by the CIP term. We refer to the latter method as CIP.
The results from numerical simulations are collected in Figure
3
. The first row depicts graph-Poisson limiting solutions for all polynomial spaces where the high-order method is stabilized using RV and CIP stabilzations. The second row of the figure present the limited solutions where the high-order method utilizes only using CIP stabilization. One can see the choice of high-order method is important for nonlinear problems.

Table
5
reports the convergence result in the relative
L^{1}
-norm for all polynomial spaces. We observe that the convergence rate is close to 1 for RV stabilization, but it detoriates for CIP stabilazation only for higher polynomial spaces.

6.4
KPP problem

In our final numerical validation, we consider the so-called KPP problem of Kurganov, Petrova, and Popov
[
19
]
. We solve (
33
) on the square domain

\Omega=[-2,2]\times[-2.5,1.5],

with the nonlinear flux

{\boldsymbol{f}}(u)=(\sin u,\cos u)^{\top},

and the initial condition

u_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{cases}3.5\pi,&\text{if }x^{2}+y^{2}<1,\\
0.25\pi,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The solution of this problem contains composite shock waves. This benchmark is known to be challenging because some high-order methods may converge to a non-entropic weak solution. For examples of such behavior, see
[
19
]
, where several finite volume methods are discussed, and
[
32
]
, where high-order finite difference methods are tested.

We again run the algorithm with and without the RV term. The results are shown in Figure
4
for two mesh resolutions. In both cases, the simulations are performed with CFL number
0.5
.

The results show that the limited CIP method does not converge to the correct entropy solution for this benchmark, whereas the limited residual-viscosity method does. This demonstrates the importance of the residual-viscosity stabilization for selecting the entropy solution in the presence of composite shock waves.
