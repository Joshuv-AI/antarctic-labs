The Game Changer Problem: Controlling Equilibria with Discrete Rewards
Brandon Han, Young Wu, Shiyun Cheng, Xiaojin Zhu

Abstract
We introduce the game changer problem, where an external designer modifies a game’s reward matrix to make a target pure action profile the unique equilibrium, subject to the constraint that all entries of the reward matrix come from a finite set. We give simple feasibility characterizations for two-player zero-sum games and general-sum games, and the discrete reward structure yields exact optimality and enables efficient dynamic programming algorithms, providing a sharper alternative to prior continuous reward redesign formulations based on linear programming.



1
Introduction

We consider the task of controlling joint player behaviors by changing payoffs. In many real multi-agent systems, payoffs cannot be arbitrary real numbers; they must come from a small, meaningful set. This discreteness creates a new algorithmic problem and, in fact, enables exact optimal redesign via efficient dynamic programming rather than generic linear-programming formulations. In our setup, there is a new system administrator (the game changer), and
n
strategic agents (players), for example, a company with a new CEO and
n
employees, a sports competition with a new tournament organizer and
n
competitors, or even a kingdom with a new king and
n
subjects. The existing system is operated as an
n
-player general-sum game with reward function
\mathcal{R}^{o}
, set by the previous administrator and played by the agents.
\mathcal{R}^{o}_{i}\left(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{-i}\right)
is the payoff to agent
i\in\left[n\right]
with joint action
a=\left(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{-i}\right)
. Suppose it is known that the players are rational and seek a Nash Equilibrium
\left(\mathrm{NE}\right)
. While a possibly mixed
\mathrm{NE}
is guaranteed to exist, there could be many different NEs, so it is still hard for the new administrator to predict how the game will actually play out. In addition, the new administrator may have a different value
V\left(\mathbf{a}\right)
for a joint action
\mathbf{a}
than the "social welfare"
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)
. For example, the administrator may prefer the joint action in which all agents work together on a larger project rather than the current equilibrium, in which the agents work on smaller projects on their own. The NEs under the old game
\mathcal{R}^{o}
may have low values. Thus, the new administrator wants to reform the system (or change the game, hence the name game changer). More importantly, our paper introduces a novel consideration when changing the game, which is that the new game rewards only contain entries from a finite set. In our system administrator story, entries could be restricted to
5
valid values, say,
A,B,C,D,F
for an agent’s evaluation score. In the other examples, the discrete-valued entries represent integer hourly wages in the company environment, a finite number of ratings in sports competitions, or the number of gold coins in the kingdom example. The discrete reward constraint is not merely a modeling restriction: because finite sets have a minimum positive gap between distinct values, strict inequalities can be handled exactly by adjacent reward levels, and this avoids the relaxations in prior continuous formulations and allows us to compute the exact optimal redesigned games.

Concretely, the
n
players and their action spaces are kept the same, but the game changer changes the payoff function
\mathcal{R}^{o}
to
\mathcal{R}
with the following considerations,

1.

What actions the players will choose under
\mathcal{R}
should be highly predictable. For example, it would be nice if
\mathcal{R}
has a unique pure
\mathrm{NE}

\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
.

2.

What action profile the players use is valuable to the game changer; for example,
V\left(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right)
is high in the unique pure
\mathrm{NE}
.

3.

The game changer may have preferences on
\mathcal{R}
itself regardless of the players’ behaviors. For example, the reform is palatable if
\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1}
is small,
\mathcal{R}
should contain only entries from a discrete set, and in fact, one focus of the present paper is an extreme form of the latter:
\mathcal{R}
must take value in a given discrete (and potentially finite) set
\Omega
.

Our problem is adjacent to mechanism design and implementation theory. Our problem does not involve players with private types, and rather than designing message spaces, allocation rules, or transfers, we keep the players and action spaces fixed and directly redesign the reward matrices. We also do not consider the game changer as one of the players due to its asymmetric role in the problem,

•

The game changer’s action set is qualitatively different and includes all game reward functions
\mathcal{R}
with some fixed action space
\mathcal{A}
, which is not an ordinary move like a finite set
\mathcal{A}_{i}
for player
i
,

•

The game changer has full commitment power, and there is no strategic uncertainty from the players’ perspective. In particular, even in the Markov game setting with sequential interactions, the game changer cannot adaptively change the rules of the game.

The main contributions of this paper are,

•

We formulate the discrete game-changer problem, where an external game changer redesigns a reward matrix using only values from a prescribed finite set
\Omega
, with the goal of inducing a unique target equilibrium while minimizing the deviation from the original game.

•

We provide necessary and sufficient feasibility conditions:
|\Omega|\geq 3
for zero-sum games, for installing a unique strict pure Nash equilibrium, which also yields a unique Nash equilibrium, correlated equilibrium, or coarse correlated equilibrium; and
|\Omega|\geq 2
for general-sum games, for installing a strict dominant strategy equilibrium.

•

We derive an exact dynamic-programming algorithm for the discrete game-changer problem, avoiding generic integer/linear programming formulations, thereby efficiently computing the optimal redesigned reward matrix. Unlike prior continuous reward-redesign formulations, the discrete setting gives exact optimality rather than approximate optimality through strict-inequality relaxations.

It is also easy to see the security relevance of this task: an adversarial attacker may play the role of the administrator (game changer) and hack the payoffs to control agent (player) behavior, and our work exposes the quantitative game-theoretic threat. This will enable defenders to target specifically these types of attacks. For instance, by diligently checking and catching small changes to
\mathcal{R}^{o}
, the defender effectively imposes a high cost on
\left\|\mathcal{R}^{o}-\mathcal{R}\right\|
and may force the attacker to give up because
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{o}
will be the optimal solution to the reform. But a benevolent policymaker can also benefit from the present paper by computing the optimal new policy
\mathcal{R}
to encourage beneficial behaviors (for example, all players volunteer in the volunteer game).

Problem Setup

We call the administrator’s reform "the game changer problem" because it changes the old game
\mathcal{R}^{o}
to a new game
\mathcal{R}
. We define the general form of this problem below.

First we define the players by the tuple
\left(n,\left\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{S}\right)
where
\mathcal{S}
is the solution concept they seek, for example, Nash Equilibrium
\left(\mathrm{NE}\right)
, Dominant Strategy Equilibrium
\left(\mathrm{DSE}\right)
, Correlated Equilibrium
\left(\mathrm{CE}\right)
, and so on. Given a game
\mathcal{R}
, we also overload notation and let
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
denote the set of equilibria under that solution concept. For example,
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
can be the set of NEs (pure and mixed) of
\mathcal{R}
.

We characterize the game changer’s three considerations with three functions, which are inputs to our problem,

1.

P\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)
is the predictability of player behaviors. A simple example is
P\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)=-\left|\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right|
, the negative cardinality, for instance, the number of distinct NEs. The negation is to make predictability high when the solution is simple;

2.

V\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)
is the extrinsic value to the game changer on how the game will be played out. If
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
is a unique pure
\mathrm{NE}
with joint action
\mathbf{a},V\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)=V\left(\mathbf{a}\right)
specifies the value to the game changer when the players are essentially forced to play the only rational option
\mathbf{a}
. For example,
\mathbf{a}_{1}=\mathbf{a}_{2}=...=\mathbf{a}_{n}=\text{\;volunteer\;}
may have higher value to the game changer. Note
V
is an input to our problem, and is in general distinct from the sum of payoffs to the players
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)
. This flexibility sets us apart from the standard notion of social welfare maximization. When
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
has multiple equilibria,
V
can be the average or worst-case value of these members.

3.

C\left(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{o}\right)
is the cost of installing the new game
\mathcal{R}
, potentially with reference to the old game
\mathcal{R}^{o}
. For example,
C\left(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{o}\right)=\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1.}

With these definitions, the game-changer problem is the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\mathcal{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{\times_{i=1}^{n}\left|\mathcal{A}_{i}\right|}}P\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)+V\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)-C\left(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{o}\right)

(1)

The general problem (
1
) is powerful but difficult to solve for arbitrary
P,V,C
functions. In this paper, we focus on a special yet meaningful case often encountered in applications.

We impose two restrictions,

1.

The new game
\mathcal{R}
must induce a unique pure strategy solution:
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)=\left\{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right\}
. For example, the new game must have a unique pure
\mathrm{NE}
. It does not matter how many solutions the old game has. This restriction is highly desirable to the game changer: the game changer will know precisely how the players will behave under
\mathcal{R}
. We may stylize the predictability function as an infinite indicator such that
P\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right)=0
if
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
is a unique pure joint action, and
-\infty
otherwise.

2.

The entries in
\mathcal{R}
must be in a given discrete set
\Omega
. For example, in win-lose-tie games such as rock-paper-scissors, it is natural to map each outcome to a different number so
\Omega=\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
. It is less natural (though not impossible) to allow entries of
\mathcal{R}
to take arbitrary real values. Another justification stems from the security angle, where an attack with "strange" payoff values after reward poisoning may be easily detected, but an attack that stays within
\Omega
can be hard to detect. We may stylize the cost function as
C\left(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{o}\right)=\infty
if some elements in
R\neq\Omega
, and
\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1}
otherwise. We consider finite sets
\Omega
, but in the appendix, we explain how problems with
\Omega=\mathbb{Z}
are equivalent to problems with finite
\Omega
.

Under these restrictions, we arrive at the Fully Predictable Discrete Game Change Problem defined by the tuple
\left(\mathcal{R}^{o},\Omega,n,\left\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{S}\right)
. Since the solution to
\mathcal{R}
must be a unique joint action
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
, we can first enumerate (pure) joint actions
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
, then, conditioned on it, find the optimal
\mathcal{R}
with discrete entries. Furthermore, we can separate the cost function
C\left(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{o}\right)
into a hard discrete constraint and a soft distance-to
\mathcal{R}^{o}
norm, that is,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}}\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle\,V\left(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right)-\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1}

(2)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)=\left\{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)\in\Omega,\forall\;\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}

The optimization (
2
) for the Fully Predictable Discrete Game Changer Problem has a difficult hard constraint
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)=\left\{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right\}
, namely the solution to
\mathcal{R}
must be the unique pure strategy
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
. Our approach depends on whether
\mathcal{R}
should be a two-player zero-sum game or
n
-player general-sum game,

•

If
\mathcal{R}
is a two-player zero-sum game, we aim to install a strict
\mathrm{NE}
at
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
.

•

If
\mathcal{R}
is an
n
-player general-sum game, we aim to install a strict
\mathrm{DSE}
at
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
.

In the following sections, we decompose (
2
) into independent inner problems indexed by
a^{\dagger}
, and focus on efficient algorithms to solve the inner problem exactly; the outer problem can be solved by enumeration over the (finite number of) pure action profiles.

4
Game Changer on Two-Player Zero-sum Games

Our first setting focuses on two-player zero-sum games. Here, we require both the given reward matrix
\mathcal{R}^{o}
and the output matrix
\mathcal{R}
to be zero-sum. Our strategy is to iterate through every pure strategy
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
and to install each
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
as a strict Nash equilibrium.

Definition 1
(Strict Nash Equilibrium)

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the reward matrix of a zero-sum normal form game with action set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
. A pure joint strategy
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
is a strict Nash Equilibrium (
\mathrm{sNE}
) if for every
a_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
, we have,

\displaystyle a_{1}\neq a^{\dagger}_{1}\implies\mathcal{R}\left(a_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right)<\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle a_{2}\neq a^{\dagger}_{2}\implies\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a_{2}\right)>\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right).

Mathematically, we are solving the following minimization equation.

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}}\max_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle\,V\left(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right)-\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1}

(3)

s.t.

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}\left(a_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right)<\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right)\quad\forall a_{1}\neq a^{\dagger}_{1}

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a_{2}\right)>\mathcal{R}\left(a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2}\right)\quad\forall a_{2}\neq a^{\dagger}_{2}

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)\in\Omega\quad\forall\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}

This approach has several advantages. One such advantage is that the only additional constraints are linear inequalities, allowing us to directly apply integer programming. Moreover, every inequality involves the term
\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right)
. This additional structure allows us to develop an efficient, closed-form algorithm for finding optimal solutions. We discuss this algorithm in Section
4.1

Another advantage is feasibility. We show a sufficient and necessary condition for the existence of a feasible solution to (
3

2

The most important advantage of our approach is its strength. With the reward matrix constructed by our algorithm, the game changer can simultaneously control players seeking a wide variety of solution concepts beyond those that seek Nash equilibria. Notably, the game changer may control players that follow strict Nash Equilibria (sNE), pure Nash Equilibria (pNE), Nash Equilibria (NE), Correlated Equilibria (CE), and Coarse Correlated Equilibria (CCE). This stems from the fact that
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
is a strict Nash equilibrium of a finite two-player zero-sum game if and only if it is also the unique NE, CE, and CCE.

Lemma 3
(
A-Zero
Implications for Game Changer Feasibility and Optimality)

If
\mathcal{R}
is a feasible solution to (
3
), then
\mathcal{R}
is a feasible solution to (
2
) for
S\in\left\{\mathrm{sNE},\mathrm{pNE},\mathrm{NE},\mathrm{CE},\mathrm{CCE}\right\}
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{R}
is an optimal solution to (

\mathcal{R}
is an optimal solution to (

Proof

In zero-sum games, CE and CCE cannot improve beyond the minimax value, and if a pure action profile is a strict saddle point, all equilibrium distributions must put probability one on it. Conversely, since all NEs are also CEs and CCEs,

being a unique CE or CCE implies that it is also a unique NE.

5
Game Changer on General-sum Games

We continue with general-sum games, permitting an arbitrary number of players. Here, we drop the zero-sum requirement on the output reward matrices (technically, it is a reward tensor/function). Note that a zero-sum game may be given as
\mathcal{R}^{o}
, but we will treat it as a general-sum game. Our strategy is to iterate through every pure strategy
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
and to install each
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}
as a strict dominant strategy equilibrium.

Definition 6
(Dominant Strategy Equilibrium)

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the reward function of a normal form game with action set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}_{n}
. A pure joint strategy
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
is a strict dominant strategy equilibrium (
\mathrm{sDSE}
) if for every player
i\in\left[{n}\right]
and every action
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle a_{i}\neq a^{\dagger}_{i}\implies\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(a_{i},a_{-i}\right)<\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(a^{\dagger}_{i},a_{-i}\right),\forall a_{-i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-i}.

Mathematically, we are solving the following minimization equation.

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}}\max_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle\,V\left(\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\right)-\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{o}\right\|_{1}

(14)

s.t.

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(a_{i},a_{-i}\right)<\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(a^{\dagger}_{i},a_{-i}\right)\quad\forall i\in\left[{n}\right],\forall a_{i}\neq a^{\dagger}_{i},\forall a_{-i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-i}

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)\in\Omega\quad\forall\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}

This approach shares several advantages with our zero-sum case. The only additional constraints are linear inequalities, allowing us to directly apply integer programming. We discuss an efficient closed-form algorithm in Section
5.1
. We also have a similar sufficient and necessary condition for the existence of a feasible solution to (
14
). Again, we assume that each player has at least
2
actions since degenerate one-action cases are trivial.

Proposition 7
(
A-General
Feasibility)

(
14
) has a feasible solution if and only if
\left|\Omega\right|\geq 2
.

Proof

We follow a similar proof by construction. Take
\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}\in\Omega
and pick any
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{A}
. Construct the following reward matrix.

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}\quad\forall i\in\left[{n}\right]

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(\mathbf{a}\right)=\begin{cases}\omega_{2}&\text{if $a_{i}=a^{\dagger}_{i}$}\\
\omega_{1}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The strength advantage for our general sum case requires extra consideration. Without the zero-sum restriction, it is difficult to determine whether a reward matrix has a unique Nash equilibrium. We choose to install a strict dominant strategy equilibrium because it guarantees control over players seeking a wide variety of solution concepts beyond those that seek Nash equilibria. Notably, the game changer may control players that follow Dominant Strategy Equilibria (DSE), weak Dominant Strategy Equilibria (wDSE), Nash equilibria, correlated equilibria, and coarse correlated equilibria. However, the dominant strategy equilibrium condition is not necessary for these equilibria to be unique.

Lemma 8
(

A unique sDSE implies that the action profile is also a unique DSE, wDSE, NE, CE, and CCE; but the converse is not necessarily true, for example,
\mathcal{R}=\begin{bmatrix}(1,1)&(0,0)\\
(0,3)&(0,2)\end{bmatrix}
has a unique NE that is not a sDSE.
