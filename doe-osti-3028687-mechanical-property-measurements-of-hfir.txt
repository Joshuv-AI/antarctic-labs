 ABSTRACT

This report provides a summary of the post-irradiation examination of the tensile properties of three ODS
ferritic alloys, 14YWT-SM13, 9YWTV-PM2 and OFRAC, and two HT9 ferritic/martensitic steels, one
with low N content and the other with high N content. The neutron irradiations were conducted in the flux
trap of the High Flux Isotope Reactor as part of the Additive Manufacturing, Oxide Dispersion
Strengthened Alloys and Wrought Steels (AMOW) irradiation campaign. Sub-sized tensile specimens
fabricated from each material were exposed to nominal target temperatures and neutron doses of 300°C,
385°C, and 525°C and 8 dpa, 16 dpa, and 50 dpa, respectively.

 vii

 1. INTRODUCTION
In 2018, the Advanced Fuels Campaign initiated a forward-looking irradiation campaign to assess the
irradiation resistance of various nuclear fuel cladding candidate materials relevant to both conventional
light-water reactors (LWRs) as well as advanced reactors. This campaign was designated the Additive
Manufacturing, Oxide Dispersion Strengthened, Wrought (AMOW) irradiation campaign. Included in
this campaign were 15 different materials spanning ferritic and austenitic alloy compositions. These
materials also differed in terms of the manufacturing approaches used, ranging from conventionally
produced wrought forms, extruded oxide-dispersion–strengthened (ODS) variants, as well as powder-
metallurgically (PM) produced variants using additive manufacturing techniques. For more information
about the AMOW irradiation campaign, the reader is referred to Le Coq et al. [1].

Since 2018, several irradiation vehicles have completed irradiation in the High Flux Isotope Reactor
(HFIR) to doses ranging from 8 dpa to 50 dpa (with additional rabbits remaining under irradiation to 75
dpa). Due to the large number of specimens, irradiation temperatures, and doses achieved in this
campaign, it was decided to characterize only the high-priority samples relevant to the immediate goals of
the DOE Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC), which include the qualification of the UZr/HT9 fuel cladding
system and the development of advanced cladding materials for next-generation liquid-metal–cooled fast
reactors. Therefore, of the 15 materials irradiated within the AMOW campaign, this milestone report
pertains to two HT9 variants (one with low-N content and one with high-N content), as well as three FeCr
ODS variants (9YWTV, OFRAC, and 14YWT).

To date, both room temperature and elevated-temperature testing has been completed on all of these
materials, but the results in this report pertain solely to the room temperature trends in mechanical
properties that have been observed for each material from the unirradiated condition through irradiation to
50 dpa at target irradiation temperatures of 300, 385, and 525°C. This report summarizes the materials
irradiated and their irradiation resistance to high dose levels in HFIR.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
2.1. Materials Background
The materials covered in this section include three oxide dispersion–strengthened (ODS) ferritic alloys
and two HT9 ferritic-martensitic (FM) steels that are candidates for applications in advanced reactors.
The three ODS ferritic alloys consist of 14YWT-SM13, 9YWTV-PM2, and OFRAC, and the two HT9
FM Steels contain either low-N or high-N concentrations. SS-J2 tensile specimens were fabricated from
these materials and were included in the AMOW neutron irradiation campaign [1]. A brief background of
the three ODS ferritic alloys and two HT9 FM steels is provided in this section.

2.1.1. ODS Alloys

2.1.1.1 14YWT

The ODS 14YWT-SM13 alloy was the last of 13 heats of 14YWT that were produced at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) from ~2002 to 2012 using pre-alloyed Fe-14Cr-3W-0.4Ti (wt.%) powder
that was produced by Ar gas atomization by Special Metals, which correlates with the SM (1-13) heat
designation. The development of 14YWT began at ORNL in 2001 and continued with several Nuclear
Energy (NE) programs through 2019. In 2008, the DOE-NE Fuel Cycle Research and Development
(FCRD) program picked up support for the development of 14YWT as fuel cladding in sodium fast
reactors until FY 2019, when the Advanced Reactors (AR) program in the DOE-NE AFC was terminated.

Initially, the heat designation of 14YWT-SM13 was 14YWT-IAEA during the author’s participation in
the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Coordinated Research Project (CRP) from 2011 to 2014
on benchmarking structural materials pre-selected for advanced nuclear reactors. This CRP (RC-1186.1)
was focused on ODS steels, and seven participating countries supplied ODS alloys for effectively round-
robin testing and characterization studies. The 14YWT-IAEA heat was produced from 5.4 kg of
mechanically alloyed powder that was extruded into three large rectangular-shaped bars. Two extruded
bars were cut in half, resulting in five plates that were produced by hot rolling. Three plates were supplied
to the CRP, and the two remaining plates were kept at ORNL for research studies within the FCRD
program. It was during this period that the heat designation was changed to 14YWT-SM13.

The 13 heats of 14YWT were produced by mechanical alloying (MA) pre-alloyed Fe-14Cr-3W-0.4Ti
powder with 0.3Y2O3 (wt.%) powder using the high kinetic energy CM01 and CM08 Simoloyer (Zoz
GmbH). For 14YWT-SM13 (IAEA heat), 0.35FeO (wt.%) powder was added primarily to increase the
strength properties. The ball-milled powders were canned in mild steel, vacuum degassed, and most of the
13 heats of 14YWT were extruded after heating the can for 1 or 2 hours at 850°C. All five plates of
14YWT (-SM13/-IAEA heat) were cross rolled at 1000°C to ~50% reduction in thickness (RIT) to a
thickness of 1 cm. One of the 14YWT-SM13 plates was rolled an additional 50% RIT to a thickness of
0.5 cm at 1000°C for fabricating SS-J2 specimens to supply to the AMOW neutron irradiation campaign.
Figure 1 shows uncorrected engineering stress-strain curves from room temperature to 800°C using SS-J2
specimens that were fabricated from the extruded bar of 14YWT-SM13. The tensile data demonstrate the
high strengths but low ductilities over the test temperatures.

2.1.1.2 9WYTV

The 9WYTV-PM2 ODS alloy was developed during the International Nuclear Energy Research Initiative
(I-NERI) between the United States (ORNL-PI T.S. Byun and D.T. Hoelzer) and Republic of Korea
(Korean Atomic Energy Research Institute-PI J.H. Yoon) within the FCRD program from 2011 to 2013.
The objective of this I-NERI project was to develop nanoparticle-strengthened Fe-9Cr alloys with high
fracture toughness for application to high-performance reactor core structures. Two ODS Fe-9Cr alloys

were developed with alloy and heat designations 9YWTV-PM1 and 9YWTV-PM2. The main difference
in composition between PM1 and PM2 was the carbon content; PM1 contained 1110 wppm C and PM2
contained a lower C content of 599 wppm. A series of thermomechanical treatments (TMTs) consisting of
isothermal annealing and controlled rolling treatments over a range of elevated temperatures showed
9YWTV-PM2 to possess high fracture toughness (KJQ) of 240–280 MPa√m at room temperature and
160–220 MPa√m at 500°C, which surpassed the 100 MPa√m at high temperatures [2].

Both 9YWTV-PM1 and 9YWTV-PM2 were produced using similar MA parameters developed for
14YWT using pre-alloyed Fe-9Cr-2W-0.4Ti-0.2V and 0.12C for PM1 and 0.05C for PM2. The high
kinetic energy CM08 Simoloyer was used for MA. The MA powder was placed in mild steel cans and
vacuum degassed. One can of PM1 and PM2 was heated for 1 h at 820°C, and two cans of PM1 and PM2
were heated for 1 h at 830°C, followed by extrusion through a rectangular shaped die. The TMT
experiments were then performed on the decanned ODS bars of PM1 and PM2 to optimize the
nanocluster dispersion parameters and the grain structure in the microstructure for improving the high
temperature fracture toughness. Figure 2 shows uncorrected engineering stress-strain curves from room
temperature to 800°C for SS-J2 specimens of the extruded bar of 9YWTV-PM2. The tensile data
demonstrated a greater balance of high strength with better ductility over the test temperatures.

2.1.1.3 OFRAC

OFRAC was the most recently developed ODS alloy for fuel cladding in sodium fast reactors at ORNL in
the advanced reactor program of AFC starting in 2016. The composition of OFRAC was modified from
14YWT by lowering the Cr level to 12%, replacing W with Mo, and adding Nb in addition to Ti designed
for reacting with impurities of C and N to leave Ti to react with Y and O from the Y2O3 dispersoid. The
pre-alloyed Fe-12Cr-1Mo-0.3Ti-0.3Nb powder was mixed with 0.3Y2O3 (wt.%) and mechanically alloyed
using the same parameters developed for 14YWT.

The key outcome with respect to the OFRAC was the successful fabrication of thin-wall tubing in two
campaigns as part of a collaboration with Nippon Nuclear Fuel Development Co., Ltd, Japan. The first
campaign in 2017 consisted of cold-pilgering in four passes with a starting master tube that was
18 OD × 12 ID × 200 L (mm) and a final thin-wall tube that was 8.51 mm OD × 0.5 mm WT (wall
thickness) × 1.78 m long with no cracks [3]. The second campaign was more challenging because the
target dimensions of the thin wall tubing of OFRAC was 6 mm OD and 0.5 mm WT. To achieve this
goal, a total of seven high-precision tube rolling (HPTR) passes at room temperature were required.
Starting in 2019, five master tubes of OFRAC were prepared for the HPTR passes, and of these, four thin
wall tubes were successfully produced with no cracks within the tubes. The final dimensions of the four
thin-wall tubes were 6.0 mm OD × 0.5 mm WT and 2.17 to 2.56 m long. Unfortunately, there have been
no milestone reports covering the second campaign for fabricating thin-wall fuel cladding due to the
elimination of the advanced reactor program in AFC in FY 2020.

When the AMOW campaign was initiated, OFRAC was in peak development. Therefore, SS-J2 tensile
specimens were fabricated from extruded bars for neutron irradiation in HFIR. Figure 5 shows
uncorrected engineering stress-strain curves from room temperature to 800°C obtained from SS-J2
specimens of OFRAC. The tensile curves revealed the lowest strength for OFRAC compared to 14YWT-
SM13 and 9YWTV-PM2 but the highest ductility over the test temperatures.

 Figure 5. Stress-strain curves of unirradiated OFRAC from room temperature to 800°C.

2.1.2. HT9
HT9 is a 12Cr ferritic/martensitic steel that serves as the reference cladding for use in sodium-cooled fast
reactors. Extensive literature exists on this cladding type, and this cladding has the most robust irradiation
history within the historical EBRII reactor. As such, the UZr/HT9 system serves as the reference fuel
specification for many nuclear reactor vendors that seek to license modern sodium-cooled fast reactors in
the United States. However, it is yet unclear how variations in impurity content within HT9
fundamentally affects irradiation performance at various irradiation temperatures. For this reason, this
HFIR irradiation is particularly timely in the generation of this data to enable more accurate refinement of
the chemical specification for nuclear-grade HT9 material.

The compositions of the two heats of material are given in Table 2; compositions were reported by the
vendor. The materials were normalized at 1040°C for 30 min, air-cooled, then tempered at 760°C for 1 h
and air-cooled prior to shipment to ORNL for inclusion in the irradiation test matrix.

2.2. HFIR Irradiation Experiments

The irradiation experiments pertaining to the tensile tested materials in this report were performed in the
flux trap of ORNL’s HFIR. The flux trap region is located inside the fuel region of the reactor core, which
consists of two annular regions. The HFIR fuel and all experiment vessels are cooled by the reactor’s
primary coolant, which is approximately 50–60°C. A typical cycle for HFIR is ~25 days.

The AMOW irradiation campaign used the existing GENeral TENsile (GENTEN) capsule design, which
consists of three specimen holders sequentially stacked inside the rabbit containment. Each holder
assembly held four sets of the sub-sized tensile specimens and contained four SiC thermometers (TMs)
for passive temperature monitoring. The heating of the tensile specimens to the target temperatures
resulted from neutron and gamma radiation from the HFIR fuel during exposure to neutron irradiation.
The specimen irradiation temperature was determined using the following parameters: irradiation
position, size of the gas gap between the outer wall of the holder and the inner wall of the housing, and
He/Ar gas fill mixture. A detailed description of the capsule design and discussion of the neutronic and
thermal analysis that was required to achieve the target temperatures are reported in Le Coq et al. 0.

The irradiation details of the various capsules that are the subject of this report are provided in

Table 3. The capsules started irradiation in HFIR between October 2019 and January 2020 and completed
irradiation between June 2020 and October 2024. They were irradiated for 4 to 25 cycles, corresponding
to an irradiation damage between 8 and 50 dpa. It should be noted that some capsules were removed from
the reactor core mid-irradiation to allow other experiments to be inserted and were then re-inserted in the
core to complete their irradiation. As a result, Table 3 lists two irradiation positions for these capsules,
corresponding to each insertion period.

2.3. Post-Irradiation Examination
The capsules were shipped to the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing (IMET) hot cell facility
for disassembly. This disassembly process consisted of cutting the capsule on one end using a low-speed
saw and sliding out the 3 holders. A push-tool was used to remove all components from the holders.
Figure 6 shows the capsule components recovered from 1 capsule disassembly. The tensile specimens are
set aside for subsequent in-cell tensile testing at IMET while the TMs are set aside for analysis in the
Low-Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory.

 Figure 6. Example of the components recovered from one AMOW capsule disassembly.

2.3.1. Tensile Tests

Subsize flat SS-J2 tensile specimens were fabricated from processed material of the three ODS alloys and
two HT9 FM steels. Figure 6 shows the dimensions of the SS-J2 specimens: 5.0 mm gauge length,
1.2 mm gauge width, and 0.5 mm thick. For the AMOW campaign, a total of 36 SS-J2 specimens were
fabricated from material of 14YWT-SM13 and 9YWTV-PM2, and 35 SS-J2 specimens were fabricated
from material of OFRAC.

 Figure 7. Dimensions of the SS-J2 tensile specimen.

Uniaxial tensile tests were performed using the Instron screw-driven 100 kN load frame located in IMET.
The tests were conducted with shoulder loading at a crosshead speed of 0.0055 mm/s corresponding to an
initial strain rate of ~10−3 s−1. The engineering stress was calculated by dividing the applied load by the
initial cross-sectional area of the SS-J2 specimens. The engineering strain was calculated from the
recorded crosshead separation relative to the initial gauge length of 5.0 mm. One tensile test at room
temperature and one tensile test at irradiation temperature were performed for each material.

2.3.2. Thermometry Analysis

The SiC TM was analyzed in LAMDA laboratory by continuous dilatometry 00. A total of 6 TMs per

compared to the predicted temperatures to determine the average specimen temperature for each
experiment.

3.2. Tensile Properties

The stress-strain curves obtained from tensile tests of the three ODS alloys and two HT9 FM steels at
room temperature are compiled with constant dose and constant temperature to compare how the neutron
irradiation conditions influenced changes in strength and ductility. At constant neutron dose, the stress-
strain curves show how increasing the irradiation temperature affects the strength and ductility properties.
The stress-strain curves at constant temperature show the effects of increasing neutron dose on the
strength and ductility properties, which is a good indicator of how the material will behave in an operating
nuclear reactor.

To explore the neutron irradiation tolerances of the three ODS ferritic alloys and two HT9 FM steels, the
average and standard deviation values were calculated from the compiled tensile properties as a function
of the three neutron doses and three irradiation temperatures listed in Tables 2–6. Figure 19 shows the
results of the calculated average tensile property and standard deviation values of the three ODS ferritic
alloys and two HT9 FM steels. These results show that the two ODS alloys with severe cold work before
irradiation (9YWTV and 14YWT) had high strength but very low ductility, whereas the OFRAC alloy,
with balanced strength/ductility before irradiation, showed very good ductility following irradiation as
well. It also appears that the high-N HT9 performs marginally better than the low-N variant, and both
F/M alloys have similar ductility to the OFRAC ODS alloy, despite having a lower strength. These results
clearly demonstrate the irradiation resistance of the high-N HT9 heat, as well as the superior mechanical
performance of correctly processed ODS alloys (such as the OFRAC alloy shown here). They also
highlight how non-ideal processing variables can influence post-irradiation ductility and that the
combination of processing and composition are both important when evaluating irradiation effects.

 4. SUMMARY / FUTURE WORK

The neutron irradiation tolerance of three ODS ferritic alloys and two HT9 ferritic-martensitic steels was
evaluated by post-irradiation examination of their tensile properties following neutron irradiations at
HFIR as part of the AMOW irradiation campaign. Sub-sized tensile specimens were fabricated from the
ODS ferritic alloys 14YWT-SM13, 9YWTV-PM2, and OFRAC and two FM steels HTR-LN (low
nitrogen) and HT9-HN (high nitrogen). Specimens were exposed to nominal target temperatures and
neutron doses of 300°C, 385°C, and 525°C and 8, 16, and 50 dpa, respectively. The results highlight the
importance of processing condition by showing that the severe cold working for 9YWTV and 14YWT
yielded high strengths but very low ductility after neutron irradiation, while the improved processing
condition for OFRAC with a balanced strength/ductility before irradiation showed very good ductility
following irradiation. The results also show that the high-N HT9 FM steel performed marginally better in
terms of ductility than the low-N HT9 variant. Both of the HT9 F/M steels had similar ductility to that of
the OFRAC despite having lower strengths. These results clearly demonstrate the irradiation resistance of
the high-N HT9 heat, as well as the superior mechanical performance ODS ferritic alloys produced using
correct processing conditions such as those for OFRAC shown in this report.

This report also highlights the need to fully understand the specific strengthening mechanisms leading to
the superior (or subpar) irradiation performance of each of these alloys following neutron irradiation.
Future work will focus on detailed microstructural evaluations of the HT9 materials as well as specific
ODS alloys irradiated to 50 dpa. This will help identify if simply the difference in cold work (dislocation
density) was the root cause for differential irradiation performance, or if other factors such as secondary
precipitation differences within the varied Cr-content ODS alloys also plays a key role.
