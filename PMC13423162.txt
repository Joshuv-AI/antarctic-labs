Preventing the next stroke: nurse-led decision and adherence coaching to empower people with stroke (StrokeCompass) versus usual care – study protocol for a stepped-wedge pilot trial
Authors: Jasmin Helbach, Julia Hickstein, Anne-Marei Jarchow, Rafael Bourry, Laura Hofner, Fabian Simon Frielitz, Antonia Zapf, Anna Suling, Götz Thomalla, Falk Hoffmann, Christoph Heesen, Anne Christin Rahn, StrokeCompass study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRecurrent stroke prevention is important for people who have suffered a stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA), as they are at increased risk of having another stroke. Nevertheless, treatment adherence is often low. Therefore, the StrokeCompass project was initiated to enhance medication adherence by empowering individuals who have had a stroke to take an active role in recurrent stroke prevention and medication management. In accordance with the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) guidelines, a complex intervention was developed that integrated evidence-based decision aids and a personalised, nurse-led coaching programme. The present stepped-wedge cluster-randomised controlled study addresses the second phase of the MRC framework by pilot testing the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention in a clinical setting.Methods and analysisA total of 452 patients with a recent ischaemic stroke or TIA will be recruited over a period of 16 months from seven stroke units across Germany. The study centres will be randomised and sequentially crossed over from the control phase to the intervention phase in three time periods (4, 8 and 12 months after initiation) until all clusters have been exposed to both conditions. The intervention comprises four core elements: first, a decision coaching programme, second, a physician consultation to discuss treatment targets, third, an adherence coaching programme, and fourth, a web-based patient decision aid. Furthermore, we developed a preparatory training programme for nurses and physicians. Feasibility outcomes (eg, recruitment strategies, participation, implementation and utilisation of intervention) will be analysed descriptively, while preliminary estimates of effectiveness, measured by changes in risk factor targets (eg, blood pressure and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels), will be analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle. Furthermore, an economic evaluation will be conducted focusing on the implementation costs. These findings could prove pivotal in the subsequent planning and rationale for a full-powered effectiveness study.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of the University of Lübeck (reference 2024-522). We will disseminate the findings in peer-reviewed journals, at conferences, and on relevant patient websites.Trial registration numberDRKS00036091.Keywords: Medication Adherence, Patient Participation, Decision Making

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide.1 People who have suffered a stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA), summarised as people with stroke, are at high risk of having another stroke.2 3 The estimated cumulative risk of stroke recurrence increases from 3% within 30 days to 11% within the first 12 months and further to 39% within the following 10 years.3 Therefore, management of risk factors to reduce the risk of stroke recurrence is highly relevant in stroke prevention.1 4

Approximately 90% of strokes can be attributed to modifiable risk factors, including hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, atrial fibrillation, smoking, obesity and diabetes.5–7 While lifestyle modifications, encompassing regular physical activity, a healthy diet, smoking and alcohol cessation, are important for the prevention of both initial and recurrent strokes, the prescription of medication to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke is recommended for most patients.8 A range of drug treatment approaches, including antiplatelet therapy or oral anticoagulation, statins and antihypertensive medication, is available to address the various risk factors associated with recurrent stroke.6 8 9 However, people with stroke are often unaware of their options,10 and overall medication adherence is low (64%), with adherence to antithrombotics being higher than adherence to antihypertensives and hypolipidaemics (78%, 63% and 55%, respectively).11 Poor medication adherence is often associated with individual and social determinants such as health literacy and health perception, the patient−physician relationship and inappropriate beliefs about medication or its side effects.11–13 Moreover, people with stroke frequently encounter challenges in the context of long-term management following a stroke, mainly due to the presence of both physical and psychological disabilities and concerns.14–16 Approximately 50% of people affected by a stroke experience chronic disabilities or neurological deficits.17 18 In addition, health outcomes such as depression, anxiety, fatigue and apathy have been demonstrated to not only diminish quality of life but also reduce functionality in activities of daily living or work.18 19

To facilitate self-management and empower people with stroke to make independent health-related decisions, support by healthcare professionals in gaining knowledge about life after stroke through targeted, patient-centred and evidence-based information is essential.20 21 One potential approach to providing evidence-based information is through the utilisation of patient decision aids (PtDAs), which are explicitly designed to inform and help individuals cope with decision-making in health-related matters.22 23 PtDAs lead to improved empowerment outcomes such as decision preparedness and participation in decision-making processes.24 They are often used in the context of shared decision-making (SDM), in which providing knowledge and clarifying values and fears are fundamental components. SDM is characterised by a collaborative relationship between patients and healthcare professionals, in which all treatment options are discussed and the most suitable therapy is agreed on.25 However, the manner in which clinicians provide information can have a substantial impact on patients’ choices and treatment adherence, emphasising the importance of standardised information, such as PtDAs.24 Research suggests that SDM interventions may improve SDM adoption, but it is unclear whether these interventions improve health outcomes and the experiences of people with stroke.26 However, it is not only the decision itself that is important, but also changing behaviour in the long term. One approach that appears promising in supporting behaviour changes and improving treatment adherence is motivational interviewing (MI).27–30 MI is a collaborative conversation style that aims to strengthen a patient’s own motivation and commitment to change. A cooperative relationship is established in which goals are identified and defined, and patients are encouraged to examine their motivations and resolve any ambivalence toward change. The final stage of MI is the formulation of a comprehensive action plan after the patient’s commitment to changing a target behaviour.31 Previous research has demonstrated the potential of these strategies to enhance secondary prevention outcomes.27–29 32 However, so far, no intervention has incorporated the combination of PtDAs, interprofessional SDM and a structured follow-up intervention addressing medication management using MI in people with stroke in one complex intervention.

In accordance with the Medical Research Council (MRC) guidance on developing and evaluating complex interventions,33 the mixed methods project StrokeCompass34 35 was planned and initiated. The objective of this project is to enhance medication adherence by empowering individuals who have had a stroke to take an active role in recurrent stroke prevention and medication management by complex intervention that integrates evidence-based decision aids and a personalised, nurse-led coaching programme. The present stepped-wedge cluster-randomised controlled study addresses the pilot testing of the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention in a clinical setting. Moreover, the secondary objective is to obtain preliminary estimates of the effectiveness of the StrokeCompass intervention.

Methods

The StrokeCompass project is a multiphase project, encompassing comprehensive planning and preliminary studies, the development of the intervention components, as well as alpha and beta testing, ultimately concluding with a feasibility study (figure 1).

Intervention development

The development of the comprehensive web-based PtDA followed established guidelines for evidence-based patient information36 and the international PtDA standards,37 and was informed by several sources. First, we performed a scoping review on the information needs of people with stroke38 and a systematic review of the potential of MI in people with stroke.30 Second, we conducted a case study to explore experiences of support and the care process at different timepoints after stroke or TIA, with a focus on treatment decisions and adherence. Third, we conducted a comprehensive search and assessment of existing guidelines, encompassing both national and international guidelines on drug-based recurrent stroke prevention. This was complemented by a comprehensive overview of systematic reviews on recurrent stroke prevention. Finally, we conducted a survey of 206 people with stroke, comprising a knowledge questionnaire as well as questions on information needs and lifestyle factors.

The training programme for nurses is based on a previously developed and evaluated intervention programme for people with multiple sclerosis on immunotherapy decision-making.39 We adapted this training programme to the context of stroke, informed by the Ottawa Decision Support Framework40 and the principles of person-centred41 care, as well as MI.42

The physician training includes an online module on interprofessional SDM developed within the Share to Care programme.43 We have expanded this programme to additionally address the role of the decision coach, target values, and adherence coaching.

Alpha and beta testing

After developing the first version of the intervention, all components were pretested using a two-step approach (alpha and beta testing), as described by Coulter et al.23 The testing process involved clinical experts, a representative from a self-help organisation and patient representatives. Data were collected using a parallel mixed-methods approach involving questionnaires and semi-structured interviews. Feedback from both steps was predominantly positive, indicating that our intervention would be helpful and beneficial. Detailed results of the alpha and beta testing will be published separately.

Trial design and patient recruitment

This pilot study will address the second phase of the MRC Framework, specifically assessing the feasibility of the StrokeCompass programme. We will use a stepped-wedge randomised study design with seven clusters and four time periods. This design involves randomising and sequentially crossing clusters from the control phase to the intervention phase until all clusters have been exposed to both treatment conditions.44 In contrast to a classical cluster randomised trial, this approach has the advantage that all study centres will receive the StrokeCompass programme. It is anticipated that this approach will provide comprehensive information on the programme’s feasibility and economic viability, regardless of hospital size and operator (university medical centres and community hospitals), as well as other contextual influencing factors. The trial is preregistered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS): DRKS00036091.

The recruitment period is scheduled to span 16 months (figure 2). Each study centre will initiate the process by recruiting controls. All study centres will be randomly allocated to one of three time periods for the transition from the control to the intervention phase (at 4, 8 or 12 months after study initiation). The number of cases and controls to be recruited is contingent on the group allocation of the centres. Each participant will be followed up for 12 months.

Recruitment strategy.

Study setting

The study will be conducted in five university medical centres (Lübeck, Hamburg, Oldenburg, Bielefeld, Leipzig) and two community hospitals (Hamburg-Altona, Lüneburg). Five of the hospitals are located in northern Germany, one in western Germany (Bielefeld) and one in eastern Germany (Leipzig). All centres have their own stroke units. The size of the stroke unit ranges from 10 to 20 beds, and the number of stroke patients treated per year ranges from 890 to 1.300.

Eligibility criteria

The study population comprises individuals aged 18 years and over who are admitted to the stroke unit following a first ischaemic stroke or TIA. Individuals with other stroke etiologies (eg, haemorrhagic stroke, iatrogenic stroke or TIA (eg, following vascular surgery or neuroradiological interventions), symptomatic intracranial or extracranial arterial stenosis) will be excluded as these are associated with distinct mechanisms of disease, risk factors, severity and prevention strategies. To ensure that treatment is administered to individuals with an appropriate risk profile and to minimise the risk of including stroke mimics, we will only include TIA patients with an ABCD2 score45 of at least three. People with stroke can participate irrespective of their discharge destination (eg, home, rehabilitation facility or care facility), as long as they have internet access there. Participants must provide written informed consent to be eligible for inclusion.

Individuals with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale subscores46 greater than 0 on any of the items 1a–c (level of consciousness, orientation, ability to follow commands) to 3 (Level of Consciousness, Gaze, Visual fields) and 9 (Language) will be excluded from the study. The most recent score prior to the decision on study inclusion will be used. This can be the scoring on admission or during the patient’s hospital stay. Patients who demonstrate signs of improvement may be reassessed at a later time and, if appropriate, included in the study.

Individuals already engaged in existing aftercare programmes or clinical trials with a competing intervention (specialised aftercare, extended secondary prevention, etc) will also be excluded from the study.

Intervention and participation timeline

Relatives, friends or caregivers are permitted to attend coaching sessions if the participant wishes, and it can be organised easily.

After admission to the stroke unit, a member of the study centre’s research team will inform eligible individuals with stroke about the study and invite them to participate (see online supplemental file 3). Once consent is obtained, people with stroke will receive access to an evidence-based, individually tailored web-based PtDA that contains information on the aetiology of stroke, treatment options (including associated risks and benefits), and practical advice on making lifestyle changes, coping with sequelae and disabilities, and managing medication after stroke. Participants will have access to and be able to use this web-based PtDA throughout the project, including both the inpatient period and the postdischarge phase. Access to the PtDA will primarily be via private mobile devices, such as smartphones, tablets or laptops. In addition to the online version, a brief printed version will be provided to people with stroke.

During the index hospital stay, participants will receive one or two non-directive decision coaching sessions according to six SDM steps.48 These sessions, with an anticipated duration of approximately 60–90 min, will be led by trained nurses (hereafter referred to as ‘coaches’) and will focus on the first four steps of SDM: defining the goal of the consultation, explaining the need for patient participation, explaining the available treatment options and associated possible harms and benefits, and exploring patient preferences and needs. Following this, a physician consultation of approximately 10–20 min will be scheduled. The focus of this consultation will be on the two final steps of SDM: making a shared decision and putting the decision into practice. The consultation will be led by a physician who has completed the online SDM training programme (hereinafter referred to as ‘trained physicians’). This consultation aims to collaboratively formulate a therapeutic plan and define target values for cholesterol and blood pressure. In order to facilitate the execution of all intervention components prior to discharge, a minimum stay of 48 hours is deemed necessary.

After discharge, the StrokeCompass programme provides ongoing self-management support through three adherence coaching sessions led by the same coaches. The first session will be conducted by phone 2 weeks after discharge, the second during a clinical visit 3 months after discharge, and the third by phone 8 months after discharge. The duration of the sessions will depend on the needs of the participants and is anticipated to take approximately 30–60 min. Grounded in the principles of MI,42 these coaching sessions aim to support people with stroke in strengthening their motivation and achieving individual goals by exploring and reinforcing their needs. The sessions will focus on medication adherence and management. If necessary, treatment changes will be discussed.

After 12 months, the programme will conclude with a follow-up visit for outcome assessment, including clinical evaluation, assessment of risk factor targets and completion of questionnaires.

Preintervention training programme

As part of the StrokeCompass programme, we will train nurses and neurologists who will be responsible for delivering the intervention (table 2), structured according to the TIDieR checklist.47 The nurse training consists of an online preparation (8 hours), a 3-day course (15 hours) and an on-the-job training (4–6 hours). It aims to train nurses to perform decision and adherence coaching. The neurologist course (around 1 hour) includes an online module on interprofessional SDM, developed in the Share to Care programme.43 The module has been expanded to address the role of the decision coach, target values and adherence coaching.

Control group

The control group will receive usual care, accompanied by an online brochure on stroke developed by the German Stroke Society (Deutsche Schlaganfall Gesellschaft) in cooperation with the self-help organisation ‘Stiftung Deutsche Schlaganfall-Hilfe’.49 Furthermore, people with stroke will receive a brief, standardised conversation about the brochure with a nurse or study nurse during the index hospital stay, which should last no longer than 5 min.

After 12 months, the programme will conclude with a follow-up visit for outcome assessment, including clinical evaluation and completion of questionnaires.

Data collection

Data will be collected from people with stroke at five distinct time points (see table 3) through self-reported questionnaires by the participants. The initial three assessments—at baseline (t0), after the last decision coaching session (t1, only intervention group), and after the physician consultation (intervention group) or at the end of the index hospital stay (control group) (t2)—will be conducted during the index hospital stay. The fourth assessment is scheduled to take place 3 months after inclusion (t3), and will be collected via post. The final assessment will be conducted during the 12-month follow-up visit at the hospital (t4). We will send a reminder to the participants about the follow-up visit. The research team at the study centre will assess clinical characteristics and risk factor targets at baseline (t0) and at the 12-month follow-up (t4).

To evaluate the involvement of people with stroke during the decision-making process and the application of MI principles by the coaches, the research teams at the study centres will record a total of 40 decision coaching sessions (t1), 40 physician consultations (t2) and 20 adherence coaching sessions (second coaching at 3 months). Video recordings of the decision coaching sessions and physician consultations will also be used in a complementary doctoral project, which focuses on communicative interaction between stroke patients and nursing and medical professionals as an effective component of person-centred care.

In addition, once all coaching sessions have been completed, a sample of coaches, trained physicians and participants will be interviewed. These interviews will focus on the acceptability, satisfaction with, and usefulness of the intervention components.

Outcomes and study measures

Feasibility measures

The main outcomes of this study will be feasibility and acceptability of the intervention. In accordance with the MRC framework33 as well as the MRC guidance for process evaluation,50 we will consider the feasibility and progression criteria described by Simpson et al.51 These include the feasibility of the recruitment strategy, the retention and participation numbers, the feasibility of the outcomes, the capacity and time required to implement the intervention and collect the data, and the usefulness of the components of the intervention themselves (table 4). In addition, we will assess the impact of the underlying contextual factors (eg, characteristics of the centres, coaches, trained physicians and people with stroke, attitudes towards the project and intrinsic motivation to participate) and organisational factors (eg, additional workload, organisational challenges, dynamics of interprofessional communication and collaboration) based on our logic model (see online supplemental file 4). The contributions of several relevant stakeholders will be taken into account.

Primary efficacy outcomes

In addition to the primary aim of evaluating the feasibility of the intervention, this study seeks to obtain initial estimates of the potential impact of the intervention on adherence of people with stroke to medication for the prevention of recurrent stroke. Therefore, as part of a clinical follow-up (month 12) in accordance with the applicable guidelines, risk factor targets associated with those medications, such as blood pressure and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels, will be measured.

We will assess adherence to prescribed medications, including cholesterol-lowering and antihypertensive medications, after 12 months by achieving guideline-based therapy target values (within 10% of the upper limit values) as outlined in the physician’s letter. The following target values are to be applied, with the possibility of adjustment by the attending physicians: LDL is less than 70 mg/dL, and blood pressure is less than 140/90 mm Hg.52

Secondary efficacy outcomes

Secondary outcomes relevant to planning a subsequent efficacy study will include the overall health status of people with stroke, their quality of life, knowledge, medication adherence, empowerment and the extent of SDM. The objective is to ascertain whether these outcomes can be evaluated with the instruments employed in this pilot study (table 3) and to gain preliminary insights into their relevance.

Overall health status

We will use a sociodemographic questionnaire, as well as patient records to assess the overall health status of people with stroke. This will include measuring age, gender, living situation, social support, vascular and non-vascular comorbidities. Furthermore, the patient’s functional status will be assessed by the simplified modified Rankin Scale Questionnaire.53 This scale ranges from 0 (‘no symptoms’) to 6 (‘death’). The measurement of outcomes related to overall survival and stroke recurrence will be conducted in accordance with the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) standard for stroke.54

Health-related quality of life

We will use the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 10-Question Short Form (PROMIS-10) to assess the health-related quality of life of people with stroke.55 The PROMIS-10 is a standardised questionnaire validated in stroke populations and recommended by ICHOM as a core patient-reported outcome measure after stroke.56 57 The PROMIS-10 comprises two scales: one measuring mental health and the other measuring physical health. The scores are interpreted as follows: first, a sum score is calculated; then, a t-score with standardised t-values is derived.55 For approximate interpretation, T-scores >58 reflect excellent health, scores of 50–58 very good health, scores of 42–50 good health, scores of 35–42 fair health and scores <35 indicate poor health.58

Anxiety and depression

We will use the Patient Health Questionnaire-459 to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression. The instrument comprises four items, with two items each assessing anxiety and depressive symptoms. Total scores range from 0 to 12, and subscale scores of ≥3 indicate probable anxiety or depression.59

Medication adherence

We will use the Adherence Assessment Questionnaire60 to evaluate self-assessed medication adherence. The questionnaire comprises eleven items, of which ten are rated on a 4-point Likert scale and one on a visual analogue scale. Total scores range from 9 to 36, with lower scores indicating better adherence. Scores of ≥27 indicate a very high probability of non-adherence, scores of 18–26 indicate a high probability of non-adherence and scores of 10–17 indicate a possible risk of non-adherence. A score of 9 indicates full adherence.

In addition, we will use the Adherence Barriers Questionnaire (ABQ)61 to identify the factors that influence medication adherence. The ABQ comprises 16 items reflecting intentional, unintentional, and healthcare system-related barriers, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Higher total scores indicate a greater likelihood of adherence barriers. A single item score of >2 indicates the presence of a barrier, and a total score of ≥25 indicates a substantially increased probability of non-adherence.

Empowerment

We will use the Patient Activation Measure (PAM13)62 to assess patient empowerment. The PAM13 consists of 13 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Raw total scores are transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater patient activation. Four activation levels are distinguished: level 1 (≤47.0), reflecting limited belief in the importance of activation; level 2 (47.1–55.1), indicating limited knowledge and confidence to take action; level 3 (55.2–67.0), reflecting the beginning to take action; and level 4 (≥67.1), indicating actively taking action.63

In addition, we translated and pretested a German version of the Patient-Professional Interaction Questionnaire (PPIQ)64 to assess patients’ perceptions of their interactions with the healthcare professional. The PPIQ comprises 16 items, with four items each addressing the clinician’s communication behaviours, interest in the patient’s agenda, empathy and patient involvement in care. These items can be evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale with higher scores indicating a higher provision of patient-centred care.64

Stroke knowledge

We will use a self-developed and validated questionnaire comprising 20 items to assess the stroke-related knowledge of people with stroke (see online supplemental appendix 1). The questionnaire includes four domains: general stroke knowledge, blood-thinning medication, blood pressure-lowering medication and lipid-lowering medication. All knowledge questions are single-choice questions.

Shared decision-making

The involvement of people with stroke during the decision-making process will be assessed with the Multifocal Approach to Sharing in Shared Decision Making (MAPPIN’SDM).65 MAPPIN’SDM considers the nurses’ perspective, the neurologists’ perspective, the perspective of the people with stroke as well as the observer perspective. The latter is based on video recordings of the decision coaching sessions, the respective physician consultations and the adherence coaching sessions. MAPPIN’SDM comprises 11 items, with scores ranging from 0 to 4, with 4 denoting the highest degree of SDM. In the control group, SDM will be assessed only from the perspective of people with stroke using the MAPPIN’SDM questionnaire at the end of their index hospital stay.

Economic evaluation

In addition to the feasibility and exploratory results, an economic evaluation will be conducted to determine whether the expected benefits of the intervention justify its cost, as recommended in the MRC framework.33 Therefore, we will determine the implementation costs of the intervention, including development, study materials, training costs and time, and conduct a cost–benefit analysis. This will give us an estimate of whether implementing the intervention would be financially attractive and feasible for the clinics.

Sample size

As this study is of a pilot nature, with a primary focus on the assessment of feasibility, a formal statistical power calculation is not generally necessary.66 Nevertheless, with the objective of deriving a preliminary estimate of the potential impact of the intervention on medication adherence, we decided to do a sample size calculation based on the hypothesis that medication adherence could be increased from 60% to 80% by the intervention. The sample size calculation was conducted using PASS V.22.0.4, employing the tests for two proportions in a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised design comprising three steps and six clusters, a 15% drop-out rate, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power between 63% and 75% corresponding to an intracluster correlation coefficient ranging from 0.2 to 0.001. The calculation yielded a sample size of 452, deemed sufficient to test the intervention’s feasibility and provide a preliminary estimate of its potential impact on medication adherence.

Treatment allocation

Prior to the initiation of the intervention phase, the centres will be randomly assigned to one of three crossover points (4, 8 and 12 months after study initiation). The randomisation process will be conducted at the hospital level by an independent statistician using computer-generated simple randomisation.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding physicians and nurses is not feasible. To minimise potential performance bias, participants will not be informed of the presence of a control and an intervention group, nor about their individual treatment allocation. To ensure this, two distinct patient information and consent forms were developed (see online supplemental appendix 2). Nevertheless, participants may still be able to infer aspects of their treatment. As the primary objective of this study is to assess feasibility, it is not anticipated that any potential unblinding will have a substantial impact on the evaluation.

Data management

The clinical data will be entered into REDCap, an online data management programme hosted by the Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg.

For all other data, original paper-based questionnaires will be stored at the respective study centres, and copies will either be scanned and uploaded to REDCap or posted to the central study team in Lübeck for data entry. All data will be collected and analysed in pseudonymised form. A pseudonymisation list will be generated for each study centre and stored securely and separately from the study data.

In accordance with Article 30 of the European General Data Protection Regulations,67 records of joint processing activities are prepared and provided to study centres and cooperation partners.

All video recordings (decision coaching sessions, physician consultation and adherence coaching sessions) are transmitted in pseudonymised form by the nurses or physicians via a project-internal online storage medium (cloud) of the University of Lübeck. All recipients are obliged to maintain confidentiality. No further transfer of data to third parties will take place.

Statistical methods

The analysis of the data will be conducted by the Institute of Medical Biometry and Epidemiology at the University Medical Center Hamburg Eppendorf (UKE). The collected feasibility criteria will be analysed descriptively, for example using frequency tables, and presented graphically. Interviews will be recorded, transcribed and analysed using template analysis.68 69 Furthermore, a joint display analysis will be conducted, integrating quantitative (questionnaires) and qualitative data (video records and interviews), to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility of the StrokeCompass programme.

Preliminary estimates of effectiveness regarding the primary endpoints will be based on the full analysis set, analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle. A mixed logistic regression model will be employed, with intervention group, type of centre, and the time period as fixed effects as well as cluster as a random effect. Effect estimates will be presented as ORs with 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted, with missing values being imputed using multiple imputation methods. In accordance with the as-treated principle, a further sensitivity analysis will be conducted, in which patients are analysed according to the actual intervention phase of the cluster. Secondary outcomes will be analysed using analogous mixed-effect models, taking into account the distribution and scale of each outcome. Subgroup analyses will be conducted to examine possible effects of gender, disease severity, depression and education on the primary outcome. It is imperative to note that all analyses will be exploratory in nature, thus precluding the formulation of any confirmatory statements. Before analysis, a detailed statistical analysis plan will be developed and shared publicly.

Monitoring

We will monitor the recruitment and implementation procedures to ensure adherence to the pre-defined centre-specific standard operating procedures. Study centres will document recruitment, as well as the time required to prepare and perform each coaching session. A monthly review will be conducted to verify that the recruitment target of five participants per month is met and that the time required to prepare and conduct the coaching session does not exceed the required 2 hours per coaching session. Furthermore, we will track staff availability at each centre, including temporary absences due to illness or leave, and assess their potential impact on the implementation of the intervention.

Patient and public involvement

A multidisciplinary team is involved in the project. An expert advisory board, including medical experts, representatives of the German Stroke Foundation and patient representatives, was included in developing the intervention to ensure that the needs and perspectives of people with stroke were appropriately addressed. They support and accompany the project throughout its entire duration and contribute their feedback (eg, on study design, intervention components and implementation strategies). During the pilot study, we will hold bimonthly project meetings with the advisory board to discuss study progress and preliminary findings.

Discussion

In accordance with the MRC framework,33 the objective of this pilot study is to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the StrokeCompass programme. The stepped-wedge cluster-randomised design will enable the collection of comprehensive information on implementation processes, contextual and organisational factors across seven study centres, and the economic implications of delivering the StrokeCompass programme. Furthermore, the pilot study will provide preliminary estimates of the effectiveness of the StrokeCompass programme, as well as whether the anticipated benefits of the intervention justify its costs. These findings will be crucial for the justification and planning of a subsequent full-powered effectiveness study.

The StrokeCompass project constitutes a complex intervention aimed at improving medication adherence through the use of PtDAs, involving people with stroke in decision-making processes and providing long-term support and assistance with medication adherence. The intervention’s complexity requires substantial engagement and time resources from the participating centres and particularly from the nursing staff. However, there is a paucity of nursing staff in Germany,70 which means that they are often exposed to high workloads and have limited time resources. It may prove challenging to motivate nurses to participate in the study. Furthermore, a high level of organisational effort is to be anticipated, especially during study initiation and the early intervention phase, which may result in delays or reduced recruitment efficiency. A further challenge for recruitment may arise from poststroke symptoms that are common among patients and may diminish their willingness to participate. These symptoms may include neurological or cognitive dysfunction, anxiety71 or depression.72–74 Furthermore, participation in this study requires people with stroke to be able to read and comprehend the informational materials, in addition to attending the planned coaching session. This approach, therefore, excludes severe strokes. Nevertheless, if the intervention proves both feasible and effective, it may be possible to expand the scope to include individuals with severe strokes. This could be achieved by incorporating family members, friends, caregivers or other professionals, such as speech and language therapists, into the intervention.

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of physicians and nurses is not feasible, and full blinding of participants may not be entirely achievable. Although participants will not be informed about the presence of a control and intervention group or the respective allocation of centres at the time of recruitment, they may still infer their group assignment, which could influence their motivation to enrol in the study or remain engaged throughout follow-up. Furthermore, differential levels of study contact across treatment groups may also lead to higher engagement and response rates in the intervention group compared with the control group. It is anticipated that some participants in both groups will be unable or unwilling to return to the study centres for the 12-month follow-up visit, resulting in incomplete outcome data.

Harms

The intervention is not expected to pose significant risks of adverse events to participants; therefore, harms will not be systematically evaluated. No interim analyses or stopping rules have been scheduled. Blood samples will be collected as part of the outcome assessment. Although venipuncture is generally safe, minor local complications such as infection, haematoma, thrombosis or nerve injury may occur. Participants can leave the study at any time and may withdraw consent without providing a reason. No specific criteria for discontinuing the allocated intervention are defined.

Ethics and dissemination

The project is planned and carried out in accordance with the ethical principles of the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’ and the ICH guideline on Good Clinical Practice (GCP). We have obtained approval from the Medical Ethics Committee in Lübeck (reference 2024-522; version 1 from 4 November 2024; version 4 from 17 December 2025) and have informed the responsible ethics committees of all other centres about the study in accordance with §15 BO ÄKN. All protocol amendments will be submitted and approved through the responsible ethics committees. All changes will be noted in the study registration. Participation is voluntary, and only patients who provide written informed consent will be included in the study.

We will publish study results in an international, peer-reviewed journal and present them at relevant scientific conferences. All results will be reported in accordance with this study protocol and established reporting guidelines.

Trial status

Recruitment started in April 2025 and will end in April 2026 unless extended.
