Arachnoid granulation morphologies and β‐amyloid and tau pathology in older adults
Authors: Rashi I Mehta, Tianhao Wang, Aubrey Lewis, Claire E Walker, Caroline Gebczak, Lisa L Barnes, David A Bennett, Rupal I Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONArachnoid granulations (AG) prominently enlarge with age, yet limited studies evaluate AG in neurodegeneration. Here, we investigate the associations of AG morphologies with Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODSMacroscopic AG properties were evaluated along dorsal post mortem brain specimens (n = 882). BACKGROUND

Arachnoid granulations (AG) are exophytic protrusions of the brain's middle meningeal layer, i.e., the arachnoid mater, that function in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) resorption. 1 , 2 These structures were described as early as the sixteenth century, 1 , 2 yet few studies investigate AG biology in humans. Moreover, varied descriptions of AG morphology in lower animals, including sheep and monkeys, have led to conflicting concepts regarding their structure and function in humans. 3 , 4 Thus, investigation of AG is a critical gap area in clinical anatomy and physiology.

Despite known roles of AG in intracranial fluid clearance, the associations of AG with diseases are largely uninvestigated. 5 , 6 This is due, in large part, to uncertainty regarding their typical structural properties and functions. Recent work by our group demonstrates marked heterogeneity in AG anatomy across the age spectrum, 7 suggesting potential associations of AG with age‐related neuropathologies. However, this prior analysis was limited to post mortem investigation of AG along frontal cerebral cortical regions of a limited number of hospital decedents devoid of intracranial pathology. 7 Thus, additional clinicopathologic analyses of AG, including characterization of their morphology across brain regions and associations with diseases are needed.

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent form of dementia and a major global health challenge with no known cure. 8 It is characterized by the intracerebral aggregation of β‐amyloid and paired helical filament‐tau (PHF‐Tau) proteins, biomarkers recognized in the “A/T/N” framework. 9 Though its cause remains uncertain, β‐amyloid and PHF‐Tau (i.e., “A/T”) accumulation, possibly a result of compromised brain clearance mechanisms, contributes to neurodegeneration. 10 , 11 Given the predominance of AG along parasagittal brain borders, presumed sites of fluid and molecular egress, and the importance of intracranial fluid physiology in brain maintenance and diseases, 12 , 13 we theorize that AG serve critical roles in cerebral protein clearance. To investigate the potential impact of AG on AD, we studied macroscopic properties of AG along dorsal brain surfaces of individuals who were followed in longitudinal studies of aging and donated their brain at the time of death. Herein, we report the associations of various AG morphologic properties with neuropathologic A/T indices in older adults.

METHODS

Participants

Participants derived from five ongoing community‐based longitudinal, clinical‐pathologic studies of aging at the Rush Alzheimer's Disease Center: The Religious Orders Study (ROS), 14 , 15 the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP), 15 the Minority Aging Research Study (MARS), 16 the Rush Clinical Core (ClinCore), 17 and the Latino Core (LATC). 18 ROS began enrolling Catholic clergy (nuns, priests, and brothers) in 1994 from over 40 monasteries or convents across the United States, whereas MAP began enrolling older laypersons from the Chicago region in 1997. Both studies require autopsy. MARS, ClinCore, and LATC began recruiting minoritized seniors from a variety of community‐based settings in the metropolitan Chicago area and outlying suburbs beginning in 2004, 2008, and 2015, respectively, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 and autopsy is optional but actively pursued. All studies were approved by an Institutional Review Board of the Rush University Medical Center. Adults with no known dementia at baseline who consented to undergo annual clinical evaluations were included in the studies. Across all five cohorts, the autopsy rate is over 75%. Additional details regarding study designs and components have been published previously. 15 The studies have harmonized methods for enrollment and procedures including data collection and summarization, allowing for combined datasets to be analyzed. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

Clinical evaluation

Participants completed a battery of neuropsychological tests encompassing five cognitive domains at baseline and at annual follow‐up visits. Eighteen of the tests were used to determine an overall score for global cognition. 19 For calculation, raw test scores were converted into z‐scores using the baseline mean and standard deviation for each cohort. Standardized scores were then averaged to obtain the global cognitive score. 19 After death, a board‐certified neurologist who was blinded to autopsy data reviewed available clinical information to assess whether dementia was present proximate to death as well as the likely etiology. A diagnosis of AD dementia was made according to National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer's Association (NINCDS‐ADRDA) criteria 20 , 21 and a dichotomous variable was used for analysis. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping protocol was published previously. 22 For analyses, the APOE genotypes were grouped by presence or absence of an APOE ε2 or ε4 allele.

Brain autopsy

Brain autopsies were performed by trained staff who were blinded to all demographic, genetic, clinical, and other neuropathologic data. As previously described, a standardized procedure was used for autopsy procedures following variable post mortem intervals (PMI). 15 In brief, the brains of deceased participants were carefully and systematically extracted, weighed, and photographed in various precise planes after intracranial removal and brain images were stored in an internal database. For processing, one cerebral hemisphere was frozen while the other cerebral hemisphere was sliced into 1 cm coronal slabs that were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 to 21 days. Brain photographs were examined to evaluate AG and brain lobe lengths, which were measured using landmarks on the medial hemispheric surface, i.e., in the longitudinal fissure. Specifically, lines between the frontal pole and central sulcus, central sulcus and parietooccipital sulcus, and parietooccipital sulcus and occipital pole were measured to account for frontal, parietal and occipital lobe lengths, respectively. The values for each lobe length for a given brain were summated to determine the brain length, which was also recorded. Fixed brain slabs were examined to evaluate macroscopic tissue lesions and diagnostic tissue blocks were dissected from standardized brain regions (i.e., at least nine brain regions in total, encompassing middle frontal, superior temporal and middle temporal, amygdala and entorhinal cortex (anterior section), hippocampus, inferior parietal lobule/angular gyrus, occipital/calcarine, basal ganglia, thalamus, and midbrain) as well as areas with grossly visible brain lesions. Collected tissue blocks were paraffin embedded and serially sliced into thin or thick sections (6 or 20 µm, respectively) that were mounted onto glass slides. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained sections were prepared from each block and modified Bielschowsky silver stain and immunohistochemistry slides were prepared from designated blocks, according to consensus protocols. 23 , 24 H&E and special stain preparations used 6 µm sections, whereas IHC preparations used thin or thick sections. Diagnostic examinations were overseen by board‐certified neuropathologists who were blinded to all demographic, genetic, and clinical data.

For evaluation of macroscopic AG properties, bilateral frontal, parietal, and occipital regions were examined for the presence or absence of AG on post mortem brain photographs. AG characteristics were recorded for a given brain region only if a dorsal brain photograph was available in the database and if the leptomeninges were present and intact on the brain surface. To ensure systematic assessment of AG, brains with obscuration by hemorrhage, bonesaw damage and/or other lesions that obstructed visualization of the dorsal brain surface were excluded (Figure S1). Data collection on AG properties was overseen by a licensed pathologist who was experienced and board‐certified in both anatomic pathology and neuropathology. Each brain photograph was reviewed by at least two reviewers who underwent extensive training with the neuropathologist. The hue, silhouette, sheen, texture, shadow, and/or embossment characteristics were all assessed during evaluation of AG. AG were gray‐to‐cream or off‐white in color, but not white. They had smooth, round‐to‐ovoid or irregular borders with raised edges (i.e., convex surfaces with peri‐lobar/peri‐lobular shadows), and a glistening membranous covering. For this study, AG were regarded as arachnoid mater herniations that were at least 1 mm in greatest dimension and were easily distinguishable from pathologic lesions or artifacts (Figure S1). Histology was assessed in select cases to rule out AG (e.g., Figures S1–S3).

For each brain specimen, the presence, number (i.e., count/abundance), size, location(s), and morphologic type(s) of AG were assessed on bilateral dorsal brain surfaces. For AG count, AG were semiquantitatively scored using an ordinal scale: 0 (none), 1 (1 to 5), 2 (6 to 10), 3 (11 to 15), 4 (16 to 20), 5 (21 to 50), 6 (51 to 100), 7 (101 to 200), or 8 (over 200) per brain region. The maximum (max) dimension (in mm) and location of the ten largest AG per brain specimen were recorded. For AG type, AG were characterized as solitary, linear, or patch morphology. The solitary type was characterized by single, discrete, or individually scattered unilobar AG, whereas linear type included conglomerated AG that were composed of multiple AG present in elongated strands (at least 1 cm long and less than 5 mm wide). When present, the linear type was always oriented parallel to (and situated within 1 cm from) the midsagittal plane. Patch type included conglomerated AG (five or more contiguous AG) that were ovoid, round or irregular in shape, in which the area occupied at least 8 mm by 8 mm area. Properties were assessed for each cerebral lobe, which often had more than one AG morphologic type present. For AG count, the mean semiquantitative score between the left and right sides and max in the left or right side (whichever was greatest) were used for analyses. For max AG size, the largest AG dimension across bilateral lobes or hemispheres was used. For AG type, each AG morphology (i.e., solitary, linear, or patch) was summarized as present or absent and the number of patch type AG (i.e., “patch count”) was recorded for each brain region using an ordinal score representing 0 (none), 1 (singular patch), or 2 (more than 1 patch). For analyses of patch count, the mean of left and right lobes (or hemispheres) was used. AG counts and AG size were found to be reliable between raters when considering ordinal scores and max dimension, respectively. Two other AG types (i.e., plaque type and type with satellite lobes/lobules; Figures S2,S3, respectively) were observed rarely. They were not further studied here, and it is unclear if they represent variations of the patch type. For this study, data were not analyzed with regard to laterality.

Parenchymal β‐amyloid was digitally quantified on immunohistochemistry (IHC) preparations. 25 In brief, β‐amyloid was measured in eight regions (angular gyrus, anterior cingulate, calcarine cortex, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, inferior temporal, and superior frontal and midfrontal gyrus). For specimens collected prior to 2019, 20‐micron thick tissue sections were labeled using one of three monoclonal anti–β‐amyloid antibodies: 4G8 (1:9000; Covance Labs, Madison, WI), 6F/3D (1:50; Dako North America, Inc., Carpinteria, CA), or 10D5 (1:600; Elan Pharmaceuticals, San Francisco, CA). Regions of interest (ROIs) excluding meninges were manually traced using Stereo Investigator and the systematic random sampling (SRS) grid was applied. The percentage of images positive for β‐amyloid was calculated. Since 2019, anti–β‐amyloid (4G8) labeled slides were digitally scanned using the Aperio AT2 whole slide scanning system. ROIs were manually outlined and digital image analysis for β‐amyloid was quantified using the Aperio Image Analysis Toolbox Positive Pixel Count algorithm, which was applied to each of the 8 brain regions. β‐amyloid burden was calculated for each slide by the percentage of area with positive pixels, resulting in a value for each slide. For β‐amyloid scores, digital β‐amyloid data was used when available; otherwise, a digital score was predicted from the historical non‐digital data and the final value represents the mean across each of the eight brain regions.

The burden of PHF‐Tau‐positive NFT within brain parenchyma was similarly evaluated and digitally quantified on IHC. 25 Tangle scores (AT8; 1:2000, ThermoScientific) were measured in the same eight regions used for β‐amyloid IHC. From historical sections, ROI were manually traced using Stereo Investigator and the SRS grid was applied. PHF‐Tau–positive tangle counts within a defined area were manually calculated as a density (count) per mm2. Since, 2019, anti–PHF‐Tau labeled slides were digitally scanned using the Aperio AT2 whole slide scanning system. Digital image analysis for tau‐tangles was quantified using the Aperio Image Analysis Toolbox Nuclear algorithm, which was applied to whole slide images. Tau‐tangle burden was calculated for each slide by the sum of nuclei with medium/strong anti–PHF‐Tau label divided by the area analyzed, resulting in a value of nuclei in counts per mm2. Digital scores represented the density of tangle counts per mm2. For PHF‐Tau tangle scores, digital tangle scores were used when available; otherwise, a digital score was predicted from historical non‐digital data and the final value represents the mean value across each brain region.

Presence of AD pathology and A/T indices were also evaluated. The presence of diffuse plaques, neuritic plaques, and neurofibrillary tangles was evaluated on modified Bielschowsky silver stain. Each lesion type was also counted in the entorhinal, hippocampal CA1, middle frontal, superior temporal, and angular gyrus regions exhibiting the highest count in a 1 mm2 area. Per National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer Association criteria, 23 Alzheimer's disease neuropathologic change (ADNC) was used to render a pathologic diagnosis of AD. Disease severity was graded as: 0 (no ADNC), 1 (low ADNC), 2 (intermediate ADNC), and 3 (high ADNC) and a pathologic diagnosis of AD required intermediate or high ADNC. 24 For this study, an ordinal variable indicating none, low, intermediate, or high ADNC was used along with a quantitative diffuse plaque variable that reflected the intracerebral diffuse plaque count. Quantitative neuritic plaque and neurofibrillary tangle variables (based on silver stain and reflecting the count of the individual lesions) and IHC A/T indices (β‐amyloid and PHF‐Tau) were used and were square root transformed due to positive skewness. Total plaques (a combined measure of diffuse plaques and neuritic plaques), A score (a measure of Thal phase, i.e., topographical intraparenchymal β‐amyloid score), B score (a measure of Braak stage, i.e., topographical neurofibrillary tangle score), and C score (a measure of neocortical neuritic plaque density) were additionally analyzed. 24

Statistical analysis

For AG type, dichotomous variables representing the presence or absence of solitary, linear, or patch type AG were used. For AG size, a continuous measure was used, whereas ordinal scores were used for AG count and patch count. Chi‐square and t tests were used to compare demographic and AG properties. Multiple regression analyses were used to evaluate the associations of AG properties with the following: (1) ADNC score 24 ; (2) individual neuropathologic lesions [diffuse plaques, neuritic plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, β‐amyloid, and PHF‐Tau]; (3) combined neuropathologic lesions or summary scores (total plaques and A/B/C scores); and (4) presence of dementia. Specifically, logistic regression models were used to test the associations of AG type with neuropathologic indices and AG properties with presence of dementia. Ordinal regression models were used to test the associations of AG count and AG patch count with neuropathologic indices or presence of dementia. Linear regression models were used to test the associations of AG size with neuropathologic indices or presence of dementia. Using these models, we also checked the potential interaction effects of AG properties with each other and with sex and race (i.e., non‐Latino Black versus non‐Latino White individuals). In additional secondary analyses, we adjusted the primary models for brain weight, lobe length, PMI, presence of APOE Ɛ2 or Ɛ4 allele, and anti–β‐amyloid antibody and checked potential interactions of AG properties by sex and AD status (i.e., presence versus absence of intermediate to high ADNC). All models controlled for age at death, education, and sex (except for models that were stratified by sex). Analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). A threshold of p < 0.05 was used for statistical significance throughout.

RESULTS

Of 5408 participants who enrolled and completed baseline evaluation, 2905 died, 2213 underwent autopsy, 2157 had completed autopsy reports available in the RADC database at the time of this study, and 882 of the autopsied participants (649 MAP, 134 ROS, 70 MARS, 27 ClinCore, and two LATC participants) had available gross photographs that depicted intact leptomeninges and unobstructed dorsal brain surfaces (Figure S1). Thus, 882 brains from deceased community‐dwelling older persons were studied. The eligible participants included 326 participants without a pathologic diagnosis of AD (i.e., with no or low ADNC) and 556 participants with a pathologic diagnosis of AD (i.e., intermediate or high ADNC). APOE genotype was available in 808 (91.61%) of these participants. Demographic characteristics are summarized in the entire group and by ADNC score in Table 1 (see Tables S1, S2 for summaries by sex and race, respectively). Briefly, participants died on average at the age of 90.62 ± 6.98 (SD) years, were highly educated (15.45 ± 3.26 years), and mostly female (n = 660, 75%) Of the total, 130 (14.7%) were APOE ε2 allele carriers and 208 (23.6%) were APOE ε4 allele carriers. The anti–β‐amyloid antibody used at autopsy could be retrospectively confirmed and was limited to a single clone in 788 participants (89.34%). AG properties

Nearly all participants (880, 99.8%) exhibited presence of AG on the dorsal brain surface (referred to hereafter as “brain”). The AG were present in variable morphologic type, distribution, number, and size (Table 2). The presence and frequency of AG were highest in the frontal region and diminished gradually along the posterior brain convexity (Figure 1A,B). Representative images depicting AG morphologic types, including solitary, patch, and linear type are shown in Figure 1C. Overall, the solitary type was most frequent, followed by patch and then linear type (Figure 1D, Table 2; see Tables S3,S4 for summaries by sex and race, respectively). Overall, the AG size ranged from 1 mm to 5.7 cm (mean, 9.9 ± 4.2 mm). The frontal AG size correlated with parietal (corr = 0.34, p ≤ 0.001) and occipital (corr = 0.34, p = 0.006) AG sizes (Pearson correlation coefficient). Overall, the brain AG size correlated with the brain AG count (corr = 0.13, p = 0.002) (Figure 1E). Within lobar regions, the AG patch count correlated with the AG count (p ≤ 0.001 for all lobes), but not with AG size.

AG properties, age, and brain characteristics

AG counts in the frontal region (corr = 0.09, p = 0.006), parietal region (corr = 0.09, p = 0.007), and brain (corr = 0.11, p = 0.002) each correlated with age at death (Figure 1F). However, AG count in the occipital region did not correlate with age at death (corr ≤ 0.01, p = 0.992). Interestingly, AG size did not correlate with age at death in this cohort (Figure 1G), though the frontal lobe patch count (corr = 0.09, p = 0.007) and brain patch count (0.09, p = 0.011) both correlated with age at death. Notably, AG count, size, and patch count did not correlate with brain weight or PMI in any brain region. There were some differences in AG properties by sex (Table S3). In regression analyses adjusting for demographics, occipital lobe length was associated with occipital AG count (odds ratio {OR}, 1.03 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 1.01–1.06]). Brain length was associated with brain AG count (OR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.98–1.00]) as well as presence of patch type AG in the composite brain region (OR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.98–1.00]). Other AG properties were not associated with lobe lengths, brain length, or other brain characteristics when adjusting for demographics.

AG count and A/T indices

We next studied the associations of neuropathologic indices with AG count using regression models. The associations between regional AG count (max on left or right side) and various neuropathologic indices are summarized in Figure 2 and Table S5. We found that brain β‐amyloid load was inversely associated with AG count in the frontal region (OR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.69–0.98]), parietal region (OR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.63–0.89]), occipital region (OR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.67–0.97]), and brain (OR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.66–0.95]). Similarly, brain neurofibrillary tangle load was inversely associated with AG count in the frontal region (OR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.73–1.00]), parietal region (OR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.72–0.98]), occipital region (OR, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.65–0.90]), and brain (OR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.71–0.98]). Multiple additional associations were found between neuropathologic indices and AG count (Figure 3 and Table S5). Occipital AG count was inversely associated with ADNC score (OR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.75–0.99]), neuritic plaque count (OR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.73–0.99]), and PHF‐Tau burden (OR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.75–0.97]). Parietal AG count (OR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.64–0.93]) and occipital AG count (OR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.58–0.88]) were each inversely associated with B score. Parietal AG count was inversely associated with total plaque load (OR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.75–1.00]), and brain AG count was inversely associated with diffuse plaque load (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.65–0.99]). The associations were largely similar when considering the mean AG count across the left and right sides (not depicted in figures).

AG type and A/T indices

Next, we studied the associations of neuropathologic indices with AG type (i.e., solitary, patch, and linear) using regression models (Table S6). We found that brain β‐amyloid load was inversely associated with presence of patch type AG in the frontal region (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.66–0.96]) and brain (OR, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.63–0.92]). Additionally, B score was inversely associated with presence of patch type AG in the brain (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.64–0.99]). There were no associations between any other neuropathologic indices and presence of patch type AG in any region. Additionally, there were no associations between any neuropathologic index and presence of solitary or linear AG types (not depicted in Table). Upon consideration of more quantitative variables for analysis of patch type AG, we next studied the associations of neuropathologic indices with AG patch count in different brain regions. As summarized in Figure 3 and Table S7, we found that β‐amyloid load was inversely associated with AG patch count in the frontal region (OR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.67–0.95]) and brain (OR, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.65–0.92]), total plaques were inversely associated with AG patch count in the parietal region (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.70–0.99]), and B score was inversely associated with AG patch count in the brain (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.66–0.96]). Although distance of AG from the midline was not examined in this study, it was noted that linear type AG always closely approximated the midline (i.e., they were present within 1 cm of the midsagittal plane) whereas solitary and patch type were more variable in location.

AG size and A/T indices

No significant associations were found between any neuropathologic indices and AG size in any brain region (i.e., frontal, parietal, occipital, or brain; not illustrated in figures) when considering max size in various dorsal brain regions evaluated using regression models.

AG properties and dementia

Next, we studied the association of dementia with AG properties. We found that presence of linear type AG in the frontal (OR, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.03–2.07]) and parietal (OR, 1.57 [95% CI, 1.02–2.43]) regions was associated with higher odds of dementia (Table S8). Due to the rarity of linear type AG in the occipital region (Table 2), robust analysis was not available for this region. Presence of solitary or patch type AG (in any brain region) was not associated with dementia (Table S8). No significant associations were found between presence of AG, AG count, AG patch count, or AG size (in any brain region) and dementia (not depicted in Table).

Interaction effects of AG properties on their associations with A/T indices

Next, we examined whether combinations of AG properties affect the associations with A/T indices (Table S17). An interaction was found between frontal AG count and frontal AG size for the outcome of PHF‐Tau (coef. = ‐0.01, p = 0.014), whereby if decedents had larger AG size in the frontal region, then the inverse association of PHF‐Tau load with AG count in the frontal region would be higher than those with smaller AG size. Additionally, an interaction was found between brain AG patch count and brain AG size for the outcome of B score (coef. = 0.03, p = 0.043), whereby if decedents had larger AG size in the brain, then the inverse association of B score with AG patch count would be lower than those with smaller AG size.

Interactions were also found when stratifying by AD status (i.e., presence versus absence of intermediate to high ADNC) 23 (Table S18). Negative interactions between frontal AG count and frontal AG size were identified, whereby if frontal AG size was larger, then the inverse association of PHF‐Tau burden with frontal AG count was higher than those with smaller AG size in participants both with a pathologic diagnosis of AD (coef. = ‐0.02, p = 0.046) and without a pathologic diagnosis of AD (coef. = ‐0.01, p = 0.008). Additionally, there was a negative interaction between brain AG count and brain AG size, whereby if brain AG size was larger, then the inverse association of PHF‐Tau burden with brain AG count was higher than those with smaller AG size in participants without (but not with) a pathologic diagnosis of AD (coef. = ‐0.01, p = 0.043). Additional interactions testing for effects of sex and race are summarized in supplementary Tables (see Tables S19–S21). Notably, interaction effects of race were found in the associations of AG count with various neuropathologic indices, implying that their associations differed in non‐Latino Black versus White participants (Table S21).

DISCUSSION

We explored anatomic AG properties in nearly 900 post mortem brains derived from older adults who enrolled in longitudinal studies of aging and found that almost all (over 99%) exhibit AG along the dorsal brain surface. As expected, the morphology, distribution, abundance, and dimensions of AG varied widely among participants. Overall, AG were most frequent in the frontal region but were largest along the parietal convexity. Despite prominent morphologic heterogeneity, the study identified inverse associations between dorsal brain AG count and intracerebral β‐amyloid load as well as neurofibrillary tangle counts. Notably, the abundance of these neuropathologies inversely associated with AG count in all regions (i.e., frontal, parietal, occipital, and composite dorsal brain). Additional AD neuropathologic indices were inversely associated with AG count in specific brain regions. The findings suggest that AG may play a role in the pathologic aggregation of intracerebral β‐amyloid and hyperphosphorylated tau‐bearing neurofibrillary tangles. More specifically, higher number of AG may promote clearance of these AD‐related protein aggregates.

This clinicopathologic study also highlights the heterogeneity of AG morphologies. While the simple, solitary type was most common across and within brain specimens, complex shapes with conglomerated patch or linear arrangements were also observed in subsets of individuals. To our knowledge the linear type, which appears as an elongated narrow strand parallel to the midline, is not well described in prior literature. Here, we found that this type always approximated the midline, while the solitary and patch types were more variable in location. The etiology and biological significance of conglomerated AG types are unclear, and these were rarely present in the occipital region. In dorsal brain, the AG patch count correlated highly with age and AG count, but not with AG size. Presence of patch type AG in frontal, parietal, and dorsal brain regions each inversely associated with multiple AD‐related neuropathologic indices, but not with dementia. Specifically, regression analyses showed that total plaques, B score, and β‐amyloid were each inversely associated with patch count in one or more brain regions. Conversely, presence of linear type AG was not associated with any AD‐related neuropathologic index, but its presence in the frontal and parietal regions was associated with approximately 50% increased risk of dementia. These data may indicate that AG types have distinct roles in brain function and disease, though much more work and confirmatory studies are needed.

Despite the association of various AD neuropathologic indices with AG counts and AG morphologic type, no AD neuropathologic index examined in this study was independently associated with AG size when considering the maximum size per brain region. However, in interaction analyses, we found that larger AG size in the frontal region increased the inverse association between AG count with PHF‐Tau load. Additionally, larger AG size in the dorsal brain mitigated the inverse association between AG patch count and B score. The reasons for these effects are unclear. However, these results raise the possibility that combinations of AG morphologic properties could have biologically important roles in cerebral protein clearance that may also depend on brain region.

We also explored how demographic factors affect associations of AG properties with AD neuropathologic indices. We found that many of the associations identified in the entire group of participants were identified in females, but not in males. Interestingly, striking differences were also identified across race. Whereas multiple AD‐related neuropathologic measures were associated with lower AG count in non‐Latino White individuals, they were associated with higher AG count in non‐Latino Black individuals. This was true across multiple dorsal brain regions and was statistically significant for select indices when considering AG count in the frontal region. The reasons for these discordant findings are unknown. As the study group consisted mostly of females and non‐Latino White individuals, these differences could be due, in part, to the sample size considerations. For sex differences, further interaction analysis only confirms potential sex difference in association with diffuse plaques and presence of solitary type AG in the frontal lobe and frontal AG count (Table S19). For racial differences, we observed significant interactions in almost all neuropathologic indices apart from neurofibrillary tangles (Table S21). As AG may contribute to immunity and various diseases, 26 and comorbidities vary by race, 27 , 28 , 29 the roles of other factors should be considered in future analyses incorporating diverse participants.

Recent literature suggests that fluid circulates through the brain in a cyclical manner and eventually drains along meningeal routes. 26 , 30 Increasing evidence suggests that this fluid circuit influences the transport and elimination of various biological molecules including waste proteins. However, studies on the specific anatomic conduits and fluid egress pathways involved in this clearance mechanism are limited in humans, despite potential signifiance for neurotherapeutics. While AG are located at the interface between the brain and the periphery, including at the site of CSF drainage into parasagittal dura in humans, 7 , 31 they have received limited attention in clinical literature. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the associations between AG properties and brain β‐amyloid as well as neurofibrillary tangle densities. The associations identified here suggest that AG morphology may influence AD‐related proteinopathies. In light of the present findings, additional longitudinal clinicopathological studies assessing potential relationships between AD neuropathologic changes and AG structure and function may reveal new insights into relevant biomarkers in AD.

Finally, this study has limitations. The participants were predominantly highly educated non‐Latino White adults above the age of 65 years 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 and effects of metabolic, immune, lifestyle, and comorbid factors were not examined. Given the post mortem and cross‐sectional nature of the analysis, intradural AG may be underrepresented and cause and effect relationships cannot be drawn. This exploratory work did not adjust for multiple comparisons. We observed differential associations between White and Black individuals. While the statistical significance was not strong, this may be due to either differences in biology between races or insufficient control of potential confounders. More rigorous matching methods might provide a more accurate inference here but would require an even larger sample size, which deserves further careful consideration. Artificial intelligence‐assisted quantification of AG may also enhance future analyses. Additionally, cored and granular plaque types were not specifically evaluated and AG counts were measured using two‐dimensional post mortem digital brain images, whereas three‐dimensional volumetric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) analyses of AG may enhance future analyses. 32 Despite these limitations, this study also has some strengths. The post mortem approach allowed for direct visualization of complex AG morphologic properties that may not be recognizable by MRI. The analysis also incorporated findings from almost 900 brains that were processed and evaluated in a systematic manner. Various AG morphologic properties were studied and several neuropathologic indices were incorporated in the analysis. Given the large sample size, it was also possible to explore AG properties across demographics and AD status. Thus, this analysis should help inform the design of future investigations of AG in older adults and suggests the need for consideration of demographic, neuropathologic, and comorbid factors.
