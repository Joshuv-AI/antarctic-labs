Executive Summary
The repetitive pulsed electric drill (RePED-250) is a novel prototype geothermal drilling method
that requires a high temperature alternator to produce power down well. The RePED-250
alternator has been designed and built to operate at 35 kilowatts (kW) under 250°C ambient
temperature conditions. The operating efficiency is measured using a 225 kW motor to drive the
machine in a back-to-back configuration. The goal of this test is to determine if the RePED-250
alternator can deliver 35 kW at a rotational speed of 1,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) while
operating at 250°C ambient temperature and have a minimum efficiency of 60%. Dynamometer
testing of a high-temperature alternator for geothermal drilling presents several unique
challenges, especially around maintaining and managing the various sources and types of energy
passing through the system without impacting data quality. The objectives of this report are to
(1) describe the test stand in detail, the relative orientation of each major component, and the
data acquisition system and (2) discuss the behavior of the test stand components such that this
report can serve as a reference document for future projects.

1 Introduction
The repetitive pulsed electric drill (RePED-250) is a novel prototype geothermal drilling method
that requires a high temperature alternator to produce power down well. The RePED-250
alternator has been designed and built to operate at 35 kilowatts (kW) under 250°C ambient
temperature conditions. The operating efficiency is measured using a 225 kW motor to drive the
machine in a back-to-back configuration. The alternator is heated to temperatures up to 250°C
and loaded using an adjustable bidirectional AC power supply. The goal is to determine if the
RePED-250 alternator can deliver 35 kW at a rotational speed of 1,000 revolutions per minute
(rpm) while operating at 250°C ambient temperature and have a minimum efficiency of 60%.

Dynamometer testing of a high-temperature alternator for geothermal drilling presents several
unique challenges, especially around maintaining and managing the various sources and types of
energy passing through the system without impacting data quality. To accomplish these
objectives, a custom test stand was designed. It included an insulated housing with temperature
rated input/output ports, high-temperature electrical enclosures rated for the expected currents
and voltages, and data acquisition (DAQ) systems to collect time-synced data from various
sensors distributed throughout the test stand.

The objectives of this report are to (1) describe the test stand in detail, the relative orientation of
each major component, and the DAQ system and (2) discuss the behavior of the components
such that this report can serve as a reference document for future projects. The authors believe
this document can greatly speed up the design, setup, and commissioning of similar
dynamometer test stands in the future to reduce cost and lower the barrier to energy for future
projects.

2 RePED-250 Alternator Test Stand
2.1 Stand Overview
A high-level diagram of the test stand is shown in Figure 1. This system is simple with only a
few major components. For clarity, minor components such as low-voltage power supplies and
electrical disconnects have been omitted. The arrows and coloring in the diagram are used to
differentiate the type and direction of energy flow through the system. The diagram identifies
what components are connected and what each component is sending to the next. Here, black
indicates command signals, orange is mechanical energy from the drive shafts, blue is electrical
energy (specific voltage and currents omitted), red is thermal energy, and green indicates data
signals.

 Figure 1. Conceptual block diagram showing signal and energy flow throughout the RePED-250
 alternator test stand. The insulated housing is shown as a dotted line. Black indicates command
 signals, orange indicates mechanical energy direction, blue represents electrical signals and
 power, red indicates thermal energy direction, and green indicates data signals.
Figure 2 shows the as-built test stand with the notable equipment labeled. The alternator is
connected to the 225 kW dynamometer motor through a contactless torque transducer and a
custom-designed drive shaft. The RePED-250 alternator is contained inside the insulated housing
to maximize thermal stability. A series of heaters, described below, are also wrapped around the
RePED-250 alternator to assist with preheating and thermal stability. The opposite side of the
test stand is shown in Figure 3. This side supports the landings for the RePED-250 alternator’s
three-phase power output and DAQ. The dynamometer and generator DAQ systems will be
discussed below.

The dynamometer is powered by a 225 kW three-phase AC motor supplied by a 480-volt (V), 50
ampere (A) connection. The motor is controlled by an ABB ACS880 series variable frequency
drive (VFD). The drive is supplied by an ACS880-207-0400A-5 supply unit, and the motor is
driven by an ACS880-107-0440A-5 drive unit. Motor output torque is measured with a Lebow
model 1105-10k torque transducer with a maximum speed and torque rating of 8,500 rpm and
1,130 newton-meters (Nm) (Honeywell 2006). The driveshaft is a custom automotive-type
universal joint driveshaft with the ability to compensate for small misalignments between the

motor and the RePED-250 alternator. The universal joints are 1350 series with 1,680 Nm
maximum torque rating. This yielded a factor of safety larger than 2 times, so it was deemed
acceptable. Single flex-disk style shaft couplings are used between the rotating components. The
visible yellow guarding is to prevent accidental contact with rotating components.

The insulated housing is constructed from 22-gauge bent sheet metal with Rockwool
Comfortbatt R15 housing insulation glued to the inside. The insulation temperature rating of
1,000°C was more than adequate for the 300°C maximum temperature operation of the alternator
under testing (Rockwool 2020). The heaters were expected to draw 5 kW during operation.

Figure 3 shows the opposite side of the test stand. Here, the three-phase raw AC power output
from the RePED-250 alternator is connected to a resistive load bank through low-resistive
copper bus bars, shown in Figure 4. Electric power output from the RePED-250 alternator is
handled with three 10 American Wire Gauge (AWG) high-temperature wires—Gore magnetic
wire rated up to 260°C (W. L. Gore & Associations 2021). Internal National Laboratory of the
Rockies testing demonstrated that these wires can operate at up to 316°C for up to 3 hours. These
wires are expected to carry a maximum of 480 volts alternating current (VAC) root-mean-square
(RMS) and 50 A. It is possible for these wires to generate significant heat during operation and
reach a temperature of 300°C. This exceeds the wire’s rated temperature; however, this is the
highest rated readily available wire, and the risks are mitigated by active cooling of the enclosure
and monitoring. It was important to use temperature-rated terminals and actively cool the bus
bars with the inlet and exhaust fans shown on the left and right side of the enclosure. Within the
alternator output junction box, the output wires are connected to large copper buses with high-
temperature-rated terminal ends to accommodate the possible heating. The bus bars are isolated
from the enclosure with electrical and thermal insulating spacers. The three-phase load bank
connection is made with 4-AWG cables rated for the expected volage and current. Current and
voltage transducers are also fixed to this end of the bus bars to protect them from excessive
heating. Additionally, temperatures within the enclosure are monitored continuously to ensure
safe operation.

2.2 Housing Insulation and Heating
To establish and maintain the necessary testing temperature, the RePED-250 alternator is
wrapped in eight resistive heating elements controlled by a programable logic controller (PLC).
These are flexible resistive tape-type heating elements (4550T142 McMaster-Carr, Elmhurst, IL)
with rated maximum temperature and power of 760°C and 624 watts (W) each, respectively.
They are fixed to the RePED-250 alternator’s outer surface with moisture activated fiberglass
plaster tape. The heaters and fan were controlled by a CLICK Plus C2-03CPU-2 PLC. This unit
also controls a small fan that circulates air within the insulated housing to create a more uniform
heating environment. The heating and fan units are fed by an XP Power DNR120 power supply
inside the PLC enclosure, shown on the far right of Figure 2.

The logic circuit for the heaters is shown in Figure 5. The most notable features of this circuit are
the two independent fault detection systems. An emergency stop button (Estop) and thermal
cutoff switch were placed in series with the PLC control signal. The Estop is a manual interlock
that can be triggered by the operators. The thermal cutoff switch monitors the housing
temperature and can automatically engage the interlock in the event of thermal runaway.
Essentially, the fault detection interlock acts as a Boolean Or gate, disabling the heating system
if either the Estop or cutoff switch is triggered. Due to the elevated temperatures and long test
durations, it was critical to establish this logic circuit to maintain the desired temperatures while
protecting equipment and personnel.

 5

 Figure 5. Heater control logic circuit. The PLC control signal is connected in series with two
  independent error detectors, an E-stop switch and a thermal cutoff switch. Either of these can
 disable the entire circuit in the event of a fault.
The heating elements are wrapped tightly around the RePED-250 alternator in eight concentric
rings organized into two groups. The first four rings cover the middle two quarters of alternator’s
axial length while the second four rings cover the first and last quarter. Due to heat sinking into
the fixture (Figure 6), the ends of the alternator are expected to require more heating to maintain
the desired temperature. Creating two heating zones provides a greater degree of temperature
control.

The housing is lined with Rockwool Comfortbatt R15 fire resistant insulation rated up to
1,000°C. A solid model of the inside of the housing with the RePED-250 alternator is shown in
Figure 6. To reduce conduction out of the alternator through the fixture, a series of ceramic
insulating spacers are bolted to either end of the RePED-250 alternator and mounted to the
adjustment plate. The spacers float in the plate to compensate for differential thermal expansion
of the alternator and fixture. The adjustment plates can slide vertically to create an air gap
between the aluminum fixture and the alternator’s outer wall. A thin, high-temperature ceramic
pipe insulating strip was placed in this air gap to minimize convective heat transfer between
those two surfaces. With this setup, the only conduction path is from the high-temperature
alternator into the ceramic spacers, then into the fixture. Convection cannot be eliminated, but
the relatively small internal volume and the fan were intended to create a uniform stable housing
temperature. The shaft coupling protruded through a small hole in the front of the housing (not
shown) and connected to the rest of the drivetrain visible in Figure 2.

2.3 Sensors and Data Acquisition
The RePED-250 alternator, as-built from the manufacturer, has 10 k-type thermocouples
installed throughout the length of the stator to measure the internal temperature. The
thermocouples, resistive temperature devices (RTD), and current and voltage transducers are
connected to NI 9211, NI 9217, and NI 9239 cards, respectively. These are mounted in an NI
9144 chassis. A block diagram of the heating and feedback system is shown in Figure 8. For

 8

simplicity, a single line diagram for each sensor is not included; however, proper wiring
diagrams for thermocouples, RTDs, and transducers are available from National Instruments
(National Instruments 2023; 2024a; 2024b). A CLICK PLC (Automation Direct n.d.) manages
the feedback from the RTDs to control the heater temperatures. The current transducers used for
this work were LEM HAL 100-S. These are Hall effect 100-A range transducers with voltage
output. The voltage measurement transducers were Verivolt IsoBlock V-1c (1,000 V:10 V) units
with a 1,000 V (AC or DC) range and 10-VDC output.

 The dynamometer-alternator shaft torque was measured with a Lebow 1105-10k torque
transducer (1,130 Nm capacity) connected to NI 9237 bridge input module. The dynamometer
speed was measured by a BEI HS25 encoder. This encoder was directly connected to the ABB
880 variable frequency drive to provide control feedback.

Data were collected in two groups, fast and slow, through an EtherCAT network controlled with
a custom LabVIEW program. The fast group recorded data at 1,000 hertz (Hz) and included the
voltage, current, motor speed, and torque data. The slow group recorded data at 100 Hz and
included the thermocouples and RTDs. Given the alternator’s size and thermal mass, the thermal
response time was expected to be relatively slow; therefore, the slower sample rate reduced file
sizes without decreasing resolution. However, a higher sample rate was deemed necessary to
accurately record the dynamometer behavior and the alternator’s electrical performance.

3 Test Stand Verification
Preliminary sensor data were collected to verify all sensors were functioning properly prior to
testing and to validate the thermal housing operation. These data are not meant to characterize
the RePED-250 alternator but to demonstrate that the test stand performs as expected.

3.1 Thermal Stability
Temperature measurements were recorded from the thermocouples and RTDs during operation
to validate their performance. These measurements are shown in Figure 9. The internal
thermocouples, denoted by Twinding, measure rapid heat generation due to resistive joule heating.
The RTDs in Zone 1, Tstator-Z1, measure the temperature on either end of the RePED-250
alternator closest to the fixture. The temperature is consistently lower here because of heat
sinking into the fixture. While efforts were made to minimize this heat loss, it could not be
prevented altogether. The temperature in Zone 2, Tstator-Z2, near the middle of the RePED-250
alternator, does not rise as quickly as the temperature of the windings because of the RePED-250
alternator’s thermal mass. After approximately 3 hours, the external temperature normalizes with
the internal temperature measured in the windings. Stable temperature readings from all
thermocouples and RTDs across this temperature range indicate the sensors are functioning as
intended.

Figure 9. Temperature rise of the RePED-250 alternator during operation. The RTDs in Zone 1 can
be clearly seen on either end of the RePED-250 alternator while those in the middle are in Zone 2.

3.2.1 Voltage and Current
The voltage and current transducers were also successfully able to record the three-phase output
from the RePED-250 alternator. Voltage for each phase was recorded at 300 V and 80 A under
nominal operating speeds and temperatures. Figure 10 shows the output from a single phase for
clarity, but all three phases showed similar results.

3.2.2 Speed and Torque
The input power is directly proportional to the speed and torque of the motor, both of which were
measured using the test stand. Similarly, the output power is determined from the measured
voltage and current produced by the alternator. The measured results for the input and output
power of the alternator are shown in Figure 11 The input and output power measurements
correlate well with the finite element analysis predicted performance of the RePED-250
alternator (Labuschagne et al. 2025); therefore, the sensors and data acquisition system are
accepted as functioning properly. The offset between the measured and predicted results are
caused by frictional losses in the system between the motor and alternator, which were not
accounted for in the finite element analysis of the alternator, only the iron core losses and
resistive losses in the windings were accounted for.

4 Conclusions
Conducting high-temperature dynamometer testing of the alternator presented several unique
challenges related to safety concerns, temperature control requirements, and data collection. To
address the temperature control requirements, an insulated housing and isolation fixture were
designed and fabricated. Theoretical calculations and empirical measurements showed the
housing could maintain the desired temperature and minimize risk to personnel. Along with the
thermal insulation, a convection fan was added to the housing to recirculate hot air around the
housing, and heating elements were wrapped around the RePED-250 alternator. The fan and
heating elements were controlled by a PLC.

Developing the DAQ system also required paying special attention to the temperature, expected
voltages, and expected currents. A high-temperature electrical enclosure was constructed with
active cooling over the bus bars to prevent overheating the electrical connections. Data were
collected for the 10 thermocouples and 4 RTDs at 100 Hz, and the voltage, current, speed, and
torque were recorded at 1,000 Hz. This test stand successfully completed the more than 50 hours
of testing, the full regime required for the RePED-250 project.
