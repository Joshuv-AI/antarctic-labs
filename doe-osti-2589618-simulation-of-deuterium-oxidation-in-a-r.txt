ABSTRACT
A series of experiments where deuterium was released in trace amounts into a room with a fire were
performed at Sandia National Laboratories. This report describes the corresponding effort to model
the test series using SIERRA/FUEGO. The objective of this modeling effort was to produce a
simulation test matrix that can be utilized to help interpret the corresponding experiments and be used
to assess the credibility of using the SIERRA/FUEGO simulations as a surrogate for real tritium
reaction data for mock fire and release studies. The simulations assessed multiple configurations of
fire size and location and overall found that there was low conversion of deuterium to oxidized
deuterium. There are some notable differences between the simulation predictions and the
experimental results, such as the simulations generally predicting higher amounts of deuterium
conversion compared to the experiment. A strategy to address modeling uncertainties and unknowns
is also given in this report.

1. INTRODUCTION
Tritium is a synthetic radioactive isotope of hydrogen found in many labs throughout the Department
of Energy (DOE) complex. Tritium (3H) hazard is largely linked to the fire scenario involving a release,
since human exposure to the oxidized form of tritium is much more hazardous. Despite the
importance of fires to the final hazard, data regarding the behavior of tritium in fires are nearly non-
existent. Laboratory uses of tritium generally do not involve sufficient quantities to create sustained
flames or explosions, which are the main concerns for protium (1H). Furthermore, there has been
minimal effort to expose tritium to a fire to assess the behavior of either the released gas or the tritium
containers.
Lacking specific data regarding the hazard, one good approach to safety is testing with similar but
safer materials. An example of how to do this would be to perform fire testing with a less radioactive
isotope under typical tritium handling or release conditions. The other good safety approach is
employing modeling and simulation to assess scenario hazards, which can address scenarios through
computational physics. The modeling and simulation approach takes a step beyond experimental
performance characterization with a surrogate. When surrogates are used to characterize hazards, there
is some degree of uncertainty that the target and surrogate materials will behave similarly. When
modeling and simulation is used, generally the surrogate tests are used to characterize or validate the
accuracy of the models that are designed based on physics principles. Comparisons between the
surrogate tests and the models can help establish the accuracy of the models through direct
comparisons to physically measured parameters. The models can then be extended slightly into the
operational space of the actual hazard where testing does not exist, but the simulations retain an
expected accuracy due to the demonstrated validity of the physical models. Put more simply, one
might test the performance of deuterium in a relevant fire scenario, characterize the accuracy of the
tests versus a model, then extend the modeling using the same fire scenario except with the release
adjusted to be tritium.
The quality of a surrogate depends on similarity with the actual hazard. In the case of tritium,
deuterium is the closest isotope. Deuterium lacks the radiological hazards of tritium, as it is a stable
isotope with an infinite half-life. Although deuterium is a close isotope to tritium, differing by a single
neutron, the fact that the hydrogen atom is the lightest and simplest of the elements makes this a
difference of moderate size. The molecular weight of D2 is approximately 4 AMU, whereas the
molecular weight of T2 is 6 AMU, a factor of 50% greater. This translates to a standard density
difference of about the same magnitude difference. Since tritium quantities at storage facilities are
generally low, the density effect most prominent is thought to be that of buoyancy in air. Although
the density difference is 50%, the buoyancy difference is better quantified in relation to the medium
in which it is dispersing. Assuming air has an approximate mean molecular weight of 29 AMU, the
AMU ratio between D2 and air is 4/29, or 13.7%. The AMU ratio between T2 and air is 6/29, or
20.7%. These are not as different as the 50% density difference in molecular weight, and so the
buoyant effect is likely to be a contributing factor, but not a significant one in the differences between
the buoyant behavior of the two molecules. Using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to simulate
the behavior, the density differences can generally be accounted for in the constitutive models.
A series of experiments leveraging a ¾ “scaled” ISO-9705 standard were performed at Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL) in 2023. The test selected was nominally a 75% of full-scale room with a variably
sized methanol fire. The injection was 4% molar D2 in nitrogen, with a total injection of 0.1 g of D2
per test. The Abnormal Test Environments (ATE) lab at the Thermal Test Complex was selected for
the tests because it supported a 100 kW fire, laser diagnostics, and had a hood pre-approved for fires
of this scale. Methanol was selected as a fuel because it burns cleanly, producing minimal soot. This

 13

provides a clear view for the optical sampling methods, and also provides low risk of clogging and
deposition growth for the other sampling methods. The feed of D2 in N2 was selected at 4% molar
because this was below the lower flammability limit (LFL) and precluded any risk of a detonation from
the hydrogen gas. N2 was selected because it is common, inert, and inexpensive. Because methanol
fuel forms minimal soot, the radiative feedback to the fuel surface is smaller than for most other sooty
fuels. The evaporation rate is slower, and it consequently burns slower. The fuel burn rate and power
from a pan of a given size for methanol is therefore lower than that for a similar transportation fuel
that produces soot, despite the low evaporation temperature. To provide a relevant fire power, a larger
fuel pan was needed to get the same energy output as would be expected from a smaller pan with
other fuels. These experiments were performed to develop a work-space relevant full or scale
experimental model of the ISO-9705 geometry and to perform fire tests in the model releasing
deuterium instead of tritium. While most of the pre-test simulations resulted in low conversion rates,
the pre-test simulations also indicated that high conversion could be obtained in certain scenarios,
such as the scenario of the fuel pan and deuterium diffuser located close to each other (Brown et al.,
2024); however, the experimental results indicated a low conversion for all tested scenarios.
A set of post-test simulations of the ¾ scale ISO-9705 standard described above were performed
during 2024. The simulations were performed for a series of 3 different pan/fire sizes (~22 kW, 43
kW, and 76 kW), located at varying distances from the diffuser. These simulations were performed
somewhat blind; i.e., conversations did occur between the computational analysists and the
experimentalists, but simulations were performed before the experimental results were reviewed in
any detail. Details and insights from this simulation effort and a retrospective comparison to the
experimental data are the focus of this report. One of the objectives of the simulation campaign is to
better understand the experimental results and why the scenario resulted in low conversion (even
though pre-test simulations resulted in high conversion). The hope is that the combined
experimentation and simulation efforts that will provide information and insights to better interpret
the experimental results and also improve existing models and justify using SIERRA/FUEGO as a
surrogate for real tritium reaction data for the purpose of safety assessments.

1.1. Review of Prior Work
This effort is relatively unique. We are unaware of external organizations outside ours conducting
similar tests or simulations. There are a lot of historical chemical dispersal studies, and many prior
studies on hydrogen safety. There are a lot of historical fire simulation studies. What makes this effort
particularly unique is the importance to the magnitude of the problem of the chemical being dispersed
reacting in a fire, and that the chemical of concern is a rare isotope of hydrogen. The work was initially
motivated by the regulatory guidelines for tritium safety in the U.S. These include DOE handbook
3010, DOE-STD-1027 and DOE-STD-1129. Changes to the regulatory guidelines have been
proposed that would adjust the inventory thresholds for certain process categorizations, making it
more difficult and operationally complex to conduct operations with tritium for many of the
laboratories in the DOE complex. The labs have been trying to fill knowledge gaps to enable a
reasonable bounding assumption for tritium hazards. This work is an extension of the initial drive to
fill the gap identified, that being that there was little to no performance data on the oxidation of tritium
in a release environment.
The team at Sandia National Labs started addressing regulatory guidance issues by working on a
project joint with a team at Savannah River National Laboratory, with the objective of developing
capabilities in modeling tools to predict the tritium fire dispersal in a facility. This was conducted
together with some lab-scale tests of protium and deuterium to obtain oxidation reaction kinetics for

 14

trace hydrogen in a thermal environment. These studies were jointly published in a SAND report
(Brown et al., 2022-4187). This was followed-up with a study on the dispersal of tritium in a confined
release to help better understand the prospects for flammability and explosions during a release from
a variety of laboratory configurations (Shurtz et al., 2023).
There was a prior Sandia history of examining computational capabilities for use in managing
radionuclide hazards in fire environments (see Brown et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2022-12448; Louie et
al., 2016). There are also experimental efforts to better understand the fire entrainment of
radionuclides from solid and liquid combustibles (Hubbard et al., 2021; Hubbard et al., 2022). These
activities focused more on the radionuclides that are solid or liquid at room temperature, not gases
like tritium.
Some selected external work is highlighted below, much of which is cited in the prior mentioned
studies. Most of what is cited is of secondary relevance to the main thrust of this work, with primary
relevance to aspects of the problem. A notable exception to this is the work of Mishima and Steele
(2002), who produced a report on the general safety of tritium.
There is a body of work on the release of tritium from production and use facilities. The majority of
these relate to the Savannah River laboratories (Buddemeier et al., 1987; Cadwallader and Petti, 2002;
Hamby et al., 1992; Hoel et al., 1990; Kurzeja et al., 1987, 1995; Murphy et al., 1991). There are also
similar Canadian studies (CNSC 2009), evaluation of a LLNL test site (Buddemeier, 1987), evaluation
of the Hanford site (Barnett et al., 2003), and many others. We provide here a sample only of the
available monitoring studies. Most of these are post-release assessments.
There are more limited studies on incidents of tritium release from facilities that were not intended to
be tritium handling facilities. Among these are post-September 11, 2001 evaluations of the world
trade center site (Semkow et al., 2002), office exposures from an Indian facility (Babla, 2010), and a
fire-related release in Alaska (Jensen and Martin, 1988).
There are also a number of computational and fluid mechanical scenario studies of hydrogen
dispersion and reaction under release scenarios (e.g., Theilacker and White, 2005; Bauwens and
Dorofeef, 2014; Lacome et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2015). These are generally not concerned with
isotopes, with a focus on protium.
The potential for fusion power is an emerging source of tritium use and generation. There are recent
studies on the emissions and issues related to tritium handling in the fusion power community (e.g.,
Tanabe, 2011; Larsen and Babineau, 2020).
Few studies are found where the dispersion and safety are evaluated directly for tritium, whether
computational or experimental. Given the hazards of handling tritium, we do not anticipate significant
growth in the experimental study of this problem. Computational studies, on the other hand, can be
conducted with minimal hazard. The peculiar challenge to all who are modeling a tritium release and
fire scenario is the lack of datasets, which are needed for quality model validation studies. This could
be model validation experiments similar to the target evaluation in this report, but it might also extend
to testing designed to deduce properties and behaviors specific to tritium (see for example references
in Mishima and Steele, 2002).

2. DEUTERIUM EXPERIMENTS SUMMARY (CY23)
This section contains a summary of the experimental report (Brown et al., 2024) to give context to the
simulation results. The material contained in this section is largely reproduced directly from Brown et
al. (2024) for convenience while reading the simulation report, with little or no changes made.
Emphasis is given to aspects of the experiment most relevant to simulation boundary conditions;
those details deemed less relevant to simulation methods have largely been omitted.

2.1. Test Chamber and Hood
The ISO-9705 standard specifies a small room in which a fire is to be located in the back corner. A
design requirement of this specific test series was that gas leakage from the test chamber to the exterior
only may occur at the door. For this test, the ISO-9705 standard was scaled to ¾ length. This scaling
was selected as a compromise between a desire for full-scale and the need to accommodate the test
facility and the diagnostics. The dimensions of the full scale and ¾ scale utilized test chamber in testing
are included in Table 2-1. Further scale-down from the ¾ scale is undesirable because it would make
it difficult to walk into the chamber to perform work, and the fire will be initially too close to the

2.4. Gas Sampling System and FTIR
The gas sampling system consisted of ½ inch tubing that extended from a location pre-determined by

3. SIMULATION
The following portions of the report present and discuss the series of simulations corresponding to
the deuterium fire test matrix. The simulation matrix included three different pan sizes (11” round,
15” square, and 20” square) at three different pan distances from the diffuser which are described as
“near”, “far”, and “in-between”. The product D2O was measured and reported as fractions of the
total D2 release.
In the simulations, D2O could be measured directly by determining the amount of D2O present within
the simulation volume or accounted by outflow out of the simulation bounds. In contrast, the
experimental results are reported as a concentration of DHO from the FTIR measurements. To allow
comparisons between the experimental and simulation results, an additional simulation metric of
estimated DHO concentration was also calculated. It should be noted that DHO was not a species
included in the modeling; this quantity was calculated by postprocessing the simulation D2O results.
The following thought process was followed to estimate DHO concentration from the simulation
D2O results:
The oxidation of deuterium (D2) results in two main product species, DHO and D2O. The simulation
does not include the DHO species, so all oxidized deuterium is modeled as D2O, with no DHO
component. The deuterium oxidation conversion process is believed to be biased towards DHO over
D2O as there are more available H2 molecules to pair with deuterium to produce oxidized deuterium.
One mole of D2 can react to form either one mole of D2O or two moles of DHO. To convert the
simulation result expressed in moles D2O to moles DHO, it was assumed that all simulation D2O
would result in DHO; for every one mole of D2O in the simulation results, it was assumed that this
would correspond to two moles of DHO.
SIERRA/Fuego was the tool used to simulation and analysis. All cases were run with version 5.16.3 of
the SIERRA/Fuego code. SIERRA is an architecture for computational engineering simulations that
is developed and maintained by Sandia National Labs. It provides generalized solvers, data structures,
and tools for the computational physics necessary to simulate a wide variety of engineering
computational problems. The Fuego sub-unit is one of the Thermal/Fluid sub-mechanics tools. Fuego
is a fire simulation sub-unit, which is designed with many of the elements needed to predict the
consequences of an abnormal thermal event. It is focused on predicting thermal and reacting flows,
and has additional capabilities for particle and aerosol transport, radiative heat transport, and thermal
conductivity (SIERRA, 2023).
Fire simulations are core to the capability for SIERRA/Fuego. The Navier Stokes equations are solved
for momentum and continuity. Many conventional approaches to turbulent modeling are available,
with the TFNS model (Tieszen et al., 2005) being the one used for all simulations in this work. Species
and energy equations are solved for chemical reactions and energy transport. In this case, the EDC
model (Magnussen, 1981; Magnussen and Hjertager, 1977) is used to simulate the fire that assumes
the grid resolution is much coarser than is needed to resolve the flame dynamics. This is a mixing
limited approximation and is typically used more for under-resolved fire predictions, and less for high-
fidelity combustion applications. The trace release is solved using an Arrhenius kinetic rate, which is
based on the mean grid cell temperature and mean cell species concentrations. The use of Arrhenius
kinetics for flaming combustion and fire predictions is not advisable except for the highest fidelity
cases, but since the hydrogen releases in this scenario are all sub-flammable, the model approximation
is not thought to be as challenged by the lack of resolution as would be the case if the predictions
were in a flammable regime.

 26

Participating media radiation was modeled using the SIERRA Participating Media Radiation (PMR)
solver (SIERRA, 2023) coupling to SIERRA/Fuego, which mostly involves a heat loss from the fire
to the surroundings. The PMR solver uses a discrete ordinates method to solve the radiative heat
transfer. The test chamber walls and ceiling are modeled as a 4 thick cm, 600 kg/m3 density material
with a 1D conduction model and become heated during the duration of the burn, providing thermal
feedback.
The deuterium fire simulation used previous tritium fire simulations as a starting point for deuterium
fire model development, with the model input being based off the ISO-9705 tritium input documented
in Brown et al., 2022. Simulations are enabled for deuterium using SIERRA/Fuego through custom
thermodynamic property data that were derived as described in Brown et al. (2022). The Brown et al.
(2022) report contains tabulated thermodynamic properties of D2 and D2O which were employed in
this simulation effort. The ISO-9705 fire simulations as described in Brown et al. (2022) provided a
basis for model input construction and as such, many model particulars such as the properties of
deuterium are the same.

The deuterium diffuser was modeled as a constant inflow condition. The deuterium diffuser is
modeled as injecting a deuterium/nitrogen mixture that is some percentage deuterium, with the
remaining species being nitrogen at a contact rate over an interval of 60 seconds. In the experiments,
a 4% (molar) deuterium, 96% nitrogen mixture was used, resulting in a total 0.1 grams of deuterium
being released (except for the cases with a double injection and no injection). This same release was
initially used in the simulations; however, there were some difficulties that arose from this set of
conditions.
The nominal release of 0.1 g deuterium injected over an interval of 60 seconds results in trace
quantities of D2 and D2O of D2 within the individual mesh elements. When summing the total amount
of D2 and D2O within the simulation as well as the integrated flux of D2 and D2O out of the simulation
boundaries, this should yield 0.1 g deuterium in the simulation at the end of the injection interval as
all D2 mass should be accounted for; however, the simulation resulted in a less-than-ideal conservation
of mass of D2 with the summed amount of deuterium exceeding the anticipated 0.1 g deuterium. There
are a few possible origins of this “extra” D2 mass. One possible explanation for this behavior is due
to the trace amounts of D2 and D2O within the elements, this potentially results in an accumulation
of “accounting” errors, possibly related to numerical precision, integration method, or grid
convergence. Another possible explanation is it is related to the chosen solver (TPETRA solver in
SIERRA/Fuego), which is a different solver from the previous tritium simulations, and was chosen
due to SIERRA/Fuego code updates no longer supporting the previously utilized solver.
We were able to take multiple actions to reduce the deuterium mass error. One such action was using
a more refined mesh (medium+). The deuterium mass error was highly sensitive to mesh resolution,
with increasingly refined meshes leading to lower errors in the accounting of D2 mass. This is further
discussed in Section 4.1.3, which includes the deuterium error as a metric for assessing mesh
convergence. Additionally, adding additional iterations to the nonlinear solvers and additional
iterations of the continuity and momentum equations reduced equation residuals and lead to a more
well-behaved accounting of the D2 mass. Also, the nominal inflow of deuterium was artificially
increased by x4. This was done by increasing the mixture percentage of deuterium to 16% and keeping
the same inflow velocity as the 4% deuterium condition for a total of 0.4 g D2 released over an interval
of 60 seconds. This increased D2 injection was chosen as it would not severely impact the mixture
buoyancy while also allowing a larger total amount of D2 released, and moved the simulation away
from the extremely small trace amounts of deuterium species we suspected as contributing to the
simulation error. Ideally in future simulation efforts, the scaling up of the D2 injection will no longer
be necessary and the D2 error can be resolved by other means. This action did help resolve some
numerical issues related to the D2 and D2O accounting and did not seem to impact the conversion
ratio of D2O, so it was an acceptable solution for this analysis. Results for the D2 and the D2O are
therefore divided by four to convert back to the “nominal” quantity of 0.1 g D2. All shown results are
scaled back to the nominal 0.1 g D2 quantity.

For all simulations, the time step was fixed such that the Courant number was below 1.0. The

momentum equations. The simulations were run assuming ambient conditions of 0.8 atm,

3.3. Simulation Matrix
Each simulation was composed of the following stages:
 1. Time = 0.0 s, the initial state with no fire,
 2. Time = 0.5 s, time of the ignition of EDC model and methanol fire,
 3. Time = 60 s, the start of the deuterium release,
 4. Time = 120 s, the end of the deuterium release, and
 5. Time = 240 s or 300 s (for 11” pan), the end of simulation.
The approximately 60 second delay between the fire ignition and the start of the deuterium release
was chosen after sensitivity analysis showed that for the nominal 15” square near pan (i.e., Case 1), a
60 second delay would result in the fire reaching a steady state condition before beginning the
deuterium release interval. The experiments utilized a longer initial “burn” period of about 90 seconds
before the start of the D2 injection, but this was not used in the simulations. A steady-state fire
condition was sufficient and also allowed for less computational time per simulation case. For the
sake of run expediency, the initial 45 seconds of simulation time used reduced solver iterations. After
a SIERRA/Fuego restart, the simulation would proceed using additional solver iterations and the fire
would be allowed to burn at the “new” condition for an additional 15 seconds to reach steady state
before the deuterium release began at time 60 seconds.
The 11” pan results included an extra 60 seconds of simulation time compared to the 15” and 20” pan
cases. The 11” pan D2O signal had not yet reached a steady state at 240 seconds, leading to an
additional 60 seconds of simulation time for a total of 300 seconds.

The experimental results were expressed as an integrated concentration of DHO in units of PPMV*s.
As such, an equivalent calculation was performed for the simulation results. This quantity was
calculated only at the probe location. The experimental results require a non-trivial separation of the
contributions of DHO from the ambient background, fire, and D2. However, in the case of the
simulation postprocessing, this is a simple process as the DHO species will only originate from the
oxidation of D2. As the simulation includes D2O, and no DHO species, it was assumed during post-
processing of the D2O simulation data that 2 moles of DHO would form for every mole of D2O or
rather: D2O → 2 DHO.
The integrated concentration of DHO in units of PPMV*s was calculated by taking the probe D2O
volume fraction, multiplying the quantity by 2.0 to account for an assumed 2 moles DHO forming
for every 1 mole of D2O and then multiplying this resulting number by 1.0x106 to convert from
volume fraction to PPMV (parts per million volume). After this step was completed, the PPMV DHO
at each time step was multiplied by the time step duration and then summed to give an integrated
concentration of DHO in units of PPMV*s. This calculation is shown in the following equation:

The coarse mesh exposes the importance of mesh sensitivity to the prime results, resulting in a

used to set up the modeling scenario as it was computationally inexpensive; it was not anticipated to
be used for the actual analysis. The medium results on the other hand display the largest amount of

from the Table 4-1 results, where the final D2O conversion is within 1% of each other. This can also
be observed by looking at Figure 4-12 and Figure 4-13. In Figure 4-12, which is a plot of all the D2
and D2O in the simulation, the medium+ and fine lines are nearly identical to each other for both the

Table 4-1 additionally shows that the D2 error decreases as mesh refinement is increased. It seems to
scale directly with mesh refinement. However, the effect of this error on the conversion ratio of D2O
is small, especially for the medium+ and fine meshes.
Multiple small studies were performed to attempt to isolate where the observed D2 error was
originating. The mesh convergence study did show that mesh resolution does affect the error—more
mesh refinement will lead to smaller percent error. Adding additional linear and nonlinear solver steps
(especially with regard to the continuity solver) also resulted in lower percent error. This was discussed
earlier in Section 3.2. The results shown utilize 6 nonlinear iterations and an additional 3 iterations of
the continuity-momentum equations. The inclusion of these additional steps did decrease the error,
to a point. It was also found that increasing the total amount of D2 (and then scaling results
accordingly) also resulted in a lower percentage of D2 error, which was a technique used in this analysis.
A smaller magnitude of mass accounting error was observed in previous analysis of tritium, which was
performed using an earlier version of SIERRA/Fuego (version 4.54 vs 5.16 for the current analysis)

 48

and also a different set of boundary conditions and a larger release quantity of tritium. While the error
in the D2 quantity is troublesome and less than ideal, it should be noted that error in the mass closure
is not expected to significantly affect the D2O conversion ratios, which are the main thrust of the
assessment.
These simulations were computationally expensive (~18,000 core hours for medium+ mesh), and
simulation run time was a major limiting factor in the analysis. The medium+ mesh was selected for
analysis as it was an acceptable compromise between decreasing D2 closure error and while still
behaving similarly to the fine mesh and reporting a similar D2O conversion as the fine mesh. Critically,
it also ran significantly faster than the fine mesh (~x2 speedup). All following results are of the
medium+ mesh results.

4.3. Simulation Matrix Summary Table
Results of the simulation matrix are characterized by the total amount of D2O/D2 conversion. Table
4-2 lists the case number, the total amount of D2O in grams D2, the conversion ratio of D2O, and the
integrated concentration of DHO.
In Table 4-2, there are two conversion ratios. The first conversion ratio is the total amount of D2O
divided by 0.1 g D2. The total amount of D2O includes the amount of D2O within the simulation
boundaries as well as the integrated flux of D2O out of the simulation boundary. The second
conversion ratio is the total amount of D2O divided by the total amount of D2 within the simulation.
These two separate D2O ratios were included as there is some closure error in the amount of D2 within
the simulations; however, the difference between these two values is very small. This suggests the
utility of the simulations, despite the closure issues, which appear to be minor.
The final two columns of the table are the integrated quantity of the concentration of DHO. The
experimental results were expressed as an integrated concentration DHO in units of PPMV*s. As
such, an equivalent calculation was performed for the simulation results. This quantity was calculated
only at the probe location. See Figure 4-11 for a representation of the probe location relative to the
test chamber and door. The DHO from D2O quantity excludes any DHO from H2O (which is
assumed to contain some natural abundance DHO) and includes only DHO as a result of the
converted D2O. It was assumed that 2 DHO would form for every D2O or rather: D2O → 2 DHO
based on the mechanism derived from tests that did not include the possibility of DHO due to lack
of H2O in the flow. As noted earlier, the integrated DHO in the last column appears to be accurate
for the 15” and 20” pan cases, but would be higher had the 11” pan simulation scenarios run for
longer time. The fire background integrated concentration of DHO appears in the final table column.
This quantity extrapolates the fire to 7 minutes and assumes a natural abundance of DHO in H2O of
312 ppm.
The Table 4-2 results illustrate that the D2O conversion ratio increases with pan size (i.e., fire intensity)
at all locations. However, for a given pan type, the conversion ratio did not have a monotonic
relationship with the pan proximity. This was an interesting result, and the simulation results indicate
that the relationship between the pan proximity and the conversion ratio is greatly affected by the
internal gas dynamics of the test chamber.
The integrated DHO concentration results also generally show the relationship that the integrated
DHO concentration increases with pan size at all locations, with a few exceptions. One is Cases 1 and
7, which are the near-located 15” and 20” fuel pan cases. Case 1 has a higher integrated DHO value
than Case 7, despite having a lower conversion of D2O. This is because while generally higher amounts
D2O conversion will lead to a higher quantity of DHO, the integrated DHO concentration is taken at
a single point. This is expanded upon in Section 4.4.1.
Similar to the D2O conversion ratio results, the integrated DHO concentration also does not display
a monotonic relationship with pan proximity.

floor and towards the fuel pan; the D2 mixture appears to be drawn into the hot region of the fire
located near the fuel pan surface. The streamlines of the diffuser inflow show that after interacting
with the fire, the flow is directed towards the diffuser end of the test chamber. This could explain why
Case 9 has a conversion rate between that of Cases 7 and 8.
In summary, in Case 7, the diffuser streamlines indicate that unreacted D2 may bypass the hottest
regions of the fire and then be blown forwards towards the door. In Case 8, the diffuser streamlines
indicate that unreacted D2 is drawn into the fire near the fuel pan surface and then recirculates in the
back of the test chamber, where it may interact with the fire, which is at the center of the back wall.

the test chamber. Figure 4-37 shows that the amount of D2 that has exited unreacted from the
simulation bounds by time 120 seconds is significantly higher for Case 7 than Cases 8 and 9, which
supports this theory.

The experimental report for the deuterium fire tests is contained within Brown et al. (2024). This
section contains some reproduced text and figures from this report to utilize when comparing to the
simulation results.
The experiments collected FTIR DHO data for each of the pan types and locations. The resulting
FTIR DHO curves showed an initial background DHO from the water vapor in the air. Once the fire
started, there is a noticeable increase in signal from the DHO formed from the natural abundance of
deuterated methanol in the fuel pan. A 90 second interval was used to establish a baseline fire emission
DHO level. Some nitrogen only (no deuterium) injections were also used to determine the baseline
fire emission. In cases where D2 was injected, it resulted in an obvious rise in the DHO signal
compared to the baseline fire conditions. The pre-test simulations suggested a full conversion scenario
would produce an increase of at least an order of magnitude in DHO in the case of full conversion.
Instead, only the one case where the D2 injection volume was doubled the concentration curve shows
indication of D2 conversion that approaches the same magnitude as that from the methanol fire. This
suggests that the experimental conversion factors were small.

On the simulation side of things, the most likely explanation for the higher DHO signal compared to
the experiment is an under-resolved grid. This will happen if high temperatures are smeared across a
larger volume by the mesh, resulting in a larger region over the critical oxidation threshold. A mesh
convergence study was conducted as part of the simulation analysis (refer to Section 4.1.3), but our
choice of mesh was ultimately driven by a compromise of mesh resolution and computational time.
Higher refinement meshes lead to increased element count, which will dramatically increase simulation
run time. In an ideal situation with unlimited computational resources and time, we could run the
simulations with an exceptionally refined mesh and fully resolve the fire modeling physics. A coarse,
medium, medium+, and fine mesh were presented as part of the mesh convergence study, but it is
likely that a properly resolved mesh for the test chamber fire scenario is well beyond even that of the
fine mesh in terms of refinement and overall element count. However, such a mesh would likely
require runtimes of an impractical scale. The medium+ mesh was already extremely computationally
expensive, with individual runs taking over a week to complete. We were constrained by limitations
of time and computational resource; we had to make the best of what resources were available, and as
a result, we chose the medium+ mesh for analysis.
It should be noted however that both the deuterium accounting error and also the total amount of
D2O conversion was shown to decrease as mesh refinement was increased from the medium to the
fine mesh. It is likely that the trend continues with additional mesh refinements and that total amount

 95

of D2O conversion will continue to decrease with mesh refinement. Thus, the simulations results are
likely conservative in that they are biased high from what would be expected from a fully resolved
simulation with an extremely refined mesh.
As additional evidence of this, the simulation flame height was different than that observed in the
experiment. Figure 5-12 is a reproduced figure from the Brown et al. (2024) experimental report,
showing the mature methanol fire with the 15” square pan near to the deuterium diffuser. The flame
height can be estimated to be about the same as the pan length. The simulation however shows a
significantly increased flame height. Case 1, which utilizes the 15” square pan at the location near to
the diffuser, is shown in Figure 5-13. The flame height in the simulation is illustrated using a 900
Kelvin isocontour of temperature. The simulation image shows the simulated flame height is
approximately 3 times that of the fuel pan length, which is further evidence to the mesh being under-
resolved, which could lead to differences exhibited by the results of the simulation and experiment.
The subtle geometry of a pan lip is a known sensitivity factor for fires, a feature under-resolved in
these simulations. This may also be a contributing factor.
Simplifying the effort to examine the accuracy of simulating a methanol fire might be a good interim
activity. Methanol fires of this scale have been previously studied, and there are good datasets for this
characteristic fire. This could be done with a smaller geometry where added spatial resolution would
be feasible to better understand the effect of mesh resolution on the fire dynamics.

The experimental results are similar to the post-test simulations in that the far location is not the

The simulations showed that the far location resulted in either the first or second highest amount of
conversion for each pan type (Table 4-2). The simulation results make it clear that the combination of
the fire size (i.e., pan type) and location of the fire pan relative to the diffuser will create different gas
flow conditions within the volume that will affect results and conversion of D2O. In summary, the
simulation shows that the while the far location exhibited the first or second highest amount of
conversion, the overall relationship of the conversion of D2O with respect to pan proximity was not
monotonic; this difference between the experimental and simulation results warrants further
investigation.
The simulation and experimental fire background DHO results differ in that the simulation fire
background is approximately 2 to 3 times that of the experimental integrated DHO concentration. An
approximation of the D2O conversion from the fire background DHO for both the experiment and
the simulation exhibited interesting behavior. In summary, the simulation output D2O and probe
location simulation approximated D2O conversion (from the fire background) were very close with
approximately 0.005 conversion fraction, or 0.5% total D2, of each other. The far location (all pans)

 97

approximated D2O conversion for the simulation was relatively close to the experimental value (within
0.03 conversion fraction), despite the large difference in the magnitude of the DHO signal (from fire
and D2). This result seems to indicate that the far pan simulations demonstrate a smaller discrepancy
between the experimental data, and the simulation may perform “better” when the fire pan is located
further from the diffuser. Additionally, the smallest pan had the smallest percent difference between
the approximated experimental and simulation conversion ratios, which may indicate that the current
simulation may more closely reflect the experiment if a less aggressive fire condition is utilized,
producing less overall D2O conversion.
Additional simulation and the incorporation of validation data will likely result in the closure of
simulation uncertainties. As of the time of the report writing, the large discrepancy between the
experimental results and the simulation results is believed to be a combination of multiple factors,
which includes a possible low measurement of the DHO concentration in the experiment and an
under-resolved simulation mesh as the two major contributors.
The simulations overall showed low conversion, with all but one case showing less than 0.15 fractional
conversion to D2O. The simulation case with highest amount of D2O conversion was Case 8, which
showed approximately 0.19 conversion to D2O. Case 8 corresponds to the 20” pan located at middle
distance from the diffuser. With additional mesh refinement, the conversion ratios of all cases,
including Case 8, are anticipated to decrease.

6. DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS
These simulations were constructed to be equivalent to an experimental analysis of a deuterium-
nitrogen mixture interacting with a methanol fire in a scaled test chamber. The simulations were meant
to help develop an understanding of the potential results of a tritium accident scenario and also shed
light on areas that would be difficult or impractical to measure experimentally. One of these quantities
is the amount of converted D2. The experiments yielded a time-integrated DHO PPMV quantity, but
not a total amount of D2O conversion. The simulations can be used to bridge the gap between the
available experimental data and the information available from simulation. We intend to continue
working on iterating the simulation approach and methods to improve the simulation quality and
credibility. The simulations can be used to help design future experiments by identifying parameters
and specific scenarios that are likely of interest for experiments. Furthermore, by using experimental
validation data as it becomes available, this will further improve the simulation credibility.
The overall amount of D2O conversion in the simulations was low in all nine cases, with the highest
amount of conversion being seen in Case 8, which was ~0.19 conversion. Case 8 was for the largest
pan/fire size (20” pan and 75.6 kW fire) and at the in-between distance. The 0.19 D2O conversion
was much lower than a previous simulation test series predicted for tritium (Brown et al., 2024).
However, those simulations included notable differences in geometry to distinguish them from the
current set of simulations, i.e., the assumption that the deuterium diffuser and pans were level in
height. The fire size directly corresponded to the amount of conversion, with a larger fire resulting in
a larger amount of D2O conversion, consistent with prior simulations involving tritium (Brown et al.,
2022). A closer examination of Case 8 showed that compared to Case 7, which used the 20” pan at
the near location, Case 8 appeared to show significantly more circulation of the diffuser inflow near
the back of the test chamber, which may have led to a greater conversion of D2O.
All other eight cases showed D2O conversion being below 0.15. It is critical to note that even though
a mesh convergence study was conducted as part of the simulation campaign, the mesh was likely
under resolved. The mesh convergence study showed that with increasing mesh refinement, the
amount of D2O conversion decreases. Therefore, with additional mesh refinements, the total amount
of D2O conversion will likely also decrease. The medium+ mesh was chosen for analysis as it was a
compromise between mesh resolution and computational cost. However, even the medium+ mesh
resulted in a large number of elements and extremely long run times. Ideally, the fine mesh, and even
additional mesh refinements beyond the fine mesh would have been used for the analysis, but this was
not feasible due to the scope and timeline of the simulation test matrix.
One of the more interesting insights from the simulations was that the far fire location did not yield
the smallest amount of D2O. Furthermore, a similar result was also observed in the experimental
analysis. The set of simulations documented in this report were performed relatively blind—
discussions took place between the analysts and experimentalists, but a final set of experimental data
was not made available for detailed examination until the conclusion of the analysis. This was by design
to allow conclusions to be made with the simulation data prior to comparison to the experimental
data so as to not bias the modeling effort towards matching the data. The experimental tests generally
showed monotonic behavior, with integrated DHO concentration increasing as distance of the fuel
pan to the diffuser was increased. But there was a lot of experimental uncertainty, and this trend is
difficult to articulate with confidence.
The relationship shown in the simulations was not monotonic with regard to pan distance from the
diffuser. This indicates that the interior gas flow dynamics as a result of the fire size and positioning
contribute to the amount of total D2O conversion. It also indicates that this is a likely area for

 100

additional analysis. Due to time constraints, additional sub-iterations could not be performed on the
different pans. A sub-iteration might include using smaller intervals for the pan location. The modeled
intervals of near, in-between, and far could be expanded to also include locations between the three
primary pan locations. This would help determine the relationship of D2O conversion to distance for
each fire size.
Another area for additional attention is the error in the total amount of D2. Multiple one-off studies
were performed to attempt to isolate exactly what was causing the discrepancy and also determine
ways that could be used to mitigate it. The mesh convergence study shows that mesh resolution is one
such way—more mesh refinement will lead to smaller percent error. Adding additional linear and
nonlinear solver steps (especially with regard to the continuity-momentum solver) also resulted in
lower percent error. There are more factors relating to the numerical scheme that could be explored.
Additionally, increasing the total amount of D2 (and then scaling results accordingly) also resulted in
a lower percentage of D2 error. This last item raises the question as well if the discrepancy might be
related to code precision: Is it possible that the trace amounts of D2 and D2O resulted in an
accumulation of error as a result of numerical precision? However, the error in the amount of D2O
is still small in comparison to the total amount of D2, and therefore is not anticipated to change major
conclusions of the current analysis, but is less-than ideal.
Additionally, investigating the effect of the diffuser height relative to the pan height would be
interesting and insightful for both modeling and future experimental efforts. The diffuser height
relative to the pan height was identified as a critical difference between pre-test simulations and the
experiment, and this simulation set’s geometry was updated to reflect this difference.
The selection of the physics models may also play a role in the prediction accuracy. In these
simulations, the TFNS turbulence model was used, which has previously been shown to have
comparable accuracy to LES-type models. Using a k- or k- (RANS) turbulence formulation is likely
to produce lower accuracy results, although they may converge better for the mesh fidelity range we
were utilizing. The flow dynamics were clearly turbulent, although the methanol fire is itself in a
transitional regime for a quiescent pool fire. Turbulence is generated in the plume due to buoyancy,
as well as in the facility due to the jetting inflow of air and wall shear. This problem is expected to
necessitate a dynamic turbulent model, and historical validation of the code suggests the ability to
make sound predictions for characteristic flows of this nature. The turbulence model selection is not
thought to be a major source of discrepancies except as it relates to the formerly identified resolution
concerns.
The method of simulating the fire on the other hand is common to CFD approaches in the fire
community. The EDC model is a mixing-limited approximation that treats the reaction as a sub-grid
phenomenon. The deuterium reaction, on the other hand, was handled via a kinetic rate that would
presume full resolution of the flame for predictions of highest accuracy. This mixture of methods is
enabled by the fact that the deuterium concentrations are sub-flaming due to the trace nature of the
release. It would otherwise be inadvisable to mix these methods. There may be some resolution issues
in relation to the reaction methods assumed causing an overprediction of the deuterium oxidation
based on temperature-smearing, but we are inclined at this point to attribute more concern to the fire
spatial resolution as a point of focus for future assessments. A mixture fraction method is
implemented in the code and was not used for this study due to the scope of the effort and the prior
utilization of EDC. The mixture fraction method might provide higher accuracy results for the fire,
as the physics of the reactions are better represented in some ways. It might be instructive to examine
this factor in subsequent analyses. This factor also has relation to the mesh resolution and could be
examined in tandem with a mesh resolution study.

 101

We frequently wonder how the fuel vaporization affects the fire. We considered a thermal feedback
model to approximate the burn rate as it varies across the pool surface. Given that it was not providing
a high degree of accuracy to the burn rate, we elected to solve the fuel vaporization with a uniform
injection of fuel across the pool surface at the mean measured mass consumption rate. This may be
a sensitivity factor worth exploring in the future.
The simulation results in this document are meant to update geometry and model input that is
reflective of the actual deuterium fire test matrix. Subsequent discussion after the conclusion of the
simulation campaign highlighted some critical differences in experiment and simulation results; namely
that the simulations predict a much higher quantity of integrated concentration DHO than the
experiments, suggesting that the simulations are predicting higher total D2O conversion than was
observed experimentally.
This discrepancy is likely due to the already noted mesh refinement utilized in the simulation.
However, it also possibly originates from the FTIR DHO data measured by the experiment possibly
being biased low. This was noted when comparing the background FTIR DHO concentration
compared to a calculated approximation of DHO concentration based on ambient conditions. The
measured FTIR DHO concentration was shown to be significantly lower than the calculated
approximation from the weather and an assumed isotopic abundance. This could indicate that the
FTIR DHO concentration data is overall biased low, which would result in lower DHO
concentrations when compared to the simulation.
The incorporation of additional validation data from the experiments will allow the simulations to be
vetted and further improved. As the simulations are meant to be a surrogate for the real-life scenarios,
this will result in better simulation credibility and also improved ability to use the simulations to inform
experimental design as well as enable using the simulation as surrogates for real-life scenarios that are
not practical to fully investigate experimentally for reasons related to time, budget, hazard of the
materials, and scale of the scenarios (i.e., room size).
Both simulations and experiment indicated that the amount of conversion of D2O was low, and below
approximately 0.2 for all conditions tested. The simulation conversion of D2O results would likely
continue to trend downward with additional mesh refinements, leading to even smaller amounts of
D2O conversion. While the low conversion rates proved to be frustrating from an experimental
perspective, this is encouraging from a safety standpoint as the experiments were engineered
specifically to result in high amounts of D2O conversion, and both experiments and simulations
showed low conversion. This could help motivate the selection of a low conversion fraction for safety
assessments, and help defray expensive operational costs to meet a safety standard that is unnecessarily
conservative owing to a lack of fidelity in the safety approximations. The relationship of D2O
conversion to pan size and proximity is not simple and indicates that the fluid dynamics involved likely
make it difficult to get a “perfect” flow resulting in a high amount of D2O conversion in a “ISO-9705”
standard or similar fire scenario, such as the scaled ISO-9705 utilized in the current test series.

7. Conclusions
Here we describe simulation of a deuterium release in a room with a methanol fire corresponding to
a formerly conducted test series. 25 experiments were conducted of a deuterium release in a scaled
ISO-9705 Standard room. Deuterium was used as a surrogate for tritium, which is a synthetic
radioactive isotope of hydrogen found in many labs throughout the DOE complex. Deuterium is the
closest isotope to tritium and also lacks its hazards, making it a safe surrogate for tritium. As part of
these experiments, DHO measurements were taken using a FTIR, which were then used to infer
deuterium conversion after isolating the DHO signal from the background and fire DHO amounts
present in the test chamber.
A post-test simulation campaign was also conducted, which was the focus of this SAND report. This
simulation campaign was performed partially blind. To define in this instance, that means that while
the analyst was able to speak with the experimentalists and also broadly discuss results, a detailed
experimental report or analysis was not provided until after the conclusion of the simulation campaign
so as not to bias the modeling effort. These simulations were meant to reflect the deuterium
experiments and thus included multiple fire sizes and fire distances. The simulations were based on
previously done tritium simulations, with updates to reflect the inclusion of deuterium and the
experimental geometry as built, including elevating the deuterium diffuser above the fuel pan.
The simulations showed universally low amounts of deuterium conversion for the 21.7, 43.34, and
75.9 kW fires corresponding to 11”, 15”, and 20” pan located at locations near, in-between and far
from the deuterium diffuser. This is in contrast to pre-test analysis, which suggested high amounts of
conversion at locations near the fire. The experiments also resulted in deuterium conversion in
significantly lower quantities than was predicted by the previous tritium fire simulation campaign.
It was anticipated that deuterium conversion would increase with closer proximity to the fire. The
experiments showed broadly monotonic behavior; however, the experiment resulted in higher
conversion as the pan was moved away from the fire location. The simulations did not display
monotonic behavior, and each of the pan sizes showed differing behaviors at varying distances
(though none of them had the lowest conversion at the farthest fire location). This indicates that the
gas flow dynamics within the test chamber are complex and the fire size and proximity both contribute
to the total conversion of deuterium.
The simulations showed higher DHO concentration than the experiments. This difference is likely
due to mesh convergence resulting in a higher amount of overall D2O conversion. A mesh
convergence study showed that as mesh resolution was increased, D2O conversion decreased. We
were ultimately constrained in terms of mesh resolution due to time constraints and the large
computational expense associated with increasingly refined meshes. Another possible factor is that
that the interpretation of the experimental results were biased low, resulting in lower amount of
measured DHO concentration.
These simulation results provide insight into the credibility of the simulation tools as surrogates for
real-life hazard scenarios that are not practical to fully investigate experimentally for reasons related
to time, budget, hazard of the materials, and scale of the scenarios (i.e., room size). The current and
future experimental campaigns will provide data to enable the simulations to be validated and further
improved. As the simulations are meant to be a surrogate for the real-life scenarios, this will result in
better simulation credibility and also improved ability to use the simulations to inform experimental
design.
