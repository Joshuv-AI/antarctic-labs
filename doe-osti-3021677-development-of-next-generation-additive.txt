 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
 Introducing antioxidant additives into amine-based DAC sorbents can extend their lifetime.
 These sorbents are readily prepared by physically mixing additives with amines, a
 straightforward approach using commercially available materials. Advancing this strategy
 requires understanding how additives function under varying conditions, especially humidity.
 This project aims to reveal how humidity and additive chemistry influence oxidative degradation
 of PEI-based sorbents, levereging LLNL’s expertise in physics-based computational modeling,
 and Global Thermostat (GT)’s expertise in materials synthesis, characterization, and
 degradation kinetics testing, to gain fundamental insights into the chemistries and mechanisms
 of PEI oxidative degradation, and develop design principles that enhance sorbent durability.

Summary of Research Results:
 Technical Objectives:
 The project was guided by two central hypotheses: first, that a thorough mechanistic
 understanding of how additives function, particularly under varying environmental conditions
 such as humidity, can provide insights for developing sorbents with enhanced durability; and
 second, that environmental conditions influence oxidation product distributions and oxidation
 rates. To test these hypotheses, the project leveraged the complementary expertise of
 Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and Global Thermostat (GT). LLNL contributed
 its quantum simulation capabilities, while GT provided advanced materials characterization of
 direct air capture (DAC) sorbents. The collaborative goal was to elucidate the oxidative
 degradation mechanisms of polyethyleneimine (PEI)-based DAC adsorbents in the presence of
 humidity and antioxidant additives, and to develop design principles for new additive
 chemistries that improve material lifespan.

 LLNL conducted a suite of computational modeling activities, including quantum chemical
 calculations to determine N–H bond energies of various amine-based additives, as well as ab
 initio molecular dynamics and enhanced sampling (metadynamics) simulations to study
 reaction kinetics associated with PEI degradation under different humidity conditions. In
 parallel, GT performed accelerated oxidation experiments to determine oxidation kinetics of PEI
 with different additives.

 Figure 1. N-H bond energies and deprotonation energies determined using quantum chemical
 calculations for certain amine-based additives.

 LLNL’s quantum calculations revealed that amine additives accelerating oxidation exhibited
 lower N–H deprotonation energies than the amines in PEI itself, indicating that proton transfer
 from such additives to PEI amines is thermodynamically favorable. This finding aligns with prior
 LLNL work showing that amine protonation lowers the C–N bond cleavage barrier and thus
 accelerates oxidation, helping to identify additive types that are detrimental rather than
 protective. Conversely, additives that act as radical scavengers were found to have lower N–H

 bond energies compared to PEI, though the correlation between bond energies and oxidation
 protection was not strictly quantitative, suggesting additional influences such as steric effects.

 Due to shifting project priorities, the team refocused second-year activities toward identifying
 oxidation products and understanding the mechanistic role of water in PEI oxidation. The first
 new focus aimed to establish how distinct oxidation products could be differentiated
 spectroscopically. LLNL performed quantum chemical calculations to predict infrared (IR), 13C
 nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and X-ray absorption spectral features for a
 comprehensive library of potential oxidation products. As shown in Figure 2, the predicted IR,
 NMR, and X-ray absorption spectra were sufficiently distinct to differentiate among chemical
 species, particularly when used in combination. The computed spectral database was
 transferred to GT for direct comparison with experimental results.

 Figure 2. DFT-computed vibrational modes,13C NMR chemical shifts, and X-ray adsorption spectra for
 certain possible chemical species after PEI oxidation.

 The second new focus was motivated by observation reported in the literature that
 environmental moisture accelerated PEI oxidation. To elucidate the underlying mechanism,
 LLNL constructed explicit PEI–water models with varying water contents and structural
 configurations (Figure 3) and performed first-principles metadynamics simulations to
 determine how water clustering affects degradation kinetics.

 Figure 3. Structural models of water-containing PEI with varying water content and structures.

 The simulations (Figure 4) revealed that free energy barriers for oxidation-driving reactions
 decrease with increasing water cluster size, confirming that water clusters catalyze oxidation.
 Trajectory analyses further indicated that clustered water promotes proton transfer and acts as
 a hydrogen shuttle that facilitates radical propagation. The simulations predict that oxidation
 rates of PEI increase nonlinearly with water uptake due to enhanced water clustering. These
 findings provide predictive insights into how humidity influences oxidative stability.

 Actual Accomplishments:
 The project successfully integrated computational modeling and experimental characterization
 to clarify the oxidative degradation mechanisms of PEI-based DAC sorbents and to reveal how
 additives and humidity affect their stability. The first hypothesis—that understanding additive
 mechanisms could guide the design of more durable sorbents—was validated through the
 combination of LLNL simulations and GT oxidation data. The joint analysis demonstrated a
 direct correlation between additive N–H energetics and oxidation behavior, rationalizing the the
 experimentally protective and detrimental additive chemistries. The second hypothesis—that
 environmental conditions influence oxidation product distributions and rates—was also
 confirmed. LLNL simulations showed that clustered water lowers reaction barriers and
 accelerates degradation, consistent with experimental observations of moisture-accelerated
 oxidation.

 state of knowledge for antioxidant chemistry. Task2, characterizing additive impacts on PEI
 oxidation, was completed through combined kinetic measurements and computational
 energetics that established mechanistic descriptors for additive behavior. Task 3, developing

 mechanistic models of oxidative degradation, was achieved via ab initio simulations that
 clarified the roles of proton transfer and radical scavenging in oxidation. Task 5, GT performing
 accelerated aging and characterization experiments. Task 6, identifying oxidation products
 through spectroscopy, was partially completed; LLNL delivered a comprehensive
 computational spectral database, while GT’s experimental validation remains ongoing. Task 7,
 simulated key degradation reactions that lead to different oxidation products. Task 8,
 investigating humidity and water effects based on reaction mechanisms identified from Task 7,
 yielding direct mechanistic evidence of water’s catalytic role in PEI oxidation.

 The main deviation from the original plan resulted from delays in CRADA funding, which
 necessitated a shift in project focus during the second year. Consequently, Task 4 that aimed
 at developing novel antioxidant chemistries for radical scavenging in amines was abandoned in
 FY24. Despite this, the revised research directions led to significant technical progress,
 particularly in understanding the role of water and in identifying spectral fingerprints of
 oxidation products. Overall, the project met its primary scientific objectives and delivered key
 insights into degradation mechanisms that will guide the design of more stable amine-based
 sorbents. The results establish a validated computational–experimental framework for additive
 screening and degradation prediction, providing a foundation for future development of durable
 materials for carbon capture applications.
