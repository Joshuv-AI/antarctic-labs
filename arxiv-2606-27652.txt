MER-R1: Multimodal Emotion Reasoning via Slow-Fast Thinking Synergy
Zhiyuan Han 1,2,4 Beier Zhu 1, † \dagger Wenwen Tong 2 Chengwei Qin 3 Xinyi Wang 1 Jiayu Zhang 1 Jiangnan Chen 2 Hewei Guo 2 Dongchuan Ran 2 Lewei Lu 2 Xun Yang 1, † \dagger 1 University of Science and Technology of China 2 SenseTime Research 3 The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) 4 Institute of Artificial Intelligence, Hefei Comprehensive National Science Center beier.zhu@ustc.edu.cn , xyang21@ustc.edu.cn † Corresponding authors.

Abstract
We find that explicit reasoning does not necessarily translate into better multimodal emotion recognition (MER) accuracy, even though it makes predictions more interpretable. Specifically, for reasoning-based MLLMs, fast thinking by triggering direct answers often outperforms slow thinking after deliberative reasoning. Our empirical analyses show that fast thinking improves recall with broader and more confident predictions, whereas slow thinking favors precision through conservative filtering of incorrect categories. Building on these insights, we propose MER-R1 , a reinforcement learning framework that turns slow-fast complementarity into explicit optimization. Dual-objective disentanglement separates recall and precision into two optimization signals, allowing them to be jointly optimized rather than traded off against each other. Slow-fast confidence calibration further aligns the final slow-thinking answer with fast-thinking intuition, strengthening correct emotions while suppressing incorrect ones. In this way, MER-R1 unifies the recall-oriented intuition of fast thinking with the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking. We further provide theoretical justification for this synergy, showing that it mitigates variance-induced interference during optimization. Extensive experiments on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion show that MER-R1 achieves SoTA performance and makes reasoning genuinely benefit emotion recognition.

, recent RLVR-based approaches
[

,

]
further equip emotion MLLMs with explicit reasoning chains that ground emotion predictions in visual, acoustic, and textual cues.

Despite this promise, we find that such reasoning does not necessarily lead to stronger emotion recognition. Specifically, we consider two thinking modes for an R1-like emotion reasoning model
[
34
,
17
]
:
(1) Slow thinking
produces an answer after standard reasoning process, and
(2) Fast thinking
elicits an answer directly without reasoning. Despite well-formed reasoning traces (Figure
1

thinking on current OV-MER benchmarks such as MER-UniBench
[
11
,
13
]
(Figure

(b)). This reveals a
thinking paradox
in MER:

Reasoning improves interpretability, yet fails to improve recognition accuracy itself.

To understand this paradox, we analyze fast and slow thinking in Section
3
from prediction and confidence perspectives. At the prediction level, fast thinking exhibits stronger System-1 intuition
[
10
]
, producing broader emotion coverage and higher recall, whereas slow thinking makes more concentrated predictions and favors precision. At the confidence level, fast thinking is more confident on correct emotions, while slow thinking more conservatively suppresses incorrect categories but may also lower confidence in correct ones. These findings suggest two
slow-fast synergy desiderata
for effective MER reasoning: preserving the recall-oriented coverage and correct-category confidence of fast thinking, while retaining the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking.

:

To realize these desiderata, we propose
MER-R1
, a reinforcement learning (RL) framework with two components.
First,
dual-objective disentanglement
separates recall and precision into two optimization targets and preserves them in both reward and advantage spaces.
Unlike prior MER reasoning methods that directly optimize
F_{1}
reward
[
17
]
, our design prevents recall and precision interference during advantage normalization.
Our theoretical analysis further shows that standard
F_{1}
optimization can be biased toward the objective with larger group-wise variation, whereas the dual-objective advantage establishes a balanced coupling to both recall and precision signals.
Second,
slow-fast confidence calibration
transfers fast thinking’s confidence behavior to the final slow-thinking answer while preserving
slow thinking’s selectivity. Specifically, we compare category-level confidence between the two thinking modes and calibrate correct and incorrect categories in opposite directions: correct categories are encouraged to retain or exceed fast-thinking confidence, whereas incorrect categories are encouraged to remain suppressed. This enables
MER-R1
to strengthen correct emotions without absorbing the noisy over-coverage of
fast thinking.

Our contributions
are three-fold:

(1) Findings:
We reveal a
thinking paradox
in MER: for reasoning-based MLLMs, fast thinking can outperform slow thinking. We diagnose this paradox from prediction and confidence perspectives and derive two desiderata for slow-fast synergy.

(2) Methodology:
We propose
MER-R1
, an RL framework with dual-objective disentanglement and slow-fast confidence calibration, enabling joint recall–precision optimization and confidence calibration over correct and incorrect categories.

(3) Performance:

MER-R1
achieves state-of-the-art results on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion, and validates that satisfying the two slow-fast synergy desiderata makes reasoning genuinely beneficial for emotion recognition rather than merely improving interpretability.

MLLM reasoning.

Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs)
[

,

,

have acquired reasoning ability through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)
[

[

In contrast, our work studies their underlying complementarity and turns it into an explicit synergy framework for multimodal reasoning, thereby making slow thinking genuinely more effective.

. Open-Vocabulary Multimodal Emotion Recognition (OV-MER)
[

]

[

]
further evaluates both recognition and reasoning ability with LLM-based judges. Recent emotion MLLMs
[

,

,
17
,

]
increasingly introduce reasoning for better interpretability and reinforcement learning in an attempt to further improve emotion recognition. However, whether reasoning itself truly improves emotion recognition remains unclear. We identify and systematically study the
thinking paradox
in multimodal emotion reasoning, where fast thinking can outperform slow thinking on recognition, and resolve it through an explicit slow-fast thinking synergy framework.

3
Understanding the thinking paradox

This section investigates the counter-intuitive finding that fast thinking outperforms slow thinking in MER tasks (Figure
1
(b)).
We show that fast thinking improves recall with more diverse and confident predictions, whereas slow thinking is more conservative, favoring precision by suppressing incorrect categories but lowering confidence in correct ones. Under current evaluation, which mainly relies on recall or F1 metrics, the advantage of slow thinking is not fully reflected. We begin by introducing the emotion reasoning formulation and the emotion-wheel-based evaluation protocol.

3.1
Problem formulation and emotion wheel-based evaluation

Figure 2
:
Emotion wheel.

Given a multimodal input
x
, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
generates an output

y=(y^{\mathsf{cot}},y^{\mathsf{ans}})
, where
y^{\mathsf{cot}}
denotes the
chain-of-thought reasoning trace and
y^{\mathsf{ans}}
denotes the final emotion answer.
Slow thinking follows the standard think-then-answer format, while fast thinking is elicited
with an answer-only prompt that directly produces
y^{\mathsf{ans}}
without reasoning,

i.e.
,
y^{\mathsf{cot}}=\emptyset
.
Since both the model answer
y^{\mathsf{ans}}
and the ground-truth emotions

y^{\mathsf{gt}}
may contain multiple free-form emotion words, we follow prior
work
[
11
,
13
]
and map them through the emotion wheel
to level-1 categories. For example, as shown in Figure
2
, the
innermost sectors define
level-1 emotion categories
, such as
angry
,
happy
, and

sad
. Fine-grained emotion words in the outer sectors are then mapped
to these categories; for instance,
joyful
,
content
, and

cheerful
are mapped to the level-1 category
happy
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\mathcal{G}
denote the predicted and ground-truth
level-1 category sets obtained from
y^{\mathsf{ans}}
and
y^{\mathsf{gt}}
,
respectively. We evaluate the prediction by set-level matching. Specifically, recall, precision, and
F_{1}
are defined as:

\mathsf{R}=\tfrac{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}|},\mathsf{P}=\tfrac{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}|},\mathsf{F}=\tfrac{2\mathsf{PR}}{\mathsf{P+R}}.

Current OV-MER evaluation mainly relies on recall-based metrics
[
11
]

or F1-based metrics
[
13
]
. We further examine precision
and category-level confidence to diagnose the thinking paradox.

3.2
Precision–recall trade-off

In Figure
3
(a), we report the performance gaps between fast and slow thinking in terms of precision, recall, and F1 on five widely used datasets
[
13
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
25
,
1
]
. Fast thinking consistently leads in recall, while slow thinking leads in precision; overall, fast thinking retains a slight advantage in F1.
Figure
3
(b) reports the average number of emotion categories predicted by fast and slow thinking.
The results show a clear difference in prediction diversity: fast thinking covers more emotion categories, whereas slow thinking produces more concentrated predictions. Together, these two analyses lead to the following finding:

Finding 1:

Fast thinking improves recall with broader emotion coverage, whereas slow thinking favors precision with more concentrated predictions.

Previous benchmarks such as MER-UniBench
[
11
]
mainly use hitrate as the evaluation metric, which reflects recall but does not penalize over-prediction (low precision).
This helps explain why fast thinking appears more favorable than slow thinking under current evaluation.

3.3
Confident vs. conservative predictions

To compare the confidence behavior, we first aggregate the probability mass of free-form emotion words mapped to the level-1 category (Definition
1
).
We then contrast this confidence between ground-truth and hard-negative categories to define a confidence margin (Definition
2
).

Definition 0

(
Category-level log-confidence
)
.

For a level-1 emotion category
c
, let
\mathcal{V}(c)
denote the set of emotion words associated with
c
defined by the emotion wheel
[
13
]
. Given a thinking mode
m\in\{\mathsf{fast},\mathsf{slow}\}
, we define the category-level confidence of
c
as:

\mathsf{conf}^{m}(c)=\log\sum_{e\in\mathcal{V}(c)}p^{m}(e),

(1)

where
p^{m}(e)
denotes the probability assigned to the first token of emotion word
e
.

Definition 0

(
Confidence margin
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of ground-truth level-1 categories for a sample, and let
\mathcal{H}^{m}
denote the set of top-3 hard-negative categories. We define

\mathsf{conf}_{\mathsf{gt}}^{m}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{conf}^{m}(c)
and
\mathsf{conf}_{\mathsf{neg}}^{m}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}^{m}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{H}^{m}}\mathsf{conf}^{m}(c)

as the average confidence assigned to correct and incorrect categories, respectively. Their relative confidence margin is defined as:

\gamma^{m}=\mathsf{conf}_{\mathsf{gt}}^{m}-\mathsf{conf}_{\mathsf{neg}}^{m}.

(2)

Figure
3
(c) reports the category-level log-confidence on ground-truth emotion categories, showing that fast thinking consistently assigns higher confidence to correct emotions.
Figure
3
(d) reports the confidence margin between ground-truth and hard-negative categories, showing that slow thinking yields larger margins by suppressing hard negatives.
Together, these results lead to Finding 2:

Finding 2:

Fast thinking is more confident on correct emotions, while slow thinking is more conservative, better suppressing incorrect categories but under-confidence correct ones.

3.4
Desiderata for slow-fast thinking synergy

These observations suggest that an effective emotion reasoning model should not
simply prefer one thinking mode over the other. Instead, it should satisfy two
desiderata.

•

Desideratum I
: At the prediction level, the model should preserve the
recall-oriented coverage of fast thinking while maintaining the precision-oriented
selectivity of slow thinking.

•

Desideratum II
: At the confidence level, the model should preserve
fast thinking’s strong confidence on correct categories, while retaining slow
thinking’s suppression of incorrect ones.

These desiderata motivate our slow-fast thinking synergy framework in
Section
4
.

4
Method

We propose
MER-R1
, an RL framework for slow-fast thinking synergy.
Section
4.1
introduces the GRPO baseline and its limitations. Section
4.2
proposes dual-objective disentanglement to preserve the recall-oriented coverage and the precision-oriented selectivity in the advantage
space (Desideratum I). Section
4.3
introduces fast-thinking
confidence calibration to strengthen correct categories while suppressing incorrect ones (Desideratum II). The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure
4
.

4.1
Preliminary on GRPO baseline

Reasoning-oriented MLLMs are commonly trained with a two-stage recipe: a
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage,
followed by GRPO-style RL.
Given input
x
, GRPO samples
K
outputs
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
from

\pi_{\theta_{\mathsf{old}}}
, computes rewards
\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
, normalizes
them into relative advantages
A_{i}=\tfrac{R_{i}-\mu}{\sigma}
, and optimizes:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\min\!\big(r_{i}A_{i},\;\mathrm{clip}(r_{i},1-\eta,1+\eta)A_{i}\big)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}),

(3)

where
r_{i}=\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathsf{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}
is the importance ratio, and
\beta\geq 0
controls the KL regularization strength.
As a typical reward design
[
17
]
, the baseline combines an
emotion-wheel-based
F_{1}
reward with a format reward:

R_{\mathsf{base}}=R_{\mathsf{F}}+R_{\mathsf{fmt}},

where
R_{\mathsf{fmt}}
encourages
<think>
then
<answer>
structure.

However, the GRPO baseline has two limitations:
the
F_{1}
-style reward entangles recall and precision, allowing the model to trade off
one for the other, and the training objective does not explicitly preserve the
recall-oriented intuition of fast thinking.

:
Framework of
MER-R1
.
Our

MER-R1
consists of dual-objective disentanglement for Desideratum I and slow-fast confidence calibration for Desideratum II.

4.2
Dual-objective disentanglement for Desideratum I

To avoid the ambiguous trade-off induced by a single
F_{1}
-style reward, we explicitly separate the two optimization goals behind emotion recognition: covering correct categories and avoiding spurious ones. We first decompose the task reward into recall and precision rewards, and then compute their group-normalized advantages separately as the optimization signals.

Reward-level disentanglement.

We decompose
F_{1}
reward into recall and precision rewards:

R_{\mathsf{R}}=\tfrac{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}|},\qquad R_{\mathsf{P}}=\tfrac{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\hat{\mathcal{Y}}|}.

(4)

This decomposition makes the hidden bi-objective structure of
F_{1}
explicit:

R_{\mathsf{R}}
promotes coverage of correct categories, while
R_{\mathsf{P}}

penalizes spurious predictions. Since different recall–precision trade-offs can yield the same
F_{1}
value,
separating
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and
R_{\mathsf{P}}
avoids an ambiguous optimization target.

Advantage-level disentanglement.

Given the two rewards
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and
R_{\mathsf{P}}
, a straightforward GRPO pipeline would first aggregate them into a scalar reward and then apply group normalization to obtain the advantage.
However, when the two rewards have different group-wise variances, the combined scalar reward is dominated by the higher-variance objective, causing the lower-variance objective to be underrepresented in the final advantage.
We therefore first compute group-normalized advantages for
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and
R_{\mathsf{P}}
separately then compute the final advantage
A_{\mathsf{dual}}
:

A_{\mathsf{R},i}=\tfrac{R_{\mathsf{R},i}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}},\qquad A_{\mathsf{P},i}=\tfrac{R_{\mathsf{P},i}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}},\qquad A_{\mathsf{dual},i}=A_{\mathsf{R},i}+A_{\mathsf{P},i}.

(5)

Theoretical justification.

The following proposition shows that standard
F_{1}
optimization can be biased toward the objective with larger normalized variation, whereas our dual-objective disentanglement removes this correlation imbalance (proof in Appendix
A
).

Proposition 0
.

Let
\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}}
and

\sigma_{\mathsf{R}},\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}
denote the group means and standard
deviations of the recall and precision rewards, and let

\tau=\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}/\mu_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}/\mu_{\mathsf{P}}^{2}}
be the normalized group-wise variance ratio.
The correlations between each reward and the standard
F_{1}
advantage satisfy

\begin{cases}\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})>\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}}),&\text{if }\tau>1,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})\leq\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}}),&\text{if }\tau\leq 1.\end{cases}

(6)

In contrast, our dual-objective advantage gives
balanced correlations
:

\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{dual}}).

(7)

4.3
Slow-fast confidence calibration for Desideratum II

For Desideratum II, we propose slow-fast confidence calibration, which guides the final slow-thinking answer to preserve fast thinking’s confidence on correct categories while retaining its suppression of incorrect ones.
For each response
y_{i}=(y_{i}^{\mathsf{cot}},y_{i}^{\mathsf{ans}})
, we construct two answer-generation distributions:

p_{i}^{\mathsf{slow}}(e)=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i}^{\mathsf{cot}},y_{i,<e}^{\mathsf{ans}}),\qquad p_{i}^{\mathsf{fast}}(e)=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<e}^{\mathsf{ans}}).

(8)

For each generated emotion word
e\in y_{i}^{\mathsf{ans}}
, we map it to its
level-1 category
c(e)
and compute its slow-fast confidence gap using
Eq. (
1
):

\Delta_{i}(e)=\mathsf{conf}_{i}^{\mathsf{slow}}(c(e))-\mathsf{conf}_{i}^{\mathsf{fast}}(c(e)).

(9)

We split the emotion words parsed from
y_{i}^{\mathsf{ans}}
into correct (
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{+}
) and incorrect (
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{-}
) sets:

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{+}=\{e\in y_{i}^{\mathsf{ans}}\mid c(e)\in\mathcal{G}\},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{i}^{-}=\{e\in y_{i}^{\mathsf{ans}}\mid c(e)\notin\mathcal{G}\}.

(10)

We use the
slow-fast confidence gap
to calibrate the answer in two opposite
directions. For emotion words mapped to correct categories, we reward a positive
gap so that slow thinking preserves or exceeds the strong confidence of fast
thinking. For incorrect emotion words, we reward a negative
gap so that slow thinking retains its conservative suppression of spurious
predictions:

R_{i}^{+}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{+}|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{+}}\Delta_{i}(e),\qquad R_{i}^{-}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{-}|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{-}}-\Delta_{i}(e).

(11)

Following the same disentanglement strategy, we first compute group-normalized
advantages for
R_{i}^{+}
and
R_{i}^{-}
separately, denoted as
A_{i}^{+}
and
A_{i}^{-}
,
and then combine them as
A_{\mathsf{cali},i}=A_{i}^{+}+A_{i}^{-}
.
The overall advantage is combined as:

A_{i}=A_{\mathsf{dual},i}+\lambda_{\mathsf{cali}}A_{\mathsf{cali},i}+\lambda_{\mathsf{fmt}}A_{\mathsf{fmt},i}.

(12)

Finally, we substitute
A_{i}
into Eq. (
3
) for policy optimization.

Main ablation study.

Table

.

ablates the design of Slow-Fast Confidence Calibration.

6
Conclusion and limitation

Conclusion.

We reveal a thinking paradox in MER, where explicit reasoning does not necessarily improve recognition accuracy.
Our analysis shows that fast thinking provides recall-oriented coverage and correct-category confidence, while slow thinking offers precision-oriented selectivity.
Based on this complementarity, we propose
MER-R1
, an RL framework that realizes slow-fast thinking synergy through dual-objective disentanglement and slow-fast confidence calibration.
Experiments on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion show that
MER-R1
achieves SoTA performance and fulfills the Desiderata, making the slow-thinking genuinely beneficial to emotion recognition.

Limitation.

This work is evaluated mainly on MER benchmarks, so its generalization to broader multimodal reasoning tasks remains open
[
43
,
44
]
.
Moreover,
MER-R1
depends on emotion-wheel-based category mapping and requires an additional fast-thinking forward pass during training, which may limit coverage of fine-grained emotions and increase computational cost.

Appendix A
Theoretical analysis of dual-objective disentanglement

We provide a theoretical analysis of the dual-objective disentanglement in
Section
4.2
. We first show that the standard
F_{1}
-based
advantage is affected by both the local sensitivities of
F_{1}
and the reward variances. We then show that our two-step disentanglement removes these
factors by separating recall and precision before group normalization.

A.1
Limitation of the
F_{1}
advantage

For the
i
-th response in a GRPO group, let
R_{\mathsf{R},i}
and

R_{\mathsf{P},i}
denote its recall and precision rewards. The baseline first
combines them into a scalar
F_{1}
reward:

R_{\mathsf{F},i}=\frac{2R_{\mathsf{R},i}R_{\mathsf{P},i}}{R_{\mathsf{R},i}+R_{\mathsf{P},i}},

(13)

and then computes the group-normalized advantage:

A_{\mathsf{F},i}=\frac{R_{\mathsf{F},i}-\mu_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}},

(14)

where
\mu_{\mathsf{F}}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}
are the group mean and
standard deviation of
R_{\mathsf{F}}
.

Proposition 0
.

For
\mathsf{k}\in\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{P}\}
and

\mathsf{l}\neq\mathsf{k}
, the correlation between reward

R_{\mathsf{k}}
and the
F_{1}
advantage
A_{\mathsf{F}}
is approximated by:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{k}},A_{\mathsf{F}})\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{k}}\sigma_{\mathsf{k}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{l}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{l}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(15)

Here,
\rho
is the correlation between

R_{\mathsf{R}}
and
R_{\mathsf{P}}
;

\alpha_{\mathsf{k}}
is the local
F_{1}
sensitivity to
R_{\mathsf{k}}
; and

\sigma_{\mathsf{k}}
is the standard deviation of
R_{\mathsf{k}}
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we omit the response index
i
when no ambiguity arises. Let

\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}}
be the group means of
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and
R_{\mathsf{P}}
respectively. By the first-order Taylor expansion around

(\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}})
, the scalar
F_{1}
reward can be locally
approximated as:

R_{\mathsf{F}}\approx\frac{2\mu_{\mathsf{R}}\mu_{\mathsf{P}}}{\mu_{\mathsf{R}}+\mu_{\mathsf{P}}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}(R_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}})+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}(R_{\mathsf{P}}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}}),

(16)

where:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial R_{\mathsf{F}}}{\partial R_{\mathsf{R}}}\right|_{(\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}})}=\frac{2\mu_{\mathsf{P}}^{2}}{(\mu_{\mathsf{R}}+\mu_{\mathsf{P}})^{2}},\qquad\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}=\left.\frac{\partial R_{\mathsf{F}}}{\partial R_{\mathsf{P}}}\right|_{(\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}})}=\frac{2\mu_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{(\mu_{\mathsf{R}}+\mu_{\mathsf{P}})^{2}}.

(17)

Subtracting the group mean from the local approximation and normalizing by

\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}
give:

R_{\mathsf{F}}-\mu_{\mathsf{F}}\approx\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}(R_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}})+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}(R_{\mathsf{P}}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}}),\qquad A_{\mathsf{F}}\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}(R_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}})+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}(R_{\mathsf{P}}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(18)

We first compute the correlation between
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and

A_{\mathsf{F}}
. By definition:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{A_{\mathsf{F}}}}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}},

(19)

where the equality follows from the group normalization of

A_{\mathsf{F}}
, which gives
\sigma_{A_{\mathsf{F}}}=1
. Using the expression of

A_{\mathsf{F}}
, together with

\mathrm{Var}(R_{\mathsf{R}})=\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}
and

\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},R_{\mathsf{P}})=\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\mathrm{Var}(R_{\mathsf{R}})+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},R_{\mathsf{P}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}=\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(20)

By symmetry, the corresponding result for
R_{\mathsf{P}}
can be obtained in the same way.
Substituting the two covariance results into the correlation definition gives:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(21)

This completes the proof.
∎

Implication.
Proposition 2 shows that the correlation between each reward and the
F_{1}

advantage depends on both the local
F_{1}
sensitivity

\alpha_{\mathsf{k}}
and the reward standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathsf{k}}
,
especially when
\rho
is small. Therefore, the baseline
tends to emphasize the reward with larger local sensitivity or larger standard
deviation, even when both recall and precision are important.

A.2
Effect of dual-objective disentanglement

Proposition 0
.

The correlation between each reward and the disentangled advantage is:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{2}}.

(22)

Proof.

By definition,
A_{\mathsf{R}}
and
A_{\mathsf{P}}
are standardized rewards:

A_{\mathsf{R}}=\frac{R_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}},\qquad A_{\mathsf{P}}=\frac{R_{\mathsf{P}}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}.

(23)

Thus, both have zero mean and unit variance, and their covariance is

\mathrm{Cov}(A_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{P}})=\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},R_{\mathsf{P}})=\rho
.
Since
A_{\mathsf{dual}}=A_{\mathsf{R}}+A_{\mathsf{P}}
, its variance is:

\mathrm{Var}(A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\mathrm{Var}(A_{\mathsf{R}})+\mathrm{Var}(A_{\mathsf{P}})+2\mathrm{Cov}(A_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{P}})=2+2\rho.

(24)

We first compute the correlation between
R_{\mathsf{R}}
and

A_{\mathsf{dual}}
. The covariance term can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{R}})+\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{P}})

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}\left(R_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{R_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)+\mathrm{Cov}\left(R_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{R_{\mathsf{P}}-\mu_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}+\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}=(1+\rho)\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}.

Combining this with

\sigma_{A_{\mathsf{dual}}}=\sqrt{2+2\rho}
, we obtain:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{A_{\mathsf{dual}}}}=\frac{(1+\rho)\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\sqrt{2+2\rho}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{2}}.

(26)

By symmetry, the same derivation gives:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{2}}.

(27)

This completes the proof.
∎

Implication.
Proposition 3 shows that dual-objective
disentanglement removes the local-sensitivity and standard-deviation factors
identified in Proposition 2 by normalizing recall and precision separately. As
a result, both rewards are equally correlated with
A_{\mathsf{dual}}
, preventing
optimization from improving one objective at the expense of the other.

A.3
Proof of Proposition 1

Restated Proposition 0 (Proposition
1
).

Let
\mu_{\mathsf{R}},\mu_{\mathsf{P}}
and

\sigma_{\mathsf{R}},\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}
denote the group means and standard
deviations of the recall and precision rewards, and let

\tau=\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}/\mu_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}/\mu_{\mathsf{P}}^{2}}
be the normalized group-wise variance ratio.
The correlations between each reward and the standard
F_{1}
advantage satisfy

\begin{cases}\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})>\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}}),&\text{if }\tau>1,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})\leq\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}}),&\text{if }\tau\leq 1.\end{cases}

(28)

In contrast, our dual-objective advantage gives
balanced correlations
:

\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\mathsf{corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{dual}}).

(29)

Proof.

From Proposition 2, the correlations between recall/precision rewards and the
standard
F_{1}
advantage are:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}+\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\rho\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(30)

Taking their difference gives:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})-\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}})\approx\frac{(1-\rho)(\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}-\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}})}{\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}}.

(31)

In the non-degenerate case with
\rho<1
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{F}}>0
, the sign of
this difference is determined by

\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}-\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}
. Using the local sensitivities in
Proposition 2, we have:

\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}-\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}=\frac{2}{(\mu_{\mathsf{R}}+\mu_{\mathsf{P}})^{2}}\left(\mu_{\mathsf{P}}^{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}-\mu_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}\right).

(32)

Therefore:

\alpha_{\mathsf{R}}\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}>\alpha_{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{R}}}{\mu_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}>\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}{\mu_{\mathsf{P}}^{2}}\Longleftrightarrow\tau>1.

(33)

Thus, when
\tau>1
,

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})>\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}})
; otherwise,

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{F}})\leq\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{F}})
.

Finally, Proposition 3 directly gives:

\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{R}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\mathrm{Corr}(R_{\mathsf{P}},A_{\mathsf{dual}})=\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{2}},

(34)

which proves the balanced-correlation result for the dual-objective advantage.
∎

Task details

We evaluate
MER-R1
on two multimodal emotion benchmarks: MER-UniBench
[
11
]
and MME-Emotion
[

]
.
MER-UniBench evaluates open-vocabulary multimodal emotion recognition under emotion-wheel-based metrics, while MME-Emotion provides a llm-as-a-judge based evaluation of both emotion recognition and emotion reasoning.

B.1
MER-UniBench

MER-UniBench
[
11
]
unifies nine multimodal emotion datasets into three task groups: fine-grained emotion recognition, basic emotion recognition, and sentiment analysis.
Since model outputs are free-form emotion words, direct string matching is insufficient.
Following prior work
[
11
,
13
]
, we evaluate recognition outputs with an emotion-wheel-based normalization protocol.

Emotion-wheel normalization.

The emotion wheel maps diverse fine-grained emotion words into a shared category space.
We apply three normalization steps to both predictions and ground-truth labels.
First, morphological variants are reduced to their base form,
e.g.
,
happier
and
happiness
are normalized to
happy
.
Second, semantically equivalent emotion words are mapped to the same canonical form,
e.g.
,
joyful
and
happy
.
Third, fine-grained emotion words are further mapped to their corresponding inner emotion categories according to each emotion wheel.
Following prior work
[
11
]
, we adopt
K=5
emotion wheels, as shown in Figure
6
.
In each wheel, the outer labels correspond to fine-grained emotions, and the inner sectors define the corresponding level-1 emotion categories.

(a)

(b)

We adopt five emotion wheels (W1–W5) to map free-form emotion words into level-1 emotion categories.

For the
k
-th wheel
w_{k}
, the complete mapping function is defined as:

G_{w_{k}}(\cdot)=F_{l_{3}}^{w_{k}}\bigl(F_{l_{2}}(F_{l_{1}}(\cdot))\bigr),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

(35)

where
F_{l_{1}}
,
F_{l_{2}}
, and
F_{l_{3}}^{w_{k}}
denote morphological normalization, semantic normalization, and wheel-specific category mapping, respectively.

Set-level precision, recall, and F1.

For sample
i
, let
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
denote the ground-truth emotion set and

\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}
denote the predicted emotion set extracted from the

<answer>
field, with duplicate emotion words removed.
For each emotion wheel
w_{k}
, we compute the set-level precision and recall as:

\mathsf{P}_{s}^{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\left|G_{w_{k}}(\mathcal{Y}_{i})\cap G_{w_{k}}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i})\right|}{\left|G_{w_{k}}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i})\right|},

(36)

\mathsf{R}_{s}^{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\left|G_{w_{k}}(\mathcal{Y}_{i})\cap G_{w_{k}}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i})\right|}{\left|G_{w_{k}}(\mathcal{Y}_{i})\right|}.

(37)

The corresponding F1 score is defined as:

\mathsf{F}_{s}^{k}=\frac{2\mathsf{P}_{s}^{k}\mathsf{R}_{s}^{k}}{\mathsf{P}_{s}^{k}+\mathsf{R}_{s}^{k}}.

(38)

The final emotion-wheel-based F1 score is obtained by averaging over all emotion wheels:

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{EW}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathsf{F}_{s}^{k}.

(39)

Fine-grained emotion recognition.

Fine-grained emotion recognition requires the model to predict one or more open-vocabulary emotion descriptors.
We evaluate this task on OV-MERD+
[
13
]
, which extends OV-MERD
[
16
]
.
Following MER-UniBench, we use the emotion-wheel-based set-level F1 score as the primary metric.

Basic emotion recognition.

Basic emotion recognition asks the model to infer a basic emotion label from multimodal input.
We evaluate this task on MER2023
[
14
]
, MER2024
[
15
]
, MELD
[
25
]
, and IEMOCAP
[
1
]
.
Each sample is associated with a single majority-voted emotion label.
Under the official MER-UniBench protocol, this task is evaluated by Hitrate (HIT).
For each wheel
w_{k}
, HIT is defined as:

\mathrm{HIT}^{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[G_{w_{k}}(y_{i})\in G_{w_{k}}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i})\right],

(40)

where
y_{i}
is the ground-truth basic emotion label and
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
denotes the indicator function.
The final HIT score is averaged over the five emotion wheels.
Since HIT mainly reflects recall and does not penalize over-prediction,
we additionally report an F1-based evaluation for these four datasets
after the same emotion-wheel normalization.

B.2
MME-Emotion

MME-Emotion
[
41
]
is a holistic benchmark for evaluating emotional intelligence in multimodal large language models.
Different from MER-UniBench, which mainly evaluates open-vocabulary emotion recognition with emotion-wheel-based metrics, MME-Emotion uses LLM-based judging to assess both the final emotion prediction and the reasoning process.

Tasks.

MME-Emotion contains eight evaluation tasks:
emotion recognition in laboratory scenarios (ER-Lab), emotion recognition in the wild (ER-Wild), emotion recognition under noisy conditions (Noise-ER), fine-grained emotion recognition (FG-ER), multi-label emotion recognition (ML-ER), sentiment analysis (SA), fine-grained sentiment analysis (FG-SA), and intent recognition (IR).
These tasks cover both controlled and in-the-wild scenarios, and evaluate different aspects of affective understanding, including categorical emotion recognition, fine-grained affective interpretation, sentiment polarity, and intent inference.

Evaluation metrics.

MME-Emotion reports three metrics.

Recognition
measures whether the final predicted emotion or affective label is correct.

Reasoning
evaluates whether the generated explanation is coherent, grounded, and supportive of the final answer.

CoT
is the average of Recognition and Reasoning.
Following the benchmark protocol, task-level results are judged by an evaluator model.
Since the original GPT-4o evaluator used by the benchmark is no longer available in our setting, we use
gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview
as the evaluator for all compared methods to ensure a consistent evaluation protocol.
In the Table
2
, we report the CoT score for each task and the averaged Recognition, Reasoning, and CoT scores across all tasks.

Appendix C
Implementation details

We use Qwen2.5-Omni
[
34
]
as the backbone and train only its Thinker module.
All models are trained on MER-Caption+
[
11
]
with a two-stage SFT-RL pipeline based on GRPO-style optimization.
We consider two reasoning baselines.
The reproduced AffectGPT-R1
[
17
]
follows its original SFT/RL data allocation, while keeping the backbone and optimization framework consistent with our implementation.
Our stronger
Baseline
adopts an RL-heavy allocation: we randomly sample 5k MER-Caption+ instances for SFT and use the remaining samples for RL.
For this
Baseline
and
MER-R1
, we train the SFT stage for 2 epochs with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, and train the RL stage for 1 epoch with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-6}
.
During RL, we sample
G=4
responses per prompt, train for 3262 optimization steps, set the batch size to 1, and use gradient accumulation with 2 steps.
We set both
\lambda_{\mathsf{cali}}
and
\lambda_{\mathsf{fmt}}
to
0.1
.
All experiments are conducted on 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, and the RL stage takes approximately 18 hours.
For fair comparison, we evaluate all datasets using a single final checkpoint without dataset-specific checkpoint selection.

Appendix E
Qualitative analysis

We provide qualitative examples to further illustrate how
MER-R1
realizes slow-fast thinking synergy in multimodal emotion recognition.
Specifically, we present three types of cases.
(i)
Fast-intuition retention
refers to cases where fast thinking already predicts a correct emotion, but the baseline slow-thinking answer removes it after deliberation;
MER-R1
keeps this useful fast-thinking intuition in the final answer.
(ii)
Spurious emotion suppression
refers to cases where fast thinking introduces incorrect emotions due to over-coverage;
MER-R1
suppresses these spurious predictions through slow-thinking selectivity.
(iii)
Missed emotion recovery
refers to cases where baseline fast thinking fails to cover the ground-truth emotion, while
MER-R1
recovers it after slow reasoning.

For each case type, we show examples from both fine-grained emotion recognition and basic emotion recognition.
The fast-intuition retention examples show that
MER-R1
does not simply discard the useful predictions produced by fast thinking; instead, it preserves correct categories such as
angry
or
happy
that are missed by the baseline slow-thinking answer.
The spurious emotion suppression examples show that
MER-R1
can remove unsupported emotions such as
angry
or
resentment
, keeping the final prediction focused on the ground-truth emotions.
The missed emotion recovery examples further show that
MER-R1
can recover emotions overlooked by baseline fast thinking, such as
surprised
,
worried
, and other positive fine-grained emotions.
Together, these examples qualitatively demonstrate that
MER-R1
selectively combines the recall-oriented intuition of fast thinking with the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking.
