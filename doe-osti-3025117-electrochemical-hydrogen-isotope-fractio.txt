Introduction
Deuterium and tritium, the two isotopes of hydrogen besides protium, are utilized in many industries.
Deuterium is routinely used as a nuclear reactor moderator and molecular labeling agent, while tritium
is used as an autoradiography label in medical imaging, and in an emerging market for self-powered
lighting for watches, exit signs, firearm night-sights and future fusion fuel. Tritium is produced naturally
at very low steady-state levels due to cosmic rays, natural nuclear decay reactions, as well as commercial
nuclear fission reactors, and terrestrial nuclear testing conducted primarily in the mid-twentieth century.
Tritium often accumulates in the heavy water used in nuclear fission reactors and sometimes appears in
groundwater near nuclear reactors due to slight leakage from water sources in the reactor. Because of the
health and environmental impacts from tritium leaking into the water sources, as well as the value of this
precious material, it is desirable to remove and recover as much tritium as reasonably possible from
contaminated water [1].
Efficient and economical water detritiation is very challenging for large quantities of tritium-
contaminated water since tritium is part of the water molecule and exhibits almost identical chemical and
physical properties to that of its non-radioactive counterpart. Over the past half a century, no
breakthrough technologies have been developed despite continuing interest and efforts [2-6]. Combined
Electrolysis Catalytic Exchange (CECE), water distillation (WD), and Girdler-Sulfide (G-S) processes
are still among the leading processes to separate the water isotopes. Both CECE and WD are very energy
intensive and G-S uses the toxic and corrosive chemical hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which is not a good
combination with radiological hazards. The potential for a direct isotope separation of water molecules
is of considerable interest among the nuclear power industry. For example, the opportunity to clean up
tritium contaminated water from the damaged Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant following the 2011
Japanese earthquake is substantial, but a practical separation technology on an industrial scale does not
exist. In another example, the Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Plant was permanently shut down on
December 29, 2014, triggered by an event that tritium, the radioactive isotope of hydrogen, had been
discovered in the groundwater in 2010.
In 2016, the University of Manchester reported a 10:1 H/D isotopic selectivity across graphene and two
other types of 2-D crystals, theorizing a 30:1 H/T selectivity by zero-point energy [7]. However, the
pronounced isotope effect independent of the types of 2-D membrane remained puzzling. The fact that
the selectivity depends on isotope mass but not the type of membrane or membrane thickness prompted
us to consider a quantum tunneling mechanism. A quantum tunneling solution was derived at SRNL
from one-dimensional Schrödinger equation that explained the data reported by the University of
Manchester, and further predicted 58.5:1 H/T selectivity.

Pristine graphene and related 2D materials have been described as near-perfect barrier materials [8-10].
Even in single-layer form, pristine graphene will completely block transport of nearly all atomic, ionic,
and molecular species at ambient temperature, including gas-phase helium [11]. Any transport that
occurs through graphene has been viewed as the result of diffusion through defects present in the
graphene, where the defects could be tailored for selective ion separation [12]. Much work has been
done over the past decade to demonstrate and understand selective transport through defect sites in a
wide variety of 2D materials, especially graphene [13-19]. However, it has been shown that graphene
and similar 2D materials are capable of proton transport (shown in Figure 1A) [20], as well as exhibiting
a preference for proton transport over deuterium transport by a factor of 10:1 in microfabricated
structures and macroscopically sized cells [7,21].

The objective of this project is to test the following hypothesis: Hydrogen isotope selectivity increased
by graphene in a proton-exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysis cell is the result of quantum tunneling
due to mass differences of H/D/T and how this selectivity can be maintained during scale-up.

In collaboration with Clemson University (Clemson) and Vanderbilt University (Vanderbilt), Savannah
River National Laboratory (SRNL) has been preparing for a tritium study to obtain H/T selectivity using
graphene-containing electrochemical cells from Clemson with graphene from Vanderbilt. SRNL
developed a quantum tunneling mechanism on the isotopic sieving and initiated an investigation to
identify key technical needs for process scale up. In parallel, Clemson is developing PEM-style
electrochemical hydrogen pump cells with graphene included in the MEA. Vanderbilt is studying
monolayer graphene and graphene/polycarbonate assembly to characterize the graphene lattice over
centimeter-scales and probing the influence of diffusive transport on H/D selectivity in liquid state.

SRNL Tritium lab setup and uncertainty estimate
SRNL obtained a radiological working permit (RWP) in the tritium lab, including formal review by
Radiological Protection officers. Due to 45 mCi RWP limit (9619 Ci/g for tritium) in the radiological
lab, a dilute tritium stream was prepared for the test. Figure 2 shows a schematic of the test apparatus.
Figure 3 shows the tritium test experimental setup in a radiological hood at SRNL. A PG Plus 100
Hydrogen Generator was used to convert dilute tritiated water into an H2/HT mixture. Instrumentation
(e.g., potentiostat, control box for mass flow controller, data acquisition) was set up in a clean space
next to the radiological hood, with only the cables crossing the boundary of the radiological hood.

Initial tests consisted of an electrochemical cell operated in the hydrogen pumping mode by applying
an electrical potential provided by a galvanostat/potentiostat (CH Instrument 1127C model) and
measuring the electrical current produced. Driven by an electrical potential, small quantities of
hydrogen could be pumped to the other side of Nafion Membrane Electrode Assembly (MEA)
proportional to the quantity of electric charge (electrical current multiplied by time). The feed hydrogen
isotope gas stream is produced via electrolysis by the hydrogen generator (Proton Exchange Membrane
electrolysis), sent through a mass flow controller (MKS Instruments Model 1179A) and switching valve
(V3) to the electrochemical cell before discharging into the HNO3 bubbler and venting in the hood. The
gaseous hydrogen isotopes were oxidized by HNO3 for the tritium to be measured by liquid scintillation
counter (LSC). Alternatively, the feed stream could bypass the electrochemical cell (vis V3, V4, V2) to
a heated oxidation column and chilled bubbler for tritium measurement by LSC. The electrochemical
cell has a layer of graphene sandwiched between Nafion membranes in the MEA. A dry nitrogen gas
swept the evolving hydrogen isotopes through a heated catalyst bed to convert the hydrogen into water,
which would accumulate in a chilled bubbler with scintillation cocktail. After sufficient time passed,
an aliquot was taken and analyzed for tritium activity by a Quantulus GCT LSC 6220TR 110V ultra
low-level LSC. The detection limits for this system are on the order of 10-16 T/H ratio (0.01 Bq/mL).
Samples are dark adapted for one hour and cooled to 12°C prior to counting for 20 minutes. The LSC
was calibrated annually using a PerkinElmer Ultima Gold low level tritium quench standard set.

Instrument performance was verified daily prior to counting samples. From the cold trap volume
measurement and the cold trap/bubbler tritium concentration, the H/T selectivity able to be calculated.
The total hydrogen isotopes across the graphene membrane were computed from the integration of
electrochemical cell current over time.

SRNL preparation of electrochemical cell
While Clemson worked to recreate the MEA fabrication and cell assembly done previously, SRNL
researchers and summer interns attempted inhouse cell fabrication in 2022 before the activity was put
on hold in order to save project funds for the critical tritium tests, with the expectation that Clemson
would provide a qualified cell to measure the H/T selectivity.

Fabrication Factors Affecting Membrane Quality
Several factors involved in the MEA fabrication, and the cell in its entirety, were investigated to
optimize the cell’s electrochemical performance during testing. The factors included the size and shape
of the graphene, electrode size, methods of etching away the copper substrate, and cell tightness.
Additional observations on voltages that irreversibly damaged the MEA and rates of isotopic exchange
and hydration were also recorded.

Various graphene shapes and sizes (ranging from 4 cm2 squares to ¾” circles) were incorporated into
MEAs and tested. It was found that punching out a circle of graphene mirroring the diameter of the ¾”
Nafion was the best choice for both ease of assembly and performance. The rubber molds used in
pressing the layers together were made to fit the largest element of the MEA, the Nafion membranes. It
was observed that graphene layers that were smaller than the Nafion membranes tended to shift during
pressing and led to poor centering and overlap with the Nafion layers; however, graphene layers that
matched the Nafion size were held in place by the mold and led to MEAs with near perfect overlap. In
addition, the larger graphene layers minimized the chances of misaligning the electrodes over an area
without graphene later in the cell fabrication process. The preferred electrode configuration consisted
of a smaller cathode and larger anode. The smaller cathode ensured the cathode could be fully contained
within the larger anode, guaranteeing that the working area of the cell would be equal to the surface
area of the cathode. While the current varied with electrode size, the current density was an inherent
property of the MEA being tested and didn’t change with electrode size. Therefore, the electrode sizes
were 5/32” for the cathode and 5/16” for the anode, as these sizes made assembly easier.

Commercial Nafion is sandwiched between two plastic sheets: one a thicker, harder plastic, and the
second a thinner, softer plastic. Because Nafion is hydrophilic and absorbs moisture, it was possible
that Nafion expanding in the x-y plane would stretching and damage the graphene layer during copper
etching in an ammonium persulfate bath. To address this potential problem, the copper etching step was
carried out leaving the hard plastic on the Nafion, the soft plastic on the Nafion, and no plastic on either
side of the Nafion to determine the impact of Nafion expansion on cell performance. The hard plastic
always detached from the Nafion when placed into the ammonium persulfate solution, so it was ruled
out from further evaluation. It was found that MEAs that were etched with a soft plastic cover displayed
higher currents than those etched without a cover.

The effect of under- or over-tightening the plastic cell body had on membrane performance was also
investigated. If the connection was too loose, then the electrical circuit would not complete and would
prevent any current flow. If the connection was too tight, the electrodes could be damaged, decreasing
the current output. It was found that hand tightening the ¾” cell assembly was optimal to reach a
maximum current.

Normal testing on the MEAs involved voltages between -0.3 and 0.3 V. However, to determine the
limits at which the cells could be tested, the applied voltage was gradually scanned through a wider
range and the resulting current was observed to determine at what voltage the IV curve begins to break
down. It was noted that above ± 1.6 V, the IV curve changed shape significantly and repeated testing in
the normal ± 0.3 V range after exposure to this higher voltage never returned the original curve,
suggesting irreparable damage to the membrane. Other findings on cell operation involved measuring
the lengths of time required to hydrate a MEA after starting hydrated gas flow and to exchange the
protium in the Nafion for deuterium. In a previously isotopically exchanged and dry cell, it took

approximately 25 minutes for the IV curve to reach steady state. This was identified as the length of
time needed to allow the membrane to fully hydrate before IV measurements could be taken. A freshly
prepared MEA was placed in the plastic cell assembly and put under hydrated deuterium flow and IV
curves were taken every 5 minutes to observe how isotopic exchange affected the measured current.
After an hour, the curve reached a steady state and was not observed to change further after an additional
two days of measurements. Thus, the minimum time needed to carry out an acceptable isotopic
exchange in the cell was determined to be one hour.

Symmetric Deuterium Testing
Using the lessons learned from the cell fabrication tests in the previous section, high-quality cells were
produced at SRNL, tested under deuterium flow, and the resulting IV curves were compared to those
generated by Clemson. Symmetric testing consists of hydrated deuterium gas flowing on both sides of
the membrane while deuterium is pumped across the membrane. This setup allowed for the
determination of the resistance of the different components of the membrane, and the resistance of the
graphene layer can be used to calculate the graphene conductance. The ratio of proton conductance to
deuteron conductance yields the isotopic selectivity. Results of the symmetric testing are shown in
Figure 4 and compared to those from Clemson.

Figure 4. Symmetric D2 results from SRNL (left) and Clemson (right)

The IV curves generated at SRNL closely track with the ones generated by Clemson. In this comparison,
NGN on the SRNL results denotes Nafion|Graphene|Nafion which is written as MEA-(N211 double
with graphene) in the Clemson results, while NN is Nafion|Nafion or MEA-(N211 double). The
resistances and deuteron conductance generated from this data are almost identical to the ones measured
by Clemson, indicating that all of the components used in the SRNL cells are equivalent to the ones at
Clemson.

Asymmetric Deuterium Testing
Asymmetric testing involves flowing hydrated deuterium through the cathode side of the cell and
hydrated nitrogen though the anode side of the cell. This type of testing is useful for studying the
protium/deuterium evolution reactions and observing general trends in proton/deuteron transport
through the membrane. A comparison of asymmetric deuterium testing results obtained by SRNL and
by Clemson is shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. Asymmetric D2 results from SRNL (left) and Clemson (right)

While the ratios of current density between the MEA with and without a graphene layer are similar
between the SRNL and Clemson results, the absolute current density values observed by SRNL are
roughly 200 mA/cm2 less than those recorded at Clemson. This could result from less efficient cells
being produced at SRNL or it could be indicative of the change in current density observed over the
first couple hours of a cell’s testing lifetime. All SRNL data was recorded after several hours of a
membrane being placed in the cell assembly, whereas conversations with Clemson indicate that the
published results were generated within minutes of the cells being placed in the assembly. Both SRNL
and Clemson independently observed in recent research that current density drops to a steady-state
value after an hour or two in the cell assembly, and this drop could explain the discrepancy between
SRNL and Clemson results for the asymmetric testing. However, it is impossible to confirm this
hypothesis without obtaining recent testing data from Clemson.

Clemson electrochemical cell development
The original graduate student at Clemson (Saheed Bukola) had since graduated, and the new graduate
student (Mayura Silva) had difficulties reproducing the 14:1 H/D selectivity. The student spent much
of his focus on careful calibration of H and D analysis by mass spectrometry and proved no isotopic
selectivity from electrochemical cell, and only discovered the issue when he was about to graduate. The
cell membrane assembly was prepared by hot pressing Nafion with graphene on a copper plate. The
hot-pressed membrane was then digested with ammonium persulfate to remove the copper plate. The
digested copper ions were expected to ion-exchanged into Nafion membrane. Previous work used a
brief water rinse to remove excess copper solution. The new student exchanged the membrane assembly
with sulfuric acid (H or D version) so that copper ions were replaced by proton to make a “perfect”
proton-exchanged-membrane. At very end of his PhD study, he demonstrated isotope selectivity if the
copper ions were not exchanged with sulfuric acid. Therefore, Clemson never provided a cell to SRNL
for tritium testing.

Figure 6 shows measured resistance of hydrogen ion transport over time through
Nafion|Graphene|Nafion sandwich structure at 70 mV applied potential. Colored letters indicate times
at which the feed gas was changed between D2 and H2. Higher resistances were observed for both D
and H transport in the NGN membrane compared with the similar experiment conducted with NN
membrane, with an especially high initial resistance observed for deuterium transport. This initial
resistance progressively decreased over time. A transition to a hydrogen gas feed resulted in a sharp
drop in resistance to a steady state resistance of approximately 3.1 ohms. Interestingly, when switching
back to a deuterium gas feed, the resistance increased but did not revert to the initial level. Subsequent
measurements of graphene’s H/D areal conductance ratio from NGN and NN amperometric data at
steady-state conditions revealed a value of approximately 1.1, indicative of almost no isotope selectivity
in these MEAs. This observation, combined with temporal analysis of deuteron transport resistance,
suggests that a critical reassessment of the efficacy and selectivity of graphene-embedded Nafion
membranes for hydrogen isotope separation is warranted.

Vanderbilt efforts for an electrochemical cell
Vanderbilt, in addition to delivering higher quality graphene growth, built electrochemical cells to study
the H/D selectivity of graphene. Vanderbilt modified the preparation by using a proton-impermeable
polymer to seal defects and cracks in the graphene membrane. By switching the proton-conducting
Nafion with the proton-impermeable polymer, the Vanderbilt cell had a proton flux about 1/100th of the
Clemson design. In other words, 1 hour test using the Clemson-style cell with tritium would require
100 hours of tritium testing using the Vanderbilt-style cell. In addition, the Vanderbilt cell design was
fragile due to numerous connections held together with epoxy.

A Vanderbilt Ph.D. student was awarded a Graduate Student Research fellowship from DOE Office of
Science to continue H/D tests at SRNL. Figure 7 shows the design and fabrication of the system for
H/D separation analysis at Vanderbilt. A Vanderbilt cell was tested at SRNL for H2/D2 hydrogen
pumping. The electrochemical cell current was substantially lower than that published by Clemson.
After H2/D2 evaluation, the Vanderbilt cell was installed in the radiological hood, but a leak was
identified when the system was initialized. The leak was identified to be an epoxy joint on the gas feed
line. An attempt to repair the leaking cell with epoxy was unsuccessful due to the tensile stress caused
by weight of metal compression fittings and the absence of supporting hardware in a radiological hood
at SRNL while the epoxy was hardening.

Refinement of the quantum tunneling model by SRNL
Quantum tunneling is one of the most striking phenomena in quantum mechanics. It allows particles to
pass through potential energy barriers that would be impossible to overcome according to classical
physics. The probability of tunneling depends on several factors: barrier thickness (L), barrier potential
energy (V₀), particle’s total energy (E), and particle’s mass (m). The tunneling probability can be
quantitatively described using the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation for a particle encountering a
potential barrier between x=0 and x = L. As shown in Figure 8, the incident wave function begins in
Region I, where it partially reflects and partially penetrates the barrier at the interfaces between Regions
I/II and II/III. The resulting wave functions in Regions I, II and III are represented by ΨI, ΨII, ΨIII,
respectively.
Because deuterium (D⁺) has twice the mass of proton (H⁺), the deuteron tunneling probability is
significantly lower and leads to the observed isotope selectivity. This mass-dependent tunneling
behavior provides a compelling explanation for the consistent H/D ≈ 10 ratio across different

Final electrochemical cell evaluation at SRNL
Nearing the end of the project, SRNL made one more attempt to fabricate a working electrochemical
cell inhouse since the tritium lab had been ready for 3 years. The criterion was to generate 2 reproducible
cells with H/D selectivity around 10:1, then one cell would be tested in the radiological lab for H/T
selectivity.

Nafion|Graphene|Nafion sandwiched MEAs were prepared according to the literature [22,24]. In brief,
Nafion 211 sheet (Fuel Cell store, part number 1600001-1) having a thickness of approximately 25 μm
was punched into 3/4 inch diameter disks. The hard plastic cover was easily removed from the Nafion
membrane, but the soft plastic cover required a drop of DI water to initialize the plastic cover removal.
A small piece of copper-supported graphene (ACS Material, SKU# CVCU1064) was cut from a larger
piece at approximately 2 cm square and placed on top of the Nafion 211 disk. This assembly was placed
in a Carver (Model 3851-0) hot press and pressed at 140 °C for 2 min. Teflon-coated fabric sheets were
used to prevent the Nafion from adhering to the hot press. Next, the Nafion|graphene|copper assembly
was placed in a 0.3 M aqueous solution of ammonium persulfate to react overnight until the copper
layer dissolved, as indicated by visual inspection. After a brief water rinse, a second Nafion 211 disk
was placed on top of the graphene layer, and the assembly was again hot-pressed using the same
conditions as for the initial hot press.

Each MEA assembly had two electrodes: one anode and one cathode. Anodes consisted of a 5/16" disc
from carbon cloth coated with 4 mg/cm2 of platinum black (Fuel Cell Store, part number 1610008), and
cathodes consisted of a 3/16” disc from platinized carbon cloth containing 0.3 mg Pt/cm2 (Fuel Cell
Store, part number 1610002). Additional Nafion ionomer was added to each electrode from a Nafion
suspension (5 wt % Nafion solution); 3.5 μL of Nafion suspension was used for the anodes, and 0.5 μL
was used for the cathodes. The different diameters of anode and cathode electrodes worked out well in
case alignment was not perfect. Following drying, the electrodes were assembled onto a Nafion disk,
with or without embedded graphene, with catalyst- coated sides facing toward the Nafion and centered
along disks. This assembly was then hot-pressed at 140 °C for 5 min.

The cell assembly consisted of a modified fluoropolymer compression fitting suitable for holding 3/4
in. tubes and adapted to allow for insertion of two flat-ended graphite rods that serve as current
collectors. Two holes were drilled in each graphite rod to allow for continuous gas flow. Gas diffusion
layer (GDL) disks (AvCarb grade-P50T Carbon Fiber Paper, Fuel Cell store, part number 590242) were
placed between the MEA electrodes and the graphite rods to ensure electrical contact and improve gas
flow to the electrodes. Hydrogen pumping experiments were performed with humidified hydrogen or
deuterium gas. Cell testing was done at room temperature (21°C) with gases humidified with H2O for
hydrogen and D2O for deuterium. Current-voltage curves were acquired using a CH Instruments (model

1127C) potentiostat in cyclic voltammetry mode between ±0.1V. Scan rates were 10 mV/s for H/D-
pumping experiments; currents were independent of scan rate.

Figure 10 shows the Nafion|graphene|copper assembly during copper etching in ammonium persulfate
solution. After the hot press, the Teflon fabric sheet adheres to the Nafion|graphene|copper sheet. The
copper was dissolved, as evidenced by the blue coloring of the aqueous copper sulfate solution, leaving
the transparent Nafion|graphene assembly on top of Teflon fabric sheet. After decantation of the
solution and gently rinsing with DI water, the assembly was air dried, shown in Figure 11. The Nafion
membrane curved during drying. After hot pressing another layer of Nafion, the
Nafion|graphene|Nafion assembly (transparent) and the assembly with the anode and cathode added are
shown in Figure 12. The cell assembly is very similar to fuel cell construction in reference [24],
illustrated in Figure 13.

Summary
The collaboration project has generated fruitful results on research, development and training in isotope
production. The project resulted in 4 publications, 1 conference presentation, 1 SCGSR fellowship, 2
Ph.D. dissertations, 2 Ph.D. graduates, training of 2 summer intern students, 1 researcher to pursue PhD
study, and contributed to chapter topic in an upcoming clean energy book.

However, determining H/T selectivity across graphene remains highly challenging. Despite the fact that
the H/D selectivity was reported for over 10 years, reproducing the results varies. A combination of the
difficult and reproducible fabrication of the membranes, fragility of the assembled electrochemical
systems, and proton conductance reduction with impermeable polymers impeded both the academic
research but also the delivery of functional cells to test with tritium. On the other hand, the University
of Manchester has been actively pursuing H/T selectivity but has not published any results to date.

SRNL has made substantial progress on the tritium lab setup and uncertainty estimate. The tritium
measurement uncertainty estimate was hypothesized to be the most difficult part at beginning of the
project but instead resulted in the greatest progress. The development could one day apply to the
determination of H/T selectivity over graphene once a reproducible test cell becomes available. At the
same time, SRNL has gained experience in electrochemical cell and MEA fabrication. The quantum
tunneling model was further refined but is still pending H/T selectivity data.
