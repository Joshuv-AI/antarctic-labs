Optical Test Bench 2

Introduction
The National Ignition Facility (NIF) is a research facility that focuses on High Energy Density Science and
Inertial Confinement Fusion. In the NIF, 192 laser beams are directed into a miniscule Target Capsule to
generate an implosion – this is called a NIF Shot. Many NIF Users conduct experiments using different
Targets and laser inputs to generate reactions that are of interest to the scientific community. Those
reactions are monitored using various Target Diagnostic instruments, including Shot Time Photography –
which provides crucial images of the Target as the implosion occurs.

A subset of these NIF Users is less interested in the reaction itself and more interested in subjecting
materials or devices to the intense environment that these reactions can create. Using the Target as a
source of x-rays, neutrons, or blast loading for particular materials has been significant to the shock
physics community and to those who work in extreme design spaces. However, the Material and Radiation
Effects team – that performs these experiments on behalf of the NIF Users – has not been able to make
good use of the existing Shot Time Photography Diagnostics.

The STP cameras are focused on the Targets rather than the MRE Customers’ materials, the current system
is not oriented to have a good view of the MRE Customers’ materials, and the cameras are limited in their
capabilities (black and white, poor resolution, etc.). As MRE experiments increase in quantity and
importance to the scientific community, the desire for visual monitoring of the experiments to substantiate
models and inform other data is also rising.

Current Situation
Current Systems
The NIF Target Chamber, or TC, is a sphere with multiple ports that can be used to field experiments (see
Figure 1). The port positions are indicated by two angles, the first angle denotes the vertical position of the
port (from the top), while the second angle denotes the equatorial position of the port (see Figure 2).
Current shot time photography systems on the NIF TC include Shot Time Photography (STP) – fielded at 78-
054 to record the Target interaction (see Figure 3) – and the Near Backscatter Imager (NBI) – fielded in
various locations to record the backscatter around multiple beam apertures (see Figure 4). Since NIF users
for Materials and Radiation Effects (MRE) experiments field their diagnostics most often at the position 90-
124, the current STP diagnostic can only photograph the side of these experiments (see Figure 3).

Deficiencies, Opportunities and Need Statement
In recent years, there has been an increase in demand for shot time photography at differing angles to view
debris coming off the front of diagnostics. The current shot time photography diagnostic is limited to a
single view into the NIF Target Chamber (see Figure 3) of NIF targets and does not have adequate space or
an appropriate mounting system to accommodate larger optical equipment needed to properly capture

greatly expand the experimental capabilities and inherent value of MRE experiments in the NIF Target

Acceptance Criteria
The key acceptance criteria are the most critical stakeholder requirements. If a concept for the system
does not fulfill all of the key acceptance criteria, it cannot be considered for selection as the candidate
design. Based on the stakeholder requirements and the voice of the customer the following requirements
were determined to be the key acceptance criteria:

 1) The system shall allow for camera exchanges in less than 1 hour.

 2) The system shall provide a mechanical interface that can be adapted to support multiple types of
 cameras.

 3) The system shall allow all cameras to be focused in-situ.

Concept Generation
Magnetic Kinematic Base Test Bench Concept
A magnetic kinematic base is a standard type of optical equipment that uses magnets on both the camera
and the optical test bench to lock a camera into a set position (see Figure 8). With this solution, the optical
test bench will be embedded with magnets at pre-determined positions. Cameras can then be fitted with
magnetic adapters that match the position of the magnets on the optical test bench to lock the cameras in
place.

 Figure 8 – Newport M-BK-2A magnetic kinematic mount for optical equipment

The positive of this solution is that it allows for efficient swapping of cameras between experiments with
high repeatability. Additionally, a variety of cameras can be installed onto this system with minimal
additional effort; only a magnetic adapter needs to be designed to lock a new camera into place. The
drawbacks of this solution are that the magnets on the optical test bench will have a weight limit, which
may limit the size of cameras that can be installed. If there are multiple sets of magnets on the optical test
bench, to allow for more than one camera to be installed, the magnets may negatively affect the alignment
of optical equipment depending on magnetic strength.

Pinned Kinematic Base Test Bench
A pinned kinematic base uses a pin and slot solution to mechanical mount cameras onto the optical test
bench. With this solution the optical test bench will have set pins at pre-determined positions. Cameras
can then be fitted with a slot adapter that matches the position of the pin on the optical test bench to lock
the camera in place.

The positive of this solution is that it allows for efficient swapping of cameras between Xray experiments
and lowers future design costs by only requiring the design of a new adapter. The drawback of this solution
is that the pin and slot require tight tolerances to guarantee proper alignment, which can increase the
additional upfront cost of machined parts. Additionally, pins can mechanically fail, which may cause
misalignment of cameras or permanently damage the optical test bench and adapters.

 Figure 11 – Pinned Kinematic Base Test Bench, Side View

Tube Camera
A tube camera solution places the desired camera into a barrel of pre-determined diameter. The camera is
then locked at a desired depth inside of the barrel with set screws, and the barrel is slotted into a tube
mounted on the optical test bench with a clocking feature to lock the rotation of the camera.

The benefit of this solution is that it allows for additional flexibility for the sponsor of the experiment on the
position of the camera. If a camera needs a specific distance away from the diagnostic inside of the NIF
Target Chamber, the barrel provides the ability to adjust the camera depth without additional modifications
to the optical test bench. The negative of this solution is that it is inflexible to new cameras. If a camera size
is incompatible with the designed barrels, a new barrel and mounting tube will need to be designed, which

increases the cost for future changes. Additionally, this solution is more difficult to swap cameras for,
requiring potentially both the barrel and the mounting tube to be swapped for cameras of different sizes.

Vertically Mounted Cameras
A vertically mounted solution sets the cameras for the Optical Test Bench along the Y-axis instead of the X-
axis. With this solution multiple vertically oriented breadboards can be installed on top of the Optical Test
Bench. Cameras are then slotted into place at predetermined angles to properly focus on the diagnostic
inside of the NIF Target Chamber.

The benefit of this solution is that it allows for more cameras to be placed on the Optical Test Bench and
maximizes the space. The negative of this solution is that it needs to work against gravity and will have more
strict weight requirements to prevent cameras from falling during extended use. Additionally, a kinematic
interface to guarantee a proper alignment is more difficult to implement in this solution without
exacerbating the existing weight concerns.

Concept Selection
To down-select from these solutions, a Pugh Matrix was used to evaluate each concept. The criteria of the
Pugh matrix were Cost, Development, Accessibility, Quantity and Safety. Cost refers to the amount of
money needed for new or replacement parts for repeated use of the Optical Test Bench. Development
refers to the amount of time needed for a new camera profile to be adapted to the system. This also refers
to how often a new interface needs to be developed for a new camera profile. Accessibility refers to how
easy it is to access the cameras on the Optical Test Bench to focus them during installation. Quantity refers
to the number of cameras that can be placed simultaneously on the Optical Test Bench. Finally, Safety

Optical Test Bench 17

refers to the overall structural integrity of each solution. Each concept was considered under these criteria
and rated from 1 to 5, with 1 being the worst and 5 being the best. Safety and Accessibility were determined
to be the most critical to the design since a structurally robust design will expand the variety of cameras
that can be used on the Optical Test Bench and Accessibility directly relates to how efficiently a camera
can interact with focusing or pulling data. Cost and Quantity were determined to be the next most critical
aspects of the designs; A costly solution that requires a consistent stream of replacement parts
discourages continued use of the system, while being able to field a large quantity of cameras can double
or triple data output per experiment. Development was considered the least critical for designs to satisfy
since a shorter development time is considered a quality-of-life improvement but does not directly affect
the performance of the Optical Test Bench. From the Pugh Matrix it was determined that the Magnetic
Kinematic Base concept was the best as it performed the highest on the most desired criteria of
Accessibility and Safety. The Pinned Kinematic Base and the Vertical Mount solutions were also considered
due to their high total scores. The Pinned Kinematic Base was determined to carry too burdensome of a
replacement cost for parts with a high tolerance pin and slot mechanism, while the Vertical Mount has too
high of a safety risk compared to the other horizontally mounted solutions.

System Context
The context diagram illustrates how both active and passive stakeholders interact with the system. The
context diagram represents how stakeholders are related to system by describing the exchanges that are
made by the individual stakeholder and the system. Each exchange is represented by a noun to describe
what is being exchanged with the direction of the exchange being denoted by an arrow. Figure 17 below is
the full context diagram, which represents all stakeholders’ role in the system.

 Figure 17 – Context Diagram

The active stakeholders, the MRE team, the NIF TC Experiment Sponsors, and NIF Operations, all have a
bilateral exchange with the system. These stakeholders are the primary users of the Optical Test Bench,
and both provide a service that allows the system to function and have interest in the output of the system.
These interactions will be further expanded upon in the Use Case and Sequence Diagrams but can be
summarized as follows. The MRE team provides the engineering support needed to field cameras on the
Optical Test Bench and in exchange receives the camera outputs to appropriately focus cameras and the
final photography data that the system took during a shot. NIF Operations provide the technician support
required to install and exchange cameras during an experiment and in exchange receive the camera
outputs needed to focus the cameras. Finally, the NIF TC Experiment Sponsors provide the cameras they
would like to field on the Optical Test Bench and receive the final photography data from the system.

The passive stakeholders, TDES, ICF, NNSA, HED, and D&S, all provide or receive a unilateral exchange
from the system. These stakeholders do not directly interact with the system but are influenced by the
system instead. The TDES group provides the rigorous engineering review needed to develop and field the

Operational Scenarios
The Concept of Operations or ConOps, is a visual representation of how the system functions in its
environment. The ConOps diagram shows direct exchanges between stakeholders and the system as well
as the interactions of internal components of the system. Not all stakeholders are represented in the
ConOps diagram, for example the ICF, NNSA and HED programs provide funding needed to develop the
Optical Test Bench but does not directly interact with the functioning system or the other stakeholders in a
meaningful capacity related to the system and is thus not represented in the ConOps diagram. The
boundary of the Optical Test Bench is represented by the green box. As is shown in the ConOps Diagram, a
request for a camera is initially given to the MRE team by a NIF TC Experiment Sponsor. From there the MRE
team will develop a camera mount that is approved by the TDES team. The camera mount and camera are
then mounted on the Optical Test Bench with the help of NIF Operations. NIF Operations aditionallly
provides the controls needed to trigger the cameras. This allows the camera to output its view for use by
NIF Operations and the MRE team for alignment. Once it has been aligned the NIF Operations team can
once again control the camera and trigger it when the experiment begins. The final photo data is output by
the cameras and is delivered to the NIF TC Experiment Sponsor and other interested parties.

 Figure 18 – Concept of Operations Diagram

With current shot time photography diagnostics, sponsors are unable to take front-facing photos of 90-124
diagnostics and are limited to specific cameras that only take black and white photos. With the Magnetic

Optical Test Bench, sponsors can adapt multiple cameras to a magnetic kinematic interface and field
specific cameras based around their experimental goals.

Camera Exchanges
With the Magnetic Optical Test Bench, technicians will be able to efficiently exchange cameras chosen by
the experiment sponsor. The cameras will be compatible with an adapter that interacts with a magnetic
kinematic base installed onto the Optical Test Bench. A sponsor simply needs to provide the MRE team
with the dimensions of the camera they would like to field and an adapter that fits the form factor of the
camera will be used to install the camera onto the Optical Test Bench. This solution that separates the
camera from the locking mechanism to the bench grants users flexibility for camera choices and has the
potential to change cameras for an experiment in a short period of time.

Data Extraction
The Magnetic Optical Test Bench will also be able to provide sponsor’s data in a streamlined manner.
Current shot-time photography diagnostics upload photos to existing archives that are difficult to navigate
without prior knowledge of the NIF databases. This leads to frustration when attempting to find historic
photography data. Additionally, photos often require post-processing that is time intensive due to the low-
quality cameras used. The Magnetic Optical Test Bench will output its data directly to a dashboard
controlled by NIF Operations that can be directly observed by sponsors and the MRE team to view on the
day of the experiment. This serves as an initial verification that the cameras functioned properly during an
experiment. Additionally, the ease of removal of the cameras from the Magnetic Optical Test Bench allows
for quick access to camera SD cards for direct data transfer to sponsors removing additional steps.

Maintenance
Finally, with the combined ability of efficient camera exchanges and providing photos on-demand,
problems with alignment of cameras on the Magnetic Optical Test Bench can be easily diagnosed. During
an experiment, cycle sponsors and the MRE team can simply request NIF Operations to trigger the cameras
to observe their alignment and to see if a camera is properly focused. If the focus is unsatisfactory the
Magnetic Optical Test Bench offers open access to the cameras on it for quick adjustments. If the
alignment is problematic, the kinematic base or adapter can be replaced. The simplistic magnet
mechanism used to hold cameras in place means spare parts can be ordered in bulk for cheap, meaning a
part can always be readily available.

QFD Analysis
Now that a candidate design has been selected and its uses have been considered, a quality function
diagram or QFD was generated to determine how design characteristics of the Magnetic Optical Test Bench
interact with each other. The QFD is used to define the relationship between the request stakeholder
characteristics, the “WHATs” in the left column of the QFD, and the design features, the “HOWs in the top
row of the QFD. Additionally, the QFD provides a forum to investigate the correlations between
characteristics and features, specifically how one feature might affect all characteristics.

In the diagram, all stakeholder characteristics are assigned an importance value shown in the right-hand
column. The most important characteristics were related to the performance of the system, specifically
proper sightline to 90-124, a versatile mechanical interface and safety compliance with NIF standards. The
design features in the top row are then positively or negatively correlated with all stakeholder
characteristics. These correlations are scaled, meaning some design features have a stronger correlation
to specific stakeholder characteristics than others. This allows for needed granularity to determine the
most important features to be included.

Based on the QFD, the most important design features were a metric-style optical breadboard and a
standardized interface for the mechanical mount. These features ranked high since they encourage the use
of a variety of cameras on the system. Both features simplify the design work required to adapt a camera to
the Magnetic Optical Test Bench, which reduces time and cost for development as well as reduces risk of
complicated installation procedures that bloat camera exchange time. Camera enclosure size and weight
capacity follow close behind as these will have a significant impact on the type and quantity of cameras
that can be implemented. Finally, the port location was considered a desirable feature as a single camera
on a port with an optimal angle to 90-124 can provide the same amount of data as multiple cameras on
sub-optimal port locations.

The roof of the chart displays the relationships between the different design features. While most design
features on the QFD have neutral interactions with one another, the QFD highlighted the positive
relationship between the form factor of the camera enclosure and most other features of the system. If a
larger enclosure can be created for the cameras, heavier and larger cameras can be placed inside; this
creates a positive correlation with the maximum weight the system can accommodate. Additionally, the
larger enclosure also provides space for a bigger access port (to ease operational complexity) and the
option to install lock hasps as an information control.

The functional architecture of the Magnetic Optical Test Bench is based on the main sub-functions of the
system. The overall purpose of the Magnetic Optical Test Bench is to take shot-time photography, and this
is fulfilled by the following sub-functions of the system: Mount Cameras, Focus Cameras, Take Photos, and
Provide Data. These sub-functions make up the first-level decomposition, which informs the user the main

architecture shows the major inputs and outputs of the Magnetic Optical Test Bench by stakeholders as the

The first major function of the OTB is accepting the camera for use in the system. The camera is provided by
the Experiment Sponsor, the adapter is provided by the MRE Team, and the technician support for the
installation is provided by NIF Ops. Once the camera is mounted, the OTB enables focusing of the camera
by providing sufficient space for the operator to physically tune the camera lens. A trigger can then be sent
to the system to initiate a photo capture, which is then stored on the removable or built-in camera storage.
The data can be retrieved by digitally requesting the photos through a data line or by physically removing
the media storage (e.g. SD card or film). With these data (photos), the iterative process of focusing and
checking photos will continue with the same inputs and outputs to the OTB system.

 1) If the average time to exchange or install a camera is too long, the system will interfere with other
 experiment-critical activities and may be dropped in favor of higher priority diagnostics.

 M1: The Magnetic Optical Test Bench will be designed to have a single interface to adapt cameras
 to the bench; this simplification reduces the time required for installation and minimizes
 confusion for technicians.

 M2: Camera exchanges will be planned in advance and may be performed outside of a NIF shot
 cycle. This provides technicians multiple opportunities to exchange a camera and troubleshoot

Reflection
The development of the Magnetic Optical Test Bench as a conceptual design highlighted the iterative
nature of the Systems Engineering process. One of the most interesting aspects of the process was how
everything related back to the Stakeholder feedback and prompted deeper reflection on their comments as
the other sections of the process developed. With the lack of shot-time photography diagnostics available
to take front-facing photos of 90-124, many teams were interested in this capability and pulling together all
of their requirements into a concise list proved to be challenging.

Once the problem was fully understood and requirements were agreed upon, concept generation and
selection ended up being straightforward. Using other shot-time photography diagnostics as a base, the
new system needs only to expand on what is currently used at NIF to improve versatility and expand the
variety of cameras that could be used. This versatility feature was the main focus for us when thinking of
new solutions. This created a surprise at the Pugh Matrix as we had expected the Vertical Mounted Camera
solution to do the best, since it provided one of the simplest interfaces while maximizing the space
available. However, due to the Vertical concept carrying a higher safety risk than the others, the Magnetic
Kinematic Base ended up being our best concept. Although initially surprised by this result, after careful
reconsideration of our Pugh Matrix we agreed that the ratings of the concepts were reasonable and saw the
consequence of mainly focusing on a singular feature when creating a concept.

The use case models, context diagrams, and concept of operations diagrams were vital for us to flesh out
the interactions that our active stakeholders would have with the system. These diagrams also served as
confirmation of our definition of active and passive stakeholders.

The QFD challenged us to consider all interactions in our system. The tool brought valuable discussion
between us on the importance of each design feature as well as the effect each design feature had on each
other. It was interesting to see that most of our design features were independent of one another; we
interpreted this to mean that our solution was sufficiently adaptable to a variety of circumstances.

The risk assessment and functional architecture expanded upon our concept of operations and was useful
to further define the explicit functions of the system and gain insight into the stakeholder-system
interactions. Seeing that most of the system interactions were with the NIF Operations team reinforced our
emphasis on the concerns from that team including ease of installation/operations.

Throughout the Systems Engineering process for this design, each activity prompted us to re-consider what
we had previously defined and helped refine our approach. There were multiple points where we had to
reconsider our need statement and verify with our stakeholder requirements to ensure that our direction
and focus was aligned with our overall purpose. At the end of this process, we are confident in the
robustness of our design; we fully understand the problem, stakeholders and solution, which sets our team
for success as we move forwards with implementing the Magnetic Optical Test Bench.
