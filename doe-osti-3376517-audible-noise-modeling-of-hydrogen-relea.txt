ABSTRACT
This study implemented validated literature models to predict audible noise due to pressurized
gaseous hydrogen releases through a thermally-activated pressure relief device (TPRD) and attached
vent stack. A literature survey discovered limited hydrogen-specific noise prediction models
validated by experiments. However, empirical noise prediction models for air flowing through pipes
and valves were identified. These empirical models were used to predict noise levels and compared
against hydrogen noise data reported in two studies: one experimental study of noise from hydrogen
leaking through a pipe and another which modeled hydrogen flowing through a solenoid valve
during a fuel cell vehicle refueling. The valve flow model was then applied to predict noise for
hydrogen releases through a TPRD. Results show that hydrogen releases through a TPRD can
produce harmful noise levels varying from 134 to 150 dB. However, further model validation and
additional experimental data are needed to improve prediction confidence and accuracy.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Understanding the risks of hydrogen fuel is important to ensure safe storage and usage in railroad
applications. Specifically, it is important to characterize audible noise produced by high-pressure
hydrogen releases and identify risks associated with audible noise exposure in confined spaces such
as maintenance facilities. This report details work to investigate and evaluate an audible noise
prediction model for gaseous pressurized hydrogen releases through thermally-activated pressure
relief devices (TPRDs), valves, and vent stacks. The study also characterizes the potential noise
hazards associated with hydrogen releases, particularly in enclosed environments like maintenance
depots or facilities.
This work progressed through three objectives. First, a literature survey identified models to predict
audible noise from gas flowing through pipes, orifices, or valves. Second, predictions from these
models were compared with available experimental data. Lastly, these empirical models were applied
to predict noise from a high-pressure hydrogen release through a TPRD.
The literature survey showed a scarcity of experimental data on hydrogen-specific release audible
noise. This highlights the need for hydrogen release noise data to help characterize hazards
associated with hydrogen leaks in both outdoor and enclosed settings. The literature survey showed
that gas flowing through valves and pipes generates aerodynamic sound due to the interaction of
turbulent flow with valves and pipe structures, and that high-velocity flows can potentially cause
sonic booms. Empirical models from Baumann and Coney [1], which incorporate parameters such
as flow velocity, pressure, and valve characteristics, can be used to calculate sound pressure levels.
However, these models are not validated for lighter-than-air gases and are meant to model ≤0.3 Ma
flows.
Two studies were identified which enabled validation of the empirical models of Baumann and
Coney for hydrogen. A study by Maeda and Tamura [2] experimentally measured sound produced
by hydrogen leaks from fuel cell vehicles. This study measured hydrogen leakage sounds amidst
background noise by varying hydrogen flow rates and noise detection location. Another study by
Ariyadi et al. [3] computationally modeled solenoid valve noise caused by hydrogen flow, providing
insights into the noise generated during fast fuel charging of hydrogen fuel cell vehicles.
The modeling approach in this current work involved applying two sets of empirical equations from
Baumann and Coney [1]: one for pipe noise prediction and another for valve noise prediction. These
equations were used in three scenarios. First, results from Baumann and Coney’s model were
compared to experimental pipe noise results from Maeda and Tamura [2]. Then, Baumann and
Coney’s valve noise equations were compared to computational results from Ariyadi et al. [3]. Lastly,
these same valve noise equations were applied to predict hydrogen audible noise levels for a
pressurized release through a TPRD valve.
For hydrogen released through a pipe, the predicted pipe sound pressure level (SPL) varies from 20
to 94 dB and significantly depends on the diameter of the pipe and the flow conditions, with smaller
diameter pipes producing higher noise levels due to increased gas velocity. These results agree with
the values and trends observed by Maeda and Tamura [2] for crimped pipes, but only by assuming a
loss factor which is within a specific range. The loss factor is varied in this current work to match
the sound pressure level range observed by Maeda and Tamura [2]. While these noise levels may not
be hazardous for short exposure times, they can adversely affect human hearing and raise safety
concerns in work environments.

 7

The empirical equations of Baumann and Coney [1] were used to predict valve noise. The predicted
valve SPL noise for a generic non-TPRD valve was calculated to vary between 116 and 122 dB,
aligning well with results from the computational study of Ariyadi et al. [3]. Predictions for high-
pressure hydrogen releases through a TPRD at the very start of the release show potentially harmful
SPL noise between 134 and 150 dB, with larger valves and higher tank pressures leading to higher
sound levels.
The literature empirical and modeling studies and the modeling studies performed as part of this
current work show that TPRD noise is louder than generic non-TPRD valve noise and significantly
louder than pipe noise. This is due to the TPRD’s smaller flow diameter, which results in a high
velocity flow with high flow turbulence. Notably, these values correspond to the noise emitted at
the very beginning of a release, and as the pressure decreases, the noise levels will also decrease and
become less hazardous. Also, for TPRD vent stacks, the loudest noise may not be from the pipe
length itself, rather from the underexpanded jet supersonic gas flow release at the outlet of the vent
stack. Since there were no studies of jet noise from an underexpanded hydrogen release through a
vent stack outlet, this aspect of the noise profile was neglected in this study because it could not be
validated.
The Baumann and Coney [1] empirical models used in this work provide an initial framework for
predicting noise hazards from hydrogen releases. However, further research to validate these models
for supersonic hydrogen release conditions are needed. Limited pressurized hydrogen release
experimental data means that there is significant uncertainty in the model results which may be
improved with application of a better model, especially one suited for supersonic jet flow. Also,
additional computational and/or experimental investigations of TPRD and vent stack noise would
help develop more accurate prediction models.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report presents work to develop and validate a noise prediction model for a hydrogen release
through a valve using existing data from experimental studies. The report summarizes predictions
from a model validated by data from two existing studies to identify the potential noise hazard
associated with hydrogen releases. This work highlights challenges associated with the dearth of
hydrogen noise data and the need for further model validation.

1.2. Noise and Hydrogen Locomotives
Hydrogen may be used as a fuel source for locomotives, and so it is necessary to study and
understand the risks associated with the use of hydrogen technology for rail to inform safety
recommendations [10]. One important area of study is estimating noise from high pressure
hydrogen releases to determine potential health or safety hazards, especially in enclosed areas such as
a rail maintenance facility.

Tanks of gaseous hydrogen are stored on the roof or within the powerpack of hydrogen rail cars and
feature thermally-activated pressure relief devices (TPRDs) which open to release the contents of
the tanks if a maximum set temperature level is met. This controlled release occurs to prevent tank
rupture which would result in an uncontrolled release of hydrogen, and could lead to fire or other
consequences. The valve releases the hydrogen to an attached tube called a vent stack. The vent
stack is designed to direct the gas release to a location that can minimize hazardous effects on
people and structures.

Audible noise characterization, especially related to hydrogen TPRDs, is not well explored
experimentally or computationally in the literature. This study aims to apply current noise control
models to more effectively assess noise hazards associated with hydrogen tank releases.

1.3. Objectives
The main objective of this work is to investigate sonic hazards associated with hydrogen releases
through a TPRD or vent stack. The task objectives are threefold:

 1. Conduct a literature survey of experimentally validated models to predict audible noise from
 gas flowing through a pipe, orifice, or a valve,
 2. Compare noise prediction from models to experimental studies, and
 3. Use a validated model to predict noise from high pressure hydrogen releases through a
 TPRD.

1.4. Overall Approach
A literature survey was conducted to identify models or correlations for sound produced by gas
flowing through a pipe, orifice, or valve. Only models validated by experimental studies were
considered in order to reduce model assumption uncertainties associated with different shapes and
sizes of release orifices. From the literature survey, model parameters necessary for noise prediction
were identified based on a variety of release conditions. Next, a correlation model was applied to
predict noise levels for a given set of hydrogen release parameters (e.g. mass flow rate, velocity,
density, and pressure).

 12

This report discusses empirical noise models developed for gas flowing through a pipe and throttling
valves. The literature review identified very limited experimental data and experimentally validated
models; only two relevant studies are presented in this report. A comparison of noise predictions
from the empirical models and two published studies are discussed to demonstrate the applicability
of these empirical models for hydrogen releases in railroad applications. Finally, results for a
parametric study of hydrogen release through a TPRD at conditions relevant to railroad applications
are discussed to characterize the associated noise hazard.

1.5. Organization of the Report
There are four sections in this report. Section 1 provides an overview of the project objectives,
approach and scope. Section 2 summarizes a literature survey of empirical models for noise
prediction and studies on hydrogen release noise. Section 3 compares empirical model predictions to
noise levels reported in the surveyed studies. Noise prediction for hydrogen release at conditions
relevant to rail applications are also presented in this section. Section 4 provides a conclusion of
findings for this work.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW OF NOISE PREDICTION MODELS
Gas flowing through or interacting with solid objects, such as vent stacks or valves, produce
aerodynamic sound [1, 11]. At high velocities, gas flow can result in mini sonic booms, especially if
the flow itself is sonic [1]. Understanding and predicting the sound levels from gas flows can be
helpful in identifying audible and otherwise damaging hazards associated with gas leaks.

Hydrogen leakage sound can result from a pipe leak or burst during an accident scenario, or from
venting systems, such as vent stacks and release valves. For rail systems, such releases may occur
outdoors or in enclosed spaces such as maintenance facilities. Detecting hydrogen leaks is crucial to
prevent potential hazards, and this can be done using leak detector systems. Depending on the
pressure of the tank and the size of the leak, these releases can result in noise hazards. Therefore, for
hydrogen venting or unintended leak scenarios, expected sound levels can inform safety procedures
for storage or maintenance facilities so that noise hazards can be minimized and personnel are
adequately protected.

Pressurized gas leakage sound will vary depending on the release system. Narrower nozzles can lead
to high velocity releases and different shapes may have different associated noise levels. This work
focuses on modeling sound from vent stacks and TPRD valves for high pressure releases of
hydrogen in maintenance garages. A survey on noise prediction models and experimental data for
hydrogen leakage was the first step.

2.1. Empirical Models
In this section, empirical models are presented which predict noise from fluid flow through valves
and pipes including resulting throttling noise as discussed by Baumann and Coney [1]. Prediction of
noise from gas flow through a valve and downstream piping involves estimating the noise associated
with flow through the valve, the pipe interior, and noise from the pipe vibration. The noise inside
the pipe depends on the aerodynamic characteristics of the valve, as well as pipe length, diameter,
and cross-sectional area.

frequency fc , which is the octave range for the emitted sound. Hence, the SPL may be calculated for

2.1.2. Valve Noise Sound Power Model
Valve noise is produced by the interaction of turbulent fluid flow with the valve as well as the jet
noise caused by the turbulent flow itself [1]. Turbulent flow interaction with the valve causes
dynamic drag and lift forces, also known as dipole noise. Jet noise, also known as quadrupole noise,
is dominant at high Mach numbers, while dipole, or noise from turbulent fluid flow interaction with
the valve, is dominant at low Mach numbers. Large pressure differences around the release valve
(upstream vs. downstream) can lead to supersonic flow within the valve and a shock downstream of
the valve that generates intense noise [1].

the valve. Other properties such as the gas mass flow rate and velocity are also necessary for noise

The valve flow coefficient 𝐶𝑣 and pressure recovery coefficient 𝐹𝐿 are properties that vary for
different valve types. The valve flow coefficient 𝐶𝑣 is the number of gallons per minute of 60°F
water that can flow through a valve with a pressure drop of 1 psi [15]. Therefore, 𝐶𝑣 is physically
related and proportional to a valve’s size and capacity for gas flow. The liquid pressure recovery
factor 𝐹𝐿 is a measure related to a fluid’s streamline through the valve [16]. If the fluid path through
a valve is relatively circuitous or convoluted, the 𝐹𝐿 value is proportionately higher. The acoustical
efficiency η is a value of the ratio of sound produced to the fluid flow energy available. The acoustic
efficiency is relatively small for subsonic flow and increases dramatically for supersonic shock wave
flows, where the energy associated with fluid flow is far greater than the noise generated [1].

Due to the lack of experimental studies on TPRD sound prediction, the pressure recovery
coefficient is unknown. Baumann and Coney [1] presented pressure recovery coefficients ranging
from 0.56 to 0.95 for seven valve types with different flow conditions (open or closed) and angle of
travel (10 to 100 % open). In this work, the pressure recovery coefficient of a 100%-open, single-
port parabolic plug globe valve was used for the TPRD: a pressure recovery coefficient 𝐹𝐿 = 0.90.

It is important to note that though they have the same units, the valve sound power (Lw) cannot be
directly compared to the SPL described in Section 2.1.1 since Lw is a measure of sound source and
SPL represents the effect of a sound source 1 meter away. Lw is typically used to calculate emitted
sound, while SPL accounts for sound divergence (i.e. spreading, dampening, and possible
transmission loss), which is why SPL is used in regulations such as the International Electrotechnical
Commission’s IEC 60534-8-3 for safety and regulations compliance and guidance [12, 17]. For a
pipe radius much smaller than 1 m, SPL (in dB) is approximately 8 dB lower than Lw [12].

2.2. Experimental and Computational Studies of Hydrogen Noise
Recent experimental studies of pipe noise and computational study of valve noise, both due to
hydrogen flow, specifically, were also reviewed. A summary of these studies is provided in the
following two sub-sections.

2.2.1. Hydrogen Leakage Pipe Noise
Motivated by the lack of study on leakage sound from hydrogen, a team at the Japan Automobile
Research Institute led by Maeda and Tamura [2] conducted an experimental study to detect sound
from a fuel cell vehicle in the event of a continuous hydrogen leak resulting from the failure of a leak
detection system. They also established a critical flow rate and distance from the vehicle to
distinguish hydrogen leakage sound from cross-traffic noise.

Maeda and Tamura conducted listening tests using hydrogen, helium, air, and argon to measure
noise level from gas leakage. Results from these tests showed that helium acoustic characteristics are
closer to hydrogen for most combinations of pipe diameters, with a SPL difference of 5 dB or less
between the two gases when hydrogen leak rate is 4.17 ft3/min. A frequency analysis range of 20 –
40,000 Hz was used in the tests. Pipe sizes used in this study were 0.25 inch outer diameter
(corresponding to 0.180” pipe inner diameter and leak size), 0.375 inch outer diameter
(corresponding to 0.277” pipe inner diameter and leak size), 0.25 inch crimped and 0.375 inch
crimped. The diameters of the crimped pipes were not disclosed. The crimped pipes were used to
simulate crushed pipe due to collision. Crimped pipes were used as worst case scenarios because the
fluid noise, which increases with velocity, is expected to increase as the pipe diameter decreases if
gas leak rates are the same as those for non-crimped pipes [2]. The pipes were positioned 4.9 feet
above the ground and connected to a copper tube of undisclosed size.

Using different instruments to control the flow and measure noise levels, Maeda and Tamura
reported the sound pressure level, frequency characteristics and directivity (direction, distance and
height) of hydrogen leakage. They found that hydrogen leakage sound is highest in the direction
facing the leak, 16 ft from the vehicle, and easily audible at a height of 4.9 ft. The sound becomes
harder to hear near the vehicle because of the attenuation from diffraction. Furthermore, in a 74 dB
traffic noise environment, 33 ft from the center of the vehicle, leakage sound could be heard for
flowrates which are 11.6 ft3/min or greater for the 0.375 inch pipe, or for flowrates that are greater
than or equal to 4.1 ft3/min for 0.25 inch diameter or crimped pipes [2].

 18

2.2.2. Hydrogen Flow Valve Noise
More recently, Ariyadi et al. [3] performed a computational study of solenoid valve noise due to
hydrogen flow during fast fuel charging of a hydrogen vehicle. Noise from a solenoid valve is
generated due to intense and complex turbulent flow, as well as interactions between the gas and
valve walls [3]. This valve noise can be extremely loud and harmful to users, and lead to mechanical
failure, highlighting the importance of characterization for noise reduction purposes and users’
safety.

Ariyadi et al. [3] reported studies on different valves (such as control, cavitation, or throttling valves)
used in industry and how computational fluid dynamics (CFD) tools have been used to study flow-
induced noise from air to develop noise reduction techniques. In their study, CFD software ANSYS
Fluent v.19 was used to perform simulations of hydrogen flow through solenoid valves by solving
for the mass, momentum and energy conservation equations thereby capturing important flow
characteristics such as flow turbulence and energy. The flow-induced noise was calculated by
applying a broadband noise model and the Ffowcs William-Hawking (FW-H) model. The
broadband noise model uses the flow turbulence to determine the noise or sound power generated
and its source based on Lighthill's acoustic analogy [3]. The FW-H model computes the sound
pressure fluctuation, which is used to obtain the SPL related to the actual audible noise. The
simulations were conducted for three valve diameter openings of 0.0197, 0.0118, and 0.004 inches
with outlet gauge pressures of 0, 4060, and 8120 psi, and inlet temperatures of -40, 50 and 140 oF,
respectively. Outlet pressures of 0 and 8120 psi respectively correspond to the tank pressure of an
empty and almost fully charged vehicle. The valve was fully open at 0.0197 inches, and almost fully
closed at 0.004 inches. Two simulation cases were investigated for valve inlet conditions: constant
inlet pressure of 10,150 psi, and constant inlet mass flow rate of 0.0661 lb/s.

Results from this study show that both constant inlet pressure and mass flow rate cases have similar
fluid flow and noise characteristics. Though the constant inlet pressure case has higher flow
velocities and turbulent intensities than constant inlet mass flow rate case, the velocity decreases in
both cases with decreasing valve opening. This is a result of the decreasing pressure difference
across the valve opening as the tank is gradually filled. In both cases, the flow is subsonic, and the
turbulence is the main factor contributing to the noise [3]. The dipole noise dominance observed by
Ariyadi et al. [3] agrees with Baumann and Coney [1]. For the constant inlet pressure case, a
maximum SPL of 122 dB at 12.5 kHz is observed for the 0.0197 inch opening. A maximum SPL of
110–120 dB is observed at medium to high frequencies of 5,000 to 20,000 Hz for a 0.0118 inch
opening, and for a 0.004 inch opening, SPL of 85–95 dB is observed at similar medium to high
frequencies. Like with the constant inlet pressure case, SPL ranges between 85–110 dB for the
constant inlet mass flow rate case. In all cases, the noise is highest in the direction facing the release
or valve outlet, as also observed in work by Maeda and Tamura [2].

3. HYDROGEN NOISE PREDICTION
The noise modeling work in this report involved a dual approach: first validating the valve and pipe
noise models with available experimental and computational work from the literature, and then
applying these same modeling equations to predict TPRD noise for typical hydrogen release
conditions in rail maintenance facilities. Specifically, the empirical models discussed in Section 2.1
were first used to predict and validate the pipe and valve noise results from the experimental and
computational studies described in Section 2.2. Then, the empirical models from Section 2.1.2 were
applied to conduct a parametric study of noise prediction for hydrogen flowing through a TPRD to
investigate conditions similar to a hydrogen release within a maintenance facility.

3.1. Validation of Hydrogen Release Noise Through a Pipe Using Empirical
 Equations
The SPL noise-prediction equations described in Section 2.1.1 were applied to the conditions
reported by Maeda and Tamura [2] to determine how closely the empirical model predicts subsonic
noise levels observed experimentally. The hydrogen pipe noise conditions reported by Maeda and
Tamura [2] were modeled using their chosen hydrogen leak rate of 4.17 ft3/min. The authors chose
this leak rate since it is the maximum allowable hydrogen volumetric leak flow rate in a collision as
specified in the Global Technical Regulation (GTR) [2], for both 0.25 inch (pipe bore 0.18 in) and
0.375 inch (pipe bore 0.27 inch) pipes. Frequencies of 20 to 40,000 Hz were chosen for the octave-
band center frequency fc. The ring frequency fr of the pipe was assumed to be that of steel fr=1608/D
[1]. The hydrogen flow velocity is expressed as 𝑈 = 𝑚̇/(𝜌 ∙ 𝐴) where 𝑚̇ and 𝜌 are the hydrogen
leak rate and density respectively, and 𝐴 is the pipe cross-sectional area.

The total loss factor (K) used in calculations were 0.1, 0.01, 0.005, 0.001, and 0.0002 because taking
the straight pipe’s loss factor of 0.12 velocity heads per 10-diameter segments [1] results in SPL
values greater than 200 dB, which are outside of the range of sound levels observed in experimental
studies and is non-physical [2]. The loss factor K was chosen to be varied out of convenience, to
allow the Baumann equations to better match available empirical results. If this term or some other
were not adjusted, the resulting SPL values would have exceeded 200 dB. In actuality, this
adjustment is further evidence that the Baumann empirical equations are best suited for 0.3 Ma and
lower sub-sonic flows and not supersonic expanded jets. This supports the idea that these equations
should be used cautiously in situations of modeling low density, high Mach number gaseous releases.

Figure 1 shows pipe SPL noise vs. frequency calculated using empirical equations from Section 2.1.1
compared to data from the Maeda and Tamura study [2] in the direction facing the release.
Assuming the same conditions as in the work by Maeda and Tamura, the empirical equations
expectedly predict that noise associated with the 0.25 inch pipe (0.180 inch pipe interior diameter
and leak size) is louder than for the 0.375 inch pipe (0.277 inch pipe interior diameter and leak size
because the 0.25 inch pipe has a smaller leak diameter and higher gas velocity than the 0.375 inch
pipe. This agrees with the reported experimental results from Maeda and Tamura. A parametric
study of the total loss factor K shows that the pipe sound pressure level increases with increasing
loss factor (SPL∝log 𝐾), and the SPL increases by about 22 dB between 20 and 40,000 Hz
depending on the value of K, highlighting the necessity of applying the correct pipe loss factor for
accurate noise prediction. Empirically-predicted pipe noise follows a logarithmic trend similar to
experimental data for crimped pipes. However, for non-crimped pipes, experimental data shows that
SPL increases linearly as a function of frequency up to 4,000 Hz for 0.25” pipes and up to 3,000 Hz

 21

for 0.375” pipes and then declines. This suggests that data derived from empirical models best
mimic the pipe SPL vs. frequency trend for crimped pipes, rather than non-crimped pipes.

Figure 1. Pipe audible noise due to hydrogen release for (a) 0.25” pipe and (b) 0.375” pipe where K
is the pipe loss factor (dimensionless). Modeled noise based on SPL equations from Section 2.1.1

SPL predictions using the equations from Section 2.1.1 vary between 20–94 dB, depending on K
value, for frequencies of 500–40,000 Hz. This range is comparable to noise levels associated with
whispering (20–30 dB) up to an orchestra (85–95 dB) [2]. The empirical model shows the same
trend of SPL logarithmically increasing with respect to frequency that is seen experimentally by
Maeda and Tamura for crimped pipe noise within a specific range of K values, though the
empirically-predicted noise levels increase less dramatically with respect to frequency compared to
experimental data. Both the empirical model and experiments show the same general trend for
crimped pipes, suggesting that higher frequency noise is associated with higher decibel levels
compared to lower frequency noise.

For the human audible frequency range of 20–20,000 Hz, potentially damaging noise levels are
around 80–90 dB, equivalent to crowded streets or an orchestra [2, 18]. While these noise levels are
not necessarily hazardous if exposure is limited, they may adversely affect human hearing and raise
safety concerns depending on exposure, especially in a work environment such as a rail maintenance
facility.

Because correctly predicting pipe noise levels can help implement better safety standards and reduce
safety hazards, additional experimental studies would be beneficial toward developing a more
accurate model. Improvement of the empirical model can also be achieved by applying a more
accurate estimate of the loss factor K and introducing a correction or discrepancy that captures the
effect of noise directionality. Additionally, as mentioned in Section 2.1.1, the loudest noise from vent
stacks may not be from the pipe itself, but from the underexpanded jet noise at the end of the vent
stack due to supersonic gas flow release. This underexpanded jet noise is not estimated here due to
lack of validation studies. Additional experiments measuring noise of hydrogen flow through pipes
and the resulting underexpanded jet at the outlet of the vent stack would aid greatly in validating and
creating more accurate predictive models.

3.2. Validation of Hydrogen Release Noise Through a Solenoid Valve Using
 Empirical Equations
Hydrogen valve noise was estimated using the empirical equations from section 2.1.2, assuming
solenoid valve simulation parameters from Ariyadi et al. [3]. To investigate conditions similar to a
high-pressure release into the atmosphere, a constant inlet pressure was assumed with a 0.0197 inch
valve opening, 10,150 psi gauge inlet (upstream) pressure, 0 psi gauge outlet (downstream) pressure,
and inlet temperature of -40oC. A steady hydrogen release mass flow rate of 0.0194 lbs/s was
calculated using Hydrogen Plus Other Alternative Fuels Risk Assessment Models (HyRAM+ v5.1)
[19] under these temperature and pressure conditions assuming a discharge coefficient of 1, ratio of
specific heats of 1.405, sonic speed of 761 mph, and density of 0.00518 lb/ft3. Due to a lack of
availability of the solenoid valve’s pressure recovery coefficient FL, a parametric study was
conducted using recovery coefficients ranging from 0.56 to 0.95 associated with the seven valve
types provided by Baumann and Coney [1].

Figure 2 shows the empirical model-predicted solenoid valve noise as a function of pressure
recovery coefficient FL. The predicted sound power noise levels vary from 124 dB to 130 dB, which
is equivalent to SPL of 116 to 122 dB for the 0.0197 inch valve opening. Although the empirically-
predicted noise levels are slightly higher, these results agree well with the CFD predicted noise levels
at medium to high frequencies by Ariyadi et al. [3], in which a maximum noise level of 122 dB was
predicted at 12.5 kHz. Noise levels greater than 120 dB are similar to factory noise or jet engines,
which can be harmful [2].

There are important limitations of this comparison that should be considered. First, results derived
from the empirical model are compared to a computational prediction of solenoid valve noise and
not an experiment. Additionally, though an empirical model is used to predict solenoid valve noise,
noise from this type of valve may not be realistic of TPRD noise. Ideally, results derived from the
empirical model should be compared to actual TPRD valve noise data and/or other validated
computational studies to improve accuracy.

4. CONCLUSION
This work characterized and predicted audible noise due to releases of pressurized gaseous hydrogen
through valves and pipes, meant to simulate noise from a hydrogen tank’s TPRD and vent stack,
respectively. The purpose of this study was to provide estimates of audible noise for hydrogen tank
releases on rail cars within maintenance facility environments. Such predictions of noise levels may
aid in assessing potential noise hazards and determining necessary mitigative measures to protect
workers.

A literature survey of models and validation for predicting audible noise from gas flowing through
pipes, orifices, or valves was conducted to identify the most accurate equations to model pressurized
hydrogen release noise levels. A study by Maeda and Tamura [2] reported findings from
experimental releases of hydrogen through a pipe, while a study from Ariyadi et al. [3]
computationally modeled noise from hydrogen flow through a solenoid valve. These studies showed
that audible hydrogen leakage sound varies with direction, distance, and height, and is the loudest in
the direction facing the leak. These studies also showed that hydrogen leakage sound is potentially
harmful. For the reported hydrogen release conditions in these studies, valve noise has a higher
associated SPL compared to pipe noise.

Empirical models for noise prediction of gas flowing through a pipe (equations in Section 2.1.1) and
a valve (equations in Section 2.1.2) were identified. Noise levels calculated from these empirical
models were compared against data from studies by Maeda and Tamura [2] and Ariyadi et al. [3].
Assuming the same conditions as in the work by Maeda and Tamura, pipe noise empirical equations
from Section 2.1.1 predicted SPL noise levels of 20–94 dB, showing that the equations more
accurately mimic trends observed in the experimental study for crimped pipes compared to non-
crimped pipes, assuming a specific range of values for the pipe loss factor, K. The loss factor K is
varied to predict SPL capturing both the minimum and maximum reported values for the given
frequency range. The empirical equations predicted that pipe noise increases linearly as a function of
frequency, suggesting that higher frequency noise is louder (at a higher decibel level) than lower
frequency noise. Assuming the same conditions as in the work by Ariyadi et al. [3], valve noise
empirical equations from Section 2.1.2 predicted SPL noise levels ranging between 116 to 122 dB,
agreeing well with results reported in the computational study from Ariyadi et al. [3] for the high-
pressure release case.

After validating the results from the two available studies, the empirical equations were next applied
to predict the audible noise of a pressurized hydrogen gas release which would occur if a rail car tank
released its contents through a TPRD. The software package HyRAM+ was used to estimate the
mass flow for a variety of tank pressures and valve diameters. These values were used with the valve
noise equations from 2.1.2 to predict noise levels. The calculations show that predicted SPL noise
levels from TPRDs are potentially harmful, with a range from 134 to 150 dB. The highest predicted
noise levels are associated with higher tank pressures and larger valve diameters. However, these
values correspond to the noise emitted at the very beginning of a pressurized gaseous hydrogen
release, and as the gas is released and the tank pressure decreases, the noise levels should also
decrease and become less hazardous.

Further studies are needed to validate these results due to numerous assumptions used in the
empirical model and limited experimental validation. The pressurized gas flow speed assumed in this
study was associated with the initial release of hydrogen from a fully pressurized tank, which

 27

corresponds to supersonic flow of hydrogen gas. However, only subsonic flow studies were available
to validate predictive equations for pipe and valve noise for hydrogen, and the noise associated with
sonic flow may feature a different frequency range or decibel level. For example, the empirical valve
equations from Section 2.1.2 are valid up to 0.3 Ma and may not be appropriate for supersonic flow
such as the initial high pressure gaseous releases from a hydrogen tank. This highlights the need for
additional empirical data to determine if the equations from Sections 2.1.1 and 2.1.2 are appropriate.
Additionally, the loss factor, K, typically assumed to be 0.12 velocity heads per 10-diameter
segments for the pipe noise equations in Section 2.1.1, was varied and likely underestimated since
vent stacks usually have a high aspect ratio and associated head loss. The 0.12 velocity heads per 10-
diameter segment value of K was not assumed since the resulting SPL was over 200 dB, which is
non-physical, and did not match empirical results. A drawback of the Baumann equations is that
they do not estimate highly underexpanded jets accurately and are meant for subcritical flow.
Another issue with the model is that it does not limit or scale down SPL based on a gas’s limits of
converting kinetic energy into acoustic energy. Upon release, there is an upper limit of energy
conversion and this is not reflected in the empirical equations, likely because they are valid for
releases up to 0.3 Ma. The Baumann equations should be applied cautiously when modeling low
density, high Mach number gaseous releases and should be validated against empirical studies.
Further investigation into the most accurate empirical model should be conducted in subsequent
work.

Also, for TPRD vent stacks, the largest contribution to audible noise is likely not from the pipe
length, but from the jet noise at the end of the vent stack due to release of supersonic velocity gas.
This aspect of the noise contribution should be characterized empirically so that available jet flow
noise models can be identified, applied, and verified.

This work demonstrates that TPRD valve noise may be potentially harmful, especially for high-
velocity and high-pressure hydrogen releases, but there is significant uncertainty associated with
calculated noise levels due to a lack of studies on hydrogen leak noise and a lack of empirical models
appropriate for supersonic flows. Further computational and experimental investigations of
hydrogen leak audible noise are needed to develop robust prediction tools and address hydrogen
sonic hazards.
