FINAL SCIENTIFIC / TECHNICAL REPORT

DOE Award Number: DE-SC0020292
Project Title: Lithium–Divertor Interactions and Helium/Hydrogen Trapping in Lithiated Metals
Recipient: University of Missouri
Principal Investigator: Prof. Karl D. Hammond
Project Period: September 1, 2019 – August 31, 2025

Executive Summary

The goal of this project was to develop a fundamental understanding of helium and hydrogen
behavior in lithium, both in bulk and at interfaces with tungsten, in order to inform the design of
lithium-based plasma-facing components for fusion devices. Over the course of the award, the
project produced the first comprehensive, peer-reviewed dataset describing helium energetics,
migration behaviors, and defect interactions in lithium. The research demonstrated that helium
behaves in ways not previously observed in any other body-centered cubic (BCC) metal: its
interstitial configurations are more stable than substitutional ones, and its migration barriers are
extraordinarily low, in some cases more than an order of magnitude below those in tungsten or
iron. These discoveries reveal that helium in lithium diffuses so rapidly that its transport may be
dominated by translational motion rather than the vibrationally activated mechanisms that
underpin conventional solid-state diffusion. This work lays a scientific foundation for
understanding gas retention, bubble formation, and wall evolution in lithium-based fusion
environments and provides new computational tools, most notably a newly developed Li–He
interatomic potential, for advancing future modeling efforts.

Project Accomplishments and Alignment with Objectives

The first objective of the project was to generate a complete database of density functional
theory calculations describing helium in lithium. This task was fully accomplished, and the
results were published in Computational Materials Science. The dataset encompasses vacancy
formation and migration energies in pure lithium, the formation and migration energies of
interstitial helium, the binding behavior of helium atoms with lithium vacancies, and the
energetics of di-helium dumbbell configurations.

The second objective involved developing and validating a Li–He interatomic potential for
molecular dynamics simulations. This work required more iteration than anticipated because
several existing Li–Li interatomic potentials proved physically unreliable, producing negative
vacancy energies and unstable phases even at nominal temperatures. The research team
ultimately identified a suitable Li–Li basis potential and completed the development and tuning
of a Li–He potential consistent with the DFT dataset.

The third objective centered on molecular-scale simulations of helium and hydrogen clustering.
Extensive simulations of helium near tungsten grain boundaries were completed and
disseminated at scientific conferences. These results not only informed the lithium-focused
simulations, but also provided valuable benchmarks given tungsten’s relevance as a
plasma-facing material. Lithium–helium interface simulations began once the potential
development matured. These simulations required careful consideration of physical effects
unique to lithium, including its comparatively low melting point and the significance of electronic
stopping in He–Li collisions.

The fourth and fifth objectives involved preparing for and initiating long-timescale kinetic Monte
Carlo modeling of helium behavior near lithium–tungsten interfaces. Rate catalogs are being
constructed from the molecular dynamics simulations, with the overarching KMC framework
already in place. These efforts were delayed primarily because of early staffing challenges but
are now supported by the completed DFT dataset and the new Li–He potential.

Summary of Project Activities Over the Award Period

At the outset of the project, work focused on performing DFT calculations to characterize helium
defects in lithium thoroughly. The results revealed several unexpected features. Most notably,
helium’s preferred configuration in lithium is interstitial rather than substitutional, with tetrahedral
and octahedral interstitial sites exhibiting lower formation energies than vacancy sites. This
behavior is not known to occur in other BCC metals, where helium typically binds strongly to
vacancies. In addition, migration energy barriers were extraordinarily small. The
tetrahedral-to-tetrahedral hop was found to require only 0.003 eV, and the tetrahedral–
octahedral–tetrahedral pathway required only 0.013 eV. For comparison, the same jumps in
tungsten and iron require roughly 0.06 eV. These differences suggest that helium in lithium
moves so easily that vibrationally activated diffusion may play only a minor role; instead, its
behavior may be governed by near-barrier-free translational motion.

Following these insights, the research team developed a Li–He interatomic potential that could
faithfully reproduce the DFT energetics. This required switching from the originally planned Li–Li
potential to an alternative potential that exhibited stable behavior across relevant phases. Once
the Li–He potential reached adequate accuracy, the team began interfacial simulations involving
lithium, helium, and tungsten. Simulations of helium near tungsten grain boundaries, performed
in parallel with another DOE-supported project, produced well-developed bubble structures and
provided essential reference conditions for validating helium–lithium interface behavior.

During the latter stages of the project, the group initiated production-scale simulations on DOE
high-performance computing systems, primarily NERSC’s Perlmutter architecture. These
simulations focused on helium implantation into lithium under physically plausible conditions,
with particular attention to avoiding regimes where lithium would melt or sublime. The project’s
undergraduate researchers contributed to early parametrization efforts intended to support the
kinetic Monte Carlo simulations.

While early delays in recruiting graduate personnel slowed progress, these challenges did not
compromise the scientific integrity of the work. By the conclusion of the project, all core scientific
objectives had been met: the DFT database was complete and published, the Li–He potential
was developed, major molecular dynamics simulations had been carried out, and the
foundations of the KMC framework were established.

Products Developed Under the Award

The project produced several scientific outputs. The complete DFT study of helium impurities in
lithium was published in Computational Materials Science (Volume 204, 111176, 2022),
providing the community with the first systematic quantification of helium energetics in this
metal. Additional work on bubble dynamics in tungsten, based on research by undergraduate
student Brandon Lee, was published in the Journal of Nuclear Materials. Conference papers
were presented at AIChE Annual Meetings in 2021 and 2022, and at the APS Division of Plasma
Physics Meeting, covering helium transport and clustering near tungsten grain boundaries.

Other products include the newly created Li–He interatomic potential, which will be submitted for
publication, and contributions by the principal investigator to the development of the LAMMPS
molecular dynamics package, specifically modernizing the Fortran interface used for
library-based workflows. No intellectual property, technologies, or patent applications were
generated through this award.

Computer Modeling Approach and Verification

The computational modeling developed during the project included both first-principles and
atomistic methods. DFT models were constructed using standard electronic-structure
formalisms to evaluate formation energies, defect configurations, and migration barriers.
Molecular dynamics models relied on the LAMMPS platform and the newly built Li–He pair
potential. Model performance was assessed by comparing MD results with DFT benchmarks
wherever possible, particularly for migration energies and defect binding behavior.

The kinetic Monte Carlo approach currently under construction draws its rate constants from
nudged elastic band calculations and temperature-dependent MD simulations. These models
are intended for future studies of long-timescale helium evolution in lithium, though no KMC
results were completed before the end of the award. Hardware requirements included both local
computational clusters and DOE high-performance supercomputing facilities

Conclusions

Throughout the award period, this project substantially advanced the scientific understanding of
helium behavior in lithium and developed new modeling tools that the fusion materials
community previously lacked. The project produced several key findings that alter how helium
transport in low-Z materials should be conceptualized, particularly the uniquely low migration
barriers and unusual defect energetics. These results have direct implications for divertor
design, lithium-based plasma conditioning strategies, and the interpretation of helium retention
behavior in tokamak environments.
