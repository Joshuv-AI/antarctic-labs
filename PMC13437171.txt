Recovery phase after dental intravenous sedation: airway vigilance should continue until discharge
Authors: Toru Yamamoto, Hiroko Kanemaru, Shigenobu Kurata, Yutaka Tanaka, Naotaka Kishimoto
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Intravenous sedation is widely used in dentistry to facilitate treatment in patients with dental anxiety, special healthcare needs, a pronounced gag reflex, or limited cooperation. Discussions of sedation safety typically focus on drug selection, intraoperative respiratory depression, and monitoring during the procedure. Although these issues are important, the recovery phase also warrants careful attention. This phase represents a clinically vulnerable period during which airway obstruction, secretion-related events, laryngospasm, and hypoxemia may still occur, particularly in dental settings where the upper airway is shared with the procedural field. Furthermore, the recovery period may receive less attention as clinical focus shifts to documentation, communication, cleaning, or preparation for discharge.

A recent Japanese case report highlights an important consideration for clinicians involved in dental sedation. Matsumura et al. described a 29-year-old man with intellectual disability who developed negative pressure pulmonary edema (NPPE) following intravenous sedation for dental treatment [1]. The intraoperative course was uneventful; however, shortly after the procedure, the patient developed persistent coughing and copious oral secretions. Despite suctioning, oxygen saturation rapidly declined to 75%, accompanied by stridor and labored breathing. Positive-pressure ventilation and continuous positive airway pressure were required, and chest radiography showed bilateral perihilar opacities consistent with pulmonary edema [1]. The educational value of this report lies not in retrospective scrutiny of an individual case, but in prompting shared self-reflection. It reminds us that even an apparently uneventful procedure can be followed by a serious airway event during recovery.

Recovery-phase airway events differ according to anesthetic technique and clinical setting. After general anesthesia in medical operating rooms, complications are often related to extubation, laryngeal mask removal, residual neuromuscular blockade, glossoptosis, or events in the post-anesthesia care unit. Management of these events may include advanced airway intervention, positive pressure ventilation, or reintubation when necessary [2]. In contrast, during dental intravenous sedation, the airway is usually unprotected and remains within the operative field. Saliva, irrigation fluid, blood, pharyngeal stimulation, residual sedation, and limited cooperation may therefore contribute to airway obstruction, secretion-related hypoxemia, or laryngospasm during recovery. Although NPPE is uncommon in intravenous sedation, reports from dental and other non-intubated procedural sedation settings, including upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, suggest that upper airway obstruction during recovery can, albeit rarely, result in this serious complication [1,3].

These differences have practical implications. In medical post-anesthesia care units, recovery is typically supervised in a dedicated area equipped with personnel and resources for advanced airway management. In many dental settings, however, recovery may occur near the treatment area, while the same team simultaneously manages documentation, room turnover, caregiver communication, and discharge preparation. Dental sedation standards emphasize that recovery and discharge remain integral components of sedation care and require trained personnel, appropriate equipment, and preparedness for complications [4]. Accordingly, the interval between treatment completion and discharge should be regarded as a distinct phase of patient safety rather than simply a waiting period.

Several practical points should be emphasized. First, intravenous access should not be removed immediately when the patient remains sedated or has not clearly recovered protective airway reflexes. If laryngospasm, agitation, or airway obstruction occurs, rapid administration of rescue medication may be required. Second, suction, oxygen delivery, positive pressure ventilation equipment, and airway adjuncts should remain immediately available until respiratory stability is established. Third, discharge decisions should be based not only on consciousness and oxygen saturation, but also on respiratory pattern, secretion control, airway patency, and the patient’s ability to maintain protective responses. These considerations are particularly relevant for patients with disability, children, older adults, and those undergoing procedures associated with blood or irrigation fluid in the oral cavity.

Continuous visual observation and clinical assessment remain the foundation of safe recovery. Nevertheless, adjunctive respiratory monitoring tools may help maintain awareness during vulnerable moments in real-world workflow. A previous report described the use of a wireless digital auscultation device for respiratory monitoring during intravenous sedation [5]. Such devices can transmit respiratory sounds in real time and may help clinicians recognize changes in breathing when attention is briefly compromised. This approach should be considered only as an additional layer of safety, not as a substitute for direct observation, clinical judgment, or emergency readiness.

The essential requirement is a shared safety culture in which the dental team recognizes recovery as an active phase of sedation care. The team should clearly define who is responsible for patient monitoring, the parameters to be observed, when intravenous access can be removed, and the criteria that must be met before discharge. Dental sedation safety does not end with completion of the procedure. Airway vigilance, emergency readiness, and respiratory monitoring should continue until recovery is clearly established. In dental intravenous sedation, when the handpiece stops, vigilance must continue.

Recovery-phase airway vigilance after dental intravenous sedation. The recovery phase after dental intravenous sedation should be considered as an active safety phase rather than a passive waiting period. After treatment completion, attention may become fragmented because of documentation, staff communication, cleaning, or preparation for discharge. During this phase, residual sedation, secretion retention, airway obstruction, laryngospasm, hypoxemia, and NPPE may occur. Airway vigilance, emergency readiness, and respiratory monitoring should therefore continue until recovery is clearly established. Abbreviations: NPPE, negative pressure pulmonary edema.
