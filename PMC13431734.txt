Interpreting Gastrointestinal Recovery After Tegileridine-Based PCIA in Elderly Colorectal Surgery Patients [Letter]
Authors: Chenjie Qiu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the randomized trial by He et al comparing tegileridine-based with sufentanil-based patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) for gastrointestinal recovery in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery.1 The authors should be commended for addressing a clinically relevant question within an enhanced recovery framework, particularly because opioid-related gastrointestinal dysfunction remains a major barrier to recovery after colorectal surgery.2–4 Nevertheless, several methodological and interpretative issues deserve careful consideration before tegileridine-based PCIA is adopted as a safer or gastrointestinal-protective strategy in this population.

First, the clinical meaning of the primary endpoint is uncertain. The adjusted between-group difference in I-FEED score on postoperative day 3 was statistically significant but modest, at −0.72 points. The I-FEED score has preliminary validity evidence in colorectal surgery,5 but a generally accepted minimal clinically important difference has not been established. Moreover, the reported difference in time to first flatus was only 4 hours, and time to first defecation did not differ significantly. These findings suggest that the observed effect may represent earlier symptom improvement rather than a clearly meaningful acceleration of bowel recovery. Component-level I-FEED data would be particularly useful, because nausea and emesis are components of I-FEED and were also counted as postoperative adverse events. Without showing whether intake, abdominal examination, and duration of symptoms improved independently of nausea/vomiting, it is difficult to separate true gastrointestinal motility recovery from a reduction in opioid-related PONV.

Second, the choice of the per-protocol set as the primary analysis may have introduced selection bias. Fourteen randomized patients were excluded, including patients with conversion to open surgery, operative time exceeding 4 hours, postoperative ICU transfer, and early discontinuation of the analgesia pump. These are not random inconveniences; they are clinically related to surgical stress, opioid exposure, complications, and delayed gastrointestinal recovery. CONSORT principles favor preserving randomization through intention-to-treat analyses wherever possible.6 A full treatment-policy estimand, multiple-imputation analysis, or worst-case sensitivity analysis would better clarify whether the results are robust in the population clinicians actually treat.

Third, the comparison was not equianalgesic. The authors transparently acknowledge that no validated conversion ratio exists between tegileridine and sufentanil. However, this limitation is central to causal interpretation. Similar NRS scores and PCIA press counts do not prove comparable opioid exposure, especially when background infusions, early discontinuation, rescue antiemetics, and drug-specific pharmacokinetics may differ. Reporting total delivered study drug, PCIA duration, unsuccessful demands, sedation scores, antiemetic rescue timing, and ideally exposure-response analyses would help distinguish a pharmacologic gastrointestinal advantage from a lower effective opioid burden. This is important because postoperative opioid dose itself has been associated with prolonged ileus after laparoscopic colorectal surgery.4

Fourth, several secondary findings require a more conservative interpretation. The study was powered for POD3 I-FEED, not for POGD incidence, QoR-15, patient satisfaction, PONV, dizziness, or respiratory depression. Apart from prespecified repeated I-FEED comparisons, many secondary outcomes appear to rely on nominal P values. In addition, respiratory depression was rare, and adverse events were recorded only within 48 hours. Therefore, the absence of a respiratory safety signal should not be read as evidence of respiratory safety equivalence or superiority.

Finally, the mechanistic framing should remain cautious. Tegileridine is a G protein-biased mu-opioid receptor agonist approved in China for postoperative pain, and recent Phase II/III and Phase III trials support its analgesic efficacy compared with placebo and morphine.7–9 However, the clinical relationship between biased agonism, intrinsic efficacy, respiratory effects, nausea, and gastrointestinal outcomes remain debated.10 The present trial is clinically hypothesis-generating, but it cannot by itself attribute improved gastrointestinal scores to beta-arrestin sparing rather than differences in dose intensity, nausea control, or other perioperative factors.

In summary, He et al provide promising preliminary evidence that tegileridine-based PCIA may improve early postoperative comfort after laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. We suggest that the conclusion be framed more cautiously: tegileridine may be associated with better early I-FEED scores and less PONV under the tested regimen, but larger multicenter, prospectively registered, intention-to-treat, dose-ranging trials with component-level gastrointestinal outcomes, clinically meaningful endpoints, and longer safety follow-up are needed before firm claims about gastrointestinal recovery or safety can be made.
