How to stop Canada’s emergency physicians from leaving their profession
Authors: Catherine Varner
Published: 2026

In this issue, research by de Wit and colleagues paints a grim picture of emergency department staffing in Canada.1 By 2025, 10% of the emergency physician respondents who had been surveyed 5 years earlier no longer practised emergency medicine, and an additional 48% had reduced their hours. Physicians also reported high levels of burnout and depersonalization that do not appear to have resolved following the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings accentuate those of a recent Canadian Institute for Health Information report showing year-over-year worsening of emergency department metrics, whereby admitted patients’ emergency department length of stay increased again in 2025, and 7.7% of patients left without being seen by a physician.2

In the most recent iteration of the researchers’ Canadian Longitudinal Emergency Physician Well-Being Survey, emergency physicians described helplessness and resignation while working in overcrowded emergency departments. This research finds that respondents are unable to provide timely or safe care to patients in waiting rooms and hallways, unable to change the system in which they are working, and consequently are deciding to leave emergency medicine or decrease their shifts.1 Their collective distress and quiet attrition signals a serious threat: Canada’s 50-year investment in developing a highly effective fleet of physicians with specialized or additional training in emergency medicine, who provide 24-hour coverage to most emergency departments across the country, could be squandered.

Attrition will continue until emergency department overcrowding is mitigated. Given the multifaceted problem that this overcrowding poses, solving it will require substantial investment in acute care and long-term care capacity, as discussed previously in CMAJ.3,4 As Canada’s population ages — along with rising health complexity, climate-related disasters, and threats of international conflict — all levels of government should urgently prioritize preventing attrition in emergency medicine. However, the profession cannot wait for complex systems-level solutions to be realized; immediate actions must be taken to protect emergency department personnel now.

Emergency physicians, nurses, and other front-line providers are frequently blamed directly when adverse outcomes occur in emergency departments. Moreover, they are often publicly named as part of provincial inquiries into patient deaths in emergency department waiting rooms, and recommendations that follow are provider directed rather than system focused.5,6 Recent examples of “trial by social media” when tragic outcomes occur in emergency department settings underscore this shift of blame from system to individual. The deaths of Prashant Sreekumar (a 44-year-old who died after waiting more than 8 hours while experiencing chest pain in Edmonton) and Adam Burgoyne (a 39-year-old who died of an aortic dissection after leaving a Montréal hospital without being seen by a physician) resulted in extensive social media discussions about health care providers and criticism of their clinical decision-making. This needs to stop.

Consider the stark contrast with the communications on, and inquiry into, the tragic collision of an Air Canada flight with a fire truck on the runway at LaGuardia Airport on Mar. 22, 2026. The plane’s captain and first officer were killed, and 6 others were injured. Yet, air traffic controllers were not publicly named, and the media were swift to acknowledge the many system-level events that precipitated this tragic occurrence.7,8 Similarly, the investigation of tragic events related to emergency department overcrowding should seek whole-of-system accountability and follow protocols similar to those of aviation tragedies, including not publicly identifying health care providers. Health care system leaders must not use individuals as convenient human shields to avoid taking accountability for a complex system problem.

Provincial and federal leaders also need to acknowledge the root cause of the emergency department crisis. Emergency department overcrowding is often blamed on patients seeking care for low-acuity visits or on poor patient access to primary care. However, the main cause of emergency department overcrowding in Canada is hospitals running at or over capacity most of the time, resulting in admitted patients boarding in the emergency department.9,10 Persistent misinformation about drivers of overcrowding leads to governments and hospital systems focusing on and funding mechanisms to divert low-acuity visits, such as expanding the scope of practice for pharmacists, investing in virtual urgent care programs, and encouraging telephone medical advisory services. Although these programs are important for solving other problems in the health care system, they have clearly not ameliorated emergency department overcrowding, as emergency department lengths of stay have increased and associated adverse outcomes are not being prevented.2 Because an ever-higher proportion of emergency department patients, with increasing medical complexity, require admission to hospital, emergency departments and hospitals are likely to remain in crisis now and for the foreseeable future until solutions target the root cause.

Of people visiting a Canadian emergency department last year, 1 in 13 left without being seen by a provider, and patients are frequently being assessed in waiting room chairs.2 This is not because these patients are “low acuity” and didn’t need to be there, as research has repeatedly shown high rates of adverse outcomes and increased risk of death among patients who leave without being seen.10 High rates of leaving the emergency department without being seen constitute a failure of provinces to comply with the principles of the Canada Health Act, whereby insured persons must have reasonable access to medically necessary care.11 It is now time for the federal government to take more deliberate action, as federal and provincial governments negotiate bilateral agreements for Canada Health Transfers, to hold provinces accountable for emergency department wait times and associated inadequate access to acute care. Unconditional Canada Health Transfers and one-time top-ups have resulted in more health care spending by provinces, but not necessarily in the areas of greatest need to solve the emergency department overcrowding problem, as access to emergency care has not increased.12 Submission of emergency department data to the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System is not mandated in Prince Edward Island, Nova Scotia, Quebec, Manitoba, or Saskatchewan.13 The federal government can use levers requiring all provinces to report their emergency department data and demand accountability for emergency department wait times by applying conditional requirements to Canada Health Transfers.

For several decades, people living in Canada have had reasonable access to physicians trained in emergency medicine when seeking care in an emergency department, but this access is decreasing.9 Highly skilled emergency physicians and nurses are burned out and are quietly leaving the profession. With the loss of experience and unfilled shifts, their colleagues who remain will increasingly struggle to provide safe and timely care. Without thoughtful and intentional action by provincial and national leaders, access to 24-hour emergency care in Canada is at risk, and preventable tragedies will continue to occur in our waiting rooms.
