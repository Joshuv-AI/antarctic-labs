Algebraic Equivalence and Operational Source Normalization in Rastall-Type Ricci–Trace Gravity
José A. C. Nogales, Karen-Luz Burgoa Rosso, Marcelo H Alvarenga

Abstract
The central point of this work is not that the ϵ \epsilon - and λ \lambda -representatives fail to be algebraically equivalent. On the contrary, they are exactly equivalent if the source coupling is transformed together with the trace parameter. The question addressed here is whether this algebraic equivalence is automatically an operational equivalence once the same T μ ​ ν T_{\mu\nu} is identified with the same operationally normalized matter source in both descriptions. We adopt a fixed-source-normalization convention: the tensor T μ ​ ν T_{\mu\nu} is not merely a formal source whose normalization may be freely rescaled, but the stress tensor assigned to matter by the operational prescription, and the coefficient multiplying it is held fixed as part of that prescription. Under this convention, the algebraic map between the two parametrizations is no longer a passive change of coordinates in theory space, because it requires a nontrivial rescaling of the source normalization. We show that compatibility between the algebraic map and fixed source normalization is possible only at the Einstein point. We also explain why this non-equivalence disappears if κ ϵ \kappa_{\epsilon} and κ λ \kappa_{\lambda} are instead interpreted as bare couplings to be separately calibrated from the same observed Newton constant. Thus the result is not a denial of the formal Rastall–Einstein rewriting, but a statement about the extra physical convention needed to turn algebraic equivalence into operational equivalence.

Introduction

General Relativity (GR) is highly constrained by the simultaneous presence of a geometric identity,

\nabla^{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}=0,

(1)

and the standard interpretation of the source as a covariantly conserved stress-energy tensor
51
;
12
;
33
. These two facts are not merely formal. They determine how matter is coupled to geometry, how the weak-field limit is normalized, and how the measured Newton constant is identified with the coupling appearing in the field equations. Any modification of Einstein’s equations that preserves a second-order metric structure must therefore specify both the modified geometric operator and the physical meaning of the tensor placed on the right-hand side.

This issue is especially delicate for field equations that linearly reweight the Ricci tensor and the scalar-curvature trace sector. In such models the Bianchi identity does not allow an arbitrary matter balance law: once the coefficient of
g_{\mu\nu}R
differs from its Einstein value, the divergence of the field equations fixes a curvature-induced exchange between geometry and the tensor denoted by
T_{\mu\nu}
. This is the basic setting of Rastall-type gravity, originally proposed as a non-conservative modification of GR
41
;
42
, and later revisited in several contexts involving modified conservation laws, effective fluids, cosmology, black holes, and matter–curvature couplings
17
;
49
;
21
;

The class considered in this work is deliberately minimal. We focus on linear Ricci–trace deformations of the form

R_{\mu\nu}-a\,g_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu},

(2)

where
a

\kappa_{b}
is a bare gravitational coupling. The Einstein equation corresponds to
a=1/2

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\epsilon}T_{\mu\nu},

(3)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1-\lambda}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\lambda}T_{\mu\nu}.

(4)

The first parametrization rescales the Ricci sector directly, while the second keeps the coefficient of
R_{\mu\nu}
fixed and modifies the trace sector. The second representative is the usual Rastall-type form after a notational identification of parameters. In one common convention, Rastall’s equation is written as

R_{\mu\nu}+\left(\kappa_{\lambda}\lambda_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\right)g_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{\lambda}T_{\mu\nu},

(5)

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{\lambda}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}T\right),\qquad\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\nabla_{\nu}T.

(6)

The status of Rastall gravity has been debated for decades. Early criticism of non-conservative theories emphasized that a modified conservation law must be reconciled with matter dynamics and with the operational interpretation of the stress tensor
28
;
44
;
45
. More recently, Visser argued that generic Rastall gravity is equivalent to Einstein gravity because the apparent non-conservation can be absorbed into a redefinition of the source tensor
50

15
;
21
;
48

This conclusion is mathematically sound, but it does not by itself settle the physical interpretation of the model. The statement that two field equations are related by algebraic rearrangement is not the same as the statement that they are operationally indistinguishable. In particular, one must distinguish at least four levels of equivalence:

(i)

equality after algebraic rearrangement of the field equations;

(ii)

equality after redefining the source tensor;

(iii)

equality after redefining the parameters of the theory;

(iv)

equality after fixing the matter interpretation and the operational normalization of the source.

The first three are mathematical equivalences. The fourth is an operational equivalence. The central claim of this paper is that these mathematical equivalences do not automatically imply operational equivalence.

This distinction matters because the Newtonian limit calibrates the gravitational coupling experimentally. In ordinary GR, the constant
\kappa=8\pi G_{N}/c^{4}
is fixed by matching the weak-field
00
-component of the field equations to the Poisson equation. For a Ricci–trace deformation, however, the effective Newtonian source contains a trace contribution. Consequently, the bare gravitational coupling
\kappa_{b}
in Eq. (
2
) is not generally identical to the measured Newton constant unless both a matter interpretation and a calibration prescription are specified. A parameter transformation that is harmless algebraically may therefore fail to be a passive reparametrization once the same matter tensor and the same operational source normalization are held fixed. The point is not that a free Newtonian recalibration is impossible; rather, a free recalibration already changes the normalization of the source and therefore changes what is being kept fixed operationally.

The purpose of the present work is structural. We do not introduce a new propagating gravitational degree of freedom, nor do we claim that the class (
2
) by itself constitutes a complete fundamental theory. Instead, we classify linear Ricci–trace deformations, clarify their relation to Rastall gravity, and isolate the conditions under which algebraic equivalence fails to be a passive operational equivalence under fixed source normalization. This separation is useful because discussions of Rastall-type gravity often move between source redefinition, parameter redefinition, matter interpretation, and physical calibration. We keep these notions distinct throughout.

2
Variational status and the obstruction to a minimal metric action

Before classifying the Ricci–trace deformations algebraically, it is important to state what is, and what is not, being assumed at the variational level. The field equations studied in this work are written directly as second-order metric equations. We do not assume, at this stage, that they necessarily follow from an unconstrained Einstein–Hilbert-type action with minimally coupled matter. In fact, such an assumption is generically too strong. This point is consistent with the standard Noether identities of diffeomorphism-invariant metric theories and with the general rigidity of second-order metric dynamics in four dimensions
51
;
12
;
31
;
32
;
35
.

Consider a standard metric variational principle of the form

S[g,\psi]=S_{g}[g]+S_{m}[g,\psi],

(8)

where
g_{\mu\nu}
is the only gravitational field,
\psi
denotes collectively the matter fields, and the matter stress tensor is defined by

T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}:=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S_{m}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}.

(9)

If the total action is diffeomorphism invariant and the matter equations of motion hold, the Noether identity associated with diffeomorphism invariance implies

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}=0.

(10)

This statement is independent of the particular coordinates used and expresses the standard covariant balance law for minimally coupled laboratory matter
51
;
12
.

By contrast, the Ricci–trace class considered in this paper,

R_{\mu\nu}-ag_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu},

(11)

implies, by taking the divergence and using

\nabla^{\mu}R_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\nu}R
,

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{\kappa_{b}}\nabla_{\nu}R.

(12)

Thus, unless
a=1/2
or
\nabla_{\nu}R=0
in a special sector, the tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
appearing in Eq. (
11
) cannot be simultaneously identified with the standard minimally coupled laboratory stress tensor
T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}
of Eq. (
9
).

This gives a minimal metric-action obstruction. Let
S[g,\psi]=S_{g}[g]+S_{m}[g,\psi]
be an unconstrained diffeomorphism-invariant metric action with minimally coupled matter, and let
T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}
be defined by Eq. (
9
). If the metric field equations are of the linear Ricci–trace form

R_{\mu\nu}-ag_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu},

(13)

then consistency with the Noether identity requires

\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)\nabla_{\nu}R=0.

(14)

Therefore, for generic configurations with
\nabla_{\nu}R\neq 0
, the only regular member compatible with a standard minimally coupled matter interpretation is the Einstein value
a=1/2
.

This conclusion follows directly by taking the divergence of Eq. (
13
), which gives

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{\kappa_{b}}\nabla_{\nu}R.

(15)

However, diffeomorphism invariance of the minimally coupled matter action implies

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}=0
on the matter equations of motion. Combining both relations yields Eq. (
14
). For generic configurations,
\nabla_{\nu}R\neq 0
, hence
a=1/2
.

Equations (
13
)–(
14
) imply that any attempt to treat
T_{\mu\nu}
in Eq. (
11
) as the standard minimally coupled laboratory stress tensor forces the theory to Einstein gravity (
a=1/2
) unless one abandons either diffeomorphism invariance or minimal coupling. Consequently, in the Ricci-trace class with
a\neq 1/2
, the tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
cannot be interpreted as ordinary matter without additional structure. This conclusion underpins the calibration analysis of Sec.
6
.

This obstruction should not be read as showing that no variational formulation of any Rastall-type model is possible. Rather, it identifies the precise assumption that must be abandoned. A variational completion must do at least one of the following: redefine the source tensor, introduce nonminimal matter–curvature couplings, impose a constraint through auxiliary fields or Lagrange multipliers, or restrict the metric variations as in unimodular gravity. These possibilities are logically distinct. Early variational attempts, modern source-redefinition analyses, and recent embeddings into broader matter–curvature frameworks should therefore be read as different implementations of this choice rather than as one unique action principle
45
;
17
;
34
;
21
;
43
.

A first possibility is the conservative Einstein rewriting. For
a\neq 1/4
, Eq. (
11
) can be written as

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu},\qquad T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)g_{\mu\nu}T,

(16)

where

\alpha(a)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{1-4a}.

(17)

The Bianchi identity then implies

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=0.

(18)

In this interpretation, a standard Einstein–Hilbert action may be written for the conserved tensor
T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}
, while the nonconserved tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
is a derived algebraic object. This is the sense in which generic Rastall-type equations may be regarded as Einstein gravity with a redefined source tensor
50
;
15
;
48
.

A second possibility is to introduce an explicitly nonminimal coupling between matter and curvature, as in
f(R,T)
,
f(R,{\mathcal{L}}_{m})
, or related curvature–matter coupled models. Such theories possess action principles, but their field equations generally contain additional terms depending on the matter Lagrangian and on the metric variation of
T
or
{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
. Therefore they are not automatically identical to the linear Ricci–trace class studied here. They may reproduce Rastall-like balance equations only under additional restrictions or in special sectors
8
;
23
;
24
;
21
;
43
. More broadly, these models belong to the larger landscape of modified metric gravity and matter–geometry couplings
10
;
46
;
16
;
36
;
37
;
13
.

A third possibility is to use constrained actions with auxiliary fields. Schematically, one may consider

S[g,\psi,\chi]=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{b}}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,R+S_{m}[g,\psi]+S_{\rm c}[g,\psi,\chi],

(19)

where
\chi
denotes auxiliary variables and
S_{\rm c}
enforces a relation between curvature and matter trace. Such a construction can produce effective nonconservation in a reduced description, but its physical content depends on the auxiliary sector and on which stress tensor is identified with laboratory matter.

Finally, one may restrict the metric variation itself, as in Unimodular Gravity. This route produces trace-free Einstein equations and, when the usual conservation of the energy-momentum tensor is imposed as an additional condition, an integration-constant cosmological term is obtained, but it is not equivalent to an algebraic Ricci–trace deformation of the unrestricted metric equations
47
;
25
;
18
;
2
;
38
;
3
. For this reason, unimodular gravity is treated separately in Sec.
10
.

The conclusion of this section is therefore conservative. The Ricci–trace equations studied below should be understood as an effective field-equation class unless a nonminimal, constrained, or source-redefined variational completion is specified. This does not weaken the classification; rather, it clarifies its domain of validity and prevents the algebraic equivalence results from being confused with the existence of a unique fundamental action.

3
General linear Ricci–trace deformations

We consider the class of metric field equations

R_{\mu\nu}-a\,g_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu},

(20)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
is constant and
\kappa_{b}
is a bare gravitational coupling. The Einstein equation corresponds to
a=1/2
. We assume that the metric is the only gravitational field and that the field equations are second order in the metric. This restriction is consistent with the spirit of Lovelock-type rigidity results in four dimensions, although here we work directly at the level of field equations rather than deriving Eq. (
20
) from a local action
31
;
32
;
35
;
51
.

The two families of interest are

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\epsilon}T_{\mu\nu},

(21)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1-\lambda}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\lambda}T_{\mu\nu}.

(22)

For
\epsilon\neq 1
, Eq. (
21
) may be divided by
1-\epsilon
, yielding

R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)}g_{\mu\nu}R=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}T_{\mu\nu}.

(23)

Thus its canonical parameters are

a_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)},\qquad\kappa_{b}^{(\epsilon)}=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}.

(24)

The
\lambda
-family is already in the form (
20
), with

a_{\lambda}=\frac{1-\lambda}{2},\qquad\kappa_{b}^{(\lambda)}=\kappa_{\lambda}.

(25)

Taking the trace of Eq. (
20
) gives

(1-4a)R=\kappa_{b}T.

(26)

For
a\neq 1/4
,

R=\frac{\kappa_{b}}{1-4a}T,

(27)

and Eq. (
20
) becomes

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}\left(T_{\mu\nu}+\frac{a-\frac{1}{2}}{1-4a}g_{\mu\nu}T\right).

(28)

It is useful to write this as

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)g_{\mu\nu}T\right),

(29)

where

\alpha(a):=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{1-4a}.

(30)

For every regular member
a\neq 1/4
, Eq. (
20
) is algebraically equivalent to Einstein’s equation sourced by the effective tensor

T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}:=T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)g_{\mu\nu}T.

(31)

This follows directly from the trace relation (
26
). Substituting (
27
) into (
20
) gives (
28
); identifying the coefficient of
g_{\mu\nu}T
gives (
30
). Hence (
29
) is precisely
G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}
.

The divergence of Eq. (
20
) fixes the non-conservation law. Since

\nabla^{\mu}R_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\nu}R,

(32)

it is valid for Bianchi’s identities, one obtains

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{\kappa_{b}}\nabla_{\nu}R.

(33)

The sign in Eq. (
33
) fixes the direction of the curvature–matter exchange and is essential for the parameter conversion rules. In particular, the
\epsilon
- and
\lambda
-representatives carry opposite signs in the curvature-gradient balance; missing this relative sign would lead to an incorrect identification
\lambda\sim\epsilon
rather than the algebraic map derived below. For the two original parametrizations, Eq. (
33
) gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon}{2\kappa_{\epsilon}}\nabla_{\nu}R,

(34)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2\kappa_{\lambda}}\nabla_{\nu}R.

(35)

Using (
26
), Eq. (
33
) may also be written as

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\alpha(a)\nabla_{\nu}T.

(36)

Consequently,

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)\nabla_{\nu}T=0.

(37)

Thus the apparent non-conservation of
T_{\mu\nu}
is exactly compensated by the trace contribution in
T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}
.

4
Parameter equivalence and singular loci

The algebraic equivalence between the two parametrizations follows immediately by comparing coefficients. Consider the two families written side by side:

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\epsilon}T_{\mu\nu},

(38)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1-\lambda}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\lambda}T_{\mu\nu}.

(39)

For
\epsilon\neq 1
, Eq. (
38
) is identical to Eq. (
39
) if and only if

\lambda=-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon},\qquad\kappa_{\lambda}=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}.

(40)

The inverse parameter map exists for
\lambda\neq 1
and is

\epsilon=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1},\qquad\kappa_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)\kappa_{\lambda}.

(41)

To see this, divide Eq. (
38
) by
1-\epsilon
, obtaining

R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)}g_{\mu\nu}R=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}T_{\mu\nu}.

(42)

Equality with Eq. (
39
) requires

\frac{1-\lambda}{2}=\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)},

(43)

and therefore

\lambda=1-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}=-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}.

(44)

Equality of the right-hand sides gives the corresponding coupling transformation. Conversely, substituting Eq. (
40
) into Eq. (
39
) reproduces the divided form of Eq. (
38
). Hence the map is necessary and sufficient.

As a consequence, away from the coordinate singularities of the map, the two parametrizations determine the same local solution space for a fixed metric, fixed source tensor, and fixed boundary data, provided the coupling is transformed according to Eq. (
40
).

A separate point concerns singularities. There are two distinct kinds of singular behavior. First, the coordinate map between
\epsilon
and
\lambda
fails at
\epsilon=1
, while its inverse fails at
\lambda=1
. These are singularities of the parametrization map. Second, the Einstein-like trace elimination fails at

a=\frac{1}{4},\qquad\lambda=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\epsilon=-1.

(45)

This is not a failure of the
\epsilon\leftrightarrow\lambda
coordinate map itself; it is the degeneracy of the trace equation
(1-4a)R=\kappa_{b}T
. These two notions must not be conflated.

The explicit Ricci forms are

R_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2(1+\epsilon)}g_{\mu\nu}T\right),

(46)

and

R_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{\lambda}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1-\lambda}{2(1-2\lambda)}g_{\mu\nu}T\right),

(47)

where the first requires
\epsilon\neq\pm 1
for the displayed trace-eliminated form and the second requires
\lambda\neq 1/2
. Under Eq. (
40
), the coefficients match identically.

5
Effective sources and the physical meaning of matter

The Einstein rewriting (
29
) is mathematically exact for
a\neq 1/4
, but it introduces an interpretational ambiguity. There are at least three tensors one may discuss:

(i)

a laboratory stress tensor
T^{\rm lab}_{\mu\nu}
, defined by the matter action and measured by non-gravitational local experiments;

(ii)

a Rastall-type tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
, which appears on the right-hand side of the non-conservative field equation;

(iii)

an effective conserved tensor
T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha g_{\mu\nu}T
, which sources the Einstein tensor.

Visser’s equivalence argument is strongest when
T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}
is regarded as the physical conserved stress tensor and
T_{\mu\nu}
as a non-unique split of it
50
;
15
;
48
;
21
. By contrast, if
T_{\mu\nu}
is postulated to be the laboratory stress tensor, then the same algebraic rewriting does not by itself establish physical equivalence; it changes the relation between laboratory matter and geometry.

This is the operational issue developed in the next section. The same algebraic rewriting supports different physical readings depending on which tensor is identified with laboratory matter and how the gravitational coupling is calibrated. Without these specifications, statements such as “Rastall gravity is GR” and “Rastall gravity is physically inequivalent to GR” refer to different identifications of the source sector rather than to a single well-defined comparison.

6
Operational non-equivalence under fixed source normalization

We now address the operational core of this paper. In Einstein gravity,
the weak-field limit fixes the conversion between the stress tensor
assigned to matter and the curvature sourced by it. In the
Ricci–trace class, this conversion is more subtle because the
Newtonian source contains a trace contribution. Hence two different
questions must be kept separate. The first is whether two
parametrizations can be made algebraically identical by transforming
both the trace parameter and the source coupling. The answer is yes.
The second is whether this transformation is operationally passive
when the same
T_{\mu\nu}
and the same source normalization are held
fixed. The answer, as shown below, is no, except at the Einstein point.

We use signature
(-+++)
and write the weak-field metric as

g_{00}=-(1+2\Phi/c^{2}),\qquad|\Phi|/c^{2}\ll 1.

(48)

For nonrelativistic matter,

T_{00}\simeq\rho c^{2},\qquad T\simeq-\rho c^{2}.

(49)

The linearized Einstein tensor satisfies

G_{00}^{(1)}\simeq\frac{2}{c^{2}}\nabla^{2}\Phi.

(50)

For the Einstein-like form

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha g_{\mu\nu}T\right),

(51)

the effective
00
-source is

T^{\rm eff}_{00}\simeq(1-\alpha)\rho c^{2}.

(52)

Therefore the Poisson equation becomes

\nabla^{2}\Phi=4\pi G_{N}\rho,\qquad G_{N}=\frac{\kappa_{b}c^{4}}{8\pi}(1-\alpha).

(53)

For a fluid with pressure
p=w\rho c^{2}
, one obtains

T^{\rm eff}_{00}\simeq\left[1-\alpha(1-3w)\right]\rho c^{2},

(54)

so the pressure-dependent active Newtonian strength is

G_{N}(w)=\frac{\kappa_{b}c^{4}}{8\pi}\left[1-\alpha(1-3w)\right].

(55)

For radiation,
w=1/3
, the trace correction disappears.

For the two parametrizations,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2(1+\epsilon)},

\displaystyle\kappa_{b}^{(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon},

(56)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2(1-2\lambda)},

\displaystyle\kappa_{b}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\lambda}.

(57)

Thus pressureless matter gives

\displaystyle G_{N}^{(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}c^{4}}{8\pi}\frac{2+\epsilon}{2(1-\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)},

(58)

\displaystyle G_{N}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{\lambda}c^{4}}{8\pi}\frac{2-3\lambda}{2(1-2\lambda)}.

(59)

Equations (
58
) and (
59
) show explicitly
that the measured Newton constant is not, in general, identical to the
bare coefficient multiplying the source. If the couplings are treated
as freely adjustable bare parameters, then each representative may be
separately calibrated to the same observed
G_{N}
. In that case the
algebraic equivalence is preserved.

Indeed, solving Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
) for the
bare couplings gives

\kappa_{\epsilon}^{\rm cal}=\frac{8\pi G_{N}}{c^{4}}\frac{2(1-\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}{2+\epsilon},

(60)

and

\kappa_{\lambda}^{\rm cal}=\frac{8\pi G_{N}}{c^{4}}\frac{2(1-2\lambda)}{2-3\lambda}.

(61)

Under the algebraic map

\lambda=-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon},

(62)

these calibrated couplings satisfy

\kappa_{\lambda}^{\rm cal}=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}^{\rm cal}}{1-\epsilon}.

(63)

Therefore, a free Newtonian recalibration of the bare couplings
restores the algebraic equivalence.

The operational question addressed in this work is different. Suppose
that the same tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
is identified with the same
operationally normalized matter source in both parametrizations, and
that the coefficient multiplying this source is held fixed:

\kappa_{\epsilon}=\kappa_{\lambda}=\kappa_{\rm op}.

(64)

Here
\kappa_{\rm op}
denotes the fixed source normalization used in
the operational identification of
T_{\mu\nu}
. In the Einstein
limit it coincides with the usual Newtonian normalization

8\pi G_{N}/c^{4}
, but away from the Einstein point it should be
understood as the fixed coefficient assigned to the same matter source,
not as a freely recalibrated bare parameter.

With this convention, the algebraic isomorphism

\kappa_{\lambda}=\frac{\kappa_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}

(65)

is compatible with Eq. (
64
) only at
the Einstein point. To see this, impose

\kappa_{\lambda}=\kappa_{\epsilon}=\kappa_{\rm op}
in
Eq. (
65
). For nonzero finite

\kappa_{\rm op}
, one obtains

\kappa_{\rm op}=\frac{\kappa_{\rm op}}{1-\epsilon},

(66)

and therefore

\epsilon=0.

(67)

The parameter map then gives

\lambda=0.

(68)

Conversely, at
\epsilon=\lambda=0
, both representatives reduce to
Einstein’s equation with the same source normalization. Thus, under
fixed source normalization, the algebraic map is operationally passive
only at the Einstein point.

This is not a mathematical inconsistency and it is not a denial of the
formal Einstein rewriting. It is a distinction between two different
notions of equivalence. If
\kappa_{\epsilon}
and
\kappa_{\lambda}

are bare parameters, they may be transformed and recalibrated, and the
two representatives remain equivalent. If, however, the same

T_{\mu\nu}
and the same coefficient multiplying it are part of the
operational identification of the source, then the required rescaling
of
\kappa
changes the source normalization. In this second sense,
algebraic equivalence does not imply operational equivalence away from
the Einstein point.

Identification with Rastall gravity

We now establish the precise relation between the

For the

10
Why unimodular gravity is structurally different

While Rastall-type and Ricci–trace theories modify the algebraic relation between the Ricci scalar
R
and the trace of the energy–momentum tensor
T
, UG follows a fundamentally different route. Rather than deforming the trace sector, the trace equation is removed at the variational level through a restriction on the metric determinant, leading to traceless field equations. Consequently, the scalar curvature is not determined by the matter trace at the level of the fundamental equations.

In UG, the determinant of the metric tensor is fixed as a constant,

4
;
47
;
25
;
18
;
2
;
38
;
3
.

\sqrt{-g}=\omega,

(103)

This constraint, at the action level, is added using a Lagrange multiplier
according to

\displaystyle S_{UG}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{b}}\int d^{4}x\left[\sqrt{-g}R-\chi\left(\sqrt{-g}-\omega\right)\right]+S_{m},

(104)

where
\chi
is Lagrange multiplier,
S_{m}
is action of matter fields and
\omega
is a fixed scalar density, often identified with the volume element associated with a nondynamical fiducial metric. Varying the action (
104
) with respect to
\chi
enforces the unimodular condition (
103
). On the other hand, by varying the action (
104
) with respect to the metric
\delta g^{\mu\nu}

and eliminating the Lagrange multiplier, we obtain.

R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}T\right).

(105)

The fundamental field equations are traceless. The field equations can be written in a compact form using the Einstein tensor as

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}R

\displaystyle=\kappa_{b}\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}T\right).

(106)

Bianchi’s identities lead to

\displaystyle\kappa_{b}\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{\nu}\left(R+\kappa_{b}T\right)}{4}.

(107)

This implies that the usual conservation of the energy-momentum tensor is not directly satisfied, for discussions of this approach in cosmological scenarios, see
22
;
1
;
14
;
40
. If the usual conservation of the physical matter tensor is imposed as an additional condition, we obtain from (
107
)

\nabla_{\nu}(R+\kappa_{b}T)=0,

(108)

and hence

R+\kappa_{b}T=4\Lambda_{\rm int},

(109)

where
\Lambda_{\rm int}
is an integration constant. One recovers Einstein’s equations with a cosmological constant,

G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda_{\rm int}g_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu}.

(110)

Under the additional assumption of covariant conservation, UG becomes classically equivalent to GR with a cosmological constant. Although UG reproduces the Einstein equations with a cosmological constant under the assumption of covariant conservation, this equivalence is not intrinsic to the traceless field equations themselves, since it relies on an additional conservation condition. This distinction becomes particularly relevant when comparing UG with Rastall-type and Ricci–trace theories. In those frameworks, the scalar curvature remains algebraically constrained by the matter trace through a modified trace relation. By contrast, in UG the trace equation is absent at the fundamental level and is recovered only after imposing covariant conservation.

The previous equivalence relies crucially on the conservation law
\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=0
. However, Eq. (
107
) itself does not require this condition. One may therefore consider the more general situation in which the energy–momentum tensor is not covariantly conserved. In this case, Eq. (
107
) becomes

\displaystyle 4\kappa_{b}\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\nu}\left(R+\kappa_{b}T\right)

showing that departures from conservation are directly encoded in the evolution of the quantity
R+\kappa_{b}T
. Consequently, the quantity
R+\kappa_{b}T
is no longer constrained to be constant and the standard trace relation is not recovered. The scalar curvature is therefore not algebraically determined by the trace of the energy–momentum tensor.

This behavior differs substantially from Rastall-type and Ricci–trace theories. In those frameworks, even in the presence of modified conservation laws, the scalar curvature remains algebraically constrained by the matter trace through a generalized trace equation. In contrast, the traceless structure of unimodular gravity remains intact, and no fundamental algebraic relation between
R
and
T
exists.

The regular linear Ricci–trace class (
20
) is structurally inequivalent to unimodular gravity. There are four independent obstructions.

First, the Ricci–trace class is defined by algebraically changing the relative coefficient of
R_{\mu\nu}
and
g_{\mu\nu}R
in the field equations, while UG is defined by a restricted variational principle. Equality of a trace-free equation does not imply equality of variational configuration spaces.

Second, in the Ricci–trace class the non-conservation law is fixed by Eq. (
33
). In UG, by contrast, the standard construction assumes conservation of the physical matter tensor and uses the Bianchi identity to generate an integration constant.

Third, the point
a=1/4
is singular for the Ricci–trace class because the algebraic trace relation degenerates. UG does not arise by an algebraic inversion of this singular point; it imposes the trace-free equation at the level of the variational principle through the unimodular restriction. Therefore the singular traceless corner of Eq. (
20
) and UG may share a formal resemblance without being the same theory.

Fourth, for every regular member of the Ricci–trace class
\left(a\neq 1/4\right)
,
the trace of the field equations yields an algebraic relation between the Ricci scalar and the trace of the energy–momentum tensor,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{b}}{\left(1-4a\right)}T.

(111)

Thus, the scalar curvature remains algebraically constrained by matter. In UG, by contrast, the trace equation is absent at the fundamental level, and no algebraic
R
-
T
relation exists prior to imposing additional conditions such as covariant conservation.

This distinction also clarifies the relation to the cosmological constant problem. In the Ricci–trace class, no cosmological constant appears as an integration constant for regular
a\neq 1/4
. In UG, the integration constant is a structural output of the restricted variational principle
53
;
47
;
25
;
39
.
Therefore, UG cannot be interpreted as a regular or singular member of the linear Ricci–trace class. The absence of a fundamental trace equation, together with its distinct variational origin and conservation structure, establishes a genuine structural inequivalence between the two frameworks.

Discussion

The analysis above suggests a hierarchy of equivalence notions. Table

summarizes the distinction.

Table 2:
Hierarchy of equivalence notions for linear Ricci-trace deformations.

Meaning

Algebraic equivalence

The field equations can be mapped into each other by re-arranging geometric terms and transforming parameters.

Source-redefinition equivalence

The modified equation can be rewritten as Einstein’s equation sourced by a conserved effective tensor

12
Conclusions

We have revised the classification of linear Ricci–trace deformations of Einstein’s equations and separated algebraic equivalence from operational equivalence. The main results are:

(i)

The general class
R_{\mu\nu}-ag_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu}
is equivalent, for
a\neq 1/4
, to Einstein’s equation with the conserved effective source
T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha g_{\mu\nu}T
, where
\alpha=(1/2-a)/(1-4a)
.

(ii)

The
\epsilon
- and
\lambda
-parametrizations are algebraically isomorphic under
\lambda=-\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)
and
\kappa_{\lambda}=\kappa_{\epsilon}/(1-\epsilon)
. The coordinate singularities of this map are distinct from the trace singularity
a=1/4
.

(iii)

The corrected Rastall dictionary is
\kappa_{\lambda}\lambda_{R}=\lambda/2
,
\alpha_{\lambda}=-\lambda/[2(1-2\lambda)]
, and
\gamma=(1-3\lambda)/(1-2\lambda)
.

(iv)

For pressureless matter, Newtonian matching gives

G_{N}=\kappa_{b}c^{4}(1-\alpha)/(8\pi)
. Thus the bare gravitational coupling and the measured Newton constant coincide only in special cases.

(v)

If the same matter tensor and the same operational source normalization are held fixed, the algebraic isomorphism is operationally passive only at the Einstein point
\epsilon=\lambda=0
. If the couplings are instead treated as freely recalibrated bare parameters, the equivalence is restored.

(vi)

In FLRW, the deformation appears through
\rho_{\rm eff}=(1-\alpha)\rho+3\alpha p
and
p_{\rm eff}=\alpha\rho+(1-3\alpha)p
, while radiation and trace-free matter form degenerate sectors.

(vii)

Unimodular gravity is structurally distinct from the linear Ricci–trace class. Its traceless field equations arise from a restricted variational principle rather than from an algebraic deformation of the Ricci–trace. While Ricci–trace theories preserve an algebraic relation between the Ricci scalar and the trace of the energy–momentum tensor through a modified trace equation, UG removes the trace equation at the variational level. Consequently, there is no fundamental algebraic trace relation between
R
and
T
; such a relation emerges only after additional assumptions, such as the usual covariant conservation law, are imposed.

One might argue that the non-equivalence derived above is an artifact
of an overly restrictive convention: why keep the source normalization
fixed instead of recalibrating each parametrization to the same
observed Newton constant? This is precisely the distinction we wish to
make. If
\kappa_{\epsilon}
and
\kappa_{\lambda}
are interpreted as
bare couplings, then the recalibrations obtained from
Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
) satisfy the algebraic
map and the equivalence is restored. However, this restoration is not
passive under the operational convention adopted here, because it
requires changing the coefficient multiplying the same matter tensor.
Thus the issue is not whether the algebraic map exists; it does. The
issue is whether the rescaling of the source coupling is regarded as a
mere change of notation or as a change in the operational
normalization of matter. Under fixed source normalization, the two
parametrizations are operationally equivalent only at the Einstein
point.
The resulting conclusion is deliberately modest but robust. Linear Ricci–trace deformations do not generically define a new metric gravitational operator beyond the Einstein tensor. Nevertheless, their physical interpretation is not fixed by algebra alone: it depends on the identification of the matter tensor and on whether the source coupling is treated as a freely recalibrated bare parameter or as part of the fixed operational normalization of matter.

Appendix B
Conservation of the effective source

For the regular class,

R_{\mu\nu}-ag_{\mu\nu}R=\kappa_{b}T_{\mu\nu},\qquad a\neq\frac{1}{4},

(121)

define

\alpha(a)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{1-4a},\qquad T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)g_{\mu\nu}T.

(122)

Then

G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa_{b}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}.

(123)

Taking the divergence gives

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=0.

(124)

Equivalently, using

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-a}{\kappa_{b}}\nabla_{\nu}R,\qquad R=\frac{\kappa_{b}}{1-4a}T,

(125)

one obtains

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\alpha(a)\nabla_{\nu}T.

(126)

Therefore

\nabla^{\mu}T^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu}=\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}-\alpha(a)\nabla_{\nu}T=0.

(127)

This appendix makes explicit that the conservation of the effective source is not an extra postulate; it follows from the Bianchi identity and the trace-eliminated Einstein rewriting.
