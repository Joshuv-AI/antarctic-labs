Operando state monitoring of diversified lithium-ion batteries via laser-excited ultrasonic sensing with transformer networks
Authors: Gaolong Lv, Chongbo Sun, Peihan Zhao, Kaihui Huang, Qimin Zhu, Yehai Li, Huanqing Cao, Xinyu Wu, Xuan Zhang, Guangmin Zhou, Wei Feng, Shifeng Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging contact-based and immersion-based piezoelectric ultrasonic techniques encounter challenges in achieving accurate battery state monitoring under high-rate operations, where temperature fluctuations distort ultrasound and couplant contamination compromises practical applicability. Here, we propose air-coupled (couplant-free), noncontact laser-excited ultrasonic sensing (LEUS) with transformer networks for operando battery state estimation. The unique LEUS system pioneers a dual-laser design that uses a ring-shaped pulsed laser and a continuous laser to generate and detect high-quality ultrasound with a 10-fold increase in amplitude and a signal-to-noise ratio of 30 dB (16 dB higher than typical configurations), thereby enabling precise tracking of internal changes associated with state of charge (SoC) and state of health (SoH). By transforming ultrasonic signals into time-frequency scalograms, transformer networks autonomously extract discriminative features, eliminating manual feature engineering while achieving accurate prediction with mean errors below 5.7% for SoC and 2.1% for SoH. Through transfer learning, the base model generalizes rapidly to unseen chemistries, high-rate cycling, reducing training time and cost with minimal ultrasonic data. Extensive validation on over 100,000 ultrasonic signals from 40 commercial batteries, spanning two chemistries, three capacities, and 13 protocols, demonstrates the method’s robustness and reliability for operando battery state monitoring, paving the way for next-generation battery-management systems.

INTRODUCTION

Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are central to modern energy solutions, including electric vehicles and grid storage due to due to their long cycle life, high energy, and power density (1). However, frequent high-rate charging and discharging, essential for automotive and high-performance applications (2), substantially accelerate battery aging, leading to capacity fade, power loss, and potential safety hazards (3). To assess battery operational status and degradation, two critical parameters are typically used: state of charge (SoC), indicating the remaining capacity, and state of health (SoH), representing cumulative degradation (4). Inaccurate SoC monitoring may lead to overcharging (SoC exceeding 100%) or deep discharging (SoC below 0%), causing irreversible damage and accelerated SoH decline (5). Conventional methods for estimating SoC and SoH, relying on measurements of voltage (6), current (7), and ohmic resistance (8), typically require complete or partial charge-discharge cycles. Such approaches involve extended test durations and complicated feature extraction processes (9, 10), limiting their practical flexibility. Furthermore, high-rate cycling exacerbates polarization and voltage hysteresis effects (11), causing inaccurate electrical measurements and consequently reduced accuracy in SoC and SoH estimation during prolonged aging (12). Therefore, developing operando monitoring techniques that accurately assess battery status is essential for practical battery management systems.

Ultrasonic sensing emerges as a promising alternative to electrochemical testing for operando battery monitoring due to its sensitivity to changes in mechanical properties during cycling and aging (13). Variations in battery material properties (e.g., electrode modulus, density, Poisson’s ratio, and hardness) affect the propagation, reflection, and transmission of acoustic waves within battery structures (14–16), enabling inference of internal states such as SoC and SoH. Typical ultrasonic detection methods use piezoelectric transducers attached to the cell surface or immersed in a silicone oil medium and demonstrate the feasibility of correlating acoustic features [e.g., time of flight (TOF), attenuation coefficient, amplitude, and velocity] with electrochemical states (17, 18). However, contact configurations require acoustic couplants and fixtures, and are prone to ultrasonic signal distortion induced by temperature fluctuations during high-rate cycling, hindering accurate state monitoring (19). The immersion media, such as silicone oil, not only cool battery temperature during operation, thereby failing to reflect actual thermal conditions and reducing detection reliability, but also contaminate the battery and require complex cleaning processes, limiting their practical applicability. Notably, high-rate cycling can induce temperature excursions of up to 20°C that markedly distort ultrasonic signals (20, 21), and couplant-based contact and immersion piezoelectric techniques cannot record these waveforms at the true cell temperature, thereby hindering accurate SoC and SoH estimation under high-rate conditions.

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, laser-excited ultrasonic sensing (LEUS) has attracted substantial attention for battery monitoring by employing a pulsed laser and a continuous laser to generate and detect ultrasound (22), enabling air-coupled and noncontact sensing without disturbing battery operation or thermal behavior. In general, LEUS operates in thermoelastic or ablation regimes depending on whether the incident laser fluence falls below or exceeds the ablation threshold (23). As the ablation regime damages inspected structures, the thermoelastic regime is always preferred. However, the low–signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), low-amplitude ultrasonic waves generated in the thermoelastic regime attenuate rapidly and cannot traverse the thick multilayer structure of commercial cells, rendering subsurface regions acoustically inaccessible. These limitations highlight the need for an optimized LEUS capable of generating high-amplitude, high-SNR ultrasonic waves that propagate through the bulk of the cell, thereby facilitating robust monitoring of complex and spatially heterogeneous degradation processes.

Beyond acquiring ultrasonic data, accurate SoC and SoH estimation requires robust correlations between ultrasonic signatures and electrochemical status under dynamic real-world conditions. Conventional analytical approaches heavily rely on handcrafted features and intensive quantitative analysis to correlate ultrasonic changes with battery SoC and SoH, making them inflexible and unsuitable for real-time deployment (24). In addition, these approaches struggle with high-dimensional ultrasonic data, leading to low prediction accuracy and poor generalizability across different battery types, usage profiles, and operational environments. By contrast, machine learning (ML) techniques excel in capturing complex, nonlinear dependencies in multimodal data and offer a powerful data-driven alternative to conventional analytical methods (25). ML models have demonstrated impressive accuracy in regression tasks using high-dimensional sensor data and are increasingly adopted for SoC and SoH estimation from ultrasonic signals (26). However, current ultrasonic-based ML studies remain confined to small datasets comprising low C-rates (27), early aging stages (28), and single chemistry (29), and rarely consider model robustness, cross-scenario adaptability, or practical deployment (30, 31). This limitation conflicts with real-world scenarios in which batteries with diverse chemistries, manufacturers, and aging profiles operate under dynamic and high-rate conditions. Therefore, fully exploiting the advantages of noncontact LEUS and ML requires a holistic framework that combines high-throughput signal acquisition with scalable, generalizable models, enabling accurate real-time predictions across a broad range of battery chemistries, manufacturers, and operating conditions.

In this work, an operando, fully noncontact LEUS system integrated with a transformer-based deep learning model, is proposed for real-time SoC and SoH monitoring of commercial lithium iron phosphate (LFP)/graphite and nickel manganese cobalt (NCM)/graphite batteries across diverse high-rate conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the high-level workflow encompasses battery testing, ultrasonic signal acquisition and processing, model training, and real-world deployment, forming a generalized strategy for battery state estimation. To eliminate acoustic couplants and prevent operational interference, the LEUS system replaces conventional ultrasound by using an optimized ring-shaped pulsed laser for ultrasonic generation and a continuous laser for ultrasonic detection. This configuration generates high-amplitude, high-SNR (30 dB) ultrasonic waves that penetrate deeply through multilayer battery structures (Fig. 1B), thereby enabling characterization of battery internal structural and compositional changes related to SoC and SoH. Building on this platform, we constructed the largest LEUS dataset to date, comprising over 100,000 ultrasonic measurements across diverse SoC and SoH levels from 40 commercial batteries, which span two cathode material types, three capacity designs, and 13 high-rate protocols. The acquired ultrasonic signals were converted into time-frequency scalograms that reflect internal structural and compositional evolution, and labeled with electrochemical references. These scalograms were subsequently input into a transformer model (Fig. 1C), which autonomously extracted salient features relevant to SoC and SoH without handcrafted processing or electrochemical inputs. The resulting model achieves mean errors below 5.7% for SoC and 2.1% for SoH and transfers to new chemistries, capacities and high-rate conditions with minimal data, cutting training time by over 18% and validation loss by over 10%. Across all independent transfer scenarios, the model achieves average prediction errors below 7.5% for SoC and 3.4% for SoH. The deployment provides a scalable, generalizable framework for accurate, real-time operando battery state assessment under high-rate conditions.

RESULTS

Optimized LEUS for operando battery state monitoring

To monitor SoC and SoH in real-time, a unique LEUS system that integrates with a multichannel electrochemical workstation is designed to obtain the synchronized ultrasonic and electrochemical data across multiple batteries during cycling, as detailed in Fig. 2 and fig. S1. To achieve fully noncontact ultrasonic excitation and all-optical detection under air-coupled conditions, LEUS uses a pulsed laser to irradiate the battery surface, thereby converting optical energy into ultrasonic waves via thermoelastic expansion, and uses a continuous laser to detect these waves (Fig. 2A), enabling fully noncontact, couplant-free acoustic sensing compared to conventional couplant-based ultrasonic methods. An optimized ring-shaped laser source is specifically designed to generate high-amplitude, high-SNR ultrasonic bulk waves under the nondestructive thermoelastic regime to address the challenge of acoustic attenuation in multilayer battery structures. A computer-controlled motor stage is used to scan over the eight-channel batteries and synchronously acquire both ultrasonic and electrochemical signals of each channel (Materials and Methods), covering diverse battery cathode chemistries, nominal capacities, and operational conditions. The obtained ultrasonic signals during cycling offer critical insights into battery physical changes, essential for battery state prediction, and the electrochemical data provide data-rich and actual SoC and SoH values for ML training.

In detail, a lens-axicon-based optical assembly (Fig. 2B), comprising a convex lens and an axicon, is specifically designed to create a ring-shaped laser source on the battery surface for generating high-amplitude, high-SNR focused ultrasonic waves under the nondestructive thermoelastic regime. This optical assembly allows for the manipulation of ring-shaped beam patterns, with a tunable ring radius achievable by adjusting distances between the axicon, lens, and battery (denoted as Z1 and Z2). The critical aspect of focused LEUS is that the laser ring functions as a circular array of point sources, simultaneously emitting ultrasonic bulk waves that converge at a common detection point (Fig. 2C). The ring size is optimized based on the acoustic directional angle θ and battery thickness T to ensure effective convergence of ultrasound at the rear-surface detection point. Focused ultrasonic waves generated by the ring-shaped source exhibit a peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.199 mV, more than 10 times the 0.019 mV of the unfocused wave produced by the conventional point source, and achieve an SNR of 30.1 dB, 16.2 dB higher than the 13.9 dB unfocused case (Fig. 2D), thereby providing clearer waveforms for reliable postprocessing and accurate battery state estimation. During battery aging, each LEUS measurement lasts approximately 10 s, using a 5-mJ pulsed laser for ultrasonic generation and a sub–1 mJ continuous laser for detection. These fully noncontact measurements neither disrupt electrochemical operation (Fig. 2E) nor degrade cell performance, as proved by the consistent charge-discharge curves obtained with and without LEUS operation (Fig. 2F).

LEUS dataset construction and analysis

To validate whether the noncontact ultrasonic sensing approach can reliably capture state-dependent signatures under dynamically perturbed and thermally variable conditions, commercial LFP/graphite cells and NCM/graphite cells were cycled under high-rate protocols on the above LEUS system to collect data. Each cell was charged with constant current (CC) to an upper voltage limit, followed by constant voltage (CV) charging until the cutoff current dropped to C/20, ensuring a full charge (100% SoC). Then, CC discharge was applied until the cutoff voltage was reached. During cycling, ultrasonic and electrochemical signals are recorded simultaneously for subsequent analysis. The resulting dataset comprises over 100,000 ultrasonic signals synchronized with electrochemical measurements from 40 commercial LIBs (LFP and NCM chemistries, detailed in tables S1 and S2) tested under various extreme fast-charge/discharge protocols (Fig. 3, A and B). The datasets consist of three batches to evaluate model performance and robustness. Dataset I, including eight LFP cells charged at 6 C and discharged at 8 C, is used to train and test the base model. Dataset II examines charging rates of 7 to 10 C and discharging rates of 8 to 16 C to validate model performance across different operating conditions. Dataset III focuses on NCM cells to assess model robustness and generalizability across cathode chemistries and capacities under different working conditions. Despite structural variations inherent in the batteries themselves (see table S2), we can mitigate the impact of these structural factors on signal detection by adjusting the device’s focusing mechanism, thereby ensuring the validity and feasibility of data acquisition. Figure 3B illustrates the distribution of LEUS sampling counts across the SoC-SoH range for three datasets. LEUS measurements cover the full 0 to 100% SoC and 90 to 100% SoH, with a higher density of ultrasonic data collected at higher SoC levels. We emphasize that the highly heterogeneous LEUS testing yields the largest ultrasonic dataset to date for SoC and SoH estimation, facilitating model robustness and generalizability beyond conventional ultrasonic evaluations.

In a single charge-discharge cycle (Fig. 3C), LEUS measurements are performed concurrently with electrochemical stages comprising a CC-CV charge to 3.65 V, a postcharge rest, a CC discharge to 2.0 V, and a postdischarge rest. Figure 3D presents representative ultrasonic waves at different SoC levels during the discharge process, recorded from the same battery position, showing that ultrasonic TOF and minimum signal amplitude (MSA) change with SoC. Specifically, the MSA declines markedly as SoC decreases, and the TOF shifts progressively to the right. These trends likely arise from microstructural changes induced by lithiation/delithiation, which modify electrode thickness, density, and elastic modulus, thereby affecting acoustic impedance, amplitude, and TOF (17, 30). Although time-domain ultrasonic characteristics (TOF and MSA) have been widely used to characterize battery states, they represent only partial aspects of the signal. To obtain a more comprehensive representation, we further use continuous wavelet transforms to convert raw ultrasonic signals into time-frequency scalograms. These scalograms retain the essential TOF and MSA information while additionally capturing localized spectral and temporal variations, providing a richer feature space. The resulting representation enhances sensitivity to fine-grained features or compositional changes and improves model prediction accuracy by leveraging signal components that may be overlooked in traditional time- or frequency-domain analyses (32). Figure 3E illustrates ultrasonic scalograms across varying SoC levels, where high SoC states (top left) are characterized by intense, well-localized frequency bands and sharp temporal boundaries. As SoC decreases, these patterns gradually diffuse, with broadened spectral regions and diminished peak coherence, suggesting increased acoustic dispersion within the cell. This behavior reveals that SoC affects ultrasonic transmission and energy distribution, enabling the ultrasonic response to encode valuable state-dependent information in the time-frequency domain.

Meanwhile, Fig. 3 (F and G) illustrates the nonlinear relationship between discharge capacity and long-term cycles, along with corresponding ultrasound responses during the postcharge rest. As cycles progress, SoH degradation leads to a decrease in MSA and a rightward shift in TOF (Fig. 3G). These changes are attributable to the formation of the solid electrolyte interface layer (33), which consumes lithium and degrades the graphite active material, leading to irreversible transformations in the electrode structure. In addition, prolonged high-rate cycling may expand cell volume and increase TOF. Figure 3H shows the evolution of ultrasonic scalograms across battery aging cycles, highlighting progressive temporal shifts and subtle frequency broadening of energy peaks. While overall intensity remains relatively stable, the dominant signal components gradually migrate toward longer arrival times and broader spectral regions as degradation accumulates. These dynamics reflect delayed ultrasonic propagation and increased attenuation within the aged cell, indicating that scalogram features are sensitive to SoH-related microstructural changes during cycling.

Ultrasonic-driven neural inference of battery states

Given the complexity and nonlinear relationships between ultrasonic scalograms and battery states, manual feature extraction is unreliable and challenging. ML algorithms, which facilitate decision-making by analyzing complex data, have been widely used to interpret electrochemical states through conventional ultrasonic waves (34). However, traditional ML methods rely heavily on manual selection of ultrasonic features from either time or frequency domains, making them inherently subjective, empirical, and labor-intensive. In contrast, deep learning models overcome these limitations by automatically learning features directly from the raw data, thereby eliminating subjectivity and substantially enhancing analysis efficiency. Because ultrasonic scalograms encode richer information in both time and frequency domains, we adopt an image-recognition paradigm that treats scalograms as inputs and SoC/SoH as outputs, thereby training a transformer-based network (Fig. 4A). Specifically, transformers equipped with multihead self-attention mechanisms capture long-range dependencies and global correlations within scalograms (35), while auxiliary convolutional layers extract fine-grained local representations that pure transformers may miss (36, 37). Accordingly, we deploy a convolutional-transformer network that automatically learns salient time-frequency features from ultrasonic scalograms and accurately predicts SoC and SoH (see Materials and Methods).

To establish a baseline, we first trained and validated the model under identical operating conditions using eight 650 mAh LFP cells from dataset I, each cell cycled at a 6 C charge rate and an 8 C discharge rate. We systematically assessed three key parameters affecting battery state prediction accuracy, including wavelet basis functions, window lengths of sectioned scalograms, and uncertainty calibration within ML modes. Figure 4B shows a comparative analysis across conventionally used wavelet bases in ultrasonic signal processing, including db9, meyer, morse, db4, and morlet. The db9 was identified as the optimal choice to generate scalograms in battery state prediction, verified by the highest prediction accuracy with the lowest root mean square percentage error (RMSPE) of 0.3, mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 0.1, and coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.984. As a higher-order, compactly supported orthonormal wavelet, db9 provides superior time-frequency localization, captures transient ultrasonic signal features, and attenuates noise more effectively than other wavelet bases (38). For reliability assessment, recalibration is performed using isotonic regression (39). Figure S2 evaluates the uncertainty calibration by computing the miscalibration area, defined as the area between a measured curve and the diagonal line. Compared to the uncalibrated model, the calibration curve aligns more closely with the ideal diagonal, reducing miscalibration area and increasing predictive confidence (40). Figure 4C shows the absolute estimation error of SoC and SoH predictions across different scalogram window lengths for calibrated and uncalibrated models. The labels “All,” “Part I,” and “Part II” correspond to progressively narrower time windows of 0 to 20 μs, 0 to 6 μs, and 2 to 6 μs, respectively. As the input window narrows, the model focuses more specifically on time-frequency regions that contain the most salient ultrasonic features associated with battery state, while minimizing the influence of irrelevant or noisy signal components (fig. S3). This strategy not only reduces model complexity and computational cost, but also enhances predictive performance. Notably, with calibration, SoC prediction errors drop from 10.3 to 8.1%, and SoH errors reduce from 2.2 to 2.0%. These results underscore that selecting an appropriate wavelet basis (e.g., db9) and refining the scalogram window (e.g., 2 to 6 μs) are critical to improving the time-frequency resolution and predictive accuracy. Furthermore, incorporating uncertainty calibration into the ML model enhances its confidence and correction capability, resulting in substantially improved SoC and SoH prediction performance.

Figure 4 (D and E) presents the transformer-based SoC prediction results during 6 C charging and 8 C discharging processes, respectively, for cell no. 1 in dataset I. The predicted values align closely with the electrochemical references over the entire SoC range, achieving coefficients of determination (R2) of 0.97 and 0.98 for charge of SoC (C-SoC) and discharge of SoC (D-SoC), respectively. Insets display histograms of absolute prediction errors, showing that the majority of estimates cluster tightly around the true values, with MAPE remaining below 7%. To further evaluate temporal prediction stability, Fig. 4F visualizes a cycle-resolved heatmap of SoC prediction error (MAPE) as a function of SoC level and cycle number. Overall, errors remain consistently low (<6%) in mid-to-high SoC regions across cycles. In contrast, larger deviations appear at lower SoC values, where MAPE increases to approximately 8 to 9%, with only moderate variation along the cycle dimension. This SoC-dependent error pattern arises from both data-driven and physics-driven factors. From a data perspective, dataset I exhibits an imbalanced distribution, with considerably fewer samples collected at low SoC levels (Fig. 3B), leading to a sparsity-induced generalization gap. In addition, ultrasonic signals at low SoC tend to exhibit reduced amplitudes and lower SNRs (Fig. 3D), making it more challenging for the model to extract stable and discriminative features. This limitation can be mitigated in future work by adopting denser sampling in the low-SoC range to improve data balance and model calibration in this regime. Moreover, the quality of low-SoC ultrasonic signals may be further enhanced through signal averaging and optimization of laser energy. From a physical perspective, this behavior is closely related to the intrinsic reaction mechanism of LiFePO4. LFP operates through a two-phase transition between LiFePO4 and FePO4 over a wide composition range, resulting in pronounced phase coexistence, interfacial migration, and coherency strain (41). At low SoC, the increased density of phase boundaries, together with particle-level heterogeneity and asynchronous transformation, leads to enhanced acoustic heterogeneity within the cell. Since ultrasonic responses are sensitive to elastic mismatch and interfacial scattering, the mapping between ultrasonic features and electrochemical state becomes less deterministic in this regime, contributing to the increased prediction error.

Notably, the variation of prediction error along the cycle dimension (i.e., with decreasing SOH) is comparatively limited within the tested range. At a fixed SoC, the MAPE remains within a relatively narrow band across cycles, suggesting that aging primarily introduces a moderate drift and dispersion in the feature-state relationship rather than a severe loss of mapping consistency. From the modeling perspective, incorporating SOH or cycle index as an auxiliary input could enable an SOH-aware or degradation-aware model to better capture aging-induced feature shifts at the same SoC. These results indicate that, for LFP cells, the prediction performance is predominantly governed by intrinsic SoC-dependent phase-transition complexity, while the influence of SOH is mainly reflected as a gradual and continuous evolution of the feature-state mapping. To quantitatively assess the relative influence of SoC and cycle/SOH on prediction error, we performed a main-effect variance decomposition analysis based on the heatmap data in Fig. 4F. The decomposition shows that SoC accounts for approximately 98.6% of the total variance in prediction error, whereas the cycle/SOH dimension contributes only about 0.5%. The remaining ∼0.9% corresponds to residual unexplained fluctuation. These results quantitatively confirm that the dominant error stratification is organized along the SoC dimension rather than the cycle/SOH dimension within the tested aging range. Overall, the model maintains robust predictive capability across both SoC and SOH dimensions, particularly within the practically relevant operating range (10 to 90% SoC).

Figure 4 (G and H) illustrates the model’s ability to estimate battery SoH from LEUS measurements. The regression plot in Fig. 4G shows a strong linear correlation between predicted and reference SoH values, with R2 reaching 0.98 and error distributions exhibiting only minimal deviation (<1%), as evidenced by the inset histogram. Figure 4H further demonstrates how the predicted SoH evolves throughout the cycling process. The model accurately tracks both the initial plateau and the subsequent nonlinear degradation phase. The inset shows the absolute error at each cycle, confirming the model’s stability and reliability for long-term degradation monitoring using ultrasonic scalograms. This high prediction accuracy may be attributed to the fact that SoH-related ultrasonic signals are typically acquired at high SoC levels, where signal amplitude is stronger and SNR is higher (Fig. 3G). As a result, the extracted features are more stable, enabling the model to converge more effectively and generalize reliably across cycles.

To further examine the robustness of the model beyond random splitting, we implemented a leave-one-cell-out cross-validation strategy across all eight pouch cells in dataset I. As shown in fig. S4, even when the test cell is entirely unseen during training, the prediction error remains within a similar range (MAPE = 9% for both SoC and SoH), with no systematic degradation in performance. These results confirm that the model captures transferable state-dependent ultrasonic features rather than relying on cell-specific signatures. Because high-rate cycling is accompanied by temperature variation, we further investigated whether temperature could act as a surrogate variable in prediction. As shown in fig. S5, under temperature-matched conditions (ΔT ≤ 1°C), the prediction error still varies across SoC intervals, whereas under SoC-matched conditions, the variation across temperature remains limited. The error distributions across temperature intervals exhibit substantial overlap, with comparable medians and interquartile ranges. These observations indicate that temperature does not dominate the prediction outcome; instead, the model primarily responds to intrinsic electromechanical state changes encoded in the ultrasonic signals. To extend the validation to deeper aging conditions, the model was further evaluated on an expanded dataset covering a broader SoH range down to ∼80%. As shown in fig. S6, the predicted SoH continues to closely follow the reference degradation trajectory, maintaining high correlation and stable error distribution across both early and late-stage aging. The standard deviation and variance of the absolute relative errors are 4.76 × 10−2 and 2.27 × 10−3 for charging SoC, 5.89 × 10−2 and 3.46 × 10−3 for discharging SoC, and 2.03 × 10−3 and 4.11 × 10−6 for SoH, respectively. These results demonstrate that the proposed LEUS-ML framework remains effective over an extended degradation threshold, supporting its applicability for long-term battery health monitoring.

Last, Fig. 4I summarizes the model’s quantitative performance across the entire dataset I, which consists of LFP/graphite cells tested under a single high-rate protocol. The transformer-based network achieves R2 values above 0.96 for all tasks, with corresponding MAPE values of 4.7% (C-SoC), 5.7% (D-SoC), and 2.1% (SoH). To further quantify the practical significance of these errors, the absolute differences are statistically analyzed, as shown in fig. S7. The median absolute error is approximately 14 mAh for C-SoC, 12 mAh for D-SoC, and below 8 mAh for SoH. The interquartile ranges indicate that most prediction deviations are confined within ∼5 to 25 mAh for SoC and within ∼5 to 10 mAh for SoH, with only a limited number of outliers extending to higher values. These results validate the capability of the proposed LEUS-ML system to deliver high-accuracy, real-time battery state prediction under fixed operating conditions using only ultrasonic data.

Robustness of prediction over various cell operation conditions and types

Building on the validated performance of the proposed LEUS-ML framework under standard operating conditions (Fig. 4), we further investigated its generalizability across different battery cathode chemistries, cell designs, and usage protocols by leveraging transfer learning. Transfer learning is widely used in deep learning for scenarios in which the prediction tasks are similar but fewer training data are available (42). As illustrated in Fig. 5A, a transformer-based model was first pretrained on a large-scale dataset (dataset I), and then fine-tuned on two additional small-scale datasets (datasets II and III), covering various cell types and operational conditions. In our implementation, the transferred model copies the pretrained weights, appends a lightweight task-specific head, and retrains only the newly added and higher-level layers on limited target data, thereby efficiently reusing knowledge captured on the large-scale task. To evaluate the effectiveness of transfer learning, we trained models on the same data and initialized their parameters with the pretrained weights of the aforementioned transformer model. Compared with ab initio training, transfer learning substantially reduces validation loss by over 10% (Fig. 5B) and training time by over 18% (fig. S8) for both SoC and SoH prediction tasks, underscoring the adaptability of the pretrained model to new domains with limited data and time resources.

We test the robustness of the proposed approach using 32 additional LFP cells in datasets II and III across different operation conditions. Figure 5 (C and D) presents the SoC prediction results under 7 C charging and 8 C discharging conditions for a representative cell (no. 10). The predicted SoC values exhibit strong agreement with reference values, with R2 values of 0.95 for charge of SoC and 0.97 for discharge of SoC, and MAPE around 8%. Insets display the distribution of prediction errors, which are narrowly distributed around a negligible magnitude. The heatmap in Fig. 5E shows the variation of SoC prediction error with SoC and cycle number. Errors remain consistently low at high SoC levels (>60%) but increase at low SoC and later cycles, likely due to reduced SNR and fewer training samples under those conditions, which is consistent with earlier observations in dataset I. Figure 5 (F and G) evaluates the SoH prediction performance for the same cell. A tight linear correlation is observed between predicted and reference SoH values (R2 = 0.95 and MAPE = 0.01), with error distribution shown in the inset (Fig. 5F). Figure 5G illustrates the evolution of SoH over 150 cycles. The model successfully tracks degradation progression, including nonlinear transitions. The cycle-resolved error plot confirms that prediction accuracy is maintained throughout the full lifetime window.

Figure 5H presents the prediction error distributions for SoC and SoH across five representative cells of varying formats and chemistries from datasets II and III. The selected cells are grouped to allow controlled comparisons: some share identical chemistries but differ in charge/discharge conditions, while others vary in different battery types with the same charge/discharge protocol. Overall, the prediction errors remain within a relatively narrow range across all cells. The mean SoC prediction error ranges from approximately 1.5 to 4.1%, while the mean SoH prediction error remains below about 2.3%. Although slight variations in error distributions can be observed between LFP-based and NCM-based cells as well as under different cycling conditions, the overall prediction performance remains consistently high. The fluctuations observed in the transfer-learning results for both SoC and SoH prediction can be attributed to domain shifts between the source and target datasets, including differences in cycling protocols, degradation trajectories, and the limited amount of target-domain data available for fine-tuning. Such differences may lead to insufficient adaptation of the transferred model, thereby increasing prediction variability. To quantitatively evaluate these differences, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. The results indicate statistically significant differences among groups for both SoC errors (F = 196.50 and P <<0.01) and SoH errors (F = 696.06 and P <<0.01). However, the magnitude of variation remains limited, suggesting that the proposed LEUS-ML framework maintains robust predictive capability across different cathode chemistries and operating conditions. Figure 5I summarizes the model’s quantitative performance across the entire datasets II and III, which consists of LFP/graphite and NCM/graphite cells across diverse high-rate protocols. Although slightly lower than the pretrained model’s performance in dataset I, the transformer-based network maintains acceptable predictive performance, achieving RMSPE values below 0.11 for tasks and corresponding MAPE values below 7.6% (C-SoC), 8.2% (D-SoC), and 4.1% (SoH). To further assess the practical significance of these prediction errors, the absolute differences are statistically analyzed for both datasets (figs. S9 and S10). For dataset II, the median absolute errors are approximately 14 mAh (C-SoC), 28 mAh (D-SoC), and ∼10 mAh (SoH), with most deviations distributed within ∼5 to 40 mAh for SoC and below ∼15 mAh for SoH. For dataset III, which introduces additional variations in cell chemistry and nominal capacity (1000 mAh and 700 mAh), the median errors remain at a comparable level, with C-SoC and D-SoC typically within ∼10 to 30 mAh and SoH within ∼5 to 15 mAh, despite a broader spread under certain conditions. Notably, while D-SoC exhibits relatively larger dispersion, likely due to increased polarization and dynamic hysteresis effects under high-rate discharge, the overall error distributions remain well-bounded. This indicates that the model preserves a stable absolute prediction resolution even under domain shifts involving different cathode chemistries, capacities, and operating protocols. Across all independent transfer scenarios (details provided in table S3), the model achieves average prediction errors below 7.5% for SoC and 3.4% for SoH. These results suggest that the model captures intrinsic ultrasonic-electrochemical correlations robustly and generalizes well under changes in cell type, protocol, or usage variation, supporting its applicability across a range of real-world conditions using only noncontact LEUS data. Although the electrochemical origins of ultrasonic signatures differ across cathode and anode chemistries, the LEUS-ML framework is not tied to a specific voltage plateau, but instead learns transferable electro-chemo-mechanical signatures from ultrasonic responses, enabling adaptation to different chemistries through calibration and transfer learning.

To comprehensively evaluate model performance, Fig. 5J and figs. Under this in-distribution evaluation using dataset I, the transformer achieves the lowest MAPE (0.08), RMSPE (0.11), and the highest R2 (0.97) for SoC, as well as the lowest MAPE (0.01), RMSPE (0.01), and the highest R2 (0.95) for SoH, highlighting its superior predictive precision. To further assess model robustness under stricter data independence, additional evaluations based on a cell-wise holdout strategy are provided in figs. Under this more challenging setting, the proposed model continues to outperform baseline methods in both SoC and SoH prediction, with only a slight reduction in overall accuracy compared to Fig. 5J. The corresponding absolute error distributions (fig. S15) remain tightly bounded, further confirming the robustness and generalization capability of the proposed framework across different cells.

Together, these results highlight the LEUS-ML framework as a scalable and practical solution for real-time battery state prediction.

DISCUSSION

This work reports an air-coupled and noncontact LEUS technique combined with ML algorithms for operando monitoring of battery SoC and SoH. The LEUS system uses a ring-shaped pulsed laser and a continuous laser to generate and detect ultrasound with 10-fold greater amplitude and a 16 dB higher SNR than conventional systems, enabling high-resolution time-frequency scalograms through wavelet analysis. These scalograms effectively suppress interfering signals and preserve subtle features, thus enabling accurate SoC and SoH predictions. Integrated with a multichannel electrochemical workstation, the unique LEUS system synchronously records more than 100,000 ultrasonic and electrochemical data from 40 commercial batteries under various operating conditions, constituting the largest dataset of its kind to date. This platform substantially enhances testing throughput and allows for detailed comparison across different battery types and charge/discharge protocols. The proposed LEUS-ML framework is developed to extract salient features directly from ultrasonic scalograms without relying on handcrafted features or domain-specific priors. The base model enables real-time state prediction with an average prediction error below 5.7% for SoC and 2.1% for SoH. Moreover, this approach adapts rapidly to unseen cell chemistries, nominal capacities, and high-rate conditions through transfer learning with minimal data, reducing training time by over 18% and validation loss by over 10%. We believe this integrated LEUS-ML approach offers a powerful and specified solution for real-time, nondestructive, and cross-compatible SoC and SoH monitoring in high-rate operating battery system, with potential applicability to LIBs with more complex cathode and anode chemistries, nominal capacities, and protocols.

The developed technique can be readily miniaturized for integration into electric vehicles, energy storage systems, and other battery-powered platforms, because it relies on laser-based ultrasonic excitation and detection that act only on the cell surface. Shortening the optical path and substituting peripherals with compact beam-delivery components condense the current setup into a compact optical module. This paves the way for deployment in embedded battery monitoring units or portable diagnostic tools. Beyond SoC and SoH estimation, the noncontact nature and high spatiotemporal resolution of the LEUS system open possibilities for broader battery diagnostics. Inspired by prior advances in medium-coupled ultrasonics, this technique can be extended to operando imaging and early-stage detection of critical internal phenomena, including gas evolution, electrolyte wetting heterogeneity, and structural degradation, which closely relate to thermal runaway and explosion risks. Together, we envision that the LEUS system, when coupled with advanced data-driven models, will serve as a powerful and indispensable complement to conventional battery management systems, enabling comprehensive real-time diagnostics of both operational status and internal structural evolution under practical usage conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

System configuration

Figure 2A illustrates the schematic of the proposed LEUS-electrochemistry system, innovatively designed for operando battery state measurement. The system integrates a fully noncontact LEUS, a multichannel electrochemical workstation, and a motorized translational stage for automatic and scanning-type tracking of SoC and SoH during cycling. The excited beam is produced by a 1064 nm passively Q-switched pulsed laser (LUMIBIRD, Merion C-S4) with a near-flattop distribution and an average energy of about 5 mJ at a 400-Hz repetition rate. In this setup, the beam is initially expanded to a diameter of 8 mm using a beam expander (Daheng Optics, GCO-2502), and then transmitted to the lens-axicon assembly via a mirror (Thorlabs, NB1-K14). This assembly consists of a plano-convex lens (Thorlabs, LA1172-AB N-BK7, f = 400 mm) and an axicon with a 0.5° physical angle (Thorlabs, AX2505-C), projecting the beam onto the battery surface as an annulus ring through a dichroic mirror (Thorlabs, DMSP1000) for exciting focused ultrasound. Optimization strategies of this ring-shaped illumination to suit various battery types are detailed in note S2. Optical detection of ultrasonic signals is performed using a 1550-nm laser Doppler Vibrometer (Polytec, PSV-500-HV), placed at the ring center on the battery rear surface to measure the out-of-plane displacement from transmitted ultrasound.

During cycling, ultrasonic data are synchronized with an eight-channel electrochemical workstation (CT-4008Tn-5V12A-S1 model; Neware Technology Limited) that manages charging and discharging from 0 to 100% SoC, while simultaneously recording ultrasonic and electrochemical data for real-time analysis using a data acquisition unit (National Instruments, NI PXIe-5160). The LEUS scanning detection, introduced in note S3 and fig. S16, is used for simultaneous multichannel testing. A custom software program on the computer, developed with LabVIEW 2019 (National Instruments), controls two lasers, motor stages, an electrochemical workstation, and the acquisition of both ultrasonic and electrochemical signals, ensuring synchronized control and data integration.

Cell cycling and data generation

To evaluate the proposed method’s effectiveness against complex battery fade mechanisms and manufacturing variability, ultrasonic and electrochemical data were generated using 40 commercial LFP and NCM cells (models GRP4635062, GRP5519096, and GRP5017095, Shenzhen Grepow Battery Co. Ltd., China, with nominal capacities of 650, 1000, and 700 mAh, respectively). Long-term cycling of all cells was conducted under various charging and discharging conditions at room temperature. Detailed information about battery parameters and aging profiles is provided in tables S1 and S2.

Before aging, each battery was examined using a conventional ultrasound probe imaging to assess cell structure integrity and potential issues from electrolyte unwetting and manufacturing defects (fig. S17). Then, batteries were subjected to aging experiments via repeated cycles under various CC-CV fast-charging protocols with C-rates spanning 6 to 10 C and a current cutoff of C/20, and fast-discharging protocols at C-rates from 8 to 16 C. These C-rates were calculated on the basis of the nominal capacity of batteries, with voltage windows ranging from 2.0 to 3.65 V for LFP and 3.0 to 4.2 V for NCM, following manufacturer recommendations. A rest time of 30 s was maintained between the charging and discharging phases. Cycle life was defined as the number of cycles until the discharge capacity fell below 90% of the nominal capacity. SoC was calculated via high-accuracy coulomb counting using precisely measured current data, with full-charge and cutoff boundaries serving as anchoring points. Drift was constrained through defined boundary conditions and calibration steps under controlled laboratory settings. SoH was determined from standardized reference capacity tests conducted under controlled conditions (fixed temperature and defined charge/discharge protocol). These measurements serve as experimental benchmarks rather than real-time inferred estimates. After prolonged cycling, spatially resolved ultrasonic images of the battery at different SoC and SoH states are provided in figs. S18 and S19. The spatial ultrasonic mappings were acquired within a selected region of interest (ROI) located in the central active-stack area of the pouch cell, rather than across the entire cell surface. The ROI was intentionally chosen to avoid tabs, sealing edges, and other macroscopic structural components that may introduce boundary-related ultrasonic artifacts.

LEUS measurements were synchronized with electrochemical testing to simultaneously capture ultrasonic and electrochemical data during charging, discharging, and rest steps (Fig. 3). Electrochemical data were used to determine battery SoC and SoH levels based on capacity variations. Ultrasonic signals were transformed into scalograms via continuous wavelet transformation (detailed in note S4), providing detailed insights into battery states: scalograms obtained during charging and discharging processes were categorized by SoC levels, whereas those from postcharge rest steps were labeled with SoH levels. Each data unit, comprising scalograms and corresponding SoC and SoH labels under various high-rate operation conditions, was used for model verification.

Machine-learning model development

The proposed model uses a hybrid architecture combining a ResNet50 CNN with transformer encoders, leveraging the inherent strengths of both to improve predictive ability. We adapted the ResNet-50 by replacing its original fully connected layer with a linear layer tailored for ultrasonic scalogram data, facilitating effective feature extraction and dimensionality reduction. A transformer encoder then processes these features, which uses multiple layers and self-attention mechanisms to capture complex dependencies within the scalogram. The output of the transformer encoder feeds into a fully connected layer that performs regression tasks to predict battery states. Some key hyperparameters, such as dropout rate, L2 regularization, and Adam optimizer learning rate, were tuned to avoid overfitting. Details on comparative ML algorithms (43–47), such as RNN, CNN, and LSTM, are provided in note S1.

Inputs for the ML model are ultrasonic scalograms derived from one-dimensional ultrasonic signals via wavelet analysis, labeled according to electrochemical testing results (Fig. 4). The dataset is split into training and validation sets, with 80% for training and the remainder for validation, selected randomly to eliminate order-related bias. Model training involves dynamic input scaling and gradient clipping to stabilize learning, while validation on a separate set helps monitor and mitigate overfitting. Systematic hyperparameter tuning (table S4) and cross-validation ensure robust performance across various battery chemistries, nominal capacities, and usage conditions. Using the Adam optimizer and a dynamic learning rate schedule, the model minimizes the mean squared error, closely aligning predictions with actual battery states. Transfer learning strategy further allows the model to adapt the established architecture to new conditions for state prediction without needing to train from scratch. All data processing and wavelet analysis are conducted in MATLAB, while battery state prediction using the ML model is implemented in Python scripts using PyTorch packages.
