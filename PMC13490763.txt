Thermophilization: Concepts, Methods, and Implications of Changes in Community Composition due to Global Warming
Authors: Kenneth J Feeley, Evelin Iseli, Billur Bektaş, Rubén D Manzanedo, Crystal H McMichael, Jake M Alexander, Janneke Hille Ris Lambers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWith global warming, many species will shift their geographic ranges coldward (i.e., towards higher latitudes and elevations) to remain within suitable thermal conditions. At the community level, these range shifts result in “thermophilization”—the increasing relative abundance of warm‐adapted (“thermophilic”) species and the concomitant decrease of cold‐adapted species over time. Quantifying rates of thermophilization provides a powerful approach to assessing responses to rising temperatures at the community level, particularly in high‐diversity systems where the detailed spatial data needed to calculate species‐level responses to warming for thousands of individual species are often unavailable. This review synthesizes our current understanding of thermophilization across terrestrial, marine, and aquatic systems. We detail the most common methods used to quantify thermophilization, primarily through the calculation of the Community Temperature Index (CTI, °C), which aggregates species' thermal affiliations into a single community‐level metric that can then be tracked through time to calculate an annualized thermophilization rate (TR, °C year−1). We evaluate the advantages of this approach, such as its ability to partition the contributions of underlying demographic processes (i.e., recruitment vs. growth vs. mortality, or colonization vs. extinction) and/or individual species and species groups, its flexibility in using diverse data sources like herbarium records and citizen science, and its ability to test relationships between TR and site‐specific conditions such as elevation, landcover, and anthropogenic disturbances. We also discuss some of the standing challenges and limitations of the thermophilization approach, including a lack of standardization that hinders large‐scale synthetic analyses, the challenge of propagating estimation errors from the species‐ to community‐level, and difficulties in interpreting and communicating results. We then discuss the implications of thermophilization for ecosystem stability and function, including the potential for biodiversity losses and altered ecosystem services. Finally, we highlight several important and exciting avenues for future research on climate‐driven shifts in community composition.Keywords: biotic attrition, Community Temperature Index, global warming, niche truncation, Species Temperature Index, thermal niche, xerophilization

Ecological responses to climate change are occurring across all scales of biological organization, from individuals to ecosystems, with responses at each level intrinsically linked to those above and below it (MacDonald et al. 2008; Parmesan 2006; Walther et al. 2002; Figure 1). At the individual level, organisms can respond through shifts in physiology, phenology, behavior, and gene expression. These individual adjustments scale up to the population‐ and species‐levels, resulting in changes in the distributions of species and potential shifts in genetic variation. At the community level, these dynamics emerge as shifts in the relative abundances and compositions of interacting species (Feeley and Zuleta 2022). Ultimately, these compositional changes can alter community‐ and ecosystem‐level properties, such as productivity, diversity, and stability, as well as the provisioning of ecosystem services, which will directly impact human livelihoods (Stemkovski et al. 2026; Tilman 1998).

Climate‐driven responses across levels of ecological organization. Community thermophilization arises from interacting ecological processes operating across hierarchical scales. At the individual level, environmental changes trigger shifts in individual fitness metrics. These changes scale up to the population level, where shifts in demography, occurrences, and density drive species range shifts. Range shifts reshape community composition, richness, and spatial structure, producing measurable changes in community thermophilization metrics and diversity. Across landscapes, community reorganization cascades to the ecosystem level, altering ecosystem functions and services and, consequently, the capacity of ecosystems to provision services.

In this narrative review, we focus on community‐level responses to global warming, and specifically on temperature‐driven changes in species composition and the phenomenon of thermophilization (see Section 8). A growing number of studies have tested for thermophilization in different communities (floral, faunal, and fungal) and systems (terrestrial, marine, and aquatic), but these studies remain disparate, with different motivations, methods, and terminology. Our goal with this review is to help synthesize key insights from seminal thermophilization studies to help motivate future large‐scale or comparative research and generalizations. Our intention is not to provide a comprehensive review or meta‐analysis of every documented case of thermophilization. Rather, we provide an overview of the process of thermophilization and the methods through which thermophilization rates are commonly calculated and assessed, highlighting exemplar studies of thermophilization in different communities and systems (the literature included in this review were selected primarily based on the authors' a priori knowledge of the subjects followed by “pearl growing” and “snowballing” literature searches (Greenhalgh and Peacock 2005; Schlosser et al. 2006)). We also indicate some of the most important strengths and challenges of measuring thermophilization, especially as compared to other common approaches for assessing species‐ and population‐level responses to rising temperatures. We conclude our review by discussing the implications of thermophilization for ecosystem diversity, structure and function, and highlight several exciting directions for future research.

Temperature‐Driven Changes in Composition

One of the principal ways that species respond to climate change is through shifts in their geographic ranges (Freeman et al. 2026; Hille Ris Lambers et al. 2026; Parmesan and Yohe 2003; Walther et al. 2002). More specifically, as temperatures increase due to global warming, most species are expected to shift their ranges coldward—that is, away from lower latitudes and/or elevations as those areas become unsuitably hot, and into higher latitudes and/or elevations where temperatures become newly suitable. Although these range shifts will have important implications for the species themselves (e.g., potentially leading to range contractions and increased risks of population declines or even extinctions (Wiens 2016)), there will also be broader implications due to associated changes in composition as some species increase in local abundance or colonize new areas, and other species decrease in abundance or go locally extinct. Specifically, it is expected that rising temperatures will cause community thermophilization, which is the increasing relative abundance of heat‐loving, thermophilic species and a concomitant decrease in the relative abundance of less‐thermophilic species through time (changes in relative abundance can be due to the altered population sizes of species already present in the community and/or local extinction and colonization) (Gottfried et al. 2012). Depending on the speed of thermophilization, communities can remain at equilibrium with temperature, or they may fall out of equilibrium with rising temperatures due to slow thermophilization rates, resulting in increasing thermal lags. Given the importance of species composition in determining ecosystem diversity, structure, stability, function, and services (Carrick and Forsythe 2020; Díaz et al. 2006), it is critical that we understand thermophilization as well as the context and consequences of climate change at the community and ecosystem‐levels (Michalet et al. 2025).

From Species Temperature Indices to community thermophilization. (A) The Species Temperature Index (STI) is typically estimated by combining occurrence data (e.g., from presence/absence records, herbaria collections, monitoring schemes, landings records, or expert knowledge maps) with spatially explicit environmental data. Temperature values associated with each geographic occurrence are extracted and used to estimate probability of occurrence curves. STIs are typically calculated as the mean or optimum temperature on the probability curve. (B) The Community Temperature Index (CTI) is then calculated for each focal community and at each time‐step by averaging the STIs of all species occurring in each plot, often weighted by abundance. Changes in species' relative abundances and/or local species extinctions and colonizations influence the mean STI, resulting in shifts in CTI through time. An increase in CTI indicates thermophilization—that is, a shift towards greater dominance by warm‐affiliated species. Comparing temporal trends in CTI (thermophilization rate, TR) with warming rates (climate velocity or Δt) provides a measure of thermal lag, indicating the pace of community adjustment relative to changing temperatures.

Below, we describe each of these steps in detail, highlighting common methodological choices, alternative approaches, and their implications.

Characterizing Community Composition

First, the composition of species in a focal community is characterized at a given time. Community composition is typically characterized using established species lists (e.g., check lists) or by censusing all the species or individuals present in a defined area (e.g., with plot‐ or transect‐based surveys). Alternatively, the species in a focal region or area can be determined through data sources such as paleoecological records (Box 1), fish landings, herbarium records, natural history collections, or citizen science records (e.g., iNaturalist (Jensen et al. 2025; White et al. 2023), ebird (Sullivan et al. 2009), The Amazon Fish database (Jézéquel et al. 2020), Pl@ntNET (Bonnet et al. 2020, etc.)). For example, Feeley et al. (2020) characterized the plant composition of ecoregions throughout North and South America based on whether or not species had been collected in the ecoregion during defined time periods.

Thermophilization in the paleorecord.

Although thermophilization is commonly used to describe responses of modern communities to modern anthropogenic warming, the general concepts and methods were originally developed for reconstructing paleo climates and are still widely used for this purpose (Birks 2014; Birks et al. 2010; Sachs et al. 1977). For example, paleoecologists can identify pollen, diatoms, organic biomarkers such as GDGTs (Glycerol Dialkyl Glycerol Tetraethers), and other forms of organic tissue samples preserved at different depths and ages in sedimentary archives that retain temporal continuity (e.g., lake sediment cores or packrat middens). Based on the assemblages present in each sample, the past climate can be inferred based on the estimated thermal affiliations (i.e., STIs) of the taxa, typically using transfer function models. The most common transfer function modelling approaches used with paleoecological data include Weighted Averaging Partial Least Squares and Modern Analog Approaches, though newer multi‐ensemble machine learning approaches are becoming more widespread (Sun et al. 2024).

Pollen grains from plants are one of the most widely used proxies to reconstruct past temperatures. Temperature trends across Europe have been reconstructed for the last ~12,000 years using a series of over 500 lake sediment records and modern analog matching algorithms. Modern analog matching uses a calibration dataset of modern microfossil assemblages with the environmental conditions from which they are found (Simpson 2012), which is equivalent to looking at the CTIs. Specifically, in the reconstructions of European climate, > 2000 samples of modern pollen were collected and linked with modern climate parameters for each location (Davis et al. 2003). Similar endeavors have occurred across temperate North America (Kerwin et al. 2004). Temperature changes from the last glacial period until the present in Central America have also been reconstructed using pollen‐based transfer functions and the plant's thermal optima (Correa‐Metrio et al. 2012). Pollen assemblages have even been used for global syntheses of temperature change for the last ~21,000 years (Bartlein et al. 2011). Paleoecological data are sparser in other regions of the world, but temperature reconstructions using pollen data in the Andes of South America have shown that directional species turnover over the last ~21,000 years can be used to infer approximately 6°C postglacial warming (Bush et al. 2004).

The use of organic biomarkers, particularly GDGTs, to reconstruct past temperatures is growing (Loomis et al. 2012; Pearson et al. 2011; Tierney 2012). GDGTs are derived from bacteria, for which, like other species, STIs can be estimated. GDGT reconstructions of temperature have been used to identify several climatic changes over the last ~4000 years in China (Hu et al. 2015), and reconstructions from other regions, including the USA, South America, and Africa, span glacial–interglacial time scales (Loomis et al. 2012; Watson et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2024). A benefit of using non‐plant derived material (such as GDGTs or diatoms) to reconstruct paleotemperatures is that the thermophilization arrest of plants or other species can then be compared with the estimated temperature changes to determine thermal lags.

Beyond taxon‐based approaches, paleoecologists have also used functional proxies of thermal adaptation to reconstruct past climates (Spicer 2000; Spicer et al. 2021; Wolfe 1993; Yang et al. 2015). One of the most widely used examples is leaf margin analysis, which exploits the strong relationship between the proportion of woody dicot species with entire (untoothed) leaf margins and mean annual temperature. This relationship is grounded in physiological and biomechanical constraints on leaf form under different thermal regimes and has been extensively validated across broad climatic gradients (Peppe et al. 2011; Wilf 1997). In southern South America, Aizen and Ezcurra (2008) demonstrated that community‐level variation in leaf margin composition provides a robust and taxonomically independent proxy for thermal conditions. Changes through time in the functional composition of fossil leaf assemblages can therefore be used to infer directional thermal shifts in plant communities, conceptually analogous to CTI‐based reconstructions, but applicable even when species‐level identification of samples is not possible. Together, taxon‐based indices and functional proxies, such as leaf margin composition, highlight that thermophilization‐like signals have long been inferred from community assemblages, reinforcing the conceptual continuity between paleoecological climate reconstructions and modern CTI‐based analyses of thermophilization in response to anthropogenic warming (Blois et al. 2025).

Estimating Species‐Specific Thermal Affinities

Next, a measure of each incumbent species' thermal affiliation, preference, or optimum is determined—often referred to as the Species Temperature Index (STI) (Alba and Chamberlain 2025; Devictor et al. 2008; Oliver et al. 2017). STIs are most often based on the baseline geographic distributions of the species, under the assumption that species' occurrences reflect their realized temperature niches (Colwell and Rangel 2009). These distributions can be estimated from literature reviews, range maps, census networks, or from collated observations or collections from a defined starting period (Burrows et al. 2020). For example, for many species the coordinates of known occurrences can be extracted from historical collections available through GBIF.org or other online repositories (Sparrius et al. 2018). A measure of temperature (often Mean Annual Temperature = MAT) at each occurrence location is then estimated from extrapolated climate rasters such as WorldClim (Fick and Hijmans 2017), CHELSA (Karger et al. 2017), TerraClim (Abatzoglou et al. 2018), or Interpolated Sea Surface Temperature (Reynolds et al. 2007) datasets. Ideally, these temperature estimates should be from the same time period as the occurrences and the occurrences should be independent of those used to characterize composition (although this is not always the case). The mean or median of the temperatures across the occurrence locations is then calculated as an estimate of the STI. Alternatively, presence or presence/absence data for a species can be used to create models of occurrence probability vs. temperature (e.g., species distribution models or logistic regression models), which can better account for variation in sampling efforts and the covariation of temperature with other environmental variables (De Frenne et al. 2013). The temperature with maximum probability of occurrence in these models can be used as the STI (Dietz et al. 2020; Kullberg and Feeley 2022; Rosenblad et al. 2023). In some cases, it may be preferable to use the maximum or minimum temperatures where a species occurs, or some other distributional moment that is better related to the hypotheses being tested, as the STI. For example, Fortier et al. (2024) were interested in the effects of extreme high temperatures on lowland tropical forests, so characterized the species composition of several small Amazonian tree plots using STIs based on the 95% quantiles of maximum temperatures at the species' known occurrence locations. As an alternative to occurrence records, STIs can be estimated using expert‐derived ecological indicator systems, ordinal classifications of thermophily, or estimates of species' physiological tolerances.

Aggregating Species‐Level Thermal Affinities at the Community Level

Once the STIs have been estimated for all species, they are then combined with the community composition data to calculate a compositionally based temperature estimate, or Community Temperature Index (CTI) (Devictor et al. 2008). CTI is typically calculated as the mean (or median or other distributional moment) of the STIs of all species occurring in the focal community at the time the census was conducted. Depending on the hypotheses being tested, the CTI can be calculated using the unweighted mean of STIs (i.e., all species counted equally) or the mean of STIs weighted by the species' relative abundances, with abundances based on various metrics such as number of individuals per species, total biomass per species, percent cover per species, etc. In most cases, a weighted mean is preferable since the resultant CTI is usually better correlated with temperature at the community location than the CTI based on unweighted means, but sometimes abundance information is not available or studies are focused on the arrival or loss (i.e., presence vs. absence) of species rather than on changes in abundance.

Rather than a simple weighted or unweighted mean, a smaller number of studies have used transfer functions to estimate CTI based on the species composition. For example, Bertrand et al. (2011), Pacheco‐Riaño et al. (2023), and Iseli et al. (2025) used Weighted Averaging Partial Least Squares to model CTI from the species compositions of plant species recorded in French, Norwegian, and Swiss floristic surveys, respectively. The use of transfer functions is required if STIs are not expressed in temperature units (i.e., °C). For example, Lenoir et al. (2013) used transfer functions to model the local temperatures across Northern Europe based on vascular plant community composition data and STIs based on ordinal species‐indicator values for temperature (see section below on “Integration of different data sources”).

Tracking Community‐Level Thermal Affinities Through Time to Estimate Thermophilization Rates

To test for thermophilization and changes in composition through time, the above procedure is repeated to calculate CTI for the same focal community using census data from a different time (but using the same STIs as calculated above). The annualized difference in the community's CTI is its thermophilization rate (TR), such that a positive TR indicates increasing relative abundances of thermophilic species through time, consistent with the predicted coldward migrations of species and/or the dieback of heat‐intolerant species. If CTI is calculated for more than two time periods, TR can be calculated for each separate time interval, or an overall TR can be calculated (e.g., based on the average TR across all intervals, the overall change in CTI from first to final census, or the regression slope of CTI vs. year).

Comparing Observed Versus Expected Thermophilization Rates to Estimate Thermal Lag

Once TR is calculated, it is often compared to expected rates of change based on concurrent regional warming to calculate a community thermal tracking score or estimate of thermal lag (Freeman et al. 2021). In most cases the expected rate of change that is used to calculate the thermal lag assumes a perfect relationship between CTI and temperature (i.e., a 0.5°C increase in temperature should cause a 0.5°C increase in CTI); however, this may not be realistic since documented relationships between CTI and temperature are typically less than 1:1 (e.g., reported slope values range from < 0.25 for European birds, Swiss plants, and Swiss butterflies, to ~0.85 for British near shore marine animals and new world plant communities (Burrows et al. 2020; Cuni‐Sanchez et al. 2024; Devictor et al. 2012; Fadrique et al. 2018; Feeley et al. 2020; Feeley et al. 2013; Kwon 2017; Roth et al. 2014; Scherrer et al. 2022)). The reason for this shallow slope has not been fully explored, but it is potentially attributable to the inclusion of species whose distributions are determined primarily by factors other than temperature (e.g., edaphic specialists or species responding primarily to water availability) or due to niche truncation (Adde et al. 2024; Bush et al. 2018; Chevalier et al. 2024; Feeley et al. 2020; Feeley and Silman 2010; Nogués Bravo 2024), which will flatten the overall CTI vs. temperature relationship (Box 2). Another possible explanation for shallow CTI vs. temperature slopes is that even baseline communities may not be at equilibrium with temperature but rather may still be responding to past climate change or climate events (Blonder et al. 2015; Sandel et al. 2025; Svenning and Skov 2004). Whatever the cause(s), the implication of this shallow slope is that we should expect smaller changes in CTI per unit warming (e.g., if the slope of the initial CTI vs. temperature is 0.5, we should expect only 0.5°C change in CTI for each 1°C of warming (Devictor et al. 2008; Kwon 2017)). To improve the temperature vs. CTI relationship, a transfer function can be used to generate estimates of CTI more closely align with actual temperatures (as in Bertrand et al. 2011), or the predicted changes in CTI and/or subsequent thermal lag rates can be calculated accounting for the observed CTI vs. temperature relationship.

Niche truncation.

Niche truncation occurs when the range of environmental conditions currently available on Earth represents only a subset of a species' fundamental niche (i.e., the full suite of conditions it is physiologically capable of tolerating) (Chevalier et al. 2024). Niche truncation can also be driven by competition or other biotic constraints that prevent species from occupying their full thermal range (Chevalier et al. 2024), as well as geographic or survey limits (i.e., where national borders or restricted sampling efforts cut off the observed species distribution even when hotter or colder areas exist and occurrences extend beyond these artificial limit).

As one example of niche truncation, an analysis of Australian plants showed that nearly half of examined species were able to tolerate thermal extremes beyond those occurring within their native distributions. This finding is consistent with the broader observation that warm range limits often do not coincide with physiological heat tolerances and that warm‐edge thermal limits are less conserved across regions than cold‐edge limits, indicating substantial context dependence of realized thermal niches (Araújo et al. 2013; Pellissier et al. 2013; Sunday et al. 2012).

Since niche truncation can make the hottest or coldest portions of a species' niche unobservable to researchers relying on observational occurrence data, it can introduce systematic biases into estimates of the Species Temperature Index (STI) and Community Temperature Index (CTI) (Seki et al. 2026). For the most‐thermophilic species, the absence of hotter areas will result in underestimates of their true thermal optimum; in contrast, for the least thermophilic species, the absence of colder areas leads to an overestimate of their thermal optimum. This dual‐ended bias creates a compression of STI distributions. When these STIs are aggregated into the CTI, the compression propagates, resulting in underestimates at high temperatures and overestimates at low temperatures, thereby flattening the CTI vs. temperature slope. Recent simulations demonstrate that even when species distributions are not physically truncated, the interaction between sampling effort and niche limits can cause thermophilization rates (TR) to be substantially lower than actual warming rates (Seki et al. 2026). This can inflate estimates of thermal lags or climatic debts, as changes in CTI fail to capture the full magnitude of the compositional change (Seki et al. 2026).

The Contributions of Underlying Demographic Processes and Individual Species or Species Groups

Thermophilization can arise from the colonization of warm‐adapted species, the local loss of cold‐adapted species, shifts in the relative abundance of persistent taxa, or complex combinations of these dynamics. To understand and disentangle the relative influence of different mechanisms and species, post hoc analyses can partition the overall TR into the contributions of underlying drivers. These analyses may include partitioning TR into species gains vs. losses vs. abundance changes of persisting taxa (including in some cases the effects of recruitment vs. mortality vs. growth), and contributions of specific species or functional groups.

The separate contributions of local extinction vs. colonization to TR can be determined by comparing CTIs calculated separately for the groups of species lost, gained, or persisting between censuses in the focal community. For example, Borderieux et al. (2024) looked at understory plant communities in 80 forest ecoregions of France, finding that 90% of ecoregions had a positive TR. By looking at the individual contributions of species with declining vs. increasing numbers of plot occurrences, they were able to conclude that thermophilization was “entirely driven by extinction.” Oliver et al. (2017) also found that for British birds, thermophilization was primarily attributable to the loss of the less‐thermophilic, cold‐associated species. Another approach for distinguishing between the loss of cold‐adapted species and the gain of warm‐adapted species has been to examine temporal changes in the warm and cold tails of STI distributions within communities, or in resampled CTI distributions through time. Using this latter approach, Caron et al. (2026) concluded that thermophilization in European forest tree communities was driven more by gains in warm‐adapted species than by the loss of cold‐adapted species, whereas De Frenne et al. (2013) found that thermophilization of understory plant communities in temperate Europe and North America had been driven by both gains of thermophilic species and losses of cold‐adapted species. Even in the absence of colonization and extinction, compositional shifts can still occur due to changes in the abundances of species that persist in the community throughout the entire study period. For instance, whereas British butterfly communities did not show evidence of overall thermophilization through species turnover, they did exhibit a signal of increasing abundances of their warm‐associated species (Devictor et al. 2012). Comparing weighted and unweighted metrics can potentially help clarify whether thermophilization is caused primarily by turnover or changes in abundance.

In the case of long‐lived, individually tracked organisms with repeated censuses—most commonly trees or other long‐lived plants—it may be possible to further decompose the demographic processes underlying thermophilization into recruitment vs. growth vs. mortality. This decomposition of demographic effects can achieved by calculating what the TR would have been in the absence of one or more of these processes. For example, the contribution of mortality to TR can be determined by calculating the difference in the focal community's CTI when estimated using the initial sizes of all individuals recorded in the first census vs. its CTI when estimated using the initial sizes of only the individuals that survived through the census interval (i.e., excluding growth and recruitment). Using this method, Feeley et al. (2013) showed that thermophilization of Costa Rican montane forests was due primarily to “the disproportionate death of highland vs. lowland species”. Likewise, Farfan‐Rios et al. (2025) showed that thermophilization in Andean and Amazonian tree communities was “driven primarily by tree mortality and decreased growth of highland (cool‐adapted) species rather than an influx of lowland species with higher thermal optima”. In their study of western USA forests, Rosenblad et al. (2023) found that tree species recruiting into census plots had STIs that were similar to the communities' initial CTIs and that thermophilization was mostly attributable to elevated mortality of species with low STIs. It is important to note that this level of demographic detail is highly contingent on life history and data type and is generally not possible for short‐lived taxa like annual plants or insects, or in systems where only species‐level occurrence records are available.

Although thermophilization is a community‐level metric, techniques analogous to those described above can be used assess the contributions of individual species, or groups of species sharing traits of interest, to the overall plot‐level changes (Bertrand et al. 2011). For example, Alba and Chamberlain (2025) determined the relative contributions of different bird species to the thermophilization of avian communities in the Italian Alps over a 13‐year period. Using a jackknife resampling approach, they recalculated TR for their communities of interest with each individual species removed, then used the difference in TR with and without the species as a measure of its influence (e.g., if the TR without the species was slower than with the species, then that species contributed to an increase of CTI through time). Using this same approach, Curley et al. (2022) found that less than half of species contributed positively to the increasing CTI of eastern North American bird communities through time. Subsequent analyses can then be used to determine if there are any species characteristics associated with their influence on CTI. In the case of birds in the Italian Alps, the species contributing most to faster TR were those associated with alpine grasslands and shrubby treeline habitats (Alba and Chamberlain 2025), whereas in the case of eastern North American birds, species with high STI had the greatest impact on TR (Curley et al. 2022). An alternative approach is to calculate the contributions of individual species to a community's thermophilization as the product of their abundance changes through time and their “temperature anomaly,” which is the difference between the species' STI and the community's initial CTI. This approach was used by Borderieux et al. (2024) to look at contributions of different species to the thermophilization of understory plant communities in France. This same method was also used by Dobson et al. (2025) to analyze thermophilization in a set of experimentally warmed plant communities, showing that the top contributing species were not closely related either phylogenetically or in terms of their functional traits.

Another approach for assessing the influence of taxonomy or functional traits on thermophilization compares TR between different communities. For example, Caron et al. (2026) compared thermophilization rates of mature vs. young European forest tree communities and found that thermophilization was seven times faster in juveniles than in mature trees. In contrast, Duque et al. (2015) looked at thermophilization rates in the Colombian Andes and found that the average rate of thermophilization for young trees did not differ significantly from that of larger trees. Duque et al. also found that amongst the mature tree communities, changes in tree composition were significantly associated with the relative representation of different seed dispersal modes in the community. Specifically, thermophilization rates were slowest in communities dominated by species with dispersal modes associated with short‐distance dispersal (i.e., ballistic dispersal, gravity dispersal, hydrochory, and myrmecochory), whereas communities with greater relative abundances of wind‐ or vertebrate‐dispersed species exhibited faster TRs (Duque et al. 2015). Kiebacher et al. (2023) compared thermophilization of bryophyte vs. vascular plant communities in Switzerland and found that TR was more than twice as fast for bryophytes, likely due in part to their greater dispersal capacity. Khaliq et al. (2024) compared thermophilization of various temperate communities and found that the predictors of thermophilization differed between terrestrial vs. freshwater systems and between different taxonomic groups. For example, thermophilization rates declined with increasing thermal niche breadth in all groups except freshwater insects, for which thermophilization rates increased strongly with greater niche breadth and body size (Khaliq et al. 2024).

Some Strengths of the Thermophilization Approach

Thermophilization studies provide important insights into the effects of global warming at the community level, which can ultimately help us to understand and predict changes at the ecosystem level. A primary advantage of the thermophilization approach is that it can detect subtle, cumulative signals across a broad suite of taxa that might be obscured by sampling noise or idiosyncratic responses of individual species (Elmendorf et al. 2015; Ferner et al. 2002). By aggregating data across many species, the community metric provides a synthetic “emergent property” that is highly sensitive to climate warming (Elmendorf et al. 2015). In addition, thermophilization analyses have several practical advantages. Namely, thermophilization analyses can take advantage of various data sources, which helps to increase geographic and taxonomic coverage, thereby reducing some of the biases that plague other climate change studies (Feeley et al. 2017; Metcalfe et al. 2025). Another advantage is that thermophilization rates can be tied directly to site‐level factors. For example, whether disturbances facilitate or hinder climate change responses, or how responses vary with topography, is difficult to address with species‐level range shift studies but can be readily assessed by comparing thermophilization rates of communities at different locations and under different conditions.

Integration of Different Data Sources

A significant practical strength of the thermophilization approach is its flexibility in utilizing diverse data sources for estimating community composition and STIs. Potential data sources include non‐traditional or informal records such as fish landing reports, herbarium collections, and citizen science observations (e.g., Cheung et al. 2013; Gaüzère et al. 2020; Seki et al. 2026), which can help reduce the geographic and taxonomic biases common in climate change research (Feeley et al. 2017).

Alternatively, STIs can be based on expert knowledge or rankings (Landolt and Bäumler 2010; Tichý et al. 2023) that classify species on ordinal scales from least‐ to most‐thermophilic based on their presumed environmental preferences or affiliations. For example, STIs based on ecological indicator values have been used to test for thermophilization of vegetation in Switzerland (Boch et al. 2026; Kiebacher et al. 2023; Scherrer et al. 2022), Germany (Braziunas et al. 2024), France (Dietz et al. 2020), Italy's Apennine Mountains (Evangelista et al. 2015), and alpine systems across Europe (Gottfried et al. 2012; Zolotova et al. 2022). STIs can even be just a simpler categorization of species into coarse thermophilic classes based on their physiological tolerances or distributions. For example, Di Nuzzo et al. (2021) looked at temperature‐driven changes in the composition of lichens, bryophytes, and vascular plants in the Apennine Mountains by categorizing species as “cryophilous,” “intermediate,” “thermophilous” on the basis of their thermal affiliations. As another example, Ntirugulirwa et al. (2023) tested for thermophilization in experimental tree stands in Rwanda simply by classifying species as affiliated with colder tropical montane forests vs. lower, warmer transitional forests.

Better Geographic Coverage

There is a recognized sparsity of studies looking at climate change effects in tropical systems (Feeley et al. 2015, 2017). This paucity is especially pronounced in studies looking at species range shifts since these analyses need detailed information about species occurrences over large spatial and temporal scales. For example, Parker et al. (2024) used the “largest compilation of geographic range change observations to date, comprised of 33,016 potential redistributions across 12,009 species, to formally assess within‐ and cross‐species coverage and biases and to motivate future data collection.” Unsurprisingly, they showed concentrations of terrestrial studies in Europe, the USA, and South Africa, and concentrations of marine studies in the waters off Europe, the USA, southeastern Australia, southern Africa, and Japan. In contrast, they found an almost complete lack of terrestrial or marine studies from the tropics (Parker et al. 2024). This finding mirrors those of previous reviews and meta‐analyses that had likewise all shown strong geographic biases against low latitude species and systems (e.g., Chen et al. 2011; Colwell and Feeley 2025; Freeman et al. 2021; Lenoir et al. 2020; Lenoir and Svenning 2015); although, notably, since these reviews there has been at least one new study that looked at species range shifts for over 1000 species of Mesoamerican cloudforest plants, finding a mean upslope shift of > 2 m year−1 over the past four decades (Ramírez‐Barahona et al. 2025).

As discussed by Parker et al. (2024), the absence of studies from the tropics may be due in part to disparities in research funding and infrastructure, as well as the inherent challenges of working in remote systems and developing nations. Additionally, mapping changes in species ranges generally does not work well in poorly sampled areas or in high‐diversity systems where there is limited historical and/or modern information about individual species, and where there may not be clear focal species receiving special attention. Adding to this problem, tropical species tend to be more specialized than temperate species, with narrower thermal niche breadths and smaller geographic ranges (McCain 2009). Greater specialization makes it harder to accurately map tropical species' ranges, and especially changes in species' range limits, through time, since a greater spatial resolution of censuses across gradients is required (e.g., across a network of > 60 1‐ha plots in the Andes to Amazon gradient of the Bolivian and Peruvian Andes, > ½ of the ca. 2000 tree species have only been recorded in just a single plot, making it impossible to estimate even just their current elevational range limits without installing many new plots to increase sample resolution (Farfan‐Rios 2019)). This bias against high‐diversity, low‐latitude systems is particularly worrisome given the predicted sensitivity of these systems to climate change (Pinsky et al. 2019), the expected differences in responses between temperate and tropical systems (e.g., Colwell and Feeley 2025; Freeman et al. 2021), and the many important services that tropical systems provide for supporting human livelihoods (Fedele et al. 2021).

In comparison to range shift studies, thermophilization studies have a more equitable geographic distribution, including many studies from the tropics and other high‐diversity systems (Freeman et al. 2021; Hille Ris Lambers et al. 2026; Khaliq et al. 2024). The improved coverage of thermophilization studies is likely due in part to differences in the availability of the data required by species‐ vs. community‐level approaches as discussed above. For example, while there is very little information about the range limits of individual tree species in tropical South America, there are a large number of herbarium collections (Cardoso et al. 2017; Feeley 2015) that can potentially be used to estimate STIs, especially since STI is a measure of species' mean thermal affiliation and does not rely on mapping range limits. In addition, there are thousands of forest inventory plots, many of which have been repeatedly censused through time (Ter Steege et al. 2023), that can be used to characterize community composition. Likewise, for marine fishes, studies have been able to estimate thermophilization rates at coastal sites worldwide, including in the tropics, using fisheries landing data to characterize community composition combined with STIs based on species distribution models (Cheung et al. 2013).

It can even be argued that the thermophilization approach is better suited for studies in the tropics and other high‐diversity systems than it is in low‐diversity systems because the strength of the relationship between CTI and temperature may increase with higher diversity and with greater thermal niche specialization of the species. In depauperate communities that are composed of just a few species with broad thermal ranges, it is possible that there will be little change in composition due to warming, but in more‐biodiverse communities with many thermal specialists, small changes in climate should drive rapid directional shifts in composition. Supporting this hypothesis, Khaliq et al. (2024) found that thermophilization rates in temperate freshwater systems are faster where species have narrower thermal niches and Freeman et al. (2021) found that thermophilization rates of montane forests decrease with latitude. In addition, since many temperate species can potentially respond to warming through phenological shifts in addition to range shifts (Davis et al. 2022; Geissler et al. 2023; Pinsky et al. 2019), changes in their local composition may be less indicative of community‐level responses than in aseasonal tropical systems.

Analyses of Site‐Level Effects on Warming Responses

Another major strength of the thermophilization approach is that since TR is typically calculated for individual sites or communities, it can be readily associated with different environmental factors. For example, Feeley et al. (2020) found that thermophilization rates varied significantly between biomes. Similarly, Kiebacher et al. (2023) compared thermophilization between land use types in Switzerland and found higher rates for managed grasslands compared to forests. In tropical forests, Farfan‐Rios et al. (2025) found that thermophilization rates were slowest in low‐elevation forests (potentially due to an absence of a strong thermal gradient and the lack of available immigrant species (Colwell and Feeley 2025)), whereas Fadrique et al. (2018) found that thermophilization rates were slowest near the cloud base and treeline ecotones along the Andean slopes (potentially attributable to factors besides climate stabilizing those ecotones). These studies provide potential clues for the regional‐ and landscape‐scale factors that could prevent some habitats or communities from responding more quickly to changing climates.

The influence of temporally variable factors, like changes in forest cover and disturbances, can also be compared to thermophilization rates to provide insights into the drivers of compositional change. For example, De Frenne et al. (2013) and Zellweger et al. (2020) found that thermophilization rates in forest understory plants depended on changes in tree density, with lower rates of compositional change where forest cover increased, potentially due to microclimate buffering. Dietz et al. (2020), Tanner et al. (2022), and Rosenblad et al. (2023) explored how thermophilization was affected by wind, hurricanes, and insect damage, respectively, finding that all three disturbances increased thermophilization rates.

Some Challenges of the Thermophilization Approach

Like any approach, the use of thermophilization to assess climate change responses does have some important limitations and challenges. These challenges include difficulties and data limitations in calculating the thermal affiliations of species, the loss of information when aggregating species‐level affinities into community‐level metrics, and difficulties with estimating the rates of community change through time, as well as with interpreting and communicating any observed changes in composition.

Challenges for Calculating the Thermal Affiliations of Species (STI)

Although thermophilization approaches are comparatively robust and data‐efficient at the community level, they inherently depend on the quality of the data used to estimate species' thermal affiliations (i.e., STIs), which can introduce data constraints. Expert‐derived STI estimates (e.g., Ellenberg or Landolt indicator values) are largely restricted to certain taxa and certain regions (primarily vascular plants in Europe). Occurrence data are more widespread but remain biased towards data‐rich regions and areas of high research intensity (Anderson et al. 2016; Feeley and Silman 2011; Meyer et al. 2016), to certain taxonomic groups, and mostly to recent years. The thermophilization approach can propagate these biases to community‐level analyses by using STIs as fixed species attributes. Consequently, biases in global occurrence data may become embedded in all downstream community‐level thermophilization metrics, even when applied to communities that have been well‐sampled and characterized locally.

Even when occurrence data are well distributed across space and time, STIs derived from these data remain constrained by how well species' climatic niches are represented by geographic occurrences. Geographic distributions reflect realized niches shaped not only by climate but also by biotic interactions, dispersal limitation, and historical contingencies (Colwell and Rangel 2009). Niche truncation can also be a substantial problem since STIs calculated from occurrence records may systematically underestimate species' fundamental thermal tolerances and thus misrepresent species' potential responses to warming (Box 2). Also, since occurrence records come from the Anthropocene, the resultant estimate of species' distributions, and hence their STIs, may already be influenced by modern climate change and/or may still be out of equilibrium with climate due to past climate change (Blonder et al. 2015; Sandel et al. 2025; Svenning and Skov 2004).

Another limitation is that STIs are usually assigned at the species‐level and therefore ignore intraspecific variation and population‐level differentiation. Thermal tolerances may vary among populations, ecotypes, or haplotypes across a species' range, particularly in widespread taxa and/or taxa with high rates of adaptation (Khaliq et al. 2024). For example, recent work on alpine plants suggests that rising temperatures could alter the frequency of cold‐ and warm‐adapted haplotypes within species, leading to eco‐evolutionary dynamics that are not captured by species‐level summaries (Wessely et al. 2022). Collapsing such intraspecific variation into a single STI value can therefore obscure meaningful geographic differences in climatic sensitivity and lead to overgeneralized estimates of species' thermal affinities (Feeley 2015).

Challenges When Calculating Aggregate Temperature Indices of Communities (CTI)

Thermophilization studies typically compress complex information about composition and species‐level thermal affiliations into a single community‐level metric. Although this simplification is an appeal of the thermophilization approach, it does inevitably cause information loss, since individual species‐level trajectories, sensitivities, and vulnerabilities are obscured once integrated into the CTI. For example, when abundance‐ or cover‐weighted CTIs are used, the metric is largely driven by dominant species (Iseli et al. 2025). As a result, communities dominated by broadly tolerant or common species may show weak signals of thermophilization even when meaningful shifts are occurring among the rarer taxa. Weighting also introduces temporal and sampling sensitivities. Newly arriving species may require time to increase in size or abundance before affecting CTI (Svenning and Sandel 2013), and species approaching local extinction often persist at low abundances before disappearing. Consequently, abundance‐weighted metrics can obscure early compositional changes. In addition, short‐term environmental fluctuations or extreme events can temporarily alter abundances independent of long‐term climatic trends, thereby adding noise to thermophilization estimates.

Challenges When Calculating Rates of Community Change Through Time (TR)

The standard methods to calculate TR, as described above, require estimates of CTI for a focal community at different times. The need for repeated censuses can be a major limitation as it is not always possible to return to or to resurvey historical plots due to, for example, a lack of precise coordinates or changes in access. Nonetheless, if repeated censuses of individual plots are not available, it may still be possible to estimate the TR of communities using alternative techniques. For example, Borderieux et al. (2024) compared the CTI of plots that were paired based on their geographic proximity, similarity in elevation, and ecoregion occurrence, but that were censused at different times. Likewise, plots or censuses can be aggregated to create comparable past vs. recent pairs of communities. For example, Fadrique et al. (2018) calculated the CTI of multiple forest sites across the tropical and sub‐tropical Andes and calculated the relationship between CTI and the plots' baseline temperatures. They then calculated CTI and its relationship with temperature using other sets of plots at later times (but always using the temperature from the same initial baseline period). The later sets of plots were overlapping but not identical to the first. The mean TRs for different temperatures/elevations were then calculated as the annualized differences in estimated CTIs at different baseline temperatures. In such cases, the thermophilization rates reflect changes among communities sharing similar conditions rather than within‐community temporal dynamics (Fadrique et al. 2018).

Challenges With Interpreting and Communicating Thermophilization

Thermophilization characterizes directional changes in species composition through time but does not directly identify the processes responsible for those changes. As described above, post hoc analyses can help to partition the contributions of species or demographic processes, thereby clarifying which compositional shifts contributed to any observed pattern. However, the underlying drivers may remain unclear even when such components are distinguished. Colonization, extinction, or abundance shifts can reflect rising temperatures, but they can also be influenced by dispersal limitation, land‐use changes, disturbances, and/or biotic interactions. Thus, similar thermophilization patterns can result from different ecological drivers, highlighting the need for experimental studies (see section on “Experimental tests of thermophilization” below).

Finally, the concepts of a “community‐weighted mean species temperature index” and “thermophilization” are relatively abstract and can be difficult to communicate beyond a specialist audience. In other words, explaining what a change of +1°C in CTI means for biodiversity, local extinctions, or ecosystem functioning is not straightforward. This abstraction can also shift attention away from individual focal species and concrete distributional changes, which are often more intuitive and compelling for stakeholders and the public than changes in a composite index. Partitioning thermophilization rates into species gains/losses, identifying top contributing species, and linking thermophilization to concurrent changes in diversity and function are all strategies that can help to communicate the processes behind compositional changes, whereas linking the effects of changes in species composition for ecosystems services (see below) can help to highlight the importance of thermophilization for biodiversity and human livelihoods.

Some Implications of Thermophilization

Thermophilization will drive large‐scale shifts not only in community composition, but also in species diversity, habitat type, and structure. These changes will in turn have many important implications for ecosystem functioning and ecosystem services.

Thermophilization‐Driven Changes in Habitat Type and Structure

Changes in community composition through thermophilization can result in a transformation of habitat types. For example, experimental warming in the Rocky Mountains, USA, drove a transition from grassland meadows to shrublands (Harte and Shaw 1995). Ecotone shifts can be viewed as special cases of thermophilization, in which communities transition between distinct ecosystem types. Perhaps the most well‐known cases are coldward shifts in treeline, driving compositional changes from alpine or high‐latitude grassland species to tree species (Harsch et al. 2009; Lu et al. 2025). Given the change between distinct habitat types, ecotone shifts are often visible and measurable using remote imaging methods, thus allowing for large‐scale mapping (Zelazowski et al. 2023). For example, studies using timeseries of Landsat satellite images have found mean upward shifts of alpine treelines in North America (Corimanya et al. 2025) and worldwide (He et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2026). Like with the alpine treeline, satellite mapping of the north boreal treeline shows that forests have shifted significantly northward over the past several decades (Feng et al. 2026). Shifts of vegetation communities consistent with thermophilization have also been documented at other ecotones, such as the transitions between nival and alpine zones (Hamid et al. 2020; Walther et al. 2005; Zhang et al. 2025; Zimmer et al. 2018), boreal and temperate forests (Beckage et al. 2008; Evans and Brown 2017), cloudforest and lower montane tropical forests (Pouteau et al. 2018), and salt marshes and mangroves (Cavanaugh et al. 2019).

Thermophilization‐Driven Changes in Diversity

Changes in species ranges and composition are typically associated with changes in biodiversity. Specifically, some high elevation/latitude areas may increase in diversity through thermophilization (Haase et al. 2019; Midolo et al. 2025; Steinbauer et al. 2018). Lower elevation/latitude areas may instead decrease in diversity, especially if there are no nearby sources of more‐thermophilic species (i.e., “biotic attrition” (Blake and Loiselle 2024; Brodie et al. 2017; Colwell et al. 2008; Colwell and Feeley 2025)). The loss of local diversity can be amplified if species' ranges do not simply shift or “march,” but instead “crash” (i.e., overall range size decreases), “lean” (e.g., when population losses are faster from hotter areas than are population gains in cooler areas), or “retract” (e.g., when species are prevented from colonizing areas with newly suitable temperatures by other biotic or abiotic factors) (Lenoir and Svenning 2015). The loss of diversity in low elevation/latitude areas may be slowed or delayed if some species can tolerate warmer conditions than expected due to niche truncation (Feeley and Silman 2010). In addition to changes in local diversity, thermophilization may also lead to decreased beta diversity and biotic homogenization (i.e., less variation between local communities within a region) as only the more‐thermophilic species are favored (Savage and Vellend 2015)—though some studies have indicated homogenization to be slow and context‐dependent (Bektaş et al. 2024; Borderieux et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025). Losses of beta diversity, coupled with changes in local alpha diversity, can result in large‐scale losses of biodiversity within landscapes (i.e., decreased gamma diversity) as warming drives thermophilization (Colwell and Feeley 2025).

Thermophilization‐Driven Changes in Ecosystem Function

Thermophilization can cause indirect or unanticipated effects on ecosystem function due to intrinsic relationships between heat tolerance and other traits (Bowler and Böhning‐Gaese 2017). Plant species affiliated with high temperatures also tend to occur in wetter areas, suggesting a negative relationship between heat tolerance and drought tolerance (with some obvious exceptions in desert species). A negative association of heat and drought tolerances is consistent with the fact that many of the traits or mechanisms that plants use to lower leaf temperatures decrease water use efficiency (i.e., larger and more abundant stomata to increase evaporative cooling) (Monteiro et al. 2016). As such, thermophilization could drive a concomitant decrease in the relative abundance of drought‐tolerant species (Rosenblad et al. 2023; but see Caron et al. (2026), who found that thermophilization was associated with an increase in drought‐tolerant species abundances). A loss of drought‐tolerant species may be maladaptive over longer time scales if rainfall or water availability decreases, and/or may put the community at greater risk of future droughts (Hwang et al. 2020).

Shifts in community composition and diversity can also alter biogeochemical cycling, productivity, and trophic structures. For example, warming waters will drive thermophilization of plankton communities, with implications for nutrient cycling and marine food webs (Beng et al. 2025). Likewise, thermophilization of coastal kelp forests is driving shifts in the circulation of organic matter, with higher rates of detritus decomposition and higher densities of invertebrate grazers (Pessarrodona et al. 2019). In terrestrial ecosystems, warming can cause shifts in litter and soil microbial communities, causing altered decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil respiration (Solly et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2025; Szczygieł et al. 2024). For example, along an elevational gradient in the subalpine forests on the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau, temperature differences were associated with different relative abundances and diversities of soil bacteria groups. These observations indicate that warming could potentially drive increases in diazotrophic diversity, which will affect soil nitrogen fixation and cycling, and hence the overlying floral and faunal communities (Li et al. 2026). In a large‐scale forest soil warming experiment in Panama, thermophilization of the soil fauna altered soil food web dynamics, with potential effects on various soil processes, including rates of microbial grazing and decomposition and carbon respiration (Nottingham et al. 2020; Szczygieł et al. 2024). Likewise, in the Rocky Mountains, USA, experimental warming of alpine meadows led to decreases of beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and increases in decomposers. These compositional changes resulted in reduced nutrient availability, which in turn contributed to changes in the vegetation, affecting food and habitat availability for cattle and wildlife (Souza et al. 2026).

Thermophilization‐Driven Changes in Ecosystem Services

Humans will appreciate the effects of thermophilization most directly through the impacts of changing community composition on ecosystem services. For example, analyses of global fisheries have shown increases in the “mean temperature of catch” (MTC; analogous to CTI) at a rate of approximately 0.02°C year−1 over the past four decades, in both tropical and temperate coastal regions (Cheung et al. 2013). This shift raises the concern of eventual decreases in catch rates due to biotic attrition. On land, studies have also shown that the selection and value of plant species for human use are not random but rather are often linked to functional traits (de Aledo et al. 2025). For example, tropical trees with large stature and high wood density are often preferentially used for construction materials, both commercially and by indigenous communities. However, rising temperatures (coupled with rising [CO2] and other environmental changes) can favor faster‐growing, lighter‐wooded species—as has been documented in some lowland Amazonian forests (Aguirre‐Gutiérrez et al. 2025; Laurance et al. 2004; Phillips et al. 2004; Pinho et al. 2025)—possibly leading to local or global declines in the availability of these materials.

Thermophilization can also feed back to affect climate. Decreases in the abundance of heavy‐wooded tree species could cause decreases in aboveground forest biomass, which will reduce rates of carbon sequestration and lead to greater increases in atmospheric [CO2] (Hubau et al. 2020). Also, since a mechanism by which some plants increase their heat tolerances is through isoprene emissions (Taylor et al. 2019), there tends to be a positive association between CTI and the relative abundance of isoprene‐emitting tree species within communities (Taylor et al. 2018), meaning that thermophilization may magnify warming rates through increases in isoprene emissions (Gomes Alves et al. 2023). Finally, it has also been found that thermophilization of microbial communities generally enhances warming‐induced carbon losses from soils, which could result in reduced soil carbon storage, further accelerating atmospheric [CO2] increases and global warming rates (Karhu et al. 2014).

Extensions, Recommendations, and Future Directions for Thermophilization Research

Thermophilization studies can continue to enhance our understanding of community responses to anthropogenic climate change in many ways. Here we highlight some avenues of ongoing and future research that are particularly exciting. The suggested directions for future research include an increased emphasis on large‐scale analyses and comparative studies, experimental tests of thermophilization that help identify underlying mechanisms, the consideration of additional climate variables, and studies aimed at determining the consequences of thermophilization for species, communities, and ecosystems.

Large‐Scale Analyses and Comparative Studies

Most studies of thermophilization have focused on specific focal communities or systems, and relatively few studies have assessed large‐scale patterns or compared patterns between systems. Indeed, while several meta‐analyses of individual range shifts have recently appeared, thermophilization studies have not received the same treatment. One reason for this disparity is probably the lack of consistency and standardization of terminology and methods. As described above, most studies of thermophilization use the common approach of tracking changes in CTI through time. However, even under this shared general framework, terminology and the specific methods used vary widely. For example, studies can vary in their use of different sources and types of data to estimate STI, their calculations of CTI (e.g., transfer function vs. weighted mean vs. unweighted mean), the calculation of TR based on repeat surveys of individual plots or based on changes in the CTI vs. temperature relationship through time, and/or the calculation of thermal lag rates based on warming rates or predicted changes in composition based on initial patterns in CTI. Due to the potential effects of these differences in methods, thermophilization studies are not necessarily comparable. Rather, as it currently stands, reported STI, CTI, TR, and thermal lags are mostly comparable only within a particular study or set of studies using a common standardized methodology. The lack of standardization unfortunately limits the potential for large‐scale studies or meta‐analyses, complicating the detection or generalization of global patterns of thermophilization.

Researchers have begun to overcome these limitations and are beginning to test for large‐scale patterns and differences between regions and taxonomic groups. For example, Freeman et al. (2021) were able to combine data from several different studies and data sources to generate comparable estimates of thermophilization rates and thermal lags across continents, showing that thermophilization was approximately 10 times faster in tropical Central and South American montane forests than in temperate American montane forests, despite faster absolute rates of warming at higher latitudes. Khaliq et al. (2024) combined data and results from > 150 studies representing > 17,000 species to compare thermophilization patterns between temperate freshwater and terrestrial communities. They found that most communities did show signs of thermophilization (the notable exception being lake phytoplankton communities), but they found no significant differences in TR between freshwater and terrestrial systems or between different taxonomic groups within these two realms. However, they did find differences between the groups and realms in how thermophilization rates were affected by factors such as body size, thermal niche breadths and baseline temperatures, and regional warming rates (Khaliq et al. 2024). Using a new model that they developed to account for collection biases and niche truncation, Seki et al. (2026) looked at thermophilization in vascular plants across all of Japan since 1900 and showed that TR kept pace with warming up until the mid‐1980s, but thermal lag increased significantly thereafter.

More studies are clearly needed to help elucidate large‐scale and long‐term patterns of thermophilization, which will in turn provide insights into the factors that cause differences in thermophilization rates and thermal lags between different taxonomic groups and ecosystems. To facilitate the upscaling of thermophilization studies, we call for increased standardization of terminology and methods (Box 3). Although different studies may need to employ different methods depending on the data sources available to them and the goals of the study or hypotheses being tested, it is critical that methods be clearly presented, ideally with a discussion of how these choices may impact the results. Whenever possible, studies should also include their code and raw data. In particular, studies should provide machine‐readable tables of STI and CTI values for focal species and communities, respectively, along with associated environmental factors such as elevation, local temperature, and other climate variables, local or regional climate change rates, and metadata on census dates, census methods, and criteria.

Tropicalization.

In some cases, the shift in community composition to include greater relative abundances of thermophilic species, is referred to as “tropicalization” rather than thermophilization (e.g., Bates et al. 2014; Chust et al. 2024; Horta e Costa et al. 2014; Hyndes et al. 2016; McLean et al. 2021; Torreblanca and Báez 2025; Vergés et al. 2014; Zarzyczny et al. 2024). The term tropicalization is primarily used for marine systems (Zarzyczny et al. 2024), although it has also been applied to some terrestrial plant and animal communities (e.g., Osland et al. 2021). There are, however, problems with the term tropicalization. First, tropicalization literally means “becoming more tropical” and thus can technically refer to changes in attributes beyond just species composition. For example, even in the absence of changes in composition, an area may be described as undergoing tropicalization if the temperature, humidity, and/or rainfall increase (Yang et al. 2020). Second, this term prohibits analyses of shifts within the tropics since the composition of a community that is already within the tropics cannot become more tropical. Likewise, it excludes changes within just the temperate zones since a shift from e.g., boreal to temperate species cannot rightfully be called tropicalization (a smaller number of studies have used the term “deborealization” to refer to this phenomenon (Chust et al. 2024; McLean et al. 2021) or “borealization” to refer to the thermophilization of fish communities in arctic waters (Fossheim et al. 2015; von Biela et al. 2023) and plants of tundra ecosystems (Verdonen et al. 2026)). The term tropicalization also implies that all tropical areas are hot, which is not the case since many tropical species do in fact live in cold places (i.e., at high elevations or at depths). Finally, the most common definitions of “tropical” are based on latitude and sun angle (i.e., the tropics can technically be defined as all areas of the Earth that receive direct overhead sunlight at some time throughout the year (Feeley and Stroud 2018), which given the Earth's current axial tilt, corresponds to all areas between 23.4° S and 23.4° N—the tropics of Capricorn and Cancer, respectively). Thus, while changes in climate can cause temperate areas to become hotter and/or wetter (Yang et al. 2020) or cause changes in composition to include more tropical species, it cannot truly cause these areas to become more tropical in the absence of shifts in the inclination of the Earth's axis (although it should be noted that some studies have suggested that global warming could in fact be causing changes in Earth's tilt and rotation due the melting of glaciers and polar ice sheets (Deng et al. 2021; Kiani Shahvandi et al. 2024; Shahvandi et al. 2024)). Given these concerns and a push for an increased consistency in terminology, we encourage future researchers to forgo using the term “tropicalization” (or “borealization,” “deborealization,” etc.) in preference for “thermophilization.”

Experimental Tests of Thermophilization

Although a growing number of studies have documented thermophilization and uncovered important patterns, many questions remain. Some examples of open questions include: Are estimates of STI, CTI, and TR biased by niche truncation? Are TR and thermal lags constant over long time periods (Stemkovski et al. 2025, 2026)? To what extent is TR determined by dispersal limitation and population‐level processes (Alexander et al. 2018; Svenning and Sandel 2013)? Experiments can provide a powerful framework for addressing these and other questions by providing a framework to test the causal role of warming while disentangling the relative roles of dispersal limitation, demographic inertia, and biotic interactions.

A vital bridge between observational and experimental studies is that the direction and rates of thermophilization in experiments generally align with those observed under global warming (Elmendorf et al. 2015; Zhu et al. 2024). The International Tundra Experiment (ITEX), for example, utilized Open‐Top Chambers (OTCs) to elevate surface temperatures and found consistent increases in the CTI due to increasing abundances of warm‐adapted species at rates similar to compositional changes caused by rising summer temperatures (Elmendorf et al. 2015).

However, experimental warming also reveals the constraints of small‐scale manipulations. Detectable thermophilization often requires several years of treatment, suggesting that community restructuring is governed by slow, transient dynamics and can be constrained by internal ecological processes such as demographic lags (Stemkovski et al. 2025). Furthermore, the experiments themselves may introduce biophysical artifacts, such as reduced wind speed or altered moisture levels, which can bias results (Elmendorf et al. 2015). Recent experimental work also indicates that thermophilization is not always a uniform community‐wide response but can instead be driven by a few “key” species whose individual performance disproportionately influences the community signal (Dobson et al. 2025).

A specific limitation of most warming experiments is that they cannot assess the contributions of newly colonizing species to overall community thermophilization and thus cannot distinguish whether thermal observed lags are a product of a species' inability to tolerate heat or their inability to reach newly suitable environments. Whole‐community transplant experiments can overcome this limitation and explicitly test the role of dispersal limitation by physically relocating intact communities (e.g., plants along with their soils) from cooler to warmer environments (Bektaş et al. 2024). This approach simultaneously imposes a warming treatment and removes dispersal barriers, allowing thermophilic species to directly colonize transplanted communities. In this way, the contributions of dispersal and post‐colonization population dynamics to community thermophilization can be monitored. For example, whole‐community transplant experiments for plants along elevation gradients in Norway indicated that no single population‐level process dominates community thermophilization; rather, the colonization of thermophilic species, local extinction of cold‐adapted species, and shifts in species dominance all contribute (Lynn et al. 2021). However, coordinated transplant experiments across the Northern Hemisphere show that even when dispersal barriers are removed, colonization and extinction remain slow (Bektaş et al. 2024). Persistent colonization and extinction lags continue to constrain community thermophilization rates, reinforcing the view that community change and thermophilization are governed by slow, transient dynamics (Stemkovski et al. 2025).

Ultimately, while observational studies provide the spatial and temporal context needed to evaluate whether these responses scale to real‐world community reorganization, experiments remain essential for validating the sensitivity of thermophilization metrics under known rates of environmental change. Future work should use experiments to not only validate observed thermophilization patterns, but also to disentangle the effects of different climate change factors, including the indirect effects of biotic interactions. Experiments can also be used to test how other environmental factors mediate thermophilization. For example, research in disturbed systems has already provided evidence that habitat fragmentation interacts with warming to drive community shifts; smaller and more isolated patches can hinder the colonization of warm‐adapted species while exacerbating the loss of cold‐adapted species (Liu et al. 2024). Finally, future experiments should ideally link changes in composition with subsequent effects on ecosystem processes and services.

Extensions to Other Climate Variables and Factors

The same methods used to assess thermophilization can also be used to look at compositional shifts associated with other climate variables. For example, using the same methods as described above, researchers can look at changes in Species Precipitation Index (SPI) or the Community Moisture Index (CMI) rather than the Community Temperature Index (CTI). In this case, an increase in CMI would indicate a shift in community composition to include greater relative abundances of more‐mesophilic species that typically occur in wetter areas (i.e., mesophilization), or conversely for greater relative abundances of more‐xerophilic species (i.e., xerophilization). For example, Duque et al. (2015) tested for mesophilization of Andean tree communities and Feeley et al. (2020) tested for mesophilization of plant communities across new world ecoregions, but in both cases, changes in the community moisture indices were much less directional than concurrent changes in the community temperature indices. The lack of mesophilization in these communities is perhaps not surprising given that there is often a weaker, or even non‐significant, relationship between CMI and precipitation at the plot locations. For example, for their Andean tree communities, Duque et al. (2015) found a strongly significant relationship between CTI and mean annual temperature, but no significant relationship between CMI and precipitation; while for their new world plant communities, Feeley et al. (2020) found a near‐perfect correlation between CTI and temperature and a weaker relationship between CMI and precipitation. In other words, the community composition of many plant communities appears to be less associated with differences with rainfall than with temperature, and as such, predictions are less clear for how changes in precipitation should drive changes in species ranges and composition.

Weak links between composition and precipitation do not mean that plant species are oblivious to water availability; rather, it may be that we have imprecise measures of local rainfall, and/or that rainfall is a poor indicator of moisture availability. Moisture availability can depend on many factors, such as the seasonality and pattern of rainfall, slope, soil composition and texture, temperature, humidity. For example, even under stable precipitation, rising temperatures may decrease plant water availability due to greater evaporation demands. Further complicating the situation are the responses of plants and other organisms to increasing [CO2], which will affect water use efficiency through carbon fertilization. Despite these complications, some studies have shown evidence of plants shifting their ranges in accord with changes in precipitation and water availability. For example, in California USA, researchers found a downward shift in plant species ranges, which they attributed to species tracking changes in water availability more than changes in temperature (Crimmins et al. 2011), and on Mount Kenya, more than half of plant species were found to have shifted their distributions downslope over the 20th century due to drying conditions at higher elevations (Fu et al. 2026). Likewise, in the western USA, more tree species have shifted their ranges westward than northward due to their tracking of changes in water availability (Fei et al. 2017). Changes in species ranges in response to precipitation changes scale up to affect composition. For example, forests on Barro Colorado Island, Panama, have experienced changes in tree species composition consistent with xerophilization (Feeley et al. 2011), as have some forests in lowland Amazon (Esquivel‐Muelbert et al. 2019).

The effects of changing water availability are not limited to plants but rather are also observed in many animal communities. For eastern North American birds, Curley et al. (2022) found no association between community composition and temperature, but a strong positive association of composition with precipitation. They also found that avian communities were changing more rapidly with precipitation changes than with warming. These observations are in accord with previous findings from the western USA, where range shifts of bird species were highly heterogeneous and could not be explained by rising temperatures alone, but rather were best explained by models that also accounted for precipitation changes (explaining why some species moved downslope). Pearce‐Higgins et al. (2015) found that changes in both temperature and precipitation extremes have influenced long‐term population trends of English breeding birds but that overall, warming had greater effects than changes in precipitation. For mammals in California USA, Abercrombie et al. (2026) found that species range shifts have generally led to both thermophilization and xerophilization in accord with a combination of rising temperatures and decreasing precipitation.

Future studies should ideally look at changes in composition in relation to other more‐biologically relevant indices of water availability depending on the specific taxon of interest (e.g., vapor pressure deficit for plants (Slot et al. 2024) or cutaneous evaporative water loss rates for herpetofauna (Mitchell and Bergmann 2016)), and/or find ways to better integrate the responses of species and communities to both temperature and water availability. In addition to incorporating additional climate variables, future studies need to improve their integration of climatic variability, climate extremes (e.g., heatwaves and cold snaps), and interacting stressors into forecasts that capture nonlinear and regionally idiosyncratic outcomes (Sato et al. 2025; Williamson et al. 2025).

Integrating Microclimate and Microrefugia

Estimates of STI, and thus CTI, are typically based on species distributions in relation to macroclimate gradients, which may fail to represent the actual thermal conditions experienced by organisms. Many species reside near or below the ground or within complex vegetation structures where microclimates are decoupled from regional macroclimatic trends. Consequently, species may be responding more to small‐scale microclimate patterns than to large‐scale atmospheric warming (De Frenne et al. 2025; Zellweger et al. 2020).

Forest canopies provide a striking example of the decoupling of macro‐ and micro‐climate patterns. Forest canopies intercept solar radiation and reduce airflow, thereby buffering the understory against both extreme heat and extreme cold (De Frenne et al. 2013). As such, disturbances that reduce canopy cover (e.g., logging, windthrow, or fire) can rapidly accelerate thermophilization by exposing understory species to increased irradiance, higher local temperatures, and increased temperature fluctuations (Stevens et al. 2015; Zellweger et al. 2020). Beyond forests, topographic heterogeneity can create a mosaic of microclimatic conditions that can serve as potential microrefugia (i.e., small areas or patches where cold‐adapted species can persist despite large‐scale changes in climate) (Hannah et al. 2014). Models that exclude variation in microclimate due to landcover and/or topography often overestimate extinction risks and predict higher rates of thermophilization than what occurs in the presence of cool microrefugia (Nadeau et al. 2022). In montane and alpine systems, for instance, valleys can provide long‐term protection against rising temperatures, acting as “holdouts” that facilitate persistence and decrease thermophilization rates, whereas communities on ridges or hilltops are more exposed to high temperatures and thus may experience faster thermophilization rates (Báez et al. 2022; Hannah et al. 2014; Jiménez‐Alfaro et al. 2024).

Although much of our understanding of microclimatic buffering is based on terrestrial systems, similar dynamics are increasingly recognized as critical drivers of thermophilization in marine and coastal systems. For example, in the intertidal zone, there is often extreme thermal heterogeneity such that the temperature of a sun‐exposed rock can differ from a shaded crevice by over 10°C (Gunderson et al. 2019; Maclean et al. 2021). Offshore, marine systems are inherently three‐dimensional, offering vertical microrefugia through deep‐water strata and thermoclines that may allow mobile species to move downward to escape surface warming. However, there is growing concern that ocean warming is “compressing” these three‐dimensional habitats, potentially limiting the availability of thermal refugia for depth‐restricted species (Jorda et al. 2020). This thermal restructuring is already driving the thermophilization of temperate reefs due to an increasing dominance of warm‐affiliated herbivorous fish and the loss of cold‐adapted kelp forests (Barrientos et al. 2022; Vergés et al. 2019; Wernberg et al. 2016).

Moving forward, researchers should incorporate mechanistic modeling tools, such as NicheMapR or microclimate, which estimate terrain‐ and landcover‐corrected microclimates at high temporal and spatial resolutions (Kearney et al. 2020; Maclean et al. 2019). Including these high‐resolution microclimatic data will help resolve the “mismatch” between large scale global warming patterns and the temperatures experienced by organisms (Klinges et al. 2026), potentially increasing the accuracy of STI, and improving estimates of CTI, thermophilization rates, and thermal lags (Soifer et al. 2026).

Determining the Implications of Thermophilization for Species, Communities, and Ecosystems

The vast majority of thermophilization studies have concluded that compositional shifts are not keeping pace with rising temperatures. Slow thermophilization rates and large thermal lags are typically taken to mean that species and communities are failing to respond fast enough to warming, potentially putting them out of equilibrium with climate and at greater risk of population and diversity decline, especially as global warming accelerates (Hansen et al. 2023). Fadrique et al. (2026) examined patterns of tree species diversity changes throughout the Andes and Amazon and found that rates of species loss were fastest in the census plots in warmer areas and areas with faster rates of warming. These conditions were the same as those previously associated with the slowest thermophilization rates and highest thermal lags along the Andes to Amazon elevation gradient (Farfan‐Rios et al. 2025).

However, greater thermal lags may not always be indicators of more negative climate change impacts. If species can tolerate rising temperatures—due to their insensitivity, acclimation, or adaptation to higher temperatures—then they will not need to shift their distributions coldward through time. Indeed, fast range shifts may be indicative of species that are especially sensitive to climate change and/or species that are experiencing population declines or range contractions. In marine fishes, for example, species with faster range shifts had greater population declines through time than the non‐shifting species, which had overall stable populations (or as the authors put it, “fast shifters are often climatic losers” (Chaikin et al. 2024)). Also, since increases in CTI can be driven by the increased mortality of cold‐affiliated species and the local extinction or dieback of species from the hottest portions of their ranges (Borderieux et al. 2024), rapid thermophilization and small lag rates may actually be indicative of communities that are in decline (Rosenblad et al. 2023). For example, Duque et al. (2021) found that in Andean forests, faster thermophilization rates drove increased carbon losses due to mortality and overall net decreases in biomass carbon stocks. Given the potentially profound, but still poorly understood, impacts of thermal lags on the performance and fitness of species, as well as ecosystem functioning and the provisioning of ecosystem services, it is imperative that we improve our understanding of these dynamics.

Beyond categorizing thermophilization as a positive or negative phenomenon, a central aim of future studies should be to determine the predictability of community restructuring under warming, and whether or not it is possible to anticipate the cascading effects of thermophilization on biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, and ecosystem services (Tilman 1998; Vergés et al. 2019). Critically, if warming produces novel communities, then predictive models trained on historical data will have limited capacities to anticipate future ecological states and functions. Understanding the processes that drive species' responses to climate change, and explicitly incorporating these processes into predictive models, therefore represents a key challenge for the future.

Conclusions

Thermophilization provides a powerful, community‐level framework for assessing ecological responses of communities to anthropogenic warming, complementing species‐specific range shift studies. By integrating species' thermal affiliations (STIs) into a single Community Temperature Index (CTI), researchers can quantify how communities are restructuring in response to rising temperatures across terrestrial, marine, and aquatic systems. This approach is particularly advantageous in high‐diversity and data‐poor regions—like the tropics—where the scarcity of detailed spatial information for individual species often precludes mapping species' geographic range limits and range shifts through time. Based on a growing number of studies, it is clear that thermophilization is a widespread phenomenon across many different taxa and systems, but that thermophilization rates are almost always slower than those required for communities to keep pace with rates of modern warming. The resulting thermal lags may indicate a growing disequilibrium between communities and climate, which will potentially increase as global warming accelerates. The possible consequences of thermophilization are profound, ranging from losses in local and global biodiversity, to altered ecosystem functions including nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and the provisioning of essential ecosystem services. Moving forward, thermophilization studies should prioritize large‐scale comparative studies to identify differences between regions and groups, and experimental tests to isolate underlying demographic mechanisms. Ultimately, understanding the processes that govern community composition and thermophilization will improve our ability to predict, and potentially mitigate, the cascading effects of climate change on the natural ecosystems and the human livelihoods that depend on them.

Glossary

Biotic attrition: The decrease of local species diversity, particularly in lower‐elevation or lower‐latitude communities, when the loss of cold‐adapted species is not fully offset by incoming thermophilic species.

Community Temperature Index (CTI): A metric that aggregates the thermal affiliations of all species occurring in a focal community into a single community‐level temperature estimate. The CTI is most often calculated as an abundance‐weighted mean of the Species Temperature Index (STI) of all species recorded in a focal community.

Microclimate: The small‐scale climatic conditions occurring in the immediate vicinity of organisms. These conditions are often decoupled from the broader regional macroclimate due to the effects of e.g., landcover and topography.

Microrefugia: Small, isolated patches of habitat with favorable microclimates that can buffer species against regional macroclimatic changes and thus allow populations to persist within a landscape despite climate change.

Niche truncation: A phenomenon where the hottest or coldest portions of a species' fundamental thermal niche are “invisible” because those temperatures do not exist in the current environment or are unavailable to the species due to dispersal limitations, biotic interactions, or other constraints. Niche truncation can lead to under‐ or overestimates of the Species Temperature Index (STI) and an overall flattening of the CTI vs. temperature relationship.

Species Temperature Index (STI): A measure of an individual species' thermal affiliation, preference, or optimum. STIs are commonly estimated based on the mean annual temperature (MAT) across a species' known geographic distribution.

Thermal lag: The disequilibrium between the rate of community compositional shifts and the actual rate of warming. It represents the degree to which a community is failing to track rising temperatures.

Thermophilization: A change in community composition to include greater relative abundances of warm‐adapted (thermophilic) species and a concomitant decrease in the relative abundances of cold‐adapted species over time.

Thermophilization rate (TR): The annualized rate of change in a community's CTI over time. A positive TR indicates that the community is becoming increasingly composed of thermophilic species.

Transfer function: A modeling approach used to estimate Community Temperature Index (CTI) based on species composition.

Xerophilization: A directional shift in community composition toward greater relative abundances of species that prefer drier conditions (more‐xerophilic species) based on temporal changes of the Community Moisture Index (CMI)—a community aggregate metric affiliation based on Species Precipitation Index (SPI). The converse is “mesophilization,” which is a shift in community composition toward greater relative abundances of species that prefer wetter conditions (more‐mesophilic species).
