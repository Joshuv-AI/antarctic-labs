CURVATURE AT INFINITY OF SCALAR-FLAT ALE FOUR-MANIFOLDS
Jiangcheng You

Abstract
. We study refined asymptotics of scalar-flat ALE four-manifolds in the Tian–Viaclovsky setting, namely for self-dual or anti-self-dual metrics and for metrics with harmonic curvature. Starting from the ALE coordinates obtained by Tian–Viaclovsky, we construct preferred coordinates at infinity and identify the homogeneous | x | − 2 |x|^{-2} term in the metric expansion. This term splits canonically into a scalar part determined by the ALE ADM mass and an algebraic Weyl tensor at infinity. As an application, we consider scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics on minimal resolutions π : X → ℂ 2 / Γ \pi:X\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma of quotient surface singularities. In this case, the leading Weyl tensor at infinity vanishes exactly when the minimal resolution is crepant.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study the refined asymptotic geometry of
scalar-flat ALE ends arising from the two curvature classes in the
Tian–Viaclovsky framework
[
23
]
: self-dual or anti-self-dual metrics, and metrics
with harmonic curvature. At a first level, an ALE end is a
region which, after passing to a finite quotient, becomes asymptotic to the
Euclidean end. The main question here is what can be said beyond this basic
Euclidean approximation: whether one can choose better coordinates at infinity,
identify the first nontrivial term in the metric expansion, and determine which
part of this term is intrinsic to the geometry of the end.

We begin by recalling the definition of an ALE end, following

[
23

(M,g)
be a smooth noncompact Riemannian

4
–manifold. An end
\Omega\subset M
is said to be

, a finite subgroup

\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SO}(4)
acting freely on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R}
, where

\displaystyle\Phi:\Omega\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R})/\Gamma

such that, if

denotes the quotient map, then the pulled-back metric
\tilde{g}:=(\Phi^{-1}\circ\pi_{\Gamma})^{*}g

satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O(|x|^{-\tau}),\qquad\partial^{\alpha}\tilde{g}_{ij}=O(|x|^{-\tau-|\alpha|})

for every multi-index

1.1.
Main results and related works

In the first part of our paper, we construct a preferred coordinate system
near infinity for scalar-flat ALE manifolds satisfying
Assumption
1.1
. This provides a clearer understanding of
the geometry at infinity. This study is related to several developments in ALE geometry, particularly concerning refined asymptotics at infinity.

Under fast curvature decay and maximal volume growth, Bando–Kasue–Nakajima
constructed global coordinates at infinity and proved ALE structure
[
3
]
.
In the Ricci-flat setting, Cheeger–Tian studied asymptotic cones under Euclidean
volume growth and critical integral curvature bounds
[
8
]
. In dimension
4
,
Kronheimer constructed ALE hyper-Kähler spaces, relating ALE geometry to quotient
surface singularities and their resolutions
[
15
]
. Minerbe further studied
gravitational instantons from the viewpoint of volume growth, including the ALF case
arising from cubic volume growth
[
19
]
.

More refined asymptotic invariants have been studied in the Einstein and Ricci-flat ALE
setting. Biquard–Hein introduced the renormalized volume of four-dimensional
Ricci-flat ALE spaces and proved its sharp nonpositivity and rigidity
[
6
]
.
Wang–Yin later constructed preferred coordinates at infinity, defined a Weyl tensor at
infinity, and related these data to the renormalized volume for Ricci-flat ALE spaces
and orbifolds
[
27
]
. These works show that the leading terms at infinity can carry
intrinsic geometric information.

ALE Kähler geometry provides another important source of examples and comparison
results. Besides Kronheimer’s hyper-Kähler spaces, Calderbank–Singer constructed
complete ALE scalar-flat Kähler metrics on toric resolutions of isolated cyclic quotient
singularities
[
7
]
. Further existence, deformation, compactness, and
classification results for ALE Kähler manifolds and scalar-flat Kähler ALE surfaces
were developed by Lock–Viaclovsky, Han–Viaclovsky, and
Hein–Răsdeaconu–Şuvaina; see for example
[
18
,
10
,
11
,
13
]
.
Hein–LeBrun proved a mass formula in ALE Kähler geometry and showed that, in the
scalar-flat Kähler ALE case, the mass has a topological interpretation
[
12
]
.
Related analytic and metric characterizations of ALE Kähler manifolds were obtained
by Ni–Shi–Tam
[
21
]
.

The analytic framework most directly used in this paper comes from
Tian–Viaclovsky’s work on four-dimensional critical metrics. Their results give
curvature decay, ALE structure, and compactness for Bach-flat metrics and metrics with
harmonic curvature under finite
L^{2}
curvature, Sobolev control, and mild topological

Remark 1.6

The decomposition in (

1.2.
Idea of proof

The proof is organized around two ideas. The first is to separate the geometric
part of the leading coefficient in the metric expansion at infinity. Starting
from the Tian–Viaclovsky ALE chart, we put the metric into Euclidean Bianchi
gauge. In this gauge, the equations coming from the two alternatives in
Assumption
1.1
become a fourth-order elliptic system whose principal part is the Euclidean bi-Laplacian. This gives a
first expansion at the borderline order
r^{-2}
, but the leading term coefficient, denote by
A
, obtained at
this stage still contains coordinate freedom.

The next step is algebraic. The point is not to guess the final normal form directly. Instead, we start from the algebraic properties that a Weyl-type tensor must satisfy, as detailed in Proposition
3.1
. Under the map
s
defined as (
21
), a Weyl
tensor gives a pair-symmetric tensor satisfying two more conditions: vanishing partial traces, and the first Bianchi identity. We therefore use these conditions as a guide to trace the Weyl-type part inside
A
.

The pair symmetry is already present in the rough expansion. The main issue appears when we try to achieve the partial trace-free property by using coordinate transformation

(2)

\displaystyle x=\varphi(y)=y+u(y),\quad u_{i}(y)=B_{ij}\tfrac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}},

where the constant matrix
B
is to be determined, to eliminate the remaining coordinate freedom. The target partial trace-free equations cannot, in general, be solved completely. A scalar
obstruction remains (c.f. Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
). This obstruction is not an unwanted error; rather, it is precisely the component that cannot be eliminated by the above coordinate transformation, thereby holding the promise of being an intrinsic geometric quantity on the end. In fact, it arises from our tracking of the Weyl-type tensor and can be identified with the ALE ADM mass.

Thus, by choosing a suitable constant matrix
B
and performing the coordinate transformation again, the residual partial trace-free coefficient satisfies the first Bianchi identity and therefore comes from an algebraic Weyl tensor
\operatorname{W}_{\infty}
at infinity by Proposition
3.1
. At this stage, the leading coefficient
A
is transformed into
A^{\sharp}
, which decomposes into a mass part and a Weyl-type part, both of which are preferred data:

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}=\Xi\bigl(9|\Gamma|\,m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\bigr)+s(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}).

This is the main mechanism behind Theorem
1.3
. The uniqueness of the
preferred data is then obtained by comparing two such elimination normalizations; the
transition map is asymptotically orthogonal, so only the natural
\mathrm{O}(4)
–action remains.

It is evident that the key operation in carrying out the first idea is the
coordinate transformation (
2
). The
coordinate-change technique used here is adapted from the work of Wang–Yin on
Ricci-flat ALE ends
[
27
]
. Their argument combines suitable decaying
coordinate transformations with algebraic decompositions of the leading
coefficient to obtain a Weyl-type normal form. We use this strategy in the present scalar-flat ALE setting, where the normalization problem has an additional
feature: besides the Weyl-type component, a scalar component remains, and this
remaining scalar component is identified with the ADM mass.

The second idea enters when the ALE space is scalar-flat Kähler and the end is
the holomorphic quotient end coming from the minimal resolution

\pi:X\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
. In this case, we carry out the same computational procedure as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, but now keeping track of the Kähler structure. The
|x|^{-2}
coefficient in the modified metric expansion can then
be computed more explicitly. Its scalar part is exactly the ADM mass term
already isolated in Theorem
1.3
; after this mass contribution is
removed, the remaining preferred component must be the Weyl-type part. Of course, it is not difficult to see that this residual Weyl tensor is determined by the limiting complex structure
J_{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Thus, in preferred coordinates
coming from the holomorphic ALE end, one obtains

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=C_{W}\,\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}},

where the coefficient
C_{W}
is, at this stage, an explicit universal multiple of
the ADM mass.

The next task is to interpret this ADM mass geometrically. Here we use the mass formula of
Hein–LeBrun
[
12
]
: for scalar-flat Kähler ALE surfaces, the ADM mass is
expressed in terms of the pairing between the first Chern class of the complex
surface and the Kähler class. Since our end comes from the minimal resolution
of the quotient singularity, this pairing can be rewritten on the
exceptional set by using the discrepancy of the resolution. This gives

\displaystyle C_{W}=-\frac{|\Gamma|}{\pi}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}A_{i},

where the
\mu_{i}
are the discrepancy coefficients and
A_{i}=\int_{E_{i}}\omega
are the Kähler areas of the exceptional curves. Meanwhile, the non-crepant property of the minimal resolution can also be characterized by a linear combination of the exceptional divisors, with the corresponding coefficients being precisely
\mu_{i}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.10
now becomes straightforward, simply by observing that the discrepancy coefficients
\mu_{i}
are either all zero or all strictly positive.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we construct
the Euclidean Bianchi gauge on an ALE end and derive the fourth-order elliptic
equation governing the metric perturbation. This gives the rough homogeneous
|x|^{-2}
expansion. In Section
3
, we analyze the
remaining coordinate freedom, separate the mass part from the Weyl-type part,
introduce the Schwarzschild relative gauge, and prove the uniqueness of the
preferred data; the Burns metric is also computed as an explicit example.
Finally, in Section
4
, we specialize the
construction to scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics on minimal quotient
resolutions and prove the crepancy criterion for the vanishing of the Weyl tensor at infinity.

2.
Asymptotic Expansion of the Metric in the Bianchi Gauge

In this section, we carry out the first analytic step toward understanding the metric at infinity.
Starting from the baseline ALE chart given by Theorem
1.2
, we seek a better coordinate system in which the metric perturbation
h=g-g_{E}
satisfies a Bianchi-type gauge condition.
This gauge is introduced to control the remaining coordinate freedom and to
put the relevant curvature equations derived from Assumption
1.1
into forms adapted to elliptic analysis
on the exterior region. With this preparation, the equation for
h
becomes a fourth-order system whose principal part is the Euclidean bi-Laplacian, this allows us to obtain a first asymptotic expansion of the metric.
The outcome is that the first nontrivial term in the expansion occurs at order
r^{-2}
; this is the first order at which the geometry at infinity will later be seen to carry nontrivial information.

2.1.
Weighted function spaces

Before commencing the formal analysis, we first systematically outline the analytical framework of the entire study. This subsection imports the scale-invariant weighted Hölder spaces used in
[
27
]
and records a small
toolkit: differentiation, multiplication, composition under small diffeomorphisms, and a bounded right-inverse
for the Euclidean Laplacian on an exterior domain. These are the crucial analytic inputs needed later.

We first introduce the weighted Hölder spaces on an exterior domain.
Fix
R>0
and set
\Omega_{R}:=\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R}
.
For
r>0
define the scaling map on annuli

\displaystyle S_{r}:\ B_{2}\setminus B_{1}\to B_{2r}\setminus B_{r},\qquad S_{r}(x)=rx.

For a function
v
defined on
\Omega_{R}
, and for
\alpha>4
and
\beta>0
with
\beta\notin\mathbb{N}
,
define

(3)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}:=\sup_{r\geq R}r^{\beta}\|v\circ S_{r}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}.

We denote by
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})
the Banach space of functions with finite norm
(
3
). If there is no ambiguity, we write
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Remark 2.1

(Quotients by a finite group)
.

When working on an ALE end
(\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R})/\Gamma
, we define
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R}/\Gamma)

by lifting
\Gamma
-invariant functions to
\Omega_{R}
and using (
3
). Since
\Gamma
is finite,
this is equivalent (up to constants depending only on
\Gamma
) to defining the norm using local charts on the quotient.

Then we collect some calculus rules used repeatedly: derivatives increase weight by
1
,
products add weights, and composition with small decaying diffeomorphisms preserves weights.

Lemma 2.2

(Derivative estimate)
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,4\}
, there exists
C=C(\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{i}}v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta+1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}.

Lemma 2.3

(Product estimate)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(\Omega_{R})
and
g\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}(\Omega_{R})
.
Then

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\min(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(\Omega_{R})}\,\|g\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}(\Omega_{R})}.

Lemma 2.4

(Composition under a small decaying diffeomorphism)
.

Let
f
be defined on
\Omega_{R/2}
and satisfy
f=\mathcal{O}(r^{-\ell})
for some
\ell>0
.
Let
u:\Omega_{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{4}
and set
\varphi(x)=x+u(x)
.
If
\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\Omega_{R})}\leq 1
for some
\gamma>0
, then for all sufficiently large
R
,

\displaystyle\|f\circ\varphi\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\ell}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C.

Remark 2.5
.

Lemmas
2.2
–
2.4
are standard results from
[
27
]
and will be used tacitly.
In the sequel, we also assume the following elementary fact without further mention:
if
T=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-\beta})
, then
T\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}
for any
\alpha
, and differentiation improves the weight by one order.

Having established the definition of the basic weighted norms and their algebraic properties,
we now state the key analytic input: the existence of a bounded right inverse for
\Delta_{E}
on these weighted spaces.
This operator, originally constructed in
[
27
]
, will be employed as a black box.

Theorem 2.6
.

There exists a bounded linear map

\displaystyle\Psi:\ \mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})

such that for all
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}\,\Psi(f)=f\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R},

and

\displaystyle\|\Psi(f)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})},

where
C
is independent of
R
.

Remark 2.7
.

This theorem imposes less restrictive conditions on the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
. Specifically,
\alpha>2
,
\beta>0
, and
\beta
is non-integer.

To address the challenges associated with fourth-order equations in our study, we require a corresponding result concerning the inverse mapping of
\Delta_{E}^{2}
. In fact, Theorem
2.6
stated above admits the following direct corollary:

Corollary 2.8
.

Define
\Psi^{(2)}:=\Psi\circ\Psi
. Then
\Psi^{(2)}
is a bounded linear map

\displaystyle\Psi^{(2)}:\ \mathcal{X}_{\alpha-4,\beta+4}(\Omega_{R})\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})

such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}\,\Psi^{(2)}(f)=f\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R},

and

\displaystyle\|\Psi^{(2)}(f)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-4,\beta+4}(\Omega_{R})}.

2.2.
Construction of the Euclidean Bianchi gauge

We now construct a change of coordinates under which the metric perturbation satisfies the Euclidean Bianchi gauge condition.
Starting from the baseline ALE chart of Theorem
1.2
, write

\displaystyle g=g_{\mathrm{E}}+h_{0}

on
\Omega_{R}
, where for every fixed
\tau\in(1,2)
one has
h_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau}(\Omega_{R})
after enlarging
R
if necessary.

Recall that for a symmetric
2
-tensor
k
on
\Omega_{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(k):=\operatorname{div}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}k-\frac{1}{2}d(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}k).

Our goal is to find a small vector field
u\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
such that the map
\varphi(x)=x+u(x)
is a diffeomorphism of
\Omega_{R}
onto its image, and the pulled-back perturbation
h:=\varphi^{*}g-g_{\mathrm{E}}
satisfies the Euclidean Bianchi gauge condition

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=0.

The key point is that, after linearizing the gauge equation with respect to the coordinate correction
u
, the leading term is simply the Euclidean Laplacian
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}u
. Indeed, for a smooth vector field
X
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\mathcal{L}_{X}g_{\mathrm{E}})_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial^{j}(\partial_{i}X_{j}+\partial_{j}X_{i})-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\bigl(2\partial^{j}X_{j}\bigr)=\partial^{j}\partial_{j}X_{i}=(\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}X)_{i}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\mathcal{L}_{X}g_{\mathrm{E}})=\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}X.

This identity is what makes Theorem
2.6
directly applicable.

For
u\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
sufficiently small, set
\varphi=\operatorname{Id}+u
and define

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(u):=\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\varphi^{*}g-g_{\mathrm{E}}).

Our task is to employ the fixed-point principle in
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
to find a vector field
u
that vanishes under the map
\mathscr{F}
. This procedure is standard.

In fact, the structure of the gauge equation can be described as follows. For arbitrary fixed
\alpha\in(4,\infty)
and
\tau\in(1,2)
, there exist
R
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that for every
u\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R}),\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}<\varepsilon
, the map
\mathscr{F}(u)
is well-defined on
\Omega_{R}
and admits the expansion

(5)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(u)=\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h_{0})+\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}u+\mathscr{N}(u,h_{0}),

where the nonlinear remainder satisfies

(6)

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{N}(u,h_{0})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tau+1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\Bigl(\|h_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau}(\Omega_{R})}\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}+\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}^{2}\Bigr).

Moreover,
\mathscr{N}(\cdot,h_{0})
is locally Lipschitz: for all sufficiently small

u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
,

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\mathscr{N}(u_{1},h_{0})-\mathscr{N}(u_{2},h_{0})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tau+1}(\Omega_{R})}\\
&\qquad\leq C\Bigl(\|h_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau}(\Omega_{R})}+\|u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}+\|u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}\Bigr)\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}.\end{split}

With the gauge equation prepared, the existence of a Bianchi gauge follows from the fixed-point principle.

More precisely, according to the expansion (
5
), solving
\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\varphi^{*}g-g_{\mathrm{E}})=0
on
\Omega_{R}
is equivalent to finding a fixed point of

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(u):=-\Psi\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h_{0})+\mathscr{N}(u,h_{0})\bigr),

where
\Psi
is the bounded right inverse of
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}
given by Theorem
2.6
. Thanks to the estimates (
6
) and (
7
), Banach’s fixed-point theorem yields a unique

u\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
such that
u=\mathcal{T}(u)
. Applying
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}
to the fixed-point identity, we derive a solution to the Bianchi gauge condition (
4
). To summarize,

Proposition 2.9
.

Fix
\tau\in(1,2)
and a non-integer
\alpha\in(4,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
.
There exists
R
sufficiently large and a vector field

u\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})
such that
\varphi(x)=x+u(x)

is a diffeomorphism of
\Omega_{R}
(onto its image) and satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\varphi^{*}g-g_{\mathrm{E}})=0.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\tau-1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|h_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\tau}(\Omega_{R})},

where
C
depends only on
\alpha,\tau
and the fixed baseline chart.

Proposition
2.9
provides the first analytic normalization of the ALE end: after a small asymptotically trivial change of coordinates, the perturbation
h=g-g_{\mathrm{E}}
satisfies the Euclidean Bianchi condition. This is the correct starting point for the fourth-order analysis of the curvature equations derived from Assumption
1.1
. In the next subsection, we will use this gauge to derive the fourth-order
equation satisfied by the metric perturbation on the exterior region.

2.3.
The fourth-order equation in Euclidean Bianchi gauge

We now continue from the Euclidean Bianchi gauge constructed in the previous subsection. Our goal here is to show that the two alternatives in Assumption
1.1
lead, in these coordinates, to a fourth-order equation whose principal part is
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}h
. This is the form required for the exterior biharmonic analysis in the subsequent discussion. We will use the schematic notation
T_{1}*\cdots*T_{N}
for a finite linear combination of Euclidean contractions of the tensors
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}
with universal coefficients.

Let
h:=\varphi^{*}\Phi^{*}g-g_{\mathrm{E}}
be the perturbation produced by Proposition
2.9
, then
\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=0
holds on
\Omega_{R}
. So in Euclidean coordinates,

(8)

\displaystyle\partial^{k}h_{ik}-\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{i}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h)=0.

We shall use (
8
) repeatedly below. Moreover,
h
is still a small decaying perturbation of
g_{\mathrm{E}}
on the exterior region. Notice that under Assumption
1.1
, we have
\operatorname{R}(g)=0
, and that either
g
is Bach-flat or
g
has harmonic curvature.

In the Bach-flat case, by the naturality of the Bach tensor, we conclude that
\operatorname{B}(g_{\mathrm{E}}+h)
vanishes on
\Omega_{R}
. For the four-dimensional formula

\displaystyle\operatorname{B}_{ij}=\nabla^{k}C_{kij}+P^{k\ell}W_{ikj\ell},

where
W
is the Weyl tensor,
P
is the Schouten tensor, and
C
is the Cotton tensor, linearizing the term
P^{k\ell}W_{ikj\ell}
at
g_{\mathrm{E}}
and using the product rule gives

\displaystyle\bigl(D(P^{k\ell}W_{ikj\ell})\bigr)_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=(DP)_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)^{k\ell}W(g_{\mathrm{E}})_{ikj\ell}+P(g_{\mathrm{E}})^{k\ell}(DW)_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)_{ikj\ell}=0,

since
g_{\mathrm{E}}
is flat. Therefore, in the Bach-flat case, the equation

\operatorname{B}(g_{\mathrm{E}}+h)=0
can be written in the form

(9)

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}C_{kij}=-P^{k\ell}W_{ikj\ell},

and the right-hand side has no linear term at
g_{\mathrm{E}}
. Thus it is absorbed into
the nonlinear remainder when we extract the linearized fourth-order operator.

In the harmonic-curvature case,
\mathrm{div}\operatorname{Rm}=0
is equivalent to
\nabla_{k}\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}=\nabla_{j}\operatorname{Ric}_{ik}
. Since
\operatorname{R}(g)=0
, the Schouten tensor is
P=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Ric}
, and hence the Cotton tensor
C_{kij}
, defined as
\nabla_{k}P_{ij}-\nabla_{j}P_{ik}
, vanishes. In particular,
\nabla^{k}C_{kij}=0
on
\Omega_{R}
. Thus in both cases we may derive the perturbed bi-harmonic equation for
h
via linearizing the tensor
\nabla^{k}C_{kij}
to obtain the fourth-order linear principal part.

Under the gauge condition (
8
), the standard linearized curvature formulas reduce to

\displaystyle(\delta\operatorname{Ric})_{ij}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h_{ij},\quad\delta\operatorname{R}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h).

Hence the linearized Schouten tensor is

(10)

\displaystyle(\delta P)_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl((\delta\operatorname{Ric})_{ij}-\frac{1}{6}\,\delta\operatorname{R}\,\delta_{ij}\Bigr)=-\frac{1}{4}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h_{ij}+\frac{1}{24}\,\delta_{ij}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h).

To pass from
\delta P
to the linearized Cotton-divergence term, we linearize the Cotton tensor. Since
P(g_{\mathrm{E}})=0
, all connection-variation terms are multiplied by the
background Schouten tensor and hence vanish. Therefore

(\delta C)_{kij}
reduces to

\partial_{k}(\delta P)_{ij}-\partial_{j}(\delta P)_{ik}
.
Since also
C(g_{\mathrm{E}})=0
, linearizing the divergence gives

(11)

\displaystyle\bigl(D(\nabla^{k}C_{kij})\bigr)_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=\partial^{k}(\delta C)_{kij}=\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\delta P)_{ij}-\partial_{j}\bigl(\partial^{k}(\delta P)_{ik}\bigr).

As for the divergence of
\delta P
, combining formula (
10
) for
(\delta P)_{ij}
with the Bianchi identity (
8
) yields

\displaystyle\partial^{k}(\delta P)_{ik}=-\frac{1}{12}\,\partial_{i}\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h),

substituting this into the preceding identity (
11
) yields

(12)

\displaystyle\bigl(D(\nabla^{k}C_{kij})\bigr)_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=-\frac{1}{4}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}h_{ij}+\frac{1}{24}\,\delta_{ij}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h)+\frac{1}{12}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h).

To handle the trace terms, we use the scalar-flat condition. Since
\operatorname{R}(g_{\mathrm{E}}+h)
vanishes and the linearization of the scalar curvature in Bianchi gauge is
\delta\operatorname{R}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h)
, the remaining terms in the scalar-curvature expansion are at least quadratic. Hence

(13)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}h)=h*\partial^{2}h+\partial h*\partial h.

Differentiating twice, we obtain only quadratic terms, namely combinations of
h*\partial^{4}h
,
\partial h*\partial^{3}h
, and
\partial^{2}h*\partial^{2}h
. In particular, the trace terms in (
12
) do not produce any new linear fourth-order term, and may therefore be included in the nonlinear error.

Finally, we expand the corresponding nonlinear equation around
g_{\mathrm{E}}
.
In the harmonic-curvature case we use the consequence

\nabla^{k}C_{kij}=0
; in the Bach-flat case we use (
9
). The two
equations have the same linear fourth-order part, namely

D(\nabla^{k}C_{kij})_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)
.
Using (
12
) and then applying
(
13
) to absorb the trace terms
into the nonlinear part, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}h+h*\partial^{4}h+\partial h*\partial^{3}h+\partial^{2}h*\partial^{2}h+\partial h*\partial h*\partial^{2}h+\partial h*\partial h*\partial h*\partial h=0\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}.

Thus, in Euclidean Bianchi gauge, both the scalar-flat Bach-flat equation and the scalar-flat harmonic-curvature equation are small quasilinear perturbations of the Euclidean biharmonic equation, with principal part
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}h
.

2.4.
Biharmonic reduction and rough metric expansion

In the previous subsection, we showed that, in Euclidean Bianchi gauge, the
equations arising from both alternatives in Assumption
1.1

take the common schematic form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}h=\mathcal{N}(h).

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(h):=h*\partial^{4}h+\partial h*\partial^{3}h+\partial^{2}h*\partial^{2}h+\partial h*\partial h*\partial^{2}h+(\partial h)^{*4}.

We now use this equation to extract the first term in the expansion of
h
. The basic idea is to subtract a particular solution of the bi-Poisson equation and reduce the problem to a biharmonic one.

From the baseline ALE decay and the gauge-fixing step, we may choose a non-integer

\alpha>4
and
\tau_{0}\in(3/2,2)
such that
h\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\tau_{0}}(\Omega_{R})
. Since every term in
\mathcal{N}(h)
contains at least two factors of
h
or its derivatives, it follows directly that
\mathcal{N}(h)\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-4,\,2\tau_{0}+4}(\Omega_{R})
. Because
2\tau_{0}\in(3,4)
, the weight
2\tau_{0}
is admissible for Corollary
2.8
. We therefore take
v:=\Psi^{(2)}\bigl(4\,\mathcal{N}(h)\bigr)\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\,2\tau_{0}}(\Omega_{R})
and define
w:=h-v
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}v=4\,\mathcal{N}(h),\qquad\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}^{2}w=0\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R},

Since
\tau_{0}>1
, both
h
and
v
are
o(r^{-1})
, and therefore so is
w
.

We now invoke the standard asymptotic expansion for biharmonic functions on an exterior domain; see, for instance,
[
14
]
. Applied componentwise to the
\Gamma
-invariant tensor
w
, it gives an expansion in spherical harmonics on
S^{3}/\Gamma
. Under the decay condition
w=o(r^{-1})
, all growing modes, the constant and logarithmic modes in the
\ell=0
channel, and the
r^{-1}
mode in the
\ell=1
channel are excluded. Hence the first possible nonzero term occurs at order
r^{-2}
, and only the
\ell=0
and
\ell=2
modes can appear at that order. It follows that there exists a smooth
\Gamma
-invariant spherical tensor
H
on
S^{3}
such that

(14)

\displaystyle w(x)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}H(\theta)+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}),\qquad r=|x|,\quad\theta=\frac{x}{r}.

Meanwhile,
v\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\,2\tau_{0}}(\Omega_{R})
implies
v=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3})
, therefore, combining
h=w+v
with (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle h(x)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}H(\theta)+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}).

Furthermore, since
H
only contains the
\ell=0
and
\ell=2
modes, the tensor
r^{-2}H(\theta)
can be written in Cartesian form as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}H(\theta)=A_{ijk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}},

where the constant tensor
A=(A_{ijk\ell})
is defined as

(15)

\displaystyle A_{ijk\ell}:=(D_{0})_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}(C_{2,a})_{ij}Q^{(a)}_{k\ell},

here
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is an index set for a basis
\{Y_{2,a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}
of the
\Gamma
-invariant
\ell=2
eigenspace on
S^{3}/\Gamma
; each
Y_{2,a}
can be expressed as
Y_{2,a}(\theta)=Q^{(a)}_{k\ell}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}
for some
Q^{(a)}\in S^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
, and
D_{0}
and
C_{2,a}
are constant symmetric
2
-tensors.

We have therefore proved the following proposition.

Proposition 2.10
.

There exist
R\gg 1
and a constant tensor
A=(A_{ijk\ell})
such that

(16)

\displaystyle h_{ij}(x)=A_{ijk\ell}\,\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3})\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}.

Remark 2.11
.

For convenience, we shall refer to (
16
) as the rough expansion of the metric in the sequel.

We now examine the coefficient
A
in the rough expansion (
16
).

Write
(h^{A})_{ij}(x):=A_{ijk\ell}\,\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}
, then the Euclidean Bianchi gauge
\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=0
imposes a condition on the leading term
h^{A}
. Directly computation reads

\displaystyle(\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h^{A}))_{i}=\frac{1}{r^{3}}\Bigl(-4\,A_{ijk\ell}\,\theta_{j}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}+\bigl(2A_{ijj\ell}-A_{ppi\ell}\bigr)\theta_{\ell}+2A_{ppk\ell}\,\theta_{i}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}\Bigr),

where
\theta=\frac{x}{r}
. Since
h=h^{A}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h^{A})+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-4}),

and therefore
\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=0
forces

(17)

\displaystyle-4\,A_{ijk\ell}\,\theta_{j}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}+\bigl(2A_{ijj\ell}-A_{ppi\ell}\bigr)\theta_{\ell}+2A_{ppk\ell}\,\theta_{i}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}\equiv 0\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{S}^{3}.

It is convenient to rewrite this condition in tensor form. Set
U_{k\ell}:=A_{ppk\ell}
and
V_{i\ell}:=A_{ijj\ell}
, since only the full symmetrization of
A
in
j,k,\ell
contributes to

A_{ijk\ell}\theta_{j}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}
, and
|\theta|=1
, the identity (
17
) is equivalent to

(18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{i(jk\ell)}&=\frac{1}{12}\Bigl(\delta_{jk}(2V_{i\ell}-U_{i\ell})+\delta_{j\ell}(2V_{ik}-U_{ik})+\delta_{k\ell}(2V_{ij}-U_{ij})\Bigr)\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(\delta_{ij}U_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}U_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}U_{jk}\Bigr),\end{split}

where
A_{i(jk\ell)}
denotes the full symmetrization in
j,k,\ell
. Contracting
(
18
) with
\delta_{jk}
further gives

(19)

\displaystyle 2A_{ijj\ell}-2A_{i\ell kk}-A_{ppi\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}A_{ppkk}=0.

We summarize the outcome as follows.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
A
be the tensor in Proposition
2.10
. Then:

(1)

A
satisfies the pair symmetries

\displaystyle A_{ijk\ell}=A_{jik\ell}=A_{ij\ell k}.

(2)

A
satisfies the tensor identity (

).

(3)

In particular, the partial traces of

satisfy (

).

Proof.

The pair symmetries follow directly from the definition (

∎

So far, we have established a rough asymptotic expansion for the metric in the Euclidean Bianchi gauge, namely (

). Furthermore, the leading coefficient
A_{ijk\ell}
satisfies a set of constraints, as detailed in Proposition

. This gives a concrete leading term for the metric at infinity. In the next section, we will refine this rough

3.
Identification of Curvature at Infinity

This section proves Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.7
.
We start from the rough
r^{-2}
coefficient
A
obtained in the Euclidean Bianchi gauge in the previous section and analyze its remaining coordinate ambiguity as well as the potential intrinsic geometric information it contains.

Motivated by the work of Wang–Yin
[
27
]
, we consider whether the leading coefficient
A
can be further simplified by a coordinate change so that it has the algebraic form expected of a Weyl-type term. Specifically, we impose the following coordinate transformation on the current coordinate system:

(20)

\displaystyle x=\varphi(y)=y+u(y),

where
u
is chosen to be suitable for our purposes. We aim to track the Weyl-type information from the leading coefficient
A
by the coordinate transformation (
20
), naturally, we proceed by targeting the properties satisfied by the Weyl-type tensor.

A canonical target, which is precisely the one considered in
[
27
]
, can be described as follows.

Let us begin with some notation. Denote the space of algebraic curvature tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
by
\mathcal{C}
, and the algebraic Weyl space by
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{C}
. Let

(21)

\displaystyle s:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4}),\quad(s(R))_{ijk\ell}:=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(R_{ik\ell j}+R_{i\ell kj}\bigr).

Write
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}:=s(\mathcal{C})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}:=s(\mathcal{W})
. In fact, the so-called Weyl-type tensor that we aim to track from
A
is precisely an element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
. Therefore, we need to gain a clear understanding of the elements in
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

The following position reveals a useful criterion for a tensor to belong to
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A\in S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Then
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
if and only if

A
satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

A_{ijk\ell}=A_{jik\ell}=A_{ij\ell k}
;

(ii)

A_{ppk\ell}=0
and
A_{ijj\ell}=0
;

(iii)

A_{ijk\ell}+A_{ik\ell j}+A_{i\ell jk}=0.

Moreover, if these conditions hold, then
A=s(\mathcal{W}_{\infty})
for a unique

\mathcal{W}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{W}
, and in fact

(22)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{R}A)_{ijk\ell}:=A_{j\ell ik}-A_{jki\ell}-A_{i\ell jk}+A_{ikj\ell},

satisfies

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\infty}=-\frac{1}{3}\,\mathbf{R}A.

This proposition is essentially a standard algebraic fact. We only record a brief proof.

Proof.

The implication
A=s(\mathcal{W}_{\infty})\Rightarrow
(i)–(iii) is immediate from the algebraic symmetries, trace-freeness, and first Bianchi identity of
\mathcal{W}_{\infty}
.

Conversely, assume that
A
satisfies (i)–(iii). A routine use of (iii) together with the pairwise symmetries in (i) shows that
A
also satisfies the pair-exchange symmetry
A_{ijk\ell}=A_{k\ell ij}
. Now define
R:=\mathbf{R}A
by (
22
). Using (i), (iii), and the pair-exchange symmetry, one checks directly that
R
is an algebraic curvature tensor. Moreover, (ii) implies that its Ricci contraction vanishes, while Ricci-flatness is equivalent to
total trace-freeness, so in fact
R\in\mathcal{W}
.

Finally, substituting (
22
) into the definition of
s
and simplifying by (i) and (iii), one obtains

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{R}A)=-3A,

which proves that
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
and gives the formula (
23
).

Uniqueness follows from the same identity: if
A=s(\mathcal{W}_{1})=s(\mathcal{W}_{2})
, then

\displaystyle-3\mathcal{W}_{1}=\mathbf{R}(s(\mathcal{W}_{1}))=\mathbf{R}(s(\mathcal{W}_{2}))=-3\mathcal{W}_{2}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

For notational convenience, we shall refer to the three properties summarized in the proposition as: pair symmetry, partial trace-free, and first Bianchi identity.

Returning to our previous discussion, since we aim to trace the Weyl-type tensor within the leading coefficient
A
, it is natural to track the three properties satisfied by the Weyl-type tensor introduced in Proposition
3.1
. Of course, we cannot expect
A
to satisfy these properties at this stage. However, starting from the coordinate transformation (
20
), we can eliminate the coordinate freedom to track these properties. If a certain property is not satisfied, we can remove the obstruction so that the remaining part satisfies the corresponding property, allowing us to proceed to the next one. This is the overall philosophy of this section.

3.1.
First elimination attempt and the partial trace-Free obstruction

We continue from the rough expansion in Euclidean Bianchi gauge

(24)

\displaystyle h_{ij}(x)=A_{ijk\ell}\,\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}),\qquad r=|x|,

obtained in Subsection
2.4
. The coefficient
A
still depends on the choice of exterior
coordinates. We must remove as much of this ambiguity as possible.

We now formally begin tracking the three properties from
A
mentioned in Proposition
3.1
. First, the pair symmetry is evident; indeed, it is guaranteed by Proposition
2.12
. Let us now turn our attention to the second property, namely the partial trace-free condition.

As previously mentioned, our first tool for tracking the Weyl-type tensor from the leading coefficient
A
is the coordinate transformation of the form (
20
). Therefore, let us first examine how
A
transforms under the change of coordinates (
20
).

Denote the metric after the transformation of coordinates as
\tilde{g}=g_{\mathrm{E}}+\tilde{h}
, corresponding to the original
g=g_{\mathrm{E}}+h
. If we require
u
in the coordinate transformation (
20
) to satisfy
u=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-1})
, and combine this with
h=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-2})
, we obtain the following expansion for the first non-trivial term
\tilde{h}
:

(25)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{ij}(y)=h_{ij}(y)+\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}).

Thus, at the level of the leading coefficient, the pullback is governed by the linear term
\mathcal{L}_{u}g_{\mathrm{E}}
.

We further specialize the function
u
in the coordinate transformation (
20
) to be
u_{i}(y)=B_{ij}\tfrac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}}
, where
B
is a constant matrix, then the linear term
\mathcal{L}_{u}g_{\mathrm{E}}
in (
25
) can be computed as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{u}g_{\mathrm{E}})_{ij}(y)=\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijk\ell}\,\frac{y_{k}y_{\ell}}{r^{4}},

where

(26)

\displaystyle\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijk\ell}:=(B_{ij}+B_{ji})\,\delta_{k\ell}-\Bigl(B_{ik}\,\delta_{j\ell}+B_{i\ell}\,\delta_{jk}+B_{jk}\,\delta_{i\ell}+B_{j\ell}\,\delta_{ik}\Bigr).

Therefore, corresponding to the asymptotic expansion (
24
) of the first non-trivial term before the coordinate transformation, under
x_{i}=y_{i}+B_{ij}\tfrac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}}
,
h
transforms into

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{ij}(y)=\Bigl(A_{ijk\ell}+\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijk\ell}\Bigr)\frac{y_{k}y_{\ell}}{r^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}),

so the leading coefficient transforms by

\displaystyle A\longmapsto\widetilde{A}:=A+\Psi_{4}(B).

After averaging over
\Gamma
, we may assume that
B
is
\Gamma
-invariant.

The next lemma records the effect of
\Psi_{4}(B)
on the two partial traces of
A
. This is a direct contraction of (
26
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
m=4
and let
\Psi_{4}(B)
be defined by (
26
). Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijkk}&=2\,(B_{ij}+B_{ji}),\\
\Psi_{4}(B)_{ppk\ell}&=2\,\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{k\ell}-4\,B_{(k\ell)},\\
\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijj\ell}&=-4\,B_{(i\ell)}-6\,B_{[i\ell]}-\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{i\ell},\end{split}

where
B_{(i\ell)}=\frac{1}{2}(B_{i\ell}+B_{\ell i})
and

B_{[i\ell]}=\frac{1}{2}(B_{i\ell}-B_{\ell i})
.

Now let
U_{k\ell}:=A_{ppk\ell}
and
V_{i\ell}:=A_{ijj\ell}
. If one wants
\widetilde{A}
to be trace-free in both partial traces, then one must solve

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{ppk\ell}=0,\qquad\widetilde{A}_{ijj\ell}=0.

The next lemma shows that this system is not always solvable.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
A
satisfies the pair symmetries, i.e.
A_{ijk\ell}=A_{jik\ell}=A_{ij\ell k}
. Then there exists a matrix
B
such that, with
W:=A+\Psi_{4}(B)
, there hold
W_{ppk\ell}=0
and
W_{ijj\ell}=0
, if and only if

(27)

\displaystyle\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{tr}(V)=-2\,\operatorname{tr}(U).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, the condition
W_{ppk\ell}=0,W_{ijj\ell}=0
is equivalent to

(28a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=U_{k\ell}+2\,\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{k\ell}-4\,B_{(k\ell)},

(28b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=V_{i\ell}-4\,B_{(i\ell)}-6\,B_{[i\ell]}-\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{i\ell}.

We first prove the necessity of (
27
). Assume that equations (
28
) are solvable. Starting from (
28
), we extract relevant information to deduce (
27
). Taking traces of both equations in (
28
), we obtain

\operatorname{tr}(B)=-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}(U)
and
\operatorname{tr}(B)=\frac{1}{8}\operatorname{tr}(V)
, hence
\operatorname{tr}(V)=-2\operatorname{tr}(U)
.

The verification of
(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell})_{0}=0
is also straightforward. Equation (
28a
) yields
B_{(k\ell)}=\frac{1}{4}\,U_{k\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{k\ell}
.
Substituting this into the symmetric part of equation (
28b
), we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=V_{(i\ell)}-4\,B_{(i\ell)}-\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{i\ell}

\displaystyle=V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}-3\,\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{i\ell}.

Therefore
V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}
is pure trace, that is,

\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0
.
This proves the necessity of (
27
).

Conversely, assume that (
27
) holds. Since

\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0
, the tensor
V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}
is pure trace.
Its trace equals
\operatorname{tr}(V)-\operatorname{tr}(U)=-3\operatorname{tr}(U)
by the second condition in
(
27
). Hence

\displaystyle V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}=-\frac{3}{4}\,\operatorname{tr}(U)\,\delta_{i\ell}.

We now define
B
by setting

B_{[i\ell]}:=\frac{1}{6}\,V_{[i\ell]}
,

\operatorname{tr}(B):=-\frac{1}{4}\,\operatorname{tr}(U)
, and

B_{(k\ell)}:=\frac{1}{4}\,U_{k\ell}-\frac{1}{8}\,\operatorname{tr}(U)\,\delta_{k\ell}
.
Then equation (
28a
) holds by construction, and the skew-symmetric
part of equation (
28b
) also holds by the definition of
B_{[i\ell]}
.
For the symmetric part, we compute

\displaystyle V_{(i\ell)}-4\,B_{(i\ell)}-\operatorname{tr}(B)\,\delta_{i\ell}=V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}+\frac{3}{4}\,\operatorname{tr}(U)\,\delta_{i\ell}=0.

Thus (
28
) is solvable, and therefore
W_{ppk\ell}=0
and
W_{ijj\ell}=0
follow.
∎

The lemma shows that the trace-killing problem is not always solvable, and the failure of the two equalities in (
27
) constitutes a potential obstruction. This naturally leads us to analyze whether the tensor
\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}
can indeed vanish under the current conditions, and whether the trace relation
\operatorname{tr}(V)=-2\,\operatorname{tr}(U)
holds.

In fact, under the current setting, particularly based on the previously constructed Euclidean Bianchi gauge, the second trace relation is problematic. This is because directly contracting (
19
) yields
\operatorname{tr}(U)=-2\operatorname{tr}(V)
, which clearly does not directly imply the trace relation required by (
27
). Fortunately, however, we are able to prove that
\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0
is indeed valid.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that on
\Omega_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}/\Gamma
we have a metric
g=g_{\mathrm{E}}+h
in Euclidean
Bianchi gauge
\mathcal{B}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)=0
and with
\operatorname{R}(g)=0
. Assume moreover that
h=h^{A}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3})
, where
(h^{A})_{ij}(x)=A_{ijk\ell}\tfrac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}
. Write
U_{k\ell}:=A_{ppk\ell}
and
V_{i\ell}:=A_{ijj\ell}
, then

(29)

\displaystyle\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0.

Proof.

Recall that in the Euclidean Bianchi gauge, by carrying out the Euclidean linearization of the scalar curvature at
g_{\mathrm{E}}
, we obtain the following expansion:

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}(g)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(|h|\,|\partial^{2}h|+|\partial h|^{2}\bigr),

more precisely, the remainder term is of order
\mathcal{O}(r^{-6})
.

We first prove
U
is pure-trace.

Denote
h=h^{A}+\eta
and the Euclidean linearization
\operatorname{R}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\eta)=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h)
, then
\operatorname{R}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\eta)=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-5})
. Plugging this into
\operatorname{R}(g)=0
gives
\operatorname{R}^{\mathrm{lin}}(h^{A})=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-5})
. On the other hand, (denote
\theta_{i}=\tfrac{x_{i}}{r}
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(h^{A})=U_{k\ell}\,\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}=r^{-2}U_{k\ell}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}=r^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}(U)+(U_{k\ell})_{0}\,\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}\right),

thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}^{\mathrm{lin}}(h^{A})=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}\left[r^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}(U)+(U_{k\ell})_{0}\,\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}\right)\right]=4\,r^{-4}(U_{k\ell})_{0}\,\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell},

Since this is
\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-5})
, we get

\displaystyle(U_{k\ell})_{0}=0.

So to prove (
29
), it suffices to show that
\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}\bigr)_{0}
also vanishes.

Subtracting from (
19
) the same identity with
i
and

\ell
interchanged, and using the pair symmetry of
A
, we obtain
V_{i\ell}=V_{\ell i}
. Therefore, the goal of the proof has been reduced from
\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0
to
\bigl(V_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}=0
.

Now, let us denote the trace-less symmetric 2-tensor
\bigl(V_{(i\ell)}-U_{i\ell}\bigr)_{0}
by
\mathcal{S}
. Then the symmetric 2-tensors
U
and
V
can be decomposed respectively as

\displaystyle V_{i\ell}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(V)}{4}\delta_{i\ell}+\mathcal{S}_{i\ell};\quad U_{k\ell}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{4}\delta_{k\ell}.

Substituting these decompositions into
(
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{i(jk\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(\delta_{jk}\mathcal{S}_{i\ell}+\delta_{j\ell}\mathcal{S}_{ik}+\delta_{k\ell}\mathcal{S}_{ij}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2\operatorname{tr}(V)+\operatorname{tr}(U)}{48}\Bigl(\delta_{jk}\delta_{i\ell}+\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{ik}+\delta_{k\ell}\delta_{ij}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(\delta_{jk}\mathcal{S}_{i\ell}+\delta_{j\ell}\mathcal{S}_{ik}+\delta_{k\ell}\mathcal{S}_{ij}\Bigr).

Since
\operatorname{tr}(S)=0
, setting
i=j=p
in the above identity yields

\displaystyle A_{p(pk\ell)}=\frac{1}{3}\,\mathcal{S}_{k\ell}.

On the other hand, expanding the symmetrization and using the pair symmetries of
A
and
V
, we have

\displaystyle A_{p(pk\ell)}=\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(A_{ppk\ell}+A_{pp\ell k}+A_{pkp\ell}+A_{pk\ell p}+A_{p\ell pk}+A_{p\ell kp}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(U_{k\ell}+2V_{k\ell}\bigr).

Therefore

\displaystyle U_{k\ell}+2V_{k\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{k\ell}.

Taking trace-free parts and using
U_{0}=0
and
V_{0}=\mathcal{S}
, we obtain (
29
).
∎

So far we have identified the main obstruction to the vanishing of the partial traces of the rough coefficient
A
, namely the potential failure of
\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\operatorname{tr}(U)=0
. This leads naturally to the next step: we split off from
A
the part that gives rise to the failure.

3.2.
Canonical splitting of the rough leading coefficient

Continuing from the end of the previous subsection, since directly rendering the rough coefficient
A
in (
16
) partial trace-free via the coordinate transformation
x_{i}=y_{i}+B_{ij}\tfrac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}}
encounters an obstruction, which is quantified by
\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\operatorname{tr}(U)
, we relax the requirement. Instead of requiring both partial traces to vanish, we seek a coordinate transformation such that the transformed
A
satisfies

\displaystyle A_{ppk\ell}=0,\qquad A_{ijj\ell}=A_{\ell jji}.

That is, we kill the first partial trace and only require the second one to be symmetric. This weaker system holds promise of being solvable. The point of this adjustment is that it allows us to separate from
A
the part responsible for the failure of the partial trace-free condition, while the remaining part can then be arranged to satisfy both partial trace-free identities.

Specifically, if we seek a constant matrix
B
within the orbit
A^{\sharp}=A+\Psi_{4}(B)
, where
\Psi_{4}
is defined by (
26
) and
A
satisfies
A_{ijk\ell}=A_{jik\ell}=A_{ij\ell k}
, such that
A^{\sharp}_{ppk\ell}=0
and
A^{\sharp}_{ijj\ell}=A^{\sharp}_{\ell jji}
, it actually suffices to choose
B
as follows:

(30)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(B)=-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}(U),\quad B_{(k\ell)}=\frac{1}{4}U_{k\ell}-\frac{1}{8}\operatorname{tr}(U)\delta_{k\ell},\quad B_{[i\ell]}=\frac{1}{6}\,V_{[i\ell]}.

With this choice, set

\displaystyle\lambda(A):=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\,\operatorname{tr}(U)\bigr)

and define

(31)

\displaystyle\Xi(\mu)_{ijk\ell}:=-\frac{\mu}{9}\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{2\mu}{9}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr).

We then separate the scalar part from
A^{\sharp}
by setting

(32)

\displaystyle W(A):=A^{\sharp}-\Xi\bigl(\lambda(A)\bigr).

The following proposition shows that, after the scalar obstruction
\lambda(A)
has been tracked and removed, the remaining part has the partial trace-free property
required of a Weyl-type term.

Proposition 3.6
.

With
B
,
A^{\sharp}
and
W(A)
defined as above, one has

\displaystyle W(A)_{ppk\ell}=0,\qquad W(A)_{ijj\ell}=0.

Proof.

The map
\Xi
possesses the following partial trace property:

\displaystyle\Xi(\mu)_{ppk\ell}=0,\qquad\Xi(\mu)_{ijj\ell}=\mu\,\delta_{i\ell},

hence
W(A)_{ppk\ell}=0
holds obviously. As for the second property
W(A)_{ijj\ell}=0
, we compute

\displaystyle W(A)_{ijj\ell}

\displaystyle=A_{ijj\ell}+\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijj\ell}-\Xi\bigl(\lambda(A)\bigr)_{ijj\ell}

\displaystyle=V_{i\ell}-4B_{(i\ell)}-6B_{[i\ell]}-\operatorname{tr}(B)\delta_{i\ell}-\lambda(A)\delta_{i\ell}

\displaystyle=V_{i\ell}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}(V)}{4}\delta_{i\ell}=(V_{i\ell})_{0}=0,

where we used Proposition
3.5
in the last step. Proof complete.
∎

At this stage, after separating out the obstruction term
\lambda(A)=\tfrac{1}{4}(\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\,\operatorname{tr}(U))
as in (
32
), the remainder tensor
W(A)
already satisfies the two partial trace-free conditions. In other words, recalling the goal we set at the beginning of this section—to trace the three properties required of the Weyl-type tensor in Proposition
3.1
—we have so far successfully traced the first two properties from the leading coefficient
A
of the rough expansion: pair symmetry and partial trace-free. Therefore, in what follows, we proceed to trace the remaining third property from
W(A)
: the first Bianchi identity.

For convenience, we introduce the notation
T_{\mathrm{cyc}}
to denote the cyclic sum
T_{ijk\ell}+T_{ik\ell j}+T_{i\ell jk}
associated with a tensor
T
. We now compute
W(A)_{\mathrm{cyc}}
using the decomposition (
32
), i.e.

(33)

\displaystyle W(A)_{\mathrm{cyc}}=A^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{cyc}}-\Xi(\lambda(A))_{\mathrm{cyc}}.

We need to compute terms in RHS of (
33
) separately.

It’s worthy to notice that, Proposition
3.5
implies both
U
and
V
are pure-trace, i.e.

\displaystyle U_{k\ell}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{4}\delta_{k\ell};\qquad V_{i\ell}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(v)}{4}\delta_{i\ell}.

For
\Xi(\lambda(A))_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, using Definition
31
,

(34)

\displaystyle\Xi(\lambda(A))_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(V)}{12}+\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{6}\right)\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{\ell j}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}+\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}\right).

For
A^{\sharp}
, notice that
A^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{cyc}}=A_{\mathrm{cyc}}+\Psi_{4}(B)_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, therefore, we need to compute
A_{\mathrm{cyc}}
and
\Psi_{4}(B)_{\mathrm{cyc}}
.

First, one may exploit the constraint imposed by the Bianchi gauge—namely, the algebraic identity (
18
)—to rewrite
A_{\mathrm{cyc}}
; that is,

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(V)}{8}+\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{16}\right)\left(\delta_{jk}\delta_{i\ell}+\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{ik}+\delta_{k\ell}\delta_{ij}\right).\end{split}

Similarly, the Definition
26
reads

\displaystyle\Psi_{4}(B)_{\mathrm{cyc}}=-2\Bigl(\delta_{k\ell}B_{[ij]}+\delta_{j\ell}B_{[ik]}+\delta_{jk}B_{[i\ell]}\Bigr)-2\Bigl(\delta_{i\ell}B_{(jk)}+\delta_{ik}B_{(j\ell)}+\delta_{ij}B_{(k\ell)}\Bigr)

then using the “solution”

(defined in (

)),

and

, above expression can be reformulated as

(36)

\displaystyle\Psi_{4}(B)_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\left(-\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{8}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}(V)}{2}\right)\Bigl(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}\Bigr).

Combining (
35
) with (
36
) yields

(37)

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\left(-\frac{\operatorname{tr}(U)}{16}-\frac{3\operatorname{tr}(V)}{8}\right)\Bigl(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}\Bigr)

Plugging (

), (
34
) into (

) gives

(38)

\displaystyle W(A)_{cyc}=\left(-\frac{11\operatorname{tr}(U)}{48}-\frac{22\operatorname{tr}(V)}{48}\right)\Bigl(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}\Bigr)=0,

here we used
\operatorname{tr}(U)+2\operatorname{tr}(V)=0
.

Therefore, in view of (

), plugging the properties
W(A)_{ppk\ell}=0,W(A)_{ijj\ell}=0
previously obtained, we deduce that
W(A)
is a Weyl-type tensor, i.e.
W(A)\in\widetilde{W}
, thanks to Proposition
3.1
. So far, we have accomplished the goal set forth at the beginning of this section: to track the Weyl-type tensor from the leading coefficient
A
of the rough expansion (
16
), based on the three equivalent properties established in Proposition
3.1
. In other words, after performing the coordinate transformation
x_{i}=y_{i}+B_{ij}\frac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}}
based on (
30
), the leading coefficient
A^{\sharp}
admits the splitting:

(39)

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}=s\left(W_{\infty}\right)+\Xi\bigl(\lambda(A)\bigr),

where
W_{\infty}
is a Weyl tensor, the symmetrization map
s
defined in (
21
), and
\lambda(A)=\tfrac{1}{4}(\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\operatorname{tr}(U))
to be determined. For convenience, we shall refer to these two terms as the Weyl-type term and the scalar term, respectively.

3.3.
The scalar
\lambda(A)
and the ALE ADM mass

At the end of the previous subsection we obtained the splitting (
39
), in which the geometric interpretation of
s(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}(A))
is clear. We still needs to identify the scalar quantity
\lambda(A)
.

For ALE metrics, the ADM mass is the basic scalar invariant defined from the asymptotic behavior of the metric at infinity. It is therefore natural to compare the scalar part
\lambda(A)
in (
39
) with the ADM mass. We first recall the definition of the ALE ADM mass.

On the universal cover of an ALE end modeled on
\mathbb{R}^{4}/\Gamma
, let
S_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
denote the Euclidean sphere of radius
r
, with outward unit normal
\nu
and Euclidean surface measure
d\mu
. In dimension
4
, write
\omega_{3}:=|S^{3}|=2\pi^{2}
, and define the ADM mass by

(40)

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g):=\frac{1}{6\,\omega_{3}\,|\Gamma|}\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{S_{r}}\bigl(\partial_{j}g_{ij}-\partial_{i}g_{jj}\bigr)\,\nu_{i}\,d\mu,

whenever the limit exists. Under the asymptotic model produced above, the metric already has a
Cartesian
r^{-2}
leading term, so the integrand in (
40
) is of order
O(r^{-3})
.

Actually, in our ALE geometry, given the asymptotic expansion of the metric
g_{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}(x)
, where

\displaystyle h_{ij}(x)=A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}\tfrac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}),

a straightforward fact is that, the ADM mass is determined by the
r^{-2}
term alone:

(41)

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)=\frac{1}{6\,\omega_{3}\,|\Gamma|}\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{S_{r}}\Bigl(\partial_{j}\Bigl(A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}\Bigr)-\partial_{i}\Bigl(A^{\sharp}_{jjk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\nu_{i}\,dS.

This reduces the problem to the homogeneous coefficient
A^{\sharp}
itself.
Motivated by (
41
), for any constant tensor

A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}
satisfying pair-symmetry, i.e.
A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}=A^{\sharp}_{jik\ell}=A^{\sharp}_{ij\ell k}
, we denote
A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}\tfrac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}
by
h^{(2)}[A^{\sharp}]_{ij}(x)
, and introduce the linear functional

(42)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp}):=\frac{1}{6\,\omega_{3}\,|\Gamma|}\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{S_{r}}\bigl(\partial_{j}h^{(2)}[A^{\sharp}]_{ij}-\partial_{i}h^{(2)}[A^{\sharp}]_{jj}\bigr)\nu_{i}\,dS.

For the tensor
A^{\sharp}
arising from the ALE expansion of
g
,
(
41
) is exactly the statement that

(43)

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)=\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp}).

Thus the remaining task is to compare the two scalar quantities
\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})
and
\lambda(A)
. The key point is that
\mathfrak{m}
is linear and invariant under the natural

\mathrm{O}(4)
-action on the space of pair-symmetric tensors. Therefore

\mathfrak{m}
can only depend on the
\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant part of

A^{\sharp}
. We use the standard classification of isotropic fourth-order tensors
with minor symmetries; see
[
20
, (5.1)–(5.3)]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
V=\mathbb{R}^{4}
with the standard Euclidean metric
g_{\mathrm{E}}
, and let
\mathcal{T}

be the vector space of constant
(0,4)
-tensors
A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}
satisfying
A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}=A^{\sharp}_{jik\ell}=A^{\sharp}_{ij\ell k}
. Equip
\mathcal{T}
with the natural
\mathrm{O}(4)
-action

\displaystyle(A\cdot A^{\sharp})_{ijk\ell}:=A_{ai}A_{bj}A_{ck}A_{d\ell}\,A^{\sharp}_{abcd}.

Then the fixed subspace
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
is two-dimensional, and

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}=\alpha\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\beta\,(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk})

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,

\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}
and
\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}
form a basis of

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
.

The previous lemma describes all
\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant tensors in the ambient
space
\mathcal{T}
. In our situation, however, the tensors arising from the ALE
expansion satisfy one more algebraic condition, namely
A^{\sharp}_{ppk\ell}=0
. We therefore restrict to the subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\bigl\{A^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{T}:\ A^{\sharp}_{ppk\ell}=0\bigr\}.

Our goal is to compute the scalar quantity
\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})
, as claimed before. Since
\mathfrak{m}
is linear and
\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant, only the
invariant component of
A^{\sharp}
can contribute to its value. We isolate this
component by the standard compact-group averaging procedure
[
9
]
.
Define

\displaystyle P:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V},\qquad P(A^{\sharp}):=\int_{\mathrm{O}(4)}A\cdot A^{\sharp}\,dA,

where
dA
denotes the normalized Haar measure on
\mathrm{O}(4)
.

The space
\mathcal{V}
is preserved by the natural
\mathrm{O}(4)
-action, so
P
is
well-defined. By the left-invariance of the Haar measure,
P(A^{\sharp})
is

\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant, and hence
P(A^{\sharp})\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
.
Moreover, since
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant and linear,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{O}(4)}\mathfrak{m}(A\cdot A^{\sharp})\,dA

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\left(\int_{\mathrm{O}(4)}A\cdot A^{\sharp}\,dA\right)=\mathfrak{m}\bigl(P(A^{\sharp})\bigr),

Thus, in computing
\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})
, we may replace
A^{\sharp}
by its
\mathrm{O}(4)
-average.

It remains to understand
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
. Since

\displaystyle\Xi(1)_{ijk\ell}=-\frac{1}{9}\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{2}{9}\,(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}).

satisfies
\Xi(1)_{ppk\ell}=0
, we have
\Xi(1)\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
. Conversely, if
A^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
, then by
Lemma
3.7
we may write

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}=\alpha\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\beta\,(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk})

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
A^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{V}
, contracting in the
first pair gives
\beta=-2\alpha
. It follows that
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}
is
one-dimensional, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{O}(4)}=\mathbb{R}\,\Xi(1).

Consequently, for every
A^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists a unique scalar

c(A^{\sharp})\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle P(A^{\sharp})=c(A^{\sharp})\,\Xi(1).

Therefore

(44)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})=\mathfrak{m}\bigl(P(A^{\sharp})\bigr)=c(A^{\sharp})\,\mathfrak{m}(\Xi(1)).

To identify
\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})
, or the ALE ADM mass
m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)
in view of (
43
), it remains to compute the
coefficient
c(A^{\sharp})
and the number
\mathfrak{m}(\Xi(1))
.

•

the computation of
c(A^{\sharp})
:
The full scalar contraction

A^{\sharp}_{ijji}
is also
\mathrm{O}(4)
-invariant:

\displaystyle(A\cdot A^{\sharp})_{ijji}=A_{ai}A_{bj}A_{cj}A_{di}\,A^{\sharp}_{abcd}=\delta_{bc}\delta_{ad}\,A^{\sharp}_{abcd}=A^{\sharp}_{abba}.

Hence

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}_{ijji}=\bigl(P(A^{\sharp})\bigr)_{ijji}=c(A^{\sharp})\,\Xi(1)_{ijji}=4\,c(A^{\sharp}).

Therefore, since

\displaystyle\lambda(A)=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\operatorname{tr}(V)+2\operatorname{tr}(U)\Bigr)=\frac{1}{4}A^{\sharp}_{ijji},

then

\displaystyle c(A^{\sharp})=\lambda(A).

•

the computation of
\mathfrak{m}(\Xi(1))

From the formula for
\Xi(1)
we get

\displaystyle\partial_{j}h^{(2)}[\Xi(1)]_{ij}=\frac{2}{3}\,x_{i}r^{-4},\qquad\partial_{i}h^{(2)}[\Xi(1)]_{jj}=0.

Substituting into the definition (
42
) for
\mathfrak{m}
, we find

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(\Xi(1))=\frac{1}{6\,\omega_{3}\,|\Gamma|}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\,\omega_{3}=\frac{1}{9\,|\Gamma|}.

Combining these two computations into (
44
) yields

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}=\mathfrak{m}(A^{\sharp})=\frac{1}{9\,|\Gamma|}\,\lambda(A).

We now summarize the current computation in the following proposition, which clarifies the connection between the scalar term
\Xi\bigl(\lambda(A)\bigr)
in the splitting (
39
) and the ALE ADM mass.

Proposition 3.8
.

In the splitting (
39
) we derived before, the scalar term
\Xi(\lambda)
is actually determined by the ALE ADM mass of the end. More precisely, (
39
) can be further rewritten as

(45)

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}=\Xi\bigl(9|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}\bigr)+s\bigl(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}(A)\bigr).

Ultimately, the true identity of the unknown term
\Xi(\lambda(A))
in the splitting of the leading coefficient
A^{\sharp}
has been identified. This suggests a potential connection between the ALE geometric background under consideration and General Relativity.

3.4.
A Schwarzschild background and a relative repair

After performing the coordinate elimination, the homogeneous
r^{-2}

coefficient in the rough expansion (
16
) has been reduced to the form
A^{\sharp}
. However, the Euclidean Bianchi gauge obtained earlier is in
general no longer preserved. It is therefore natural to ask whether one can repair
the gauge once more, as was done in the Ricci-flat case in
[
27
]
,

Unfortunately, a direct Euclidean Bianchi correction is not suitable here. Indeed, by
(
45
), the obstruction is exactly the scalar term
\Xi(\lambda)
, where

\lambda:=\lambda(A)=9|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)
. Trying to remove it directly produces a correction vector field of order
r^{-1}
,
whose Lie derivative changes the homogeneous
r^{-2}
term. So this repair does not preserve
A^{\sharp}
. We therefore proceed differently. We will absorb the scalar part of

A^{\sharp}
into a reference Schwarzschild metric, and then impose a relative gauge with respect to
this background.

Now we introduce the reference Schwarzschild metric. Set
m_{\mathrm{Sch}}:=\frac{\lambda}{9}=|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)
, consider the isotropic Schwarzschild metric on the cover
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R}
,

\displaystyle U(x):=1+\frac{\lambda}{18\,r^{2}}=1+\frac{m_{\mathrm{Sch}}}{2\,r^{2}},\qquad g^{\mathrm{Sch}}:=U^{2}g_{\mathrm{E}}.

Since
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}(r^{-2})=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\}
, the metric
g^{\mathrm{Sch}}
is
scalar-flat on every exterior region.

To match the form
h^{(2)}[\Xi(\lambda)]
, we apply the radial
change of variables
\Phi^{\mathrm{Sch}}(x):=x-|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\frac{x}{r^{2}}
, and define
g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}:=(\Phi^{\mathrm{Sch}})^{*}g^{\mathrm{Sch}}
. Then

(46)

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}=g_{\mathrm{E}}+h^{(2)}[\Xi(\lambda)]+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-4}),

where, for any constant pair-symmetric
(0,4)
-tensor
A
,
h^{(2)}[A]_{ij}(x):=A_{ijk\ell}\tfrac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}
.

From the construction,
g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}
encodes the mass and acts as the reference metric naturally associated with the end. Consequently, it provides the background for the final relative gauge.

With the reference metric established, we define the relative gauge considered in this paper as follows:

Definition 3.9
.

For two metrics
g,\bar{g}
on
\Omega_{R}
, define

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}(g,\bar{g})^{k}:=g^{ij}\Bigl(\Gamma(g)^{k}_{ij}-\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{ij}\Bigr).

We say that
g
is in relative gauge with respect to
g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}
if

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}\left(g,g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}\right)=0\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}.

Just as in the construction of the Euclidean Bianchi gauge, we reformulate the problem of finding coordinates in the relative gauge as an equation for a vector field. Let
\widetilde{g}
be the metric resulting from the coordinate transformation, where the coefficient of the
-2
-order term is given by
A^{\sharp}
. Let
\varphi(x)=x+w(x)
with
w\to 0
at infinity, and set

g:=\varphi^{*}\widetilde{g}
. To require that
g
be in relative gauge with respect to
g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}
is exactly to require

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}\left(\varphi^{*}\widetilde{g},g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}\right)=0\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}.

We therefore define

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w):=\mathscr{V}\left(\varphi^{*}\widetilde{g},g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}\right),

and seek
w
as a zero of the mapping
\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{Sch}}
.

The strategy remains the same: we linearize
\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{Sch}}
at
w=0
, isolate
\Delta_{E}
as the principal part, and then solve the resulting equation by the same fixed-point argument as in the Euclidean Bianchi gauge-fixing.

For arbitrary fixed
\alpha\in(4,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
, there exist

R
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that, for every

w\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})
with

\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}<\varepsilon
, one has

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w)=\mathscr{V}(\widetilde{g},\bar{g}_{\lambda,\mathcal{S}})+\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}w+\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w),

where
\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{Sch}}
satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\,3+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl(R^{-2}\,\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}+R^{-(2+\sigma)}\,\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}^{2}\Bigr),

and

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w_{1})-\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(w_{2})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\,3+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\Bigl(R^{-2}+R^{-(2+\sigma)}(\|w_{1}\|+\|w_{2}\|)\Bigr)\,\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}.

The desired gauge repair then follows from the same fixed-point argument as before. We therefore omit the repetition and state the resulting proposition.

Proposition 3.10
.

There exists
\sigma\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(4,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
,
R
sufficiently large and
w\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})
, such that
\varphi(x)=x+w(x)
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism of
\Omega_{R}
onto its image,

\varphi(\Omega_{R})\subset\Omega_{R/2}
, and

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}\left(\varphi^{*}\widetilde{g},g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp}\right)=0\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\,1+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\,\|\mathscr{V}(\widetilde{g},g^{\mathrm{Sch},\sharp})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\,3+\sigma}(\Omega_{R})}.

If
g:=\varphi^{*}\widetilde{g}
, then

\displaystyle g_{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}+A^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-2-\sigma}),

so the homogeneous
r^{-2}
coefficient is unchanged.

Remark 3.11
.

In what follows, we will refer to a coordinate system as a preferred coordinate system if it simultaneously satisfies the condition that the coefficient of the order
-2
term in the asymptotic expansion of the metric at infinity is
A^{\sharp}
, and the relative gauge considered in Proposition
3.10
.

Proposition
3.10
shows that the relative gauge can be imposed without changing the homogeneous
r^{-2}
coefficient
A^{\sharp}
. So far, we have completed the proof of the first main theorem, Theorem
1.3
.

3.5.
Rigidity and Euclidean motion at infinity

Up to this point, we have thoroughly investigated the metric at infinity, with a specific focus on the coefficient of the
-2
-order term. A basic issue remains: is the curvature information encoded in this term an intrinsic feature
of the end, or is it still affected by the residual choice of coordinates? This is the main uniqueness problem
in the asymptotic analysis. Unless it is resolved, the coefficient
A^{\sharp}
and the quantities extracted from
it cannot yet be regarded as geometric invariants of the end.

In this subsection we show that the remaining freedom is exactly the expected one. Any two preferred
coordinate systems on the same end differ, to leading order, by a Euclidean motion at infinity. This rigidity
gives a precise transformation law for
A^{\sharp}
, and shows that the data

(\lambda,\operatorname{W}_{\infty})
obtained from its canonical splitting are intrinsic, modulo the natural

O(4)
-action. Thus the
r^{-2}
term is not merely a convenient normal form: it provides a canonical way
to identify the curvature information at infinity.

We begin with the rigidity statement for the transition map.

Theorem 3.12
.

Assume
(M^{4},g)
admits two preferred coordinate systems
x
and
y
on the same end
(after lifting to the universal cover if necessary), both defined on exterior domains
\Omega_{R}=\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R}
and
\Omega_{R^{\prime}}=\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}}
. Let
g^{(x)}
and
g^{(y)}
be the coordinate expressions of
g
,
and let

\displaystyle F:=y\circ x^{-1}:\Omega_{R}\to\Omega_{R^{\prime}}.

Assume that for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
one has

(47)

\displaystyle\begin{split}g^{(x)}_{ij}(x)&=\delta_{ij}+A^{\sharp,(x)}_{ijk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{|x|^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-2-\sigma}),\\
g^{(y)}_{ab}(y)&=\delta_{ab}+A^{\sharp,(y)}_{abpq}\frac{y_{p}y_{q}}{|y|^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|y|^{-2-\sigma}),\end{split}

where
A^{\sharp,(x)}
and
A^{\sharp,(y)}
are constant pair-symmetric tensors. Then there exist
A\in O(4)
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
such that

(48)

\displaystyle F(x)=Ax+b+\mathcal{E}(x),

with

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-2}\log|x|),\qquad\partial^{(k)}\mathcal{E}(x)=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-2-k}\log|x|)\quad(k\geq 1).

If both preferred charts are
\Gamma
-compatible, then
A
normalizes
\Gamma
.

The proof uses two standard analytic inputs. The first is a standard exterior Poisson estimate in dimension four. We record only the form needed below and omit the proof. Briefly, one multiplies
u
by a cutoff to obtain a global
v
with
\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}v=g
decaying like
f
. The Newton potential
w=\Gamma*g
then decays as asserted. By the assumed growth bound on
u
, the harmonic remainder
h=v-w
has at most linear growth, hence is affine by Liouville. The asymptotic expansion of
u
follows by restricting back to
\Omega_{2R}
. With the extra regularity, a rescaling and interior Schauder estimates give the derivative bounds.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\Omega_{R}:=\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus B_{R}
, and let
u:\Omega_{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be smooth. Assume
u
has at most linear growth, i.e.
|u(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)
for
|x|\gg 1
, and

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{\mathrm{E}}}u=f,\qquad f=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-3-\sigma})\qquad\text{on }\Omega_{R}

for some
\sigma\geq 0
. Then there exist
L\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^{4},\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

such that

\displaystyle u(x)=Lx+c+E(x),

where

\displaystyle E(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-1-\sigma}),&0\leq\sigma<1,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-2}\log|x|),&\sigma=1,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-2}),&\sigma>1.\end{cases}

We now turn to the transition map
F=y\circ x^{-1}:\Omega_{R}\to\Omega_{R^{\prime}}
. We will establish an estimate for
D^{2}F
, this serves as the second analytic input required for the proof of Theorem
3.12
.

Since
F
is a local isometry between
g^{(x)}
and
g^{(y)}
, its components satisfy the harmonic map system

(50)

\displaystyle(g^{(x)})^{ij}\Bigl(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}F^{a}-\Gamma^{k}_{ij}\partial_{k}F^{a}+\widetilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}(F)\,\partial_{i}F^{b}\,\partial_{j}F^{c}\Bigr)=0.

The asymptotics (
47
) imply that, after rescaling on fixed annuli,
this becomes a uniformly elliptic system whose coefficients are close to the Euclidean ones.
Moreover, the metric equivalence
g^{(x)}\sim g_{\mathrm{E}}
and
g^{(y)}\sim g_{\mathrm{E}}
gives
|DF|\leq C
and
|F(x)|\simeq|x|
for
|x|\gg 1
. The next lemma gives the bound for
D^{2}F
.

Lemma 3.14
.

In the situation of Theorem
3.12
, there exist
R_{1}\geq R
and
C>0
such that

(51)

\displaystyle|D^{2}F(x)|\leq C\,|x|^{-1}

for all
x\in\Omega_{R_{1}}
.

Proof.

The argument is a standard rescaling argument on a fixed annulus. We zoom in at the scale

r_{0}:=|x_{0}|
, rewrite the equation for
F
on a unit-size annulus, and then use interior elliptic estimates
to obtain a uniform
C^{2,\alpha}
bound at that scale.

Fix
x_{0}\in\Omega_{R_{1}}
with
r_{0}:=|x_{0}|
large, and set

\displaystyle F_{r_{0}}(z):=\frac{1}{r_{0}}F(r_{0}z)

on the fixed annulus
A:=B_{2}\setminus B_{1/2}
. Then

\displaystyle|D_{x}^{2}F(x_{0})|=r_{0}^{-1}|D_{z}^{2}F_{r_{0}}(z_{0})|,\qquad z_{0}:=x_{0}/r_{0}\in A.

So it suffices to bound
D_{z}^{2}F_{r_{0}}
uniformly on a smaller annulus.

Because
|F(x)|\simeq|x|
and
|DF|\leq C
for
|x|\gg 1
, the rescaled maps
F_{r_{0}}
are uniformly bounded in

C^{0}
and
C^{1}
on
A
. Rescaling the harmonic map equation (

\displaystyle a^{ij}(r_{0}z)\,\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}F_{r_{0}}^{a}=B_{r_{0}}^{k}(z)\,\partial_{z_{k}}F_{r_{0}}^{a}-C^{a}_{r_{0},ij,bc}(z)\,\partial_{z_{i}}F_{r_{0}}^{b}\,\partial_{z_{j}}F_{r_{0}}^{c}

on
A
, where
a^{ij}(r_{0}z)
is uniformly elliptic. Moreover, by the decay of the metric coefficients and Christoffel symbols, together with the chain rule,
the rescaled coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle\|a^{ij}(r_{0}\cdot)-\delta^{ij}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(A)}\leq Cr_{0}^{-2},\quad\|B_{r_{0}}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(A)}+\|C_{r_{0}}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(A)}\leq Cr_{0}^{-2}.

In particular, the system is uniformly elliptic on
A
for
r_{0}
large, with coefficient bounds independent of
r_{0}
.

Now choose nested annuli
A^{\prime}\Subset A^{\prime\prime}\Subset A
. Applying the interior

,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|F_{r_{0}}\|_{W^{2,p}(A^{\prime\prime})}\leq C_{p}.

Then Sobolev–Morrey gives a uniform

bound on
A^{\prime\prime}
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
, so the right-hand side
of the rescaled system is uniformly bounded in
C^{0,\alpha}(A^{\prime\prime})
. A further application of the interior Schauder
estimate on

yields

\displaystyle\|F_{r_{0}}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(A^{\prime})}\leq C,

with

independent of

.

Since

, we have
|D_{z}^{2}F_{r_{0}}(z_{0})|\leq C
, and scaling back gives

\displaystyle|D_{x}^{2}F(x_{0})|\leq Cr_{0}^{-1}.

This proves (

The coordinate change identity is

From Theorem

\displaystyle\partial_{i}F^{a}(x)=A_{ai}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3}\log r),\quad F(x)=Ax+b+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-2}\log r),

Since

Comparing with the

\displaystyle\begin{split}s\bigl(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}\bigr)_{ijk\ell}&=A^{\sharp,(x)}_{ijk\ell}-\Xi\bigl(\lambda\bigr)_{ijk\ell},\\

3.6.
An example: the Burns metric

We now present the detailed computation for the Burns metric, announced in the introduction, to illustrate the statements of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.7
. We work in the standard ALE coordinates on the end of
(\mathrm{Bl}_{0}\mathbb{C}^{2},g^{\mathrm{Burns}})
, where the group at infinity is trivial. Starting from the Kähler potential, we compute the rough

r^{-2}
coefficient
A^{\mathrm{Burns}}
, then, just as we previously tracked the Weyl-type tensor from the rough coefficient, we will perform the corresponding coordinate transformation, and finally obtain
the splitting

\displaystyle(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})^{\sharp}=\Xi(3)+s(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{Burns}}),\qquad\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{Burns}}\neq 0.

This shows concretely that the mass part and the Weyl-type part in
Theorem
1.3
may both be nonzero.

On the end, we identify
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
with
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\}
and write

r=|x|
. We use the convention under which the flat Kähler potential

s=|z|^{2}
gives the standard Euclidean metric
g_{E}
on

\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
. With this convention, the Burns metric is
given by the Kähler potential

\Phi(s)=s+\log s
, where
s=|z|^{2}=r^{2}
. Hence

\displaystyle g_{a\bar{b}}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=\partial_{a}\partial_{\bar{b}}\Phi=\left(1+\frac{1}{s}\right)\delta_{ab}-\frac{\bar{z}_{a}z_{b}}{s^{2}}.

Let
J
be the standard complex structure on

\mathbb{R}^{4}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and write

(Jx)_{i}=J_{ik}x_{k}
, where the index of
J
is lowered using the Euclidean
metric. In real coordinates, the preceding identity gives the exact formula

(63)

\displaystyle g_{ij}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=\delta_{ij}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\delta_{ij}-\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{r^{4}}-\frac{(Jx)_{i}(Jx)_{j}}{r^{4}}.

Thus
g^{\mathrm{Burns}}=g_{E}+h^{\mathrm{Burns}}
on the end, where the order
r^{-2}
perturbation has the form

(64)

\displaystyle h_{ij}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=A_{ijk\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{r^{4}},\quad A_{ijk\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(J_{ik}J_{j\ell}+J_{i\ell}J_{jk}\bigr).

A natural next step is to compute the two partial traces of the rough coefficient
A^{\mathrm{Burns}}
and the ADM mass of the metric
g^{\mathrm{Burns}}
. Still set

U_{k\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}:=A_{ppk\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}
and
V_{i\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}:=A_{ijj\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}
. Since
J
is an
orthogonal complex structure, there holds
J_{pk}J_{p\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}
and
J_{ij}J_{j\ell}=-\delta_{i\ell}
. Then we have

\displaystyle U_{k\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=2\delta_{k\ell},\qquad V_{i\ell}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=-\delta_{i\ell},

and

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathrm{Burns}}(A)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(V^{\mathrm{Burns}})+2\operatorname{tr}(U^{\mathrm{Burns}})\bigr)=3.

Since the group at infinity is trivial, Theorem
1.3
predicts that
this scalar quantity
\lambda^{\mathrm{Burns}}(A)
should be
9m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g^{\mathrm{Burns}})
, which means the Burns metric should satisfy

(65)

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g^{\mathrm{Burns}})=\frac{1}{3}.

This value agrees with the standard computation of the ADM mass of the Burns
metric in Kähler geometry. Indeed, the Burns metric with potential

\Phi(s)=s+\log s
has ADM mass
1/3
under the normalization used here; this is
consistent with the general ALE Kähler mass formula of Hein–LeBrun

[
12
]
.

Now we carry out the coordinate transformation analogous to the general case; see Proposition
3.6
for details. Consider the following coordinate transformation:

(66)

\displaystyle x_{i}=y_{i}+B_{ij}\frac{y_{j}}{|y|^{2}},\quad B=-\frac{1}{2}I.

At the level of the
r^{-2}
coefficient, this changes
A^{\mathrm{Burns}}
into

(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})^{\sharp}=A^{\mathrm{Burns}}+\Psi_{4}(B)
. For this choice of
B
, the definition of
\Psi_{4}
(c.f. (
26
)) gives

\displaystyle\Psi_{4}(B)_{ijk\ell}=-\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}.

Combining this with (
64
), we get

\displaystyle(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(J_{ik}J_{j\ell}+J_{i\ell}J_{jk}\bigr).

Analogous to the splitting we performed on
A^{\sharp}
in (
45
), we may separate the mass part of
A^{\mathrm{Burns}}
. Since
\lambda(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})=3
, the corresponding scalar
tensor is

\displaystyle\Xi(3)_{ijk\ell}=-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{2}{3}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr).

Hence the remaining part is

\displaystyle\begin{split}W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})_{ijk\ell}&:=(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}-\Xi(3)_{ijk\ell}\\
&=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}-\frac{1}{6}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(J_{ik}J_{j\ell}+J_{i\ell}J_{jk}\bigr).\end{split}

This tensor is not zero. For example, if the orthonormal basis is chosen so
that
J_{12}J_{21}=-1
, then
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})_{1212}=\frac{1}{3}
. On the other hand,
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})
has exactly the algebraic properties expected from
the Weyl-type part. Direct contraction shows
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})_{ppk\ell}=0
and
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})_{ijj\ell}=0
hold. Moreover, using
J_{ij}=-J_{ji}
and
J^{2}=-I
, one checks that
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})
satisfies the first Bianchi identity. Therefore
W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})
lies in the image of the map
s
applied to algebraic Weyl
tensors by Proposition
3.1
. Thus there exists a nonzero algebraic Weyl tensor

\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{Burns}}
such that

\displaystyle W(A^{\mathrm{Burns}})=s\bigl(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{Burns}}\bigr).

Consequently, after applying the coordinate transformation (
66
), the Burns metric (
63
) has
the expansion

\displaystyle g_{ij}^{\mathrm{Burns}}=\delta_{ij}+\left(\Xi(3)_{ijk\ell}+s\bigl(\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{Burns}}\bigr)_{ijk\ell}\right)\frac{y_{k}y_{\ell}}{|y|^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|y|^{-3}).

Since
\Gamma=\{1\}
and
3=9m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g^{\mathrm{Burns}})
by (
65
), this is
precisely the form predicted by Theorem
1.3
. The subsequent gauge correction used to impose the relative Schwarzschild gauge
does not change this displayed
r^{-2}
coefficient. Hence the Burns metric
gives a concrete example in which the leading term contains both a nonzero mass part and a nonzero Weyl-type part. Furthermore, Theorem
1.7
can also be checked directly from this explicit coefficient.

4.
Leading Weyl data and crepancy for minimal quotient resolutions

In this section we specialize the preferred asymptotic expansion obtained above
to the scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics on minimal resolutions of quotient
surface singularities. Let

\Gamma\subset\mathrm{U}(2)
be a finite subgroup acting freely on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,
set
Y=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
, and let

\displaystyle\pi:X\longrightarrow Y

be the minimal resolution. The resolution map identifies

X\setminus\pi^{-1}(0)
with the smooth part of the quotient, and therefore
gives
X
a natural holomorphic ALE end.

Scalar-flat Kähler ALE metrics on such spaces are provided by the existence
results of Calderbank–Singer in the cyclic case and Lock–Viaclovsky in the
non-cyclic case (c.f.
[
7
]
,
[
18
]
). Our aim here is to use the leading asymptotic invariant
constructed in Theorem
1.3
in the reverse direction: starting from a
scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric on
X
, we ask what the Weyl tensor at infinity
remembers about the resolution
\pi
.

The first step is to compute the preferred

|x|^{-2}
coefficient in the Kähler setting and relate its Weyl part to the
ADM mass and the limiting complex structure at infinity. The second step uses
the Hein–LeBrun mass formula to rewrite this coefficient in terms of the
exceptional curves of the minimal resolution. This will give the crepancy
criterion in Theorem
1.10
.

4.1.
The Kähler ALE coefficient at infinity

Let
g
be a complete scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric on
(X,J)
, and let

\omega=g(J\cdot,\cdot)
be its Kähler form. On the quotient end described
above, lift to the
\Gamma
-cover and use the holomorphic coordinates

z=(z^{1},z^{2})
coming from the quotient map. Let
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{4})
be the
associated real coordinates and put
r=|u|=|z|
. We first compute the
homogeneous
r^{-2}
coefficient in these holomorphic coordinates.

In these coordinates, the scalar-flat Kähler ALE potential has the form

\displaystyle\omega=\sqrt{-1}\,\partial\bar{\partial}\bigl(\rho+c\log\rho+\psi\bigr),\qquad\rho=|z|^{2}=r^{2},\qquad\psi=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-1}),

for some real constant
c
. We use the convention that the flat potential

\rho=|z|^{2}
gives the Euclidean metric. Such expansions are standard in the
scalar-flat Kähler ALE setting; see for example
[
12
,
2
,
13
]
. The
constant
c
denotes the logarithmic coefficient, and should not be confused with
the discrepancy coefficients used later.

Let
J_{\infty}
denote the standard limiting complex structure in the holomorphic
coordinates
u
. If
F=F(\rho)
is a radial Kähler potential, then the
associated real metric is

\displaystyle(g_{F})_{ij}=F^{\prime}(\rho)\delta_{ij}+F^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\Bigl(u_{i}u_{j}+(J_{\infty}u)_{i}(J_{\infty}u)_{j}\Bigr).

For
F(\rho)=\rho+c\log\rho
, one has
F^{\prime}(\rho)=1+c/\rho
and

F^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=-c/\rho^{2}
. Since
\psi=\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-1})
, its contribution
to the metric is
\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(r^{-3})
. Hence

(67)

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+c\left(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|u|^{2}}-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{|u|^{4}}-\frac{(J_{\infty}u)_{i}(J_{\infty}u)_{j}}{|u|^{4}}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|u|^{-3}).

The coefficient
c
is determined by the ADM mass. Put
h_{ij}=g_{ij}-\delta_{ij}
.
From (
67
), one obtains

\displaystyle\partial_{j}h_{ij}-\partial_{i}h_{jj}=2c\frac{u_{i}}{|u|^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|u|^{-4}).

Contracting with the Euclidean unit normal on
S_{r}
in the cover and using the
ADM convention (
40
), we get

\displaystyle c=3|\Gamma|\,m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g).

The holomorphic ALE coordinates are not yet the preferred coordinates of
Theorem
1.3
. At the level of the homogeneous
|u|^{-2}
coefficient, the required coordinate transformation is

\displaystyle u_{i}=x_{i}-\frac{c}{2}\frac{x_{i}}{|x|^{2}}.

Equivalently, this is
u_{i}=x_{i}+B_{ij}x_{j}/|x|^{2}
with
B=-\frac{c}{2}I
. Using
(
26
), or directly differentiating the coordinate change, the
metric becomes

(68)

\displaystyle g^{\sharp}_{ab}=\delta_{ab}+c\left(\frac{x_{a}x_{b}}{|x|^{4}}-\frac{(J_{\infty}x)_{a}(J_{\infty}x)_{b}}{|x|^{4}}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-3}).

It remains to pass from the intermediate coordinates in
(
68
) to the preferred coordinates of
Theorem
1.3
. This last coordinate change only affects lower-order
terms. Therefore the homogeneous
|x|^{-2}
coefficient in
(
68
) is unchanged. The only remaining freedom is
the orthogonal ambiguity described in Theorem
1.7
, so we keep the
coordinate label in the final formula.

Rewrite (
68
) as

(69)

\displaystyle g^{\sharp}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+(A^{K})^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}\frac{x_{k}x_{\ell}}{|x|^{4}}+\mathcal{O}_{\infty}(|x|^{-3}),

here

\displaystyle(A^{K})^{\sharp}_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr)-\frac{c}{2}\bigl((J_{\infty})_{ik}(J_{\infty})_{j\ell}+(J_{\infty})_{i\ell}(J_{\infty})_{jk}\bigr).

The scalar part in the preferred expansion (
69

\Xi(9|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g))=\Xi(3c)
according to (
1

(A^{K})^{\sharp}
gives

\displaystyle(A^{K})^{\sharp}-\Xi(3c)=c\,Q_{J_{\infty}},

where

\displaystyle(Q_{J_{\infty}})_{ijk\ell}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}-\frac{1}{6}\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigl((J_{\infty})_{ik}(J_{\infty})_{j\ell}+(J_{\infty})_{i\ell}(J_{\infty})_{jk}\bigr).

Using the algebraic characterization in Proposition
3.1
, one
checks that
Q_{J_{\infty}}
is the image under
s
of a unique algebraic Weyl
tensor. We denote this tensor by
\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}}
, so that

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}})=Q_{J_{\infty}}.

Equivalently, it is given by

(70)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}})_{ijk\ell}=&\frac{1}{3}\bigl(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}\bigr)+\frac{2}{3}(J_{\infty})_{ij}(J_{\infty})_{k\ell}\\
&+\frac{1}{3}(J_{\infty})_{ik}(J_{\infty})_{j\ell}-\frac{1}{3}(J_{\infty})_{i\ell}(J_{\infty})_{jk}.\end{split}

This tensor is nonzero. For example, in a unitary orthonormal basis one has

(Q_{J_{\infty}})_{1212}=1/3
.

Thus the homogeneous coefficient in the preferred expansion (
69
) satisfies

\displaystyle(A^{K})^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=\Xi(3c)+s(c\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}})

\displaystyle=\Xi\bigl(9|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\bigr)+s\bigl(3|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}}\bigr).

Comparing with the preferred expansion in (
1
), and using
the injectivity of
s
on algebraic Weyl tensors, gives the leading Weyl term in
the preferred coordinates:

(71)

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}(g)=3|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}}.

The coordinate covariance of formula (
71
) is also a necessary consideration. If
A\in\mathrm{O}(4)
, we regard
J
as a two-form using the Euclidean metric
and write

\displaystyle(A\cdot J)_{ij}=A_{ai}A_{bj}J_{ab}

for the induced orthogonal action. Recall for an algebraic Weyl tensor
W
, we write

\displaystyle(A\cdot W)_{ijk\ell}=A_{ai}A_{bj}A_{ck}A_{d\ell}W_{abcd}.

Then the tensor in (
70
) is equivariant under this action:

(72)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{A\cdot J}=A\cdot\mathcal{W}_{J}.

The preceding computation identifies the leading Weyl part in a preferred
coordinate system obtained from the holomorphic ALE end. Since

\mathcal{W}_{J}
is equivariant under the orthogonal action
(
72
), this identification is preserved when one
changes between preferred coordinate systems. We record the result in the
following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\pi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
be the minimal resolution fixed
above, and let
g
be a scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric on
(X,J)
. Let
x

be a preferred coordinate system obtained by applying Theorem
1.3

starting from the holomorphic ALE end induced by
\pi
. Denote by

J_{\infty}^{(x)}
the limiting Euclidean complex structure in the
x
-coordinates,
and denote by
\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)
the Weyl tensor at infinity in the same
coordinates. Then

(73)

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=3|\Gamma|\,m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\,\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}.

Moreover, if
x
and
y
are two such preferred coordinate systems and
A
is
the orthogonal linear part of the transition map at infinity in view of Theorem
3.12
, then

\displaystyle J_{\infty}^{(x)}=A\cdot J_{\infty}^{(y)},\qquad\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=A\cdot\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(y)}(g).

Consequently, the formula (
73
) is
preserved under every change between such preferred coordinate systems.

Proof.

The formula in one preferred coordinate system follows from the computation
above. It remains only to check the behavior under a change of preferred
coordinates. By Theorem
1.7
, for the asymptotic expansion of
F=y\circ x^{-1}
, with leading coefficient
A\in\mathrm{O}(4)
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=A\cdot\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(y)}(g).

The same transition map
F=y\circ x^{-1}
sends the limiting complex structure in the

y
-coordinates to the limiting complex structure in the
x
-coordinates, so

J_{\infty}^{(x)}=A\cdot J_{\infty}^{(y)}
. Using
(
72
), we get

\displaystyle A\cdot\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(y)}}=\mathcal{W}_{A\cdot J_{\infty}^{(y)}}=\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}.

Therefore, if the formula holds in the
y
-coordinates, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)

\displaystyle=A\cdot\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(y)}(g)

\displaystyle=A\cdot\left(3|\Gamma|\,m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\,\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(y)}}\right)

\displaystyle=3|\Gamma|\,m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)\,\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}.

The converse follows by interchanging
x
and
y
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Formula (
73
) shows that, in the
scalar-flat Kähler ALE setting, the Weyl tensor at infinity is not an
arbitrary Weyl-type boundary datum. The Kähler structure singles out a
distinguished algebraic Weyl tensor

\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}
, and the whole Weyl contribution is a scalar
multiple of this tensor. The scalar multiple is exactly

3|\Gamma|m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)
. Thus the ADM mass determines both the scalar part of the preferred expansion
and the size of the Weyl-type part, while the limiting complex structure
determines the tensor direction.

We now replace the mass coefficient in
(
73
) by a complex-geometric quantity. For
a scalar-flat Kähler ALE surface, the Hein–LeBrun mass formula gives

(74)

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{ADM}}(g)=-\frac{1}{3\pi}\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle.

Here
\clubsuit
is the inverse of the natural map from compactly supported
cohomology to ordinary cohomology
[
12
]
. In the present ALE setting, this
map is an isomorphism over
\mathbb{R}
, and the bracket is the Poincaré duality
pairing

\displaystyle H_{c}^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})\times H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Combining (
73
) and
(
74
) gives the form used below.

Corollary 4.3
.

In every preferred coordinate system
x
obtained from the holomorphic end of
the resolution, one has

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=-\frac{|\Gamma|}{\pi}\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}.

Thus, after the holomorphic end of the minimal resolution is fixed, the
vanishing of
\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)
is equivalent to the vanishing of the
Chern–Kähler pairing
\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle
. The vanishing statement is independent of the preferred coordinate system, since
two representatives of
\operatorname{W}_{\infty}
differ by an orthogonal action. We now
express this pairing in terms of the exceptional curves of the minimal resolution.

4.2.
Minimal quotient resolutions and crepancy

We keep the notation fixed above. Thus
\pi:X\to Y=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
is the minimal resolution, and
g
is a scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric on

(X,J)
with Kähler form
\omega
. The resolution map identifies the end of

X
with
Y\setminus\{0\}
, so the holomorphic end used in
Subsection
4.1
is the one determined by
\pi
. In this subsection, we will proof Theorem
1.10
.

Write the exceptional divisor as
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}E_{i}
, and let

Q=(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})
be its intersection matrix. If
\Gamma
is trivial, then

E=\emptyset
and the sums below are understood to be zero. Otherwise, for a
quotient surface singularity, the curves
E_{i}
are rational curves,
E
is
connected, and
Q
is negative definite
[
4
]
. Since the resolution is
minimal,
E
contains no
(-1)
-curves. Thus, writing
E_{i}^{2}=-e_{i}
, one has

e_{i}\geq 2
for every
i
.

Set
H=-Q
. Then
H
is positive definite, with positive diagonal entries and
nonpositive off-diagonal entries. The connectedness of
E
means that
H
is
irreducible. Hence
H
is an irreducible symmetric nonsingular
M
-matrix, and
its inverse is strictly positive
[
5
]
:

(75)

\displaystyle(H^{-1})_{ij}>0\qquad\text{for all }i,j.

We next compute the relative canonical divisor. Since

Y=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
is a quotient surface singularity, it is

\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein. Indeed, a suitable positive tensor power of the standard
holomorphic two-form on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is
\Gamma
-invariant and descends to the
smooth part of
Y
. Thus
K_{Y}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier, and
\pi^{*}K_{Y}
is defined
as a
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor. The difference
K_{X}-\pi^{*}K_{Y}
is supported on the
exceptional divisor, so we may write

(76)

\displaystyle K_{X}-\pi^{*}K_{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}E_{i}.

For each exceptional curve
E_{j}
, one has
\pi^{*}K_{Y}\cdot E_{j}=0
, because
E_{j}
is
contracted by
\pi
. On the other hand, by adjunction and by
E_{j}^{2}=-e_{j}
,

\displaystyle K_{X}\cdot E_{j}=-2-E_{j}^{2}=e_{j}-2.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{i}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})=e_{j}-2\qquad\text{for every }j.

Define
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})^{T}
by
H\mu=(e_{1}-2,\dots,e_{k}-2)^{T}
, since
H=-Q
, equation (
76
) becomes

(77)

\displaystyle K_{X}-\pi^{*}K_{Y}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}E_{i}.

Define the areas of the exceptional curves by

\displaystyle A_{i}:=\int_{E_{i}}\omega,\qquad i=1,\dots,k.

Since
[\omega]
is a Kähler class and each
E_{i}
is an effective curve, one has

A_{i}>0
.

We now compute the Chern–Kähler pairing appearing in Corollary
4.3
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For the scalar-flat Kähler ALE metric
g
on
X
, one has

\displaystyle\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}A_{i}.

Proof.

The classes of the exceptional curves form a basis of
H_{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
. We use the
intersection-matrix form of the Hein–LeBrun mass formula

[
12
, Theorem 5.3]
. In their notation, if

\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{b}
is a basis of
H_{2}(M;\mathbb{R})
, if

Q^{\Sigma}=(\Sigma_{\alpha}\cdot\Sigma_{\beta})
, and if

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{b})^{T}=(Q^{\Sigma})^{-1}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{1}}c_{1},\dots,\int_{\Sigma_{b}}c_{1}\right)^{T},

then the Chern–Kähler pairing in (
74
) is

\displaystyle\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{b}\lambda_{\alpha}\int_{\Sigma_{\alpha}}\omega.

In the present case, take
\Sigma_{i}=E_{i}
. Then
Q^{\Sigma}=Q
. By adjunction,

\displaystyle\int_{E_{j}}c_{1}(X,J)=-K_{X}\cdot E_{j}=2-e_{j}.

Therefore the Hein–LeBrun coefficient vector is

\displaystyle Q^{-1}(2-e_{1},\dots,2-e_{k})^{T}

\displaystyle=H^{-1}(e_{1}-2,\dots,e_{k}-2)^{T}=\mu.

Thus

\displaystyle\left\langle\clubsuit(c_{1}(X,J)),[\omega]\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\int_{E_{i}}\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}A_{i}.

∎

Combining Lemma
4.4
with
Corollary
4.3
gives the formula which will be used in
the crepancy criterion.

Proposition 4.5
.

In every preferred coordinate system
x
obtained from the holomorphic end
induced by the resolution map
\pi:X\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
, one has

(78)

\displaystyle\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=-\frac{|\Gamma|}{\pi}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}A_{i}\right)\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}.

We now use minimality. Since
e_{i}\geq 2
, the vector

(e_{1}-2,\dots,e_{k}-2)^{T}
is nonnegative. If this vector is zero, then
\mu
vanishes. If it is nonzero, then the
strict positivity of
H^{-1}
in (
75
) gives

\mu_{i}>0
for every
i
. Since every
A_{i}
is positive, the sum

\sum_{i}\mu_{i}A_{i}
can vanish only when
\mu=0
.

This proves the third main theorem mentioned in Subsection
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

By Proposition
4.5
and the fact that

\mathcal{W}_{J_{\infty}^{(x)}}\neq 0
, the condition

\operatorname{W}_{\infty}^{(x)}(g)=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}A_{i}=0.

By the positivity argument above, this holds if and only if
\mu=0
. Finally,
by (
77
), the condition
\mu=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle K_{X}-\pi^{*}K_{Y}=0,

namely
K_{X}=\pi^{*}K_{Y}
. Since the formula
(
78
) holds in every preferred coordinate
system obtained from the holomorphic end, the vanishing condition is independent
of the chosen preferred coordinates.
∎
