 1. ABSTRACT

This CRADA between UT-Battelle, LLC (Contractor) and re:3D Inc. (Participant) aims to develop an
affordable large-scale granulator system designed to process reclaimed plastic feedstock suitable for
additive manufacturing applications. The 24-month project will focus on testing, prototyping, and
optimizing a shredder/granulator system. This project also focuses on the processing steps (e.g. washing
and sorting) that can convert waste plastics, like discarded water bottles, into flake/granules which can be
3D printed using re:3D’s Fused Granular Fabrication (FGF) 3D printers. If successful, this could enable
more localized 3D printing using recycled plastics, reducing waste and feedstock costs.

This technical collaboration project (MDF-TC-2022-238) began on March 06, 2022, and concluded on

modifying a commercial granulator to handle granulating plastic bottles containing liquid, as well as
testing methods to separate and dry the resulting plastic flakes to meet size and purity requirements for
3D printing.

 2.1. BACKGROUND

re:3D Inc. is a cutting-edge company specializing in large-scale 3D printing technology. Founded in
2013, the Texas-based company is renowned for its innovative Gigabot and GigabotX lines of industrial
3D printers, empowering users to create large objects with recycled materials for applications ranging
from art to engineering. re:3D faces significant challenges when attempting to print with recycled
feedstock, primarily due to the labor-intensive processes of sorting and cleaning the plastic before
printing. This presents a major limitation to throughput, impacting scalability for 3D printing initiatives.

The proposed solution to this challenge was the development of a granulator that can accept wet and/or
contaminated plastics, and automate the processes of granulating, washing, and separating. The proposed
solution can take in unwashed plastic waste and output plastic flakes of suitable size and purity to be fed
directly into a 3D printer extruder. Success is measured by the ability of the granulator/separator to
produce plastics flakes to the given size and purity specifications. Success is also measured by the process
efficiency, namely the time required to process a given number of water bottles.

 2.2.2. Development of Granulation and Separation System

The granulator was modified from the original Shini SG-2042 granulator provided by re:3D. ORNL
disassembled the system and analyzed the internal components to determine what modifications needed to
be made to safely run it with water running through it. The modified granulator had to be able to

 6

withstand continuous water flowing through it during operations without generating any electrical hazards
or water leaks. To do this sealing had to be performed, along with additional mechanical and electrical
modification to the granulator.

The sealing was performed by installing closed cell foam gaskets along the primary sealing joints of the
granulator. When the granulator is closed, water does not leak from any of the joints that can be opened
and closed for maintenance between operations. Most of the internal joints are formed sheet metal that is
bolted or riveted together. These joints were all coated with silicone to prevent ingress of water into the
structure of the granulator. Waterproofing and sealing of the blades and the bearing were considered, but
since the bearing would not be submerged for long periods of time and they are already sealed bearing,
for the purpose of this project they were not sealed. However, for a final system a better shaft sealing
system should be considered to prevent water from corroding the bearing.

The mechanical operations involved rebuilding the foundation of the granulator and moving the drive
motor from under the granulator where the potential water splash zone existed. To move the drive motor,
the drivetrain and the drivetrain housing had to be modified to accommodate the new drive shaft location.
The new drive motor mount also had to include the appropriate belt-tensioning mechanism. The new
motor had a new housing created for it to ensure any splashes would not hit the electric motor. This cover
also protected the modified drivetrain from exposure while maintaining sufficient air flow to cool the
electric motor during operation.

All the electrical control components were removed and incorporated into a separate IP-rated enclosure to
help mitigate any potential splashing. An additional 15-foot tether was added to allow for operation of the
machine at a safe distance away from any water. The control buttons were added to an additional tether
and handheld controller. This gives the option of mounting the control box on an external frame or wall
away from any water and enables the operator to hold the controller and move around while the machine
is operating.

A containment system was purchased to collect and contain any plastic flake and water that may spill
during the process. The containment system had to be flexible and resilient enough to enable the
granulator system to roll over the walls without damaging either the granulator system or the containment
wall. The selected containment system has foam inserts inside a waterproof membrane that when
compressed will rebound and retain its original shape. The granulator system can be rolled over the wall
into the containment without the need of a forklift or other lifting apparatus. The containment system was
highly effective in containing any spilled water or plastic flake.

The collection system went through several iterations. Initially the collection system drained into a low
pan which was periodically collected and dumped into a larger container (a 5gallon or 55gallon bucket).
This proved to be tedious and resulted in a significant amount of spilled water and plastic flake which
required time and effort to clean up after every run. Also, at the end of each run there was a significant
amount of water remaining in the granulator which led to rust forming and staining the water and plastic
flake, which can be seen in Figure 2. The next iteration of the collection system utilized a shop vacuum
that connected directly to the granulator’s outlet port. This was found to be highly effective in removing
the plastic flake and water from the granulator, but it was difficult to collect the plastic flake out of the

 7

shop vacuum. Finally, a cyclone separator was connected inline between the granulator and the shop
vacuum. This cyclone collected all of the water and plastic flake into a 5-gallon bucket and was highly
effective in rapidly clearing the water and plastic flake. The water was cleared so quickly that virtually
no rust was observed building up during the shredding process even with full water bottles.

A gravity sortation method was used to segregate the desirable PET flake from the PP and the PE labels.
A flotation tank was used to separate the materials. Water was used as the floatation media since PET is
denser than water, and PP and PE are less dense, allowing the PET to settle to the bottom of the water
tank. The PP and PE flake would then be skimmed off of the surface of the water using a strainer. The
PET collected from the bottom of the flotation tank was then placed in a clean flotation tank and agitated
again and allowed to settle several more times to further purify the PET flake. A system was developed
and demonstrated in which the flake and water mixture is pumped into a floatation tank and a skimmer
would automatically collect the PP and PE flake from the surface while a conveyor belt collected the PET
flake from the bottom of the tank. The demonstration showed the low-cost scalability of this process,
however for the remaining lab scale work the separation used simple static flotation tanks of small
containers typically five gallons or less in volume.

After the PP and PE flake was skimmed off of the surface, the PET flake was collected by pouring the
water and flake mix through a screen with a mesh size of roughly 1mm, retaining the PET flake. The
PET flake was then dried in an oven before use in a 3D printer. The yield of rPET was calculated from
Eq. 1 for wet and dry bottle conditions, where wrPET is the weight of the dry rPET flake and wBOT is the
weight of the dry bottle. For full bottle runs, the total weight of bottles was estimated by calculating the

 2.2.3. Pre-processing and Post-processing of the Recyclate

The most common size single-use water bottle sold in the United States is 500 mL (16.9 fl oz)1. The body
of the bottle is typically polyethylene terephthalate (PET), while the lid, safety ring and label may be
combinations of polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP.) An analysis by re:3D of 45 commercial
brands of 500 mL water bottles found the average total mass to be 10.6 (Figure 3) while the body portion
was 9.3g - approximately 88% PET content. In a 2010 study commissioned by Nestle Waters North

America2, the average 500 mL bottle mass (without lids) across 55 national brands was 17.7g. The
difference likely reflects an industry trend towards bottle lightweighting since the report’s publication.

Because PET is not generally miscible with PP or PE in the extrusion melt, it is desirable to separate the
PET from the other polymers prior to extrusion processing. To benchmark the labor input required for
this preprocessing step, nearly 1000 single-use water bottles of assorted sizes were collected by the staff
at re:3D’s manufacturing facility (Figure 4a). During five separate monitored sessions, the bottles were
individually preprocessed by pouring out any residual water and manually removing the lids, safety rings
and labels3. The average processing time for this separation technique was 18.1 seconds per bottle.
The prepared bottles were then ground into flake using an FX712 Beside-the-Press Granulator
(Cumberland, New Berlin, WI, USA)4 fitted with a 3/16 in (4.75 mm) screen. The bottles were crumpled
by hand and dropped into the granulator’s chute at regular intervals, with pauses as needed to allow the
granulator to ‘catch up’ when the intake became full. Though the bottle walls are thin and not a challenge
for the granulator to cut, the empty bottles are so light they tend to bounce off the rotating knives rather
than getting captured between the rotor and the stationary blades. This slowed the granulation process,
and over multiple sessions the granulator required an average of 7.7 seconds to reduce each bottle.

The resulting recyclate flake (Figure 4b) was collected and weighed after granulation. Comparing the

mass of flake collected results in an average PET yield of 77%. This is significantly less than the 88%

The target mass throughput of re:3D’s GigabotX FGF printer for rPET flake is 0.7 kg/hr (1.5 lb/hr) as
discussed in Section 3.2.4. This would require over 16 kg in feedstock to support a 24-hour print. Using
the measured rates for manual separation (18 sec/bottle) and granulation (8 sec/bottle) with an assumed
rPET flake yield of 10 grams per 500 mL single-use bottle, more than 1600 such bottles would need to be
collected and preprocessed, requiring over 11.5 hours of labor input to meet the demand5. This
significantly impacts the financial viability of using recycled PET water bottles as a 3D printing feedstock
when relying on manual processing and drives the demand for an automated solution, even for relatively
small batches of bottles.

After the rPET flake is produced, it must be dried prior to printing not just to remove any residual surface
water present on the recyclate, but also to reduce the flake’s internal moisture. PET is a very hygroscopic
polymer, and high moisture content during the extrusion process can lead to problems with fabricated
articles including poor surface quality and reduced part strength due to foaming and hydrolysis. PET has
an equilibrium moisture content of 2000-6000 PPM (0.20-0.60%)6, but published references
recommended a maximum of 100-500 PPM (or less)7 for satisfactory extrusion of PET. An analysis by

 2.2.4. Development of Requirements for, and Analysis of, Flake for 3D printing

re:3D was responsible for developing requirements for granulate size and purity for 3D printing using the
GBX. Requirements for granulate size were based on experimental results and standard practices in the
extrusion industry.
In plasticating extruders, it is typical to size material such that the largest dimension is 40-50% the depth
of the screw’s solids conveying zone. The screw on the GBX (Figure 6) has a depth of 0.15” (3.81 mm),
therefore industry guidelines would suggest that material be no larger than 1.9 mm.

 Figure 6: Extrusion screw used in Gigabot X 3D printer.

After receipt of the preliminary12 and second13 samples of granulated recycled polyethylene terephthalate
(rPET) flake from ORNL, re:3D dried the material in a forced-air drying oven (FO19240, Across
International) at 60℃ for 24 hours and sifted using 1.2 mm and 3.4 mm stainless steel sieves (Fisher
Scientific). A particle size analysis14 (Figure 7) was then performed to study the effects of sifting on bulk
morphology. While this type of particle size analysis only provides a single-point snapshot, it can be a
useful and low-cost starting point.

of the material before/after sifting and drying (Table 2). Sifting provides a 15.6% increase in bulk density,
and drying provides a 9.6% increase. It is likely that a combination of drying and sifting will result in the

cycle on the different components), and the onset of thermal degradation was determined from thermal
gravimetric analysis (TGA). The bottle, Resin Identification Code (RIC) 1, is polyethylene terephthalate
(PET), a semi-crystalline thermoplastic polyester with a peak melting temperature of 250°C and onset of
thermal decomposition (in air) at 406°C. The other components of the water bottle are the cap and the
label. The cap is typically a polyethylene (PE, RIC 2 or RIC 4) or polypropylene (PP, RIC 5), while the
label is sometimes polyethylene or coated paper. In the case of polyethylene and polypropylene, these
materials can be separated using density separation and typically exhibit higher thermal degradation
temperatures. Paper labels, however, pose a challenge for the current process due to the lower thermal
degradation temperature of these materials and higher density (i.e., they would sink in water). Paper
labels also disintegrate in water, meaning these materials would easily become incorporated into the rPET
fraction instead of being separated with the caps. Table 4 data is from a Cumberland Gap Spring water
bottle and based on the melting points of the cap and label, these components are most likely low- or

high-density polyethylene (LDPE or HDPE), or a composition of multiple materials in the case of the
label. With regards to the thermogram of the label, the thermogram is complex with a higher melting
point than expected for HDPE and an exothermic peak about the melting endotherm, which could be
caused by a lower temperature additive like a plasticizer evaporating during the experiment. The onset of
thermal decomposition is also around 240°C, which would support this hypothesis.

Figure 11 shows the melt rheology for rPET flake at three isothermal temperatures over the print
temperature range: 250, 255, and 260 °C. The 250°C isotherm coincides with the peak melting
temperature in the thermogram of rPET, and the polymer exhibits highly shear-thinning behavior over the
temperature range explored. As temperature increases, there is a significant drop in complex viscosity
from 250°C to 255°C and then a smaller drop from 255°C to 260°C. At 250°C, the change in viscosity
with shear aids in the printing of the material, since at high shear rates within the nozzle, the melted
polymer will be easier to print. but once deposited, the viscosity increases again, helping with shape
retention as the material starts to cool down.

 2.2.5. Phase 2 Introduction

During Phase 2, a low-cost system was developed that takes in full water bottles and outputs dried
granulate ready for feeding into the GigabotX printer. This phase of the project focused on the design of

 18

the overall pre- and post- processing steps. For each processing step, an approach was conceptualized,
designed, constructed, tested and iterated upon. The overall system is summarized in four steps:

 1. Granulate full water bottles
 2. Separate rPET flakes from rPP and rPE flakes
 3. Dewater rPET flakes
 4. Dry rPET flakes

The granulation system from Phase 1 was reused and tested with different types of water bottles.

Density separation was chosen to separate the rPET from rPP and rPE flakes. As discussed in Section
3.2.4, the granulated plastic flakes typically consist of PET, PE and PP, with PET being denser (1.38
g/cm3) than PE (0.80- 0.97 g/cm3) and PP (0.89-0.93 g/cm3). This density contrast enables downstream
separation of rPET “heavies” from rPE and rPP “lights.” The separation process development focused on
evaluating hydrocyclones as a more efficient alternative to gravity separation. Gravity settling requires
long settling times and repeated agitation. In contrast, a hydrocyclone separates particles based on
centrifugal rather than gravitational forces. Slurry enters the cyclone tangentially, generating a vortex:
low-density flakes (lights) exit via the overflow on top, while higher-density particles (heavies) migrate
toward the walls and discharge through the underflow at the bottom.

 2.2.8. Separation System

Initial separation tests were conducted using a 2″ hydrocyclone (Figure 17) to evaluate its ability to
separate PET flakes (heavies) from lighter PP and PE flakes (lights). During each trial, flakes from the
shredding step were dispersed into the 55-gallon intake drum. One or two submersible pump(s), each with
a flow rate of 60-70 gallons per minute, supplied the hydrocyclone through clear intake hoses, while a
blue return hose delivered lights from the overflow back into the drum. The resulting separated products,
lights and heavies, were collected on dedicated drum strainers

Figure 17: Example hydrocyclone setup in two mounting configurations: (i,left) positioned directly on top of a heavies collection
barrel, and (ii, right) suspended above the intake drum so that the overflow (lights stream) could return immediately to the slurry
 reservoir. In both configurations, heavies were collected on a strainer placed beneath the underflow outlet.

During early trials, the majority of the plastic flakes, regardless of density, were returned through the
lights/overflow outlet. Inspection of the 2″ hydrocyclone unit revealed that the underflow was fully

 23

obstructed at an internal plastic baffle plate. Flakes accumulated at this restriction, bridging across the
opening and ultimately choking the underflow. Once choked, the cyclone lost its separation function, and
all material was redirected through the overflow. This behavior persisted even after the baffle plate was
removed and the feed composition was altered by adding approximately 4 lbs. of pre-shredded PET
provided by re:3D. Increasing the relative fraction of heavies in the slurry did not improve performance,
indicating that the failure mechanism was not related to solids composition.

Further experimentation revealed that separation performance was restored when the choking at
underflow was continuously cleared manually. The manual clearing consisted of scraping off the bottom
of the plug that formed at the bottom of the cyclone. The plug would slowly move downwards as long as
the bottom of the plug continued to be scrapped off. During this process, the material coming out of the
underflow showed PET with no PP/PE contamination, while the overflow showed only PP/PE without
any PET.

The 2″ unit was replaced with a 4″ hydrocyclone, which offered a larger underflow outlet diameter and
was expected to reduce the propensity for choking at the underflow. However, the 4″ hydrocyclone
required a substantially higher inlet velocity to achieve separation. This velocity was achieved by
operating two sump pumps in parallel, connected through a wye-connector to the cyclone inlet increasing
the flow rate to 120-140 gpm. Under these conditions, the 4″ hydrocyclone produced clear separation
between PET heavies and PP/PE lights, suggesting that insufficient inlet velocity rather than cyclone
geometry had limited the original 2″ system.

The 2″ hydrocyclone was re-tested using two pumps. With the higher inlet flow provided by the parallel
pumps, the 2″ cyclone successfully separated heavies and lights (Figures 18 and 19), demonstrating that
the earlier lack of separation was caused by inadequate inlet pressure and flow.

Further, on testing using PL bottle flakes, nearly all material was discharged from the heavies outlet, with
minimal flow returning through the overflow. Closer inspection showed that the PL flakes were
significantly coarser than the GVAW flakes, likely due to the thinner, more flexible bottle walls that
resisted complete shredding. The flakes also were curled in shape, which allowed for larger pieces to pass
through the screen in the granulator. The curved shape traps air and negatively affects the separation
performance. The curved shapes were likely the result of dull blades in the granulator due to wear over
time, which would cause the granulator to crush and tear creating abnormal flake geometry.

The flakes were re-shredded using a 3 mm screen in a shredder with new blades, after which separation
performance improved substantially. Following re-shredding, the final separated product contained
approximately 6% lights by mass (Figure 20). The lower lights fraction can be attributed to substantial
process losses of PL labels during shredding and handling as label fragments adhered to surfaces in both
wet and dry conditions and were difficult to fully collect.

Several operational observations were identified during hydrocyclone testing. First, introducing flakes
before establishing cyclone flow caused both heavies and lights to discharge via the underflow. To
prevent this failure mode, the operating procedure was revised to begin with clean water circulation until
a stable vortex formed, after which the slurry was gradually introduced. This adjustment produced
consistent and repeatable separation. Secondly, pump cavitation, caused by low water levels or intake of
air bubbles was found to disrupt the internal vortex, leading to mixed-material discharge through the
underflow. Therefore, it is important to control the position of the return flow to prevent it from
generating air bubbles that may get ingested by the pumps.

 Figure 21: Skimmer system for collecting floating lights in the drum.

Additionally, it was found that the lights will often collect on the surface of the water in the drum even
when using the return water to force the light flakes to become submerged. A skimmer system (Figure 21)
can be used that collects the light flakes from the surface of the water and forces it into the intake line of
the hydrocyclone. This was simulated by placing a 5-gallon bucket into the drum, placing the pump inside
the bucket and adjusting the water level while running such that the water would flow over the outside
edge of the bucket to feed the pump. This forces the flakes floating on the surface to be ingested into the
pump and hydrocyclone. The mixed flake can be fed directly into the bucket or the second pump can be
placed outside the bucket allowing for the second pump to collect the heavies. A venture-type system can
also be used, similar to what is used in automatic swimming pool skimmers to collect the lights that are
floating.

 26

 Figure 22:22 Design of the hydrocyclone system (left), and fully assembled hydrocyclone system at re:3D in Austin, Texas
 (right).

Based on the success of the hydrocyclone testing, a hydrocyclone system (Figure 22) was designed and
built at re:3D. The design includes two pumps operating independently in parallel and multiple control
valves for fine control of the inlet flow rate, as well as the overflow and underflow conditions. The system
provides for recirculation of the overflows if needed to improve collection efficiency of the system with
check-valves for flow and contamination control. This system also allows for continuous operation by
shutting off the underflow or redirecting the overflow and removing the respective heavy and light
material from collection screens.

Preliminary testing of the system at re:3D has provided promising separation results with two primary
observations. First, the discharge into the collection screens is very forceful, causing excessive splashing
as the water and/or slurries impact the screen material. Secondly, the return line back into the “dirty”
reservoir causes excessive air bubbles in and around the pump, leading to cavitation and the disruption of
the vortices within the hydrocyclone. These concerns can be addressed through minor design revisions
which re:3D will implement in future work.

 2.2.9. Dewatering System

The dewatering step of the process removes the excess water from the plastic. The final result is a plastic
feedstock that is surface dry and flowable – that is, it is dry to the touch and does not agglomerate. Once
this moisture level is achieved, the plastic flakes can be placed in the hot air dryer for final conditioning
prior to use as a 3D printing feedstock.

 27

To achieve this, a commercial spin dryer was provided by re:3D (Figure 23). ORNL built a custom
control panel for the spin dryer that enabled the control of both the spin rate and the direction of rotation.
Testing found that 3-5 lbs. of flakes could be dewatered in approximately 30 minutes to be dry to the
touch. Drying can be accelerated by periodically stopping the dryer and manually agitating the plastic
flake inside the drum.

 Figure 23: 23 Spin dryer is shown drying the wet PET flake.

 2.2.10. Drying System

The drying system is a Dri-Air MPD 30D pellet dryer. This dryer dries the flake to the appropriate
moisture levels for extrusion processing and 3D printing. The drying studies were completed by re:3D in
Phase 1 of the CRADA, so additional studies were not performed.

This techno-economic analysis demonstrates that process throughput is the dominant driver of economic
viability for reclaimed PET processing under the cost structure considered. In both the baseline and
experimental configurations, operating expenses are dominated by fixed labor costs. As shown in Figure

production of recycled polymer feedstock for additive manufacturing applications.

 2.4. IMPACTS

This project has explored and de-risked low-cost solutions for small scale local material recovery and
sourcing from waste streams. The localization and democratization of material and resource sourcing that
this project enables is critical for low resource demand point of need advanced manufacturing. Given the
increasing global supply chain instability, it becomes increasingly critical to locally and domestically
source raw materials to ensure the resilience of domestic manufacturing. While this project has
demonstrated the technological and economic feasibility of material recovery from plastic bottles for use
additive manufacturing, it more importantly serves as a model as to how small-scale local material
recovery and production can be deployed to support the growth of small industrial manufacturing
companies.

 2.6. CONCLUSIONS

This CRADA establishes a technical pathway for converting post‑consumer PET waste into additive
manufacturing feedstock using an integrated sequence of wet granulation, hydrocyclone‑based separation,
mechanical dewatering, and controlled thermal drying. The process produces PET flakes with dimensions
and moisture levels suitable for fused granular fabrication, and system testing confirmed that automated
slurry handling and density‑based separation significantly reduce reliance on manual preprocessing. The
workflow improves throughput relative to conventional manual methods and yields consistent material
properties that support downstream extrusion requirements.
The process aligns with DOE objectives in energy optimized manufacturing, reduced materials transport,
and expanded reuse of polymer waste streams. A key outcome of this work is the demonstration that PET
waste can be processed proximal to its point of generation, reducing transportation energy and
minimizing the need for centralized sorting infrastructure. The project also generated quantifiable mass
balances, separation efficiencies, and drying parameters that support DOE’s efforts to develop
lower‑energy, distributed materials processing technologies. The ability to produce PET, PP, and PE
fractions with stable purity levels using commercially available equipment provides data relevant to
DOE’s interest in practical, lower‑energy material recovery systems.
This system configuration also provides a technical model for supporting small‑scale local manufacturing
by enabling users to generate production feedstock from locally available plastic waste. By establishing a
repeatable method for material preparation at modest scales, the approach supports manufacturing activity
in areas where access to commercial materials supply chains may be limited or variable. The availability
of locally produced feedstock can help reduce dependence on external supply networks and allow smaller
manufacturing operations to maintain more stable production workflows. As future developments extend
the process toward continuous operation and broader material categories, this approach may contribute to
DOE’s goals in supporting adaptable, regionally distributed manufacturing capabilities that utilize locally
sourced inputs.
