EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The U.S. Advanced Battery Consortium, LLC (USABC) is a partnership, formed in 1991,
between the three U.S. domestic automakers Chrysler, Ford, and General Motors, to sponsor
development of advanced high-performance batteries for electric vehicle (EV), plug-in
hybrid electric vehicle (PHEV), and hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) applications. The USABC
provides a unique opportunity for developers to leverage their resources in combination with
those of the automotive industry and the Federal government. This type of pre-competitive
cooperation minimizes duplication of effort and risk of failure and maximizes the benefits to
the public of the government funds.

The USABC defines and establishes consensus goals, conducts pre-competitive, vehicle-
related research and development (R&D) in advanced battery technologies. The R&D carried
out by the USABC is an integral part of the DOE EERE’s efforts to develop advanced
transportation technologies that will significantly improve vehicle energy efficiency, comply
with projected emissions and safety regulations, and use domestically produced fuels and
energy sources.

On October 1, 2013, the Department of Energy (DOE) awarded USABC Cooperative
Agreement DE-EE0006250 to fund the project “Research and Development on Advanced
Electrochemical Energy Storage Devices Enabling a Spectrum of Electrified Vehicles”, which
was in response to the U.S. Department of Energy’s Funding Opportunity Announcement DE-
FOA-0000722, "Electrochemical Storage Technologies Suitable for Automobile Industry
Applications”.

The USABC Advanced Battery development plan had the following three focus areas:
 1. Existing technology validation, implementation, and cost reduction
 2. Identification of the next viable technology with emphasis on the potential to meet
 USABC cost and energy density goals.
 3. Support high-risk, high-reward battery technology R&D

Agreement, and is intended to serve as a reference for the outcomes of the following 10
project technology areas conducted by the USABC under award from the DOE’s EERE

INTRODUCTION
Background
The accomplishments and technology progress made during the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE) Cooperative Agreement No.
DE-EE0006250 titled “Research and Development on Advanced Electrochemical Energy
Storage Devices Enabling a Spectrum of Electrified Vehicles” (duration: October 1, 2013
through September 30, 2024), funded $125 million in cost-shared research, is a compilation
of 42 collaborative programs organized by (a research consortium including, Ford Motor
Company and General Motors Holdings, LLC and Stellantis).

Objectives
A major goal of this program is to promote advanced battery development that can lead to
commercialization within the domestic, and as appropriate, the foreign battery industry. A
further goal of this program is to maintain a consortium that engages the battery
manufacturers with the automobile manufacturers and other key stakeholders, universities,
the National Laboratories, and manufacturers and developers that supply critical materials
and components to the battery industry.
 Figure 1 Key Stakeholders

Beyond the efficient and timely usage of resources, the auto makers recognize that
successful commercialization of these technologies will only be completed when the
supplier base has been established for the selected components and subsystems. It is,
therefore, a major business objective of USABC to enhance a supplier base as the
development progresses.

Specific to the Cooperative Agreement DE-EE0006250, addressing Research and
Development on Advanced Electrochemical Energy Storage Devices Enabling a Spectrum of
Electrified Vehicles, USABC’s goal was to 1) solicit and manage the funding and technical
direction of proposals from various developers for the research, development, build and
testing of electrochemical energy storage devices that have the potential of meeting DOE’s
light-duty electric drive vehicles battery cost and performance target of $100/kWh or less;
and, 2) continue to update and develop performance and abuse testing standards for HEV,
including 12V Start/Stop, 48V hybrid vehicle, PHEV, and EV batteries.

DE-EE0006250 Final Technical Report-Releasable Page 9 of 57

The primary aim of this project was focused on the barriers that most impede wider public
adoption of electrification in vehicles-cost, energy density, low-temp performance, calendar
life, and improvements in abuse tolerance. The specific objectives are as follows:

Cost Reduction: drive to significantly reduce battery cost to be in-line with the $100/kWh or
less (by the end of calendar year 2020) DOE cost goals. Additionally, goals of $75/KWh were
set for 2023 aligned with fast charge and some offset of energy density.

Energy Density Increase: focus on improving the cell-level specific energy and energy
densities to >350 watt hours per kilogram (Wh/kg) and >750Wh/L.

Low Temperature Performance Increase: strive toward developments that improve the
discharge power and eliminate or dramatically reduce the life limiting lithium plating
associated with regenerative braking at low temperatures, during the program.

Calendar Life Increase: drive to achieve a 15-year calendar life.

Abuse Tolerance Improvement: strive to develop improvements in Li-ion abuse tolerance
and/or development of electrochemical energy storage technologies with inherently better
response to abuse circumstances.

Emerging Areas: initiate new programs that address emerging technologies that arise during
the contract period.

Scope
Manage the funding and technical direction of projects selected from various developers of
electrochemical energy storage devices that had the potential to meet or exceed the stated
USABC and DOE objectives. The efforts focused on high-power batteries, high-energy
batteries, and ultracapacitors for automobile applications including, but not limited to,
Lithium-ion, Li-polymer, and beyond-lithium-ion technologies such as lithium sulfur, lithium
metal, lithium air, ultracaps, and evaluation of alternative electrochemical energy storage
technologies.

METHODOLOGY
USABC used its Request for Proposal Information (RFPI) competitive solicitation and project
selection process to award energy storage technology R&D projects to developers.

 1. Identify the specific segments of the electrochemical energy storage technology that
 needs focused development to meet DOE electrification energy density storage
 goals,
 2. Develop specific energy and power density goals, for the development effort,
 3. Identify technical requirements needed to meet goals at the system level.

All developers submitting a proposal were required to demonstrate they have the potential
to develop a commercially viable business, which can produce sufficient volumes to meet
automotive requirements, and provide engineering and testing support to meet automotive
implementation requirements. At the time of submission, all developers were required to
have demonstration hardware and test results-performed in accordance with the USABC
battery test procedures-available for USABC inspection.

Each proposal was evaluated with respect to the technical merit of technology chemistry (or
other key technology attributes), proposed technical approach, project cost, technology
advancement risk and, the developer’s past or expected project performance and
manufacturing capability.

All proposals, regardless of application, were evaluated against USABC technology goals.
Proposals that did not meet all the USABC goals, as defined in the attached Appendix A, but
provided a credible plan toward achieving them, were considered. However, proposals that
met all the USABC goals were given preferred ranking over those with alternative business
cases.

APPROACH
Once awarded, the technical progress of each developer’s project was continuously
monitored by the USABC Technical Leads and Work Groups-collectively referred to as the
Technical Advisory Committee (TAC)-throughout the period of performance. Quarterly
meetings were held with each developer to review two key areas:

 1. Battery technology improvements, tracking using the Gap Table for the specific
 Technology Area to measure progress against performance targets.
 2. Project’s spending assessed through a spending forecast that compared proposed
 versus actual spending.

In addition, the TAC met with the DOE every six weeks to provide summary updates on each
developer’s progress.

All developers conducted routine evaluations of both technology performance and
projected costs using standardized analytical tools and battery cost models established
through prior DOE collaborations. These include the Battery Technology Life Verification Test
Manual, Battery Test Manuals for PHEVs, EVs, and HEVs, the Energy Storage Abuse Test
Manual for HEV Applications, and the Systems Configuration Guidelines for Batteries.

RESULTS AND FINDINGS
A summary of the objectives and outcomes for each sub-award for the ten technology areas
covered by this award are provided below.

A. 12V Start Stop
Collaborated with developers that had electrochemical energy storage technologies with
the capability of meeting or approaching the USABC goals, as listed in the attached Appendix
A- USABC Goals for 12V Start Stop. The approach for this area of technology was to develop
an advanced energy storage cell first and then, if cell level data and system integration
experience warranted full system work, built and tested a full prototype system to meet or
surpass the technical requirements. When project goals approached but did not meet all of
the USABC goals, a credible plan toward achieving all the USABC goals was provided.

 1. LG Chem Power
 A 12V Start-Stop Li Polymer Battery Pack

 The objective of this Program was to develop a Li-ion battery pack for use in 12V Start-
 Stop applications using the lithium manganese oxide/lithium titanium oxide (LMO/LTO)
 chemistry that can meet the cold-cranking, cost and life targets of the USABC.

 Several generations of cells were developed and tested to achieve the above goals. While
 the first-generation cell delivered good performance and reasonable durability, its cold-
 cranking power capability was poor and the USABC target can be met only at 100% state-
 of-charge (SOC). Through tailoring principally, the cathode, separator and electrolyte
 properties, the cell characteristics were improved, and the final cell is able to deliver
 cold-cranking power at SOC = 70%, whereas to meet the USABC requirement the cold
 crank power needed to be met at SOC = 40%. In addition, the durability of the cell was
 also significantly enhanced, as well as gassing issue at high temperatures minimized.
 The program cells exhibited exceptional abuse-tolerance.

 An innovative design approach was pursued to build a simple and low-cost battery pack
 structure. Since the pack will be under hood, a thermal management strategy was
 adopted to insulate the cells from the exterior environment using a foam which was also
 used as the cell retention frame. Tests showed that this material can enable the pack to

DE-EE0006250 Final Technical Report-Releasable Page 13 of 57

 meet performance targets over the extremes of temperature specified in the
 requirements. Thermal analysis and tests demonstrated efficient cooling of the cells
 during operation and storage. A simple and low-cost battery management system was
 developed that was capable of cell balancing and state-of-charge estimation. Two
 versions of packs were developed and delivered to the USABC for testing and evaluation.
 We believe that this two-year program was able to achieve significant progress which will
 contribute to the successful development of a 12V Start-Stop battery that meets the
 cold-cranking, durability and cost targets of USABC. Additional improvement in cold
 cranking power and reductions in pack volume and cost was required to meet all 12V SS
 requirements.

 2. Maxwell Technologies, Inc.
 Development of Advanced High-Performance Batteries for 12V Start Stop Vehicle
 Applications

 This 19-month program was designed to determine the technological and economic
 feasibility of adopting a hybrid energy storage system consisting of lithium-ion batteries
 and Maxwell ultracapacitors to an automotive start-stop application as specified by
 USABC and the Department of Energy.

 The goal of the program was pursued through the execution of three parallel development
 tracks. The first track developed a proof-of-concept module to study the electrical, life
 cycle and control/communication performance of the hybrid pack. The second will be
 the development of a prismatic pouch cell configuration of the Maxwell ultracapacitor:
 needed for weight, volume and cost conformance. The third will be the detailed CAD
 design of the envisioned prototype module to validate the system weight, volume, and
 cost and determine critical thermal and vibration characteristics of the proposed 12V
 Start-Stop system. When combined, the results of each of these development tracks will
 provide a complete, verified comparison of the proposed system’s performance and cost
 to the USABC 12V Start Stop Gap Chart.

 The program contained two fundamental technical challenges: the development of an
 effective battery/capacitor management system and the development of a pouch cell
 configuration of the Maxwell ultracapacitor. Both represented difficult objectives whose
 resolutions were required for the full success of the program and pushed the related
 state-of-the-art forward.

 Ultimately, the program failed to produce a module that fully met USABC gap chart goals.
 Some initial promising results had to be re-evaluated in light of Failure Analysis
 conducted on an early risk reduction module late in the program, resulting in a final
 prototype design that missed gap chart targets in both cost, mass, and volume.

 Nevertheless, the program did provide insight into how to best hybridize different forms
 of energy storage technologies for compact, high performance, and cost-effective
 solutions, which provide both cost and weight advantages over a baseline battery only
 design.

 3. SAFT America, Inc.
 12 Volt Start/Stop Battery Development

 SAFT proposed to develop an advanced, high-performance battery module for 12V Start-
 Stop (12VSS) vehicle applications. This technology was based on SAFT’s proprietary LTO
 lithium-ion battery technology and work undertaken in the Phase I development project
 with USABC. Efforts in support of the USABC project were performed at Saft America Inc.
 located in Cockeysville (MD), Jacksonville (FL) and Bordeaux (France). The original goal
 of the Phase II project was to develop and deliver to USABC a fully functional prototype
 monoblock assembly with pouch cells including a 12 V Start-Stop battery management
 system. However, due to some concerns related to water vapor permeation through the
 polymer and questioned ability of the monoblock to meet requirements, project was
 refocused at the end of 2016 on the electrochemistry development for 46A cells and
 design, and manufacturing of the 46Ah modules. During Phase II development Saft
 delivered multiple lots of 1.5Ah pouch cells, 15Ah prismatic cells, 46Ah cells and 46Ah
 modules.

 4. XALT Energy
 12V Start/Stop Battery Development Program

 XALT Energy proposed to develop a 1Ah LMO-LTO Start/Stop (“S/S”) battery that will meet
 the requirements for power and cold crank at the beginning of life in one year. To achieve
 this goal, XALT employed high surface area cathode and anode materials. These high
 surface area active materials were to be optimized for durability at high temperatures by
 employing the Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) process to stabilize LMO by partnering with
 Forge Nano (FN). To address the high cost of anode active material, XALT worked with
 Amastan Technologies to synthesize LTO using their Unimelt process method which was
 based on plasma spray pyrolysis.

 • XALT developed a 1Ah cell design (denoted as D0.1) that is capable of extremely
 long cycle life at 30°C, 45°C and 55°C at 2C/2C and 100% DOD.
 • Cycle life durability was also tested at a very high C-rate of 5C charge and 5C
 discharge at 30°C without any major difference in cycling performance and
 without significant increase in internal temperature.
 • Cells were also tested for cycle life and calendar life as per the USABC protocols
 and showed no significant degradation in capacity or increase in impedance at
 30°C.

DE-EE0006250 Final Technical Report-Releasable Page 15 of 57

 • Calendar life test were performed at 100% SOC and 70% SOC, at both 30°C and
 45°C. No significant capacity fade or increase in impedance measured at 30°C
 was observed after about 45 weeks.
 • XALT further developed the D0.2 cell design that is capable of meeting all the cold
 cranking requirement at the beginning of life, with no observed tradeoff in cycle
 life performance. This design also showed improved impedance retention
 compared to D0.1 cell design.
 • Gap analysis for both D0.1 and D0.2 cell designs were also presented.
 • Amastan technologies successfully synthesized LTO with a specific capacity of
 160 mAh/g using their Unimelt process method.
 • XALT and Forge Nano found several ALD chemistry and post-treatment
 combinations that significantly suppressed -30°C impedance growth and Mn
 dissolution, affecting cold crank performance at EOL.

B. Electric Vehicle Applications (EV)
Collaborated with developers that possessed the electrochemical energy storage
technologies and had the capability of meeting or approaching the USABC
commercialization criteria, as listed in the attached Appendix A - USABC Goals for EV. The
approach for this area of technology was to develop an advanced energy storage cell first
and then, if cell performance and the integration system experience of the developer was
appropriate, a module or system based on state-of-the-art technologies was built to meet or
surpass the technical requirements. The goals established aimed to drive development
efforts that would result in commercialization of the cells or systems by CY 2020.

 1. LG Chem Power, Inc.
 Development of a High Energy Density Cell and Module for EV Application

 The development of high energy-density, low-cost EV battery, fulfilling USABC
 requirements, necessitated the use of very high-capacity cathode as well as the use of
 high energy density anode. A survey of the crop of cathode and anode materials that had
 the potential for meeting the performance, life and cost targets showed that high energy
 density cathodes such as Li rich NCM or high Ni-content NCM and Si-based anodes were
 the most attractive choices for this purpose. The program aimed to utilize these electrode
 materials for achieving the objectives.

 Despite comprehensive studies involving doping, surface treatment and electrolyte
 optimization, among others, did not lead to any significant improvement in the durability
 of Mn-rich cathode cells so that work with this cathode was abandoned in favor of Ni-rich
 cathode. LG Chem showed that ALD coating improves the durability of the cells.
 Significant number of studies were carried out to develop high performance Si-anodes
 by optimizing binder, conductive agents especially for high loading electrodes where

DE-EE0006250 Final Technical Report-Releasable Page 16 of 57

 adhesion issues become very pronounced. Preliminary studies with high loading of Si-
 based anode showed reasonably good cycling at room temperature but the capacity fade
 and resistance rise at elevated temperature were still quite unsatisfactory.

 However, work with Si-based anodes showed that they were unable to meet the USABC
 program targets and still be able to scale up and deliver large size EV cells (> 50 Ah) that
 are practical and have reasonable life targets.

 2. Zenlabs Energy Inc.
 Development of High Energy Lithium Batteries for Electric Vehicles

 The objective of the program was to develop high capacity NMC cathode and Silicon-
 based anode materials, screen commercial electrolytes and separators, optimize a pre-
 lithiation process and integrate to build high capacity pouch cells that meet the USABC
 electric vehicle (EV) battery goals for the 2020 calendar year (CY). Zenlabs worked with
 various materials, processing and cell companies to evaluate their technology with
 respect to performance, cost, manufacturability and compatibility. Throughout the
 program, Zenlabs developed and used large-format high-capacity (11 Ah and 50 Ah
 capacity) pouch cells to evaluate the development and progress towards meeting the
 program cell goals. Independent validation of the developed cells was also performed by
 National Laboratories at the beginning, midpoint and conclusion of the project.

 The key accomplishments at the conclusion of the project were Zenlabs developing,

 2. Physical Sciences Inc. (PSI)
 Demonstration of HA Technology for Next Generation EV Batteries

 The objective of this program with USABC was to demonstrate PSI’s HA technology in a
 commercial cell design more suitable for an EV application.

 PSI’s High Active (HA) technology is a patented coating technology, which forms an
 electronically conductive coating on the surface of the active material. The coating
 eliminates the need for diluent carbon in the electrode formulation and reduces the
 amount of binder. As a result, the high active content significantly densifies the coated
 electrode (>98.5% active material by mass). By employing this technology, specific
 energy (Wh/kg), energy density (Wh/L) and power density (W/L) are increased for any
 given chemistry. Processing materials using HA technology requires significantly less
 NMP solvent and can be reproducibly coated as thin or thick electrodes.

 Based on the results provided in this technology assessment program, there are several
 key impacts to the future development of lithium-ion technology for EV applications. The
 first impact is that costs related to electrode production, binder, NMP, high surface area
 carbons, and electrolyte can be reduced by implementing the HA technology on the
 cathode. The cost model indicated that this would result in 9% reduction in the cell cost.
 As a result, in cell designs with HA, the mass percentage of cathode relative to the overall
 mass of the cell increases. It follows that in the advanced development of current
 lithium-ion cell technology, the goal for efficiency would be to maximize the mass
 percentage of the cathode materials since all the lithium available for cycling comes
 from the cathode material. The HA technology allows for the construction of electrodes
 with 99% active material coupled with the reduction of electrolyte needed for the denser
 electrodes. The HA technology also enables power performance to be maintained at
 higher energy densities, potentially substantially impacting future cell designs and
 enabling increases in achievable range while maintaining charge performance. In
 addition, further optimization and processing innovations have the potential to
 completely eliminate the need for NMP solvents during casting of electrodes.

 3. Farasis

Over the 11 years of the award period of performance, commencing in October 2013 and
concluding in September 2024, USABC maintained a project portfolio that engaged with over
25 first-tier sub-recipients across 42 projects to achieve USABC’s goals within the ten
defined technology areas of interest. In addition to the achievements highlighted above, the
development community contributed to the advancement of the technology by submitting
over 20 subject inventions and publishing numerous articles that captured the insights and
challenges within their respective technology area of development.

research, informed analysis, and refined test methods for validation, ensuring more credible
outcomes.

Project Constraints
The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on the project’s timeline and deliverables
due to constraints in resource availability. Key challenges included limited access to human
resources as team members and developers faced remote work transitions. Additionally,
restrictions on physical operating spaces and infrastructure led to delays in collaborative
development, testing and deployment activities. These disruptions necessitated extensions
to project milestones and deliverable schedules to accommodate individual developer
circumstances.

Benefits to the US Market Place and Economic Viability
The public benefit served by this USABC program is that it continues the development of
critical advanced battery technologies that are needed to make electric, hybrid electric, and
fuel cell vehicles an attractive option to a wide segment of the vehicle market. As these
vehicles are introduced into the nation’s transportation system, their expanded use will
result in:

 1. Substantial consumer savings in petroleum fuel use.
 2. Reduction in US reliance on foreign oil.
 3. Secure the domestic supply chain for critical battery minerals and materials.
 4. Reduction in automotive air pollution emissions in critical areas that are currently
 classified as non-attainment by the Environmental Protection Agency.
 5. Help ensure the long-term health and viability of the U.S. Battery and Manufacturing
 Industry.

The DOE VTO projects EV truck sales will match light duty vehicles by 2030 due to their lower
cost of operation. USABC will leverage the OEMs MD/HD applications experience with their
engagement in the US Drive 21st Century Truck partnership. This partnership along with the
National Labs will help the development of future objectives for this segment.

Other-ion Systems

Non-lithium-ion technologies historically were presumed to be li-metal anode, with options
on cathode and electrolyte type. Consider other ion systems such as Na, K, Ca or Mg that
focus on cells that contain solid electrolyte and li-metal systems.

Policy or Operational Recommendations
Streamline the Request for Proposal Information (RFPI) Process
Under this cooperative agreement, potential sub-recipients received significant guidance in
the preparation of a proposal. In the future, USABC will consider a concept paper approach
with a fast feedback, proposal-by-proposal process, where a potential developer will be
encouraged to submit a complete proposal, or not, as well as guidance on the RFPI proposal
scope and content versus the RFPI intentions.

 a. Develop future RFPIs that will merge the needs of the OEMs and the Mobility Industry
 within the major energy storage technology topics.
 b. Manage each area of interest through the RFPI process and create a portfolio of
 promising technologies to identify those with greatest merit, along with encouraging
 the success of its developer(s).
 c. Ensure that the OEMs assessment of scientifically meritorious work will continue to
 align with the DOE throughout the programs through active participation of DOE VTO
 members in all USABC meetings.

Offering a Tiered Cost Share Structure

To encourage broader participation, particularly from developers in emerging technology
sections, USABC will consider a tiered cost share structure. This approach is designed to
lower barriers to entry and incentivize innovative developers to respond to USABC’s Request
for Proposal solicitations. The objective is to enhance the diversity and potential project
outcomes.

G. USABC Goals for Recycling
Recycling projects did not adhere to standardized Goals charts for cell or material
technology. Instead, the following Goals Templates were created to provide flexibility and
address the content for each recycling technology. Recycling RFPIs included 3-tier gap
charts that were structured to allow assessment of the recycling technology being
developed, using a mature and mainstream NMC chemistry. This was done intentionally to
mitigate risk. Unlike other RFPIs, the targets were not absolute targets pushing the envelope
beyond state-of -the art materials, but rather the targets were constructed to provide a
measurable means to evaluate efficiency and promise of the recycling technologies
themselves. Programs were crafted to include deliverable cells made with recycled
electrode materials and equivalent control cells made with reference commercial electrode
materials. The ‘targets’ were defined as the deviation between performance and attributes
of the control cells and those of the cells made with recycled materials. The 3-tier gap charts
consisted of 1) Cell Performance 2) Process Capability and 3) Active Material Characteristics
(cathode/anode). Appropriate targets for each program were further refined after proposals
were submitted, helping to define the most meaningful charts for measuring program
outcomes.

Program Objectives: Process & Cell Level Gap Analysis

Program objective is for 100% recycled content cells to be within 5% of commercial control
equivalent for performance and life at cell level; Optimized (blended) recycled content cells
to be within 2%.
