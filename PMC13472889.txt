Glutamate excitatory inputs to the sublaterodorsal tegmental nucleus are necessary for REM sleep—where are they located?
Authors: Steven Ngo, Gerard Kim, HanHee Lee, Anita Taksokhan, John Peever, Jimmy J Fraigne
Published: 2026

Our recent work aimed to identify the brain-wide glutamate inputs to the sublaterodorsal tegmental nucleus (SLD), which is at the core of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep control [1]. We found that the SLD received excitatory inputs from both cortical and brainstem structures, providing insight into potential pathways that modulate REM sleep and its characteristics. We found that glutamate neurons of the ventrolateral peri-aqueductal gray (vlPAG) that project to the SLD have a profile of activity that suggests an active role in initiating REM sleep. We thank Drs. Zhao and Rozpedek-Kaminska for their thoughtful letter regarding our paper [2]. Their comments have highlighted the strengths and limitations of our study while suggesting some future directions. We are grateful for their feedback and wish to respond to some of their points to further the conversation on the impact of our findings.

As pointed out by Drs. Zhao and Rozpedek-Kaminska, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of the fiber photometry technique. It is widely known that the fiber photometry technique has low cellular resolution and reports only population-level calcium activity. The work by Legaria et al. mentioned in the letter used endoscopic calcium imaging to record photometry signals in the striatum and found that the bulk fluorescence signal correlates weakly with somatic calcium signals [3]. However, we would like to emphasize that this particular study recorded photometry signals from the striatum. The striatum is mainly comprised of medium spiny neurons with extensive dendritic arborizations, making it highly distinct from other heterogeneous brain regions, such as the SLD and vlPAG. Legaria and colleagues mention that the dendritic architecture of the striatum may emphasize non-somatic contributions to the fiber photometry signal, offering a possible explanation. In addition, Lipton and colleagues have investigated the relationship between somato-dendritic photometry signal and axonal activity [4]. They found a robust correlation between calcium signals observed in the cell bodies and axons of spiny projecting neurons, indicating that fiber photometry effectively captures neuronal activity that propagates downstream to axons. As such, we believe that fiber photometry remains a reliable reporter of action-potential firing and that the increase in population-level calcium activity seen in our data during the transitions from non-REM (NREM) sleep into REM sleep corresponds to firing of glutamatergic vlPAG neurons.

Our data sheds new light on the central role of the vlPAG region for REM sleep control. Previous work has shown that GABA cells of the vlPAG gate REM sleep by silencing SLD neurons [5]. However, crucial work by Dr. Luppi and Dr. Sakai’s groups showed that glutamate input to the SLD is necessary to initiate REM sleep [6, 7]. In brief, REM sleep is rapidly initiated when glutamate receptor agonists are applied to the SLD, while glutamate receptor antagonism prevents REM sleep [6, 7]. These suggest that excitatory inputs are necessary, but where they come from remains unclear. We observed that glutamatergic vlPAG neurons projecting to the SLD were more active during REM sleep and displayed an activity pattern characteristic of REM sleep-promoting neurons. Of course, this observation remains correlative, as pointed out by Drs. Zhao and Rozpedek-Kaminska [2]. Fiber photometry records the bulk fluorescence signal across a target neuronal population; it is ineffective at visualizing the activity patterns of individual neurons or subpopulations of neurons that display functional heterogeneity. Future experiments utilizing optogenetics will be necessary to characterize the functional contribution of glutamatergic vlPAG neurons in the initiation and/or maintenance of REM sleep [5]. In addition, to determine if the neurotransmitter glutamate specifically mediates the effects seen, optogenetic manipulations can be coupled with pharmacological administration of glutamate receptor antagonists (e.g. kynurenic acid) or ShRNA-mediated or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockdown of vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (vGLUT2) expression to selectively eliminate glutamate neurotransmission in vlPAG neurons [8, 9]. Importantly, future studies should investigate how the interplay between different excitatory and inhibitory inputs regulates REM sleep control. The combination of genetically encoded glutamate (e.g. iGluSnFR) [10] and GABAergic (e.g. iGABASnFR) [11] multi-color biosensors could detect the different timing of release of these neurotransmitters during REM sleep transitions. Finally, cumulative inputs from different excitatory nuclei could be needed in this mechanism. We focused on glutamate neurons of the vlPAG in our study; however, we also revealed strong inputs from cortical regions (including motor cortex) and lower brainstem structures such as the vestibular nucleus. Surprisingly, we found little to no glutamate inputs coming from the hypothalamus. This is surprising as orexin neurons that send projections to the SLD express both vGLUT2 and vGLUT1 [12] and hence co-release glutamate, as mentioned in Drs. Zhao and Rozpedek-Kaminska’s letter [2, 13]. We hope that future work combining more precise mono-synaptic tracing methods, genetic silencing of vGLUT2 expression in orexin neurons, and optogenetic manipulation could clarify the important connection between the hypothalamus and the SLD [14].
