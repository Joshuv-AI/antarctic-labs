Impact of Perceived Stress on Work Engagement Among Intensive Care Unit Nurses: The Mediating Effects of Dispositional Mindfulness and Sleep Quality
Authors: Jiongjiong Lu, Yajie Song, Xue Li, Huijie Hu, Hongjuan Chang, Lina Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate the mediating effects of mindfulness and sleep quality on the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement among intensive care unit (ICU) nurses in China.DesignA cross‐sectional survey was conducted with 623 ICU nurses from tertiary hospitals in China.MethodsData were collected using validated questionnaires, including the Perceived Stress Scale, Utrecht Work Engagement Scale‐9, Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire, and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Correlation analysis and structural equation modelling were used to examine the mediating effects of mindfulness and sleep quality. Furthermore, partial correlations were conducted to control for confounders such as night shifts, and an a posteriori power analysis was performed.ResultsPerceived stress was negatively correlated with work engagement (r = −0.559, p < 0.001) and dispositional mindfulness (r = −0.686, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with poor sleep quality (r = 0.475, p < 0.001). Structural equation modelling showed that perceived stress had a direct negative effect on work engagement (beta [β] = −0.432, p < 0.001) and an indirect effect mediated by mindfulness (β = −0.083, p < 0.001) and sleep quality (β = −0.043, p < 0.001). The combined mediating effects accounted for 22.44% of the total effect. The parallel mediation model showed superior fit compared to serial mediation and alternative models (AIC = 145.2 vs. 162.8; delta‐chi‐square = 18.6, p < 0.01).ConclusionDispositional mindfulness and sleep quality were significantly associated with the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement among ICU nurses. These findings suggest that strategies aimed at improving dispositional mindfulness through training and sleep quality warrant investigation as potential means to mitigate perceived stress and enhance work engagement in this population.Patient or Public ContributionThese findings provide actionable insights for nursing managers to develop interventions promoting mental well‐being and productivity in ICU settings.

Introduction

Stress is an inevitable part of nursing, particularly for those working in high‐stakes environments such as intensive care units (ICUs) (Moss et al. 2016; Sprung et al. 2016). Nurses working in ICUs are frequently exposed to complex patient conditions, long working hours, and emotionally demanding situations, making them especially vulnerable to high levels of perceived stress (Yang et al. 2018). Prolonged stress can adversely affect their physical and psychological health, ultimately diminishing their work engagement—a critical factor influencing nursing performance, patient outcomes, and job satisfaction (Holmberg et al. 2020).

Related studies (De Simone et al. 2018) have also shown that the higher nurses' work engagement is, the higher their job satisfaction and patient satisfaction. However, few studies have explored the impact of the mediating effects of perceived stress on ICU work engagement. Clarifying the impact of mediating factors that relieve perceived stress on ICU nurses' work engagement may be an effective strategy for work engagement management. Thus, identifying the protective factors that increase work engagement among ICU nurses is essential.

The construct of sleep quality, defined as an individual's subjective satisfaction with their sleep, encompassing aspects such as sleep latency, duration, efficiency, and disturbances, is another critical factor likely impacted by stress and one that is consequential for work functioning (Kairi et al. 2025). For ICU nurses, the combination of shift work, psychological distress, and the physical demands of the job poses a major threat to sleep quality. Compromised sleep impairs cognitive function, emotional regulation, and sustained attention—capacities essential for engagement in complex and demanding work (Petrosino et al. 2024). Empirical evidence supports the notion that poor sleep quality is prevalent among nurses and is associated with lower work engagement and higher burnout. Therefore, we posit that both mindfulness and sleep quality are significant mediating variables that can alleviate the impact of perceived stress on ICU nurses' work engagement.

Previous research has reported that dispositional mindfulness is a protective factor for work engagement (Liu et al. 2020). Mindfulness is the process of observing, being alert, and bringing attention to the current self‐experience by adopting an attitude of curiosity, openness, and acceptance (Bishop 2004). Mindfulness‐based stress reduction (MBSR) is a core part of mindfulness training, and through its continuous practice, participants are able to bring mindful awareness into their daily lives, pay attention to current and present experiences, transform negative emotions and chaotic thinking, relieve pain, effectively reduce stress, increase emotional regulation ability, and strengthen the ability to concentrate and relax (Gu et al. 2015). A previous study showed that dispositional mindfulness can positively predict employees' level of work engagement (Malinowski and Lim 2015). Moreover, mindfulness‐based interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing nurses' anxiety, tension, stress, and fatigue and alleviating their negative emotions (Gauthier et al. 2015). Therefore, we postulate that dispositional mindfulness is a mediating variable that can alleviate the impact of perceived stress on ICU nurses' work engagement.

Taken together, these previous studies suggest that sleep and mindfulness (both as a trait and a skill developed through training) can act as protective factors against the negative impact of perceived stress on work engagement. However, to date, research has only examined the relationship between work engagement and perceived stress and its influencing factors, without further analysing the indirect effects and potential mechanisms. Moreover, sleep quality and dispositional mindfulness have not been considered (Hetzel‐Riggin et al. 2020; Pérez‐Fuentes et al. 2018). There is little to no evidence on the influence of perceived stress and work engagement when sleep quality and dispositional mindfulness coexist, and there is a paucity of research on why and for whom this relationship may exist. Therefore, we aimed to clarify the relationship between stress perception and work engagement and provide a research basis for future studies.

This study investigates the dispositional mindfulness level, sleep quality, perceived stress, and work engagement of Chinese ICU nurses. Structural equation modelling (SEM) is used to clarify the relationship between stress perception and work engagement and to assess the mediating effect of mindfulness level and sleep quality. More specifically, we propose that if ICU nurses have better sleep quality and a higher level of dispositional mindfulness in their work, this will relieve their work‐related pressure and increase their work engagement. While we acknowledge that mindfulness may also influence stress appraisal, the specified path from perceived stress to mindfulness is grounded in the resource‐depletion perspective of the Transactional Model of Stress and aligns with research examining how occupational stress can erode personal resources like dispositional mindfulness in healthcare settings.

Our hypothesised model positions perceived stress as an antecedent, based on the transactional model of stress, which posits that the appraisal of environmental demands as stressful can deplete an individual's psychological resources (Petley et al. 2022). In this framework, high perceived stress may erode the capacity for present moment awareness and non‐judgemental attention (i.e., deplete dispositional mindfulness) and disrupt physiological and cognitive processes necessary for restorative sleep. We therefore test a model wherein perceived stress negatively impacts work engagement both directly and indirectly by impairing dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality, two critical personal resources. Specifically, the following hypotheses are tested: Perceived stress is negatively correlated with work engagement; higher perceived stress is associated with lower work engagement among ICU nurses. Sleep quality and dispositional mindfulness have a mediating effect on the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement among ICU nurses.

Perceived stress is negatively correlated with work engagement; higher perceived stress is associated with lower work engagement among ICU nurses.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study was conducted among Chinese ICU nurses from multiple hospitals. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling between January and March 2021. The inclusion criteria were registered nurses with at least 6 months of ICU experience and a willingness to participate in the study. Nurses on long‐term leave or with severe physical or mental illnesses, as determined by self‐report during screening, were excluded.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (ethics review number: 2021013). The study was conducted with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology list. All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to survey completion, and a method ensuring anonymity was adopted during the investigation. The standardised measurement instruments used, including the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Utrecht Work Engagement Scale‐9 (UWES‐9), Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), are widely available for non‑commercial scientific research, and no formal licence permission was required for this study.

Data Collection

Data were collected between January and March 2021 using a structured, self‐administered online questionnaire distributed through a WeChat secure survey platform. Invitations were sent to ICU nurses via professional networks, hospital collaborations, and nursing associations. Participants were encouraged to complete the survey during their non‐working hours to reduce bias related to work‐related fatigue or stress.

Before accessing the questionnaire, participants provided informed consent electronically, ensuring their voluntary participation. Confidentiality and anonymity were emphasised to protect personal information and encourage honest responses. To enhance data reliability, survey completion was monitored to ensure a high response rate, and any incomplete or invalid responses were excluded from the final analysis. Invalid responses were determined based on the following criteria: a uniform response pattern (e.g., selecting the same answer for all items), an unreasonably short completion time (less than one‐third of the estimated average time), or a substantial amount of missing data (more than 10% of items left unanswered).

Socio‐Demographic and Work‐Related Information

Socio‐demographic characteristics included gender, age, working hours, number of night shifts per week, professional title, and years of clinical nursing experience.

Perceived Stress Scale

Perceived stress was measured using the PSS developed by Cohen et al. (Sprung et al. 2016) and translated into Chinese by Yang et al. (Yang and Huang 2003). The Chinese version of the scale was used in the present study. This scale measures general perceived stress over the previous month. The Chinese PSS includes two dimensions: tension (7 items) and loss of control (7 items), totalling 14 items. Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The total score ranges from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. The total scale yielded a Cronbach's alpha (α) coefficient of 0.78.

Utrecht Work Engagement Scale‐9

A shortened Chinese version of the UWES‐9 was utilised to assess work engagement (Li et al. 2013; Schaufeli 2016). The Chinese version of the scale was used in the present study (Li et al. 2013). The UWES includes three subscales that reflect the underlying dimensions of engagement: vigour (three items, e.g., ‘At my job, I feel strong and vigorous’), dedication (three items, e.g., ‘I am enthusiastic about my job’), and absorption (three items, e.g., ‘I am immersed in my work’). Each item was scored on a 7‐point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (always). A score of 0–18 indicates a low level, a score of 19–36 a medium level, and a score > 37 a high level. The UWES‐9 demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.89 (Li et al. 2013; Schaufeli 2016). The mean score of the entire scale was calculated and utilised as the work engagement score.

Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire

The FFMQ (Baer et al. 2006) is a 39‐item questionnaire that measures five facets of dispositional mindfulness that a person generally possesses across time and situations: observing, describing, acting with awareness, being non‐judgemental, and nonreacting. This study uses the Chinese version revised by Deng (Deng et al. 2011) in 2011. Each item is scored on a scale of 1–5 from ‘Not at all’ to ‘Completely true’, with 19 items being scored in reverse. A higher total score indicates a higher level of dispositional mindfulness. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the scale in the current study is 0.917.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

The PSQI is a measure of subjective sleep quality (Buysse et al. 1989). This study uses the Chinese version revised by Liu (Liu et al. 1996) in 1996. The scale contains 19 self‐evaluated items and 5 other‐evaluated items. The 19 self‐evaluated items contributing to the scoring constitute seven components: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disorders, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. This scale is a validated, standardised, and reliable tool. The global score ranges from 0 (better sleep) to 21 (worst sleep), and a value > 5 indicates the presence of poor sleep quality. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the PSQI in this study was 0.82, indicating good internal consistency.

Data Analysis

Excel and SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). were used in this study to organise and analyse the data. All the tests were two tailed, and p‐values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. An a priori power analysis was not conducted. However, with a sample size of 623 and the smallest hypothesised indirect effect (β = −0.043), a post hoc power analysis using G*Power 3.1 indicated power greater than 0.95 to detect this effect, suggesting the study was sufficiently powered.

The general demographic information of ICU nurses was analysed using descriptive statistics with frequencies and percentages. Pearson's correlation tests were used to determine the relationships between the PSS, UWES‐9, FFMQ, and PSQI scores. To account for potential confounding by work schedules, partial correlations adjusting for the number of night shifts per week were calculated, and the number of night shifts was subsequently included as a covariate in all structural equation models. The hypothesised model was tested using SEM in AMOS 23.0. This model was specified based on our study aim to investigate whether perceived stress is associated with work engagement through the depletion of personal resources (dispositional mindfulness) and the impairment of restorative processes (sleep). Although alternative directional models are theoretically plausible, this structure directly assesses the resource‐depletion pathway highly relevant to occupational stress contexts. Prior to SEM analysis, assumptions of multivariate normality, linearity, and absence of multicollinearity were assessed. The data exhibited acceptable multivariate normality (Mardia coefficient less than 5). Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for all predictor variables were below 2.5, indicating no concerning multicollinearity despite the high bivariate correlation between perceived stress and dispositional mindfulness. Model fit was assessed using the following indices: chi square (χ 2)/df, RMSEA, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), with standards for acceptable fit drawn from Hu and Bentler (1999) (McNeish 2025).

A model specifying a serial mediation path (perceived stress to dispositional mindfulness to sleep quality to work engagement) was also tested. Model comparison was performed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit. A model specifying a serial mediation path was also tested.

In line with standard SEM practice for cross‐sectional data, an alternative model was tested for comparison purposes. This alternative model specified dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality as exogenous variables predicting perceived stress and work engagement. The model fit of this alternative structure was compared with that of our original, theoretically derived model.

A total of 700 questionnaires were distributed, of which 623 were valid and 77 were invalid, making the effective recovery rate of the questionnaire 89.0%.

Correlation Analysis of Intensive Care Unit Nurses' Perceived Stress, Work Engagement, Mindfulness, and Sleep Quality

Table 2 presents the correlations between perceived stress, dispositional mindfulness, sleep quality, and work engagement among ICU nurses. Perceived stress was negatively correlated with work engagement (r = −0.559, p < 0.001) and dispositional mindfulness (r = −0.686, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with poor sleep quality (r = 0.475, p < 0.001). Dispositional mindfulness showed a strong positive correlation with work engagement (r = 0.449, p < 0.001) and a negative correlation with poor sleep quality (r = −0.353, p < 0.001). Poor sleep quality was also negatively correlated with work engagement (r = −0.341, p < 0.001). These results indicate significant relationships among the variables, suggesting that higher perceived stress is associated with lower mindfulness, poorer sleep quality, and reduced work engagement, whereas dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality positively influence work engagement.

Mediating Effect of Mindfulness and Sleep Quality Between Perceived Stress and Work Engagement

Based on correlation analysis, this study used SEM to explore the relationships between the variables. Prior to modification, the initial structural model yielded the following fit indices: chi‐square/DF = 3.821, RMSEA = 0.068, CFI = 0.941, GFI = 0.925. The initial model was modified based on modification indices, which suggested adding a covariance between the disturbance terms of the two mediators (dispositional mindfulness and poor sleep quality), reflecting their significant correlation, as shown in Table 2. This data‑driven modification was theoretically justified, given the well‑documented correlation between dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality. Following this modification, the model fit indices were as follows: χ 2/DF = 2.032, RMSEA = 0.032, CFI = 0.999, GFI = 0.997, Normed Fit Index = 0.992, and Adjusted Goodness‐of‐Fit Index = 1.000. These indices indicate a very good fit to the data (McNeish 2025). The effect size of each variable is shown in Table 4.

Analysis of the mediating model indicated that the direct effect of perceived stress on work engagement was significant (β = −0.432, p < 0.001). The specific path coefficients from the model were as follows: perceived stress to sleep quality (β = 0.468, p < 0.001), sleep quality to work engagement (β = −0.091, p < 0.001), perceived stress to dispositional mindfulness (β = −0.692, p < 0.001), and dispositional mindfulness to work engagement (β = 0.120, p < 0.001). The result for indirect effect through sleep quality was β = −0.043 (product of 0.468 and −0.091) and that for the indirect effect through mindfulness was β = −0.083 (product of −0.692 and 0.120). The total indirect effect was therefore β = −0.126. The total effect of perceived stress on work engagement was β = −0.558 (p < 0.001), which aligns closely with the bivariate correlation (r = −0.559). The combined indirect effects accounted for 22.44% of the total effect.

Following Preacher and Kelley (2011), the completely standardised indirect effect for the sleep quality pathway was 0.043 and for the mindfulness pathway was 0.083, representing small to medium effect sizes within the context of complex behavioural mediation. Although the unique variance explained by each individual pathway is modest, the identification of these specific mechanisms is valuable for designing targeted interventions.

To analyse the mediating effect of sleep quality and dispositional mindfulness between the two variables, perceived stress was adopted as the independent variable and work engagement as the outcome variable. The first indirect path showed that sleep quality has a negative effect on work engagement (β = −0.09, p < 0.001), but perceived stress has a positive effect on sleep quality (β = 0.47, p < 0.001). Therefore, the mediating effect of sleep quality between perceived stress and work engagement was established. The mediating effect value was −0.043, and the contribution rate of the mediating effect to the total effect was 7.71%.

Based on this model, the second indirect path showed that dispositional mindfulness has a positive effect on work engagement (β = 0.120, p < 0.001), whereas perceived stress has a negative effect on dispositional mindfulness (β = −0.692, p < 0.001). Perceived stress indirectly affects work engagement through dispositional mindfulness. The details of direct and indirect effects for the final model are shown in Figure 1. The number of bootstrap samples selected was 5000. The results (Table 4) showed that the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval of all mediating effects and direct effects did not contain zero, indicating that sleep quality and dispositional mindfulness had significant multiple mediating effects between perceived stress and work engagement. The results validated both H1 and H2.

A post hoc power analysis computed for the small indirect effect (β = −0.043) with our sample size (N = 623) and α = 0.05 yielded a statistical power (1‐β) greater than 0.95. This confirms that the statistical significance observed represents a practically meaningful effect and is not merely an artefact of a large sample size.

Comparison of Alternative Models

The alternative model, specifying dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality as predictors of perceived stress and work engagement, was tested for comparative purposes. This model also demonstrated acceptable fit to the data (χ 2/df = 2.158, RMSEA = 0.043, CFI = 0.998, GFI = 0.996). However, our original parallel mediation model demonstrated a more optimal fit based on information criteria (AIC = 145.2, BIC = 210.4) compared to this alternative model (AIC = 162.8, BIC = 228.0), alongside a significant chi‐square difference (delta‐chi‐square = 18.6, p < 0.01). Furthermore, we tested a serial mediation model (stress to mindfulness to sleep to engagement). While the serial paths were significant, the overall fit indices (chi‐square/df = 2.610, RMSEA = 0.051, AIC = 155.6) were inferior to the hypothesised parallel model (Table 5). Thus, the original model presented in Figure 1 was retained for further interpretation and discussion, as it was deemed to have a stronger a priori theoretical foundation based on the stress–resource framework guiding this investigation.

Discussion

This study explored the mediating roles of dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality in the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement among ICU nurses. Perceived stress negatively impacts work engagement, whereas dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality both play significant mediating roles. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence on factors influencing nurses' well‐being and productivity in high‐stress environments such as ICUs. The participating nurses in our study reported their work engagement and perceived stress to be at moderate levels. The effect of perceived stress on work engagement was partially mediated by sleep quality and mindfulness.

Perceived Stress and Work Engagement

Our findings indicate a significant negative relationship between perceived stress and work engagement. This aligns with previous studies reporting that high perceived stress diminishes nurses' engagement, energy, and dedication (Zhang et al. 2021). The high‐intensity work environment in ICUs exacerbates this issue, as nurses face constant stressors, including patient criticality, long shifts, and emotional demands. These factors contribute to disengagement, leading to reduced job performance and poorer patient outcomes. The strong correlation observed highlights the necessity of addressing stress as a priority in workplace interventions. The correlation indicates that the greater the perceived stress of ICU nurses is, the lower their work engagement. Possible reasons for this are that ICU nurses work long hours and frequent night shifts and often experience fatigue at work, which could increase their stress while limiting their energy, thereby reducing their ability to concentrate. A positive correlation was found between perceived stress and sleep quality, which supports the findings of the study by Karabulut et al. (Karabulut et al. 2021). Therefore, when ICU nurses feel increased pressure at work, they will become anxious, and their sleep quality will correspondingly decrease.

We found a negative correlation between sleep quality and work engagement among ICU nurses, indicating that the higher the sleep quality (PSQI) score is, the worse the sleep quality, and the lower the work engagement score is, the lower the work engagement level. Work engagement is a form of psychological recognition of work. It is a positive mental condition related to work, and it mainly reflects vigour, dedication, and absorption. Nurses with high work engagement are more active and proactive in the workplace, and they can provide patients with better nursing services, improve patients' satisfaction with nursing work, and reduce patient fatality rates and complications.

Mediating Roles of Dispositional Mindfulness and Sleep Quality

Dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality both emerged as significant mediators in the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement. Dispositional mindfulness was positively associated with work engagement and negatively correlated with perceived stress (Klatt et al. 2015; Smith 2014). This supports previous research that found that mindfulness enhances emotional regulation and reduces stress‐related disengagement in nursing populations (Xiao et al. 2020). Furthermore, intervention studies have demonstrated that mindfulness‐based programmes, such as MBSR, can effectively reduce perceived stress and improve well‐being among healthcare workers (Gauthier et al. 2015; Smith 2014). Therefore, our finding of a significant negative association between perceived stress and dispositional mindfulness, coupled with this existing intervention evidence, suggests that mindfulness training could be a valuable component of workplace strategies. In our model, dispositional mindfulness accounted for a notable portion of the indirect effect. Poor sleep quality was positively associated with perceived stress (r = 0.475, p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with work engagement (r = −0.341, p < 0.001). These findings are consistent with studies demonstrating that sleep impairment is linked to reduced cognitive and emotional functioning, which, in turn, is associated with diminished engagement and productivity (Milojevich and Lukowski 2016; Li et al. 2021).

In the integrated model, sleep quality also contributed significantly to the mediating pathway. The combined indirect effects through both mediators accounted for a substantial proportion of the total association of stress with engagement. While the individual standardised indirect effects were modest (β = −0.043 and −0.083), they are consistent with effect sizes typically observed in complex psychosocial models and are practically meaningful given the modifiable nature of the mediators. These associative findings suggest that interventions targeting dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality could be promising avenues for mitigating the negative association between stress and engagement in ICU nurses.

In this study, sleep quality accounted for 7.71% of the mediating effect. The cumulative effect of sleep disturbances due to shift work, long hours, and night shifts is well documented among healthcare workers. Improving sleep hygiene through workplace interventions, such as shift adjustments, relaxation training, and sleep health education, could alleviate this issue and enhance nurses' well‐being.

Considerations Regarding the Path Model

This study tested a theoretical model in which perceived stress precedes dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality. This is consistent with the view that chronic stress can deplete psychological capacities, including the ability to maintain mindful awareness. Our path model specified perceived stress as an antecedent to dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality, testing a resource‐depletion pathway highly relevant to the ICU work environment (Lindsay and Creswell 2014). This specification is consistent with the theoretical perspective that chronic work stress can deplete psychological capacities, including the ability to maintain mindful awareness. We incorporated the significant correlation between dispositional mindfulness and poor sleep quality into the model through a covariance between their disturbance terms, as supported by our data (r = −0.353, p < 0.001; Table 2). We recognise the well‐articulated bidirectional theoretical relationships, wherein dispositional mindfulness may also reduce stress perception. Our cross‐sectional analysis focused on testing one primary, practically relevant pathway to inform organisational interventions aimed at mitigating stress depleting effects. The acceptable fit of an alternative model highlights the need for future longitudinal research to disentangle these temporal relationships.

In summary, one suggestion is that nursing managers can provide nurses with MBSR training to guide them towards integrating the core values of mindfulness into their work, through which they can effectively reduce professional pressure and improve life satisfaction while bringing stability to the nursing team (Klatt et al. 2015). Another suggestion is that nursing managers can optimise the scheduling system, add night shift staff, and provide night shift nurses with a certain amount of sleep time as much as possible. It is recommended that time management methods at the individual level be formulated, personal work and rest regulated as much as possible, all behaviours that affect sleep avoided, and individual sleep quality improved.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design of this study precludes the determination of causal relationships between the variables. Second, this study used self‐report questionnaires, and participants may have provided socially desirable responses. Therefore, future research could employ multi‐method assessments, including observational measures and qualitative interviews, to complement self‐report data. Third, the use of convenience sampling from tertiary hospitals introduces potential selection bias, meaning our findings may not fully represent all ICU nurses, particularly those in lower‐tier hospitals. While our sensitivity analysis showed consistency across subgroups, future research should employ randomised multi‐centre sampling.

Regarding model specification and causality, our model assessed perceived stress as an antecedent to dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality, consistent with a theoretical pathway where occupational stress depletes personal resources and disrupts physiological recovery. We incorporated the correlation between dispositional mindfulness and poor sleep quality into the model, as supported by our data. We acknowledge that the relationships between these constructs are likely complex and bidirectional. For example, trait mindfulness may influence stress appraisal, and sleep quality may affect next‐day stress resilience. Our model tests one salient pathway within the ICU work context. Future longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to establish temporal precedence and causality more firmly.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant associative roles of dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality in the relationship between perceived stress and work engagement among ICU nurses. The associations identified in this cross‐sectional study highlight dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality as potential intervention targets. Future longitudinal and intervention research is needed to establish causal efficacy and to examine the long‐term effects of programmes designed to improve dispositional mindfulness through training and sleep quality in supporting the well‐being and work engagement of ICU nurses.

Clinical Practice

A consideration of the practical significance of our findings is warranted. Although the unique variance in work engagement explained individually by the dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality pathways is small, this is common in complex behavioural models where outcomes are influenced by a multitude of factors. The clinical relevance lies in the fact that both mindfulness and sleep quality represent modifiable targets. In the demanding context of ICU nursing, implementing organisational strategies to foster dispositional mindfulness (e.g., through tailored MBSR programmes) and to protect sleep (e.g., through optimised shift scheduling and sleep hygiene education) could yield meaningful improvements in nurses well‐being. Even a small enhancement in work engagement across the nursing workforce, by mitigating disengagement and burnout, has the potential to substantially impact patient safety and care quality.

This study shows that ICU nurses' work engagement level is moderate, which may be related to their perceived stress. Through analysis of mediating effects, dispositional mindfulness and sleep quality were found to be associated with the relationship between ICU nurses' perceived stress and work engagement. Therefore, healthcare departments and nursing managers could consider implementing evidence‐based intervention measures aimed at improving dispositional mindfulness through training and sleep quality. Such strategies, suggested by the associations reported here and supported by prior intervention research (Gauthier et al. 2015; Smith 2014), may help in fostering work engagement, thereby potentially reducing nurses' job burnout and promoting the quality of critical care services.
