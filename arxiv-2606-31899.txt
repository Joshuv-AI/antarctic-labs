Nonlinear kinetic Fokker-Planck equations: existence and diffusion limits
Émeric Bouin, Jean Dolbeault, Antoine Mellet

Abstract
. In this paper, we focus on a new type of non-linear kinetic Fokker-Planck equation where the non-linearity comes from a non-linear diffusion in the velocity variable. The existence of solutions in suitable Lebesgue spaces is proved, together with important entropy estimates on these solutions. We then study the diffusive limit of such equation.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
A nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck equation

Kinetic equations provide a fundamental framework for describing the evolution of particle systems at an intermediate scale between microscopic stochastic dynamics and macroscopic continuum models. Among them, kinetic Fokker–Planck equations are used to describe various physical phenomena in statistical mechanics, plasma physics and molecular dynamics. They typically describe the evolution of a distribution function
f(t,x,v)
in the phase space, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the position variable and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the velocity. A prototypical kinetic Fokker–Planck equation takes the form

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\nabla_{v}f+vf\right),

which corresponds to the Kolmogorov forward equation associated with a Langevin process involving friction and stochastic forcing. Such models play an important role in the mathematical description of interacting particle systems subject to noise and relaxation mechanisms.

This paper is devoted to the analysis of the non-linear Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation

(1)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\Delta_{v}f^{m}+\nabla_{v}\cdot(v\,f),\qquad t>0,\;x\in\Omega,\;v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

When
m=1
, we recover the classical equation written above, so we are interested in the case
m\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, though further restrictions on
m
shall arise later on.

One of the central problems in kinetic theory is the derivation of macroscopic equations from kinetic models through suitable asymptotic limits. These limits provide a rigorous link between microscopic particle dynamics and continuum descriptions such as diffusion or hydrodynamic equations. Among the most classical regimes is the
diffusion limit
, in which the kinetic equation is studied under a scaling where collisions or relaxation effects dominate the dynamics on fast time scales. In this regime, the distribution function rapidly relaxes toward a local equilibrium in velocity, while the macroscopic density evolves on a slower time scale according to a diffusion equation.

The mathematical analysis of diffusion limits for kinetic equations has a long history and has been extensively studied for various models, including the linear Boltzmann equation, BGK-type models, and kinetic Fokker–Planck equations. Early rigorous derivations of diffusion limits for linear transport equations can be traced back to the works of Larsen and Keller
[
13
]
and to the Hilbert expansion method developed in the context of neutron transport theory. Later developments include the systematic study of diffusive limits for kinetic equations with relaxation operators, as presented for instance in works of Bardos, Golse, and Levermore
[
2
,
1
]
and in the book of Cercignani, Illner, and Pulvirenti
[
4
]
.

In the case of the linear Boltzmann equation, diffusion limits have been analyzed using a variety of techniques including asymptotic expansions, compactness arguments, and entropy methods. Notable contributions include the works of Golse, Lions, Perthame, and Sentis
[
9
]
, who developed a general framework for deriving macroscopic limits from kinetic models, and later extensions that addressed more general collision operators and boundary conditions. These methods have also been adapted to treat nonlinear kinetic equations and systems with more complex interaction mechanisms.

For kinetic Fokker–Planck equations, the derivation of diffusion limits presents both similarities and specific challenges compared to the Boltzmann setting. The collision operator acts as a diffusion operator in velocity space and generates a relaxation toward a Maxwellian equilibrium. However, the transport operator couples spatial and velocity variables, leading to a degenerate structure in which dissipation occurs only in part of the phase space variables. This feature makes the analysis closely related to the theory of hypoelliptic and hypocoercive operators.

Nonlinear diffusion equations arise in a wide range of physical, biological, and engineering contexts where transport processes depend on the local density of the evolving quantity. A prototypical example is the nonlinear diffusion equation

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u^{k},

where
u=u(t,x)
denotes a nonnegative density and
m>0
is a parameter describing the nonlinear dependence of the diffusion flux on the density. This equation, commonly referred to as the
porous medium or fast diffusion equation
, provides a fundamental model for density-dependent diffusion phenomena.

When
k>1
, the equation is known as the
porous medium equation
(PME). In this regime the diffusion becomes degenerate as
u
approaches
0
, leading to the formation of free boundaries and finite propagation speed. This behavior contrasts sharply with classical linear diffusion, where disturbances propagate instantaneously. The porous medium equation was originally introduced in the context of gas flow through porous media and filtration processes; see the early works of Muskat and Wyckoff as well as the systematic mathematical analysis developed later. Since then, the PME has become a paradigmatic nonlinear partial differential equation describing degenerate diffusion phenomena.

When
0<k<1
, the equation is called the
fast diffusion equation
(FDE). In this regime the diffusion coefficient becomes singular as
u\to 0
, leading to very different qualitative properties. In particular, solutions may exhibit instantaneous propagation, strong smoothing effects, and, depending on the value of
m
, even finite-time extinction. The fast diffusion equation appears in various applications including plasma physics, thin film dynamics, population dynamics, and geometric flows.

The mathematical theory of nonlinear diffusion equations has been extensively developed over the past decades. Fundamental analytical results concerning existence, uniqueness, regularity, and asymptotic behavior of solutions can be found in the classical works of Aronson, Bénilan, Crandall, and Pierre. The systematic treatment of the porous medium equation was developed in particular by Vázquez and collaborators, culminating in the comprehensive monograph
[
17
]
. Another important reference is the book by DiBenedetto
[
7
]
, which provides a detailed analysis of degenerate parabolic equations and their regularity properties.

One of the most remarkable features of nonlinear diffusion equations is the presence of self-similar solutions governing the large-time behavior of solutions. In the porous medium regime, the long-time asymptotics of solutions are described by the celebrated Barenblatt–Pattle self-similar profiles. These solutions play a role analogous to the Gaussian kernel in linear diffusion and provide the fundamental attractors of the dynamics. The study of convergence toward these self-similar solutions has been carried out using a variety of techniques including entropy methods, scaling arguments, and functional inequalities.

In the fast diffusion regime, the asymptotic behavior is more subtle and strongly depends on the value of the exponent
m
. Several critical exponents appear that determine different qualitative behaviors of solutions. For instance, below certain thresholds solutions may vanish in finite time, while above them the dynamics resembles that of the porous medium equation but with faster spreading. The mathematical analysis of these phenomena has been developed in a number of influential works, including those of Bénilan, Brezis, and Crandall, and later extensions addressing extinction phenomena and asymptotic profiles.

In the context of kinetic equations, relative entropy methods have been extensively applied to prove rigorous diffusion or hydrodynamic limits. The idea is to measure the “distance” between the solution of a kinetic equation and a local equilibrium parameterized by macroscopic variables. By controlling the evolution of the relative entropy and its production rate, one can obtain uniform-in-
\varepsilon
estimates that justify the convergence to macroscopic equations such as diffusion, Euler, or Navier–Stokes equations in suitable scaling limits
[
1
,
10
]
.

Relative entropy techniques are also widely used in the analysis of nonlinear diffusion equations. For instance, in the study of the porous medium and fast diffusion equations, the relative entropy with respect to self-similar Barenblatt profiles provides a natural functional to quantify convergence rates and asymptotic behavior
[
17
,
16
]
. This approach allows one to derive functional inequalities and decay estimates that are often sharper than those obtained from purely energy-based methods.

Beyond deterministic PDEs, relative entropy has found significant applications in probability theory, interacting particle systems, and stochastic processes. It provides a natural framework to study propagation of chaos, large deviations, and the mean-field limit of many-body systems. In these contexts, the entropy functional quantifies the deviation of the empirical measure from its limiting law and allows one to control fluctuations in a probabilistically meaningful way
[
15
]
.

The present work aims to establish existence and uniqueness of solution for (
1
) and rigorously establish macroscopic limits.

1.2.
The nonlinear Fokker-Planck operator

The operator

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}[f](v)

\displaystyle:=\Delta_{v}f^{m}+\nabla_{v}(v\,f)=\text{div}_{v}\left(f\,\nabla_{v}\left(\frac{m}{m-1}f^{m-1}+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)\right)

is associated to the family of local equilibria,

v\mapsto\left(\mu-\frac{m-1}{2\,m}\,|v|^{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\,,

parametrised by a constant
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Observe that for any
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the mass of an equilibrium is finite if and only if

m>\frac{d-2}{d}.

Under this condition, we can parametrise these equilibria by their mass: we define
G[\rho]
by,

G[\rho](v)=\left(\mu[\rho]-\frac{m-1}{2\,m}\,|v|^{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}},\qquad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{G[\rho](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho.

This defines the function
\mu:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and a quick computation gives, for all
m>\frac{d-2}{d}
(and
m\neq 1
):

(2)

\mu[\rho]=\mu_{1}\,\rho^{k-1},\qquad\mu_{1}:=\left(2\left(\frac{2\,\pi\,m}{1-m}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-k}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{m-1}\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-k}},

where
m
and
k
are related by the relation

(3)

\frac{1}{k-1}=\frac{1}{m-1}+\frac{d}{2}.

The function
v\mapsto G[\rho](v)
is the classical Barenblatt-Pattle equilibrium.
In particular, it is compactly supported when
m>1
and heavy-tailed when
m<1
.

Next, we recall that the non-linear Fokker-Planck operator is associated with the entropy functional

H[f]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\frac{f(v)^{m}}{m-1}+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f(v)\right){\,\mathrm{d}}v.

This functional is convex for all values of
m>0
(with the usual convention that
\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}
is replaced by
f\ln f
when
m=1
), although it may take negative values when
m<1
and the condition that
H[G[\rho]]
is finite requires the additional restriction on
m
:

m>\frac{d}{d+2}

in order to ensure the integrability of
G[\rho]^{m}
and
|v|^{2}G[\rho]
.
In fact, we have the following classical result, relying on the Jensen inequality,

Proposition 1.1
.

For all
m>\frac{d}{d+2}

and for any
\rho\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

H[f]\geq H[G[\rho]]\,,\qquad\mbox{ for all }f(v)\geq 0\mbox{ such that }\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f(v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho

with equality if and only if
f(v)=G[\rho](v)
.

When
m>\frac{d}{d+2}
, we define:

(4)

\nu[\rho]:=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|v|^{2}\,G[\rho](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{G[\rho](v)^{m}}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\nu_{1}\rho^{k}

with
k
given by (
3
) and where
\nu_{1}
and
\mu_{1}
(defined in (
2
)) satisfy

(5)

\frac{k}{1-k}\nu_{1}=\frac{m}{1-m}\mu_{1}.

We then have

H[G[\rho]]=\frac{\nu_{1}}{k-1}\rho^{k}.

The relation between
k
and
m
, (
3
), plays an important role in the paper. We note that when
m>1
, we find
k\in(1,\frac{d+2}{d})
(in dimension
3
, it yields
k\in(1,\frac{5}{3})
).

When
m<1
, there are several critical values that play a role in the analysis of the nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation.
We already mentioned the condition
m>\frac{d-2}{d}
required for the integrability of
G[\rho]
and the more restrictive
m>\frac{d}{d+2}
to ensure finite entropy.
The condition
m\in\left(\frac{d}{d+2},1\right)
is equivalent to
k\in(0,1)
.

The intermediate regime
m\in\left(\frac{d-2}{d},\frac{d}{d+2}\right]
, for which
G[\rho]
has no variance, will not be discussed in terms of diffusion limits: a fractional regime may appear, and is out of the scope of the present paper. Note that a moment of order
a
of
G
is finite when
m>\frac{d+a-2}{d+a}
.

Returning to the full kinetic equation (
1
), we define the entropy functional

\mathcal{E}[f]=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\left(\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f\right)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

A simple (formal) computation, assuming enough regularity to justify all integrations by parts, shows that solutions of (
1
) satisfy the following:

(6)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathscr{E}(f)(t)+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\left|v+\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla_{v}f^{m-1}\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=0.

This entropy inequality plays a central role in the derivation of the diffusion limit.

1.3.
Existence of weak solutions

Our first task is to show the existence and uniqueness of a global-in-time solution to (
1
). We state the results separately for the case
m>1
and
m<1
has some of the statements differ slightly. In both cases, we address both the case of a bounded domain with periodic boundary conditions
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and the whole set
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. As part of our result, we show that these solutions satisfy the entropy inequality that will be needed to carry out the diffusion limit.

Theorem 1.2

(Well-posedness when
m>1
)
.

Fix
m>1
and let
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
For any non-negative initial condition
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

(7)

\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(|x|^{2}+|v|^{2})f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v<\infty,

there exists a unique non-negative weak solution
f(t,x,v)
to

(8)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\Delta_{v}f^{m}+\text{div}_{v}(vf)&\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\\
f(0,x,v)=f_{\text{in}}(x,v)&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.\end{cases}

For any
T>0
, we have
f\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
,
\nabla_{v}f^{\frac{q}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for all
q>m
and

f
satisfies the entropy inequality

(9)

\mathcal{E}[f(t)]+\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\left|v+\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla_{v}f^{m-1}\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\mathcal{E}[f_{\text{in}}].

Furthermore, if there is
M_{0}
such that
f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\leq\left(M_{0}-\frac{m-1}{m}\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}}
in
\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then

(10)

f(t,x,v)\leq\left(M_{0}-\frac{m-1}{m}\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In the fast diffusion case
m<1
, the heavy tail of the equilibrium state suggests that some additional difficulties arise when trying to control large velocity (and large
|x|
when
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).

Theorem 1.3

(Well-posedness when
m<1
)
.

Fix
m\in\left(\frac{d-2}{d},1\right)
if
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
m\in\left(\frac{d-1}{d},1\right)
if
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Then for any non-negative initial condition
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(|x|^{a}+|v|^{b})f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v<\infty

for
a
,
b\in(0,2]
, small enough,
there exists a unique nonnegative weak solution
f
to

(11)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\Delta_{v}f^{m}+\text{div}_{v}(vf)&\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\\
f(0,x,v)=f_{\text{in}}(x,v)&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.\end{cases}

For all
T>0
, we have
f\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
and
\nabla_{v}f^{\frac{q}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for all
q>m
.

Furthermore, if
m\in\left(\frac{d}{d+2},1\right)
when
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
m\in\left(\frac{d}{d+1},1\right)
when
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then

f
satisfies the entropy inequality

(12)

\mathcal{E}[f(t)]+\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\left|v+\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla_{v}f^{m-1}\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\mathcal{E}[f_{\text{in}}].

Finally, if there is
M_{0}
such that
f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\leq\left(M_{0}+\frac{1-m}{m}\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)^{\frac{-1}{1-m}}
in
\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then

(13)

f(t,x,v)\leq\left(M_{0}+\frac{1-m}{m}\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)^{\frac{-1}{1-m}}\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

1.4.
Diffusion limits

1.4.1.
Formal asymptotics

We now turn to diffusion limits of (
1
). We consider the rescaled equation

(14)

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\varepsilon}=\Delta_{v}f_{\varepsilon}^{m}+\nabla_{v}\cdot(v\,f_{\varepsilon}),\qquad t>0,\;x\in\Omega,\;v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

with initial condition

(15)

f_{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=f_{in,\varepsilon}(x,v).

The solutions of (
14
) given by Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
satisfy in particular

(16)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}[f_{\varepsilon}(t)]=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f_{\varepsilon}\left|v+\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}^{m-1}\right|^{2}}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq 0.

When
m=1
, it is a classical result that in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0

the solution
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
converges to a thermodynamical equilibrium function
G[\rho](v)
whose density solves a linear diffusion equation.
We will prove that when
m\neq 1
, the same limit leads to a nonlinear equation, of porous media type when
m>1
and fast diffusion type when
m<1
.

In order to formally derive these limiting equations, we define the macroscopic quantities associated to (
14
):

\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v,\qquad j_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\,f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v,

and

P_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\otimes v\,f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

These functions solve, at least formally,

(17)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon}+\text{div}_{x}j_{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}j_{\varepsilon}+\text{div}_{x}P_{\varepsilon}=-j_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(the continuity equation is obtained by integrating (
14
) with respect to
v
,
while the momentum equation follows from multiplying (
14
) by
v
and integrating).
The entropy inequality (
16
) suggests that
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
converges to
G[\rho(t,x)](v)
when
\varepsilon\to 0
and therefore

P_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\to\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\otimes v\,G[\rho(t,x)](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

The fact that
G[\rho(t,x)](v)
only depends on
|v|^{2}
implies that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v_{i}v_{j}\,G[\rho(t,x)](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=0
for all
i\neq j
and so (recalling the definition (
4
))

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\otimes v\,G[\rho(t,x)](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|v|^{2}\,G[\rho(t,x)](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathbb{I}}=\nu(\rho)\,{\mathbb{I}}.

Passing to the limit in (
17
) thus yields (formally of course)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+{\mathrm{div}}\,_{x}j=0,\qquad j=-\nabla_{x}\nu(\rho).

Hence the limiting density
\rho(t,x)
solves the nonlinear diffusion equation

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\Delta_{x}\nu(\rho),\qquad\nu(\rho)=\nu_{1}\rho^{k}.

This is a nonlinear diffusion equation, of porous media type (
k\in(1,\frac{d+2}{d})
) when
m>1
and fast diffusion type (
k\in(0,1)
) when
\frac{d}{d+2}<m<1
.

Differentiating the equation
\nu(\rho)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{[\rho](v)^{m}}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v
(see (
4
)), we derive the following relation that will be useful later on:

(18)

\nu^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{m}{m-1}\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho.

This allows us to write the limiting equation in the form

(19)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\text{div}_{x}\left(\rho\nabla\frac{m}{m-1}\mu(\rho)\right)

with
p=\frac{m}{m-1}\mu(\rho)
playing the classical role of the pressure in the porous media equation.

Establishing the convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
to an equilibrium
G[\rho(t,x)](v)
with
\rho(t,x)
solution of (
19
) is more challenging than in the linear case
m=1
.
We will develop here two approaches to such a result: The first approach relies on a classical compactness argument and the second one relies on a relative entropy method, which is not so classical in the context of diffusion limit.

The first method requires fewer assumptions on the initial data but involves stronger restrictions on
m
(when
m<1
). It does not provide any rate of convergence.
The second approach requires the existence of smooth solutions of the limiting equation (which we can only reasonably expect to hold in bounded domain when
m>1
and requires smooth initial conditions).
This approach provides some convergence result in the full range
m>\frac{d}{d+2}
and gives a convergence rate.

1.4.2.
Diffusion limit via compactness method

Theorem 1.4

(Diffusion limit: compactness method)
.

Assume
m>1
,
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
f_{\text{in}}
be a non-negative function satisfying

(20)

f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\mathcal{E}[f_{\text{in}}]<\infty

and there exists
M>0
such that

(21)

f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\leq G[M](v)\qquad\forall v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;x\in\Omega.

When
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we further assume

\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\lfloor x\rceil^{\delta}f_{\text{in}}(x,v){\,\mathrm{d}}v{\,\mathrm{d}}x<\infty\qquad\mbox{ for some }\delta\in(0,1).

Denote by
f_{\varepsilon}
the (unique) solution to (
1
) given by Theorem
1.2
and define
\rho_{\varepsilon}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v
.
Then, for all
T>0
,
\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
converges strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
to
\rho(t,x)
and
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
converges strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to the function
G[\rho(t,x)](v)
where
\rho
is the (unique) solution to

\partial_{t}\rho-\nu_{1}\Delta\rho^{k}=0\quad\mbox{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,\qquad\rho(0,\cdot)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\text{in}}(\cdot,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v,

with
k
and
\nu_{1}
defined by (
3
) and (
5
).

Note that the result holds if
f_{\text{in},\varepsilon}
depends on
\varepsilon

20

\varepsilon
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\text{in},\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\rho_{\text{in}}
when
\varepsilon\to 0

Theorem 1.5

(Diffusion limit: compactness method)
.

Assume
m\in(\frac{d}{d+2},1)
when
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
(and
m>\frac{1}{2}
when
d=2
and
m>\frac{1}{3}
when
d=1
) and
m\in(\frac{d}{d+1},1)

\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}

Let
f_{\text{in}}
be a non-negative function satisfying

(22)

f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\mathcal{E}[f_{\text{in}}]<\infty

and

(23)

f_{\text{in}}(x,v)\leq G[M](v)\qquad\forall v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;x\in\Omega.

When
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we further assume

\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}f_{\text{in}}(x,v){\,\mathrm{d}}v{\,\mathrm{d}}x<\infty.

Denote by
f_{\varepsilon}
the (unique) solution of (
1
) on
[0,T]
given by Theorem
1.2
and define
\rho_{\varepsilon}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v
.
Then for all
T>0

1.4.3.
Diffusion limit via relative entropy method.

The convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}
can also be proved using some carefully defined relative entropy.
A first attempt at such a proof would be to compare the microscopic distribution
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
to the thermodynamical equilibrium
G[\rho_{0}(t,x)](v)
(where
\rho_{0}
solves (
1.4.1

\mathcal{E}[f_{\varepsilon}|\rho_{0}](t)=\frac{1}{m-1}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{m}-G[\rho_{0}]^{m}-mG[\rho_{0}]^{m-1}(f_{\varepsilon}-G[\rho_{0}])}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

This approach, however, does not seem to work. Instead, we compare
f_{\varepsilon}
to the local equilibrium with density
\rho_{0}
and with macroscopic flux close to
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{vf_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\varepsilon j^{\varepsilon}\sim-\varepsilon\nabla_{x}\nu(\rho_{0})
.
For a given density
\rho>0
and velocity

, we thus define

(24)

G[\rho,u]:v\mapsto\left(\mu[\rho]-\frac{m-1}{2\,m}\,|v-u|^{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}}.

which satisfies

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{G[\rho,u](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho,\qquad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{vG[\rho,u](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho u.

Recalling the formulation (
19
) of the asymptotic equation, we define
the macroscopic velocity

(25)

w_{0}=-\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla_{x}\mu(\rho_{0}),

and we will compare
f_{\varepsilon}
to
G[\rho_{0},\varepsilon w_{0}]
(which has flux
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{vG[\rho_{0},\varepsilon w_{0}](v)}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho_{0}w_{0}
).

This leads us to the following relative entropy functional:

(26)

\mathcal{E}[f_{\varepsilon}|\rho_{0},w]=\frac{1}{m-1}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{m}-G[\rho_{0},\varepsilon w_{0}]^{m}-mG[\rho_{0},\varepsilon w_{0}]^{m-1}(f_{\varepsilon}-G[\rho_{0},\varepsilon w_{0}])}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

The convexity of the function

implies that
\mathcal{E}[f_{\varepsilon}|\rho_{0},w_{0}]\geq 0
and
\mathcal{E}[f_{\varepsilon}|\rho_{0},w_{0}]=0
if and only if
f(t,x,v)=G[\rho_{0}(t,x),\varepsilon w_{0}(t,x)](v)
.

2.
Existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to (

)

We will provide below (
Section

2.1
) a complete proof of the existence of weak solutions to (
14
) when
m>1
and
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.
We will then describe the modifications required to handle the case

with
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
(

) and then with
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(

2.1.
Existence of weak solutions when
m>1
and
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
(
m>1
)

The outline of the proof is as follows:

(1)

The first step consists in proving the existence of a solution for a regularised (uniformly elliptic) equation (see (
31
)) via a fixed point argument.

(2)

Next, we will show that up to the truncation and regularisation of the initial data, the solution of this regularised equation is smooth and satisfies a number of important a priori estimates.

(3)

We then pass to the limit in the regularisation of the initial data by deriving other important estimates.

(4)

Finally, we will pass to the limit in the elliptic regularisation of the equation.

Both the fixed point argument in the first step and the limits in the final step require some compactness arguments.
Such compactness will be obtained following a classical approach, first developed by
Lions in
[
14
]
, which relies on a combination of an averaging lemma and a
H^{1}_{v}
regularity of the solution.
For the final step, we cannot expect to use this method to get compactness on
f
, due to the degeneracy of the diffusion, and we will instead prove some compactness for
f^{m}
.

# Step 1:

Existence for a regularised equation
.

The first step is to regularise the equation into a non-linear but uniformly elliptic equation.
More precisely, we have the following result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuous function such that

0<\kappa\leq\Phi(s)\leq M,\qquad\mbox{ for all }s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Given a non-negative function
f_{\text{in}}
as in Theorem
1.2
, there exists a nonnegative solution
f
to,

(31)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\text{div}_{v}(\Phi(f)\nabla_{v}f+vf)\qquad&\mbox{ in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\[8.0pt]
f(0,\cdot,\cdot)=f_{\text{in}}&\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\end{cases}

such that

(32)

\|f(t)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\quad\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq e^{dT}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

and

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}|f(t,x,v)|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq C(\kappa,M,f_{\text{in}},T).

Furthermore, if
s\mapsto\Phi(s)
is smooth and
f_{\text{in}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then
f
is smooth.

Concerning the last statement (regularity of
f
), we note that when
s\mapsto\Phi(s)
is smooth and
f_{\text{in}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the regularity of
f
follows from the standard regularity theory:
since the solution is in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, De Giorgi estimates yield some
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
regularity.
We can then iterate Schauder estimates to get as much smoothness as desired. The main difficulty is thus to show the existence of
f
, and this will be done
by a fixed point argument.

Given
g\in L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we denote by
\mathsf{T}[g]:=\tilde{g}
the unique solution to

(33)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{g}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}=\text{div}_{v}(\Phi(g)\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}+v\tilde{g})&\mbox{ in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
\tilde{g}(0,\cdot,\cdot)=f_{\text{in}}&\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.\end{cases}

The existence and uniqueness of
\tilde{g}
is ensured by the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
f_{\text{in}}\geq 0
and
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then
equation (
33
) has a unique weak solution
\tilde{g}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d};H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
which satisfies (
33
) in the sense of distributions. Furthermore,
\tilde{g}
is non-negative and satisfies:

(34)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\kappa\|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C(T)\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

(with
C(T)
dependent on
T
but independent of
g
) and

(35)

\|\tilde{g}(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}},\qquad\|\tilde{g}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\exp(dt)\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

Finally, there exists
C=C(\kappa,M,T,f_{\text{in}})
(independent of
g
) such that

(36)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}\tilde{g}(t,x,v)^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\text{in}}(x,v)^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\exp({C(\kappa,M,d)T}).

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

The existence and uniqueness of
\tilde{g}
solution to (

[
5
]
.

This solution satisfies
\tilde{g}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d};H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, and
\partial_{t}\tilde{g}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d};H^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
. The bounds (
34

) are classical and the estimate (
36
) follows from the following computation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}\tilde{g}(t,x,v)^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{v}(|v|^{2}\tilde{g})\cdot(\Phi(g)\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}+v\tilde{g})\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq-\kappa\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-2\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{g}v\cdot(\Phi(g)\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}+v\tilde{g})\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\quad-2\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}\tilde{g}^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{d+2}{2}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}\tilde{g}^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

This computation can be justified rigorously by approximating

\displaystyle\leq-\kappa\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\beta_{k}(v)|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+M\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{g}|\nabla_{v}\beta_{k}(v)||\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}|\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\quad-\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\cdot\nabla_{v}\beta_{k}(v)\tilde{g}^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathrm{div}}\,(v\beta_{k}(v))\tilde{g}^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

This yields (

\left(\tilde{g}_{k}\star\psi_{\delta}\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mbox{ is relatively compact in }L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R})\mbox{ for all $\delta>0$}.

and

\|\tilde{g}_{k}\star\psi_{\delta}-\tilde{g}_{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq\delta\|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}_{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|u|\psi(u)\,du\leq C\delta.

It follows that for all
R>0
, there exists a subsequence along which

\tilde{g}_{k}\to{\bf g}\qquad\mbox{ strongly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R}).

Since
\tilde{g}_{k}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the convergence also holds in
L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R})
.

Finally, we use the third bound to say that since
|v|\tilde{g}_{k}
is bounded in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, one can also extract to ensure that
|v|\tilde{g}_{k}
converges weakly to
|v|{\bf g}
in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
. By lower semi-continuity, that
\||v|{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))}
is finite and smaller than
\sup_{k}\||v|\tilde{g}_{k}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))}:=\mathfrak{C}_{k}
. Altogether, we write,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{g}_{k}-{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{g}_{k}-{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2R^{2}}\iiint_{[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R}^{c}}|v|^{2}\left(\tilde{g}_{k}^{2}+{\bf g}^{2}\right)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{g}_{k}-{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times B_{R})}^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{C}_{k}}{R^{2}}.

We deduce that
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\tilde{g}_{k}-{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\leq\frac{CT}{R^{2}}
and since this holds for all
R>0
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\tilde{g}_{k}-{\bf g}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}=0.

∎

Looking for positive solutions vanishing at infinity, leads to

which is

\frac{m}{m-1}f_{\kappa}^{m-1}+\kappa\ln(f_{\kappa})=-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}+\mu.

The function

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\psi_{\kappa}^{-1}(s)=0,\quad\lim_{s\to+\infty}\psi_{\kappa}^{-1}(s)=+\infty.

For any

which is therefore a local equilibrium and a stationary solution to (

(recall that

), we have

Next, we note that

for

We thus have

Since (

The key estimate is the following:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(|v|^{2}+|x|^{2})f(t,x,v){\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\cdot vf{\,\mathrm{d}}v+d\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

More generally, when

and we conclude similarly as previously, recalling (

2.5.
Uniqueness of the solution

The uniqueness part of the theorem follows from the following proposition which we prove in this section:

Proposition 2.3

(Uniqueness)
.

Let
\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Given
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
T>0
, equation (
8
) has at most one solution

f\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
such that
\nabla_{v}f^{m}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
be two solutions and set
g_{i}=f_{i}^{m}
.
The difference
h=f_{1}-f_{2}
solves

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h-\text{div}_{v}(vh)=\Delta_{v}(g_{1}-g_{2})&\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\\[5.0pt]
h(0,x,v)=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

in the following sense:

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{h\left(-\partial_{t}\psi-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+v\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\right)+\nabla_{v}(g_{1}-g_{2})\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi}\,dt=0,

for test functions
\psi\in\mathcal{D}([0,T))
(thus satisfying
\psi(T,x,v)=0
).

Define
\psi
, solution to the backward transport equation

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}\psi-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+v\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi=e^{-\lambda t}(g_{1}-g_{2})_{k}&\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
\psi(T,x,v)=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\end{cases}

where
(g_{1}-g_{2})_{k}
is a regularisation of
g_{1}-g_{2}
in all variables. The function
\psi
is thus a acceptable test function. We get,

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{-\lambda t}h(g_{1}-g_{2})_{k}-e^{\lambda t}\nabla_{v}\left[\partial_{t}\psi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi-v\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\right]\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi}\,dt=0.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{\lambda t}\nabla_{v}\left[\partial_{t}\psi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi-v\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\right]\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi}\,dt

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{\lambda T}\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}(T,x,v)}{2}-\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}(0,x,v)}{2}}-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\lambda e^{\lambda t}\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(d-2\right)\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{\lambda t}\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}(0,x,v)}{2}}-\left(\lambda+2-d\right)\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{\lambda t}\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}}{2}}\,dt

We now take
\lambda=d-2
, and we deduce

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{-\lambda t}h(g_{1}-g_{2})_{k}}\,dt+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\frac{|\nabla_{v}\psi|^{2}(0,x,v)}{2}}=0,

and so

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{-\lambda t}h(g_{1}-g_{2})_{k}}\,dt\leq 0.

We can now pass to the limit
k\to\infty
so get

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{-\lambda t}h(g_{1}-g_{2})}\,dt\leq 0.

Since
h(g_{1}-g_{2})=(f_{1}-f_{2})(f_{1}^{m}-f_{2}^{m})\geq 0
a.e., we deduce that
f_{1}=f_{2}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\int_{|v|^{2}\geq R^{2}+\eta^{2}}(|v|^{2}-R^{2})f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{R^{2}\leq|v|^{2}\leq R^{2}+\eta^{2}}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\int_{|v|^{2}\geq R^{2}}(|v|^{2}-R^{2})f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v+\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}\left(\omega_{d}((R^{2}+\eta^{2})^{d/2}-R^{d})\right)^{\frac{m-1}{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{K_{R}}{\eta^{2}}+K_{R}^{\frac{1}{m}}R^{\frac{d(m-1)}{m}}\left(\left(1+\left(\frac{\eta}{R}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\right)^{\frac{m-1}{m}}.

We write the right-hand-side under the form
R^{-2}\Phi\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)
with

\Phi(x)=\frac{K_{R}}{R^{2}x}+K_{R}^{\frac{1}{m}}R^{\frac{d(m-1)}{m}}\left((1+x)^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\right)^{\frac{m-1}{m}},

and we search for a minimum value of
\Phi
.
We note that

\Phi^{\prime}(1)=-R^{-2}K_{R}+\frac{m-1}{m}\left(2^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\right)^{\frac{-1}{m}}\frac{d}{2}2^{\frac{d}{2}-1}K_{R}^{\frac{1}{m}}R^{\frac{d(m-1)}{m}}

and so a transition occurs when

K_{R}=C(d,m)R^{\frac{2m+d(m-1)}{m-1}},

with
C(d,m)=\left[\frac{d}{2}\,\frac{m-1}{m}\,2^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(2^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\right)^{-\frac{1}{m}}\right]^{\frac{m}{m-1}}.

•

When
K_{R}<C(d,m)R^{\frac{2m+d(m-1)}{m-1}}
, we have
\Phi^{\prime}(1)>0
, so the minimum of
\Phi
is reached for some
x\in(0,1)
. In that region, we have
(1+x)^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\lesssim x
and so

\Phi(x)\lesssim\frac{R^{-2}K_{R}}{x}+K_{R}^{\frac{1}{m}}R^{\frac{d(m-1)}{m}}x^{\frac{m-1}{m}},

and a standard minimization argument yields:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in(0,1)}\Phi(x)\lesssim K_{R}^{\frac{m}{2m-1}}R^{\frac{(d-2)(m-1)}{2m-1}}\lesssim K_{R}^{\frac{m}{2m-1}}\rho^{\frac{(d-2)(m-1)(k-1)}{2(2m-1)}}\lesssim K_{R}^{\frac{m}{2m-1}}\rho^{\frac{m(2-k)-1}{2m-1}},

where we used the fact that
R^{2}=\frac{2m}{m-1}\mu_{1}\rho^{k-1}
and the equality

(d-2)(m-1)(k-1)=2(m(2-k)-1).

•

When
K_{R}>C(d,m)R^{\frac{2m+d(m-1)}{m-1}}
, we have
\Phi^{\prime}(1)<0
, so the minimum of
\Phi
is reached in the region for some
x>1
. In that region, we have

(1+x)^{\frac{d}{2}}-1\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}x^{\frac{d}{2}}
and so

\Phi(x)=\frac{K_{R}}{xR^{2}}+2^{\frac{d}{2}}K_{R}^{\frac{1}{m}}R^{\frac{d(m-1)}{m}}x^{\frac{d(m-1)}{2m}},

and a standard minimization argument yields:

\min_{x>1}\Phi(x)\lesssim K_{R}^{\frac{d(m-1)+2}{d(m-1)+2m}}=K_{R}^{\frac{1}{k}}.

We deduce

\displaystyle\int_{|v|\geq R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{K_{R}^{\frac{m}{2m-1}}\rho^{\frac{m(2-k)-1}{2m-1}},K_{R}^{\frac{1}{k}}\right\},

and so

\displaystyle\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{k}\lesssim\max\left\{K_{R}^{\frac{mk}{2m-1}}\rho^{k\frac{m(2-k)-1}{2m-1}},K_{R}\right\}.

The result follows from (
87
) with
q=\frac{2m-1}{mk}
and
1-\frac{1}{q}=\frac{m(2-k)-1}{2m-1}
.
∎

Remark A.6
.

We did not extract all the information we could have from (
85
):
Using the convexity of the function
s\mapsto\frac{s^{m}}{m-1}
, (
86
) also gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[f|G[\rho]]

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}-\frac{G[\rho]^{m}}{m-1}-\frac{m}{m-1}G[\rho]^{m-1}(f-G[\rho]){\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}+G[\rho]^{m}-\frac{m}{m-1}G[\rho]^{m-1}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

so that (
85
) implies

\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}+G[\rho]^{m}-\frac{m}{m-1}G[\rho]^{m-1}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]

Using Young’s inequality
\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{m}{m-1}G[\rho]^{m-1}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq 2^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\int_{|v|\leq R}G[\rho]^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{1}{2}\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}v
, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+(2^{\frac{1}{m-1}}-1)\int_{|v|\leq R}G[\rho]^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v

(88)

\displaystyle\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+\nu_{1}(2^{\frac{1}{m-1}}-1)\rho^{k}

.

Finally, we can state the main inequality that we use when
m>1
:

Proposition A.7
.

Let
m>1
and assume that
f
and
G[\rho]
have the same mass
\rho
. There exists a constant
C_{d,m}
such that

(89)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq C_{d,m}\,\rho^{\frac{2-k}{2}}\left(\left(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]\right)+\rho^{k-1}M(R)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

where (see Lemma
A.5
)

M(R):=\int_{|v|\geq R}f(v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\max\left\{(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])^{\frac{1}{kq}}\rho^{\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)},(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])^{\frac{1}{k}}\right\}.

In particular, for all
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta,m,d}
such that

\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{k}\leq\delta\rho^{k}+C_{\delta,m,d}(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]).

Proof of
Proposition

A.7
.

We recall that, under the condition
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}G[\rho](v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho
,
f(v)
and
G[\rho](v)
are such that

R^{2}=\frac{2m}{m-1}\mu(\rho)=\mu_{1}\frac{2m}{m-1}\rho^{k-1}
.
We consider two cases.

Case 1: When
\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\frac{1}{2}\rho
.

In that case, Lemma
A.5
implies

\displaystyle\rho^{k}\leq 2^{k}\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\left(C(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])^{\frac{1}{q}}\rho^{k\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}+C(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{k}

(using Young’s inequality).
We deduce

\rho^{k}\leq C(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])

hence

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq 2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v)+G[\rho](v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=2\rho\leq C(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])^{\frac{1}{k}}.

Writing
\rho=\left(\rho^{2-k}\rho^{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we can also write

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq C\left(\rho^{2-k}(H[f]-H[G[\rho]])\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

thus showing that (
89
) holds in that case.

Case 2: When
\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\frac{1}{2}\rho
.

In that case, we define

\widetilde{f}(v)=\begin{cases}\alpha f(v)&\mbox{ if }|v|\leq R\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

with
\alpha
chosen such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widetilde{f}(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho,

that is

\rho=\alpha(\rho-h),\qquad\alpha-1=\frac{h}{g-h}

The condition
\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho
implies

\alpha=\frac{\rho}{\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v}\in[1,2]

and

0\leq\alpha-1\leq\frac{2}{\rho}\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

We now compute:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-\widetilde{f}(v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v=(\alpha-1)\int_{|v|\leq R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{|v|>R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v=2\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

Furthermore, since
\widetilde{f}(v)
and
G[\rho]
have the same mass
\rho
and same support
B_{R}
, we can use Proposition
A.4
to write

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\widetilde{f}(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{B_{R}}|\widetilde{f}(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq C_{d,m}\,\rho^{\frac{2-k}{2}}\,(H[\widetilde{f}]-H[G[\rho]])^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Finally, using the fact that
\alpha\in[0,2]
, we can write:

\displaystyle H[\widetilde{f}]-H[G[\rho]]

\displaystyle=H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+H[\widetilde{f}]-H[f]

\displaystyle\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+\int_{|v|\leq R}(\alpha^{m}-1)\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}+(\alpha-1)\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+C(\alpha-1)\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{R^{2}}{2}(\alpha-1)\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+\left(C\frac{1}{\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v}\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right)\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v

where we used the fact that
(\alpha-1)\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v
.

Using Remark
A.6
(see (
88
)) and fact that
R^{2}=\frac{2m}{m-1}\mu(\rho)=\mu_{1}\frac{2m}{m-1}\rho^{k-1}
, we can write:

\displaystyle\left(C\frac{1}{\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v}\int_{|v|\leq R}\frac{f^{m}}{m-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v}(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+C\rho^{k})+C\rho^{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\rho}(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]+C\rho^{k-1}

where we used (again) the fact that
\int_{|v|\leq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\geq\frac{1}{2}\rho
.
Putting things together, we get:

\displaystyle H[\widetilde{f}]-H[G[\rho]]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]\right)+C\rho^{k-1}\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

We deduce:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-\widetilde{f}(v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\widetilde{f}(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v+C_{d,m}\,\rho^{\frac{2-k}{2}}\,\left(\left(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]\right)+\rho^{k-1}\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v+C_{d,m}\,\left(\rho^{2-k}\left(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]\right)+\rho\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Finally, since
\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\rho
, we have
\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\left(\rho\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
so we can simply write:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(v)-G[\rho](v)|{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq C_{d,m}\,\left(\rho^{2-k}\left(H[f]-H[G[\rho]]\right)+\rho\int_{|v|\geq R}f{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

which gives (
89
) in this second case.
∎

Appendix B
A generalised Carlson-Levin inequality

First we recall the Carlson-Levin inequality:

Proposition B.1
.

Given
d\geq 2
and for
m\in(\frac{d}{d+2},1)

there holds:

(90)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v)^{m}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\lesssim\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{2m-d(1-m)}{2}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f(v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{d(1-m)}{2}}.

Next, we prove the following generalization:

Proposition B.2
.

Given
d\geq 2
and
a\in(0,2]
, and for
m
satisfying

(91)

\frac{d+a-2}{d+a}<m<\frac{d+a-2}{d},

there holds:

(92)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\lesssim\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{a(m-1)+2-d(1-m)}{a}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{a}f(v)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{a-2+d(1-m)}{a}}.

Note that for every
m\in(\frac{d-2}{2},1)
, there exists
a\in(0,2)
such that (
91
) holds (since
\frac{d+a-2}{d+a}\to\frac{d-2}{2}
as
a\to 0
and
\frac{d+a-2}{d}\to 1
as
a\to 2
).

Proof of
Proposition

B.2
.

We write

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\int_{|v|\leq R}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\int_{|v|\geq R}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v

Using Hölder’s inequality, we write,

\int_{|v|\leq R}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\left(\int_{|v|\leq R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}\left(\int_{|v|\leq R}|v|^{\frac{a-2}{1-m}}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{1-m}\\
\leq\left(\int_{|v|\leq R}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}R^{a-2+d(1-m)}.

This inequality requires
\frac{a-2}{1-m}>-d
which is equivalent to the right inequality in (
91
).
Similarly, we have

\int_{|v|\geq R}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}|v|^{a}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}|v|^{a-\frac{2}{1-m}}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{1-m}\\
\leq\left(\int_{|v|\geq R}|v|^{a}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}R^{a(1-m)-2+d(1-m)}.

This inequality requires
a-\frac{2}{1-m}<-d
, which is equivalent to the left inequality in (
91
).
Putting things together, we deduce:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{a-2}f(v)^{m}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}R^{a-2+d(1-m)}+\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{a}f(v){\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{m}R^{a(1-m)-2+d(1-m)}.

We note that
a-2+d(1-m)>0
and
a(1-m)-2+d(1-m)<0
so we can optimize with respect to
R
to get (
92
).
∎

Appendix C
Relative entropy method when
m=1
: Proof of
Theorem

C.1

In this appendix, we present the relative entropy method for the linear Fokker-Planck equation. Consider the following (linear) rescaled equation,

(93)

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\varepsilon}=\text{div}_{v}\left[\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+vf_{\varepsilon}\right],\qquad t>0,\;x\in\Omega,\;v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

complemented with an initial condition
f_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
. This equation is associated to the entropy,

\forall t>0,\qquad\mathscr{E}(f_{\varepsilon})(t)=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

In particular,
solutions of (
93
) satisfy

\frac{d}{dt}\mathscr{E}(f_{\varepsilon})(t)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+vf_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

Classically, the minimizers of
\mathscr{E}(f)
under mass constraint
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{f}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=\rho
are given by Maxwellian distributions, that is

\rho\,M(v):=\frac{\rho}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right).

In particular,
f_{\varepsilon}
is expected to converge to
\rho_{0}\,M
where
(t,x)\mapsto\rho_{0}(t,x)
solves

\partial_{t}\rho_{0}-\Delta\rho_{0}=0
, or equivalently,

(94)

\partial_{t}\rho_{0}+\text{div}_{x}(\rho_{0}w)=0,\qquad w=-\nabla\ln\rho_{0}.

Given
f_{\varepsilon}
a solution to (
93
) and
\rho_{0}
a solution to (
94
), we define
the modulated energy (or relative entropy):

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\log\left(\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)}{\rho_{0}(t,x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}(t,x)|^{2}}}\right)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{f_{\varepsilon}}{\rho_{0}}\right)+\frac{|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}}{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

Observe that this quantity is not the relative entropy with respect to the expected limit
\rho_{0}\,M
, but takes into account a first order correction of the flux.

Theorem C.1
.

Let
f_{\varepsilon}
be a weak solution to (
93
) and
\rho_{0}
be a solution to (
94
). Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\varepsilon}|u_{\varepsilon}-w|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}C\Big(\|\partial_{t}w\|_{L^{\infty}},\|Dw\|_{L^{\infty}},\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\Big)

Define
g_{\varepsilon}=\frac{f_{\varepsilon}}{\rho_{0}}
. The function
g_{\varepsilon}
satisfies,

(95)

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\rho_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\right)g_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}^{-1}=\text{div}_{v}\left[\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+vg_{\varepsilon}\right],\qquad t>0,\;x\in\Omega,\;v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The relative entropy may be rewritten,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left[g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\right]\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

Compute,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(v-\varepsilon w_{0})(-\varepsilon\partial_{t}w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left[g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\right]\,\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

We shall estimate the three integrals separately.

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}I_{1}:=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(\text{div}_{v}\left[\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+vg_{\varepsilon}\right]\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(-\varepsilon v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}^{-1}\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(-\varepsilon^{2}\left(\rho_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\right)g_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=I_{1,1}+I_{1,2}+I_{1,3}.

Again, we estimate all integrals separately.

\displaystyle I_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(\text{div}_{v}\left[\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+vg_{\varepsilon}\right]\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\right)\cdot\left(\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+vg_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varepsilon\left(\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot w\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varepsilon(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\cdot w_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

Then,

\displaystyle I_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(-\varepsilon v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}^{-1}\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla_{x}g_{\varepsilon}}{g_{\varepsilon}}-\varepsilon(D_{x}w_{0})(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\right)\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\cdot w_{0}\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\rho_{0}g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

Finally,

\displaystyle I_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(-\varepsilon^{2}\left(\rho_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\right)g_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(1+\log g_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|v-\varepsilon w_{0}|^{2}\right)\left(\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\right)g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

Observe that
I_{1,3}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{3}=-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

As a consequence, using a cancellation between
I_{1,1}
and
I_{1,2}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}I_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{3}=I_{1,1}+I_{1,2}+I_{1,3}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{3}

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})((v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varepsilon^{2}|w_{0}|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(v-\varepsilon w_{0})(-\varepsilon\partial_{t}w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

From this, observe that, since

\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\text{div}_{x}w\,g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v,

we have

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})(\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|w|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(-\text{div}_{x}w\,\rho_{0}+|w_{0}|^{2}\,\rho_{0}-\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\right)\,g_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v=0,

recalling the identity
-\text{div}_{x}w\,\rho_{0}+|w_{0}|^{2}\,\rho_{0}=\partial_{t}\rho_{0}
.
The last step is to bound,

\displaystyle\left|\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v^{\top}(D_{x}w_{0})((v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon})\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\varepsilon^{2}\|D_{x}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

and,

\displaystyle\left|\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(v-\varepsilon w_{0})\partial_{t}w_{0}\,g_{\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right|=\left|\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left((v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\partial_{t}w_{0}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{3}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\varepsilon^{3}\|\partial_{t}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g_{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

to conclude that,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{g_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}g_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})g_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{4}\|D_{x}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\varepsilon^{6}\|\partial_{t}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g_{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-\varepsilon w_{0})f_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}\|D_{x}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v+\varepsilon^{4}\|\partial_{t}w_{0}\|_{\infty}\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x\,{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

This ends the proof.
