 ABSTRACT

Reliable, scalable methods for producing high-purity actinide chloride salts are needed to support molten
salt reactor fuel development and deployment. This report describes the continued development and
demonstration of a bench-scale chlorination and purification apparatus using a zinc chloride-based method
for synthesizing uranium chloride fuel salts. In this approach, uranium metal is chlorinated by ZnCl 2 to
produce LiCl-KCl-UCl3. Reaction with three aliquots of added uranium metal was used to generate a target
uranium concentration of 30 wt %. While this concentration was chosen for initial testing of the apparatus
and method, the final uranium concentration is not limited to 30 wt %. The zinc metal generated in the
reaction forms an immiscible layer that was removed by volatilization at moderately high temperatures.
Electrochemical measurements confirmed the removal of zinc and applied sensing methods indicated the
uranium concentration to be approximately 25 wt %. These initial results demonstrate that the bench-scale
chlorination apparatus is an effective platform for the synthesis and purification of uranium chloride salts
using ZnCl2. This method shows promise for application to industry-relevant salt systems such as NaCl-
UCl3. Further development is recommended to optimize reagent loading, zinc removal, and avoid possible
U-Zn alloy formation.

1. Introduction
Molten salt reactor (MSR) designs have continued to mature and are moving rapidly toward industrial-scale
demonstration and deployment as part of next-generation nuclear energy systems. To support future
deployment, reliable methods for synthesizing high-purity actinide chloride salts will be needed to meet the
large-scale (>100 kilograms) production demands for MSR operation. In addition to challenges related to
scale and purity, supply chain development is complicated by the diversity of reactor designs which can
involve different actinide halides and carrier salts. Therefore, the preferred synthesis method can be context-
dependent and must align with the salt composition, quantity, and purity requirements defined by the MSR
vendor. Meeting the composition and purity requirement for the MSR salt is essential for long-term reactor
operation and for minimizing performance degradation.
Synthetic methods for generating actinide chlorides can be divided into classes based on the chlorination
agent, including gaseous, metal chloride, and ammonium chloride routes. The appropriate synthesis route
for a given process may consider maturity level and demonstrated scale, suitable actinide precursor
composition, and available infrastructure. Metal chloride reagents are attractive for the synthesis of
uranium-containing salts because they have demonstrated selectivity of UCl3 over higher uranium valence
states in comparison to other chlorinating agents. However, implementation of processes using metal
chloride reagents must include purification and removal of the reagent metal or by-products after synthesis.
Historically, cadmium chloride (Miller & Tomczuk, 2004; Williamson & Willit, 2021) and lead chloride
(Inman 1959) have been used to effectively chlorinate uranium at large scales (>250 kg). During the
exchange reaction with uranium metal, a metallic layer of cadmium or lead is generated that is immiscible
with the molten chloride salt. This immiscibility of the layer can be advantageous in the separation of the
metal from the synthesized salt, since the metal is segregated in a separate phase and can be physically
separated from the frozen salt. However, cadmium and lead are RCRA metals and concerns regarding their
toxicity and disposal cost have led to the exploration of methods using alternative metal chloride reagents.
The reaction of zinc chloride with uranium metal follows the same mechanism as reaction with lead and
cadmium chlorides, and an immiscible zinc metal layer is formed. Synthesis of uranium chlorides using
zinc chloride has been previously demonstrated at Argonne National Laboratory to support thermophysical
property measurements of NaCl-UCl3 and NaCl-KCl-UCl3 salts (Rose et al., 2023), as well as for the
development of process monitoring during MSR operation at high actinide concentrations in
LiCl‑KCl‑UCl3 (Doniger et al., 2025). Removal of the zinc metal during those syntheses was achieved by
vaporization at temperatures ranging from 700 – 800 °C. Further efforts are needed to translate and optimize
this synthesis method to industry-relevant salt systems such as NaCl-UCl3, to minimize residual zinc metal
contamination in the salt, and to maximize chlorination efficiency in support of scale-up processes.
This report describes the continued development of actinide chloride synthesis technology using zinc
chloride as a metal reagent. A bench-scale synthesis assembly was designed, constructed, and installed in a
glovebox to support optimization of this uranium chloride synthesis method. Initial syntheses of uranium
chloride in LiCl-KCl at a high concentration were performed using a stepwise synthesis protocol where
10 wt% U was synthesized in each step. LiCl-KCl was chosen for use in the initial tests because the melting
temperature is much lower than that of NaCl. Future synthesis operations will be performed in NaCl at a
higher temperature. Electrochemical measurements were used to confirm completion the effective removal
of the zinc metal, and the measured concentration of the synthesized LiCl-KCl-UCl3 was 25 wt % uranium.
The stepwise synthesis protocol involving three sequential additions of ZnCl2 demonstrated the flexibility
of the assembly, which can be used to further optimize synthesis parameters (e.g. reagent composition, zinc
removal methods) in the application of relevant MSR fuels such as NaCl-UCl3.

2. Bench-Scale Chlorination Test Apparatus
2.1 Chlorination Apparatus Design
The chlorination assembly for reaction of uranium metal with zinc chloride consisted of two separate
vessels and was installed in a glovebox. The assembly was designed with two stages: a synthesis vessel
containing the molten salt and a scrubber vessel for capturing volatilized metal during purification. The
synthesis vessel was constructed using a stainless-steel half-nipple with a welded flange plate (procured
from Kurt-Lesker) and custom-made cover designed to maintain an inert environment for the controlled
addition of uranium metal precursor into the salt and effective capture of generated zinc metal. (Figure 1).
The synthesis vessel and cover have a versatile design that enables testing of different synthesis and
purification procedures (e.g., introduction of an impurity scavenger or multiple precursor baskets for
sequential additions). The vessel cap is equipped with five adjustable gas-tight ports for introduction of a
carrier gas, baskets containing precursor materials, salt sampling equipment, and monitoring equipment
such as thermocouples and electrodes. The five ports are staggered at different heights to allow for ease in
accessibility. The vessel is contained in a furnace capable of operating at temperatures up to 1100 °C, which
is necessary for synthesis of industry-relevant salts (i.e., NaCl-UCl3).

Figure 1: SolidWorks rendering of the chlorination synthesis vessel cap. The cap is equipped with ports
for inserting electrochemical sensors and flowing argon carrier gas to remove zinc metal fumes from the
synthesis vessel.

The scrubber vessel was designed using the same stainless steel capped pipe with flange and cover with
ports to allow gas sparging. The scrubber was packed with copper mesh, which reacts with zinc metal to
form brass. The scrubber was placed in a furnace capable of operating at temperatures up to 1100 °C. While
typical scrubber operating temperatures are on the order of 400-600 °C, using the high-temperature furnace

provides flexibility for optimization of salt purification technology. During operation, the headspace of the
synthesis vessel was sparged with argon gas to maintain an inert atmosphere and carry zinc vapor to the
scrubber, where the copper mesh captured the zinc vapor (Figure 2). The two vessels were connected by
stainless-steel tubing that was wrapped with heating tape and encased in insulation. Maintaining elevated
temperatures (> 450 °C) along the path to the scrubber was necessary to prevent zinc vapor from condensing
and clogging the tubing, thereby allowing for the effective capture of zinc in the scrubber. A HEPA filter
was connected downstream of the scrubber to capture any residual salt vapor.

2.2 Operation of Chlorination Assembly
Chlorination of uranium metal was performed in the synthesis vessel using a stainless-steel crucible
containing approximately 100 grams of total salt. Individual salt components were baked out at 200 °C
overnight before mixing. To demonstrate the operation of this assembly, a LiCl-KCl-UCl3 synthesis was
performed to achieve a target concentration of 30 wt % U. The synthesis was performed with sequential
additions of uranium metal to generate three consecutive 10 wt % aliquots of UCl3 to reach the target
concentration. Sequential additions were implemented for this synthesis to closely monitor reaction
progress. However, further optimization can examine the effect of a single-batch synthesis approach on the
chlorination efficiency and purity of the salt. Prior to heating, the stoichiometric amount of ZnCl2 needed
to generate 10% U was added to the LiCl-KCl base salt. The synthesis vessel was then heated to 740 °C at
a ramp rate of 4 °C per hour. While the melting point of the LiCl-KCl salt is much lower than 740 °C,
operation at 740 °C is necessary to vaporize the zinc metal generated in the reaction. Synthesis of UCl3 in
NaCl will require application of higher temperatures (>801 °C) due to the higher melting point of the base
salt. The LiCl-KCl salt was left to equilibrate for 1 hour before uranium metal shots were immersed into
the salt within a porous stainless-steel basket. The reaction was allowed to continue for 16 hours, at which

 6

point the stainless-steel basket was removed from the salt and tungsten wires were inserted so that
electrochemical sensing of the salt composition could be performed. Electrochemical sensing was
conducted using a potentiostat (Gamry Reference 5000) and tungsten wires as the working, counter, and
quasi-reference electrodes. Cyclic voltammetry was performed by scanning in the positive direction from
the open circuit potential (OCP) to the onset of chlorine generation and then reversing the scan to the
reducing regime. Voltammograms were collected with the working electrode immersed at different depths
within the salt to quantify the uranium concentration following established electrochemical sensing
protocols (Tylka et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2021; Doniger et al., 2025).

3. Demonstration of Uranium Chloride Synthesis
3.1 Synthesis of LiCl-KCl-UCl3
Each addition of uranium metal during the synthesis of LiCl-KCl-UCl3 utilized a fresh stainless-steel basket
that was assembled from a porous mesh and spot-welded to shape. Figure 3 shows the basket before and
after a synthesis interval. The basket remained structurally intact throughout the synthesis, and a layer of
dark purple salt was observed to have coated the basket, which is consistent with the color of
LiCl‑KCl‑UCl3. The salt remaining in the stainless-steel crucible after completion of the synthesis was a
similar dark purple color (Figure 4).

Electrochemical techniques were used to quantify the uranium concentration and identify any significant
zinc contamination in the salt. Following all three synthesis steps, the salt was characterized using cyclic

associated with zinc chloride was detected (the reduction of Zn2+ to Zn0 occurs at approximately –2.2 V vs
Cl-/Cl2), although ppm level quantities may be present.

3.2 Recommendations for Further Synthesis Development
Chlorination of high-concentration LiCl-KCl-UCl3 was successfully demonstrated in the purpose-built
bench-scale chlorination apparatus, demonstrating successful implementation of chlorination and
purification equipment with the capacity to perform multiple uranium additions and monitor the uranium
concentration in situ. This apparatus can be used for further development and optimization of actinide
chloride salt synthesis of industry-relevant fuel salts such as NaCl-UCl3. Synthesis of pure NaCl-UCl3
eutectic (52 wt % U) will require a robust process for synthesis and purification of high concentration
actinide salts. While synthesis using ZnCl2 is possible, optimization of parameters such as synthesis time,
operating temperature, number of batches needed, and ensuring complete zinc removal occurs at preferred
synthesis conditions will be required. Additionally, transitioning to the NaCl-UCl3 salt system will require
consideration of changes in salt properties and the behaviors of the metal and metal chloride reagents during
synthesis. The high melting point of the NaCl base salt (801 °C) will present benefits and challenges to the
synthesis. For example, the rate of volatilization of ZnCl2 is expected to increase with the operation
temperature and so synthesis may require the use of additional reagent ZnCl2. However, the increased rate

 10

of volatilization might benefit removal of the zinc metal layer and result in less zinc metal impurity within
the salt. Recommendations for optimization of NaCl-UCl3 synthesis and purification are described below.

Reagent optimization
Additional testing will be required to determine the optimum concentration of the ZnCl2 reagent and
understand ZnCl2 and zinc metal behavior in NaCl in comparison to LiCl-KCl. The relative volatilities of
ZnCl2 and zinc might differ in NaCl and LiCl-KCl, which will influence the chlorination efficiency.
Furthermore, optimization of the metal chloride reagent-to-uranium metal ratio will support the
advancement of this synthesis method while maintaining low operating cost and time. For example, a high
loading of ZnCl2 in a single synthesis step might be beneficial because it decreases the melting point of the
NaCl base salt but may result in excessive loss of ZnCl2 through volatilization because of the higher ZnCl2
concentration, ultimately increasing cost and requiring subsequent ZnCl2 additions to maintain the target
concentration. Alternatively, if a stepwise synthesis procedure is adopted for NaCl-UCl3, the first synthesis
step will require high operating temperatures, but subsequent intervals could operate at lower temperatures
as the melting point decreases with increasing UCl3 concentration in the salt. Comparing a single batch
approach with a multi-batch approach may be valuable for identifying operational requirements for process
scale-up. Additionally, alternatives to ZnCl2 may have more favorable volatilities in NaCl, which would
result in less reagent volatilization during synthesis.

Effective zinc removal
In a previous NaCl-UCl3 synthesis by Rose et al. (2023), appreciable amounts of zinc remained in the salt,
with an average concentration of 8800 ppm measured by using ICP-MS. That synthesis was performed in
a single step and was not optimized for complete zinc removal. If thermal treatment alone is not sufficient
for zinc removal, evaluation of alternative purification methods will be required. For example, the zinc
could be reacted with a scavenger such as stainless steel or copper immersed in the molten salt, but the
introduction of contamination into the salt from corrosion of the metal scavenger must be evaluated.
Another potential method for zinc removal involves electrochemical techniques as demonstrated by Lee et
al. (Lee 2019, KAERI). In that report, the presence of U-Zn alloys were identified following the chlorination
of uranium metal with ZnCl2 in LiCl-KCl, which formed through the reaction of generated zinc metal with
unreacted uranium metal. KAERI evaluated electrorefining of zinc from U-Zn alloys to remove the zinc
from metal surface, recovering zinc metal at a cathode. This allowed for increased chlorination efficiency
and decreased impurities within the final salt product.

Metal precursor and structural characterization
Uranium metal precursors for fuel synthesis are not limited to a single source or morphology. Therefore,
synthesis reaction rate and yield should be evaluated for different morphologies such as ingots, dendrites,
and powder. The extent of U-Zn alloy formation on the uranium metal surface may be affected by precursor
morphology and exposed surface area, in turn affecting reaction efficacy and zinc contamination. The effect
of precursor morphology and exposed surface area on reaction speed, efficiency, and purity should be
evaluated. Techniques such as X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy should be employed to
identify U-Zn alloy formation on uranium metal precursor materials.

4. Conclusions
A bench-scale chlorination apparatus for the synthesis of uranium chloride salts was successfully
developed, installed, and demonstrated using zinc chloride as a reagent. The apparatus was designed to
enable development and optimization of actinide chloride synthesis in a variety of base salts with under a
range of operating conditions to provide recommendations for scale up of MSR salt synthesis and
purification processes. Using this assembly, LiCl-KCl-UCl3 was successfully synthesized at approximately
25 wt% uranium concentration through a sequential chlorination approach. The apparatus operated
effectively at elevated temperatures to enable removal of zinc metal generated during the reaction from the
salt. Electrochemical characterization did not detect residual zinc metal in the synthesized salt and was used
for the determination of uranium concentration in the salt. Final confirmation of the salt composition and
impurity content will be obtained by ICP-MS analysis of salt samples taken after each synthesis stage.
These results demonstrated the bench-scale apparatus as a suitable platform for further development and
optimization of actinide chloride fuel salt synthesis, particularly for the preparation of high-uranium-content
salts that are of interest for advanced reactor applications. The flexible design of the bench-scale apparatus
provides a means to evaluate synthesis conditions, reagent loading, purification strategies, and process
monitoring methods at the bench-scale. Future work should focus on optimizing metal chloride synthesis
methods in additional salt systems, particularly NaCl-UCl3, which is a likely candidate for industry-relevant
fuel applications.
Overall, the results presented here support practical development of actinide chloride fuel salt synthesis
processes. Further optimization will provide a scalable pathway for producing high-purity salts in support
of MSR fuel development and salt qualification efforts.
