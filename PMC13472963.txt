Maternal 6-valent group B Streptococcus vaccine in non-pregnant and pregnant females: a randomized phase 1/2 trial
Authors: Shabir A Madhi, Hasra Snaggs, Zahra Skogeby, Ye Feng, Shaun Barnabas, Jeffrey Baker, Lee Fairlie, Johannes Beeslaar, David Radley, Han-Qing Jiang, Natalie C Silmon de Monerri, Raphael Simon, Lindsay McLaughlin, Michelle Gaylord, Julie Skinner, Iona Munjal, Annaliesa S Anderson, Alejandra Gurtman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroup B Streptococcus (GBS) disease is a major cause of infant morbidity and mortality. Capsular polysaccharide conjugate GBS vaccines have been evaluated for decades; however, none is licensed. This phase 1/2 trial evaluated an investigational maternal hexavalent polysaccharide−protein conjugate GBS vaccine (GBS6) in non-pregnant females and pregnant participants and their infants. In stage 1 (conducted in South Africa), healthy non-pregnant participants received GBS6, GBS6 + aluminum phosphate (AlPO4) or placebo; a subset received GBS6 + AlPO4 booster approximately 2 years later. Stage 2 (conducted in South Africa) maternal dose-finding data were reported previously and are not presented here. In stage 3, healthy pregnant participants from South Africa, the United States and the United Kingdom received GBS6 or placebo (24–36 weeks’ gestation). In stage 1, 66 non-pregnant participants received GBS6, GBS6 + AlPO4 or placebo (n = 22 each); 26 also received GBS6 + AlPO4 booster. In stage 3, 216 pregnant participants were included (GBS6, n = 108; placebo, n = 108); 209 infants were born to maternal participants (GBS6, n = 104; placebo, n = 105). Primary objectives described GBS6 safety/tolerability profiles. Nearly all reactogenicity events were mild or moderate. Among non-pregnant participants, no serious adverse events (SAEs) or adverse event (AE)-associated withdrawals were reported after vaccination; medically attended adverse events (MAEs) were reported in similar percentages across primary vaccination groups (GBS6 groups = 41−45%; placebo = 41%). Rates of AEs (GBS6 = 75/108 (69%); placebo = 70/108 (65%)), SAEs (GBS6 = 21/108 (19%); placebo = 23/108 (21%)) and delivery outcome frequencies were similar in GBS6 and placebo maternal participants. Most infants were born full-term. Infant AE (GBS6 = 87/104 (84%); placebo = 86/105 (82%)), SAE (GBS6 = 45/104 (43%); placebo = 46/105 (44%)) and MAE (GBS6 = 65/104 (63%); placebo = 66/105 (63%)) frequencies were similar across groups. Two neonatal sepsis-associated deaths occurred (GBS6 = 1; placebo = 1); neither was considered vaccine related. Secondary objectives described immune responses. GBS6 elicited robust immune responses in non-pregnant and pregnant individuals; additionally, serotype-specific quantitative and functional antibody responses were observed in infants whose parent received GBS6. These data support continuing clinical investigation of maternal GBS6 vaccination. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03765073.Subject terms: Diseases, Bacterial infection, Vaccines, Paediatrics

Main

GBS is a Gram-positive commensal bacterium associated with gastrointestinal and genital tract colonization1. Maternal GBS colonization is common during pregnancy, affecting approximately 20 million individuals in 2020 worldwide, although prevalence varies geographically and by socioeconomic status and method of assessment2–4. GBS colonization in pregnancy is associated with maternal invasive GBS disease, preterm delivery and stillbirth2,5.

In colonized mothers, GBS can also be transmitted to the fetus by ascending to the uterine compartment or from vertical transmission through the vaginal canal during delivery, which collectively results in approximately 231,800 cases of early-onset disease (occurring during the first 7 days of life) in neonates each year2,5,6. Late-onset disease (7–89 days of life) in neonates, which is usually caused by horizontal GBS acquisition from a colonized individual, is also common, with an estimated 162,200 cases worldwide each year2,5. Although occurring with different frequency in early-onset versus late-onset disease, clinical manifestations for both invasive disease categories often include sepsis, pneumonia and meningitis2,5, with estimated case fatality rates of 6−23% for early-onset disease and 6−10% for late-onset disease, amounting to approximately 91,900 infant deaths annually worldwide2,7–9. Moreover, each year, an estimated 3−21% of infant survivors of invasive GBS disease develop moderate or severe neurodevelopmental impairment2.

Although infant GBS infections are a global concern, epidemiology varies geographically, with the greatest burden of disease occurring outside high-income regions, including Africa, followed by Asia2,10. GBS capsular polysaccharide (CPS) serotypes Ia, Ib, II, III, IV and V collectively account for more than 97% of infant disease globally, with serotype III causing the majority of cases in most regions9,11. Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis significantly reduces the incidence and associated case fatality rate of early-onset disease12–14. Late-onset disease can result from postnatal exposure or horizontal transmission from colonized individuals. The protective effects of intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis end shortly after birth and, thus, are not effective against late-onset disease15,16. In the United States and other high-income countries, intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis is prescribed for pregnant individuals who test positive for GBS colonization during routine microbiologic screening in late pregnancy or exhibit clinical risk factors for invasive GBS infection (for example, prolonged preterm rupture of membranes and previous birth of infant with GBS disease)17. Prevention guidelines focus on either universal screening during the third trimester of pregnancy, as done in the United States18, or risk-based methods, as done in the United Kingdom, to identify pregnant individuals colonized with GBS who should receive intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis19,20. It is important to recognize that, in resource-limited settings, implementing either strategy can be challenging, and countries outside of high-income regions often lack intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis strategies17.

Protecting young infants from disease via maternal vaccination is an established strategy for tetanus, pertussis and influenza virus and has been introduced more recently for COVID-19 and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)-associated disease21–23. A favorable safety profile for mothers and infants as well as efficacy and effectiveness for infants have been demonstrated for these maternal vaccines24–26. Mechanistically, maternal vaccination protects infants by inducing production of vaccine-specific antibodies that are then transferred to the fetus via the placenta during pregnancy or via breastmilk after birth21.

For GBS, numerous seroepidemiology studies correlating GBS serotype-specific anti-CPS immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels in maternal, cord or infant serum with reduced risk of invasive GBS disease in early life have suggested the potential utility of a maternal vaccine for preventing invasive infant disease in the first few months of life27. CPS-conjugate GBS vaccines targeting 1−3 serotypes have been evaluated in clinical studies since the 1980s, demonstrating a favorable safety profile and the ability to transfer maternal antibodies to infants; however, no GBS vaccines are licensed currently28. One challenge to the development of a GBS vaccine is that, because of low disease incidence, a prohibitively large sample size of pregnant individuals and their infants would be needed for clinical trials to assess efficacy29. Trials may, therefore, need to have immunologic endpoints, although correlates of protection have not been established29,30. To help inform immunologic endpoints in maternal GBS vaccine trials, a case−control analysis recently assessed the relationship between infant serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG concentrations and reduction in disease risk to estimate 80% thresholds associated with risk reduction for early-onset and late-onset infant disease of 0.52−2.49 µg ml−1 and 0.02−0.14 µg ml−1, respectively30.

The GBS6 vaccine used in this study is one of the most advanced maternal vaccine candidates being developed for prevention of invasive GBS disease in infants. It is a hexavalent polysaccharide−protein conjugate vaccine containing serotypes Ia, Ib, II, III, IV and V polysaccharide individually conjugated to cross-reactive material 197 (CRM197)11,23. A first-in-human phase 1/2 study in non-pregnant adults 18−49 years of age found that GBS6 doses of up to 20 μg per serotype were well tolerated with acceptable safety profiles, and antibody responses were elicited after vaccination31.

In this subsequent phase 1/2 trial assessing maternal GBS6 vaccination completed in three stages, from which results of the maternal dose-finding stage 2 were reported previously, a GBS6 dose of up to 20 μg per serotype co-formulated with and without AlPO4 adjuvant in maternal participants 18−40 years of age was well tolerated and had acceptable safety profiles in the maternal participants and their infants (NCT03765073)32. At the time stage 2 of the study was completed, stage 1 in non-pregnant participants (reported here) was ongoing because of inclusion of a booster to be administered approximately 2 years after initial study intervention; for this reason, the results of stage 2 were reported before completion of stage 1. Additionally, to correlate vaccine-induced antibody concentrations, results from a case−control seroepidemiology study in South Africa of parent−infant pairs and monitoring for development of invasive GBS disease were published at that time alongside those from stage 2 of this phase 1/2 maternal vaccination clinical study32. Among maternal participants in stage 2, immune responses to all six serotypes persisted through delivery and were transferred to infants32. The seroepidemiology study and the phase 1/2 trial used the same standardized serologic assay to quantify anti-CPS IgG levels33 and together showed that antibody concentrations in infants after maternal GBS6 vaccination were at levels associated with reduced risk of invasive disease32, with similar results from another seroepidemiology study in Finland34.

Because co-formulation with AlPO4 adjuvant in stage 2, which was the maternal dose-finding portion of this phase 1/2 trial, did not increase immunogenicity32, the 20-μg per serotype dose level without AlPO4 adjuvant was chosen for further clinical development in stage 3 of the trial, in which a larger cohort of healthy pregnant individuals was enrolled to receive maternal vaccination with GBS6 without adjuvant at the 20-µg dose level (hereafter GBS6) or placebo. Here we report study data from stage 1 and stage 3 of this maternal GBS6 vaccine trial, including assessments of the safety and immunogenicity of primary vaccination with GBS6 20 µg with AlPO4 (GBS6 + AlPO4), and GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination in non-pregnant females (stage 1) and maternal vaccination with GBS6 at 24−36 weeks’ gestation in pregnant individuals and their infants, with the first presentation of long-term immunogenicity data through 12 months after delivery or birth (stage 3).

Results

Trial design

This phase 1/2, randomized, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation, observer-blinded study, which was conducted in three stages, evaluated the GBS6 vaccine in healthy, non-pregnant female participants as well as pregnant participants and their infants (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03765073; EudraCT: 2020-005074-96). Sex was as self-reported by the participants when asked to confirm by study staff at enrollment. This was a descriptive study, and, therefore, no prespecified power calculations were performed. All the outcomes described in Methods are included in this publication.

Stage 1 was conducted in a cohort of non-pregnant participants who were 18−40 years of age at enrollment and who received the highest tested GBS6 dose level (20 µg) formulated with and without AlPO4. After a protocol amendment, stage 1 non-pregnant participants could return and re-consent to receive a booster dose of GBS6 + AlPO4 at the 20-µg dose level approximately 2 years after the primary dose.

After review of the safety data from stage 1 non-pregnant participants obtained 1 month after receipt of study intervention, the maternal dose-finding stage 2 of the study assessed three GBS6 dose levels (5 µg, 10 µg and 20 µg) with or without AlPO4 in healthy pregnant participants 18−40 years of age and at 27−36 weeks’ gestation. Stage 2 results were reported previously and are not presented here32. After review of safety and immunogenicity data at delivery/birth from maternal participants and their infants, the GBS6 20-µg dose level formulated without AlPO4 was selected for stage 3. In stage 3, a cohort of healthy pregnant individuals 18−40 years of age who were at 24−36 weeks’ gestation were enrolled to receive GBS6 or placebo.

Primary safety endpoints included percentages of participants reporting prompted local reactions or systemic events within 7 days after each vaccination, AEs through 1 month after each vaccination and MAEs and SAEs through 6 months after the primary dose and approximately 7−12 months after the booster dose (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only) or throughout the study (that is, through the 12-month postdelivery visit; stage 3 maternal participants only). In stage 3, additional primary safety endpoints were percentages of maternal participants with obstetric complications throughout the study and with each delivery outcome. In stage 3, primary safety endpoints for infants included percentages with specific birth outcomes, AEs from birth to approximately 6 weeks of age and SAEs, AEs of special interest (major congenital anomalies, developmental delays and suspected/confirmed GBS infections) and MAEs through 12 months of age.

Secondary immunogenicity objectives were to describe immune responses of GBS6 (both stages) and GBS6 + AlPO4 (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only) after primary vaccination, and GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only). Additionally, immunogenicity objectives in stage 3 maternal and infant participants were to describe antibody levels in infants delivered to maternal participants vaccinated with GBS6 and to assess placental transfer of antibody from maternal participants to their infants.

Immunogenicity endpoints in stage 1 non-pregnant participants included GBS serotype-specific IgG geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) before and after primary and booster vaccination. Immunogenicity endpoints in stage 3 maternal participants included anti-CPS IgG GMCs and serotype-specific opsonophagocytic activity (OPA) geometric mean titers (GMTs) measured before and after vaccination and at delivery. Serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs and OPA GMTs were also measured in cord serum at birth. An exploratory objective was the GBS colonization status of participants.

Primary and booster vaccination in stage 1 non-pregnant participants of female sex

Non-pregnant participants

Study recruitment in South Africa among stage 1 non-pregnant participants occurred from 14 January to 8 February 2019, with follow-up lasting through 21 September 2022. There were 78 participants enrolled, 66 of whom were randomized and received primary vaccination (22 participants each in the GBS6, GBS6 + AlPO4 and placebo groups), and 97% (64/66) completed the study (Fig. 1a). Of the 66 participants who received primary vaccination with GBS6, GBS6 + AlPO4 or placebo, a subset of 26 participants received a GBS6 + AlPO4 booster dose approximately 2 years after the primary dose, and 88% (23/26) completed the booster phase of the study. All stage 1 non-pregnant participants were Black (Table 1). The mean (s.d.) age at primary vaccination was 27.8 (5.6) years and 29.1 (4.7) years at the time of GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination.

Safety

In stage 1 non-pregnant participants, injection site pain was the most frequently reported local reaction within 7 days after primary and booster vaccination, and percentages of participants reporting local reactions were slightly higher in the GBS6 groups (27.3−31.8%) compared to the placebo group (18.2%) after primary vaccination (Fig. 2a (primary vaccination) and Extended Data Fig. 1 (booster vaccination)). For the subset of participants who received the GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination, those who received GBS6 + AlPO4 as the primary vaccination reported higher percentages of local reactions after booster vaccination compared to those who received GBS6 or placebo as the primary vaccination. All local reactions were mild or moderate in severity. After primary vaccination, median onset for local reactions was 1−7 days and 1−2 days after vaccination in the GBS6 groups and placebo group, respectively, and resolved with a median duration of approximately 3.5−13.5 days after onset in the GBS6 groups and 1 day after onset with placebo. Among participants receiving GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination, median onset of local reactions was 1−2.5 days after booster vaccination and resolved 1−3.5 days after onset.

In stage 1 non-pregnant participants, the most frequently reported systemic event across study groups within 7 days after both primary and booster vaccination was headache (Fig. 2b (primary vaccination) and Extended Data Fig. 1 (booster vaccination)). All systemic events were mild or moderate in severity except for a single report of severe headache in the placebo group after primary vaccination. Across study groups, the median onset for systemic events was 1−2 days after primary and booster vaccinations, and the events resolved with a median duration of approximately 1−5.5 days and 1−10 days after primary and booster vaccinations, respectively.

In the GBS6 groups, the percentages of stage 1 non-pregnant participants reporting unsolicited AEs throughout the study ranged from 50.0% to 59.1% after primary vaccination and from 14.3% to 18.2% after booster vaccination, with no immediate or severe AEs reported (Fig. 3a (primary vaccination) and Extended Data Fig. 2 (booster vaccination)). Related AEs in five participants were reported after primary vaccination. No SAEs or AEs leading to withdrawal were reported after either primary or booster vaccination. MAEs were reported by similar numbers of participants after both primary and booster vaccination.

Immunogenicity

All 66 stage 1 non-pregnant participants who received primary vaccination were included in the evaluable immunogenicity population (Fig. 1a and Methods). GBS serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs increased from baseline to 2 weeks and from baseline to 1 month after primary vaccination for all serotypes in the GBS6 and GBS6 + AlPO4 groups compared to placebo (Extended Data Fig. 3). Geometric mean fold rises (GMFRs) from before vaccination to the after-vaccination timepoints were higher in the GBS6 groups compared to placebo (Extended Data Fig. 3). In the GBS6 group, the GMFRs across serotypes ranged from 46.8 (serotype V) to 318.4 (serotype IV) from before to 2 weeks after vaccination and from 35.4 (serotype V) to 228.0 (serotype IV) from before to 1 month after vaccination.

Stage 3 maternal vaccination in pregnant participants

Maternal and infant participants

Study recruitment in South Africa (GBS6, n = 80; placebo, n = 79), the United Kingdom (GBS6, n = 1; placebo, n = 2) and the United States (GBS6, n = 27; placebo, n = 27) among stage 3 maternal participants occurred from 10 October 2021 to 10 November 2022, with follow-up lasting through 4 March 2024. Of the 313 maternal participants enrolled, 216 were randomized and vaccinated (108 participants each in the GBS6 and placebo groups), and 83% (179/216) completed the study (Fig. 1b). Of the 209 infants born to maternal participants who received GBS6 (n = 104) or placebo (n = 105), 83% (173/209) completed the study (Fig. 1c).

Demographic characteristics among maternal participants were similar across study groups (Table 2). Overall, 75% (162/216) of stage 3 maternal participants were Black; mean (s.d.) age at vaccination was 27.0 (5.1) years; and 60% (129/216) were 28 to <32 weeks’ gestation at vaccination. Most infants (90% (188/209)) were born full-term (37 to <42 weeks’ gestation).

Maternal safety

Injection site pain was the only local reaction reported within 7 days after vaccination in stage 3 maternal participants, occurring in 30% (32/106) in the GBS6 group and in 18% (19/103) in the placebo group; all were mild or moderate in severity (Fig. 2a). In both study groups, the median onset day for injection site pain was on the day of vaccination and resolved at a median of 1 day after onset.

Systemic events occurring within 7 days after vaccination were reported in similar percentages of stage 3 maternal participants across study groups and were mostly mild or moderate in severity (Fig. 2b). Fatigue and headache were the most commonly reported systemic events, reported in 42% (45/106) and 37% (39/106) of maternal participants in the GBS6 group, respectively, and in 43% (44/103) and 38% (39/103) of maternal participants in the placebo group. Severe systemic events were reported in four participants in the placebo group (joint pain, n = 1; fatigue, n = 3); no severe systemic events were reported in the GBS6 group. The median onset day for any systemic event was 1−2 days after vaccination in both groups, and median time to resolution was 1−5 days after onset.

Unsolicited AEs occurring throughout the study were reported in 69% (75/108) of stage 3 maternal participants who received GBS6 and in 65% (70/108) of those who received placebo (Fig. 3b). A related AE of dizziness occurred in a maternal placebo recipient, which occurred on the day of vaccination and resolved the following day. SAEs were reported in 19% (21/108) and 21% (23/108) of maternal GBS6 and placebo recipients, respectively; the most common SAE was fetal distress syndrome (GBS6, n = 9; placebo, n = 14). One maternal participant in the placebo group withdrew from the study because of an SAE of bladder pain, which was characterized as not related to study intervention.

Delivery outcomes in stage 3 maternal participants were similar in the GBS6 and placebo groups. Overall, 72% (78/108) and 76% (82/108) of GBS6 and placebo recipients, respectively, had vaginal deliveries, and most births (88.0% and 91.7% of GBS6 and placebo recipients, respectively) were full-term, live deliveries (Extended Data Table 2). Fourteen participants delivered preterm (GBS6, 7.4% (8/108); placebo, 5.6% (6/108)); most of these preterm deliveries were late preterm (34 to <37 weeks’ gestational age at birth; GBS6, n = 7; placebo, n = 4). One stillbirth of unknown cause was reported in the GBS6 group. The median time between vaccination and delivery was 65 days (range, 14−115) in the GBS6 group and 68 days (range, 9−114) in the placebo group.

Maternal and infant immunogenicity

The evaluable immunogenicity populations included 94% (202/216) of stage 3 maternal participants (GBS6, 94% (102/108); placebo, 93% (100/108)) and 83% (174/209) of their infants (GBS6, 83% (86/104); placebo, 84% (88/105)) (Fig. 1b,c). In the GBS6 group, maternal GBS serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs increased 2 weeks after vaccination and were maintained at 1 month after vaccination and through delivery (Fig. 4). Maternal GBS serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs at delivery ranged from 0.5 μg ml−1 to 22.5 μg ml−1 compared to placebo (range, 0.01–0.05 μg ml−1). GMFRs from baseline to 1 month after vaccination were higher in the GBS6 group (range, 43.8 for serotype V to 267.7 for serotype IV) compared to placebo (all 1.0) (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Table 4), which were similar to the responses observed in stage 1 non-pregnant participants. In maternal participants, GMFRs from baseline to 12 months after vaccination ranged from 25.1 (serotype V) to 75.2 (serotype III) in the GBS6 group and from 1.3 (serotype III) to 2.7 (serotype IV) in the placebo group.

Discussion

Overall, vaccination with GBS6 was well tolerated and demonstrated an acceptable safety profile in both non-pregnant and maternal participants with no safety concerns reported in infant participants. Local reactions were generally mild or moderate in severity across all stages of this study32. Consistent with previously reported stage 2 dose-finding safety and tolerability data in maternal participants from this study, fatigue and headache were the most commonly reported systemic events among maternal participants whether vaccinated with GBS6 or receiving placebo32. For both maternal and infant participants, the frequency of AEs and SAEs was similar whether receiving GBS6 or placebo. Additionally, delivery and birth outcomes were similar between GBS6 and placebo recipients (that is, preterm births (79% of which were late preterm—34 to <37 weeks’ gestational age)) or not observed (that is, major congenital anomalies and suspected/confirmed GBS infections). Alongside the previously reported dose-finding maternal vaccination data from this trial, these results demonstrated all dose formulations of GBS6 to be safe and well tolerated in healthy pregnant individuals32.

In non-pregnant individuals, GBS6 induced robust serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG responses for all six serotypes in the vaccine through 1 month after the primary dose of GBS6. In this first description of results of booster dosing, in which a subset of non-pregnant participants from stage 1 received a GBS6 + AlPO4 booster dose regardless of primary study vaccination, this response gradually declined over approximately 2 years after the primary dose but remained above levels observed with the placebo primary dose before the booster dose and at 3 months and 6 months after the booster dose. These results are consistent with a phase 2 study in non-pregnant adults who showed serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs at 1 month after a booster dose of GBS6 increased compared to before the booster dose regardless of baseline anti-CPS IgG GMCs before primary vaccination or booster vaccination35.

Consistent with previously published stage 2 dose-finding results of the present study in pregnant individuals32, GBS6 induced robust serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG responses in pregnant individuals to all six vaccine serotypes in the present maternal population. The longer-term follow-up in stage 3 compared to stage 2 showed encouragingly durable immune responses in maternal participants through 12 months after delivery. Similarly, infants born to GBS6-vaccinated maternal participants had higher anti-CPS IgG concentrations than those born to maternal participants who received placebo, and maternal-to-infant anti-CPS IgG geometric mean transfer ratios at birth/delivery of 0.6−0.8 were suggestive of placental transfer of antibodies. Additionally, robust serotype-specific functional antibody responses to all serotypes were observed in infants born to maternal participants who received GBS6. Long-term infant immunogenicity data through 12 months after birth, which have not been previously available, showed that, although IgG GMCs waned over time, responses were consistently higher for the GBS6 group versus placebo.

No cases of early-onset or late-onset GBS disease were reported in infants through 12 months of age during the study in either group; this is not an unexpected finding given the low incidence of GBS disease9 and the small number of infants included in the trial. Nevertheless, the robust antibody responses observed in infants born to maternal participants who received GBS6 are encouraging; if approved, long-term real-world and surveillance data should be monitored to confirm GBS6 effectiveness.

Strengths of this study include the randomized study design conducted in three stages, allowing for investigation of GBS6 at different formulations and dose levels in pregnant and non-pregnant individuals as well as assessment of the feasibility of booster dosing. Simultaneous assessment of safety and immunogenicity in different populations may shorten the development timeline and progression to phase 3 trials and ultimate licensure, such as has occurred in the clinical development and approval of new pneumococcal polysaccharide−protein conjugate vaccines and meningococcal, influenza and COVID-19 vaccines36–38.

Limitations of this study include the descriptive study design, small sample size for all analyses, particularly the booster subset, and the inclusion of participants mostly from South Africa, which may restrict generalization to other countries and regions. There was a high proportion of Black participants in this study, but few Asian participants were included; this may be important because Asia has one of the highest case numbers of infant GBS infections after Africa2. Another limitation is the exclusion of maternal participants with high-risk pregnancies, including history of preterm birth, current gestational hypertension, preeclampsia or eclampsia or current confirmed GBS bacteriuria. Further study will be required to determine whether antibody transfer to infants is sustained in high-risk pregnancies and in infants born preterm as well as the potential use of GBS6 in subsequent pregnancies. Additionally, sample sizes also precluded a formal comparison between the non-pregnant and pregnant study groups. Nevertheless, pregnant participants had good overall responses and a safety and tolerability profile that was similar to the non-pregnant group. Finally, intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis was administered according to local standard of care; thus, only a subset of GBS-positive participants were treated, which is a potential confounding factor.

Safe and effective maternal vaccination strategies providing protection for infants have been established for other vaccines21,22,39. The addition of GBS6 maternal vaccines alongside currently available tetanus, acellular pertussis, influenza, COVID-19 and RSV maternal vaccines may contribute to improved infant health. A recent randomized, placebo-controlled, phase 2b study evaluating concomitant administration of GBS6 with Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria and acellular pertussis vaccine) in healthy non-pregnant female participants provided evidence supporting vaccine co-administration and will likely inform future studies in pregnant individuals40. Another randomized placebo-controlled phase 2 study (NCT05832502) is assessing GBS6 among pregnant participants with and without HIV infection. Based on this supporting evidence, a phase 3 trial of GBS6 in pregnant participants is ongoing (NCT07160244). These vaccine investigations are especially important to advance knowledge that may help to protect those most vulnerable to GBS infection and decrease the GBS disease burden in resource-limited regions that lack intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis strategies as well as address late-onset GBS disease and increasing incidence of GBS-associated bacterial meningitis in high-income settings41,42.

Methods

Trial design

This phase 1/2, randomized, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation, observer-blinded study, which was conducted in three stages, evaluated GBS6 in healthy non-pregnant female participants and in pregnant participants and their infants (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03765073; EudraCT: 2020-005074-96). As GBS6 is being studied for maternal vaccination, the study included only participants of female sex; no disaggregated sex data and analyses were obtained or performed.

Stage 1 was conducted in South Africa in a cohort of non-pregnant participants who received the highest tested GBS6 dose level (20 µg) formulated with and without AlPO4. After a protocol amendment, stage 1 non-pregnant participants could return and re-consent to receive a booster dose of GBS6 with AlPO4 (GBS6 + AlPO4) at the 20-µg dose level approximately 2 years after the primary dose.

After review of the 1-month postvaccination safety data from stage 1 non-pregnant participants, the maternal dose-finding stage 2 of the study assessed three GBS6 dose levels (5 µg, 10 µg and 20 µg) with or without AlPO4 in healthy pregnant participants 18−40 years of age at 27−36 weeks’ gestation in South Africa. Stage 2 maternal dose-finding results were reported previously and are not presented here32. After review of safety and immunogenicity data at delivery/birth from stage 2 maternal participants and their infants, the GBS6 20-µg dose level formulated without AlPO4 was selected for stage 3. In stage 3, a cohort of healthy pregnant individuals from South Africa, the United States and the United Kingdom at 24−36 weeks’ gestation were enrolled to receive GBS6 or placebo.

Eligibility criteria

Stage 1 non-pregnant female participants

Participants enrolled to receive primary vaccination in stage 1 were healthy non-pregnant individuals (as confirmed by a negative urine pregnancy test at visit 1 (before vaccination)) of female sex and who were 18−40 years of age at enrollment. Stage 1 non-pregnant participants were to have evidence of a personally signed and dated informed consent document indicating that the participant was informed of all pertinent aspects of the study. Participants were to be willing and able to comply with scheduled visits, the investigational plan, laboratory tests and other study procedures, including completion of the electronic diary (e-diary) from days 1 to 7 after administration of the investigational product. Participants of non-childbearing potential were to have (1) achieved postmenopausal status, (2) undergone a documented hysterectomy and/or bilateral oophorectomy or (3) had medically confirmed ovarian failure. All other participants (including participants with tubal ligations) were considered to be of childbearing potential. Participants were also to be documented as negative for HIV, hepatitis C virus (HCV) and acute or chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection at screening.

Stage 1 non-pregnant participants who were eligible to receive booster vaccination had to have evidence of a personally signed and dated informed consent document indicating that the participant was informed of all pertinent aspects for the booster vaccination and subsequent visits; had to have received investigational product (primary vaccination) at visit 1; and had to be willing and able to comply with scheduled visits, the investigational plan, laboratory tests and other study procedures, including completion of the e-diary from days 1 to 7 after booster vaccination with GBS6 + AlPO4. To be eligible for booster vaccination, participants had to also continue to meet all stage 1 inclusion criteria and none of the stage 1 exclusion criteria for primary vaccination, including being healthy and not pregnant as determined by medical history, physical examination and clinical judgment of the investigator and having received investigational product (primary vaccination) at vsit 1. Participants were also required to have a negative urine pregnancy test at visit 6 (before vaccination). Participants of non-childbearing potential were required to meet at least one of the criteria outlined for primary vaccination. All other participants (including participants with tubal ligations) were considered to be of childbearing potential. Participants were also to be documented as negative for HIV, HCV and acute or chronic HBV infection at screening.

Exclusion criteria for stage 1 non-pregnant participants enrolled to primary or booster vaccination included participation in other studies involving investigational drug(s), vaccines or medical devices within 28 days before study entry and/or during study participation. Participants could not have had other medical or psychiatric conditions including recent (within the past year) or active suicidal ideation or behavior or laboratory abnormality that could increase the risk associated with study participation or investigational product administration or could interfere with the interpretation of study results and, in the judgment of the investigator, would make the participant inappropriate for entry into this study. Participants who had a history of severe adverse reaction associated with a vaccine and/or severe allergic reaction (for example, anaphylaxis) to any component of the investigational product or any diphtheria toxoid-containing or CRM197-containing vaccine; had a history of microbiologically proven invasive disease caused by GBS (Streptococcus agalactiae); were immunocompromised with known or suspected immunodeficiency; or received treatment with immunosuppressive therapy, including cytotoxic agents or systemic corticosteroids (for example, for cancer or an autoimmune disease) or planned receipt through the blood draw occurring 1 month after vaccination were also excluded. If systemic corticosteroids were administered short term (<14 days) for treatment of an acute illness, participants could not be enrolled into the study until corticosteroid therapy was discontinued for at least 30 days before investigational product administration. Inhaled/nebulized, intra-articular, intrabursal or topical (skin or eyes) corticosteroids were permitted. Bleeding diathesis or a condition associated with prolonged bleeding that would contraindicate intramuscular injection; any known or suspected autoimmune or neuroinflammatory disease; current alcohol abuse or illicit drug use; previous vaccination with any licensed or investigational GBS vaccine (other than GBS6 received as a primary vaccination at visit 1) or planned receipt during the participant’s participation in the study (through the last blood draw); vaccination with diphtheria-containing or CRM197-containing vaccine(s) from 6 months before investigational product administration; and receipt or planned receipt of blood/plasma products or immunoglobulin from 60 days before investigational product administration through the blood draw occurring 1 month after vaccination were additional exclusion criteria. Individuals who were breastfeeding or of childbearing potential who were unwilling or unable to use a highly effective method of contraception for at least 3 months after administration of the investigational product were also excluded.

Stage 3 maternal participants

Eligible stage 3 maternal participants were healthy individuals 18−40 years of age who were 24 0/7 to 35 6/7 weeks’ gestation on the day of planned vaccination with an uncomplicated, singleton pregnancy, were at no increased risk for complications and had no significant fetal abnormalities observed on ultrasound performed at any time before study entry and/or at the screening visit.

Gestational age of maternal participants was documented based on one of the following composite criteria based on timing and availability of data on the last menstrual period (LMP), ultrasound and physical examination. The earliest ultrasound data available during the current pregnancy were used to establish gestational age. If first-trimester data were available (data obtained at ≤13 6/7 weeks’ gestation), the date of the first day of the reported LMP was used to establish the gestational age if corroborated by a first-trimester ultrasound. If there was a discrepancy of more than 7 days between the LMP-determined gestational age and a first-trimester ultrasound or the LMP was uncertain/unknown, then the gestational age was determined using the first-trimester ultrasound. If second-trimester data were available (data obtained at 14 0/7 to 27 6/7 weeks’ gestation), the date of the first day of the reported LMP was used to establish the gestational age if corroborated by a second-trimester ultrasound or a physical examination including fundal height. If there was a discrepancy of more than 10 days between the LMP-determined gestational age and the second-trimester ultrasound or if the LMP was uncertain/unknown, then the gestational age was determined using the second-trimester ultrasound. If third-trimester data were available (data obtained at ≥28 weeks), the date of the first day of the reported LMP was used to establish the gestational age if corroborated by a third-trimester ultrasound. If there was a discrepancy of more than 21 days between the LMP-determined gestational age and the third-trimester ultrasound or if the LMP was uncertain/unknown, then the gestational age was determined using the third-trimester ultrasound. Pregnant participants must have been receiving prenatal standard of care at the clinics, physician offices or hospital networks affiliated with the clinical study site and be documented as negative for HIV antibody, HBV surface antigen, HCV antibody and syphilis at screening.

Maternal participants also had to be willing and able to comply with scheduled visits, the investigational plan, laboratory tests and other study procedures, including completion of the e-diary from day 1 to day 7 after administration of investigational product. Maternal participants were expected to be available for the duration of the study and for contact by telephone during study participation and were expected to give informed consent for their infant to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria for stage 3 maternal participants included participation in other studies involving investigational drug(s), vaccines or medical devices within 28 days before study entry and/or during study participation. Additional exclusion criteria included a history of severe adverse reaction associated with a vaccine and/or severe allergic reaction (for example, anaphylaxis) to any component of the investigational product or any related vaccine; history of microbiologically proven invasive disease caused by GBS (S. agalactiae) or history of an infant with GBS disease; or current alcohol abuse or illicit drug use.

Maternal participants with a pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) of at least 40 kg m−2 were excluded. If a pre-pregnancy BMI was not available, the BMI at the time of the first obstetric visit during the current pregnancy could be used. Those with a history of or current pregnancy complications or abnormalities that could increase the risk associated with the participant’s participation in and completion of the study, including, but not limited to, gestational hypertension or preeclampsia/eclampsia, placental abnormality, polyhydramnios or oligohydramnios, significant bleeding or blood clotting disorder, gestational diabetes, any signs of premature labor with the current pregnancy, prior stillbirth or neonatal death, prior low birth weight or preterm delivery, a history of at least three miscarriages, more than five previous pregnancies, previous delivery of an infant with a known genetic disorder or major congenital anomaly or confirmed GBS bacteriuria during the current pregnancy were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were major illness of the maternal participant or conditions of the fetus that could substantially increase the risk associated with participation in and completion of the study or could preclude the evaluation of the participant’s response.

Maternal participants with a clinical history of primary genital herpes simplex virus infection during the current pregnancy; with known or suspected immunodeficiency; or who received treatment with immunosuppressive therapy, including cytotoxic agents or systemic corticosteroids (for example, for cancer or an autoimmune disease) or planned to receive these through the postvaccination blood draw were also excluded. If systemic corticosteroids were administered short term (<14 days) for treatment of an acute illness, participants could not be enrolled into the study until corticosteroid therapy had been discontinued for at least 30 days before investigational product administration. Inhaled/nebulized, intra-articular, intrabursal or topical (skin or eyes) corticosteroids were permitted.

Maternal participants with other medical or psychiatric conditions, including recent (within the past year) or active suicidal ideation or behavior or laboratory abnormality that could increase the risk associated with study participation or investigational product administration or could interfere with the interpretation of study results and, in the judgment of the investigator, would make the individual inappropriate for entry into this study, were excluded.

Exclusion criteria also included bleeding diathesis or a condition associated with prolonged bleeding that would contraindicate intramuscular injection, previous vaccination with any licensed or investigational GBS vaccine or planned receipt during study participation or vaccination with diphtheria-containing or CRM197-containing vaccine from 6 months before investigational product administration. Licensed Tdap could be given as per local recommendation for immunization in pregnant individuals, if it was administered at a minimum of 15 days before or after study vaccination.

Receipt of blood/plasma products or immunoglobulin from 60 days before investigational product administration or planned receipt through delivery, except for anti-D immunoglobulin, which could be given at any time, as well as any known or suspected autoimmune or neuroinflammatory disease, were also exclusion criteria.

Stage 3 infant participants

Inclusion criteria for infant participants in stage 3 included evidence of a dated informed consent document signed by the parent(s). The maternal participant must have participated in the informed consent process and signed and dated the informed consent document for themselves and their fetus/infant before taking part in the study. Informed consent was obtained from the other parent of the fetus/infant if mandated by local requirements. To be included, parent(s) had to be willing and able to comply with scheduled visits, the investigational plan, laboratory tests and other study procedures. Infants who were direct descendants (child or grandchild) of study personnel were excluded.

Ethics and inclusion statement

This trial was designed in alignment with established collaborations between global clinical research partners and local investigators, ensuring that study implementation reflected site-specific context and feasibility. Study sites were selected based on demonstrated clinical infrastructure and experience, with input from local investigators to support appropriate participant recruitment strategies and sustainable study conduct.

Consistent with an implementation-focused approach, local study teams played a central role in study execution, including site selection, participant engagement and oversight of study procedures. Responsibilities and governance were clearly defined across collaborators to ensure compliance with ethical standards, data integrity and appropriate authorship and data ownership practices.

All study procedures prioritized participant safety and wellbeing. Protocol-defined safeguards were set in place to ensure confidentiality, appropriate handling of sensitive information and clear communication of study procedures and results to participants. Study staff were trained to provide informed, supportive and culturally appropriate interactions, with oversight to ensure adherence to Good Clinical Practice and ethical research standards.

Ethics approval

The protocol, protocol amendments, informed consent forms, investigator brochure and other relevant documents were submitted to an investigational review board (IRB)/independent ethics committee (IEC) by the investigator and reviewed and approved by the IRB/IEC before study initiation. The protocol and statistical analysis plan, including a summary of all amendments, are available at ClinicalTrials.gov. The ethics committees for this study included the University of the Witwatersrand Research Ethics Committee (South Africa; reference number 180701); the Stellenbosch University Human Research Ethics Committee (South Africa; reference number 180701); the London – Brent Ethics Committee (United Kingdom; reference number 294701); and the WCG Institutional Review Board (United States; reference number 20222638).

This study was conducted in accordance with the protocol and consensus ethical principles derived from international guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki Council and Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, and other applicable laws and regulations, including privacy laws. All participants or their legally authorized representative(s) provided written informed consent before enrollment. Stage 3 maternal participants provided consent for themselves and their infants.

Randomization and blinding

Participant randomization used a web-based interactive response technology system. In stage 1, non-pregnant participants were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive GBS6 at the 20-µg dose level formulated with or without AlPO4 or placebo. In stage 3, pregnant participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive GBS6 at the 20-µg dose level without AlPO4 or placebo. Infants born to stage 3 maternal participants were assigned a participant number at birth. Blocked randomization was used; the block size was six for stage 1 non-pregnant participants and four for stage 3 maternal participants.

This was an observer-blinded study, apart from the stage 1 booster vaccination in non-pregnant participants, which was administered open label to a subset of participants who had received primary study vaccination. The appearance of GBS6 and placebo were not matched, and only the unblinded dispensers and administrators could see the investigational product containers. Study staff dispensing and administering study intervention were unblinded, but all other study personnel, including investigators, participants and the sponsor, were blinded.

This study used an external data monitoring committee (EDMC). The sponsor’s internal review committee and the EDMC reviewed unblinded stage 1 safety data in non-pregnant participants from 1 month after vaccination and the 1-month safety and immunogenicity data from the various GBS6 dose levels and formulations from the phase 1/2 first-in-human study31 before progressing to subsequent stages of this study. The internal review committee and the EDMC were not part of the study team and were not involved in trial conduct. In addition, unblinded sponsor personnel who were not part of the study team and also not involved in trial conduct were assigned to assess whether any protocol prespecified stopping rule was triggered for ongoing safety review. If unblinded sponsor personnel confirmed that a stopping rule was met, the internal review committee was to review all safety data and provide recommendations to the EDMC; the EDMC would then review the safety data and internal review committee recommendations and agree or provide an alternate recommendation. Stopping rules for this study were not met. Laboratory personnel performing the immunologic assays remained blinded to the study intervention assigned or received throughout the study.

Procedures

GBS6 at the 20-µg dose level, GBS6 + AlPO4 at the 20-µg dose level (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only) or saline placebo (0.9% sodium chloride) in a 0.5-ml volume was administered into the deltoid muscle preferably of the nondominant arm. GBS6 is composed of anti-CPS of serotypes Ia, Ib, II, III, IV and V, individually conjugated to CRM197 (refs. 11,31,32).

In stage 1 non-pregnant participants, blood draws for immunogenicity assessments were performed before vaccination; 2 weeks and 1 month after primary vaccination; before booster vaccination; and 1 month, 3 months and 6 months after booster vaccination. In stage 3 maternal participants, blood draws were performed before vaccination, 2 weeks and 1 month after vaccination, at delivery and 6 weeks and 12 months after delivery. In stage 3 infant participants, blood draws were performed at birth (cord blood) and at 6 weeks and 14 weeks and 12 months after birth. Infant sera were obtained within 72 hours of birth in cases where cord blood was not available. Swabs for assessment of GBS colonization in stage 1 non-pregnant, stage 3 maternal and stage 3 infant participants were collected as described previously32. In brief, the GBS colonization status in stage 1 non-pregnant participants was assessed by vaginal and rectal swabs before and 1 month after primary vaccination. In stage 3 maternal participants, vaginal and rectal swabs were collected on day 1, at delivery (swabs could be collected from maternal participants up to 72 hours after delivery) and 6 weeks after delivery. Oral and rectal swabs were also taken from stage 3 infant participants at delivery and at 6 weeks and 14 weeks of age. Swabs were cultured for GBS to assess colonization status.

Stage 1 non-pregnant and stage 3 maternal participants recorded prespecified local reactions (injection site pain, redness, swelling) and systemic events (fever, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fatigue, muscle pain, joint pain) in an e-diary. Reactogenicity events were categorized by grade using established guidelines43. Redness and swelling were categorized as mild (>2.5 cm to 5.0 cm), moderate (>5.0 cm to 10.0 cm), severe (>10 cm) or grade 4 (necrosis for redness and swelling and exfoliative dermatitis for redness only). Injection site pain and headache were graded as mild (does not interfere with activity), moderate (repeated use of non-narcotic pain reliever for >24 hours or interferes with activity), severe (use of any narcotic pain reliever or prevents daily activity) or grade 4 (emergency department visit or hospitalization). Fever was graded as a temperature of 38.0−38.4 °C, >38.4 °C to 38.9 °C, >38.9 °C to 40.0 °C or >40.0 °C. Nausea/vomiting was categorized as mild (no interference with activity or 1−2 times in 24 hours), moderate (some interference with activity or more than two times in 24 hours), severe (prevents daily activity or requires intravenous hydration) or grade 4 (emergency department visit or hospitalization for hypotensive shock). Diarrhea was categorized as mild (2−3 loose stools in 24 hours), moderate (4−5 loose stools in 24 hours), severe (≥6 loose stools in 24 hours) or grade 4 (emergency department visit or hospitalization). Fatigue/tiredness, muscle pain and joint pain were classified as mild (no interference with activity), moderate (some interference with activity), severe (prevents daily activity) or grade 4 (emergency department visit or hospitalization). Grade 4 local reactions and systemic events were categorized by the investigator and were recorded as AEs.

Unsolicited AEs were categorized according to Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terms (version 27.0), with study investigators determining causality and severity.

There were eight amendments to the original protocol. Amendments were made to expand the study through the inclusion of the booster cohorts and to provide clarification of the study cohort amid operational recruitment challenges encountered during the pandemic. Amendments also strengthened safety monitoring and sample collection procedures and shifted the statistical approach to focus on IgG-based correlates of protection. Protocol amendment 1 was issued on 4 February 2019. The overall reason for the amendment was to update exploratory objectives and schedule of activities. Amendment 2 was issued on 18 September 2019. The overall reason for the amendment was to update study procedures and AE reporting. Amendment 3 was issued on 27 May 2020. The overall reason for the amendment was to expand the gestational age window at vaccination for stage 3 participants from ≥27 0/7 to ≤35 6/7 weeks’ gestation to ≥24 0/7 to ≤35 6/7 weeks’ gestation. Amendment 4 was issued on 25 November 2020. The overall reason for the amendment was to add booster evaluation. Amendment 5 (3 November 2021) added additional blood draws for booster cohorts. Amendment 6 (26 April 2022) expanded US enrollment in stage 3 and introduced a revised statistical approach, with the removal of OPA endpoints and a transition to IgG-based correlates of protection. Amendment 7 (20 October 2022) further adjusted US enrollment, updated infant blood draw schedules in the United States and the United Kingdom and refined eligibility criteria. Finally, Amendment 8 (27 March 2023) reflected finalized regional enrollment numbers and updated the study duration.

Outcomes

Primary safety endpoints included percentages of participants reporting prompted local reactions or systemic events within 7 days after each vaccination, AEs through 1 month after each vaccination and MAEs and SAEs through 6 months after the primary dose and approximately 7−12 months after the booster dose (stage 1) or throughout the study (that is, through the 12-month post-delivery visit; stage 3). In stage 3 maternal participants, additional primary safety endpoints included percentages of maternal participants with obstetric complications (prepartum, intrapartum, postpartum) throughout the study and with each delivery outcome (live birth and delivery mode). In stage 3 infant participants, primary safety endpoints included percentages of infant participants with specific birth outcomes; AEs from birth to approximately 6 weeks of age; and SAEs, AEs of special interest (major congenital anomalies, developmental delays, suspected/confirmed GBS infections) and MAEs through 12 months of age.

Secondary immunogenicity objectives were to describe immune responses of GBS6 (both stages) and GBS6 + AlPO4 (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only) after primary vaccination and GBS6 + AlPO4 booster vaccination (stage 1 non-pregnant participants only). Additionally, immunogenicity objectives in stage 3 maternal and infant participants were to describe antibody levels in infants delivered to maternal participants vaccinated with GBS6 and to assess placental transfer of antibody from maternal participants to their infants.

Immunogenicity endpoints in stage 1 non-pregnant participants included GBS serotype-specific IgG GMCs before and after primary and booster vaccination. Immunogenicity endpoints in stage 3 maternal and infant participants were anti-CPS IgG GMCs and serotype-specific OPA GMTs measured before and after vaccination as well as at delivery. Serotype-specific anti-CPS IgG GMCs and OPA GMTs were also measured in cord serum at birth. The methods for these immunogenicity assays were reported previously11,32,33,44.

An exploratory objective in non-pregnant participants, as well as maternal participants and their infants, was the GBS colonization status.

Analysis populations

The safety population in stage 1 non-pregnant participants included all participants who received GBS6, GBS6 + AlPO4 or placebo as the primary vaccination; the safety population for stage 1 booster included all non-pregnant participants who received a booster of GBS6 + AlPO4. The safety populations in stage 3 included all maternal participants who received GBS6 or placebo and all infants whose parent received GBS6 or placebo.

The evaluable immunogenicity population was the primary population for all immunogenicity data analyses. The immunogenicity data based on the evaluable immunogenicity population for stage 1 non-pregnant participants and stage 3 maternal participants were summarized according to the vaccine group as administered. The immunogenicity data for stage 3 infant participants were summarized according to the vaccine group as administered to their parent.

In stage 1 non-pregnant participants, the primary vaccination evaluable immunogenicity population included participants who were eligible and randomized to the study; who received GBS6, GBS6 + AlPO4 or placebo as randomized; who had blood drawn within 27−49 days after primary vaccination; who had at least one valid and determinate assay result at 1 month after primary vaccination; and who had no major protocol violations. The booster vaccination evaluable immunogenicity population among stage 1 non-pregnant participants included all participants who were eligible and assigned to booster vaccination; who received a booster dose of GBS6 + AlPO4; who had blood drawn for assay testing within 27−49 days after booster vaccination; who had at least one valid and determinate assay result at 1 month after booster vaccination; and who had no major protocol violations.

In stage 3 maternal participants, the evaluable immunogenicity population included all participants who were eligible and randomized into the study; who received GBS6 or placebo as randomized; who had blood drawn within 27−49 days after receipt of study intervention or within 72 hours after delivery (if the maternal participant did not have the 1-month-after-vaccination visit and this occurred before day 49); who had at least one valid and determinate assay result at either 1 month after vaccination or 1 month after delivery; and who had no major protocol violations. The evaluable immunogenicity population for stage 3 infant participants included those who were eligible (had a parent-signed informed consent form, met all eligibility criteria and was randomized into the study), whose parent received GBS6 or placebo as randomized, who had cord blood available or a blood draw for assay testing within 72 hours after birth, who had at least one valid and determinate assay result at the birth visit and who had no major protocol violations.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute). Statistical analyses were descriptive in nature. Sample sizes at each stage were not driven by specific hypothesis testing but were guided by the probability of observing AEs and by enrollment feasibility considerations; therefore, a power calculation was not performed. All safety and immunogenicity data were analyzed separately for stage 1 non-pregnant participants and for stage 3 maternal participants and their infants.

Descriptive summary statistics are provided for all data, with two-sided 95% confidence intervals for all categorical outcomes and for continuous outcomes. The GBS6 serotype-specific IgG and OPA antibody levels at selected blood sampling timepoints were summarized by geometric means (GMCs or GMTs) and the associated two-sided 95% confidence intervals by vaccine group. The GMCs (GMTs) were calculated as the mean of the logarithmically transformed assay results and back transformed to the original units. The two-sided 95% confidence intervals were constructed by back transformation of the confidence intervals for the means of the logarithmically transformed assay results using the Studentʼs t distribution.

The fold rises in GBS6 serotype-specific antibody levels from before vaccination to each selected postvaccination timepoint for stage 1 non-pregnant participants and stage 3 maternal participants or from birth to each selected post-birth timepoint for the stage 3 infant participants were summarized by vaccine group by geometric means and associated two-sided 95% confidence intervals. The GMFRs were calculated as the mean of the difference of logarithmically transformed antibody levels at postvaccination and prevaccination timepoints and back transformed to the original units. The associated two-sided 95% confidence intervals were computed by back transformation of the confidence intervals using the Studentʼs t distribution for the mean difference of measures on the logarithmically transformed assay results.
