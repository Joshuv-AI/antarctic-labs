Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Objective performance and economic modeling of solar thermal plants is of keen interest to
many EPRI funders. One solar thermal technology that is not currently available to model in any
non-vendor, non-proprietary tool is linear Fresnel. This technology has garnered enough interest
from EPRI funders to merit investing in a tool to objectively model its performance.

 1

Early in 2010, EPRI performed a comparison of modeling solar thermal power plants using the
IPSEPRO, CNRS and Solar Advisor (SAM) tools. After completing this effort, EPRI decided to
adopt NREL’s Solar Advisor Model as its default modeling tool based in part on user
friendliness, flexibility, number of technologies covered, integrated financial model and ease of
running sensitivities. Furthermore, it was recognized that NREL continues to invest considerable
time and resources into improving capabilities and functionality of the model.

CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

As Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) technologies seek to achieve lower energy cost, public
utilities and private investors look increasingly at alternative CSP configurations. Among these
are the “Linear Fresnel” (LF) technology that uses long, rotating mirror panels to reflect sunlight
onto a linear receiver. The thermal energy absorbed by the receiver can be used for electricity
production, process heat applications, or as a supplemental energy source in a combined-cycle
power plant.

Currently, no detailed LF technology performance model is publicly available, and this
circumstance hinders understanding of the potential benefits and risks associated with the
technology. Work under this CRADA will focus on developing a robust LF simulation tool that
will accurately assess the performance of LF systems over a range of design configurations,
climates, and operating modes. This work benefits the above-mentioned parties in the following
ways:

DOE:

 • Allows researchers to more accurately quantify the viability of the LF technology,
 especially compared to other CSP technologies like power towers and parabolic troughs.
 • Allows policy-makers to identify the capacity in which LF systems can further renewable
 energy goals.

Participants:

 • Provides EPRI with a model for a technology that industry partners and other
 stakeholders have requested more information on.
 • Provides NREL with an accurate tool for identifying cost-reduction and performance-
 improvement opportunities under the line-focus CSP activity.
 • Furthers NREL’s goal of providing up-to-date technology modeling capabilities,
 especially within the System Advisor Model (SAM) framework.

US Taxpayer:

 • Furthers the goals of increasing renewable energy production, energy independence, and
 domestic energy production by improving energy researchers’ understanding of an
 increasingly important renewable technology.
 • Provides researchers and utilities with the ability to better identify the most promising
 renewable energy technology options; this allows utilities to deliver an “optimized”
 lowest-cost renewable energy portfolio.

Summary of Research Results:

This project develops a new simulation model for Linear Fresnel technology using molten salt
(or other sensible-heat fluids) as the heat transfer fluid. The model characterizes the performance
of a solar field, power cycle, and (optionally) a thermal storage system by simulating plant
performance at the subsystem level with hourly time-steps over the course of a typical year. The
model is developed in NREL’s Transient Component Simulator (TCS) development kit,and will
be integrated into NREL’s System Advisor Model (SAM). The final report describes model
formulations for the various subsystems, including the solar field collector system, thermal
receiver, piping and distribution system, thermal storage system, storage heat exchanger,
auxiliary heater, power cycle, and power cycle heat rejection subsystems.

The model is built on simulation modules from existing technology models in SAM - including
the Molten Salt Power Tower, Physical Trough, and Direct Steam Linear Fresnel technologies.

Purpose

The objective of this effort is to engage NREL to develop linear Fresnel modeling capability
within SAM so that EPRI funders have an objective way to predict performance of this
technology, understand its limitations and compare the results with alternative solar options on a
consistent basis. NREL will make the version of SAM resulting from this effort publicly
available on its website.

Statement of Work

Task Descriptions

The scope of this contract includes the following tasks:

1.1 Linear Fresnel Model Development

1.1.1 Develop linear Fresnel functionality within SAM. The model will take into account effects
due to ambient temperature, humidity, wind-speed and solar irradiation. The user will have the
ability to model different solar field geometries (mirror surface area per receiver, receiver design,
receiver height, receiver length, etc.), sizes, and mirror and collector properties, or alternatively,
the user can specify the desired outlet steam conditions for a generic standalone system or to be
used externally to model the performance of a solar hybrid system. The user can specify the inlet
feedwater conditions and flow rate. Other user inputs include solar field parameters, parasitic
losses, power block specifications, and financing assumptions. Default ‘typical’ inputs should be
provided for all user inputs. The model will be able to estimate plant performance for whatever
climate file the user selects. The results to be generated (and the time necessary to generate
them) should be comparable to that of other solar thermal technologies already in SAM: hourly
and annual solar field thermal output (MWht), hourly and annual electricity output (MWh),
typical financial indicators, etc. All systems are assumed to be direct steam, and both saturated
and superheated steam systems will be developed for once through and re-circulated systems. If
time and budget permits, alternative heat transfer fluids should be considered.

integrated with two-tank TES and a salt-heated steam power cycle. NREL included this model in
