Evaluating the Effectiveness of Antivirals for COVID-19 in the Post-vaccine, Omicron Era: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Laura J Edwards, Bette Liu, James G Wood, Nicola Stephens, Allen C Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) of oral antivirals for COVID-19 conducted early in the pandemic had favorable results in unvaccinated populations. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and molnupiravir against hospitalization and death in vaccinated populations in the post-Omicron era.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane library for RCTs conducted from January 2020, and observational studies in adults who were vaccinated conducted from January 2022. We performed a random effects meta-analysis using the inverse variance method with subgroup analysis by age (<65 vs ≥65 years) and study quality (excluding vs including studies at serious risk of bias).ResultsWe identified 43 studies (8 RCTs, 35 observational). Two RCTs were in vaccinated populations in which no association between treatment with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and a reduction in death, or treatment with molnupiravir and a reduction in hospitalization or death, was observed. In the meta-analysis of observational studies, nirmatrelvir-ritonavir was associated with a reduction in hospitalization (n = 11; odds ratio (OR): 0.60; 95% CI .54–.67, hazard ratio (HR): 0.69; 95% CI .62–.76) and mortality (n = 9; OR: 0.33, 95% CI .22–.48; HR: 0.56, 95% CI .36–.87). Molnupiravir was not associated with a significant reduction in hospitalization (n = 5; OR = 0.83; 95% CI .65–1.07) with mixed results for mortality (n = 9; OR = 0.61; 95% CI .41–.91; HR = 0.65; 95% CI .42–1.01).ConclusionsIn vaccinated populations, RCTs have not demonstrated a reduction in hospitalization or death among populations treated with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir or with molnupiravir. However, RCTs of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir had limited statistical power to detect clinically important differences. Observational studies of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, but not molnupiravir, consistently suggested a benefit against these outcomes.Clinical Trail RegistrationPROSPERO (CRD420251266532)

SARS-CoV-2 has been a leading contributor to the global burden of disease since it emerged in 2019, causing millions of deaths [1]. Severe outcomes are most common in older people, and people with immunosuppression or chronic medical conditions [2, 3]. Hybrid immunity introduced through vaccination and natural infection, along with evolution of the SARS-CoV-2 virus itself, has reduced morbidity and mortality from COVID-19, which went from the leading cause of death globally in 2021 to the 20th leading cause in 2023 [4].

A milestone in the management of SARS-CoV-2 was the approval of two novel antivirals for COVID-19 in December 2021 [5, 6]. Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (Paxlovid, Pfizer) and molnupiravir (Lagevrio, Merck Sharp and Dohme) both inhibit SARS-CoV-2 replication [7]. Two randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in unvaccinated participants during 2021 demonstrated that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir was 89% efficacious, and molnupiravir was 30% efficacious, at reducing hospitalization and death in people at high risk for severe disease [8, 9]. Government subsidization of both antivirals commenced during 2022 for people at high risk of severe disease in the UK, USA, Australia and many other countries [8–10].

Concerns regarding the published evidence for molnupiravir have been raised, including that the observed benefit in the MOVe-OUT trial was among participants enrolled in the first half of the study only [11], and a number of registered clinical trials of molnupiravir have not published their full study results potentially resulting in selective reporting or publication bias [12].

More recent evidence has emerged to suggest that antivirals may be less effective in vaccinated populations. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses including both RCTs and observational studies have been conducted [13–18]. However, these have been conducted across different pandemic risk periods—incorporating studies in unvaccinated and vaccinated participants during the pre- and post-Omicron periods. Notable RCTs include PANORAMIC, published in October 2022, which found molnupiravir was no more effective than placebo at reducing hospitalization or death in outpatients [19], and RECOVERY, which found no significant differences in mortality in inpatients treated with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir or molnupiravir [20].

Given the reduced severity of SARS-CoV-2 due to hybrid immunity and emergence of the Omicron variant, re-appraisal of the effectiveness of oral antivirals is needed to inform decisions about their ongoing role in the treatment of COVID-19. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and molnupiravir compared with no treatment and with each other, against hospitalization and death in vaccinated populations infected with the Omicron variant of SARS-CoV-2. We also examined results of RCTs conducted in unvaccinated and vaccinated participants regardless of the SARS-CoV-2 variant type.

METHODS

We searched for studies that examined the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and molnupiravir to prevent severe COVID-19. The review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251266532) and conducted under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.

Study Selection

We included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted from 1 January 2020 onwards regardless of the vaccination status of participants; and observational studies conducted since 1 January 2022 involving adults 18 years and over who were vaccinated. A vaccinated population was defined as one where at least 70% of participants had received their primary course (two or more doses of COVID-19 vaccination) or where the study had analyzed and reported the results for the outcomes of interest separately for vaccinated participants.

Eligibility Criteria: Exclusions

We excluded observational studies that did not have a comparator group (either no antiviral or an alternative antiviral); that did not meet the vaccination criteria described above; and studies that did not report the vaccination status of participants or the interventions or outcomes of interest. We excluded case reports, abstracts from conferences, and pre-prints.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Our database searches were current to December 2024. In PubMed, we used the MeSH terms “COVID-19”, and the supplementary concepts, “nirmatrelvir” and “molnupiravir”. In Embase we used the terms “coronavirus disease 2019/dt [Drug Therapy]”, “nirmatrelvir plus ritonavir/or nirmatrelvir/” and “molnupiravir”. In the Cochrane Library we used the search terms “nirmatrelvir” and “molnupiravir”. After identifying the RCTs we restricted the searches to 2022 onwards. We also searched the reference lists of systematic reviews to identify other studies.

Selection Process

One researcher manually screened each title and abstract to determine if it should have a full review. Following the initial screening, one researcher (LE) reviewed the full text of each remaining study to determine if it met the inclusion criteria and any uncertainties were discussed between at least two authors. A sample of studies was independently reviewed by a second author to confirm the selection process.

Data Collection Process and Data Items

One researcher (LE) manually extracted the data from the studies. Where there was any uncertainty, a second researcher independently verified the data extraction. Our primary outcomes of interest were all-cause or COVID-19 related hospitalization, and all-cause or COVID-19 related death within 7 to 90 days after the COVID-19 diagnosis date. Our secondary outcome was a composite comprising either all-cause or COVID-19 related hospitalization or death. Full details of the data extracted from each study are provided in the Supplementary materials (Appendix 1).

Risk of Bias Assessment

We assessed the quality of the observational studies using the ROBINS-I tool [21], which categorizes studies as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias. RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB 2) tool. Studies at critical risk of bias were excluded.

Effect Measures and Synthesis Methods

We combined the results for each intervention and outcome separately for RCTs and observational studies in a meta-analysis. For the observational studies, because the outcomes of interest are considered rare, we combined studies that reported relative risks and odds ratios (collectively referred to as odds ratios in the results) [21]. Studies that reported hazard ratios were analyzed separately.

Where studies reported more than one intervention or outcome of interest, each result was included, resulting in some studies appearing in multiple meta-analyses. In accordance with our inclusion criteria, if the study provided a subgroup analysis of vaccinated participants, we included it in the synthesis. Some studies provided the results for subgroups (eg, high risk, very high risk) in which case we included each analysis that met our inclusion criteria separately. Where more than one study was conducted in the same population within the same time period, we only included the study assessed as lowest risk of bias, or if the bias assessment was the same, the largest study.

For each study estimate we used the effect measure provided if this was appropriate. Where only crude numbers were provided or if the effect measure was reported in the inverse direction (eg, no treatment compared to nirmatrelvir-ritonavir), we manually calculated the effect measure. We contacted the corresponding author if information required for the meta-analysis was not reported in the publication or its Supplementary material.

The risk of bias of each study was displayed using the robvis tool [22]. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 using the meta package. Where two or more studies reported on the outcome of interest, we conducted meta-analysis using the random effects model with the inverse variance method. We assessed the heterogeneity of studies by visually inspecting the forest plots and using the i2 statistic. We interpreted the i2 statistic and associated P-values using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews for Interventions with the following interpretation: 0–40% = “might not be important”, 30–60% = “may represent moderate”, 50–90% “may represent substantial”, 75–100% = “considerable” [23].

To determine if the results were influenced by the age of participants, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of studies with an estimated median age less than and greater than 65 years. To ascertain if the results were impacted by studies at serious risk of bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses of studies at low or moderate risk of bias only. To assess the likelihood of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E values using the formula described by Van Der Weele et al [24].

Reporting Bias Assessment

To assess the risk of bias due to missing results, we created funnel plots for each intervention and outcome where there were 10 or more studies. We then assessed funnel plot asymmetry and used Egger's test to assess for small study effects.

RESULTS

Study Selection

We identified 2306 studies of which 304 were duplicates. We excluded 1797 studies through screening of titles and abstracts. This resulted in 205 studies that were assessed in full text from which 50 studies were included in the analysis. The risk of bias assessment deemed 7 studies at critical risk of bias leaving 43 studies for inclusion in the quantitative synthesis including 8 RCTs and 35 observational studies. Risk of Bias

Two RCTs were assessed to be at low risk of bias and six had some concerns. The most common reason for the concerns related to domain 2, “bias due to deviations from intended interventions” (Supplementary Figure 1). Of the observational studies, 25 were assessed to be at moderate risk of bias and 10 studies at serious risk of bias (Supplementary Figure 2).

Meta-analysis

Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir Versus no Treatment

Randomized Controlled Trials

Two RCTs of vaccinated participants assessed the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared with no treatment and neither found a reduction in mortality. These included the RECOVERY trial, in which 13 of 68 participants in the nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and 13 of 59 participants in the untreated group died (HR 1.02; 95% CI .47–2.23) [20], and a subgroup analysis of vaccinated participants in a small RCT in which 0 of 28 participants in the nirmatrelvir-ritonavir group and 1 of 38 participants in the untreated group died (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.44; 95% CI .003–62.67) [30]. Meta-analysis of the two RCTs could not be undertaken due to the different outcomes reported. No RCTs of vaccinated participants were identified that assessed the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir against hospitalization or a composite outcome. Meta-analyses of studies conducted in predominately unvaccinated participants are provided in the supplementary file (Figures 3 and 4).

Observational Studies

Hospitalization

Eleven studies assessed the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir against hospitalization with ten reporting a significant reduction (Figure 2). The pooled estimate was 0.60 (95% CI .54–.67) for the nine studies reporting odds ratios and 0.69 (95% CI .62–.76) for the two studies reporting hazard ratios. There was no evidence of substantial heterogeneity among studies reporting odds ratios or among studies reporting hazard ratios with i2 of 46.8% (P = .059) and 0.0% (P = .894), respectively.

Forest plot and meta-analysis of the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared with no treatment against hospitalization. Includes studies reporting A) odds ratio and risk ratio, and B) hazard ratios [32–34, 38, 42, 43, 46, 49, 60, 61, 64]. *Studies that defined hospitalization as “COVID-19 related”. …, event numbers not reported.

Mortality

Nine studies assessed the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir against mortality with seven of nine reporting a significant benefit. The pooled estimate was 0.33 (95% CI .22–.48) for the seven studies reporting odds ratios and 0.56 (95% CI .36–.87) for the two studies reporting hazard ratios. Heterogeneity was substantial across both analyses with i2 values of 65.6% (P = .008) for studies reporting odds ratios and 78.6% (P = .031) for studies reporting hazard ratios (Figure 3).

Forest plot and meta-analysis of the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared with no treatment against mortality with A) studies reporting odds ratios or risk ratios, and B) studies reporting hazard ratios [32, 33, 42, 46, 48, 54, 57, 60, 62]. *Studies that defined hospitalization as “COVID-19 related”. …, event numbers not reported.

Composite outcome

Eleven studies assessed a composite outcome of severe disease. In the meta-analysis, the pooled estimates were 0.62 (95% CI .48–.81) and 0.60 (95% CI .55–.65) for studies reporting odds ratios (n = 7) and hazard ratios (n = 4) respectively (Supplementary Figure 7).

Molnupiravir Versus No Treatment

Randomized Controlled Trials

There were two RCTs of vaccinated participants (PANORAMIC and RECOVERY) and neither found a reduction among those treated with molnupiravir compared with no treatment for any of the three outcomes of interest, hospitalization, mortality or a composite or both. Meta-analysis could not be performed due to the different outcomes reported.

In PANORAMIC, 0.82% (103/12 529) of participants in the molnupiravir group were hospitalized compared to 0.77% (96/12 525) in the untreated group (RR 1.07; 95% CI .81–1.42) [67]. Against mortality, 0.02% (3/12 529) of participants in the molnupiravir group died compared to 0.04% (5/12 525) of participants in the untreated group (RR 0.60; 95% CI .14–2.51) [67]. For the composite outcome, hospitalization or death was recorded in 0.8% (105/12 529) of participants in the molnupiravir group and 0.8% (98/12 525) of the participants in the comparison group (adjusted OR 1.06; 95% CI .81–1.41). In RECOVERY, 16.6% (74/445) of participants in the molnupiravir group died compared with 16.5% (79/478) in the untreated group (HR 0.93; 95% CI .68–1.28) [20].

A meta-analysis of RCTs conducted in predominately unvaccinated participants is provided in the supplementary file (Supplementary Figure 5).

Observational Studies

Hospitalization

Five studies investigated the effectiveness of molnupiravir compared with no treatment against hospitalization. The pooled estimate in the meta-analysis was 0.83 (95% CI .65–1.07) for four studies reporting odds ratios. Heterogeneity was high (i2 = 83.9%; P = .0003) (Figure 4). One study reported a hazard ratio which found no benefit (HR 0.92; 95% CI .83–1.02) [68].

Forest plot and meta-analysis of observational studies reporting effectiveness of molnupiravir against hospitalization, studies reporting odds ratio or risk ratio [45, 46, 60, 65].

Mortality

Nine studies were included in the meta-analysis of the effectiveness of molnupiravir compared with no treatment against mortality (Figure 5). The effect size differed across meta-analyses with the pooled odds ratio from five studies of 0.61 (95% CI .41–.91), and pooled hazard ratio from four studies of 0.65 (95% CI .42–1.01). There was evidence of substantial heterogeneity in both analyses with i2 of 91.4% (P < .0001) for studies reporting odds ratios and i2 = 86.2% (P < .0001) for studies reporting hazard ratios.

Forest plot and meta-analysis of observational studies reporting effectiveness of molnupiravir against mortality; A) studies reporting odds ratio or risk ratio and B) studies reporting hazard ratio [46–48, 52, 55, 58, 60, 62, 65]. *Studies that defined hospitalization as “COVID-19 related”. …, event numbers not reported.

Composite outcome

The pooled estimate for three studies that reported hazard ratios was 0.66 (95% CI .58–.75) and there was evidence of substantial heterogeneity (i2 = 68.0%; P = .044) (Supplementary Figure 8). Only one study provided results using an odds ratio (OR 1.09; 95% CI .72–1.64).

Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir Versus Molnupiravir

Randomized Controlled Trials

No RCTs were identified that compared nirmatrelvir-ritonavir with molnupiravir for the outcomes of interest.

Observational Studies

Hospitalization

Five studies compared the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir with molnupiravir against hospitalization. The three studies that reported odds ratios had a pooled effect estimate that was equivocal (OR 1.11; 95% CI .76–1.62). For the two studies that reported hazard ratios, the pooled estimate favored nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (HR 0.73; 95% CI .68–.77). The heterogeneity across the two analyses was low (i2 = 0.0, P = .865 for studies that reported odds ratios and i2 = 0.0%, P = .439 for studies that reported hazard ratios) (Supplementary Figure 9).

Mortality

The pooled hazard ratio of three studies assessing effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared with molnupiravir against mortality was 0.63 (95% CI .56–.71). Heterogeneity was low (i2 = 0.0, P = .588) (Supplementary Figure 10).

Composite outcome

The pooled estimate from two studies that assessed the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared with molnupiravir against a composite outcome was 0.76 (95% CI .59–.96). Heterogeneity was low (i2 = 0.0%, P = .374) (Supplementary Figure 11).

Subgroup Analysis

There were no material differences between results when comparing observational studies with a median age of less than and greater than 65 years (Supplementary Table 1).

Sensitivity Analysis

In the sensitivity analysis of studies at moderate risk of bias, the pooled estimate for molnupiravir compared with no treatment against mortality became non-significant for studies reporting odds ratios (Supplementary Table 2). There were no other significant differences.

The E value of each synthesis ranged from 1.46 for studies comparing nirmatrelvir-ritonavir with molnupiravir against hospitalization to 5.51 for studies comparing nirmatrelvir-ritonavir with no treatment (Supplementary Table 3).

We were unable to assess the risk of publication bias due to insufficient studies within our syntheses.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, which included two RCTs and 35 observational studies in vaccinated populations, we did not identify any RCTs that demonstrated that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir or molnupiravir reduced hospitalization or death in the Omicron era. In contrast, we identified six RCTs in predominately unvaccinated populations conducted pre-Omicron with mixed results.

Our meta-analyses of observational studies suggest a significant reduction in hospitalization and death in people treated with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir compared to no treatment but not in people treated with molnupiravir. We found evidence that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir is more effective than molnupiravir at reducing mortality and a composite outcome.

The findings from the RCTs undertaken in vaccinated populations contrast with some of the RCTs in unvaccinated populations. In RECOVERY [20], the nirmatrelvir-ritonavir arm ended early due to low recruitment with only 68 participants (compared to 445 who received molnupiravir) resulting in the study being underpowered. RECOVERY was also undertaken in hospitalized patients with a median duration of symptoms of 4–5 days before randomization occurred. Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir has been shown to have greater efficacy if taken early [69, 70], therefore the late initiation may have reduced its effectiveness.

Following completion of this systematic review, the results of two RCTs, assessing the effectiveness of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir against a composite outcome of hospitalization or death in predominately vaccinated populations were published in April 2026 (PANORAMIC and CanTreatCOVID) [71]. Among 2414 included participants nirmatrelvir-ritonavir did not reduce hospitalization or death in either of the RCTs (PANORAMIC: adjusted odds ratio, 1.18; 95% Bayesian credible interval, 0.55 to 2.62; CanTreatCOVID: adjusted odds ratio, 0.48; 95% Bayesian credible interval, 0.08 to 2.23). We estimated a pooled estimate of effect of 1.00 (95% CI .49–2.03) with low heterogeneity (i2 0.0%, P = .337 (Supplementary Figure 6). We note this is imprecise and therefore we cannot exclude a smaller treatment effect, but it is notable that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir appears not to have had any effect. The CanTreatCOVID trial was stopped early due to low recruitment and the supply of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir being discontinued, which resulted in the trial being underpowered. There was no evidence for a reduction in the composite outcome in any of the subgroup analyses (by age, duration of symptoms or comorbidities) but one secondary outcome, early sustained recovery, was shorter in people who were treated.

Our findings for observational studies of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir are consistent with other systematic reviews that have found nirmatrelvir-ritonavir to be effective against hospitalization and death, including an umbrella review [13, 16, 72, 73]. Two systematic reviews included a subgroup analysis by vaccination status and found nirmatrelvir-ritonavir reduced severe outcomes of COVID-19 among both vaccinated and unvaccinated participants with one review finding a smaller effect size in vaccinated participants [13, 72].

Our findings for molnupiravir are consistent with other systematic reviews that have concluded the evidence for a reduction in hospitalization or death is insufficient or of low certainty [13, 14, 74–78]. A Cochrane Systematic Review of RCTs comparing molnupiravir with no treatment concluded that molnupiravir probably results in little to no difference in all-cause mortality and may result in little to no difference in rates of hospitalization [79]. Subgroup analysis by vaccination status was not able to be performed due to insufficient information. Our finding that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir is more effective than molnupiravir at reducing mortality and a composite outcome is consistent with two other systematic reviews [15, 80].

In addition to the low numbers of participants in some RCTs, which resulted in the studies being underpowered to detect a clinically meaningful result, there are other potential explanations for the differences between the results of RCTs and observational studies for both molnupiravir and nirmatrelvir-ritonavir. These include (1) differences in population risk profiles, with some RCTs, such as PANORAMIC, conducted in lower risk patients with milder disease in whom antivirals may be less effective; (2) selection bias, such as the severity of symptoms, or healthcare seeking behavior; and (3) differences due to uncontrolled or residual confounding, which may have occurred in the observational studies.

There are several potential explanations for the heterogeneity in our meta-analyses, including study size, variations in the study populations (eg, age group), outcome definitions (eg, COVID-19 related or all-cause), and the length of follow up, which ranged from 14–90 days.

Many of the observational studies adjusted for the most likely confounders, including age, sex, comorbidities, immunosuppression and socioeconomic status. Other potential confounders including the severity of symptoms, recent infection, reinfections and the timing of treatment were more inconsistently reported and adjusted for. The E values associated with our results for nirmatrelvir-ritonavir were all over 2.5 suggesting that a strong unmeasured confounder would need to be present to fully explain the observed effect. For molnupiravir, the E values ranged from 1.7 to 2.7 indicating that a relatively smaller degree of confounding could explain the results.

A strength of our systematic review is its conduct in the Omicron era in vaccinated populations therefore providing more contemporary practice-relevant data. By excluding duplicate studies within the same population at the same time, and studies at critical risk of bias, we have reduced the likelihood of biased results.

There were several limitations to our study. We could not analyze studies according to the type of vaccine, the number of doses or the timing of vaccination because it was not consistently reported. Several studies have found that people who access treatment are more likely to be up to date with vaccinations [81, 82], therefore it is possible that our results from observational studies were biased in favor of antivirals, although some studies used statistical methods to adjust for vaccinations. Finally, in conducting the systematic review, only one researcher performed the searches and extracted the data; however, a selection of studies was reviewed by a second researcher to confirm the study selection and data quality.

We were only able to compare the results for studies with a median age older or younger than 65 years. Finer age stratification in the available data, such as for the highest risk groups aged 75+ years [83] would be useful to clarify the groups most likely to benefit from use of antivirals. Most studies in this review included participants at risk of severe disease, which aligned with the eligibility criteria for access to COVID-19 antivirals. However, the definition of high risk was not consistent, which limits assessment of the risk factors or chronic conditions that are most likely to benefit. Assessing antiviral effectiveness across target groups and in people with specific risk factors, such as people with immunosuppression, would support refinement of clinical guidelines for the use of COVID-19 antivirals.

In the pre-Omicron era, the number needed to treat (NNT) from RCTs in unvaccinated populations to prevent one hospitalization or death was approximately 18 for nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and 35 for molnupiravir [26, 27]. Using our pooled estimate from observational studies of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir effectiveness, and the reported case hospitalization rate and mortality rate from UK Omicron-era data from November 2023 to March 2024 among adults aged 75 years and over (3.28% and 0.725%, respectively) [84], the number needed to treat (NNT) with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir to prevent one hospitalization and one death is 76 and 204, respectively. For adults 65 to 74 years with an estimated case hospitalization risk of 0.686% and a mortality risk of 0.107%, we estimate the NNT to prevent one hospitalization or death is 364 and 1394, respectively. These estimates demonstrate that the potential benefits of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir are not equally distributed across the population and continue to change as hospitalization and mortality decline over time. They highlight the relevance for cost effectiveness analyses and future policy decisions.

CONCLUSION

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found consistent evidence from observational studies conducted in vaccinated populations in the post-Omicron era that nirmatrelvir-ritonavir reduces severe outcomes of COVID-19, but this was not supported by the limited RCT evidence therefore suggesting a low certainty of benefit. A contemporary RCT would strengthen the certainty and support updated clinical management guidelines and policymaker decisions. However, clinical equipoise and the feasibility of recruiting sufficient participants to demonstrate a benefit poses challenges now that COVID-19 related hospitalization and death is less common. Our findings for molnupiravir, taken together with the recent Cochrane meta-analysis, raise questions regarding its ongoing place in the treatment of COVID-19.
