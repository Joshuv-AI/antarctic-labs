Sexual health behaviors and contraceptive practices among Canadian childhood, adolescent, and young adult cancer survivors and controls
Authors: Ismay A. E. de Beijer, Selina R. van den Oever, Oluwaseyi A. Lawal, Amanda Button, Jenny Duong, Heide Adham, Jennifer Giles, Kathleen Reynolds, Fiona S. M. Schulte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSexual health, central to overall well‐being, remains under assessed in childhood, adolescent and young adult (CAYA) cancer survivorship. This study examined sexual health outcomes among CAYA cancer survivors using repeated assessments collected as part of routine survivorship care and compared these outcomes with population‐based controls.MethodsSurvivors completed one to six assessments on sexual activity, concerns about sex life, and contraceptive and condom use. Controls included age‐matched Canadian Community Health Survey respondents. Multilevel and Bayesian models quantified predictors of sexual health outcomes whereas latent class growth analysis tested for high‐risk subgroups.ResultsAmong survivors (n = 305; median age, 21.9 years; 49.8% male), 47.5% were not sexually active, compared with 11.2% of controls (unweighted n = 108,252; χ 2 = 404.35; p < .001; odds ratio [OR], 7.18; 95% Confidence Interval [CI], 5.73–8.99). Being in a relationship predicted sexual activity (Bayesian OR, 1806.83; 95% Credible Interval [CrI], 255.08–20,535.08). Condom use was less frequent among survivors than controls (45.6% vs. 27.2%; χ 2 = 27.48; p < .001; OR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.42–1.99) and negatively associated with age (OR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.26–0.74) and relationship status (OR, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.03–0.24). Not using birth control was similar between survivors and controls (26.9% vs. 25.8%; χ 2 = 0.10; p = .758). Within survivors, older age (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.20–0.85) and higher treatment intensity (OR, 0.02; 95% CI, 0.00–0.47) were negatively associated with birth control use.ConclusionsCAYA cancer survivors demonstrate less sexual activity and condom use than controls. Higher treatment intensity is associated with less birth control use, suggesting potential misaligned fertility perceptions. Survivorship programs should integrate structured sexual health counselling.

INTRODUCTION

With significant advances in pediatric oncology, survival rates for childhood, adolescent, and young adult (CAYA) cancer have improved markedly over the past several decades. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 As a result, CAYA cancer survivors represent a growing population with complex and evolving health care needs. The focus of survivorship care and research has increasingly shifted to understanding and managing the long‐term and late effects of CAYA cancer and its treatment. 5 For example, CAYA cancer survivors are at increased risk for a variety of late health problems, such as endocrine dysfunction, cardiovascular disease, and psychological distress. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Sexual health is a key component of overall well‐being and encompasses physical, emotional, mental, and social aspects of sexuality. 15 Disruptions in sexual health may have lasting implications for quality of life, identity development, and intimate relationships, especially during the transition from adolescence to adulthood. 16 CAYA cancer survivors may be particularly vulnerable to challenges in this domain due to both biological and psychosocial mechanisms. Cancer therapies can affect endocrine and reproductive function, 17 , 18 whereas the timing of diagnosis and treatment during critical developmental periods may disrupt psychosexual development. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 In addition, visible or perceived changes in physical appearance resulting from treatment, such as scarring or altered body morphology, can contribute to body image concerns and diminished self‐esteem, which in turn can affect the ability to initiate or maintain romantic and sexual relationships. 23 , 24 , 25

Previous research on sexual health, including the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, the St. Jude Lifetime Cohort Study, and national cohorts such as the Dutch Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, has demonstrated impairments in sexual functioning and psychosexual development 21 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 in CAYA cancer survivors. However, several knowledge gaps remain. First, much of the existing literature relies on cross‐sectional or single follow‐up assessments, limiting insight into how sexual health presents across survivorship. Second, although sexual functioning has been a central focus of prior studies, survivorship research has devoted comparatively less attention to behavioral outcomes, including sexual activity, contraceptive practices, and condom use, despite their direct implications for unintended pregnancy and sexually transmitted infection risk. Third, little evidence exists on how treatment‐related factors may influence sexual health behaviors in ways that can directly inform clinical decision‐making, including identifying subgroups of survivors who may require tailored counseling. A recent systematic review further highlights the limited availability of longitudinal and behaviorally oriented sexual health data in the CAYA cancer survivor population. 30 Understanding sexual health behaviors in this population has direct implications for survivorship care, as these behaviors are modifiable and represent key targets for preventive counseling and intervention. The present study aims to examine sexual health outcomes among Canadian CAYA cancer survivors using repeated assessments collected as part of routine survivorship care. Specifically, we aim to describe the prevalence of sexual activity, concerns about sex life, and contraceptive and condom use across follow‐up assessments, and compare these outcomes with those of age‐matched individuals from the general Canadian population. Furthermore, we evaluate sociodemographic and cancer‐related predictors, including treatment intensity, to identify subgroups that may benefit from targeted sexual and reproductive health counseling.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Participants were CAYA cancer survivors enrolled in the long‐term survivorship clinic at the Alberta Children's Hospital in Calgary, Alberta, Canada. Inclusion criteria were a cancer diagnosis before the age of 21, at least 2 years off active treatment, and at least 15 years of age at the time of the study, as this is the age at which survivors were first asked to self‐report data on their sexual health.

Study design and procedure

Data were collected between July 2021 and July 2025 as part of routine clinical care. All survivors were asked to complete the Long‐term Survivorship Questionnaire (LTSQ) to assess their current health status before each clinic appointment. Those who did not complete the questionnaire received up to two reminders, or were asked to do so during their clinic visit. Survivors attend the clinic once every 6 months, once a year, or once every 2 years, as determined by a risk‐based stratification model. The complete LTSQ and program have been described elsewhere. 30 The LTSQ received ethical approval from the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta Cancer Committee (HREBA.CC‐19‐0498; Edmonton, AB, Canada). All participants gave informed consent for their data to be used for research purposes.

Study measures

Outcomes of interest included sexuality (heterosexual/homosexual/bisexual/prefer not to answer/prefer to self‐describe); being sexually active (yes/no); concerns about sex life (yes/no); use of contraception with a partner in the past 3 months (always/more than half/half/less than half/never); and use of condoms with a partner in the past 3 months (always/more than half/half/less than half/never). Demographic and clinical characteristics included age at the time of appointment, sex at birth, gender identity, education, employment status, relationship status, cancer diagnosis, treatment modalities, treatment intensity level according to the Intensity of Treatment Rating Scale 3 (ITR‐3) classification system (levels 1–4), 31 cancer recurrence, age at diagnosis, and time since diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics and the prevalence of sexual health outcomes at the participants’ first and last measurements. Additionally, we compared those with one measurement with those with at least two measurements. Next, we compared sexual activity, birth control use, and condom use with age‐matched respondents from the publicly available 2019–2020 Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS; www23.statcan.gc.ca). We used survivors’ first LTSQ measurement to approximate the 2019–2020 CCHS timeframe. In the CCHS, sexual activity was defined as answering “yes” to “Have you ever had sex? Please include vaginal and anal sex.” Because of differences in how items were formulated across the LTSQ and the CCHS, we focused on not using birth control and condoms. Not using birth control was defined as a “Never” response in the LTSQ and a “Yes” response to the CCHS questions “What other methods of protection did you and your partner use the last time you had sex?—Withdrawal or pulling out” and “What other methods of protection did you and your partner use the last time you had sex?—None.” Likewise, participants who answered “Never” to the LTSQ question about condom use were classified as not using condoms, whereas all other response categories (“Less than half the time,” “Half the time,” “More than half the time,” and “Always”) were classified as condom use. For CCHS participants, answering “No” to the question “Did you or your partner use a condom the last time you had sex?” was classified as not using condoms. All variables were harmonized into binary yes/no indicators for both groups (see the harmonization scheme Supporting Information S1). We conducted complete cases comparisons between survivors and controls using χ 2 tests with survey weights. We could not compare concerns about sex life due to the absence of equivalent CCHS data. Last, we could not restrict controls to respondents from Alberta and British Columbia only because most of their data on the relevant CCHS questions were missing.

To examine associated factors, we employed mixed‐effects modeling and Bayesian alternatives to evaluate the robustness of the findings. The measured demographic and clinical characteristics were included in all models as covariates. Sexual activity and concerns about sex life were modeled as binary outcomes using generalized mixed models. Model diagnostics were evaluated using simulation‐based residuals, including tests for dispersion and zero‐inflation. For birth control use and condom use, we employed cumulative link mixed models with a logit link, treating the response as ordered categorical. All models included random intercepts for participants to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Parallel Bayesian models used an analogous structure with weakly informative priors on all fixed and random effects. For the concerns about sex life model, analyses were restricted to participants who were sexually active. Because no participants in the lowest treatment group reported any concerns, the treatment intensity variable was collapsed into “low” (levels 1–2) and “high” (levels 3–4) to address the issue of separation. Model assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics for frequentist models and posterior predictive checks for Bayesian models. For binary models, we examined dispersion and the distribution of predicted probabilities, and for ordinal models, we verified the proportional odds assumption.

Finally, we performed latent class growth analysis to identify subgroups at elevated risk of concerns regarding their sex lives. However, no meaningful subgroups were identified, likely due to low event rates and limited power (see detailed analysis in Supporting Information S2). We employed SPSS (version 30) and R (version 4.0.3) for all statistical analyses, using a level of α = 0.05 to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Study population

Table 1 shows the participants characteristics at their first measurement (N = 305). Participants completed between one and six LTSQ measurements, with only one participant contributing six measurements. The median number of measurements per participant was two, and 57 participants provided data from a single measurement only. The mean time interval between measurements was 13.3 months (SD = 5.00), with a median of 12.2 months (range, 1.3–40.5). The sample was nearly equally distributed between individuals assigned male (49.8%) and female (50.2%) at birth. In terms of gender identity, 49.2% identified as male, 49.5% as female and 1.0% as another gender (0.3% was missing). One participant did not provide information on their gender identity. Most participants identified as heterosexual (83.6%), with smaller proportions identifying as bisexual (5.6%), homosexual (2.3%), or having another sexual orientation (1.6%). Twenty participants (6.6%) did not want to disclose their sexual orientation.

The median age at cancer diagnosis was 7.4 years (interquartile range [IQR], 3.1–13.2), and the median age at first measurement was 21.9 years (IQR, 17.3–26.0). The median time since diagnosis was 15.9 years (IQR, 11.3–20.6). The most common diagnosis was leukemia (38.0%), followed by solid tumors (34.4%), lymphoma (19.7%), and brain tumors (7.9%). Nearly all participants received chemotherapy (96.1%), whereas 62.3% underwent surgery and 36.1% received radiotherapy. Treatment intensity levels included 3.9% (level 1), 30.5% (level 2), 36.1% (level 3), and 17.7% (level 4), with 11.8% of data missing. In addition, 9.8% of participants experienced cancer relapse.

In terms of education, 23.3% had graduated from a post‐secondary or university program, 21.0% had completed some post‐secondary or university education, and 12.1% had completed high school. Educational data at were missing for 36.7% of participants. At the time of their first assessment, 46.2% were employed, and 34.1% reported being in a relationship.

Participants with multiple measurements were, on average, further from their time of diagnosis (p = .013) as compared to those with only one measurement (see all results in Supporting Information S3). Significant differences were also observed in the distribution of gender identity, sexual orientation, cancer diagnosis, highest level of education achieved, employment status, relationship status, and history of surgery (all p < .001).

Participants with two or more measurements were older at first assessment (Mean [M] = 22.08; SD = 5.34) than those with a single measurement (M = 18.78, SD = 3.40; p = .027) and had a longer time since diagnosis (M = 16.36, SD = 6.49 vs M = 13.98, SD = 6.50 years; p = .013). The groups also differed in gender identity distribution (p = .003), with a higher proportion of participants identifying as female in the two or more measurement group (52.0% vs. 33.3%) and more missing data in the single‐measurement group (see analysis details in Supporting Information S3). Furthermore, the two or more measurement group included a slightly higher proportion of leukemia (38.7% vs. 31.6%), lymphoma (19.4% vs. 17.5%), and solid tumor survivors (34.7% vs. 31.6%), but a lower proportion of brain tumor survivors (7.3% vs. 8.8%) compared with the single‐measurement group (p < .001). Last, the two or more measurement group had a higher proportion of surgical treatment (62.5% vs. 56.1%; p < .001) compared to the single measurement group.

Prevalence of sexual health outcomes

Table 2 shows the sexual health outcomes at the initial and most recent measurements. The proportion of participants who reported being sexually active increased slightly over time, from 52.5% to 56.4%. Among those who were sexually active, concerns about their sex life were uncommon, with 7.5% reporting this at the initial measurement and 7.0% at the most recent one. Furthermore, 46.9% reported consistent use of birth control compared to 47.7% at the most recent follow‐up. Meanwhile, 26.9% and 30.2% reported never using birth control. Adherence to condom use was lower, with 34.4% and 28.5% reporting “always” use at the first and last follow‐up measurements, respectively, and 45.6% and 48.8% reporting “never” use, respectively. Among participants not in a relationship, consistent condom use was higher, with 56.3% and 45.7% reporting “always” use at the two time points, whereas 25.0% and 28.6% reported “never” using condoms. Comparison with controls of the Canadian general population

Significant differences were observed between childhood cancer survivors (n = 305) and respondents from the 2019–2020 CCHS (unweighted n = 108,252; weighted to represent the Canadian population) in terms of sexual activity as well as the use of birth control and condoms (Table 3). Compared with controls, survivors were over seven times more likely to report not being sexually active (47.5% vs. 11.2%; χ 2(1) = 404.35; p < .001; Odds ratio [OR] = 7.18; 95% Confidence Interval [CI], 5.73–8.99). Among those who were sexually active, the proportion of birth control use was similar (26.9% for survivors vs. 25.8% for controls; χ 2(1) = 0.10; p = .758). In contrast, 45.6% of survivors reported not using condoms, compared with 27.2% of controls. This difference was statistically significant (χ 2(1) = 27.48; p < .001), with survivors showing 1.68 times higher odds of not using condoms than controls (OR = 1.68; 95% CI, 1.42–1.99).

Risk factors

Sexual activity

Being in a relationship was associated with a large increase in log‐odds of being sexually active (estimate = 24.12; p < .001; OR ≈ 3.00 × 1010; 95% CI, 7.10 × 107–1.20 × 1013; Bayesian posterior mean log‐odds = 7.55; OR, 1806.83; 95% Credible Interval [CrI], 255.08–20,535.08). Other covariates were not significantly associated with sexual activity. Random effects indicated substantial between‐participant variability in baseline likelihood of being sexually active (frequentist SD = 25.45; Bayesian SD = 4.33; 95% CrI, 2.79–6.20), with additional variability across repeated measurements within individuals. Model diagnostics reflected the strong association between relationship status and sexual activity, resulting in highly distinct predicted probabilities.

Birth control use

Older age was associated with lower odds of birth control use (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.20–0.85; p = .021; Bayesian OR, 0.36; 95% CrI, 0.12–0.92). Furthermore, higher treatment intensity was associated with lower odds of birth control use, with the OR for level 4 treatment = 0.02 (95% CI, 0.00–0.47; p = .018; Bayesian OR, 0.01; 95% CrI, 0.00–0.53). Other associations were not significant. Random effects revealed substantial between‐participant variability (frequentist SD = 2.49; Bayesian SD = 3.44; 95% CrI, 2.21–5.20).

Condom use

Older age was associated with lower odds of condom use (OR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.26–0.74; p = .005; Bayesian OR, 0.41; 95% CrI, 0.20–0.75). In addition, participants in a relationship had lower odds of condom use than those not in a relationship (frequentist OR, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.03–0.24; p < .001; Bayesian OR, 0.09; 95% CrI, 0.03–0.24). Survivor sex and treatment intensity showed no reliable association with condom use. Random effects pointed to meaningful between‐participant variability (frequentist SD = 2.22; Bayesian SD = 2.75; 95% CrI, 1.99–3.68).

Concerns about sex life

There were no significant associations in the risk factor models with sex life concerns.

DISCUSSION

This study examined sexual health outcomes among CAYA cancer survivors using repeated assessments collected as part of routine survivorship care and compared these outcomes with population‐based controls. Almost half of survivors reported no sexual activity, with a likelihood of sexual inactivity that was more than seven times higher than in the general population. Being in a relationship was a strong predictor of being sexually active. Additionally, sex life concerns were low across measurements. Among survivors who were sexually active, condom use was significantly less frequent than in the control group. Also, those in a relationship and those of older age were less likely to use condoms. Although there were no significant differences in birth control use frequency between survivors and controls, we found that older survivors and those who underwent more intensive treatment were less likely to report frequent use of birth control. Our finding that survivors are significantly less likely to be sexually active than their peers in the general population aligns with previous literature. 27 , 28 This likely reflects a constellation of medical and psychosocial factors. Survivors often experience delayed psychosocial maturation resulting from years of medicalization, chronic health complications, and persistent concerns about body image and fertility. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Among male survivors, in particular, exposure to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery involving the reproductive organs is associated with impaired spermatogenesis, testosterone deficiency, and physical sexual dysfunction. 32 In female survivors, a history of pelvic surgery or hypogonadism (both as a result of cancer therapy) is associated with a higher risk of overall sexual dysfunction. 27 In turn, physical limitations and/or impaired sexual functioning can cause considerable distress and anxiety in survivors, 27 , 33 who may consequently postpone or avoid sexual experiences altogether. This highlights the importance of taking a holistic approach to sexual health counselling in both medical as well as psychosocial survivorship care. Reported concerns about sex life were infrequent across assessments. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Concerns were assessed using single‐item, dichotomized measures that likely lack sensitivity to capture the multidimensional nature of sexual well‐being. In addition, social desirability bias and discomfort discussing sexual concerns in a clinical context may have contributed to underreporting. Qualitative research has shown that active cancer patients and survivors often desire more open and informed communication about sexual and reproductive health, both to receive general education and guidance on how to manage treatment‐related changes and long‐term risks. 34 Yet, these discussions are often prevented from happening because survivors are reluctant to initiate conversations, are uncertain about the best time to talk, and because family members are present during consultations. Patients and survivors have identified several strategies to improve these discussions, including initiating the conversation proactively, providing private time without family members, normalizing sexual health as part of routine care, maintaining open dialogue throughout treatment and survivorship, tailoring communication to individual needs, and addressing survivors directly. 34 Future research should focus on multi‐method designs that combine quantitative data with qualitative interviews, to better capture the complexity of survivors' experiences of sexuality and intimacy.

Condom use was significantly less frequent in sexually active survivors than in the general population. Relationship status strongly predicted condom use, suggesting that survivors in relationships often discontinue condoms with or without adopting alternative contraceptive methods. Notably, even among sexually active survivors who were not in a relationship, one in four reported never using a condom in the previous 3 months. This pattern highlights the need for counselling that addresses sexually transmitted infections and unintended pregnancies, as it raises concerns about the increased risk of these issues.

The prevalence of birth control use in the current study is consistent with a review of four studies reporting contraceptive use between 47% and 84%. 35 Lower birth control use in older survivors may reflect planned pregnancy or completed family building. However, survivors who underwent the most intensive cancer treatment were also significantly less likely to use birth control. This may reflect a perceived loss of fertility. Although fertility perceptions and intentions were not directly measured in this study, they could be potentially reinforced by prior information about gonadotoxic risks during treatment, leading to the belief that use of contraceptives is unnecessary. Such assumptions can be unsafe if infertility has not been medically confirmed and with regard to the risk of sexually transmitted infections. A German study indeed demonstrated that only 13% of young adult cancer survivors had received a fertility assessment, even though more than half said they were interested in having their fertility tested. 36 Relatedly, only 56% of cancer survivors of reproductive age in the United States reported receiving counseling, prescriptions, or procedures related to birth control since their cancer diagnosis. 37 A systematic review from the International Late Effects of Childhood Cancer Guideline Harmonization Group (IGHG) revealed that health care providers often lack knowledge of or are unfamiliar with relevant fertility preservation guidelines. 38 This limits discussion about reproductive health and may contribute to the uncertainty about contraceptive use among CAYA cancer survivors. Evidence‐based counseling that addresses both fertility awareness and contraceptive options is therefore essential to enable survivors to make informed decisions about their reproductive health. Incorporating brief, structured screening questions into routine survivorship visits may facilitate identification of individuals who would benefit from tailored counseling on pregnancy prevention and sexually transmitted infection risk. Specifically, treatment intensity may serve as a useful stratification factor in survivorship care, with higher intensity exposures potentially signaling a need for more targeted counseling regarding fertility uncertainty and contraceptive decision‐making. The IGHG guideline on communication and ethical considerations for fertility preservation provides additional directions for implementing such counseling in clinical practice. 38

This study is subject to several limitations. First, self‐reported outcomes increase the potential risk of social desirability bias and recall bias. Second, participants were recruited from a single specialized survivorship clinic and may represent individuals more engaged in care, potentially limiting generalizability.

Third, the combination of heterogeneous follow‐up intervals and the limited number of repeated observations per participant restricts the ability to draw conclusions about changes over time. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as patterns across repeated assessments rather than as longitudinal trajectories. Relatedly, the observed differences between those with only one measurement versus those with at least two measurements requires that the findings of our study be interpreted with caution, as they may have limited generalizability. Fourth, sexual activity was defined based on penetrative sex, and therefore does not capture the full spectrum of sexual behaviors. Relatedly, the dichotomization of contraceptive and condom use for comparison with population‐based data may have reduced sensitivity to detect inconsistent use, which may still represent clinically meaningful risk. Last, we applied survey weights to compare prevalence with the CCHS control data. Variance estimation was conducted under the assumption of simple random sampling, which is a conservative approach and may have reduced the precision of the resulting estimates. Future work should consist of longitudinal designs with standardized follow‐up intervals, larger samples, and validated multidimensional sexual health instruments to enhance generalizability and enable more detailed examination of longitudinal sexual health outcomes.

In conclusion, this study shows that sexual activity and contraceptive use are less prevalent among Canadian CAYA cancer survivors than among their noncancer peers. Sexual activity and condom use were strongly associated with relationship status, whereas less frequent birth control use was associated with exposure to more intensive cancer treatment. These findings highlight the importance of addressing sexual and reproductive health as an integral part of survivorship care. Clinicians should be supported and encouraged to initiate open and personal conversations about sexual activity, intimacy concerns, and contraception. Implementing evidence‐based guidelines and training programs that promote patient‐centered communication can help ensure survivors receive the necessary information and support to make informed and confident decisions regarding their sexual and reproductive well‐being.
