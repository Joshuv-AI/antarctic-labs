Drag-induced skin effect in a Bose-Fermi mixture
Wenjie Liu, Ching Hua Lee, Yi Qin

Abstract
The non-Hermitian skin effect (NHSE) represents one of the most distinctive phenomena in non-Hermitian physics. Here, we uncover a new drag-induced NHSE mechanism in interacting Bose–Fermi mixtures where only bosons and not fermions experience asymmetric hoppings. We show that strong Bose–Fermi interactions enable fermions to inherit boundary accumulation through correlated bound states. The interplay of interactions, quantum statistics, and non-Hermitian dynamics gives rise to an interaction-induced blockade mechanism, leading to highly asymmetric fermionic transport. We demonstrate that the drag-induced NHSE is dynamically stable and propose a feasible realization in ultracold Bose–Fermi mixtures with Floquet-engineered asymmetric tunneling. Our results establish a general interaction-mediated mechanism for emergent non-Hermitian localization in hybrid quantum matter.

These advances motivate a basic yet unexplored question in the non-Hermitian regime:
Can a species that is intrinsically impervious to the NHSE acquire non-Hermitian localization through its interaction with another species that is sensitive to the NHSE?

In this work, we investigate and uncover a drag-induced NHSE mechanism in a one-dimensional Bose–Fermi mixture: bosons exhibit NHSE due to asymmetric tunneling, while fermions—Hermitian in isolation—inherit boundary accumulation through interspecies interaction.
Starting from the minimal one-boson–one-fermion problem, we show a sharp dichotomy between bound and scattering sectors: only the bound composite displays correlated skin localization, consistent with an effective strong-coupling description.
We then move to the few-body regime (e.g., two bosons and two fermions) and demonstrate that fermionic statistics can cooperate with drag in selected energy clusters but can also suppress it, yielding an interaction-induced blockade mechanism with striking dynamical consequences, including directional control of fermionic propagation.
Finally, we show that the drag-induced skin effect is dynamically stable and outline a feasible cold-atom implementation based on Floquet engineering of nonreciprocal tunneling and tunable Bose–Fermi interactions.

Model and formalism
.—

We consider a one-dimensional Bose–Fermi mixture governed by the Hamiltonian

H=H_{b}+H_{f}+H_{bf},

(1)

which comprises bosonic (
H_{b}
), fermionic (
H_{f}
), and interspecies
interaction (
H_{bf}
) terms.
The bosonic sector is described by a dimerized lattice with non-reciprocal inter-cell tunneling:

\displaystyle H_{b}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{L/2}\left(t_{0}\,b_{2j-1}^{\dagger}b_{2j}+\text{H.c.}\right)+\frac{U_{bb}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n_{j}^{b}(n_{j}^{b}-1)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{L/2-1}\left(t_{L}\,b_{2j}^{\dagger}b_{2j+1}+t_{R}\,b_{2j+1}^{\dagger}b_{2j}\right),

(2)

where
t_{0}
denotes the intra-cell (Hermitian) hopping amplitude,
while
t_{L}
and
t_{R}
introduce asymmetric (non-Hermitian) inter-cell tunneling that underlies the bosonic skin effect.
The fermionic component features uniform hopping and nearest-neighbor interactions:

H_{f}=-\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\left(t_{0}\,c_{j+1}^{\dagger}c_{j}+t_{0}\,c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}\right)+U_{ff}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}n_{j}^{f}n_{j+1}^{f}

(3)

with
t_{0}
also serving as the fermionic hopping strength and
U_{ff}

representing nearest-neighbor interactions.
The two species are coupled via Bose-Fermi interactions:

H_{bf}=U_{bf}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n_{j}^{b}n_{j}^{f},

(4)

where
n_{j}^{b}=b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}
and
n_{j}^{f}=c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}
.
This model represents a minimal yet experimentally realistic setting for studying interaction-mediated non-Hermitian physics. Bose–Fermi mixtures naturally provide tunable interspecies interactions, while non-reciprocal hopping can be engineered through Floquet protocols, making the present setup directly accessible in ultracold-atom experiments. As schematically depicted in Fig.
1
(a), bosons experience asymmetric hopping and exhibit the NHSE, whereas fermions remain intrinsically Hermitian. This raises a natural question: can non-Hermitian localization be transferred across species through interactions alone? While an intuitive picture may suggest an affirmative answer, the actual situation is considerably more subtle. As we show below, the transfer of the NHSE depends sensitively on the structure of the correlated many-body states: it occurs in strongly bound Bose–Fermi composites but is absent in scattering states. Moreover, quantum statistics and interaction-induced kinetic constraints can partially or even completely hinder this transfer, leading to blockade effects and strongly asymmetric fermionic transport.

One boson and one fermion
.—

To illustrate the most basic mechanism behind interaction-driven non-Hermitian physics in Bose-Fermi mixtures, we begin with the minimal case of a single boson and a single fermion – as we show later, subtleties appear when we consider more interacting channels.
We begin in the strong-coupling regime, taking
t_{0}=0.2
,
t_{L}=0.5
,
t_{R}=1.5
and
U_{bf}=-160
, such that
|U_{bf}|
is the largest energy scale in the problem. In this limit, Bose–Fermi bound states are expected to be energetically well separated from scattering states, allowing a transparent identification of interaction-mediated skin localization and providing a controlled setting for comparison with the effective strong-coupling theory developed below.
The energy spectrum of this hybrid system is shown in Fig.
1
(b).
Despite the non-Hermitian nature of the bosonic sector, the full spectrum remains real—reflecting the existence of an equivalent Hermitian representation (see Sec.I of Supplemental Materials). Nevertheless, the non-unitary nature of the mapping leads to qualitatively different eigenstate localization and dynamical behavior, which underlie the drag-induced skin effect studied here.
A sharply isolated band appears at
E\approx-160
, signaling a nearly stable bound pair formed by strong boson–fermion interaction. In contrast, a continuum of scattering states occupies the energy window
E
around zero.
The degree of spatial correlation between the two particles is quantified by the correlation function

C_{n}=\sum_{j}\langle\psi_{n}|n_{j}^{b}n_{j}^{f}|\psi_{n}\rangle

where
C_{n}=1
indicates perfect co-localization on the same site, while
C_{n}=0
corresponds to uncorrelated, extended distributions. As shown in Fig.
1
(c), the deep-bound state (
E_{n}\approx-160
) exhibits
C_{n}\approx 1
, confirming a tightly bound composite, whereas states near
E_{n}\approx 0
yield
C_{n}\approx 0
, reflecting weak correlations.
This dichotomy is directly visualized in the density profiles of Figs.
1
(d) and (e).
In the bound regime [Fig.
1
(d)], the boson displays a pronounced NHSE—its density accumulates sharply at the right edge due to asymmetric tunneling. Remarkably, the fermion, though governed by Hermitian dynamics, inherits this localization through strong
U_{bf}
coupling, resulting in a correlated “drag-induced” skin effect. The inset highlights the near-perfect spatial overlap of both densities for representative bound states.
Conversely, in the scattering band [Fig.
1
(e)], the bosonic density remains right-localized, but the fermionic density is nearly uniform across the lattice. This decoupling arises because scattering states involve weak effective interactions: the fermion retains its intrinsic delocalized character and does not respond to the bosonic skin accumulation.
Importantly, the drag-induced NHSE persists beyond the strong-coupling regime and continuously evolves into a correlation-assisted localization effect at weaker interactions (see Sec. II of the Supplemental Materials).

The effective Hamiltonian in the bound-state subspace can be obtained via a strong-coupling expansion (see Sec. III of the Supplemental Materials). Up to an overall energy shift and weak boundary corrections, the dynamics is described by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=2\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{U_{bf}}\sum_{j}\left(\hat{S}_{2j-1}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{2j}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle+2\frac{t_{0}}{U_{bf}}\sum_{j}\left(t_{L}\,\hat{S}_{2j}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{2j+1}+t_{R}\,\hat{S}_{2j+1}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{2j}\right)

where
\hat{S}_{j}^{\dagger}
creates a bound boson–fermion composite at site
j
.
The effective inter-cell hoppings remain asymmetric, being proportional to

t_{L}t_{0}/U_{bf}
and
t_{R}t_{0}/U_{bf}
, respectively. Consequently, the bound composite inherits the non-reciprocity of the bosonic sector and exhibits the same skin accumulation under open boundary conditions.

Dynamical stability of the drag-induced skin effect
.—

Having established the emergence of a drag-induced skin effect through boson–fermion correlations, we now examine its dynamics. Fig.
2
shows the time evolution of bosonic and fermionic densities for two distinct initial conditions: a bound pair [Figs.
2

By contrast, for scattering initial states, while bosons still accumulate at the right edge due to their intrinsic non-Hermitian dynamics, fermions spread symmetrically in a light-cone pattern characteristic of Hermitian ballistic transport. This dichotomy mirrors the static spectral structure shown in Fig.

To quantify the fermionic skin response, we introduce the mean density imbalance

\Delta n_{f}=\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{L/2}\left(\bar{n}^{f}_{L-j+1}-\bar{n}^{f}_{j}\right),

(6)

where
N_{f}
denotes the total number of fermions in the system, and
\bar{n}_{j}^{f}=\langle\psi_{n}|n_{j}^{f}|\psi_{n}\rangle
is the average fermion density at site
j
, evaluated over eigenstates within the bound band. A large positive
\Delta n_{f}
indicates strong right-edge accumulation, while
\Delta n_{f}\approx 0
corresponds to delocalized fermions.
The parameter dependence of
\Delta n_{f}
is shown in Figs.
2
(e) and (f).
Fig.
2
(e) shows that the drag-induced skin effect strongly depends on the Bose–Fermi interaction strength
U_{bf}
.
For weak interactions,
\Delta n_{f}
remains small, indicating that the fermions largely retain their intrinsic Hermitian character. As
|U_{bf}|
increases,
\Delta n_{f}
rapidly grows and saturates in the strong-coupling regime, reflecting the formation of tightly bound boson–fermion composites that efficiently inherit the bosonic skin localization. The weak dependence on
t_{0}
further indicates that, once the bound states are established, the drag-induced skin effect is governed primarily by the Bose–Fermi interaction strength and the bosonic non-reciprocity.

By contrast, Fig.
2
(f) shows that the non-reciprocal hopping amplitude
t_{R}
acts as the primary driving mechanism for the drag-induced skin effect. Increasing
t_{R}
strongly enhances
\Delta n_{f}
, leading to pronounced fermionic boundary accumulation inherited from the bosonic NHSE. Together, these results show that the drag-induced skin effect requires both sufficiently strong Bose–Fermi correlations and finite non-reciprocal bosonic hopping.
The blank regions in Figs.
2
(e) and (f) correspond to parameter regimes where the bound and scattering sectors strongly hybridize, such that no sufficiently isolated bound band can be defined and
\Delta n_{f}
ceases to be well defined.
Additional results over an extended interaction regime are presented in Sec. IV of the Supplemental Materials, which further demonstrate the crossover from weak correlation-assisted localization to the strong-coupling drag-induced skin effect.

Band hierarchy due to boson-fermion statistics and non-Hermitian interplay
—

Having established the interaction-induced drag skin effect in the minimal one-boson–one-fermion setting, we now explore richer physics emerging in a few-body regime where quantum statistics become decisive.
We consider a system with two bosons and two fermions.
Fig.
3
(a) displays the full energy spectrum—ordered by increasing
E
—alongside the eigenstate-resolved boson–fermion correlation function.
The spectrum organizes into ten distinct bands, each exhibiting a characteristic value of
C_{n}
.
High-
C_{n}
bands correspond to states where strong interspecies interaction enforces local boson–fermion binding, while low-
C_{n}
bands reflect spatial segregation driven by interparticle interactions and statistics.
While the band hierarchy is largely dictated by interactions and quantum statistics, non-Hermiticity controls how different correlated configurations respond to the bosonic skin drive. This interplay leads to band-dependent drag-induced localization and, in certain configurations, interaction-induced blockade effects.

Figs.
3
(b)–(k) show the bosonic and fermionic density distributions for each of these ten bands, ordered by energy.
The faint curves represent the density profiles of individual eigenstates within each band, while the thick curves with markers denote the corresponding band-averaged densities.
This visualization not only captures the average localization tendency of each band, but also reveals the internal state-dependent structure and configuration fluctuations within the band.

In the lowest-energy bands (I–II), all particles tend to cluster: typically, both bosons and one fermion occupy the same site, while the second fermion resides on a nearest- (Band I) or more distant (Band II) neighbor site.
Middle-energy bands (III–IV) feature one tightly bound boson–fermion pair, with the remaining particles forming secondary pairs at adjacent or farther sites.
Higher bands (V–VIII) display more intricate arrangements—for instance, a localized boson–fermion dimer coexisting with unpaired particles on non-overlapping sites (Bands V, VII), or two bosons co-localized with two fermions on neighboring sites (Bands VI, VIII).
In the highest-energy bands (IX–X), fermions localize on adjacent or separated sites, while bosons avoid fermion-occupied regions entirely.
Notably, in Band VI, the density distribution is such that two bosons localize at the same lattice site, while the two fermions occupy the two nearest-neighbor sites excluding the one occupied by the bosons.
This configuration becomes particularly transparent from the individual eigenstate density profiles, where the spatial avoidance between bosons and fermions can be directly identified.
An interaction-induced blockade emerges from the interplay among boson–boson, fermion–fermion, and boson–fermion interactions, which collectively stabilize a localized many-body configuration. This correlated configuration effectively forms a many-body domain wall separating distinct occupation sectors. Due to fermionic statistics and kinetic constraints, certain transport channels become strongly suppressed, leading to highly asymmetric fermionic dynamics and contributing to the drag-induced skin effect.

These interaction-driven correlations, combined with statistical constraints, contribute to distinct localization behaviors. The transition from tight co-localization to extended spatial segregation highlights the emergent complexity inherent in boson–fermion mixtures.
Such interaction-dependent localization underscores the necessity of band-specific analysis, as single-boson and single-fermion paradigms fail to capture the multifaceted spatial organization induced by multicomponent interactions and particle statistics.
The inclusion of individual eigenstate density profiles further demonstrates that states within the same band can still exhibit distinct microscopic configurations, even when sharing similar average correlations and energies.

Blockade Effect and Its Dynamical Consequences
.—

Our few-body analysis reveals a striking interaction-induced
blockade effect
. To illustrate this, Fig.
4
shows the time evolution of bosonic and fermionic densities for two representative product-state configurations motivated by the occupation ordering associated with Band VI in Fig.
3
. We set
t_{L}=0.2
and
t_{R}=0.5
(all other parameters identical to Fig.
3
) to activate the non-Hermitian skin drive.

Due to the asymmetric tunneling, the bosons are driven toward the right edge, resulting in a pronounced non-Hermitian skin effect, as shown in Figs.
4
(a) and (c).
Specifically, Figs.
4
(a) and (b) show the case in which the bosons are initially positioned to the left of the fermions
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|2_{b}\right\rangle_{j}\left|1_{f}\right\rangle_{j+4}\left|1_{f}\right\rangle_{j+5}
. In this configuration, both species exhibit a rightward skin effect: the bosons are driven to the right due to asymmetric hopping, while the fermions, though Hermitian, are pulled along via the strong boson-fermion interaction
U_{bf}=-160
. This co-propagation reflects the drag-induced localization mechanism discussed above.
In contrast, when the initial state has the bosons positioned to the right of the fermions
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|1_{f}\right\rangle_{j}\left|1_{f}\right\rangle_{j+1}\left|2_{b}\right\rangle_{j+2}
, the bosons continue to exhibit rightward skin propagation, while the fermions display asymmetric behavior, primarily propagating to the left [Figs.
4
(c) and (d)].
This striking reversal arises from the interplay between the interaction blockade and the directionality of the non-Hermitian drive. The effect of
U_{bf}
dynamically stabilizes a localized many-body domain that separates distinct occupation configurations. Due to fermionic statistics and the associated kinetic constraints, certain right-moving fermionic channels cannot efficiently penetrate or reconstruct this moving domain and therefore become strongly suppressed. As a result, the fermions predominantly propagate in the opposite direction.

This directional control of fermionic transport, mediated entirely by the spatial arrangement of the non-Hermitian bosonic component, highlights the nontrivial role of interactions in shaping quantum dynamics. In stark contrast, for the case of
U_{bf}=0
, all fermions exhibit symmetric light-cone spreading, as shown in Sec.VI of Supplemental Materials, confirming that the observed asymmetry is interaction-driven.
These results demonstrate that the blockade effect is not merely a static feature of the energy eigenstates, but also has profound dynamical consequences: it enables interaction-mediated control over the propagation direction of otherwise Hermitian particles. This provides a mechanism for engineering non-reciprocal dynamics in hybrid quantum systems, in which the non-Hermiticity of one species can be harnessed to manipulate another through strong correlations.

can be engineered through density-assisted tunneling or Raman dressing in optical lattices
Ozawa et al. 2019
.

The non-Hermitian skin effect—manifested as boundary-localized particle accumulation—is directly detectable via quantum gas microscopy
Bakr et al. 2009
;
Sherson et al. 2010
, which provides single-site resolution of
\langle n_{j}^{b}\rangle
and
\langle n_{j}^{f}\rangle
.
This setup offers a highly controllable platform to observe interaction-mediated non-Hermitian phenomena and explore correlated skin effects in mixed quantum matter.

Conclusion and discussion
.—
We have investigated the interplay of interactions and statistics in few-body Bose–Fermi mixtures on one-dimensional lattices. Our study reveals rich band-dependent localization behaviors, arising from the combined effects of boson–boson, fermion–fermion, and boson–fermion interactions. In low-energy bands, particles tend to co-localize, forming tightly bound clusters, whereas in higher bands, bosons and fermions exhibit extended spatial segregation. We show that fermions acquire a non-Hermitian skin effect through their interaction with bosons, a phenomenon we term the drag-induced skin effect.
We further identify a unique interaction blockade mechanism, in which the interplay among boson–boson, fermion–fermion, and boson–fermion interactions generates a localized many-body domain wall that kinetically constrains fermionic motion and freezes selected transport channels, thereby inducing strongly asymmetric fermionic dynamics and a drag-induced skin effect. Dynamical simulations further confirm the dynamical stability of the drag-induced skin effect and demonstrate that the interaction blockade restricts fermion propagation, resulting in asymmetric transport. These findings underscore the necessity of a band-resolved analysis for multicomponent systems and highlight how many-body interactions and quantum statistics conspire to produce complex spatial organization and emergent dynamical phenomena in Bose–Fermi mixtures.

Furthermore, we emphasize that the drag-induced skin effect is not intrinsically suppressed with increasing particle number, but rather depends on the interplay between particle number, system size, and many-body state structure.
For instance, in the three-boson–three-fermion case (see Sec.VIII of Supplemental Materials), the motion of tightly bound composites arises from higher-order virtual processes (e.g., sixth-order in perturbation theory), leading to a reduced effective hopping amplitude. Nevertheless, the non-reciprocal mechanism inherited from the bosonic sector remains intact.
The relatively weak localization observed in small systems (e.g.,
L=10
) should therefore be understood as a finite-size effect, where the reduced effective hopping competes with the limited spatial extent of the system. In larger systems or at lower filling fractions, the directional accumulation is expected to become more pronounced.
These results indicate that the drag-induced skin effect persists in many-body settings, with its observability governed primarily by system size and density.

Mapping the Non-Hermitian Model to a Hermitian Counterpart

The non-Hermitian nature of the Hamiltonian arises from the asymmetric
inter-cell hopping amplitudes
t_{L}\neq t_{R}
in the bosonic sector. Under open
boundary conditions, and provided that
t_{L}/t_{R}>0
, this non-Hermitian
Hamiltonian can be mapped to a Hermitian counterpart by a non-unitary
similarity transformation. This implies that the Hamiltonian is
pseudo-Hermitian and hence possesses a real spectrum
Mostafazadeh 2002
.
Importantly, the existence of such a Hermitian counterpart does not make the
skin effect trivial. The similarity transformation is non-unitary and therefore
changes the spatial structure of eigenstates and physical observables such as
density profiles and non-equilibrium dynamics. The drag-induced NHSE discussed
in the main text is thus encoded in the transformed eigenstates and observables,
rather than in complex eigenvalues.

We introduce the cell-dependent similarity transformation

\tilde{H}=\eta H\eta^{-1},

(S1)

with

\eta=\exp\left[\kappa\sum_{m=1}^{L/2}m\left(n^{b}_{2m-1}+n^{b}_{2m}\right)\right],\qquad\kappa=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{t_{L}}{t_{R}}\right).

(S2)

This transformation is well-defined for
t_{L}/t_{R}>0
, for which
\kappa
is real
and
\eta
is Hermitian, positive definite, and invertible. It acts on the
bosonic operators as

\displaystyle\eta b_{2m-1}\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa m}b_{2m-1},

\displaystyle\eta b^{\dagger}_{2m-1}\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa m}b^{\dagger}_{2m-1},

(S3)

\displaystyle\eta b_{2m}\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa m}b_{2m},

\displaystyle\eta b^{\dagger}_{2m}\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa m}b^{\dagger}_{2m}.

(S4)

The bosonic number operators are invariant,

\eta n^{b}_{j}\eta^{-1}=n^{b}_{j}.

(S5)

The fermionic operators are also unchanged because
\eta
acts only on the
bosonic sector.

We now transform the bosonic hopping terms. The intra-cell hopping is unchanged:

\displaystyle\eta\left[-t_{0}\sum_{m=1}^{L/2}\left(b^{\dagger}_{2m-1}b_{2m}+b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m-1}\right)\right]\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=-t_{0}\sum_{m=1}^{L/2}\left(b^{\dagger}_{2m-1}b_{2m}+b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m-1}\right),

(S6)

because the two sites within the same unit cell carry the same scaling factor.
For the inter-cell hopping, one obtains

\displaystyle\eta\left[-\sum_{m=1}^{L/2-1}\left(t_{L}b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m+1}+t_{R}b^{\dagger}_{2m+1}b_{2m}\right)\right]\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=1}^{L/2-1}\left(t_{L}e^{-\kappa}b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m+1}+t_{R}e^{\kappa}b^{\dagger}_{2m+1}b_{2m}\right).

(S7)

Using
e^{\kappa}=\sqrt{t_{L}/t_{R}}
, we have

t_{L}e^{-\kappa}=t_{R}e^{\kappa}=\sqrt{t_{L}t_{R}}.

(S8)

Therefore, the transformed inter-cell hopping becomes Hermitian:

-\sqrt{t_{L}t_{R}}\sum_{m=1}^{L/2-1}\left(b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m+1}+b^{\dagger}_{2m+1}b_{2m}\right).

(S9)

Since all density operators are invariant under
\eta
, the boson–boson
interaction and the Bose–Fermi interaction remain unchanged:

\displaystyle\eta\left[\frac{U_{bb}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}(n^{b}_{j}-1)\right]\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{bb}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}(n^{b}_{j}-1),

(S10)

\displaystyle\eta\left[U_{bf}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}n^{f}_{j}\right]\eta^{-1}

\displaystyle=U_{bf}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}n^{f}_{j}.

(S11)

The fermionic Hamiltonian is also invariant because the transformation acts
only on bosons.

Combining these terms, the transformed Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{b}

\displaystyle=-t_{0}\sum_{m=1}^{L/2}\left(b^{\dagger}_{2m-1}b_{2m}+b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{t_{L}t_{R}}\sum_{m=1}^{L/2-1}\left(b^{\dagger}_{2m}b_{2m+1}+b^{\dagger}_{2m+1}b_{2m}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{U_{bb}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}(n^{b}_{j}-1),

(S12)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{f}

\displaystyle=H_{f},

(S13)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{bf}

\displaystyle=U_{bf}\sum_{j=1}^{L}n^{b}_{j}n^{f}_{j}.

(S14)

Thus,

\tilde{H}=\tilde{H}_{b}+\tilde{H}_{f}+\tilde{H}_{bf}

(S15)

is Hermitian for real
t_{0}
,
U_{bb}
,
U_{ff}
,
U_{bf}
, and
t_{L}t_{R}>0
.
Consequently, the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian has a real spectrum under
open boundary conditions, while its right eigenstates exhibit non-unitary
spatial reshaping responsible for the non-Hermitian skin effect.

We separate the hybrid system into two parts with the dominant part
H_{bf}

and the perturbation one
H_{b}
+
H_{f}

The domination

\mathcal{V}

The subspace

(S17)

(S18)

The zero-order perturbation calculation is

(S19)

The first-order perturbation calculation is zero.

VII
Floquet Engineering of Non-Reciprocal Tunneling

In static Hermitian systems, microscopic reversibility enforces symmetric tunneling amplitudes (
t_{L}=t_{R}
). To realize the asymmetric hopping
t_{L}\neq t_{R}
required for the NHSE within a conservative cold-atom platform, we employ
Floquet engineering
—a technique that uses periodic driving to generate effective Hamiltonians with tailored complex-valued or non-reciprocal couplings.

A concrete implementation utilizes a bichromatic optical lattice subjected to a time-periodic phase modulation. Consider a 1D lattice potential of the form

V(x,t)=V_{0}\cos^{2}(kx)+V_{1}\cos^{2}\!\big(kx+\phi(t)\big),

(S22)

where the relative phase is modulated as
\phi(t)=\omega t
, with driving frequency
\omega
. In the high-frequency limit (
\omega\gg t_{0},U
), the system is described by an effective time-independent Floquet Hamiltonian obtained via the Magnus expansion
Bukov et al. 2015
. To leading order, the nearest-neighbor tunneling acquires a complex Peierls-like phase:

t_{j,j+1}^{\rm eff}=t_{0}\,e^{i\theta},\quad t_{j+1,j}^{\rm eff}=t_{0}\,e^{-i\theta},

(S23)

with
\theta\propto(V_{1}/V_{0})\sin(\omega t)
averaged over one period. While this yields Hermitian dynamics with reciprocal magnitude (
|t_{L}|=|t_{R}|
), true non-reciprocity in amplitude can be achieved by combining phase modulation with an additional energy offset (e.g., a linear potential gradient) or by driving multiple internal states. Through the Magnus expansion, Floquet engineering has also been proposed to generate effective many-body interactions
Lee et al. 2018
.

DRAG-INDUCED SKIN EFFECT AT HIGHER PARTICLE NUMBERS

To investigate the behavior of the drag-induced skin effect at larger particle numbers, we analyze a system with three bosons and three fermions (

These results indicate that the drag-induced skin effect persists at higher particle numbers, with its observability controlled by system size and density rather than being fundamentally suppressed.
