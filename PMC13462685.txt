Rapid acceleration of ice-cover loss from Northern Hemisphere lakes above critical air temperature thresholds
Authors: Jian Zhou, Weijia Wang, Yaru Ma, Linwang Yuan, Changchun Huang, Kun Shi, Peter R. Leavitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIce cover is a fundamental regulator of northern lake ecosystems, yet the specific thermal thresholds that trigger abrupt phenological changes have remained elusive. We demonstrate that lake-ice sensitivity to atmospheric warming is not only asymmetrical, with spring thaw being far more volatile than autumn freeze, but also undergoes a rapid nonlinear acceleration. We identify critical winter temperature thresholds that, once breached, cause phenological responsiveness to increase by more than an order of magnitude. Our results reveal that many mid-latitude lakes are currently transitioning into this state of extreme vulnerability. This finding provides a mechanistic framework for predicting threshold-dependent cryospheric loss and underscores the need to incorporate nonlinear thermal responses into climate impact assessments to safeguard global inland water integrity.

As more than half of Earth’s approximately 117 million lakes freezing annually (1), lake ice constitutes a pivotal component of the global cryosphere and serves as a sensitive metric (aka Essential Climate Variable) for tracking planetary warming (2). Recent decades have seen widespread and accelerating reductions in ice extent and duration (3, 4), with the most acute losses occurring over the last quarter-century (5). This shift manifests as delayed freeze up, advanced break up, and an increasing frequency of years where a growing subset of basins fails to freeze entirely (1, 3). The vulnerability of these systems is heightened by their geographic distribution, as nearly 87% of lakes larger than 10 ha are concentrated between 45°N and 75°N (6). Because this latitudinal band has experienced both magnitude and rate of warming approximately twice the global average (7, 8), the spatial overlap between high lake density and intense polar amplification disproportionately exposes Northern Hemisphere ecosystems to rapid cryospheric transformation (9). This heightened risk necessitates a rigorous and mechanistic characterization of lake-ice sensitivity to thermal forcing to identify high-risk ecosystems and inform effective conservation and adaptation strategies (10, 11).

Shifts in ice phenology fundamentally reorganize lake thermal stratification, nutrient availability, and the timing of biological productivity (9, 12). Earlier ice-off and later ice-on dates alter seasonal energy budgets, light penetration, and dissolved oxygen (13), which subsequently shift the phenology of phytoplankton blooms and greenhouse gas exchange (14). Prolonged open-water conditions enhance solar irradiance absorption and modify the magnitude and timing of water evaporation (12). Recent evidence indicates that ice cover does not merely act as a physical barrier to evaporation, but rather that evaporation rates are governed by the complex interplay between ice-free duration and the air–water vapor pressure gradient (15). These physical alterations amplify summer water-column warming, with cascading consequences for water quality and ecosystem services (12). Beyond these ecological shifts, lake ice supports critical transportation, subsistence fishing, and cultural identities for millions, meaning the loss of reliable ice poses a profound threat to human livelihoods (9, 16). A mechanistic and spatially explicit understanding of how lake ice responds to atmospheric warming is therefore essential for anticipating these multifaceted impacts (17).

Although solar irradiance supplies the primary energy required for ice decay (18), the interannual variation of these radiative fluxes is relatively limited (19). Consequently, regional air mass dynamics and associated temperature anomalies act as the principal drivers of ice phenology (10, 20). While local air temperature (AT) governs much of the interannual variation in ice-on and ice-off dates (21), substantial spatial heterogeneity remains among lakes within the same climatic zone (22). This divergence is mediated by large-scale atmospheric circulation patterns that modulate regional wind-induced water-column mixing and snow insulation, interacting with lake-specific attributes such as surface area and depth (23, 24). Small, shallow lakes typically exhibit rapid thermodynamic responses to thermal forcing (20), yet deep lakes in specific regions can display comparable or even heightened sensitivity to warming (1, 24, 25). This physical complexity highlights the limitations of traditional linear climate-ice models (10, 21). Because current predictive frameworks frequently rely on linear assumptions, they risk overlooking abrupt, threshold-like transitions (14). Resolving these nonlinear phenological sensitivities is therefore critical to identifying the specific thermal thresholds that trigger rapid, systemic shifts in lake-ice cover (26).

Here, we analyzed long-term (2000–2022) in situ and remote sensing observations of ice phenology from 724 lakes across the Northern Hemisphere to quantify the sensitivity of ice-cover phenology to changes in AT. Our analysis accounts for local climate variables, lake geography, morphology, hydrology, and watershed characteristics, and anthropogenic influences. Because lake-ice dynamics are fundamentally rooted in the physics of phase transitions, we hypothesize that phenological sensitivity will exhibit nonlinear patterns governed by threshold dynamics of atmospheric energy exchange (22, 27–29). Under such conditions, even modest warming may trigger disproportionate or accelerating rates of ice loss as systems approach critical thermal limits. By mapping these sensitivities and projecting future trajectories, we aim to identify high-risk ecosystems and provide a refined, policy-relevant framework for conservation and adaptation planning.

Results

Sensitivity of Lake-Ice Phenology to AT Change.

Analysis of 724 Northern Hemisphere lakes spanning 28°N to 68°N from 2000 to 2022 reveals that lake-ice phenology exhibits a marked and asymmetrical sensitivity to AT. We quantified phenological sensitivity as the shift in days per degree Celsius of annual mean AT change. Sensitivity was calculated using linear regressions between six ice metrics (initial ice on, complete freeze up, initial break up, complete ice off, complete ice-cover duration, and partial ice-cover duration) and annual hydrological AT (here defined as the period from September 1 to August 31 of the following year). The proportion of lakes exhibiting significant responses to increased AT varied with the parameter, ranging from 22.9% for metrics of ice formation to 66.1% for those related to ice off melting (P < 0.05; SI Appendix, Table S1). Across the Northern Hemisphere, rising AT predominantly resulted in delayed dates of initial ice on (83.0%) and complete freeze up (87.8%), alongside advanced dates of initial break up (94.2%) and complete ice off (98.7%), leading to a near-universal contraction of ice-cover duration (Fig. 1A). Quantitatively, phenological sensitivity followed a distinct hierarchical gradient where duration metrics were significantly more responsive than were timing metrics (P < 0.001; Fig. 1B). Mean sensitivities for initial ice on and complete freeze up were 2.52 ± 3.09 and 2.45 ± 2.64 d °C−1, respectively (Fig. 1B). In contrast, ice-decay metrics exhibited significantly higher sensitivities (P < 0.001), with initial break up at −3.76 ± 3. 04 d °C−1 and complete ice off at −4.62 ± 2.28 d °C−1 (Fig. 1B). These shifts culminated in the highest sensitivities for complete and partial ice-cover durations, which shortened by −6.92 ± 4.99 and −7.13 ± 4.37 d °C−1 (Fig. 1B).

Paired comparison of ice formation and loss dynamics within individual lakes confirmed that the heightened sensitivity of ice loss to atmospheric warming is a robust and widespread feature across the lacustrine cryosphere (Fig. 1C). The magnitude of sensitivity for complete ice off and initial break up exceeded that of initial ice on and complete freeze up in 76.9% and 69.1% of the examined systems, respectively (Fig. 1C). On average, the sensitivity of complete ice off was 1.77 ± 3.14 d °C−1 greater than that of initial ice on, while the sensitivity of initial break up surpassed that of complete freeze up by 1.51 ± 3.12 d °C−1 (Fig. 1C). This pronounced disparity between the onset and termination of the ice season underscores an asymmetrical vulnerability of lake-ice phenology to atmospheric forcing, suggesting that spring warming exerts a disproportionately stronger influence on the annual ice cycle than does autumn cooling.

The spatial distribution of a lake’s phenological sensitivity to warming exhibited a pronounced latitudinal gradient across the Northern Hemisphere (Fig. 1 D–I). Sensitivities generally declined as latitude increased (P < 0.001), a pattern that was particularly robust for the duration of complete ice cover (Spearman’s r = −0.463, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We identified high-sensitivity regions primarily within the mid-latitude temperate and southern boreal zones of North America and Europe (Fig. 1 D–I). Lakes in these regions showed the most marked phenological responses to warming, with substantial delays in ice formation and rapid advances in ice loss (Fig. 1 D–I). Conversely, sensitivities were markedly lower in high-latitude Arctic and Subarctic basins (Fig. 1 D–I). This spatial heterogeneity suggests that lakes situated near the southern limit of seasonal ice cover are more vulnerable to atmospheric forcing.

Acceleration of Lake-Ice Sensitivity Beyond Thermal Thresholds.

Given the fundamental role of winter conditions in governing the annual ice cycle (23, 30), we quantified the relationship between phenological sensitivity of ice cover to atmospheric warming and mean winter AT (Fig. 2). Our analysis revealed that the sensitivity of all ice events increased significantly and nonlinearly as winter temperatures rose (P < 0.001; Fig. 2). By applying piecewise linear regression to identify significant inflections in the relationship between ice phenological sensitivity and winter AT, we identified critical thermal thresholds ranging from −13.7 °C to −6.8 °C across the six phenological metrics (Fig. 2). Specifically, these breakpoints occurred at −10.5 °C for initial ice on and −13.7 °C for complete freeze up (Fig. 2 A and B), while the thresholds for ice loss were notably warmer at −6.8 °C for initial break up and −8.8 °C for complete ice off (Fig. 2 C and D). The duration metrics exhibited similar thresholds near −8.8 °C for complete ice cover and −8.6 °C for partial ice cover (Fig. 2 E and F).

Beyond these thermal limits, the sensitivity of ice phenology to warming intensified sharply, signaling a threshold-dependent acceleration in the cryospheric response to climate change (Fig. 2). The rate of sensitivity change after the inflection point was substantially higher than the rate observed under colder conditions, with this divergence being most extreme for ice loss processes (Fig. 2). The sensitivity of initial break up and complete ice off to warming increased by factors of 22.2 and 17.7, respectively, following the breach of their thermal thresholds, whereas initial ice on and complete freeze up showed more moderate increases of 4.4- and 3.5-fold (Fig. 2 A–D). Such nonlinear behavior implies that even marginal increases in winter AT can trigger disproportionately large shifts in the annual ice cycle once these critical thresholds are surpassed, with the spring thaw being particularly susceptible to abrupt transformation.

Drivers of Lake-Ice Phenology Sensitivity to Warming.

Pairwise correlation analysis revealed that climatic variables, particularly AT, are the primary correlates of phenological sensitivity of lacustrine ice cover across the Northern Hemisphere (Fig. 3). Annual and seasonal AT metrics (except summer AT) exhibited strong and consistent associations with all phenological shifts (P < 0.05; Fig. 3), indicating that lakes situated in warmer baseline climates generally display significantly higher sensitivities to further atmospheric warming (Figs. 1 D–I and 2). Geographic factors such as latitude and radiative variables including forecast albedo (FAL) and surface solar radiation downward (SSRD) also showed significant correlations with the sensitivity of ice events (Fig. 3). Conversely, local environmental factors such as lake morphological (e.g., depth and volume) and hydrological characteristics (e.g., water residence time and discharge) displayed comparatively weak or nonsignificant relationships with phenological sensitivity (P > 0.05; Fig. 3).

Random forest models further elucidated the hierarchical importance of these drivers and explained a substantial portion of the observed variation in the sensitivity of ice cover to warming, with cross-validation R2 values reaching as high as 0.55 for the sensitivity of partial ice cover (Fig. 3). Winter AT emerged as the most influential predictor across nearly all metrics (Fig. 3), a result which reinforces the observed nonlinear acceleration of lake-ice sensitivity to warming as basins approach critical thermal thresholds (Fig. 2). Forecast albedo also ranked as a primary predictor, particularly for the sensitivity of initial break up and complete ice off (Fig. 3), which highlights the significance of surface reflectivity feedbacks in regulating ice-melt dynamics. Although anthropogenic factors such as human footprint and population density contributed to the predictive performance for specific metrics, their influence remained secondary to that of climatic forcing (Fig. 3). Overall, the predominant predictive power of winter AT and surface albedo suggests that the transformation of the lacustrine cryosphere is fundamentally driven by broad-scale energy balance shifts that effectively supersede the physical features of individual lakes to offset effects of atmospheric warming (e.g., heat storage capacity).

Projections of Lake-Ice Phenology under Future Climate Change.

To assess the spatiotemporal evolution of lake-ice cover through the end of the 21st century, we applied a sensitivity-based framework that integrates historical phenological responsiveness with site-specific warming projections across four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). These projections were forced by downscaled near-surface AT outputs from four high-resolution global climate models within the NASA Earth Exchange dataset. Projections of lake-ice phenology reveal that the future state of the lacustrine cryosphere will be fundamentally determined by the selected mitigation pathway (Fig. 4). A pronounced divergence in phenological trends becomes evident after 2050, where shifts under sustainable and intermediate-emission scenarios such as SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5 are projected to stabilize by mid-century (Fig. 4 A–F). In contrast, the high-emission pathways (SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5) lead to a continued and accelerating transformation of the ice cycle with no signs of stabilization by 2100 (Fig. 4 A–F). ANOVA confirms that the magnitude of phenological shifts is significantly lower under stringent mitigation scenarios than under the high-emission trajectories of SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 (P < 0.05; SI Appendix, Fig. S2). For instance, under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, initial ice on and complete freeze up are projected to be delayed by 13.2 ± 15.5 and 12.9 ± 13.8 d by the end of the century, whereas the timing of initial break up and complete ice off is expected to advance by 22.5 ± 18.4 and 26.9 ± 14.5 d, respectively (Fig. 4 A–D). This cumulative effect leads to a profound erosion of the ice season, with complete and partial ice-cover durations each estimated to decline by approximately 40 d (Fig. 4 E and F), resulting in suggesting many lakes with currently reliable ice-cover transitioning toward intermittent or even ice-free states under unmitigated warming.

Hemispheric maps demonstrate pronounced geographic heterogeneity in the response of lake ice to projected atmospheric warming (Fig. 4 G–L), identifying a consistent pattern of vulnerability across all scenarios (0.80 < Spearman’s r < 0.996, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Under SSP3-7.0, the most substantial phenological shifts are expected to occur within low-latitude boreal and temperate regions, with particularly severe disruptions centered around the Baltic Sea and Fennoscandia in Europe as well as the Great Lakes region and southern boreal belt of North America (Fig. 4 G–L). In these mid-latitude hotspots, the convergence of high intrinsic ice sensitivity to warming and significant regional AT increases creates a synergistic effect that accelerates phenological disruption. Conversely, high-latitude and high-altitude regions are projected to experience more attenuated changes because their colder baseline temperatures provide a greater thermal buffer against immediate ice loss (Fig. 4 G–L). These findings identify temperate and southern boreal lake systems as global centers of vulnerability, facing disproportionate ecological risks as they are pushed toward unprecedented thermal regimes that may fundamentally destabilize their winter ecosystems.

Future projections further indicate a pronounced increase in the proportion of lakes crossing critical thermal thresholds by the end of the century, signaling a widespread transition toward heightened sensitivity to atmospheric forcing (Fig. 5). By applying threshold criteria derived from inflection points in the relationship between ice sensitivity and winter AT, we quantified the expansion of these vulnerable populations across a range of emission pathways (Fig. 5). From a historical baseline of 23.3 ± 1.3%, the fraction of highly sensitive lakes (here defined as lakes cross critical thermal thresholds of mean winter AT) is projected to rise substantially, reaching 28.6 ± 13.9% under SSP1-2.6 and escalating to 69.6 ± 16.0% under the extreme SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 5 A–D). When averaged across all phenological metrics, the mean proportion of highly sensitive systems increases progressively from 27.1 ± 13.0% under the lowest emission scenario to 40.3 ± 15.3% under the highest, underscoring a systemic shift toward strongly diminished ice regimes (Fig. 5 E–J). Complete freeze up displayed the largest fraction of highly sensitive lakes, ranging from 55.2% under SSP1-2.6 to 98.5% under SSP5-8.5 (Fig. 5 A–D). In contrast, the timing of initial break up consistently represented the most stable metric with the smallest fraction of sensitive lakes (Fig. 5 A–D). The widespread transition of lakes into these high-sensitivity categories implies that the lacustrine cryosphere is moving toward a state where even minor incremental warming will trigger disproportionately large and potentially irreversible changes in ice-cover duration and stability.

Discussion

Our hemispheric analysis of 724 lakes reveals that the lacustrine cryosphere is characterized by an asymmetrical vulnerability to atmospheric warming where ice-decay processes are significantly more responsive to changes in AT than are ice-formation events (Fig. 1). We identified clear thermal thresholds for lake-ice response to changing winter AT ranging from −13.7 °C to −6.8 °C across phenological metrics beyond which ice sensitivity accelerates nonlinearly by as much as 22-fold (Fig. 2). These threshold-dependent responses are primarily driven by broad-scale thermal and radiative regimes, particularly winter AT and surface albedo, rather than by localized lake morphology (Fig. 3). Spatially these dynamics identify mid-latitude temperate and southern boreal regions as global hotspots of ice loss risk (Fig. 4). Future projections indicate that the trajectory of lake ice will diverge sharply based on the future pattern of greenhouse gas emissions, with the proportion of lakes crossing critical thermal thresholds potentially escalating from 23% to nearly 70% by 2100 under high-emission scenarios (Fig. 5). Such widespread transits of critical thresholds imply that the annual ice cycle is moving toward a state of heightened volatility where even marginal warming could trigger abrupt and potentially irreversible regime shifts across the Northern Hemisphere (12).

The heightened thermal sensitivity of ice decay compared to ice formation is a robust feature of the lacustrine cryosphere (3, 31–33), a pattern reinforced by our random forest models which identify winter AT and forecast albedo as the primary drivers of phenological responsiveness (Fig. 3). While ice formation is fundamentally constrained by the heat storage capacity (thermal inertia) of the water column (31, 33), requiring sustained heat loss to reach the freezing point (25, 33), ice decay is governed by a more dynamic interplay of radiative and thermodynamic processes (34). In spring, the onset of melt is rapidly amplified by positive feedbacks, particularly the reduction in surface albedo and the subsequent increase in solar radiation absorption, which our mechanistic analysis highlights as a critical predictor of the sensitivity of initial break up and complete ice off (4). This radiative feedback mechanism, coupled with the thinner ice characteristic of warmer winters, renders the termination of the ice season significantly more susceptible to atmospheric forcing than is its initiation (31, 35, 36). These hemispheric findings are consistent with lake-specific studies which show that timing of ice melt is more sensitive to changes in AT than are dates of ice formation (3, 31). For example, Vavrus et al. (37) found that ice off advanced by 5.6 d per 1 °C warming whereas ice on was delayed by only 3.9 d °C−1. When scaled to the Northern Hemisphere, these findings show that future erosion of ice-cover duration will be driven primarily by earlier spring melt, with profound implications for subsequent lake thermal structure, mixing regimes and ecosystem functions (5, 38).

Our analysis reveals distinct atmospheric temperature thresholds for ice formation and loss, beyond which sensitivity to warming accelerates sharply (Fig. 2). These nonlinear responses are governed by the combined thermodynamic and structural properties of the ice cover (22, 27–29). In persistently cold climates, extended subzero temperature facilitates the formation of thick, clear ice with low porosity that effectively resists mid-winter warming (16). As winter AT approaches the identified thresholds, the duration of cold conditions limits the depth of ice growth, resulting in thinner, more porous white ice formed under snow cover (9, 18). This ice structure exhibits reduced mechanical strength and lower thermal resistance, making it vulnerable to rapid decay (20, 39). The melt threshold also reflects a fundamentally different regime (29, 31) where end-of-season ice is thinner and less dense, lowering the latent heat required to initiate melting (40, 41). Furthermore, the early production of meltwater on the surface of white ice increases heat transfer efficiency, as liquid water possesses higher heat capacity and thermal conductivity than the atmosphere (4, 39). This lens accelerates conductive and convective melting from both the surface and the ice–water interface (4, 31). Thinning ice and reduced albedo after surface snow melt also amplify heating under ice, while ponded meltwater enhances shortwave energy capture (31, 42). Once porosity exceeds critical levels (0.3 to 0.5%), the ice cover weakens rapidly and fractures under moderate wind stress, triggering abrupt decay (34). This threshold-dependent behavior, driven by broad-scale energy balance shifts rather than localized basin traits, underscores the potential for rapid, nonlinear regime shifts in the lacustrine cryosphere as warming continues.

Our projections indicate that the future state of the lacustrine cryosphere is fundamentally dependent on the emissions pathway taken by humanity (Fig. 4), with the extent of ice-cover loss by the end of the century dictated by the selected mitigation pathway (4, 21, 31). While sustainable emission scenarios allow for a mid-century stabilization of phenological trends, high-emission pathways trigger a relentless and accelerating transformation of the annual ice cycle with no signs of stabilization by 2100 (Fig. 4 A–F). This disruption is spatially heterogeneous, with mid-continental boreal and temperate regions emerging as global hotspots where the convergence of high intrinsic sensitivity and significant regional warming accelerates phenological shifts (7, 8). The projected expansion of the highly sensitive lake population from a historical baseline of 23% to nearly 70% under extreme scenarios (Fig. 5) also shows that many ecosystems are approaching critical thermal tipping points (27, 31). These findings demonstrate that effective climate mitigation is essential to constrain the geographic extent of ice loss, as unmitigated warming threatens to push a substantial fraction of Northern Hemisphere lakes into unprecedented thermal regimes characterized by intermittent or permanent ice-free conditions (1).

Our findings establish that the lacustrine cryosphere of the Northern Hemisphere is approaching a critical thermal threshold, with important implications for the ecological integrity and socioeconomic systems dependent on seasonal ice cover (9, 16). The transition toward intermittent or ice-free winters threatens to destabilize traditional livelihoods, cultural heritage, and regional transportation networks while simultaneously disrupting critical wildlife migratory and hunting corridors (12). Furthermore, the loss of ice cover fundamentally alters surface energy budgets and hydrological fluxes (43), potentially accelerating methane emissions and reinforcing positive climate feedbacks that amplify regional warming (44, 45). By identifying distinct atmospheric temperature thresholds for ice formation and decay, our study provides a mechanistic framework for interpreting the observed regional disparities in lake vulnerability (26). Lakes situated in mid-latitude regions are currently positioned nearest to these critical thresholds and are therefore at the most immediate risk of shifting to intermittent or ice-free states, a transformation likely to propagate northward as global temperatures rise (22). Incorporating these threshold dynamics into predictive models is essential to refine projections of climate impacts and to develop robust strategies aimed at sustaining the hydrological, ecological, and cultural functions of lake ecosystems in a rapidly warming world (10, 12).

Materials and Methods

Data Sources.

Lake-ice phenology data.

Global lake-ice observations span over 150 y, yet continuous, high-quality records remain limited due to inconsistent monitoring protocols and spatial gaps (46). Recent advances in remote sensing, notably satellite products such as MODIS have enabled consistent, large-scale, and high-frequency monitoring of lake-ice phenology, substantially improving spatial and temporal coverage. Several studies report an accelerated rate of lake-ice phenological change since the mid-1990s (5, 47). Therefore, to maximize data quality and coverage, we focused on the period 2000–2022, when reliable time-series datasets became widely available.

We compiled publicly accessible lake-ice phenology datasets (46, 48–51), retaining only lakes with at least 15 valid observations per phenological metric during 2000–2022, resulting in a mean record length of 18.2 ± 1.7 y. Our synthesis included 724 Northern Hemisphere lakes, comprising 483 in North America, 136 in Asia, and 105 in Europe, integrating both in situ (50 lakes) and remote sensing (674 lakes) records (Dataset S1). These lakes span diverse morphometries, with surface areas and depths ranging from 0.3 to 377,002 km2 (median 213.6 km2) and 0.3 to 738.7 m (median 11.3 m), respectively (Dataset S1). To ensure a rigorous analysis of lake-ice phenology, we defined key metrics based on standardized limnological protocols. Initial ice on represents the first day of detectable skim ice on the lake surface, while complete freeze up denotes the transition to a stable and continuous ice sheet. The decay phase is characterized by initial break up, which signifies the initial fracturing of the ice or the appearance of open water, and complete ice off, which is the date when all ice has vanished from the lake surface. Based on these markers, the duration of complete ice cover is calculated as the interval between complete freeze up and initial break up. In contrast, the duration of partial ice cover spans the period from the first day of ice formation to the final day of ice loss. The number of lakes with sufficient data for each metric ranged from 123 for complete ice cover to 694 for ice off (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Across the study period, mean ice-phenology events occurred at day −48.8 ± 20.5 for initial ice on and −36.9 ± 20.5 for complete freeze up, while initial break up and complete ice off occurred at day 113.9 ± 20.1 and 146.8 ± 28.8, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Days are referenced relative to the start of the calendar year. Complete and partial ice-cover duration averaged 151.6 ± 37.2 d and 195.0 ± 47.3 d, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Lake characteristics data.

Geomorphic and environmental attributes were extracted from the LakeATLAS dataset, which extends the HydroLAKES by providing detailed lake-linked parameters related to geomorphology, hydrology, land cover, and anthropogenic influences (52). We compiled surface area, shoreline development, volume, mean depth, area to depth ratio, elevation, watershed area, runoff, land use (within a 3 km buffer), and anthropogenic metrics including population density, human footprint, and human development index for all study lakes (Dataset S1). Discharge and water residence time were also obtained for the majority of lakes, although these specific hydrological parameters were unavailable for a subset of 27 lakes (Dataset S1). In addition, to approximate marine influences, we calculated the shortest geodesic distance from each lake to the nearest coastline using lake coordinates and coastline polygons from the Global Self-consistent Hierarchical High-resolution Geography Database (53). We computed these distances using the pyproj Geod module (54).

Climate data.

Monthly climate variables were extracted from the ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset for 1999–2023 using Google Earth Engine (55, 56). These data were sampled at 1 km spatial resolution and included AT, relative humidity (RH), total precipitation (TP), total evaporation (TE), wind speed (WS), forecast albedo (FAL), and energy fluxes [surface solar radiation downward (SSRD), surface thermal radiation downward (STRD), surface latent heat flux (SLHF), surface net solar radiation (SNSR), surface net thermal radiation (SNTR), surface sensible heat flux (SSHF)]. For each lake, we extracted data from the nearest grid cell or calculated the averaged across all grid cells contained within the lake polygon for larger lakes.

To account for the seasonal nature of lake ice, we defined the hydrological year as the period from September 1 to August 31 of the following year. We computed annual averages for all climate variables over this period. Furthermore, we calculated seasonal means for AT to capture relevant variability (57), defining spring as March through May, summer as June through August, autumn as September through November, and winter as December through February of the following year.

Sensitivity of Lake-Ice Phenology to AT Change.

We quantified phenological sensitivity to interannual variation in annual hydrological AT during 2000 to 2022 via linear regression for each lake and phenological metric. Outliers beyond ±3 SD from the mean were excluded (58). Regression outputs included slope, intercept, coefficient of determination (R2), and P-value, providing estimates of the magnitude and significance of temperature effects (Dataset S2). The slope of this regression served as our measure of phenological sensitivity. For ice-formation metrics, a negative slope indicates earlier ice formation with warming; for ice-decay and duration metrics, a negative slope indicates earlier melt and shorter ice cover, respectively.

Identification of Nonlinear Ice Phenological Responses and Thermal Thresholds.

To identify potential nonlinearities and threshold effects in lake-ice responses to atmospheric warming, we employed piecewise linear regression analysis (59). This approach allows for the detection of one or more breakpoints where the relationship between mean winter AT and ice phenological sensitivity undergoes a significant change in slope. We implemented this analysis by estimating the optimal breakpoint position through an iterative optimization process that minimizes the total residual sum of squares (60). We confirmed the statistical significance of each identified breakpoint using the Davies test, which evaluates the null hypothesis of a constant slope against the alternative of a change in slope at an unknown breakpoint. Furthermore, we compared the piecewise models against simple linear models using the Akaike Information Criterion to ensure that the increased model complexity was justified by a substantial improvement in explanatory power. This framework allowed us to partition the sensitivity data into distinct segments, thereby identifying critical thermal thresholds for lake-ice phenological transitions. In this study, highly sensitive lakes were defined as those crossing critical thermal thresholds and entering a state of accelerated phenological sensitivity to atmospheric forcing.

Attribution of Drivers for Lake-Ice Phenological Sensitivity.

To evaluate preliminary associations between phenological metrics and environmental drivers, we computed Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. This nonparametric approach was selected to accommodate the non-normal distributions characteristic of lake geographic (latitude, longitude, elevation, coast distance), morphological (area, mean depth, volume, area to depth ratio, shore development), hydrological (watershed area, discharge, runoff, water residence time), climatic (annual and seasonal mean AT, RH, TP, TE, WS, FAL, SSRD, STRD, SLHF, SNSR, SNTR, SSHF), land-use (percent forest, shrubland, barren, grasslands, wetlands, croplands, water, snow & ice, urban), and anthropogenic (population density, human footprint, human development index) datasets. Furthermore, to identify the primary drivers of lake-ice phenological sensitivity to atmospheric warming, we employed random-forest regression, which is capable of modeling complex nonlinear interactions among predictors (61). Our predictor set included variables related to lake geography, morphology, hydrology, climate, land use, and human activity. We trained models using 500 regression trees and evaluated performance through fivefold cross-validation and the coefficient of determination. We quantified feature importance using the mean decrease in impurity and validated these estimates through permutation testing with 100 iterations to derive empirical P-values (62).

Projections of Lake-Ice Phenology Under Future Climate Change.

To project future lake-ice phenology through 2100, we integrated lake-specific sensitivity parameters, calibrated against 2000 to 2022 observations, with near-surface AT projections from four global climate models (ACCESS-CM2, CESM2, FGOALS-g3, and GFDL-ESM4) sourced from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections dataset. For each of the 724 study lakes, we extracted daily 2-meter AT data from the grid cell nearest to the lake centroid at a 0.25-degree spatial resolution. We defined the hydrological year as the period from September 1 to August 31 of the following year. For each hydrological year from 2023 to 2100, we calculated the mean AT and subtracted the corresponding mean from the 2000 to 2022 historical baseline to determine annual temperature anomalies. To mitigate the influence of stochastic interannual variability, these anomalies were smoothed using a 5-y moving average centered on each target year. We applied a delta-change approach to project shifts in the six ice phenology metrics by multiplying each lake’s unique sensitivity coefficient by the projected annual hydrological year temperature anomaly. This analysis was performed across four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5), yielding 16 distinct projection pathways per lake and a total of 11,600 simulation runs (63).

Statistical Analyses.

We assessed differences in phenological sensitivities and future emission scenarios among lake groups using one-way ANOVA. For significant ANOVA results, we employed Tukey’s honest significant difference tests to determine pairwise group differences. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Data manipulation, statistical modeling, and visualization were conducted using the pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, matplotlib, and seaborn libraries. Statistical analyses were performed within the Python programming environment.
