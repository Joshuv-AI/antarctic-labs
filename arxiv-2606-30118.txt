I.i.d. Prophet Inequalities with Discounted Rewards: As Hard as the Non-i.i.d. Case
Jung-hun Kim, Vianney Perchet

Abstract
We study prophet inequalities with discounted rewards, where i.i.d. base rewards are multiplicatively discounted over time. Our main message is that even this structured and arbitrarily weak form of nonstationarity can erase the classical advantage of the stationary i.i.d. setting. Focusing on single-quantile threshold policies, we show that the competitive ratio transitions from the classical 1 − 1 / e 1-1/e guarantee to a fundamental 1 / 2 1/2 barrier as discounting accumulates over many phases in a canonical regime with a common-decay factor and equal-length phases. We further show that, in the same regime, the 1 / 2 1/2 barrier persists even for arbitrary stopping rules. Consequently, i.i.d. base rewards under discounting can be as hard as the fully non-i.i.d. case. On the algorithmic side, we design single-quantile threshold rules that attain the tight bounds by calibrating acceptance decisions to an effective horizon induced by discounting, and we extend this calibration to heterogeneous decay factors and unequal phase lengths. We further show that a similar discontinuous breakdown persists in an infinite-horizon continuous-decay benchmark, where arbitrarily weak decay collapses the stationary benchmark from 1 1 to 1 / 2 1/2 .

Introduction

Prophet inequalities constitute a fundamental class of problems in online optimal stopping
(
16
)
.
A decision maker (the “gambler”) sequentially observes a stream of non-negative random variables (the “rewards”), revealed one at a time, and must decide at each stage whether to
accept the current reward and stop
or to
continue observing future rewards
.
Performance is benchmarked against a
prophet
, an omniscient agent who observes all realizations in advance and can always select the maximum reward.
The goal of the gambler is to design an online stopping rule whose expected payoff is competitive with that of the prophet, typically measured through the competitive ratio.
Owing to their rich mathematical structure and practical relevance, prophet inequalities have been extensively studied
(
9
)
and have found applications in areas such as posted-price mechanisms
(
18
)
, online advertisement allocation
(
2
)
, and hiring processes in labor markets
(
5
)
.

The prophet-inequalities literature has established tight constant-factor guarantees.
On one side, in stationary i.i.d. reward settings, the classical
1-1/e

competitive-ratio bound is known to be tight for a class of single-threshold policies
(
9
;
8
)
.
On the other side, for independent but non-identically
distributed (non-i.i.d.) reward sequences, the optimal competitive ratio degrades to
1/2
, as shown by

21
.
Despite this extensive literature, existing results largely focus on these two
extreme endpoints and have not addressed structured forms of nonstationarity that

interpolate
between them.
We study a natural mechanism to bridge between the stationary i.i.d. and fully non-i.i.d. settings: rewards are discounted with time, i.e., the scale of the reward distribution decreases over time.
This framework isolates a discounting-induced transition from stationarity to
nonstationarity, allowing a precise characterization of how classical guarantees
break down.
In particular, we show that even arbitrarily small discounting, when accumulated
over long horizons, rules out any competitive ratio exceeding
1/2
, matching
the worst-case guarantee in the non-i.i.d. setting. In other words,
i.i.d. prophet inequalities with slightly discounted rewards are as hard as
the non-i.i.d. case.

Beyond its theoretical interest, reward decay arises
naturally in many practical environments where opportunities deteriorate over
time due to time preferences, depreciation, congestion effects, or market
saturation
(
11
;
12
;
22
;
20
;
7
)
.

We shall focus on
single-quantile threshold
stopping policies. Such a policy fixes an acceptance quantile in advance and accepts the first observation whose value exceeds the corresponding quantile threshold of the current reward distribution. In the stationary i.i.d. setting, this is equivalent, up to tie-breaking, to using a fixed value threshold. Under reward decay, however, fixed-value and fixed-quantile thresholds no longer coincide. We formulate our policies in terms of a fixed acceptance quantile, and refer to them as single-threshold policies throughout.

Threshold policies are a cornerstone of prophet inequalities due to their simplicity and sharp guarantees. In the stationary i.i.d. setting, single-threshold policies achieve the classical
1-1/e
guarantee, which is tight within that policy class
(
8
;
9
)
. In the independent non-i.i.d. setting, a fixed value-threshold rule attains the optimal worst-case competitive ratio
1/2

(
21
)
(see also
10
;
14
). From an economic perspective, single-threshold stopping rules naturally correspond
to standard posted-price mechanisms
(
4
)
, in which the decision maker commits to a fixed
acceptance rule in advance. Such mechanisms are appealing due to their single-parameter structure, which
reduces approximation to choosing a single price and yields
transparent and tractable policies
(
19
)
.

As a consequence, we will study the performance of the single-threshold policies under decaying rewards
and characterize their achievable competitive ratios, to answer the following natural question:

To what extent do single-threshold policies retain their classical
performance guarantees under decaying rewards?

We tackle this question by identifying the fundamental performance limits and
designing single-threshold algorithms that match these limits in this setting.
Our main contributions are the following.

•

Fundamental limits under reward decay.

We derive a tight worst-case upper bound for single-quantile threshold policies under
decaying rewards for a common decay factor and equal-length phases.
These bounds reveal a sharp transition from the classical
1-1/e
guarantee to a
worst-case
1/2
barrier in the boundary regime between stationarity and discounting.
This
1/2
barrier is worst-case optimal, matching the classical non-i.i.d. impossibility bound.
We also show that the
1/2
barrier is not an artifact of single-threshold policies: any arbitrary stopping rules face the same worst-case barrier.

•

Single-threshold optimality via an effective-horizon principle.

We design single-threshold algorithms that match the above upper bound for a common decay factor with equal-length phases.
The key insight is an
effective-horizon
principle: at the level of the
prophet benchmark (expected maxima), a decaying reward sequence behaves like a stationary i.i.d. sequence observed over a shorter horizon.
Calibrating the acceptance quantile to this effective horizon yields tight guarantees. The same calibration extends to heterogeneous decay factors and unequal phase lengths.

•

Infinite-horizon continuous-decay benchmark.

As a clean benchmark for the long-run effect of discounting, we study an infinite-horizon model with
continuously decaying rewards.
This benchmark reveals a sharp
discontinuity: while the stationary infinite-horizon setting admits a trivial competitive ratio
of
1
, introducing arbitrarily weak decay induces a sharp breakdown, yielding a
worst-case competitive ratio of
1/2
.
Moreover, we prove that our single-threshold rule is optimal in this benchmark and
achieves this bound.

Related Work

The study of prophet inequalities dates back to the seminal work of

17
.
Subsequent work established sharp constant-factor benchmarks in the independent
non-i.i.d. and stationary i.i.d. settings.
In the independent but non-identically distributed (non-i.i.d.) case,

21
showed that a threshold stopping rule attains the
optimal competitive ratio of
1/2
.
In the i.i.d. setting,
15
established a competitive
ratio of at least
1-1/e
, later reinterpreted in algorithmic terms as a
single-quantile threshold policy that accepts the first observation exceeding a
suitable quantile
(
9
;
10
;
14
)
.
Beyond single-threshold policies, more adaptive but computationally involved
approaches have been proposed to improve performance in related settings, such
as
1
and
8
.

In contrast to the above literature, which primarily focuses on stationary i.i.d. rewards or fully general non-identically distributed reward sequences, our work
studies single-threshold stopping policies under a

discounting-induced
nonstationary reward model.
This framework captures a continuous transition from stationarity to reward
decay, isolating the effect of discounting as a structured departure from the
i.i.d. setting.

Discounting has also appeared
in related prophet-region, secretary-type, and random-horizon settings. In the
prophet-region literature,
3
studies uniformly
bounded random variables under discounting, and characterizes the corresponding
prophet regions, yielding sharp additive comparisons between the expected
maximum and the optimal stopping value. In the discounted secretary problem,

6
consider random-order arrivals with
time-dependent discount factors and obtain competitive guarantees for fixed
sets of arbitrary values. Relatedly,
13
study
i.i.d. prophet inequalities with a random horizon, where the horizon
distribution is known but its realization is not; this model can be viewed
through a discounted optimal stopping problem with discount factors given by
the horizon survival probabilities. While these works feature discounting or
horizon-induced discounting, they address different mechanisms and objectives:
prophet-region/additive-gap comparisons, random-order secretary models, and
uncertainty in the horizon length. In contrast, our focus is on multiplicative competitive ratios for i.i.d.
draws from a base distribution under reward decay, and in
particular on how structured reward decay induces a transition from the
stationary i.i.d. prophet setting to non-i.i.d.-type hardness.

3
Problem Statement

We consider the classical prophet-inequality
framework over a finite horizon
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where a nonnegative random
reward
X_{i}\geq 0
is revealed sequentially to a
gambler at each stage
i\in[n]
.
To model
decaying reward distributions
, we partition the time horizon into

S\geq 1
consecutive phases, or time intervals,

\{\mathcal{T}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, which form a disjoint partition of
[n]
.
We denote by
n_{s}:=|\mathcal{T}_{s}|
the length of phase
s
, so that

\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}=n
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. samples from a base reward distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with finite mean. Let
\gamma_{s}\in[0,1]
for all
s\in[S-1]
denote phase-wise decay factors, and define
the cumulative decay coefficients

\Gamma_{s}:=\prod_{t=1}^{s}\gamma_{t},\quad s=1,\dots,S-1,\text{ and }\;\Gamma_{0}:=1.

Then, for each phase
s\in[S]
and index
i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}
, the observed (decayed)
reward is defined as

X_{i}:=\begin{cases}Y_{i},&s=1,\\[4.0pt]
\Gamma_{s-1}\,Y_{i},&s\geq 2,\end{cases}

where
\Gamma_{s-1}
captures the cumulative decay in reward magnitude across
phases.

We first analyze the canonical setting with a common decay factor and
equal-length phases, where
\gamma_{s}=\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1]
for all
s\in[S-1]
and
n_{s}=n/S
for all
s\in[S]
(or asymptotically
n_{s}/n\to 1/S
). In this case, the observed rewards take the simple form
X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}Y_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}
. We focus on this canonical setting first because it isolates the effect of accumulated discounting in its simplest form. We later return to the general phase-dependent formulation to handle heterogeneous decay factors and unequal phase lengths.

Following most of the prior literature in prophet inequalities

(
15
;
21
;
8
;
10
;
14
)
,
we assume that the reward distributions are known, which corresponds
to knowing the base distribution
\mathcal{D}
, as well as the phase partition

\{\mathcal{T}_{s}\}_{s\in[S]}
and decay factors
(\gamma_{s})_{s\in[S-1]}
in advance.

Stopping rule.

At each stage
i
, after observing
X_{i}
, the decision maker must either
stop and accept the current reward
or
continue to the next stage
, thereby irrevocably forfeiting the option to stop at any earlier stage.
A stopping rule (or policy)
\tau
is a random variable taking values in

\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\cup\{n+1\}
such that
\tau
is a stopping time with respect to
the natural filtration generated by
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
. The value
\tau=n+1
corresponds to the event that no reward is
accepted within the horizon, in which case the payoff is defined to be zero.
The expected reward of a policy
\tau
is given by

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\tau=i\}].

Prophet benchmark and competitive ratio.

We compare the performance of an online stopping rule
\tau
with that of a
prophet
who knows all realizations
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
in advance and can always select the maximum reward.
The prophet’s expected reward is therefore

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right].

The performance of a stopping policy
\tau
is measured by its
competitive ratio
(CR) in the decay rewards with horizon
n
, which is defined as

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]}.

The quantity
\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)
implicitly depends on the decay profile, the
phase partition, and the base distribution; we suppress this dependence to keep
the notation light. We seek stopping policies that maximize
\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)

uniformly over the class of base reward distributions
\mathcal{D}
.

Quantile rather than value thresholds.

Throughout the paper, we focus on single-quantile threshold policies. This choice is natural under reward decay because a fixed quantile of the reward distribution induces phase-dependent value thresholds that scale with the discount factors. In particular, if the first-phase threshold is
\alpha_{1}
, then the corresponding threshold in phase
s+1
is obtained simply as

\alpha_{s+1}=\gamma_{s}\alpha_{s}.

Thus single-quantile thresholds retain the same one-parameter simplicity and online implementation cost as static value thresholds.

Single-quantile and single-value threshold policies have comparable implementation
cost, and in the stationary i.i.d. setting they coincide up to tie-breaking.
Under reward decay, however, this equivalence breaks down: a single fixed value
threshold is not invariant to the changing reward scale and can be strictly
suboptimal. Appendix
A.1
provides a separating example showing
that, even with a common decay factor and equal-length phases, single-value
thresholds cannot attain the single-quantile benchmark characterized below.
Henceforth, we focus on single-quantile threshold policies, which we refer to simply as
single-threshold policies
.

4
Fundamental Limits in the Canonical Decay Model

We begin with the canonical case of a common decay factor

\gamma_{s}=\widetilde{\gamma}
for all
s\in[S-1]
and equal asymptotic
phase lengths,
n_{s}=n/S
for all
s\in[S]
. For simplicity, we assume that

S
divides
n
. This symmetric setting
captures the central phenomenon: accumulated discounting can drive the
competitive ratio from the classical i.i.d. value
1-1/e
down to the
non-i.i.d. barrier
1/2
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the setting with a common decay factor and equal-length phases described above.
There exists a family of decaying reward distributions such that for any single-threshold stopping policy
\tau
,
the competitive ratio satisfies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)\;\leq\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\big(1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}\exp\!\big(-S\tfrac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}\big)\big)\big(1-\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}\big)\big)}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}\,\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}\big)},&\text{if }\;0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1,\\[12.0pt]
1-\tfrac{1}{e},&\text{if }\;\widetilde{\gamma}=1.\end{cases}

Proof Sketch.

Here we provide a proof sketch and the full version is provided in
Appendix
A.2
.
The key is to construct a single hard
distribution, depending only on
(S,\widetilde{\gamma})
, that upper bounds every
single-threshold policy.

Step 1: Hard instance and prophet benchmark.

Let the phases have equal length
n/S
, and consider

Y_{i}=\begin{cases}n,&\text{with probability }1/n^{2},\\
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}},&\text{with probability }1-1/n^{2},\end{cases}\qquad X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}Y_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{T}_{s},

where the constant
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\geq 0
will be chosen below. Define

c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})},&0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1,\\[6.0pt]
1,&\widetilde{\gamma}=1.\end{cases}

Then we show that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]=y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+o(1).

Step 2: Upper bound for an arbitrary threshold.

A single-quantile threshold is characterized by a per-stage acceptance
probability
p
. If
R_{s}(p)
is the
expected reward conditional on acceptance in phase
s
, then we show that, uniformly in
p
,

pR_{s}(p)\leq\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}}{n}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}p.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}p\,R_{s}(p)\,(1-p)^{i-1}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}}{n}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}p\right)(1-p)^{(s-1)n/S}\frac{1-(1-p)^{n/S}}{p}.

Writing
x=np
, for every fixed
x>0
this yields

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\leq\left(\frac{1}{x}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\right)A_{S}(x)+o(1),

where

A_{S}(x):=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}e^{-(s-1)x/S}(1-e^{-x/S})=\frac{(1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}e^{-x})(1-e^{-x/S})}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}e^{-x/S}}.

Thus

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)\leq f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})+o(1),\qquad f_{S}(x,y):=\frac{(\frac{1}{x}+y)A_{S}(x)}{y+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}.

Step 3: Choosing a fixed hard value
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
.

Set

x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}

and define

y(x):=\frac{A_{S}(x)}{x^{2}A_{S}^{\prime}(x)}-\frac{1}{x},\qquad y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=y(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}).

We show that, for
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=y(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}})
,

f_{S}(\cdot,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})
is increasing before
x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
and decreasing
after
x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
. Hence

\sup_{x\in[0,\infty]}f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=f_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}},y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=A_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}),

where the last equality follows from
1/x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
. Thus the
single choice
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
defeats every single threshold.

Step 4: Evaluation.

For
0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1
,

x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=\frac{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}},

and hence

A_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=\frac{\left(1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}\exp\!\left(-S\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{S}}\right)\right)\left(1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{S}}\right)\right)}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-{\widetilde{\gamma}}^{S}}\right)}.

When
\widetilde{\gamma}=1
, we have
x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=1
and

A_{S}(1)=1-1/e
. Therefore,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)\leq A_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}),

which is the claimed bound.

Discontinuous breakdown at the stationarity boundary

Under
S\to\infty
, the regime
\widetilde{\gamma}\uparrow 1
corresponds to the boundary between stationary and
decay reward sequences.
Over short time scales, the reward process is nearly
indistinguishable from the i.i.d. case, while over long horizons the cumulative
effect of decay becomes significant. At this boundary, the algorithm faces the sharpest trade-off: waiting is tempting because over short horizons the process looks nearly i.i.d., but waiting is expensive because over long horizons the cumulative decay becomes substantial.
This trade-off is reflected in the worst-case bound, whose limiting
value approaches
1/2
as
\widetilde{\gamma}\uparrow 1
under
S\to\infty
.
Thus the boundary regime exhibits a
discontinuous breakdown
from the
stationary value
1-1/e
attained at
\widetilde{\gamma}=1
(see the case
(S=10000) in Figure
1
).

This observation raises two natural questions. First, is this upper bound tight within the class of single-threshold policies? Second, is the resulting
1/2
limit merely an artifact of the single-threshold restriction, or is it an intrinsic limitation of the discounted-reward model? The next section answers the first question by giving a matching effective-horizon threshold rule; we return to the second question later by analyzing the optimal dynamic-programming policy.

5
Single-Threshold Algorithm for the Canonical Decay Model

In this section, we present a single-quantile threshold algorithm that achieves tight competitive ratios for a common decay factor with
\widetilde{\gamma}
and equal asymptotic
phase lengths with
n_{s}=n/S
. Both the design of the
algorithm and its analysis are calibrated through the notion of an

effective horizon
:

B:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\,\frac{n}{S},

which aggregates the total discounted mass of opportunities across all phases.

The central insight underlying our analysis is an effective-horizon principle: in terms of expected maxima, the prophet benchmark under decay is bounded above by that of a stationary i.i.d. sequence observed over an
effective horizon
of length
B
. We begin by formalizing this intuition through a key comparison lemma,
which plays a crucial role in both the
calibration of the acceptance threshold and the ensuing competitive-ratio
analysis.

5.1
Bounding the Expected Maximum

We bound the prophet’s expected maximum in terms of an effective horizon.
The proof is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
F
denote the cumulative distribution function of the base reward distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Consider a common decay factor and equal-length phases.
Then, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-F(z)^{B}\Bigr)\,dz\;\geq\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{S}F\!\bigl(z/\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}\bigr)^{n/S}\Bigr)\,dz,

(1)

where we adopt the convention
F(z/0):=\lim_{u\to\infty}F(u)=1
for
z\geq 0
.

We now explain the implication of the preceding lemma.
For each
i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}
, the cumulative distribution function of
X_{i}
is

z\mapsto F\!\bigl(z/\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\bigr)
.
Combining this with the standard survival-function formula for maxima yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[B]}Y_{i}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F(z)^{B}\bigr)\,dz\;\geq\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{S}F\!\bigl(z/\Gamma_{s-1}\bigr)^{n/S}\Bigr)\,dz=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right],

where, for notational simplicity, we assume
B\in\mathbb{N}
. This inequality identifies
B
as an
effective horizon
for the prophet
benchmark (expected maxima). We use it to calibrate the acceptance quantile and
to analyze the competitive ratio.

5.2
Single-Threshold Policy

We consider a single-quantile stopping rule whose acceptance threshold adapts to
the nonstationary scaling of the reward distribution.
Let
Y\sim\mathcal{D}
denote a random variable drawn from the base reward distribution. For each phase
s\in[S]
, corresponding to time indices
i\in\mathcal{T}_{s},

the algorithm employs a phase-specific threshold
\alpha_{s}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>\alpha_{s})=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}Y>\alpha_{s})=\frac{a}{n},

(2)

where the parameter
a
determines the acceptance quantile and is set as the inverse of
average effective decay weight
across all phases as follows:

a=\frac{1}{(1/S)+\sum_{s=1}^{S-1}(1/S)\widetilde{\gamma}^{s}}.

Importantly, the thresholds satisfy the simple recursion such that for
s\in[S-1]

\alpha_{s+1}=\widetilde{\gamma}\alpha_{s}.

Given the initial threshold
\alpha_{1}
, this recursion allows efficient
updates of the thresholds across phases.

.

Remark 4

(Interpretation of the threshold choice)
.

As shown in Lemma
3
, the decaying reward sequence can be
interpreted, in terms of expected maxima, as a stationary i.i.d. sequence with effective horizon
length
B=(1+\sum_{s=1}^{S-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s})\frac{n}{S}
.
Accordingly, a natural acceptance quantile is
1/B
, mirroring the classical
calibration in stationary i.i.d. prophet problems where the acceptance
probability scales as
1/n

(
9
)
.
This leads precisely to the choice
a/n
in (
2
).

Remark 5

(Atomic base distributions)
.

If the distribution of
X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}Y_{i}
has atoms, the strict
threshold equation in (
2
) may fail to admit a solution due to
discontinuities of the tail distribution.
Following

Here we provide a proof sketch, and the full version is provided in Appendix

\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}]

Step 1: Expand

Define

Substituting into the expansion yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=\left(1-(1-a/n)^{n/S}\right)\left(1+\sum_{s=2}^{S}(1-a/n)^{(s-1)n/S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\right)\,\frac{n}{a}\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}].

(3)

Step 4: Lower bound

From the definition,
B=\frac{n}{S}\bigl(1+\sum_{s=1}^{S-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s}\bigr)

It remains to relate

Step 5: Plug in and take

From

We now show that the

Since the DP policy dominates every admissible stopping rule, an upper bound on its competitive ratio immediately yields a fundamental limitation for all stopping rules. Theorem 7

(Dynamic Programming: Worst-Case Asymptotics)
.

Consider the setting with a common decay factor
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)

Structural implication.

in the presence of decaying rewards.
Interestingly, this

Having established that the

and show that the effective-horizon calibration extends beyond the symmetric

7
Extension to General Decay Factors and Phase Lengths

The common-decay, equal-length setting makes the effective-horizon principle
transparent: discounting reduces the total effective mass of opportunities, and
the appropriate acceptance quantile should be calibrated to this discounted
horizon rather than to the raw horizon. We now show that the same idea extends to heterogeneous
phase profiles.

Specifically, we allow the phase-wise discount factors to vary arbitrarily,
with
\gamma_{s}\in[0,1]
for
s\in[S-1]
, and allow the phase lengths

n_{s}
to vary across phases, assuming that each phase occupies a
nonvanishing asymptotic fraction of the horizon. Formally, there exist phase
proportions
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{S}\in(0,1]
, with

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}=1
, such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{n_{s}}{n}\to\lambda_{s},\qquad s\in[S].

In this setting, the contribution of each phase is determined by two quantities:
its relative length
\lambda_{s}
, and its cumulative reward scale

\Gamma_{s-1}
. Accordingly, define

\Lambda_{s-1}:=\sum_{t=1}^{s-1}\lambda_{t}\quad(\Lambda_{0}:=0),\qquad C_{S}:=\sum_{t=1}^{S}\Gamma_{t-1}\lambda_{t}.

We also define the effective horizon as

B_{G}:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}n_{s}.

The effective-horizon comparison for expected maxima in
Lemma
3
extends to this generalized horizon
B_{G}

(Appendix
A.3
). Moreover, since
B_{G}/n\to C_{S}
, the quantity

C_{S}
can be viewed as the normalized effective horizon associated with the
heterogeneous phase profile.

Fundamental Limits.

We first investigate the fundamental limitations of single-threshold stopping
policies under this generalized phase profile. The following theorem shows
that the upper-bound expression from the case of common decay and equal-length phases extends directly
once each phase is weighted by its length and cumulative discount. The proof is
deferred to Appendix
A.5

Theorem 8
.

Fix any
S\geq 1
, decay factors

(\gamma_{s})_{s=1}^{S-1}\in[0,1]^{S-1}

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}=1

Conclusion

We studied prophet inequalities under
decaying
reward sequences, where i.i.d.
base rewards are observed through multiplicative discounts, and analyzed the
performance limits of single-threshold stopping rules.
Our results identify a sharp transition from the classical stationary i.i.d.

While the stationary case recovers the optimal
1-1/e

Overall, we show that discounting alone can erase the classical advantage of stationarity, fundamentally reshaping the guarantees achievable by single-threshold stopping rules under decaying rewards.

Proposition 12

(Single value-thresholds are dominated by the single-quantile bound)

Fix an integer
S\geq 2
and a common decay factor

Assume equal phase lengths,

and define

\Gamma_{s-1}:=\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1},\qquad C_{\widetilde{\gamma},S}:=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}=\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})}.

q_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}},

, define

X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}Y_{i}.

We first compute the prophet benchmark. If no rare value appears, then
the prophet obtains
y

in the first phase. A rare value in phase
s
has observed value

\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n

\frac{n_{s}}{n^{2}}+o(1/n)=\frac{1}{Sn}+o(1/n),

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n\left(\frac{1}{Sn}+o(1/n)\right)=C_{\widetilde{\gamma},S}+o(1).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}]=y+C_{\widetilde{\gamma},S}+o(1).

Now consider an arbitrary static observed-value threshold policy. Such a
policy fixes a single value threshold
\alpha
and accepts the first

\alpha

If
\alpha<y
, then the policy stops immediately at time

. Hence

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{v}}]=y+o(1).

If
\alpha>y

y
. Thus the policy can only collect
rare values, and its expected reward is at most the value of accepting
every rare value when it first appears:

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{v}}]\leq C_{\widetilde{\gamma},S}+o(1).

It remains to consider the boundary case
\alpha=y
. Let
\rho_{n}\in[0,1]

be the tie-breaking probability at the value
y

y
occurs as

\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y<y

.

For finite

y

Rare values in phase

\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z,x),

where

F_{r}(z,x):=\frac{z(1-e^{-x})+r\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x}+(1-r)e^{-x}}{z+1},

with the convention
(1-e^{-x})/x=1
at
x=0
. Optimizing over the hard
instance parameter
y
, equivalently over
z>0
, gives

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S):=\inf_{z>0}\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z,x).

We now compute this optimized upper bound. Define

a(x):=1-e^{-x},

and

b_{r}(x):=r\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x}+(1-r)e^{-x}.

Then

F_{r}(z,x)=\frac{za(x)+b_{r}(x)}{z+1}.

The function
a
is strictly increasing, while
b_{r}
is strictly
decreasing. Moreover,

a(0)=0,\qquad b_{r}(0)=1,

and

\lim_{x\to\infty}a(x)=1,\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}b_{r}(x)=0.

Hence there is a unique
x_{r}\in(0,\infty)
satisfying

a(x_{r})=b_{r}(x_{r}).

This equation is exactly

r\left(\frac{1-e^{-x_{r}}}{x_{r}}-e^{-x_{r}}\right)=1-2e^{-x_{r}}.

Since the left-hand side minus the right-hand side is positive at

x=\ln 2
and nonpositive at
x=1
, we have

x_{r}\in[\ln 2,1].

For every
z>0
,

F_{r}(z,x_{r})=\frac{za(x_{r})+b_{r}(x_{r})}{z+1}=a(x_{r}).

Therefore,

\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z,x)\geq a(x_{r})\qquad\text{for every }z>0.

Thus

\inf_{z>0}\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z,x)\geq a(x_{r}).

To prove the reverse inequality, define

h_{r}(x):=1-r+r\frac{e^{x}-x-1}{x^{2}}.

A direct calculation gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(za(x)+b_{r}(x)\bigr)=e^{-x}\bigl(z-h_{r}(x)\bigr).

Moreover,
h_{r}
is increasing on
(0,\infty)
because

h_{r}^{\prime}(x)=r\frac{e^{x}(x-2)+x+2}{x^{3}}\geq 0,

where the numerator is nonnegative since the function

\phi(x):=e^{x}(x-2)+x+2

satisfies
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
, and

\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=xe^{x}\geq 0.

Now choose

z^{\star}:=h_{r}(x_{r}).

Then
z^{\star}a(x)+b_{r}(x)
is increasing on
(0,x_{r})
and decreasing on

(x_{r},\infty)
. Hence

\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z^{\star},x)=F_{r}(z^{\star},x_{r})=a(x_{r}).

Therefore,

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)=a(x_{r})=1-e^{-x_{r}}.

It remains to compare this static-value upper bound with the
single-quantile benchmark. We first prove a slightly stronger
intermediate bound. Define

Q(r):=\frac{1-e^{-r}}{1-(1-r)e^{-r}}.

We claim that

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)\leq Q(r).

Since

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)=\inf_{z>0}\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z,x),

it suffices to exhibit one value of
z
whose supremum is at most

Q(r)
. Let

z_{0}:=h_{r}(r)=1-r+r\frac{e^{r}-r-1}{r^{2}}=\frac{e^{r}-1-r^{2}}{r}.

Since
e^{r}\geq 1+r
, we have
z_{0}\geq 0
. As above, because
h_{r}
is
increasing, the function
z_{0}a(x)+b_{r}(x)
is maximized at
x=r
.
Therefore,

\sup_{x\geq 0}F_{r}(z_{0},x)=F_{r}(z_{0},r).

A direct simplification yields

Q(r)-F_{r}(z_{0},r)=\frac{(1-r)e^{-r}\bigl((e^{r}-1)^{2}-r^{2}e^{r}\bigr)}{(e^{r}-1+r)(e^{r}-1+r-r^{2})}.

The denominator is positive. The numerator is nonnegative because

(e^{r}-1)^{2}\geq r^{2}e^{r}

is equivalent to

e^{r/2}-e^{-r/2}\geq r,

which follows from
\sinh u\geq u
with
u=r/2
. Hence

F_{r}(z_{0},r)\leq Q(r),

and therefore

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)\leq Q(r).

Finally, we show that

Q(r)<R_{q}(\widetilde{\gamma},S).

Using

r=\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}},

the single-quantile bound can be rewritten as

R_{q}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)=(1-e^{-r})\frac{1-(\widetilde{\gamma}e^{-r})^{S}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}e^{-r}}.

Let

a:=\widetilde{\gamma}e^{-r},\qquad b:=(1-r)e^{-r}.

Then

R_{q}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)=(1-e^{-r})\frac{1-a^{S}}{1-a},\qquad Q(r)=\frac{1-e^{-r}}{1-b}.

Thus it suffices to prove

\frac{1-a^{S}}{1-a}\geq\frac{1}{1-b}.

This is equivalent to

a-b\geq(1-b)a^{S}.

Since

r(1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S})=1-\widetilde{\gamma},

we have

\widetilde{\gamma}-1+r=r\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}.

Therefore,

a-b=(\widetilde{\gamma}-1+r)e^{-r}=r\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}e^{-r}.

Also,

a^{S}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}e^{-rS}.

Hence it remains to show

re^{-r}>\left(1-(1-r)e^{-r}\right)e^{-rS}.

Because

r=\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}+\cdots+\widetilde{\gamma}^{S-1}},

we have
r>1/S
, and therefore
rS>1
. Thus
e^{-rS}<e^{-1}
.
It is enough to prove

re^{-r}>\frac{1}{e}\left(1-(1-r)e^{-r}\right).

Multiplying by
e^{r}
and then by
e
, this is equivalent to

er>e^{r}-1+r.

But this follows from the convexity chord bound

e^{r}<(1-r)e^{0}+re^{1}=1+(e-1)r,\qquad r\in(0,1).

Therefore,

Q(r)<R_{q}(\widetilde{\gamma},S).

Combining the inequalities, we obtain

\overline{R}_{v}^{\mathrm{stat}}(\widetilde{\gamma},S)=1-e^{-x_{\widetilde{\gamma},S}}\leq Q(r_{\widetilde{\gamma},S})<R_{q}(\widetilde{\gamma},S).

This completes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
1
: Upper bound for common decay and equal length phases

Consider a horizon of
n
stages and partition it into
S
consecutive phases of equal length
(assume
S\mid n
for simplicity; rounding effects do not affect the asymptotic bounds):

\mathcal{T}_{s}:=\Bigl\{(s-1)\tfrac{n}{S}+1,\dots,s\tfrac{n}{S}\Bigr\},\qquad s\in[S].

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. base rewards and define the observed rewards by a phase-dependent decay:

X_{i}:=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}Y_{i},\qquad i\in\mathcal{T}_{s},

where
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1]
. (In particular, the first phase is unscaled since
\widetilde{\gamma}^{0}=1
.)

Fix
0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}\leq 1
and choose a fixed parameter
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\in[0,n)
(specified later). Let

Y_{i}\sim\begin{cases}n,&\text{with probability }\tfrac{1}{n^{2}},\\[3.0pt]
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}},&\text{with probability }1-\tfrac{1}{n^{2}},\end{cases}\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

For fixed
S\geq 1
,
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1]
and
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\geq 0
(will be specified later), for large enough
n
, we have

\widetilde{\gamma}^{S-1}n\ \geq\ y_{\widetilde{\gamma}},

(7)

so that every rare value
\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n
dominates the common value
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
(and therefore also dominates
all scaled common values
\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
).
Let

M:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}

denote the offline optimum.

Expected optimal reward.

Let

q_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}},\qquad m:=\frac{n}{S},\qquad a_{n}:=(1-q_{n})^{m}.

For each
s\in[S]
, let
E_{s}
denote the event that the first phase
containing a rare draw is phase
s
; that is, no rare draw occurs in
phases
1,\ldots,s-1
, and at least one rare draw occurs in phase
s
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(E_{s})=a_{n}^{s-1}(1-a_{n}).

Together with the event
E_{0}
that no rare draw occurs over the entire
horizon, where
\mathbb{P}(E_{0})=a_{n}^{S}
, the events

E_{0},E_{1},\ldots,E_{S}
form a partition.

On
E_{s}
, the earliest rare draw has the largest discount among all
rare draws, and hence

M=\max\!\left\{y_{\widetilde{\gamma}},\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n\right\}.

On
E_{0}
, we have
M=y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
. Therefore, for every

0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[M]=(1-a_{n})\sum_{s=1}^{S}a_{n}^{s-1}\max\!\left\{y_{\widetilde{\gamma}},\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n\right\}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}a_{n}^{S}.

(8)

Suppose first that
\widetilde{\gamma}>0
. Since

y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
is fixed, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\widetilde{\gamma}^{S-1}n\geq y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}.

Consequently, (
8
) becomes

\mathbb{E}[M]=n(1-a_{n})\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}a_{n}^{s-1}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}a_{n}^{S}.

When
\widetilde{\gamma}=0
, only a rare draw in the first phase can
exceed
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[M]=n(1-a_{n})+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\left((1-a_{n})\sum_{s=2}^{S}a_{n}^{s-1}+a_{n}^{S}\right)=n(1-a_{n})+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}a_{n}.

Finally,

a_{n}=\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)^{n/S}=1-\frac{1}{Sn}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

and therefore

n(1-a_{n})=\frac{1}{S}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),\qquad a_{n}^{k}=1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\quad\text{for each fixed }k.

It follows in both cases that

\mathbb{E}[M]=y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}+o(1)=y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+o(1),

(9)

where

c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})},&0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1,\\[5.16663pt]
1,&\widetilde{\gamma}=1.\end{cases}

Expected performance of a single-threshold algorithm.

We consider a single-threshold policy with
0\leq p\leq 1
that accepts
X_{i}
if
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\geq\alpha_{s})=p
for some
\alpha_{s}\geq 0
for
s\in[S]
. Let
\tau
be the (random) acceptance time and write
R_{s}(p):=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mid\text{accept at }i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}]
.
Because
Y_{i}
is two-point, the conditional reward has an explicit form. If
0\leq p\leq 1/n^{2}
,
the algorithm can only accept the rare value, so

R_{s}(p)=\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n.

If
p>1/n^{2}
, the algorithm accepts the rare value whenever it occurs and accepts the common value
with additional probability to reach total acceptance probability
p
, giving

R_{s}(p)=\frac{1}{p}\Bigl(\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}n}{n^{2}}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\bigl(p-\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\bigr)\Bigr).

In either case we have the uniform upper bound

p\,R_{s}(p)\ \leq\ \frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}}{n}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\,p.

(10)

Since the per-stage acceptance probability is
p
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\tau=i)=(1-p)^{i-1}p
and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p\,R_{s}(p)\,(1-p)^{i-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}p\,R_{s}(p)\,(1-p)^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Bigl(\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}}{n}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\,p\Bigr)(1-p)^{(s-1)n/S}\sum_{j=1}^{n/S}(1-p)^{j-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Bigl(\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}}{n}+\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\,p\Bigr)(1-p)^{(s-1)n/S}\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n/S}}{p}.

(11)

As shown in Lemma
18
,
p=\Theta(1/n)
for the best single threshold policy.
We set
p=x/n
with fixed
x>0
, which may depend on the reward distributions.
Letting
n\to\infty
in (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\frac{1}{x}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\Bigr)\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(s-1)x}{S}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-e^{-x/S}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{1}{x}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\Bigr)\,\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}e^{-x}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}e^{-x/S}}\Bigl(1-e^{-x/S}\Bigr).

(12)

Dividing (
12
) by (
9
) (and ignoring the
o(1)
terms) gives the
asymptotic competitive-ratio bound

\mathrm{CR}\ \leq\ \frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{x}+y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\bigr)\,A_{S}(x)}{y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}=:f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}),

where
A_{S}
and
c_{S}
are defined in (
14
)–(
15
) below.
We now compute the minimax value of
\inf_{y\geq 0}\sup_{x>0}f_{S}(x,y)

under equal phase lengths.

Minimax upper bound for
\inf_{y\geq 0}\sup_{x>0}f_{S}(x,y)
.

Recall that

f_{S}(x,y):=\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{x}+y\bigr)\,A_{S}(x)}{y+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}},\qquad x>0,\;y\geq 0,

(13)

where

\displaystyle A_{S}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(s-1)x}{S}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-e^{-x/S}\Bigr),\qquad x>0,

(14)

and

c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}}{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})},&0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1,\\[6.0pt]
1,&\widetilde{\gamma}=1.\end{cases}

(15)

We also define the special point

x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{S(1-\widetilde{\gamma})}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}},&0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1,\\[6.0pt]
1,&\widetilde{\gamma}=1.\end{cases}

(16)

Step 1: Construct
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
such that
x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
is a global maximizer.

Define

y(x):=\frac{A_{S}(x)}{x^{2}A_{S}^{\prime}(x)}-\frac{1}{x},\qquad x>0,

(17)

and set

y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}:=y(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}).

(18)

We will prove:

(i)

y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\geq 0
;

(ii)

x\mapsto f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})
attains its global maximum at
x=x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
.

Granting these facts,

\sup_{x>0}f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=f_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}},y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=A_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}),

and hence

\inf_{y\geq 0}\sup_{x>0}f_{S}(x,y)\ \leq\ \sup_{x>0}f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})=A_{S}(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}).

(19)

It remains to justify (i)–(ii).

Step 2:
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\geq 0
(concavity argument).

From (
14
),

A_{S}(x)=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}e^{-(s-1)x/S}-\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}e^{-sx/S}=\sum_{j=0}^{S}m_{j}e^{-xj/S},

where
m_{0}=1
and
m_{j}\leq 0
for all
j\geq 1
. Differentiating twice gives
A_{S}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
for
x>0
,
and also
A_{S}(0)=0
. Hence
A_{S}
is concave on
(0,\infty)
, and the standard concavity inequality yields

A_{S}(x)\ \geq\ xA_{S}^{\prime}(x)\qquad(x>0).

Therefore,

y(x)=\frac{A_{S}(x)-xA_{S}^{\prime}(x)}{x^{2}A_{S}^{\prime}(x)}\ \geq\ 0,

since
A_{S}^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x>0
. In particular,
y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=y(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}})\geq 0
.

Step 3: Derivative factorization and reduction to monotonicity of
y(x)
.

Differentiate (
13
) in
x
(for fixed
y
):

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{S}(x,y)=\frac{1}{y+c_{\widetilde{\gamma}}}\Big(A_{S}^{\prime}(x)\big(\tfrac{1}{x}+y\big)-\tfrac{A_{S}(x)}{x^{2}}\Big).

Using (
17
), the bracket equals
A_{S}^{\prime}(x)\,(y-y(x))
, so

\operatorname{sign}\!\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{S}(x,y)\Big)=\operatorname{sign}\big(y-y(x)\big),\qquad x>0.

(20)

Therefore, if
y(\cdot)
is non-decreasing, then for
y=y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}=y(x_{\widetilde{\gamma}})
we have

x<x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\implies y(x)\leq y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\implies\partial_{x}f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})\geq 0,\qquad x>x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\implies y(x)\geq y_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\implies\partial_{x}f_{S}(x,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})\leq 0,

so
f_{S}(\cdot,y_{\widetilde{\gamma}})
is maximized at
x=x_{\widetilde{\gamma}}
, proving (ii).
Thus it suffices to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 13

(Monotonicity of
y(x)
)
.

Fix
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1]
.
Then the function
y:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined in (
17
) is non-decreasing:

y^{\prime}(x)\ \geq\ 0\qquad\text{for all }x>0.

Proof.

The proof consists of 4 steps (A-D) as follows.

(A) An integral representation and the
m
-parametrization.

Define the piecewise-constant weight function on
[0,1)
,

w(u):=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\lfloor Su\rfloor}.

For
k=0,1,2
define

L_{k}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}w(u)\,u^{k}\,e^{-xu}\,du.

From (
14
),

A_{S}(x)=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}e^{-(s-1)x/S}-\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}e^{-sx/S}

Then partitioning
[0,1)
into the
S
equal subintervals yields

A_{S}(x)=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}\int_{(s-1)/S}^{s/S}xe^{-xu}\,du=x\int_{0}^{1}w(u)e^{-xu}\,du=xL_{0}(x).

(21)

Differentiating under the integral sign gives

L_{0}^{\prime}(x)=-L_{1}(x),\qquad L_{1}^{\prime}(x)=-L_{2}(x),

and therefore

A_{S}^{\prime}(x)=L_{0}(x)-xL_{1}(x).

(22)

Define the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{x}
on
[0,1]
with density proportional to
w(u)e^{-xu}
:

\mathbb{P}_{x}(du)=\frac{w(u)e^{-xu}}{L_{0}(x)}\,du,\qquad L_{0}(x)>0.

Let
U
be the identity random variable under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
. Then

m(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[U]=\frac{L_{1}(x)}{L_{0}(x)}.

Using (
21
)–(
22
),

y(x)=\frac{A_{S}(x)}{x^{2}A_{S}^{\prime}(x)}-\frac{1}{x}=\frac{xL_{0}(x)}{x^{2}(L_{0}(x)-xL_{1}(x))}-\frac{1}{x}=\frac{L_{1}(x)}{L_{0}(x)-xL_{1}(x)}=\frac{m(x)}{1-xm(x)}.

Differentiate
m(x)=L_{1}(x)/L_{0}(x)
:

\displaystyle m^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}(x)L_{0}(x)-L_{1}(x)L_{0}^{\prime}(x)}{L_{0}(x)^{2}}=\frac{-L_{2}(x)L_{0}(x)+L_{1}(x)^{2}}{L_{0}(x)^{2}}=-\Big(\frac{L_{2}(x)}{L_{0}(x)}-\Big(\frac{L_{1}(x)}{L_{0}(x)}\Big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x}[U^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}_{x}[U])^{2}\Big)=-\operatorname{Var}_{x}(U).

Finally, differentiating
y(x)=m(x)/(1-xm(x))
gives

y^{\prime}(x)=\frac{m^{\prime}(x)+m(x)^{2}}{(1-xm(x))^{2}}=\frac{m(x)^{2}-\operatorname{Var}_{x}(U)}{(1-xm(x))^{2}}.

(23)

Since
1-xm(x)>0
(equivalently
A_{S}^{\prime}(x)>0
), it suffices to prove

\operatorname{Var}_{x}(U)\ \leq\ m(x)^{2}\qquad(x>0).

(24)

(B) Decomposition
U=(K+V)/S
and independence.

Let
K:=\lfloor SU\rfloor\in\{0,1,\dots,S-1\}
and
V:=SU-K\in[0,1)
, so that
U=(K+V)/S
.
Fix
x>0
and set

a:=\frac{x}{S},\qquad t:=e^{-a}=e^{-x/S},\qquad r:=\widetilde{\gamma}t\in[0,1).

For
k\in\{0,\dots,S-1\}
and
v\in[0,1)
, write
u=(k+v)/S
, so
du=dv/S
and
w(u)=\widetilde{\gamma}^{k}
. Then

w(u)e^{-xu}\,du=\widetilde{\gamma}^{k}e^{-x(k+v)/S}\frac{dv}{S}=(\gamma e^{-a})^{k}e^{-av}\frac{dv}{S}=r^{k}e^{-av}\frac{dv}{S}.

Hence the joint law factorizes as

\mathbb{P}_{x}(K=k,\ V\in dv)\propto r^{k}\cdot e^{-av}\,dv,

so
K
and
V
are independent, with

\mathbb{P}_{x}(K=k)=\frac{r^{k}}{\sum_{j=0}^{S-1}r^{j}},\qquad k=0,\dots,S-1,\qquad f_{V}(v)=\frac{ae^{-av}}{1-e^{-a}},\qquad v\in[0,1).

Consequently,

m(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[U]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[K]+\mathbb{E}[V]}{S},\qquad\operatorname{Var}_{x}(U)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(K)+\operatorname{Var}(V)}{S^{2}},

and (
24
) is equivalent to

\operatorname{Var}(K)+\operatorname{Var}(V)\ \leq\ (\mathbb{E}[K]+\mathbb{E}[V])^{2}.

(25)

(C) Reduce (
25
) to one-dimensional bounds.

For any square-integrable
Z
define

\Delta(Z):=(\mathbb{E}[Z])^{2}-\operatorname{Var}(Z)=2(\mathbb{E}[Z])^{2}-\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}].

Then (
25
) is equivalent to
\Delta(K+V)\geq 0
, and by independence

\Delta(K+V)=\Delta(K)+\Delta(V)+2\,\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[V].

(i) The
V
-terms.

A direct integration yields

\mathbb{E}[V]=\int_{0}^{1}v\,\frac{ae^{-av}}{1-e^{-a}}\,dv=\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{e^{a}-1},

(26)

and

\Delta(V)=\frac{ae^{a}+a-2e^{a}+2}{a(e^{a}-1)^{2}}=\frac{1-2\mathbb{E}[V]}{e^{a}-1}.

(27)

Since
f_{V}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1)
for
a>0
, we have
\mathbb{E}[V]<1/2
and thus

1-2\mathbb{E}[V]>0
.

(ii) The
K
-terms.

We use two elementary lemmas.

Lemma 14
.

Let
K
have truncated geometric law
\mathbb{P}(K=k)\propto r^{k}
on
\{0,\dots,S-1\}
with
r\in[0,1)
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[K]\ \leq\ \frac{r}{1-r}.

Proof.

Let
G
be geometric on
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
with
\mathbb{P}(G=k)=(1-r)r^{k}
. Then

K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G\mid(G\leq S-1)
, so conditioning can only decrease the mean:

\mathbb{E}[K]\leq\mathbb{E}[G]=r/(1-r)
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
K
have truncated geometric law
\mathbb{P}(K=k)\propto r^{k}
on
\{0,\dots,S-1\}
with
r\in[0,1)
.
Then

\Delta(K)\ \geq\ -\mathbb{E}[K],\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\mathbb{E}[K^{2}]\ \leq\ 2(\mathbb{E}[K])^{2}+\mathbb{E}[K].

Proof.

Write
G_{0}:=\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}r^{k}
,
G_{1}:=\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}kr^{k}
,
G_{2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}k^{2}r^{k}
.
Then
\mathbb{E}[K]=G_{1}/G_{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[K^{2}]=G_{2}/G_{0}
.
The desired inequality is equivalent to

H(r):=2G_{1}^{2}+G_{0}G_{1}-G_{0}G_{2}\ \geq\ 0.

(28)

Using
G_{0}=(1-r^{S})/(1-r)
and the identities
G_{1}=rG_{0}^{\prime}(r)
and
G_{2}=rG_{1}^{\prime}(r)
,
a direct computation gives

H(r)=-\,\frac{Sr^{S}}{(1-r)^{3}}\,B(r),

where

B(r):=(S+1)r-(S-1)+r^{S}\bigl((S-1)r-(S+1)\bigr).

Since
Sr^{S}/(1-r)^{3}>0
for
r\in(0,1)
, it suffices to show
B(r)\leq 0
on
(0,1)
.
Differentiate:

B^{\prime}(r)=(S+1)\Bigl(1-r^{S-1}\bigl(S-(S-1)r\bigr)\Bigr).

Apply AM–GM to the
S
positive numbers
\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{S-1\text{ times}}
and
S-(S-1)r
,
whose arithmetic mean equals
1
; hence their geometric mean is at most
1
:

r^{S-1}\bigl(S-(S-1)r\bigr)\leq 1,

so
B^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
on
(0,1)
. Since
B(1)=0
and
B
is increasing,
B(r)\leq 0
on
(0,1)
, proving

H(r)\geq 0
and the lemma.
∎

Step 4: Complete the variance bound.

Using
\Delta(K+V)=\Delta(K)+\Delta(V)+2\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[V]
and Lemma
15
,

\Delta(K+V)\geq-\mathbb{E}[K]+\Delta(V)+2\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[V]=\Delta(V)-\mathbb{E}[K]\bigl(1-2\mathbb{E}[V]\bigr).

By (
27
),

\Delta(K+V)\geq\bigl(1-2\mathbb{E}[V]\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{e^{a}-1}-\mathbb{E}[K]\Bigr).

We already have
1-2\mathbb{E}[V]>0
. It remains to show
\mathbb{E}[K]\leq 1/(e^{a}-1)
.
By Lemma
14
and monotonicity of
u\mapsto u/(1-u)
on
(0,1)
,

\mathbb{E}[K]\leq\frac{r}{1-r}\leq\frac{t}{1-t}=\frac{e^{-a}}{1-e^{-a}}=\frac{1}{e^{a}-1},

because
r=\widetilde{\gamma}t\leq t
. Hence
\Delta(K+V)\geq 0
, proving (
25
), hence
(
24
). Finally, (
23
) implies
y^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x>0
.
∎
We complete the proof of the theorem.

A.3
Proof of Lemma
3
: Effective-horizon comparison for expected maxima

Rather than treating the common-decay, equal-length case, we prove the
comparison directly in the general setting with arbitrary phase-wise decay
factors and phase lengths. In this setting, the effective horizon is defined as

B_{G}:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}n_{s}.

Lemma 16
.

Let
F
denote the cumulative distribution function of the base reward distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Fix an integer
S\geq 1
, phase
lengths
n_{1},\dots,n_{S}\in\mathbb{N}
, and decay factors

(\gamma_{t})_{t=1}^{S-1}\in[0,1]^{S-1}
.
Then, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-F(z)^{B_{G}}\Bigr)\,dz\;\geq\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{S}F\!\bigl(z/\Gamma_{\,s-1}\bigr)^{n_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz,

(29)

where we adopt the convention
F(z/0):=\lim_{u\to\infty}F(u)=1
for
z\geq 0
.

Proof.

For
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{S})\in[0,1]^{S}
define

G_{1}(w):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-F(z)^{\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}w_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz,\qquad G_{2}(w):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{S}F(z/w_{s})^{n_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz,

with the convention
F(z/0)=1
for
z>0
.
Note that (
1
) is exactly
G_{1}(v)\geq G_{2}(v)

for the geometric weight vector
v=(1,\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\dots,\Gamma_{S-1})
.

Step 1:
G_{1}
is concave in
w
.

Fix
z\geq 0
and set
a:=F(z)\in[0,1]
.
The function
u\mapsto 1-a^{u}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
because

\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}\bigl(1-a^{u}\bigr)=-a^{u}(\log a)^{2}\leq 0.

Since
u=\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}w_{s}
is affine in
w
, the map

w\mapsto 1-F(z)^{\sum_{s}n_{s}w_{s}}
is concave.
Integrating over
z
preserves concavity (all integrals are finite by the
assumption
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F(z)^{N})dz<\infty
), hence
G_{1}
is concave on
[0,1]^{S}
.

Step 2:
G_{2}
is convex in
w
.

Let
\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s},\;1\leq s\leq S}
be independent i.i.d. copies of
Y\sim\mathcal{D}

and define
M_{s}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}Y_{i}
.
For
w_{s}>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq s\leq S}w_{s}M_{s}\leq z\right)=\prod_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{P}(M_{s}\leq z/w_{s})=\prod_{s=1}^{S}F(z/w_{s})^{n_{s}}.

Thus, using
\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X>z)dz
for
X\geq 0
,

G_{2}(w)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq S}w_{s}M_{s}\right],

and the same holds for
w_{s}=0
by the convention
0\cdot M_{s}=0
and
F(z/0)=1
.
For each fixed realization
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{S})
,
the function
w\mapsto\max_{s}w_{s}m_{s}
is a pointwise maximum of linear functions,
hence convex. Taking expectation preserves convexity, so
G_{2}
is convex on
[0,1]^{S}
.

Step 3: Equality on step vectors.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,S\}
define the step vector

e^{(k)}:=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{S-k\text{ times}}).

Then

G_{1}(e^{(k)})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-F(z)^{\sum_{s=1}^{k}n_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz,

and, since
F(z/1)=F(z)
and
F(z/0)=1
,

G_{2}(e^{(k)})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{k}F(z)^{n_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-F(z)^{\sum_{s=1}^{k}n_{s}}\Bigr)\,dz=G_{1}(e^{(k)}).

Step 4: Represent
v
as a convex combination of step vectors.

Recall
\Gamma_{s}=\prod_{t\in[s]}\gamma_{t}
and
\Gamma_{0}:=1
. Define weights

p_{k}:=(1-\gamma_{k})\Gamma_{k-1}\quad(k=1,\dots,S-1),\qquad p_{S}:=\Gamma_{S-1}.

Then
p_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}=1
.
Moreover, for each
s\in\{1,\dots,S\}
,

\sum_{k=s}^{S}p_{k}=\sum_{k=s}^{S-1}(1-\gamma_{k})\Gamma_{k-1}+\Gamma_{S-1}=\Gamma_{s-1}.

Hence

v=(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{S-1})=\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}\,e^{(k)}.

Step 5: Jensen concave–convex sandwich.

Using concavity of
G_{1}
, convexity of
G_{2}
, and
G_{1}(e^{(k)})=G_{2}(e^{(k)})
,

G_{1}(v)=G_{1}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}e^{(k)}\Bigr)\;\geq\;\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}G_{1}(e^{(k)})=\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}G_{2}(e^{(k)})\;\geq\;G_{2}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}e^{(k)}\Bigr)=G_{2}(v).

This is exactly (
1
).∎

Proof of Theorem

A.5
Proof of Theorem
8
: Upper bound under general decay factors and phase lengths

We consider a time horizon of
n
stages, partitioned into
S
phases

\mathcal{T}_{s}\subset[n],\qquad|\mathcal{T}_{s}|=n_{s}>0,\qquad\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}=n.

We define

N_{s-1}:=\sum_{t=1}^{s-1}n_{t},\qquad N_{0}:=0,

and we have

\mathcal{T}_{s}=\{N_{s-1}+1,\dots,N_{s-1}+n_{s}\},\qquad s\in[S].

Recall
\Gamma_{s-1}:=\prod_{t\in[s-1]}\gamma_{t}
. The reward sequence is defined by

X_{i}=\begin{cases}Y_{i},&i\in\mathcal{T}_{1},\\[4.0pt]
\Gamma_{s-1}Y_{i},&i\in\mathcal{T}_{s},\;s\in[2,S],\end{cases}

where
0\leq\gamma_{t}\leq 1
for
t\in[s-1]
represent the decay factors across phases.

Fix
0\leq\gamma_{t}<1
for
t\in[S-1]
and a parameter

1/n\leq y\leq\sqrt{n}
(to be specified later).
Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the two-point distribution

Y_{i}\sim\begin{cases}n,&\text{with probability }\tfrac{1}{n^{2}},\\[3.0pt]
y,&\text{with probability }1-\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}.\end{cases}

The observed rewards
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are scaled according to the phase-dependent factors

\Gamma_{s-1}
defined above.
We will work in an asymptotic regime
n\to\infty
where the relative phase lengths converge:

\lambda_{s}:=\frac{n_{s}}{n}\in(0,1],\qquad\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}=1,

and hence

\frac{N_{s-1}}{n}=\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}\frac{n_{r}}{n}\longrightarrow\Lambda_{s-1}:=\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}\lambda_{r},\qquad s\in[S],

where
N_{0}:=0
and
\Lambda_{0}:=0
.

Expected optimal reward.
We first consider
\Gamma_{s-1}>0
for all
s
.
As
n\to\infty
, the maximum reward is either
\Gamma_{s-1}n
for some
s
, if a value

Y_{i}=n
appears first in phase
s
, or
y
if no such value appears.
For each phase
s\in[S]
, the probability that the first occurrence of
Y_{i}=n
falls in

\mathcal{T}_{s}
is

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=\Gamma_{s-1}n\right)=\big(1-(1-1/n^{2})^{n_{s}}\big)\,(1-1/n^{2})^{N_{s-1}}.

Using
n_{s}=\lambda_{s}n
and
N_{s-1}=O(n)
, a Taylor expansion gives

(1-1/n^{2})^{n_{s}}=1-\frac{n_{s}}{n^{2}}+o\bigl(1/n\bigr)=1-\frac{\lambda_{s}}{n}+o\bigl(1/n\bigr),

and

(1-1/n^{2})^{N_{s-1}}=1-\frac{N_{s-1}}{n^{2}}+o\bigl(1/n\bigr).

Hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=\Gamma_{s-1}n\right)=\frac{\lambda_{s}}{n}+o\bigl(1/n\bigr).

Similarly,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=y\right)=(1-1/n^{2})^{n}=1-\frac{1}{n}+o\bigl(1/n\bigr).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}n\,\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=\Gamma_{s-1}n\right)+y\,\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=y\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}y+\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}\Gamma_{s-1}.

Recall

C_{S}:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}\Gamma_{s-1},

so that, asymptotically,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]\;\to\;y+C_{S}.

The same result can be obtained for the case when
\Gamma_{s-1}=0
for some
s
with
\Gamma_{0}:=1
.

Expected performance of a single-threshold algorithm.

We consider a single-threshold policy with
0\leq p\leq 1
that accepts
X_{i}
if

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>\alpha)=p
for some
\alpha\geq 0
.
For
i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}
, let
R_{i}(p)
denote the expected reward conditional on
acceptance at stage
i
. If
p\leq 1/n^{2}
, then the threshold is so high that acceptance can only occur when

Y_{i}=n
, hence
R_{i}(p)=\Gamma_{s-1}n
and

pR_{i}(p)=p\,\Gamma_{s-1}n\leq\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}.

If
p>1/n^{2}
, then both values
n
and
y
are allowed to be accepted, and a direct computation yields

R_{i}(p)=\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}n}{n^{2}}+\Gamma_{s-1}y\bigl(p-1/n^{2}\bigr)\right),

so that

pR_{i}(p)=\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}y\Bigl(p-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Bigr)\leq\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp.

Combining both regimes, we obtain the unified bound

pR_{i}(p)\;\leq\;\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp\qquad\text{for all }p\in[0,1],\;i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}.

(42)

Hence the expected reward of the single-threshold algorithm satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}pR_{i}(p)(1-p)^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}\Bigl(\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp\Bigr)(1-p)^{i-1}.

(43)

For each phase
s
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{s}=\{N_{s-1}+1,\dots,N_{s-1}+n_{s}\}
and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}(1-p)^{i-1}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{s}-1}(1-p)^{j}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}}{1-(1-p)}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}}{p}.

Substituting into (
43
) yields

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\;\leq\;\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Bigl(\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp\Bigr)(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}}{p},

(44)

valid for all
p\in(0,1]
.

Asymptotic parametrization
p=x/n
.
In what follows, we first show that any sequence of single-threshold policies with non-vanishing asymptotic worst-case
competitive ratio on this hard family must satisfy
x=\Theta(1)
, which justifies restricting attention to the parametrization

with fixed

.

Lemma 18

(Reduction to the scale
p=\Theta(1/n)
)
.

Let
\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p,y)
denote the competitive ratio of a single-threshold policy whose
(per-stage) acceptance probability is
p=p_{n}\in(0,1]
on this instance, i.e.,

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p,y):=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}]}.

Define
x_{n}:=np_{n}
. Then:

1.

If
x_{n}\to 0
, then

\inf_{1/n\leq y\leq\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},y)\longrightarrow 0.

In particular, the choice
p_{n}=1/n^{2}
satisfies
x_{n}=1/n\to 0
and hence has vanishing worst-case
competitive ratio on this hard family.

2.

If
x_{n}\to\infty
, then

\inf_{1/n\leq y\leq\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},y)\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

Recall the bound (
44
): for every
p\in(0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\;\leq\;\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Bigl(\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp\Bigr)(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\,\frac{1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}}{p}.

Also, from the earlier computation of the offline optimum,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]=y+C_{S}+o(1),\qquad C_{S}:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\lambda_{s}\Gamma_{s-1}\in(0,1].

Case 1:
x_{n}=np_{n}\to 0
.

Choose
y=\sqrt{n}
. Using
(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\leq 1
and
1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}\leq n_{s}p
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Bigl(\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp\Bigr)\,n_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}\frac{n_{s}}{n}\;+\;yp\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}n_{s}

\displaystyle=C_{S}+o(1)\;+\;y\,(np)\,(C_{S}+o(1))\;=\;C_{S}+o(1)\;+\;\sqrt{n}\,x_{n}\,(C_{S}+o(1)).

Since
\mathbb{E}[\max_{i}X_{i}]=\sqrt{n}+C_{S}+o(1)
, we obtain

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},\sqrt{n})\;\leq\;\frac{C_{S}+o(1)+\sqrt{n}\,x_{n}\,(C_{S}+o(1))}{\sqrt{n}+C_{S}+o(1)}\;\leq\;x_{n}C_{S}\;+\;\frac{C_{S}}{\sqrt{n}}\;+\;o(1)\;\longrightarrow\;0.

Hence
\inf_{y}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},y)\to 0
.

Case 2:
x_{n}=np_{n}\to\infty
.

Choose
y=1/n
. Then

\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp=\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}\Bigl(1+p\Bigr)\leq\frac{2\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}.

Plugging into (
44
) and using
1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}\leq 1
yields

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\leq\frac{2}{np}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}.

For
s=1
,
N_{0}=0
so the term equals
\Gamma_{0}=1
.
For
s\geq 2
, we have
N_{s-1}\geq n_{1}
, hence

(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}\leq(1-p)^{n_{1}}\leq\exp(-n_{1}p)=\exp(-(\lambda_{1}+o(1))x_{n})\to 0.

Therefore
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}=1+o(1)
and thus

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\leq\frac{2+o(1)}{np}=\frac{2+o(1)}{x_{n}}\longrightarrow 0.

Meanwhile
\mathbb{E}[\max_{i}X_{i}]=y+C_{S}+o(1)=C_{S}+o(1)
for

, so

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},1/n)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i}X_{i}]}\leq\frac{(2+o(1))/x_{n}}{C_{S}+o(1)}\longrightarrow 0.

Hence
\inf_{y}\mathrm{CR}_{n}(p_{n},y)\to 0
.

Combining the two cases proves the claim and justifies restricting to
p=x/n
with fixed
x\in(0,\infty)
.
∎

From the above lemma, we set
p=x/n
with fixed

and let
n\to\infty
.
Using

and
N_{s-1}=(\sum_{r<s}\lambda_{r})n

(1-p)^{N_{s-1}}=\Bigl(1-\frac{x}{n}\Bigr)^{N_{s-1}}\longrightarrow\exp\bigl(-x\Lambda_{s-1}\bigr),

1-(1-p)^{n_{s}}=1-\Bigl(1-\frac{x}{n}\Bigr)^{n_{s}}\longrightarrow 1-e^{-x\lambda_{s}},

and

\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}yp=\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}+\Gamma_{s-1}y\frac{x}{n}=\frac{\Gamma_{s-1}}{n}(1+yx).

The factor

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\;\leq\;\frac{1+yx}{x}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}\exp\bigl(-x\Lambda_{s-1}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-x\lambda_{s}}\bigr)+o(1).

Define the phase-weighted function

A_{S}(x):=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Gamma_{s-1}\exp\bigl(-x\Lambda_{s-1}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-x\lambda_{s}}\bigr),\qquad x>0.

(45)

Then, asymptotically,

Dividing by the optimal expected reward

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)\;\leq\;\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{x}+y\bigr)A_{S}(x)}{y+C_{S}}+o(1),\qquad x>0,\;y>0.

Step 1: Worst-case in

Note that

If

•

If

If

Step 2: Properties of

.
We now show that

From (

Step 3: Maximizing

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{{DP}}}].

Proof.

We give a standard Snell-envelope argument and then connect it to the scalar recursion above.

(A) Define the Snell envelope.

Let
Z_{i}:=X_{i}
. Define the process
(W_{i})_{i=1}^{n+1}
by

W_{n+1}:=0,\qquad W_{i}:=\max\Big\{Z_{i},\ \mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\Big\},\qquad i=n,\ldots,1.

(48)

Then
W_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i}
-measurable and satisfies
W_{i}\geq Z_{i}
almost surely for all
i

(B)
(W_{i})
is a supermartingale.

By construction,

W_{i}\ \geq\ \mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}],

so
(W_{i})_{i=1}^{n+1}
is a supermartingale.

(C) Any stopping rule is dominated by
W_{1}
.

Let
\tau
be any
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
-stopping time with values in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since
Z_{\tau}\leq W_{\tau}

a.s., it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau}]
. Because
(W_{i})
is a supermartingale and
\tau\leq n
is bounded, we may write

\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\big[(W_{i+1}-W_{i})\mathbf{1}\{\tau>i\}\big].

Now
\mathbf{1}\{\tau>i\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i}
-measurable, hence

\mathbb{E}\big[(W_{i+1}-W_{i})\mathbf{1}\{\tau>i\}\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}\{\tau>i\}\,\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}-W_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\Big]\leq 0,

since
\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]-W_{i}\leq 0
. Therefore
\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]
,
and thus

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{\tau}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[W_{\tau}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[W_{1}].

(49)

(D) DP attains
W_{1}

Define the stopping time

\tau^{\star}:=\inf\{\,i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:Z_{i}=W_{i}\,\}.

On the event
\{i<\tau^{\star}\}
we have
Z_{i}<W_{i}
, hence by (
48

W_{i}=\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\qquad\text{on }\{i<\tau^{\star}\}.

This implies the stopped process
(W_{i\wedge\tau^{\star}})_{i=1}^{n+1}
is a martingale, and since

\tau^{\star}\leq n
is bounded,

\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}].

Moreover, by definition of
\tau^{\star}
we have
W_{\tau^{\star}}=Z_{\tau^{\star}}=X_{\tau^{\star}}
almost surely.
Hence

\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau^{\star}}].

(50)

Combining (
49
) and (
50

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{DP}}]\qquad\text{for all stopping rules }\tau.

∎

Conclusion.

From the above results, we conclude that for any stopping rule
\tau

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau_{DP}).

Moreover, in the joint limit

,
\widetilde{\gamma}\uparrow 1
, and
S\to\infty

such that
\widetilde{\gamma}^{S}\to 0

\mathrm{CR}_{n}(\tau_{DP})\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}.

Propositions for optimal competitive ratios in the classical infinite-horizon setting

Proposition 20

(Stationary i.i.d. infinite horizon: attainability up to
\varepsilon
)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be i.i.d. with distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
finite essential supremum
M:=\operatorname{ess\,sup}X_{1}<\infty
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a stopping rule
\tau_{\varepsilon}
such that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}]}{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}]}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

In particular, the optimal competitive ratio in this stationary infinite-horizon
setting equals
1
.

Proof.

Since
M=\operatorname{ess\,sup}X_{1}<\infty
, we have
\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}=M
almost surely,
hence
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}]=M
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define
a_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon)M
.
By definition of essential supremum,
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>a_{\varepsilon})>0
.
Let

\tau_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{i\geq 1:X_{i}>a_{\varepsilon}\}.

Then
\tau_{\varepsilon}<\infty
almost surely and, on the event
\{\tau_{\varepsilon}=i\}
,
we have
X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}>a_{\varepsilon}
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}]\;\geq\;a_{\varepsilon}\;=\;M(1-\varepsilon).

Dividing by
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}]=M
yields

\mathrm{CR}_{\infty}(\tau_{\varepsilon})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}]}{M}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, the optimal competitive ratio equals
1
.
∎

Proposition 21

(Infinite horizon, non-identical rewards)
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a sequence of independent but non-identically distributed rewards

(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
such that, for any stopping rule
\tau
,

\mathrm{CR}_{\infty}(\tau)\leq\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Define the reward sequence as follows:

X_{1}=1\quad\text{a.s.},

X_{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\varepsilon},&\text{with probability }\varepsilon,\\
0,&\text{with probability }1-\varepsilon,\end{cases}

X_{i}=0\quad\text{a.s.}\qquad\text{for all }i\geq 3.

The prophet benchmark is

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}]=(1-\varepsilon)\cdot 1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}=2-\varepsilon.

Consider any stopping rule
\tau
.
If
\tau=1
, then
\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=1
.
If
\tau=2
, then

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=(1-\varepsilon)\cdot 0+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}=1.

Any stopping rule that stops after time

yields zero reward. Any (possibly randomized) stopping rule induces a convex combination of stopping
at times
1
,
2
, or later; since rewards after time

are zero and

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]=1
, linearity of expectation implies

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\leq 1
. Therefore, for any stopping rule
\tau

\mathrm{CR}_{\infty}(\tau)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}]}\leq\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}.

A.9
Proof of Theorem
10
: Impossibility bound for continuous decay

We analyze the continuous-decay (unit-phase) model on an
infinite horizon
.
We first upper bound the competitive ratio achieved by the dynamic programming (DP) policy

\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}
on a hard instance family. We then use optimality of DP among all stopping
rules to conclude that no stopping policy can achieve a larger competitive ratio on this family.

Setup (unit phases, geometric decay, infinite horizon).

Set
S=n=\infty
and
n_{s}\equiv 1
, so the phases are singletons

\mathcal{T}_{s}=\{s\},\qquad s=1,2,\ldots

Fix a common decay factor
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)
and define

X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}Y_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots

Consider the two-point “hard” base distribution, parameterized by
N>1
:

Y_{i}=\begin{cases}N&\text{with probability }q:=1/N,\\
y&\text{with probability }1-q,\end{cases}\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,

where
y\in[0,N)
is a parameter. Let
r:=1-q
.

Throughout this proof, the prophet benchmark is

\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}:=\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\leq z)\Bigr)\,dz.

Dynamic programming values (infinite horizon via truncation).

For each truncation level
T\in\mathbb{N}
, define the finite-horizon DP values

V_{T+1}^{(T)}:=0,\qquad V_{i}^{(T)}:=\mathbb{E}\big[\max\{X_{i},V_{i+1}^{(T)}\}\big],\qquad i=T,T-1,\ldots,1.

The sequence
(V_{i}^{(T)})_{T\geq i}
is nondecreasing in
T
, and is uniformly bounded by
N
.
Hence the limit

V_{i}:=\lim_{T\to\infty}V_{i}^{(T)}

exists for each fixed
i
. We refer to
(V_{i})_{i\geq 1}
as the infinite-horizon DP values.

Step 1: DP always accepts upon seeing
Y=N
.

Fix
i\geq 1
. If
Y_{i}=N
, then
X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}N
. For any
j\geq i+1
,

X_{j}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,j-1}Y_{j}\leq\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,j-1}N\leq\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}N=X_{i}.

Therefore the continuation value satisfies
V_{i+1}\leq X_{i}
, and it is optimal to stop.

Step 2: DP reduces to a time-cutoff rule on
y
.

The only nontrivial decision is what to do upon observing
Y_{i}=y
.
Conditioning on the event that the process has not stopped before time
i
and that no
N

has appeared so far, the observed history is a deterministic string of
y
’s and carries no
additional information (by independence). Thus the optimal decision upon observing
Y_{i}=y

depends only on the time index
i
.

Consequently, DP is captured by a cutoff time
K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
:
continue upon observing
y
for all
i<K
, and stop at time
K
(if no
N
has appeared)
taking
\widetilde{\gamma}^{K-1}y
. Equivalently, it suffices to consider the family of stopping
times
\{\tau_{K}\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
:

\tau_{K}:=\min\Big\{\,\min\{i\leq K:Y_{i}=N\},\ K\Big\},\qquad K\in\mathbb{N},

and

\tau_{\infty}:=\min\{i\geq 1:Y_{i}=N\}.

The infinite-horizon DP optimal stopping time satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}\in\arg\max_{K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{K}}].

Step 3: Exact expected reward of
\tau_{K}
.

Recall
r=1-q=1-1/N
. For
K\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Pr(\text{no $N$ occurs in times }1,\ldots,K)=r^{K},\qquad\Pr(\text{first $N$ occurs at time }s)=r^{s-1}q.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{K}}]

\displaystyle=r^{K}\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{K-1}y\;+\;\sum_{s=1}^{K}r^{s-1}q\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N.

(64)

For
K=\infty
we obtain the geometric-series identity

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\infty}}]=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}r^{s-1}q\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N=\frac{qN}{1-r\widetilde{\gamma}}.

(65)

Step 4: The optimal cutoff is
K=1
or
K=\infty
.

Using (
64
), for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{K+1}}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{K}}]

\displaystyle=r^{K+1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{K}y-r^{K}\widetilde{\gamma}^{K-1}y\;+\;r^{K}q\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{K}N

\displaystyle=r^{K}\widetilde{\gamma}^{K-1}\Big(\widetilde{\gamma}N(1-r)+y(\widetilde{\gamma}r-1)\Big).

Since
r^{K}\widetilde{\gamma}^{K-1}\geq 0
, the sign is determined by

D_{N}(y):=\widetilde{\gamma}N(1-r)+y(\widetilde{\gamma}r-1),

which does not depend on
K
. Therefore
\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{K}}]
is monotone in
K
, and the
optimizer satisfies

K^{\star}\in\{1,\infty\}\qquad(\text{ties possible}).

Step 5: Asymptotics of the two DP endpoints as
N\to\infty
.

Recall
q=1/N
and
r=1-q
. For
K=1
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{1}}]=ry+qN=\Big(1-\frac{1}{N}\Big)y+1.

(66)

For
K=\infty
, by (
65
),

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\infty}}]=\frac{qN}{1-r\widetilde{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}+\widetilde{\gamma}/N}=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big).

(67)

Step 6: Asymptotics of
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]

The worst-case regime is
y=\Theta(1)
. Accordingly, set

y={\alpha}\qquad\text{for some constant }\alpha\geq 0.

Let
T:=\min\{i\geq 1:Y_{i}=N\}
denote the first arrival time of
N
, so

\Pr(T=s)=r^{s-1}q
. Since
\max_{i<T}\widetilde{\gamma}^{i-1}y=y
(the largest discounted
y
is at time

),
we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}r^{s-1}q\,\max\{y,\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N\}.

(68)

Define the index

s_{0}:=\min\Big\{s\geq 1:\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N<y\Big\},

\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N\geq y

and
\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N<y
for
s\geq s_{0}
.
Then (
68

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=y\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{\infty}r^{s-1}q\;+\;\sum_{s=1}^{s_{0}-1}r^{s-1}q\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{s-1}N=y\,r^{s_{0}-1}\;+\;qN\sum_{s=1}^{s_{0}-1}(r\widetilde{\gamma})^{s-1}.

(69)

Note that
s_{0}=O(\log N)
.
Hence
r^{s_{0}-1}=(1-1/N)^{O(\log N)}=1+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big)
, so

y\,r^{s_{0}-1}=y+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big).

Moreover,

qN\sum_{s=1}^{s_{0}-1}(r\widetilde{\gamma})^{s-1}=\frac{qN}{1-r\widetilde{\gamma}}\Big(1-(r\widetilde{\gamma})^{s_{0}-1}\Big)=\frac{qN}{1-r\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),

(r\widetilde{\gamma})^{s_{0}-1}\leq\widetilde{\gamma}^{s_{0}-1}<\alpha/N
.
Combining with (

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=y+\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big)=\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big).

(70)

Step 7: Limiting competitive ratios and worst-case
y
.

With
y=\alpha
, (
66
), (
67
), and (
70
) give

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{1}}]=\alpha+1+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),\qquad\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\infty}}]=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big).

Therefore the limiting competitive ratios for
K=1
and
K=\infty
are

A(\alpha):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}(\tau_{1})=\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}},\qquad B(\alpha):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}(\tau_{\infty})=\frac{\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}}{\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha(1-\widetilde{\gamma})}.

Since
\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}
chooses the better endpoint,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}(\tau_{\mathrm{DP}})=\max\{A(\alpha),B(\alpha)\}.

For
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)
,
A(\alpha)
is increasing and
B(\alpha)
is decreasing in
\alpha
,
so
\max\{A(\alpha),B(\alpha)\}
is minimized at
A(\alpha)=B(\alpha)
, i.e.,

\alpha=\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}.

Thus the worst-case parameter is
y^{\star}=\alpha=\widetilde{\gamma}/(1-\widetilde{\gamma})
and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{CR}(\tau_{\mathrm{DP}})=\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}}.

Step 8: Optimality of DP (DP dominates any stopping rule on each instance).

Let
\mathcal{F}_{i}:=\sigma(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i})=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i})
be the natural filtration.
A stopping rule is an
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
-stopping time
\tau
taking values in
\mathbb{N}
; we also allow

\tau=\infty
with payoff
X_{\infty}:=0
.

In our instance,
0\leq Y_{i}\leq N
a.s., hence
0\leq X_{i}\leq N
a.s. and
X_{i}\to 0
a.s. since

X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{i-1}Y_{i}
with
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)
.

(A) Finite-horizon truncations.

Fix a horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the truncated problem on times
\{1,\ldots,T\}
.
Define the finite-horizon Snell envelope
(W_{i}^{(T)})_{i=1}^{T+1}
by

W_{T+1}^{(T)}:=0,\qquad W_{i}^{(T)}:=\max\Big\{X_{i},\ \mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}^{(T)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\Big\},\qquad i=T,\ldots,1.

(71)

Let the corresponding finite-horizon optimal stopping time be

\tau^{(T)}:=\inf\{\,i\in\{1,\ldots,T\}:X_{i}=W_{i}^{(T)}\,\},\qquad(\inf\emptyset:=T).

By the standard finite-horizon Snell-envelope theorem, we have for every
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
-stopping time

\sigma
with values in
\{1,\ldots,T\}
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{(T)}]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\tau^{(T)}}].

(72)

In particular, the finite-horizon DP rule
\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}^{(T)}
attains
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{(T)}]
; in our
independent setting,
\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}^{(T)}
coincides with
\tau^{(T)}
. Define the finite-horizon optimal value

V_{1}^{(T)}:=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{(T)}]=\sup_{\sigma\leq T}\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}],

where
\sigma\leq T
means
\sigma
takes values in
\{1,\ldots,T\}
.

(B) Domination for an arbitrary (possibly unbounded) stopping rule.

Let
\tau
be any
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
-stopping time taking values in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For each
T
, the truncated time
\tau\wedge T
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
-stopping time taking values in

\{1,\ldots,T\}
. Applying (
72
) with
\sigma=\tau\wedge T
yields

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau\wedge T}]\ \leq\ V_{1}^{(T)}\qquad\text{for every }T\in\mathbb{N}.

(73)

We now pass to the limit
T\to\infty
on the left-hand side.
On the event
\{\tau<\infty\}
, we have
\tau\wedge T=\tau
for all
T\geq\tau
, hence

X_{\tau\wedge T}\to X_{\tau}
a.s.
On the event
\{\tau=\infty\}
, we have
X_{\tau\wedge T}=X_{T}\to 0=X_{\infty}=X_{\tau}
a.s.
Therefore
X_{\tau\wedge T}\to X_{\tau}
almost surely.

Moreover,
0\leq X_{\tau\wedge T}\leq N
a.s. for all
T
, so by dominated convergence,

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau\wedge T}]\ \xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}].

(74)

(C) Monotone limit of the truncated values.

Since the set of admissible stopping times grows with
T
,

V_{1}^{(T)}=\sup_{\sigma\leq T}\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}]\ \leq\ \sup_{\sigma\leq T+1}\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}]=V_{1}^{(T+1)}.

Thus
(V_{1}^{(T)})_{T\geq 1}
is nondecreasing and bounded above by
N
, hence the limit

V_{1}:=\lim_{T\to\infty}V_{1}^{(T)}\in[0,N]

exists. Combining (
73
) with (
74
) and letting
T\to\infty
gives

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\ \leq\ V_{1}\qquad\text{for every stopping rule }\tau.

(75)

In particular,
V_{1}
is an upper bound on the optimal infinite-horizon value

\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]
.

Conversely,
V_{1}^{(T)}\leq\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]
for every
T
, so taking
T\to\infty
yields

V_{1}\leq\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]
. Hence

V_{1}=\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}],

i.e.,
V_{1}
is the infinite-horizon optimal value.

(D) Existence of an optimal DP stopping time attaining
V_{1}
.

Define the infinite-horizon Snell envelope as the monotone limit

W_{i}:=\lim_{T\to\infty}W_{i}^{(T)},\qquad i\geq 1,

which exists a.s. because
W_{i}^{(T)}
is nondecreasing in
T
for each fixed
i
(this follows directly
from the recursion (
71
) by backward induction) and
0\leq W_{i}^{(T)}\leq N
.

Define the candidate infinite-horizon optimal stopping time

\tau^{\star}:=\inf\{\,i\geq 1:\ X_{i}=W_{i}\,\},

with the convention that
\tau^{\star}=\infty
if the set is empty.

On the event
\{i<\tau^{\star}\}
we have
X_{i}<W_{i}
, hence necessarily

W_{i}=\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\qquad\text{on }\{i<\tau^{\star}\},

because
W_{i}=\max\{X_{i},\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]\}
(this identity follows by taking

T\to\infty
in (
71
) using conditional monotone convergence).
Therefore the stopped process
(W_{i\wedge\tau^{\star}})_{i\geq 1}
is a martingale.

Applying the optional stopping theorem to the bounded stopping time
\tau^{\star}\wedge T
yields

\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}\wedge T}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]\qquad\text{for all }T\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
0\leq W_{\tau^{\star}\wedge T}\leq N
and
W_{\tau^{\star}\wedge T}\to W_{\tau^{\star}}
a.s.,
dominated convergence gives
\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}\wedge T}]\to\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}}]
.
Thus

\mathbb{E}[W_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}].

By definition of
\tau^{\star}
, we have
W_{\tau^{\star}}=X_{\tau^{\star}}
a.s. (and on

\{\tau^{\star}=\infty\}
we use
X_{\infty}=0
).
Hence

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}].

Taking expectations in
W_{1}=\lim_{T}W_{1}^{(T)}
and using monotone convergence yields

\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{(T)}]=\lim_{T\to\infty}V_{1}^{(T)}=V_{1}.

Therefore
\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau^{\star}}]=V_{1}=\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]
, so
\tau^{\star}
is optimal.

Finally, in our setting with independent rewards and deterministic decay, the conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}[W_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]
is deterministic and equals
V_{i+1}
, so the optimal rule can be written
as the DP threshold rule

\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}=\inf\{\,i\geq 1:\ X_{i}\geq V_{i+1}\,\}.

(E) Conclusion.

Combining (
75
) with
\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}}]=V_{1}
gives

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}}]\qquad\text{for every stopping rule }\tau,

so DP dominates any stopping rule on this (infinite-horizon) instance family.

Conclusion.

For every
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)
and every
\varepsilon>0
, choosing
N
sufficiently large in the
above hard instance gives

\mathrm{CR}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{CR}(\tau_{\mathrm{DP}})\leq\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}}+\varepsilon.

Hence no stopping policy can guarantee a competitive ratio strictly larger than
1/(1+\widetilde{\gamma})

in the
S=n=\infty
continuous-decay setting.

A.10
Proof of Theorem
11
: Lower bound under
continuous decay

For simplicity we assume
\mathcal{D}
is atomless; for distributions with atoms, the same bound
follows by randomized tie-breaking (cf. Remark
17
).

Setup (
S=n=\infty
, unit phases).

Let
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,1)
and define
X_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}Y_{i}
for
i\geq 1
, where

(Y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
with CDF
F
and
\mathbb{E}[Y]<\infty
.
Define the infinite-horizon prophet benchmark

\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}:=\sup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}.

The effective horizon equals

B_{\infty}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}}.

Let
\alpha_{1}
be chosen so that

\mathbb{P}(Y>\alpha_{1})=\frac{1}{B_{\infty}}=1-\widetilde{\gamma},\qquad\text{equivalently }F(\alpha_{1})=\widetilde{\gamma},

and set the phase-
i
threshold
\alpha_{i}:=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}\alpha_{1}
.
The policy
\tau
stops at the first time
i
such that
X_{i}>\alpha_{i}
.

Step 1: Compute
\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]
(geometric series).

Since
X_{i}>\alpha_{i}
iff
Y_{i}>\alpha_{1}
, the stopping time is

\tau=\inf\{i\geq 1:Y_{i}>\alpha_{1}\}.

Using independence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{i}\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}>\alpha_{1}\}\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{j}\leq\alpha_{1}\ \forall j<i\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\alpha_{1})^{i-1}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,i-1}Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(\widetilde{\gamma}F(\alpha_{1})\big)^{i-1}.

Since
F(\alpha_{1})=\widetilde{\gamma}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\gamma}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right]=\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}}\,B_{\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right].

(76)

Step 2: Relate
B_{\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}]
to the prophet.

We use two bounds.

(2a) A truncation inequality for the fractional maximum.

For any
b\geq 1
and any
\alpha\geq 0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F(z)^{b}\bigr)\,dz\leq\alpha+b\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}(1-F(z))\,dz=\alpha+b\,\mathbb{E}[(Y-\alpha)_{+}].

Indeed, on
[0,\alpha]
the integrand is at most
1
, and on
[\alpha,\infty)
we use

1-u^{b}\leq b(1-u)
for
u\in[0,1]
.
If
\alpha
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(Y>\alpha)=1/b
, then

b\,\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha\}]=\alpha+b\mathbb{E}[(Y-\alpha)_{+}]
, so

b\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha\}\right]\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F(z)^{b}\bigr)\,dz.

(77)

Applying (
77
) with
b=B_{\infty}
and
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
gives

B_{\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right]\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F(z)^{B_{\infty}}\bigr)\,dz.

(78)

(2b) Multistage comparison (
S=\infty
, unit phases).

By the infinite-stage extension of Lemma
3
for

\Gamma_{s-1}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}
and
n_{s}\equiv 1
(from the monotone convergence theorem),

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F(z)^{B_{\infty}}\bigr)\,dz\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}F\bigl(z/\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dz.

(79)

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\leq z)=\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}F\bigl(z/\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}\bigr),

so by the tail-integral identity,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}F\bigl(z/\widetilde{\gamma}^{\,s-1}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dz.

(80)

Combining (
78
)–(
80
) yields

B_{\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y\,\mathbbm{1}\{Y>\alpha_{1}\}\right]\ \geq\ \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}].

(81)

Step 3: Competitive ratio.

Combining (
76
) with (
81
) gives

\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}],

so

\mathrm{CR}_{\infty}(\tau)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}},\qquad 0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}<1.

This concludes the proof.
