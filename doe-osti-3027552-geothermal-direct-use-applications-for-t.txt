Executive Summary
The city of Bucharest, Romania, hosts the second-largest district energy system (DES) in the
world. Geothermal resources can be considered as a supplementary heat source to support the
demand for domestic hot water and space heating in the winter and shoulder seasons. The
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) has conducted a study that considers geothermal
energy to serve a fraction of the existing district heating network operated by Electrocentrale
București (ELCEN), the utility operating the DES. Lower Cretaceous and Jurassic limestones
make up the main geothermal aquifer underlying Bucharest, which hosts temperatures suitable
for district heating (up to 90°C to the north of the city). Anomalous geothermal gradients have
been observed to the north of the city, where a pumped well has produced 82°C brine at the
wellhead to feed the Therme Bucharest Spa. An anomalous gradient has also been reported to the
southeast of the city (35°C/km). NLR modeled the building heating loads of a small portion of
the DES (a block of nine prototypical buildings) in its Urban Renewable Building and
Neighborhood Optimization (URBANopt™) platform. To simulate meeting a baseload
benchmark of 20 MWₜₕ deliverable to a small portion of the DES, the NLR team used
GEOPHIRES to model production scenarios for (1) hydrothermal systems coupled with heat
pumps targeting the main geothermal aquifer in the north, (2) enhanced geothermal systems
targeting hot dry rock in the southeast, and (3) huff-and-puff systems targeting a gradient of
25ºC/km. Finally, NLR conducted a high-level sensitivity study around the techno-economics of
these systems. Based on the results of this study, hot dry rock geothermal resources that can
deliver at least 90°C hot water to the GeoDES are sensitive to the critical role of infrastructure
and geological parameters in determining the LCOH of operating and installing a GeoDES. The
LCOH may be improved by targeting suitable subsurface parameters with elevated gradients,
and by reducing distribution pipe capital cost. Further investigation into country rock lithology,
permeability, porosity, and thermophysical properties deeper than 2 km is required to update
simulated heat flow and improve the study. Planned future work could involve updating the end-
use efficiency factor to 80% and accounting for the cost of replacing pipe within the district
energy system and updating heat pump scenarios to refine calculations of heat pump energy use
in GEOPHIRES.

1 Introduction
The Geothermal District Energy Systems Feasibility Study for Romania investigates the
feasibility of generating baseload geothermal heat for the existing district energy system (DES)
in the city of Bucharest, Romania, in coordination with Electrocentrale București (ELCEN). For
reference, the total installed capacity of ELCEN’s four combined thermal and electric zones
(CTEs)/centralized thermal zones (CTZs)—which serve approximately 550,000 apartments in
the capital and more than 4,100 companies and public institutions on the existing district heating
network—was 1,450 Gcal/h (approximately 5,750 MMBTU/h) as of 2024 (ELCEN 2024b). The
geothermal district energy system (GeoDES) can be integrated at the nearest CTE or CTZ; these
already exist on the system.

Bucharest is located near Ilfov County, which hosts the partially delimited (300-km²) Otopeni
hydrothermal resource found in the Lower Cretaceous and Upper Jurassic sediments (fissured,
locally karstified limestones and dolomites) to the north of the city. As reported on a field visit to
Bucharest in 2024, a well is currently producing 82°C at 15 L/s, with 25 L/s achievable, for the
Therme Bucharest Spa, which is 16 km from the city center by road. Flow rates are steadily
maintained to avoid particulate fouling. Historical production from the Otopeni hydrothermal
resource shows flow rates that range from 12–35 L/s (Petrică 2016; Bendea and Rosca 2015).
Downhole pumping was required for reported wells. The main geothermal aquifer is tilted and
locally fissured and/or karstic, deepening (e.g., to 3 km) to the north.

Optimistically, less permeable rock may be accessed at shallower depths south of the city. An
optimal target for enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) prior to the current study was the
Precambrian bedrock. However, in all oil and gas structures reported across Bucharest that have
associated lithological logs, no encounters with the top of the Precambrian bedrock were
reported; therefore, the depths-to-bedrock across the city are currently unknown. To capture the
unique geothermal resource potential around Bucharest, we (the National Laboratory of the
Rockies [NLR] team) simulated, for a heat flow analysis, two hydrothermal scenarios to the
north, two EGS scenarios to the southwest and southeast, and one huff-and-puff EGS scenario.
We also conducted a high-level techno-economic study.

Given the subsurface parameters and existence of both a hydrothermal system and conductive
domains throughout the basin, the following geothermal technologies are considered to support
the DES:

 • Hydrothermal Wells: in locations where permeability is sufficient (to the North of the
 city, where the hydrothermal Balotesti well exists), production from a conventional
 hydrothermal resource is considered. Wells producing from the resource are considered
 “open-loop.” These wells are not ground source heat pumps and are drilled to/completed
 in the reservoir formation, which is assumed to have sufficient permeability to support
 this production. A heat pump has been considered for hydrothermal scenarios on the DES
 side to boost fluid temperatures to the 90ºC deliverable fluid temperature required by the
 DES.

 1

 • Enhanced Geothermal Systems: an enhanced geothermal system is a pair of wells
 consisting of one injection and one production well, connected at the target production
 depth with stimulated fractures. In domains where permeability is low enough for
 stimulation and creation of an engineered geothermal reservoir, these systems may
 provide >90°C geofluid temperatures, depending on the formation temperature at the
 target depth, the formation thermophysical and hydraulic properties, and the geothermal
 gradient.

 • Huff and Puff Enhanced Geothermal Systems: a cyclic geothermal operation where
 heated fluid (usually water or steam) is injected into an engineered geothermal reservoir
 through a well (huff phase), allowed to soak to transfer heat to surrounding rock and
 fluids (soak phase), and then produced back through the same well (puff phase) to extract
 thermal energy from the subsurface.

1.1 Subsurface Characterization
The Institute of Geology of Romania conducted initial subsurface characterization efforts. In the
resulting report, they documented two systems in the study region: conductive geothermal
systems, which are dominated by thermal conduction in rocks associated with higher subsurface
temperatures and heat flow, and convective geothermal systems, which are dominated by the
circulation of water through fissured or locally karstic carbonate rocks (Institute of Geology of
Romania, 2024).

The research conducted by the Institute of Geology of Romania was in the context of the main
geothermal aquifer, which, per the survey, is a convective (hydrothermal) domain (Institute of
Geology of Romania, 2024). Additionally, deep boreholes in the study area (Ilfov County and
Bucharest municipality) did not penetrate bedrock formations, though three boreholes in the east
were noted to have hit sericite-chlorite shales of the Lower Cambrian, and three wells drilled in
the northwest encountered amphibole shales with epidote and chlorite as well as quartzite, which
may indicate higher proximity to the crystalline and green shale Precambrian basement (Institute
of Geology of Romania 2024). The depths of these encounters were not reported. Thus, more
research must be done to determine the depth of basement rock (i.e., Precambrian) for EGS
targets, and whether the basement is accessible at <6-km depth.

1.1.3 Thermophysical Rock Properties
Thermophysical properties for the Cretaceous sediments in Bucharest were provided; they were
measured on borings collected in-situ and analyzed in a lab setting (Institute of Geology of
Romania 2024). Production from the hydrothermal reservoir at the Therme Balotești well was
from the Lower Cretaceous units at 3.1 km (Petrică 2016) and thus, this depth and location
serves as the target formation depth and location for one hydrothermal scenario. Another
hydrothermal scenario closer to the city targets the same formation top (Lower Cretaceous unit)

Additional rock property information are available for the permeable units (i.e., the Triassic,
Jurassic, and Cretaceous units), but minimal quantitative data are available for the lower units (i.e.,
the Carboniferous and Precambrian units). Thus, further research should be considered to
characterize the lower subsurface units and identify whether there are possible impermeable
formation targets for EGS shallower than the Precambrian bedrock.
For the purposes of this study, we have adopted the target reservoir as the Precambrian unit and
thus have assumed that the thermophysical properties are comparable to granite, as this is the
unit with the lowest permeability which would be better suited for EGS applications. Note that
sedimentary rocks with high permeability induce fluid losses in EGS systems and should be
avoided.

Crystalline formations such as granitic plutons have a higher average thermal conductivity and
heat capacity (mean 3.1 W/m/K and mean 1185 kJ/kg/K) than sedimentary rocks, which
improves thermal output of the system, but the depth to such formations on the local scale in
Bucharest is currently unknown (Schön 2011). In these simulations, we have assumed that the
bedrock surface follows the denudation of the basin (e.g., deeper in the north (5.7 km), shallower
in the south (3.5 km)). It is possible that depths between 3.5 and 6 km could intersect the
Carboniferous units, which may be more permeable, supporting further exploration of the
subsurface and careful documentation of high-risk areas. Properties used for modeling purposes
are provided in Table 2.

1.1.4 Structural Geology
During the Late Devonian to Early Triassic period, the Moesian Platform experienced Hercynian
deformation, forming large, moderately faulted anticlines that established its early structural
framework (Pawlewicz 2007). Subsequently, in the Early Jurassic, the platform was uplifted and
exposed subaerially before evolving into a north-facing Tethyan passive margin in the Late
Jurassic and Early Cretaceous, accumulating marine sediments during continuous subsidence
(Pawlewicz 2007). Between the Cretaceous and Miocene, the Moesian Platform collided with the
emerging Carpathian-Balkan orogens, a major tectonic event that shaped the Carpathian double-
bend and created thrust wedges along the platform margins while the interior remained relatively
stable (Stanciu and Ioane 2023; Pawlewicz 2007).

 6

From the Pliocene to the Quaternary, extensional tectonics influenced the platform, driven by
stresses related to the Carpathian double-bend formation. This resulted in various fault systems,
including northwest–southeast strike-slip faults, east–west normal faults parallel to orogens,
northeast–southwest faults linked to the East Carpathians bend, and north–south faults (Stanciu
and Ioane 2023).

In the mid-20th century and onward, geophysical investigations intensified, particularly seismic
studies and downhole well data, revealing the Moesian Platform’s crustal structure, with its
uplifted basement blocks (e.g., Central Dobrogea, Balș-Optași) and deep sedimentary basins
(e.g., Alexandria, Focșani, Getic Depressions; Stanciu and Ioane, 2023). Later, advanced gravity
geophysical methods were applied, merging Romanian and Bulgarian Bouguer gravity datasets
to map the Moesian Platform’s structural features regionally (Stanciu and Ioane 2021). The
residual gravity anomaly map generated from this regional study, derived from filtered Bouguer
gravity data, allowed detailed detection of the Intramoesian Fault’s segments crossing Romania
and Bulgaria, as well as other concealed tectonic features, including a northwest–southeast
graben structure east of Bucharest (Stanciu and Ioane 2021).

Other important regional faults interpreted include the Capidava-Ovidiu Fault and the
Peceneaga-Camena Fault in Dobrogea, which are known bisecting geological formations and
concealed structures beneath thick Quaternary cover. Modern seismic profiles, such as the
Vrancea surveys, indicated the platform’s deep crustal geometry and its interaction with
surrounding orogens (Stanciu and Ioane 2023). The Intramoesian Fault is now recognized as a
major transcrustal feature dividing Western and Eastern Moesia, influencing tectonic
deformation and basin architecture. Currently, the platform’s structure shows stepwise plunging
beneath the Carpathian and Balkan thrust wedges, controlled by east–west faulting, with ongoing
tectonic activity continuing to modify the landscape (Stanciu and Ioane 2023).

Geothermal anomalies north and east of Bucharest align with fault-controlled pathways along the
graben and Intramoesian Fault, where permeable zones enhance fluid flow and heat transfer,
contrasting with the more conductive, less permeable areas that may surround those zones. This
distinction in heat transfer mechanisms highlights the role of fault and graben architecture in
shaping the spatial distribution of geothermal activity across the Moesian Platform and near
Bucharest (Stanciu and Ioane 2021).

2 Heat Flow and System Load Modeling Approach
We considered multiple approaches to support the existing district energy system (DES) with
direct-use geothermal heat. First, we modeled a heating load profile to be served by the GeoDES
in Urban Renewable Building and Neighborhood Optimization (URBANopt™) to represent a
hypothetical block of nine buildings. This system was scaled up to reflect the desired load to be
served by the GeoDES.

2.1 GeoDES Load Profile Modeling
Heating load profiles are necessary to analyze the performance of geothermal applications (e.g.,
the 3 technologies described in the Introduction) as a source for the district heating system.
Given the scale of the system considered in this study, we created a load profile based on energy
modeling of a “synthetic block” of ten “prototypical” buildings, representing common building
types in Bucharest, and then scaled to the desired level of load (around 20 MWₜₕ). We selected
building types in the synthetic block based on an analysis of Romania’s building stock and
information gathered during NLR’s site visit to Bucharest. We used data from the Buildings
Performance Institute Europe (BPIE) to characterize Romania’s existing building stock and
adjusted the data to reflect expected conditions in Bucharest. Specifically, single-family homes
were removed from the Romania-wide building type distribution, as these are rare in Bucharest
(BPIE 2014). The adjusted distribution is shown in Figure 4. We selected representatives of the
following building types: office, clinic, retail, school, sports facility, and high- and low-rise
residential.

 Figure 4. Building type distribution by floor area for Romania, adjusted for Bucharest

We selected specific buildings based on a review of Google satellite images of the neighborhood
surrounding the historic Casa Presei complex, within an area served by a newly renovated

further refined using OpenStudio measures in the URBANopt workflow. OpenStudio measures

2.2.1 Hydrothermal Resource
To evaluate geothermal heat delivery from the Otopeni and Balotești hydrothermal resources, we
used GEOPHIRES v.3.9.8 (Beckers and Ross 2023) to simulate high-level hydrothermal
performance, assuming direct-use heat exchange at the surface and integration with the GeoDES.
All simulated doublet pairs were modeled as vertical wells with a uniform internal diameter of
8.5 inches, based on the wellbore configuration from the Therme Balotești vertical well (Nganga
et al. 2021; Petrică 2016). This is considered representative for both sites. Note that the goal of
this study was to meet the 20-MWₜₕ and 90°C benchmarks in the final year of the simulation,
given an expected lifetime of 30 years for hydrothermal production scenarios. Parameters are
displayed in Table 4.

Values are based on available data from the Institute of Geology of Romania (2024), site-specific
literature (e.g., Petrică 2016), and assumed values for a low-permeability sandstone formation
(Schön 2011). The model uses GEOPHIRES’ standard volume-only reservoir approach. Inlet
temperatures, flow rates, and well design reflect assumptions or analogs from the existing
hydrothermal well (e.g., Therme Balotești). The drawdown parameter assumes a minimum
temperature drop of 0.5°C annually. The average volume of the main geothermal aquifer was
calculated based on a modeled 600-m thickness with a 300-km² extent (Institute of Geology of
Romania 2024; Bendea and Rosca 2015).

The Otopeni and Balotești wells were modeled to depths of 2.3 km and 3.1 km, respectively,
reflecting the midpoint of the geothermal aquifer due to the absence of a distinct boundary
between the Lower Cretaceous and Jurassic units. Formation temperatures were estimated using
the local geothermal gradients reported at those locations (22°C–24°C/km). At Balotești, the
modeled wellhead temperature was also matched at 82°C, based on production from the existing

 11

Therme Balotești well. Heat transmission along the wellbore was calculated using the Ramey
model (Ramey 1962).
For both scenarios, we assumed a baseline production flow rate of 24 L/s, consistent with
reported hydrothermal well capacities in the Otopeni resource (12–35 L/s). Downhole circulation
pumps operating at 75% efficiency were included to maintain steady flow and prevent fouling.
Surface thermal losses were captured through an end-use efficiency of 60%, accounting for an
estimated 40% loss across the existing GeoDES infrastructure without replacing existing pipes.
A ~68% utilization factor was calculated in GEOPHIRES for direct use scenarios, assuming
consistent baseload geothermal supply for space heating and domestic hot water was delivered
and the scaled-up modeled load profile considered.

In all scenarios, the geothermal fluid is produced from the subsurface and transported to a central
surface plant, where heat is transferred to a secondary fluid loop via a plate heat exchanger. This
ensures that the geothermal brine and the district heating water remain hydraulically separated,
which is a standard design practice to prevent scaling, corrosion, and contamination in the
distribution network. This fluid is then carried down to the heat pump via distribution pipe and
lifted to 90°C, as is required by the DES.

In GEOPHIRES, heat losses between the DES and the plant itself are not modeled, as this is a
limitation in the software. In Modelica, the heat losses along the distribution pipe from the plant
to the DES were modeled. Results indicate the electricity consumption by the heat pump in
hydrothermal scenarios is conservatively modeled in GEOPHIRES (e.g., Modelica modeling
indicates ~5 MWth operational electricity load for the heat pump, and GEOPHIRES indicates ~9
MWth).

The secondary fluid, now carrying the extracted heat, is then piped from the surface plant to the
end-use site, where a heat pump elevates the temperature for space heating or domestic hot water
use. Heat loss inside of the DES and the distribution pipe from the plant to the site were modeled
in Modelica, resulting in a total heat loss of 10 to 20%, assuming the replacement of existing
DES infrastructure with insulated pipe. In the GEOPHIRES scenarios, a more conservative
estimate was made based on current heat loss inside of the DES, assuming replacement of the
infrastructure would not occur, and the 40% heat loss expected in the system that was shared
with us on a field visit to Bucharest in 2024, resulting in a 60% end-use efficiency factor
(Section 3).

The length of this transport route depends on the location: approximately 16 km for Balotești and
8 km for Otopeni to reach the heat pump and CTZ Casa Presei Libere. Again, the heat losses
through the distribution system to reach the heat pump have been modeled (see work in this
section presented after Figure 7). Additionally, the cost of the distribution pipe from the plant to
the DES has been considered in the high-level techno economic simulations in Section 3.

benchmark criteria for both hydrothermal cases. Assuming the district heating load is constant,
and supply temperature is fixed at 90°C, there should be a COP decrease and thus an increase in
electricity required to operate the heat pump. However, the heat produced from a hydrothermal
resource has been modeled to naturally decline over time in GEOPHIRES. The heat pump
electricity use to boost the outlet temperature declines over time because the heat produced at the
wellhead declines over time (e.g., heat pump output is controlled by geothermal supply).

2.2.2 Hot Dry Rock Resource
For the Glina and CTE București Vest locations, we modeled EGS doublets to assess the
potential for direct-use heat extraction from deeper, low-permeability formations. Subsurface
temperature estimates were constrained using corrected geothermal gradients from the Institute
of Geology of Romania (2024). Due to the limited subsurface data for deep units at both sites,
we had to make assumptions to complete EGS modeling. Each scenario followed the Gringarten
Multiple Fracture modeling approach (Gringarten et al. 1975). Note that these estimates are high-
level, considering the assumptions of the Gringarten model, and that the goal of this study was to
meet the 20-MWₜₕ and 90°C benchmarks in the final year of the simulation, given an assumed
20-year target lifetime for EGS doublets (Beckers et al. 2025).

Vertical wellbores extending to the reservoir depth were assumed for both sites, with thermal
losses along the wellbore calculated using the Ramey (1962) method. A single representative
doublet was simulated using design parameters informed by Fercho et al. (2025) and adapted to
match thermal decline constraints—specifically, limiting the outlet geofluid temperature decline
to no more than 20% over the project lifetime, consistent with modeled simulations from the
deep direct use (DDU) socioeconomic study and Fervo Cape Geothermal Project (Fercho et al.
2025; Beckers et al. 2025). The effective heat transfer area and number of fractures along the
lateral wellbore segments (1.5 km) were adjusted accordingly, assuming uniform well spacing of
15 m.

This refined EGS doublet system was then scaled up to meet the 20-MWₜₕ benchmark. The Glina
and CTE București Vest wells were modeled to depths of 3.5 km and 5.8 km, respectively,
targeting resources that would yield an ending temperature of 90°C by the last year of the 20-
year simulation. Reinjection temperatures were assumed 60°C for Glina and 50°C for CTE
București Vest, due to the higher supply temperature from Glina, with a uniform flow rate of 40
kg/s at each location (Table 7).

2.2.3 Huff-and-Puff Heat Flow Modeling
HeatCycle, developed by Sage Geosystems, is a novel geothermal technology designed for hot
dry rock formations and assessed here for applicability to Bucharest’s low-permeability
subsurface (Sage Geosystems, Personal Communication). Unlike EGS, which depend on
interconnected fracture networks, and closed-loop systems, which use sealed conduits,
HeatCycle employs a single-well configuration per reservoir. The system operates slightly above
fracture opening pressures, enhancing fluid dispersion while minimizing friction losses (Figure
12).

A defining feature is the use of gravity-driven fractures, created by injecting heavy barite mud to
promote downward fracture propagation. Barite is replaced with water during operations, which
is cyclically injected and produced in a “huff-and-puff” cycle. Fractures inflate during injection
and deflate during production, maximizing conductive heat exchange. This process allows for
consistent thermal delivery across multiple closely spaced wells, making it suitable for district
heating applications.

 Figure 12. Schematic of the Sage Geosystems HeatCycle technology, showing key components
for efficient geothermal energy extraction in hot dry rock formations. Note that this does not show
  an injection-production well doublet, but rather different temporal phases within a single well.

We developed a COMSOL Multiphysics thermal model to evaluate HeatCycle performance in
the Moesian Platform at ~4 km depth, where temperatures range from 100°C–120°C (Bendea
and Rosca 2015). The reservoir model assumes low-permeability, isotropic rock with heat
transfer dominated by conduction. Simpkins et al. (2023) provided the mathematical basis for
modeling fluid and fracture behavior. Simulations were run for a 12-hour operational cycle, with
water injected at 100 kg/s for 5.5 hours, followed by a 1-hour shut-in period and 5.5 hours of
production at the same rate. Water entered the system at 50°C and reached a wellhead
temperature of ~110°C, which is sufficient for district heating use (Figure 13, Figure 14).

  Figure 13. Modeled operational cycle illustrating the injection and production phases. Water is
injected at a rate of 100 kg/s for 5.5 hours, followed by a 1-hour shut-in period to allow for thermal

 of 100 kg/s for an additional 5.5 hours.

Figure 14. Modeled temperature profiles for wellhead and bottomhole conditions over the 12-hour

We also evaluated pressure dynamics, as HeatCycle leverages both thermal and pressure energy.
Geomechanical properties such as rock deformation were defined by Young’s modulus and
Poisson’s ratio, with Darcy’s law applied to simulate fluid flow through fractures. Fracture
volume varied predictably with the cycle, confirming inflation during injection and deflation
during production. The average modeled aperture was ~2 mm, indicating no unwanted fracture
propagation or excessive pressure buildup (Figure 15, Figure 16). However, COMSOL does not
explicitly model fracture growth, so long-term mechanical effects remain unassessed.

 21

 During the production phase, the volume decreases, demonstrating controlled fracture deflation

These models demonstrate that HeatCycle is a feasible solution to deliver steady, high-
temperature output under Bucharest’s geological conditions. Although short-term performance is
promising, future studies should incorporate long-term thermal drawdown, fracture propagation,
and inter-reservoir interactions to fully evaluate system longevity and optimize pressure
management.

Direct-use geothermal systems are typically designed to meet a portion of district heating demand,
with auxiliary boilers used to handle peak loads (Beckers and Young 2017). In this study, each
scenario targets a 20-MWₜₕ benchmark capacity that has been scaled-up from the modeled load
profile. This approach is justified by the capacity of the existing DES.
Additionally, after an update in GEOPHIRES that allowed the user to not consider the capital cost
of peaking boilers which already exist on Bucharest’s DES (e.g., CTE/CTZ plants are already
operational) the cost of peaking boiler operation over 20 years was simulated for the EGS and Huff
and Puff EGS base-cases. In these simulations, additional capital for peaking infrastructure or new
DES construction is unnecessary, and the capital cost of implementing new peaking boilers and
the cost of constructing piping for the DES itself has not been included (i.e., buildings are
connected to the distribution system within the city itself already, and thus the costs of replacing
the pipe or building a new district are not considered). The capital cost of installing distribution
pipe cost from the geothermal plant to the CTE/CTZ, however, has been considered across all
simulations.
This benchmark of 20 MWth enables a focused analysis of the LCOH for each geothermal
option. Past studies on the discount rate of DDU projects in base cases ranged from 1%–8%
(Beckers et al. 2021). We assumed a higher-end discount rate ranging from 6%–8% for all
scenarios as an uncertainty insurance around risks of employing the technology. EGS
simulations include peaking boilers (fuel rate = 0.0591 $/kWh) and a utilization factor of
approximately 68%, which is modeled in GEOPHIRES based on the demand trend of the

 25

modeled load profile and the scenario’s modeled capacities, which was scaled up for a baseload
of ~20MWth (Figure 17).

Because hydrothermal cases made use of the heat pump module, it was not possible to simulate
the district heating end use option in addition to the module. Representation of a heat pump
option and the modeled district heating system would be a valuable future update to this analysis.
Additionally, after Modelica modeling of the heat pump COP and electricity consumption, we
have determined that the cost of running the heat pump in GEOPHIRES is conservative,
considering the power input is 9.1 MW in GEOPHIRES and ~5 MW as determined by the
higher-fidelity Modelica modeling effort. In the future, updating the GEOPHIRES case to
consider 5 MW electricity use should be considered for hydrothermal scenarios.

The huff-and-puff system exhibits a lower LCOH than the hydrothermal and EGS scenarios. The
difference is attributed to the cycle of fluid within each well, i.e., each well is only producing
50% of the time. This is offset by the drilling cost savings associated with using one-well

 27

systems instead of doublets and the 50 kg/s flow rate with the intent of simulating possible
LCOH parameters for this system.

Compared to scenarios from the EGS DDU base case scenarios, the LCOH of the system
modeled for direct-use EGS heat is on the higher end, based on case studies conducted in the
United States (Beckers et al. 2021). The reason for the variance in LCOH between base-case
studies is the broad range of input parameters. The geothermal gradient, and therefore the
reservoir temperature, drilling cost, flow rate, and discount rate, have the greatest impact on
LCOH. In this study, a parameter that heavily influenced LCOH was the distribution network
capital cost (to transport hot fluid to the relevant CTE/CTZ in the case of Glina) and the peaking
boiler operational cost for scenarios in which that cost was considered. In the case of CTE Vest,
the drilling cost contributes to the elevated LCOH, as five doublets drilled vertically to 5.7 km
with laterals on the order of 1,500 m were required to meet the benchmark load deliverable to the
GeoDES. Other results are as expected; for instance, lower discount rates and fewer injection
wells (in the hydrothermal scenario cases) yielded a lower LCOH than higher rates and more
wells, as the drilling cost and discount rate both greatly impact the LCOH.

These results show the economic sensitivity of direct-use geothermal development to both
drilling and distribution infrastructure costs. In the hydrothermal scenarios (Otopeni and
Balotești), the high LCOH values—ranging from approximately $24–$33/MMBTU, depending
on scenario inputs—can primarily be attributed to the capital-intensive distribution network. As
detailed in Table 9, the total estimated cost for installing 250-mm (10-inch) insulated pipes over
round-trip distances of 16–28 km was up to $36.8 million for Balotești. Although these scenarios
benefit from the assumption of naturally permeable reservoirs and thereby avoid stimulation
costs, the financial burden of piping infrastructure is impactful.

The EGS scenarios, particularly Glina, consistently yielded lower LCOH outcomes, despite
incorporating stimulation and lateral drilling. Glina’s LCOH falls as low as $19/MMBTU under
ideal conditions, a result that highlights the greater economic viability of EGS when both
geological and logistical variables are optimized. Although the upfront costs associated with well
stimulation and directional drilling (e.g. horizontal laterals are priced at $1,300/m) are nontrivial,
they are mitigated by the reduced need for extensive surface distribution networks, especially in
locations where direct proximity to district heating infrastructure enables simplified integration.
The large cost is also associated with operating peaking boilers to cover peaking demand.

The huff-and-puff EGS configuration—represented by the Sage Geosystems scenario—also
demonstrates economic competitiveness, with LCOH values ranging from $22.75–
$25.49/MMBTU across varying discount rates. This system leverages a one-well cyclic
operation that minimizes drilling costs while maintaining a 100 kg/s flow rate. Although the
intermittent production profile reduces annual energy output per well, the cost savings from
eliminating dual-well configurations result in an overall economic advantage even when 5 wells
are required to meet the benchmark; note that, the base-case conditions for the discount rate
scenarios also considered a gradient of 25°C/km, a target depth of 4.1 km, and a drilling cost
consistent with the Glina and CTE Vest cases. The base-case LCOH for this technology is still
comparable to that of Glina and higher than that of CTE Vest, even with Glina’s higher
simulated gradient (34°C/km). This outcome supports the emerging argument in the geothermal

 28

literature that unconventional well architectures may offer feasible pathways to cost parity,
especially when paired with cost reductions in drilling technology.

The sensitivity analyses for heat pump capital cost and injection well numbers in the
hydrothermal scenarios reveal important financial thresholds. For both Otopeni and Balotești,
reducing the number of injection wells and optimizing heat pump sizing contribute significantly
to LCOH reductions. For instance, Otopeni achieves a minimum LCOH of $27.81/MMBTU with
only three injection wells and a lower-cost heat pump, and the base-case drilling cost. These
results suggest that careful thermal load matching and strategic modularization of surface plant
investments could help improve the economics of hydrothermal systems in urban contexts like
Bucharest.

Ultimately, this study affirms the critical role of local infrastructure and geological context in
determining the LCOH of operating and installing a GeoDES. Although hydrothermal scenarios
remain feasible in areas with naturally permeable formations, cost of installation is closely tied to
the cost and complexity of district heat distribution. Engineered solutions such as EGS—when
decoupled from costly surface piping and targeting elevated gradients for direct use purposes—
could offer a pathway for expanding Bucharest’s geothermal portfolio. The LCOH thus
associated with these scenarios is 19 to 36 $/MMBTU (which translates to ~65 $/MWth to ~123
$/MWth).

 29

4 Conclusions
Based on the results of this study, hot dry rock geothermal resources that can deliver at least
90°C hot water to the GeoDES are sensitive to the critical role of infrastructure and geological
parameters in determining the LCOH of operating and installing a GeoDES. The LCOH may be
improved by targeting suitable subsurface parameters with elevated gradients, and by reducing
distribution pipe capital cost. Further investigation into country rock lithology, permeability,
porosity, and thermophysical properties deeper than 2 km is required to update simulated heat
flow and improve the study. Planned future work could involve updating the end-use efficiency
factor to 80% and accounting for the cost of replacing pipe within the district energy system and
updating heat pump scenarios to reflect a more refined calculation of power draw in
GEOPHIRES.
