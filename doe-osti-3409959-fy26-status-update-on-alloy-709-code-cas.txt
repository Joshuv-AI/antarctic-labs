ABSTRACT
 This report provides an update on the status of the creep rupture testing on the precipitation-
treated (PT) Alloy 709 samples fabricated from the Carlson, the ATI-1 and the ATI-2 commercial
heats, as a continuation of the 100,000-hour Alloy 709 Code Case development effort under the
US Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) program.
While the code case has been submitted to the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME)
Section III, Division 5, some creep tests are still ongoing in Argonne National Laboratory (ANL).
In Fiscal Year 2026 (FY26), no new tests were initiated, while 3 previously loaded tests were
completed. 4 tests are ongoing at the time of writing this report. This report presents an overview
of the creep-rupture data gathered at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) since FY21 that totaled
55 completed tests. Key conclusions include: 1) the creep behavior of the three heats of Alloy 709
can be well described by the Larson-Miller relationship; 2) Alloy 709 is overall very ductile under
creep; 3) the creep voids of the 3 specimens ruptured in FY26 are primarily along grain boundaries.

FY26 Status Update on Alloy 709 Code Case Testing at ANL 1

1 Introduction
 Advanced materials are a key element in the deployment of advanced nuclear energy
systems. U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Reactor Technologies
(ART) Program is conducting R&D on qualifying an advanced austenite stainless steel, Alloy 709,
for ASME Section III Division 5 Class A constructions. A Phase I implementation of this plan that
includes a 100,000-hour, 760°C ASME code case and the initiation of very long-term creep tests,
thermal aging, and sodium exposure of Alloy 709 was established.
 The Alloy 709 fabrication scale-up began in FY16 and was completed in FY17, resulting
in the procurement of the first commercial Carlson heat (45,000 lb) (Natesan, et al., ANL-ART-
89, 2017), followed by two ATI heats in FY22 (40,000 lb) (Bass, et al, INL/RPT-22-69073-
Rev000, 2022) and FY23 (38,000 lb) (Wright, et al., INL/MIS-23-72773-Rev000, 2023). A multi-
laboratory round-robin testing campaign in FY18 selected electro-slag remelting (ESR) as the final
production process, and subsequent ANL activities focused on developing the preliminary design
basis and conducting 100,000-hour code case testing. A precipitation treatment (PT) of 10 hours
at 775°C was also developed to promote nanoscale precipitation and improve creep-fatigue
performance (Zhang, et al., ANL-ART-170, 2019), with extensive microstructural characterization
performed to correlate precipitation and damage evolution with creep behavior (Zhang, et al.,
ANL-ART-233, 2021). From FY19 through FY25, long-term code case testing progressed from
ESR-1100°C solution-annealed (SA) material to ESR-1150°C-PT material from the Carlson, ATI-
1, and ATI-2 heats. Results are summarized in a series of milestone reports (Zhang, et al., ANL-
ART-172, 2019; Zhang, et al., ANL-ART-193, 2020; Zhang, et al., ANL-ART-231, 2021; Zhang,
et al., ANL-ART-251, 2022; Zhang, ANL-ART-271, 2023; Zhang, et al., ANL-ART-282, 2024;
Zhang, et al., ANL-ART-304, 2025).
 This report summarizes the status of the remaining Alloy 709 creep tests in ANL in FY26.
The combined creep data obtained from FY21 to FY26 are presented.

2.2 Creep test status

ANL on lever-arm creep testers, following ASTM E139 “Standard Test Methods for Conducting

“Standard Guide for Use of Thermocouples in Creep and Stress-Rupture Testing to 1800°F
(1000°C) in Air”. Tables 2-2 lists activities in FY26 on the ESR-1150°C-PT materials. In
summary, no new tests were initiated, 3 tests were completed, and 4 tests were ongoing at the

3 FY21-FY26 combined creep data
 As mentioned previously, 3 creep tests were completed in FY26. Figure 3-1 plots the creep
curves. Their creep life spans from 26081 hours (2.98 years) to 40436 hours (4.62 years).
 Combing the rupture data obtained in ANL on the ESR-1150°C-PT materials from the past
few years, the Larson-Miller plot is shown in Figure 3-2, color-coded by material batches. The
Larson-Miller parameter LMP was calculated as
                                   LMP = (T + 273.15)  (log10tr + C) ,
where 𝑡𝑟 is the rupture time in h, T is the temperature in °C, and C is a constant taken to be 17.00.
It is seen that the creep behavior of Alloy 709 can be well described by a linear Larson-Miller
relationship, with all heats perform similarly and no difference between SA and PT treatments.
 Figures 3-3 and 3-4 present the creep properties for the A709 ESR-1150°C-PT material
with data collected between FY21 and FY26. 55 tests are included. Figure 3-3 plots the creep
rupture life and the minimum creep rate as a function of test temperature and stress. It is seen that
higher temperature and larger stress leads to shorter creep life and higher minimum creep rate.
Figure 3-4 plots the elongation and the area reduction as a function of test temperature and stress.
It is seen that the specimens under those test conditions were all very ductile.

4 Crack morphology
 Figures 4-1, 4-2 and 4-3 are metallographic images showing grain structures and crack
morphologies near the fracture surfaces of specimens BC-AH-04 (Carlson heat, 725°C, 75 MPa),
455-PT-13 (ATI-1 heat, 675°C, 115 MPa), and 455-PT-11 (ATI-1 heat, 650°C, 140 MPa),

along grain boundaries.

5 Summary and Future Work
 In FY26, ANL continued the creep testing of Alloy 709 specimens from the three
commercial heats, in support of the 100,000-h ASME Code Case development. 3 tests were
completed and 4 tests were on-going. The results enabled a preliminary analysis of the creep
behavior of this material. Key conclusions include: 1) the creep behavior of the three heats of Alloy
709 can be well described by the Larson-Miller relationship; 2) Alloy 709 is overall very ductile
under creep; 3) the creep voids of the 3 ruptured specimens are primarily along grain boundaries.
 Future work involves the completion of the remaining creep tests and perform a systematic
study of the creep mechanisms of Alloy 709 in a large temperature and stress window.
