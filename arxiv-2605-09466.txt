Counting subgraphs in bounded-size Achlioptas processes
Mihyun Kang, Oliver Riordan

Abstract
Achlioptas processes such as the Bohman–Frieze process are much harder to analyse than the classical Erdős–Rényi process, due to the dependence between edges added at different stages. This dependence means that most analysis so far is dynamic, often based on the differential equation method. In the Erdős–Rényi case there is an alternative static approach, pioneered by Erdős, Rényi and Bollobás, based on evaluating the expectation (and higher moments) of various subgraph counts, and using this to study the component structure. Here we show that this latter approach can be applied (with some complications) to the Bohman–Frieze process. For example, we are able to show that the expected number μ k , t , n \mu_{k,t,n} of k k -vertex tree components after t ​ n tn steps satisfies (essentially) μ k , t , n = c k , t ​ n ​ ( 1 + O ⁡ ( k / n ) ) \mu_{k,t,n}=c_{k,t}n(1+O(k/\sqrt{n})) . Our method gives a very complicated formula for c k , t c_{k,t} , which seems to be unusable. However, since c k , t c_{k,t} does not depend on n n , we may use recent results obtained by the differential equation method and branching process analysis to find the asymptotics of c k , t c_{k,t} as k → ∞ k\to\infty . The latter results also give a formula for μ k , t , n \mu_{k,t,n} of the form c k , t ​ n c_{k,t}n plus an error term, with a much more usable description of c k , t c_{k,t} but a much worse error term. We combine the best of both worlds to prove a number of new results about the process near criticality. In particular, we obtain extremely sharp bounds on the size of the largest non-giant component near criticality, including the limiting distribution of its fluctuations.

1.1
Relationship to earlier results

There has been considerable interest in studying the near-critical behaviour of random graph processes
other than the classical Erdős–Rényi (ER) process, and in particular that of the Bohman–Frieze process,
as one of the simplest examples that does not have the independence properties of the ER process.
The behaviour inside the ‘scaling window’ of the phase transition was established by Bhamidi, Budhiraja and Wang
[
3
,
1
]
,
but that outside, where
t=m/n=t_{\mathrm{c}}+\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\to 0
with
\varepsilon^{3}n\to\infty
, is only partially understood.

As an example, Kang, Perkins and Spencer
[
15
]
conjectured (their Conjecture 1)
that for the Bohman–Frieze process
(G_{m}^{(n)})

there exists a constant
K
such that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(L_{1}(G_{(t_{\mathrm{c}}-\varepsilon)n}^{(n)})\leqslant K\varepsilon^{-2}\log n\bigr)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
Bhamidi, Budhiraja and Wang
[
2
]
made some progress towards this, showing that for any bounded-size rule
and any
\beta\in(0,1/4)
, there exists
K\in(0,\infty)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(L_{1}(G_{tn}^{(n)})\leqslant K(t_{\mathrm{c}}-t)^{-2}\log^{4}n,\ \forall t\leqslant t_{\mathrm{c}}-n^{-\beta}\bigr)\to 1
. This bound was in fact a key ingredient in their analysis of
the behaviour inside the window in
[
1
]
. Sen
[
26
]
proved Conjecture 1 of
[
15
]

and Warnke and the second author
[
25

proved a much tighter form
of the conjecture, obtaining a bound of the form

L_{1}(G_{m}^{(n)})=\delta(t_{\mathrm{c}}-\varepsilon)^{-1}\left(\log(\varepsilon^{3}n)-\frac{5}{2}\log\log(\varepsilon^{3}n)+O_{\mathrm{p}}(1)\right)

Specifically for the Bohman–Frieze rule, and subject to an additional restriction on
\varepsilon(n)
,

3
sharpens this result yet further, finding the limiting distribution of the
O_{\mathrm{p}}(1)
term.
This is especially significant since it is only here that, via the function
c(t)
, a (small!) difference
from the Erdős–Rényi behaviour is visible.
It might in principle be possible to push the first and second moment arguments of
[
25
]
to higher moments
and so obtain a result like Theorem
3
by the methods of that paper, but the arguments for two moments there are already
extremely complicated, so this would likely be difficult.

Considering the size
L_{2}
of the second largest component in the supercritical case,
the previously known bounds are
much
weaker: we are not aware of anything at all comparable in accuracy
to (
7
). For example, in
[
25
, Theorem 2.8]
only a (not very strong) upper bound is given.
A key advantage of our tree-counting methods is that they work equally well just
above and just below the critical point, so we obtain an extremely sharp bound on

in the supercritical case.
As far as we are aware, no similarly sharp bounds have been shown for any type of random graph process
with dependence;
the problem is that any ‘duality principle’ is much harder to understand precisely than in the Erdős–Rényi case.

Except for the restrictions (

To replace this condition with ‘what it should be’ (namely,

Remark 1
.

In this paper we concentrate on the Bohman–Frieze process. As noted in Section
3.3
(in a footnote),
our arguments extend with only notational complications to a class of bounded-size Achlioptas processes
satisfying a certain monotonicity property. Furthermore, that monotonicity is only used in one place,
to deal with a certain estimate, and is presumably not needed for our results. We have not investigated
this further for fear of burying the key ideas – in particular that we can take the evolution
of the number of isolated vertices as a ‘background’ and effectively recover enough
independence to apply tree-counting methods – in superficial complications.

The basic method

A key property of the process

are not independent,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(e_{m+1}=e\mid G_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}^{-1}+\left(1-\binom{I_{m}}{2}\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}^{-1}

\displaystyle 2n^{-2}\bigl(2-(I_{m}/n)^{2}\bigr)+O(n^{-3}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(e_{m+1}=e\mid G_{m})

\displaystyle\left(1-\binom{I_{m}}{2}\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-2\left(\frac{I_{m}}{n}\right)^{2}-2\left(1-\left(\frac{I_{m}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)\frac{I_{m}}{n}-\rho_{1}^{\prime}(t)+e^{\prime}(t)\omega n^{-1/2}+O(n^{-1}).

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\exists 0\leqslant m\leqslant Cn\hbox{ s.t.\ }\hat{I}_{m}^{+}<0\ \vee\ \hat{I}_{m}^{-}>0\bigr)

Remark 3
.

It is easy to see that we cannot prove tighter bounds on the fraction

in isolated components than the

1.3
A continuous-time idealised model

In the light of Lemma
5
and the formulae (
9
) and (
10
), if we consider
a not-too-large set
V
of vertices, then we may expect the edges incident with
V
to
evolve roughly according to the following continuous-time model.

Firstly, in a small interval
[t,t+{\mathrm{d}t}]
(corresponding to
n{\mathrm{d}t}
steps
of the random graph process) a given edge
uv
within
V
appears with probability

2n^{-1}(2-\rho_{1}(t)^{2}){\mathrm{d}t}\hbox{ \ or \ }2n^{-1}(1-\rho_{1}(t)^{2}){\mathrm{d}t}

(22)

according to whether or not
u
and
v
are currently both isolated. Secondly, a given vertex
v\in V
becomes
connected to some vertex outside
V
with probability

2(1-\rho_{1}(t)^{2}+\rho_{1}(t)){\mathrm{d}t}\hbox{ \ or \ }2(1-\rho_{1}(t)^{2}){\mathrm{d}t},

(23)

according to whether
v
is currently isolated or not. Here the second formula
comes from multiplying (
10
) by the number
n{\mathrm{d}t}
of steps and the number
\sim n
of
vertices outside
V
, and the first from adding to this a term corresponding
to the difference between (
9
) and (
10
) for each of the
\sim\rho_{1}(t)n
isolated
vertices outside

.

In what follows, we show that the discrete process is indeed well approximated by this continuous model, as
long as
|V|=o(\sqrt{n})
.

2
Ignoring bad events

From now on we fix a constant
C
, and consider only
m\leqslant Cn
. Let

W_{m}
denote the event
|I_{m}/n-\rho_{1}(m/n)|\leqslant\omega n^{-1/2}
, and
W
the event

\bigcap_{m\leqslant Cn}W_{m}
, so
W
holds whp
by Lemma
5
. The next lemma
captures in a precise way the idea that we can ‘ignore what happens when
W
fails’.
We must be a little careful, as we shall calculate the probabilities of very unlikely
events
A
, and then sum over many events. So a bound of the form
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(A)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\widetilde{A})\pm\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W^{\mathrm{c}})

is not enough, and we need the slightly fussy result below. From now on we
adopt the convention that if
(A_{i})
is a sequence of events, then
A_{\leqslant i}
denotes
the event
\bigcap_{j=1}^{i}A_{j}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}})
be a probability space without atoms. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{m})_{m\geqslant 0}
be a filtration
of
\mathcal{F}
, and for each
i
let
W_{i}
and
A_{i}
be
\mathcal{F}_{i}
-measurable events.
Suppose that for all
i\leqslant m
we have

p_{i}\leqslant\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})\leqslant q_{i}

(24)

whenever
A_{\leqslant i-1}\cap W_{\leqslant i-1}
holds. Then there exist events
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
such that

A_{\leqslant i}\bigtriangleup B_{\leqslant i}\subseteq(W_{\leqslant i-1})^{\mathrm{c}}

(25)

for
i\leqslant m
and

\prod_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}\leqslant\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(B_{\leqslant i})\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{i}q_{j}.

(26)

In other words, in evaluating
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(A_{\leqslant m})
we can ‘pretend’ that the conditional probability bound (
24
)
holds whenever
A_{\leqslant i-1}
holds (in which case
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(A_{\leqslant m})
would satisfy the inequalities
in (
26
)), except that we must allow ourselves to ‘cook’ (i.e., modify) the event
A_{\leqslant m}
when
W_{\leqslant m}
fails to hold.

Proof.

The idea is simply to ‘cook’ the event whose probability we are estimating when (if ever)
W_{i}
first fails to hold.
It should be clear that this can be made to work, but let us spell out the details.

First, replacing each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
by the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
A_{1},\ldots,A_{i}
and
W_{1},\ldots,W_{i}
, we may
assume that each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is finite. Since our probability space is atomless, we may construct
random variables
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
that are uniformly distributed
on
[0,1]
and are independent of each other and of
\mathcal{F}_{m}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{*}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{i}
.
Set

B_{i}=\bigl(W_{\leqslant i-1}\cap A_{i}\bigr)\cup\bigl((W_{\leqslant i-1})^{\mathrm{c}}\cap\{U_{i}\leqslant p_{i}\}\bigr).

Then
B_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{*}
-measurable. Also, since
W_{\leqslant i-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{*}
-measurable,
we have
(i)
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(B_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{*})=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{*})
on the event
W_{\leqslant i-1}
and
(ii)
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(B_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{*})=p_{i}
on
(W_{\leqslant i-1})^{\mathrm{c}}
.

By definition
B_{i}\bigtriangleup A_{i}\subseteq(W_{\leqslant i-1})^{\mathrm{c}}
, from which (
25
) follows. From (i)
and (
24
) it follows that when
W_{\leqslant i-1}\cap B_{\leqslant i-1}=W_{\leqslant i-1}\cap A_{\leqslant i-1}
holds
we have
p_{i}\leqslant\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(B_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{*})\leqslant q_{i}
. Hence, by (ii), this inequality
holds whenever
B_{\leqslant i-1}
holds, and (
26
) follows by induction.
∎

Lemma
6
effectively allows us to pretend that the event
W
defined in (
13
)
always holds when applying the first and second moment methods. More precisely,
suppose that for each

we have some list

of events, for example
the events that each possible tree with

vertices is present as a component
in the
n
-vertex,
m

G_{m}=G_{m}^{(n)}
, and let

be the number of these events
that hold. Let
\widetilde{A}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{A}_{N(n)}
be the corresponding events
whose existence is guaranteed by the lemma, whose probabilities we can estimate using (

),
and let
\widetilde{X}_{n}
be the number of
\widetilde{A}_{i}
that hold.
If
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[\widetilde{X}_{n}]\to 0

then

whp, and hence (since

holds whp, and

, or indeed if

. Similarly, if we can show that the expected number

.
