Inverse problems for a nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger operator with magnetic potential
Mandeep Kumar, Boya Liu, Manmohan Vashisth

Abstract
. We study two inverse problems for a nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger operator with magnetic and electric potentials. Under suitable analyticity assumptions, we show that the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map uniquely determines time-dependent magnetic and electric potentials. We establish the uniqueness of these potentials from both full data and partial data. In particular, for the partial data problem, the desired uniqueness is established by assuming that the potentials are known near the boundary, and the Neumann data is measured on arbitrarily small open subsets of the boundary. In addition, we establish the well-posedness of the forward problem, where we obtain the optimal Sobolev regularity for solutions.



1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded open set with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. For any
T>0
, throughout this paper we shall denote
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
the space-time domain, whose lateral boundary is given by
\Sigma:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
. We also denote
\nu(x)
to be the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
, and
\nabla
to be the gradient with respect to the spatial variable.

Consider the nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A},q}
defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A},q}u

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u-\left(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)\right)^{2}u+q(t,x,u)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+2\mathrm{i}\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)\cdot\nabla u+\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}(t,x,u))u-|\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)|^{2}u+q(t,x,u),

with time-dependent coefficients
\mathcal{A}(t,x,u):=\left(\mathcal{A}_{1}(t,x,u),\mathcal{A}_{2}(t,x,u),\dots,\mathcal{A}_{n}(t,x,u)\right)
, called
magnetic potential
, as well as the
electric potential

q(t,x,u)
. In this paper, we assume that the potentials
\mathcal{A}:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
q:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

satisfy the following properties:

Assumption 1
.

The map
\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot,z)
is holomorphic with values in
C^{\infty}(\overline{Q},\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Assumption 2
.

The map
\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto q(\cdot,\cdot,z)
is holomorphic with values in
C^{\infty}(\overline{Q},\mathbb{C})
.

Assumption 3
.

q(t,x,0)=0
for all
(t,x)\in\overline{Q}
.

Then it follows from Assumptions
1
–
3
that
\mathcal{A}
and
q
pose the following power series expansion:

(1.2)

\mathcal{A}(t,x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k}(t,x)\frac{z^{k}}{k!},\quad A_{k}(t,x):=\partial_{z}^{k}\mathcal{A}(t,x,0),

and

(1.3)

q(t,x,z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}(t,x)\frac{z^{k}}{k!},\quad q_{k}(t,x):=\partial_{z}^{k}q(t,x,0),

where both series converge in the
C^{\infty}
-topology.
From a physical perspective, the coefficients
A_{k}
and
q_{k}
represent higher order nonlinear electromagnetic response coefficients of the medium. In particular,
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
represent the background magnetic and electric potentials, while higher order coefficients encode nonlinear self-interactions. Such expansions are analogous to nonlinear susceptibility expansions in nonlinear optics and provide a natural framework for higher order linearization methods in inverse problems.

In this paper, we consider the initial boundary value problem for a quasilinear dynamical Schrödinger equation given by

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A},q}u=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
u=f&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

It is established in Theorem
3.8
that this problem has a unique solution for sufficiently small boundary value
f
in a suitable sense.

The purpose of this paper is to uniquely recover the potentials
\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)
and
q(t,x,u)
in the domain
Q\times\mathbb{C}
from boundary measurements using both full data and partial data, which we describe in detail in the next two subsections.

1.1.
Full data problem

We begin with defining the boundary measurement used in this paper. To this end, associated with the problem (
1.4
), we define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}
via the formula

(1.5)

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}(f)=(\partial_{\nu}+i(\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)\cdot\nu))u|_{\Sigma}.

In view of Theorem
3.8
,
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}
is well-defined for sufficiently small
f
.

The first inverse problem we study in this paper is whether
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}
determines the nonlinear magnetic and electric potentials uniquely. Let us observe that the coefficients
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
in the expansion (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) correspond to a linear magnetic potential and scalar potential, respectively. After applying the first order linearization, the unique recovery of
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
reduces to an inverse problem of determining the magnetic and electric potentials appearing in a linear dynamical Schrödinger equation, and
the gauge invariance associated with the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of the linearized equation implies that the unique recovery of
A_{0}
is possible only when
\nabla\cdot A_{0}
is known, see
[
25
,
36
,
55
]
.

We are now ready to state the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, and let
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
with
0<T<\infty
. Suppose that the nonlinear magnetic potentials
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\mathcal{A}^{(2)}:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
and electric potentials
q^{(1)},q^{(2)}:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy Assumptions
1
–
3
. Let

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(i)},q^{(i)}}
,
i=1,2
, denote the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (
1.5
) with
(\mathcal{A},q)=(\mathcal{A}^{(i)},q^{(i)})
. Then
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)},q^{(1)}}=\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(2)},q^{(2)}}
implies that
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
and
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
in
\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}
, provided that
\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(1)}=\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(2)}
in
Q
, and
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
,
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
on
\Sigma\times\mathbb{C}
.

1.2.
Partial data problem

We now turn our attention to the second inverse problem we study in this paper. Specifically, assuming that the nonlinear potentials
\mathcal{A}
and
q
are known near the lateral boundary
\Sigma
, our goal is to prove that measurements made on arbitrarily small open subsets of
\Sigma
determines
\mathcal{A}
and
q
uniquely.

Let us remark that the assumption mentioned above is realistic in practice, as the coefficients are either known or can be easily determined near the boundary. The study of partial data inverse problems in this setting was initiated in
[
2
]
, which established a uniqueness result for the linear Schrödinger operator. We refer readers to
[
6
,
17
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
49
,
58
,
59
]
and the references therein for related results for various linear operators, as well as
[
35
,
36
]
for nonlinear operators.

We next proceed to describe the partial boundary measurement used in this paper. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
be an arbitrary nonempty open set. In what follows, let us denote
\Sigma^{\sharp}:=(0,T)\times\Gamma
and define a
partial Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
by the formula

(1.6)

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}^{\sharp}(f)=(\partial_{\nu}+i(\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)\cdot\nu))u|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}}.

The inverse problem we are interested in is whether
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A},q}^{\sharp}
uniquely determines the time-dependent nonlinear potentials
\mathcal{A}
and
q
. Similar to the full data case, it is necessary to assume that
\nabla\cdot A_{0}
is known to obtain uniqueness of
A_{0}
.

Our second main result of this paper is as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, and let
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
with
0<T<\infty
. Suppose that the nonlinear magnetic potentials
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\mathcal{A}^{(2)}:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
and electric potentials
q^{(1)},q^{(2)}:\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy Assumptions
1
–
3
. Let

\Lambda^{\sharp}_{\mathcal{A}^{(i)},q^{(i)}}
,
i=1,2
, denote the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (
1.6
) with
(\mathcal{A},q)=(\mathcal{A}^{(i)},q^{(i)})
. Furthermore, assume that
\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(1)}=\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(2)}
in
Q
, and
(\mathcal{A}^{(1)},q^{(1)})=(\mathcal{A}^{(2)},q^{(2)})
in
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
. Then
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)},q^{(1)}}^{\sharp}=\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(2)},q^{(2)}}^{\sharp}
implies that
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
and
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
in
\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}
.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, to the best of our knowledge, inverse problems for nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger equations with time-dependent magnetic potentials have not been previously studied. Theorem
1.1
is the first result to uniquely recover time-dependent coefficients of a quasilinear dynamical Schrödinger operator from full boundary measurements, and Theorem
1.2
provides the first corresponding partial data result.
In addition, we establish the well-posedness of the nonlinear initial-boundary value problem (
1.4
).
Furthermore, we obtain solutions with optimal Sobolev regularity.

1.3.
Previous literature

In the last decade, inverse problems for nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) have gained considerable attention. The seminal work
[
34
]
demonstrated that nonlinearity can be helpful in solving inverse problems for hyperbolic equations. This approach has been adopted to study inverse problems for various types of nonlinear PDEs. Among the extensive literature on the subject, we refer readers to
[
20
,
21
,
24
,
40
,
42
,
50
,
52
,
57
]
for results of hyperbolic equations,
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
35
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
for elliptic equations, and
[
18
,
27
,
35
,
36
]
for parabolic equations.

A crucial tool in the study of nonlinear inverse problems is the method of linearization, which was introduced in
[
22
]
to study an inverse problem for nonlinear parabolic equations. By employing this method, nonlinear inverse problems can often be reduced to a sequence of inverse problems for linearized equations. A well-known technique to solve inverse problems for linear PDEs is using the geometric optics solution, which was first used in
[
56
]
to address the uniqueness for the conductivity equation. Geometric optics solutions remain one of the principal tools in the study of inverse problems for linear PDEs and have been successfully applied to a wide variety of operators and geometric settings. We refer readers to
[
47
,
48
,
51
,
53
]
and the references therein for its applications in inverse problems of time-dependent coefficients.

Let us next discuss inverse problems for the dynamical Schrödinger equation. Similar to its hyperbolic counterpart, results in this direction fall into two categories: time-independent and time-dependent coefficients. For linear operators, in the absence of vector potential, uniqueness and stability for time-independent scalar potential have been established in
[
4
]
from a single boundary measurement map. Subsequently, stability results were shown in
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
and the references therein. In the case of time-dependent coefficients, the first result concerning the uniqueness of time-dependent magnetic and electric potentials was obtained in
[
15
]
, whereas stability results for this problem, from full or partial boundary measurements, were established in
[
10
,
14
,
25
,
26
]
.

Finally, we briefly review inverse problems for nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger equations. The authors of
[
41
]
established the uniqueness of both time-dependent linear and nonlinear potentials from the source-to-solution map. For power-type nonlinearities, a logarithmic stability from partial boundary measurements was obtained in
[
35
]
. We further mention the partial-data uniqueness result of
[
36
]
for a different class of nonlinearities , as well as a corresponding reconstruction result established in
[
33
]
.
Moreover, let us mention a recent stability result of
[
3
]
concerning a time-independent nonlinearity.

1.4.
Ideas of the proof of main results

There are several major components in the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, the first of which is the construction of geometric solutions to linear dynamical Schrödinger equations, which is discussed in Section
4
. Specifically, the solutions are of the form

v_{\lambda}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\bigg(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\bigg)+R_{\lambda}(t,x),

where
\lambda\gg 1
is a large parameter, the vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the smooth amplitudes
m_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,N
, satisfy some transport equations, and
R_{\lambda}
is a correction term that vanishes in a suitable sense as
\lambda\to\infty
. These solutions are substituted into appropriate integral identities to reduce the unique recovery of coefficients to the injectivity of ray transforms or Fourier transforms. Let us highlight that it is sufficient to obtain a decay in the space
H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, as given in Proposition
4.3
, to recover the potentials
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
. However, we need to have a remainder that decays in
H^{m+1}(Q)
, where
m>0
is sufficiently large, to uniquely recover higher order nonlinearities. This is accomplished in Corollary
4.4
.

Another crucial step in the proof is applying the technique of higher order linearization. Since the seminal work
[
34
]
, nonlinearity has been exploited in the study of inverse problems, and higher order linearization plays a crucial role in it. Linearization is needed several times in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. In Subsection
5.1
, we apply the first order linearization and establish an integral identity that connects the unknown coefficients with the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, as well as constructing geometric optics solutions, to recover
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
. Indeed, we observe from (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) that
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
represent the linear coefficients of the dynamical Schrödinger operator. Hence, due to the gauge invariance of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, it is only possible to recover
A_{0}
up to a gauge given in (
5.29
). Therefore, it is necessary to assume that
\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(1)}=\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(2)}
in
Q
to achieve uniqueness of
A_{0}
. Afterwards, we utilize the second order linearization in Subsection
5.2
to obtain uniqueness for
A_{1}
and
q_{2}
, followed by higher order linearization and induction arguments to recover higher order nonlinearities uniquely in Subsection
5.3
.

On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
1.2
mainly relies on a unique continuation property, which is stated in Lemma
6.1
. It enables us to reduce the partial data problem to a full data one, from which we can apply Theorem
1.1
to obtain the uniqueness of magnetic and electric potentials. This strategy has also been applied in the study of partial data inverse problems for nonlinear dynamical Schrödinger operators, see
[
35
,
36
]
and the references therein.

Let us finally discuss the main ideas to establish the well-posedness for the initial boundary value problem (
1.4
). We shall begin with the well-posedness of the linear dynamical Schrödinger equation. Subsequently, we utilize the expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) to rewrite the nonlinear initial boundary value problem (
1.4
) as one for a linear operator, where the source term includes nonlinearities. Then we apply a fixed point argument and the well-posedness of the linear equation to obtain the desired result for the problem (
1.4
).

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce some notations that will be used throughout this paper and recall some properties about time-dependent Sobolev spaces. Afterwards, we establish the well-posedness of the initial boundary value problem (
1.4
) with small boundary data in Section
3
. We then move to the construction of the geometric optics solutions in Section
4
, followed by establishing the full data result, namely, Theorem
1.1
, in Section
5
. Finally, we prove the partial data result stated in Theorem
1.2
in Section
6
.

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

In this short section, we introduce the functional spaces that will be used in the subsequent analysis. Let
(\omega,\mu)
be a measure space, and let
\left(X,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X}\right)
be a Hilbert space. For
1\leq p<\infty
, we define the Bochner space
L^{p}(\omega;X)
by

L^{p}(\omega;X):=\left\{f:\omega\to X:f\text{ is strongly measurable and }\int_{\omega}\|f(x)\|_{X}^{p}d\mu(x)<\infty\right\}.

This space is equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\omega;X)}:=\left(\int_{\omega}\|f(x)\|_{X}^{p}d\mu(x)\right)^{1/p}.

In particular, when
p=2
, the space
L^{2}(\omega;X)
is a Hilbert space with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega;X)}:=\int_{\omega}\langle f(x),g(x)\rangle_{X}d\mu(x).

Also, when
p=\infty
, we define the space

L^{\infty}(\omega;X):=\left\{f:\omega\to X:f\text{ is strongly measurable and }\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\omega}\|f(x)\|_{X}<\infty\right\},

where

\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\omega}\|f(x)\|_{X}:=\inf\left\{M>0:\mu\left(\{x\in\omega:\|f(x)\|_{X}>M\}\right)=0\right\},

and the corresponding norm is given by

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\omega;X)}:=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\omega}\|f(x)\|_{X}.

We refer readers to
[
54
, Chapter 3]
for more details on Bochner spaces.

We next briefly discuss some properties of Sobolev spaces that will be needed in this paper. Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

be an open set with boundary
\partial\omega
. For any integer
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we define
W^{m,p}(\omega;X)
as the space of all functions
u\in L^{p}(\omega;X)
whose weak derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}u\in L^{p}(\omega;X)
exist for every multi-index
\alpha
such that
|\alpha|\leq m
. Here, if
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\right)^{n}
, its length
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
.
The space
W^{m,p}(\omega;X)
is a Banach space with a norm

\|u\|_{W^{m,p}(\omega;X)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{p}(\omega;X)}.

In particular, when
m=0
, we have
W^{0,p}(\omega;X)=L^{p}(\omega;X)
.
Also, in the special case
p=2
, we have

H^{m}(\omega;X):=W^{m,2}(\omega;X)
, which is a Hilbert space with an inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{m}(\omega;X)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\langle\partial^{\alpha}u,\partial^{\alpha}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega;X)}.

Moreover, we denote by
H_{0}^{m}(\omega;X)
the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\omega;X)
in
H^{m}(\omega;X)
, namely,

(2.1)

H_{0}^{m}(\omega;X):=\overline{C_{c}^{\infty}(\omega;X)}^{H^{m}(\omega;X)}.

where
C_{c}^{\infty}(\omega;X)
denotes the space of compactly supported smooth functions valued in
X
.

If
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we denote the space
W^{m,p}(\omega;X)
as
W^{m,p}(\omega)
. Furthermore, if
\partial\omega
is smooth and

m\geq 1
, then an equivalent characterization of (
2.1
) is given by

H_{0}^{m}(\omega)=\left\{u\in H^{m}(\omega):\partial^{\alpha}u|_{\partial\omega}=0\ \text{for all }|\alpha|\leq m-1\right\},

where the boundary values are understood in the sense of traces. We refer readers to
[
1
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.37]
for more details.

Let us now recall some properties of Sobolev spaces involving time. For
Q=(0,T)\times\Omega
and integers
r,s\geq 0
, we define the anisotropic Sobolev space

H^{r,s}(Q):=H^{r}\bigl(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\bigr)\cap L^{2}\bigl(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)\bigr)
,
equipped with the norm

(2.2)

\|u\|_{H^{r,s}(Q)}:=\|u\|_{H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega))}.

In particular, when
r=s=m
, by the standard characterization of Sobolev spaces on product domains, we have
H^{m,m}(Q)=H^{m}(Q)
with equivalent norms, see for instance
[
44
, Chapter 4, Proposition 2.1]
.
In particular, we have
H^{0,0}(Q)=L^{2}(Q)
when
m=0
.

3.
Well-posedness of the Forward Problem

Our goal of this section is to show that the initial boundary value problem
(
1.4
) is well-posed for small boundary data
f
. The argument is lengthy and technical. Thus, we shall divide it into several steps. First, in Proposition
3.1
, we establish the well-posedness of a nonhomogeneous linear dynamical Schrödinger equation with zero initial and boundary conditions. In particular, the regularity of solutions is optimal. Subsequently, under stronger regularity assumptions on the coefficients and the source term, we show in Lemma
3.3
that the solution poses better regularity as well.
We then incorporate nonhomogeneous boundary conditions and obtain a corresponding well-posedness result in Lemma
3.4
. Finally, building on these results for linear equations, provided that
f
is small in a suitable sense, we establish the well-posedness for the initial boundary value problem (
1.4
) in Theorem
3.8
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded open set with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. For any
T>0
, let
Q=(0,T)\times\Omega
and
\Sigma=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
.
Suppose that
A\in W^{1,\infty}(Q;\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
q\in L^{\infty}(Q;\mathbb{C})
, and
F\in L^{2}(Q)
. Then the initial boundary value problem

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla u+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot Au-|A|^{2}u+qu=F&\text{ in }Q,\\
u=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a unique solution
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
which satisfies the estimate

(3.2)

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|u\|_{H^{1,1}(Q)}\leq C_{1}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Here the constant
C_{1}>0
depends on

\|A\|_{W^{1,\infty}(Q)}
,
\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}
, and
T
, but is independent of
u
, and the norm
\|u\|_{H^{1,1}(Q)}
is given by (
2.2
).

Proof.

The proof is completed via several steps. First, we introduce an equivalent weak formulation of the initial boundary value problem (
3.1
), followed by applying the Faedo-Galerkin method to establish the existence of a solution. At this stage, the solution
u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right).

Next, we verify that the constructed solution satisfies the initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=0
in
\Omega
, as well as the uniqueness of solutions. Finally, using elliptic regularity, we improve the spatial regularity of the solution and conclude that
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right)\cap H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right).

For almost every
t\in(0,T)
, and for all
u(t,\cdot)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, we define a sesquilinear form

(3.3)

\displaystyle a(t,u,v)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla u(t,\cdot),\nabla v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\langle\left(-2\mathrm{i}A(t,\cdot)\cdot\nabla-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot)\right)u(t,\cdot),v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle:=a_{0}(t,u,v)+a_{1}(t,u,v).

It then follows from the triangle inequality that

(3.4)

|a(t,u,u)|\geq|a_{0}(t,u,u)|-|a_{1}(t,u,u)|.

We also observe that

(3.5)

a_{0}(t,u,u)=\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Let us now estimate
a_{1}(t,u,u)
. For the first order term, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, together with the inequality

(3.6)

\|fg\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{ for }f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ and }g\in L^{2}(\Omega),

we get

(3.7)

\left|\left\langle-2\mathrm{i}A(t,\cdot)\cdot\nabla u(t,\cdot),u(t,\cdot)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|\leq 2\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Applying the inequality
2ab\leq\widetilde{\varepsilon}a^{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{\widetilde{\varepsilon}}

for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
\widetilde{\varepsilon}>0
, we obtain

(3.8)

2\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\widetilde{\varepsilon}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{\varepsilon}}\left\|A(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Similarly, for the zeroth-order terms appearing in
a_{1}(t,u,u)
, it holds that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\left(-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot)\right)u(t,\cdot),u(t,\cdot)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Thus, using (
3.4
)–(
3.9
), we deduce that

(3.10)

\displaystyle|a(t,u,u)|\geq(1-\widetilde{\varepsilon})\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\gamma\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for some constant
\gamma>0
, which depends on
\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}
,
\|q(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, and
\widetilde{\varepsilon}
, but is independent of
u(t,\cdot)
.
Furthermore, choosing
\widetilde{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{4}
, we get

(3.11)

|a(t,u,u)|\geq\frac{3}{4}\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\gamma\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

The weak formulation of the initial boundary value problem (
3.1
) is to find a unique function
u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right)
such that for all

v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, we have

(3.12)

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u,v)=\langle F(t,\cdot),v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{ and }\quad u(0,x)=0.

To construct such a solution, we use the Faedo-Galerkin method, see
[
45
, Section 3, Theorem 10.1]
. This method is also applied in for instance
[
25
,
36
]
.
Let
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
such that it is also
orthogonal in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, see
[
13
, Theorem 9.31]
, and set

E_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
.
We seek an approximate solution of the form

u_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{k}^{(n)}(t)w_{k}(x),\quad(t,x)\in Q.

Here the coefficient functions
g_{k}^{(n)}(t)
are chosen in such a way that
u_{n}
,
k=1,\dots,n
, satisfy the initial value problem

(3.13)

\begin{cases}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),w_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u_{n},w_{k})=\langle F(t,\cdot),w_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\\
u_{n}(0,x)=0,\end{cases}

Using the orthonormality of
\{w_{k}\}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, the condition
u_{n}(0,x)=0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, and the sesquilinearity of
a
, we see that the coefficient
g^{(n)}_{j}
satisfies the initial value problem

(3.14)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}g_{j}^{(n)}(t)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}a(t,w_{k},w_{j})g_{k}^{(n)}(t)=f_{j}(t,\cdot),\\
g_{j}^{(n)}(0)=0,\end{cases}

where
f_{j}(t,\cdot):=\langle F(t,\cdot),w_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\in L^{2}\left((0,T);\mathbb{C}\right)
for
j=1,2\dots n
.
Let us introduce the vectors
g^{(n)}(t):=(g_{1}^{(n)}(t),\dots,g_{n}^{(n)}(t))^{T}
and
F_{n}(t,\cdot):=(f_{1}(t,\cdot),\dots,f_{n}(t,\cdot))^{T}
, as well as the matrix
\mathbb{A}(t,\cdot):=(a(t,w_{k},w_{j}))_{j,k=1}^{n}
. Then we may rewrite (
3.14
) as the vector system given by

(3.15)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}g^{(n)}(t)-\mathbb{A}(t,\cdot)g^{(n)}(t)=F_{n}(t,\cdot),\\
g^{(n)}(0)=0.\end{cases}

By integrating the first equation of (
3.15
) over the interval
(0,t)
and using the initial condition

g^{(n)}(0)=0
, we obtain an equivalent integral equation

(3.16)

g^{(n)}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{A}(s)g^{(n)}(s)ds-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}F_{n}(s)ds,\quad t\in(0,T).

We now show that for any vector
F_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n})
and matrix
\mathbb{A}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
, the equation (
3.16
) has a unique solution
g^{(n)}\in H^{1}\left(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
, which also satisfies (
3.15
).
The idea is to introduce an operator whose fixed point solves
(
3.16
). Denoting this fixed point by
g^{(n)}(t)
, we will prove that the operator is a contraction on a sufficiently small time interval. The existence of a local solution then follows from the Banach fixed point theorem. This solution is subsequently extended to the whole
interval
(0,T)
by an iterative argument.

Let the operator
\Phi:H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n})\rightarrow H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n})
be given by the formula

\Phi(\widetilde{g})(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{A}(s)\widetilde{g}(s)ds-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}F_{n}(s)ds,

where
\mathbb{A}
and
F_{n}
are as in the initial value problem
(
3.15
). We shall prove that
\Phi
is a contraction for some suitable time
T_{\star}\in(0,T)
, which will be chosen later. For any
\widetilde{g}_{1},\widetilde{g}_{2}\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we have

(3.17)

\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})(t)-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{A}(s)\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}(s)-\widetilde{g}_{2}(s)\right)ds.

Using Hölder’s inequality and the boundedness of
\mathbb{A}
, we obtain for any
t\in(0,t^{\prime})\subseteq(0,T)
that

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})(t)-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}|\widetilde{g}_{1}(s)-\widetilde{g}_{2}(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\|\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\|_{L^{2}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}.

Taking the supremum over
t\in(0,t^{\prime})
yields that

(3.18)

\left\|\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}\leq\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\left\|\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}.

Let us next estimate the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the derivative. To this end, by differentiating the equation (
3.17
) with respect to
t
and using the Leibniz rule, we get

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})(t)-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})(t)\right)=-\mathrm{i}\mathbb{A}(t)\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}(t)-\widetilde{g}_{2}(t)\right).

Hence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have for
t\in(0,t^{\prime})
that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})(t)-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})(t)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}(s)-\widetilde{g}_{2}(s)\right)ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\left|\frac{d}{ds}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}(s)-\widetilde{g}_{2}(s)\right)\right|ds

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}.

Taking the supremum over
t\in(0,t^{\prime})
, we obtain

(3.19)

\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}.

Therefore, it follows from the estimates (
3.18
) and (
3.19
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}+\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\left(\|\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}+\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}\right).

Furthermore, using the inequality

(3.20)

\|f\|_{L^{2}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}\leq\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)},\quad f\in L^{\infty}\left(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right),

along with estimates (
3.18
) and (
3.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right\|_{H^{1}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{t^{\prime}}\left(\left\|\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}+\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{1})-\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{2})\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq t^{\prime}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})}\left(\|\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}+\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq t^{\prime}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\right)}\|\widetilde{g}_{1}-\widetilde{g}_{2}\|_{H^{1}(0,t^{\prime};\mathbb{C}^{n})}.

Let us choose
t^{\prime}>0
sufficiently small such that
t^{\prime}\|\mathbb{A}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\right)}<1
and denote such
t^{\prime}=T_{\star}
.
Thus, the map
\Phi:H^{1}(0,T_{\star};\mathbb{C}^{n})\to H^{1}(0,T_{\star};\mathbb{C}^{n})
is a contraction.
Hence, by the Banach fixed point theorem, there exists a unique

g^{(n)}\in H^{1}(0,T_{\star};\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying the integral equation (
3.16
).

We next show that the solution
g^{(n)}
can be extended to the interval
\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2}\right)
, namely,
g^{(n)}\in H^{1}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
, which satisfies the equation (
3.16
).
To this end, we define the operator
\widetilde{\Phi}:H^{1}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)\rightarrow H^{1}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
by

\widetilde{\widetilde{g}}\mapsto-\mathrm{i}\int_{\frac{T_{\star}}{2}}^{t}\mathbb{A}(s)\widetilde{\widetilde{g}}(s)ds-\mathrm{i}\int_{\frac{T_{\star}}{2}}^{t}F_{n}(s)ds+\widetilde{\widetilde{g}}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2}\right),

where
\mathbb{A}
and
F_{n}
are as in the initial value problem (
3.15
). By following similar arguments as above, we conclude that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is a contraction.
Hence, by the Banach fixed point theorem,
\widetilde{\Phi}
admits a unique fixed point in

H^{1}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2};\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
,
which solves the equation (
3.16
) on the interval

\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},\frac{3T_{\star}}{2}\right)
. Furthermore, by the uniqueness of the fixed point, this solution agrees with the previously
constructed
g^{(n)}
in the overlapping interval

\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{2},T_{\star}\right)
. As a consequence,
g^{(n)}
satisfies
(
3.16
) on the larger interval

\left(0,\frac{3T_{\star}}{2}\right)
.
Proceeding iteratively, we obtain a solution
g^{(n)}\in H^{1}\left(0,T;\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
to (
3.16
), which implies that
u_{n}\in H^{1}\left(0,T;E_{n}\right)
.

Our next goal is to derive the uniform estimate

(3.21)

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|_{L^{2}\left(Q\right)}\leq\widetilde{K}_{1}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

where the constant
\widetilde{K}_{1}>0
depends on
T
,
\|A\|_{W^{1,\infty}(Q)}
, and
\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}
, but is independent of
n
. To achieve this, we first establish the estimate

(3.22)

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Let us start by multiplying the equation (
3.13
) by
\overline{g_{k}^{(n)}(t)}
and summing over
k=1,2,\dots n
to get

(3.23)

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u_{n},u_{n})=\langle F(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then it follows immediately from (
3.3
) that

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})=\langle F(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n}).

By equating the real parts of the previous equation, we have

-a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})=\mathrm{Re}\left(\langle F(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+\mathrm{Re}\left(a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right).

Hence, using the inequality
|\mathrm{Re}(z)|\leq|z|
for any

z\in\mathbb{C}
, as well as the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\left|a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|\leq\left\|F(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|.

Therefore, it follows immediately that

\left|a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|-|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})|\leq\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Applying the inequality

(3.24)

ab\leq a^{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{4},\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R},

we deduce that

(3.25)

\left|a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|-|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})|\leq\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Let us recall from the equation (
3.5
) that

\left|a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|=\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Furthermore, we deduce from the estimates (
3.7
)–(
3.9
) that

(3.26)

|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Here
\gamma>0
is a constant depending on
\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}
and
\|q(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, but is independent of
u_{n}(t,\cdot)
.
Therefore, it holds that

(3.27)

\left|a_{0}(t,u_{n},u_{n})\right|-|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})|\geq\frac{3}{4}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\gamma\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Moreover, it follows immediately from the estimates (
3.25
) and (
3.27
) that

(3.28)

\frac{3}{4}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\gamma\right)\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

On the other hand, we take the imaginary parts of the identity (
3.23
) and utilize the fact that

\mathrm{Im}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

to obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\mathrm{Im}\langle F(t,\cdot),u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathrm{Im}\,a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n}).

Due to the inequality
|\mathrm{Im}(z)|\leq|z|
for any

z\in\mathbb{C}
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+|a_{1}(t,u_{n},u_{n})|.

From here, an application of the inequality (
3.24
) and the estimate (
3.26
) gives us

(3.29)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|F(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\gamma\right)\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, by adding the estimates (
3.28
) and (
3.29
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\left(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\right)\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\|F(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Multiplying both sides of the inequality above by the function

\frac{1}{2}e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}
and applying the product rule, we obtain

\frac{1}{4}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{4}e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Integrating the inequality above over
(0,t)
and using the initial condition
u_{n}(0,x)=0
, we see that

(3.30)

\frac{e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}}{4}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{4}e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})s}\|\nabla u_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})s}\|F(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds.

Since the function
e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}
is positive and decreasing on
[0,T]
, it follows that

e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\leq e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})t}\leq 1\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

Applying this bound to (
3.30
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}}{4}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{4}e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})s}\|\nabla u_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|F(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds.

Furthermore, due to the fact that
e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})s}\geq e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}
for
s\in[0,T]
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}}{4}\left(\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\right)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|F(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds.

We now take the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
to conclude that

(3.31)

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq 4e^{2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

From here, we obtain the estimate (
3.22
) by utilizing the inequality
\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{a+b}
for
a,b\geq 0
.

Let us next derive an estimate for
\partial_{t}u_{n}
. By multiplying equation (
3.13
) with
\partial_{t}\overline{g_{k}^{(n)}(t)}
and summing over
k=1,2,\dots n
, we have

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{0}(t,u_{n},\partial_{t}u_{n})=\langle F(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+a_{1}(t,u_{n},\partial_{t}u_{n}).

We observe that

\mathrm{Im}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

and argue similarly as before to deduce that

(3.32)

\|\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\left|a_{1}(t,u_{n},\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot))\right|+\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

To estimate the term
\left|a_{1}(t,u_{n},\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot))\right|
, using the definition of
a_{1}
in (
3.3
), in conjunction with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the inequality (
3.24
), we obtain

(3.33)

\displaystyle\left|\langle(-2\mathrm{i}A(t,\cdot)\cdot\nabla-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot))u_{n}(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

We now substitute (
3.33
) into (
3.32
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4\|A(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\nabla u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)+|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}-q(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Integrating it over the interval
(0,T)
and using the inequality (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq 4\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\left\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A+|A|^{2}-q\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

Moreover, we derive from the inequality (
3.20
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}

\displaystyle+T\left\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A+|A|^{2}-q\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

Let

(3.34)

\frac{\widetilde{K}}{4}:=\max\left\{4\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2},T\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A+|A|^{2}-q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\right\}.

Then the previous estimate reads

\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\widetilde{K}\left(\|\nabla u_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|u_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}\right)+4\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

Furthermore, an application of the estimate (
3.31
) gives us

\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq 4\widetilde{K}e^{2(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+4\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

Taking the square root of both sides and using the inequality
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for any
a,b\geq 0
, we get

(3.35)

\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{K}}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+1\right)\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Finally, we obtain the estimate (
3.21
) by combining the estimates (
3.22
) and (
3.35
). Here we have defined

\widetilde{K}_{1}:=\max\left\{2\sqrt{\widetilde{K}}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+2,\;\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\right\},

where
\widetilde{K}
is given by (
3.34
).

In view of the uniform bound (
3.21
), the sequence

\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded in
H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
. Hence, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a subsequence

\{u_{n_{l}}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
and a function

u\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))

such that
u_{n_{l}}\to u
weakly in
L^{2}(Q)
,
\nabla u_{n_{l}}\to\nabla u

weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;(L^{2}(\Omega))^{n})
, and

\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}\to\partial_{t}u

weakly in
L^{2}(Q)
.

For a fixed integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
n_{l}\geq m
, and suppose
w\in E_{m}
be a test function.
By (
3.12
), we obtain

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}(t,\cdot),w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u_{n_{l}},w)=\langle F(t,\cdot),w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{n_{l}}(0,x)=0.

We next multiply the identity above by the function
\overline{\phi(t)}
, where
\phi\in C^{\infty}([0,T])
satisfies
\phi(T)=0
, and integrate over the interval
(0,T)
to deduce that

(3.36)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt-\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u_{n_{l}},\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt.

Let us now analyze the limit of each term as
n_{l}\to\infty
. To this end, since
\phi w\in L^{2}(Q)
, an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields that

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}(u_{n_{l}}-u)(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|dt\leq\sqrt{T}\left\|\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}(u_{n_{l}}-u)(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}.

Thus, as
\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}\to\partial_{t}u
weakly in
L^{2}(Q)
, it holds that

(3.37)

\lim_{n_{l}\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt.

We next turn our attention to the term involving the sesquilinear form
a(t,u_{n_{l}},\phi w)
, which is defined in (
3.3
). First, we consider the principal part
a_{0}
. Since
\nabla(\phi w)\in L^{2}(Q)
,
u_{n_{l}}\to u
weakly in
L^{2}(Q)
, and
\nabla u_{n_{l}}\to\nabla u
weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;(L^{2}(\Omega))^{n})
, it follows that

\lim_{n_{l}\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}a_{0}(t,u_{n_{l}},\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}a_{0}(t,u,\phi w)dt

and

\lim_{n_{l}\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}a_{1}(t,u_{n_{l}},\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}a_{1}(t,u,\phi w)dt.

Hence, we conclude from the two equations above that

(3.38)

\lim_{n_{l}\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u_{n_{l}},\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u,\phi w)dt.

Therefore, by passing to the limit
n_{l}\to\infty
in (
3.36
), due to
(
3.37
) and (
3.38
), we have for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}([0,T])
satisfying
\phi(T)=0
that

(3.39)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt-\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u,\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt

In particular, this equation holds for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
. Therefore, using the Fundamental Lemma of calculus of variation, see
[
12
, Chapter 1, Lemma 1.4.2]
, the identity

(3.40)

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u,w)=\langle F(t,\cdot),w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

is valid for every
w\in E_{m}
. Since
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is orthogonal in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the union

\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}E_{m}

is dense in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
This allows us to extend (
3.40
) to all

w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

To complete the verification that
u
satisfies (
3.12
), it remains to show that
u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. To that end, since
\phi(T)=0
, we integrate by parts in the first term of (
3.39
) to obtain

(3.41)

-\langle\mathrm{i}u(0,\cdot),\phi(0)w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}u(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}(\phi w)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt-\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u,\phi w)dt=\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt.

On the other hand, integrating by parts in (
3.36
) and passing to the limit
n_{l}\to\infty
, we get

(3.42)

\displaystyle-\lim_{n_{l}\to\infty}\langle\mathrm{i}u_{n_{l}}(0,\cdot),\phi(0)w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathrm{i}u(t,\cdot),\partial_{t}(\phi w)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt-\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u,\phi w)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(t,\cdot),\phi w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt.

In particular, the first term vanishes. Thus, choosing
\phi
such that
\phi(0)\neq 0
, we deduce from (
3.41
) and (
3.42
) that
\langle\mathrm{i}u(0,\cdot),w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
for all
w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Hence, we conclude that
u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. Therefore, for any
F\in L^{2}(Q)
, there exists a function
u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)
that solves the inverse boundary value problem (
3.1
) in the sense that the weak formulation (
3.12
) is satisfied.

We next establish the uniqueness of solutions to the initial boundary value problem (
3.1
). To this end, let us assume that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
both satisfy (
3.12
) with the same source term
F
. Then their difference
u:=u_{1}-u_{2}
satisfies the equation

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u,v)=0,\quad\text{ for every }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

In particular, choosing
v=u(t,\cdot)
yields that

(3.43)

\langle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot),u(t,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(t,u,u)=0.

Let us recall from (
3.3
) that
a=a_{0}+a_{1}
. Thus, taking the real part of the previous equation gives us

a_{0}(t,u,u)=-\mathrm{Re}\left(a_{1}(t,u,u)\right).

Using the identity (
3.5
), as well as the inequality (
3.11
), we obtain

(3.44)

\frac{3}{4}\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\gamma\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for some constant
\gamma>0
.

On the other hand, we take the imaginary part of (
3.43
) to get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathrm{Im}\left(a_{1}(t,u,u)\right).

Using the estimates (
3.7
)–(
3.9
), we deduce that

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\gamma\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

In addition, in view of (
3.44
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{8\gamma}{3}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Multiplying the above inequality by
e^{-\frac{8\gamma}{3}t}
yields that

\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-\frac{8\gamma}{3}t}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\leq 0.

Thus, integrating from
0
to
t
and using the initial condition
u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
, we obtain

e^{-\frac{8\gamma}{3}t}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0.

Hence, we conclude that
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
,
which implies that
u\equiv 0
almost everywhere in
Q
. Therefore, the solution to the initial boundary value problem (
3.1
) is unique.

Our next aim is to prove that
u
possesses higher Sobolev regularity. More precisely, under the same assumptions on the coefficients, i.e.,
A\in W^{1,\infty}(Q)
,
q\in L^{\infty}(Q)
, and

F\in L^{2}(Q)
, we show that
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)
, which still satisfies the estimate (
3.2
).

As
u\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
satisfies the problem (
3.1
), for almost every fixed
t\in(0,T)
, the function
u(t,\cdot)
satisfies
the elliptic boundary value problem

\begin{cases}Lu(t,\cdot)=F(t,\cdot)-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot)&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u(t,\cdot)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where the second order elliptic partial differential operator
L
is given by

L:=\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A(t,\cdot)\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,\cdot)-|A(t,\cdot)|^{2}+q(t,\cdot).

Let us observe from the regularity of
u
established above that
\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(Q)
, which yields that
F-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(Q)
. Thus, it follows from
[
19
, Chapter 8, Theorem 8.12]
that
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)
and satisfies the estimate

(3.45)

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle\|2A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A-|A|^{2}+q\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Let us estimate the terms appearing on the right-hand side using the
lower semicontinuity of the norm with respect to weak convergence, see

[
13
, Chapter 3, Proposition 3.5]
.
Since
u_{n_{l}}\to u
and
\nabla u_{n_{l}}\to\nabla u
weakly as
n_{l}\to\infty
, we deduce from with the estimates (
3.20
) and
(
3.22
) that

(3.46)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Similarly, since
\partial_{t}u_{n_{l}}\to\partial_{t}u
weakly as
n_{l}\to\infty
, we apply
[
13
, Chapter 3, Proposition 3.5]
again, in conjunction with the estimate (
3.35
), to obtain

(3.47)

\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{K}}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+1\right)\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

where the constant
\widetilde{K}>0
is given by (
3.34
).
Therefore, by combining (
3.46
)
and (
3.47
), we conclude from (
3.45
) that

(3.48)

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\|2A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+1+\left\|\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A-|A|^{2}+q\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}

\displaystyle+2\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{K}}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+1\right)\bigg)\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Finally, we obtain the estimate (
3.2
) from (
3.46
)–(
3.48
). In particular, the constant
C_{1}>0
is given by

(3.49)

\displaystyle C_{1}:=

\displaystyle\left(\|2A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+1\right)\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+\left(\|\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A-|A|^{2}+q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+2\right)\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}

\displaystyle+8\sqrt{\max\left\{4\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2},T\|-\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A+|A|^{2}-q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{2}\right\}}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}+5.

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Let
u
be the solution obtained in Proposition

. For almost every

,
u(t,\cdot)
is the solution of the elliptic boundary value problem

(3.50)

\begin{cases}\Delta u(t,\cdot)=\mathfrak{F}(t,\cdot)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u(t,\cdot)=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

\mathfrak{F}=F-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u-2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla u-\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A)u+|A|^{2}u-qu

by the initial boundary value problem (

).
We first verify that

. Indeed, using

\displaystyle\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+2\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\|a\|\leq C\|b\|

C>0
is independent of
a
and

Since
\Omega
has a smooth boundary, elliptic regularity for the

[
16

yields, for almost every

Squaring the preceding estimate and integrating over

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|\mathfrak{F}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Therefore, we can improve the estimate (
3.2
) in the sense that

In the next lemma, we assume that the coefficients
A
and
q
, along with the source term

(3.56)

\displaystyle-\left.\partial_{t}^{k}|A(t,x)|^{2}\partial_{t}^{m-k}u+\partial_{t}^{k}q(t,x)\partial_{t}^{m-k}u\right).

Therefore, using equations (

3
), along with the assumption

and satisfies the estimate

(3.57)

\|u\|_{H^{m+2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}\leq\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(t)}\|F\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)},

where the constant
\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(t)}>0
is independent of
u
.
To achieve this, we apply
\partial_{t}^{m+1}
to the initial boundary value problem (
3.1
). By the Leibniz rule and the compatibility conditions, it holds that
\partial_{t}^{m+1}u
satisfies the initial boundary value problem

(3.58)

\begin{cases}\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}+\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A-|A|^{2}+q\right)\partial_{t}^{m+1}u=F_{1}&\text{ in }Q,\\
\partial^{m+1}_{t}u=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\partial^{m+1}_{t}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle F_{1}=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{m+1}F-2\mathrm{i}\left(\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\right)\cdot\nabla u-\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A)u+\left(\partial_{t}^{m+1}\left(|A|^{2}\right)\right)u-\left(\partial_{t}^{m+1}q\right)u

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m+1}{k}\left(2\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}^{k}A\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}(\nabla u)+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{k}A\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u-\partial_{t}^{k}|A|^{2}\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\partial_{t}^{k}q\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right).

In what follows, let us denote

\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m+1}{k}\left(2\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}^{k}A\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{k}A\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\partial_{t}^{k}|A|^{2}\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u+\partial_{t}^{k}q\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right).

Applying Proposition
3.1
to
the problem (
3.58
), together with the induction hypothesis, we conclude that

u\in H^{m+2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Furthermore, due to the estimate (
3.2
), we have

(3.59)

\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}u\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C_{1}\|F_{1}\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

where the constant
C_{1}>0
is given by (
3.49
).

In view of the expression of
F_{1}
given above, as well as the triangle inequality, we get

(3.60)

\displaystyle\|F_{1}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|2\mathrm{i}(\partial_{t}^{m+1}A)\cdot\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A)u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|(\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2}))u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|(\partial_{t}^{m+1}q)u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

To estimate the terms involving
A
and
q
, an application of the inequality (
3.6
) yields that

(3.61)

\displaystyle\left\|2\mathrm{i}(\partial_{t}^{m+1}A)\cdot\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A)u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

\displaystyle\left\|(\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2}))u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

\displaystyle\left\|(\partial_{t}^{m+1}q)u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Hence, it follows from (
3.59
)–(
3.61
) that

(3.62)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}u\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\left(\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+2\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\|\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right).

We next derive an upper bound for
\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}
. Using its definition given previously, in conjunction with the inequality (
3.6
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m+1}{k}\left[2\left\|\partial_{t}^{k}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla(\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|\partial_{t}^{k}(\nabla\cdot A)\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left\|\partial_{t}^{k}(|A|^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|\partial_{t}^{k}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\partial_{t}^{m+1-k}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right].

Let us recall the monotonicity of Sobolev norms given by

\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(Q)}\leq\|f\|_{W^{m,p}(Q)},\quad p=2,\infty.

Therefore, we obtain for
k\leq m
that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m+1}{k}\left[2\left\|A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\right\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\right\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|q\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right].

Moreover, it follows from the binomial theorem that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m+1}{k}=2^{m+1}-2\leq 2^{m+1}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\left(2\left\|A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\right\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\right\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|q\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

Therefore, using the estimate (
3.53
), we deduce from the previous estimate that

(3.63)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\left(2\left\|A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\right\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right),

where
K_{\mathrm{ind.}}
is the same as in (
3.53
).

It still remains to estimate
\|\nabla u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
. To this end, we observe that it suffices to deduce an upper bound for
\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}
. Thanks to the induction hypothesis (
3.52
), we have
u\in H^{m+1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))
. Hence, by an interpolation property, see for instance
[
44
, Chapter 4, Proposition 2.1]
, it follows that
u\in H^{m}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. Moreover, applying the interpolation estimate given in
[
45
, Chapter 1, Proposition 2.3]
, we obtain

(3.64)

\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C_{m,T}\|u\|_{H^{m+1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{m}{m+1}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{m+1}}.

Here
C_{m,T}>0
is a constant depending only on
m
and
T
.
From here, we apply the induction hypothesis (
3.53
) to get

\|u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C_{m,T}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m,m}(Q)}.

Then it follows immediately that

(3.65)

\|\nabla u\|_{H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C_{m,T}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m,m}(Q)}.

Therefore, by substituting (
3.65
) into (
3.63
), we have

(3.66)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{m+1}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\left(2C_{m,T}\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)\|F\|_{H^{m,m}(Q)}.

Using the definition of Sobolev norms, we get

(3.67)

\left\|u\right\|_{H^{m+2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}\leq\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}u\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Hence, in view of the estimates (
3.46
), (
3.53
), (
3.62
), (
3.66
), and (
3.67
), we obtain the estimate (
3.57
),
where the constant
\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(t)}>0
is given by

(3.68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(t)}=

\displaystyle C_{1}\left[1+2^{m+1}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}C_{m,T}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)+\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}}\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\right)\right]+K_{\mathrm{ind.}}.

We now turn to establish the regularity of
u
in the spatial variable
x
. The strategy is analogous to the time-differentiation argument presented earlier, although the computations are slightly more involved due to the presence of multi-indices.
In the same spirit as (
3.58
), we consider spatial derivatives of order
|\alpha|=m+1
, where
\alpha
is a multi-index. Applying the differential operator
\partial^{\alpha}
to the problem (
3.1
), and using the
Leibniz rule, along with the induction hypothesis and the compatibility condition, we deduce that
\partial^{\alpha}u
satisfies the inverse boundary value problem

(3.69)

\begin{cases}\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}+\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A-|A|^{2}+q\right)\partial^{\alpha}u=F_{2}&\text{ in }Q,\\
\partial^{\alpha}u=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\partial^{\alpha}u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where the source term
F_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle F_{2}(t,x)=

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}F-2\mathrm{i}\left(\partial^{\alpha}A\right)\cdot\nabla u-\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot\partial^{\alpha}A)u+\left(\partial^{\alpha}\left(|A|^{2}\right)\right)u-\left(\partial^{\alpha}q\right)u

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\alpha\\
0<|\beta|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left(2\mathrm{i}\partial^{\beta}A\cdot\nabla\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot\partial^{\beta}A\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u-\partial^{\beta}\left(|A|^{2}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u+\partial^{\beta}q\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right).

Let us denote

\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\alpha\\
0<|\beta|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left(2\mathrm{i}\partial^{\beta}A\cdot\nabla\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot\partial^{\beta}A\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u-\partial^{\beta}\left(|A|^{2}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u+\partial^{\beta}q\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right).

We get induction hypothesis (
3.52
) that
F_{2}\in L^{2}(Q)
. Therefore, an application of Proposition
3.1
to the problem (
3.69
) yields that

\partial^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
Thus, it follows immediately that

u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{m+3}(\Omega)\cap H^{m+2}_{0}(\Omega)\right)
.
Moreover, the following estimate holds:

\|\Delta\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C_{1}\|F_{2}\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

where
C_{1}>0
is a constant given by (
3.49
).

Using analogous calculations to those in the estimates (
3.60
) and (
3.61
), we obtain

(3.70)

\displaystyle\|\Delta\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\left(\|\partial^{\alpha}F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+2\|\partial^{\alpha}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot\partial^{\alpha}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\partial^{\alpha}(|A|^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial^{\alpha}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right).

Also, by arguing similarly as in the time-derivative case, we estimate
\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\alpha\\
0<|\beta|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left(2\left\|\partial^{\beta}A\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left\|\nabla(\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot\partial^{\beta}A\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left\|\partial^{\beta}\left(|A|^{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\left\|\partial^{\beta}q\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right).

Since
|\beta|\leq m
, the coefficient terms above are bounded in
L^{\infty}(Q)
by the
W^{m,\infty}(Q)
-norms of
A
and
q
. Similarly, as
|\alpha-\beta|\leq m
, we can bound the derivatives of
u
by the
H^{m}(\Omega)
-norms. Thus, it holds that

(3.71)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\alpha\\
0<|\beta|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right).

Using the multinomial identity, we obtain for any multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})
that

(3.72)

\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}=\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}\left\{\sum_{\beta_{j}=0}^{\alpha_{j}}\binom{\alpha_{j}}{\beta_{j}}\right\}=\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}2^{\alpha_{j}}=2^{|\alpha|}.

Excluding the cases
\beta=0
and
\beta=\alpha
, when
|\alpha|=m+1
, we have

(3.73)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\alpha\\
0<|\beta|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}=2^{|\alpha|}-2\leq 2^{m+1}.

Thus, it follows immediately from estimates (
3.71
) and (
3.73
) that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}_{A,q}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right).

We next substitute the previous estimate into the estimate
(
3.70
)
and utilize the monotonicity of Sobolev norms to obtain

\displaystyle\|\Delta\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\left[\|F\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+2^{m+1}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right)+2\|A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|q\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right].

Furthermore, we utilize the triangle inequality and the definition of Sobolev norms to deduce that

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\left\|\Delta u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega)\right)}+\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega)\right)}.

Hence, using the induction hypothesis (
3.53
), as well as the estimate (
3.46
), we get

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+C_{1}\left[\|F\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+2^{m+1}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\left(2\|A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|q\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+2\|A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|q\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right],

where
C_{1}>0
is given by (
3.49
), and
K_{\mathrm{ind.}}>0
is the same as in (
3.53
).
Furthermore, due to the fact that

\|F\|_{H^{s}(Q)}\leq\|F\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},\quad s=0,m,

the previous estimate reads

(3.74)

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\leq\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(x)}\|F\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

where the constant
\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(x)}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m+1}^{(x)}:=

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{ind.}}+C_{1}\left[1+2^{m+1}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)+\sqrt{8}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\left(2\|A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\sqrt{T}\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\sqrt{T}\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\sqrt{T}\|q\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right)\right].

Finally, we obtain the estimate (
3.54
) by adding the estimates (
3.57
) and (
3.74
). Here the constant
C_{2}>0
is given by

(3.75)

\displaystyle C_{2}=

\displaystyle 2K_{\mathrm{ind.}}+\widetilde{K}_{3}\left[2+2^{m+2}K_{\mathrm{ind.}}\left(\left(C_{m,T}+1\right)\|A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|A\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|q\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)+\sqrt{8T}e^{(2\gamma+\frac{1}{2})T}\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}}\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{m+1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}(|A|^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}^{m+1}q\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}}\|A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\|\nabla\cdot A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\||A|^{2}\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\|q\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right)\right].

This completes the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

We next consider the case of nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. To establish the well-posedness, which is stated below, we reduce the problem to one with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and a non-zero source term.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega,Q
, and
\Sigma
be the same as in Proposition
3.1
. Let
A\in W^{m+1,\infty}(Q;\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
q\in W^{m,\infty}(Q;\mathbb{C})
,
F\in H^{m}(Q)
, and
f\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Assume that
\partial_{t}^{k}F(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,\dots,m-1
, and that
\partial^{k}_{t}f(0,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
for
k=0,\dots,m
. Then the initial boundary value problem

(3.76)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla u+\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A)u-|A|^{2}u+qu=F&\text{ in }Q,\\
u=f&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a unique solution
u\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
,
which satisfies the compatibility condition
\partial_{t}^{k}u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,1,\dots,m
. Moreover, we have the estimate

(3.77)

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq C_{3}\left(\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right).

Here the constant
C_{3}>0
depends on
\Omega
,
T
,
m
,
\|A\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}
, and
\|q\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}
, but is independent of
u
.

Proof.

By
[
44
, Chapter 4, Theorem 2.3]
, there exists a function
w\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
such that

(3.78)

w|_{\Sigma}=f,\quad\text{ and }\quad\partial_{t}^{k}w(0,x)=0\quad\text{for almost every }x\in\Omega\text{ and all }k=0,1,\dots,m,

and satisfies the estimate

(3.79)

\|w\|_{H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)}\leq C(m,T,\Omega)\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

where
C(m,T,\Omega)>0
is a constant that depends on
m
,
\Omega
, and
T
, but is independent of
w
.

By Lemma
3.3
, the initial boundary value problem

(3.80)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}v+\Delta v+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla v+\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A)v-|A|^{2}v+qv=F-\widetilde{F}_{1}&\text{ in }Q,\\
v=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
v(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where

\widetilde{F}_{1}:=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}w+\Delta w+2\mathrm{i}A\cdot\nabla w+\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A)w-|A|^{2}w+qw\in H^{m}(Q),

has a unique solution
v\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
.
Furthermore, the following estimate holds:

(3.81)

\displaystyle\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\left(\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|\partial_{t}w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|\Delta w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+2\left\|A\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|(\nabla\cdot A)w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\||A|^{2}w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|qw\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right).

Let us next estimate
\left\|A\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}
. To this end, using the Leibniz rule, in conjunction with the inequality (
3.6
), we obtain

(3.82)

\displaystyle\left\|A\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\left\|\partial^{\alpha}\left(A\cdot\nabla w\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\left\|\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\left(A\right)\cdot\partial^{\alpha-\beta}\left(\nabla w\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left\|\partial^{\beta}A\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}\left(\nabla w\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

By the monotonicity of the Sobolev norm, along with (
3.73
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|A\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}2^{|\alpha|}\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}1\right)2^{m}\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}.

Since the multi-indices
\alpha
are taken in
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
, it holds that

(3.83)

\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}1=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{n+k}{k}=\binom{n+m+1}{m}.

Therefore, we get

(3.84)

\left\|A\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq 2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}.

Arguing similarly as above, we obtain the following estimates:

(3.85)

\|(\nabla\cdot A)w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq 2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)},

(3.86)

\||A|^{2}w\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq 2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left\||A|^{2}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)},

and

(3.87)

\|qw\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq 2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

In view of (
3.84
)–(
3.87
), we get from the estimate (
3.81
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta v\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{m+1}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\left[\left\|F\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1,m}(Q)}+\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m,m+2}(Q)}+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\||A|^{2}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\right],

where the constant
C_{2}>0
is given by (
3.75
). Moreover, the estimate (
3.79
) and the monotonicity of Sobolev norms yield that

(3.88)

\displaystyle\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|v\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\left\{\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+C(m,T,\Omega)\left[2+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.+\left\||A|^{2}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\ \right)\right]\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right\}.

Let
u:=v+w\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
. Then it is straightforward to see that
u
satisfies the problem (
3.76
). Also, we utilize the estimates (
3.79
) and (
3.88
), as well as the triangle inequality, to obtain

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|v\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}+\|\Delta w\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|w\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|v\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}+2\|w\|_{H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\left\{\|F\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+C(m,T,\Omega)\left[2+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.+\left\||A|^{2}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\ \right)\right]\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right\}

\displaystyle+2C(m,T,\Omega)\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}.

Thus, the estimate (
3.77
) follows immediately, where the constant
C_{3}>0
is defined by

(3.89)

\displaystyle C_{3}:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{C_{2},C_{2}C(m,T,\Omega)\left[2+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}\left(2\left\|A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot A\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.+\left\||A|^{2}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|q\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\ \right)\right]+2C(m,T,\Omega)\right\}.

Let us now turn to verifying the compatibility conditions, i.e.,
\partial_{t}^{k}u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,1,\dots,m
. Since
v
solves the initial boundary value problem (
3.80
), by the Lemma
3.3
, we get
\partial_{t}^{k}v(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,1,\dots,m
. Due to the fact that
u=v+w
, in view of (
3.78
), we have the compatibility condition for
u
.

It remains to prove the uniqueness of the solution to the initial boundary value problem (
3.76
). We argue exactly as in Proposition
3.1
. Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two solutions of (
3.76
) corresponding to the same source term
F
and the same Dirichlet data
f
. Setting
u:=u_{1}-u_{2}
and using the linearity of the equation, we see that
u
satisfies the problem (
3.1
). Therefore, by Lemma
3.3
, we have
u\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
. Applying the energy estimate (
3.51
), we conclude that
u\equiv 0
almost everywhere in
Q
. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Before establishing the well-posedness of the nonlinear initial boundary value problem (
1.4
), we state and prove some auxiliary results that will be crucial for the proof of Theorem
3.8
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Omega,Q
, and
\Sigma
be the same as in Proposition
3.1
. Suppose that

m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
If
f\in W^{m,\infty}(Q)
and
g\in H^{m}(Q)
, then
fg\in H^{m}(Q)
and

(3.90)

\|fg\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\|f\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)},

where

(3.91)

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}:=2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}.

Proof.

The proof follows from analogous arguments leading from (
3.82
) to (
3.84
).
∎

Next, we show that the Sobolev space
H^{m}(Q)
is closed under pointwise multiplication with a suitable regularity assumption. In general, it is not necessarily true that
fg\in H^{m}(Q)
if
f,g\in H^{m}(Q)
. However, if
m
is sufficiently large, then
H^{m}(Q)
becomes a Banach algebra, as stated in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
,
Q
, and
\Sigma
be the same as in Proposition
3.1
. Suppose

m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
is such that
m>n+1
. Then we have
fg\in H^{m}(Q)
for all
f,g\in H^{m}(Q)
. Moreover, there exists a constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}>0
such that

(3.92)

\|fg\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Proof.

Applying Leibniz’s formula, we obtain

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|\partial^{\alpha}(fg)\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left\|\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

We split the inner sum into two cases.

First, suppose that
|\beta|\leq\frac{m}{2}
. Then it follows immediately that
m-|\beta|\geq\frac{m}{2}
. Since
m>n+1
, we have
\frac{m}{2}>\frac{n+1}{2}
.
By the Sobolev embedding theorem,
it holds that
H^{m-|\beta|}(Q)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(Q)
. Thus, there exists a constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}>0
such that

\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{H^{m-|\beta|}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Consequently, using the inequality (
3.6
), we obtain

\left\|\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq K_{1}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

for some constant
K_{1}>0
.

Next, suppose that
|\beta|>\frac{m}{2}
. Since
\beta\leq\alpha
and
|\alpha|\leq m
, we have

|\alpha-\beta|=|\alpha|-|\beta|<m-\frac{m}{2}=\frac{m}{2}.

Hence, it holds that

m-|\alpha-\beta|>\frac{m}{2}>\frac{n+1}{2}.

Applying the Sobolev embedding theorem again,
we get for some constant
K_{2}>0
that

\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq K_{2}\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\|_{H^{m-|\alpha-\beta|}(Q)}\leq K_{2}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Thus, an application of the inequality (
3.6
) gives us

\left\|\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq K_{2}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Combining the previous two cases, we obtain the estimate

\left\|\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\alpha-\beta}g\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}=\max\{K_{1},K_{2}\}
.
Hence, arguing similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\|f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|g\|_{H^{m}(Q)},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}>0
is given by (
3.91
).
Finally, we obtain the estimate (
3.92
) by setting
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Embedding}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{m}(Q)
, then we have

(3.93)

\bigl|\|f_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}-\|f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\bigr|\leq\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Proof.

Clearly, if
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{m}(Q)
, we have
f_{1}-f_{2}\in H^{m}(Q)
. Thus, by the triangle inequality, we get

\|f_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Hence, it follows immediately that

\|f_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}-\|f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Using similar arguments, we get

\|f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}-\|f_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Therefore, we conclude from the previous two inequalities that (
3.93
) holds. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.7
.
∎

We next turn to establish the well-posedness of the nonlinear problem (
1.4
), provided that the boundary data
f
is sufficiently small in a suitable sense. Let us begin by introducing some notations used to describe the nonlinear magnetic and electric potentials. Let
\Psi
be a holomorphic function in a neighborhood of
z=0
, where
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
\Psi
admits the following power series expansion around
z=0
:

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\Psi(0)+\Psi^{\prime}(0)z+z^{2}\widetilde{\Psi}(z),

where

\widetilde{\Psi}(z):=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Psi^{(k)}(0)}{k!}z^{k-2}.

In view of Assumptions
1
–
3
, as well as (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), the nonlinear magnetic potential
\mathcal{A}
admits the expansion

\mathcal{A}(t,x,u)=A_{0}(t,x)+A_{1}(t,x)u+u^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,u).

Similarly, the nonlinear electric potential
q
can be written as

q(t,x,u)=q_{1}(t,x)u+\widetilde{q}(t,x,u)u^{2}.

Let us define the corresponding linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}_{0},q_{1}}
by

(3.94)

\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}u:=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u-\left(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0}(t,x)\right)^{2}u+q_{1}(t,x)u.

Then the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A},q}
defined in (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A},q}u=\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}u-\mathcal{R}(u),

where the nonlinear remainder
\mathcal{R}(u)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(u)=

\displaystyle(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0}(t,x))\left(A_{1}(t,x)u^{2}+u^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,u)\right)

\displaystyle+\left(A_{1}(t,x)u+u^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,u)\right)\left(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0}(t,x)\right)u+\left|A_{1}(t,x)u+u^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,u)\right|^{2}u

\displaystyle-\widetilde{q}(t,x,u)u^{2}.

Therefore, the nonlinear initial boundary value problem (
1.4
) can be reformulated as

(3.95)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}u=\mathcal{R}(u)&\text{in }Q,\\
u=f&\text{on }\Sigma,\\
u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

We are now ready to state and prove the well-posedness of the nonlinear initial boundary value problem (
1.4
) with small Dirichlet data
f
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}(t,x,z)
and
q(t,x,z)
satisfy Assumptions
1
-
3
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
m>n+1
, and let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be sufficiently small.
Then for all
f\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)
such that
\partial^{k}_{t}f(0,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
for all integers
0\leq k\leq m
and
\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, the problem (
1.4
) admits a unique solution
u\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
that satisfies the compatibility conditions

\partial_{t}^{k}u(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,1,\dots m
. Moreover, the following estimate holds:

(3.96)

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq 2C_{3}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

where the constant
C_{3}>0
is given by (
3.89
).

Proof.

We will use the standard fixed point argument. For this purpose, we shall work with the problem (
3.95
), which is an equivalent formulation of the nonlinear problem (
1.4
). This allows us to separate the linear part from the nonlinear remainder, which is well-suited to apply the fixed point argument. Then, we introduce an appropriate map on a suitable Banach space. We first show that this map is well-defined, followed by establishing that it is a contraction. Therefore, from the Banach fixed point theorem, we get a unique fixed point for this map, which is indeed a solution to the initial boundary value problem (
3.95
).

Motivated by the discussion above, let us define
\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, which is a closed subset of the Sobolev
H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}:=

\displaystyle\left\{g\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q):\|\Delta g\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|g\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq R,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\partial_{t}^{k}g(0,x)=0,\;\text{ for almost every }x\in\Omega\text{ and all }k=0,\dots,m\right\},

where
R>0
will be chosen later. We equip
\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
with the norm

\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}:=\|\Delta g\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|g\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}.

Let
v\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
. Consider the linear initial boundary value problem

(3.97)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}u=\mathcal{R}(v)&\text{in }Q,\\[3.00003pt]
u=f&\text{on }\Sigma,\\[3.00003pt]
u(0,\cdot)=0&\text{in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where the nonlinear remainder
\mathcal{R}(v)
is given by

(3.98)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v):=

\displaystyle(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0}(t,x))\left(A_{1}(t,x)v^{2}+v^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,v)\right)

\displaystyle+\left(A_{1}(t,x)v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,v)\right)\left(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0}(t,x)\right)v+\left|A_{1}(t,x)v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(t,x,v)\right|^{2}v

\displaystyle-\widetilde{q}(t,x,v)v^{2}.

Since
v\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, the coefficients
A_{0},A_{1},\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\in W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)
, and
\widetilde{q}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\in W^{m,\infty}(Q)
, it follows from Lemma
3.6
and the definition of
\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
that
\mathcal{R}(v)\in H^{m}(Q)
,
and
\partial_{t}^{k}v(0,x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and all
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
. Therefore, by Lemma
3.4
, there exists a unique solution

u\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
to the problem (
3.97
)
satisfying the estimate

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq C_{3}\left[\|\mathcal{R}(v)\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right].

Here the constant
C_{3}>0
is given by (
3.89
).
Thus, using (
3.98
), as well as the estimate (
3.90
) and the inequality
\|\nabla f\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\binom{n+m+2}{m+1}C_{3}\left[\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\right]

\displaystyle+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}C_{3}\left[2\|A_{0}A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+2\|A_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{2}\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{q}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right]

\displaystyle+C_{3}\left[\left\||A_{1}v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)|^{2}v\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right].

Furthermore, in view of the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}b_{i}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}\right),\quad a_{i},b_{i}\geq 0,\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

and keeping the last two terms above separate, we obtain

(3.99)

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\binom{n+m+2}{m+1}C_{3}\left[\left(\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right)\left(\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}C_{3}\left[\left(2\|A_{0}A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+2\|A_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{q}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right)\left(2\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\|v^{2}\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\right]+C_{3}\left[\big\||A_{1}v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)|^{2}v\big\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right].

Since
v\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, we infer from the estimate (
3.92
) that

(3.100)

\displaystyle\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},\quad\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}R^{3},\quad\|v\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},

\displaystyle\|v^{2}\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}R^{3}.

Here the constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}>0
is the same as in Lemma
3.6
.

On the other hand, to estimate the last two terms in (
3.99
), due to the estimates (
3.90
) and (
3.92
), in conjunction with the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\||A_{1}v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)|^{2}v\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\Big(\|A_{1}v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}^{2}+\|v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{H^{m}(Q)}^{2}\Big)\|v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2m+1}\binom{n+m+1}{m}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\Big(\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}^{2}R^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}^{2}R^{4}\Big)R.

Let us now assume that
0<R\leq 1
. Then we get immediately from (
3.100
) that

\displaystyle\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\big(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\big)R^{2},

(3.101)

\displaystyle 2\|v^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v^{3}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v^{2}\nabla v\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\big(3\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}+2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\big)R^{2}.

Thus, it holds for
0<R\leq 1
that

(3.102)

\displaystyle\big\||A_{1}v+v^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)|^{2}v\big\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2m+1}\binom{n+m+1}{m}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\Big(\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}^{2}\Big)R^{2}.

Therefore, substituting the estimates (
3
) and (
3.102
) into (
3.99
) yields that

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq C^{*}R^{2}+C_{3}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

where

(3.103)

\displaystyle C^{*}:=

\displaystyle 2^{m+1}\binom{n+m+2}{m+1}C_{3}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\right)\left(\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)\right)}\right)

\displaystyle+2^{m}\binom{n+m+1}{m}C_{3}\left(3\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}+2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\right)\left(2\|A_{0}A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+2\|A_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)\right)}+\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)\right)}+\|\widetilde{q}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C};W^{m,\infty}(Q)\right)}\right)

\displaystyle+2^{2m+1}\binom{n+m+1}{m}^{2}C_{3}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\Big(\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)\right)}^{2}\Big).

Therefore, by choosing
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
R>0
sufficiently small such that
C_{3}\varepsilon_{0}\leq\frac{R}{2}
and

C^{*}R^{2}\leq\frac{R}{2}
,
we obtain the estimate

(3.104)

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq R.

Consider the operator
\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}:\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}\to\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
defined by

(3.105)

\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}(v)=u,

where
u
is the solution to the linear problem (
3.97
) associated with

v\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
. We first note that the estimate (
3.104
) yields that
\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}
is well-defined.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}
is a contraction for a sufficiently small
R>0
that will be specified later. To simplify the expression of
\mathcal{R}(v)
defined in (
3.98
), let us introduce the notation
\mathcal{B}(v):=A_{1}+v\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)
, which leads to the following expression:

(3.106)

\mathcal{R}(v)=(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})\left(v^{2}\mathcal{B}(v)\right)+\mathcal{B}(v)v(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})v+|\mathcal{B}(v)|^{2}|v|^{2}v-\widetilde{q}(v)v^{2}.

Let
v_{j}\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
,
j=1,2
, and let

u_{j}=\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}(v_{j})
. Then
w:=u_{1}-u_{2}
satisfies the following initial boundary value problem:

(3.107)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{{A}_{0},q_{1}}w=\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2})&\text{in }Q,\\[3.00003pt]
w=0&\text{on }\Sigma,\\[3.00003pt]
w(0,\cdot)=0&\text{in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2}):=\mathcal{R}(v_{1})-\mathcal{R}(v_{2})
. Using (
3.106
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2})=

\displaystyle(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})\left[\mathcal{B}(v_{1})(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})+\left(\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right)v_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right)v_{1}(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})v_{1}+\mathcal{B}(v_{2})(v_{1}-v_{2})(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})v_{1}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{B}(v_{2})v_{2}(-\mathrm{i}\nabla+A_{0})(v_{1}-v_{2})+\left(|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})|^{2}-|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}\right)|v_{1}|^{2}v_{1}

\displaystyle+|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}|v_{1}|^{2}\left(v_{1}-v_{2}\right)+|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}\left(|v_{1}|^{2}-|v_{2}|^{2}\right)v_{2}

\displaystyle-\widetilde{q}(v_{1})(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})+\left(\widetilde{q}(v_{2})-\widetilde{q}(v_{1})\right)v_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, by utilizing the estimates (
3.90
) and (
3.92
), as well as the triangle inequality, we deduce that

(3.108)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left[\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\|(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\|v_{2}^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|v_{1}\nabla v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\big\||\mathcal{B}(v_{1})|^{2}-|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}\big\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\left\||v_{1}|^{2}v_{1}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\widetilde{q}(v_{2})-\widetilde{q}(v_{1})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|v_{2}^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|(v_{1}-v_{2})\nabla v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|v_{2}\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\|A_{0}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\left[\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\left(\|v_{2}^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v_{1}^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\left(\|(v_{1}-v_{2})v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\|v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\|\widetilde{q}(v_{1})\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{4}\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}^{2}\left(\|v_{1}\|^{2}_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left(\left\|v_{1}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\|v_{2}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\|v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\left\|v_{1}-v_{2}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

We next derive an upper bound for the quantities appearing on the right-hand side of (
3.108
). To estimate the products involving
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
, and their derivatives, it follows from the estimate (
3.92
), together with the fact that
v_{j}\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
,
j=1,2
, that

(3.109)

\displaystyle\|v_{1}\nabla v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},\quad\|v_{1}^{3}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}R^{3},\quad\|v_{1}^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},\quad

\displaystyle\|v_{2}^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},\quad\|v_{2}^{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R^{2},

\displaystyle\|(v_{1}-v_{2})(v_{1}+v_{2})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq 2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

\displaystyle\|(v_{1}-v_{2})\nabla v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

\displaystyle\|v_{2}\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

\displaystyle\|(v_{1}-v_{2})v_{1}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

\displaystyle\|v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}.

Turning our attention to
\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}
, let us recall that
\mathcal{B}(v_{1}):=A_{1}+v_{1}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})
. Therefore, it follows from the triangle inequality and the estimate (
3.90
) that

(3.110)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{1}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\|v_{1}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\|\mathcal{A}(v_{1})\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left(|Q|^{1/2}\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\|v_{1}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\|\mathcal{A}(v_{1})\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\right),

where
|Q|
stands for the volume of the space-time
Q
.
Similar arguments also yield that

(3.111)

\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left(|Q|^{1/2}\|A_{1}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\|v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|\mathcal{A}(v_{2})\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\right).

We now move to establish the estimate

(3.112)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{B,R}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

Indeed, by the definition of
\mathcal{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})

\displaystyle=v_{1}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-v_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})

\displaystyle=(v_{1}-v_{2})\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})+v_{2}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})\right)

Thus, using the triangle inequality, as well as the estimates (
3.90
) and (
3.92
), we obtain

(3.113)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})-\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left(v_{1}-v_{2}\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}+\left\|v_{2}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})\right)\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})\right\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\left\|v_{1}-v_{2}\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left\|v_{2}\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we get

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2})ds=\int_{0}^{1}D\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}\bigr)(v_{1}-v_{2})ds.

Here
D\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v)
denotes the derivative of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
at
v
.
Therefore, we utilize the estimate (
3.92
) to deduce that

(3.114)

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|D\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}\bigr)(v_{1}-v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|D\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}\bigr)\right\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q))}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}.

As
\|v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq R
, we obtain the estimate (
3.112
) by substituting (
3.114
) into (
3.113
) and set

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{B,R}:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q))}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}R\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q))}.

Let us next verify the estimate

(3.115)

\|\widetilde{q}(v_{1})-\widetilde{q}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{q}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

To this end, applying the estimate (
3.92
) and the fundamental theorem of calculus again, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{q}(v_{1})-\widetilde{q}(v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|D\widetilde{q}\bigl(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}\bigr)(v_{1}-v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|D\widetilde{q}\bigl(sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}\bigr)\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|\widetilde{q}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{C};W^{m,\infty}(Q))}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

From here, we obtain (
3.115
) immediately by setting

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{q}:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left\|\widetilde{q}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{C};W^{m,\infty}(Q))}.

Finally, we derive an upper bound for
\||\mathcal{B}(v_{1})|^{2}-|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}
. To this end, since

|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})|^{2}-|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}=\left(\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\right|-\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right|\right)\left(\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right|\right),

it follows from the estimates (
3.92
), (
3.110
)–(
3.112
), as well as the triangle inequality, that

(3.116)

\displaystyle\big\||\mathcal{B}(v_{1})|^{2}-|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})|^{2}\big\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{B,R}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left\|\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right|\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{B,R}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left(\left\|\mathcal{B}(v_{1})\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+\left\|\mathcal{B}(v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\right)\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{B,R}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Here the constant
\mathcal{C}_{B,R}>0
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{B,R}:=2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{B,R}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\left(\sqrt{|Q|}\left\|{A}_{1}\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n},W^{m,\infty}(Q)\right)}R\right).

Let us denote

\mathcal{M}_{R}:=|Q|^{1/2}\left\|{A}_{1}\right\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};W^{m+1,\infty}(Q)\right)}R.

By substituting the estimates (
3.109
)–(
3.112
), (
3.115
), and (
3.116
) into (
3.108
),
and incorporating the assumption
0<R\leq 1
,
we obtain

(3.117)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2})\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)},

where the constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}>0
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{R}=

\displaystyle 4\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{R}+2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\mathcal{C}^{B,R}_{\mathrm{Lip}}+\mathcal{C}^{q}_{\mathrm{Lip}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{B,R}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{3}

\displaystyle+2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\mathcal{C}^{B,R}_{\mathrm{Lip}}\|A_{0}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}+2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left\|\widetilde{q}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C};W^{m,\infty}(Q)\right)}

\displaystyle+4\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}^{2}\|A_{0}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(Q)}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{R}+3\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Binomial}}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{4}\mathcal{M}_{R}^{2}.

Thus, an application of Lemma
3.4
yields that
w\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
and satisfies the following estimate:

\displaystyle\left\|w\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}\leq C_{3}\left\|\mathcal{R}(v_{1},v_{2})\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Contraction}}R\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

where
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Contraction}}:=C_{3}\mathcal{C}_{R}
, and
C_{3}
is given by (
3.89
).

Let us now choose

R_{0}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{2C^{\star}},\frac{1}{2\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Contraction}}}\right\},

where
C^{\ast}
is given by (
3.103
),
and fix
R\in(0,R_{0}]
. Then it is straightforward to see that

(3.118)

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Contraction}}R\leq\frac{1}{2}<1.

Hence, the map
\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}
defined by (
3.105
) is a contraction on

\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
. By the Banach fixed point theorem,
\mathcal{L}^{[0,R]}
admits a unique fixed point

u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
. Furthermore, due to the fact that
\mathcal{R}(0)=0
, which follows immediately from (
3.106
), we conclude that
u
satisfies the following estimate for any

f\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)
:

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\|\mathcal{R}(u,0)\|_{H^{m}(Q)}+C_{3}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Contraction}}R\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}+C_{3}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

where last step follows from the estimate (
3.117
).
Using (
3.118
), we obtain

\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}\leq 2C_{3}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}.

Moreover, we observe that
\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}}
coincides with the quantities appearing on the left-hand side of the estimate (
3.96
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.8
.
∎

4.
Construction of Geometric Optics Solutions

In this section, we construct geometric optics solutions that will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Our approach follows the methods developed in
[
25
,
35
]
.

More precisely, we first construct solutions for the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}
that vanish at
t=0
. These solutions depend on a large parameter
\lambda>0
and contain a principle part and a remainder term.
When such solutions are substituted into the integral identities derived later in Section
5
, the principal part of the solution gives rise to an appropriate integral transform. The inversion of this transform will imply the desired uniqueness result.
On the other hand, the remainder term will vanish in a suitable norm as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Let us highlight the subtlety and the difficulty of this construction. To obtain the uniqueness of coefficients
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
, it is suffices to prove the
L^{2}
-decay of the remainder term, which is stated in Lemma
4.3
. However, to achieve the uniqueness of the higher order coefficients
A_{i}
and
q_{i+1}
,
i\geq 1
,
we need a stronger decay estimate. Therefore, we also prove the decay of the remainder term in
H^{m}(Q)
for sufficiently large
m
such that
H^{m}(Q)
is a Banach algebra in Corollary
4.4
.

Finally, in Lemma
4.5
and Corollary
4.6
, we construct the corresponding solutions for the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}^{\ast}
that vanish at
t=T
. The construction follows the same strategy as in the previous case, combined with a time-reversal argument.

Our first result in this section provides the existence of a smooth cutoff function that is constant on prescribed intervals, and whose derivatives satisfy suitable decay estimates.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<\frac{T}{4}
, there exists a function

\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

0\leq\chi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
.

(2)

\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)=0
on
[0,\varepsilon]\cup[T-\varepsilon,T]
.

(3)

\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)=1
on
[2\varepsilon,T-2\varepsilon]
.

(4)

For every integer
k\geq 0
, there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

(4.1)

\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\leq C_{k}\varepsilon^{-k},\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Define the function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(t):=\begin{cases}e^{-1/t},&t>0,\\
0,&t\leq 0.\end{cases}

By
[
43
, Lemma 2.20]
, we get

f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
We then define another function

h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(t):=\frac{f(t)}{f(t)+f(1-t)}
.
Then it follows immediately that
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

(4.2)

h(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq 0,\\
0<h(t)<1,&0<t<1,\\
1,&t\geq 1.\end{cases}

For any fixed
0<\varepsilon<\frac{T}{4}
, let us define the function
\chi_{\varepsilon}:(0,T)\to[0,1]

by

\chi_{\varepsilon}(t):=\begin{cases}0,&t\in[0,\varepsilon],\\
h\left(\frac{t-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right),&t\in(\varepsilon,2\varepsilon),\\
1,&t\in[2\varepsilon,T-2\varepsilon],\\
h\left(\frac{T-\varepsilon-t}{\varepsilon}\right),&t\in(T-2\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon),\\
0,&t\in[T-\varepsilon,T].\end{cases}

By (
4.2
), we have
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(0,T)
and
0\leq\chi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
. This proves (1).

Let us next verify (2) and (3). To this end, we observe that
\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)=0

on
[0,\varepsilon]\cup[T-\varepsilon,T]
. This yields that

\hbox{supp }(\chi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]
.
Thus, we conclude that
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
.

It remains to prove the estimate (
4.1
).
When
k=0
, we obtain the estimate immediately from the bound
0\leq\chi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
. In particular, we take
C_{0}=1
in this case.

When
k\geq 1
, we first observe that all derivatives of positive order vanish on the intervals where

\chi_{\varepsilon}
is constant. On

(\varepsilon,2\varepsilon)
, we compute that

\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)=\varepsilon^{-k}h^{(k)}\left(\frac{t-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right),

where
h^{(k)}
denotes the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
derivative of
h
. Similarly, on
(T-2\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon)
, we have

\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)=(-1)^{k}\varepsilon^{-k}h^{(k)}\left(\frac{T-\varepsilon-t}{\varepsilon}\right).

Since
h^{(k)}
is continuous on the compact interval
[0,1]
, it holds that

\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\leq\max_{s\in[0,1]}|h^{(k)}(s)|\varepsilon^{-k},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

which yields the estimate (
4.1
) with
C_{k}:=\max_{s\in[0,1]}|h^{(k)}(s)|
. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Our next result states the existence of amplitude terms in the geometric optics solutions, which satisfy certain transport equations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Q
be the same as in Proposition
3.1
, and let
A,q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
.
For any integer
N\geq 0
and any vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, there exist functions

m_{j}\in C^{\infty}({\overline{Q}})
,
0\leq j\leq N
, that satisfy
the following system of transport equations:

(4.3)

\begin{cases}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A\right)m_{0}=0,\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A\right)m_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{0},\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A\right)m_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{1},\\
\hskip 71.13188pt\vdots\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A\right)m_{N}=\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N-1}.\end{cases}

In particular, we have
m_{j}(0,x)=m_{j}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to construct explicit smooth solutions to the system (
4.3
). Let us write any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
uniquely as

(4.4)

x=x^{\prime}+s\alpha,\quad x^{\prime}\in\alpha^{\perp},s\in\mathbb{R}.

We first construct
m_{0}
. To this end, we define

(4.5)

\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s):=\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot\widetilde{A}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp.

where
\widetilde{A}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an extension of
A
. Let us note that such extension can be obtained by the Sobolev extension theorem, see for instance

[
16
, Section 5.4, Theorem 1]
.

Let
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
be a cutoff function as in Lemma
4.1
, and suppose
that
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\alpha^{\perp})
. We then define

(4.6)

m_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha):=\chi_{\varepsilon}(t)\eta(x^{\prime})e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}.

Then we have
m_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. To verify that
m_{0}
satisfies the first equation in (
4.3
), it follows from direct computations that

\displaystyle\partial_{s}m_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\widetilde{A}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)m_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha),

where we have used that
\widetilde{A}
is compactly supported.
On the other hand, we compute that

\partial_{s}m_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)=\alpha\cdot\nabla m_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha).

Furthermore, as
\widetilde{A}=A
in
\overline{Q}
, it follows that

\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A\right)m_{0}=0\quad\text{in }Q.

Moreover, since
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
, we have
m_{0}(0,x)=m_{0}(T,x)=0
in
\Omega
.

We now proceed to construct
m_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq N
, inductively.
Suppose that the amplitude term

m_{j-1}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
satisfying the
(j-1)
-th transport equation in (
4.3
) and
m_{j-1}(0,x)=m_{j-1}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
, has already been obtained.
Let us recall that the
j
-th transport equation reads

(4.7)

-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A)m_{j}=\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}.

We set

(4.8)

m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha):=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},\ell)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}(t,x^{\prime}+\ell\alpha)d\ell

and observe that
m_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. Next, we show that
m_{j}
satisfies (
4.7
).
To that end, let us rewrite (
4.8
) as

(4.9)

m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},\ell)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}(t,x^{\prime}+\ell\alpha)\,d\ell.

Differentiating both sides with respect to
s
, we obtain

(4.10)

\partial_{s}\left[m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha).

Using (
4.5
),
the left-hand side of equation (
4.10
) becomes

e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t,x^{\prime},s)}\left[\partial_{s}m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)+\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\widetilde{A}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)\right].

Hence, we get from the previous computations that

\partial_{s}m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)+\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\widetilde{A}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha).

Moreover, since

\partial_{s}m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)=\alpha\cdot\nabla m_{j}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha),

and
\widetilde{A}
is a compactly supported extension of
A
, we have

\alpha\cdot(\nabla+\mathrm{i}A)m_{j}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{j-1}\quad\text{ in }Q.

From here, we immediately obtain the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
transport equation (
4.7
).
This shows that
m_{j}
satisfies the
j
-th transport equation.

It remains to verify
m_{j}(0,x)=m_{j}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
. Since

\chi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
, the function
m_{0}
vanishes in a neighborhood
of
t=0
and
t=T
. Thus, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{0}=0
at

t=0
and
t=T
. Therefore, using (
4.9
) with
j=1
gives us
m_{1}(0,x)=m_{1}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
.
Repeating this argument inductively, we obtain
m_{j}(0,x)=m_{j}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
,
0\leq j\leq N
.
This completes proof of Lemma
4.2
.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the existence of geometric optics solutions to the linear operator

(4.11)

\mathcal{L}_{A,q}:=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}+\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A(t,x)\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A(t,x)-|A(t,x)|^{2}+q(t,x).

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Omega,Q
, and
\Sigma
be as in Proposition
3.1
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}
be the linear dynamical Schrödinger operator given by (
4.11
), where
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
and
q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. Then for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}>1
, there exists a solution
v_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
of the initial value problem

(4.12)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}v=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
v(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

having the form

(4.13)

v_{\lambda}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\bigg(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\bigg)+R_{\lambda}(t,x),

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
N\geq 1
, and the amplitudes
m_{j}
satisfy the transport equation (
4.3
) for
0\leq j\leq N
. Moreover, the remainder term
R_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
satisfies
R_{\lambda}(0,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
, as well as the estimate

(4.14)

\|R_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1,2}(Q)}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda^{N}}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)},

where
C_{1}>0
is the constant appearing in the estimate (
3.2
).

Proof.

The existence of smooth amplitude terms
m_{j}
,
0\leq j\leq N
, were established in Lemma
4.2
. Hence, it suffices to construct a remainder term
R_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
satisfying the initial boundary value problem

(4.15)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}R_{\lambda}=-\mathcal{L}_{A,q}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\left(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\right)\right]&\text{ in }Q,\\
R_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
R_{\lambda}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\end{cases}

and the estimate (
4.14
).

Let us first simplify the right-hand side of the first equation above. To this end, since the amplitudes
m_{j}
,
0\leq j\leq N
, satisfies the respective transport equations in (
4.3
), we get from direct computations that

\mathcal{L}_{A,q}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\left(m_{0}+\frac{m_{1}}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}}{\lambda^{N}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}.

Hence, the problem (
4.15
) reads

(4.16)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}R_{\lambda}=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}&\text{ in }Q,\\
R_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
R_{\lambda}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma.\end{cases}

Since
-\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\in L^{2}(Q)
,
an application of Proposition
3.1
yields that the problem (
4.15
) admits a unique solution
R_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
.
Finally, we apply the estimate (
3.2
) to the problem above to obtain the estimate (
4.14
).
This completes the proof of Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Our next result gives us the existence of geometric solutions whose remainders decay in the Sobolev space
H^{m}(Q)
, where
m
is sufficiently large. Such decay rate is necessary to recover higher order nonlinearities.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
m>n+1
. Let the sets
\Omega
,
Q
, and
\Sigma
be as in Proposition
3.1
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}
be the linear dynamical Schrödinger operator given by (
4.11
), where
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
and
q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. Then for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}>1
, the initial boundary value problem (
4.12
) admits a solution
v_{\lambda}\in H^{m+1}(Q)
of the form

v_{\lambda}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\bigg(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\bigg)+R_{\lambda}(t,x)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
N\geq 2m+1
, and the amplitudes
m_{j}
satisfy the transport equation (
4.3
) for
0\leq j\leq N
. Moreover, the remainder term
R_{\lambda}\in H^{m+1}(Q)
satisfies
R_{\lambda}(0,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
, and it satisfies the estimate

(4.17)

\|R_{\lambda}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}(m,\alpha,Q)}{\lambda}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Here the constant
\mathcal{C}(m,\alpha,Q)>0
is given by

\mathcal{C}(m,\alpha,Q):=\widetilde{C}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}|Q|^{1/2}\sum_{k+|\gamma|\leq m}|\alpha|^{2k}|\alpha^{\gamma}|,

where
\widetilde{C}>0
is the same as in Lemma
3.3
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}>0
is the same as in Lemma
3.6
.

Proof.

By similar arguments as in the proof of Proposition
4.3
, we only need to seek the remainder
R_{\lambda}
that satisfies the initial boundary value problem (
4.16
). By the Sobolev embedding theorem, we have
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{k}(Q)=C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
.
As
-\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
, it follows immediately that
-\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\in H^{m}(Q)
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Hence, by Lemma
3.3
, we get
R_{\lambda}\in H^{m+1}(Q)
and

\displaystyle\|R_{\lambda}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}}{\lambda^{N}}\left\|e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Moreover, since
m>n+1
, in view of Lemma
3.6
, we have

\displaystyle\|R_{\lambda}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}\widetilde{C}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}\left\|e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}|Q|^{1/2}\sum_{k+|\gamma|\leq m}|\alpha|^{2k}|\alpha^{\gamma}|}{\lambda^{N-2m}}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{C}(m,\alpha,Q)}{\lambda^{N-2m}}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

Finally, the claimed estimate (
4.17
) follows immediately from above, as well as the fact that
\lambda>1
and
N-2m\geq 1
.
This completes the proof of Corollary
4.4
.
∎

We now turn to the geometric solutions for the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}
that vanish at
t=T
. Let us note that
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}
is given by

(4.18)

\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}v+\Delta v+2\mathrm{i}\overline{A}(t,x)\cdot\nabla v+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot\overline{A}(t,x)v-\left|\overline{A}(t,x)\right|^{2}v+\overline{q}(t,x)v.

In the next two results, we give the existence of geometric solution for
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}
vanishing at
t=T
, whose remainder terms also vanish in a suitable sense as
\lambda\to\infty
. The proofs are very similar to those of Proposition
4.3
and Corollary
4.4
, and incorporate a time-reversal argument, which was also utilized in
[
11
]
. Thus, we shall omit the details.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Omega,Q
, and
\Sigma
be as in Proposition
3.1
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}
be the linear dynamical Schrödinger adjoint operator given by (
4.18
), where
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
and
q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. Then for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}>1
, the problem

(4.19)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}v=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
v(T,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a solution
u_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
of the form

u_{\lambda}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\left(\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\right)+\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(t,x),

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
N\geq 1
. Here the smooth amplitudes
\widetilde{m}_{j}
satisfy
\widetilde{m}_{j}(0,x)=\widetilde{m}_{j}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
,
0\leq j\leq N
, as well as the following system of transport equations:

(4.20)

\begin{cases}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}\overline{A}\right)\widetilde{m}_{0}=0,\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}\overline{A}\right)\widetilde{m}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\overline{A},\overline{q}}\widetilde{m}_{0},\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}\overline{A}\right)\widetilde{m}_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{\overline{A},\overline{q}}\widetilde{m}_{1},\\
\hskip 71.13188pt\vdots\\
-2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot\left(\nabla+\mathrm{i}\overline{A}\right)\widetilde{m}_{N}=\mathcal{L}_{\overline{A},\overline{q}}\widetilde{m}_{N-1}.\\
\end{cases}

Moreover, the remainder term
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
satisfies
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
and the estimate

(4.21)

\|\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1,2}(Q)}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda^{N}}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
m>n+1
. Let
\Omega,Q
, and
\Sigma
be as in Proposition
3.1
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}
be the linear dynamical Schrödinger adjoint operator given by (
4.18
), where
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
and
q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
. Then for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}>1
, the problem (
4.19
) has a solution
u_{\lambda}\in H^{m+1}(Q)
of the form

u_{\lambda}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}t)}\left(\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}\right)+\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(t,x)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
N\geq 2m+1
. The smooth amplitudes
\widetilde{m}_{j}
satisfy the transport equation (
4.20
) for
0\leq j\leq N
. Furthermore, the remainder term
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\in H^{m+1}(Q)
satisfies
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(T,x)=0
for
x\in\Omega
, as well as the estimate

(4.22)

\|\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}(m,\alpha,Q)}{\lambda}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\right\|_{H^{m}(Q)}.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we establish the uniqueness of nonlinear potentials
\mathcal{A}
and
q
given by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), respectively, from the full Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (
1.5
). The proof is split into several steps. First, in Subsection
5.1
, we utilize the first order linearization, as well as the geometric optics solutions from Proposition
4.3
and Lemma
4.5
, to recover the coefficients
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
. Due to the gauge invariance, we need the assumption
\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(1)}=\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(2)}
in
Q
to achieve uniqueness of
A_{0}
. Subsequently, we apply the second order linearization, in conjunction with the geometric solutions given in Corollaries
4.4
and
4.6
to obtain the uniqueness of
A_{1}
and
q_{2}
in Subsection
5.2
. Finally, we uniquely recover higher order nonlinearities in Subsection
5.3
via higher order linearization.

5.1.
Recovery of
A_{0}
and
q_{1}

Let us start by establishing the uniqueness of the vector potential
A_{0}
and the scalar potential
q_{1}
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. We shall use the linearization method, which has been extensively studied in the literature, see for instance
[
11
,
39
]
and the references therein.

Let
\left(\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}\right)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})\times C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
,
j=1,2
, be nonlinear potentials given by the expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), and let
f\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)
be such that
\partial^{k}_{t}f(0,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
for all
0\leq k\leq m
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be a constant, and let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small such that
\left\|\varepsilon f\right\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, and that the function
u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)
solves the following nonlinear initial boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}}u_{j}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
u_{j}=\varepsilon f&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}}
is given by (
1.1
) with potentials
\mathcal{A}^{(j)}
and
q^{(j)}
. Then it follows from Theorem
3.8
that
u_{j}(\cdot,\cdot;\varepsilon)\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
and satisfies the estimate

\|\Delta u_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}+\|u_{j}\|_{H^{m+1,m+1}(Q)}\leq 2C_{3}\|\varepsilon f\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}.

Let us note that
u_{j}(t,x;0)=0
.

Next, we apply the first order linearization to the problem above. Using that
u_{j}(t,x;0)=0
, we see that all terms containing positive powers of
u_{j}
or
\nabla u_{j}
vanish when
\varepsilon=0
. Therefore, the function
v_{j}:=\partial_{\varepsilon}u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)|_{\varepsilon=0}
satisfies the initial boundary value problem

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}v_{j}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
v_{j}=f&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
v_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}
is a linear operator given by (
4.11
) with potentials
A_{0}^{(j)}
and
q_{1}^{(j)}
.

Let us define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the operator
\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}
by the formula

(5.2)

\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}(f)=(\partial_{\nu}+iA_{0}^{(j)})v_{j}.

In view of the assumption
\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)},q^{(1)}}(\varepsilon f)=\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}^{(2)},q^{(2)}}(\varepsilon f)
, applying
\partial_{\varepsilon}|_{\varepsilon=0}
on both sides gives us
\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}=\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}^{(2)},q_{1}^{(2)}}
.

In what follows, we shall denote
A_{0}:=A_{0}^{(2)}-A_{0}^{(1)}
, and
q_{1}:=q_{1}^{(2)}-q_{1}^{(1)}
. Then it follows from direct computations that the function
v:=v_{1}-v_{2}
satisfies the following initial boundary value problem:

(5.3)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}v=\left(2\mathrm{i}A_{0}\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A_{0}-|A_{0}^{(2)}|^{2}+|A_{0}^{(1)}|^{2}+q_{1}\right)v_{2}&\text{ in }Q,\\
v=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
v(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Let
v_{1}
be a solution to the problem

(5.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\overline{A_{0}^{(1)}},\overline{q_{1}^{(1)}}}v_{1}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
v_{1}(T,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Multiplying the first equation of (
5.3
) by
\overline{v}_{1}
and integrating over
Q
, we get

(5.5)

\int_{Q}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}v\overline{v}_{1}dxdt=\int_{Q}\left(2\mathrm{i}A_{0}\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A_{0}-|A_{0}^{(2)}|^{2}+|A_{0}^{(1)}|^{2}+q_{1}\right)v_{2}\overline{v}_{1}dxdt.

In view of the homogeneous initial and boundary conditions of (
5.3
), the problem (
5.4
), and that
\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}=\mathcal{N}_{A_{0}^{(2)},q_{1}^{(2)}}
,
we integrate by parts on the left-hand side of (
5.5
)
to get

(5.6)

\int_{Q}\left(2\mathrm{i}A_{0}\cdot\nabla v_{2}+\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{0})v_{2}-\big(|A_{0}^{(2)}|^{2}-|A_{0}^{(1)}|^{2}\big)v_{2}+q_{1}v_{2}\right)\overline{v}_{1}dxdt=0.

From Proposition
4.3
and Lemma
4.5
, we get geometric optics solutions

(5.7)

v_{2}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\left(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}+R_{\lambda}(t,x)\right),

and

(5.8)

v_{1}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t)}\left(\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)}{\lambda}+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\widetilde{m}_{N}(t,x)}{\lambda^{N}}+\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(t,x)\right).

Here the smooth amplitudes
m_{j}
and
\widetilde{m}_{j}
,
0\leq j\leq N
, satisfy the transport equations in (
4.3
) and (
4.20
), respectively, and the remainders
R_{\lambda},\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\in H^{1,2}(Q)
satisfy the estimates (
4.14
) and (
4.21
), respectively.

To simplify notations, in what follows, let us denote the phase function
\Phi_{\lambda}(t,x):=\lambda x\cdot\alpha-\lambda^{2}|\alpha|^{2}t
,
and the sum of smooth amplitudes as

M_{\lambda}(t,x):=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}(t,x):=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{m}_{k}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}.

Then the geometric optics solutions (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) can be written as

v_{2}=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}M_{\lambda}+R_{\lambda}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad v_{1}=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}+\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}.

Furthermore, we set

W_{\mathrm{rem}}(t,x):=\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A_{0}(t,x)-|A_{0}^{(2)}(t,x)|^{2}+|A_{0}^{(1)}(t,x)|^{2}+q_{1}(t,x).

Then the integral identity (
5.6
) becomes

(5.9)

\int_{Q}\left(2\mathrm{i}A_{0}\cdot\nabla v_{2}+W_{\mathrm{rem}}v_{2}\right)\overline{v}_{1}dxdt=0.

On the other hand, applying the product rule, we have

(5.10)

\nabla v_{2}=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}(t,x)}\left[\mathrm{i}\lambda\alpha M_{\lambda}(t,x)+\nabla M_{\lambda}(t,x)\right]+\nabla R_{\lambda}(t,x).

Therefore, by substituting the previous expressions for
v_{1},

v_{2}
, and
\nabla v_{2}
into (
5.9
), we obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-2\lambda\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt-2\lambda\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\alpha M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}\nabla R_{\lambda}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt+\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt+\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt.

We now isolate the leading term in
M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}
. To this end, since
M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}=m_{0}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}G_{\lambda},
where

G_{\lambda}:=m_{1}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}}+m_{0}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j,k\leq N,j+k\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{m_{j}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{k}}}{\lambda^{j+k-1}},

we get

-2\lambda\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt=-2\lambda\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha m_{0}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}}dxdt-2\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha G_{\lambda}dxdt.

Therefore, (
5.11
) can be written as

(5.12)

-2\lambda\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha m_{0}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}}dxdt+\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=0,

where

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:=

\displaystyle-2\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha G_{\lambda}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt-2\lambda\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\alpha M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\nabla R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt+2\mathrm{i}\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt+\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt+\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt.

We next show that there exists a constant
\lambda_{0}
such that

(5.14)

|\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}|\leq C

for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, where
C>0
is independent of
\lambda
. This requires us to estimate each term in
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
. To that end, we deduce from the inequality (
3.6
) and the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality that

\displaystyle 2\left|\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha G_{\lambda}dxdt\right|\leq

\displaystyle 2|\alpha|\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}|Q|^{1/2}\left[\|m_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\widetilde{m}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|m_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\widetilde{m}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j,k\leq N,j+k\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{\|m_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\widetilde{m}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{j+k-1}}\right].

Since
\lambda\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C>0
, which is independent of
\lambda
, such that

(5.15)

2\left|\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\alpha G_{\lambda}dxdt\right|\leq\frac{C}{10}.

Similarly, we obtain the following two estimates:

(5.16)

\displaystyle 2\left|\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\nabla m_{k}\overline{\widetilde{m}_{\ell}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k+\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq 2|Q|^{1/2}\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\nabla m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\widetilde{m}_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k+\ell}}\leq\frac{C}{10},

and

(5.17)

\left|\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|\leq|Q|^{1/2}\|W_{\mathrm{rem}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\widetilde{m}_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{\ell}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{10}.

We next estimate the term containing the remainder
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}
. To achieve this, we utilize the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the facts that
|e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}|=1
and
\lambda\geq 1
, along with the estimates (
3.6
) and (
4.21
), to get

(5.18)

\displaystyle 2\lambda\left|\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\alpha M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda|\alpha|\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}\right)\|\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}|\alpha|\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}\right)\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{1-N}\leq\frac{C}{10}.

The same arguments also yields that

(5.19)

\displaystyle 2\left|\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\nabla M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|\nabla m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}\right)\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-N}\leq\frac{C}{10}.

Turning our attention to the terms involving
\nabla R_{\lambda}
, it follows again from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the facts that
|e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}|=1
and
\lambda\geq 1
, as well as the estimates (
3.6
) and (
4.14
), that

(5.20)

\displaystyle 2\left|\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}A_{0}\cdot\nabla R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\widetilde{m}_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{\ell}}\right)\|\nabla R_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}\|A_{0}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\widetilde{m}_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{\ell}}\right)\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-N}\leq\frac{C}{10}.

The same reasoning also gives us

(5.21)

2\left|\int_{Q}A_{0}\cdot\nabla R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|\leq 2C_{1}^{2}\|A_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-2N}\leq\frac{C}{10}.

Finally, for the remaining three terms containing
W_{\mathrm{rem}}
, we utilize analogous computations as above to deduce that

(5.22)

\left|\int_{Q}e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}M_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|\leq C_{1}\|W_{\mathrm{rem}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|m_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}\right)\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}^{\ast}\widetilde{m}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-N}\leq\frac{C}{10},

(5.23)

\left|\int_{Q}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{\lambda}}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|\leq C_{1}\|W_{\mathrm{rem}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\frac{\|\widetilde{m}_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}}{\lambda^{\ell}}\right)\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-N}\leq\frac{C}{10},

and

(5.24)

\left|\int_{Q}W_{\mathrm{rem}}R_{\lambda}\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}dxdt\right|\leq C_{1}^{2}\|W_{\mathrm{rem}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\|\mathcal{L}_{A,q}m_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\|\mathcal{L}_{\overline{A},\overline{q}}\widetilde{m}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\lambda^{-2N}\leq\frac{C}{10}.

In the previous estimates, the constant
C_{1}>0
is the same as in Proposition
3.1
. Since
N\geq 1
and
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}>1
, the claimed estimate (
5.14
) follow immediately from (
5.15
)–(
5.24
).

We now multiply the identity (
5.12
) by
\frac{2i}{\lambda}
on both sides. Due to (
5.14
), it follows immediately that
\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}{\lambda}\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Therefore, we obtain

\int_{Q}2\mathrm{i}A_{0}(t,x)\cdot\alpha m_{0}(t,x)\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)}dxdt=0.

Using the expressions for
m_{0}
and
\widetilde{m}_{0}
from (
4.6
), we get

(5.25)

\int_{Q}2\mathrm{i}A_{0}(t,x)\cdot\alpha\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t)\eta^{2}(x^{\prime})\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)dxdt=0.

In contrast to the extension of
\widetilde{A}
used in the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we extend
A_{0}
by zero outside
Q
without enlarging the spatial domain. This is possible because the assumption
A_{0}^{(1)}=A_{0}^{(2)}
on the lateral boundary
\Sigma
implies that
A_{0}=0
on
\Sigma
.
Furthermore, in view of the decomposition (
4.4
), we use Fubini’s theorem to rewrite (
5.25
) as

(5.26)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha^{\perp}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}2\mathrm{i}A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)\cdot\alpha\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t)\eta^{2}(x^{\prime})\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)dsdx^{\prime}dt=0.

Let us note that

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+s\alpha)\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)=-2\frac{d}{ds}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right).

Therefore, we get from (
5.26
) that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha^{\perp}}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t)\eta^{2}(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{ds}\left[\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)\right]dsdx^{\prime}dt=0.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha^{\perp}}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t)\eta^{2}(x^{\prime})\left[1-\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)\right]dx^{\prime}dt=0.

Since
\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}
and
\eta^{2}
can be arbitrarily chosen with supports contained in
(0,T)
and
\alpha^{\perp}
, respectively, and
A_{0}
is smooth, the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations implies that

(5.27)

\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp\right)=1.

for every
(t,x^{\prime})\in(0,T)\times\alpha^{\perp}
.

We next show that

(5.28)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp=0

by following the technique from
[
46
, Section 4]
. To this end, for fixed
t\in(0,T)
, we set

F_{t}(x^{\prime}):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp,\quad x^{\prime}\in\alpha^{\perp}.

Since
A_{0}
is smooth, it holds that
F_{t}
is smooth on
\alpha^{\perp}
. From
(
5.27
), we have
F_{t}(x^{\prime})\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
for
x^{\prime}\in\alpha^{\perp}
. Since
\alpha^{\perp}
is connected, and
F_{t}
is continuous, it holds that
F_{t}(\alpha^{\perp})
is also connected. On the other hand, since

F_{t}(\alpha^{\perp})\subset 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and the only connected subsets of the discrete set
2\pi\mathbb{Z}
are singletons,
F_{t}
must be constant on
\alpha^{\perp}
.
Moreover, since
A_{0}(t,\cdot)
is compactly supported, we have

F_{t}(x^{\prime})=0
for
|x^{\prime}|
sufficiently large. From here, we get (
5.28
) immediately.

By
[
53
, Lemma 6.1]
, for each
t\in(0,T)
, there exists
a function
\Psi(t,\cdot)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(5.29)

A_{0}^{(2)}(t,x)=A_{0}^{(1)}(t,x)+\nabla\Psi(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in Q.

In view of the assumption that
\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(1)}=\nabla\cdot A_{0}^{(2)}
for each
t\in(0,T)
, we get

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x}\Psi(t,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\Psi(t,\cdot)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By
[
19
, Theorem 8.12]
, we have
\Psi(t,\cdot)|_{\Omega}=0
for each
t\in(0,T)
. Hence, we conclude from (
5.29
) that
A_{0}^{(2)}=A_{0}^{(1)}
in
Q
.

We now proceed to show that
q_{1}=0
in
Q
. By substituting
A_{0}^{(2)}=A_{0}^{(1)}
into the integral identity (
5.6
), we obtain

\int_{Q}q_{1}v_{2}\overline{v}_{1}dxdt=0.

By substituting the geometric optics solutions
v_{1}(t,x)
and
v_{2}(t,x)
from (
5.7
) and (
5.8
), we obtain

\int_{Q}q_{1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\overline{\widetilde{m}_{k}}}{\lambda^{k}}+\overline{\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}}\right)dxdt=0.

Arguing similarly as in the proof for the magnetic potential
A_{0}
, we have

\int_{Q}q_{1}(t,x)m_{0}(t,x)\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)}dxdt=0.

We next choose

m_{0}=\chi(t)e^{-2\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot A_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp}\quad\text{ and }\widetilde{m}_{0}=\chi(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot\overline{A}_{0}(t,x^{\prime}+p\alpha)dp}

for any
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
and
\xi\in\alpha^{\perp}
, which are valid according to (
4.6
). Therefore, we obtain

m_{0}(t,x)\overline{\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)}=\chi^{2}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}.

Hence, it follows that

\int_{Q}q_{1}(t,x)\chi^{2}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}dxdt=0

for every
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
and every
\xi\in\alpha^{\perp}
. Thus, an application of Fubini’s theorem yields that

\int_{0}^{T}\chi^{2}(t)\left(\int_{\Omega}q_{1}(t,x)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}dx\right)dt=0.

By the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variation, it holds that

\int_{\Omega}q_{1}(t,x)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}dx=0

for every
t\in(0,T)
and every
\xi\in\alpha^{\perp}
.
Since the direction
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is arbitrary for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can choose
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\xi\in\alpha^{\perp}
. Therefore, we have

\int_{\Omega}q_{1}(t,x)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}dx=0,\quad\text{ for all }(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For each fixed
t\in(0,T)
, we extend
q_{1}(t,\cdot)
by zero outside
\Omega
and denote this extension by the same letter. Then we have
\widehat{q_{1}}(t,\xi)=0
for all
(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the Fourier transform is taken with respect to the spatial variable.
By the injectivity of the Fourier transform, we conclude that
q_{1}=0
in
Q
.

5.2.
Recovery of
A_{1}
and
q_{2}

The goal of subsection is to establish the unique determination of the vector potential
A_{1}
and the scalar potential
q_{2}
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map defined in (
1.5
). The proof follows the same general strategy as in Subsection
5.1
. Nevertheless, we need a stronger decay estimate for the remainder term
R_{\lambda}
, which is given in Corollary
4.4
.

Let us begin with the second order linearization. To this end, let
\left(\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}\right)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})\times C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
, and let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Let
u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)
,
j=1,2
, be solutions to the initial boundary value problem

(5.30)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}}u_{j}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
u_{j}=\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\left\|\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}\right\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, and
f_{p}\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)
,
p=1,2
, satisfies the condition
\partial^{k}_{t}f_{p}(0,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
for all
0\leq k\leq m
.
From Theorem
3.8
, we have
u_{j}(\cdot,\cdot;\varepsilon)\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
.

By applying
\partial_{\varepsilon_{p}}|_{\varepsilon=0}
to (
5.30
) and using that
u_{j}(t,x;0)=0
,
j=1,2
, we see that the function
v_{j,p}:=\partial_{\varepsilon_{p}}u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)|_{\varepsilon=0}
satisfies the following initial boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}v_{j,p}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
v_{j,p}=f_{p}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
v_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

where the linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(j)},q_{1}^{(j)}}
is given by (
4.11
) with potentials
A_{0}^{(j)}
and
q_{1}^{(j)}
. We have already established in Subsection
5.1
that
A_{0}:=A_{0}^{(1)}=A_{0}^{(2)}
and
q_{1}:=q_{1}^{(1)}=q_{1}^{(2)}
.
Thus, by the uniqueness for the linear problem, we obtain
v_{p}:=v_{1,p}=v_{2,p}
,
p=1,2
.

We next apply the operator
\partial^{2}_{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}|_{\varepsilon=0}
to the problem (
5.30
) to get that the function
w_{j}:=\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}u_{j}|_{\varepsilon=0}
satisfies the initial boundary value problem

(5.31)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}w_{j}+2\mathrm{i}A_{1}^{(j)}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2})+2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1}^{(j)})v_{1}v_{2}-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1}^{(j)})v_{1}v_{2}+2q_{2}^{(j)}v_{1}v_{2}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
w_{j}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
w_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Let us denote
w:=w_{1}-w_{2}
,
A_{1}:=A_{1}^{(1)}-A_{1}^{(2)}
, and
q_{2}:=q_{2}^{(1)}-q_{2}^{(2)}
. Then we immediately obtain from
(
5.31
) that

(5.32)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}w+2\mathrm{i}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2})+2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1})v_{1}v_{2}-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1})v_{1}v_{2}+2q_{2}v_{1}v_{2}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
w=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
w(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Let
w_{0}
be a solution to the problem

(5.33)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}^{*}w_{0}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
w_{0}(T,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Multiplying the first equation in
(
5.32
) by
\overline{w_{0}}
and integrating over
Q
, and arguing similarly as in Subsection
5.1
, we have the integral identity

(5.34)

\int_{Q}\left[2\mathrm{i}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2})+2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1})v_{1}v_{2}-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1})v_{1}v_{2}+2q_{2}v_{1}v_{2}\right]\overline{w_{0}}dxdt=0.

We now choose the vectors
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(5.35)

\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=|\alpha_{3}|^{2}.

By Corollaries
4.4
and
4.6
, we have geometric optics solutions given by

(5.36)

v_{1}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{1}-\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{1}|^{2}t)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{1}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{1}(t,x)\right),

(5.37)

v_{2}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{2}-\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{2}|^{2}t)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{2}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{2}(t,x)\right),

and

(5.38)

w_{0}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{3}+\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{3}|^{2}t)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{3}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{3}(t,x)\right).

Here the smooth amplitude
m_{k}^{j}
,
j=1,2
, solves the transport equation (
4.3
) with
\alpha=\alpha_{j}
and
A=A_{0}
, while
m_{k}^{3}
is a solution to the same equation with
\alpha=\alpha_{3}
and
A=\overline{A_{0}}
. Moreover, the remainder terms
R_{\lambda}^{1}
and
R_{\lambda}^{2}
satisfy the estimate (
4.17
), whereas
R_{\lambda}^{3}
satisfies the estimate (
4.22
).
For
j=1,2,3
, let us write
\Phi_{j}(t,x):=\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{j}-\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}t
, and denote

(5.39)

a_{j}(t,x):=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{j}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{j}(t,x).

Using these notations, the solutions (
5.36
)–(
5.38
) read
v_{j}=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{j}}a_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
.

Let us observe from (
5.35
) that

(5.40)

\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{3}=0.

Also, we utilize (
5.40
) to compute that

(5.41)

\nabla(v_{1}v_{2})=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{3}}\left[\mathrm{i}\lambda\alpha_{3}a_{1}a_{2}+\nabla(a_{1}a_{2})\right].

Thus, by substitute the geometric solutions into the integral identity (
5.34
) and dividing the resulted expression by
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-2\int_{Q}(A_{1}\cdot\alpha_{3})a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}dxdt+\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\lambda}\int_{Q}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(a_{1}a_{2})\overline{a_{3}}dxdt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{Q}\left[2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1})-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1})+2q_{2}\right]a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}dxdt

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-2I_{\lambda}+\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda},

where

(5.42)

I_{\lambda}:=\int_{Q}(A_{1}\cdot\alpha_{3})a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}dxdt

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\lambda}\int_{Q}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(a_{1}a_{2})\overline{a_{3}}dxdt+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{Q}\left[2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1})-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1})+2q_{2}\right]a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}dxdt.

We next proceed to show that
\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
.
First, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the boundedness of
A_{1}
, and Lemma
3.6
, we deduce from the geometric solutions (
5.36
)–(
5.38
) that

\left|\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\lambda}\int_{Q}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(a_{1}a_{2})\overline{a_{3}}dxdt\right|\leq\frac{2\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\mathrm{Banach}}}{\lambda}\|A_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\|m_{k}^{j}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}}{\lambda^{k}}+\|R_{\lambda}^{j}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\right),

where the constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}>0
is the same as in Lemma
3.6
.
Using the estimates (
4.17
) and (
4.22
), we have

\|R_{\lambda}^{j}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{j}(m,\alpha,Q)}{\lambda},\quad j=1,2,3.

Therefore, we get for
\lambda\geq 1
that

\left|\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\lambda}\int_{Q}A_{1}\cdot\nabla(a_{1}a_{2})\overline{a_{3}}dxdt\right|\to 0,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

By analogous arguments, we estimate the second term in
\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{Q}\left[2\mathrm{i}(\nabla\cdot A_{1})-4(A_{0}\cdot A_{1})+2q_{2}\right]a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|Q|^{1/2}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Banach}}^{2}}{\lambda}\left(2\|\nabla\cdot A_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+4\|A_{0}\cdot A_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+2\|q_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{3}\|a_{j}\|_{H^{m+1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\to 0,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

Therefore, we conclude that
\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
by combining the previous two estimates.

We now turn to analyze the integral
I_{\lambda}
as
\lambda\to\infty
. To this end, by Corollaries
4.4
and
4.6
, we get from (
5.39
) that
a_{j}\to m_{0}^{j}
in
H^{m+1}(Q)
as
\lambda\to\infty
,
j=1,2,3
.
Furthermore, since
H^{m+1}(Q)
is a Banach algebra, it follows that
a_{1}a_{2}\overline{a_{3}}\to m_{0}^{1}m_{0}^{2}\overline{m_{0}^{3}}
in
H^{m+1}(Q)
as
\lambda\to\infty
.
Therefore, we obtain from (
5.42
) that

(5.43)

I_{\lambda}\longrightarrow\int_{Q}(A_{1}(t,x)\cdot\alpha_{3})m_{0}^{1}(t,x)m_{0}^{2}(t,x)\overline{m_{0}^{3}(t,x)}dxdt,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

Hence, we conclude from the previous analysis that

(5.44)

0=-2\int_{Q}(A_{1}(t,x)\cdot\alpha_{3})m_{0}^{1}(t,x)m_{0}^{2}(t,x)\overline{m_{0}^{3}(t,x)}dxdt.

We now choose the smooth amplitudes

m_{0}^{1}
,
m_{0}^{2}
, and
m_{0}^{3}
such that

m_{0}^{1}(t,x)m_{0}^{2}(t,x)\overline{m_{0}^{3}(t,x)}=\chi^{3}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi},

where
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
and
\xi\in\alpha^{\perp}
. Then the identity (
5.44
) gives us

\int_{Q}(A_{1}(t,x)\cdot\alpha_{3})\chi^{3}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}dxdt=0.

Proceeding as in the proof of uniqueness of the scalar potential
q_{1}
, we obtain

(5.45)

\widehat{A_{1}}(t,\xi)\cdot\alpha_{3}=0,\quad\text{ for all }(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Let us show that, when
n\geq 2
, the vector
\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
can be chosen arbitrarily while satisfying the conditions in (
5.35
). Since

n\geq 2
, the orthogonal complement
\alpha_{3}^{\perp}
is nontrivial. Hence, we
can choose
\beta\in\alpha_{3}^{\perp}
such that
|\beta|=\frac{|\alpha_{3}|}{2}
. Define
\alpha_{1}:=\frac{\alpha_{3}}{2}+\beta
and
\alpha_{2}:=\frac{\alpha_{3}}{2}-\beta
. Then clearly
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}
.
Moreover, since
\beta\in\alpha_{3}^{\perp}
, we have

\alpha_{3}\cdot\beta=0
, which yields that
\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}=0
.
Consequently, we have
|\alpha_{3}|^{2}=|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
.
Thus, for every nonzero
\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we can choose

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying (
5.35
) as above.

Since
\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
can be chosen arbitrarily, it follows from (
5.45
) that
\widehat{A_{1}}(t,\xi)=0
for all
(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By the injectivity of the Fourier transform, we conclude that
A_{1}=0
in
Q
. Finally, by substituting
A_{1}=0
in the integral identity (
5.34
) and using similar calculations as in the proof for the uniqueness of
q_{1}
, we obtain
q_{2}=0
in
Q
.

5.3.
Recovery of
A_{i}
and
q_{i+1}
,
i\geq 2

In this subsection, we prove the uniqueness result for the higher order vector and scalar potentials appearing in the power series expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)
by applying higher order linearization and using an induction argument.
Assume that we have already proved for some integer
r\geq 2
that

(5.46)

A_{\ell}^{(1)}=A_{\ell}^{(2)}\quad\text{for }0\leq\ell\leq r-2,\qquad q_{\ell}^{(1)}=q_{\ell}^{(2)}\quad\text{for }1\leq\ell\leq r-1\quad\text{ in }Q.

Our goal is to show that
A_{r-1}^{(1)}=A_{r-1}^{(2)}
and
q_{r}^{(1)}=q_{r}^{(2)}
in
Q
.

Let
\left(\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}\right)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})\times C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
be the nonlinear potentials given by the expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Let
f_{p}\in H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)
,
p=1,\dots,r
, be such that
\partial^{k}_{t}f_{p}(0,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
for all
0\leq k\leq m
, and let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
For
|\varepsilon|>0
sufficiently small such that
\left\|\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{r}f_{r}\right\|_{H^{\frac{m+1}{m+2}(m+\frac{3}{2}),m+\frac{3}{2}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, suppose that the function
u_{j}(\cdot,\cdot;\varepsilon)\in H^{m+1,m+2}(Q)
,
j=1,2
, is a solution to the initial boundary value problem

(5.47)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{(j)},q^{(j)}}u_{j}=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
u_{j}=\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{r}f_{r}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u_{j}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

We now perform the higher order linearization.
Let us again denote
v_{j,p}:=\partial_{\varepsilon_{p}}u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}
. By the induction hypothesis, as well as the uniqueness of the linearized problem, we have
v_{p}:=v_{1,p}=v_{2,p}
for all
p=1,\ldots,r
. Hence, by applying
\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots\partial_{\varepsilon_{r}}u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}
to the problem (
5.47
), direct computations yield that the function
w_{j}^{(r)}:=\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots\partial_{\varepsilon_{r}}u_{j}(t,x;\varepsilon)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}
satisfies the initial boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}w_{j}^{(r)}&+2\mathrm{i}(r-1)!A_{r-1}^{(j)}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r})+\mathrm{i}r!(\nabla\cdot A_{r-1}^{(j)})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}\\
&-2r!(A_{0}\cdot A_{r-1}^{(j)})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}+r!q_{r}^{(j)}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}+\mathcal{F}_{r}^{(j)}=0\end{aligned}&\text{ in }Q,\\
w_{j}^{(r)}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
w_{j}^{(r)}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Arguing similarly as in
[
31
,
50
]
, we see that the function
\mathcal{F}_{r}^{(j)}
contains only lower order coefficients and lower order derivative terms, which are independent of
j=1,2
. By the induction hypothesis (
5.46
), we have
\mathcal{F}_{r}^{(1)}=\mathcal{F}_{r}^{(2)}
. Hence, by setting
A_{r-1}:=A_{r-1}^{(1)}-A_{r-1}^{(2)}
and
q_{r}:=q_{r}^{(1)}-q_{r}^{(2)}
, the function
w^{(r)}:=w_{1}^{(r)}-w_{2}^{(r)}
is a solution to the following problem:

(5.48)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}w^{(r)}&+2\mathrm{i}(r-1)!A_{r-1}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r})+\mathrm{i}r!(\nabla\cdot A_{r-1})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}\\
&-2r!(A_{0}\cdot A_{r-1})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}+r!q_{r}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}=0\end{aligned}&\text{ in }Q,\\
w^{(r)}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
w^{(r)}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Multiplying (
5.48
) by
\overline{w_{0}}
, where
w_{0}
solves the problem (
5.33
), and integrating over
Q
, we obtain the integral identity

\displaystyle 0=\int_{Q}

\displaystyle\left[2\mathrm{i}(r-1)!A_{r-1}\cdot\nabla(v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r})+\mathrm{i}r!(\nabla\cdot A_{r-1})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2r!(A_{0}\cdot A_{r-1})v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}+r!q_{r}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{r}\right]\overline{w_{0}}dxdt.

Next, we apply Corollaries
4.4
and
4.6
to choose geometric optics solutions of the form

v_{p}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{p}-\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{p}|^{2}t)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{p}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{p}(t,x)\right),\quad p=1,\ldots,r,

and

w_{0}(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\lambda x\cdot\alpha_{r+1}+\lambda^{2}|\alpha_{r+1}|^{2}t)}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{m_{k}^{r+1}(t,x)}{\lambda^{k}}+R_{\lambda}^{r+1}(t,x)\right).

Here the smooth amplitude
m_{k}^{p}
,
p=1,2,\dots,r
, solves the transport equation (
4.3
) with
\alpha=\alpha_{p}
and
A=A_{0}
, and
m_{k}^{3}
is a solution to the same equation with
\alpha=\alpha_{r+1}
and
A=\overline{A_{0}}
. The remainder
R_{\lambda}^{p}
,
p=1,\dots,r
, satisfies the estimate (
4.17
), while
R_{\lambda}^{r+1}
satisfies the estimate (
4.22
). Moreover, the vectors
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r},\alpha_{r+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
are chosen such that

\sum_{p=1}^{r}\alpha_{p}=\alpha_{r+1},\qquad\sum_{p=1}^{r}|\alpha_{p}|^{2}=|\alpha_{r+1}|^{2}.

From here, we argue analogously as in Subsection
5.2
to conclude that
A_{r-1}=0
and
q_{r}=0
in
Q
.

Therefore, it follows from induction that
A_{\ell}^{(1)}=A_{\ell}^{(2)}
,
\ell\geq 0
, as well as
q_{\ell}^{(1)}=q_{\ell}^{(2)}
,
\ell\geq 1
, in
Q
. Finally, in view of the expansions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), we conclude that
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
and
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
in
\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The main objective of this section is to establish the partial data result described in Theorem
1.2
. The key step of the proof is to transform the partial data problem to a full data problem by invoking the unique continuation principle given in Lemma
6.1
.

Let us begin this section with the following construction. Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
be a non-empty set such that
\mathcal{O}
is an open neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
.
We first define the admissible class of coefficients
A_{0}
and
q_{1}
. For
R>0
, we define the set

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(R):=

\displaystyle\left\{(A_{0},q_{1})\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})\times C^{\infty}(\overline{Q}):A_{0}=0\text{ and }q_{1}=0\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}\right\}.

Suppose that
\mathcal{O}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
are open sets such that
\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{j+1}\subset\mathcal{O}_{j}
, and assume that
\mathcal{O}_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, is a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
.
We then define the set
\Omega_{j}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\mathcal{O}_{j}}
,
and the cut-off function
\theta\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
0\leq\theta\leq 1
by the following formula:

(6.2)

\theta:=\begin{cases}0\qquad\text{ in }\mathcal{O}_{3},\\
1\qquad\text{ in }\Omega_{2}.\end{cases}

We are now ready to introduce the unique continuation property. It was originally establish in
[
6
, Corollary 1]
. We state it for the convenience of readers.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
T>0
, and let
Q=(0,T)\times\Omega
. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
be an arbitrary nonempty open set, and let
\Sigma^{\sharp}:=(0,T)\times\Gamma
. Let
(A_{0},q_{1})\in\mathscr{A}(R)
. Suppose that
v\in H^{1,2}(Q)
is a solution to the linear initial boundary value problem

(6.3)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0},q_{1}}v=g_{0}&\text{ in }Q,\\
v=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
v(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where

g_{0}\in L^{2}(Q)
and
\operatorname{supp}(g_{0})\subset(0,T)\times(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O}),

such that
\partial_{\nu}v=0
on
\Sigma^{\sharp}
. Then
v=0\text{ in }(0,T)\times(\Omega_{3}\setminus\Omega_{2})
.

Suppose that the function
v\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
is a solution to the problem (
5.3
). By a direct computation, the function
\widetilde{v}:=\theta v
satisfies the initial boundary value problem

(6.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}\widetilde{v}=\theta F+\left[\theta,\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A_{0}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\right]v&\text{ in }Q,\\
\widetilde{v}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\widetilde{v}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where
F:=\left(2\mathrm{i}A_{0}\cdot\nabla+\mathrm{i}\nabla\cdot A_{0}-\|A_{0}^{(2)}\|^{2}+\|A_{0}^{(1)}\|^{2}+q_{1}\right)v_{2}
and
\left[\cdot,\cdot\right]
denote the commutator.
Since
A_{0}=0
and
q_{1}=0
in
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
, it follows immediately that
F=0
in
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
, suggesting that
\hbox{supp }F\subset(0,T)\times(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O})
. Thus, by applying Lemma
6.1
, we conclude that

(6.5)

\widetilde{v}=0\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times(\Omega_{3}\setminus\Omega_{2}).

Moreover, as
\theta=1
in
\Omega_{2}
and

\theta=0
in
\mathcal{O}_{3}\subset\mathcal{O}
, we get that

(6.6)

\theta F=F\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

Let us now compute that

[\theta,\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A_{0}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla]v=-2\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla v-\left(\Delta\theta+2\mathrm{i}A_{0}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\theta\right)v.

Since
\theta
is constant on
\mathcal{O}_{3}\cup\Omega_{2}
, we have
\nabla\theta=0
and
\Delta\theta=0
in
(0,T)\times(\mathcal{O}_{3}\cup\Omega_{2})
. Therefore, in view of (
6.5
), we have

(6.7)

[\theta,\Delta+2\mathrm{i}A_{0}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla]v=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

Thus, using (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), we may rewrite the problem (
6.4
) as follows:

(6.8)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{A_{0}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)}}\widetilde{v}=F&\text{ in }Q,\\
\widetilde{v}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\widetilde{v}(0,\cdot)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

On the other hand, by (
6.2
), we have

\theta=0
in
\mathcal{O}_{3}
. Hence, it holds that
\widetilde{v}=0
in
\mathcal{O}_{3}
. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{O}_{3}
is a neighborhood of

\partial\Omega
, it follows that

(6.9)

\partial_{\nu}\widetilde{v}\big|_{\Sigma}=0.

Let
v_{1}
be a solution to the problem (
5.4
). We next multiply the first equation in (
6.8
) by
\overline{v}_{1}
and integrate over
Q
. Due to the conditions
\widetilde{v}=0
on
\Sigma
and
\widetilde{v}(0,\cdot)=v_{1}(T,\cdot)=0
in
\Omega
, in conjunction with (
6.9
), an integration by parts gives us the integral identity (
5.6
).
Therefore, applying the same analysis in Subsection
5.1
from the identity (
5.6
) onwards, we obtain (
5.29
) and
q_{1}^{(1)}=q_{1}^{(2)}
in
Q
.

Finally, let us remark that the same method can be applied to higher order linearization. Indeed, let the function
w^{(r)}
be as in the initial boundary problem (
5.48
). Due to the assumption that
A_{0}=0
and
q_{1}=0
on
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
, the function
\theta w^{(r)}
satisfies a similar problem with a source term supported away from
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
, where the commutator terms again vanish. Hence, the partial data problem in question is reduced to a full data problem after the
r^{\mathrm{th}}
order linearization. From here, by applying the same arguments as those in Subsections
5.2
and
5.3
, we obtain

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
and
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
in
\overline{Q}\times\mathbb{C}
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
