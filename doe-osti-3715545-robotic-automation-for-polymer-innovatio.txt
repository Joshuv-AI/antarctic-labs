ABSTRACT: Silicone formulations are complex materials, containing specific compositions of 10+ materials within a single
formulation. Therefore, traditional silicone formulation development can be a very labor-intensive and tedious process. Presented
here is an automated workflow for the formulation, processing, and mechanical characterization of polydimethylsiloxane elastomers.
A Chemspeed Swing XL was adapted to aspirate and dispense viscous silicone fluids via syringe modifications, reduced flow rates,
and controlled aspiration equilibration times. Across 12 experiments, the system prepared 792 vials, representing 396 two-part
formulations, dispensing 5.2 kg of material with flow viscosities up to 7.7 Pa·s. Overall, 78% of dispenses were within 5% of their
targeted values at an average dispensing rate of 63g/hr. In comparison to manual sample preparation (57g/hr), the automated method
is more efficient, while enabling continuous operation and reduced chemical exposure. Furthermore, acoustic mixing adapted for
well plates displayed promise for large batch mixing of up to 96 samples, successfully homogenizing formulations containing 33-42
wt% of a 1 Pa·s viscosity component. For characterization, automated hardness and Young’s Modulus measurements using a Bareiss
Digi Test II and Pavone Optics 11 Life produced trends consistent with conventional characterization methods. Additionally, folding
and grip-compression screening techniques yielded a strong potential for the automation and down-selection of samples before tensile
testing. To tie each step together, a complete demonstration was completed including automated dispensing, mixing, curing, and
mechanical characterization, with minimal human intervention. This workflow may reduce silicone development timelines from
months to hours, providing a foundation for closed-loop materials discovery and rapid-response formulation development for silicone
elastomers.

 Screening for these properties is important to better understand
 a. the durability and reliability of a new formulation, especially for
 applications in flexible electronics and soft robotics. Tensile
 testing is a complicated process, requiring equipment with a
 large geometric footprint and precise sample loading, making it
 difficult to automate. However, screening to remove lower
 performing samples from tensile testing may be an effective way
 to reduce tensile testing time significantly and eliminate the last
 bottleneck left in characterization.
 Before attempting to automate ultimate mechanical
 property screening, two down-selection methods were proposed
 and evaluated as candidates. First, the “fold test” is where a
 tensile type V dogbone size sample is folded in half and pressed
 on a flat surface until each half is completely touching. The “grip
 compression test” is where one end of a tensile type V dog-bone
 is placed into a pneumatic press at a specific PSI. In both cases,
 if the sample is broken, cracked, or permanently altered in any
 b. visible way, it has failed the test. All samples remaining intact
 would pass the down-selection process in each case and be
 selected for traditional tensile testing. The results of these
 screenings compared to tensile properties can be observed in
