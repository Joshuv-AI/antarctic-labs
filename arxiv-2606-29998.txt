Optimal Posterior E-values with Non-Convex Parameter Sets with Applications to Voting Systems
Adrienne Tuynman, Timothée Mathieu

Abstract
We are interested in conducting political polls sequentially, so that one can stop acquiring data as soon as possible while safely yielding statistically significant results. Building off e-values, which have recently become a useful tool to create sequential testing methods, we develop a theory of posterior optimal e-values. We use voting as a convenient example on which to illustrate our method. First, we design statistical tests for Condorcet and Borda voting system, and also for Schulze voting system which we are the first to tackle statistically. Then, we study the construction of optimal sequential e-values in the deceptively simple setting of multivariate Bernoulli data, with general composite null and alternative hypothesis sets ℋ 0 \mathcal{H}_{0} and ℋ 1 \mathcal{H}_{1} . We give a way to compute these e-values using an efficient Frank-Wolfe algorithm, giving a pretty general way to compute Reverse Information Projections, even when ℋ 0 \mathcal{H}_{0} corresponds to a non-convex parameter set. Finally, we illustrate the efficiency, both in terms of power and sample size of our method. We compare with state of the art in both simulated and real data experiments, with application to French 2022 presidential election data. Keywords: E-values, preference voting, power, posterior optimal, bayesian sequential statistics



1
Introduction

We can only test what we can measure. In some fields, that is not an issue. It is easy to measure how far a robot can run, how many patients recover after being given a certain drug, or how much corn grows in a field after using a specific seed. The solution is to craft new numbers. It might not be feasible to ask patients to rate how good they feel about a treatment (such information may be very noisy, and not objective at all), it is way easier to ask them to compare two alternatives. During a poll, it is hard to ask voters to give politicians a grade out of ten as different citizens will not be using the same grading curve, but it is easy to ask them to compare two candidates. The frequency of one option being preferred to another is something that can be computed, estimated, and that is useful. We focus here on a simple problem. With a finite set of
N
candidates, we receive comparison data: for two options
i
and
j
, we are told how many times
i
was preferred to
j
and vice versa.

Our goal in this article will be to design sequential hypothesis tests based on e-values in the context of preference data. The idea is to collect data
X^{1},X^{2},\dots
sequentially, and to stop as soon as we can take a decision. The decision is either to say that candidate
i
is a winner for the chosen voting system or that he is not a winner.

Our goals are twofold. First we design statistical tests for Condorcet and Borda voting system, and also for Schulze voting system which we are the first to tackle statistically. Our second goal is to study the construction of optimal sequential e-values in the deceptively simple setting of multivariate Bernoulli data with composite (and possibly non-convex) null and alternative hypothesis sets
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. The setting of votes is particularly adapted to display a variety of form for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, with Condorcet being the easiest to the more complicated Schulze voting method. Contrary to adaptive settings such as duelling bandits (see
4
for a survey on that topic), we do not choose which two options are compared. We will assume that we receive the same number of comparisons for every pair of options.

We first show how our preference voting motivation can be cast as a statistical hypothesis problem on the preference matrix. Then, we develop a new (to our knowledge) methodology to construct e-variables as an extension of the GRO criterion to composite
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Using a Bayesian point of view, we assign a prior over
\Phi_{1}
the set of parameters of distributions in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and define

G_{t}(E,\pi,\tau):=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X^{:t}\sim P_{\phi}^{\otimes t}}\left[\log(E(X^{:t\wedge\tau}))\mathds{1}\{\phi\in\Phi_{1}\}\right]\right],

where
\pi
is a prior on
\Phi_{1}
,
\tau
is an arbitrary stopping time and
X^{:t}:=X^{1},\dots,X^{t}
is an i.i.d. sample from
P_{\phi}
. We show that among test supermartingales, there exists one which optimises
G_{t}(E,\pi,\tau)
. We then give its expression as product of one-step e-values, each a one-step GRO e-value with respect to the posterior distribution at time
t
. We call the resulting e-value POE (Posterior Optimal E-value). The POE is defined quite generally and is not specific to our voting scenario.

In a second time, we show how to use POE to design both a fixed confidence and fixed sample size sequential test, and we give theoretical guarantees for both of these tests showing optimality in terms of expected stopping time. To this end, we also give concentration inequalities showing that POE converges to the GRO e-variable asymptotically. Then, we give a version of Frank-Wolfe algorithm adapted to our problem that allow us to compute the KL projection needed to define POE. This Frank-Wolfe algorithm is quite general and could have an independent interest as a way to perform a Reverse Information Projection when the distributions have finite support.

Finally, we illustrate the usage of POE for our setting in both simulated and real data experiments. In a simulated setting we compare the empirical power and stopping time of our tests to the state of the art (Universal Inference
(
34
)
, GLRT and the e-value from
32
), we show how our method can in addition benefit from the knowledge of prior data. To conclude, we apply our method to political poll, using data from 2022 French Presidential elections, in an attempt to answer to the question “who would have been elected president if French citizens had used Borda voting method?”.

Statistical test for preference voting

Our first goal is to set-up a statistical testing problem of the form
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{P_{\phi}^{\otimes T},\phi\in\Phi_{0}\}
against
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{P_{\phi}^{\otimes T},\phi\in\Phi_{1}\}
and decide whether data are sampled from a distribution in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
or in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. We first give the formal definition of the different
\mathcal{H}_{0}
we will consider in this article, drawn from preference voting systems.

2.1
Different kinds of winners

Definition 1

(Preference matrix)
.

Sampling a comparison between options
i
and
j
means sampling from a Bernoulli of parameter
\theta_{i,j}
,
\mathcal{B}(\theta_{i,j})
, where
\theta_{i,j}
is the probability that

\Theta=\left(\theta_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}

is defined, arbitrarily, as

.

While the matrix is of size

\theta_{i,j}
with

. Therefore, complete samples will be of size
D={N\choose 2}
, and

will be referred to as the dimension of the problem.

\Phi^{i}

is a winner for the chosen voting method, and to

2.1.1
Condorcet

When discussing preference voting, the first option that comes to mind is the Condorcet winner. The concept is simple: if an option
i
is preferred to any option
j
, then it is a Condorcet winner.

Definition 2

(Condorcet)
.

If for an option
i^{\star}
,

\forall i\neq i^{\star},\,\theta_{i^{\star},i}\geq 1/2

then
i^{\star}
is a Condorcet winner.

In
, we show what
\Phi^{1}
looks like with three candidates. This definition makes sense, but has a major drawback: the Condorcet winner does not always exist, i.e.
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\Phi^{i}\neq\Phi
. We can spot that in the figure: adding up thrice
\Phi^{1}
rotated to represent
\Phi^{2}
and
\Phi^{3}
will not yield the entire
[0,1]^{3}
cube. This is what we call Condorcet’s voting paradox.

We add one more condition to consider: we want the result to be determined uniquely by the preference matrix
\Theta
. This allows us to only use this matrix to quantify the election results, and to conduct tests based on subsets
\Phi^{i}
of matrices, see
Appendix

A
for an example of voting system that cannot be deduced from the preference matrix.

2.1.2
Borda

In the Borda system, voters assign points to the candidates:
N-1
points to the candidate they prefer, then
N-2
to the second, all the way until
0
points for the candidate they least like. The winner is the candidate who received the most total points.
While this formulation may sound like the precise order is important, another way to look at it is that each won duel grants one point.

Definition 3
.

If for an option
i^{\star}
,

\forall i\neq i^{\star},\sum_{j}\theta_{i^{\star},j}\geq\sum_{j}\theta_{i,j}

then
i^{\star}
is a Borda winner.

We represent
\Phi^{1}
in
. While there might be tied candidates, the set of preferences matrices
\Theta
inducing ties is negligible (when selecting a preference matrix uniformly at random, drawing one with multiple winners almost surely does not happen); and Borda winners can be determined from the preference matrix. This makes it a great candidate for efficient voting. Moreover, the Borda procedure also has an important property: the winner set is convex.

Lemma 1
.

For
i\in[N]
, the set
\Phi^{i}
of matrices
\Theta
that describe a preference vote where
i
is a Borda winner is convex.

The proof follows directly from the definition of Borda winner has a linear condition which implies that Borda winner is an intersection of half-spaces, hence it is convex.

However, if the Condorcet winner exists, it might not necessarily be elected by a Borda process, as we can see in the figure.
We will therefore explore one last voting method.

2.1.3
Schulze

In the Condorcet winner method, it does not matter by
how much
a candidate
i
beats another candidate
j
; all that matters is that it does beat it. Moreover, if
j
goes on to beat a candidate
\ell
, then the Condorcet method does not deduce anything about the relationship from
i
to
\ell
. The Schulze method aims to fill these two gaps.

Definition 4

(The Schulze method
(
29
)
)
.

A path
c
from candidate
i
to candidate
j
is a sequence of candidates
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
such that
i=c_{1}
,
j=c_{n}
, and for any
\ell,m\in[n]
, if
\ell\neq m
, then
c_{\ell}\neq c_{m}
. We denote
i\rightarrow j
the set of paths from
i
to
j
. The strength of path
c
is

\mathrm{Str}(c)=\min_{m\in[n-1]}\theta_{c_{m},c_{m+1}}.

In other words, a path is only as strong as its weakest link.
We say that
i
beats
j
in the Schulze method if

\max_{c\in i\rightarrow j}\mathrm{Str}(c)\geq\max_{c\in j\rightarrow i}\mathrm{Str}(c).

i
is the Schulze winner if it beats every other candidate. In particular, a Condorcet winner is necessarily a Schulze winner.

While the Schulze winner is determined by the preference matrix, it is difficult to represent
\Phi^{i}
with this definition. In fact, it is not even convex, as we will see numerically. However, we find that it is possible to define convex subsets of
\Phi
that share a Schulze winner, through defining orderings. Indeed, what matters for the Schulze method is not how much a path beats another, but merely if it does; meaning that what matters is the ordering of
\theta_{i,j}
. We summarise this observation, and the consequences from it, in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For
\Theta
and
\Theta^{\prime}
two preference matrices, we say that they share an ordering if

•

for all
i,j,k,l
such that
i\neq j
and
k\neq l
, we have
\theta_{i,j}\geq\theta_{k,l}\iff\theta_{i,j}^{\prime}\geq\theta_{k,l}^{\prime}

•

for all
i\neq j
,
0.5\geq\theta_{i,j}\iff 0.5\geq\theta_{i,j}^{\prime}
.

If they do, then
\Theta
and
\Theta^{\prime}
share a Schulze winner.

Moreover, if
\Theta
is such that, for any
i\neq j
and
k\neq l
,
(i,j)\neq(k,l)\implies\theta_{i,j}\neq\theta_{kl}
and
\theta_{i,j}\neq 0.5
(it has a strict ordering), then
\Theta
admits only a single Schulze winner.

Finally, for any such matrix, the set of preference matrices that share an ordering with it is convex.

This gives us a way to describe
\Phi^{i}
as a reunion of convex spaces, one per possible strict ordering. Of course, not all orderings are possible, due to
\theta_{i,j}=1-\theta_{j,i}
. However, It is possible to count the number of possible orderings, by noticing that each ordering can be described exactly by the order of
\theta_{\{i,j\}}=\min\{\theta_{i,j},\theta_{j,i}\}
, and for each
i,j
, which one is bigger than the other. The number of possible strict orderings is therefore
D!\times 2^{D}
. As
D
grows, the amount of convex components grows very quickly, which is a challenge for any statistical testing method.

In
, we see
\Phi^{1}
for
N=3
candidates. Note that, although we then have
48
possible strict orderings, thus
16
orderings in
\Phi^{1}
, we only see three convex components in this figure. Indeed, if
\theta_{1,2}>0.5
and
\theta_{1,3}>0.5
, then we already know that 1 is Condorcet winner, and thus Schulze winner; the other comparisons do not matter, and thus we can merge strict orderings together. This approach of merging orderings together should be applicable to higher dimensions; however, we have no optimal way of conducting the mergings, and heuristic approaches have so far failed to significantly reduce the number of convex components. As such, in this article, we will limit ourselves to studying Schulze method for
N=3
.

3
E-values for preference voting

3.1
Basics of e-values

A recent way to conduct sequential hypothesis testing are e-variables, which are defined in a deceptively simple way.

Definition 5

(E-variable)
.

Let
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
be the data sampled from an unknown distribution
P
. A nonnegative random variable
E:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is an e-value for the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
if, for any distribution
P\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}[E(Y)]\leq 1.

E-values are more resilient than
p
-values. First of all, they are easier to interpret: since under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, we expect to see a value smaller than 1, then a big e-value makes it more likely for us to be under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. The e-value can therefore be seen as the amount of evidence against
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. That makes them easy to explain and interpret, even without a threshold.
Secondly, it becomes easy to natively combine independent experiments. For that, it suffices to notice that the product of two e-values remains an e-value. See
13
or
(
24
, Section 1.2)
for a more in-depth comparison between e-values and p-values.

To have useful e-values, that will have some power, it is good to have “big” e-values under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
; e-values that are bigger than all the other ones under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. One way to formalise this is to look for e-values that are
numeraire e-values
: for
Q
a distribution, we consider
E^{\star}
, which satisfies for any other e-value
E
that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[E/E^{\star}]\leq 1
. This is equivalent to maximising the expectation under
Q
of
\log E

(
24
, Proposition 6.3)
, called the e-power
(
24
, Definition 3.11)
. This log-optimality criterion might seem like an arbitrary choice; however, since we often want to multiply e-values together to yield other e-values, log-optimality is more convenient to consider than, for example, maximising the expectation of
E
itself.

The following result guarantees the existence of the optimal e-value under singleton
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Theorem 1

(GRO, from
11
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\{P_{\phi_{1}}\}
is a singleton. Then there exists a unique distribution
P_{0}^{\star}
such that

E^{\star}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\phi_{1}}(Y)}{\mathrm{d}P_{0}^{\star}(Y)}

is an e-variable.
Additionally,
E^{\star}
satisfies, essentially uniquely,

\sup_{E}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P_{\phi_{1}}}[\log E]=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P_{\phi_{1}}}[\log E^{\star}]=\inf_{W_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}}\KL(P_{\phi_{1}},P_{W_{0}})=\KL(P_{\phi_{1}},P_{0}^{\star})

where the sup over
E
is taken in the set of e-values over
\Phi_{0}
.

E^{\star}
is called the Growth Rate Optimal (GRO) e-value for
\phi_{1}
, or the numeraire e-value.

Theorem

1
holds under the hypothesis that
P_{\phi_{1}}
has full support and that
\inf_{W_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}}\KL(P_{\phi_{1}},P_{W_{0}})
is finite. Furthermore,
P_{0}^{\star}
can potentially be a subdistribution. Since those hypotheses are always satisfied in our setting, and since
P_{0}^{\star}
is always a distribution for us, we will omit those details in the following.

The minimisation in KL found in
Theorem

1
can be interpreted as a projection of the alternative distribution onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, this projection is called the Reverse Information Projection (RIPr) and has an important role in information theory
(
7
)
.

3.2
Turner-Grünwald approach to approximate GRO

In our setting
\Phi_{1}
, the parameters for
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, is not a singleton, so that the GRO construction cannot be carried away right away and the
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]
criterion is not adapted. A first way to solve this problem was found in
(
32
, Theorem 2)
in which the authors estimate the alternative using bayesian posterior distribution and project this posterior on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
using simplified form for the KL in this setting. Their approach depend on the fact that the GRO e-value
P_{0}^{\star}
can be taken as
P_{\phi}
, instead of general
P_{W_{0}}
in the specific case of Bernoulli distribution when
\Phi_{0}
is convex and closed, so that the only thing that needs to be found is the value of
\phi
. This type of results can be found in
(
32
, Theorem 2)
for 2-dimensional Bernoulli distributions, or in
12
for exponential families. We derive our own result for our setting, multivariate Bernoulli distributions of dimension
D
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\psi\in(0,1)^{D}
and
\Phi^{\prime}\subset[0,1]^{D}
be a convex closed set. We choose to denote
\mathcal{P}_{\Phi^{\prime}}^{T}=\left\{P_{\phi}^{\otimes T},\,\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}\right\}
.
Then, there exists
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
, independent of
T
, such that, with
Y^{:T}=Y^{1},\dots,Y^{T}
,

E^{\star}\left(Y^{:T}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\psi}^{\otimes T}(Y^{:T})}{\mathrm{d}P_{\phi}^{\otimes T}(Y^{:T})}=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\psi}(Y^{t})}{\mathrm{d}P_{\phi}(Y^{t})};

in other terms, the RIPr of
P_{\psi}^{\otimes T}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is
P_{W}^{\otimes T}
with
W
a Dirac distribution. This also means that the GRO e-value over
T
steps is the product of the GRO e-values over one step.

Lemma

3
comes from the characterisation of the GRO e-value as
\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\psi}^{\otimes T}}{\mathrm{d}P}
with
P
the RIPr of
\mathrm{d}P_{\psi}^{\otimes T}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, that is, the distribution minimising
\KL(P_{\psi}^{\otimes T},P)
and from
Theorem

1
. The proof for
Lemma

3
can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Using this Lemma
3
we can define what we call later the TG e-variable which consider at time
t
, the (fictive) alternative
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{t}=\{P(\cdot\mid X^{:t})\}
comprised only of the posterior distribution and use it to compute the GRO e-value for each individual step using Lemma
3
. Remark that this approach depend on the convexity of
\Phi_{0}
and cannot be used to test for Schulze winner. On the other hand, using a Beta prior on
[0,1]^{D}
,
32
also simplify the computation of the KL to end up with a computation of a minimum of sum of KL on each dimension. See
Section

6
for a practical comparison of TG e-values with our work.

3.3
The POE e-value

Inspired from Bayesian decision theory
(
26
, Section 2.3)
and by the definition of the GRO e-value, we define the utility function of an e-value
E
as

U(E,\phi)=\log(E)\mathds{1}\{\phi\in\Phi_{1}\}.

Remark that we place ourselves in the context of utility function instead of losses to fit the setting of
11
. We aim to find the e-value
E_{t}
which, for any stopping time
\tau
, optimises its stopped integrated utility
G_{t}
with respect to a prior
\pi
. We define

\displaystyle G_{t}(E,\pi,\tau)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X^{:t}\sim P_{\phi}^{\otimes t}}\left[\log(E(X^{:t\wedge\tau}))\mathds{1}\{\phi\in\Phi_{1}\}\right]\right].

(1)

Remark that for
\pi
a prior with support on
\Phi_{1}
this simplifies as the indicator function is not needed anymore. In what follows we suppose that the support of
\pi
is in
\Phi_{1}
.

We will search for an optimal e-variable that is also a test supermartingale, i.e. e-variables such that for any
t
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{X^{:t}\sim P_{\phi}^{\otimes t}}[E(X^{:t})\mid X^{:t-1}]\leq E(X^{:t-1})
. While not all anytime e-values can be built using them
(
19
)
, test supermartingales are an easy and well-behaved way to build them.

Among those e-values, we search for an e-value which maximises for each
t
the quantity
G_{t}(E,\pi,\tau)
, which is the growth rate for some prior
\pi\in\mathcal{W}_{1}
and stopping time
\tau
. We call it the Posterior Optimal E-value (POE for short).

Definition 6

(POE)
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{W}_{1}
be a prior over
\Phi_{1}
.

(E_{t})
is a Posterior Optimal E-value (POE) for
\pi
if it is a test supermartingale and if, for any
(E_{n}^{\prime})
test supermartingale and for any stopping time
\tau
,

\forall t,\,G_{t}(E_{t},\pi,\tau)\geq G_{t}(E_{t}^{\prime},\pi,\tau).

While the condition that POE must optimise
G_{t}(E,\pi,\tau)
for any stopping time
\tau
might seem very constraining, we will see that there indeed exists an e-value which satisfies it.

Note that this is a generalisation of the GRO e-value of
Theorem

1
, as the definitions match when
\Phi_{1}
is a singleton and for
\tau=1
. For a constant stopping time, compared to the GROW e-value, we want to be good not for the worst case, but over multiple possible parameters - or, more accurately, over a distribution of parameters.

Theorem 2

(POE is the product of one-step optimal posterior e-values)
.

Let
W_{1,t}
be the posterior distribution over
\Phi_{1}
at time
t
defined by

W_{1,t}(\phi):=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}P_{\phi}(x_{i})\pi(\phi)}{\int\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}P_{\phi}(x_{i})\pi(\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi},

and

W_{0,t}:=\argmin_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}}\KL\left(\int P_{\phi}(X^{t})\mathrm{d}W_{1,t}(\phi),\int P_{\phi}(X^{t})\mathrm{d}W(\phi)\right).

Assume that, for any
\phi,\psi\in\Phi_{0}\cup\Phi_{1}
,
\KL(P_{\phi},P_{\psi})<+\infty
.

Then, we have that the POE e-variable exists and is equal to

E_{t}(x^{:t})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{\int P_{\phi}(x_{i})\mathrm{d}W_{1,i}(\phi)}{\int P_{\phi}(x_{i})\mathrm{d}W_{0,i}(\phi)}.

(2)

The proof can be found in
Appendix

D
.
In other words
W_{0,t}
minimises the one-sample posterior KL conditionally on the past. This approach is motivated by
(
11
, Theorem 1)
applied on the posterior distribution at step
t

is the one-step optimal e-value at time

conditionally on the past.

Note that it is also similar to the e-value given by

an

4
Statistical testing with POE

In this section, we describe the two testing procedures that we consider in this article, and give their guarantees in power and sample complexity.

Capped sample size test

The first procedure has a finite, pre-computed sample size that has the advantage of having a fixed maximum sample size. However, it has the disadvantage of being non-optimal on average in terms of stopping time, and it depends on some parameters of the model that may not be known in practice.

More formally, to test
\mathcal{H}_{0}
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}
using samples
X^{1},X^{2},\dots
, we construct at each timestep
t
an e-variable
E(X^{:t})
. We also decide on a maximum sample size
T
.

The Capped sample size test then consists in collecting data and stopping either for the first
t
such that
E(X^{:t})\geq 1/\alpha
, in which case we reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}
; or stopping when we collected
T
data and never rejected before, at which point we accept
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. We call this procedure stopped e-sequential test. In the sequel, we generally call this test according to which e-value it uses (e.g. POE test that uses POE e-value).

Fixed confidence test

The second procedure is optimal in term of expected stopping time and does not depend on knowing anything about the model. Its drawback, however, is that the stopping time is not controlled almost surely and it may happen that the test takes a long time to stop.

To test
\mathcal{H}_{0}
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}
using samples
X^{1},X^{2},\dots
, we construct two sequences of e-variables
E(X^{:t})
and
E^{\prime}(X^{:t})
such that

\forall 1\leq t,\forall P\in\mathcal{H}_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}_{P}[E(X^{:t})]\leq 1,

\forall 1\leq t,\forall Q\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}_{Q}[E^{\prime}(X^{:t})]\leq 1,

meaning that
E
is a sequence of e-values for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
(against
\mathcal{H}_{1}
), and
E^{\prime}
is a sequence of e-values for
\mathcal{H}_{1}
(against
\mathcal{H}_{0}
).

The test is then to collect data and stop the first time one of two events occurs: either
E(X^{:t})\geq 1/\alpha
, in which case we reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}
; or
E^{\prime}(X^{:t})\geq 1/\beta
, and we reject
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. The stopping time is thus

\tau=\inf\left\{t\geq 1,\,E(X^{:t})\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\text{ or }E^{\prime}(X^{:t})\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}.

Remark that in this testing procedure, we do not have an almost sure bound on the stopping time. When
E
and
E^{\prime}
are constructed with POE, the resulting test is called the PPOE (parallel posterior optimal e-value) test.

5
Computing POE: a Frank-Wolfe algorithm for reverse information projection

To compute POE, we need to solve a reverse information projection problem at each timestep to compute
W_{0,t}
the projection of the posterior onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The generic method used so far (when no specific method such as
(
32
, Equation (11))
could be found) is Li’s algorithm (see details in
Appendix

B
). However, this algorithm is rather slow and, in high dimension, it is not computable, due to the very high complexity of the (non-linear) optimisation problem at each iteration. We give here an alternative way to do reverse information projection.
For that, we look towards the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, introduced in
9
. This algorithm aims to find
x
that minimises a smooth function
f
on a convex set, by making smaller and smaller steps in the directions
y_{t}
that minimise the gradient
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}y_{t}
. Here, the function we want to minimise is
P\mapsto\KL(P_{W_{1,t}},P)
.
While this algorithm can be applied in multiple settings, we will apply the results of the finite-dimension case found in
(
5
, Section 3.3)
. Indeed, one can note that, in our multi-Bernoulli setting, the support of all the
P_{W}
are finite:
P_{W}
is uniquely determined by the
2^{D}
coefficients
P_{W}(x)
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{D}
. Therefore, we can recast the problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2^{D}}
. The scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{2^{D}}
is

\langle P,Q\rangle=\sum_{x}P(x)Q(x)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[Q].

This manipulation can be conducted for any finite-support distribution.

For any function
f
and
x_{t}
, the minimisation problem
\argmin_{y_{t}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}y_{t}
is linear, we can restrict the search for the minimiser
y_{t}
in the extremal points of our convex set.
Since we want to find the
P_{W}
minimising
\KL(P_{W_{1,t}},P_{W})
, we need to know the extremal points of the set of
P_{W}
where
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
.

Lemma 5
.

The extremal points of
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}=\{P_{W},W\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\}
for
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
the set of priors over some
\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
are all in the set
\{P_{\delta_{\phi}},\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}\}
.

Proof.

By definition, an extremal point of
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
is a point which cannot be written as a non-trivial convex combination (i.e. a convex combination with all weights non-zero) of points in
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
. Suppose that
P_{W}
is an extremal point of
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
for some
W
that is not a Dirac distribution. Because
W
is not a Dirac distribution, its support is not a singleton and we can partition
\mathrm{supp}(W)
into two disjoint non-empty sets
A
and
B
. We can then write

P_{W}(\cdot)=P_{W\mid A}(\cdot)\mathbb{P}_{W}(A)+P_{W\mid B}(\cdot)\mathbb{P}_{W}(B),

which means that
P_{W}
is a convex combination of
P_{W\mid A}
and
P_{W\mid B}
and it is not a trivial convex combination because
A
and
B
are both non-empty. This is a contradiction with
P_{W}
an extremal point of
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
.
∎

We will therefore need to take steps in the direction of those
P_{\delta_{\phi}}
. To know in which direction we step, we need to know the gradient of the function to minimise. Simple computation yields the following lemma proved in
Appendix

I
.

Lemma 6
.

For any
W_{0},W^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
W_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\KL(P_{W_{1}},(1-t)P_{W_{0}}+tP_{W^{\prime}})\mid_{t=0}=1-\mathbb{E}_{P_{W^{\prime}}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{W_{0}}}\right]

so that

\argmin_{P_{W^{\prime}}}\nabla\KL(P_{W_{1}},P_{W_{0}})^{\top}P_{W^{\prime}}=\argmin_{P_{W^{\prime}}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{W^{\prime}}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{W_{0}}}\right]

(where the gradient is taken over the second argument of the KL).

The Frank Wolfe algorithm applied to our problem (compute the
W_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
minimising
\KL(P_{W_{1}},P_{W_{0}})
) thus becomes Algorithm
1
.

Data:

Initialisation
W_{0}
, distribution
P_{W_{1}}

for

k=0,1,\dots

do

1

Compute the minimiser over the corners

\phi_{k}=\argmin_{\phi\in\Phi_{0}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta_{\phi}}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{W_{0}}}\right]

Update the current estimate

W_{0}\leftarrow\left(1-\frac{2}{2+k}\right)W_{0}+\frac{2}{2+k}\delta_{\phi_{k}}

end for

2

The Frank Wolfe algorithm

While we use this manipulation (using the finiteness of support of our distributions to recast the problem in finite dimension) to be able to apply Frank-Wolfe in the simple case of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2^{D}}

(
5
)
, we hypothesise that it could be applied to more general measure sets. We keep this for future work.

In practice, the stopping criterion can be that the gap between
W_{0}
at steps
k
and
k+1
is small enough, or (if it is easily computable) that the
\KL
between
P_{W_{1}}
and
P_{W_{0}}
is not changing much anymore. However, for more theoretically guaranteed ways of stopping, we have to consider our specific setting.

5.1
Implementation details

The minimisation problem of the Frank Wolfe algorithm is easier to compute than Li’s algorithm. Indeed, in Algorithm
1
, the minimisation problem is linear in
\delta_{\phi}
(the
P_{W_{0}}
in the expectation does not depend on
\phi
). Recall that the parameter
\phi
is the preference matrix
\Theta=(\theta_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
; since the support of any
P_{W}
is finite, we can thus also see
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta_{\phi}}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{W_{0}}}\right]
as a polynomial of degree 1 in each of the
\theta_{i,j}
, which makes the optimisation problem smooth and that we can solve with off-the-shelf optimisation algorithms, even potentially for non convex
\Phi_{0}
.

Furthermore, for a
\Phi_{0}
that is a finite reunion of convex sets
\Phi_{0,1},\dots,\Phi_{0,J}
, one can solve Line
1
by solving it over each
\Phi_{0,j}
to get a
\phi_{j}
, picking
j^{\star}\in\argmin_{j\in[J]}1-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta_{\phi_{j}}}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{W_{0}}}\right]
, and using
\phi_{j^{\star}}
.

In high dimension, merely computing the
\KL
might be tricky. For
N=12
candidates,
D=66
, so computing one expectation yields a sum over
2^{66}
terms. However, if
\Phi_{0}
is convex, then, by
Lemma

3
, the RIPr of any
P_{W_{1}}
onto
P_{\Phi_{0}}
has a prior with singleton support. Therefore, if we assume that all samples are taken independently (meaning that
X_{d}
is independent from
X_{d^{\prime}}
for
d\neq d^{\prime}
), then for a Dirac initialisation
W_{0}=\delta_{\phi_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{\phi}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{\delta_{\phi_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{d=1}^{D}\frac{P_{\phi^{d}}(X_{d})}{P_{\phi_{0}^{d}}(X_{d})}P_{W_{1}}(X)\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{d=1}^{D}\frac{P_{\phi^{d}}(X_{d})}{P_{\phi_{0}^{d}}(X_{d})}\left[\int P_{\phi^{\prime}}(X)\mathrm{d}W_{1}(\phi^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{d=1}^{D}\frac{P_{\phi^{d}}(X_{d})P_{\phi^{\prime d}}(X_{d})}{P_{\phi_{0}^{d}}(X_{d})}\mathrm{d}X\mathrm{d}W_{1}(\phi^{\prime})

and, using independence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{\phi}}\left[\frac{P_{W_{1}}}{P_{\delta_{\phi_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{d=1}^{D}\int\frac{P_{\phi^{d}}(X_{d})P_{\phi^{\prime d}}(X_{d})}{P_{\phi_{0}^{d}}(X_{d})}\mathrm{d}X_{d}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{1}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}(\phi^{d})}\left[\frac{\mathcal{B}(\phi^{\prime d})}{\mathcal{B}(\phi_{0}^{d})}\right]\mathrm{d}W_{1}(\phi^{\prime}).

Thus, for each
\phi
, we can compute the objective function with linear complexity in
D
, and it remains to compute the integral over
W_{1}
(which can be done by sampling over
W_{1}
).

While the Frank-Wolfe algorithm has convergence guarantees (see
(
5
, Theorem 3.8)
), those are only valid for
\beta
-smooth functions. However, our
\KL
function is not
\beta
-smooth: for
W
arbitrarily close to the borders of
\Phi
, the gradient of
\KL(P_{W_{1}},P_{W})
explodes. To solve that, we no longer consider
\Phi
, but instead exclude the outer borders, by considering
\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\left\{\Theta,\forall i,j,\,\left|\log\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{1-\theta_{i,j}}\right|\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)\right\}
; our function is thus
\beta
-smooth with
\beta=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2D}}
(and behaves way more smoothly far from the borders of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
), but we have lost sensitivity near the outer borders of
\Phi
.

Note that the restriction
\phi\in\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is strictly less restrictive than
\phi_{d}\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
necessary in
Theorem

3
: in
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
, the parameters satisfy
\phi_{d}\in\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon},1-\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)
.

We thus have a way to compute POE for a multitude of new settings, while the approach of
32
is limited to convex parameter sets.

7
Conclusion and future work

We have exhibited a new criterion for e-value optimality, posterior optimality, and have derived properties for it in
Theorem

2
. We have then given a new algorithm based on Frank Wolfe to compute the reverse information projection in our multivariate Bernoulli setting. Unlike the state of the art which can only deal with convex
\Phi_{0}
, our approach even works in settings with non-convex parameter sets. Then, we have studied the guarantees of the oracle GRO e-value and of ours in the fixed-budget and in a fixed-confidence setting with parallel tests. Finally, we have conducted experiments in our preference-voting poll setting. We have studied and compared multiple testing procedures, their stopping time, their reject probability, on synthetic examples for three different winner definitions, and we have also implemented a test on real-life data, highlighting the feasibility of the test in real-life situations, even in high dimension.

Some questions remain open, however.
First of all, the Frank Wolfe algorithm for RIPr is probably viable for a variety of settings, and not just our multivariate Bernoulli one. In which circumstances exactly is it a correct algorithm? What can we say about its convergence rate? Is the artificial
\varepsilon
slack that we added a necessity, or can it be foregone somehow?
Moreover, we wonder if it would be possible to find better ways to test for the Schulze winner; current approaches fail due to the number of convex components exploding with the dimension (as a reminder, projecting onto the subset of
\Phi
where candidate
i
is a Schulze winner requires projecting onto
(D-1)!\times 2^{D}
convex components). Efficient methods to tackle parameter sets with a high number of convex components would be a useful addition, and would allow for many more practical applications.
Finally, we have seen that TG and POE have the same practical performance in settings where both are applicable. It remains an open question in which settings this resemblance is true: would a bigger
\mathcal{H}_{0}
cause POE to perform better, for example? When is the gap between the two appreciable? More work in the differences between TG and POE in practice is required.
