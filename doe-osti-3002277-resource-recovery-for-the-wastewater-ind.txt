 1. BACKGROUND OF THIS RESOURCE

This resource was developed through the U.S. Department of Energy’s Sustainable Wastewater
Infrastructure of the Future (SWIFt) Initiative, which ran from 2016 to 2024. SWIFt was designed to help
municipal governments tackle the significant energy demands of drinking water and wastewater plants,
which often account for 30-40% of local energy consumption. SWIFt provided technical assistance,
training, and resources to help over 328 facilities across 43 states achieve energy savings between 5-25%,
with some partners identifying annual savings opportunities exceeding $130,000.

DOE’s work with facility partners produced a number of tools and resources that have since been
compiled and published into the Wastewater Energy Management Toolkit [1]. The toolkit contains dozens
of resources to help facilities track their energy performance, identify no-and low-cost savings strategies,
learn more about funding and financing mechanisms, and establish energy improvement master plans.
SWIFt concluded in 2024, but its impact endures through the Better Plants program [2], which continues
to support water utilities in pursuing long-term energy, water, and waste reduction goals.

 2. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This information sheet discusses the technology pillar, Resource Recovery, as a pathway toward
improving wastewater infrastructure sustainability and resiliency. To supplement existing literature on
current technologies and policies for improving resiliency at wastewater (WW) treatment plants, this
document aims to accomplish the following:

• Summarize wastewater sludge recovery methods
• Summarize biogas production and codigestion methods
• Serve as a comprehensive (though not exhaustive) repository for resource recovery for wastewater
 utilities

The Resource Recovery Technical Information Sheet should be viewed as a general guide to established
best practices for the water and wastewater (W/WW) sector when considering implementing energy
capture technologies. Additional details on associated energy capture avenues such as combined heat and
power (CHP), renewable energy, and inline hydropower from tertiary effluent in W/WW facilities are
presented in the Energy Capture Technology Information Sheet.1

 3. RESOURCE RECOVERY FOR THE WASTEWATER INDUSTRY

Resource recovery in the wastewater (WW) industry transforms wastewater and its byproducts into
valuable resources, thereby creating economic opportunities toward resilient, circular WW management
systems. By rethinking waste management, WW utilities can contribute to a circular economy while
addressing challenges such as resource scarcity. For system resiliency in the W/WW sector, an active
ongoing effort seeks to view “waste” as a “resource,” thereby transitioning “wastewater treatment plants”
(WWTPs) into “wastewater resource recovery facilities.” The WWTPs produce two key products: (a)
treated effluent from the water flow train and (b) thickened sludge from the solids train. Below are three
key areas of resource recovery (Figure 1):

Resource recovery in the WW industry offers significant environmental, economic, and sustainability
benefits. By generating biogas, reducing dependency on mineral fertilizers, and sequestering carbon in
soils, resource recovery processes can effectively create independent revenue streams and reduce external
dependencies. These processes also help minimize waste and conserve valuable resources. Economically,
resource recovery offsets operational costs through energy production, fertilizer sales, and reduced waste
disposal expenses. Additionally, resource recovery fosters system resiliency by promoting a circular
economy, transforming waste into reusable materials, and closing the resource loop.

The WW industry faces several challenges in implementing resource recovery. Regulatory compliance
and permitting processes can be complex and time-consuming, often posing barriers to adoption. Public
perception also plays a critical role, with concerns about the safety and acceptability of biosolids and
wastewater reuse creating resistance in some communities. Additionally, the industry requires advanced
technologies and significant investments in infrastructure to fully realize the potential of resource

recovery, which can be a substantial hurdle for many utilities. These challenges highlight the need for
strategic planning, public engagement, and financial support to drive progress in this area.

 4. RECOVERING WASTEWATER SLUDGE

Wastewater sludge, the byproduct of WW treatment processes, is increasingly viewed as a valuable
resource with potential for recovery and reuse. The process of converting WW sludge into biosolids
involves the following steps to reduce volume, stabilize organic matter, and remove pathogens, thereby
making it suitable for reuse (Figure 2, Table 1):

• Thickening: Sludge solids are concentrated by gravity settling or air flotation, increasing solids
 content to 5%–6% while maintaining liquid properties.

• Dewatering: Solids content is further increased to 15%–30% through air drying, centrifugation, or
 filtration. Air drying also reduces pathogens, though some nutrient loss may occur.

• Anaerobic Digestion: In this widely used method, sludge is treated in the absence of air and in the
 presence of anaerobic bacteria for 15–60 days at temperatures ranging from 68°F to 131°F.

• Aerobic Digestion: Sludge is agitated with air or oxygen for 40–60 days at temperatures of 59°F to
 68°F, enabling stabilization with the help of aerobic bacteria.

• Composting: Dewatered sludge (solids content ~20%) is mixed with high-carbon organic materials,
 such as sawdust, and composted under aerobic conditions at temperatures of at least 131°F for several
 days, further stabilizing the material.

Sludge management is shaped by regulatory, environmental, technological, and public perception
challenges.[4],[5] The main drivers in solids handling and management are:

• Regulation and Policy
 o Regulatory changes at both federal and state levels are major drivers of trends in wastewater
 sludge management.

• U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and State Revisions
 o The EPA’s 2011 refinement [6] of the definition of solid waste to include sewage sludge brought
 incinerators under the Clean Air Act, requiring stricter emissions standards.
 o Criteria for renewable fuel legitimacy include being managed as a commodity, having meaningful
 heating value, and maintaining contaminant levels lower than those of traditional fuels.

• State-Specific Impacts
 o Whereas federal regulations have remained relatively stable, states have implemented changes
 affecting biosolids management. For example, the application of Class B biosolids often requires
 special permits, public hearings, and nutrient management plans.
 o In Florida and Texas, enhanced biosolids management have led to a 75% reduction in land
 appliers and a 25% decrease in the volume of applied biosolids.
 o Some counties have banned or imposed stricter limits on land application, citing challenges like
 excessive fees, and burdensome permitting requirements.

 4

• Variability Across States
 o While states like Kansas, Texas, and North Carolina successfully charge fees for biosolids
 distribution to farmers, others, like Virginia, face barriers like transition from Class B to Class A
 biosolids despite farmer support.
 o Wisconsin prohibits local ordinances from banning land application, ensuring statewide
 consistency.

• Key Concerns in Biosolids Management
 o Stability and Odor
 ▪ Measures of biosolids stability, pathogen reduction, and odor control maybe applicable under
 state/local nuisance laws or under air regulations, reflecting evolving standards for acceptable
 application.
 o Nutrient Overload
 ▪ Land application often oversupplies phosphorus when applied based on nitrogen
 requirements, leading some states to adopt the USDA Phosphorus Index to guide practices.
 o Public Perception
 ▪ Public concerns about safety and odor continue to influence biosolids policies, as surveys
 highlight persistent skepticism about land application practices.

 5. RECOVERING ENERGY FROM WASTEWATER VIA BIOGAS PRODUCTION

In the U.S., over 1,200 water resource recovery facilities (WRRFs) process WW solids using anaerobic
digesters, with 85% of these facilities using biogas beneficially and 22% generating electricity.[7] The
anaerobic digestion process generates biogas, primarily composed of methane, for heat or electricity
generation.

5.1 BIOGAS UTILIZATION AND RENEWABLE NATURAL GAS

Renewable Natural Gas (RNG), also known as biomethane, is upgraded biogas that can replace
conventional natural gas. It is produced by increasing the methane content and removing impurities such
as water vapor, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide. RNG can be used for thermal applications and
electricity generation or as vehicle fuel. Once upgraded, it is indistinguishable from natural gas and can be
transported via natural gas pipelines. RNG’s value exceeds that of conventional natural gas, particularly
when used as transportation fuel under the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) program.

Created under the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and expanded by the Energy Independence and Security Act
of 2007, the RFS mandates fuel refiners and importers to meet a renewable volume obligation.
Compliance can be achieved by obtaining credits2 generated from renewable fuels meeting Energy Policy
Act-approved pathways [8], known as renewable identification numbers (RINs). The program recognizes
four categories of renewable fuels:

• Cellulosic biofuel (D3 RINs)
• Biomass-based diesel (D4 RINs)
• Advanced biofuel (D5 RINs)
• Total renewable fuel (D6 RINs)

2 For biogas, 77,000 British thermal units (Btu) is equal to 1 gallon equivalent or 1 RIN. Reference:

http://epa.gov/lmop/information-about-renewable-fuel-standard-landfill-gas-energy-projects. Current as of 07/2025.

 5

RNG qualifies under two RIN categories: cellulosic (D3 RINs) and advanced biofuel (D5 RINs) [9]. This
allows producers to optimize which RINs to generate based on market conditions.

• Cellulosic RINs (D3) are the highest-value credits in the RFS hierarchy.
 o They can be “nested” to meet lower-tier obligations—i.e., excess D3 RINs can retire D5
 (advanced) or D6 (renewable) requirements—making them highly tradable and flexible for
 obligated parties.
 o Market value: Because of their stringent GHG requirements and scarcity of cellulosic fuels, D3
 RINs typically trade at a premium ($0.05 – 3.50 per RIN), creating significant revenue upside for
 biogas producers.

• Advanced biofuel (D5) represents any non-cellulosic, non-conventional biofuel and carry strong
 market demand from obligated parties looking to fulfill their advanced biofuel volume obligations.
 o Market value: While generally less valuable than D3, D5 RINs still command healthy prices
 (often around $0.50–$0.00 per RIN), providing an attractive revenue stream for RNG when
 cellulosic pathways aren’t available or fully utilized.
 o When used as transportation fuel, RNG generates RINs, which can be sold, thereby adding
 economic value. RNG’s diverse applications and higher market value make it a key renewable
 resource for WRRFs and other biogas producers.

5.2 CODIGESTION

Codigestion is the addition of external organic wastes, such as municipally collected scraps and food
processing byproducts, as feedstock at WRRFs. As part of the EPA’s Food Recovery Hierarchy [10],[11],
codigestion plays a key role in advancing the circular economy by turning waste into valuable resources
in the following ways:

• Diverting food waste from landfills to reduce the carbon footprint of waste management
• Minimizing municipal solid waste hauling distances
• Reducing truck traffic and associated air emissions
• Sequestering carbon into soil through the land application of biosolids
• Enhancing biogas production by incorporating fats, oil, and grease (FOG) into digesters

The choice of feedstocks is critical because it directly influences methane production and overall digester
efficiency. Methane yield varies significantly among feedstocks, emphasizing the need for careful
selection to optimize biogas production and system performance. Following are examples of feedstocks
that can be used in codigestion:

• Restaurant or cafeteria food waste
• Food processing byproducts
• FOG from restaurant grease traps
• Energy crops and crop residues

Codigestion of food waste offers potential solutions by boosting biogas production, improving project
economics, and enabling facilities to justify or expand CHP investments. Facilities can also generate
revenue through tipping fees for FOG and food waste, which, when combined with energy savings, can
help secure funding for capital improvements without raising rates. The EPA conducted a detailed study
of six WRRFs on boosting biogas production via codigestion [12], finding that biogas production
increased by 40%–300% with codigestion.

 6

However, challenges persist with codigestion. For example, the City of Mesa, Arizona, identified
technical, financial, and operational hurdles during their feasibility study. One key issue is that adding
food waste to biogas production can lower its value because the resulting gas qualifies for the less
valuable D5 RIN category over the D3 RIN.

 APPENDIX A. CASE STUDY: UPGRADING SLUDGE MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES FOR
 BIOSOLIDS PRODUCTION

 CASE STUDIES: California (1), Texas (2)

Encina Wastewater Authority, Carlsbad, California

Facility
Encina Water Pollution Control Facility

Technology
Heat drying technology

Process
After 15–20 days in digesters, wastewater solids are dewatered using Alfa Laval centrifuges, producing
Class B biosolids with 23% solids. The solids are then heat-dried in an Andritz DDS 40 rotating drum
dryer to 200°F, using natural or methane gas; this process removes moisture, kills pathogens, and creates
>90% solids in pellet form, classified as Class A biosolids. The pellets are screened for size, with rejects
recycled back to the dryer. Correctly sized pellets are cooled, sprayed with camelina oil for dust control,
stored in silos, and finally transported to their destination.

Benefits
• Reduced costs for biosolids disposal
• Revenue generation of $2 million/year from the sale of Class A biosolids

Fort Worth Biosolids Dewatering and Processing Facility
