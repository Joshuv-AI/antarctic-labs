Preoperative chlorhexidine mouthwash to prevent postoperative infections in low-risk elective abdominal surgeries: a double-blind RCT from North India among patients with poor oral hygiene
Authors: Shefali Gautam, Sanjeev Kumar, Vimala Venkatesh, Aparna Shukla, Ravi Prakash, Vinod Kumar Srivastava, Rajesh Raman, Shashank Kumar, Neel Kamal Mishra, Monica Kohli, Titiksha Singh, Vinita Singh, Mohan Tiwari, Deepali Chandra, Geeta Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative respiratory complications, particularly in patients with poor oral hygiene, contribute significantly to morbidity and healthcare burden. The oral cavity serves as a reservoir of pathogenic micro-organisms that, when aspirated during or after surgery, can lead to respiratory infections. While chlorhexidine mouthwash has shown promise in high-risk surgeries, its role in short-duration elective abdominal surgeries remains unexplored, especially in low-resource settings.MethodsThis double-blind, randomised controlledrial included 228 patients (American Society of Anaesthesiologists I/II, aged 18–65 years, Oral Hygiene Index (OHI) >6) undergoing elective abdominal surgery under general anaesthesia lasting <4 hours. Patients were randomised to receive either 0.2% chlorhexidine (group A) or normal saline (group B) mouthwash two times before surgery. Outcomes measured included postoperative sore throat, fever, pneumonia, surgical site infections (SSI) and length of hospital stay. Microbiological cultures, complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP) and chest X-rays were evaluated on postoperative days 1, 3 and 7.ResultsChlorhexidine significantly reduced sore throat (18.42% vs 47.37%, p<0.001), positive bacterial cultures (21.05% vs 62.28%, p<0.001), CRP elevation and SSI (4.39% vs 21.93%, p<0.001). Length of hospital stay was also shorter in the chlorhexidine group (p<0.001). Pneumonia rates were low and comparable.ConclusionThis double-blind randomised controlled trial demonstrates that preoperative use of chlorhexidine mouthwash significantly reduces postoperative sore throat, bacterial colonisation, SSI and length of hospital stay in patients with poor oral hygiene undergoing short-duration elective abdominal surgeries. These findings highlight the utility of a simple, low-cost oral hygiene intervention in improving surgical outcomes, particularly in low-resource settings and support its incorporation into standard perioperative protocols, even for lower risk procedures.Trial registration numberCTRI/2024/06/069511).Keywords: Global Health, Public Health, Surgery, Medical microbiology, Hospital-based study

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Postoperative respiratory and surgical site infections are major causes of morbidity and prolonged hospital stays, particularly in patients with poor oral hygiene. Chlorhexidine mouthwash has been shown to reduce postoperative infections in high-risk surgeries, such as cardiac and thoracic procedures. However, its role in lower-risk, short-duration abdominal surgeries, especially in low-resource settings, has not been thoroughly investigated through randomised controlled trials.

Introduction

Postoperative respiratory complications remain a leading cause of perioperative morbidity and mortality worldwide, contributing substantially to prolonged hospital stay, increased healthcare costs and adverse patient outcomes.1 2 Despite advances in anaesthetic and perioperative care, the incidence of these complications remains significant, prompting continued efforts to identify modifiable risk factors and effective preventive strategies. Accumulating evidence suggests that poor oral hygiene, characterised by dental caries and heavy plaque accumulation, is an important yet under-recognised risk factor for postoperative infectious complications.3 4 The oral cavity represents a complex and dynamic ecosystem that harbours diverse pathogenic micro-organisms and inflammatory mediators that readily form biofilms on dental and mucosal surfaces.5 In states of inadequate oral care, xerostomia or mucosal injury, these biofilms proliferate and may serve as reservoirs for respiratory pathogens.6 Aspiration of oral secretions containing bacteria, hydrolytic enzymes, endotoxins and proinflammatory cytokines into the lower respiratory tract can initiate significant inflammatory responses, predisposing patients to pneumonia and other respiratory tract infections.3 5 Although microaspiration may occur even in healthy individuals during sleep, intact cough reflexes, mucociliary clearance and immune mechanisms usually prevent infection. These protective mechanisms are often impaired in surgical patients due to anaesthesia, intubation, postoperative pain, reduced mobility and physiological stress, thereby increasing vulnerability to aspiration-related complications.5 Multiple observational and interventional studies have demonstrated a strong association between oral hygiene and pneumonia, particularly in elderly and institutionalised populations, where structured oral care significantly reduces pneumonia incidence and mortality.4 5 Postoperative pneumonia (POP) is typically diagnosed using a combination of clinical, laboratory and radiological criteria:7 8

A rise in temperature more than 38 °C (occurring 72 hours following surgery).

In addition, bacterial translocation from oral biofilms via aspiration or hematogenous spread has been implicated in the pathogenesis of surgical site infections (SSIs), which remain a major source of postoperative morbidity.9 10

Oral biofilms harbour respiratory pathogens such as Actinobacillus, Fusobacterium, Eikenella corrodens and Streptococcus species which are poorly eradicated by systemic antibiotic prophylaxis due to limited penetration into dental plaque biofilms.6 Consequently, non-antibiotic preventive strategies targeting oral microbial load have gained increasing attention. Chlorhexidine, a broad-spectrum antiseptic mouthwash, effectively reduces oral bacterial colonisation and has been shown to lower the incidence of POP, SSIs and length of hospital stay in selected surgical populations.9 11 12

However, existing evidence predominantly originates from studies involving major surgeries associated with high pulmonary risk, such as thoracic, cardiac or neurosurgical procedures.8 9 11 There is a notable paucity of randomised controlled data evaluating the role of preoperative oral decontamination in patients undergoing short-duration abdominal surgeries, particularly with respect to postoperative respiratory complications beyond pneumonia, such as postoperative sore throat, fever and cough.

Although postoperative sore throat is often regarded as a minor complication, it can also indicate early oropharyngeal inflammation and microbial colonisation, which may precede the development of upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) in susceptible patients. Oropharyngeal mucosal damage following endotracheal intubation promotes bacterial adherence and local inflammatory responses, potentially serving as an early clinical marker of respiratory tract involvement. Therefore, a decrease in postoperative sore throat might suggest a reduction in early infective or inflammatory processes within the upper airway, supporting its recognition as a relevant perioperative outcome alongside formal respiratory infections.13 14

Given the simplicity, low cost and favourable safety profile of chlorhexidine mouthwash, establishing high-quality evidence for its effectiveness in low-risk surgical populations has important implications for global perioperative practice, especially in low-income and middle-income settings where poor baseline oral hygiene is prevalent.15 This randomised controlled trial was therefore designed to evaluate whether preoperative chlorhexidine mouthwash reduces postoperative respiratory and infectious complications in patients undergoing short-duration abdominal surgeries.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications, defined as the occurrence of URTI or lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI). The secondary objectives were to determine the role of chlorhexidine mouthwash on SSIs and duration of hospital stay.

Methods

Study design

The proposed study was a prospective randomised controlled double-blinded study conducted in the Department of Anaesthesia of a flagship hospital in Uttar Pradesh, Lucknow in collaboration with the various surgical departments for 1 year from 1 August 2024 to 31 July 25. The study aimed to identify the role of chlorhexidine mouthwash in preventing postoperative respiratory complications in patients undergoing elective surgeries under general anaesthesia (GA).

Participants

Patients of American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) grade I/II of either sex, with poor oral hygiene (Oral Hygiene Index, OHI>6), between 18 and 65 years of age posted for elective abdominal surgeries of less than 4 hours duration under GA using endotracheal tube were included in the study. Patients who underwent laparoscopic surgeries, thoracic, cardiac and major bowel surgeries and patients in which supraglottic devices were used for GA were excluded from the study. Patients with active URTI/LRTI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/asthma, active smokers, with alcohol abuse, diabetes mellitus, morbid obesity, any immune deficiency, heart insufficiency, those with known allergy to chlorhexidine, those on broad spectrum antibiotics and negative consent to participate in trial were also excluded from the study. Patients who had not smoked for more than a year were classified as not having a habit of smoking. Patients in whom immediate postoperative extubation could not be anticipated or neurological complications were present, or those requiring reintubation or re-exploration and those surgeries that extended beyond 4 hours were dropped from the study.

Randomisation and blinding

All patients who provided written consent after meeting the study’s inclusion criteria were examined by an experienced dentist 1 day before surgery to assess and document active caries, the number of teeth affected and the presence of periodontal disease. They were randomly divided into two groups by using sealed envelopes with computer-generated numbers in a 1:1 ratio. Group A or the intervention arm received 10 mL of chlorhexidine mouthwash 0.2% (w/v) for oral rinse to be done for 30 s, while group B or the control arm received 10 mL of saline mouthwash for oral rinse for 30 s. Both the patient and the observer were blinded, as the oral solution was prepared by a third party—a dedicated anaesthesia OT technician, while the comparator was the chief investigator, who was unaware of the solution being administered. To ensure uniformity, an edible blue colouring agent was added to the normal saline to match the appearance of the commercially available chlorhexidine mouthwash, which was already blue.

Both solutions were placed in identical, unlabelled bottles to maintain blinding. As each participant received only a single type of oral rinse and had no exposure to or comparison with the alternative solution, differences in taste could not reliably indicate group allocation.

Methodology

A routine standard preoperative check-up was done. An oral examination was done by an experienced dentist. Eligibility and the state of oral hygiene was graded by the OHI (online supplemental file 2).

Patients with an OHI of 6 or more were included in this study. All patients who provided informed written consent and met the study’s inclusion criteria for participation in the study were examined by an experienced dentist 1 day before surgery. All the patients received preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis with injection cefuroxime 750 mg iv along with the two postoperative doses. They were randomly assigned to two groups using a computer-generated sequence with assignments in sealed envelopes. Group A received 10 mL of chlorhexidine mouthwash 0.2% (w/v) for oral rinse for 30 s, while group B received 10 mL of saline mouthwash for oral rinse for 30 s. Both the groups’ patients used mouthwash two times per day before surgery.

In the study, both the patient and the observer were blind.

A baseline complete blood count (CBC) was obtained in both the groups before GA was administered. Before endotracheal intubation, a throat swab was taken. The patients were then administered GA using standard anaesthetic drugs followed by endotracheal intubation. After completion of surgery, patients were extubated. Throat swab samples for microbiological culture were collected immediately after extubation and again on postoperative days (POD) 3 and 7 in patients with a positive initial culture or who developed clinical features suggestive of infection. Laboratory investigations, including CBC, C-reactive protein (CRP) and chest X-ray (CXR), were performed on POD 3 and POD 7 to evaluate postoperative infectious complications (online supplemental file 3).

Core body temperature, incidence of sore throat, excessive bronchial secretions, productive cough, abnormal breath sounds and need for supplemental oxygen were monitored till POD 7. The diagnosis of POP or LRTI was made based on the guidelines for the management of hospital-acquired pneumonia, including the following:

Tracheal secretions which are purulent and the presence of new infiltrate on CXR.

Tracheal secretions which are purulent and the presence of new infiltrate on CXR.

An increase in the requirement for oxygen.

An increase in the requirement for oxygen.

Rise in temperature >38.3°C.

Rise in temperature >38.3°C.

Raised TLC (>12 000/mm3) or a decrease in TLC (<4000/mm3).

Raised TLC (>12 000/mm3) or a decrease in TLC (<4000/mm3).

The diagnosis of POP required the presence of all the criteria in the patients. URTI was defined as patients with sore throat, cough and/or fever.

Length of hospital stay and development of SSI were recorded.

SSIs were defined and classified according to Centres for Disease Control and Prevention criteria, including superficial incisional, deep incisional and organ/space SSI.

The primary outcome of the study was the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications defined as the occurrence of URTI or LRTI and the secondary outcomes were SSI and length of hospital stay.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Discussion

This double-blind randomised controlled trial assessed the impact of preoperative chlorhexidine mouthwash in patients with poor oral hygiene undergoing short-duration elective abdominal surgeries in a low-income and middle-income country (LMIC) setting. The results show that a simple preoperative oral decontamination strategy was linked to significant reductions in postoperative sore throat, microbiological culture positivity, SSIs and length of hospital stay, despite the low baseline pulmonary risk of the study population.

Radiologically confirmed pneumonia was uncommon and did not differ between groups (0.88% vs 1.75%, p=1.000). While overall postoperative pulmonary complication rates were not significantly different between groups, preoperative chlorhexidine mouthwash had a significantly lower incidence of postoperative sore throat (18.42% vs 47.37%, p<0.001), reduced microbiological culture positivity (21.05% vs 62.28%, p<0.001), fewer elevations in TLC on POD 2 (7.02 % vs 41.23%, p<0.001) and CRP (9.65% vs 24.56%, p=0.004), lower rates of SSI (4.39% vs 21.93%, p<0.001) and a shorter median length of hospital stay (3 (IQR 2–3) vs 4 (IQR 3–5) days, p<0.001).

While prior studies have suggested that oral hygiene interventions can reduce respiratory infections, most have focused on high-risk populations including patients undergoing thoracic, cardiac or neurosurgical procedures, as well as critically ill or elderly groups.1–7 Meta-analyses have also supported the effectiveness of chlorhexidine in preventing POP in these high-risk settings.11 However, the relevance of such interventions to lower-risk surgical groups has remained uncertain. By focusing on short-duration elective abdominal surgeries, our study fills a significant gap and suggests that the benefits of preoperative oral decontamination may go beyond traditionally high-risk procedures.

The observed reduction in postoperative sore throat is clinically significant, as sore throat is a common patient-reported outcome after endotracheal intubation and contributes to early postoperative discomfort and lower patient satisfaction. Furthermore, a sore throat may indicate early oropharyngeal inflammation and microbial colonisation, which can precede URTI. The simultaneous decrease in culture positivity and inflammatory markers in the intervention group supports a biologically plausible link between oral decontamination and the reduction of early infective or inflammatory processes within the upper airway. Previous randomised trials have demonstrated that perioperative oral care solutions reduce the incidence of postoperative sore throat following airway instrumentation, supporting the biological plausibility of our findings.14

A key strength of this study is its focus on patients with objectively poor oral hygiene (OHI>6), a population highly encountered in LMICs where access to regular dental care is limited and for whom perioperative oral management remains a critical but underutilised intervention.17 Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that periodontal disease facilitates bacterial translocation through aspiration or haematogenous spread, contributing to both respiratory and SSIs.10 11 Such pathways are particularly relevant in vulnerable populations where oral health status is often compromised.18 The substantial reduction in SSI rates observed in the intervention group aligns with this biological rationale and is consistent with observational data from abdominal surgery cohorts reporting improved outcomes with perioperative oral care.12 19 Our findings extend this evidence through a randomised design with microbiological confirmation of reduced bacterial burden.

The significant reduction in hospital length of stay and infectious complications observed in this study underscores the potential impact on resource-limited settings. Chlorhexidine mouthwash, characterised by its affordability, wide accessibility and ease of use, presents a practical addition to current perioperative infection-prevention strategies. Integrating this solution into perioperative care pathways, such as Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols could be a cost-effective way to improve surgical outcomes across healthcare settings.

This study has some limitations. Its single-centre setting and focus on patients with poor oral hygiene might limit the extent to which the results apply to a wider surgical population. Evaluating multiple outcomes could increase the risk of a type I error, but using subjective measures such as microbiological cultures, inflammatory markers and SSIs—adds credibility to the findings. Although postoperative sore throat is a subjective measure, its consistent decrease, together with objective infection indicators, enhances confidence in the overall results.

Conclusion

In patients with poor oral hygiene undergoing short elective abdominal surgeries, using chlorhexidine mouthwash before surgery was linked to fewer postoperative infections, such as upper respiratory symptoms, microbiological colonisation and SSIs. It also resulted in a shorter hospital stay. While pneumonia confirmed by radiology was rare, the results indicate that simple preoperative oral cleaning could be a cost-effective and practical way to improve surgical outcomes, especially in LMICs. More multicentre research is needed to verify these findings and explore its broader application in different surgical groups.
