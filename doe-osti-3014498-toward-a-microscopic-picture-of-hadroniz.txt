1. Accomplishments

1.1 What are the major goals of the project?

At high-energy collider experiments, high-energy quarks and gluons are produced that allow for new
insights into the structure and emergence of hadrons. In addition, collider experiments facilitate studies of
the quark-gluon plasma at high temperatures and densities and enable searches of physics beyond the
Standard Model. Current and future experiments include the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC, BNL),
the Large Hadron Collider (LHC, CERN), the Continuous Electron Beam Accelerator Facility (CEBAF,
JLab) and the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC, BNL). The theory of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) describes
the elementary interaction of quarks and gluons. While QCD allows for an elegant formulation at the level
of the Lagrangian, the resulting collider phenomenology has turned out to be very challenging. The main
reason is that QCD dynamics are nonperturbative at low energies making it challenging to obtain reliable
predictions from first principles. The concept of QCD factorization has led to the following theoretical
picture of high-energy collisions. First, a high-energy scattering occurs which can be described
perturbatively in QCD. Second, a subsequent parton shower cascade evolves the scattering states from high
to low energies. Third, color-neutral hadrons are formed from low-energy quarks and gluons. These hadrons
are eventually identified by the detectors located around the interaction vertex. The last process, which is
referred to as QCD hadronization, is arguably the most challenging aspect as it occurs at low energies,
which requires an improved understanding of both perturbative and nonperturbative methods. It is the
central focus of this project. An improved understanding of the QCD hadronization process will be achieved
by tackling this problem from three distinct but related approaches: 1. Perturbative QCD calculations of
observables that are sensitive to multi-parton correlations, 2. quantum simulations of field theoretical real-
time dynamics, and 3. explainable A.I. / machine learning.

The first part involves perturbative QCD calculations of observables that are sensitive to multi-parton
dynamics, which represent an important starting point for targeted studies of the QCD hadronization
process. Examples include multi-differential measurements, hadrons inside jets and jet substructure
observables at collider experiments. These calculations are based on QCD factorization theorems that also
require nonperturbative components related to hadronization. Those universal nonperturbative functions or
parameters may eventually become accessible via real-time quantum simulations of field theories. Working
toward this goal constitutes the second component of this proposal. Specifically, important steps will be

taken by developing a hybrid quantum computing paradigm using both discrete (qubit) and continuous
(qumodes) resources. QCD requires the simulation of both quark and gluon degrees of freedom, which are
most naturally simulated by discrete and continuous variables, respectively. Different quantum platforms
(superconducting circuits, trapped ions, photonics etc.) provide access to both resources, which requires a
coupling to fully leverage the available resources. Third, complementary to the previous two approaches to
improve our understanding of QCD hadronization, I will develop explainable A.I. / machine learning
algorithms, which will provide new insights into the momentum, charge, and flavor dependence of the
hadronization process by making use of the full event-by-event information of collider experiments. Here
the focus will be on generative modeling of hadronization using diffusion models that are able to produce
high-quality samples and constraints from first principles can be incorporated. Both machine learning and
quantum computing are poised to have a profound impact on nuclear physics over the next decade. The
confluence of different perspectives and computational techniques can chip away at different aspects of the
problem, which is critical in order to make significant progress over the next years. The proposed research
is of direct relevance to existing and future nuclear facilities in the US. Dedicated studies of hadronization
and related measurements that will benefit from an improved understanding of hadronization are the focus
of the ongoing JLab 12 and RHIC programs as well as the future EIC.

1.2 What was accomplished under these goals?

Significant progress has been made in all three areas of the proposal, which will be discussed in more detail
in the following.

Track functions and multi-hadron in-jet fragmentation functions: Jet substructure measurements performed
on tracking information instead of full jets require the use of track functions on the theoretical side. Track
functions are universal nonperturbative objects that are sensitive to the multi-parton nature of the
hadronization process. In JHEP 01 (2024) 194, we introduced new semi-inclusive jet functions and
perturbatively evaluated them to next-to-leading logarithmic accuracy. They will allow for jet substructure
measurements that are most sensitive to track functions, which are not only relevant for the calculation of
other cross sections but they are also interesting nonperturbative objects in their own right. Different than
fragmentation functions for inclusive hadrons, they obey non-linear evolution equations. They describe the
momentum fraction of jets that are carried by charged particles. This necessarily involves multi-parton
dynamics making this a challenging problem that can provide unique insights into the QCD hadronization
mechanism. We provided phenomenological results for proton-proton collisions as well as for the future
EIC. These results will play an important role in order to extract track functions from experimental data in
the future. In addition, we derived factorization formulas for di-hadron and multi-hadron observables
measured inside jets, which constitutes another important milestone of this project.

Quantum simulations and real-time dynamics of field theories: In Phys. Rev. D 108 (2023) 9, 094518, we
studied the so-called string breaking process in the Schwinger model. While the Schwinger model is a
lower-dimensional field theory, it serves as an important testing ground to study QCD hadronization as it
exhibits a confining potential. We studied the string-breaking process both in vacuum and the medium and
observed a delay of the string-breaking dynamics due to dissipative effects in the medium. These results
may already have a direct impact on modeling nonperturbative hadronization effects in Monte Carlo event

generators for heavy-ion collisions. In addition, we studied late-time relaxation dynamics in this model and
assessed Trotter errors of open quantum system simulations. Interestingly, we found that Trotter errors for
the evolution of the open system can be smaller than the closed system due to cancellations between
different sources of error. Moreover, we are currently developing a hybrid qubit and qumode quantum
simulation approach using trapped ion platforms. This can play an important role in future large-scale
simulations of QCD dynamics, representing quark and gluon degrees of freedom with discrete and
continuous variables, respectively. For trapped ion platforms, qubits can be encoded in the electronic states
of the atom, whereas qumodes can be realized via collective motional degrees of freedom (or phonons) of
the ion chain. Following a recent approach by Katz and Monroe, we are introducing additional spin/boson
couplings using red and blue sideband transitions. As a first step, we have been simulating Jaynes-
Cummings lattices where we are able to prepare ground states of the theory using a mixture of discrete and
continuous variable gates. Moreover, in Phys. Rev. A 109 (2024) 5, we developed a continuous variable
quantum computing approach for simulations of the O(3) model in 1+1 dimensions. The O(3) model has a
global non-abelian symmetry group and is asymptotically free, making it an important stepping stone
toward simulations of QCD. Our results constitute the first quantum computing algorithm for a lattice model
with a non-abelian symmetry group. We demonstrated that real-time evolution can be achieved in the
infinite squeezing limit of the qumodes to the unit sphere. We also explored an alternative approach using
the Schwinger boson formulation, which was published in conference proceedings PoS LATTICE2023
(2024) 230. In addition, we demonstrated that ground and excited states of the theory can be prepared using
a non-unitary coupled cluster ansatz. This work was the key starting point for several follow-up publications
by my group, including simulations of quantum electrodynamics in 2+1 dimensions, the sine-Gordon
model, and ongoing work with the QSCOUT platform at Sandia National Laboratory. Lastly, we
investigated the use of quantum optimal control (QOC) for state preparation in lattice field theory
simulations on quantum computers, using the Schwinger model as a benchmark. Classical simulations show
that pulse-level QOC can substantially accelerate ground-state preparation compared to gate-based
approaches, with performance strongly dependent on device connectivity, coupling strengths, and noise.
These results highlight the potential of QOC as an efficient alternative for quantum simulations of
fundamental physics and motivate further hardware-informed studies. In addition, this work, will inform
state-of-the-art control of qumodes enabling their utilization for nuclear theory simulations.

AI / machine learning: In Phys.Rev.D 110 (2024) 1, 016030, we developed the first diffusion model to
simulate full collider events at the future Electron-Ion Collider. The use of generative models for simulating
collider events (and eventually QCD hadronization) faces several challenges, such as the sparsity of the
data, the presence of global and event-wide constraints, and steeply falling particle distributions near the
kinematic endpoints. We demonstrated that diffusion models can provide more accurate results than e.g.
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) that accurately capture relevant observables such as the
momentum distributions and correlations of particles, momentum sum rules and the leading electron
kinematics, which is of particular interest in electron-proton collisions. While the speed of the sampling
process, the enforcement of first-principles constraint, and the interpretability will need to be increased in
the future, our results represent a critical milestone for the completion of this project. Closely related to
these result, we published a diffusion model accelerated Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm
in Comput. Phys. Commun. 296, 109059 (2024). Here, the diffusion is used to combine global proposal
functions from the diffusion model and local updates from the regular MCMC chain. The diffusion model
is repeatedly trained on the produced samples of the MCMC chain. In several test scenarios, our algorithm

outperformed MCMC algorithms supplemented with normalizing flows by up to a factor of 10. This result
will be very helpful for the extraction of nonperturbative quantities relevant for the QCD hadronization
process in the future. Further, in JHEP 07 (2024) 257, we developed Jet Flow Networks (JFNs), a
permutation-invariant machine-learning framework based on reconstructed subjets, to systematically study
the role of infrared-collinear (IRC) unsafe information in jet classification. We showed that for quark-gluon
and Z-QCD jet tagging, JFNs achieve performance that is identical to particle-level classifiers without
relying on IRC-unsafe inputs, and that their performance is stable over a wide range of physically motivated
subjet parameters. These results clarify which features of jet structure drive classification performance and
improve the interpretability and robustness of ML tools in nuclear and particle physics.

2. Impact of the research funded under this award

3.1 What is the impact on the development of the principle discipline(s) of the
project?

Understanding the dynamics of the transition from elementary quarks and gluons to composite objects
known as hadrons is a fundamental question about the inner workings of our universe. The dynamical
process confinement of quarks and gluons is referred to as QCD hadronization. On the experimental side,
this question can be studied at high-energy collider experiments. However, since the detectors are far away
from the actual scattering process at microscopic length scales, theoretical tools need to be used to infer the
underlying physics mechanisms. This proposal will develop new theoretical tools to make progress in this
direction using first-principles calculations, AI / machine learning, and quantum computing. Aside from
answering fundamental questions about QCD hadronization, our current lack of understanding of the
relevant dynamics has severe implications on other areas in fundamental nuclear and particle physics. For
example, understanding cold and hot nuclear matter effects, the extraction of Standard Model parameters
as well as searches of new physics are often limited by our currently limited understanding of dynamical
processes associated with QCD hadronization. This project will therefore have a significant impact on
ongoing collider experiments at Jefferson Lab and Brookhaven National Laboratory as well as the future
Electron-Ion Collider. The adoption of machine learning and quantum computing techniques in
fundamental nuclear physics is still in the very early stages. As a result, the tools developed in this project
will have a broad range of applications within nuclear and particle physics and beyond.

3.2 What is the impact on other disciplines?

The tools developed in this project can have an impact beyond theoretical nuclear and particle physics. For
example, within this proposal I am developing novel generative AI tools to simulate the QCD hadronization
process. This requires the development of machine learning architectures (especially diffusion models) that
are suitable for the efficient generation of high-quality sparse data. The sparsity of the data sets is very
different compared to many commercial applications. However, other areas of research also work with
sparse data structures such as in biology and chemistry. The techniques developed in this project can
therefore be transferred to other areas. Moreover, there is overlap in the application of quantum computing
and simulations with systems that are of interest in condensed matter physics, photonics, quantum
information, AMO physics, biology etc. The development of efficient algorithms using both continuous
(qumodes) and discrete (qubits) and improving our understanding of quantum simulations will therefore
have applications in other areas of research.

3.3 Impact on human resources?

This project has already and will continue to provide opportunities for graduate and undergraduate students
to learn and improve their skills by working on different research projects. This community at ODU greatly
benefited from the resources provided by this project. This is not limited to my interactions with students
and collaborators, but for example, my student Jake Montgomery shares his experience with younger
students in his current role as the president of the ODU student organization. In addition, as a group, we
participated in the Remote Experience for Young Engineers and Scientists (REYES) program. This
program offers a unique research experience for undergraduate students aimed at inspiring the next
generation of scientists and increasing the representation of underrepresented groups in science. In addition,
it provides an opportunity for young researchers to get involved in different research groups. For example,
since the last time I participated in REYES, I have been working with an outstanding undergraduate student,
Siddhanth Bhowmick, whom I met through this program. He has been a co-author on a paper on pulse-level
thermal state preparation in the Schwinger model. He will start as a graduate student in quantum computing
at the University of Wisconsin, Madison. The postdoctoral researcher supported under this award, Dr. Jack
Araz, has successfully transitioned to a Presidential Fellow Position at City St. George’s University,
University of London. This position is expected to be converted to a lecturer position (equivalent to a tenure-
track Assistant Professor position) after three years.

3.5 Impact on society beyond science and technology?

The impact of this project goes beyond the boundaries of science and technology. By improving our
understanding of the fundamental interactions between elementary particles and emergent properties that
arise from them, this research contributes to public knowledge in this field through publications and
outreach programs. The interdisciplinary aspect of this project, where AI / machine learning and quantum
computing are developed in the context of fundamental physics can have a significant impact on other areas
of research. This includes for example chemistry, biology, and healthcare, which can drive future
technological innovations. New insights gained from this project can inform policy decisions and the

allocation of future research funding. The novelty of the techniques employed in this project results in a
relatively low barrier to entry, which is inspiring to the next generation of researchers and allows students
and early-career researchers to contribute to an exciting area of research.
