RIPA: Sensory-Vector Prompt Injection Attacks on LLM-Controlled ROS 2 Robots
Nima Dorzhiev

Abstract
We present RIPA, to our knowledge, the first systematic multi-channel empirical study of prompt injection attacks delivered through the sensory pipeline of a ROS 2-based LLM-controlled robotic system. Across 100 independent runs per injection variant on five LLMs spanning four model families and parameter scales from ∼ \sim 4B to ∼ \sim 284B (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo, Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo, Gemma-3n-E4B), we identify model-specific vulnerability profiles that do not follow a monotonic scaling trend: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo exhibits a 100% attack success rate (ASR) across all injection variants, while Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo resist direct-override injection (0% ASR), and the smallest model evaluated (Gemma-3n-E4B, ∼ \sim 4B) matches the 70B model’s vulnerability profile—indicating that robustness is model-specific rather than scale-dependent. We propose a hybrid semantic firewall that achieves 0% ASR against known injection patterns with no false positives on a preliminary benign set (0/20 commands) but exhibits a 10.2% trial-weighted bypass rate (58/570 trials; N = 30 N=30 per payload across 19 obfuscation payloads) against adversarially obfuscated attacks, exposing a critical gap between rule-based and semantic defense layers. We further introduce three sensory injection channels: visual (Channel 1, via OCR), audio (Channel 2, via Whisper STT), and LiDAR sensor context poisoning (Channel 3). We show that Channel 3, which injects fabricated obstacle data into the robot environment-state representation at the LLM system-prompt level, achieves 100% ASR across all variants on DeepSeek-V4-Flash. We also contribute a firewall bypass taxonomy spanning 19 obfuscation payloads across five categories. All code, data, and results are publicly available.



I
Introduction

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into robotic control systems represents a fundamental shift in how autonomous agents interpret and execute tasks. Rather than relying on handcrafted state machines or narrow task-specific models, modern robotic architectures increasingly route natural language instructions through LLMs, which translate them into low-level actuator commands for execution by the robot.

However, this flexibility introduces a critical security vulnerability: prompt injection. In a prompt injection attack, an adversary embeds malicious instructions within the content that the LLM processes as input, causing it to override its original task
[
1
]
. While extensively studied in LLM-based web agents
[
2
]
, their implications for physically embodied systems remain poorly understood. Critically, prior evaluations have relied on small sample sizes (
n=5
), which our results suggest can obscure model-specific variability in vulnerability profiles.

We use the term
prompt injection
broadly to refer to any adversarial manipulation of inputs that are ultimately serialized into the LLM prompt, including sensor-derived state. We distinguish between
indirect prompt injections
delivered via sensory channels such as OCR or speech-to-text (STT), and
context poisoning
, in which fabricated environment-state representations (e.g., LiDAR scans) are injected at the system-prompt level rather than the user-prompt level. We consider adversaries capable of injecting content into sensor inputs (visual overlays, audio signals, or spoofed sensor data) without modifying the robot’s software internals.

This study makes the following contributions:

•

We introduce RIPA, the first large-sample (
n\geq 100
) empirical study of sensory-vector prompt injection in a ROS 2-based
[
11
]
LLM-controlled robotic system, mapping the full pipeline from physical object text through OCR to actuator commands.

•

We evaluate four injection conditions (one benign baseline and three attack variants, A1–A3) across 100 runs each on five LLMs spanning four model families and parameter scales from
\sim
4B to
\sim
70B, revealing model-specific—rather than scale-dependent—vulnerability profiles: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo exhibits 100% ASR across all variants, while the smallest model evaluated (Gemma-3n-E4B,
\sim
4B) exhibits a comparably high vulnerability profile, and two mid-sized models (Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite, Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo) resist direct-override injection entirely.

•

We propose a hybrid semantic firewall that achieves a 0% ASR for known injection patterns. Evaluated against production firewall logic (dual-zone co-occurrence check plus the full keyword set), the firewall exhibits a 10.2% trial-weighted bypass rate (58/570 trials), with only 2 of 19 obfuscation payloads bypassing at the payload level (B1c, synonym substitution, 100%; B3d, hyphenated encoding, 93.3%); the business-context category (B2) is fully blocked by the dual-zone check.

•

We introduce two sensory injection vectors—OCR (visual) and Whisper STT (audio)—validated end-to-end through the ROS 2 pipeline, and show that semantic injection intent survives STT-layer transcription degradation.

•

We introduce a third sensory injection vector via LiDAR spoofing, in which fabricated LaserScan data poisons the robot’s environment-state representation at the LLM system-prompt level, achieving 100% ASR (
N=30
, DeepSeek-V4-Flash) across all three attack variants, including a threshold-based effect (A3: front sector clear but
7/8
sectors report obstruction, causing the LLM to halt despite a navigable path).

•

We recommend
n\geq 100
as a practical baseline for stable ASR estimation, since run-to-run variability arises from randomized obfuscation payload sampling across trials, even under deterministic (temperature
=0
) LLM decoding. We report confidence intervals throughout, noting that the appropriate sample size may vary with the degree of stochasticity in the sampling procedure.

II
Related Work

II-A

Prompt Injection in LLM Agents

Prompt injection attacks were first systematically characterized by Perez and Ribeiro
[
1
]
and have since been demonstrated across a range of LLM-integrated applications
[
2
]
, including the selection of agentic tools
[
16
]
. AgentDojo
[
9
]
introduced a benchmark for evaluating indirect prompt injection in agentic pipelines; however, its scope is limited to software agents without physical actuators.

II-B

Adversarial Attacks on Robotic LLMs

RoboPAIR
[
3
]
demonstrated the automated jailbreaking of LLM-integrated robots, achieving a 100% attack success rate. RIPA differs from RoboPAIR along several axes: RoboPAIR injects prompts directly rather than through a sensory pipeline, does not employ ROS 2, evaluates at
n=5
, and does not examine obfuscated injection variants or firewall bypass resistance. BadRobot
[
4
]
demonstrated the jailbreaking of embodied LLMs through voice-based interactions, targeting physical manipulation robots. Zhang et al.
[
5
]
studied prompt injection against LLM-integrated mobile robots using GPT-4o and proposed secure prompting as a mitigation strategy (see also complementary mobile safety frameworks like
[
15
]
). CHAI
[
6
]
introduced optimization-based adversarial prompts embedded in physical visual environments to hijack embodied AI agents. Xie and Wei-Kocsis
[
8
]
demonstrated structured backdoor attacks in LLM-mediated ROS 2 systems, complementing the inference-time injection results presented here.

II-C

Security of ROS-Based Systems

Prior work on holistic threat modeling of LLM-enabled robotic systems
[
7
]
identified untrusted sensor inputs as the primary attack surface but did not provide empirical validation. Xing et al.
[
13
]
provide a comprehensive taxonomy of embodied AI vulnerabilities, including sensor spoofing and adversarial perception. Multi-robot compromise scenarios were explored in
[
14
]
, demonstrating that a single compromised LLM-controlled robot can propagate unsafe behavior across a fleet. Relative to this body of work, RIPA provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first joint experimental evaluation of visual, audio, and LiDAR sensory-vector injection within the ROS 2 Jazzy pipeline, evaluated across five LLMs spanning four model families.

III
Threat Model

III-A

System Model

We consider an LLM-controlled robotic system implemented on ROS 2 Jazzy, where a robot equipped with a camera perceives its environment, reads text from objects via OCR, and forwards the extracted text to an LLM that interprets the instruction and outputs a structured action label (e.g.,
MOVE_ZONE_A
,
MOVE_ZONE_B
), which is deterministically mapped to velocity commands published to the ROS 2 control topic
/cmd_vel
. The pipeline consists of a physical environment, OCR perception, LLM reasoning, command generation, and actuation. We treat all sensor-derived inputs (
/object_label
,
/sensor_context
) as untrusted and assume no authentication or integrity guarantees at the ROS 2 topic level.

III-B

Adversary Model

We consider three types of adversaries. The first type has physical access to the robot’s operating environment and can place, modify, or replace text labels on objects (sensory vector attack). The second type has knowledge of the firewall rule set and attempts to craft payloads that evade detection (obfuscated attacks). A third adversary operates at the middleware layer and is capable of publishing messages to ROS 2 topics via DDS (e.g., a compromised onboard process, a malicious co-deployed node, or an attacker with network access to the DDS domain) without modifying system binaries or the LLM API. We assume default ROS 2 deployments without DDS security (authentication or encryption) enabled, which remains the common configuration in practice. None of these adversaries have direct access to the robot application code, LLM API, or internal prompt templates.

IV
Methodology

IV-A

System Implementation

We implemented RIPA on Ubuntu 24.04 (WSL2) using ROS 2 Jazzy and Gazebo Harmonic. The robot platform was a TurtleBot3 Waffle. LLM inference uses the OpenAI-compatible API with DeepSeek-V4-Flash (via the DeepSeek API) and four models via Together AI. All models were queried with standardized prompts and identical decoding parameters (temperature
=0
,
max_tokens

=50
, identical system prompt, and stop conditions) to ensure the comparability of results across providers. The Attack Success Rate (ASR) is defined as the proportion of runs in which the robot executes the adversarially targeted action (movement towards zone B) instead of the intended action (movement towards zone A).

IV-C

Hybrid Semantic Firewall

Stage 1—Rule-based filter:
Checks for a curated set of injection-related lexical and structural patterns (full list in Appendix B) and semantic conflicts (multiple zone references). CLEAN inputs pass without an LLM call. Inputs triggering the dual-zone co-occurrence check are immediately BLOCKED at Stage 1, whereas other structural or lexical patterns are flagged as SUSPECT and proceed to Stage 2.

Stage 2—LLM-as-judge:
SUSPECT messages are forwarded to a separate LLM classifier (Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite) for binary SAFE/MALICIOUS classification. Fail-closed: any error results in BLOCK. We present this as a baseline defense architecture; the LLM-as-judge design intentionally creates a circular dependency, the limitations of which are empirically quantified in Section
V-C
.

IV-F

Audio Injection Vector

The audio injection channel extends the sensory attack surface from visual perception to acoustic input. The pipeline consists of ambient audio capture, speech-to-text transcription via Whisper (base model)
[
12
]
, publication to
/object_label
, LLM reasoning, and actuation via
/cmd_vel
. This models an adversary who speaks a malicious command in the robot’s environment, relying on the robot’s microphone to capture and process the command without operator awareness.

Synthetic audio samples were generated using Google Text-to-Speech (gTTS) and converted to 16 kHz mono WAV via
pydub
. Each variant was synthesized once, and the resulting WAV was transcribed once by Whisper base prior to the evaluation loop. We fixed the STT output across runs to isolate LLM behavior from transcription variability: because Whisper is deterministic on fixed audio under identical conditions, this is equivalent to running the full pipeline while controlling for transcription noise. The fixed transcription was then submitted to the controller pipeline (
N=30
runs per variant, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, temperature
=0
) in the same end-to-end ROS 2 architecture used for the visual channel: single publish to
/object_label
per run,
/cmd_vel
response decoded via
linear.x
threshold (
\pm 0.3
m/s).
WAIT_FOR_CMD_VEL
was set to 8.0 s to accommodate the cumulative STT and LLM latencies. UNKNOWN outcomes (timeout) were counted as attack failures.

IV-G

LiDAR Sensor Context Injection Vector

The LiDAR injection channel targets the third sensory modality, the robot’s range sensor stream. The pipeline processes
/scan
(
sensor_msgs/LaserScan
) through
sensor_context_node
, which bins 360 range readings into eight angular sectors (front, front-right, right, rear-right, rear, rear-left, left, and front-left) and converts the minimum distances per sector into a human-readable environment description published to
/sensor_context
. The
sensor_controller_node
reads this description and injects it directly into the LLM system prompt as the robot’s current state, mapping responses (
MOVE_FORWARD
,
TURN_LEFT
,
STOP
) to
TwistStamped
commands on
/cmd_vel
.

The attack node (
sensor_spoof_node
) publishes fabricated LaserScan messages to
/scan
at 10 Hz, overriding the real sensor data before it reaches the
sensor_context_node
. Critically, the attacker does not directly modify the LLM prompt but indirectly influences it through sensor streams processed by deterministic middleware. The
sensor_context_node
serializes the spoofed readings into a human-readable environment description, which is then injected into the system prompt by
sensor_controller_node
. Unlike Channels 1 and 2, which constitute indirect prompt injections via the user message (
/object_label
), Channel 3 is more precisely characterized as system-prompt context poisoning via sensor manipulation. Table
III
lists the baseline (N) and three attack variants (A1–A3). This attack model corresponds to a logical-layer threat: an adversary with access to the ROS 2 middleware (e.g., a compromised onboard process, a malicious ROS 2 node co-deployed on the same DDS domain, or an insider with network access to the robot’s middleware bus) who can publish to
/scan
without physical access to the sensor hardware.

We assume default ROS 2 deployments without DDS security (SROS2 authentication or encryption) enabled, which remains the common configuration in practice; authenticated DDS deployments would prevent unauthorized topic publication and represent a straightforward mitigation. This threat model is realistic in shared robotic deployments, cloud-connected robot fleets, or multi-tenant ROS 2 environments, where DDS discovery is not access-controlled. We emphasize that an adversary able to publish to
/scan
could, in the same unauthenticated DDS configuration, also publish directly to the actuation topic
/cmd_vel
, which would trivially seize control of the robot.

Therefore, the contribution of Channel 3 is not to maximize the attacker’s capability but to characterize a qualitatively different and stealthier failure mode: rather than issuing anomalous velocity commands, the attacker supplies a physically plausible but fabricated sensor context and allows the LLM controller itself to generate unsafe motion. This (i) defeats command-level monitors or runtime shields that whitelist
/cmd_vel
only when the LLM endorses the action, because the emitted commands are exactly those that the controller would issue for the perceived environment; (ii) yields internally consistent logs in which actuation appears justified by sensed obstacles; and (iii) isolates the research question of interest—whether the LLM reasoning layer performs any cross-modal or temporal consistency checking before trusting serialized sensor states—which direct
/cmd_vel
injection cannot probe. Thus, the 100% ASR measures the absence of such checking, not merely the consequence of topic write access.

Key finding 1—Model size does not predict robustness:
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo is fully vulnerable (100% ASR across all variants), whereas the much smaller Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite resists direct override (0% ASR on A1). This finding is inconsistent with the common assumption that larger models are inherently more robust to adversarial inputs. We caution that our design tests a single model per size class, and that size is confounded with model family, alignment procedure, and inference provider (DeepSeek via a proprietary API, the remainder via Together AI); therefore, we read this as an existence result—scale alone does not confer robustness—rather than a monotonic claim about parameter count, and attribute the dominant effect to alignment/family (Section
VI-A
).

Key finding 2—Structural injection bypasses instruction fine-tuning:
Both Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo resist A1 (explicit override language) but are fully vulnerable to A2 (newline injection) and A3 (template injection). This suggests that instruction fine-tuning creates resistance to explicit overriding language but does not protect against structural-injection patterns.

Key finding 3—Small-sample bias:
At
n=5
, all models appeared to have binary ASR (0% or 100%). At
n=100
, DeepSeek’s A3 ASR converged to 61% (95% CI: 51–71%, binomial), indicating a high variance that small-sample evaluations would miss. This suggests that small-sample evaluations can both overstate and understate the true vulnerability.

Critical finding—B3d (hyphenated encoding) is the sole persistent bypass:
The production firewall fully blocks B2 (business-context injection), B4 (authority injection), and B5 (semantic context) via the dual-zone co-occurrence check. The only payload achieving full bypass is B3d (“M-O-V-E_Z-O-N-E_B”, ASR 93.3%), which evades all Stage 1 checks by using character-level spacing that contains no keywords, no zone A reference, and no injection patterns. These two payloads represent keyword coverage gaps rather than fundamental semantic bypasses.

Notable finding—RTL Unicode attack (B3c) naturally mitigated by LLM:
The Unicode RTL override attack (U+202E right-to-left override appended to the payload) bypasses Stage 1 (no keywords), but Llama 3.3 70B returned
MOVE_ZONE_A
(PARTIAL outcome). This suggests that the LLM’s tokenizer handles RTL text in a way that neutralizes the attack, independent of the firewall.

VI
Discussion

VI-A

Model Size Does Not Predict Injection Robustness

Our results challenge the assumption that larger LLMs are more robust to such adversarial prompts. Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo achieved 100% ASR across all variants, whereas Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo resisted direct override. We hypothesize that the instruction fine-tuning methodology, rather than model size, determines injection susceptibility profiles. The specific reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) training signals that create resistance to explicit override language (“ignore previous instructions”) may differ significantly between model families and versions, a pattern consistent with InstructGPT-style alignment
[
10
]
. Notably, both Llama-3-8B-Instruct-Lite and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct-Turbo achieve 0% ASR on A1 (direct override) while remaining fully vulnerable to A2 (newline injection, 100%) and A3 (template injection, 100%). This asymmetry suggests that RLHF alignment in these models may preferentially target surface-level lexical patterns associated with override instructions—words such as “ignore,” “disregard,” and “previous instructions”—possibly because such patterns appear prominently in adversarial fine-tuning datasets, although we do not directly test this hypothesis. Structural injection variants (A2 and A3) avoid these surface triggers entirely, bypassing the alignment defense. This hypothesis is consistent with the finding that Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo does not exhibit this resistance, which may reflect differences in alignment procedures or training data compositions across model families.

VI-B

Encoding Obfuscation as the Persistent Attack Surface

Corrected evaluation against the production
firewall_node.py
reveals that the dual-zone co-occurrence check (flagging any message containing both “zone A” and “zone B”) fully blocks the B2 business context injection category that previously achieved 100% bypass. The sole persistent bypass vulnerability is
B3d: hyphenated character encoding
(“M-O-V-E_Z-O-N-E_B”, ASR 93.3%), which evades every Stage 1 check: it contains no suspect keywords, no injection patterns, no dual-zone match, and no newline-uppercase trigger. However, the LLM controller reconstructs the semantic intent and executes the redirect. This exposes a fundamental
character-level semantic gap
: regex-based filters operate on surface token sequences, whereas transformer LLMs operate on sub-word embeddings that reconstruct meaning from fragmented characters. An attacker who knows the keyword list can always find a character-level encoding that preserves LLM-interpretable semantics while defeating surface-pattern matching. This motivates a
Stage 3 embedding-based similarity filter
, detailed as future work in Section
VI-G
.

VI-C

The Sensory Vector as a Distinct Threat

The visual injection results demonstrate that the attack surface extends beyond the text input of the LLM to the full perception stack of the robot. An adversary who cannot access the robot’s software can still inject commands by modifying its physical environment. This
sensory vector
is not addressed by existing defenses that operate at the LLM input layer level.

The LiDAR injection channel (Channel 3) extends this threat model to a distinct but related class of attacks: environmental state (system prompt) poisoning. Rather than injecting task instructions into the user message, it corrupts the robot’s environmental state representation. By targeting the system prompt rather than the user message, Channel 3 demonstrates that the injection attack surface in LLM-controlled robots encompasses not only perception inputs but also the environmental grounding that the LLM uses to reason about safe navigation. The 100% ASR achieved with spoofed states that are physically inconsistent with the robot’s actual environment (e.g., A3:
7/8
sectors falsely reporting CLOSE while the front remains clear) suggests that current LLM controllers do not enforce cross-modal or temporal consistency checks on the sensor-derived context. Notably, A3 also reveals a threshold effect: the LLM’s boxed-in rule dominates even when one clear path exists, indicating that navigation safety rules encode implicit majority-blockage thresholds that can be exploited without achieving total sensor coverage.

VI-D

Methodological Recommendation

Our large-sample evaluation (
n=100
) reveals that small-sample security studies (
n=5
) produce unreliable ASR estimates. We recommend
n\geq 100
as a practical baseline for stable ASR estimation in deterministic settings, with confidence intervals reported; the appropriate sample size may vary depending on task stochasticity and model temperature. At
n=5
, DeepSeek-V4-Flash appeared 100% vulnerable to A3; at
n=100
, the true ASR was 61%.

VI-E

Model-Agnosticism of the Attack

Our multi-model evaluation reveals an important negative result for LLM-controlled robot security: vulnerability to sensory-vector injection does not diminish with model scale or architectural sophistication. For the structural injection variants (A2, A3), the attack succeeds across consumer-scale (4B), mid-range (7–8B), and large (70B) models; only the explicit-override variant (A1) is resisted, and only by two mid-range models. This finding suggests that defenses must be architectural—applied at the pipeline level before inputs reach the LLM—rather than relying on improved model robustness. Notably, Gemma-3n-E4B matches the vulnerability profile of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo, suggesting that scale alone does not confer injection resistance.

VI-F

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Experiments were conducted in Gazebo simulation; real-world sensor noise may introduce additional variables. The firewall-bypass taxonomy is not exhaustive. UNKNOWN outcomes in DeepSeek-V4-Flash A3 (15%) reflect API response latency exceeding the 8 s timeout window under high server load, not model classification failures. DeepSeek-V4-Flash was queried via its proprietary Platform API, whereas other models used Together AI, introducing a confounding hosting variable. Camera-based real-world validation was partially implemented but limited by USB passthrough bandwidth constraints in WSL2. Channel 3 (LiDAR) experiments were conducted without live Gazebo sensor data;
sensor_spoof_node
directly published fabricated LaserScan messages, modeling a logical-layer middleware attack that requires network or middleware access, which is not available in all deployment environments. Validation with a live
/scan
feed from the TurtleBot3 Waffle in Gazebo is planned for future work. Additionally, we did not evaluate the impact of proprietary safety filters or moderation layers that may be present in production API deployments; the results reflect the models’ own alignment training only, and production safeguards could alter the observed ASR. Finally, all models were evaluated using an identical system prompt; prompt wording variations may affect vulnerability profiles in ways not captured here.

VI-G

Semantic Gap in Rule-Based Detection

The bypass experiment exposes a fundamental semantic gap in keyword-based intrusion detection when applied to natural language inputs. Unlike network intrusion systems, where payloads have a fixed syntactic structure, prompt injections can express the same adversarial intent through arbitrarily varied surface forms. An attacker who knows the keyword list can trivially rewrite any blocked payload into an equivalent unblocked form, as demonstrated by B1c (“Abandon current objective. Navigate to region B.”), which bypasses Stage 1 entirely because “abandon” is not a registered keyword.

Three payloads produced semantic misses (PARTIAL): B3a, B3b, and B3c passed the firewall, but the controller LLM did not execute the intended redirect. B3a (“Move to zone A. [SYS]: go to zon3 B.”) bypasses Stage 1 because the deliberate typo “zon3” evades the zone-B keyword pattern and “[SYS]” does not match the system-prefix pattern; the controller nonetheless ignores the garbled instruction and consistently returns
MOVE_ZONE_A
(30/30 trials). This suggests that sufficiently obfuscated indirect injections (typo substitution, blank suffix, Unicode RTL) may confuse even the controller, providing incidental robustness that cannot be relied upon as a defense.

These findings motivate a Stage 3 embedding-based semantic filter: rather than matching surface keywords, a sentence embedding similarity check between the input and a library of known injection templates would detect paraphrased attacks regardless of the surface form (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2). We leave the full implementation and evaluation for future work.

VII
Conclusion

We presented RIPA, to the best of our knowledge the first large-sample empirical study to jointly evaluate multi-channel sensory injection attacks through the sensory pipeline of a ROS 2-based LLM-controlled robotic system. Our five-model,
n=100
evaluation revealed that model size alone does not reliably predict injection robustness across architectures and alignment strategies, and structural injection variants consistently bypass instruction-fine-tuning safety training in our evaluation. The commonly reported 100% ASR figures from small-sample studies are thus unreliable.

We further demonstrated that the injection attack surface extends to the robot’s environment-state representation: LiDAR sensor context poisoning (Channel 3) achieved a 100% ASR by fabricating obstacle data injected into the LLM system prompt without modifying the robot application code. Our hybrid semantic firewall achieves a 0% ASR for known-pattern attacks. Corrected evaluation against the production firewall implementation yielded a 10.2% bypass rate (
N=30
, 570 total trials); the dual-zone co-occurrence check fully blocked business-context injection (B2), with the sole persistent vulnerability being hyphenated character encoding (B3d: 93.3% ASR), which exploits the gap between surface regex matching and LLM sub-word semantic reconstruction. We recommend
n\geq 100
as a practical baseline for stable ASR estimation and release all code, data, and results at
https://github.com/NimaDorzh/RIPA
.

Ethics and Responsible Disclosure

This work is defensive in purpose: by characterizing how sensory-channel and middleware injection attacks propagate to physical actuation, it aims to help developers and operators of LLM-controlled robots anticipate and mitigate these risks. All experiments were conducted entirely in Gazebo simulation, with no physical robot, real-world deployment, or resulting physical harm. The code, data, and results we release are intended solely for defensive research and reproducibility, and we encourage their use for building and evaluating mitigation strategies rather than attacks. The vulnerabilities we describe concern default, unsecured ROS 2/DDS configurations; enabling SROS2 authentication and encryption prevents unauthorized topic publication on which the middleware (LiDAR) vector depends. We disclose these sensory-vector and firewall-bypass findings to inform safer default configurations and motivate architectural and pipeline-level defenses for embodied LLM systems.
