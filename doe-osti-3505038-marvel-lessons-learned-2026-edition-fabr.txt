 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 The Microreactor Applications Research Validation and Evaluation
 (MARVEL) project is intended to be among the first U.S. advanced reactor
 demonstrations in four decades. By virtue of being sponsored directly by the U.S.
 Department of Energy (DOE), the reactor is expected to benefit the broader
 nuclear community by exercising design processes, safety reviews, and supply
 chains. This project is committed to publicly documenting key lessons learned
 along the way toward demonstration. Building on a 2025 report that focused on
 lessons learned from guard vessel fabrication and Primary Coolant Apparatus
 Test (PCAT) testing (Abou-Jaoude et al. 2025), this 2026 edition captures
 findings spanning structural fabrication, quality assurance (QA), reactivity
 control system (RCS) assembly, fuel fabrication and shipment, safety basis
 development, balance-of-plant design, and overall reactor design. A high-level
 summary of the key lessons learned in each area is provided in Table ES-1.

 The fabrication lessons center around the challenges of machining thin-
 walled, tight-tolerance 316H stainless steel components. Residual stresses
 introduced by solution-anneal/water-quench heat treatments repeatedly caused
 warping and out of tolerance (OOT) conditions in the core barrel and distribution
 plenum plates, ultimately requiring the core barrel to be refabricated via a lower-

 vii

temperature stress-relief approach. Weld distortion was similarly recurring and
was mitigated on later components by transitioning from GTAW-only welding to
a semi-automated GMAW process. Sourcing constraints for beryllium and 316H
stainless steel, along with grain-size discrepancies on procured pipe stock,
underscored the importance of early procurement, independent verification of
vendor material certifications, and close attention to specification flow-down.
 The QA lessons highlight the value of the project’s subcomponent-level
safety classification strategy and its use of CGD to expand the supplier base
while preserving safety assurance. Gaps in specifying required material test
methods and in verifying Code-required procurement clauses led to
nonconformances that required corrective testing mid-fabrication, reinforcing the
need for clear, complete procurement documentation that is reviewed prior to
purchasing.
 The RCS assembly lessons illustrate the difficulty of designing components
that must satisfy both precise operational control and fast, robust SCRAM
performance. Parametric analysis of the governing dynamic equations, rather
than reliance on finite element models alone, proved essential to finding design
solutions that were robust to real-world variability.
 The fuel fabrication and shipment lessons show that even small
compositional deviations, such as the absence of Er in the MARVEL fuel form,
can introduce unexpected manufacturing difficulties, and that grouping fuel types
into regulatory “families” can impose unintended, overly conservative
transportation limits.
 The safety basis lessons demonstrate that a commercial power reactor
licensing format (RG 1.206/RG 1.70) can be successfully tailored to a DOE
microreactor, and that NEI 18-04 RIPB concepts can be adapted qualitatively in
the absence of a full PRA.
 The design lessons highlight how early lock-in of long-lead components
(e.g., BeO reflector plates) constrained downstream flexibility and produced
thermal and sealing challenges at the primary-vessel-to-guard-vessel interface
that were not fully understood until after major design approvals. On the balance-
of-plant side specifically, the disruptive effect of continuing forward with the
reactor-mounted Stirling engine concept even in the aftermath of known
feasibility issues ultimately required a late-stage pivot to a NaK-based HES.
 Across all areas, a consistent theme emerges: issues that are identified early
through independent expert input, rigorous specification review, and analysis
performed ahead of design freeze are dramatically less costly and disruptive to
resolve than when those same issues are discovered post-fabrication or after
licensing commitments have already been made.

 MARVEL Lessons Learned – 2026 Edition
 Fuel Procurement, Quality Assurance, and Safety Basis
 1. INTRODUCTION
 This report is part of an annual series of documents that compile lessons learned for the Microreactor
Applications Research Validation and Evaluation (MARVEL) project, spanning everything from project
planning through reactor design, fabrication, construction, operation, and ultimate decommissioning. It
also serves as a narrative overview of the project’s evolution.
 The MARVEL project has three key objectives:
 1. Pathfinder: exercise U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and lab processes to train the nuclear
 workforce to support the broader advanced reactor industry. The project aims to help kickstart the
 supply chain for advanced reactors and share design features and lessons learned broadly with
 industry.
 2. Testbed: the reactor is intended to serve as a research and development (R&D) platform to test
 novel technologies with a nuclear reactor (e.g., advanced controls) as well as novel applications
 of nuclear technology (e.g., high-grade heat delivery).
 3. R&D use case: the complete design and data obtained from the reactor can be leveraged by
 various DOE and other agency programs to conduct novel R&D, including benchmarking novel
 codes, testing new safeguards/security paradigms, and training artificial intelligence.
 This report focuses primarily on objective 1 and is an attempt to serve the broader community by
disseminating insights gained from the project. Additional information on objectives 2 and 3 can be found
in the MARVEL Utilization report (Abou-Jaoude and Patterson 2024). This report is the third in a series
of “lessons learned” installments by the program. The previous two reports can be found in (Abou-Jaoude
and Patterson 2025; Patterson 2024).
 The MARVEL design consists of a beryllium- and graphite-reflected, hydrogen-moderated, solid-
fuel, loop-type reactor designed to produce up to 85 kW of thermal power at nominal hot full power, with
potentially up to 20 kW of electric output depending on interfacing systems. The core uses a 36-pin,
three-layer hexagonal patterned fuel arrangement, cooled by a sodium-potassium (NaK) loop, with
uranium-zirconium-hydride fuel—a Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics (TRIGA)-type fuel
similar to that used in university research reactors. Reactivity control combines active and passive
elements: four control drums (CDs) that rotate outside the core barrel and a central insertable absorber
(CIA) rod placed in the core’s central pin location, which is fully withdrawn during operation and inserted
during shutdown, supplemented by stationary reflectors positioned between the control drums and the
hydrogen/graphite reflector materials. The reactor is being installed at Idaho National Laboratory’s (INL)
Transient Reactor Test Facility (TREAT) as a dedicated test bed for microreactor technologies,
supporting research into remote monitoring, autonomous control, and microgrid integration, with fuel
loading and dry initial criticality targeted for the 2026 timeframe. Additional technical information on the
reactor characteristics and design evolution can be found in (Abou-Jaoude et al. 2026; Gerstner and
Arafat 2023; U.S. Department of Energy 2021).

 2.1. Machining Top and Bottom Plates
 The subcontracted machine shop reported difficulties in machining the top and bottom plates for the
distribution plenum. The design specified that these components be constructed from American Society of
Mechanical Engineers (ASME) SA-240 plate material; however, alternate material grades/forms could
have been specified for these components, such as ASME SA-182 or ASME SA-965 (F316H forging
spec). These forged material forms could have rendered a more stable part, but it is unclear whether the
initial processing technique would have significantly affected the residual stresses following a solution
annealing heat treatment at near 80% of the melting temperature. Quenching after annealing is required in
order to mitigate detrimental microstructure formation at intermediate temperatures. Rapid cooling in
thick stock can introduce significant temperature gradients and residual stresses that are initially defined
and constrained by the simple shape of the stock. The strength of the thick stock resists warping from
relatively symmetric residual stress fields produced by cooling paths for simple shapes (e.g., plates and
cylinders) that balance each other out. However, as material is removed during machining, stress-free
boundaries are introduced that can relieve residual stresses. When residual stresses no longer constrain
and balance each other out, they are relieved as elastic strain, warping the component. To mitigate this,
the part could have been dimensionally stabilized using 675°F–725°F stress relief cycles prior to and
possibly during the machining operations if necessary.

 2.2. Core Barrel: Stress Relief Following Solution Anneal
 During early design and fabrication meetings, there were several discussions on stress-relieving the
core barrel during machining operations. This was primarily due to the 0.120 in. wall thickness at the
reduced section of the core barrel and the need to dimensionally stabilize the part between rough and final
machining operations. It is known that austenitic stainless-steel work hardens significantly during
machining by inducing plastic deformation and residual stresses that exacerbate tool chatter and material
deflection (Davis 1989). This is especially significant for long, thin components, and the effect escalates
as the part increasingly deflects from its original dimensions. As the specification and design matured,
INL metallurgists determined that, because Section III of the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code
(BPVC) does not discuss stress relieving except to seemingly prohibit it in Article HBB-4212 and Table
HBB-I-14.1(a), Note (2)a, all heat treatments had to be performed at solution annealing temperatures.
Rough machining was performed on the first core barrel, producing a minor out of tolerance (OOT) with
respect to roundness. The subcontracted machine shop requested to heat treat the component to alleviate
the machining challenges that produced the OOT, and because of the heat treatment considerations
discussed above, the core barrel was solution annealed at 1,930°F and then water quenched. The high
temperature and rapid cooling warped the core barrel, exacerbating the OOT and introducing a slight bow
in the component. The rough machined dimensions of the core barrel only had 0.060 in. of excess
material remaining on the inside diameter (the outside diameter had already been machined to nominal),
and efforts to try and straighten the core barrel were unsuccessful, rendering the part unusable.
Additionally, the high‑temperature anneal produced a hard oxide scale on the exterior surface, making
post‑anneal machining difficult.

 2.3. Second Core Barrel Machining
 The machine shop that was selected for machining the core barrel signed up for the project with the
understanding that they could machine the entire length of the bore in a single operation. Due to lessons
learned from the first effort in regard to tool chatter on long tool fixturing, they then realized they would
have to access the part from both ends. This resulted in the machinist having to remove a portion of the
bottom head (a disc) to facilitate access. The disc required re-installation via unanticipated welding. This
added risk to the project due to potential weld distortion, misalignment, and repairs. The supplier should
have paid closer attention to the core barrel configuration and realized the part was incompatible with
their machining equipment. In retrospect, the lower end of the core barrel could also have been
constructed in such a way that the part was accessible from both ends.

 2.4. Weld Distortion
 Similar to the issues witnessed on the MARVEL guard vessel (Abou-Jaoude et al. 2025), the project
again experienced weld distortion on the primary coolant system (PCS). These concerns first manifested
when welding the bottom head to the first core barrel, then again on a weld repair performed on the
distribution plenum.

Figure 5. Weld shrinkage on the core-barrel-to-bottom-head weldment.

 When making two weld repairs on the circumferential joint of the distribution plenum, the welder
employed gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) for the repair and to fill in the cavity. In the absence of chill
blocks, heat sinks, or restraints, the bottom plate of the plenum “sank” almost 1/8 in. This led to
additional rework in building up the sunken area with weld metal, and thus required additional penetrant
testing (PT) and ultrasonic testing (UT). Weld repair should have been performed using the same method
utilized to create the original weld: a GTAW (TIG) root and cover pass followed by a gas metal arc
welding (GMAW) (MIG) fill. The GMAW process induces far less heat input per mass of deposited
material than does its GTAW counterpart.

 2.5. Weld Process Selection
 As referenced in a previous lessons learned document (Abou-Jaoude et al. 2025), it became apparent
that using an automated or even semi-automated GMAW welding process rendered better results than
solely relying on GTAW—especially in terms of minimizing weld distortion.
 The fabricator of the PCS was encouraged to develop a GMAW procedure for use on weld joints in
cases when the base metal thickness exceeded half an inch. The GMAW process relies on four basic
transfer modes to deposit filler metal from the electrode to the weld joint: short circuit transfer, globular
transfer, spray transfer, and pulse spray transfer. Each mode depends on the welding process, welding
power supply, and consumable involved, and each has its own distinct characteristics and applications.
The transfer mode is an essential variable per BPVC Section IX. The welding program at INL does not
favor (or even recognize) the short circuit and globular transfer modes; therefore, the fabricator developed
a pulse spray transfer GMAW process with assistance from the INL welding program.
 The GMAW process yields a higher deposition rate and is considerably faster than GTAW. As such,
more weld is deposited in a shorter amount of time and with less heat input. This proved vital when
creating the two circumferential weld joints that made up the distribution plenum. The material thickness
of the side wall measures 3/4 in., and these joints were only accessible from one side. Between the INL
supplier representative and the fabricator, it was determined that the root and hot pass on these joints

 7

would be performed using the GTAW process, and that the remaining fill and cover passes would be
completed using the GMAW process. Had the fabricator relied only on TIG welding, these joints would
have experienced inordinate weld distortion and shrinkage.

and GMAW fill (center and far right).

 2.6. MARVEL Beryllium Reflector Material Fabrication
 The MARVEL design incorporates two forms of beryllium. Beryllium metal surrounds the reactor
fuel within the core barrel, while beryllium oxide (BeO) is used in the fixed reflectors and CD
components located outside the core barrel. Both are key to providing the needed neutronics for
criticality, and beryllium in general is in high demand within the aerospace, defense, and medical
industries.
 The fact that only a limited number of beryllium suppliers are available creates intense competition
for materials. The project also observed that machining BeO into precise shapes is challenging, often
resulting in a high rejection rate due to chips, cracks, and scratches. These factors collectively contribute
to a critical bottleneck for the nuclear sector, affecting both material availability and manufacturability.
 Key lessons learned from the MARVEL project include the importance of placing beryllium orders
early to avoid schedule delays—assuming the reflector system design is mature enough. Leveraging the
Defense Priorities and Allocation System (DPAS) proved beneficial in securing priority for material
orders (note that the project realizes DPAS ratings may not be available to organizations outside of
government or military settings). The reflector design should incorporate extra mass allowances for BeO
components so as to help mitigate the impact of non-conformances such as cracks and chips, thereby
reducing the overall rejection rate during fabrication. Due to the inherent fragility of the BeO material, it
is prudent to order spare parts in order to reduce risk during reactor assembly by ensuring project
continuity and minimizing disruptions.
 As Figure 9 shows, the requirements on the manufacturing of Be-based material can also be
extremely strict. Most of the defects on the rejected pieces shown are imperceptible and challenging to
highlight. This emphasizes the tight tolerances that are often imposed on manufacturing that ideally
would have been accounted for earlier and thus potentially relaxed.

 2.7. Challenges with Tight Tolerances
 Designing a low-power micro-scale reactor core requires extremely tight hardware fabrication
tolerances, akin to a “Swiss watch” level of precision, in order to meet neutronic and thermal hydraulic
performance requirements, particularly in the core. For instance, fuel core components were required to
be fabricated with tolerances as fine as 0.0005 in. The limited space between the fuel elements further
compounded the complexity. The intricate design diverted focus and resources, presenting challenges
during fabrication.
 To streamline the fabrication process, it is important to sequence and prioritize design inputs
effectively. Choosing a fuel type and configuration that is both proven and readily available can further
simplify manufacturing and reduce risks. Additionally, maximizing the fuel core size should be
considered, as it allows for looser fabrication tolerances while still maintaining the required performance.
Overall, incorporating fabrication-based considerations early in the engineering and design phases might
have led to more optimal configurations that are more amenable to fabrication.
 2.8. Material Procurement
 The MARVEL project used various forms of 316H stainless steel: plates for large, flat components;
pipes for the core barrel and coolant pathways; and forgings for individual parts. This material was
chosen due to its superior elevated-temperature mechanical properties and creep resistance, meeting the
requirements of BPVC Section III, Division 5. However, sourcing of 316H presents challenges stemming
from low demand, uncommon chemistry, long lead times, and limited suppliers, often requiring
procurement from international vendors. Additionally, ensuring compliance with enhanced material,
fabrication, examination, and quality assurance (QA) requirements necessitates precise specification flow-
down to fabricators and vendors.
 To address these challenges, engineers should clearly identify and apply applicable specifications to
individual components, ensuring that drawings and specifications include all procurement subsection
requirements for improved vendor compliance. Material procurement vendors should be required to
submit data prior to the start of fabrication. Furthermore, to mitigate delays caused by long lead times,
materials should be ordered early and include extra inventory to accommodate potential rework needs.
 2.9. Material Grain Size Variability
 Per ASME SA-312 specifications, the MARVEL core barrel was fabricated from 12-in. 316H
stainless steel seamless pipe, requiring a grain size of 7 or coarser. While the Chinese mill’s material
certification reported a grain size of 7, confirmatory testing measured grain sizes of 1.5 and 2.5, all

 9

meeting the requirement. An engineering evaluation concluded that the larger grain size does not
compromise safety or material performance and affords sufficient design margin to ensure functionality.
 To mitigate future risks, Certified Material Test Reports (CMTRs) should be required, submitted, and
reviewed as part of vendor data requirements before fabrication in order to verify compliance with BPVC
standards. It is also recommended that foundry data be independently verified, especially from foreign
sources. Additionally, it must be recognized that elevated temperature applications may benefit from
coarser grain structures in terms of enhancing long-term creep resistance and material stability.
 2.10. Raw Material Types
 During the 316H stainless steel fabrication process, the ASME SA-240 plate used for the top and
bottom distribution plenum plates exhibited movement during machining as residual stresses were
released. Additionally, the subcontractor faced challenges in maintaining roundness and runout tolerances
with the thin-walled core barrel pipe. In contrast, forgings demonstrated better consistency in maintaining
their shape during machining.
 To mitigate machining challenges, consider using forged materials (SA-182/SA-965) when possible,
as their uniform properties reduce distortion and warping. Forgings also improve material density,
eliminate internal voids, and create a more homogeneous, fine-grained structure. For plate and pipe
applications, implement thermal stabilization processes to minimize residual stress and improve
machining stability.
 2.11. Radiography Design
 BPVC Section III, Division 5 requirements for pressure-retaining boundary welds demand rigorous
examinations, such as radiographic testing (RT) and/or UT for full-penetration butt welds. During
assembly operations, significant effort was required by the fabricator and third-party inspection agency to
sequence these weld examinations. This process involved numerous hold points and the physical
repositioning of components in order to access constrained or difficult-to-reach weld areas.
 To reduce inspection complexity and improve fabrication efficiency, consider minimizing the number
of penetrations in or through pressure vessels that require welds. Additionally, using larger raw stock
materials for feature machining could eliminate the need for certain components and associated welds
entirely.
 2.12. Sequence of Fabrication
 When the MARVEL primary coolant system project was initially awarded, BEA selected the four
downcomer piping spools as a DOE project milestone. The primary motive for this assembly selection
was that the supplier was in custody of all the necessary components to build the downcomers, and thus
there would be no delay for material procurement or qualification. Furthermore, the downcomer piping
spool was a mature design, and any potential design changes would be highly unlikely. To this end, it
seemed a safe and achievable notable outcome.
 However, as the project progressed, the MARVEL project team did not anticipate the challenges that
weld distortion would pose (as discussed in Section 2.4). Due to the extensive welding on the distribution
plenum, the project witnessed almost 5/16 in. of concavity on the plenum bottom plate and an equal
amount of convexity on the plenum top plate. This introduced several issues when fitting the downcomers
to the plenum weldment.

 In hindsight, these downcomers should have been fabricated piece by piece onto the fully welded

 3.1. Commercial Grade Dedication
 The nuclear industry utilizes CGD to accept commercial items for safety applications by providing
reasonable assurance that the items will perform their safety functions despite not having been produced
under nuclear quality assurance (NQA) standards. CGD helps expand the supplier base, reduce costs and
lead times, and maintain safety compliance.
 Several MARVEL components were designed and fabricated under the intent of BPVC Section III,
Division 5, though the project did not pursue an “N” stamp. Because Division 5 does not allow the use of
CGD for pressure‑boundary components, raw materials were procured from an BPVC Section III–
certified Material Organization and fabricated by an NQA‑1 supplier found on INL’s Qualified Suppliers
List. BEA provided oversight through a resident quality engineer acting as the authorized nuclear
inspector. This approach was documented in MARVEL’s Code of Record and approved by the design
authority.
 MARVEL’s safety‑related structures, systems, and components (SSCs) perform functions such as
shielding, confinement, reactivity control, heat removal, and core flow. The project employs a
subcomponent‑level safety classification strategy: rather than procuring entire systems under the
classification “safety‑related,” only the specific subcomponents that perform safety functions are
classified and procured as such. This targeted approach greatly reduces the scope and complexity of
safety procurements and allows for more efficient use of CGD.
 For several fabricated items, MARVEL used an NQA‑1-qualified fabricator from INL’s Qualified
Suppliers List. BEA applied its CGD process to develop technical evaluations that established critical
characteristics and acceptance criteria, and these were incorporated directly into the fabrication
specification. The fabricator then used those requirements to develop an acceptance plan for BEA review
and approval. Because BEA maintains the design basis and understands the technical and quality
requirements used to select critical characteristics, this approach is typically more efficient. It also avoids
providing full design documentation to the fabricator yet still enables them to use dedication methods
rather than rely solely on their own NQA‑1 suppliers.
 A key lesson learned from MARVEL is the importance of clearly communicating CGD requirements
to suppliers. Well‑structured procurement documents that integrate both technical and quality
requirements are essential, especially when suppliers are responsible for verification testing. Procurement
specifications must identify acceptable analytical methods, for example, distinguishing when X-ray
fluorescence (XRF) is adequate versus when optical emission spectroscopy (OES) or equivalency
equivalent (e.g. laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy [LIBS]) is required. XRF provides rapid,
non‑destructive alloy identification but may not detect light elements such as carbon or provide the
precision needed for certain safety‑critical determinations. OES offers more accurate and comprehensive
results but can be considered destructive. Clearly stating the required methods helps ensure consistent
testing and avoids qualification issues.
 Effective supplier communication throughout the procurement lifecycle is also critical, especially
when using CGD Method 2 (commercial grade survey) or Method 3 (source verification). A commercial
grade survey directly assesses a supplier’s processes to confirm critical characteristics are controlled by
their quality program. Acceptance relies primarily on a Certificate of Conformance, certifying all controls
and requirements were met. A source verification involves witnessing the work being conducted on the
procured item or service at the supplier’s facility to verify conformance of the critical characteristics.
Each of these methods rely on supply controls, and therefore, expectations must be clearly understood and
consistently applied.
 Alternatively, Method 1 may be used as a method of acceptance; this involves performing
tests/inspections or analyses upon or after receipt of the item to verify that critical characteristics meet
acceptance criteria. When Method 1 is used, planning for destructive testing is essential. Because

 12

destructive testing consumes the tested item, procurement engineers and designers must identify early
which items will require this testing and ensure that adequate spares are ordered to support both testing
and installation. This prevents schedule delays and ensures sufficient inventory.
 Finally, maintaining alignment between design and procurement is vital. Robust feedback loops
between design and procurement engineering ensure that CGD plans remain accurate as designs evolve.
Integrating procurement engineering early enables CGD strategies to be developed alongside the system
design, ensures that requirements are properly captured, and broadens sourcing options. This proactive
approach enhances the ability to use commercial components, strengthens supply chain flexibility, and
reduces overall cost and schedule risk.
 3.2. Procurement of BPVC Section III Materials
 The fabricator of the MARVEL primary coolant system purchased BPVC Section III materials from a
Material Organization; however, the pertinent material testing for BPVC Section III HB and HG class
components was not performed (i.e., grain structure analysis, PT, and UT). This resulted in a
nonconformance report and BEA requesting that additional testing be performed on the material while it
was still undergoing fabrication and/or machining operations. As the condition had progressed beyond
Code compliance (and the only remedy would be to procure new materials), BEA requested that
volumetric examination be performed, using UT and RT inspection methods to provide reasonable
assurance that the material was suitable for use on a reactor vessel. The drawings issued to the supplier all
notated that materials had to be purchased in accordance with BPVC Section III, NCA-3862, but they did
not “drill down” to the specific clauses for categories such as pressure-retaining materials and core
support materials. Subsection NCA only discusses “general requirements” for the procurement of
materials. However, the specification (SPC-3184) did specify the appropriate subsections for procurement
of materials, albeit in an obscure manner.
 This oversight could be attributed to the following error precursors:
 • Suppliers are unfamiliar with BPVC Section III.
 • BEA’s Vendor Data Schedule should have mandated that material procurement documentation be
 furnished as a submittal prior to purchasing.
 • Design drawings should have provided clarity for the appropriate subsection pertaining to the
 component being fabricated.
 • The design specification should have provided language that made the requirements more
 concise.
 3.3. Radiograph Accessibility/Design
 Accessibility for Code-compliant RT imaging was a challenge throughout the fabrication of the
distribution plenum. The configuration of the weldment did not permit 100% RT on multiple joints, let
alone RT shots from two different angles as required per BPVC Section III, Division 5, HBB-5210(b)(3).
In retrospect, the design phase of a reactor should include evaluation points to assess all pressure
boundary welds from a constructability standpoint and make sure all efforts are made to ensure adequate
access is available during assembly to perform RT inspections. Just as important, the assembly sequence
should be clearly discussed with the fabrication company to ensure access for RT inspections are factored
into their process.
 3.4. Grain Size on ASME SA-312 SMLS Pipe
 The material of construction for the core barrel was specified as Pipe, Ø12 Sch 160, 316H SST,
Seamless, ASME SA-312. This material originated from a Chinese manufacturer and was furnished by
DuBose (a Material Organization, as defined in BPVC Section III). As already stated, the CMTR from the

 13

Chinese mill reported a grain size of 7, whereas testing performed by DuBose and INL reported grain
sizes of 1.5 and 2.5, respectively.
 BPVC Section III, Division 5, Table HBB-U-1 recommends a grain size of 3 to 6. While these grain
sizes all met the “7 or coarser” requirement in ASTM A312, they fall outside the Non-Mandatory
recommendations of BPVC Section III, Division 5, Table HBB-U-1. Following a review of the MARVEL
design life and its intended startup and shutdown cycles, it was determined that sufficient margin existed
in the design to accept a coarser grain. (See the white paper “Grain Size Considerations for MARVEL
Core Barrel” [Crawford 2026]).
 A valuable lesson learned is the importance of sequencing procurement activities so that critical
documentation, such as CMTRs for pressure-retaining material, is required and reviewed prior to
fabrication, at a minimum. This would have allowed BEA engineers and QA groups to fully review
CMTRs so as to ensure compliance with Code requirements.

 The CIA actuator is configured like many industry control and shutdown rods, has minimal
resistances, and operates using reliable gravity. In this configuration, its operation and SCRAM functions
can be readily separated. During operation, the only resistances to the motor’s force are consistent linear
weight and mechanical friction (whereas no spring or damper exist), and during SCRAM, the motor force
influence falls away and the system is reduced to a free-falling device until it interfaces with the shock
absorber at the bottom of its travel. This made the design process and associated component sizing
activities relatively straightforward, so few unconventional lessons were learned during the CIA
design/analysis process.

 However, due to the CD system's configuration, all of the dynamic terms are present for both
operation and SCRAM functions. This results in a very complex system driven by several factors: the
heavy drum, many intermediate interfaces between the actuator and drum, and non-linearities in both the
environment and the components (e.g., the damper has a prominently non-linear profile). When first
sizing the components, it was consistently found that performing an adjustment to satisfy an operational
requirement would directly hinder the SCRAM requirements, and vice versa.
 Analyzing the system in light of its driving dynamic equation rather than embedding it in a finite
element model helped reveal how one adjustment influenced the system behavior; for example, a stiffer
spring would result in higher impact to the entire reactor during a SCRAM. However, many of the
parameters could only be theoretically estimated (e.g., the damper’s speed-torque profile was pulled from
a vendor data graphic, and the steel-on-graphite coefficient of friction was pulled from a similar graphite
grade rather than from the exact grade that was ultimately used).
 In 2024, the theoretically estimated parameters produced a design and prototype that was then
subjected to testing against the associated requirements. Greater bearing friction resistance than estimated
caused the SCRAM system to stop midway through its range of motion. When the condition was resolved
by increasing the torsion spring preload, the clutch would slip during operation, also at a location midway

 15

through the system’s range of motion. In response, the project employed a parametric study of the driving
equations, affording a more comprehensive view of the design solution space and initiating some
relatively minor adjustments to the design (e.g., a stronger clutch and torsion spring with more coils).
These adjustments significantly improved the design’s ability to accommodate variabilities—not only in
the utilized components but also throughout operation (e.g., changes in resistance as a result of
temperature influence).

 4.2. Reactivity Control System Manufacturing
 The RCS has several components with complex designs that must simultaneously accommodate
precise operation and competing need for robust SCRAM needs. Consequently, several parts were given
overall operating precision tolerances when, in fact, their SCRAM function did not require such precision.
A more thorough handoff from the engineer to the fabricators would have allowed each part and
dimension to be assessed from the perspective of both intended function and fabrication feasibility.
 Additionally, several flimsy parts—particularly those in the CD—were sandwiched between other
much heavier parts, and while they required tight tolerances to fit in the slots, their fragile makeup often
caused them to warp in space when removed from the milling machine. Thus, they did not satisfy the
drawing’s overall dimensions. A rigid reference representing the interfacing parts provided the solution,
and acceptance was determined by placing the part in the reference and then testing its function.
 4.3. Reactivity Control System Procurement
 Because the CD technology has seen minimal historical usage, especially as a coincident operation
and SCRAM device, few nuclear-grade versions of the designed parts exist. As such, it was appropriate to

 16

habitually identify the critical characteristics from an analysis so as to drive specs for commercial parts or
the application of CGD.
 On some occasions, it was easier to fabricate parts from quality raw materials rather than to try
dedicating a commercial item such as a screw. Other parts (e.g., dampers, springs, or clutches) were best
addressed via CGD procedures that focused solely on the critical characteristics.
 4.4. Reactivity Control System Assembly
 The system’s functional complexity also drove assembly considerations. Initial scoping assembly
efforts revealed that a simple bottom-to-top assembly, followed by a top-to-bottom tightening of all parts,
often resulted in the product being bound up and many of the parts being hard to access.
 It was found that the most reliable product assembly was achieved by progressively assembling
subparts of the system based on individual specific functions. The best progression was found to be
assembly of the structure, then the motion-enabling components, then the more sensitive measurement
components, in addition to progressively torquing the fasteners while also testing the system’s range of
motion.
 Detailed Supplemental Work Instructions proved to be a valuable framework for all systems. This
presented the system particularities and provided part inventory tables, a well-thought-out and tested
sequence of steps, links to component qualification procedures, and a summary torque table.

 5.1. Fuel Shipment
 The MARVEL fuel is uranium zirconium hydride (UZrH), more commonly referred to as TRIGA
fuel. TRIGA fuel is only certified for international air transportation in TN-BGC casks. Per historical
precedence, the program had originally expected and budgeted to transport at least three MARVEL
TRIGA elements per cask. A licensing evaluation was expected to confirm or potentially increase the
limit placed on the amount of fuel shipped. However, because the license submission grouped various
fuel types into “families,” the end result was a more conservative requirement on nearly all fuel types.
The MARVEL variant of the fuel contains 30 wt% uranium, leading to some of the most conservative
limits. As a result, only a single MARVEL fuel element per cask was licensed for transport at this stage,
which more than tripled the anticipated cost of shipping. This was found to be primarily driven by other
fuel types in the MARVEL “family” of fuel elements that imposed the more stringent requirement.
 While broader groupings of fuel types might simplify/standardize transportation requirements for
various fuels, thereby affording greater flexibility, they can also be limiting, as this scenario proved.
When applying for licensing certification for even well-known fuel (as is the case with TRIGA elements

 20

that have a wide range of different variants), one must be prudent in ensuring that the most bounding case
in a “fuel family” set does not impose overly conservative requirements on all the other sets.
 The MARVEL project experienced a challenge related to the certification of the fresh fuel shipping
cask. In accordance with annual updates for the shipping cask provided by TRIGA, all countries
potentially impacted during air transport are required to review and approve the certification. The revised
certification necessitated approval from both the United States and Canada, as the preferred carrier’s
flight path crossed Canadian airspace. Despite submission, Canadian authorities have held the
certification for over a year, with no clear prospects for approval. To address this delay, an alternative air
carrier that avoids Canadian airspace has been secured. However, this solution entails transporting fuel to
international airports located farther from the INL, thereby increasing ground transportation distances and
associated costs.
 5.2. Fuel Core Geometry
 To achieve the neutronic requirements necessary for reaching the target operating temperatures, the
MARVEL reactor employs a highly compact configuration of TRIGA fuel elements. This deliberate
arrangement is designed to minimize the quantity of fissile material required, thereby optimizing the
reactor’s footprint, efficiency, and safety. The spatial relationship between adjacent fuel rods is carefully
engineered, with a nominal minimum clad-to-clad gap of 2 mm. This dimension is critical for facilitating
the effective flow of the liquid sodium-potassium (NaK) alloy, which serves as the primary coolant. The
maintenance of this gap throughout sustained full-power, high-temperature operations is essential, as it
ensures unobstructed natural circulation of NaK. Any deviation from the prescribed spacing could result
in reduced coolant flow, leading to localized overheating and potential thermal stresses within the fuel
elements.
 The MARVEL fuel elements themselves are based on a modified TRIGA design, incorporating two
additional fuel meats and extending fuel stack. This modification increases the potential for axial
expansion, as well as bowing, particularly under full-power operational conditions. During fabrication,
the specification for fresh TRIGA elements allows for radial tolerances total runout that can accommodate
up to approximately 1 mm of bowing. Integrating these variables—thermal expansion, mechanical
bowing, and fabrication tolerances—into the design of the fuel core components presents significant
engineering challenges for preserving adequate coolant flow. To address these challenges, components
must be manufactured with exceptionally precise measurements, often down to 0.0005 in. (or 5/10,000
in.). Such stringent tolerances are necessary to ensure the structural integrity of the core, maintain the
correct fuel geometry, and allow for the dynamic effects of heat-induced expansion and mechanical
deformation during reactor operation. Sourcing precision fabrication services capable of meeting these
stringent tolerances is challenging.
 Similar geometry issues were encountered in the Primary Coolant Apparatus Test (PCAT) electrical
test assembly, as sufficient straightness could not be achieved for the simulated fuel elements. For the
PCAT assemblies, short sectional fuel spacers were eventually incorporated to avoid cladding contact, but
this solution could not be used in the reactor, as it required that spacer wire segments be welded to the
heater cladding—an approach that is unacceptable for the fuel element cladding.

 6.1. Safety Analysis Report and Regulatory Basis
 The 10 CFR 830 “safe harbor” for a DOE reactor Safety Analysis Report (SAR) is RG 1.70, which,
despite having been superseded by RG 1.206, pertains to commercial power reactors. Directly applying
both the format and content requirements from either RG to a DOE microreactor such as MARVEL
proved difficult. Developing the MARVEL SAR ultimately laid a chapter-by-chapter roadmap for how to

 23

leverage RG 1.206/RG 1.70 and apply to other microreactors. This again highlights how MARVEL is
helping develop blueprints that can be leveraged by the broader industry.
 Application of other standards (e.g., DOE-STD-3009-2014 for non-reactors, NUREG-1537, and
ANSI/ANS-15.21-2012 for non-power research reactors) presented different sets of unique challenges. To
maintain consistency with the base TREAT SAR, RG 1.206/RG 1.70 was chosen for the MARVEL SAR
format. This format is well known to both DOE and U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)
regulators and enables direct demonstration that a given microreactor meets the safety basis requirements
of 10 CFR 830. Other non-standard approaches were proposed, but in order to obtain rapid approval to
meet program objectives and achieve an accelerated timeline for demonstration, the use of RG 1.206/RG
1.70 was selected as the best licensing authorization pathway.
 However, this approach required that the content from each SAR chapter be specifically tailored to
MARVEL’s unique design. The resulting content of each MARVEL SAR chapter then necessitated
careful review of RG 1.70, NUREG-0800, NUREG-1537, and ANSI/ANS-15.21-2012, in addition to
licensing bases documents for other DOE and NRC reactors, to ensure that the MARVEL design
addressed all applicable regulatory requirements and identified all potential hazards and accidents. In
many cases, the chapters were developed independently and did not always repurpose useful content from
the aforementioned documents.
 Concurrently, it was desired in the early stages of the MARVEL project that NEI 18-04 Licensing
Modernization Project (LMP) concepts be implemented. Elements of the LMP risk-informed
performance-based (RIPB) approach were integrated into the MARVEL safety analysis process.
However, a probabilistic risk assessment (PRA) was not performed for MARVEL. Therefore, integrating
those elements required a unique qualitative approach and, when completed, in turn provided a unique
approach for demonstrating alignment with the 10 CFR 830 requirements for protecting workers and the
public.
 Finally, to meet the DOE-STD-1189-2016 requirements (U.S. Department of Energy 2016), key
safety-in-design considerations were integrated into the design early in the project to rely on passive
rather than active systems with software-based interventions whenever possible. In the safety analysis, the
MARVEL safety case was shown to provide suitable passive and inherent design features for preventing
accidents or limiting their progression, maintaining radioactive material confinement barrier integrity, and
preventing the postulated radiological consequences of a release during full-power operations.
 The process outlined in Figure 18 was adapted from the LMP to fit DOE regulatory requirements,
without the use of a quantitative PRA, by utilizing a more qualitative approach to risk analysis. (Note that
NEI 18-04 uses the term “licensing basis event” instead of “safety basis event” [SBE] as used in this
document.) This approach provides reasonable assurance of meeting the 10 CFR 830 requirements for
protecting workers and the public.

 6.2. Safety Analysis Implementation
 The following briefly summarizes the major safety analysis tasks reflected in Figure 18:
 Task 1: The MARVEL design that incorporates the aspects of safety in design is provided to support
the hazard evaluation and design-basis accident (DBA) analysis.
 Task 2: A systematic approach was used to identify initiating events (IEs) that challenge at-power
MARVEL plant operation and require successful mitigation to prevent the release of radionuclides or
hazardous material.
 Task 3: Fundamental safety functions (FSFs) were necessary to prevent the IEs identified in Task 2
from progressing to end states that could result in core damage, and the release of radioactive or
hazardous material were identified.

 25

 Task 4: Event sequences were qualitatively modeled to obtain an understanding of accident
progressions, the response of the SSCs used in performing the FSFs, and the sequence end states.
 Task 5: The full set of SBEs were examined to verify that the SSCs performing the FSFs are
sufficient to ensure that the evaluation guidelines (EGs) are met. For each of these safety SSCs, a decision
was made on whether or not it should be classified as a safety related (SR)-SSC.
 Task 6: Each SBE identified was mapped to a DBA that accounts for the FSF challenges represented
in the SBE sequence but assumes that the FSFs are performed exclusively by SR-SSCs. It also assumes
that all non-safety-related SSCs that perform these same FSFs are unavailable.
 Task 7: For each defined DBA, a deterministic transient safety analysis was performed to (1)
demonstrate EG compliance, (2) establish safety margins, and (3) define SR-SSC performance
requirements and operational limits. The DBA analysis will be documented in the MARVEL SAR,
Chapter 15, “Accident Analyses.”
 Task 8: Derivation of the MARVEL Technical Specifications will be documented in the MARVEL
SAR, Chapter 16, “Derivation of Technical Specifications.”
 Tasks 9, 11, 12: Documentation of the results of the analyses will be found in the MARVEL SAR and
in the Technical Specifications document submitted for approval by DOE.
 Task 10: This process is iterative and will be repeated as necessary.
 Additionally, MARVEL will be installed into the INL TREAT Facility Micro-Reactor Experiment
Cell (T-REXC). To assist in the higher-level DOE/INL effort to be a testbed for advanced reactor
designs/technologies, T-REXC equips the TREAT reactor building (MFC-720) north high-bay equipment
pit with SSCs so as to accommodate small microreactor designs (e.g., MARVEL) and associated
criticality tests. Among the T-REXC modifications are the addition of shielding, ventilation systems,
instrumentation and controls, fire protection systems, radiation monitoring, power systems, and seismic
sensors, as well as remote operator control of MARVEL from the TREAT reactor control room.
 To address the nuclear safety challenges entailed by having both MARVEL and TREAT in the same
reactor building, TREAT transient operations and MARVEL full-power operations will be carefully
coordinated so they do not occur simultaneously yet still allow both their missions to continue. The
impacts of MARVEL hazards and operations on TREAT SR-SSCs, in addition to the impacts of TREAT
transient test hazards and operations on MARVEL SR-SSCs, are fully evaluated in the MARVEL hazards
analysis, as discussed in later sections of this report. The fact that MARVEL and TREAT operations will
not occur simultaneously reduces the hazards to personnel working in the TREAT building. The TREAT
building will be evacuated during TREAT and MARVEL operations. MARVEL operators will be located
in the TREAT control room, approximately 770 m from the reactor building, further reducing risk to
personnel.
 6.3. Alignment with NEI 18-04
 In conclusion, the elements of the MARVEL safety-in-design and defense-in-depth strategies, along
with the implementation of NEI 18-04 RIPB methods in the safety analysis process, have demonstrated
that the MARVEL reactor can be operated while meeting the overall regulatory requirements of 10 CFR
830 for protection of workers and the public.
 The qualitative MARVEL hazard evaluation meets the intent of NEI 18-04 as follows:
 • Risk-informed: Although a PRA was not performed, the MARVEL hazard evaluation provides a
 qualitative approach to risk assessment—one that provides reasonable assurance of protection of
 workers and the public.

 26

 • Performance-based: The MARVEL hazard evaluation affords simplified analysis of the
 performance requirements for the SSCs utilized in the FSFs necessary for preventing IEs from
 progressing into sequences that could result in core damage and radiological or non-radiological

 The MARVEL hazard and accident evaluations demonstrate that the MARVEL design will provide
suitable passive and inherent design features to prevent accidents, limit accident progression, maintain
radioactive material confinement integrity, and mitigate the radiological consequences of a release. Safety
margins are provided to account for uncertainties in the analyses and in human and SSC performance.
The proposed MARVEL design can accommodate various DBA and beyond-DBA initiators without
producing conditions that might lead to a severe accident. The MARVEL design’s passive inherent

DBA and beyond-DBA sequences, limiting accident consequences without involving active safety
systems or operator actions.

 7.1. Removal of Reactor-Mounted Stirling Engines
 The original MARVEL balance-of-plant system involved the use of four commercially available
Stirling engine generators. The MARVEL plenum design provided four IHX wells, into which the
combustion heat exchangers would be inserted. The wells would be filled with a liquid-metal heat transfer
fluid that would circulate inside them, while the primary coolant flowed downward around the wells.

Figure 19. Original IHX and Stirling engine design.

 This original concept ultimately proved unworkable for a number of reasons (e.g., intolerance to the
high radiation dose, excessive vibration, lack of shutdown capability). Most of these issues were at least
partially understood from the beginning of the design process, but it was assumed they could be overcome
as the design progressed. At the time, the MARVEL project team determined to continue with the design
concept as conceived and to address these issues later in the design process. Some testing of the liquid-
metal thermal coupling was performed successfully via a single-engine test system. PCAT, an electrically
heated test loop that simulated the configuration of MARVEL’s primary coolant system, experienced
significant schedule delay and was not ready for testing until well after the MARVEL design had been
approved. Operation of multiple Stirling engines in the PCAT revealed significant vibration when
multiple engines were operated in a loop installed in a steel support structure. It was identified that the
engines could not survive for any length of time in this configuration. By the time this result was made
known, the PCS design was completed and approved, a preliminary Documented Safety Analysis (PDSA)
had been developed, and fabrication contracts for the PCS had been released.
 Due to the state of design and fabrication, it was determined that any alternative solution had to be
implemented without substantially altering the PCS configuration. The solution that was ultimately
chosen was to replace the reactor-mounted Stirling engine generators with heat exchangers that would
circulate NaK alloy. The NaK alloy would extract heat from the PCS and deliver it to a nearby location

 28

further away from the reactor. There, it could be used to generate power or supply user facilities as high-
grade heat.
 The Stirling engine design was highly compact and required very little equipment mounted away
from the reactor. The NaK circulation system was considerably larger, taking up much more space both in
the T-REXC pit and on the TREAT operating floor. This required significant changes not only to the
reactor but also to the T-REXC design. Because the magnitude of the issues with the reactor-mounted
Stirling engine concept was not recognized and mitigated earlier in the design process, extensive changes
had to be made to already-approved designs, and limitations were imposed on the allowable changes.
These changes were much more expensive and disruptive than if they had been addressed earlier, and the
resulting limitations made those changes more complex and potentially less efficient than they would
have been otherwise. It is therefore highly recommended that designs should not be declared complete
until necessary testing to verify design assumptions is also complete. To declare a design complete prior
to receipt of important test data creates a significant cost and schedule risk and could invalidate the basic
design configuration.
 7.2. Intermediate Heat Exchangers – Plenum Design
 The MARVEL IHX well configuration presents some very significant challenges in terms of
extracting heat from the MARVEL primary coolant. The heat exchange wells severely limit the system’s
heat transfer area. In addition, the design requirements for the reactor required the use of a double wall
between the primary and secondary NaK. This double wall design increases the thermal resistance of the
boundary between the primary and secondary NaK and requires application of a thermal bond compound
that is still undergoing testing and development.

Figure 20. New IHX insert design.

 Ultimately, the IHX well configuration of the plenum is very inefficient, and it significantly increases
the temperature differential between the primary and secondary NaK at any given power. This
temperature differential significantly reduces the efficiency of many potential power-generation solutions
(e.g., Stirling or Brayton cycle engines).

 7.3. Intermediate Heat Exchanger System – Upper Confinement
 System
 The original design of the MARVEL reactor included an upper confinement system (UCS), which
was essentially an enclosed “top hat” that covered the top of the reactor. Its principal function was to
provide a ventilated area for keeping the Stirling engines cool, as each engine incorporated a water-
cooling jacket and could not survive the environment at the top of the reactor plenum in the event no
cooling was available.

Figure 21. MARVEL UCS.

 The NaK piping temperature ranges from ambient (~25°C) to around 500°C. Due to the space
limitations in the T-REXC pit, the routing of the IHX NaK piping must be rather complex in order to
provide sufficient flexibility to withstand thermal expansion. Routing this piping through and around the
UCS was extremely difficult, and effective sealing of the confinement would be quite impractical.
 Complicating the design and routing of the NaK piping was necessary to provide secondary
confinement for the NaK system inside the T-REXC pit. While the NaK piping itself is relatively small
(1-in. schedule pipe), it is surrounded by a 2-in. schedule pipe that provides the secondary confinement in
the pit area. Coupled with the thermal insulation necessary to reduce heat loss from the piping, the overall
pipe size is relatively large.

Figure 22. Reactor top without the UCS.

 Ultimately, since the primary function of the UCS was no longer required, it was determined that the
UCS should be removed from the MARVEL reactor design. There was some initial concern about its
removal due to potential hard-to-foresee consequences as well as the necessity of re-evaluating the
nuclear safety aspects of its secondary functions (including limiting smoke/contamination spread during a
postulated primary coolant breach). After further evaluation of these issues, it was determined that the
consequences of removing the UCS would not create significant nuclear or industrial safety issues, and
removal was therefore acceptable.
 7.4. NaK Skid and Piping – Pipe Routing

cooling water systems for the Stirling engines. As the heat extraction system (HES) proceeded to be
developed, this concept became increasingly impractical. One of the main issues was the need for routing
the hot (~500°C) NaK piping through the shield block in the trench, which was filled with water-

 To maximize its reactivity, the system utilizes external BeO reflectors mounted outside the primary
core barrel. In addition, the wall of the core barrel is quite thin to minimize absorption of neutrons by the
stainless steel and to bring the external reflector as close as practical to the core. These factors, coupled

 34

with the overall size restrictions necessary to fit the reactor into the T-REXC facility, make the internal
volume of the primary system quite small. The guard vessel, which surrounds the PCS and is intended to
mitigate primary coolant leakage in the event of a loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA), must be large enough
to surround it entirely along with the external reflectors. The guard vessel is therefore relatively large
compared to the PCS.
 Due to the exceptionally long lead times for fabricating the BeO reflector plates, the plate design was
fixed early in the reactor design process, and long-lead procurement was initiated early on as well. By the
time these design impacts were fully understood, the fixed geometry of the BeO plates made it
impractical to significantly alter the geometries of the core barrel and reflector. These geometries created
the following significant design challenges:
 • The thin primary core barrel wall in the core region is weak compared to the parts of the core
 barrel above and below the core region. This portion of the core barrel must be machined to
 relatively tight tolerances, leaving little tolerance for deviations or defects.
 • The external reflectors are positioned close to the outer surface of the core barrel in the core
 region. The core barrel above and below the core region has a larger outside diameter to provide
 sufficient lateral support of the reflector structure so as to accommodate seismic loads. This
 geometry makes the reflector structures significantly more difficult to assemble than if the core
 barrel had a consistent outer diameter.
 • The relatively large inside volume of the guard vessel opens up the possibility of a primary leak
 uncovering the core in the event that the pressure in the PCS sufficiently exceeds the guard vessel
 pressure. This makes the pressure differential between the primary cover gas and the guard vessel
 gas a safety-significant parameter for reactor operation.
 8.2. Primary Vessel Top Corner
 The MARVEL primary vessel rests on a support ring inside the guard vessel. To seal the guard
vessel, a skirt on the top of the guard vessel extends up and welds to a U-shaped feature at the top corner
of the primary vessel distribution plenum.

Figure 27. Interface between primary coolant vessel and guard vessel, showing primary vessel “top
corner.”

 35

 Because the guard vessel does not extend above the top surface of the primary coolant vessel, the
primary coolant level is maintained below the top surface in order to maintain an argon gap between that
surface and the primary coolant. This gap is necessary to meet a design requirement mandating that there
be two barriers between the reactive primary coolant (NaK) and air.
 Full thermal/stress analysis of this geometry required the use of extensive finite element analysis,
which took several months to complete. To meet design milestones, this analysis was not completed until
late in the design process, after the 90% design review approval. The analysis identified poor heat transfer
from the PCS into the guard vessel. The argon gap between the primary coolant and the top of the
distribution plenum also significantly reduces heat transfer into the top plate of the plenum. During heatup
of the PCS, the temperature of the guard vessel, skirt, and primary vessel top plate significantly lag
behind that of the main portion of the primary vessel plenum. This temperature difference between the
primary vessel and the guard vessel and skirt creates significant stresses in the top corner where the guard
vessel skirt attaches to the primary coolant vessel. These stresses are high enough to limit the system’s
allowable heatup rate to a very low value.
 The MARVEL reactor is intended for short-duration runs, since its operation interferes with TREAT
reactor operations. The heatup rate limits add at least 1 or 2 days to each reactor run (there are cooldown
limits associated with this geometry as well, but they are not as restrictive). They also complicate the
operation of the reactor, as reactor operators must carefully monitor the differential temperature between
the reactor and the guard vessel skirt area in order to avoid exceeding the design stress limits.
 8.3. Control Drum Shaft Seals
 The MARVEL guard vessel is sealed and filled with argon gas to prevent NaK from reacting with air
and moisture in the event of leakage of the PCS. Because the reactor CDs are located inside the guard
vessel, rotating seals are necessary to minimize argon leakage from the guard vessel during reactor
operation.

Figure 28. CD seal assembly.

 When these seals were originally being designed, they were required to operate at 500°C with
absolute minimum seal drag in order to avoid interfering with the operation of the CDs. Seal leakage was
to be minimized as much as practical, but no specific leakage requirement was identified. Much later,
during development of the pressure control requirements for the reactor and guard vessel, the guard vessel
pressure became a safety-significant parameter, as too low of a pressure could allow the core to become
uncovered in the event of a LOCA. The pressure control concept developed for the primary and guard
vessels was to set the initial pressures (if required) prior to reactor startup. This would maintain the design

 36

pressure bands during reactor heatup and cooldown, without control system or operator intervention
during a reactor run. Implicit in this pressure control scheme was the assumption that both PCS and guard
vessel would be near-hermetically sealed (leakage of less than 10-4 atm/cc-sec) during operation.
 During the 90% design review, it was identified that the seals would probably be unable to meet the
required seal leakage specification. Further design efforts did not identify any seal capable of meeting the
required leak specification while still meeting the temperature and seal drag limitations. Analysis of the
pressure control requirements, using theoretical leak rate estimates provided by the manufacturer,
revealed that the leak rates should be low enough to allow the pressure in the guard vessel to remain
sufficiently high to prevent uncovering of the core during a LOCA for a sufficient amount of time in order
to support anticipated short reactor runs. Leak testing of the procured seals to verify acceptability has
been completed successfully. It is noted that the seals cannot be easily replaced after reactor operation,
due to the anticipated radiation levels at this location, and that any degradation in the seals during
operation will pose a significant issue.
 During approximately the first 2 years of the MARVEL design effort, the design requirements were
maintained in an online system. While the online system was not necessarily an issue, the project
maintained no formal administrative control over changing requirements. No formal design requirements
documents were issued until spring 2023. It appears that leakage of the seals was not identified as a
potential issue at the same time the leakage requirements for the guard vessel were identified. Note that
the manufacturer does not provide seal leakage specifications for these seals, though they were able to
calculate estimated values upon request.
 9. CONCLUSIONS
 The purpose of a lessons learned report is not to assign blame for the difficulties encountered during a
first-of-a-kind reactor development effort, but to ensure that the insight gained through those difficulties
is preserved and made available to the broader advanced reactor community. The challenges documented
in this report, ranging from residual-stress-driven fabrication distortion, to procurement and material
qualification gaps, to the late-stage redesign of the MARVEL HES, are not unique to MARVEL. They are
representative of first-of-a-kind engineering, fabrication, and licensing challenges that any organization
pursuing rapid advanced reactor deployment is likely to encounter.
 Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this edition of the MARVEL lessons learned report. First,
early and rigorous engagement with fabricators, machine shops, and material suppliers, particularly
around specification clarity and vendor data requirements, consistently prevented or reduced the severity
of downstream nonconformances. Second, design decisions made to accommodate long-lead procurement
items such as the BeO reflector plates can lock in geometric or thermal constraints well before their full
downstream implications are understood; earlier, more thorough front-end analysis would have surfaced
several of these issues at a time when corrective action remained practical. Third, when foundational
design assumptions are later found to be unworkable, as was the case with the reactor-mounted Stirling
engine concept, the cost and schedule impact of a pivot grows substantially the longer the underlying
issue goes unaddressed. Fourth, adapting existing regulatory and quality frameworks—such as RG
1.206/RG 1.70 for the safety basis and NQA-1/CGD for procurement—to the specific context of a
compact DOE microreactor proved workable but required deliberate tailoring rather than direct,
unmodified application.
 The MARVEL project team intends to continue documenting lessons learned as the project advances
toward criticality and full-power operation. This will include insights from installation, commissioning,
and eventual operation within T-REXC. It is the project’s hope that these lessons, shared openly, will help
streamline the path for the next generation of U.S. advanced and microreactor demonstrations.
