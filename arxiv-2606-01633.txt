Introduction

Euler’s totient function
\varphi(n)
giving the number of integers
k\in[1,n]
such that
k
and
n
are coprime is one of the most
fundamental arithmetic functions. The importance of the Euler totient function inspires research on generalizations of the form

\varphi_{k}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[1,n]\\
(a,n)=1\end{subarray}}a^{k},

(1)

as in the work of Büyükaşik et al.
[
2
]
, who built upon the previous work of Singh
[
4
]

concerning (
1
). Functions of the form shown in (
1
) are noted in classic texts in both advanced
combinatorics
[
3
, p. 199]
and analytic number theory
[
1
, p. 48]
. In this latter text, Apostol noted
the relations among

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{\varphi_{k}(d)}{d^{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1^{k}+2^{k}+\cdots+n^{k}}{n^{k}},

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}n\varphi(n)\ \text{for $n>1$, and}

(2)

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}n^{2}\varphi(n)+\frac{n}{6}\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p)\ \text{for $n>1$.}

In this paper, we prove a conjecture from Büyükaşik et al. concerning (
1
). It appears that this conjecture has
remained open, prior to our work.

Adopting notation from Büyükaşik et al.
[
2
]
, we write

\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{k\geq s:\forall n\geq 1\ \varphi_{s}(n)\mid\varphi_{k}(n)\},

for a fixed and nonnegative integer
s
. Büyükaşik et al. proved that

\mathcal{D}_{s}
is finite for any fixed integer
s\geq 0
,
and conjectured that
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{1,3,15\}

and provided computations to support this conjecture. We prove this conjecture in the below section,
using an argument derived from our many interactions with GPT-5.5 Pro.

2
A full solution

Since Büyükaşik et al.
[
2
, Theorem 2(2)]
proved that
\{1,3,15\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}

(noting that it is immediate that
s\in\mathcal{D}_{s}
), it remains to prove the reverse inclusion. To begin with, we recall the definition
of the sequence of Bernoulli numbers, which may be defined via the generating function relation
\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}x^{n}}{n!}
. We also require the use of the von Staudt–Clausen theorem, giving us that: For every even integer
m\geq 2
, the denominator of
B_{m}
is
\prod_{(p-1)\mid m}p
.

Letting
k>1
be an odd integer, and letting
v
be a positive integer, a key to our construction is given by Bernoulli sums of the form

C_{k}(v)=\frac{2}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}(1-2j)^{v}.

(3)

Similarly,
Bernoulli polynomials
may be defined so that

B_{N}(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\binom{N}{r}B_{r}x^{N-r}

(4)

and are required for the purposes of our proof of the following key lemma. Our construction also requires the use of
Stirling
numbers of the second kind
, which may be defined so that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}j^{n}
.
By writing
D
in place of the differential operator
x\frac{d}{dx}
, the relation

D^{m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{\ell}x^{\ell}\frac{d^{\ell}}{dx^{\ell}}

(5)

is required for our purposes and can be found (in an equivalent form)
in Comtet’s text
[
3
, p. 220]
.

Lemma 1
.

For
k>1
odd, suppose that
C_{k}(v)
is an integer for every
v\geq 1
. Then, for every even integer
h\in[2,k-1]
,
we have that

2\frac{k!}{h!}B_{h}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Write
N=k+1
, with
N\geq 4
being even. For an integer

s\in\left[1,\frac{N-2}{2}\right],

(6)

we define

T_{N,s}=T_{s}=2\frac{(N-1)!}{(N-2s)!}B_{N-2s}.

(7)

Since every even integer
h\in[2,k-1]
can be written uniquely in the form

h=N-2s
for
N
and
s
as specified,
it remains to prove that
T_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Now, define

F_{N}(x)=x^{1-N}\big(B_{N}(x)-B_{N}\big),

(8)

recalling the definition of Bernoulli polynomials in (
4
). We claim that

C_{N-1}(2s)=\frac{2}{N}\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1).

(9)

To prove the relation in (
9
), we begin by manipulating the formula in
(
4
) to obtain that

x^{1-N}B_{N}(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\binom{N}{r}B_{r}x^{1-r},

(10)

so that (
10
) gives us that

D^{2s}\big(x^{1-N}B_{N}(x)\big)\Big|_{x=1}=\sum_{r=0}^{N}\binom{N}{r}B_{r}(1-r)^{2s}.

(11)

From the vanishing of the
r=1
case of the summand on the right of (
11
) together with the vanishing of Bernoulli
numbers with odd indices greater than
1
, we find that

D^{2s}\big(x^{1-N}B_{N}(x)\big)\Big|_{x=1}=\frac{N}{2}C_{N-1}(2s)+B_{N}(1-N)^{2s},

(12)

so that an application of the definition of
F_{N}(x)
in (
8
)
via (
12
) gives us an equivalent version of (
9
).

Now, we rewrite
F_{N}(x)
, as defined in (
8
), by setting
g(x)=x^{1-N}
and
H(x)=B_{N}(x)-B_{N}
, with
F_{N}(x)=g(x)H(x)
. Observe that
B_{N}(1)=B_{N}
and
H(1)=0
.
The product rule for derivatives then gives us that

F_{N}^{(\ell)}(1)=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{r}g^{(\ell-r)}(1)B_{N}^{(r)}(1),

(13)

noting that we obtain the vanishing of
\binom{\ell}{r}g^{(\ell-r)}(1)H^{(r)}(1)

for the
r=0
case.
Observe that
g^{(\ell-r)}(1)\in\mathbb{Z}
, recalling the definition of
g(x)
as an integer power of
x
.
From the definition of Bernoulli polynomials in (
4
), one may verify that

B_{N}^{(r)}(x)=\frac{N!}{(N-r)!}B_{N-r}(x)

(14)

for positive integers
r\leq N
, and we specialize (
14
) to

B_{N}^{(r)}(1)=\frac{N!}{(N-r)!}B_{N-r}(1).

(15)

According to the product rule expansion in (
13
), we have that sum in this product rule is restricted to indices
r\in[1,\ell]
. In view of the identity in (
9
), which involves the application of
D^{2s}
to
F_{N}(1)
, it is implicit that
\ell\leq 2s
, and we recall the bounds on
s
in (
6
), giving us that
2\leq N-r
. Now, if
r
is odd, then
N-r\geq 2
is odd.
So, if
r
is odd, then
B_{N-r}(1)=B_{N-r}=0
. So, from (
15
), we have that if
r
is odd,
then
B_{N}^{(r)}(1)
vanishes.
So, for an even value
r=2i
,
we find that

\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(2i)}(1)=T_{i},

(16)

recalling the definition in (
7
). An application of
the expression in (
9
) of
C_{N-1}(2s)
in terms of
\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1)

together with
the classical Stirling number identity in (
5
)
give us that

\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1)=F_{N}^{(2s)}(1)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{2s-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2s}{\ell}F_{N}^{(\ell)}(1).

(17)

Through an application of (
13
) to (
17
), we obtain

\frac{2}{N}\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1)=\sum_{r=1}^{2s}\binom{2s}{r}g^{(2s-r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(r)}(1)+\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{2s-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2s}{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{r}g^{(\ell-r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(r)}(1),

and we rewrite this as

\frac{2}{N}\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1)=\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(2s)}(1)+\sum_{r=1}^{2s-1}\binom{2s}{r}g^{(2s-r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(r)}(1)+\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{2s-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2s}{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{r}g^{(\ell-r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(r)}(1).

For indices
r
involved in the above sums, we have that
1\leq r\leq 2s\leq N-2
,
with
N-r\geq 2
. So, if
r
is odd, then
N-r
is odd and
\geq 3
,
with
B_{N-r}(1)=B_{N-r}=0
, and hence

\frac{2}{N}\big(D^{2s}F_{N}\big)(1)=\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(2s)}(1)+\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}\binom{2s}{2r}g^{(2s-2r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(2r)}(1)+\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{2s-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2s}{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor{\ell}/{2}\rfloor}\binom{\ell}{2r}g^{(\ell-2r)}(1)\frac{2}{N}B_{N}^{(2r)}(1).

From the formula for
C_{N-1}(2s)
in (
9
) together with the definition in (
16
), we find that there exist integers

a_{s,r}
such that

T_{s}=C_{N-1}(2s)-\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}a_{s,r}T_{r}.

(18)

For the
s=1
case of (
18
), we find that
C_{k}(2)=T_{1}
, and
C_{k}(2)
is an integer by assumption. So, from the
assumption that
C_{k}(v)
is an integer for every
v\geq 1
and for
k>1
odd, and since
N\geq 4
is even, an inductive argument
gives us, from (
18
), that
T_{s}
is an integer for

s
in the interval indicated in (
6
),
as desired.

Theorem 1
.

The containment
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subseteq\{1,3,15\}
holds.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
To evaluate

\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{k\geq 1:\forall n\geq 1\ \varphi_{1}(n)\mid\varphi_{k}(n)\},

we begin by noting that
\varphi_{1}(3)=3
and that
\varphi_{k}(3)=1+2^{k}
.
So, in order for
\varphi_{1}(3)\mid\varphi_{k}(3)
to hold, the integer
k
is required to be odd.
So, the integer
k
is henceforth assumed to be odd.

Now, if
k=1
, then
k
is in the desired containing set. So, we henceforth let
k>1
,
(again while working under the assumption that
k\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
).

We proceed to make use of a standard formulation of the Faulhaber formula whereby

\sum_{a=1}^{N}a^{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k+1}{r}B_{r}N^{k+1-r}.

It was proved by Singh
[
4
, Theorem 3]
that

\varphi_{k}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)d^{k}\sum_{b=1}^{n/d}b^{k},

(19)

and the relation in (
19
) can be obtained via the Möbius inversion formula.
Write

\alpha_{j}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)d^{j-1}=\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{j-1})

for
n>1
.
Singh also obtained that

\varphi_{k}(n)=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k+1}{j}B_{j}\alpha_{j}(n)n^{k+1-j}

(20)

as a consequence of (
19
).

Now, let
n>1
. Since
B_{j}
vanishes for odd
j>1
, and since
\alpha_{1}(n)
also vanishes,
we obtain from (
20
) that

\varphi_{k}(n)=\frac{n^{k}\varphi(n)}{k+1}+\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}n^{k+1-2j}\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{2j-1}),

(21)

recalling the assumption that
k>1
is odd.

Now, suppose that
n
is squarefree and that
m
is an odd and positive integer. An application of Euler’s product formula for
\varphi

then gives us that

\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{m})=(-1)^{\omega(n)}\varphi(n)\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{m-1}).

(22)

Dividing both sides of (
21
) by

\varphi_{1}(n)
, the relations in
(
2
) and (
22
) then give us that

\frac{\varphi_{k}(n)}{\varphi_{1}(n)}=\\
\frac{2n^{k-1}}{k+1}+\frac{2(-1)^{\omega(n)}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}n^{k-2j}\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{2j-2}),

again with the assumptions that
n
is squarefree.
Since we are working under the assumption that
k\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
,
we have that the quotient
\frac{\varphi_{k}(n)}{\varphi_{1}(n)}
is an integer.

Again with the ongoing assumption that
k\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, we claim that
C_{k}(u)\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
u\geq 1
. In this
direction, we let
L
denote the least common multiple of the denominators, in lowest terms, among all expressions of the form

\frac{2}{k+1}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}
for
0\leq j\leq\frac{k-1}{2}
, noting that the
j=0
case reduces to
\frac{2}{k+1}
. Dirichlet’s
theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions then gives us that
there are infinitely many primes congruent to
1
modulo
L
.
For fixed
u\geq 1
, choose
u
distinct primes
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
\ldots
,
p_{u}

that are all congruent to
1
modulo
L
,
and set
n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{u}
.
Then
n
is squarefree, and
u=\omega(n)
, and

(23)

Moreover, the expansion of the product

\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{m-1})
reveals that

\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{m-1})\equiv m^{\omega(n)}\pmod{L}.

(24)

From (
23
) and (
24
) together, we find that

(-1)^{\omega(n)}n^{k-2j}\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{2j-2})\equiv(1-2j)^{\omega(n)}\pmod{L},

(25)

from the construction of
L
, letting
j\in\big[1,\frac{k-1}{2}\big]
. Now, we proceed to rewrite
\frac{\varphi_{k}(n)}{\varphi_{1}(n)}-C_{k}(\omega(n))
as

\frac{2n^{k-1}}{k+1}+\frac{2}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}(-1)^{\omega(n)}n^{k-2j}\prod_{p\mid n}(1+p+\cdots+p^{2j-2})-\\
\frac{2}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}(1-2j)^{\omega(n)},

\frac{2n^{k-1}}{k+1}+\frac{2}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}(z_{j}L+(1-2j)^{\omega(n)})-\\
\frac{2}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{(k-1)/2}\binom{k+1}{2j}B_{2j}(1-2j)^{\omega(n)},

for some integers

From our construction, we have that
\operatorname{den}\big(\frac{2}{k+1}\big)
divides

. Recalling (

), we find that

, so that
\frac{2}{k+1}(n^{k-1}-1)

is such that

is an integer. So, since (

\frac{\varphi_{k}(n)}{\varphi_{1}(n)}-C_{k}(\omega(n))
is an integer,
and, in turn, since
\frac{\varphi_{k}(n)}{\varphi_{1}(n)}

we can conclude that

is an integer.

Observe that
C_{k}(u)
is defined, as in (
3
), independently of the value

Moreover, for an arbitrary integer

.

So, we have shown that if

, then

is odd and
C_{k}(v)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
v\geq 1
. By Lemma

1
, we thus have that: For each even integer

,
we have that

2\frac{k!}{h!}B_{h}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(27)

As above, let
h
be an even integer such that
h\in[2,k-1]
.
Now, let
p
be an odd prime such that

.
By the von Staudt–Clausen theorem, this same prime

. Moreover, since

,

.
From (

), we can conclude that

.

, and for an odd prime

Now, let

. Since

is

, and hence the bounds

. Since

Moreover, since

.
So, our constructed value
