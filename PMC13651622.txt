Unifying Passive Air
Sampling with Paper Spray Ionization
Mass Spectrometry for the Detection of Third-Hand Cigarette Smoke

Bo-Cheng Ke, Noor Hidayat Abu Bakar, Pawel L. Urban

Abstract

To mitigate human exposure to air pollutants amid the
increasing
chemicalization of daily life, simple and rapid monitoring methods
are required. As individuals spend a significant portion of their
time indoors, indoor air quality has become a critical factor affecting
human health and is influenced by a wide range of indoor and outdoor
pollution sources. The sorption and desorption of indoor air pollutants
on solid surfaces (the so-called sink effect) can
gradually increase pollutant concentrations and lead to changes in
indoor air quality. In particular, third-hand smoke (THS), a persistent
form of indoor pollution, poses potential health risks through inhalation,
dermal contact, and ingestion. Air sampling and analysis can be tedious
and costly. Here, we present a very simple method that unifies passive
sampling of THS onto paper and subsequent analysis by paper spray
ionization (PSI) mass spectrometry (MS). Mass spectrometric results
revealed the presence of nicotine, a major constituent of tobacco
smoke, in THS samples collected using a custom-built passive sampler,
which was further confirmed by MS/MS analysis. The applicability of
this approach was further extended to a forensic context by detecting
nicotine on book pages, which were previously exposed to cigarette
smoke, using filter papers as sampling media. Overall, passive air
sampling onto papercombined with PSI-MSprovides a
simple, cost-effective, and time-efficient way for indoor air quality
monitoring and forensic analysis.

Introduction

As people spend most of their lives indoors,
indoor air quality
(IAQ) has become a key determinant of human health, influenced by
a variety of indoor and outdoor pollution sources. However, the sorption and desorption of indoor air pollution
on solid material surfacesa phenomenon known as the sink effectcan gradually increase pollutant concentrations
and lead to changes in IAQ. Third-hand
smoke (THS), which is the derivative of environmental tobacco smoke
(ETS), represents a significant source of indoor pollution. THS is the residual tobacco-related contamination
that persists on surfaces and within dust long after smoking has ceased,
followed by the release of chemicals back into the air or the reaction
with other compounds in the environment, forming carcinogenic tobacco-specific
nitrosamines (TSNAs).
,
 Unlike THS, ETSalso referred
to as secondhand smoke (SHS)consists of mainstream smoke exhaled
by smokers and sidestream smoke emitted from the burning tip of a
cigarette.
,
 Nowadays, several toxic chemicals have been
detected in THS, including nicotine, TSNAs, and polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons (PAHs). While exposure to
SHS occurs primarily through short-term inhalation, THS can lead to
prolonged exposure through inhalation, dermal absorption, and ingestion,
making it a distinct and persistent contributor to indoor air pollution,
which poses threats to nonsmokers.

Typically, monitoring of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) consists
of sampling, pretreatment, separation, and detection. Air sampling represents one of the most critical and challenging
steps in air pollution monitoring. In
general, sampling techniques are classified into two categories: active
(pumped) and passive (diffusive). Passive
air sampling (PAS) operates via diffusive transport of analyte molecules
from ambient air into a sorptive medium, eliminating the need for
pumps or electrical power. Their simplicity,
low cost, and maintenance-free design make them particularly advantageous
for large-scale monitoring and for deployment in remote or resource-limited
environments. Although PAS exhibits lower
sampling rates compared to active methods, they provide time-integrated
measurements that reflect average pollutant levels over extended periods. These characteristics make PAS an effective
and practical tool for assessing long-term trends and spatial distributions
of airborne contaminants, including those in indoor environments.

To date, multiple techniques have been
used to analyze THS, including
GC-MS
,
 and LC-MS. However,
the complicated sample preparation procedures of conventional methods
make IAQ monitoring time-consuming. Therefore, there is a need to
develop an approach capable of rapidly detecting THS with minimal
sample pretreatment. Ambient ionization MS is a technique that enables
direct analysis of raw samples in their native condition with little
or no sample pretreatment. Commonly used
techniques include desorption electrospray ionization (DESI), direct
analysis in real time (DART), and paper spray ionization (PSI). In fact, one of the ambient ionization techniquescalled
desorption corona beam ionization (DCBI)was already applied for the detection of THS. PSI is a spray-based ambient ionization technique
used for the rapid, quantitative, and qualitative analysis of complex
mixtures. PSI consists of three major analytical steps, including
sample collection, analyte separation, and analyte ionization. In brief, a small volume of sample is applied
to a triangular paper substrate, such as chromatography paper and
filter paper, followed by the addition of a spray solvent (typically
a methanol/water mixture). The solvent
migrates through the porous paper by capillary action, extracting
analytes that are subsequently ionized when a high voltage (2–5.5
kV) is applied to the paper tip. Given
its capability for rapid, direct analysis with minimal sample preparation,
PSI has been applied to several fields, including forensic science and clinical diagnostics.
,
 However, PSI shows strong potential for environmental monitoring
applications, particularly in air quality assessment, where it enables
direct sampling and analysis of pollutants, such as tobacco smoke.
,

In this study, we aim to establish a rapid approach for evaluating
IAQ using PSI-MS. Specifically, this work integrates passive paper-based
sampling with direct PSI-MS analysis to enable on-site detection of
airborne pollutants without extensive sample preparation. The study
focuses on one of the indoor contaminantsTHS, which significantly
affects indoor environments. It aims to develop a practical and adaptable
platform for real-time and retrospective indoor air assessment, advancing
the understanding of exposure risks associated with THS-related pollutants.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Methanol (LC grade) was purchased
from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Acetonitrile (LC-MS grade) and water
(LC-MS grade) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA).
Formic acid (≥98%) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific
(Fair Lawn, NJ). (S)-(−)-Nicotine (99%) standard
solution was purchased from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA). Pyrrolidine
(>99%) was purchased from Acros Organics (Geel, Belgium). Quantitative
ashless filter papers (No. 3; diameter, 90 mm) were purchased from
Advantec (Tokyo, Japan). Cigarettes (with tar and nicotine levels
of 8 mg per cigarette and 0.6 mg per cigarette, respectively, which
are indicated on the pack) were purchased in a convenience store (Hsinchu,
Taiwan).

Preparation of Paper Substrates for Passive Sampling and Paper
Spray Ionization Mass Spectrometric Analysis

Before air sampling
and PSI-MS analysis, the paper substrates were prepared following
a standardized procedure. Filter paper disks were first cut into isosceles
triangular shapes (15 mm × 10 mm, height × base) using a
razor blade. The cut pieces were sonicated in a 75% (v/v) aqueous
methanol solution for 5 min, then replaced with fresh 75% (v/v) aqueous
methanol and sonicated for another 5 min. Subsequently, the solvent
was changed to pure acetonitrile, and the paper pieces were gently
shaken (without sonication) for 5 min. Finally, the wetted paper pieces
were dried by flushing with nitrogen gas for 30 min before use. To
evaluate the effectiveness of the cleaning procedure, paper substrates
before and after cleaning were analyzed by PSI-MS. As shown in Figure S1A, several ions with relatively high
signal intensities (e.g., m/z 163,
205, and 222) were detected on the untreated paper. After the cleaning
procedure, these signals were effectively eliminated (Figure S1B), demonstrating the effectiveness
of the cleaning process in removing background contaminants.

THS Simulation Chamber and Sampling Method

The simulation
chamber (52 cm × 48 cm × 65 cm, L × W × H) was
constructed from acrylic panels (thickness, 8 mm). A portable USB
fan (XM66–28; Sheng De Fu Enterprise, Tainan, Taiwan) was placed
inside to ensure uniform air mixing within the closed system. Passive
sampler holders were designed to hold three triangular paper substrates
(15 mm × 10 mm, height × base) for the collection of THS
(Figure S2). They were fabricated by 3D
printing (UP Plus 2; Beijing Tiertime Technology Company, Beijing,
China) using ABS filament. 3D-printed materials may release contaminants,
,
 particularly when exposed to elevated temperatures, affecting the
experiment. In the present study, however, the 3D-printed samplers
were operated at room temperature, minimizing the potential for temperature-induced
VOC emissions during sampling. Before introducing the THS source,
the passive samplers were positioned in the chamber to verify the
absence of residual tobacco smoke, which served as the “field
blank”. To avoid interference from SHS, THS generation was
conducted in two consecutive stages. In the first stage, SHS was generated
by burning a cigarette for 8 min in a separate chamber, allowing the
tobacco smoke to adsorb onto solid materials (e.g., paper towel and cotton cloth; 10 cm × 10 cm, L × W),
as illustrated in A (photo shown in Figure S3A).
In the second stage, the smoke-laden substrates were transferred to
the simulation chamber, where the adsorbed compounds were re-emitted
and subsequently collected by passive samplers, as illustrated in B (photo shown in Figure S3B). After 30 min of sampling, the samplers
were removed from the chamber and directly subjected to PSI-MS analysis.
Relative humidity (RH, %) and temperature (T, °C)
were recorded at the time of sampler removal to monitor the experimental
conditions for each sampling event.

Paper Spray Ionization Mass Spectrometric Analysis

For PSI-MS analysis, a triangular paper substrate was removed from
the passive sampler and mounted onto the PSI device following the
procedure described previously. As illustrated
in C (photo
shown in Figure S3C), a custom-designed
holder was introduced to support the paper substrates, preventing
the paper tip from collapsing when the spray solvent was added, which
otherwise could affect analytical stability. After air sampling, the
prepared paper probes were mounted on the PSI device, positioned ∼10
mm away from the inlet of a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (LCMS-8030;
Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Only the middle part of the filter paper
has contact with the holder to minimize its effect on sample movement.
The spray solvent consisted of a 20 μL aliquot of 75% (v/v)
aqueous methanol containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. Pyrrolidine (20
μM)used as an internal standardwas spiked into
the spray solvent. The solvent was then applied to the rear portion
of the paper using a pipet, allowing the solution to migrate through
the paper via capillary action. Upon application of a +4.5 kV potential,
charged droplets were generated from the paper tip, producing gas-phase
ions that were subsequently analyzed by the mass spectrometer. Because
PSI exhibits spray characteristics similar to those of nanoelectrospray
ionization, drying gas was switched off
during the PSI-MS analysis to avoid disruption of spray formation
and to maintain signal stability
,
 (see also Figure S4). The heat block temperature was 400
°C while the desolvation line temperature was 250 °C and
the desolvation line voltage was 0 V. The instrument was operated
in the positive-ion full-scan mode across an m/z range of 20–500 with a total scan duration of 2
min. Product-ion scan mode (collision-induced dissociation with argon;
collision energy: 20 to 30 V, varying across different compounds)
was applied to further confirm the identities of MS signals.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Direct Sampling and Analysis by PSI-MS

To evaluate the effectiveness of direct sampling and analysis by
PSI-MS, the analytical performance of PSI-MS was compared with that
of ESI-MS. PSI-MS represents a technique that requires no sample pretreatment,
whereas extraction and sample preparation (dilution and addition of
an acidic additive, 0.1% formic acid) were performed prior to ESI-MS
analysis. As shown in A, nicotine (m/z 163) was
observed exclusively in the spectrum of the sample paper exposed to
THS as a peak at m/z 163. Nicotinebeing
one of the most abundant components of tobacco smokeplays
a crucial role in THS chemistry due to its high emission rate, elevated
surface concentrations, and persistence on indoor materials.
,,
 Its identity was further confirmed
by comparison of the tandem mass spectrum with that of a nicotine
standard (Figure S5). The close agreement
between the tandem mass spectra of nicotine obtained from THS (Figure S5C) and the nicotine standard (Figure S5D) indicates that THS constituents,
particularly nicotine, were successfully collected by the passive
samplers. Other tobacco-related compounds, including myosmine and
nicotine 1′-N-oxide, were also detected in
THS, and are discussed in the next section. In contrast, nicotine
was not detected in the sampled paper analyzed by ESI-MS (B). These results
demonstrate that integrating direct sampling with PSI-MS analysis
enables effective THS detection with minimal solvent consumption and
without the need for sample pretreatment. The absence of the protonated
nicotine peak (m/z 163) in the ESI
mass spectrum may be attributed to several factors, with sample dilution
likely being a major contributor. In PSI-MS analysis, only 20 μL
of solvent were required for both extraction and ionization. To keep
the sample preparation procedure as simple as possible and increase
the sample volume to fill the syringe and ESI source flow line, ESI-MS
analysis involved extraction with 1.5 mL of 75% (v/v) aqueous methanol
through sonication, followed by an additional 2-fold dilution with
the same solvent prior to MS analysis. This substantially greater
dilution (150-fold) may have reduced the nicotine concentration to
the point where it was below the detection limit. Moreover, PSI-MS
enables direct analysis of compounds adsorbed onto the paper surface
without sample pretreatment, allowing surface-bound residues to be
ionized and detected under a high electric field. This direct ionization
approach preserves the native chemical state of weakly adsorbed compounds,
such as nicotine, allowing for rapid tobacco smoke analysis without
the need for additional extraction or chromatographic separation.

Due to its abundance in tobacco smoke, nicotine
was selected as
the target compound for monitoring THS in the chamber experiment.
To evaluate the analytical performance of this technique, a calibration
curve was constructed using nicotine standards, with the structural
analogue internal standard, pyrrolidine (m/z 72), spiked into the spray solvent. The limit of detection
(LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ) were calculated using the following
equations: LOD = 3.3σ/S and LOQ = 10σ/S, where σ represents the standard deviation of the y-intercepts of the linear regression, and S denotes the slope of the calibration curve. As shown in Figure S6, an LOD of 1.30 μM (210.90 ng
mL–1) and an LOQ of 3.93 μM (637.56 ng mL–1) were obtained, with a linear range of 3–15
μM (R
2 = 0.9957). In addition, the
relative standard deviation (RSD) was evaluated for both the nicotine
standard and nicotine derived from THS. As shown in Figure S7A,B, the RSD for the nicotine standard was 13.5%
(n = 10) when obtained for the nicotine-to-pyrrolidine
signal ratios, compared to 24.9% when obtained from signal intensities,
indicating improved precision with the use of an internal standard.
This result is as expected because the internal standard partly compensates
for signal drift during analysis. In addition, we investigated the
repeatability of the THS samples by collecting air samples using 10
paper probes. As shown in Figure S7C, the
RSD obtained for the nicotine-to-pyrrolidine ratios was 33.7% (n = 8). In this case, the RSD was calculated only for eight
of the ten replicates due to the absence of the pyrrolidine signal
(m/z 72) in replicates 3 and 10.
Such signal loss may arise from slight but critical variations in
substrate geometry during manual preparation. Besides, the RSD obtained
from raw signal intensities was 51.6% (n = 10, Figure S7D). Although the RSD observed for real
samples was higher than that obtained for the nicotine standard, this
increased variability can be attributed to matrix effects and variations
in the sampling process, reflecting the heterogeneous nature of THS
and the inherent challenges of passive sampling. Such variability
therefore provides an insight into the practical performance of the
proposed approach under realistic sampling conditions. Overall, the
proposed method can be regarded as a rapid qualitative or semiquantitative
test for the presence of THS.

SHS vs THS

THS refers to residual tobacco pollutants
that accumulate on indoor surfaces and in dust, which can be re-emitted
into the gas phase or undergo chemical transformations to form secondary
pollutants. Meanwhile, SHS is a mixture
of sidestream smoke emitted from burning tobacco products and mainstream
smoke exhaled by smokers.
,
 In the next experiment,
THS was generated following the procedures described above, whereas
SHS was obtained by directly exposing passive samplers to sidestream
tobacco smoke. The differences between SHS and THS were evaluated
based on the mass spectra shown in . SHS and THS consist of both VOCs and semivolatile
organic compounds (SVOCs), including toluene, formaldehyde, nicotine,
nitroamines, and PAHs.
,,,
 As shown in , nicotine (structure shown in Figure S8D) was detected in both SHS and THS-derived
paper samplers (m/z 163). Due to
the unit mass resolution of the triple quadrupole mass spectrometer,
peak assignment was further confirmed by MS/MS analysis. As shown
in Figure S8A,B, the fragmentation patterns
observed for SHS were similar to those for THS. According to Smyth
and co-workers, the fragment ion at m/z 132 is due to the loss of CH3NH2 from the
saturated heterocyclic ring, whereas the fragment at m/z 106 arises from the loss of C3H5NH2 (Figure S9). In addition, the assignment of the protonated
nicotine peak was verified by comparison with the tandem mass spectrum
of an authentic nicotine standard (Figure S8C). Notably, the signal intensity of nicotine in SHS was approximately
four times higher than that in THS. This difference can be attributed
to the direct collection of SHS from freshly emitted cigarette aerosols,
whereas THS was derived from the off-gassing of volatile compounds
re-emitted from contaminated surfaces. In addition to cellulose-based
towel paper, cotton clotha commonly used textile materialwas
employed as another substrate to simulate THS formation. As shown
in Figure S10, nicotine was detected in
paper samplers exposed to THS emanating from both cotton cloth (Figure S10A) and towel paper (Figure S10B). Previous studies have reported nicotine sorption
on common indoor materials, including cotton and glass. Although the sorption behavior of nicotine among
different textile materials was not further compared in this study,
these results demonstrate that cellulose-based materials can retain
tobacco-related compounds and may serve as reservoirs for THS, potentially
contributing to prolonged human exposure.

While THS contains lower concentrations of tobacco-related
compounds
than SHS, these residues can persist in indoor environments for extended
periods and subsequently react with oxidants such as ozone and gaseous
nitrous acid (HONO), leading to the formation of secondary organic
aerosols (SOA) and TSNAs.
,
 Aside from nicotine,
oxidation products of nicotine, including myosmine and nicotine 1′-N-oxide, were detected in both SHS and THS sampled by paper,
with signal intensities more than an order of magnitude lower than
that of nicotine (). Myosmine (m/z 147; structure
shown in Figure S11C), an oxidation product
of nicotine and one of the earliest structurally characterized tobacco
alkaloids aside from nicotine, was further
confirmed by MS/MS analysis using the same approach as applied for
nicotine. As shown in Figure S11, the fragmentation
patterns observed in SHS (Figure S11A)
closely resembled those in THS (Figure S11B), indicating that the same tobacco-related compound was present
in both samples. According to the suggested fragmentations reported
by Medana et al., the fragment ion at m/z 105 is attributed to the neutral loss of C3H6 (42 Da), while the ion at m/z 120
arises from the loss of HCN (27 Da; Figure S12). Nicotine 1′-N-oxide (m/z 179; structure shown
in Figure S13C), a constituent of tobacco
products, is generally considered to be formed via oxidative transformation
of S-(−)-nicotine upon exposure to air during tobacco leaf
processing, particularly during drying and curing. As shown in Figure S13A,B, the
fragmentation patterns observed in SHS and THS were highly comparable,
confirming the presence of nicotine 1′-N-oxide
in both samples. The fragment ions at m/z 132 and 130 are consistent with the previously reported fragmentation
pathways (Figure S14), including those
described by Medana et al. and the tandem
mass spectra reported by Uhlig et al.

Residual Smoke as a Function of Time Elapsed from the First
Exposure

Indoor surfacessuch as furnishings and wallscan
serve as sinks for VOCs, altering their concentrations through sorption
and desorption (re-emission) processes, commonly referred to as the sink effect. In this study,
the temporal variation of residual smoke concentration, particularly
nicotine, was monitored over 300 min (5 h) in an acrylic test chamber
(52 × 48 × 65 cm, L × W × H) at RH of 53 ±
1% and temperature of 23 ± 2 °C. Briefly, a cigarette was
burned inside the chamber for 20 s and then removed. Monitoring was
subsequently performed every hour, consisting of 30 min of equilibration
followed by 30 min of sampling. The nicotine-to-pyrrolidine signal
ratio was used to evaluate temporal changes in nicotine concentration.
As shown in , the nicotine/pyrrolidine ratio gradually decreased over time and
approached a lower plateau. Error bars represent the standard deviation
(n = 3), and the solid red line corresponds to a
one-phase exponential decay fit. The exponential-like decay suggests
that nicotine loss was dominated by a single apparent kinetic process
within the investigated time range. Although nicotine re-emission
was not directly observed within the experimental time frame, the
progressive decrease in the detectable nicotine signal suggests time-dependent
changes in the nicotine concentration present in the room. This observation
is consistent with previous studies showing that nicotine, as a SVOC,
readily partitions to indoor surfaces and can be slowly re-emitted
over extended periods.
,
 While the chamber used in our
test was made of acrylic rather than stainless steel, a temporal decay
trend similar to that reported by Van Loy et al. was observed, suggesting that sink-related sorption behavior
is not limited to metallic surfaces.

Previous studies have demonstrated nicotine sorption
on various
indoor materials, including carpet, wallboard, glass, and textiles (e.g., cotton and
nylon). Because acrylic is a polymeric
material, nicotine retention may involve not only surface adsorption
but also absorption and diffusion within the material matrix. Therefore,
acrylic-based materials may act as long-term nicotine reservoirs that
gradually re-emit THS-related compounds into indoor environments.
These findings have important implications for indoor exposure and
THS persistence, as acrylic and other plastic-based materials are
widely used in offices, public spaces, furniture, and indoor partitions.

The observed time-dependent behavior is also influenced by environmental
factors such as RH and temperature. Nicotine
sorption depends on its protonation state and interfacial interactions,
both of which can vary with environmental conditions. Ongwandee and Sawanyapanich reported that high RH (e.g., 90%) can enhance nicotine uptake by modifying adsorbed
water layers and surface interaction sites. Temperature also affects both sorption equilibrium and mass transfer
kinetics, thereby influencing the extent of nicotine retention on
surfaces as well as its redistribution between phases.
,
 Collectively, these factors provide a mechanistic basis for the
observed time-dependent behavior of THS, particularly nicotine, and
highlight the importance of monitoring RH and temperature throughout
the chamber experiments.

Detection of Smoke Adsorbed on Pages of a Book

There
have been anecdotal reports and complaints about THS being emitted
from shared books; for example, in the library setting. One testimony mentioned: “(···)
The books reeked of cigarette smoke. The odor was chokingly strong
even from several feet away. (···)” Therefore, in another experimentaimed
to simulate a potential application of the developed methodan
open book (teaching-related, 416 pages, purchased in Kaohsiung, Taiwan)
was exposed to SHS from a burning cigarette for 10 min (A). The smoke residues were
primarily adsorbed on the surfaces of exposed pages but not on the
other pages. Subsequently, triangle-shaped filter paper pieces were
placed in between different pages of the same book (contact sampling;
see B), including
the previously exposed (40 and 41), adjacent (38 and 39), and unexposed
pages (10, 11, 20, and 21). The book was kept closed for 1 h, enabling
direct contact of the paper samplers with the different book pages.
The paper samplers were subsequently analyzed by PSI-MS to detect
any traces of residual nicotine. Prior to tobacco smoke exposure,
field blank analysis was conducted on the selected pages to ensure
that no tobacco-related compounds were present in the unexposed book
(Figure S15A). Tandem mass spectra shown
in Figure S16A demonstrate very low amount
of nicotine on unexposed book pages, as compared to the spectrum for
the authentic standard (Figure S16C).

As shown in C, nicotine (m/z 163)
was detected
on pages directly exposed to tobacco smoke (40 and 41), whereas lower
signal intensities were observed on adjacent pages (38 and 39), and
no detectable signal was found on more distant pages (10, 11, 20,
and 21). This spatial distribution indicates that nicotine deposition
under the present conditions was strongly dependent on proximity to
the exposure source. The elevated signals on pages 40 and 41 are consistent
with the efficient adsorption of tobacco smoke constituents onto solid
surfaces. Nicotine is known to partition onto various indoor materials,
including wool, polyester, and cellulose-based substrates, which can
act as reservoirs.
,
 The detection of nicotine on
unexposed adjacent pages suggests limited redistribution from the
exposed region, likely through short-range transport within the closed
book environment. In contrast, the absence of detectable nicotine
on more distant pages indicates that transport into the interior of
the book was minimal under the present experimental conditions.

To investigate temporal changes in nicotine levels, a second sampling
was conducted 1 h after the initial measurement. As shown in Figure S15B, analysis of pages that previously
showed no detectable nicotine (10, 11, 20, and 21) yielded values
below the detection limit, whereas analysis of pages with detectable
nicotine exhibited time-dependent nicotine signal changes. A pronounced
decrease of nicotine signal was observed for the exposed pages and
their adjacent pages, indicating that the amount of surface-accessible
nicotine decreased over time. The detection of nicotine in book pages
after exposure to smoke was further confirmed by tandem MS analysis,
as shown in Figure S16B. This temporal
evolution reflects the dynamic nature of nicotine–surface interactions,
in which sorbed nicotine can undergo re-emission into the gas phase
and subsequent redistribution within the indoor microenvironment. Mass transfer processes play a role in this
behavior. Sorption is not limited to surface adsorption but also involves
diffusion into the bulk of the material (in-material diffusion).
,
 As a typical SVOC, nicotine is expected to partition among the gas
phase, surface, and bulk material rather than remaining solely on
the outermost surface. Furthermore, adsorption on porous cellulose-based
materials is governed by structural properties such as pore volume
and surface area, indicating that nicotine can penetrate and be retained
within internal pore networks as well as on surface sites. In addition to the variation in nicotine signal
intensity across book pages, tobacco-related compounds such as myosmine
and nicotine 1′-N-oxidepreviously
detected in SHS and THSwere not observed on the book pages.

To further evaluate the time-dependent evolution of nicotine signals
over an extended interval, a third sampling was conducted on the same
pages 4 days after the initial measurement. The book was kept closed
during this period to minimize external contamination that could interfere
with the detection of tobacco-related compounds. As shown in D, nicotine remained
detectable on the exposed pages after several days. Tandem mass spectra
shown in Figure S17 reveal that nicotine
was detected in the exposed pages (40 and 41) among all samplings
after the tobacco smoke exposure, excluding the field blank. However,
its signal intensity was approximately nine times lower than that
observed in the initial (day 0) measurement. This observation is consistent
with the previously discussed mechanism, in which diffusion of nicotine
from surface layers into the internal structure of the material reduces
the amount of surface-accessible nicotine, thereby diminishing the
detectable signal over time.

To assess day-to-day reproducibility,
the “book test”
was performed repeatedly on three different days following the identical
procedure described above. As shown in Figure S18 the nicotine-to-pyrrolidine ratio was used as a reproducible
indicator for nicotine detection across different book pages, including
both the initial sampling and the repeated sampling performed ∼1
h later. As expected, the measurements on the exposed pages (40 and
41; 110 and 111; 180 and 181) revealed the highest nicotine-to-pyrrolidine
ratio among all the other pages. Moreover, the ratio decreased as
the distance from the exposed pages increased. Adjacent pages (including
38 and 39; 108 and 109; 178 and 179) showed the second-highest ratio
in all the measurements on the three different days, indicating that
tobacco-related compoundsnicotine in this case could
also be detected on the unexposed pages close to the exposed pages.
However, analysis of the unexposed pageslocated at least ten
pages away from the exposed pagesrevealed comparable nicotine-to-pyrrolidine
ratios to the field blank analysis among all the obtained values,
indicating no nicotine detected in field blank, the initial THS samples,
and the second samples. In addition, a sharp decrease in ratios was
observed on both exposed and adjacent pages after the initial measurement,
in agreement with the temporal decline discussed above. The consistent
spatial distribution and temporal decay patternsobserved across
three independent experimentsdemonstrate satisfactory day-to-day
reproducibility of this sampling and analytical approach, regardless
of the specific page location examined.

Conclusions

The surging chemicalization of daily life
leads to increased exposure
of humans to harmful substances calling for simple and efficient monitoring
approaches. In the absence of active smokers, THS can be harmful to
humans in home and office environments. Thus, IAQ monitoring focusing
on THS is crucial for safety assurance. We have demonstrated a very
simple method to detect the presence of cigarette smoke residues being
emitted from common absorbers (paper, cloth). A novel aspect of this
method is the use of paper for both passive THS sampling and MS analysis
by PSI-MS. This integration simplifies and streamlines the analytical
workflow reducing the sample handling steps in IAQ monitoring. Using
the developed method, we observed sorption of nicotine from SHS into
book pages and subsequent redistribution within the paper matrix.
Thus, it has the potential applicability in sampling THS emitted by
shared daily use objects such as books loaned from libraries. While
it is often hard to point out the source of THS objectively, the proposed
method addresses this issue to some extent. Moreover, the passive
samplers can be attached to the elements of indoor infrastructure
to collect traces of THS and evaluate the exposure of the indoor space
users to harmful VOCs. To detect some highly abundant smoke components
such as nicotine, a low-resolution mass spectrometer is sufficient.
Therefore, the method can be used in the field in conjunction with
portable mass spectrometers. Apart from detection of THS, the approach
can potentially be utilized for IAQ monitoring in industrial facilities
and osmology-based forensics.
