Beneath the Spike: Laminar Microcircuits of Human Interictal Epileptiform Discharges
Authors: John D Rolston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Laminar Organization of Cellular Microcircuits Modulating Human Interictal Epileptiform Discharges Silva AB, Marathe SA, Greicius QR, Xu D, Jain S, Chung JE, Yang X, Khambhati AN, Leonard MK, Kleen JK, Chang EF. Nat Neurosci. 2026;29(6):1462-1475.Background: Interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) are pathological bursts of brain activity between seizures in people with epilepsy. Despite their importance in diagnosis, cognitive comorbidities and therapeutic implications as biomarkers for neurostimulation, it is unknown how IEDs arise from structured large-scale neuronal firing across human cortical lamina. We used high-density Neuropixels probes to record from epileptogenic tissue in patients undergoing resective surgery, sampling 1152 neurons during 1094 IEDs across 9 neocortical sites. We identified microcircuits for IEDs organized by firing pattern, neocortical depth and putative cell type. Regular-spiking cells, concentrated in superficial cortical lamina, initiated and coded the amplitude of the sharp discharge and excitatory–inhibitory imbalances across neocortical lamina preceded IEDs, enabling IED prediction up to 1000 ms in advance. Most neurons that were modulated during IEDs also encoded cognitive information and adhered to physiological rhythms at baseline. Thus, neocortical IEDs are generated from predictable laminar–cellular interactions, providing the groundwork for new neurostimulation therapies that harness the granularity of single neurons. Keywords: interictal discharge, epilepsy, seizure, single unit activity, multielectrode array, local field potential

Commentary

Interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs)—the sharp waves and spikes that punctuate interictal EEG and electrocorticography recordings—are foundational to epilepsy diagnosis and increasingly recognized as clinically significant targets in their own right. IEDs correlate with seizure onset zones, impair cognitive function, and serve as feedback signals for responsive neurostimulation (RNS) devices. Yet despite this clinical centrality, the cellular and laminar mechanisms generating IEDs in the human cortex have remained largely inferred from animal models and in vitro slice experiments. Silva et al now provide a landmark dataset that addresses this gap directly, using high-density Neuropixels probes to resolve the single-neuron and laminar elements of IEDs in human epileptogenic neocortex during resective surgery. 1

The Neuropixels silicon probe was adapted for human intraoperative use in parallel by Paulk et al and members of the same Chang group, opening an era of large-scale, laminar-resolved single-unit recording in the human neocortex.2,3 These probes sample hundreds of channels spanning the cortical mantle from pia to white matter, enabling simultaneous single-neuron recording across multiple cortical layers. This is something entirely beyond the reach of clinical macroelectrodes and most conventional multielectrode arrays, like the Behnke-Fried microwire bundle or Utah array. Prior work on IED microcircuitry in humans using 24-channel laminar probes provided an informative foundation by examining local field potentials, current source density, and multiunit activity, but was constrained by low channel counts. 4 Silva et al built directly on this foundation, accumulating 1152 well-isolated single neurons across 1094 IEDs at 9 neocortical sites—a dataset that, while modest by rodent standards, is extraordinary for intraoperative human recording.

The principal finding of Silva et al is gratifyingly specific: regular-spiking (RS) cells concentrated in the superficial cortical laminae (putative superficial pyramidal neurons) initiate IEDs and encode their amplitude. This is consistent with prior evidence from animal models and human laminar local field potential studies suggesting that superficial pyramidal populations bear disproportionate excitability and serve as the primary current generators during IED formation. 4 What is qualitatively new is the direct human single-neuron confirmation. Fast-spiking cells (putative inhibitory interneurons), by contrast, dominated a “suppression” population whose firing declined up to 1000 ms before the discharge—a decay of inhibition that preceded, rather than followed, IED initiation and that proved the single most informative signal for forecasting impending IEDs. This temporal ordering closely parallels proposed models of focal seizure initiation, raising the question of whether IED and ictal microcircuits are truly distinct entities or differ primarily in the degree of synchrony and propagation achieved before inhibition reasserts control.

The most therapeutically consequential finding is that excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) imbalances across neocortical laminae precede IEDs by up to 1000 ms. This is not simply a retrospective mechanistic observation. It implies that the prevailing laminar state of local microcircuits constitutes a prospective biomarker—one that could, in principle, trigger preemptive neurostimulation before a discharge fully forms rather than reacting to it after detection. Current RNS systems operate reactively, detecting IEDs and delivering therapy afterward. A reliable 1-s predictive window could shift the paradigm toward anticipatory neuromodulation, potentially improving both efficacy and the specificity of stimulation delivery. 5 Whether such laminar E/I signatures can be detected with clinically deployable electrode configurations remains to be established (Neuropixels probes have not been chronically implanted in humans) but this work establishes the existence proof and defines the target signal. Translating cellular E/I dynamics into mesoscale biomarkers accessible by implanted hardware is a necessary, nontrivial, and now well-motivated research agenda.

Another finding with immediate clinical resonance is that most neurons modulated during IEDs also participate in physiological cognitive processing at baseline. This cellular-level confirmation echoes what electrocorticographic and behavioral studies have long suggested, that IEDs intrude on the same cortical circuitry engaged in active cognitive processing, transiently disrupting language, memory, and executive function. 6 The implication cuts 2 ways. On one hand, it reinforces the view that IED suppression carries cognitive benefit independent of and complementary to seizure reduction, a goal now supported at the cellular level. On the other, it introduces a caution for stimulation strategies targeting IED-generating superficial laminae. Those same neurons serve physiological roles, and therapeutic targeting will require spatial and temporal precision sufficient to suppress pathological synchrony while preserving normal cognitive encoding.

While a truly impressive scientific contribution, there are some limitations. Intraoperative recordings by design sample tissue selected for resection, the most pathological cortex available. Whether the laminar circuit described generalizes to seizure-propagation zones, structurally intact but functionally aberrant cortex, or extratemporal or non-neocortical epilepsy syndromes is unknown. Although these recordings were obtained during awake craniotomies rather than under general anesthesia, the sedative agents used for monitored anesthesia care still modulate GABAergic tone and cortical excitability in ways that may alter IED rate, morphology, and cellular recruitment relative to the chronic, unmedicated state. The cohort, while large for this recording modality, spans 9 neocortical sites across a small number of patients, and all sites were confined to the lateral temporal neocortex. Aggregate findings may obscure clinically meaningful heterogeneity. Finally, the observational design precludes causal conclusions. Whether superficial RS cell firing drives IEDs or whether both reflect a common upstream network input cannot be distinguished without perturbation experiments. Chemogenetic or optogenetic approaches in translational models, guided by the laminar circuit map established here, will help address causality. This is a good reminder of the need for human and animal research to engage and mutually inform.

Overall, the convergent findings by Silva et al (superficial excitatory neurons initiating IEDs, laminar E/I imbalance as a 1-s predictive signal, and cognitive circuits as co-recruited participants) form a coherent and actionable framework. The foundation for layer-targeted, predictive neuromodulation is now supported by direct human neural evidence. As high-density Neuropixels-class probes become more widely and safely deployed in human neurosurgery, 7 and as chronic high-density implantable devices continue to advance, the laminar microcircuit architecture established here will be an essential reference point for both the basic science of ictogenesis and the engineering of next-generation closed-loop therapies.
