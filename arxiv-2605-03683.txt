A multivariate Strassmann theorem
Guido Maria Lido & Luca Mauri, luca.mauri@phd.unipi.it, Università di Pisa

Abstract
By a theorem of Strassmann, a non-zero convergent power series in one variable over a complete non-Archimedean field has finitely many zeros, with an explicit bound on their number. We generalize this result to convergent power series in several variables, characterizing finiteness of the zero set and bounding its cardinality in terms of the reduction of the saturated ideal defined by the power series. We discuss how to make our result effective, under suitable assumptions, when working with approximate power series.

Introduction

Let
K
be a field complete with respect to a non-trivial, non-Archimedean (possibly not discrete) valuation
v\colon K\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Its ring of integers
R\coloneqq\{a\in K\,\colon\,v(a)\geq 0\}
has Krull dimension
1
, since
v
is non-trivial and takes values in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. We denote by
\mfrak\coloneqq\{a\in K\,\colon\,v(a)>0\}
the unique maximal ideal of
R
and by
k\coloneqq R/\mfrak
the residue field of
\mfrak
.

A power series in
K[[t]]
converges on
R
if and only if the valuation of its coefficients tends to
+\infty
. In
[
Str28
]
, Strassmann proved that a non-zero convergent power series has only finitely many zeros in
R
, and gave an explicit upper bound.
1
1

1

Strassmann focused on the case where
K
is a
p
-adic field, but the result holds in greater generality

Theorem 1.1

(Strassmann)
.

Let

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}

be a non-zero power series with coefficients in
K
that converges on
R
.
Then
f
has only finitely many zeros in
R
and, defining
M=\min_{n}v(a_{n})
, the number of zeros of
f
in
R
is at most the natural number

(1.2)

\max\{n\,\colon\,v(a_{n})=M\}.

The above finiteness result can be applied to prove basic properties of
p
-adic analytic functions, e.g., that a convergent power series
f
on
R
is determined by the values
f(a_{i})
at infinitely many points
a_{i}\in R
, and that it is not periodic unless it is constant.
Moreover, Strassmann’s bound can sometimes be used to bound the number of solutions of Diophantine equations. In particular, it comes in handy when
p
-adic methods can be applied, such as Skolem’s method for Thue equations (
[
Sko34
]
) or Chabauty-Coleman’s method for rational points on curves of genus at least
2
(
[
Cha41
,
Col85
]
). Another noteworthy consequence is the Skolem–Mahler–Lech Theorem, which concerns the zero set of a linear recurrence with constant coefficients in a field of characteristic zero (
[
Sko34
,
Mah35
,
Lec53
]
).

We present a generalization of Strassmann’s theorem
to the setting of convergent power series in several variables. The set of formal power series in
n
variables that converge in the unit ball
R^{n}
is the Tate algebra

(1.3)

\mathcal{T}_{n}=K\langle x_{1},\,\dots,\,x_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\biggl\{\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}x^{I}\,\colon\,a_{I}\in K\text{ and }v(a_{I})\to+\infty\text{ as }|I|\to+\infty\biggr\}.

We also consider the algebra of its “integral” elements

(1.4)

\mathcal{R}_{n}=R\langle x_{1},\,\dots,\,x_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\biggl\{\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}x^{I}\,\colon\,a_{I}\in R\text{ and }v(a_{I})\to+\infty\text{ as }|I|\to+\infty\biggr\}.

A power series
f
in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
can be reduced modulo
\mfrak
by reducing its coefficients, and the convergence condition “
v(a_{I})\to+\infty
” implies that the reduction
\overline{f}
is in fact a polynomial in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
The same convergence condition also implies that for any non-zero power series
f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
there exists a constant
c\in K^{\times}
such that
c\cdot f
lies in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. Moreover, choosing carefully the valuation of
c
, we can suppose that the reduction
\overline{c\cdot f}
is non-zero. For such a constant
c
, Strassmann’s bound in Equation (
1.2
) equals the degree of
\overline{cf}
or, equivalently, the dimension of the
k
-vector space
k[x]\bigl/(\overline{cf})
. Our main result is a direct generalization of this observation.
Before stating the theorem, for any subset
I\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let us introduce the notation

(1.5)

V_{R}(I):=\big\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R^{n}:f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0\quad\forall f\in I\big\},

to denote the set of zeros of
I
in
R^{n}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
be power series in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and let
\overline{f_{1}},\dots,\overline{f_{r}}
be their reductions modulo
\mfrak
. If

\overline{A}\coloneqq k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}})

is a finite-dimensional
k
-vector space, then the set
of common zeros of
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}

in
R^{n}
is finite and satisfies the inequalities

(1.7)

\#V_{R}\bigl(\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}\bigr)\leq\sum_{m}\dim_{k}(\overline{A}_{m})\leq\dim_{k}(\overline{A}),

where the sum ranges over the finitely many maximal ideals
m\subset\overline{A}
such that
\overline{A}/m=k
, and
\overline{A}_{m}
denotes the localization of
\overline{A}
at
m
.

The above theorem is a more general version of
[
EL23
, Theorem 4.12]
.
We provide a different proof, which also applies when the valuation is not discrete.

In the context of Theorem
1.6
, the set of zeros
V_{R}(\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\})
is equal to
V_{R}(I)
, where
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
is the ideal generated by the
f_{i}
. Then
\overline{A}
is the reduction of
\mathcal{R}_{n}/I
.
In Section
2
, we show that the finiteness of
\overline{A}
is necessary when
K
is algebraically closed and
I
is replaced by its saturation

(1.8)

I^{\mathrm{ec}}:=\bigl\{f\in\mathcal{R}_{n}:\exists r\in R\setminus\{0\}\,\text{ such that }\,rf\in I\bigr\}.

We then study the computation of such a saturation when the generators
f_{i}
of
I
are known with finite precision, as in arithmetic applications.

In this context, we use the work of Caruso, Vaccon and Verron in
[
CVV19
,
CVV21
,
CVV22
]
, who define Gröbner bases for ideals of convergent power series, studying their properties and proposing algorithms.
Gröbner bases allow us to compute the saturation (
1.8
) in certain cases; moreover, the two notions are closely related (see Remark
3.0.3
).

We also highlight the work of Rabinoff
[
Rab12
]
, which uses the theory of toric varieties and tropical geometry to study the possible valuations of the zeros of a system of power series. Moreover, Theorem
11.7
in loc. cit. gives, in some cases, a formula for the multiplicities of isolated zeros with given valuation in terms of mixed volumes, generalizing the Bernstein–Khovanskii–Kushnirenko theorem (cf.
[
Ber75
,
Kou76
]
).
See also
[
Par16
]
and
[
KRZ18
]
for arithmetic applications.

We conclude this introduction by describing the structure of the paper. In Section
2
we prove Theorem
1.6
using Theorem
2.5
, a dimensionality result holding for all ideals. In Section
3
we describe a procedure to compute the saturation (
1.8
) of an ideal. Finally, in Section
4
we illustrate an application to a Thue equation, by first applying Skolem’s method to bound its solutions with the zeros of a system of power series in two variables, and then bounding such zeros with our results.

3
Computing saturation with approximated information

In this section we restrict to the case where the valuation is discrete, that is, the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
is generated by a single uniformizer
\pi
.
Under this assumption, the rings
\mathcal{R}_{n}
are Noetherian, by
[
GD71
, Chapter 0, Proposition
7.5.4
]
.

Motivated by the results of the previous section, we investigate how to compute generators for the saturation
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
of an ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
. In the context of (convergent) power series, exact computations are obstructed by the infinite memory needed to represent general elements of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. A natural approach is to work with finite
\pi
-adic precision, thus representing a power series in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
as

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+O(\pi^{N})

with
P
a polynomial with coefficients in
R
or in a dense subset (e.g.
\mathbb{Z}
, which is dense in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
).
We are then interested in the following problem.

Problem 3.0.1
.

Suppose that the valuation on
K
is discrete, with uniformizer
\pi
.
Given an ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
, generated by power series
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
, known with precision
O(\pi^{N})
, and given a precision level
M\leq N
, compute, if possible, generators for
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
with precision
O(\pi^{M})
.

It is not always possible to solve the above problem. Even when
M=1\ll N
, there are cases where the information modulo
\pi^{N}
is not enough to determine the reduction of
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
. Consider, for example,
I=(f_{1},f_{2})\subset\mathcal{R}_{2}
with both

f_{1}
and
f_{2}
being equal to
x+O(\pi^{N})
: this approximation might arise from
f_{1}=f_{2}=x
or from
f_{1}=x
and
f_{2}=x+\pi^{a}y
for some integer
a\geq N
; in the first case we would have
\overline{I^{\mathrm{ec}}}=(x)
, while in the second case
\overline{I^{\mathrm{ec}}}=(x,y)
.

Problem
3.0.1
is easy if
I
is generated by only one power series. Suppose
I=(f_{1})
, with
f_{1}=p_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+O(\pi^{N})
for some polynomial
p_{1}
, and let
m
be the minimum valuation among the coefficients of
p_{1}
. Then
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
is determined modulo
\pi^{M}
if and only if
M\leq N-m
, and in that case
I^{\mathrm{ec}}=(f_{1}/\pi^{m})=(p_{1}/\pi^{m}+\mathcal{O}(\pi^{M}))
.

We are especially interested in Problem
3.0.1
when
M=1
, since it allows us to apply Theorem
2.5
, and to check whether
I
has finitely many zeros in
(\mathcal{O}_{K^{c}})^{n}
, with
K^{c}
the completion of an algebraic closure of
K
, and potentially bound the number of zeros using Theorem
1.6
.
This is also desirable in arithmetic applications, such as Chabauty’s and Skolem’s methods, where one bounds the solutions of a Diophantine problem with the zeros of power series, which are usually computed with finite precision.

3.1
Saturation and syzygies

We start by looking at Problem
3.0.1
more “theoretically”, i.e., working with exact power series.
By definition, the saturation
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
contains those power series
g
such that
\pi^{l}g
lies in
I
, and more precisely in
I\cap(\pi^{l})
, for some
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
In other words, if we define the ideals

(3.1.1)

I_{l}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi^{l}}(I\cap\pi^{l}\mathcal{R}_{n})=\left(I:\pi^{l}\mathcal{R}_{n}\right),

we have

(3.1.2)

I=I_{0}\subseteq I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}\subseteq I_{3}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\bigcup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}I_{l}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{n}
is Noetherian, the chain of ideals
\{I_{l}\}_{l}
stabilizes after a finite number of steps. Indeed, it stabilizes at the first index
m
such that
I_{m}=I_{m+1}
since
we have the recursion

(3.1.3)

I_{l+1}=\frac{1}{\pi}(I_{l}\cap\pi\mathcal{R}_{n})=\left(I_{l}:\pi\mathcal{R}_{n}\right).

We can recursively “find” generators for the ideals
I_{l}
using syzygies.
Recall that, given a finitely generated ideal
J
of a ring
A
with a set of generators

\mathcal{G}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
,
we have the (first) module of syzygies

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},\,J}:=\big\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in A^{r}:\,a_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}f_{r}=0\big\}.

Fix generators
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
for
I_{l}
. If a power series
g
lies in
I_{l+1}
,
then we can write
\pi g=a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{r}f_{r}
for suitable
a_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
, which, after reducing modulo
\pi
, implies
that
(\overline{a_{1}},\ldots,\overline{a_{r}})
is a syzygy for
\overline{I_{l}}\subset k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, with generators
\overline{\mathcal{G}}=\{\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}}\}
.
Conversely,
for any syzygy

s=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I_{l}}}

of the reduction
\overline{I_{l}}
,
and for any lift
(\tilde{a}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{r})
of
s
in
(\mathcal{R}_{n})^{r}
, we can define the power series

(3.1.4)

g_{s}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{a}_{i}f_{i},

which belongs to
I_{l+1}
. Notice that the definition of
g_{s}
also depends on the choice of the lifts
\tilde{a}_{i}
, and that
g_{s}
is “new”, i.e.,
g_{s}\notin I_{l}
, if and only if
s
is not the reduction of a syzygy in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},{I_{l}}}
.
The following lemma implies that, after adding sufficiently many such elements

to
I_{l}
, we obtain
I_{l+1}
; this is made precise in Corollary
3.1.6
.

Lemma 3.1.5
.

Suppose that the valuation on
K
is discrete with uniformizer
\pi
. Let
I
be an ideal of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
with generators
\mathcal{G}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
. Let
\overline{I}\subset k[x_{1},\,\dots,\,x_{n}]
be the reduction of

, with generators

\overline{\mathcal{G}}=\{\overline{f_{1}},\dots,\overline{f_{r}}\}
.
Consider the syzygy modules
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}
, and also the reduction

\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},\,I}}\coloneqq\big\{(\overline{a_{1}},\dots,\overline{a_{r}})\,\colon\,(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},\,I}\big\}.

Then we have:

1.

the reduction
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}}
is a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}
and equality holds if and only if
I=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
;

2.

the module
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}
and its quotient
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}/\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}}
are finitely generated

-modules;

3.

given a set of generators

of the quotient
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}/\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}}
, for any choice of functions

\frac{1}{\pi}(I\cap\pi\mathcal{R}_{n})=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{s_{1}},\ldots,g_{s_{t}}).

Proof.

In part
1

one implication follows from part
3
: if the quotient
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}/\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}}
is trivial, then
I=I_{1}
, and by induction, using (

), the whole chain of ideals
I_{0}\subseteq I_{1}\subseteq\ldots
in (

) is constant and equal to
I
.
Conversely, if
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}},\overline{I}}/\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G},I}}
is not trivial, then we can pick a non-zero syzygy
s
in the quotient, so that the series

) belong to

but not to

.

Part

One inclusion in part

and

For the other inclusion take an element

Remark 3.1.7
.

Some of the generators computed by the above algorithm might be redundant, and we might be able to prove it even working with finite precision.
For example, if the first element
g
of
\mathcal{G}_{l-1}
is a multiple of
\pi
, then
s_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
is a syzygy in
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}_{l-1}},\overline{I_{l-1}}}
, and if we take
\tilde{s}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
then
\mathcal{G}_{l}
contains both an approximation of
g
and an approximation
g_{s_{1}}
of
\frac{1}{\pi}g
, but the first can be removed from
\mathcal{G}_{l}
.
We can generalize this observation as follows.

To avoid confusion, given an approximation
g
of a power series in
I_{l}
, we denote by
\widehat{g}
its exact counterpart.
For example, the elements
q_{1}+O(\pi^{N}),\ldots,q_{r}+O(\pi^{N})
in
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{0}
are approximations of certain
\widehat{q_{i}}
. Then, if we choose the lifts
\tilde{a}_{i}
in the algorithm to be polynomials in
A:=R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, we can recursively write the elements
\widehat{p_{j}}
of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
as an (exact) combination of the
\widehat{q_{i}}
’s. In other words, we can compute
b_{ij}\in A
such that

\widehat{p_{j}}=\frac{1}{\pi^{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{ij}\cdot\widehat{q_{i}}.

We then look at the
A
-module
M\subset A^{r}
generated by the vectors
B_{j}=(b_{1j},\ldots,b_{rj})
. If we can generate
M
with fewer generators
B^{\prime}_{j}=(b^{\prime}_{1j},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{rj})
,
then we are also able to generate
I_{l}
with fewer than
\#\mathcal{G}_{l}
power series: a smaller set of generators is given by the series

\widehat{p_{j}^{\prime}}:=\frac{1}{\pi^{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}b^{\prime}_{ij}\widehat{q_{i}}\quad\text{ approximated as }\quad p_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\pi^{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}b^{\prime}_{ij}q_{i}+O(\pi^{N-l}).

For example, in the case
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we can approximate convergent power series with polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and in practice we can check if some of the
B_{j}
’s are redundant using the
mingens
function in Macaulay2
[
GS
]
.

3.2
Some dimensionality results

The above Algorithm
1
would be sufficient to solve Problem
3.0.1
if we knew an index
l\leq N-M
such that
I_{l}=I_{l+1}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
.
We are not aware of any general algorithm to determine whether this happens using approximate information.
For example, since we do not have access to the generators of
I_{l}
with infinite precision, it is in general not possible to compute
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{l},I_{l}}
and to check the termination condition
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}_{l}},\overline{I_{l}}}=\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{l},I_{l}}}
in Lemma
3.1.5
.
Sometimes, though, it happens that
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{l},\overline{I}_{l}}
is as small as possible, and this gives a sufficient criterion for having
I_{l}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
, as in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

Suppose that the valuation on
K
is discrete and let
I
be an ideal of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
generated by
r
power series.

If for some integer
l\geq 0
we have
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}\bigr)<n-r
, then
I^{\mathrm{ec}}=\mathcal{R}_{n}
.
If for some integer
l\geq 0
we have
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}\bigr)=n-r
and
I_{l}
can be generated by
r
power series, then
I_{l}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
.
In particular, if
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I}\bigr)=n-r
, then
I=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
.

Proof.

We first analyze the case
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}\bigr)<n-r
.
The ideal
I^{\mathrm{e}}
is generated by the same
r
generators of
I
, hence the quotient
\mathcal{T}_{n}/I^{\mathrm{e}}
, when it is not the trivial ring, has dimension at least
n-r
by Krull’s Hauptidealsatz, together with the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is regular (see
[
Bos14
, Proposition 17, Chapter 2]
), hence catenary. If, by way of contradiction,
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
is not the full
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, then the quotient
\mathcal{T}_{n}/I^{\mathrm{e}}
is not the trivial ring and, using Theorem
2.5
, we have

\dim\left(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I^{\mathrm{ec}}}\right)=\dim\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}/I^{\mathrm{e}}\right)\geq n-r.

Anyway the ring
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I^{\mathrm{ec}}}
is a quotient of
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}
, hence it has dimension at most
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}\bigr)
which is smaller than
n-r
, a contradiction.

We now turn to the last part of the statement.
It is enough to treat the case
l=0
, i.e.,
I_{l}=I
.
Moreover, it is enough to prove that, for some extension
L\supset K
, with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{L}
, the ideal
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
is saturated in
\mathcal{O}_{L}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
.
Indeed, using that
\mathcal{O}_{L}
is a free
R
-module of finite rank, we can deduce that
(I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L})\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}=I
, hence that
I
is also saturated.
We now look for an extension
L
such that
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
is generated by power series
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
whose reductions modulo
\mfrak
form a regular sequence and, using this hypothesis, we prove that
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
is saturated.

Let
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
be a generating set for
I
, and let
k^{s}
be the separable closure of
k
. First, we prove that the extension
\overline{I}_{k^{s}}
of
\overline{I}
to
k^{s}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
can be generated by a regular sequence
(\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}})

which, up to reordering the
g_{j}
’s, is of the form

(3.2.2)

\overline{f_{i}}=\lambda_{i,\,i}\cdot\overline{g_{i}}+\lambda_{i,\,i+1}\cdot\overline{g_{i+1}}+\ldots+\lambda_{i,\,r}\cdot\overline{g_{r}},

for suitable constants
\lambda_{i,\,j}\in k^{s}
such that
\lambda_{i,i}\neq 0
.

Since
k^{s}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a domain, we set
\overline{f_{1}}=\overline{g_{1}}
which is different from zero, otherwise
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I}\bigr)
would exceed
n-r
. Proceeding by induction, suppose we have constructed a regular sequence
(\overline{f_{1}},\dots,\overline{f_{s}})
as in (
3.2.2
) for some
s<r
. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{a}
be the irreducible components of the subvariety defined by these polynomials. For any polynomial
\overline{f}
, the sequence
(\overline{f_{1}},\dots,\overline{f_{s}},\overline{f})
is regular if and only if
\overline{f}
does not vanish identically on any
C_{i}
. Since
\dim\bigl(k^{s}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{k^{s}}}\bigr)=n-r<n-s
, the polynomials
\overline{g_{s+1}},\dots,\overline{g_{r}}
cannot simultaneously vanish on any
C_{i}
. As
k^{s}
is infinite, there exists a linear combination of them with coefficients in
k^{s}
which does not vanish on all
C_{i}
’s. Up to reordering, we may assume the first coefficient of this combination to be non-zero and define
\overline{f_{s+1}}
as this combination.

Once we have constructed the
\overline{f_{i}}
as in (
3.2.2
), we pick
L
to be a finite extension of
K
whose residue field
k_{L}
contains all the
\lambda_{i,j}
’s. Then, we can lift the polynomials
\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}}
to generators
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
of
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
simply by
choosing lifts
\tilde{\lambda}_{i,j}\in\mathcal{O}_{L}
of
\lambda_{i,j}
and defining

f_{i}\coloneqq\tilde{\lambda}_{i,\,i}\cdot g_{i}+\tilde{\lambda}_{i,\,i+1}\cdot g_{i+1}+\ldots+\tilde{\lambda}_{i,\,r}\cdot g_{r}.

Notice that the
f_{i}
’s generate
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
since the
g_{i}
’s are generators and the matrix
(\tilde{\lambda}_{i,j})
has non-zero determinant modulo
the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{L}
.

Finally we prove that
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
is saturated, using syzygies. Since
\{\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}}\}
is a regular sequence in
k_{L}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
,
[
Eis95
, Corollary
17.5
]
implies that the associated Koszul complex is exact, hence the
(r-1)
-th cohomology group of the Koszul complex vanishes (the Koszul complex of Eisenbud is slightly different from the usual one). It follows that the syzygy module of
\{\overline{f_{1}},\ldots,\overline{f_{r}}\}
is generated by the elements

(0,\ldots,0,-\overline{f_{j}},0,\ldots,0,\overline{f_{i}},0,\ldots,0),\qquad\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq r,

where
-\overline{f_{j}}
is in position
i
and
\overline{f_{i}}
is in position
j
. Since these elements are the reductions of the elements

(0,\ldots,0,-f_{j},0,\ldots,0,f_{i},0,\ldots,0),\qquad\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq r,

in the syzygy module of
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\}
, Lemma
3.1.5
part
1
implies that
I\cdot\mathcal{O}_{L}
is saturated, as desired.
∎

The above statement gives a sufficient criterion to answer Problem
3.0.1
using Algorithm
1
, together with Remark
3.1.7
. For example, if
I
is generated by
r
elements and
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I}
has dimension
n-r
, then
I^{\mathrm{ec}}=I
, which we know with precision
O(\pi^{N})
. A slightly less trivial example is
I=(f_{1},f_{2})
for some
f_{1},f_{2}
known with precision
N\geq 2
, and
f_{2}=gf_{1}+\pi f_{3}+O(\pi^{N})
for some
f_{3}
such that
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(\overline{f_{1}},\overline{f_{3}})
has dimension
n-2
. The algorithm gives
I_{1}=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{\pi}(f_{2}-gf_{1}))

and, by Remark
3.1.7
,
I_{1}
is also generated by
f_{1},f_{3}^{\prime}
; in particular
\overline{I_{1}}=(\overline{f_{1}},\overline{f_{3}^{\prime}})=(\overline{f_{1}},\overline{f_{3}})
, hence, by Proposition
3.2.1
, we have
I^{\mathrm{ec}}=I_{1}
, which we know with precision
O(\pi^{N-1})
.

We now look at an example where Proposition
3.2.1
cannot be applied, even though, starting from approximate information, we are able to compute the reduction of the saturation.

Example 3.2.3
.

Consider an ideal
I=(f_{1},f_{2})\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p}\langle x,y\rangle
with
f_{1}=x^{2}+py+O(p^{2})
and
f_{2}=xy+O(p^{2})
. In other words there exist power series
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\langle x,y\rangle
such that
f_{1}=x^{2}+py+p^{2}g_{1}
and
f_{2}=xy+p^{2}g_{2}
. Then the
syzygy module associated to
\overline{I}=(x^{2},xy)
is generated by
s=(y,-x)
, and consequently

I_{1}=\left(x^{2}+py+p^{2}g_{1},xy+p^{2}g_{2},y^{2}+pyg_{1}-pxg_{2}\right),\quad\text{hence}\quad\overline{I_{1}}=(x^{2},xy,y^{2}).

The syzygy module associated to
\overline{I_{1}}
is generated by the syzygies

(y,-x,0),\quad(y^{2},0,-x^{2}),\quad(0,y,-x),

which all lift to syzygies of
I_{1}
, namely

(y,-x,-p),\quad(y^{2}-pxg_{2},pxg_{1},-x^{2}-py),\quad(-pg_{2},y+pg_{1},-x)

Then, by Corollary
3.1.6
, we have
I_{1}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
. Notice that
\overline{I_{1}}
cannot be generated by
2
elements, but the condition
\dim\bigl(\mathbb{F}_{p}[x,y]/\overline{I_{1}}\bigr)=0=n-r
holds.

Other examples of this type suggest that Proposition
3.2.1
might hold in greater generality. This leads us to formulate the following question:

Question 3.2.4
.

Suppose that the valuation on
K
is discrete.
Is it true that for all ideals
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
generated by
r
power series, if for some
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we have
\dim\bigl(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\overline{I_{l}}\bigr)=n-r
, then
I_{l}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
?

3.3
Saturation and ideals generated by polynomials…

We look at another approach, inspired by Example
3.2.3
. There, we were able to prove that
I_{1}=I^{\mathrm{ec}}
starting from approximate power series simply by giving the name “
g_{i}
” to the information hidden by the approximation.
This suggests
another possible approach to Problem
3.0.1
, namely to treat the information we don’t know as a new variable: if we know the power series
f_{i}
with precision
\pi^{N}
, say
f_{i}=p_{i}+O(\pi^{N})
for certain polynomials
p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we can look at
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{i})=p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+\pi^{N}y_{i}
and try to compute the saturation of the ideal generated by the (exact) power series
f_{i}^{\prime}
. By the following lemma, if the approximated result does not depend on the variables
y_{j}
’s, then the same result also approximates the saturation of
I
.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Suppose that the valuation on
K
is discrete, with uniformizer
\pi
, and let
N
be a positive integer. Let
I=(f_{1},\,\ldots,\,f_{r})
be an ideal of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and let
p_{i}
be polynomials in
R[x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}]
such that

f_{i}\equiv p_{i}\pmod{\pi^{N}}
. Consider the ideal
J=(p_{1}+\pi^{N}y_{1},\ldots,p_{r}+\pi^{N}y_{r})
of
R\langle x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n},\,y_{1},\,\ldots,\,y_{r}\rangle
.

For every positive integer
M
,
if
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
can be generated by power series
h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}
of the form
h_{i}=q_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+O(\pi^{M})
, that is, the polynomials
(h_{i}\bmod\pi^{M})
do not contain the variables
y_{j}
, then
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
can be generated by power series
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
of the form
g_{i}=q_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+O(\pi^{M})
.

Proof.

Denoting by
S_{1},\dots,S_{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
the power series such that

f_{i}=p_{i}+\pi^{N}S_{i},

we prove that
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
is generated by the power series

g_{j}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}):=h_{j}\bigl(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n},\,S_{1}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}),\,\ldots,\,S_{r}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n})\bigr),\qquad 1\leq j\leq s,

which implies our claim.
To prove it, it is enough to show that the ideal
L\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
generated by
g_{1},\,\ldots,\,g_{s}
is saturated and satisfies
I\subseteq L\subseteq I^{\mathrm{ec}}
.

The latter condition is a specialization of the analogous containments
J\subseteq(h_{1},\ldots,h_{s})\subseteq J^{\mathrm{ec}}
.
For example, since
h_{j}\in J^{\mathrm{ec}}
, there exist power series
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}
in
R\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\rangle
and an integer
m
such that

\pi^{m}h_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\cdot\bigl(p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+\pi^{N}y_{i}\bigr).

Specializing
y_{i}
to
S_{i}
, we see that

\pi^{m}g_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{c}_{i}^{\prime}\cdot f_{i}
, with
c^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
the specializations of
c_{i}
, hence that
g_{j}\in I^{\mathrm{ec}}
for every
1\leq j\leq s
.

Now the saturation of
L
. By Lemma
3.1.5
part
1
it is enough to show that all the syzygies of
\{\overline{g_{1}},\ldots,\overline{g_{s}}\}
can be lifted to syzygies of
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
, where
\overline{\cdot}
denotes the reduction modulo
\pi
as usual. This is again a specialization argument. Indeed, since

h_{j}\bmod\pi^{M}
does not involve the variables
y_{h}
, then we have

\overline{g_{j}}=\overline{{h}_{j}}=\overline{q_{j}}.

It follows that any syzygy
\overline{c}=(\overline{c_{1}},\ldots,\overline{c_{s}})
of the generators
\{\overline{g_{1}},\ldots,\overline{g_{s}}\}
of
\overline{L}
is also a syzygy of the generators
\{\overline{h_{1}},\ldots,\overline{h_{s}}\}
of
\overline{J^{\mathrm{ec}}}
. Since
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
is saturated, Lemma
3.1.5
part
1
implies that there exists a syzygy
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})
for
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
in
R\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\rangle
whose reduction is
\overline{c}
. In particular, from
c
we can obtain a lift
c^{\prime}=(c_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,c_{s}^{\prime})
of
\overline{c}
in
\mathcal{S}_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\},L}
by specializing the
y_{i}\mapsto S_{i}
(notice that
\overline{c_{j}}
does not contain the variables
y_{h}
, hence this specialization does not change the reduction)
∎

The primary advantage of
J
over
I
, in Lemma
3.3.1
, is that we know exact generators of it and we can compute its saturation by passing to the polynomial world.
Denote by
\tilde{J}
the ideal of
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]
generated by the polynomials
p_{i}+\pi^{N}y_{i}
, (which are also generators for
J
). Using
[
DVY15
, Algorithm
18
and Theorem
19
]
we can compute generators for its saturation in the polynomial ring

(3.3.2)

\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}:=\left\{f\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]:af\in\tilde{J}\text{ for some }a\in R\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

The next proposition shows that the generators of
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}
generate
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
too.

Proposition 3.3.3
.

Suppose the valuation on
K
is discrete and let
I
be an ideal of
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
If
I
is saturated, i.e.,
I=\bigl(I\cdot K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\bigr)\cap R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, then its extension
I\cdot\mathcal{R}_{n}
is saturated in
\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

Proof.

Once we choose a set
\mathcal{G}
of generators for
I
, the statement follows from Lemma
3.1.5
. Indeed
I\cdot\mathcal{R}_{n}
is also generated by
\mathcal{G}
and, denoting by
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
the set of reductions of the elements of
\mathcal{G}
, any syzygy of
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
can be lifted to a syzygy of
\mathcal{G}
since
I
is saturated.
∎

3.4
… and reduced Gröbner bases

We keep the setting of Lemma
3.3.1
.
If we can find generators for
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}
(defined in Equation (
3.3.2
)) whose reduction modulo
\pi^{M}
are expressible only using the variables
x_{i}
, then Proposition
3.3.3
, together with Lemma
3.3.1
, tells us there is a set of generators for
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
with the same reductions modulo
\pi^{M}
, thus solving Problem
3.0.1
. It might happen, though, that
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}
does not have a set of generators with this property, while
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
does, e.g., if
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}=(1+\pi y_{1})
, which gives
J^{\mathrm{ec}}=(1)
.

To really work with
J^{\mathrm{ec}}\subset R\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\,y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\rangle
, we can use Gröbner bases in the context of convergent power series, as defined in
[
CVV19
]
.
In loc. cit., for any monomial order
<_{\omega}
(a well-order on monomials compatible with multiplication) the authors define an order
<
on terms
a\cdot m
, with
a\in K^{\times}
and
m
a monomial, by first looking at the valuation of
a
and then at the monomial
m
; in particular
am<a^{\prime}m^{\prime}
either if
v(a)>v(a^{\prime})
or if
v(a)=v(a^{\prime})
and
m<_{\omega}m^{\prime}
. In particular, convergent power series
f
always have a leading (i.e. maximal) term
\mathrm{LT}(f)
and a Gröbner basis of an ideal
I
is a set of elements
f_{i}\in I
such that, for each
f\in I
, the leading term
\mathrm{LT}(f)
is a multiple of at least one of the
\mathrm{LT}(f_{i})
’s. We refer to the original article for properties and algorithms, and in particular to
[
CVV19
, Algorithms 1 and 2]
, namely the analogues of division with remainder and Buchberger’s algorithm.

To achieve uniqueness we give the following definition.

Definition 3.4.1
.

A Gröbner basis
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{u}\}
of an ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
is
reduced
if, for each
i
, the series
g_{i}
is monic (i.e.
\mathrm{LT}(g_{i})=1\cdot m
with
m
a monomial), no term of
g_{i}
is a multiple of
\mathrm{LT}(g_{j})
for
j\neq i
, and moreover no term of
g_{i}-\mathrm{LT}(g_{i})
is a multiple of
\mathrm{LT}(g_{i})
.

Notice that the last condition is automatic in the usual polynomial context, since if one monomial divides another, then the latter is bigger. This is not always true in the context of convergent power series: for example, in the series
f=x+\pi xy
, the term
\pi xy
is multiple of the term
x
, even though we have
\pi xy<x
(no matter which is the monomial order we started with). Notice, also, that this “problem” can be solved by dividing
f
by the unit
(1+\pi y)
.

A reduced Gröbner basis is always minimal, in the sense of
[
CVV19
]
, i.e., the leading terms of the
g_{i}
’s are the skeleton of
\mathrm{LT}(I)
(see Definition 2.9 in loc. cit.), or, equivalently, the leading terms of the
g_{i}
’s do not divide each other.
This easily implies the uniqueness of reduced Gröbner bases. Suppose
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
are two such distinct bases of the same ideal; then by the previous observation, for all
f\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
there is a
g\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
with the same leading term, and conversely. If we take
f
in
\mathcal{G}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{2}
, then
f-g
is non-zero and in particular its leading term is a multiple of one of the leading terms in
\mathrm{LT}(\mathcal{G}_{1})=\mathrm{LT}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
. Anyway, since the leading terms of
f
and
g
cancel out, then
\mathrm{LT}(f-g)
is either a term of
f-\mathrm{LT}(f)
or a term of
g-\mathrm{LT}(g)
, contradicting the reducedness.
Notice that, without the last condition in Definition
3.4.1
, uniqueness would not be guaranteed, consider for example the ideal
(x_{1},\,x_{2})=(x_{1},\,x_{2}+\pi x_{2})
.

We now see how to find the reduced Gröbner basis of a saturated ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
starting from a minimal Gröbner basis
g_{1},\ldots,g_{u}
by applying the division algorithm
[
CVV19
, Algorithm 1]
.
We start “reducing”
g_{1}
as follows: we apply the algorithm to
g_{1}-\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})
, dividing it by
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{u}
and finding power series
q_{1},\ldots,q_{u},r_{1}
such that

(3.4.2)

g_{1}-\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})=q_{1}g_{1}+\ldots q_{u}g_{u}+r_{1},

with
r_{1}
only containing terms that are not multiple of any
\mathrm{LT}(g_{i})
.
In particular, the power series

g_{1}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})+r_{1},

still belongs to the ideal
I
and, together with
g_{2},\ldots,g_{u}
, gives a minimal Gröbner basis of
I
, since
\mathrm{LT}(g_{1}^{\prime})=\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})
by construction. Since
I
is saturated and
g_{1}^{\prime}
is part of a minimal basis, we deduce that
\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})=c_{1}\cdot m
, with
m
a monomial and
c_{1}
an invertible element in
R
, hence we can make
g_{1}^{\prime}
monic by dividing by
c_{1}
.
Moreover, no term of
g_{1}^{\prime}-\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})=r_{1}
is a multiple of
\mathrm{LT}(g_{1}^{\prime})
and
g_{1}^{\prime}
has no term multiple of
\mathrm{LT}(g_{j})
for
j\neq 1
(it is true for the terms of
r_{1}
by construction and, by minimality, also for
\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})
).
If we continue, and successively substitute
g_{i}
with
\frac{1}{c_{i}}(\mathrm{LT}(g_{i})+r_{i})
, with the analogous
c_{i}
and
r_{i}
, we find a reduced Gröbner basis of
I
.

We have then proved the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4.3
.

For each saturated ideal
I
of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, there exists a unique reduced Gröbner basis for a fixed monomial order. Moreover, there exists an algorithm to compute such a basis, starting from a minimal Gröbner basis of
I
.

It should be noted that Algorithm 1 in
[
CVV19
]
does not terminate in a finite amount of time, but, as observed in loc. cit., it is possible to compute the result (i.e., the power series
q_{1},\ldots,q_{u},r_{1}
in (
3.4.2
)) with a fixed precision
O(\pi^{M})
in a finite amount of time, starting from an input known with the same precision
O(\pi^{M})
. Hence, we can compute the reduced Gröbner basis of a saturated ideal with any fixed precision in a finite amount of time, starting from a minimal Gröbner basis known with the same precision.

Reduced Gröbner bases are useful, in the context of Lemma
3.3.1
, because of the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4.4
.

Suppose the valuation on
K
is discrete, let
J=J^{\mathrm{ec}}
be a saturated ideal of
R\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\rangle
and
M
be a positive integer. The ideal
J
admits a set of generators that, reduced modulo
\pi^{M}
, does not depend on the variables
y_{j}
if and only if the reduced Gröbner basis of
J
, reduced modulo
\pi^{M}
, does not depend on the variables
y_{j}
.

Proof.

One implication is true, since by
[
CVV19
, Proposition
2.18
]
Gröbner bases are sets of generators.
For the other implication, if
\mathcal{G}
is a set of generators of
J
that does not depend on the variables
y_{j}
, then applying
[
CVV19
, Algorithm
2
]
to
\mathcal{G}
yields a Gröbner basis with the same property and, after extracting a minimal basis and applying the reduction process described above (in particular
[
CVV19
, Algorithm
1
]
), the property is preserved.
∎

The above proposition tells us that the condition on
J^{\mathrm{ec}}\bmod{\pi^{M}}
in Lemma
3.3.1
can be checked using the reduced Gröbner basis, which we know how to compute.
This gives an algorithm for Problem
3.0.1
: we can pass from the ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
, which we know with finite approximation, to the ideal
J\subset\mathcal{R}_{n+r}
defined in Lemma
3.3.1
, then compute a reduced Gröbner basis
\mathcal{G}
for
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
with precision
O(\pi^{M})
, and, if the condition in the lemma is satisfied, return
\mathcal{G}
. The lemma ensures that such an output is correct, that is that
\mathcal{G}
approximates a set of generators of
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
. This is made precise in Algorithm
2
.

Input :
A positive integer
M

and a set
\bigl\{p_{1}+O(\pi^{N}),\ldots,p_{r}+O(\pi^{N})\bigr\}
of generators of an ideal
I\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

Output :
FAIL or a set of generators of
I^{\mathrm{ec}}
known with precision
O(\pi^{M})
.

Compute generators for the saturation
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}
, inside
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]
, of the ideal
\tilde{J}=(p_{1}+\pi^{N}y_{1},\ldots,p_{r}+\pi^{N}y_{r})
(see
[
DVY15
, Algorithm
18
]
);

Choose a monomial order and compute, with precision
O(\pi^{M})
, a Gröbner basis
\mathcal{G}
of
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}\cdot R\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\rangle
(see
[
CVV19
, Algorithm
2
]
);

for each

g\in\mathcal{G}

do

if
g=O(\pi^{M})
or if
\mathrm{LT}(g)
is multiple of
\mathrm{LT}(g^{\prime})
for some

, then delete
g
from
\mathcal{G}
;

for each

g\in\mathcal{G}

do

Compute, with precision
O(\pi^{M})
, the remainder
r_{g}
of the division of
g-\mathrm{LT}(g)
by
\mathcal{G}
(see
[
CVV19
, Algorithm
1
]
);

Compute the coefficient
c
of the leading term of
g
;

if

\bigl(\tfrac{1}{c}\big(\mathrm{LT}(g)+r_{g}\big)\bmod{\pi^{M}}\bigr)
contains some variable
y_{i}

then

return

FAIL;

else

Replace
g
with
\tfrac{1}{c}\bigl(\mathrm{LT}(g)+r_{g}\bigr)+O(\pi^{M})
in
\mathcal{G}
;

\mathcal{G}
;

Algorithm 2

Either FAIL or solve Problem
3.0.1

In more detail, the algorithm proceeds as follows. Using an algorithm for polynomial rings, we compute generators for the ideal
\tilde{J}^{\mathrm{ec}}
in Equation (
3.3.2
). These are also generators for
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
(Proposition
3.3.3
). Then, using Algorithm
2
in
[
CVV19
]
(the analogue of Buchberger’s algorithm), we compute a Gröbner basis for the power series ideal
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
with precision
O(\pi^{M})
;
since we work with finite precision and by
[
CVV19
, Theorem 3.8]
,
Buchberger’s algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps and gives a correct result.

The next step in the algorithm is to extract a minimal Gröbner basis of
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
from this Gröbner basis. We do so either by checking directly the minimality condition or, when it is not possible, namely if a series
g
is equal
O(\pi^{M})
and consequently we cannot compute its leading term, we just delete the series since, by the saturatedness of
J^{\mathrm{ec}}
, all elements of a minimal basis have leading term with valuation
0
.

Finally, we reduce the Gröbner basis using the procedure described below Definition
3.4.1
.
During the reduction process we check whether the condition in Lemma
3.3.1
is satisfied: if the reduction modulo
\pi^{M}
of some element in the reduced basis depends on some variable
y_{j}
, then we cannot apply Lemma
3.3.1
and the algorithm fails; otherwise, we output the approximation of the reduced basis itself.

4
An application to a Thue equation

In this final section, we show an application of Theorem
1.6
, together with Algorithm
1
,
solving the Thue equation

(4.1)

X^{5}-X^{4}Y+X^{3}Y^{2}-X^{2}Y^{3}-Y^{5}=1

over the integers using Skolem’s method (see, e.g.,
[
Sko34
,
ST88
,
Lom22
]
). We recall that, despite Skolem’s method being a fun application of
p
-adic analysis, there are faster and more general methods to solve Thue equations, see e.g.
[
BH96
,
Wal20
]
, and, for Thue equations in families,
[
AMZ24
,
Mar25
]
.

Equation
4.1
is associated to the number field

K\coloneqq\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]/(\alpha^{5}-\alpha^{4}+\alpha^{3}-\alpha^{2}-1),

since (
4.1
) is equivalent to

(4.2)

\mathrm{Norm}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(X-Y\alpha)=1.

Since
K
has degree
5
and the group of units of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
has rank
2
, it is possible to apply Skolem’s method, which, for any chosen prime
p
, relates solutions of (
4.1
) to zeros of
p
-adic power series. We choose
p=5
and go through the method.

We have performed computations using a MAGMA (
[
BCP97
]
) script which applies Skolem’s method to Thue equations related to quintic number fields of signature
(1,2)
, assuming that the fundamental units are known. The script is attached to the arXiv version of this paper.

If
(x,y)
is an integer solution of (
4.2
), there exist two integers
n_{1},\,n_{2}
such that

(4.3)

x-y\alpha=\pm u_{1}^{n_{1}}u_{2}^{n_{2}},

where

u_{1}\coloneqq\alpha^{4}-\alpha^{3}+\alpha^{2}-\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad u_{2}\coloneqq\alpha^{3}-\alpha^{2}-1

are the fundamental units of
K
(see
https://www.lmfdb.org/NumberField/5.1.2297.1
in the
[
LMFDB
]
database).
The idea of Skolem’s method is to embed
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
in
\mathbb{Z}_{5}[\alpha]:=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{5}
: in this ambient space, the integer solutions of (
4.1
) lie in the intersection
B\,\cap\,\overline{C}
, where
B
is the
\mathbb{Z}_{5}
-subspace
spanned by
1
and
\alpha
, and
\overline{C}
is the
5
-adic closure
of the set
C\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]^{\times}=\{\pm u_{1}^{n_{1}}u_{2}^{n_{2}}\,\colon\,n_{1},\,n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Since
C
is parametrized by two variables, we expect
\overline{C}
to have dimension at most
2
, hence the intersection
B\,\cap\,\overline{C}
to be finite.

Given an integer solution of (
4.1
), there are, a priori,
25
possibilities for the pair
(x\bmod 5,y\bmod 5)
; accordingly, we separately study the
25
intersections
B\cap\overline{C}\cap U_{a,\,b}
, where
U_{a,\,b}
is the open subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{5}[\alpha]
consisting of elements congruent to
a-b\alpha
modulo
5
. Some residue disks are not relevant since the congruence modulo
5
already shows that (
4.1
) cannot hold for some pairs
(x\bmod 5,y\bmod 5)
. We are left with five pairs
(a,b)
to analyze, namely

(1,0)
,
(0,4)
,
(4,4)
,
(3,4)
,
(2,4)
.

We now focus on the case
(a,b)=(0,4)
.
We start by parametrizing the set

C\cap U_{0,4}
, i.e., the units of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
that are congruent to
\alpha
modulo
5
. The set of units congruent to
1
modulo
5
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]^{\times}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with generators

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 5\alpha^{4}-5\alpha^{3}+5\alpha^{2}-5\alpha-4,

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-17490060\alpha^{4}+27069090\alpha^{3}+18075\alpha^{2}+5549965\alpha-17135289.

Then,
C\cap U_{0,4}
is the coset

(4.4)

u\cdot\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=\{uv_{1}^{n_{1}}v_{2}^{n_{2}}\,:\,n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\},

with
u=9579030\alpha^{4}+17508135\alpha^{3}-11940095\alpha^{2}-17135289\alpha-17490060
.
Now, since both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are congruent to
1
modulo
5
, the function
(n_{1},n_{2})\mapsto u\cdot v_{1}^{n_{1}}v_{2}^{n_{2}}
can be expressed as a power series in
n_{1},n_{2}
. Indeed, denoting by
\exp
and
\log
the usual power series for the exponential and the logarithm, then
\exp(x)
and
\log(1+x)
converge
5
-adically for
x
multiple of
5
. In particular, we can compute
\log(v_{1})
and
\log(v_{2})
, which are elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{5}[\alpha]
that are congruent to
0
modulo
5
, and write

(4.5)

u\cdot v_{1}^{n_{1}}v_{2}^{n_{2}}=u\cdot\exp\bigl(n_{1}\cdot\log(v_{1})+n_{2}\cdot\log(v_{2})\bigr)=\sum_{i=0}^{4}F_{i}(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot\alpha^{i}

for suitable convergent power series
F_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{5}\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle
.
Then, we have

C\cap B\cap U_{0,4}=\left\{uv_{1}^{n_{1}}v_{2}^{n_{2}}\,:\,n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},\,F_{2}(n_{1},\,n_{2})=F_{3}(n_{1},\,n_{2})=F_{4}(n_{1},\,n_{2})=0\right\}.

It follows that the set of pairs
(n_{1},n_{2})
we look for is a subset of the common zeros of the power series
F_{2},\,F_{3},\,F_{4}
in
(\mathbb{Z}_{5})^{2}
. Notice that such common zeros correspond to the points in
\overline{C}\cap B\cap U_{0,4}
, since, when we look at
(n_{1},n_{2})
in
\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{2}
, and not only in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then formula (
4.5
) parameterizes
\overline{C}\cap U_{0,4}
.
Without changing the notation, we multiply each
F_{i}
by a suitable constant so that it has
5
-integral coefficients not all divisible by
5
(this is possible since the
F_{i}
are convergent power series). Then

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=108+3t_{2}+5(13t_{1}+9t_{1}^{2}+7t_{1}t_{2}+24t_{2}^{2})+5^{2}(3t_{1}^{3}+4t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+3t_{1}t_{2}^{2}+t_{2}^{3})+5^{3}(\cdots),

\displaystyle F_{3}

\displaystyle=61+81t_{2}+5(t_{1}^{2}+23t_{1}t_{2}+9t_{2}^{2})+5^{2}(2t_{1}^{3}+3t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+t_{2}^{3})+5^{3}(\cdots),

\displaystyle F_{4}

\displaystyle=83+93t_{2}+5(12t_{1}+9t_{1}^{2}+16t_{1}t_{2}+7t_{2}^{2})+5^{2}(t_{1}^{3}+3t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}^{2}+t_{2}^{3})+5^{3}(\cdots).

Let
I
be the ideal of
\mathbb{Z}_{5}\langle t_{1},t_{2}\rangle
generated by
F_{2},F_{3},F_{4}
and let
\overline{I}
be its reduction modulo
5
. Since

\Fbb_{5}[t_{1},\,t_{2}]\bigl/\overline{I}=\Fbb_{5}[t_{1},\,t_{2}]\bigl/\bigl(\overline{F_{2}},\,\overline{F_{3}},\,\overline{F_{4}}\bigr)=\Fbb_{5}[t_{1},\,t_{2}]\bigl/\bigl(t_{2}+1\bigr)\cong\Fbb_{5}[t_{1}]

is not finite, we cannot apply Theorem
1.6
. Applying the first step of the saturation process (see Equation (
3.1.1
) and Algorithm
1
), we see that the ideal
I_{1}
, which has the same common zeros as
I
, is generated, modulo
5
, by
t_{2}+1
and
t_{1}^{2}-t_{1}
. Since

\Fbb_{5}[t_{1},\,t_{2}]/(t_{2}+1,\,t_{1}^{2}-t_{1})\cong\Fbb_{5}\times\mathbb{F}_{5}\text{,}

Theorem
1.6
implies that the number of
5
-adic integer common zeros of the power series
F_{2},\,F_{3},\,F_{4}
is at most
2
. It follows that the intersection
B\,\cap\,C\,\cap U_{0,\,4}
contains at most two points, and in particular that (
4.1
) has at most two solutions
(x,\,y)
congruent to
(0,\,4)
modulo
5
. Indeed we know two solutions, which are
(0,\,-1)
and
(5,\,4)
.

Repeating this process for each residue polydisk, we conclude that the number of integer solutions to (
4.1
) is at most
4
.
On the other hand, we know four solutions, namely

(-1,-1),\,(0,-1),\,(1,0),\,(5,4).

Thus, these are all the solutions to our Thue equation.
