How to Treat Adjacent-Segment Pathology Following Lumbar Fusion Surgery: Decompression Alone or Decompression With Extended Fusion Surgery?
Authors: Mohamed Elmekaty, Anders Joelson, Peter Försth, Paul Gerdhem, Nils P Hailer, Yohan Robinson, Anna MacDowall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to compare the risk of subsequent surgery following surgical treatment of clinical adjacent-segment pathology (CASP) after lumbar fusion, specifically evaluating decompression alone versus decompression with extension of the fusion. MethodsUsing the Swespine registry, we included patients who had surgical treatment for CASP following index lumbar fusion surgery, 1997–2019. The cohort was divided according to CASP treatment: decompression alone or decompression with extended fusion. A comparison of subsequent surgeries was made with Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regressions adjusted for potential confounders (age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, number of operated levels at CASP surgery, number of fused levels at index fusion surgery, and diagnosis for CASP surgery). ResultsA total of 2,521 patients were included, 1,176 (47%) in the decompression-group and 1,345 (53%) in the extended fusion group and the mean follow-up was 6.1±4.4 years. Mean age at CASP surgery was 61 years and 1,588 (63%) were females. The median time to subsequent surgery was 1.7 years (range, 0.9–3.7 years). Kaplan-Meier–estimated probability of subsequent surgeries at 5 years was 18% (95% confidence interval [CI], 15%–20%) for the decompression-group and 25% (95% CI, 22%–27%) for the extended fusion group. The hazard ratio was 1.26 (95% CI, 1.02–1.56; p=0.032) for those with extended fusion. ConclusionExtension of fusion for CASP was associated with significantly higher risk of subsequent surgery, but causality should be interpreted with caution as radiographic information on instability, deformity, or foraminal stenosis could not be verified.Keywords: Adjacent-segment pathology, Lumbar fusion, Decompression surgery, Extended fusion, Spine surgery, Reoperation, Registry study

INTRODUCTION

Adjacent-segment pathology (ASP) is a well-known yet challenging long-term complication following lumbar spine fusion surgery. It includes both radiographic ASP (RASP), defined as degenerative changes observed on imaging without clinical symptoms, and clinical ASP (CASP), which refers to (RASP) associated with symptomatic deterioration at adjacent spinal levels [1,2].

The reported incidence of ASP varies widely depending on study definitions, methodologies, and follow-up duration. RASP is common, with incidence rates ranging from 8% to 100%, CASP occurs less frequently, with reported rates of approximately 5.2% to 18.5% [2,3].

Furthermore, the incidence of revision surgery for ASP is relatively low, ranging from 2% to 15%, suggesting that although radiographic degenerative changes at adjacent segments are frequently observed after lumbar fusion, only a small proportion progress to clinically significant disease requiring surgical intervention [2].

Despite its prevalence, the optimal surgical strategy for CASP is still debated. While some surgeons recommend only decompression, others would prefer decompression with extended fusion. The comparative efficacy of these 2 surgical approaches particularly in terms of clinical success, long-term outcomes, and the need for subsequent surgeries at the CASP surgery level and/or new adjacent levels, remain uncertain.

In selected patients with adjacent-segment stenosis occurring after previous lumbar fusion, decompression alone shows no inferiority compared with decompression with extended fusion in terms of subsequent surgery rates and patient-reported outcomes at short-term follow-up [4]. Its shorter operative duration and decreased length of stay favour decompression alone, particularly in elderly populations in absence of spinal instability, deformity, or marked neuroforaminal stenosis [4].

Across the broader literature on lumbar spinal stenosis, the addition of fusion to decompression, at the index surgery, does not improve clinical outcomes compared with decompression alone, whereas decompression alone is associated with lower operative morbidity and faster recovery [5-7].

However, the main limitation of decompression alone is the risk of same-level restenosis, as persistent motion at the segment may progress the degeneration and induce recurrent stenosis, particularly if decompression is insufficient. In contrast, fusion provides immobilization at the symptomatic level, reducing the risk of same-level recurrence, but shifts subsequent degeneration and increases mechanical stress toward adjacent level, thereby elevating the risk of ASP [6,8-10].

This study therefore aimed to compare the incidence and types of subsequent surgeries between patients treated with decompression alone versus patients treated with decompression and extended fusion, for CASP following lumbar spine index fusion, using data from the Swedish Spine Registry (Swespine).

We hypothesize that patients treated for CASP with decompression alone have a lower risk of subsequent surgery compared with those treated with decompression and extended fusion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Swedish Ethics Review Board (registration number: 2019-02160) and is reported according to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) Statement.

The following terminology is used consistently throughout the manuscript. Index fusion refers to the primary lumbar fusion procedure. CASP surgery refers to the first revision surgery performed for CASP following the index fusion. Recurrent CASP refers to a type of subsequent surgery performed for CASP occurring at a new level adjacent to the previously treated CASP segment. Subsequent surgery refers to any lumbar surgical procedure performed after the index CASP surgery, including (1) surgery at the previously treated CASP level (same-level failure), (2) surgery for CASP at a newly adjacent segment (recurrent CASP), and (3) any additional surgery performed at either the previously treated CASP level or a recurrent CASP level for any reason, including implant-related procedures and perioperative reoperations for complications such as hematoma, infection, or dural tear.

1. Data Source

Swespine was established in the late 1990s and 46 out of 47 spinal surgery units are participating. The registry is publicly funded, governed by the Swedish Society of Spinal Surgeons, and covers approximately 86% of all spine surgeries [11]. Patients complete baseline and follow-up questionnaires, including patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and complications, without assistance from the surgeon. Surgeons record indications for surgery, surgical technique, implants, peri- and postoperative complications, and subsequent surgeries. All individuals in Sweden have a unique social security number, enabling nationwide follow-up within the registry and minimizing referral bias [12].

Swespine records all subsequent surgical procedures related to a given index operation. These include early reoperations performed during the same hospital admission (e.g., for hematoma, infection, dural tear, or implant malposition) as well as late reoperations after discharge, such as those for restenosis, pseudoarthrosis, implant-related complications, drainage of hematoma or infection, or dural tear repair. Subsequent procedures may involve either the index surgical level or a newly affected lumbar level.

2. Study Population

This is a cohort study using prospectively collected data from Swespine. Observations were censored on January 11th 2024 or the date of death.

1) Inclusion criteria

All adults, >18 years old, with index lumbar fusion surgeries for degenerative diseases from June 5th 1997, until January 10th 2019, were analysed and followed up until January 11th 2024. Although the cohort included patients who underwent index fusion from 1997 onwards, temporal stratification was defined according to the year of CASP surgery. As CASP typically occurred several years after the index procedure, the first temporal interval therefore began in 2001. All included patients had at least 5 years of follow-up after the index fusion surgery. This requirement was implemented to ensure adequate observation time for the potential development of CASP during follow-up, including cases occurring early after the index fusion surgery. Patients who were operated for CASP following index lumbar spine fusion by either decompression alone or decompression with extended fusion were included in this study regardless of diagnosis or number of operated levels at the index fusion surgery or CASP surgery to get a comprehensive overview of subsequent surgeries for each treatment method. All types of subsequent surgeries were included in the analysis to capture the complete spectrum of reoperations and to provide a comprehensive assessment of the overall surgical failure burden following CASP surgery.

3. Variables

1) Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was any type of subsequent lumbar surgery performed after CASP surgery, comparing patients treated with decompression alone versus decompression with extension of fusion for CASP following a prior lumbar index fusion.

A manual scan of the Swespine data was performed by the first author (ME), and data validation was confirmed by the second author (AJ), to identify patients operated for CASP and all subsequent surgeries. The results were identical, confirming the accuracy of the data.

The subsequent surgeries were divided into the following groups:

2) Potential confounders

Potential confounders were identified with support of a directed acyclic graph and a review of the literature. The covariates adjusted for were age at CASP surgery, sex, body mass index (BMI) at CASP surgery, smoking status at CASP surgery, number of operated levels at CASP surgery, number of fused levels at index fusion surgery, and the registered diagnosis for CASP surgery. An overview of the included covariates and their expected influence on treatment choice and risk of subsequent surgery is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

4. Statistical Analysis

The data are presented according to whether they are numerical or categorical, and whether they are normally distributed or not, as follows: number with percentage in parentheses, mean with standard deviation in parentheses, median with interquartile range in parentheses.

Missing values for weight and height were imputed using each individual’s mean from other measurement occasions; no other variables were imputed. Despite this, BMI at CASP surgery remained missing in 26.7% of patients, and smoking status at CASP surgery was missing in 37.3% of patients, while all other covariates had complete data. In the adjusted Cox model, missing values for BMI and smoking were retained as separate categories, allowing inclusion of all patients and events in the multivariable analysis. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to compare the probabilities of any type of subsequent surgeries following CASP surgery in the decompression alone group and decompression with extended fusion group. In the survival analyses, only the first subsequent surgery after CASP was counted as an event. Additional procedures in the same patient were included solely in the descriptive analyses of surgery types. Recurrent-event analyses were not performed. As a sensitivity analysis, the cumulative incidence of subsequent surgery was also evaluated with death treated as a competing event.

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, adjusted for the potentially associated confounders (age at CASP surgery, sex, BMI at CASP surgery, smoking status at CASP surgery, number of operated levels at CASP surgery, number of fused levels at index fusion surgery, and the registered diagnosis for CASP surgery) was performed to estimate the association of the type of surgical method at CASP surgery with the risk of subsequent surgeries. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated for all variables and was fulfilled for all covariates except the number of operated levels at CASP surgery, which was therefore handled by stratification in the adjusted Cox model.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated and statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

The exposure was defined as the treatment modality for CASP, decompression alone or decompression with extended fusion. The end of observation was January 11th 2024 or death.

Additionally, a descriptive presentation was made of the total number of subsequent surgeries of each type for both treatment groups, as well as the number of subsequent surgeries specifically related to the first CASP procedure. In this summation, all events were included to capture that some patients developed recurrent CASP at a new adjacent level, which was occasionally treated with a different surgical method than that used in the first CASP surgery, resulting in some crossover between treatment groups.

All statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Temporal trends in surgical management were analysed by dividing the study period into 3 intervals: 2001–2008, 2009–2016, and 2017–2023. During 2001–2008, a total of 231 patients underwent surgery for CASP, of whom 75 were treated with decompression alone and 156 with extended fusion. Between 2009 and 2016, 1,234 patients were operated on, including 572 with decompression and 662 with extended fusion. In the most recent period (2017–2023), 1,056 CASP procedures were performed, with 529 decompressions and 527 extended fusions. Overall, there was a modest temporal shift towards a relatively lower proportion of extended fusion procedures compared with decompression in more recent years.

2. Incidence of Subsequent Surgeries

The median time to the first subsequent surgery was 1.7 years (interquartile range, 0.9–3.7 years). The Kaplan-Meier–estimated probability of subsequent surgery was lower in the decompression alone group compared with the extended fusion group. At 2 years, the rates were 11% (95% CI, 9%–13%) for the decompression alone group and 16% (95% CI, 14%–18%) for the extended fusion group. At 5 years, the corresponding rates were 18% (95% CI, 15%–20%) and 25% (95% CI, 22%–27%), respectively. At 10 years, the rates were 23% (95% CI, 20%–26%) and 31% (95% CI, 28%–34%), respectively (Table 4; Fig. 2).

The cumulative incidence analysis accounting for death as a competing event was largely comparable to the conventional estimates, suggesting that competing mortality had minimal impact on the observed differences between treatment groups.

The adjusted Cox regression analysis included 2,521 patients, of whom 580 (23%) experienced a subsequent surgery event, and was adjusted for relevant potential confounders (age at CASP surgery, sex, BMI at CASP surgery, smoking status at CASP surgery, number of operated levels at CASP surgery, number of fused levels at index fusion surgery, and the registered diagnosis for CASP surgery). The extended fusion group had significantly higher risk for subsequent surgeries compared with the decompression alone group (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.02–1.56; p=0.032).

3. Types and Distribution of Subsequent Surgeries Following CASP Surgery

The total number of all types of subsequent surgeries was 284 events in 223 individuals (19.0%), in the decompression alone group and 511 events in 357 individuals (26.5%), in the extended fusion group. The range of subsequent surgeries was 1–4 times in the decompression alone group and 1–8 times in the extended fusion group.

Surgeries for recurrent CASP were observed in 92 patients (7.8%) in the decompression alone group and 169 patients (12.6%) in the extended fusion group. Decompression was converted to fusion due to restenosis or instability, 91 times, affecting 88 patients (7.5%) in the decompression alone group and, 4 times, affecting 4 patients (0.3%) in the extended fusion group. Redecompression due to residual or re-stenosis was observed 48 times, affecting 44 patients (3.7%) in the decompression-alone group and 14 times, affecting 13 patients (1.0%) in the extended fusion group. Refusion due to pseudoarthrosis, implant loosening, or restenosis was observed 5 times, affecting 5 patients (0.4%) in the decompression alone group and 103 times, affecting 87 patients (6.5%) in extended fusion group.

DISCUSSION

The present registry-based study examined the risk of subsequent surgery following decompression alone and decompression with extension of fusion for CASP after index lumbar fusion, addressing an important area where comparative long-term evidence remains limited.

Our main finding is that the risk of subsequent surgery is significantly higher after decompression with extension of fusion than after decompression alone. The Kaplan-Meier estimates demonstrated a gradual increase in the cumulative probability of subsequent surgeries over time in both treatment groups, but the risk was consistently higher in the extended fusion group.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the limited comparative literature specifically addressing revision surgery for symptomatic ASP after lumbar fusion. Früh et al. [4] reported that, in patients with adjacent-segment stenosis after lumbar fusion and without obvious signs of instability, radiographic deformity, or significant neuroforaminal stenosis, decompression alone was not inferior to decompression with fusion in terms of reoperation rates or short-term PROMs. In addition, shorter operative time and shorter hospital stay favored decompression alone. This is particularly relevant because CASP frequently occurs in older patients with multiple comorbidities, in whom avoidance of a more extensive reconstructive procedure may offer important peri- and postoperative benefits.

It should be acknowledged that the composite outcome in the present study includes events such as implant removal and refusion that are exclusive to the extended fusion group. To assess whether this structural asymmetry influenced the findings, we examined the distribution of subsequent surgery types descriptively.

More importantly, the modes of failure in this study differed substantially between the 2 groups. Reoperations after decompression with extended fusion were more frequently associated with recurrent CASP at a new adjacent level, whereas reoperations following decompression alone were predominantly due to same-level failure as restenosis or persistent stenosis at the treated CASP level, sometimes requiring redecompression or later conversion to fusion. These events occurred in both groups rather than being driven by instrumentation-related procedures. Overall, these findings may not be explained by instrumentationrelated burden alone but may also reflect underlying disease progression and biomechanical factors.

This distinction is clinically relevant, as it shifts the focus from overall revision rates to the anatomical site and mechanism of later failure associated with each procedure. Extended fusion may protect the treated level but appears to transfer risk to the next adjacent segment, while decompression alone allow degeneration to progress at the same level causing same-level failure. This pattern aligns with the existing literature describing differential rates of same-level restenosis compared with ASP following lumbar spine surgery [6,8-10].

The broader literature on lumbar spinal stenosis, although not specific to CASP, further supports this interpretation. In a randomized trial by Karlsson et al., decompression alone was compared with decompression and fusion in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, with and without degenerative spondylolisthesis, over both short-term (2-year magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) and long-term (5-year clinical) follow-up. Across both studies, the addition of fusion did not demonstrate superior clinical outcomes or reoperation rates compared with decompression alone. At 2 years, fusion was associated with a significantly higher rate of new stenosis at the proximal adjacent segment on MRI, whereas decompression alone showed a higher rate of restenosis at the index level. At 5 years, PROMs including disability and pain scores, remained comparable between groups, and overall reoperation rates were similar. Collectively, these findings indicate that fusion does not provide additional long-term clinical benefit over decompression alone, even in the presence of degenerative spondylolisthesis, while influencing the site of failure rather than the overall rate of subsequent pathology [5,6,8].

In addition, registry-based data from Sweden reported by Joelson et al. [9,10] similarly indicated that fusion reduces the risk of reoperation at the index level but increases the likelihood of additional surgery at adjacent segments, resulting in comparable overall revision rates between treatment strategies. Particularly in the elderly, this trade-off may be acceptable, as it avoids the need for extended instrumentation and potentially reduces the risk of CASP.

Similarly, the recent reviews by Huang et al. [13] and McDonald et al. [14] suggest decompression alone may be reasonable in carefully selected stable patients with predominant neural compression, while recommending extended fusion for cases involving deformity, or instability.

Okuda et al. [15] reported in a large cohort of 1,000 patients undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) that symptomatic adjacent-segment disease (ASD) develops progressively over time with a 9.0% revision rate at a mean of 4.7 years. Among these patients, 12.2% of patients with first ASD developed a second episode and 36.4% of patients with second ASD developed a third episode, underscoring the cumulative nature of adjacentsegment degeneration.

Similarly, Miwa et al. [16] found that although repeat PLIF provides short-term symptomatic relief, outcomes deteriorate over time, with 44% developing recurrent pathology and 11% requiring further revision. These findings suggest that new adjacentsegment failure after extended fusion may represent an inherent consequent of altered spinal biomechanics [15,16].

Biomechanical studies support this concept. Grunert et al. [17], demonstrated that decompression adjacent to a fused segment increase motion, although minimally invasive techniques result in less hypermobility than more extensive procedures, highlighting the importance of preserving posterior elements. In line with this, Miyagi et al. and Simonetta et al. reported increased rates of ASP and revision surgery following decompression adjacent to fusion constructs. Together, these findings suggest that when decompression alone is performed, it should be limited and stability-preserving to minimize the risk of iatrogenic instability and subsequent restenosis [17-19].

Nevertheless, our findings should not be interpreted as opposing extended fusion in all clinical scenarios. Fusion continues to play an important role in patients with CASP associated with, spinal deformity, foraminal stenosis, and clear instability, where decompression alone is likely to result in suboptimal outcomes. Bashti et al. reported that fusion-based revision strategies, including open fusion and lateral lumbar interbody fusion, were associated with lower reoperation rates compared with less invasive procedures such as microdiscectomy or endoscopic decompression in a heterogeneous symptomatic ASP population. This likely reflects the fact that some patients need not only neural decompression but also restoration of stability [13,14,20].

From a health-economic and patient-quality-of-life perspective, our findings support consideration of a less invasive initial approach to the revision surgery for CASP. Decompression alone is likely associated with lower costs, avoids additional instrumentation, and may reduce perioperative morbidity, particularly in elderly populations. Given that PROMs following decompression and fusion are often comparable in related degenerative lumbar conditions, the selection of a less invasive yet effective intervention becomes increasingly justified [4,6].

In the context of CASP, the objective should extend beyond symptom relief to include minimization of clinically significant future failure. The outcome of this study may support surgical decision-making by helping surgeons balance the procedurespecific risks against the long-term risk of subsequent surgeries associated with each treatment method.

The strengths of our analysis include a large sample size, a long-term follow-up period, and a clear differentiation between reoperation types. National quality registries provide large datasets with high external validity.

To ensure a more homogeneous study cohort, patients with any history of nonfusion lumbar surgery prior to the index fusion were excluded to minimize confounding from previous surgical interventions that could influence fusion outcomes, healing potential, or the risk of subsequent surgeries.

This study has several limitations. First, although the data were collected prospectively, the retrospective study design introduces the potential for residual selection bias. Accordingly, the choice of treatment modality for CASP surgery introduce a selection bias with potential variables that may have influenced the treatment decision. There are no radiographic data available in the registry; therefore, residual confounding by indication cannot be completely excluded. Additional subgroup analyses restricted to more homogeneous diagnostic subgroups were not performed in the present study. Therefore, despite adjustment for diagnosis at CASP surgery, causal interpretation remains limited, particularly due to the absence of radiographic information in the registry. Furthermore, variations in surgeon expertise and technical approach may have affected surgical outcomes and could have influenced the risk of subsequent surgeries.

Second, subsequent surgery was used as an outcome measure, which is neither an independent endpoint nor a patient-reported outcome. Decisions regarding subsequent surgery are influenced by local practice patterns and treatment culture and may therefore not accurately reflect patient-centered outcomes.

Third, we included and analysed all procedures registered in Swespine since its establishment. However, some patients may have undergone surgery without their procedures being recorded in the registry, and others may have been operated on prior to the initiation of the Swespine registry which introduces a registration bias.

Fourth, missing data resulting from nonresponsiveness represent an additional limitation of the study.

Fifth, the registry captures the type of reoperation performed during the same hospital admission period, but it lacks information on the underlying diagnosis or indication. Therefore, early reoperations involving implant revision were assumed to reflect malpositioning, as no alternative causes could be ascertained from the available data. Finally, PROMs, including Oswestry Disability Index, EuroQol-5 Dimensions, and pain scores, were not available for the present analysis, preventing assessment of patient-reported function, health-related quality of life, and symptom improvement following surgery.

Sixth, there are limitations specific to registry studies, including the possibility of misclassification. Specifically, procedures reported as posterior fusion only may also include a decompression. For example, it is unlikely that a procedure reported as an open posterior fusion, with or without instrumentation, does not include a decompression. Similarly, procedures such as ALIF, TLIF, and PLIF inherently involve indirect decompression. Conversely, it is less likely that a procedure reported as decompression only also includes a fusion. In light of this, procedures recorded in Swespine as fusion-only procedures were included in the decompression with extended fusion group, according to the registry classification.

Further studies should be focused on single-level CASP only, central stenosis without spondylolisthesis with exclusion of foraminal stenosis, deformity and/or instability diagnoses to investigate this cohort separately and the effect of decompression alone versus fusion extension.

CONCLUSION

This large national registry study demonstrates that extending the fusion when surgically treating CASP following lumbar fusion was associated with a significantly higher risk of subsequent surgery compared with decompression alone. Extended fusion was predominantly associated with recurrent CASP at new adjacent levels, reflecting the continued biomechanical and degenerative cascade inherent to fusion constructs. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that extension of fusion is inherently harmful. Rather, they suggest that decompression alone may be a reasonable initial surgical option for carefully selected patients with CASP, particularly those without clear instability, significant spinal deformity, or severe foraminal stenosis. As radiographic information on instability, deformity, sagittal or coronal imbalance, and foraminal stenosis could not be fully verified within the present registry-based dataset, causality should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to determine which patients may be suitable candidates for each treatment options.
