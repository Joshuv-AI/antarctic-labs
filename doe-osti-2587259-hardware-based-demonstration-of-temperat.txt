 ABSTRACT
 Establishing autonomy in reactor control systems has become essential for
the expansion of nuclear technologies. Thermal regulation in particular remains
crucial for maintaining stable operation and ensuring the integrity of fuel. To
alleviate public skepticism of the safety of nuclear reactors, demonstrating
control over this key factor is pivotal. Utilizing electric heat pads to simulate the
heat released in a reactor core, thermocouples for temperature monitoring, and an
Arduino micro programmable logic controller (PLC) for control, a hardware-
based demonstration of a reactor heating system validates the efficacy of reactor
control over this key parameter. To improve precision, a proportional-integral-
derivative (PID) algorithm was implemented in the heating control loop to ensure
meticulous control of reactor functions. In addition, the integration of this
physical system with a digital simulator tool such as RELAP5-3D establishes a
foundation for a comprehensive testing environment. This allows for a
refinement of temperature control under various simulated reactor conditions,
bringing another layer of reliability to the operation of the system. By facilitating
a physical demonstration of reactor thermal management and control strategies,
this project provides a foundation for expanded testing and educational outreach.
Ultimately, this system advances the broader goal of demonstrating the safety
and viability of autonomous reactor operations, contributing to public trust and
future reactor deployment.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 One of the greatest barriers to the deployment of nuclear reactors remains public distrust. Major
reactor accidents such as Fukushima have sewn a deep sense of fear and skepticism in the public towards
nuclear reactors. This combined with a limited understanding of how nuclear reactors work or how the
aforementioned accidents were caused in the first place holds the public in a state of paralysis when it
comes to supporting the development of new nuclear reactors throughout the nation [1]. Though
contemporary reactor designs prioritize safety and simplicity, conveying this message to the public has
been a challenge.
 The possibility of demonstrating the benefit and true safety of nuclear reactor control systems on a
smaller, more understandable scale could alleviate public distrust in the safety of nuclear reactors. This
feat would allow the enormous environmental benefits to become the sole focus when it comes to getting
new reactor systems built.
 1.1 Background and Literature Review
 Nuclear microreactors are one of the newest in the line of reactor technologies and are designed to
improve safety, efficiency, and sustainability. A nuclear microreactor is a small-scale reactor capable of
generating up to 20 megawatts of thermal energy. There are three main features of microreactors: they are
factory fabricated, transportable, and self-adjusting [2].
 An example of a microreactor currently in development is the Microreactor Applications Research
Validation and Evaluation (MARVEL) microreactor which is projected to be completed in 2026 by the
Idaho National Laboratory. Given the public’s current skepticism in the safe operation of nuclear reactors,
having no real test microreactors in circulation acts as a barrier to market entry [3]. MARVEL will
alleviate this burden by being the first microreactor to achieve criticality and demonstrate end user
applications.
 In particular, the portability of microreactors has yielded the enabling of autonomous to semi-
autonomous reactor operations. A semi-autonomous control system enables the automation of a nuclear
power plant with the capability to respond to various equipment malfunctions and other plant faults with
limited human involvement [4]. Such a development necessitates the advancement in new reactor control
paradigms. A microreactor automated control system (MACS) testbed was developed by the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) as part of its microreactor program to test control system automation with
minimal need for human interference [5]. When integrated with frameworks like The Control and
Optimization Modular Modeling Applications for Nuclear Deployment (COMMAND), MACS proves an
ideal testbed for emulating reactor physics and evaluating machine learning based control logic.
Programable logic controller (PLC) based reactor system as well as power conversion system control and
protection are explored by several researchers [6-11]. Recently artificial intelligence (AI) and simulator
tools are also considered for remote and secure reactor operation [12-13].
 1.2 Significance
 Much like how the MARVEL project will yield the expansion of further microreactor research and
construction through its mere operation as a microreactor, this project provides proof of concept of safe
autonomous temperature controls within nuclear reactors. In addition, the MACS testbed is a prime
example of the importance of a demonstration platform to test and validate various approaches before
implementation. Doing so provides a safety net to catch critical errors that could have otherwise gone
through to a final reactor design. Where MACS can physically simulate a reactor system—complete with
control drums and LED status indicators—this project demonstrates precise thermal control. This is

achieved through the implementation of a simplified, safe, self-sustaining reactor temperature regulation

 1.3 Previous Works
 This project is a continuation of two projects conducted by interns from previous years. The first—
and most directly related—was an initial iteration of the simulated reactor heater box. Since this project
was able to successfully demonstrate the feasibility of autonomous, hardware-based control systems,
more complexity and a wider scope can be applied.
 The second project was also a simulated reactor system that mainly revolved around a piece of
hardware known as a Crow-Pi. This component is a Raspberry Pi with various built-in modules for ease
of use including a laptop screen, buttons, LEDs, and LCD displays. With this, different sensors were
connected via an Arduino microcontroller. This project demonstrated cyber scenarios and successfully
connected all hardware through a Wi-Fi access point.
 The full extent of the current project is essentially a combination and expansion of these two previous
projects.
 1.4 Full System Overview
 The heater box functions as a subsystem in a greater simulated reactor network. This full system
includes two ground stations managed by a team of four interns. Meanwhile, a team of two cybersecurity
focused interns consulted on the simulation of cybersecurity attacks. One station acts as a simulated plant
environment, housing and controlling data collected by process sensors and by the heater box. The second
is a remote monitoring station to collect data from the other station via an encrypted Wi-Fi connection.
The heater box acts as the main control demonstration. Moreover, there is simulated satellite
communication via radio frequency waves.

 1.5 Project Objectives
 Risk reduction was one of the highest priorities in this expansion to push the idea of safety in the
operation of nuclear reactors. Thus, within the system, various new additions were to be implemented to
improve safety and create an overall, more complex system. The first of which is a proportional-integral-
derivative (PID) controller in the heating control loop. This improved the precision of temperature control
functions. Next, establishing a connection to transfer data to a remote monitoring station contributed to
another layer of safety in demonstrating the ability to monitor off-normal reactor conditions from a
distance. Additionally, previous experience with CubeSat avionics provided the idea to implement some
form of satellite communication into the system, likely between the two ground stations as a form of
backup communication. Furthermore, a connection was established between the RELAP5-3D reactor
simulator. This connection provides a path towards reactor system design safety by providing a pathway
towards using an industry-standard reactor simulator as a digital twin of the hardware system. Lastly, the
design of the heater box was to be completely reworked to improve various parameters.
 2. DESIGN JUSTIFICATION
 The key factors considered in the upgrade of the previous heater box were safety, uniform heat
distribution, realism, control and precision, and visualization. To adhere to these factors, a redesign of the
box was crucial.
 2.1 Planning and Preparation
 Based on cost consideration and convenience, the Adafruit Electric Heat Pad, and components
from the Arduino PLC Starter Kit such as the Arduino Opta and the Arduino Simul8 were reutilized from
previous projects. The Arduino Opta is a micro programmable logic controller (PLC) that functions as a
microcontroller but is tailored more towards industrial purposes, thus operating at a higher voltage range
than a typical microcontroller. The Arduino Simul8’s full function is as a panel of switches, but this
project uses it for the sole purpose of powering the Opta PLC.

 2.3 Housing Options
 To house the heating box, electronic junction boxes were mainly considered as they are already
designed to house circuitry, retain heat well, and can easily be cut into and manipulated if needed.
 Three candidates were considered. The first offers full control of design as it can comfortably fit the
heating structure and provide adequate visualization. The second candidate was like the first candidate but
with a fan and thermostat. This candidate provides an extra monitoring system, but its ventilation would
likely interfere with the gathered heat data. The third candidate reduces the need for complexity in the
heating system with a fan and a thermostat built-in for precise temperature control. This box was made of
galvanized steel which doubtlessly houses heat better. Still, it was just big enough to hold the heating
structure and has fully opaque walls meaning there were no avenues for visualization. Ultimately, the first
candidate is ideal for this project as extra monitoring or cooling systems are unnecessary. Moreover, this
candidate provides the most freedom of design while staying perfectly within the budget.

 In the end, thermocouples work well with PID control systems and are used in real nuclear reactor

 Next, the configuration and amount of thermocouples had to be considered. Four different designs
were examined with seven, six, three, and one thermocouple.

 Figure 8. (a) Seven-Thermocouple Design. (b) Six-Thermocouple Design. (c) Three-Thermocouple

 The seven-thermocouple design prioritizes even distribution. More sensors enable easy identification
of a hot spot which could lead to potential circuit malfunction. The six-thermocouple design prioritizes
placing sensors in areas with a unique pad-to-pad distance. Each area with a unique distance between
pads generates a different heat value. This design measures all unique values. The three-thermocouple
design focuses on gauging the temperature by layer. The range from the hottest (center) to coldest (edges)
temperatures in the box are recorded. The single-thermocouple design places a sensor only in the area
where the temperature is the greatest.
 Ultimately, the three-thermocouple design offers the best compromise between complexity, cost, and
range of sensing.

 3. ASSEMBLY
 In this section, details on the wiring design and physical efficiency of the design will be discussed.
 3.1 Wiring
 When considering the circuit arrangement of the heat pads, it was important to adhere to the
component’s ratings to avoid damaging any part. The Adafruit heat pads are specifically rated for 1A at
12V implying, by Ohm’s law, that the resistance across each heat pad is 12Ω. With this, each heat pad
can be modeled as a 12Ω resistor, and the ideal arrangement can be calculated. The Arduino Opta
operates at a max voltage of 24V, which will be supplied through the Arduino Simul8. This will be the
voltage used in calculation.

 7

 The best way to evenly distribute the 24V load to the heat pads is to place two in series with each
other. This will divide the voltage, ensuring 12V to each. This can be repeated, placing multiple groups of
two in-series heat pads in parallel with each other. This will split the current, ensuring that each branch
receives 1A regardless of the number of branches. Thus, this design can be replicated with both the outer
and inner heat layers despite their difference in number of heat pads.

 (a) (b)

Figure 9. (a) Heat Pad Circuit Configuration Outer Layer. (b) Heat Pad Circuit Configuration Inner Layer.

 As it turned out, the measured resistance of the heat pads was around 7.8Ω. Keeping this
configuration causes a greater amount of current to flow through each heat pad, violating the 1A rating.
To negate this, the heat pads can be estimated at around 7.5Ω for conservatism, and the arrangement can
be redesigned. A consequence of this is that each heat pad will receive significantly less voltage.
Considering the high priority of safety in this project and the demonstrational aspect, this compromise
was acceptable.
 The inner layer can be adjusted to have all four of its heat pads in series. This brings it to 6V with
0.8A. Any symmetrical arrangement of the outer layer causes a current greater than 1A across heat pads,
so all six pads can be placed in series bringing it to 4V with 0.533A across each. This arrangement is not
ideal as the heat pads will heat very slowly with this smaller voltage, but given time constraints, this
arrangement was the safest and worked for demonstration purposes.

 Figure 10. Adjusted Inner Layer Heat Pad Configuration.

 Figure 11. Adjusted Outer Layer Heat Pad Configuration.

 The Adafruit mini fans are rated for 0.2A at 5V. Though they have a different rating, the fan
circuit design will operate on a similar logic to the heat pad circuit design. Once again, through Ohm’s
law, the resistance of each fan can be calculated as 25Ω. Furthermore, the fans require a max load of 5V.
This poses an issue as the power supplied through the Arduino Simul8 is 24V. This can easily be
resolved, however, by using a 24V to 5V buck converter to step down the voltage.

 Figure 12. Fan Circuit Configuration.

 In total, there are 2 branches supplying 1.33A. The Opta has 4 built-in relays to switch power on
and off to whatever is connected. The outer heat pad layer, inner heat pad layer, and fan arrangement will
each be connected to separate relays, allowing power to be sent to each of these areas discretely. The
Adafruit AD8495 K-Type Thermocouple Amplifier was a good choice to read the thermocouples as it
supports the Opta’s 0-10V analog input. Additionally, to stabilize the output of each sensor, a low-pass
RC filter will be constructed and connected between each amplifier’s data output and the Opta’s input
terminal. The amplifiers require a max load of 5V, so another 24V to 5V buck converter can be used to
power the amplifiers within the proper operating voltage.

 To precisely automate temperature control, a PID controller was programmed to maintain a given
setpoint. PID controls three different modules to optimally adjust its output: proportional, integral, and
derivative. The proportional provides immediate response to current errors. The integral works to
eliminate residual steady-state error. The derivative anticipates future errors by reacting to the rate of
change [14]. In order to make this PID controller work with relays, something called time-proportional
control can be added where the output of the PID signifies an amount of time that the relays are on within
a specific window. The rest of the time within this window, the relays will be turned off.

 3.2 Control Logic

 Figure 14. Diagram of Connections Between Circuitry and Heating System.

 The PLC is powered through the Arduino Simul8 and the circuit through a battery. Next, the PID
Controller activates each layer of heaters and fans to achieve a specified desired temperature. The
thermocouples then relay data to the amplifiers. The PLC calculates temperature reading and uses it to
adjust the output of the PID controller. This cycle continues to maintain setpoint value. Data can be sent
to the rest of the system via PLC Wi-Fi capabilities.

 4. CONCLUSIONS
 Contemporary approaches to reactor designs focus on minimizing the element of human error by
emphasizing autonomous control. This project supports this paradigm shift by presenting a system
capable of maintaining temperatures without constant human guidance. Autonomous control of reactor
core temperatures remains paramount in ensuring the safety of nuclear reactors, especially in
microreactors like MARVEL.
 4.1 Summary of Key Findings
In the end, the lower resistance of the heat pads which necessitated a redesign that halved the amount of
voltage sent to them called for an increase in aggression in the PID to compensate for the slow heating
times. This ultimately made the design not sustainable for the long run as the rapid relay switching would
wear out the system, but the project was successful for demonstration purposes. Given more time, a
voltage regulator could have been purchased to solve this voltage issue.

Still, major project milestones include:
  Successfully recreation of the physical heat profiles found in real reactors.
  Implementation of a PID logic controller to precisely automate temperature controls.
  Safe operation of a simulated reactor thermal regulation system.
 This project has demonstrated the feasibility of precise hardware-based temperature control utilizing
PID logic. Additionally, by replicating the real structure and control loops of reactors, the project serves
as a physical simulator and testbed to play out reactor scenarios safely. The potential for real-time
integration with RELAP5-3D further cements this project’s relevance in future reactor development.
 4.2 Future Research Directions
 More complexities can be added to this system to bring it closer to a real reactor. Specifically, control
mechanisms aside from heat can be implemented. Pressure, for example, is another critical parameter in
reactor operation and can be controlled using fluids. This would allow integration of simulated coolants
into the control loop. Additionally, the connection to the RELAP5-3D simulator can be expanded on.
While the scope of its use in this project was merely a proof of concept to establish a connection, using
the simulator as a digital twin of the physical system provides a step forward in terms of safety and
testing.
 4.3 Summary of Internship Experiences
 Throughout this internship, I was lucky to encounter many invaluable growth experiences. My
favorite opportunity was visiting a Human Factors Engineering Lab where Ron Boring gave an engaging
account of the importance of human factors in reactor control design. This lecture sparked many ideas,
taking human factors into consideration. Additionally, I got the chance to go on an INL bus tour of the
EBR-1 museum, and the Hot Cells Complex at the Material Fuels Complex. The extreme safety measures
they implement to prevent contamination assured me of the importance of projects like this and reinforced
many safety decisions. For example, prioritizing a transparent housing for the heater box to improve
visualization of physical system behavior. This internship provided not only growth in technical areas, but
also an expansion in the understanding of how many disciplines are important to reactor system design;
engineering, human behavior, and safety culture are all factors that must be considered for the creation of
a successful nuclear reactor.
