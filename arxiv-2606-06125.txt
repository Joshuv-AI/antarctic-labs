Regret Minimization in Single-Dimensional Contract-Design with Binary Actions
Riccardo Poiani, Martino Bernasconi, Andrea Celli

Abstract
We study principal-agent problems in which a principal commits to an outcome-dependent payment scheme ( i.e. , a contract ) in order to induce an agent to take a costly action leading to a favorable outcome. We consider the online extension of the classical (one-shot) principal-agent problem, in which the principal repeatedly interacts with agents by proposing contracts over multiple rounds. The principal has no information about the agents and, crucially, does not observe their actions. As a result, the principal must learn an optimal contract using only the realized outcomes observed at each round. We focus on the setting with binary actions and single-dimensional agent types, where the agent’s private type represents their cost per unit-of-effort. For adversarial-type sequences, we provide tight Θ ⁡ ( T 2 / 3 ) \Theta(T^{2/3}) regret guarantees. Remarkably, this rate is completely independent of the number of outcomes m m . The upper bound is based on two key components: 1) a reduction to a one-dimensional threshold optimization problem and 2) a non-uniform discretization to handle the non-Lipschitz nature of the problem. Moreover, in the case of a single (fixed) hidden type, we show that it is possible to improve the rates and provide a tight Θ ~ ​ ( T ) \widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{T}) regret bound. Our algorithm is based on an explore-then-commit strategy where we first approximately learn the hidden type via a stochastic binary search, and then we commit to a “robustified” near-optimal contract.

Introduction

Principal-agent problems model scenarios in which a principal seeks to influence the behavior of a self-interested agent capable of performing costly actions. The principal commits to an outcome-dependent payment rule (
i.e.
, a
contract
), after which the agent selects one of the available actions. In the standard hidden-action model, the principal does not observe the agent’s action directly, but only a stochastic outcome whose distribution depends on the chosen action and determines the principal’s reward. The principal’s objective is then to design a contract that incentivizes the agent to take actions leading to favorable outcomes.
This model is central to modern economic theory and has recently given rise to a thriving body of research studying it through a computational lens. Such interest is motivated by applications like online labor platforms
[
39
]
, strategic data provisioning
[
18
]
, pay-for-performance healthcare
[
10
,
11
]
, blockchain
[
28
]
and delegation to AI agents
[
37
,
46
,
44
,
48
,
45
]
.

We study the generalization of the classical (single-round) model, in which the principal faces uncertainty about the agents’ types and must learn an optimal contract from data. In particular, the principal repeatedly interacts with agents over multiple rounds. At each round, the principal commits to a new contract and observes the outcome produced by the agent’s response. The goal of the principal is minimizing the regret with respect to the best contract in hindsight (
i.e.
, given knowledge of the sequence of agent types).
The best known regret guarantees for general online contract design problems are nearly linear
[
49
]
or exponential in the instance size
[
6
]
. Stronger sublinear-regret guarantees are known under additional structural assumptions, such as regularity conditions relating action costs and outcome distributions, bounded-density assumptions, or restrictions on the class of admissible contracts (see, e.g.,
[
49
,
26
,
14
]
and the discussion in
Section

1.2
). In this work, we ask whether there are natural settings in which one can obtain tight sublinear regret for general classes of contracts,
without
imposing further assumptions on the type or outcome distributions.

Motivated by known hardness results for the Bayesian setting with multi-dimensional types
[
22
,
36
,
23
]
, we study the single-dimensional type setting introduced by
2
, in which the agent’s type is a scalar representing a cost per unit of effort (
i.e.
, a parameter that scales the cost of the chosen action).
We consider the case in which agents have binary actions, so they can either exert effort or not. This is a canonical model in economics, capturing the standard “work / shirk” formulation of moral hazard (see,
e.g.
,
[
38
,
5
,
29
,
35
]
).
A useful way to interpret this model is through a simple delegated-learning example in the spirit of
46
, where a principal outsources the training of a classifier to a provider. The provider can either invest
high effort
(
a_{1}
), for example by performing extensive data annotation and hyperparameter tuning, or training on a more expensive pipeline, or choose
low effort
(
a_{2}
), corresponding to a cheaper baseline procedure. The provider’s private type
\theta
represents its cost per unit-of-effort, so that the cost of action
a
is
\theta\cdot C_{a}
. After training, the principal does not observe which pipeline was actually used, but only a stochastic performance outcome (
e.g.
, validation accuracy), whose distribution depends on the chosen action.
A second example is a crowdsourcing or data-labeling platform. In each round, a new worker arrives to complete a task, and the platform offers an outcome-contingent payment rule, for example, a base payment together with a bonus tied to an observable quality signal (
e.g.
, agreement with other workers, expert review, or the performance of a downstream model trained on the collected data). The worker can either exert high effort, corresponding to careful completion of the task, or low effort, corresponding to a cheap but lower-quality submission. Again, the platform does not observe the worker’s effort directly, but only the realized quality outcome, whose distribution depends on the chosen action.
In both settings, a contract specifies a payment as a function of the realized performance outcome. In this binary-action setting, every contract trades off incentives and payments by determining which types find it worthwhile to choose the high-effort action, thereby inducing a threshold on the hidden type space. Learning a good contract then amounts to learning the right threshold from outcome-only feedback.

1.1
Our Results and Techniques

Main Results.
We establish a tight
\Theta(T^{2/3})
regret bound when the sequence of agent types is chosen adversarially (
Section

3
). We then turn to the setting in which the agent type is fixed throughout the interaction (though it is hidden) and show that the optimal rate improves to
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{T})
(
Section

4
).

Key Features of Our Bounds.
A notable feature of these results is that, unlike previous guarantees, they are completely independent of the number of outcomes
m
. This is particularly desirable in environments with large outcome spaces, where existing bounds may deteriorate substantially with
m
(see,
e.g.
, the
\widetilde{O}(T^{1-1/(2m+1)})
bound by
49
). Our guarantee for adversarial-type sequences also improves over the
\widetilde{O}(m^{2}T^{4/5})
bound of
6
(specialized to the two-action setting), not only in its dependence on the horizon
T
, but also in its dependence on the instance parameters, since our rate does not scale with
m
at all.
Moreover, our results are surprising if we view online contract design as a special case of repeated Stackelberg games with myopic agents
[
42
]
. Indeed, in general, known hardness results show that there exist instances for which learning an optimal commitment requires a number of samples exponential in the number of actions of the leader (
i.e.
, the principal in our terminology)
[
43
]
. In contrast, we can exploit the specific structure of our problem to obtain sublinear regret rates, despite the principal having a continuous action space and observing only outcome feedback.
Finally, our bounds provide the first evidence that no-regret learning is possible in the single-dimensional-type setting without bounded-density assumptions, addressing a question recently posed by
14
in the special case of binary actions.

Techniques.
We briefly highlight here the main technical components of our analysis, and defer a broader discussion to the later sections.

•

The first key idea to obtain the
O(T^{2/3})
upper bound is showing that, although contracts live in an
m
-dimensional space, the search for the best fixed contract can be reduced to a one-dimensional optimization problem over the expected payment gap between the two actions. The resulting one-dimensional objective is not
O(1)
-Lipschitz, so standard uniform discretization arguments fail. To overcome this, we develop a non-uniform discretization tailored to the geometry of the linear program that defines the cheapest contract implementing a given incentive level. This yields a discretization with only
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
relevant points while preserving enough control on the discretization error to obtain the desired regret bound.

•

The lower bound shows that every algorithm suffers regret
\Omega(T^{2/3})
under stochastic type generation with an absolutely continuous type distribution. The main challenges to obtain this result are the learner acts in a continuous contract space and the observed feedback does not coincide with the principal’s utility. To overcome this, we construct a family of carefully perturbed continuous instances in which the utility improves only on a small interval of contracts while the induced outcome distributions remain hard to distinguish, and then apply a change-of-measure argument directly in the continuous action space.

•

Finally, in order to obtain the
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
upper bound in the case of a single fixed type, we design an algorithm exploiting the binary-action structure to perform a stochastic binary search over the unknown threshold induced by the hidden type. The main challenge is to design exploratory contracts that are informative enough to localize the threshold while remaining close enough to optimal to keep regret under control. We address this by combining threshold-testing contracts with a robustification step in the exploitation phase.

1.2
Related Work

In the following, we review studies most directly related to our work. For a comprehensive overview of algorithmic contract theory, readers are referred to the recent survey by
31
.

Typed Agents.
Contracts for typed agents have been studied in both multi- and single-dimensional models. In the multi-dimensional setting, where the costs and outcome distribution can depend arbitrarily on the type, a series of works showed that the problem of approximating optimal (deterministic) contracts is APX-hard
[
22
,
36
,
23
]
. The single-dimensional model admits stronger positive results. In particular,
2
,
1
show tractability for a constant number of actions, and also obtain positive approximation guarantees for simple contracts. Moreover,
14
showed that the problem admits an additive PTAS. However, for multiplicative approximations, the single-dimensional problem remains as hard as the general multi-dimensional case
[
20
]
.

Online Contract Design.
The problem of learning optimal contracts was introduced by
38
and later extended in
27
.
The work most closely related to ours on learning optimal contracts in general principal-agent settings is
49
. They provide an algorithm guaranteeing cumulative regret of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{m}T^{1-1/(2m+1)})
. Moreover, they show that it is possible to learn linear contracts (which are a weaker class of contracts in which the payment is proportional to the principal’s reward) incurring regret of order
\widetilde{\Theta}(T^{2/3})
. They also provide an almost-matching lower bound of
\Omega(T^{1-1/(2m+1)})
for the case of general bounded contracts. Their lower-bound construction employs instances with a number of actions exponential in
m
. The question of whether it is possible to do better in settings with small action spaces was answered by
6
, who showed that for bounded contracts it is possible to achieve regret
\widetilde{O}(m^{n}\,T^{4/5})
, which depends polynomially on the instance size if the number of actions
n
is constant.
Moreover, for linear contracts,
7
showed that
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{ndT})
regret is attainable in settings with a finite number of types
d
and a number of actions
n
that is small relative to
T
(
i.e.
,
n\leq T^{1/3}
).

Learning under Additional Assumptions.
In order to circumvent the negative results of
49
, subsequent works have explored learning optimal contracts under additional assumptions.
26
show that if the problem satisfies First-Order Stochastic Dominance (FOSD) and the Concavity of Distribution Function Property (CDFP), then it is possible to achieve regret of order
\widetilde{O}(m^{11/21}\cdot T^{20/21}\cdot\log(1/\delta))
.
Recently,
14
studied a single-dimensional-type setting similar to ours but requiring types to be generated according to a distribution admitting density bounded by some constant
\beta
. Under this assumption, they provide a learning algorithm with regret of order
\widetilde{O}(\beta\,\text{poly}(n,m)\,T^{3/4})
. Here, the bounded-density assumption plays a crucial role, as it provides a way to control the discretization error and thereby enables a direct reduction from the continuous problem to a discrete one.

Other Feedback Models.

32
study a stronger feedback model where the principal can observe their expected utility instead of a sampled outcome. In this setting, linear contracts can be learned, incurring regret at most
O(\log\log T)
.
Finally, we mention the recent work of
30
which uses the notion of pseudo-dimension to bound the sample complexity of approximately optimal contracts. While providing fundamental barriers to learnability in the case of unbounded contracts, they provide polynomial sample complexity results for bounded contracts, which translate to a regret of order
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{mT})
under a full feedback model (i.e., the learner observes the type after committing to a contract).

Our problem also shares connections with the more general problem of learning an optimal commitment, in repeated Stackelberg games
[
42
,
43
,
16
,
9
,
8
]
and online Bayesian persuasion
[
19
,
21
,
13
]
.

Preliminaries

The single-dimensional Bayesian principal-agent problem is defined as follows.
There is a set of outcomes
\Omega
of size
|\Omega|=m
. Each possible outcome
\omega
provides to the principal a reward
r_{\omega}\in[0,1]
. There are two actions
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
which are the high- and low-effort actions, respectively. Each action is associated with a cost
C_{a}\in[0,1]
, and we can assume without loss of generality that
C_{a_{1}}\geq C_{a_{2}}
.
1
1

1

Without loss of generality, we assume that
C_{a_{1}}>0
, otherwise the problem admits a trivial solution.
Each action
a\in A:=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
is associated to a distribution over outcomes
F_{a}\in\Delta(\Omega)
. We denote with
F_{a,\omega}
the probability that action
a
leads to outcome
\omega
.
We consider the single-dimensional-type problem introduced by
2
where the agent is associated with a hidden type
\theta\in[0,1]
, and the agent’s cost of performing action
a
is
\theta\cdot C_{a}
. In this setting, the uncertainty is entirely encapsulated in the private types, the outcome distributions and costs are part of the publicly known environment.

The principal’s goal is to choose a contract
p\in[0,1]^{m}
, where each component
p_{\omega}
specifies the payment made in outcome
\omega
. Based on the proposed contract
p
, the agent takes an action
a\in A
and an outcome
\omega\sim F_{a}
is reached. When an agent of type
\theta
takes action
a
, the expected utility of the agent is
U^{A}_{\theta}(p,a)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}F_{a,\omega}p_{w}-\theta\cdot C_{a}
, while the principal has expected utility
U^{P}(p,a)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}F_{a,\omega}(r_{\omega}-p_{\omega})
. After committing to a contract, the principal observes only the realized outcome
\omega
, and observes neither the action
a\in A
taken nor the type
\theta
.

When an agent of type
\theta
observes a contract
p
, we assume that it will play an action
a\in A
that is both (1)
incentive compatible
(IC),
i.e.
, it maximizes the expected utility over actions, and (2)
individually rational
(IR),
i.e.
,
U^{A}_{\theta}(p,a)\geq 0
.

To conveniently incorporate IR into IC constraints, it is standard to assume that there exists an action
a
with cost
C_{a}=0
. When multiple actions are IC, we assume that ties are broken in favor of the principal. More formally, let
\mathcal{B}(\theta,p)=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{a\in A}U^{A}_{\theta}(p,a)
be the set of actions that are IC for type
\theta
and contract
p
. Then, we define
b(\theta,p)
as the action within
\mathcal{B}(\theta,p)
that maximizes the principal’s expected utility under
p
.
2
2

2

Whenever the utilities of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are both optimal for the principal and the agent, we assume the agent plays
a_{1}
.
Finally, we conclude by introducing the following notation. For
\epsilon>0
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\theta,p)
as the sets of actions that are
\epsilon
-IC for the agent,
i.e.
,

\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\theta,p)=\Big\{a\in A:U^{A}(p,a)\geq\max_{b\in A}U^{A}(p,b)-\epsilon\Big\}.

Online Setting.
In this work, we focus on the online adversarial setting where the principal interacts with an arbitrary sequence of
T\in\mathbb{N}
agents. During each round
t\in[T]
, a type
\theta_{t}
arrives, and the principal selects a contract
p_{t}\in[0,1]^{m}
. The agent
\theta_{t}
best responds,
i.e.
, it plays an action
a_{t}\in b(\theta_{t},p_{t})
and the principal observes
\omega_{t}\sim F_{a_{t}}
and receives
r_{\omega_{t}}-p_{w_{t}}
.
The principal’s goal is to minimize the regret against the best contract in hindsight for any fixed sequence of types,
i.e.
,

R_{T}=\sup_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}}\sup_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p,b(\theta_{t},p))-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t},b(\theta_{t},p_{t}))\right]\right\},

where the expectation is taken with respect to the stochasticity of the algorithm.
In the following, whenever it is clear from context, we write
U^{P}(p,\theta)=U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p))
. Moreover, for a fixed sequence of types, we define
\textup{{Opt}}\coloneqq\sup_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p,\theta_{t})
, omitting the dependence on the type sequence when it is clear from context. Throughout this document, we assume that
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r>F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
. If this condition does not hold, then there exists a trivial algorithm with zero regret.

3

In this case, the null contract is optimal for every agent
\theta\in[0,1]
. We prove this formally in

Adversarial Types:
\Theta(T^{2/3})
is Tight

In this section, we consider the general adversarial setting in which an arbitrary sequence of types
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
arrives. In
Section

3.1
, we propose an algorithm that attains
{O}(T^{2/3})
regret. Then, in
Section

, we show that this rate is tight in the worst-case sense by proving a matching lower bound.

3.1
The Adversarial
O(T^{2/3})
Upper Bound

In this section, we prove the following upper bound on adversarial types and provide a high-level overview of the algorithm. The formal proof is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

Theorem 1
.

There exists an algorithm for the online contract-design problem with binary actions and single-dimensional adversarial types, such that
R_{T}\leq O(T^{2/3})
.

A notable feature of the upper bound is that it is independent of the number of outcomes
m
. This is surprising since the action space is continuous and standard discretization arguments typically incur an exponential dependence on
m
. Our approach avoids this through two main ideas.
First, we show that the search for an optimal contract can be reduced from an
m
-dimensional space to a one-dimensional one. The key parameter is the threshold type,
i.e.
, the highest type that can still be induced to choose action
a_{1}
, together with the cheapest contract that implements it (see
Section

3.1.1
). This reduces the problem to optimization over the interval
[0,1]
.
Second, once we obtained the one-dimensional problem, the standard way of handling it would be to discretize the
[0,1]
interval uniformly with step
\epsilon=T^{-1/3}
. If the underlying reward function was
O(1)
-Lipschitz, this would give a
O(\sqrt{T/\epsilon}+\epsilon T)=O(T^{2/3})
upper bound (this also holds for one-sided Lipschitz functions
[
32
]
). However, when reformulated as a one-dimensional problem, the reward is not
O(1)
-Lipschitz. As a result, a clever discretization strategy is needed to avoid dependence on the number of outcomes
m
. This motivates the second key idea: a non-uniform discretization that exploits additional structure of the problem, which we discuss in more detail in
Section

3.1.2
.

3.1.1
The One-Dimensional Problem

To show that the problem can be reduced to a one-dimensional one, we start by analyzing the utility of the principal when it plays a contract
p
. To this end, we will only focus on the subset of contracts for which
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}(r-p)

i.e.
, the subset where ties in

\mathcal{P}_{1}

\displaystyle U^{P}(p,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}\{\theta\leq\tilde{\theta}_{\max}(\lambda_{p})\}F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)+\mathds{1}\{\theta>\tilde{\theta}_{\max}(\lambda_{p})\}F_{a_{2}}^{\top}(r-p)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}\{\theta\leq\tilde{\theta}_{\max}(\lambda_{p})\}((F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}r-\lambda_{p})+F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r+\lambda_{p}-F_{a_{1}}^{\top}p,

At this point, we just need to combine all the pieces together. First, we compute

3.2
A Matching
\Omega(T^{2/3})
Lower Bound

In this section, we will prove that
Theorem

1
is tight up to constants. In particular, we provide a lower bound which holds even for stochastic type generation. Importantly, we show that the lower bound holds even when the type distribution is absolutely continuous (with respect to Lebesgue measure).

The main difference from standard Multi-Armed Bandit lower bounds is that the feedback received by the learner and the reward suffered by the learner do not coincide, and that the action space of the learner is continuous.
This is a characteristic of lower bounds for similar economic models, such as dynamic pricing
[
40
,
4
]
, bilateral trade
[
25
,
12
]
, and market making
[
24
]
. A principled way to deal with such problems is to reduce them to an instance with a finite set of “arms” and apply standard KL chain rule techniques (see
[
47
,
41
]
). This is done by carefully identifying arms that are optimal with respect to regret or information acquired. However, this would require introducing point masses into the distribution. Instead, here, to produce an absolutely continuous distribution, we do not reduce to the finite case and use the KL chain rule with continuous actions.

More formally, we create
K=\Theta(1/\epsilon)
instances
\{\mathfrak{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{I}_{K}\}
such that

•

each instance
\mathfrak{I}_{k}
is identical to a base instance
\mathfrak{I}_{0}
, apart from an interval
J_{k}
of size
O(\epsilon)
;

•

the principal utility on the interval
J_{k}
is
\Omega(\epsilon)
larger than the utility outside
J_{k}
;

•

the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the instance
\mathfrak{I}_{0}
and instance
\mathfrak{I}_{k}
is less than
O(\epsilon^{2})
times the number of times we played in expectation (under the base instance) in the interval
J_{k}
.

Then, by standard change of measure arguments, we can prove that for each algorithm, there is an instance in which the regret is at least
\Omega(\epsilon T-\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{T/K})
, and by choosing
K=\Theta(1/\epsilon)
and calibrating
\epsilon=\Theta(T^{-1/3})
gives the desired
\Omega(T^{2/3})
lower-bound (the full proof is in
Appendix

B
).

Theorem 2
.

For any algorithm, there exists an instance with stochastic types such that the expected regret is lower bounded by
\Omega(T^{2/3})
.

Theorem 3

Outline.
We start by observing that, if the principal knew the hidden type

\epsilon

for type
\hat{\theta}

The role of robustification is to make the contract insensitive to small errors in the estimated type at the cost of only a small loss in utility. By the standard robustification argument of
33

Therefore, an

To do this, we show how to build suitable threshold tests that we will use inside a stochastic binary search procedure. Crucially, the exploration contracts themselves are chosen so that each test incurs only small regret.

Testing a Threshold.

.

Any (possibly randomized) algorithm suffers at least regret

5
Future Work

Several questions on the learnability of contracts remain open. A first direction is to strengthen lower bounds for more general online contract-design settings, especially by constructing hard instances whose dependence on the instance size is only polynomial. Another important direction is to move beyond the binary-action case: with multiple actions, the type space induces a richer arrangement of incentive regions, and it is unclear whether similar outcome-independent regret guarantees are possible. More broadly, it would be interesting to study intermediate regimes between adversarial and fixed types, such as slowly drifting types.

c_{k}-\bar{\delta}=t+2\bar{\delta}(k-1)\geq t
and thus
J_{k}\subseteq[t,\infty)
. Moreover,
c_{k}+\bar{\delta}\leq c_{K}+\bar{\delta}=t+2K\bar{\delta}
. Since
t=1/5
,
\bar{\delta}=\frac{4\epsilon}{1+10\epsilon}
, we get
2K\bar{\delta}\leq 2/5
and thus
c_{k}+\bar{\delta}\leq 1-\frac{3}{2}t
, proving that
J_{k}\subseteq[t,1-\frac{3}{2}t]
.

For
(iv)
it is sufficient to observe that
U_{k}^{P}(x)
is decreasing in
x
in the interval
B_{k}
, and that
U_{k}^{P}(c_{k}+\delta_{k}/2)=t+\epsilon/2
.

For
(v)
we can observe that by
(ii)
and

Lemma

8
, for all
x\notin[c_{k}-\delta_{k},c_{k}+\delta_{k}]

\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x)=\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x).

Thus, it suffices to consider
x\in[c_{k}-\delta_{k},c_{k}+\delta_{k}]
. On this interval, the difference

D(x):=\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x)-\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x)

is nonnegative, increasing on
[c_{k}-\delta_{k},c_{k})
, and decreasing on
[c_{k},c_{k}+\delta_{k}]
, hence it is maximized at
x=c_{k}
. Therefore

\left|\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x)-\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\frac{t}{1-c_{k}-\delta_{k}}-\frac{t}{1-c_{k}}=\frac{\epsilon}{1-c_{k}}.

By item 3,
1-c_{k}\geq 3t/2
, so the right-hand side is at most

\frac{2}{3t}\epsilon\leq 4\epsilon,

concluding the proof.
∎

A standard change of measure argument concluded the proof.

Theorem 5
.

For any (possibly randomized) learning algorithm, there exists
k\in[K]
such that

\textup{{Opt}}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t})\right]=\Omega(T^{2/3}).

Proof.

Because of
Remark

x_{t}\in[0,1]
, and the associated contract is

.
For any instance

let

and

Claim 1
.

\textup{KL}(\nu_{0},\nu_{k})\leq 10^{4}\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{k}(T)].

Proof.

We can use the
KL
decomposition for continuous action spaces
[

.

\displaystyle\textup{KL}(\nu_{0},\nu_{k})=\int_{0}^{1}\textup{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x),\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x))dG_{\nu_{0}}(x),

where
G_{\nu_{0}}(B)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\sum_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{I}(x_{t}\in B)]
and
\textup{kl}(a,b)=a\log(\nicefrac{{a}}{{b}})+(1-a)\log(\nicefrac{{1-a}}{{1-b}})
.
By
Lemma

, we have that

\displaystyle\textup{KL}(\nu_{0},\nu_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{J_{k}}\textup{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x),\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x))dG_{\nu_{0}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq G_{\nu_{0}}(J_{k})\sup_{x\in J_{k}}\textup{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x),\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{k}(T)]\textup{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{k}}(\theta\leq x),\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x)).

Now we upper bound
\textup{kl}(a,b)\leq\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2\ell(1-u)}
, which holds for all
a,b\in[\ell,u]
. By using
Lemma

9
we know that
\mathbb{P}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\theta\leq x)\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}]

(v)

. This lets us conclude that

\textup{KL}(\nu_{0},\nu_{k})\leq 10^{4}\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{k}(T)],

concluding the proof.
∎

Moreover, the regret can be easily upper-bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\textup{{Opt}}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=T\left(t+\epsilon\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[\sum_{t\in[T]:x_{t}\in B_{k}(T)}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t})+\sum_{t\in[T]:x_{t}\notin B_{k}(T)}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\geq T(t+\epsilon)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[M_{k}(T)\right](t+\epsilon)-(T-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[M_{k}(T)\right])(t+\frac{\epsilon}{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(T-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k}}\left[M_{k}(T)\right]).

for any measurable event

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{k^{*}}}[M_{k^{*}}\leq T/2]\geq 1-\frac{2}{K}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{k^{*}}(T)]}\geq 1-\frac{2}{K}-10^{2}\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{T}{K}},

where the last inequality comes from our choice of

\displaystyle\textup{{Opt}}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{k^{*}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(p_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon T}{4}\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{k^{*}}}[M_{k^{*}}(T)\leq T/2]

(

C.1
Properties of contracts played by
Algorithm

1

We start by presenting the formal definition of
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
. Recall that we introduced
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
as the a type such that, for all
\theta>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, the optimal action to be induced is
a_{2}
, and hence, the optimal contract is the null contract
\bar{p}=(0,\dots,0)
. The rationale behind
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
is that incentivizing
a_{1}
for types larger than
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
is not convenient for the principal. Specifically, we define
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
as the value of the following linear program.

\theta_{\textup{lazy}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in[0,1],p\in[0,1]^{m}}&\theta\\[5.0pt]
\text{s.t.}&(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p\geq\theta C_{a_{1}}\\[5.0pt]
&F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\end{array}\right.

(18)

First of all, we observe that
Equation

18
is always well-defined since it has a non-empty and bounded feasible region. To see that this region is non-empty it is sufficient to consider
\theta=0
and
p=(0,\dots,0)
. Then, all constraints are satisfied since
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r>F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
holds by assumption. Furthermore, it is also easy to see that
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}>0
, as
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
is strictly greater than
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
and
C_{a_{1}}
is strictly greater than
0
.

Next, we observe that for all
(\theta,p)
in the feasible region,
(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p\geq\theta C_{a_{1}}
, so that
a_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\theta,p)
, and, furthermore,
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
, so that the principal utility when playing
p
is larger than the optimal utility that can be obtained when playing contracts that induce
a_{2}
,
i.e.
,
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
. Note, moreover, that
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}(r-p)

This ensures that, when playing
p
against
\theta
,
b(\theta,p)=a_{1}
. It follows directly from this definition that, for all
\theta\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, it is optimal for the principal to induce
a_{1}
, while for all types greater than
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, it is optimal to induce
a_{2}
using the null contract. This result is proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 10

(Agent behaviors and optimal contracts)
.

For all
\theta>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
,
p=(0,\dots,0)
is an optimal contract. Furthermore, for all
\theta\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, there exists a contract
p^{\star}_{\theta}
such that:

\displaystyle\text{(i)~}\max_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p))=U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\theta},b(\theta,p^{\star}_{\theta}))

\displaystyle\text{(ii)~}a_{1}=b(\tilde{\theta},p^{\star}_{\theta})\quad\forall\tilde{\theta}\leq\theta

\displaystyle\text{(iii)~}a_{2}=b(\tilde{\theta},p^{\star}_{\theta})\quad\forall\tilde{\theta}>\theta.

In particular,
p^{\star}_{\theta}
is the solution of (
9
) with
\lambda
set to
\theta C_{a_{1}}
.

Proof.

First, fix any
\theta>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
and observe that the null contract yields utility
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
to the principal. Furthermore, it is also easy to see that the null contract is optimal among all the contracts for which the agent plays
a_{2}
. Next, focus on contracts for which the agent plays
a_{1}
. Specifically, suppose that there exists
p\in[0,1]^{m}
such that
a_{1}=b(\theta,p)
and
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
,
i.e.
, agent plays
a_{1}
when offered
p
, and
p
yields the same (or more) utility to the principal than the null contract. This however is impossible, since
a_{1}=b(\theta,p)
implies that
(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p\geq\theta C_{a_{1}}
, and, therefore, since, by assumption
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
holds,
(\theta,p)
would belong to the feasible region of (
18
). Then, since
\theta>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
,
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
cannot be an optimal solution of (
18
). Thus, we proved that there is no contract which induces the agent to play
a_{1}
while guaranteeing more utility for the principal. This concludes the first part of the proof.

Second, consider
\theta\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
. We start by proving (i). Observe that, due to
Lemma

3
, the maximum over contracts is well-defined.

\displaystyle\max_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p))

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\max_{p:a_{1}=b(\theta,p)}U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p)),\max_{p:a_{2}=b(\theta,p)}U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p))\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\max_{p:a_{1}=b(\theta,p)}U^{P}(p,b(\theta,p)),F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\max_{p:(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p\geq\theta C_{a_{1}}}F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p),F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r-\min_{p:(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p=\theta C_{a_{1}}}F_{a_{1}}^{\top}p,F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p^{\star}_{\theta}),F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\right\}.

where the last equality follows directly from the definition of
p^{\star}_{\theta}
. Now, observe that, since
p^{\star}_{\theta}
is optimal for
\theta
among all the contracts for which
a_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\theta,p)
, and since
\theta\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, it must hold that the pair
(\theta,p^{\star}_{\theta})
is a feasible solution of (
18
), and, therefore,
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p^{\star}_{\theta})\geq F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
. As a consequence, we have proved (i).

To prove (ii), it is sufficient to observe that
a_{1}=b(\theta,p^{\star}_{\theta})
. Hence, it must hold that
a_{1}=b(\tilde{\theta},p^{\star}_{\theta})
for all
\tilde{\theta}<\theta
as well.

Finally, to prove (iii), we observe that, by definition
p^{\star}_{\theta}
satisfies
(F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}})^{\top}p^{\star}_{\theta}=\theta C_{a_{1}}
. Therefore, for all
\tilde{\theta}>\theta
, action
a_{1}
cannot belong to
\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\theta},p^{\star}_{\theta})
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we show that the instantaneous regret for the contracts that we designed above is upper bounded by
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}
, and, therefore, they are suitable for exploration.

Lemma 11

(Sub-optimality gaps)
.

Suppose
F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r>F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
.

and

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star}_{1},b(\theta_{1},p_{1}^{\star}))-U^{P}(p_{2}^{\star},b(\theta_{1},p_{2}^{\star}))\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}.

(19)

Furthermore, let
\alpha>0
and define

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star}_{1},b(\theta_{1},p_{1}^{\star}))-U^{P}(p_{2}^{\star},b(\theta_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}^{\star}))\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha.

(20)

Finally, let
\bar{p}=(0,\dots,0)
. It holds that:

Proof.

We start by proving

.

and

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star}_{1},b(\theta_{1},p_{1}^{\star}))-U^{P}(p_{2}^{\star},b(\theta_{1},p_{2}^{\star}))=U^{P}(p^{\star}_{1},

\displaystyle b(\theta_{1},p_{1}^{\star}))-U^{P}(p^{\star}_{2},b(\theta_{2},p^{\star}_{2}))+

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\theta}\notin[s_{L},e_{L}]\right)\leq 2L\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right).

(26)

Proof.

The lemma is a combination of
Lemma

10
and
Lemma

12
.

We start by proving
Equation

25
. If
\bar{\theta}>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, we know from
Lemma

10
, that
\{\omega^{(0)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a stream of
n
i.i.d. samples from
F_{a_{2}}
. Thus, applying
Equation

23
in
Lemma

12
yields
Equation

25
.

Second, we continue by proving
Equation

26
. For each phase
l\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{l}
the following event:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{l}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{1}}\land Z_{l}>\tau\}\right)\lor\left(\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{2}}\land Z_{l}\leq\tau\}\right)\right\}.

Now, observe that, under
\bigcap_{l=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{E}_{l}
, we are guaranteed that
\bar{\theta}\in[s_{L},e_{L}]
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{E}_{0}
holds,
Algorithm

1
enters the exploration phase. Indeed, due to
Lemma

10
,
\bar{\theta}\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
responds to
p^{\star}_{\theta_{\textup{lazy}}}
by playing
a_{1}
and
Z_{0}>\tau
holds from
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. Then, the exploration phase runs a binary search sub-routine. Let us analyze the first phase
l=1
. Suppose that
\bar{\theta}\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{\textup{lazy}},\theta_{\textup{lazy}}\right]
. Then, by
Lemma

10
,
\bar{\theta}
responds to
p^{\star}_{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{\textup{lazy}}}
by playing
a_{2}
, and since
Z_{1}\leq\tau
holds, we have that the search continues by inspecting the interval
[s_{2},b_{2}]=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{\textup{lazy}},\theta_{\textup{lazy}}\right]
which is the one that contains
\bar{\theta}
. Similarly, if
\bar{\theta}\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]
, from
Lemma

10
, we have that
\bar{\theta}
plays
a_{1}
, and since
Z_{1}>\tau
, we have that the search continues by inspecting the interval
[s_{2},b_{2}]=\left[0,\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{\textup{lazy}}\right]
which is the one that contains
\bar{\theta}
. Then, by an inductive argument, we obtain that
\bar{\theta}\in[s_{L},e_{L}]
under
\bigcap_{l=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{E}_{l}
. Then, once we reach the last phase we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\theta}\notin[s_{L},e_{L}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{l}^{\complement}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{1}}\land Z_{l}\leq\tau\}\right\}\lor\left\{\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{2}}\land Z_{l}>\tau\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{1}}\land Z_{l}\leq\tau\}\right\}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{2}}\land Z_{l}>\tau\}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{l}\leq\tau\Big|\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{1}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{l}\geq\tau\Big|\{\omega_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim F_{a_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right),

(
Lemma

12
)

which concludes the proof.
∎

C.3.1
Regret Analysis for Lazy Types

Lemma 14

(Regret lazy types)
.

Suppose that
\bar{\theta}>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
and
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}>\beta
. Then,
Algorithm

1
guarantees that:

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}n+2T(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right).

Proof.

Denote by
p^{\star}
the null contract. Recall that, from
Lemma

10
the null contract is optimal for types greater than
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
. Furthermore, since
\theta>\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
, it must be that
\theta_{\textup{lazy}}<1
. Hence, it holds that
Algorithm

1
tests whether
\bar{\theta}
is lazy. It follows that:

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\theta_{\textup{lazy}}},\bar{\theta})\right)+\sum_{t={n+1}}^{T}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}n+\sum_{t={n+1}}^{T}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)

(
Lemma

11
)

Next, fix any
t\geq n+1
and analyze
U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]
using the law of total expectation. Denote by
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\{Z_{0}\leq\tau\}
. Observe that, under
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})\,\,|\,\,\mathcal{E}_{0}]=0,

as
Algorithm

1
plays the optimal contract until the end of the horizon. Instead, when
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\complement}
holds, we split the analysis in two cases. First, consider the case when
t\leq\{n+1,\dots,nL\}
. In this case,
Algorithm

1
is playing
p^{\star}_{\theta}
for some
\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]
where
p^{\star}_{\theta}
is as in
Lemma

10
. Thus, it holds that:

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})|\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\complement}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]}U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\theta},\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}

(
Lemma

11
)

If, instead,
t\geq nL+1
,
Algorithm

1
plays
\tilde{p}=p^{\star}_{\theta}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}_{\theta})
for some
\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]
.
It follows that:

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})|\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\complement}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]}U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\theta}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}_{\theta}),\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha.

(
Lemma

11
)

Combining these results, we have that:

\sum_{t={n+1}}^{T}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)\leq 2T(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\complement}\right).

Using
Lemma

13
we can upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\complement})
, obtaining:

R_{T}\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}n+2T(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

C.3.2
Regret Analysis for Non-Lazy Types

Lemma 15

(Regret for non-lazy types)
.

Suppose that
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}>\beta
and
\bar{\theta}\leq\theta_{\textup{lazy}}
. It holds that:

R_{T}\leq 2nL\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+2T\left(\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)+2(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)L\exp\left(-\frac{n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8}\right)\right).

Proof.

Denote by
p^{\star}
the optimal contract for the unknown true type
\bar{\theta}
. It holds that:

R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{nL}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)+\sum_{t={nL+1}}^{T}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right).

Focus on the first
nL
steps and observes that, by definition,
Algorithm

1
plays an optimal contract for some type
\theta\in[0,1]
. Thus, we can apply
Lemma

11
to obtain:

\sum_{t=1}^{nL}\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)\leq 2nL\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}.

Next, fix any
t\geq n+1
and analyze
U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]
using the law of total expectation. Precisely, we will use as event
\mathcal{E}=\{\bar{\theta}\in[s_{L},e_{L}]\}
.

We first analyze the case when
\mathcal{E}
holds. In this case, we first observe that
|\bar{\theta}-\hat{\theta}_{L}|\leq\frac{e_{L}-s_{L}}{2}\leq 2^{-L}
.

Furthermore,
Algorithm

1
plays
\tilde{p}=p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}})
and we have that:

\displaystyle U^{P}(\tilde{p},\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}},b(\hat{\theta}_{L},p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}})-\left(\frac{2^{-L}C_{a_{1}}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)

(
Lemma

19
)

\displaystyle=U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}},\hat{\theta}_{L})-\left(\frac{2^{-L}C_{a_{1}}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\geq U^{P}(p^{\star}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}),\hat{\theta}_{L})-\left(\frac{2^{-L}C_{a_{1}}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)

(
p^{\star}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}}
is opt. for
\hat{\theta}_{L}
)

\displaystyle\geq U^{P}(p^{\star},b(\bar{\theta},p^{\star}))-2\left(\frac{2^{-L}C_{a_{1}}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)

(
Lemma

19
)

In particular, when we used
Lemma

19
, we have used that types
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
for which
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq\epsilon
satisfy
b(\theta_{1},p)\in\mathcal{B}_{C_{a_{1}}\cdot\epsilon}(\theta_{2},p)
for any contract
p
. Thus, under
\mathcal{E}
, we obtain that:

U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})|\mathcal{E}]\leq 2\left(\frac{2^{-L}C_{a_{1}}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)\leq 2\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right).

Next, suppose that
\mathcal{E}
does not hold. Then, we split the analysis under two events
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\complement}
which are disjoint and that are such that
\mathcal{E}^{\complement}=\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\complement}
. The first one,
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement}
is when
Algorithm

1
plays the null contract as the first test on lazy types failed. In this case,
Lemma

11
yields:

U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement}]\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}.

In the second case,
i.e.
, on
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\complement}
, instead, we only know that
Algorithm

1
plays
\tilde{p}=p^{\star}_{\theta}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}_{\theta})
for some
\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]
.
Hence, we have that:

\displaystyle U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})|\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\complement}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in[0,\theta_{\textup{lazy}}]}U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-U^{P}(p^{\star}_{\theta}+\alpha(r-p^{\star}_{\theta}),\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha.

(
Lemma

11
)

Combining these results, we have that, for any
t\geq nL+1
:

\displaystyle\left(U^{P}(p^{\star},\bar{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[U^{P}(p_{t},\bar{\theta})]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)+(2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)\left(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement}_{1})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)+(2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement})

Combining these results with
Lemma

13
, we have that:

R_{T}\leq 2nL\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+2T\left(\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)+2(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}+\alpha)L\exp\left(-\frac{n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8}\right)\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

We start by combining

,
Lemma

15
and
Lemma

16
. Observe that the upper bound on the regret in

We choose

as follows:

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}

(27)

\displaystyle L=\min\left\{T,\lceil\log_{2}(4T)\rceil\right\}

(29)

\displaystyle\alpha=\min\left\{1,\sqrt{2^{-L}}\right\}

directly leads to

15
yields a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

Lemma

15
for completeness. We have that
R_{T}\leq h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}+h_{4}
, where the different terms are as follows

\displaystyle h_{1}=2nL\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1},\quad h_{2}=2T\left(\frac{2^{-L}}{\alpha}+\alpha\right)

\displaystyle h_{3}=4T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\exp\left(-\frac{n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle h_{4}=4T\alpha L\exp\left(-\frac{n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8}\right)

We proceed by showing that each
h_{i}
belongs to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.

Upper bound on
h_{1}

Suppose that
n=\lfloor T/L\rfloor
, which implies that

T\leq L+L\max\left\{{1,\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor}\right\}.

Furthermore, observe that this choice of
n
ensures that
h_{1}\leq 2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}
. Since
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T)
) and
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\in[T^{-1/2},1]
, we have that
h_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.
Next, suppose that
n=1
. Here, from
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
we obtain
h_{1}\leq L\in\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
.
Finally, suppose that
n=\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor
. In this case,

h_{1}\in\mathcal{O}\left(L\frac{1}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}\log(T)\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(L\sqrt{T}\log(T)\right)

Since
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
,
h_{1}\in\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})

Upper bound on
h_{2}

We proceed by cases. Suppose that
\alpha=1
and

. In this case,

, and we have that
h_{2}\leq 4T2^{-T}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.
Next, suppose that
\alpha=1
and

. Here, we have that
h_{2}\leq 4T2^{-\lceil\log_{2}(4T)\rceil}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.
Next, when
\alpha=\sqrt{2^{-L}}
and
L=T
,
h_{2}=4T\sqrt{2^{-T}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.
Finally, when
\alpha=\sqrt{2^{-L}}
and
L=\lceil\log_{2}(4T)\rceil
,
h_{2}=4T\sqrt{2^{-\lceil\log_{2}(4T)\rceil}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.

Upper bound on
h_{3}

First, suppose that
n=\lfloor T/L\rfloor
, which implies that

T\leq L+L\max\left\{{1,\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor}\right\}.

We observe that
h_{3}
reduces to:

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\exp\left(-\frac{T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8L}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8L}\right)

(
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\geq 1/\sqrt{T}
)

\displaystyle\leq 2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\left(L+L\max\left\{{1,\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}+2L^{2}\max\left\{{1,\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}+2L^{2}\max\left\{{1,\Big\lfloor 8\sqrt{T}\log\left(T\right)\Big\rfloor}\right\}

(
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\geq 1/\sqrt{T}
)

Since
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
, we obtained that
h_{3}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.

Next, suppose that
n=1
. In this case,
1\geq\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)
, which implies that

T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\exp(\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}/8)}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}.

Thus, we have that:

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\exp\left(-\frac{n\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sqrt{T}

(
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\geq 1/\sqrt{T}
)

Since
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
, we have
h_{3}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.

Finally, consider the last case where
n
is attained at
\Big\lfloor\frac{8}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}\log\left(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}\right)\Big\rfloor
. In this case:

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\exp\left(-\log(T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=2T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}L\frac{1}{T\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2L}{\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sqrt{T}.

(
\|F_{a_{1}}-F_{a_{2}}\|_{1}\geq 1/\sqrt{T}
)

Since
L\in\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
, we have
h_{3}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
.

Here, we aim to upper bound

with

. This allows to directly exploit the result for

to show that this term belongs to

.
To this end, observe that

. Since

, however, we have that this is equivalent to

, which is always true for our choice of
L
.

This concludes the proof.

and in particular, in the first instance
\theta_{0}=\frac{40\epsilon}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}
and
\theta_{1}=\frac{24\epsilon}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}
. Note that in both instances it is optimal to put a zero payment on the second outcome
\omega_{2}
. Let
x=p_{\omega_{1}}
be the payment on the first outcome.

Note that an agent of type
\theta
plays action
a_{1}
if and only if
(\frac{1}{2}+4\epsilon)x-\theta\geq\frac{x}{2}
, and thus if and only if
x\geq\frac{\theta}{4\epsilon}
. Thus, in instance
\mathfrak{I}_{0}
, the principal utility and mean of the Bernoulli describing the feedback distribution
\tau(x)
(of observing outcome
\omega_{1}
) are:

\displaystyle U_{0}^{P}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(1-x)&\text{if}\quad x<\frac{10}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}\\
(\frac{1}{2}+4\epsilon)(1-x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if}\quad x<\frac{10}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}\\
\frac{1}{2}+4\epsilon&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

while for instance
\mathfrak{I}_{1}
are:

\displaystyle U_{1}^{P}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(1-x)&\text{if}\quad x<\frac{6}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}\\
(\frac{1}{2}+4\epsilon)(1-x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if}\quad x<\frac{6}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}\\
\frac{1}{2}+4\epsilon&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It is clear that the
x\in\{0,\frac{6}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8},\frac{10}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}\}
are dominating actions in all instances in terms of both feedback and rewards. Then, a standard change-of-measure argument allows us to conclude the lower bound. Define
N_{0}(T)
the number of times
x_{0}=0
was played up to time
T
, and
N_{1}(T)
and
N_{2}(T)
the number of times
x_{1}=\frac{6}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}
and
x_{2}=\frac{10}{\frac{1}{\epsilon}+8}
was played. Then, there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that:

\textnormal{KL}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})\leq C\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{1}(T)]\epsilon^{2},

moreover, direct computations show that for some constant
c>0

U_{0}^{P}(x_{0})-U_{0}^{P}(x_{1})\geq c\epsilon,\quad U_{1}^{P}(x_{1})-U_{1}^{P}(x_{0})\geq c\epsilon,\quad\text{and}\quad U_{1}^{P}(x_{1})-U_{1}^{P}(x_{2})\geq c\epsilon.

Hence

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[R_{T}]\geq c\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{1}(T)],\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[R_{T}]\geq c\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[T-N_{1}(T)].

Now, if
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{1}(T)]\geq\tfrac{T}{4}
then
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[R_{T}]\geq\Omega(\epsilon T)
. On the other hand, if
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[N_{1}(T)]<\tfrac{T}{4}
then Markov’s inequality gives:

\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{0}}\left(N_{1}(T)>\frac{T}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2},

and (with
\epsilon=\Theta(1/\sqrt{T})
), Pinsker’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{1}}\left(N_{1}(T)>\frac{T}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{0}}\left(N_{1}(T)>\frac{T}{2}\right)+TV(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{4}

which can be rewritten as
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{1}}(N_{1}(T)\leq T/2)\geq 1/4
. Finally, again by Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[R_{T}]

\displaystyle\geq c\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[T-N_{1}(T)]

\displaystyle\geq c\epsilon\frac{T}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{1}}\Big[T-N_{1}(T)\geq\frac{T}{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Omega\Big(\epsilon T\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{1}}\Big[N_{1}(T)\leq\frac{T}{2}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(\epsilon T),

concluding the proof.
∎

E.1
The
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
case

In this section, we prove that when
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
holds, there exists a trivial algorithm that yields
R_{T}=0
for any sequence
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

Lemma 17

(The
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
case)
.

Suppose that
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
. Then, there exists an algorithm for which
R_{T}=0
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{p}=(0,\dots,0)
and observe that, for any
\theta\in[0,1]
, we have that

\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{a\in\{1,2\}}F_{a}^{\top}\bar{p}-\theta\cdot C_{a}=-\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{a\in\{1,2\}}\theta\cdot C_{a}.

Hence, since
C_{a_{2}}\leq C_{a_{1}}
,
a_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\theta,\bar{p})
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, since
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
, it also holds that
a_{2}\in b(\theta,\bar{p})
for all types
\theta
. As a consequence, we have that
U^{P}(\bar{p},b(\theta,\bar{p}))=F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. Next, we observe that:

Opt

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\{a_{1}\in b(\theta_{t},p)\}F_{a_{1}}^{\top}(r-p)+\mathds{1}\{b(\theta_{t},p)=a_{2}\}F_{a_{2}}^{\top}(r-p)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{p\in[0,1]^{m}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\{a_{1}\in b(\theta_{t},p)\}F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r+\mathds{1}\{b(\theta_{t},p)=a_{2}\}F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\right)

(
p\geq 0
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r

(
F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r\geq F_{a_{1}}^{\top}r
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}U^{P}(\theta_{t},b(\theta_{t},\bar{p})).

(
U^{P}(\bar{p},b(\theta,\bar{p}))=F_{a_{2}}^{\top}r
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
)

Hence, the constant strategy
\bar{p}
is optimal for any sequence
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
thus concluding the proof.
∎

E.4
Optimization of Upper Semicontinuous Functions

We recall the definition of upper semicontinuity. Assume that
(X,d)
is a metric space and consider
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. Then,
f
is upper semicontinuous at
x_{0}\in X
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
f(x)<f(x_{0})+\epsilon
for all
x
such that
d(x,x_{0})<\delta
. We now state a well-known result for optimization of upper semicontinuous functions.

Theorem 6
.

If
C
is a compact set, and
f:C\to\mathbb{R}
is upper semicontinuous, then
f
attains a maximum on
C
.
