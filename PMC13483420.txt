Evaluation of temporomandibular joint disc displacement via MRI and computerized axiography: a case report
Authors: Pan Jiang, Liuli Jin, Qun Yang, Zhiwen Li, Junming Wang, Yuan Lin, Xiangxin Meng, Xiaohui Rausch-Fan, Bingran Du, Xueyang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMagnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is commonly used as a reference imaging modality for evaluating the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disc-condyle relationship because it provides direct visualization of the articular disc in closed- and open-mouth positions. However, routine MRI primarily provides structural information at static mandibular positions and may not adequately reflect dynamic mandibular function. Consequently, discrepancies between imaging findings and clinical symptoms or treatment response may create diagnostic uncertainty in selected patients. Computerized axiography records mandibular movement trajectories and condylar motion and may provide supplementary functional information in selected cases; nevertheless, its interpretation remains limited by operator dependency, recording conditions, device-related differences, and the absence of universally accepted diagnostic thresholds. Importantly, structural abnormalities identified by MRI do not always correspond directly with functional mandibular performance or short-term treatment response, which may create challenges in the interpretation of individual clinical cases.Case presentationA 22-year-old female presented with a 3-month history of bilateral TMJ clicking and localized pain in the left joint. According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) Axis I criteria, the clinical findings were compatible with bilateral disc displacement with reduction, based on a history of TMJ noises and examiner-detected reciprocal clicking during opening and closing. MRI further demonstrated bilateral anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR). Background

TMDs comprise a heterogeneous group of conditions involving the TMJ, masticatory muscles, and associated structures [1, 2]. Anterior disc displacement (ADD) is one of the common intra-articular findings and is generally classified as anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR) or without reduction (ADDwoR) [3–5]. When the functional coordination of the disc-condyle complex is altered, TMD-related symptoms such as joint noise, pain, and movement limitation may occur [5]. MRI is commonly regarded as the reference imaging modality for evaluating disc-condyle relationships because of its superior soft-tissue visualization [6–9]. However, routine MRI mainly provides static anatomical information at specific mandibular positions and may not fully reflect functional mandibular movement during dynamic activities [8, 9].

Computerized axiography records mandibular movement trajectories, including mandibular opening–closing, protrusive–retrusive, and lateral excursion movements, and may provide additional functional information regarding condylar motion [10–12]. Previous studies have investigated the association between axiographic trajectory patterns and TMJ disc displacement, but the method remains controversial because standardized diagnostic cut-offs are limited, reproducibility depends on examination protocols and patient cooperation, and diagnostic accuracy is insufficient for use as a standalone test [10–12].

In the present case, altered mandibular movement trajectories were identified on computerized axiography in addition to the MRI findings. Improvement in clinical symptoms and mandibular function was observed during short-term follow-up. This case is therefore presented as an exploratory observation suggesting that computerized axiography may provide supplementary functional information in selected patients; further research is required to clarify its diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic significance [10–12].

Case description

Patient description

The patient was a 22-year-old female presenting with bilateral TMJ clicking for more than three months, accompanied by localized pain in the left joint that worsened during mastication.

Timeline

Month 0: The patient presented with a 3-month history of bilateral TMJ clicking and left-side joint pain. MRI examination revealed bilateral ADDwR. ARS therapy was prescribed.

Week 1: The patient reported worsening pain in the left TMJ after ARS therapy. Clinical re-evaluation was performed, and computerized axiography was undertaken to further assess mandibular function. ARS therapy was discontinued and a pivot splint was initiated.

Week 3: After 2 weeks of pivot splint therapy, reciprocal clicking and masticatory muscle tenderness had resolved.

Week 5: Follow-up axiography showed improved mandibular movement trajectories, and CBCT acquired while the patient was wearing the pivot splint showed increased posterior and superior joint spaces.

Post-treatment: Follow-up MRI showed a more favorable disc-condyle relationship bilaterally. Orthodontic treatment was subsequently initiated to optimize occlusion and stabilize the corrected disc-condyle relationship.

Pre-treatment physical examination

Intraoral photography revealed bilateral Angle Class I molar relationships, with a deep overbite and overjet. The maxillary arch exhibited mild crowding, whereas the mandibular arch demonstrated moderate crowding. In addition, the left maxillary second premolar was displaced lingually (Fig. 1).

Pre-treatment intraoral photographs (A–E)

Clinical examination of the TMJ demonstrated a maximum unassisted mouth opening of 40 mm. Reciprocal clicking was detected bilaterally during mandibular opening and closing, with the opening click occurring during early opening and the closing click occurring near the end of closure. Palpation elicited tenderness in the retrodiscal tissues and temporomandibular ligaments on both sides, which was more pronounced in the left joint.

Pre-treatment imaging examination

The use of MRI and CBCT was based on their complementary diagnostic roles in TMJ evaluation and followed the current position statement of the American Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (AAOMR) and the American Academy of Orofacial Pain, which recommends that imaging be selected according to clinical indication and that MRI be used for soft-tissue disc assessment whereas CBCT is more appropriate for osseous evaluation [8]. Imaging findings were interpreted together with clinical examination rather than as independent determinants of diagnosis or treatment.

CBCT examinations were performed twice: the first scan was acquired in the intercuspal position (ICP), and the second scan was obtained while the patient was wearing the pivot splint. Scans were acquired using a NewTom NTVGiMK4 unit (QR Verona, Italy) with the following parameters: voxel size, 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm; image matrix, 512 × 512 × 512; tube voltage, 110 kVp; tube current, 5 mA; exposure time, 3600 ms; and reconstructed slice thickness, 0.3 mm. A small isotropic voxel and thin reconstructed slices were selected to improve visualization of condylar cortical outlines and joint-space morphology, whereas identical acquisition and reconstruction settings were used to minimize measurement bias between baseline and splint-in-place scans [8]. Three-dimensional reconstruction and image superimposition of the bilateral TMJs were performed using Mimics Research software (version 19.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). All datasets were reconstructed and reviewed using identical display settings, including image density/brightness, contrast, sharpness, and slice thickness, to ensure comparability. The field of view was restricted to the clinically relevant maxillofacial region whenever possible to comply with the principle of justified and optimized CBCT use [8].

Pre-treatment CBCT confirmed the presence of tooth germs of teeth 18, 28, 38, and 48, with mesioangular impaction of teeth 38 and 48 (Fig. 2). The morphology of the bilateral condyles was within normal limits. Cortical outlines were continuous and intact. No radiographic evidence of condylar erosion, osteophyte formation, flattening, sclerosis, or subcortical cystic changes was observed. However, reduced superior and posterior joint spaces were noted bilaterally (Fig. 3) [8].

CBCT images of the temporomandibular joints (TMJs)

MRI served as the reference imaging modality for evaluation of the disc-condyle relationship [6–9]. Bilateral TMJs were assessed on sagittal oblique images perpendicular to the long axis of the condyle in closed-mouth and maximum-opening positions; coronal oblique images were also reviewed when available to evaluate mediolateral disc position and osseous contour [6, 8]. Disc position was evaluated using the clock-face reference method: the 12-o’clock position of the condylar head was used as the reference, and anterior disc displacement was diagnosed when the posterior band of the articular disc was located anterior to the 12-o’clock position in the closed-mouth position [6, 8]. Disc reduction was considered present when the disc returned to a normal disc-condyle relationship during mouth opening [6, 8]. Joint effusion was assessed on fluid-sensitive sequences as high-signal intra-articular fluid, and degenerative osseous changes were assessed by evaluating cortical erosion, osteophyte formation, flattening, sclerosis, and subcortical cystic change [8]. These items were recorded according to the AAOMR recommendations for standardized TMJ imaging interpretation [8].

MRI examination revealed that, in the closed-mouth position, the posterior bands of both articular discs were located anterior to the 12-o’clock position relative to the condylar heads. During mouth opening, both discs were recaptured and maintained a normal disc-condyle relationship throughout condylar translation. No obvious joint effusion or degenerative osseous changes were observed. These findings were consistent with bilateral ADDwR (Fig. 4) [6, 8].

Diagnosis and treatment process

According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) Axis I diagnostic algorithm, the patient was clinically classified as having bilateral disc displacement with reduction, which was further supported by MRI findings of bilateral ADDwR [3, 6, 8]. Based on these findings, an ARS was selected as the initial therapeutic intervention [13]. The patient was instructed to wear the splint full-time, except during oral hygiene procedures. One week later, the patient reported worsening pain in the left joint. Clinical examination revealed tenderness in the left retrodiscal tissues, temporomandibular ligaments, and maxillary tuberosity region. Because the clinical response was inconsistent with the expected short-term response, further functional assessment was undertaken. Computerized axiography was therefore performed to evaluate mandibular movement trajectories and condylar motion [10–12]. The results were interpreted in conjunction with the patient’s clinical findings and MRI examination to guide subsequent treatment planning rather than as an independent diagnostic standard. The mandibular opening–closing trajectory exhibited an “8-shaped” non-overlapping configuration, with incomplete overlap between repeated movement cycles, suggesting reduced reproducibility of mandibular movement [10–12]. During protrusive-retrusive movement, the protrusive condylar path length was approximately 3–4 mm on the left side and approximately 10–11 mm on the right side. Because reported protrusive condylar path lengths in asymptomatic subjects commonly approach or exceed approximately 10 mm, the left-side value was interpreted as restricted protrusive condylar translation rather than a definitive disease-specific sign (Fig. 5) [14].Based on the exacerbation of symptoms after ARS therapy, the persistence of joint-related tenderness, and the supportive computerized axiographic findings, ARS therapy was discontinued and a pivot splint was selected as an individualized therapeutic alternative (Fig. 6).

Treatment outcome

At the two-week follow-up after initiation of pivot splint therapy, bilateral reciprocal clicking during mandibular opening and closing was no longer detected clinically, and tenderness of the masticatory muscles was not observed. Because this was an uncontrolled single-case observation, these short-term changes were described as clinical improvement rather than proof of treatment efficacy. Four weeks after treatment, computerized axiography was repeated. Compared with the baseline recordings, the protrusive condylar path length on the left side increased to approximately 8–10 mm, and the protrusive–retrusive movement recording demonstrated improved anterior condylar translation (Fig. 7A). In addition, the mandibular opening–closing trajectory showed improved reproducibility and a greater range of motion compared with the pre-treatment recordings (Fig. 7B). The “8-shaped” pattern was interpreted descriptively as a non-overlapping mandibular opening–closing trajectory that may reflect altered coordination or reduced reproducibility, rather than as a validated diagnostic sign with a fixed threshold [10–12].

CBCT acquired while the patient was wearing the pivot splint revealed that, compared with the baseline intercuspal position (ICP) scan, both condyles were positioned more anteroinferiorly within the articular fossae. This positional change was associated with an increase in the posterior and superior joint spaces bilaterally (Fig. 8).

Patient perspective

“I was initially frustrated when the first splint made my left jaw pain worse and chewing became more difficult. After the doctors examined how my jaw was moving and explained the possible reasons for changing the appliance, they changed to a pivot splint. Within a few weeks, the clicking stopped, and I could move my jaw with less pain. I understand that continued follow-up is still needed. I am satisfied with the care and the clear explanation provided by the dental team.”

Discussion

Routine MRI examinations primarily evaluate the TMJ in static positions, typically closed-mouth and maximum-opening positions. Therefore, functional abnormalities occurring during intermediate or non-routine mandibular movements, such as protrusive movement, may not be fully reflected on standard MRI examinations. This limitation may partly explain why patients with similar MRI findings sometimes demonstrate different symptoms and treatment responses [8–9].

The present case may represent an exploratory observation of a potential discrepancy between static MRI findings and dynamic mandibular functional behavior. Although MRI demonstrated bilateral anterior disc displacement with reduction, the patient showed asymmetric functional limitation and symptom aggravation after ARS therapy. This finding suggests that static imaging alone may not fully explain functional abnormalities in selected patients with TMD. From a clinical perspective, such discrepancies may be relevant when imaging findings are inconsistent with clinical symptoms or treatment response. Therefore, integrating functional mandibular motion analysis with conventional imaging may provide complementary information in selected cases. The clinical relevance of this case does not lie in demonstrating a superior diagnostic modality or a novel treatment approach, but rather in illustrating a potential discrepancy between structural imaging findings and dynamic mandibular functional behavior in an individual patient.

Computerized mandibular motion analysis is a kinematic assessment method that records mandibular or condylar movement relative to a cranial reference system. Contemporary systems can be broadly categorized into electronic axiographic or condylographic systems, ultrasonic tracking systems, and optoelectronic tracking systems according to their recording principles [12, 15–17]. Electronic axiographic systems, such as CADIAX Compact II, use measuring styli and electronic recording flags or sensors to record bilateral condylar-path movements. The CADIAX Compact II system has also been described as an electronic motion-recording system based on the voltage-division method [12]. Ultrasonic systems, such as KaVo ARCUSdigma and Zebris JMA systems, calculate mandibular movement from the time of flight of ultrasonic signals [16]. Optoelectronic systems, including MODJAW and Zebris JMA-Optic, reconstruct mandibular movement by camera-based tracking of markers attached to the mandibular dentition or a paraocclusal support [15, 17].

These systems differ not only in their sensing technologies but also in their posterior reference points, reference planes, coordinate systems, calibration procedures, and data-processing algorithms. Comparative studies have shown that individual systems may demonstrate good within-device repeatability, whereas the absolute values obtained from different devices may differ significantly [15]. Therefore, measurements obtained using different mandibular motion-recording systems should not be regarded as directly interchangeable. Moreover, none of these systems directly visualizes the articular disc or other intra-articular soft-tissue structures, and universally accepted diagnostic thresholds for specific TMD subtypes remain unavailable. Mandibular motion analysis should therefore be regarded as a complementary functional assessment and interpreted together with the patient’s history, clinical examination, and structural imaging, particularly MRI.

The present study used the CADIAX Compact II system (Gamma Dental, Austria), an electronic axiographic system for recording bilateral condylar-path movements [12, 15]. The system consists of a cranial face-bow carrying electronic recording flags or sensors and a mandibular bow connected to measuring styli. Relative movement between the mandibular and cranial components is electronically converted into condylar-path recordings during opening–closing, protrusive–retrusive, and lateral mandibular movements. The recorded trajectories permit assessment of movement path length, trajectory morphology, start- and end-point stability, bilateral condylar coordination, and the reproducibility of repeated movement cycles [10, 12, 14].

Depending on the clinical recording protocol, the posterior reference points used by CADIAX Compact II may be established arbitrarily according to the face-bow configuration or may be localized kinematically through small rotational mandibular movements. Therefore, the hinge-axis reference obtained using this system should be understood as a protocol-dependent kinematic reference rather than a directly visualized anatomical structure. In the present case, CADIAX Compact II was used primarily to characterize condylar-path length, trajectory morphology, and movement reproducibility, rather than to determine the true anatomical hinge axis. Although the system provides patient-specific functional information and permits digital superimposition of repeated movement recordings, its results may be influenced by posterior reference-point selection, face-bow and mandibular-bow positioning, head posture, patient cooperation, pain-related guarding, movement velocity, calibration, and recording conditions [10, 12, 15].

In the present case, computerized axiography demonstrated markedly restricted protrusive movement on the left side. Compared with the contralateral joint and with reported protrusive pathway values in asymptomatic subjects, the left protrusive path length was substantially reduced [14]. The mandibular opening-closing trajectory showed an “8-shaped” non-overlapping pattern, which was interpreted as reduced reproducibility of repeated mandibular movement cycles [10–12]. Based on the mandibular motion analysis and clinical findings, the treatment strategy was modified from an ARS to a pivot splint [13, 18, 19]. This adjustment was considered appropriate because the patient exhibited marked restriction of left protrusive condylar translation on functional movement assessment, together with joint-related tenderness and symptom aggravation after ARS therapy. Previous studies have suggested that, in selected patients with internal derangement, pivot splints may guide mandibular rotation around a posterior fulcrum, thereby modifying condylar position, altering joint loading, and potentially increasing the superior joint space [18, 19]. Therefore, the selection of a pivot splint in this case was an individualized treatment adjustment based on clinical symptoms, MRI findings, and computerized axiographic observations.

A stabilization splint is one of the commonly used conservative treatments for TMD. Stabilization splints are typically used to reduce parafunctional loading, improve occlusal stability, and decrease masticatory muscle hyperactivity [20]. However, it was not selected as the subsequent treatment option in this case because the main clinical concern was not generalized muscular pain or simple occlusal instability, but unilateral restriction of protrusive condylar translation accompanied by joint-related tenderness and symptom aggravation after ARS therapy.

Following pivot splint therapy, improvement was observed in clinical symptoms, mandibular movement trajectories, and imaging findings. Nevertheless, the use of a pivot splint in this case should be interpreted as an individualized clinical decision rather than a generally applicable treatment recommendation. Clinical reports and comparative studies on disc displacement-related TMD have described the use of pivot splints, either alone or as part of conservative therapy; however, the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous [18, 19]. The subsequent improvement should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Several limitations of this report should be acknowledged. First, this study describes only a single patient; therefore, the level of evidence is very low, statistical power is absent, and the findings are not generalizable. Second, the follow-up period was approximately 4–5 weeks, which is insufficient to evaluate the long-term stability of the disc-condyle relationship or the risk of symptom recurrence. Third, interpretation of mandibular movement trajectories remains partially operator-dependent, and universally accepted diagnostic thresholds have not yet been established [10–12]. Mandibular movement recordings may be influenced by patient cooperation, pain-related guarding, mandibular positioning, equipment calibration, and the selected reference point [10, 12]. Evidence regarding the diagnostic accuracy of computerized axiography for specific disc displacement subtypes is also heterogeneous [10, 11]. Therefore, computerized axiography should not be considered a standalone diagnostic tool and cannot replace MRI for assessment of disc position [10–12]. Rather, it may serve as a supplementary functional assessment method when structural imaging findings do not fully explain clinical symptoms. Fourth, the observed improvement may have been influenced by multiple concurrent factors, including splint-related changes in joint loading, patient adherence, behavioral modification, natural symptom fluctuation, and subsequent orthodontic management.

This case does not demonstrate the superiority of computerized axiography, but documents an individual clinical scenario in which dynamic mandibular motion analysis provided additional observations when structural MRI findings alone did not fully explain the patient’s clinical course.

Future research should focus on improving the standardization and interoperability of different tracking technologies to facilitate comparisons across platforms and enable multicenter studies. The integration of mandibular motion data with three-dimensional imaging, occlusal force analysis, and artificial intelligence-based data interpretation may provide more comprehensive insights into stomatognathic function [21–24]. In addition, larger prospective longitudinal studies with standardized MRI, CBCT, and computerized axiographic protocols and extended follow-up periods are needed to establish clinically meaningful diagnostic thresholds, determine the prognostic value of mandibular movement parameters, and clarify their potential role in supporting individualized clinical assessment. Although current systems provide valuable functional information, further evidence is required before computerized mandibular motion analysis can be routinely incorporated into evidence-based clinical decision-making. Several important clinical questions remain unresolved, including which mandibular motion parameters are clinically meaningful, whether specific movement patterns are associated with prognosis, and whether dynamic functional changes precede or reflect alterations in intra-articular structures.

Conclusion

Findings from this individual case should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Computerized axiography may provide supplementary functional information when routine MRI findings do not fully explain clinical symptoms or treatment response. However, because this report describes only one patient and the follow-up period was short, no causal relationship, treatment efficacy, or generalizable clinical recommendation can be established. Further prospective studies with standardized diagnostic criteria, quantitative reference parameters, and long-term follow-up are required to determine whether computerized axiography has clinical value as an adjunctive assessment method rather than a standalone diagnostic or treatment-guiding tool in selected patients with TMD.
