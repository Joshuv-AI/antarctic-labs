Unbent collections of non-planar s s -grid-drawings
Therese Biedl

Abstract
In a recent paper, Antić et al. studied collections of planar orthogonal drawings of a graph where every edge is unbent in at least one drawing. This paper generalizes this concept to non-planar drawings, and shows that then two drawings always suffice (for planar drawings three drawings are sometimes needed). The results can also be generalized to s s -grid drawings for s ≥ 3 s\geq 3 .

Introduction

In the standard
graph drawing
problem, we are given a graph and the goal is to create a
drawing (often a node-link-diagram in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but other graph drawing styles and/or higher dimensions have been studied).
See various textbooks and handbooks
[
7
,
13
,
16
,
20
]
for an overview of the field.

If the given graph is very complex, then creating one legible drawing is nearly impossible. For this reason, Hliněný and Masařík
[
11
]
proposed a different paradigm, where we are given a graph
G
and we want to create a
collection of drawings

\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}
of
G
for some
k\geq 1
that all together satisfy some property that is useful for visualization purposes. Hliněný and Masařík specifically studied collections
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}
of node-link diagrams under the constraint that every edge is drawn without crossing in at least one of
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}
. They showed that it is NP-hard to minimize the
order

k
, and gave various asymptotic bounds on the order that can be achieved for complete and other dense graphs.

The idea of collections of drawings was picked up by Antić, Liotta, Masaříc, Ortali, Pfretzschner, Stumpf, Wolff and Zink
[
1
]
who studied a very different drawing style. Specifically, they considered
planar graphs
(graphs that can be drawn in the plane without crossing) and
orthogonal drawings
(a drawing style that represents vertices as points and edges as polygonal curves that use only horizontal and vertical segments); furthermore the drawings must be planar, i.e., not have any crossings. Such drawings can exist only if the graph is a
4-graph
, i.e., every vertex has at most four incident edges. Orthogonal drawing have a long history, see for example an overview article by Duncan and Goodrich and the references therein
[
9
]
.

The goal in the paper of Antić et al.
[
1
]
was to create an
unbent collection
of planar orthogonal drawings, i.e., they want to find planar orthogonal drawings
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}
such that every edge
e
is drawn
without bend

in at least one of the drawings. They argued that for some planar 4-graphs (including the octahedron, the graph of Figure
1
), this cannot be achieved with at most two planar drawings. On the other hand it is easy to show that
k=3
drawings always suffice.

in one of the drawings. No two
planar
drawings of the octahedron with this property
exist
[

]
.

Inspired by the paper by Antic et al.
[
1
]
, we ask a very similar question, but for graphs that are not necessarily planar (and so the drawings also need not be planar). Thus, given a 4-graph
G
, we want to find an unbent collection of (not necessarily planar) orthogonal drawings of
G

whose order is small.
It is very easy to see that order
3
is always possible. But as the main result of this paper, we show that in fact order
2
suffices.

Theorem 1.1
.

Every 4-graph
G
has an unbent collection of orthogonal drawings of order 2.

Note that ‘two’ is obviously best-possible; there are many 4-graphs that do not have an orthogonal drawing without bends (consider
a triangle, for example, or graph
K_{2,3}
). Testing whether order one suffices
for a specific graph
G
is NP-hard, since this is the same as testing
whether

has an orthogonal drawing without bends, which was shown to be NP-hard by
Eades, Hong, and Poon
[
10
]
.

A natural generalizations of orthogonal drawings are

-grid drawings
. Formal definitions will be given below, but the most prominent examples are

(
s=3
) and
octilinear drawings
(
s=4
), see also Figure

2s

2s
.
Much less is known about these types of drawings, but see for example

[
2
,
3
,
12
,
21
]

and
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
17
]
for results on hexagonal and octilinear drawing results, respectively,

[

for planar drawings with

There appear to be no previous results on unbent collections of
s
-grid drawings.
We show that the results for orthogonal drawings transfer to
s
-grid drawings.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
s\geq 3
, every
2s
-graph has an unbent collection of
s
-grid drawings of order
s
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For

Clearly again order two or more is needed for the unbent collections of

2
Preliminaries

We assume familiarity with graphs, see e.g.
[
8
]
.
In this paper, the input graph
G
always has
n
vertices and is assumed to be simple.
A
drawing
of
G
is an assignment of distinct
points to vertices of
G
, and a polygonal curve to every edge such that the curve of
e=(v,w)
connects
the two points of
v
and
w
, contains no other vertex-points, and any two edge-curves share a
finite number of points.
Where no confusion arises,
the graph-theoretic name (‘vertex’, ‘edge’) also refers to its geometric representation (‘point’, ‘curve’).
The place where an edge-curve changes direction is called a
bend
, and an edge is called

unbent
if it has no bends. A drawing is called
unbent
if all its edges are.
An
unbent collection
(of drawings of some type that will be clear from context) is a set
of such drawings of the same graph such that every edge is unbent in at least one of them.

A
planar drawing
of a graph is a drawing such that edge-curves do share not points except
at their endpoints; the graph that has such a drawing is called
planar
.
A
plane graph
is a planar graph with a fixed
rotation scheme
, i.e., for every vertex

v
there is a fixed cyclical order
\rho(v)
of the incident edges. A
plane drawing

of such a graph is a drawing where the incident edges of every vertex
v
appear in the order of

\rho(v)
when enumerated in the ccw order in which they leave
v
.

Let
{\cal S}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
be a finite set of
slopes
.
The
gridlines
of an
{\cal S}
-grid
are all those lines
\ell
that have a slope in
{\cal S}

and that pass through a
grid point
, i.e., a point with integral coordinates.
(Since slopes are rational or infinity, any gridline passes through infinitely many grid points.)
The
hexagonal grid
uses
{\cal S}=\{0,1,\infty\}
1
1

1

This is, up to a shear, equivalent to the more commonly used approach of defining the hexagonal
grid to have lines of slope
\{0,\pm\sqrt{3}/2\}
.

while the
octilinear grid
uses
{\cal S}=\{0,\pm 1,\infty\}
.
An
s
-grid
(for some integer
s\geq 2
) refers to an
{\cal S}
-grid for some set
{\cal S}
with
|{\cal S}|=s
;
the specific set
{\cal S}
will not matter for the arguments.

A drawing of a graph
G
is an
s
-grid drawing

if every vertex-point and bend is at a grid point
and every edge-segment lies on a gridline of the
s
-grid. For every
grid point
there are
2s
incident
gridrays
, i.e., rays that go along gridlines,
and edges can only leave
v
along gridrays. Therefore
s
-grid drawings can only exist
if every vertex has degree at most
2s
; graphs that satisfy this are called
2s
-graphs
.

A few graph-theoretic definitions are needed. A
pseudotree
is a connected
graph that is a tree after deleting at most one edge.
A
pseudoforest
is a graph
G
where every connected component is a pseudotree.
A
cactus
is a graph for which all cycles
are edge-disjoint. A
strict cactus
is a graph for which all cycles are vertex-disjoint.
Every pseudoforest is a strict cactus.
Use the prefix ‘
2s
-’ for such graphs if their degrees are at most
2s
.

An
edge-partition
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a partition of
E
with every
edge appearing in exactly one of the sets. The
arboricity
[
pseudoarboricity
] of
a graph is the smallest number
k
of sets in an edge-partition
E_{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{k}
such that every subgraph
(V,E_{i})

is a forest [pseudoforest]. The following is known:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
15
,
19
]
)
.

The arboricity of a graph
G
is

\max_{H}\lceil\tfrac{|E(H)|}{|V(H)|-1}\rceil

while its pseudoarboricity is
\max_{H}\lceil\tfrac{|E(H)|}{|V(H)|}\rceil
,
where the maximum is taken over all subgraphs
H
of
G

with enough vertices to make the denominator non-zero.

3
Proof-skeleton

This section gives the proof of the main result: For
s\geq 2

any
2s
-graph
G
has a small unbent collection of
s
-grid drawings.
Some of the steps in these proofs
are non-trivial (and possibly of interest in their own right) and their proofs
are hence delayed till later.

As a first step, convert the
problem of finding unbent collections into an edge-partition problem.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
2s
-graph with an edge-partition
E=E_{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{k}

such that

G_{i}=(V,E_{i})
has an unbent
s
-grid-drawing
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}

for
i\in[k]
..
Then
G
has an unbent collection
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}
of
s
-grid-drawings.

Proof 3.2
.

View each
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}
as a drawing of
G
where some edges (namely, those in
E\setminus E_{i}
)
are
missing
. So it remains to add the missing edges into drawing
\Gamma^{\prime}_{i}
.
It is folklore how to do this for orthogonal drawings by adding at most two rows and two columns
per missing edge, see for example related discussions in
[
18
]
.
For
s
-grid drawings, no previous discussions of how to add missing edges seem to exist,
but it is not hard to see that this can be done after scaling the drawing suitably;
details are in Section
5
.

Note here Lemma
3.1
is
not
true if
the resulting collection must consist of
planar
drawings; this is the
main reason that Theorem
1.3
requires one more drawing
than Theorem
1.2
.

With Lemma
3.1
in hand, the next step is to find
subgraphs of
G
that have unbent
s
-grid-drawings.
The following result is well known for trees and orthogonal drawings, and can easily be transferred
to strict cacti and
s
-grid drawings. See Section
5
for details
and the thicker lines in Figure
1
for an example for triangle-free pseudoforests.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
s
, every plane
2s
-forest has a plane unbent
s
-grid drawing.

If
s\geq 3
, then every strict
2s
-cactus has a planar unbent
s
-grid-drawing.

Every triangle-free strict 4-cactus has a planar unbent orthogonal drawing.

The final piece of the puzzle is to find an edge-partition into suitable subgraphs. This is very easy if
subgraphs need not be triangle-free.

Lemma 3.4
.

Every
2s
-graph
G
has an edge-partition into
s
pseudoforests, and
an edge-partition into
s+1
forests.

Proof 3.5
.

Every subgraph
H
of
G
has maximum degree
2s
, and hence
|E(H)|\leq sh

where
h=|V(H)|
. So by Theorem
2.1
the pseudoarboricity of
G
is at most
s
.

As for the arboricity, if
h\geq s+1
then
sh\leq(s{+}1)(h{-}1)
.
If
h\leq s
then any subgraph
H
of
G
with
h
vertices has at most
h(h-1)/2\leq s(h-1)/2\leq(s+1)(h-1)
edges by simplicity.
So the arboricity of
G
is at most
s{+}1
by Theorem
2.1
.

With this the proof of Theorem
1.2
is finished: Split the edges of the given
2s
-graph
G
into
s
pseudoforests with Lemma
3.4
, each of them has an unbent
s
-grid drawing by Lemma
3.3
, and by Lemma
3.1
the theorem holds.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
is very similar, but uses an edge-partition into forests and needs some extra care to preserve planarity, see Section
5
.

For
d=2
(i.e., Theorem
1.1
) the proof is a bit harder, because here it does not suffice to take any partition into pseudoforests; we have to find
triangle-free
subgraphs. It is unknown whether a partition into triangle-free pseudoforests always exists, but the following weaker result holds (the proof is non-trivial and in Section
4

Lemma 3.6
.

Every
4
-graph has an edge-partition into two triangle-free strict cacti.

Combining this with Lemma
3.1
and
3.3

as before then proves Theorem
1.1
.

5
Adding to drawings

It remains to show the two other ingredients: Trees and strict cacti have unbent drawings, and edges can be inserted
into existing drawings if bends are allowed. Both use related techniques, and both are nearly obvious for orthogonal
drawings as long as one may insert new gridlines. So we only sketch the proofs here to ensure that
nothing breaks when building
s
-grid drawings for arbitrary
s\geq 2
instead.

Claim 3
.

Let
\Gamma
be an
s
-grid drawing and
v
be a vertex where one of the gridrays
r
emanating from
v
is not
used by an edge. Then, after scaling the drawing suitably, we can add a new vertex
w
to
\Gamma
as well
as an edge
(v,w)
that is routed along
r
without bend or crossing.

Proof 5.1
.

Walk along ray
r
from
v
until we hit another grid point
p
(this must happen since slopes are rational).
Let
q

6
Outlook

In this paper, we studied how to create collections of
s
-grid drawings such that every edge of the graph is drawn without bend in at least one of them. This can always be done with
s
drawings if crossings are allowed, and
s{+}1
drawings otherwise. For
s=2
this is best-possible; for
s\geq 3
it remains an open problem whether there are graphs that require more than two drawings in such a collection. (
K_{2s+1}
would be a natural candidate to try.)

We note that for all our results we paid no attention to the grid size, especially when scaling in Section
5
. For orthogonal drawings we can always postprocess the result and remove unused gridlines; the non-planar drawings then have size
O(n)\times O(n)
since we only use a constant number of gridlines for each edge. For
s
-grid drawings for
s\geq 3
the drawings may well end up with exponential size; reducing this to linear size (or proving it impossible) remains for future study.

A graph-theoretic open question concerns
Lemma
3.6
where we proved that a 4-graph has
an edge-partition into two triangle-free strict cacti. Can we
replace ‘strict cacti’ by ‘pseudoforest’? Also, does the result hold
for higher degrees, i.e., can we split
2s
-graphs into
s
triangle-free
strict cacti for
s\geq 3
? The latter would be useful for unbent collection of
orthogonal drawings in
d
-dimensional space (hence for
2d
-graphs). Here
it is easy to achieve order
d+1
by partitioning into
forests, but can we reduce this to order
d
or even less?
