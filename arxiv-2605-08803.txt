Fixed-point approximation for self-consistent transfer operators with Newton’s method
Wael Bahsoun, Gary Froyland, Maxence Phalempin

Abstract
Self-consistent transfer operators arise naturally in the study of mean-field coupled dynamical systems and are closely related to kinetic PDEs such as the Vlasov equation. Despite substantial progress on existence and uniqueness of fixed points for self-consistent transfer operators, the development of fast, reliable, and provably accurate numerical methods remains largely unresolved. In this work, we construct a nonlinear Fourier–Fejér discretisation and establish convergence of the resulting finite-dimensional fixed point to that of the original self-consistent transfer operator. Further, using the nonlinear Fourier–Fejér discretisation, we prove exponential convergence of a sequential iteration scheme and develop a Newton framework with quadratic convergence. We present numerical examples demonstrating the efficiency and flexibility of the above methods.



1
Introduction

Self-consistent transfer operators are
nonlinear
transfer operators. They are considered as discrete-time analogues of the Vlasov equation
26
, whose general long-term behaviour is far from being understood
21
. This relation between self-consistent transfer operators and PDEs has generated important new connections between semi-classical analysis
8
and dynamical systems
3
;
2
. In the field of dynamical systems, self-consistent transfer operators arise from considering the thermodynamic limit of the evolution of states (probability measures) in mean-field coupled dynamical systems. Such systems are of paramount importance for applications in physical and biological sciences, see
10
;
23
;
24
and references therein.

There is a clear connection between infinite-dimensional mean-field coupled systems and self-consistent transfer operators: invariant states of the coupled system are fixed points of the self-consistent transfer operator. In particular,
attracting
fixed points of self-consistent transfer operators, when they exist, correspond to attracting invariant states
of the corresponding infinite-dimensional mean field dynamical system
5
.
Starting with the work of Keller
19
, several authors studied existence and uniqueness of attracting fixed points of self-consistent transfer operators
1
;
2
;
4
;
22
;
16
. However, as is the case for any evolution-type operator, proving the existence of a fixed point of a self-consistent transfer operator does not directly lead to an explicit solution for the fixed point.
Thus, it is important to develop techniques to find approximate solutions in an appropriate topology. For classical
linear
transfer operators, this is a very old problem going back to Ulam
25
, who suggested to partition the phase space of the dynamical system into a finite number of small-diameter connected subsets to approximate the transfer operator by a finite rank operator, see
20
, references therein for an account. Because repeated application of the self-consistent transfer operator amounts to sequential application of distinct linear transfer operators, it is tempting to try to apply Ulam’s method in a sequential way, creating finite-rank estimates of each linear transfer operator in a long sequence and multiplying together against an initial probability density. Such an approach has been proven to converge to quenched random invariant densities of cocycles of general random sequences of maps
14
;
13
.

In the setting of self-consistent transfer operators there are two main obstacles to this approach.
The first is that
one needs a discretisation that preserves two regular Banach spaces where the (weak) derivative in the more regular space belongs to the less regular one, such as the Sobolev spaces
W^{1,1}
and
W^{2,1}
. Obviously a discretisation based on Ulam’s scheme would fail
because the indicators that form the Ulam basis do not lie in
W^{1,1}
.
This regularity requirement creates a second issue, namely that the finite-rank operator that we use to map functions from our Banach space to a computer-accessible finite-dimensional space should be norm-reducing in the Banach spaces so that the contraction properties encoded in Lasota–Yorke inequalities are not disturbed.
Convolution with Féjer kernels
11
overcomes both of these obstacles.
Order-
n
Féjer kernel convolution maps functions in
W^{k,1}
,
k\geq 1
to linear combinations of Fourier modes of order at most
n-1
, and is a weak contraction in the
W^{k,1}
norm (unlike the sharper Dirichlet kernel
27
, which has other advantages in different settings).
Féjer kernel discretisation has been used to study perturbations and approximations of linear transfer operators
9
;
14
;
15
, and we show that this scheme is particularly well-suited to nonlinear self-consistent transfer operators.
Using Féjer kernel convolution we form an
approximate nonlinear
self-consistent transfer operator as a finite-rank map onto a finite-dimensional approximation space spanned by Fourier modes.
In Theorem
2.1
we prove that the approximate self-consistent transfer operator has a fixed point,
h^{*}_{\varepsilon,N}
, and that this fixed point converges to the unique fixed point of the original self-consistent transfer operator,
h^{*}_{\varepsilon}
with a rate
\log{N}/N
, where
N
is the maximal order of the Fourier modes forming the approximation space, and
\varepsilon
is the coupling strength in the underlying mean-field system.
Having established the theoretical efficacy of our approximation we turn to its numerical realisation. When the true fixed point of the self-consistent transfer operator is unique, one has the option of sequentially applying approximate linear transfer operators to effect a “power method” of estimating the fixed point of the approximate self-consistent transfer operator. In Theorem
2.2
we show that such an iteration scheme converges exponentially quickly; this is proved in Section
3.1
.

The self-consistent transfer operator is a nonlinear operator and it is therefore natural to develop a fixed point scheme for nonlinear operators.
Newton’s method is a good candidate due to its known rapid convergence and its ability to find both attracting and repelling fixed points. Developing the required theoretical underpinnings of Newton’s method in our context is the main goal of Section
4
. In Proposition
4.5
we formulate an explicit expression for the Fréchet derivative of the approximate self-consistent transfer operator, and in Theorem
4.1
we check the necessary regularity and resolvent-bound requirements to run Newton’s method
6
;
17
on the self-consistent transfer operator. This leads to a theoretical superexponential (quadratic or order-2) convergence rate toward the fixed point of the approximate self-consistent transfer operator in Newton iteration
count.
In Section
5
we exploit the explicit representation of the Fréchet derivative developed in Section
4
to construct a novel and efficient implementation of the Newton scheme using our Féjer-based Fourier approximation. We also include theoretical consideration of the numerical errors incurred.
Finally in Section
6
we apply our numerical schemes for sequential iteration and Newton’s method to two dynamical systems with distinct couplings. We observe that Newton’s method significantly outperforms sequential iteration due to a much faster convergence rate at the expense of only a modest increase in computation time.
These experiments confirm Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
with exponential convergence rates observed for both schemes.
We also validate the theoretical result of
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}\to h_{\varepsilon}^{*}
with rate
\log N/N
(Theorem
2.1
).
Our work opens the door to efficient and reliable numerical exploration of coupling effects on
fixed points of the nonlinear self-consistent transfer operator,
and thus to the influence of couplings on the invariant states of the associated infinite-dimensional mean-field systems.

2.1
The self-consistent transfer operator

Let
T:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a
C^{5}
uniformly expanding circle map.
Let
W^{i,1}
,
i=1,2,3,4,
denote the Sobolev space equipped with usual norm: for
f\in W^{i,1}
,
\|f\|_{1,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\|f^{(j)}\|_{1}+\|f\|_{1}<\infty.
We sometimes in the text below abuse notation and write
W^{0,1}
instead of
L^{1}
.
Define
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{f\in L^{1}:f\geq 0,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}f=1\}
. Similarly denote
\mathcal{D}_{i,1}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\cap W^{i,1}
for
i=1,2
.
Define
V_{0}=\{f\in L^{1}:\int f=0\}
and
V^{i,1}=W^{i,1}\cap V_{0}
for
i=1,2
.

For
\varepsilon>0
,
g\in C^{5}
,
f\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
consider the function

I_{\varepsilon,f}(x)=x+\varepsilon\int g(x,y)f(y)dy,

(1)

and set

T_{\varepsilon,f}=I_{\varepsilon,f}\circ T.

(2)

T_{\varepsilon,f}
is understood as a coupled system representing infinitely many particles interacting via their mean field, the local dynamics of each particle is described by
T
,
\varepsilon
is the coupling strength and
f
is the state of the system. For more information about such concepts see
19
and the review article
24
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{T}:L^{1}\to L^{1}
denote the transfer (Perron-Frobenius) operator associated with
T
; i.e., for
\psi\in L^{1}
,
\varphi\in L^{\infty}

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\varphi\circ T\cdot\psi dx=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{L}_{T}\psi dx.

It is well known that when acting on
W^{i,1}

\mathcal{L}_{T}
admits a Lasota-Yorke inequality and a spectral gap. Consequently,
T
admits a unique absolutely continuous invariant measure whose density is in
W^{4,1}
(See
20
subsection 10.2).

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
be the set of probability density functions on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. For
i=0,1,2
, consider the
self-consistent transfer operator

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}:W^{i,1}\cap{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
which is defined by

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(h)=\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h}}h.

(3)

for
n=0,1,2,\dots
, we also write

h^{n+1}=\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{n}}}h^{n}.

(4)

Note that fixed points
h^{*}
of the operator defined in (
3
) corresponds to absolutely continuous invariant measures with density
h^{*}
of the coupled system defined in (
2
).

Let
\varepsilon_{0}^{*}>0
be small, choose
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
C_{0}>0
so that

\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon_{0}^{*},C_{0})=\{T_{\varepsilon,f}:\forall\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0}^{*}],\forall f\in\mathcal{D}_{1},\sup_{x}\frac{1}{|T_{\varepsilon,f}^{\prime}(x)|}\leq\lambda<1\text{ and }\|T_{\varepsilon,f}\|_{C^{5}}<C_{0}\,\}

is nonempty.
This makes
{\mathcal{T}}:=\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon_{0}^{*},C_{0})
a family of uniformly ‘nearby’, uniformly expanding maps that satisfy uniform (independent of
\varepsilon,f
and
g
) Lasota-Yorke inequalities on
W^{i,1}
(See
20
subsection 10.2 for a proof of such inequalities):

\exists C_{LY}>0
such that for all
T_{\varepsilon,f}\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
h\in W^{i,1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,2,3,4
,

\|\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}^{n}h\|_{W^{i,1}}\leq\lambda^{in}\|h\|_{W^{i,1}}+C_{LY}\|h\|_{W^{i-1,1}},

(5)

and consequently
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}
admits a uniform-in-
\varepsilon
spectral gap when acting on
W^{i,1}
,
i=1,\dots,4
.
We fix throughout the constants
\varepsilon_{0}^{*},\lambda
, and
C_{0}
and also assume throughout that all maps
T_{\varepsilon,f}
lie in
\mathcal{T}
.
Let
K\geq\frac{C_{LY}}{1-\lambda^{2}}
be fixed throughout, and define a fixed ball

B_{K}=\{h\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\cap W^{2,1}:\|h\|_{W^{2,1}}\leq K\}

in which all initial densities lie, in which we will show all iterated densities and fixed points lie.

It is well known (see Theorem
A.2
) that for
\varepsilon_{0}^{*}

small enough
, for each
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0}^{*}]
,
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
admits a unique fixed point
h_{\varepsilon}^{*}\in B_{K}
; this property defines
\varepsilon^{*}_{0}
. In this work, we are concerned with obtaining a numerical approximation of
h_{\varepsilon}^{*}
. Our numerical approximation relies on the following discretisation scheme.

2.2
Approximation scheme and convergence results

Denote the standard Fourier modes on the circle by
e_{k}(x):=e^{2\pi ikx}
.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}

the Fejér kernel
11
is defined by
K_{N}=\sum_{|k|\leq N-1}(1-|k|/N)e_{k}
.
There are finitely many nonzero Fourier coefficients of
K_{N}
and the operator convolving a function with
K_{N}
is finite rank given by: for
f\in L^{1}

\Pi_{N}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}K_{N}(x-y)f(y)dy.

(6)

The finite-rank operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}
is defined by
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}:=\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Define

F_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{e_{k}:k=-N+1,-N+2,\ldots,N\}.

(7)

For an initial
h^{0}_{N}\in F_{N}
,

\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}h^{0}_{N}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{0}_{N}}}h^{0}_{N},

and we iterate this finite-rank operation as follows:

h^{n}_{N}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{h^{n-1}_{N}}}h^{n-1}_{N},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots

(8)

Recall that for
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0}^{*}]
the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
has at least one fixed point.
The following theorem shows that
\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
has a fixed point
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}
, and in the situation where
h^{*}_{\varepsilon}
is unique,
h_{\varepsilon,N}
converges to
h^{*}_{\varepsilon}
in the large-
N
limit.

2.2.1
Existence of a discrete fixed point and its convergence

Theorem 2.1
.

1.

For
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0}^{*}]
the operator
\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
has a fixed point
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}\in B_{K}
;

2.

There is
\varepsilon_{3}^{*}\in(0,\varepsilon_{2}^{*}]
, where
\varepsilon_{2}^{*}
is defined in Theorem
A.2
, such that for
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{3}^{*}]
if
h_{\varepsilon}^{*}
is the unique fixed point of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
and

is a fixed point of
\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
then

\|h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}-h_{\varepsilon}^{*}\|_{W^{1,1}}=O\left(\frac{\ln N}{N}\right).

Proof.

See Section
3.1

∎

2.2.2
Sequential iteration

We now describe our first approach to numerically approximating
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}
, which is based on sequentially iterating the sequence of linear transfer operators that appear in the self-consistent transfer operator iteration.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon^{*}_{4}>0
such that for
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{4}^{*}]
and sufficiently large
N
(independent of
\varepsilon
) the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}
has a unique fixed point
h^{*}_{\varepsilon,N}\in B_{K}
.
Further, there exists
C_{K}>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that for all
h^{0}\in B_{K}
, we have

\|{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{\varepsilon,N}^{n}(h^{0})-h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}\|_{W^{1,1}}\leq C_{K}\gamma^{n};

\|\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{n-1}_{N}}}\circ\cdots\circ\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{1}_{N}}}\circ\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{0}_{N}}}h^{0}-h^{*}_{\varepsilon,N}\|_{W^{1,1}}\leq C_{K}\gamma^{n}.

(9)

Proof.

See Section
3.3
.
∎

In practice, one could initialise (
9
) with
h^{0}=h_{0,N}^{*}
, the fixed point of the transfer operator
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
, obtained from the leading eigenvector of this linear operator.
Theorem
2.2
guarantees an exponential convergence rate to the discrete fixed point
h^{*}_{\varepsilon,N}
.

2.2.3
Newton’s method

Newton’s method promises superexponential convergence rates to
h_{N}^{*}
in iteration count
n
, rather than the exponential convergence in
n
for sequential iteration provided by Theorem
2.2
.
Sequential iteration requires the numerical construction of a new transfer operator at each iteration, so reducing the number of iterations would be advantageous.

To set up the Newton approach, denote
\mathcal{N}h:=\Pi_{N}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}
, and note that
\mathcal{N}h_{N}^{*}=0
; that is,
h_{N}^{*}
is a zero of the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{N}
.
For an initial
h\in F_{N}
, the Newton update
is

h-(D_{h}\mathcal{N})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{N}h
, where
D_{f}\mathcal{N}
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{N}
at
f
.
Rewriting this explicitly in terms of our transfer operators, one has the update rule

h_{\varepsilon,N}^{n+1}:=h_{\varepsilon,N}^{n}-\left(\Pi_{N}-D_{h_{\varepsilon,N}^{n}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}\right)^{-1}\circ\left(\Pi_{N}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}\right)h_{\varepsilon,N}^{n},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots

(10)

Newton requires an initialisation and for simplicity, we choose
h^{0}_{\varepsilon,N}=h_{0,N}
, the fixed point of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{0,N}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
, which is obtained from the leading eigenvector of the transfer operator
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

There is
\varepsilon_{7}^{*}>0
(see Theorem
4.1
) such that for
\varepsilon<\min\{\varepsilon^{*}_{2},\varepsilon^{*}_{7}\}
,
there is a
W^{1,1}
neighbourhood
B_{K,N}^{\prime}\subset B_{K}
of
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}
such that for sufficiently large
N
and all
h^{0}_{\varepsilon,N}\in B_{K,N}^{\prime}
, the Newton iteration (
10
) has order-
2
convergence toward
h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}
, that is there is
C>0
and
0<\tilde{\gamma}<1
, such that

\|h^{n}_{\varepsilon,N}-h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}\|_{1,1}\leq C\tilde{\gamma}^{2^{n}}.

Proof.

See the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

3
Proof of existence and convergence of the discretised fixed points of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}

3.1
Proof of Part 1 of Theorem
2.1

We start by proving that
\Pi_{N}
has good regularity properties and it provides a good approximation. This result may be considered classical (see
28
[ Ch. II, §2.4–2.6] for the main ingredients on trigonometric estimates) and its proof is provided for completeness.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following statements hold:

1)

for
f\in W^{i,1}
,
i=0,1,\dots
,
\|\Pi_{N}f\|_{W^{i,1}}\leq\|f\|_{W^{i,1}}
;

2)

\exists C>0
such that for
f\in W^{i+1,1}
,
i=0,1,\dots

\|\Pi_{N}f-f\|_{W^{i,1}}\leq C\frac{\ln(N)}{N}\|f\|_{W^{i+1,1}}.

Proof.

For the first item, note that

\|\Pi_{N}f\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|K_{N}\|_{L^{1}}\|f\|_{L^{1}}
and
\|K_{N}\|_{L^{1}}=1
. This covers the
i=0

i\geq 1
, notice that by definition for
f\in L^{1}
,
\Pi_{N}f\in C^{\infty}
. Therefore, for
f\in W^{i,1}
,
i=1,2,\dots

\displaystyle(\Pi_{N}f)^{(i)}(x):=

\displaystyle\left(\int K_{N}(x-y)f(y)dy\right)^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int K_{n}^{(i)}(x-y)f(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int K_{n}(x-y)f^{(i)}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}f^{(i)}(x).

Then the second statement follows by using the above and the first statement of the Lemma. To prove the last item, it is sufficient to prove for
f\in W^{1,1}
,

\|\Pi_{N}f-f\|_{L^{1}}\leq A\frac{\ln(N)}{N}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}.

(11)

Also the proof of the inequality in the case of
W^{i+1,1}
,
i=1,\dots
follows from (
11
) and the fact that
(\Pi_{N}f)^{(i)}(x)=\Pi_{N}f^{(i)}(x)
. To prove (
11
) we first change variables in the definition of
\Pi_{n}
(see equation (
6
)) and write:

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{N}f-f\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int\int|K_{N}(y)(f(x+y)-f(x))|dydx

\displaystyle\leq\int\int K_{n}(y)\int|f^{\prime}(u)|1_{[x,y+x]}(u)dudydx

\displaystyle\leq\int\int|f^{\prime}(u)|K_{N}(y)\int 1_{[x,y+x]}(u)dxdydu

\displaystyle\leq\int\int|f^{\prime}(u)|K_{N}(y)\int 1_{[0,y]}(u-x)dxdydu

\displaystyle\leq\int\int|f^{\prime}(u)|K_{N}(y)ydydu

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}\|yK_{N}(y)\|_{L^{1}}.

To compute
\|yK_{N}(y)\|_{L^{1}}
, we recall the following expression of
K_{n}
: for any
y\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
\mathbb{S}^{1}
identified with
[-\pi,\pi]
,

\displaystyle K_{N}(y):=\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\sin(Ny/2)}{\sin(y/2)}\right)^{2}.

We will control it according to the value of
y
.

Case 1:
|y|\geq 1
.

One can control
yK_{N}(y)
as follows,

\displaystyle|yK_{N}(y)|\leq|y|\frac{1}{N\sin^{2}(\frac{1}{2})}.

Case 2:
\frac{1}{N^{2}}\leq|y|<1.

We use the following control of
\sin
,
using
y\geq\sin(y)\geq y-\frac{y^{3}}{6}
,

\displaystyle|yK_{N}(y)|\leq\frac{2}{N|y|(1-\frac{y^{2}}{6})^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{N|y|}

Case 3:
|y|\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}
.

\displaystyle|yK_{N}(y)|\leq|y|\frac{2N^{2}y^{2}}{Ny^{2}(1-\frac{y^{2}}{6})^{2}}\leq 8N|y|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int|yK_{N}(y)|dy\leq

\displaystyle\int_{1\geq|y|\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}}\frac{8}{N|y|}dy

\displaystyle+\int_{|y|\geq 1}y\frac{1}{N\sin^{2}(\frac{1}{2})}dy+\int_{|y|\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}}8N|y|dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16\ln(N)}{N}+\frac{16\pi}{N}+\frac{2\pi}{N\sin^{2}(\frac{1}{2})}.

Thus, proving
\|yK_{N}(y)\|_{L^{1}}\leq A\frac{\ln(N)}{N}
for some constant

and the last item of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Part 2 of Theorem

4
Fixed point approximation by Newton iteration

In this section we develop a version of Newton’s method that will be used for our numerical scheme in order to estimate a fixed point
h_{N}^{*}
. Newton’s method requires the existence of, and an explicit expression for, the Fréchet derivative for the discrete operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}
.
We note that the existence of a Fréchet derivative for the true self-consistent operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
in a neighbourhood of the fixed point has been shown in
7
.
The development of an explicit expression for the derivative of the discretised self-consistent transfer operator is the focus of Section
4.1

4.5

In order to clarify the general requirements for Newton’s method on Banach spaces, we assume the existence of a Fréchet derivative for
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}
and for brevity denote
h_{N}^{*}:=h_{\varepsilon,N}^{*}
. Let

\mathcal{N}h:=\Pi_{N}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon,N}

; that is,
h_{N}^{*}
is a zero of the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{N}
. We follow the approach of
17

Recall the definition of the finite dimensional space
F_{N}
introduced in (
7
). For an initial

\displaystyle D_{h^{n}}\mathcal{N}(h^{*}_{N}-h^{n+1})

Lemma 4.3
.

For
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\|g\|_{C^{1}}}
,
f\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, the map
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\mapsto(I_{f}^{\prime})^{-1}(x):=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\int\partial_{1}g(x,y)f(y)dy}
belongs to
W^{4,1}
and for any
k\in\{0,\dots,4\}
(recall we assume
W^{0,1}:=L^{1}
),
I_{f}^{\prime}\in W^{4,1}
and there is a constant
R_{k}>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
and
f
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{k,1}}\leq R_{k}\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{k+1}},\ \mbox{for $0\leq k\leq 4$ and all $f\in\mathcal{D}_{1}$.}

Proof.

We proceed by induction. Recall that
g\in C^{k}
and by construction of
I_{f}
, the following estimates hold for any
k\in\{1,\dots,4\}
:

1+\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{k}}\geq\|I_{f}^{(k)}\|_{\infty}\,\text{ and }|I_{f}^{\prime}(x)|\geq 1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}}\,,\forall x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Consequently, we can initialize the induction at
k=0
with
\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}}}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{R_{0}}{1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}}},

and so we may take
R_{0}=1
.
To proceed by induction we assume that there is
n\leq 3
such that for any
k\leq n
,

\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{k,1}}\leq R_{k}\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{k+1}}.

Then, using Lemma
B.1
, there is
K_{n+1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\left(\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\left(\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}\right)^{(n)}\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\frac{I_{f}^{\prime\prime}}{(I_{f}^{\prime})^{2}}\right)^{(n)}\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\choose k}\left\|\left(\frac{I_{f}^{\prime\prime}}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{(n-k)}\left(\frac{1}{(I_{f}^{\prime})}\right)^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}{n\choose k}\left\|\left(\frac{I_{f}^{\prime\prime}}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{(n-k)}\right\|_{W^{1,1}}\left\|\left(\frac{1}{(I_{f}^{\prime})}\right)^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\left(\frac{I_{f}^{\prime\prime}}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{(n)}\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|\frac{1}{(I_{f}^{\prime})}\right\|_{W^{1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{n-k+1}{n\choose k}\left\|I_{f}^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{W^{n-k+1,1}}\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{n-k+1,1}}\left\|\frac{1}{(I_{f}^{\prime})}\right\|_{W^{k,1}}

\displaystyle+2^{n}\left\|I_{f}^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{W^{n,1}}\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{n,1}}\left\|\frac{1}{(I_{f}^{\prime})}\right\|_{W^{1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{n-k+1}{n\choose k}\|I_{f}^{\prime\prime}\|_{W^{n-k+1,1}}\frac{R_{n-k+1}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{n-k+1}}\frac{R_{k}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{k+1}}

\displaystyle+2^{n}\|I_{f}^{\prime\prime}\|_{W^{n,1}}\frac{R_{n}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{n+1}}\frac{R_{1}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{n+1}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{n+2}},

Where we used the fact that
I_{f}^{\prime\prime}=\varepsilon\int g^{\prime\prime}(x,y)f(y)dy
and thus
\|I_{f}^{\prime\prime}\|_{W^{n,1}}\leq\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{n+2}}
.
Consequently, using the induction property along with the above equation, there is
R_{n+1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{n+1,1}}\leq\left\|\left(\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{(n+1)}\right\|_{L^{1}}+\left\|\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right\|_{W^{n,1}}\leq\frac{R_{n+1}}{(1-\varepsilon\|g\|_{C^{1}})^{n+2}},

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\|g\|_{C^{1}}}
,
f\in\mathcal{D}_{1}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}h\|_{W^{1,1}}\leq C_{0}\|h\|_{W^{1,1}}.

Proof.

Indeed notice that for

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}(h)\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{h}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\circ I_{f}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{h}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}\circ I_{f}^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\circ I_{f}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}\left(\left(\frac{h}{I_{f}^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}\right).

(27)

Now recall that for

. Notice that using (

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}h\|_{W^{1,1}}\leq C_{0}\|h\|_{W^{1,1}}.

\displaystyle F(f+e,.)

we construct a sum as above for

, which is typically then sampled on the
16N
points.

We denote by
\hat{L}_{N}
the action on the Fourier coefficients of
h
induced by the action of
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
on
h
.
Recalling the definition of
K_{N}
from §
2.2
one has

\displaystyle\widehat{\left((\Pi_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{T})h\right)}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{N}\circ(\mathcal{L}_{T}h),e_{k}\rangle

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{N}(k)\cdot\langle h,e_{k}\circ T\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{N}(k)\sum_{i=-N+1}^{N}\hat{h}(i)\langle e_{i},e_{k}\circ T\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{N}(k)\sum_{i=-N+1}^{N}\hat{h}(i)\langle e_{-k}\circ T,e_{-i}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{N}(k)\sum_{i=-N+1}^{N}\hat{h}(i)\cdot\widehat{(e_{-k}\circ T)}(-i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-N+1}^{N}\underbrace{\hat{K}_{N}(k)\cdot\widehat{(e_{-k}\circ T)}(-i)}_{=:\hat{L}_{N,ki}}\cdot\hat{h}(i).

This construction requires
2N
FFTs of
e_{k}\circ T
, for
k=-N+1,\ldots,N
.
Note that we have an explicit expression for
\hat{K}_{N}(k)
.

In our numerical experiments in Section
6
, both our sequential iteration scheme and Newton iteration scheme will be initialised at
h_{0,N}^{*}
, whose Fourier coefficients are computed as the leading right eigenvector of the
2N\times 2N
matrix
\hat{L}_{N}
defined in (
72
).

5.2
Numerical construction of
\Pi_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}

To obtain an estimate of
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}
we follow the construction above for
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
, replacing
T
with an estimate of
T_{\varepsilon,f}
.
Note that
T_{\varepsilon,f}
will arise from numerical estimates of
f
, which live in the span of
F_{N}
.
To simplify the numerical computations, we assume that the coupling function is of the form
g(x,y)=g(x-y)
.
Using (
1
), (
2
), and the form of
g
, we have
T_{\varepsilon,f}=T+\varepsilon((g\circ T)\ast f)
, where
\ast
denotes convolution.
To compute
(g\circ T)\ast f
we calculate
\widehat{(g\circ T)\ast f}=\widehat{(g\circ T)}\cdot\hat{f}
.
We then estimate

T_{\varepsilon,f}(x)\approx T(x)+\varepsilon\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N}\left(\widehat{(g\circ T)}(k)\cdot\hat{f}(k)\right)\cdot e_{k}(x).

(73)

The calculation of
\widehat{g\circ T}
only has to be done once and is fixed forever, and at a given Newton iteration, we already have
\hat{f}
for the current
f
in the iteration.
We now insert this estimate for
T_{\varepsilon,f}

into (
72
) to compute
\widehat{e_{-k}\circ T_{\varepsilon,f}}
for
k=-N+1,\ldots,N
.
Thus, every computation of
\Pi_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}
requires
2N
FFTs.

5.3
Numerical construction of
\Pi_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}

We again follow the construction above for
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
, replacing
T
with
I_{f}
.
We have
I_{f}=\mathrm{Id}+\varepsilon(g\ast f)
, so (
73
) becomes

I_{f}(x)\approx x+\varepsilon\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N}\hat{g}(k)\cdot\hat{f}(k)\cdot e_{k}(x).

(74)

The computation of
\hat{g}
can be done once and is fixed forever.
We now insert this estimate for
I_{f}

into (
72
) to compute
\widehat{e_{-k}\circ I_{f}}
for
k=-N+1,\ldots,N
.
Thus, every computation of
\Pi_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}
requires
2N
FFTs.

5.4
Computation of the terms in the Fréchet derivative

Referring to (
70
), we have already described how to estimate
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}},\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}
, and
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}
.
In this section we detail how we estimate the derivative of
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}(f)
, the derivatives of
I_{f}
, and the functions
G(e_{k})
and their derivatives.

5.4.1
Derivative of
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}(f)

The term
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}(f)^{\prime}
is estimated by first computing
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T}(f)
, which amounts to multiplying the matrix
\hat{L}_{N}
by the Fourier coefficients of
f
.
For
f\in F_{N}
, this yields
(\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T})(f)=\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N}(\hat{L}_{N}\hat{f})_{k}\cdot e_{k}
.
To take the spatial derivative we write

(\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T})(f)^{\prime}=\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N}(2\pi ki)(\hat{L}_{N}\hat{f})_{k}\cdot e_{k}.

5.4.2
Derivatives of
I_{f}

The derivatives of
I_{f}
are treated as follows.
One has
I^{\prime}_{f}=1+\varepsilon(g^{\prime}\ast f)
. Thus
\widehat{I^{\prime}_{f}}(k)=\delta_{0k}+\varepsilon\cdot(2\pi ik)\hat{g}(k)\cdot\hat{f}(k)
.
The approximate spatial representation of
I^{\prime}_{f}
is then assembled as

I^{\prime}_{f}(x)\approx 1+\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N}\widehat{I^{\prime}_{f}}(k)e_{k}(x)
.

Similarly,
I^{\prime\prime}_{f}=\varepsilon(g^{\prime\prime}\ast f)
and so
\widehat{I^{\prime\prime}_{f}}(k)=\varepsilon\cdot(2\pi ik)^{2}\hat{g}(k)\cdot\hat{f}(k)
.
The approximate spatial representation of
I^{\prime\prime}_{f}
is assembled as above.

5.4.3
G(e_{k})
and its derivative

In the Newton iteration we require an estimate of the operator
\Pi_{N}\circ D\tilde{\mathcal{L}}|_{f}
, represented in the basis
F_{N}
.
Thus we require the action of
G(e)(x)=\int g(x-y)e(y)\ dy
in the RHS of (
70
) on each
e\in F_{N}
, and in particular on each basis element
e_{k}
.
Because we require estimates of
G(e)
only for
e=e_{k}
, the terms
g\ast e_{k}
can be dealt with as follows:
\widehat{g\ast e_{k}}(j)=\hat{g}(j)\cdot\hat{e}_{k}(j)=\hat{g}(j)\cdot\delta_{kj}
. We may Fourier transform
g
once and store it (in fact, this transform has already been computed for creating
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}
, see (
74
)).
We recover
G(e_{k})\approx\sum_{j=-N+1}^{N}\hat{g}(j)\delta_{kj}e_{k}=\hat{g}(k)e_{k}.

The spatial derivative
G(e_{k})^{\prime}
is thus estimated as
2\pi ki\cdot\hat{g}(k)e_{k}
.

5.5
Combining the terms

Having constructed spatial estimates of all of the individual terms in the square brackets of (
70
) for a given
k
(recall we estimate
G(e_{k})
one
k
at a time), one simply multiplies/divides/adds/subtracts as appropriate to estimate the combination in the square brackets, let us temporarily call it
\mathrm{Comb}_{N,k}
.
We now need to return to frequency space, so we Fourier transform this combination
and calculate (as per (
70
) in frequency space)

\widehat{D_{f}\Pi_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(e_{k})}=\widehat{\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,f}}(e_{k})}-\varepsilon\widehat{\Pi_{N}\mathcal{L}_{I_{f}}}(\widehat{\mathrm{Comb}_{N,k}}).

For each
k=\{-N+1,\ldots,N\}
we place the resulting
2N
-vector in the
k+N
th column of a
2N\times 2N
matrix called
\hat{D}_{N,f}
, representing the Fréchet derivative in frequency space.

5.6
Newton iteration

We are now ready to implement the Newton iteration in frequency space.
We initialise Newton with
\hat{h}_{0,N}^{*}
, obtained as described at the end of Section
5.1
.
Because
{h}_{0,N}^{*}
is a density, we know that
\hat{h}_{0,N}^{*}(0)=1
.
Further, since we wish to remain in the class of densities, it will be convenient to restrict the Newton iteration to the subspace
\bar{F}_{N}=\{e_{k}:k\in\{-N+1,\ldots,-2,-1,1,2,\ldots,N\}\}\subset F_{N}
spanned by nonconstant Fourier modes, or equivalently nonzero frequencies.

Our Newton update is:

\hat{h}_{N}^{n+1}:=\hat{h}_{N}^{n}-(I-\hat{D}_{N,h_{N}^{n}})^{-1}\cdot(\hat{h}_{N}^{n}-\hat{L}_{N,h_{N}^{n}}\hat{h}_{N}^{n}),

(75)

where
\hat{D}_{N,h_{N}^{n}}
is constructed as in Section
5.5
using
f=h_{N}^{n}
, and
\hat{L}_{N,h_{N}^{n}}
is constructed as in Section
5.2
using
f=h_{N}^{n}
.
Equation (
75
) is to be understood as computed over the nonzero frequencies.
While in theory, the constant mode of
\hat{h}_{N}^{n}-\hat{L}_{N,h_{N}^{n}}\hat{h}_{N}^{n}
should be zero, because of small machine-precision errors, it is more accurate to simply do the computation in (
75
) over the nonzero frequencies.

5.7
Computational cost

At each Newton iteration, we have a new fixed-point estimate
h^{n}_{N}
and require new estimates of
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{n}_{N}}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{I_{h^{n}_{N}}}
, costing
2N
FFTs each over a finer spatial grid of size
16N
.
The Fréchet derivative also changes at each iteration and the
2N\times 2N
matrix
I-\hat{D}_{N,h^{n}_{N}}
must be inverted.
There are also some reconstruction costs when converting from frequency space back to physical space.
FFTs are generally extremely fast to evaluate and at modest values of
N
around 100, inverting
I-\hat{D}_{N,h^{n}_{N}}
is very cheap.
In contrast, sequential iteration requires half the number of FFTs per iteration because one only constructs a single approximate operator
\mathcal{L}_{T_{\varepsilon,h^{n}_{N}}}
, namely
\hat{L}_{N,h^{n}_{N}}
, to apply to the current fixed-point estimate.

In terms of advantages of Newton iteration over sequential iteration, we note that sequential iteration can only find attracting fixed points while Newton iteration is also able to find repelling fixed points.
As we show in the next section, the modest per-iteration computational cost increase of Newton, relative to sequential iteration, is offset by a significantly faster convergence rate.
This greatly reduces the total number of iterations required and leads to an overall computational cost improvement with Newton.

Appendix B
An auxiliary lemma

Lemma B.1
.

If
k\geq 1
and
f,g\in W^{k,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R})
, then

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,1}},

(78)

and

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{W^{k,1}}\leq 2^{k}\|f\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g\|_{W^{k,1}}.

Proof.

the proof of (
78
) is a consequence of Sobolev inequality for Sobolev maps on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
: if
f,g\in W^{k,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R})
, then
f,g\in C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,1}}
.
The proof of the other equation is done by induction. To initialize, let
f,g\in W^{1,1}
, notice that by (
78
),

\displaystyle\|(fg)^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\prime}g\|_{L^{1}}+\|fg^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}\|g\|_{W^{1,1}}+\|f\|_{W^{1,1}}\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}

and since

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,1}}\|g\|_{L^{1}},

we obtain by summing the two inequalities,

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{W^{1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{W^{1,1}}\|g\|_{W^{1,1}}.

For the induction, assume the Lemma holds up to
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and any
f,g\in W^{k,1}
. Let
f,g\in W^{k+1,1}
,

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{W^{k+1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(fg)^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|fg\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\prime}g\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|fg^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|fg\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\|f^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g\|_{W^{k,1}}+2^{k}\|f\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|fg\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\|f^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g\|_{W^{k,1}}+2^{k}\|f\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|f\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\|f^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}\|g\|_{W^{k,1}}+2^{k}\|f\|_{W^{k,1}}\left(\|g^{\prime}\|_{W^{k,1}}+\|g\|_{L^{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k+1}\|f\|_{W^{k+1,1}}\|g\|_{W^{k+1,1}}.

∎
