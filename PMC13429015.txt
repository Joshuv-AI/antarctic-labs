Validity and reliability of the smile perception scale in Turkish children aged 8–10 years
Authors: Esra Özgöçmen Tula, Merve Ayvallı Karagöz, Merve Alkış
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDental aesthetics plays a key role in children’s social perception and psychosocial development. However, evidence on smile perception in children remains limited, highlighting the need for valid and reliable assessment tools. This study aimed to perform the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Smile Perception Questionnaire for Children Aged 8–10 Years (SPQ 8–10) for use in Turkish children.MethodsThis observational methodological validity–reliability study included 300 healthy children aged 8–10 years who attended the Department of Pediatric Dentistry at Bursa Uludag University and met the predefined eligibility criteria. Standardized perioral smile images were digitally generated to represent different dental conditions, and each participant evaluated one randomly assigned image using a five-point Likert scale. Introduction

Physical appearance is an important factor in social perception, and facial aesthetics are a fundamental component of this perception [1]. The orofacial region is particularly important, as it is the most visible area during interpersonal interactions [2]. Changes in dental aesthetics can influence how individuals are perceived and may affect social interactions and psychosocial outcomes [3, 4].

The mixed dentition period is a dynamic developmental process that does not always define malocclusion; however, occlusal variations can be observed [5]. At this age, as the position, alignment, and appearance of teeth change rapidly [5], children’s self-perceptions related to aesthetics and their levels of social acceptance also begin to develop [6]. Therefore, dental aesthetics should be considered during the developmental period [7].

Malocclusions affecting dental aesthetics may negatively influence psychological well-being and peer relationships in children [4]. Conditions such as increased overjet, deep bite, anterior diastema and malocclusion are commonly associated with dissatisfaction with dental appearance. These conditions may also affect daily functions, including chewing, speaking, swallowing, and communication [8]. Perceptions of tooth color, shape, size, position, and visibility may have psychosocial relevance, regardless of functional impairment [3, 4, 9]. Therefore, early assessment of the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics may reduce the risk of peer bullying and promote healthy psychological development [10]. Smile appearance may also function as a visual cue in children’s social judgments, leading peers to make inferences about personality-related traits. Therefore, children’s evaluations of smile aesthetics may reflect not only perceptions of dental appearance but also broader social and personality-related interpretations [6, 9].

Although smile aesthetics has been extensively investigated in adults [2, 11, 12], evidence regarding how children and adolescents perceive smile-related characteristics remains relatively limited [13, 14]. In addition, validated instruments specifically designed to assess children’s smile perception are limited, particularly in non-English-speaking populations. In Turkey, there is currently no valid and reliable measurement tool for evaluating smile perception, especially among children aged 8–10 years. Although the SPQ (8–10) was developed for this specific age group, direct use of the original version in Turkish children may not be appropriate without linguistic and cultural adaptation. Because children’s interpretation of smile-related characteristics may be influenced by language, culture, and developmental stage, it is essential to establish whether the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10) measures smile perception accurately and consistently in this population. This gap limits both the level of evidence and the comparability of findings across studies. Therefore, adapting instruments that assess children’s smile perception to Turkish society and determining their psychometric properties is important for better understanding children’s perceptions and expectations in clinical practice and for improving the comparability of research data. In the literature, validity-reliability reporting for short clinical scales is often limited to content consistency and Cronbach’s α; however, the structural integrity of the measurement tool, item-level contributions, and the reliability of repeated measurements must be empirically demonstrated. This study aims to provide comprehensive evidence regarding both the structure and reliability dimensions of the measurement by testing the scale’s construct validity using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA, CFA) to reveal the factor structure and item loadings; reporting internal consistency using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω; and reporting test-retest reliability using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [15–17].

In this context, the primary aim of the present study was to perform the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Smile Perception Questionnaire for Children Aged 8–10 Years (SPQ 8–10) for use in Turkish children. Specifically, the study aimed to examine the factorial structure of the Turkish version of the scale (using EFA and CFA) and internal consistency and test-retest reliability (Cronbach’s α, McDonald ω, ICC) in the current sample, and to provide clear, evidence-based recommendations for practitioners by demonstrating the scale’s suitability for clinical use and any potential item modification/deletion requirements based on the findings.

Materials and methods

This was a two-stage observational methodological validity–reliability study. The first stage included the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the SPQ (8–10) into Turkish, whereas the second stage included the administration of the Turkish version of the scale and the evaluation of its validity and reliability properties.

Ethical considerations

This was a two-stage observational validity-reliability study of a methodological nature. In the first stage, Giovanni Bruno was contacted via email to obtain permission for the adaptation and use of the scale [13] in Turkish, and information regarding the translation-back translation processes was provided, and approval was obtained. Subsequently, permission was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Bursa Uludag University Faculty of Medicine for the study protocol (Approval Number: 2025/709/11–19). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants and parents were informed about the purpose, method, potential risks, and benefits of the study and provided written consent. Bursa Uludağ University Faculty of Dentistry provides services at Bursa Oral and Dental Health Training and Research Hospital in accordance with the affiliation protocol.

Sample size calculation

To ensure that the validity and reliability analyses of the scale produce robust psychometric evidence, a sample size of at least 300 participants was deemed “adequate” according to the recommendations of Comrey and Lee [18].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method from children who attended the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Bursa Uludag University, between June 30, 2025, and December 3, 2025. The study included healthy children aged 8–10 years (n = 300) who met the inclusion criteria and whose parents were informed about the study objectives and provided informed consent. Children who had undergone or were undergoing orthodontic treatment, had systemic diseases, or had mental, physical, hearing, or visual impairments that could prevent them from answering questions or communicating were not included in the study.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the SPQ (8–10)

During the translation of the scale items, the scale was first translated into Turkish by two independent translators fluent in both English and Turkish and familiar with Turkish culture. Before beginning the translation, one of the translators was provided with information on the scale’s subject, the study’s purpose, how the scale is used, and the literature related to the scale. The other translator was only responsible for translating the text. Subsequently, the consistency and conceptual equivalence of the two Turkish translations were examined, and the translation was found to be consistent with the original. The Turkish draft form was reviewed by a Turkish language expert for language and meaning and by a measurement and evaluation expert for psychometrics. Following the expert evaluation, the first Turkish version of the scale was created. Subsequently, the Turkish scale form was translated back into English by a person who was fluent in both English and Turkish, had no knowledge of the scale, and had not been involved in the initial translation process. Researchers compared the consistency between the back-translation and the original English scale and found no semantic inconsistencies beyond minor linguistic differences. During the translation process, care was taken to preserve the overall meaning rather than to translate the expressions word-for-word. Finally, the scale was finalized with the authors’ approval regarding the translation process. A formal structured pilot study was not conducted prior to primary data collection. Nonetheless, an informal assessment of the comprehensibility of the Turkish items and response format was conducted with a small sample of children from the target age group, revealing no significant comprehension issues.

Image acquisition and editing

Following the acquisition of informed consent, a female participant was photographed utilizing a Canon EOS 1100 (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The participant was photographed while seated in an upright position on the dental unit and smiling naturally. This participant did not respond to the survey questions. Unlike the study by Verdecchia et al. [13], only smile photographs of the perioral region were used rather than full-face photographs to prevent children from being influenced by overall facial appearance. The smile was positioned with the mouth sufficiently open so as not to focus on the lower teeth, and the upper lip was adjusted to cover the gum line. The photograph was digitally edited using Adobe Photoshop 26.7.0 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA) to achieve symmetry and eliminate irregularities, thereby reducing distracting and confusing variables. The image was then digitally manipulated to change the position of the incisors. Photographs were arranged to show diastema between the incisors, missing right and left lateral teeth, incisor crowding, widespread carious lesions on the incisors, and differences in tooth height in the vertical direction. As a result of this process, five different images were obtained. The selected variations, including diastema, missing lateral incisors, incisor crowding, carious lesions, and vertical tooth height differences, were chosen to represent visually recognizable anterior dental conditions that may be encountered in this age group and may affect children’s smile perception. Consequently, a total of six images were obtained, including five variations created on the maxillary incisors and the original image. Additionally, to ensure symmetry on the right and left sides of the image, one side of the posterior dental arch was adjusted, then mirrored and duplicated, and the two sides were combined. Administration of the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10)

The SPQ (8–10) consists of a sample photo and 13 direct questions. The questions were evaluated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree.” The options are scored as follows: 1 point for “Strongly Agree,” 2 points for “Agree,” 3 points for “Somewhat Agree,” 4 points for “Disagree,” and 5 points for “Strongly Disagree.” The SPQ (8–10) contains questions related to five common areas of interest: satisfaction, honesty, extroversion, personal happiness, and intelligence. Questions 1, 5, and 8 refer to satisfaction/dissatisfaction among participants, while questions 2, 4, and 6 refer to honesty, selflessness/deception, and selfishness; questions 3, 7, and 12 refer to extroversion/introversion; questions 9, 10, and 13 refer to happiness; and question 11 refers to intelligence [13]. Participants were shown an image on a laptop computer (Apple Macbook Air M3, Cupertino, California, USA) with a black background via a PowerPoint presentation (version 16.96 [25041326]; Microsoft 365, Microsoft Corporation, USA). The photograph to be shown to each child was randomly selected using the envelope method. Six envelopes were prepared. The first child selected one of the six envelopes, the second selected one of the remaining five, and the cycle ended when all six envelopes had been used. Thus, randomness was ensured in the distribution of photos to participants. Each child evaluated the photograph corresponding to the letter they chose from a distance of approximately 50–60 cm, representing the face-to-face interaction distance encountered in daily life. Participants were asked to review a photograph and rate it appropriately in the survey. To minimize potential bias, participants were not informed whether the photos had been digitally edited. The survey was conducted face-to-face and took approximately 20 min to complete. All questionnaire administrations were performed face-to-face by the same researcher using standardized instructions. Participants completed the questionnaire independently, as they were literate children aged 8–10 years. All evaluations were conducted under standard indoor lighting conditions to ensure consistent environmental conditions; the screen was positioned to prevent direct sunlight from reflecting onto it. Each of the six photos was reviewed by 50 participants. No participant’s identity information was requested, and the data were collected anonymously.

Test–retest reliability was evaluated in a subsample of 30 participants who completed the SPQ (8–10) twice with a two-week interval. In the second administration, the same scale, instructions, and administration procedure were used under similar conditions. Participants were not shown their previous responses, and the two administrations were matched anonymously using participant codes. The two-week interval was chosen to reduce recall bias while minimizing the likelihood of true change in the construct being measured. Temporal stability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient, based on a two-way mixed-effects absolute-agreement model, and an additional correlation coefficient between the two administrations.

Data analysis

Before conducting the exploratory factor analysis, the dataset was scrutinized to ensure compliance with the necessary assumptions. The examination of univariate and multivariate outliers revealed no anomalies. Additionally, the dataset was complete, with no missing data, allowing the analysis of data collected from 300 participants. Before the factor analyses, all reverse-scored items were recoded so that higher scores would indicate more positive social evaluation, and the codings were checked prior to both EFA and CFA.

Upon examining the distribution characteristics, the skewness values ranged from − 0.936 to 0.870, whereas the kurtosis values ranged from − 1.378 to − 0.323. Notably, for all items, the skewness and kurtosis values fell within the ± 1.5 cut-off specified by Tabachnick and Fidell [19]. The mean values ranged from 2.28 to 3.89, and the standard deviations ranged between 1.17 and 1.38. These findings suggest that the dataset exhibits appropriate distribution characteristics necessary for proceeding with the factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using the Principal Axis Factoring method to investigate the underlying factor structure of the SPQ (8–10). This method was selected to identify the common latent factor structure underlying the item responses, rather than to reduce the items into principal components. The adequacy of the sample for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) coefficient and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The number of factors was determined based on eigenvalues, the percentage of explained variance, and the interpretability of the factor solution. Items exhibiting weak psychometric performance, such as very low factor loadings and communalities, were considered for removal from the scale. As the final EFA solution was unidimensional, no rotation was applied; factor rotation is only meaningful when two or more factors are extracted to enhance the interpretability. Subsequently, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to validate the unidimensional structure derived from the exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Owing to the ordinal nature of the items, the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimation method was employed. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and test–retest reliability based on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 26, JAMOVI, and the lavaan package in R [20].

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 300 children were included in the study. The mean age of the children was 8.99 ± 0.83 years. Of the participants, 106 children (35.3%) were 8 years old, 92 (30.7%) were 9 years old, and 102 (34.0%) were 10 years old. Regarding sex distribution, 159 children (53.0%) were female and 141 (47.0%) were male.

The mean number of siblings was 2.64 ± 0.93. Twenty-two children (7.3%) had one sibling, 120 (40.0%) had two siblings, 117 (39.0%) had three siblings, and 41 (13.7%) had four or more siblings. Most parent respondents were female (n = 221, 73.7%), whereas 79 (26.3%) were male. The mean age of the parent respondents was 37.73 ± 5.81 years. Parent age groups were distributed as follows: 25–29 years (n = 25, 8.3%), 30–34 years (n = 66, 22.0%), 35–39 years (n = 100, 33.3%), 40–44 years (n = 66, 22.0%), and 45 years or older (n = 43, 14.3%). Regarding parent education level, 4 parents (1.3%) were in category 0, 139 (46.3%) in category 1, 86 (28.7%) in category 2, and 71 (23.7%) in category 3. In terms of employment status, 180 parents (60.0%) were not employed, while 120 (40.0%) were employed.

Exploratory factor analysis results

Exploratory factor analysis was performed using the Principal Axis Factoring method to investigate the factor structure of the SPQ (8–10) scale. The adequacy of the sample was evaluated using the KMO coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.87. This value suggests that the sample size was highly suitable for factor analysis. Additionally, the results of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant χ²(78) = 928.94, p < .001.

Upon analyzing the initial eigenvalues, it was found that three factors exhibited eigenvalues exceeding 1. (λ₁ = 4.32, λ₂ = 1.18, λ₃ = 1.02). An analysis of the factor matrix and communality values indicated that the second and third factors did not form a coherent structure, either theoretically or statistically. Consequently, the application of the Principal Axis Factoring method resulted in a single-factor solution, which accounted for 28.40% of the total variance. Following item analyses, three items (Q7, Q8, and Q12) were removed from the scale due to their low factor loadings (< 0.30) and very low communalities (Appendix 1). Exploratory factor analysis using Principal Axis Factoring was repeated for the remaining 10-items in the final model. The sample adequacy coefficient increased, reaching KMO = 0.89. The Bartlett test of sphericity was significant (χ²(45) = 870.90, p < .001), Upon examining the eigenvalues, it was determined that only one factor possessed an eigenvalue exceeding 1 (λ₁ = 4.22). This factor accounted for 36.04% of the total variance. The eigenvalues of the remaining factors were all below 1. A scree plot illustrating dimensionality is presented in Fig. 2.

Confirmatory factor analysis results

The model fit indices indicated that the single-factor structure provided an overall acceptable fit to the data. Specifically, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI = 0.964) and Lewis Index (TLI = 0.953) surpassed the 0.95 criterion for excellent fit [17]. Additionally, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR = 0.052) was below the 0.08 threshold for a good fit and approached the 0.05 criterion for an excellent fit [16, 17]. The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA = 0.084, 90% CI [0.066, 0.102]) suggested a borderline yet acceptable fit when compared to the commonly used cutoffs of ≤ 0.08 for good fit and ≤ 0.10 for weak fit [19]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the overall model fit was acceptable (Table 2). The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.43 to 0.77, with all loadings being significant (p < .001). The explained variances (R²) of the items varied from 0.19 to 0.59 (Appendix 2). These findings corroborate the unidimensional structure of the SPQ (8–10). Reliability analysis results

Internal consistency

The internal consistency of the final 10-item SPQ (8–10) was assessed. Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.844, while McDonald’s ω coefficient was 0.847. These values suggest that the scale exhibits a high degree of internal consistency. The item-total (item-rest) correlations ranged from 0.369 to 0.626, indicating that all items possessed an acceptable level of distinction. No significant increase in Cronbach’s α coefficient was observed upon the removal of any item. These findings support the conclusion that the scale demonstrates a homogeneous structure (See Appendix 3).

Test-retest results

The temporal stability of the scale was evaluated using measurements obtained from 30 participants over two weeks. The absolute agreement between the two measurements was calculated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC; two-way mixed effects, absolute agreement). The analysis yielded ICC(3,1) = 0.803, 95% CI [0.628, 0.901], indicating that the scale had good temporal stability. Additionally, the correlation for test-retest reliability was calculated, with the Pearson correlation between the two measurements being high (ρ = 0.802, p < .001), further supporting the scale’s ability to provide stable measurements.

Discussion

Studies evaluating the effects of different smile characteristics on aesthetic perception have been conducted. However, these studies primarily focus on adult populations, and research examining perceptions and attitudes toward smile aesthetics in children and adolescents is more limited [2, 13, 14]. In this context, our study aimed to perform the cross-cultural adaptation, validity, and reliability assessment of the Smile Perception Questionnaire for Children Aged 8–10 Years (SPQ 8–10), developed by Verdecchia et al. [13], for use in Turkish children. Specifically, the factorial structure of the Turkish version was examined using EFA and CFA, and its internal consistency and test–retest reliability were assessed using Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, and ICC.

The findings regarding the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10) in this study suggest that the scale’s construct validity and reliability were acceptable. Following the removal of three items based on exploratory factor analysis, the scale exhibited a unidimensional structure, with the final model accounting for 36% of the total variance. In single-factor psychological constructs, explained variance rates of 30% and above are deemed acceptable [21]. Based on the item analyses, it was decided to remove three items (Q7, Q8, Q12) from the scale due to their low factor loadings (< 0.30) and very low common variance values.

Therefore, the final validated Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10) consisted of 10-items. The poor psychometric performance of these items may not be attributable to a single factor. A common feature of Q7, Q8, and Q12 is that they may require inferences about more abstract and contextual personality traits, such as “enjoying being alone,” “a tendency to joke,” and “being quiet,” rather than directly observable facial or smile-related characteristics. In contrast, other items on the scale, such as being sociable, polite, or happy, reflect traits that children may encounter more frequently in daily social interactions and may more easily associate with observable cues. Therefore, children may provide more consistent evaluations when items are based on concrete social cues, whereas they may experience greater difficulty when making inferences about less directly observable personality traits. From a developmental perspective, because social cognition and abstract reasoning skills continue to develop during childhood, children aged 8–10 years may have had difficulty evaluating internal personality traits based only on cropped perioral smile images. In addition, cultural differences in the interpretation of traits such as “silence” and “humor,” the presence of fever-worded items, and the limited visual information provided by cropped perioral photographs may have contributed to increased response variability. Thus, although the removal of these items was supported by the statistical findings, their weak performance should be interpreted as potentially related to the combined influence of developmental, cultural, item-related, and methodological factors. In this context, the five content areas outlined in the original questionnaire should not be regarded as distinct, validated subscales in the Turkish version. Although the original SPQ (8–10) encompassed content areas related to satisfaction, honesty, extroversion, happiness, and intelligence, these areas were not empirically validated as separate latent subdimensions in the original study. The current findings indicate that the final 10-item Turkish version exhibits a unidimensional structure, potentially representing a general social perception or an overall positive evaluation associated with the smile aesthetics. Consequently, the Turkish version should be interpreted using the total score rather than the individual subscale scores.

The DFA results also indicated that the single-factor model with 10 items showed an acceptable level of fit. The CFI and TLI values were above 0.95, and the SRMR value was below 0.06, supporting the model fit [16, 17]. Although the RMSEA value was borderline (0.084), it has been noted that RMSEA may yield more conservative results in small and restricted models and should be evaluated together with other fit indices [21]. Standardized factor loadings above 0.40 indicate that the items represent the relevant construct at an acceptable level [16]. Cronbach’s α = 0.844 and McDonald’s ω = 0.847 coefficients obtained from internal consistency analyses demonstrated that the final 10-item form has good internal consistency [22, 23]. The ICC(3,1) value of 0.803 obtained from the test–retest analysis also provides good evidence for the temporal stability of the scale [24, 25]. No other studies examining the cultural adaptation or validity and reliability of the SPQ (8–10) have been found in the literature. In the original study, the functionality of the scale was supported at the total score level with logistic regression and at the item level with test–retest correlations; however, the latent structure of the scale was not investigated using factor analytic methods [13]. The defensible use of a measurement tool in scientific and applied contexts depends on the adequacy of psychometric evidence presented regarding the interpretation and intended use of scores [26, 27]. The COSMIN checklist also recommends a systematic evaluation of measurement tools in terms of reliability, validity, and other measurement properties [28]. In this respect, the present study contributes to the literature by examining the measurement structure of the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10) using contemporary psychometric approaches. In this study, after the removal of items Q7, Q8, and Q12, a single-factor structure was obtained for the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10). While in the original study, the domains of satisfaction, honesty, extraversion, personal happiness, and intelligence were content-based definitions, these areas were not psychometrically validated subscales. The current findings suggest that the final 10-item Turkish form measures the construct of general positive social evaluation related to smile perception. Therefore, based on the current evidence, the Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10) should be interpreted using the total score rather than the subscale scores. The main rationale for the decision to remove items was the low factor loadings, very low communalities, and poor item performance of Q7, Q8, and Q12. There is insufficient evidence to interpret this finding as a culture-specific issue unique to the Turkish version. The poor performance of these items may be related to children’s difficulty in evaluating abstract personality traits, differences in cultural interpretations, negative item phrasing, or limited visual information provided by cropped perioral photographs. However, these explanations should be considered as possible interpretations; it should be emphasized that the main basis for the decision to remove items was psychometric findings. Overall, the present study provides initial acceptable psychometric evidence for the structural validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability of the 10-item Turkish version of the SPQ (8–10). However, the broader clinical applicability of the scale should be supported by additional validity evidence from independent and clinically diverse samples.

This study has some limitations. First, the study was conducted in a single clinical center, and the sample consisted of children aged 8–10 years who attended a pediatric dentistry department. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent the general population. However, since the main purpose of the study was to perform the cross-cultural adaptation, validity, and reliability assessment of a scale intended for use in this age group, the direct evaluation of the target age group was considered methodologically appropriate. Second, all visual stimuli were created by digitally modifying a perioral smile photograph of a single child. This approach provided standardization across the images and reduced potential confounding factors; however, the use of cropped perioral images rather than full-face photographs may have limited the visual information available to children, particularly when evaluating more abstract personality-related traits such as honesty, extroversion, or intelligence. This may partly explain the weak psychometric performance of some personality-related items. Third, each participant evaluated only one image. This approach was preferred to reduce response bias that could arise from comparing multiple images of the same child and to minimize the potential influence of previously viewed images; however, it did not allow within-subject comparisons across different smile characteristics. Due to the removal of three items, the final Turkish version of the scale deviated from the original form, resulting in a 10-item structure. Furthermore, the five content areas identified in the original study did not emerge as distinct, validated subscales in the Turkish version. Consequently, the final 10-item form should be interpreted as a unidimensional, total-score measure. Future research should investigate the stability of this unidimensional structure in independent and more diverse samples to further substantiate the validity and reliability of the final form. Additionally, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, changes in children’s smile perception or the stability of the scale structure over time could not be evaluated. Longitudinal studies may provide further evidence regarding the temporal stability and broader applicability of the final Turkish version of the scale. Another limitation is the absence of analyses of external validation, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and criterion-related validity partly due to the lack of comparable Turkish instruments for children aged 8–10 years. Therefore, the findings provide preliminary psychometric evidence for the final 10-item Turkish version, and additional studies are needed to broaden the scale’s validity evidence. Another limitation is that the EFA and CFA were conducted using the same sample. Ideally, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses should be performed on independent samples to cross-validate the factor structure. However, because the original SPQ (8–10) study did not report a validated factor analytic structure, an exploratory approach was necessary before CFA was used to test the resulting structure. Recruiting a separate validation sample of children aged 8–10 years in a clinical setting was not feasible within the study. Therefore, the CFA findings should be interpreted as preliminary support for a unidimensional structure, not definitive cross-validation. Future studies should examine the stability of the final 10-item Turkish version using independent samples or split-sample designs. Moreover, because the original SPQ (8–10) lacked a reported factor analytic structure, future adaptations in other languages should evaluate the scale’s latent structure across linguistic and cultural contexts. Given that reliability coefficients can differ across various samples and contexts, it is recommended that future research employing the SPQ (8–10) provide estimates of internal consistency and temporal stability specific to their samples. This practice will enhance the assessment of the generalizability of the reliability evidence. Future studies should also investigate the relationships between SPQ scores and relevant psychosocial variables or conceptually related measurement tools.

Conclusion

The current study offers preliminary psychometric evidence for the Turkish adaptation of the SPQ (8–10). The final 10-item Turkish version exhibited a unidimensional structure, acceptable construct validity, good internal consistency, and reliable test–retest outcomes. The single factor predominantly reflects a global positive social perception or an overall evaluative tendency regarding smile aesthetics rather than distinct personality-related subdomains. Consequently, the Turkish version should be interpreted as a total score measure. This study contributes to the literature by analyzing the measurement structure of the SPQ (8–10) using contemporary psychometric methods. Additionally, the items excluded in this study may indicate issues not only specific to the Turkish adaptation but also suggest the necessity of re-evaluating the original item set in future research. Further studies are required to confirm the stability of the 10-item structure and expand the validity evidence of the scale across different samples and cultural contexts. Although additional validation studies are warranted, the Turkish SPQ (8–10) has the potential to be used as a practical tool for assessing social perceptions associated with smile aesthetics in both clinical practice and research settings.
