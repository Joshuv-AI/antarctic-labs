Autologous fat-derived products for skin and scar optimization in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction: A systematic review
Authors: Danah Abdullah Aljahdali, Naif Abdullah Aljahdali, Rana Abdullah Alharbi, Ghada AlAbdullah, Ayman M Albarrati, Norah Ali Almohaimeed, Hanin Jaber Mobarki, Tala Abdullah Aljahdali, Rayan Alalola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction are technically demanding procedures in which optimization of the skin and soft-tissue envelope remains challenging, particularly in revision surgery and in patients with thin or scarred skin. Autologous fat-derived products have emerged as regenerative adjuncts that may improve scar quality, skin texture, and aesthetic outcomes beyond simple volumetric augmentation.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the clinical outcomes of autologous fat-derived product grafting in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction, focusing on scar quality, skin characteristics, aesthetic outcomes, patient satisfaction, safety, and durability.MethodsThis systematic review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251087473). A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar was conducted from database inception to June 2025. Human clinical studies reporting outcomes of autologous fat-derived products in rhinoplasty or nasal reconstruction were included. Study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment were performed using predefined criteria. Because of substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity, a structured qualitative (narrative) synthesis informed by Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidance was undertaken, with outcomes organised by product class.ResultsTwelve studies published between 2007 and 2024, involving 768 patients in aggregate across non-pooled studies, met the inclusion criteria. Interventions included microfat, nanofat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells, platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat, and diced fat composites. Most studies reported improvements in scar appearance, skin quality, and tissue pliability, with favorable aesthetic outcomes and high patient satisfaction. Only one study used objective three-dimensional imaging; the remainder relied on subjective assessment. Complications were infrequent and generally minor.ConclusionsAutologous fat-derived products appear to be well tolerated and may be useful adjuncts in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction, with a consistently low rate of minor, self-limiting complications. However, the available evidence is of low certainty: it is drawn predominantly from small, uncontrolled, subjectively assessed studies that pool biologically distinct fat preparations and is geographically concentrated in East Asian populations. Reported aesthetic benefits should be interpreted with caution. Well-designed comparative studies using standardised processing protocols, blinded three-dimensional outcome assessment, and adequate follow-up are needed before firm clinical recommendations can be made.Keywords: Rhinoplasty, Nasal reconstruction, Autologous fat grafting, Nanofat, Stromal vascular fraction, Scar optimisation

Introduction

Rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction remain among the most technically demanding procedures in plastic and reconstructive surgery, requiring a balance between structural restoration and aesthetic refinement. While the underlying nasal framework can often be corrected, challenges persist in optimising the overlying skin and soft-tissue envelope. Poor scar quality, unfavourable skin texture and patient dissatisfaction continue to pose significant difficulties, particularly in revision surgery or in patients with thin or damaged skin.

Traditional methods such as implants, synthetic fillers and autologous cartilage grafts can provide structural support and contour enhancement, but they are associated with complications including infection, extrusion, resorption and foreign body reactions.1,2 In one study of ePTFE-repaired traumatic deformities, one of 35 patients developed a postoperative infection that progressed to implant exposure despite oral antibiotic therapy, necessitating implant removal, while another patient required revision surgery due to contour irregularity and aesthetic concerns.3 Autologous costal cartilage, although biocompatible and widely used, can still result in complications such as warping, infection, displacement or extrusion, graft fracture and long-term graft resorption.4 These limitations underscore the need for alternative materials and techniques that minimise these risks while providing reliable support, improved contour and critically enhanced skin integrity.

Autologous fat grafting has emerged as a promising adjunct in aesthetic and reconstructive procedures. Beyond its volumetric properties, adipose tissue contains a stromal vascular fraction rich in adipose-derived stem cells, embedded within an extracellular matrix that supports tissue regeneration, enhances skin quality and modulates scarring.5 Clinical studies in facial surgery have reported encouraging outcomes using fat-derived products, with improvements observed in scar appearance, skin texture, elasticity and overall patient satisfaction.6,7

It is important to distinguish two conceptually different goals when fat-derived products are used in the nose. The first is regenerative: improving the quality of the soft-tissue envelope itself — scar remodelling, skin texture and pliability, vascularity, and overall tissue optimisation — particularly in scarred, contracted, or thin-skinned envelopes. The second is volumetric: adding measurable contour through projection, augmentation, or camouflage of underlying irregularities. Although the two objectives frequently overlap in practice, they correspond to different clinical indications, outcome measures, and product choices, and conflating them has historically obscured the specific role of fat-derived adjuncts. This review is primarily concerned with the regenerative, skin- and scar-optimising role of these products, while recognising and reporting their volumetric contribution where relevant.

This systematic review was conducted to evaluate the role of autologous fat-derived product grafting in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction. The review focuses on outcomes measured using validated scar assessment tools such as the Vancouver Scar Scale and the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale, and examines the effects of fat-derived products on scar quality, skin characteristics, patient satisfaction and the longevity of results. Additional aims include assessing complication rates, understanding the influence of technical variables such as injection technique, donor site and graft volume, and identifying current evidence gaps to guide future research.

Methodology

PRISMA registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The protocol was developed prior to data collection and followed predefined eligibility criteria. The review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251087473).

PICO question

The review was structured around the following population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes (PICO) framework. The population included patients undergoing rhinoplasty, septorhinoplasty or nasal reconstruction. The intervention consisted of autologous fat-derived products, including nanofat, microfat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells and platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat. The comparison involved standard rhinoplasty techniques without fat-derived biologic adjuncts. Outcomes of interest included scar quality, skin thickness and elasticity, aesthetic outcomes, patient satisfaction, complications and durability of results.

Search strategy

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar from database inception to June 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to rhinoplasty, nasal reconstruction, and autologous fat-derived products. The following search string was used, with minor adaptations according to the requirements of each database:

(“rhinoplasty” OR “nasal tip” OR “nasal augmentation” OR “augmentation rhinoplasty” OR “revision rhinoplasty” OR “revisional rhinoplasty” OR “nasal revision”) AND (“fat” OR “lipofilling” OR “lipoinjection” OR “adipose graft” OR “nanofat” OR “microfat” OR “stromal vascular fraction” OR “SVF” OR “adipose-derived stem cells” OR “ADSCs” OR “PRF” OR “fat-derived product”).

The reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: human clinical studies involving rhinoplasty, septorhinoplasty or nasal reconstruction; use of autologous fat-derived products injected into the nasal soft-tissue envelope; reporting of outcomes related to scar appearance, skin quality, aesthetic refinement, patient satisfaction or complications; study designs limited to randomised controlled trials, non-randomised comparative studies and case series enrolling at least ten patients; and articles published in English. A minimum threshold of ten patients was adopted a priori as a pragmatic balance between two competing requirements in a sparse and emerging evidence base: retaining sufficient sensitivity to capture the limited available literature, while excluding individual case reports and very small series that provide minimal methodological substance and are most vulnerable to selection bias and chance findings. We acknowledge that this threshold remains low for a review of clinical outcomes, and the statistical-power implications of the smaller included series are addressed explicitly in the Discussion. Studies were excluded if they involved animal or cadaveric models, case reports, case series with fewer than ten patients, the use of non-autologous or non–fat-derived biologic materials, or lacked clinically relevant outcomes.

Study selection

All search results were imported into Rayyan for organisation and blinded screening. Duplicate records were removed automatically. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, and full texts were retrieved for all studies meeting initial criteria or requiring clarification. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. The search identified 857 records. After removal of 368 duplicates, 489 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 460 were excluded. Twenty-nine full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and 17 were excluded for the reasons detailed in Fig. 1, leaving twelve studies in the qualitative synthesis; the numbers reported here correspond exactly to the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by four reviewers using a standardised extraction form developed for this review. Extracted variables included study design, sample size, patient demographics, clinical indication, type of fat-derived product, donor site, processing method, injection technique, outcome measures, duration of follow-up and the presence or absence of complications. The complete data extraction table is provided as Supplementary Table S1.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed at the study level according to study design. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was applied to the randomised controlled trial. Non-randomised comparative studies were assessed using the 12-item Methodological Index for Non-Randomised Studies (MINORS) tool (maximum score 24), while non-comparative case series were assessed using the eight-item MINORS version (maximum score 16). Risk of bias categorisation was defined as low (≥20/24 or ≥13/16), moderate (13–19/24 or 9–12/16), and high (<13/24 or <9/16). Risk of bias was assessed independently by one reviewer and verified by a second reviewer. Detailed results are presented in Table 2, Table 3 and in Supplementary Table S3.

Synthesis of results

Because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity in study design, fat-product type, graft processing methods, injection planes, outcome measures, and follow-up durations, the data could not be pooled quantitatively and a meta-analysis was not appropriate. A structured qualitative (narrative) synthesis was therefore undertaken, informed by Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidance.8 The synthesis method consisted of structured tabulation of study-level outcomes followed by narrative description of the direction and consistency of reported effects within each product class; no effect sizes were combined and no statistical vote-counting thresholds were applied. Given that the included interventions represent biologically and clinically distinct preparations, outcomes were additionally organised and reported by product class — (i) volumetric preparations (microfat, lipofilling); (ii) cell-enriched or regenerative preparations (nanofat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells); (iii) platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat; and (iv) composite fat–cartilage grafts — to avoid treating mechanistically different products as equivalent. Findings were interpreted in the light of each study’s risk-of-bias rating, with conclusions drawn primarily from lower-risk studies and a narrative sensitivity appraisal performed around the single high-risk randomised controlled trial. The overall certainty of evidence was appraised narratively, considering study design, risk of bias, consistency, and directness; given the predominance of uncontrolled studies and subjective outcomes, certainty was judged to be low.

Results

Study selection

The electronic database search identified 857 records. After removal of 368 duplicates, 489 unique studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 460 were excluded for failing to meet the eligibility criteria. Twenty-nine full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 17 were excluded due to insufficient sample size, lack of relevant clinical outcomes, use of non-autologous or non–fat-derived materials, or high risk of bias. Twelve studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The twelve included studies were published between 2007 and 2024 and comprised a total of 768 patients undergoing primary rhinoplasty, revision rhinoplasty or nasal reconstruction with adjunctive use of autologous fat-derived products. The aggregate of 768 patients reflects summation across heterogeneous, non-pooled studies and should not be interpreted as a combined sample analysed together. Study designs included one randomised controlled trial, four non-randomised comparative studies and seven case series. Interventions involved a range of fat-derived products, including microfat, nanofat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells, platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat and diced fat composites. Clinical indications included primary aesthetic refinement, correction of rhinoplasty sequelae, post-traumatic deformities, thin-skin camouflage, saddle nose deformity and treatment of contracted or scarred nasal envelopes. Follow-Up duration

Follow-up duration ranged from three months to 42 months. Short-term follow-up of three to six months was reported in studies primarily assessing early aesthetic refinement and patient satisfaction. Longer follow-up exceeding twelve months was reported in several studies, allowing evaluation of scar remodelling, skin quality and durability of outcomes following autologous fat-derived treatments.5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Risk of bias assessment

The single randomised controlled trial demonstrated a high overall risk of bias, primarily owing to concerns regarding deviations from intended interventions and limited reporting of allocation concealment.14 Because of this rating, the findings of this trial were not used as primary support for the review’s conclusions. A narrative sensitivity appraisal was performed: when this high-risk study was set aside, the overall direction of the evidence — reported improvements in skin and scar quality with a low rate of minor complications — was unchanged, as the same signal was present across the lower-risk comparative studies and case series. A quantitative sensitivity analysis was not feasible because the data were not pooled.

The non-randomised comparative studies demonstrated moderate methodological quality overall, with common limitations including retrospective design, absence of blinding and lack of prospective sample size calculation.7,10,11,15

The included case series demonstrated variable methodological quality. Strengths included clear surgical descriptions and consistent follow-up; however, the absence of control groups and heterogeneous outcome measures limited the overall level of evidence. MINORS scores for all seven non-comparative case series are reported in Supplementary Table S3; ratings ranged from moderate to high risk of bias. A summary of the risk-of-bias assessment is presented in Table 2, Table 3, with detailed domain-level assessments provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Scar-Related outcomes

Most included studies reported improvements in nasal scar appearance, texture and pliability following autologous fat-based treatments. Cárdenas and Carvajal reported improved skin texture and contour refinement in both primary and revision cases without postoperative scar irregularities.9 Oh et al. demonstrated significant softening of scarred nasal skin and improvement in contracture severity following adipose-derived stromal cell–enriched fat grafting in patients with severely contracted noses.7 Similarly, An et al. and Ahn et al. reported reductions in fibrosis and improved skin pliability in revision rhinoplasty patients treated with fat-derived products.10,11

Kovacevic et al. described preservation of skin mobility and prevention of “shrink-wrap” scarring in thin-skinned patients treated with fat transfer combined with platelet-rich fibrin.12 In patients with postoperative contour deformities, lipofilling techniques were also associated with improved soft-tissue quality and scar appearance.16

Skin quality and soft-tissue envelope optimisation

Improvement in skin quality was consistently reported across studies focusing on compromised nasal soft-tissue envelopes. Kovacevic et al. demonstrated improved skin mobility and envelope quality at 12 months, supported by imaging findings confirming persistence of a thin regenerative fat layer.12 Kao et al. reported enhanced skin texture and surface smoothness following microautologous fat transplantation in primary augmentation rhinoplasty patients, particularly after staged treatment sessions.5 These findings support a regenerative effect of fat-derived products beyond simple volumetric augmentation.

Aesthetic outcomes

Favourable aesthetic outcomes were reported across the included studies, although the methods of assessment varied widely and were predominantly subjective. Gowda et al. reported reductions in Rhinoplasty Assessment Scale scores across all nasal views, with improvement maintained over follow-up.13 Notably, only Lin et al. employed objective three-dimensional photogrammetric imaging to quantify dorsal contour change following fat injection.17; the remaining studies relied on two-dimensional photography, observer rating scales, or patient-reported satisfaction. This near-absence of objective volumetric or morphometric outcome data is an important limitation, as subjective and unblinded assessment is susceptible to observer and expectation bias and cannot reliably quantify nasal contour change (see Discussion). In revision and complex cases, fat-derived adjuncts were associated with reported improvements in nasal projection, contour refinement and overall aesthetic harmony.7,10,11 Ossanna et al. reported favourable aesthetic outcomes and high patient satisfaction following non-surgical reshaping of saddle-nose deformity using adipose-derived injections (a non-surgical adipose-injection cohort, included because it reports the same soft-tissue and aesthetic outcomes of interest, though its non-operative context should be borne in mind when interpreting comparability), maintained at 12 months.15 Overall, reported aesthetic improvements were observed in both primary and revision settings, but should be interpreted in the context of the largely subjective assessment methods used (Supplementary Table S2).

Patient satisfaction

High levels of patient satisfaction were reported in most studies. Kao et al. reported satisfactory outcomes in the majority of patients undergoing microautologous fat transplantation.5 Challita et al. demonstrated favourable patient-reported outcomes in secondary rhinoplasty cases treated with lipofilling.6 Gowda et al. and Lin et al. similarly reported sustained patient satisfaction over follow-up.13,17 Studies incorporating platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat grafts and adipose-derived cell–enriched preparations reported particularly favourable subjective outcomes.7,12,15

Complications

Complication rates across the included studies were low. Most studies reported no major adverse events. Minor complications included transient oedema, ecchymosis and temporary erythema, which resolved spontaneously.

Kovacevic et al. documented a single case of haematoma resulting in fat graft displacement; this was managed conservatively with intralesional triamcinolone therapy.12 Ahn et al. reported wound infections in both treatment and control groups, with tip necrosis occurring only in the control group.11 The included randomised controlled trial reported no major complications related to PRF-enriched fat grafting.14 Overall, autologous fat-based treatments demonstrated a favourable safety profile in nasal applications.

Durability of outcomes

Several studies reported sustained improvements in scar quality, skin texture and aesthetic outcomes over extended follow-up periods. Gowda et al. demonstrated stable dorsal contour beyond 18 months.13 Ahn et al. reported maintained improvements in nasal length, projection and skin quality at 18 months.11 Imaging in a single study was consistent with persistence of a thin fat layer rather than purely transient volumetric augmentation.12; however, graft retention was not quantified in most studies, was assessed using non-standardised methods, and follow-up was short (≤6 months) in several series. The durability of fat-derived treatments in nasal surgery therefore remains incompletely established.12,15

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This structured qualitative systematic review of twelve studies (768 patients) found that autologous fat-derived products, used as adjuncts in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction, were associated with reported improvements in scar appearance, skin texture, and tissue pliability, together with high patient satisfaction and a low rate of minor, self-limiting complications. These observations are encouraging and align with broader evidence that fat transfer can remodel scar tissue and improve skin elasticity.18,19 However, the strength of these conclusions is constrained by the predominantly uncontrolled study designs, the small sample sizes, the largely subjective outcome assessment, and the biological and technical heterogeneity of the interventions. Studies of the size included here (as few as 13–14 patients) are vulnerable to type II error, selection bias, and regression to the mean, and in real practice outcomes are strongly modified by skin thickness, vascularity, scar tissue, and previous surgery. The findings are therefore best interpreted as hypothesis-generating signals of benefit rather than confirmation of efficacy.

Regenerative versus volumetric effects

A recurring theme across the included studies is the proposition that fat-derived products act beyond simple volume restoration — improving the quality, pliability, and vascularity of the nasal soft-tissue envelope. This regenerative hypothesis is biologically plausible: the stromal vascular fraction contains adipose-derived stromal cells and an extracellular-matrix scaffold that may support angiogenesis, modulate fibrosis, and improve tissue trophicity.5 The clearest supporting observation in this review is the imaging finding by Kovacevic et al. consistent with persistence of a thin regenerative fat layer at 12 months.12 Nevertheless, no included study provided histological, elastographic, or molecular confirmation of regeneration, and the distinction between a durable regenerative effect and retained graft volume cannot be resolved from the available data. The regenerative and volumetric contributions of these products should therefore be regarded as complementary but, at present, individually unquantified.

Biological heterogeneity of fat-derived products

The included interventions — microfat, nanofat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells, platelet-rich fibrin–enriched fat, and composite fat–cartilage grafts — are not biologically equivalent and should not be interpreted as a single intervention (Table 4). Microfat and lipofilling retain intact adipocytes and act primarily through viable graft volume with a secondary trophic effect. Nanofat, by contrast, contains few viable adipocytes but is rich in stromal vascular cells and is used principally for its regenerative rather than volumetric properties. Stromal vascular fraction and adipose-derived stromal cell preparations concentrate the regenerative cellular component further, while platelet-rich fibrin–enriched preparations add a source of autologous growth factors intended to enhance graft survival and tissue healing. Composite fat–cartilage grafts combine structural and biological objectives. Because cellular viability, regenerative-cell content, and mechanism of action differ across these products, identical outcomes should not be expected, and pooled interpretation risks obscuring product-specific effects. In keeping with this, we synthesised outcomes by product class (Table 5); the consistency of the qualitative signal across classes is reassuring, but the available data do not permit any conclusion about the comparative superiority of one preparation over another. Head-to-head comparison of standardised preparations is a priority for future research.

Technical heterogeneity of harvesting, processing, and injection

Beyond biological differences, the included studies varied considerably in harvesting site, mechanical and enzymatic processing, graft volume, number of sessions, and injection technique. Although Table 1 records the injection plane as subcutaneous in each study, this label conceals meaningful variation: the nasal soft-tissue envelope comprises distinct layers (superficial and deep fatty, fibromuscular, and deep areolar), and the precise depth, volume, and anatomical target were inconsistently reported. This distinction is not merely academic. The injection plane is a principal determinant of safety in nasal soft-tissue augmentation, as, in the broader facial soft-tissue augmentation literature, inadvertent intravascular injection has rarely been associated with vascular compromise and, in exceptional cases, skin necrosis or visual loss.20 The inconsistent reporting of technical parameters across the included studies precludes any reliable association between technique and outcome and represents an important reporting deficiency in the primary literature. Standardised, layer-specific description of injection technique and volume should be considered a minimum reporting requirement in future studies.

Objective outcome assessment limitations

A major limitation of the current evidence base is the near-absence of objective outcome assessment. Only one of the twelve included studies used three-dimensional photogrammetric imaging to quantify nasal contour change.17; the remainder relied on two-dimensional photography, observer-rated scales, or patient-reported satisfaction. Although validated scar scales such as the Vancouver Scar Scale and the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale provide semi-quantitative structure, they remain subjective and, in most included studies, were applied without blinding. Subjective and unblinded assessment is well recognised to overestimate aesthetic benefit through observer and expectation bias and cannot reliably quantify three-dimensional contour or volumetric change. Consequently, the aesthetic and skin-quality benefits reported in this review rest substantially on assessments that are vulnerable to bias. Future studies should adopt standardised, blinded three-dimensional photogrammetric and, where feasible, objective skin-characterisation outcomes (for example, elastography or cutometry) to place efficacy claims on a quantitative footing.

Durability and graft retention

Durability is central to the clinical value of any grafting technique, yet it remains poorly characterised in the included literature. Several studies reported maintained improvement in scar quality, skin texture, and contour beyond twelve to eighteen months11,13, and imaging in one study was consistent with persistence of a thin fat layer at twelve months.12 However, graft retention was rarely quantified, was assessed using non-standardised methods, and follow-up was short (≤6 months) in several series (for example, An et al.). Fat-graft survival is known to be variable and dependent on harvesting and processing technique, recipient-site vascularity, and graft volume.21 The current evidence is therefore insufficient to define expected long-term retention or to compare durability across product types. Prospective studies with objective volumetric follow-up of at least twelve to twenty-four months are required.

Potential clinical applications

Despite these limitations, the consistency of the qualitative signal suggests several scenarios in which fat-derived adjuncts may have a defined role. Cell-enriched and regenerative preparations (nanofat, stromal vascular fraction, adipose-derived stromal cells) appear most relevant where the principal problem is soft-tissue quality — the contracted or scarred nasal envelope, thin-skinned revision rhinoplasty, and post-traumatic or post-surgical fibrosis — in which improving pliability and skin trophicity, rather than adding volume, is the goal.7,10,11,12 Volumetric preparations (microfat, lipofilling) and composite grafts may be more applicable where mild contour augmentation or camouflage of minor irregularities is required, particularly over a compromised envelope where rigid implants carry higher risk.5,9,13 Platelet-rich fibrin–enriched preparations have been used with the aim of improving graft survival and wound healing.12,14 These suggested indications are pragmatic syntheses of reported use rather than evidence-based recommendations, and should be tested prospectively.

Geographic and ethnic considerations

The evidence base is geographically and ethnically concentrated: a substantial proportion of the included studies were conducted in East Asian populations (South Korea, Taiwan, and China).5,7,10,11,14,17 This concentration has important implications for generalisability. Fat grafting for nasal augmentation is particularly prevalent in East Asian rhinoplasty, where a thicker, more sebaceous skin–soft-tissue envelope and aesthetic goals oriented toward dorsal and tip augmentation differ from those commonly encountered in Caucasian or other patient populations.22 Skin thickness, sebaceous-gland density, scarring tendency, and baseline soft-tissue characteristics all influence both the indication for and the outcome of soft-tissue grafting. The predominance of East Asian cohorts therefore limits the direct applicability of these findings to more diverse populations, and conclusions should be extrapolated to non-Asian patients only with caution. Future studies should report ethnicity and skin characteristics explicitly and should aim to recruit demographically diverse cohorts.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths: a prospectively registered PRISMA-guided protocol, a comprehensive multi-database search, duplicate independent screening, formal risk-of-bias assessment, and a product-stratified synthesis that avoids treating distinct preparations as equivalent. Its limitations reflect the underlying literature. The evidence is dominated by small, uncontrolled, retrospective series with a single high-risk randomised trial; outcome assessment is largely subjective and unblinded with minimal objective imaging; the interventions are biologically and technically heterogeneous; harvesting, processing, and injection protocols are not standardised; graft retention and durability are poorly quantified; the evidence is geographically concentrated in East Asian populations; and the restriction to English-language publications together with the predominance of small positive series raises the possibility of publication and language bias, which formal small-study tests could not assess in the absence of pooling. We could also not fully exclude patient overlap between studies originating from the same centres. Overall, the certainty of evidence is low, and the conclusions of this review should be regarded as provisional.

Conclusion

Across twelve studies involving 768 patients, autologous fat-derived products used as adjuncts in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction were associated with reported improvements in scar appearance, skin quality, and aesthetic outcomes in both primary and revision settings, together with a consistently low rate of minor, self-limiting complications. However, these findings are derived from a low-certainty evidence base characterised by small and frequently uncontrolled studies, a single high-risk randomised trial, biologically and technically heterogeneous interventions, largely subjective and unblinded outcome assessment, poorly quantified graft durability, an absence of standardised harvesting, processing, and injection protocols, and a marked geographic concentration in East Asian populations. The available evidence therefore supports these products as promising and well-tolerated adjuncts but does not yet establish their efficacy or define their optimal use. Adequately powered comparative studies using standardised protocols, blinded three-dimensional outcome assessment, and follow-up of at least twelve to twenty-four months are required before firm clinical recommendations can be made.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

Autologous fat-derived products may enhance rhinoplasty outcomes by improving scar quality and skin texture, particularly in revision cases and compromised soft-tissue envelopes. Surgeons should consider these techniques as regenerative adjuncts rather than purely volumising agents. Future research should focus on randomised trials, standardised outcome measures and comparative evaluation of fat-derived formulations.
