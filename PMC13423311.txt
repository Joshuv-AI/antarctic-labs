Introducing anxiety as a risk factor of gastric cancer
Authors: Aliasghar Keramatinia, Babak Arjmand, Nastaran Asri, Somayeh Jahani‐Sherafat, Mostafa Rezaei-Tavirani, Farideh Razi, Fatemeh Bandarian, Reza M Robati, Mitra Rezaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: This study aimed to assess the association between anxiety and gastric cancer via a bioinformatics approach. Background: Gastric cancer is a major health burden in the world and a threat to human health. Several risk factors, such as genetics, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, sex, age, race, unhealthy dietary habits and lifestyle, obesity, and alcohol consumption, are highlighted by researchers.Methods: Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database related to gastric cancer were validated by the GeneCards database. The validated DEGs were compared with gastric cancer central genes from the “Disease” database to explore critical DEGs. The critical DEGs were searched in the anxiety-related genes from GeneCards to find the common genes between anxiety and gastric cancer. The common genes were evaluated via The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Kaplan-Meier Plotter analysis. Results: Among 658 significant DEGs, CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 were identified as gastric cancer-related critical genes common with the dysregulated genes in anxiety. Up-regulation of these genes was confirmed via GEO and TCGA analyses.Conclusion: It can be concluded that there is clear association between anxiety and gastric cancer. Anxiety shares dysregulated genes with gastric cancer It can be suggested that, there is a possible molecular link between them.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a critical health threat in the world because of the high burden of disease and mortality. Gastric cancer is most prevalent in eastern Asia, followed by eastern and central Europe (1). Immunotherapy, Radiotherapy, surgery, systemic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and especially multidisciplinary treatment have demonstrated appreciable efficacy in gastric cancer management (2). Due to the late diagnosis of gastric cancer, it is associated with a high mortality rate. The conservative diagnostic methods of gastric cancer are accompanied by severe trauma and a high rate of misdiagnosis (3). H. pylori infection, reflux, and obesity are pointed out as the most critical risk factors of gastric cancer (4). Different types of risk factors are attributed to gastric cancer incidence, such as the non-modifiable ones, like sex, genetics, age, and race, the controllable ones, including gastrointestinal microbiota, H. pylori infection, unhealthy dietary habits and lifestyle, obesity, alcohol and tobacco consumption, exposure to virus, radiation, or chemicals (5).

On the other hand, anxiety disorders are categorized by indicators such as social and performance fears, worry, preventive anxiety, unexpected and/or triggered panic attacks, and escape behaviors (6). Investigation indicates that major depressive disorder and anxiety disorders have common risk factors such as: number of traumatic experiences, low self-esteem, White race, family history of depression, disturbed family environment, female sex, years of education, and childhood sexual abuse (7). Based on the literature, up to 10% and 20% of patients with cancer experience anxiety and depression, respectively (8). Investigations revealed that there is a remarkable occurrence of depression and anxiety in patients with digestive cancers (9). A significant increase in HADS-anxiety score, severity, and prevalence of anxiety in patients with gastric cancer is recorded (10). Continuous treatment of gastric cancer associated with body embarrassment and hopelessness from recurrence of disease increased psychiatric disorders (especially depression and anxiety) progression in patients. In turn, the mentioned psychiatric disorders accelerate gastric cancer progression and development. This effect is induced via regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and alteration of reactive oxygen species-activated ABL1, accompanied by worsening disease and increased reappearance in gastric cancer patients (11). In the present study, common genes between gastric cancer and anxiety are mined and analyzed to find the impact of anxiety on the onset and progression of gastric cancer.

Methods

Collection and analysis of cancer-related genes from the GEO database

Data about gastric cancer were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/?acc=GSE248612). This gene set contain data from human tissue and is suitable to be analyzed. There are six gene expression profiles of gastric cancer samples versus five profiles of adjacent normal individuals. The samples were compared using the GEO2R interactive web tool to find the significant DEGs. A Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot analysis was considered to evaluate the possible separation of the cancerous samples from controls. The significant DEGs were determined based on adjusted p-value < 0.05. The significant DEGs were screened based on -0.6 > (Log fold change) > 0.6 to find the genes with at least 50% expression change. However, selection of fold change cutoff is often arbitrary; thus, we defined 50% change of gene expression as cutoff. The uncharacterized genes and the noncoding genes were ignored for further analysis.

Validation of GEO gastric cancer-related data via GeneCards

The screened significant DEGs were assessed via the GeneCards database. “Gastric cancer” was searched in GeneCards, and the related genes were extracted. The genes were classified based on GIFtS value via the Sturgess equation, K (1+3.3log(N)). This equation establishes a correlation between the number of samples (N) and the number of groups (K). Data were grouped based on an interval of 5 of GIFtS value. To find the most closely related genes to gastric cancer, the common genes between gene members of the top group and the screened significant DEGs were identified as critical gastric cancer-related genes.

Retrieve gastric cancer-related genes from “Disease” database and PPI network analysis to assess the critical gastric cancer-related genes

To evaluate the five introduced gastric cancer-related genes, the top 2000 gastric cancer-associated genes were retrieved from “Disease query” of STRING database and included in an interactome by Cytoscape software v 3.10.1. The “Network Analyzer” application of Cytoscape analyzed the constructed undirected PPI network to find the hub and bottleneck nodes. The hubs were determined based on degree value > (mean+2(standard deviation)), and the top 5% of nodes based on betweenness centrality value were explored as bottleneck genes. The normalized betweenness centrality values were obtained using the Min-Max normalization method ratio; X, Min, and Max stand for the betweenness centrality of a node and the minimum and maximum values of betweenness centrality, respectively. The critical gastric cancer-related genes were assessed amongst hubs and bottlenecks.

Extraction of anxiety-related genes from GeneCards to explore the critical gastric cancer-related genes

Anxiety was searched through the GeneCards database. The related genes were downloaded, and the critical gastric cancer-related genes were explored among them.

Comparison of expression associated of the critical gastric cancer-related genes in GEO analysis and TCGA database: Expression change of the critical gastric cancer-related genes was validated via the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database.

Kaplan-Meier Plotter analysis

To explore the impact of expression changes of the critical gastric cancer-related genes on survival of patients with gastric cancer, a Kaplan-Meier Plot analysis was done via the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service&cancer=gastric) (12).

Results

Analysis of data from the GEO database

Seventy-eight significant DEGs among the 22534 genes were determined to be the separator genes of the compared cancerous and control samples. After screening, 658 significant DEGs were identified as the eligible genes for analysis. UMAP analysis demonstrated that the cancerous samples are distinguishable from the controls (Figure 1).

Validation of GEO cancer-related data via GeneCards

The 7014 explored gastric cancer-related genes from GeneCards were grouped in 14 classes (Figure 2). The top group based on GIFS value (group-14) included 80 genes. Analysis administered that, five genes including CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 are common genes between the 658 significant DES from the GEO database and the genes members of group-14 from GeneCards. These genes were considered the critical gastric cancer-related genes.

Retrieve gastric cancer-related genes from “Disease” database and PPI network analysis

An undirected PPI network was formed from the top 2000 gastric cancer-related genes from “Disease query” of the STRING database. Analysis showed that degree value = 374 and betweenness centrality = 0.043 are cutoff values to identify hubs and bottlenecks, respectively. The topological values of degree and betweenness centrality for the five critical genes are demonstrated in Table 1. As depicted in Table 1, SLC2A1 is not a hub or bottleneck gene. Betweenness centrality for CHEK2 was 0.015, which falls below the cutoff value (0.043). Therefore, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 were introduced as hub-bottlenecks, and CHEK2 was considered a hub node.

Extraction of anxiety-related genes from GeneCards to explore the critical gastric cancer-related genes

There were 10695 anxiety-related genes in GeneCards. Assessment showed that the introduced five critical gastric cancer-related genes are familiar with the anxiety-related genes. Table 1 shows these genes are included among the top 0.6% anxiety-related genes based on GIFtS value rank.

Comparison of expression change of the critical gastric cancer related genes in GEO analysis and Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database

Expression changes of CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 in gastric cancer (Stomach adenocarcinoma) were mined from The University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer data analysis Portal (UALCAN) (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) linked with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (Figure 3). As depicted in Figure 3, all critical gastric cancer genes are up-regulated in stomach adenocarcinoma samples. As shown in Table 1, CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 are up-regulated in GEO data.

Kaplan-Meier Plotter analysis

Kaplan-Meier Plot for the critical gastric cancer-related genes, including CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9, is shown in Figure 4. As depicted the result for CHEK2 is not statistically significant. At the same time, the patients with a higher value of SLC2A1 had lower survival relative to the controls. This analysis demonstrated that patients with high levels of EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 had a significantly better prognosis than the control individuals.

Discussion

Many documents about psychological problems in cancer patients imply more attention to solve them (13, 14). Investigations indicate that cancer patients suffer from anxiety and depression during disease treatment (15, 16). There is a question about the role of psychological disorders in the initiation and progression of cancers. To find an appreciated response to this question, there is conflicting evidence in the literature. the impact of stress-related psychosocial factors on the incidence of cancer. Based on this investigation, the results of the analysis of 165 documents revealed an association between stress-related psychosocial factors and higher cancer incidence in initially healthy populations (17). On the other hand, Heikkilä K et al have reported results of an inquiry that show work stress is not a risk factor for colorectal, lung, breast, or prostate cancers. In this investigation, 12 European cohort studies about 116056 people free from cancer, who were followed up for about 12 years, were evaluated via meta-analysis (18). In the present study, the possible correlation between anxiety and gastric cancer is assessed via comparison of gene expression changes in gastric cancer and anxiety disorder. Analysis of GEO at the introducing CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 as the most related genes to gastric cancer. PPI network analysis demonstrated that CHEK2, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 (80% percent of these genes) are hub genes in gastric cancer. There is evidence about the remarkable roles of these critical genes in gastric cancer. Huo C et al.'s investigation showed that inhibition of transcriptional activity of tripartite motif-containing protein 21 (as a tumor-suppressive gene) by STAT1 is associated with gastric cancer development (19). Shan YQ et al suggested that MMP9 and HER2 are appreciated targets in gastric cancer therapy (20). Solute carrier family two member 1 (SLC2A1) is involved in intracellular glucose enhancement. This role is associated with tumor growth and its consequences. Correlation between high levels of SLC2A1 and large tumor size, advanced stages of tumor, and lymphatic invasion is demonstrated in patients with gastric cancer. A high level of SLC2A1 expression is associated with poorer quality of disease-free survival and the zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) in the stomach. The up-regulation of the zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) in the stomach leads to the development of gastric cancer. Multiple pathways are suggested in this regard (21). McKinley SK et al have introduced Checkpoint kinase 2 (CHEK2) as a gastric cancer vulnerability gene (22). Both TCGA and GEO analyses demonstrated that CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 are up-regulated in gastric cancer patients. Kaplan-Meier Plot analysis indicates that the survival of gastric cancer patients is different for high and low levels of SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 (this finding was not validated for CHEK2).

As depicted in Table 1, GIFtS values of CHEK2, SLC2A1, EZH2, STAT1, and MMP9 in gastric cancer and anxiety are equivalent, and their ranks are close. Yang J et al published a document about up-regulation of AU020206-IRFs-STAT1 axis in the brain of the Maternal immune activation mouse model, which was associated with intensification of psychiatric disorders in female descendants. Based on this report, anxiety is increased in the studied samples (in female, not male progenies of mice). It should be mentioned that STAT1 and the IRF family are involved in immune response (23). Injection of lipopolysaccharide to adult rats increases anxiety-like behavior, which is accompanied by elevated levels of MMP-9 in the amygdala and neuroinflammation (24). SLC2A1 encodes glucose transporter type 1 (Glut1); based on the report of Wang D et al, “Glut1 deficiency syndrome (Glut1DS) as an energy metabolism disorder is connected with exercise-induced dyskinesia. It is demonstrated that anxiety, prolonged exercise, and emotional stress are the triggers of dyskinesia (25). Cohen JL et al. investigation indicates that zeste homolog 2 (Ezh2) is involved in anxiety-like behavior in rodents (26). Maintenance of genomic stability following the setting of DNA damage plays a significant role of checkpoint kinase 2 (CHK2). Involvement in DNA repair, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis is attributed to CHEK2 function (27).

Conclusion

In conclusion, molecular analysis revealed shared molecular signatures between gastric cancer and anxiety disorder. There are key driver genes identified in gastric cancer that have the same importance in anxiety. The finding indicates that, there is association between anxiety and gastric cancer development. It can be suggested that incorporating psychological profiling into clinical assessments to confirm this outcome.
