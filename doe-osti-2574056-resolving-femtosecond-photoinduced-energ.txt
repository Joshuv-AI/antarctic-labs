 Abstract

The dynamics that take place within just tens to hundreds of femtoseconds following the
absorption of light by a molecule can play a critical role in how the absorbed energy is
directed, allowing it to be used for a specific function or dissipated harmlessly. The form
of chemical change that occurs rapidly in these molecules is called a “nonadiabatic
electronic transition.” Such transitions are known to mediate energy flow in natural
biological systems such as the ultraviolet photoprotection mechanism of DNA and the first
step of the human vision response. Understanding how these mechanisms work precisely
may help scientists achieve controlled manipulation of solar energy or optical control of a
wide range of energy management functions in artificial systems.
Experimental methods, however, have not yet allowed a precisely resolved and complete
measurement of nonadiabatic electronic transitions. This constitutes a major obstacle to
progress in the field. For progress to occur that would inform a wide body of research
aiming to efficiently harness the energy of light for practical purposes, it is especially
important to benchmark computational models of the molecules undergoing these rapid
changes with experimental measurements, in order to learn which models are accurate.
With Dept. of Energy funding, we have made strong progress towards establishing a new
optical method for experimentally detecting the full nonadiabatic electronic transition. This
requires having coordinated pulses of light covering the visible through the mid-infrared
range of the electromagnetic spectrum that last only ten femtoseconds. We have
developed a new, relatively simple approach for generating such pulses of laser light, and
have incorporated them into a time-resolved spectrometer for measuring rapid changes
in molecules. These tools can provide the greater precision and new types of data that
are needed to benchmark computational models of molecular change and thus to make
progress in the field. Our tools were tested on graphene, an excellent solid-state sample
for verifying the capabilities and limitations of our instrumentation. The investment made
in these tools by the Dept. of Energy Office of Science will allow new fundamental
scientific understanding of energy dynamics in molecules in future studies.

 Summary of Project Activities

The main activities performed during the project were as follows, described in brief:
 • Construction of a multi-spectral transient absorption spectrometer with
 simultaneous near-IR and mid-IR pulses, using the recent approach of adiabatic
 frequency conversion [1]. We encountered challenges implementing a dispersion
 management scheme allowing simultaneous compression of the two beamlines,
 even with a commercial 4-f pulse shaper in each beamline, due to the significant
 optical bandwidth and inherent limitations of the pulse shaper. We found ways to
 optimize the use of the 4-f pulse shaper, but concluded that a better dispersion
 management scheme was required to establish a practical scientific tool. Still, we
 are able to generate pulses under 20 fs in each beamline.
 • Implementation of an in situ technique for full-field pulse characterization of
 octave-spanning pulses, based on Frequency Resolved Optical Switching
 (FROSt). By allowing measurement of the group-delay dispersion (GDD) of our
 transient absorption spectrometer pulses at the location of the experiment, new
 precision can be achieved in the interpretation of the transient electronic
 dynamics.
 • Development and demonstration of a new approach to optical frequency
 conversion that allows dispersion-free generation of single-cycle pulses, which we
 call adiabatic frequency conversion with intrinsic dispersion management. We
 demonstrated frequency downconversion of energetic near-IR 11.1-fs pulses to
 11.6 fs mid-IR pulses in a single nonlinear optical device, without additional
 necessary dispersion management.
 We believe that this new approach allows a significant leap in hyperspectral
 transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) for furthering basic research on
 ultrafast coupled electronic and nuclear energy relaxation mechanisms,
 with ~10-fs instrument response function (IRF) in multi-spectral
 spectroscopy involving pump and probe pulses arbitrarily selected within
 the visible through mid-IR range. The technology is far simpler than optical
 parametric amplifier (OPA) based multi-spectral systems, potentially
 allowing its widespread use for science.
 • Benchmarking our novel instrumentation for hyperspectral 10-fs-resolved TAS by
 observation of ultrafast Pauli blocking during hot electron relaxation in
 photoexcited graphene. We were able to observe probe-frequency dependent
 electronic relaxation dynamics with ~20-fs IRF, using a near-IR pump pulse and
 mid-IR octave-spanning probe pulse in our transient absorption spectrometer,
 incorporating FROSt for an in situ reference of the probe pulse group delay
 dispersion.

1) First construction phase of the 10-fs hyperspectral transient absorption
 spectrometer and technical roadblocks related to dispersion management
The initial phase of our program included instrumentation development using the capital
equipment funding provided by this grant program and preliminary experiments to
assess and optimize the IRF of our transient absorption pump-probe setup.
We added two spectrometers with fast array detectors and high dynamic range. In
addition to an existing 2D LN2-cooled HgCdTe array (128 x 128 pixel, 2-11 μm
sensitivity) detector-based spectrometer, we added a dual-port spectrometer housing
low noise and fast acquisition Si CCD (128 pixel, 200-1000 nm sensitivity) and TE-
cooled extended InGaAs CCD (256 pixel, 1000-2200 nm sensitivity) detectors. To allow
measurements with adequate sensitivity to resolve a |∆T/T| of 1 x 10-4, we chose
detectors with high enough well depth, low enough readout noise, and low enough dark
noise integrated over 100 μs (our laser pulse interval) to achieve a single-shot dynamic
range of 104 above the noise floor. Moreover, all three detectors are capable of ≥10 kHz
readout speeds – the Si and InGaAs linear arrays at full pixel number, and the 2D
HgCdTe array when acquisition is binned down to 96 x 16 pixels.

The pulse shapers are used to compensate group velocity dispersion as well as high
orders of material dispersion and allow generation of compressed 10-fs pulses.
We carried out characterization of the two pulses to be used first in multi-spectral TAS
measurements, at 2.0-4.0 and 0.66-1.0 micron wavelengths. This revealed practical
obstacles due to the complexity and technical limitations of the dispersion management
system. As is typical of OPCPA systems, a large amount of material dispersion is
encountered in the beamlines. Due to the very large bandwidth of the pulses, this
dispersion must be precisely compensated. Otherwise, their durations will be much
longer than 10 fs. Additionally, the AFC stages carry a large amount of material
dispersion. Thus, as we did in our prior work [1], the hybrid OPCPA and AFC based
pulse system required a complex dispersion management scheme incorporating pulse
shapers. The complexity of the optics reduced the stability of the system, introducing
drift and requiring frequent realignment of the optical components. This, and the overall
complexity of dispersion management devices needed for multiple beamlines (each
needed to support 10-fs pulse generation), was a hindrance to the use of the instrument
for precise TAS as originally conceived.
Additionally, in the course of developing the optical system, we noted and corrected a
flaw of our commercial near-IR 4F pulse shaper design that employed one-dimensional,
off-axis parabolic mirrors. This created aberrations on our near-IR OPCPA beam that
significantly reduced the conversion efficiency of our adiabatic frequency conversion
stage and prevented optimal pulse compression. After studying the origin of this
aberration, which was due to residual angular dispersion imparted by the off-axis
parabolic mirrors, we redesigned the 4F pulse shapers to use cylindrical mirrors which
eliminated the residual angular dispersion. This work represents a contribution to the
development of technology for ultrabroadband TAS and was presented at the 2019
Ultrafast Optics Conference [2].

However, additional unavoidable aberrations related to wide bandwidth in an acousto-
optic modulator (AOM) based 4F pulse shaper limited the amount of bandwidth we
could successfully employ in our system. In order to have efficient diffraction for all
optical frequencies from the AOM, where all frequencies emerge with uniform diffraction
angle, the pulse shaper should be operated with a phase mask that allows diffraction at
the Bragg angle for all frequencies. This phase mask is called the “Bragg mask”. As
shown in Fig. 2 (from the PhD thesis of Dylan Heberle [3], who earned his degree with
support from this program), when using a nonzero dispersion function in the pulse
shaper, the required AOM phase mask differs from the Bragg mask. This known
problem has been successfully dealt with for modest bandwidths [4,5], but is a limitation
for the extreme bandwidth of our optical system. Indeed, we found that the significant
dispersion needed in our 4F pulse shaper to achieve full compression to 10 fs for each
of our beamlines, together with the large bandwidth spanning a large part of an octave,
resulted in too widespread a range of diffraction angles. This led to strong spatial chirp
in the shaped beamlines that hindered the implementation of TAS. As a result, we
needed to limit both the dispersion function and the pulse bandwidth. To do this,
additional optical elements were needed in the dispersion management system that
greatly increased the overall optical complexity and the pulse durations were limited to
greater than 20 fs.
These issues led us to conclude that the establishment of an optical system with
the new capabilities we required for our hyperspectral TAS system would require
a smarter approach to dispersion management. This became a significant focus of
our research, as summarized below.

2) Implementation of Frequency Resolved Optical Switching (FROSt) for in situ
 full-field characterization of octave-spanning pump and probe pulses
In order to precisely assess and optimize the IRF of our hyperspectral TAS system, we
implemented a recent method for in situ full pulse amplitude and phase characterization
of octave-spanning pulses at the location of the transient absorption experiment. We
constructed a new ultrafast pulse characterization scheme for our octave-spanning
near-IR (1.1-2.2 micron) and mid-IR (2-4 micron) probe pulses based on the recent
FROSt (frequency resolved optical switching) approach [6], which uses phase-
matching-free ultrafast transient absorption in a semiconductor and a ptychographic
retrieval algorithm to reconstruct the electric field of the probe pulse. Transient
absorption is used as a temporal pulse gating mechanism, in which an ultrashort pump
pulse changes the temporal absorption profile of a thin semiconductor relative to the
moving frame of the pulse to be tested. This allows the technique to be particularly
suitable for our ultrabroadband transient absorption experiments, as it can be performed
in situ in the experiment. Since the technique does not rely on a nonlinear conversion
process, there is no phase-matching bandwidth limiting its applicability for ultrashort
pulses, and the test pulse bandwidth can in fact span the full transmission window of the
semiconductor medium. We have collaborated with the group of François Légeré at
INRS, Quebec, Canada, to implement this in our laboratory.

3) Establishment of the 10-fs hyperspectral TAS system, using adiabatic
 frequency conversion with intrinisic dispersion management, a novel method
 developed in this program
To avoid the complexity discussed above and create a robust instrument for science, we
developed an alternative source technology and architecture. In an AFC device, photons
are upshifted or downshifted following dynamics analogous to rapid adiabatic
passage [7]. The device employs quasi-phase matching (QPM) with a slowly changing
position-dependent chirped poling frequency 𝐺(𝑧) = 2𝜋/Λ(𝑧). In 2017, we first
demonstrated single-cycle mid-IR pulse generation (the first ever high-energy single-
cycle pulse in the 2-5 micron range, measuring 11 fs) [1], which required a highly complex
dispersion management scheme incorporating two grism-pair stretchers, an acousto-
optic programmable dispersive filter, and a bulk silicon compressor. In this program, as
described above, our attempted implementation of a five-channel system based on AFC
for spectroscopy was thwarted by the complexity of needing five separate dispersion
management schemes for pulses £10 fs, each requiring multiple orders of dispersion
compensation and long uncommon beam paths incorporating highly alignment-sensitive
devices, which led to exactly the unwieldly and non-robust complex device that we were
hoping to avoid; i.e., a device that was still unpractical for carrying out our scientific goals.
As described above, the 4F pulse shapers needed for dispersion compensation also
limited the pulse bandwidth.
To solve these issues of dispersion management that hinder a 10-fs hyperspectral
system for TAS, we developed a novel, efficient variant of AFC that can not only
handle octave-spanning bandwidths, but can also monolithically manage its own
large material dispersion to eliminate group delay dispersion (GDD) entirely [8],

4) Benchmarking the hyperspectral spectroscopy system in graphene
We performed a near-IR (660-950 nm) pump, long-wavelength probe TAS study of hot
photoexcited electron cooling in graphene using our hyperspectral system. With some
changes in laser source, we reproduced the 2013 experiment by Brida, et al. (Fig. 6(a)-
(c)) [9], wherein a pump-probe study with 20-fs IRF was used to detect sub-100 fs time
scale thermalization of photoexcited electrons, a time frame where electron-electron
scattering dominates the relaxation (after which cooling by electron-phonon scattering
dominates). Hot electron cooling was captured by the observation of Pauli blocking in the
TAS spectrum, as depicted by Fig. 6(c): as conduction electrons fall in energy, they
temporarily block absorption from the valence band, resulting in a transient
photobleaching that evolves from high to low probe photon energy during thermalization.
In the 2013 experiment, the lowest probe photon energy available was 0.7 eV. In our
experiment, we took advantage of a sub-20-fs IRF with photon energy as low as 0.3 eV
(7-fs near-IR pump, 12-fs mid-IR probe), allowing measurement of the dynamics during

Furthermore, the in situ pulse characterization capability allowed by our FROSt setup
described above allows for a useful group delay reference. By switching from the TAS
sample (e.g., graphene) to the FROSt semiconductor sample (e.g., bulk silicon), an in situ
measurement of the probe pulse’s GDD can be obtained. Not only does this ensure that
the duration of the probe is characterized at the location of the sample, but it can be used
as a group delay reference for the TAS measurement. For example, Fig. 6(e),(f) compare
raw Δ𝑇/𝑇 data from the MCT spectrometer (over a 400-nm subset of the full 2-4 µm probe
spectrum) for graphene photobleaching and FROSt, respectively. For illustration of how
GDD can be detected, a filter was placed in the path of the compressed probe pulse prior
to the measurements to introduce some measurable GDD (i.e., to introduce a measurable
frequency chirp). As can be seen, the onset of photobleaching in (e) largely matches the
onset of absorption in (f) at each probe frequency. Thus, any imperfection in the GDD
function of the probe pulse (relative to a flat profile) can be distinguished from real
differences in the onset of electron relaxation dynamics. This capability can aid the
precision of TAS measurements taken with the hyperspectral system.
