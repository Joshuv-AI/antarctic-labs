Rational Design of Organic Chromophores for Simultaneously Triggering Redox Imbalance and Tracking Organelles in Biological Systems Under Near‐Infrared Light Irradiation
Authors: Pragti, Bidyut Kumar Kundu, Jiajie Diao, Yujie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe design of organic photosensitizers that operate efficiently in the near‐infrared (NIR) region remains challenging due to the need to balance electronic delocalization, stability, and biological compatibility. Here we report a rational, electronegativity‐guided strategy for engineering (donor–π)3–acceptor pyrylium‐based chromophores capable of concurrent NIR phototherapy and subcellular imaging. Density functional theory calculations identified pyrylium (PYR) as the optimal acceptor for narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap and enhancing intramolecular charge transfer (ICT). The resulting octupolar chromophores, PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2, display intense absorption in the 600–800 nm range, large Stokes shift (>100 nm), and excellent photostability. Under NIR irradiation, PYR‐NPh2 catalyzes efficient NADH photooxidation and generates multiple reactive oxygen species (ROS), even under hypoxic conditions. In cancer cells (e.g., MDA‐MB‐231), PYR‐NPh2 induces redox imbalance and pronounced phototoxicity while enabling high‐fidelity lysosomal imaging at ∼100 nm resolution using structured illumination microscopy. This work introduces a unified molecular design principle for NIR organic chromophores that integrate redox modulation, ROS generation, and organelle‐level visualization, advancing the frontier of precision phototheranostics.

Introduction

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) has emerged as a minimally invasive and highly selective modality for cancer treatment, wherein a photosensitizer generates cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light activation [1, 2]. Despite its clinical promise, most photosensitizers currently used or reported rely on expensive noble metals (e.g., Ru, Ir, Os) [3, 4, 5] or possess absorption profiles outside the biological window (650–1000 nm), thereby limiting tissue penetration and inducing undesirable side effects associated with heavy‐metal toxicity. Organic photosensitizers (OPSs) offer an attractive metal‐free alternative with superior biocompatibility, tunable photophysical properties, and facile structural modification. However, most OPSs absorb and emit in the visible region (< 600 nm), resulting in shallow tissue penetration and interference from biological autofluorescence [6, 7, 8]. For example, coumarin and naphthalimide derivatives (λ em < 500 nm) suffer from strong autofluorescence background [9, 10], while BODIPY and rhodamine chromophores, despite their high brightness, exhibit narrow Stokes shifts (< 50 nm), leading to self‐excitation artifacts [11, 12]. Cyanine and hemicyanine analogues extend into the near‐infrared (NIR) region but often display poor photostability and low quantum yields due to high‐lying excited states [13, 14]. Rational design of NIR light‐absorbing OPSs thus requires balancing molecular architecture, conjugation, solubility, photostability, and ROS‐generation efficiency to achieve both effective photosensitization and biological compatibility [15, 16, 17, 18]. Moreover, OPSs capable of simultaneously producing ROS and perturbing cellular redox balance represent an ideal platform for efficient phototherapeutic intervention [17, 19, 20].

As described in Figure 1a, most clinically established photosensitizers operate via a Type II mechanism that relies on triplet energy transfer to generate singlet oxygen (1O2); however, this process is severely hampered under hypoxic tumor microenvironments due to its oxygen dependence [21]. Type I photosensitizers, which produce cytotoxic radicals through electron transfer with reduced O2 dependence, remain comparatively underexplored because electron transfer often competes unfavorably with energy transfer and rational molecular design strategies are limited [22, 23]. To address these limitations, Yang et al. enhanced Type I activity by introducing electron acceptors into supramolecular photosensitizer assemblies that promote NADH oxidation and ROS production [19]. Tang and coworkers subsequently developed donor–acceptor‐type OPSs that modulate intracellular NADH levels, though the excitation wavelengths remain below 500 nm [24]. More recently, the Sun group demonstrated NIR light‐absorbing OPSs capable of RNA‐targeted PDT, highlighting the potential of redox‐active, metal‐free chromophores [25]. Nevertheless, the development of NIR OPSs that can concurrently trigger redox imbalance and operate efficiently under biologically relevant conditions remains a significant challenge.

Beyond phototherapy, the development of NIR fluorescent probes capable of tracking subcellular organelles is of equal importance for understanding cellular dynamics and assessing therapeutic responses. NIR excitation offers multiple advantages, including deep tissue penetration, reduced photodamage, and minimized background autofluorescence, making NIR fluorescent probes ideal for both in vitro and in vivo imaging applications. In particular, the ability to visualize organelles with high spatial and temporal resolution provides valuable insight into cell metabolism, redox regulation, and apoptotic pathways during photodynamic treatment. Recent advances in super‐resolution microscopy have further expanded these possibilities [26]. For instance, structured illumination microscopy (SIM) enables imaging beyond the diffraction limit (< 200 nm) with minimal photobleaching, allowing real‐time observation of organelle morphology and dynamics at ∼100 nm resolution [27]. However, as depicted in Figure 1b, the number of NIR OPSs that can simultaneously induce redox imbalance and enable organelle tracking under SIM imaging remains exceedingly limited.

To fill this gap, we employed a rational molecular design strategy guided by density functional theory (DFT) calculations to develop redox‐active NIR OPSs with enhanced photophysical and biological performance. We hypothesized that incorporating a three‐armed donor–π–acceptor ((D–π)3–A) octupolar framework would amplify ICT relative to dipolar or quadrupolar analogues [8, 28], thereby extending absorption into the biological window (Figure 1c). By systematically tuning the electronegativity and positive charge of the central acceptor, the π‐bridge conjugation, and the donor strength, we identified the pyrylium (PYR) scaffold as the optimal acceptor for narrowing the HOMO–LUMO gap and improving charge delocalization. Computations revealed a progressive red shift in absorption from benzene (BEN), pyridinium (PYD), to pyrylium (PYR), correlating with increased ICT and polarizability. Guided by these predictions, we synthesized two octupolar chromophores, PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2, differing in donor strength to tune their redox and photophysical behavior. The resulting chromophores absorb strongly within the NIR region, exhibit broad Stokes shifts exceeding 100 nm, and demonstrate excellent photostability and biocompatibility, properties that collectively enable their dual function in redox modulation and super‐resolution imaging.

Results and Discussion

As presented in Figure 2a, the ICT of our octupolar systems increases in the order of BEN (benzene) < PYD (pyridinium) < PYR (pyrylium) depending on their χ‐values. A general trend in similar order can be observed for the ground‐state energy and polarizability (Figure S1). Moreover, the same order is observed from the HOMO‐LUMO energy gap (Figure 2b): BEN (3.71 eV) > PYD (3.26 eV) > PYR (2.86 eV). Next, the time‐dependent DFT (TD‐DFT) calculated absorption spectra show a gradual red‐shift from 313 nm (BEN), 368 nm (PYD), to 411 nm (PYR), which further validates our design hypothesis (Figure 2c). With these calculation results in hand, we decided to synthesize two (D‐π)3‐A‐based octupolar organic chromophores (PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2) by installing ‐OMe and ‐NPh2 at the peripheral phenyl rings to further enhance the electron donor strength (Figure 2d). Taking ‐NPh2‐substituted chromophore as a representative model, we further confirmed that the octupolar framework significantly enhances ICT compared to the corresponding dipolar and quadrupolar analogues (Figures S2 and S3).

Specifically, PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2 were synthesized via a straightforward one‐step condensation between commercially available 2,4,6‐trimethylpyrylium tetrafluoroborate and either 4‐methoxybenzaldehyde or 4‐diphenylaminobenzaldehyde, affording the desired octupolar chromophores in 72% and 87% yields, respectively. Full synthetic procedures and spectroscopic characterizations are detailed in the Supporting Information (Figures S4–S34). As shown in Figure 3a, PYR‐OMe displays absorption maxima at 332 nm (ε = 23,697 M− 1 cm− 1) and 521 nm (ε = 5,139 M− 1 cm− 1), whereas PYR‐NPh2 exhibits intense absorption at 388 nm (ε = 39,311 M− 1 cm− 1) and 681 nm (ε = 27,051 M− 1 cm− 1), consistent with its stronger donor strength and enhanced intramolecular charge transfer character. Notably, PYR‐NPh2 emits in the NIR region (λ em = 788 nm) with a broad Stokes shift exceeding 100 nm, whereas PYR‐OMe emits in the red region (λ em = 631 nm) (Figure 3b). The CIE chromaticity analysis or an xy‐plot [29] (Figure S34c) further confirms the bathochromic shift of PYR‐NPh2 emission relative to PYR‐OMe, in agreement with both calculated and observed trends. Moreover, the emission intensity of both PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2 remained unchanged after 1000 s of irradiation in aqueous media (Figure S35). Such photostability is comparable to those of methylene blue and rhodamine B dyes.

To assess environmental responsiveness, the fluorescence of both chromophores was examined in glycerol/PBS mixtures of varying viscosity [28]. The emission intensity increased markedly with higher glycerol content (53–57 cP, Figures 3c and S36), reflecting viscosity‐induced restriction of intramolecular rotation and E/Z isomerization across their C═C linkers. Although both chromophores exhibited negligible aggregation‐induced emission (AIE) enhancement (fivefold and sixfold increase in DMSO/toluene mixtures while no effect in THF/water mixtures as shown in Figures S37 and S38), the effects were less pronounced than those of typical AIE systems, likely due to the strong intramolecular charge transfer nature and good solubility of these compounds. Time‐correlated single‐photon counting (TCSPC) analysis revealed solvent‐dependent fluorescence lifetimes, with PYR‐NPh2 showing a longer lifetime (2.34 ns) than PYR‐OMe (1.79 ns), consistent with restricted torsional relaxation in viscous media (Figures 3d and S39). Cyclic voltammetry measurements demonstrated quasi‐reversible redox behavior for PYR‐NPh2, with reduction and oxidation peaks at −0.35 V and +1.33 V vs Ag/Ag+, respectively (Figure S40). Its excited‐state reduction potential (1.22 V vs Ag/Ag+) underscores its strong photooxidizing capability, making it well‐suited for redox‐driven photochemical transformations.

Our DFT calculations further corroborated these results, indicating planar geometries with narrowed HOMO–LUMO gaps and delocalized charge distributions conducive to NIR absorption and photostability. As shown in Figure S41, the most energetically favorable geometry of PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2 adopted the planar configuration with angles associated with the pyrylium ring of 111.5–111.9°. DFT calculations also provided deeper insight into their electronic structures and excited‐state properties (Figure 4a). For PYR‐OMe, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO, −6.05 eV) is primarily localized over two donor arms adjacent to the pyrylium oxygen center, whereas the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO, −3.41 eV) is concentrated on the electron‐deficient pyrylium core, confirming its donor–acceptor separation and strong intramolecular charge transfer character. In PYR‐NPh2, its HOMO is more destabilized due to the stronger electron‐donating –NPh2 groups, resulting in a reduced HOMO–LUMO gap (2.14 eV) relative to PYR‐OMe (2.64 eV), consistent with the observed bathochromic shift in absorption. Additional DFT‐calculated frontier molecular orbitals are summarized in Figures S42 and S43. Time‐dependent DFT calculations further elucidated the electronic transitions (Figures S44 and S45). For PYR‐OMe, the lowest singlet excited state (S1) appears at 462 nm (f = 1.44) and exhibits clear intramolecular charge transfer character from the donor arms to the pyrylium core, while a secondary transition (S2, 432 nm, f = 1.57) corresponds to excitation from the para‐substituted arm (Figure 4b). In contrast, PYR‐NPh2 shows more pronounced intramolecular charge transfer transitions (Figure 4c), with S1 and S2 located at 523 and 507 nm (f = 1.89 and 1.93, respectively), in excellent agreement with their corresponding experimental absorption spectra.

Given the flexible (D–π)3–A octupolar framework, we examined the influence of dihedral rotation on the electronic structure of PYR‐NPh2. Systematic variation of the dihedral angle between the central pyrylium core and the phenyl arms revealed that increasing torsion from 0° to 90° significantly decreased the HOMO–LUMO gap from 2.13 to 1.82 eV, followed by partial recovery at higher angles (Figures 4d and S46, S47 and Table S1). This trend indicates that conformations with enhanced planarity favor π‐conjugation and efficient charge delocalization, rationalizing the strong NIR absorption and photoinduced charge‐transfer capability. Collectively, these computational results corroborate the experimentally observed photophysical and redox properties, highlighting PYR‐NPh2 as a structurally optimized NIR‐active OPS with robust intramolecular charge transfer character and photostability suitable for photochemical and biological applications.

Encouraged by the strong NIR absorption, redox property, and photostability of PYR‐OMe and PYR‐NPh2, we next explored their capability to mediate NADH photooxidation as a model reaction for cellular redox modulation (Figures 5a and S48). NADH is a key metabolic cofactor maintaining intracellular redox balance; its oxidation to NAD+ perturbs mitochondrial homeostasis and promotes apoptotic pathways [30, 31]. Upon deep red (660 nm) and NIR (740 nm) light irradiation, PYR‐NPh2 catalyzed rapid oxidation of NADH in glycerol/PBS (3:2, v/v), as evidenced by a pronounced decrease in absorbance at 339 nm and a concomitant increase at 260 nm (Figure 5b,c). Kinetic analyses revealed a turnover number (TON) of 12.1 under 660 nm irradiation and 3.5 under 740 nm, greatly surpassing the dark control (TONdark = 0.006) and exceeding many reported transition metal‐based photocatalysts [5, 23]. PYR‐OMe also catalyzed NADH oxidation (TON = 6.1 at 660 nm), albeit with lower efficiency, suggesting that the stronger donor strength and enhanced intramolecular charge transfer of PYR‐NPh2 facilitate more effective photoinduced electron transfer (Figure 5d,e). Interestingly, increasing the viscosity of the reaction medium further enhanced catalytic activity. The TON of PYR‐NPh2 increased from 3.3 in PBS to 14.0 in 90% glycerol (Figure S49), indicating that restricted E/Z isomerization and reduced nonradiative decay under viscous conditions prolong the excited‐state lifetime and improve charge‐transfer efficiency. 1H NMR monitoring confirmed the light‐driven conversion of NADH to NAD+ (Figure 5f).

Intracellular NADH quantification in MDA‐MB‐231 cells further validated these findings: treatment with PYR‐NPh2 followed by 660 nm irradiation significantly decreased cellular NADH levels compared with dark controls (Figures 5g and S50,S51). To elucidate the underlying mechanism of NADH photooxidation and photodynamic activity (Figure 5h), we conducted emission quenching experiments (Figures 5i,j), whose results confirmed efficient photoinduced electron transfer from NADH to the excited chromophore, consistent with a Type I pathway (Figure 1a).

Furthermore, cell viability assays under 660 nm irradiation (30 mW cm−2, 20 min) revealed light‐dependent cytotoxicity in MDA‐MB‐231, HeLa, and 4T1 cell lines, while dark toxicity remained negligible (Figures 5k,l, S52–S55 and Table S2). The IC50 value of PYR‐NPh2 against the MDA‐MB‐231 cells under light was 12 µM, significantly lower than that of PYR‐OMe (IC50 = 27 µM). These findings establish PYR‐NPh2 as a potent NIR photosensitizer that integrates strong absorption, efficient ROS generation, and high photocytotoxic selectivity, which are key attributes for oxygen‐independent Type I phototherapy.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were further characterized using specific chemical probes. Both photosensitizers produced singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide radical (O2 •−), and hydroxyl radical (•OH) upon 660 nm irradiation, with PYR‐NPh2 exhibiting markedly higher activity (Figures S56–S61 and Table S3). The generation of 1O2 was quantified using 1,3‐diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as a trapping reagent, yielding singlet oxygen quantum yields (Φ) of 0.38 for PYR‐OMe and 0.61 for PYR‐NPh2 in MeOH, comparable to that of Rose Bengal (Φ = 0.80). These results indicate that the strong intramolecular charge transfer character of PYR‐NPh2 facilitates both energy and electron transfer processes, enabling concurrent Type I/II ROS generation under NIR light.

To validate the proposed ROS‐generation mechanism underlying light‐induced cellular apoptosis, we conducted a series of chemical assays to probe the capability of our organic photosensitizers to produce specific reactive oxygen species, including singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide anion (O2 •−), and hydroxyl radical (•OH), via intracellular O2 activation under photoirradiation. The 1O2 generation was first assessed using 9,10‐anthracenediyl‐bis(methylene)‐dimalonic acid (ABDA) and 1,3‐diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as trapping reagents [32]. Upon 660 or 740 nm light irradiation in the presence of PYR‐OMe or PYR‐NPh2, the characteristic absorption peaks of ABDA at 378 and 399 nm and of DPBF at 410 nm decreased progressively, confirming 1O2 formation (Figures 6a and S58,S59). Kinetic analysis revealed that PYR‐NPh2 exhibited the highest pseudo‐first‐order rate constant (0.166 min−1) and an excellent 1O2 generation quantum yield of 0.906, indicating superior photosensitization efficiency.

We next evaluated their performance to generate other radical species under NIR irradiation. Dihydroethidium (DHE) and dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) were employed as fluorescent probes for O2 •− and •OH, respectively. Upon illumination at 660 or 740 nm, a substantial enhancement in DHE fluorescence was observed (up to a 12‐fold increase) for PYR‐NPh2 compared with DHE alone; while DHR123 fluorescence rose sharply even after only 80 s of irradiation (Figures 6b,c and S60,S61). These results unambiguously demonstrate that our organic photosensitizers, particularly PYR‐NPh2, efficiently generate multiple ROS under physiologically relevant conditions, thereby validating their strong potential for redox modulation and light‐triggered apoptotic applications.

We then examined the intracellular ROS generation mediated by our organic photosensitizers through fluorescence imaging in living MDA‐MB‐231 cells. As shown in Figure 6d, cells treated with PYR‐NPh2 and subjected to light irradiation displayed intense green fluorescence corresponding to the oxidation of 2’,7’‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) to dichlorofluorescein (DCF) [33], confirming robust intracellular ROS production. In contrast, cells maintained in the dark exhibited negligible fluorescence, while pretreatment with N‐acetylcysteine (NAC), a broad‐spectrum ROS scavenger [34], markedly suppressed the signal. In addition, using the superoxide probe dihydroethidium (DHE) [35], cells incubated with PYR‐OMe or PYR‐NPh2 showed pronounced red fluorescence upon irradiation, indicating efficient generation of O2 •−. This emission was significantly attenuated by vitamin C, consistent with its role as an O2 •− scavenger. Similarly, cells stained with the 1O2 probe singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) [36] displayed negligible fluorescence in the dark but bright emission after 660 nm irradiation, which was quenched upon treatment with L‐histidine methyl ester (L‐His), a selective 1O2 scavenger. Furthermore, cells cotreated with PYR‐NPh2 and the •OH radical probe dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) [37] exhibited strong green fluorescence following irradiation, which was drastically reduced either under dark conditions or in the presence of 2‐methoxyestradiol (2‐MED) [38], a superoxide dismutase (SOD) inhibitor. These observations collectively confirm that PYR‐NPh2 and PYR‐OMe can efficiently generate multiple ROS species, including O2 •−, 1O2, and •OH, within living cells under both normoxic (21% O2) and hypoxic (2% O2) conditions upon NIR light activation.

Encouraged by the potent phototoxicity and ROS generation efficiency of these two organic chromophores, we next examined their ability to induce apoptosis in cancer cells under light irradiation. Photodynamic cell death was visualized through dual staining with Hoechst 33342 and propidium iodide (PI) in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. Hoechst permeates all nuclei, emitting blue fluorescence in both viable and dead cells, whereas PI stains only membrane‐compromised apoptotic or necrotic cells with red fluorescence. As shown in Figure 6e, cells treated with PYR‐NPh2 and subjected to light irradiation displayed both blue and red fluorescence at 2–4 µM concentrations, indicative of partial apoptosis. The red fluorescence intensity progressively increased with higher PYR‐NPh2 concentration, confirming concentration‐dependent phototoxicity (Figure S62). In contrast, cells either kept in the dark or untreated with PYR‐NPh2 exhibited only blue fluorescence, verifying the absence of light‐independent cytotoxicity.

To elucidate the molecular basis of cell death, we analyzed the expression of apoptosis‐related proteins by western blotting (Figure 6f). Poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP), a canonical substrate of caspase‐3, showed clear cleavage after 24 h of PYR‐NPh2 treatment followed by photoactivation, confirming caspase‐mediated apoptosis. While dark‐treated cells displayed only a modest PARP increase (∼ 1.2‐fold), photoirradiated cells exhibited a pronounced, concentration‐dependent rise (∼ 2.5‐fold at 8 – 10 µM), indicating activation of the caspase cascade. Concurrently, the expression of the anti‐apoptotic protein BCL‐XL was markedly downregulated under illumination. The combination of PARP cleavage and BCL‐XL suppression strongly supports the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.

Flow‐cytometry analysis further verified these findings (Figure 6g). Neither light exposure alone (∼ 1% apoptotic cells) nor dark incubation with PYR‐NPh2 (∼ 4%) induced significant apoptosis. However, upon 660 nm irradiation (30 mW cm−2) at the IC50 concentration of PYR‐NPh2, the apoptotic population increased by approximately eightfold, underscoring the light‐dependent cytotoxic mechanism. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PYR‐NPh2 functions as an efficient NIR photosensitizer that induces apoptosis through ROS‐mediated, caspase‐dependent pathways, thereby enhancing phototherapeutic efficacy.

Moreover, the intrinsic NIR fluorescence of these chromophores offers deep tissue penetration, high signal‐to‐noise ratios, and minimal photodamage, features that are particularly advantageous for high‐contrast, noninvasive bioimaging and real‐time monitoring of therapeutic responses in living systems. Encouraged by the negligible dark cytotoxicity and exceptional photophysical performance of our organic chromophores, we next leveraged their NIR excitation capability to explore deep‐tissue bioimaging, with a particular focus on subcellular organelle tracking. To this end, structured illumination microscopy (SIM) [39] was employed to achieve super‐resolution imaging with enhanced spatial detail, minimal photodamage, and compatibility with live‐cell imaging. Owing to its strong NIR absorption and high photostability, PYR‐NPh2 was selected for systematic colocalization studies with various commercial organelle trackers, including MitoTracker Green (MTG), LysoTracker Green (LTG), BODIPY (lipid tracker), and ER‐Tracker Green (ERG). As shown in Figures 7a,b and S63–S66, PYR‐NPh2 exhibited the highest overlap with LTG (Pearson's correlation coefficient, PCC = 0.83), compared with MTG (PCC = 0.37), BODIPY (PCC = 0.49), and ERG (PCC = 0.44), confirming its preferential lysosomal localization.

This lysosome‐targeting tendency can be rationalized by the distinct intracellular viscosity environment. The average viscosity in the cytoplasm is approximately 2–4 cP, whereas mitochondria and lysosomes exhibit significantly higher viscosity (∼ 62 and ∼ 65 cP, respectively) [28]. Such differences can be advantageously exploited for imaging contrast enhancement. Given its large Stokes shift and viscosity‐responsive emission, PYR‐NPh2 displays intensified fluorescence in viscous organelles like lysosomes, leading to improved signal‐to‐noise ratios and reduced background interference. To further validate this performance, PYR‐NPh2 was directly compared with the commercial lysosomal dye LTG under identical SIM imaging conditions in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. As illustrated in Figures 7c and 3D fluorescence intensity mapping of randomly selected background regions revealed substantially lower nonspecific background fluorescence for PYR‐NPh2 than for LTG. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PYR‐NPh2 serves as an efficient NIR lysosomal probe with superior imaging contrast, enabling high‐resolution visualization of subcellular structures with minimal phototoxicity.

Building upon the excellent lysosomal imaging performance of PYR‐NPh2, we further evaluated its generality and long‐term imaging capability across multiple cancer cell lines. As shown in Figure 7d, PYR‐NPh2 exhibited strong lysosomal colocalization in cervical cancer (HeLa), liver cancer (HepG2), and mammary carcinoma (4T1) cells, with Pearson's correlation coefficients (PCC) [40] of 0.92, 0.94, and 0.81, respectively, underscoring its broad applicability as a universal lysosomal probe. A slight decrease in PCC from 0.95 (6 h) to 0.90 (48 h) was observed in HepG2 cells (Figures 7e and S67), which can be attributed to gradual lysosomal degradation and redistribution of the probe during prolonged incubation.

Photostability is another essential criterion for reliable live‐cell imaging. Given its extended π‐conjugation and strong photochemical robustness, PYR‐NPh2 was expected to exhibit high resistance to photobleaching. To verify this, we compared the photostability of PYR‐NPh2 with the commercial lysosomal dye LysoTracker Red (LTR) [41] under identical SIM imaging conditions in MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure 7f–i). When subjected to continuous 100% laser irradiation, LTR fluorescence rapidly diminished, whereas PYR‐NPh2 retained intense emission over the entire observation period. Quantitative analysis revealed a photobleaching lifetime of 95.8 s for PYR‐NPh2, six times longer than that of LTR (15.8 s) (Figure 7g–k). Furthermore, in long‐term incubation studies, both dyes initially localized within lysosomes; however, after 48 h, LTR fluorescence was nearly undetectable, while PYR‐NPh2 maintained bright, filamentous lysosomal emission (Figure S68). Collectively, these findings prove PYR‐NPh2 as a highly photostable NIR lysosomal probe capable of maintaining signal integrity during extended imaging sessions.

Given its high‐resolution imaging capability, superior signal‐to‐noise ratio, and remarkable resistance to photobleaching, PYR‐NPh2 emerges as a powerful tool for visualizing subcellular structures and organelle interactions at the single‐cell level. Thus, we next sought to explore whether PYR‐NPh2 can penetrate multicellular tumor spheroids and achieve deep‐tissue imaging. Starting with the 3D tumor imaging experiment, it can be seen that PYR‐OMe is capable of staining green fluorescence while PYR‐NPh2 produced red fluorescence. No fluorescence was observed for the untreated tumor cells (Figure 8a). Inspired by these results, we further explored their ROS generation proficiency in the MDA‐MB‐231 tumor spheroid under photoirradiation. By taking PYR‐NPh2 as the sole photosensitizer and DCFH‐DA as the ROS sensor [33], MDA‐MB‐231 spheroids were treated at different concentrations of PYR‐NPh2 under 660 nm (30 mW/cm2) photoirradiation followed by incubation for 24 h. As represented in Figure 8b, with increasing concentration of PYR‐NPh2 the green fluorescence of ROS indicator DCFH‐DA prominently increases by producing DCF. Moreover, a negligible ROS production can be seen in the absence of OPS in the light control experiment.

To further assess the phototherapeutic performance of PYR‐NPh2 in a 3D tumor model, we evaluated its effect on the viability of MDA‐MB‐231 tumor spheroids using a Hoechst/PI dual‐staining live–dead assay. Confocal imaging revealed pronounced red fluorescence from PI in spheroids treated with increasing concentrations of PYR‐NPh2 under 660 nm irradiation, indicating extensive light‐induced cell death and strong phototherapeutic efficacy (Figure 8c,d). Quantitative analysis of PI fluorescence intensity demonstrated an approximately 10‐fold increase at 20 µM PYR‐NPh2 compared with untreated controls, corroborating the dose‐dependent nature of the photodynamic response. In contrast, spheroids incubated with 20 µM PYR‐NPh2 in the dark exhibited only weak PI fluorescence, suggesting minimal cytotoxicity without light activation (Figure S69). These findings clearly demonstrate that the combination of PYR‐NPh2 treatment and NIR photoirradiation is essential to achieving efficient tumor ablation within multicellular spheroids, underscoring its promise for cancer phototherapy.

Conclusion

In summary, this study establishes a rational design framework for NIR organic photosensitizers that combine photodynamic efficacy with subcellular imaging capability. Guided by electronegativity‐based computational prediction, we developed a series of (D–π)3–A octupolar pyrylium chromophores featuring narrow HOMO–LUMO gaps and enhanced intramolecular charge transfer. Among them, the NPh2‐substituted derivative, PYR‐NPh2, exhibits strong NIR absorption, a broad Stokes shift (>100 nm), and robust photostability, enabling deep‐tissue excitation and real‐time super‐resolution imaging. Mechanistically, PYR‐NPh2 mediates efficient photooxidation of NADH via both Types I and II pathways, generating multiple ROS species even under hypoxic conditions. These redox‐active and photophysical features collectively underpin potent phototherapeutic performance in cancer cells and 3D spheroids, accompanied by precise lysosomal localization and high imaging fidelity under structured illumination microscopy. Overall, the integration of NIR responsiveness, redox modulation, and organelle‐level imaging within a single molecular platform demonstrates a powerful strategy for advancing metal‐free phototheranostics and mechanistic visualization in living systems.
