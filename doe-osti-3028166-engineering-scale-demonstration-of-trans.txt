Executive Summary
ION Clean Energy® (ION) is deploying its transformational solvent-based CO2 capture technology
as part of the effort to decarbonize the electrical grid. In preparation for a full-scale commercial
deployment of ION’s solvent technology, ION has partnered with Calpine, Koch Modular Process
Systems (KMPS), and Sargent & Lundy (S&L) to design, construct and commission a 1 MWe CO2
capture pilot plant at Calpine’s Los Medanos Energy Center (LMEC), a commercially-dispatched
natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) power plant, in Pittsburg, CA. ION generated design
documents for the construction team and incorporated KMPS’s engineering expertise to produce a
Process Design Package that formed the basis for a Preliminary Design Review and HAZOP
meetings. S&L designed the balance of plant (BOP) and collaborated with Calpine to deliver
utilities to the pilot. KMPS oversaw the fabrication and delivery of the modules whereby S&L was
responsible for the final module installation and connections to the host site and BOP. ION
performed final commissioning and initial startup of the unit in September 2023.

This pilot, known as Project Enterprise (ENTP), was intended to allow ION to further demonstrate
the capabilities and superior stability of its carbon capture solvents and to provide key learnings in
preparation for the design, construction, and operation of a full-scale commercial CO2 capture
facility.

After successful commissioning of the pilot system, the initial phase of the pilot testing consisted
of baselining the system using aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) at 30 wt%, an industry standard
carbon capture solvent. Operating with MEA allowed ION to create a baseline to compare the
performance of ION’s proprietary solvents. ION operated the ENTP pilot with MEA for
approximately 250 hours from September to October of 2023, capturing 76 tonnes of CO2 (tCO2)
over that period. ION achieved capture rates as high as 89% with MEA, where foaming of the MEA
prevented operation at higher capture efficiencies. Due to solvent stability issues and the
inherently corrosive nature of MEA, ION quickly transitioned to operating with ICE-21™ to safeguard
the capital investment of the ENTP project.

The MEA campaign was followed by parametric and long-term testing using ION’s proprietary ICE-
21™ capture solvent. The objective of the ICE-21™ campaign was to create a secondary baseline
more relevant to commercially available CO2 capture technologies. ION performed parametric
testing with ICE-21™ over approximately 350 hours of testing from October 2023 to December 2023,
followed by over 800 hours of long-term testing from January 2024 to February 2024. ION captured
approximately 580 tCO2 with ICE-21™, achieving capture efficiencies as high as 99%. Due to a
discharge permitting issue (that was resolved at the start of the ICE-31® campaign), ION was
unable to operate the direct contact cooler (DCC) blowdown stream during the ICE-21™ campaign,
which prevented ION from cooling the flue gas below the dew point. The inability to cool below the
dew point of the flue gas prevented ION from achieving optimal performance with ICE-21™. Steady-

state operations with ICE-21™ were run continuously while parametric testing was performed in 12-
hour weekday-only shift work.

Emissions during the MEA and ICE-21™ campaigns were measured using a Gasmet FTIR Portable
Gas Analyzer. The Gasmet continuously measured CO2, water content, ammonia, formaldehyde,
amine emissions, and oxygen. MEA emissions showed relatively high ammonia readings (above 20
ppm NH3) while all other emissions were at or below the Gasmet limit of detection (LOD). All ICE-
21™ emissions, including ammonia, were at or below the Gasmet LOD.

The ICE-31® campaign began in February 2024 and concluded in February 2025 with 24-hour seven-
day-a-week operations. ICE-31® had over 2250 hours of parametric testing from February 2024 to
July 2024, followed by nearly 4000 hours of long-term steady-state testing concluding in February
2025. ION captured 2500 tCO2 with ICE-31® over the entire campaign and achieved capture
efficiencies as high as 99.4%. At such high capture efficiencies, the flue gas leaving the absorber
contains approximately 250 to 300 ppmv CO2, which is less than ambient air. ICE-31® exhibited
solvent stability in a high-oxygen environment with very little solvent makeup throughout the
campaign. ICE-31® emissions were too low for the Gasmet and were measured using a state-of-
the-art, research-grade, Proton-Transfer-Reaction Time-of-Flight (PTR-TOF) mass spectrometer.
For the last month of testing, ION focused on testing the aged solvent at multiple commercially
relevant flue gas inlet temperatures. Emissions of acetaldehyde, the only detected Hazardous Air
Pollutant (HAP), were generally below 2 to 3 ppbv with the highest reading around 7 ppbv. Other
Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) emissions associated with the solvent were generally below 1
ppbv with the highest reading around 3 ppbv. These results further demonstrate the excellent
emissions performance that can be achieved with ICE-31® on NGCC facilities.

The results confirm and expand ION’s expectations that ICE-31® is an exceptional solvent for flue
gases with high oxidative potential, such as NGCC facilities. The impressive results from the ENTP
pilot further demonstrate the readiness of ICE-31® as a viable, commercially deployable carbon
capture technology.

1 Introduction
Post-combustion carbon capture (PCC) is regarded as a key technology in the efforts to curb global
warming. PCC is a versatile technology that has been proven multiple times on a commercial scale
and can be deployed in various industrial settings such as power generation facilities, steel
fabrication mills, cement manufacturing, aluminum smelting, and more. As a result, PCC is an
efficient and mature technology for greenhouse gas mitigation. The most well understood PCC
technology is amine solvent-based carbon capture, which has been used in the gas sweetening
industry for decades, with aqueous MEA being the industry standard solvent. MEA performance
pales in comparison to next-generation CO2 capture solvents, which use much less energy, can
capture over 99% of CO2 in typical flue gas streams and are a critical technology to bridge the
energy demand gaps left by renewable energy sources. ION plans to deploy its transformational
solvent-based CO2 capture technology as part of the effort to decarbonize the electrical grid and
minimize the effects of climate change. ION is currently in the process of scaling its second and
third generation amine-based carbon capture solvents, ICE-21™ and ICE-31®, to the commercial
level.

In collaboration with Calpine, Sargent & Lundy, and Koch Modular Process Systems, ION has
designed, constructed, and commissioned, a bespoke, 1 MWe (10 tonnes per day) CO2 capture
pilot plant at LMEC, NGCC power plant in Pittsburg, CA – “Project Enterprise” (ENTP). The ENTP
pilot processes a slipstream of flue gas from the LMEC Unit 1 NGCC stack. ION commenced
operation of the ENTP carbon capture module on September 19, 2023. MEA was used as the
carbon capture solvent to baseline the system before testing with ION’s proprietary solvents, ICE-
21™ and ICE-31®. The MEA campaign consisted of various parametric baseline tests, and the plant
was operated for 12 hours a day on weekdays.

The ICE-21™ campaign was run from October 2023 to February 2024. The ICE-21™ campaign
commenced with a series of parametric tests designed to determine Key Performance Indicators
(KPIs) related to ICE-21™. These tests included flue gas turndown, flue gas temperature turndown,
and optimization of the liquid to gas ratio (L/G) to minimize the specific reboiler duty (SRD).
Parametric testing was followed by a long-term steady-state test intended to assess the
degradation and performance of ICE-21™ over prolonged periods. ION switched to 24/7 operations
for the long-term testing to better simulate real-world conditions.

The ICE-31® campaign began in February 2024 and ran until February 2025. Parametric testing was
run from February to July 2024. Tests included the same ones performed for ICE-21™ with the
addition of high-pressure stripper operations. Evaluation of the performance of ICE-31® at these
different conditions is critical for understanding and predicting behavior at a commercial scale
where flue gas conditions can vary significantly depending on the required load on the power plant
during non-standard demand hours. Long-term steady-state testing also included activated carbon
adsorption filtration for ICE-31®. Long-term solvent performance is another critical learning in

3 Project Summary
3.1 Statement of Project Objectives
The overall objective of this project was to empirically demonstrate the low capital and operating
costs of ION’s transformational ICE-31® solvent, featuring revolutionary stability technology, on a
1 MWe slipstream flue gas from Calpine’s LMEC, a commercially dispatched NGCC power plant.
To accomplish this, the project team designed, constructed, and operated an engineering-scale
CO2 capture pilot system that captured 10 tpd. This project served as a vital step in piloting an ION-
optimized design plant with actual NGCC flue gas prior to design, engineering, and construction of
a commercial-scale CO2 capture plant.

This project utilized a modular design and fabrication approach to deliver the engineering-scale
system and was designed to yield a CO2 product flow with >95% purity that was suitable for
compression and dehydration into a CO2 pipeline. The CO2 capture process was optimized to take
full advantage of the benefits provided by the 3rd generation ICE-31® solvent in combination with
other process improvements, all of which were derived through a process-intensification design
philosophy focused on NGCC flue gas. The benefits of this holistic approach included a smaller
physical plant, reduced energy requirements, improved CO2 product quality, less solvent
degradation, lower emissions, lower water usage, less maintenance, and lower capital costs.

Work for this project was completed in three budget periods. BP1, the design phase, was
completed as of September 15, 2021. Goals for BP1 included Process Design Basis, Process
Design Package, Cost Estimate, Balance of Plant Design Package, Subcontractor Selection, and
Permitting. BP2, the construction phase was completed as of September 15, 2023. Goals for BP2
included the balance of plant host site preparation, modular plant fabrication, installation, and
commissioning. In BP3, the testing phase was completed on February 28, 2025. Goals for Budget
Period 3 included testing with MEA, ICE-21™, and ICE-31®, data analysis, and final reporting.

3.2 Scope of Work
3.2.1 Budget Period 1 - Design Phase
In this phase, ION designed the 10 tpd CO2 capture system and the BOP systems needed to
support the 1 MWe engineering scale capture system at the NGCC facility. Additionally, during this
phase of the project, a HAZOP study was conducted for the engineering scale system, which
helped shape the detailed design. The phase concluded with design packages, a fixed price cost to
be compared against the budget, and an executed Host Site Agreement.

3.2.2 Budget Period 2 - Construction Phase
This phase encompassed the procurement of necessary equipment and materials for both the BOP
and CO2 capture systems. Upon receipt of the above, the BOP and CO2 capture systems were
fabricated according to approved designs. The control software was developed and made ready for
Factory Acceptance Testing once the construction activities were completed. ION also completed

ION Clean Energy 6

solvent procurement and Test Plan preparation. This phase concluded with the transportation of
the CO2 capture system to the host site, the fabrication of the necessary BOP tie-ins, and the Site
Acceptance Testing (SAT) to verify operations.

3.2.3 Budget Period 3 - Testing Phase
This phase commenced with the final shakedown testing of the engineering-scale system, followed
by baselining the system against the 1st generation MEA solvent. ION then ran its 2nd generation
solvent (ICE-21™) on the engineering scale system for baseline results of an advanced
solvent before switching to ICE-31®. Once the ICE-31® solvent was in the system, the test plan
involved the same baseline parametric testing performed on the previous solvents for a direct
comparison, before moving on to a long-term testing strategy to evaluate the solvent lifetime under
continuous cycling of operations required to follow the NGCC system. The purpose of this long-
term testing was to evaluate the solvent performance for a long enough duration to fully
understand the interactions and degradation pathways of the solvent and flue gas. ION maintained
the solvent and byproduct concentration through solvent analysis, solvent dosing, and continuous
slipstream reclaiming throughout the test campaign to ensure consistent performance and
to determine an accurate makeup rate for the solvent.

8 Budget Period 3 – Methodology for Operations and Analysis
8.1 Enterprise Operations

 Figure 8-1: Enterprise Process Flow Diagram

The flue gas is first contacted directly with cooling water in the Direct Contact Cooler (DCC) where
it is cooled to 80-120 °F to optimize capture performance (Figure 8-1). The flue gas is a slipstream
from the parent plant that contains 4-5.2% CO2 depending on the burn rate and duct burner firing of
the parent plant. The L/G ratio is adjusted to maintain capture efficiency when the inlet CO2
concentration changes. The flue gas then flows into the absorber at 11,000-15,000 lb/hr, where
lean solvent flows down the column, absorbing CO2 from the flue gas. Due to the exothermic
nature of CO2 absorption, the flue gas is significantly warmer at the top of the column. Thus, a two-
stage water wash is used to cool the flue gas and restore the water balance in the system while
also mitigating emissions due to solvent vapors.

Exiting the bottom of the absorber, the CO2-rich solvent flows through the lean-rich cross
exchanger where it exchanges heat with the lean solvent exiting the regenerator. A portion of the
rich solvent bypasses the cross exchanger, remaining relatively cold as it enters the regenerator to
optimize steam usage. The rich solvent then enters the top of the regenerator and flows down the
column, releasing CO2 and absorbing water vapor while CO2 and water vapor from the reboiler flow
up the column. The now semi-lean solvent is passed through the once-through thermosiphon

ION Clean Energy 21

reboiler, which utilizes steam to heat the solvent and releases CO2 to achieve lean loading. The
lean solvent is then recirculated back to the absorber through the cross-exchanger for continuous
CO2 capture. The captured CO2 enters the condenser where water is condensed, and the resulting
stream is greater than 99% CO2. In a commercial system, the captured CO2 would be fed to a
compressor to reach appropriate pressure and composition specifications for pipeline transport or
underground storage. However, at this pilot facility, the CO2 released out of the stripper is simply
vented to atmosphere.

8.2 Gas Analysis
Initially, ION installed a Horiba CO2 analyzer on the inlet flue gas to measure the inlet CO2
concentration. The Horiba consists of a sampling unit along with two analyzer units, one for low
range CO2 (0-2000 ppm) and one for high range CO2 (2000 ppm-10 vol%). The sampling unit
contains a chiller to knock out water from the gas stream. ION also installed two water knockout
pots upstream of the Horiba to handle the high-water content in the flue gas. The Horiba outputs a
current between four and twenty milliamps (mA) that represents the concentration of CO2 in the
sample gas. ION calibrated the instrument from zero to five percent CO2 with gas cylinders. ION
also calculated the inlet CO2 using the outlet oxygen concentration from the Gasmet. The inlet
oxygen can be calculated based on the inlet and outlet flue gas mass flow rates and the inlet and
outlet flue gas water content. The inlet CO2 can then be calculated from the relationship between
inlet oxygen and inlet CO2 determined from Unit 1 RATA testing results. Both the Horiba and
Gasmet data confirmed inlet CO2 concentrations within the error of the analyzers. ION typically
used the Gasmet data for performing system-wide calculations as it was more responsive and
accessible.

On the outlet flue gas, ION installed a Gasmet FTIR Portable Gas Analyzer to continuously measure
CO2, water content, ammonia, formaldehyde, and other VOC emissions. The analyzer has a
separate oxygen sensor as oxygen is not an IR active species. The FTIR came pre-calibrated, but
reference spectra were added as needed for specialty component analysis. ION also added
additional CO2 reference spectra at lower concentrations (down to 100 ppm) that improved the
readings from the Gasmet. In addition, Gasmet Technologies was able to provide ION with various
reference spectra for MEA.

In May of 2024, ION noticed a significant drift in the inlet CO2 concentration from the Gasmet O2
measurements. Thus, ION requested that Calpine report the O2 concentration of the inlet flue gas,
which was measured by Calpine and reported to ION the next day. ION used the same relationship
between inlet O2 and CO2 that had previously been determined from the LMEC RATA testing data.


ION Clean Energy 22

With the inlet CO2 concentration now calculated from Calpine data, ION moved the Horiba
sampling point to the outlet flue gas. The CO2 concentration in the outlet flue gas was then
measured and stored using the Horiba CO2 analyzer.

Moving the Horiba to the outlet flue gas allowed ION to have continuous, reliable
CO2 concentration data for both the inlet and outlet flue gas streams.

ION also replaced the Gasmet FTIR, which was not sensitive enough to measure ppb-levels of

8.3 Solvent Analysis
Solvent samples were shipped from ENTP to ION’s laboratory in Boulder, CO. Samples of the rich
and lean solvent were taken twice a day at 12-hour increments (one early morning sample and one
afternoon sample). Samples were refrigerated until shipment and again refrigerated in ION’s
laboratory. Solvent water content was measured using Karl-Fischer titration. CO2 loading was
measured using total inorganic carbon (TIC) analysis, main solvent components were measured
using gas chromatography with mass spectrometry (GC-MS), and trace heat stable salts were
measured using ion chromatography (IC). All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure
precise analysis and demonstrate high reproducibility.

8.4 Specific Reboiler Duty
Specific Reboiler Duty (heat input per unit of CO2 captured) is calculated based on the condensate
flow rate out of the reboiler. ION chose to use the condensate flow rate instead of the steam flow
rate since the condensate flowmeter can be externally calibrated gravimetrically. The calibration
utilized 140 °F water in the condensate tank and at various valve positions that matched the normal
operating range of the condensate flowmeter (Figure 8-2)

ION found the flowmeter to be very accurate in the operating range, with the flow calculated from
the scale about 1.5% higher than the flowmeter readings. However, since the calibration was done
at 140 °F instead of the normal operating temperature of 210 °F, the operating flow needed to be
corrected for temperature. To do this, ION defined the flow determined from the scale reading (the
true flow) as proportional to the flowmeter reading (which is analogous to the velocity being
measured by the flowmeter). ION then defined the proportionality constant as the product of the
density of the stream and some constant k, where the proportionality constant is equal to the slope
of the line created when graphing scale flow versus actual flow (which is where the 1.5% comes
from). Using this relationship, the true flow at 210 °F is thus equal to the ratio of density at 210 °F
and 140 °F times the flowmeter reading times 1.5%.

8.5 Heat Loss
To calculate the system heat loss, ION operated the system with no CO2 produced from the
stripper. This operating case is performed at a much lower reboiler temperature than the standard
operating case (207 °F vs 255-258 °F) because the objective is to determine how much heat is lost
from the solvent to the ambient environment. Thus, any CO2 released from the solvent will

ION Clean Energy 25

influence the heat loss calculation because the heat that is released from the solvent will be
counted in the heat loss term.
The heat loss case was then modeled by matching stream temperatures in the model, and the
model heat duty was compared to the actual duty determined from the steam flow at ENTP to
determine the base heat loss.

However, because the base heat loss case was run at a lower temperature than standard operating
conditions, the driving force for heat transfer from the solvent to the ambient environment is lower.
Thus, the base heat loss was ratioed to actual operating temperatures to determine a heat loss at
normal operating and ambient temperatures. Nominal stripper temperature was 255 °F (397.0 K),
heat loss stripper temperature was 207 °F (370.4 K), and ambient temperature was 70 °F (294.3 K).

9.1 MEA
The MEA campaign at ENTP lasted for approximately 1 month. ION operated for 12 hours per day
from September 28, 2023, to October 17, 2023. ION operated for a total of 250 hours with MEA,
capturing 76 tonnes of CO2.

The goal of the MEA campaign was to determine the baseline performance of a common carbon
capture solvent on the custom built ENTP system. Unfortunately, there was significant foaming in
the stripper during MEA operations, a phenomenon that has been observed during startup of other
MEA campaigns1. ION added anti-foam to the system, but while it initially stopped the foaming, the
effectiveness diminished over time. As a result, it was difficult to determine accurate SRDs for MEA
because the foam prevented a significant amount of heat transfer in the stripper. Thus, ION did not
observe the typical relationship between capture efficiency and SRD where the duty increases with
increasing capture efficiency. There was also no way to account for foaming in the postprocessing
of the data.

Capture efficiency with MEA ranged from approximately 60 to 90%. SRDs ranged from 4.5 to 6.7
GJ/tCO2. The liquid flow to gas flow ratio (L/G) was typically above 1. The high L/G ratio during MEA
operations may have also contributed to the poor performance observed with MEA as the ENTP
system is designed specifically to ION solvent specifications, which operate at significantly lower
L/G ratios due to their higher solvent capacity compared to MEA.

ION sampled the MEA solvent daily to measure solvent degradation rates and decomposition
product buildup. Solvent samples were sent to ION’s Boulder laboratory to undergo various
analyses. The graph of the MEA lean side composition closure is shown below in Figure 9-1. The
lean side composition closure refers to the closure in the mass balance of the various components
in the lean solvent. ION measures the concentration of various compounds in the solvent,
including water, CO2, and solvent components. The independently determined concentrations are
then summed. Ideally, the sum is 100%, which means that all mass in the solvent is accounted for.
When the solvent begins to degrade, the sum is less than 100%, and the missing mass can be
attributed to liquid degradation products. A picture of the MEA samples over the first week of
operations is also included in Figure 9-2. Degradation products are often longer-chain molecules
that cause color changes in the solvent.

9.2    ICE-21™
ION operated for 1167 hours from October 2023 to February 2024 with ICE-21™. From October
2023 to December 2023, ION performed 350 hours of parametric testing. ION switched to
continuous 24-hour operations in December 2023. Following parametric testing, ION performed
817 hours of long-term testing until February 2024. The ENTP plant was shut down over
Thanksgiving for a power plant outage. The ENTP plant was also shut down during the second half
of December 2023 due to a malfunctioning variable frequency drive (VFD) on the flue gas blower.

All setpoints were run at water neutral flue gas temperatures because the DCC blowdown was not
permitted for discharge. During standard operations, the DCC condenses water out of the flue gas
stream to cool it to the setpoint, and the condensed water is blown down out of the DCC. However,
with no DCC blowdown, the flue gas could only be cooled to its dew point, meaning that the flue
gas entered the absorber at high temperatures (>115 °F) compared to the design point (95 °F). At
lower flue gas temperatures, the equilibrium for CO2 absorption into the solvent is more favorable
as CO2 absorption is an exothermic reaction. In addition, the lower flue gas temperature results in
lower rich solvent temperatures, thus more heat can be recovered in the lean rich heat exchanger,
improving the overall efficiency of the process and lowering SRD. ION observed minimal color
change in the ICE-21™ solvent during parametric testing. In Figure 9-3 below, the solvent color is
shown during the second week of ICE-21™ operations. There is no discernible color change in the
samples.

9.3.1 Optimal L/G at 95% Capture

ION varied the L/G ratio at 95-96% CO2 capture to determine optimal performance of the solvent.
L/G is calculated based on the lean solvent flow rate and the flue gas flow rate into the absorber.
For each setpoint, ION modeled the steam duty needed for 95% capture prior to changing
operating conditions. The actual ENTP steam load was then set at a determined flow rate, and the
capture efficiency was allowed to reach a steady state. Due to the high accuracy of the ProTreat®
model, the resulting capture efficiency at ENTP was almost always within the range of the target
capture rate, allowing relatively smooth and quick transitions between setpoints. The optimal lean
loading is a balance between increasing the carrying capacity of the solvent to limit sensible heat
loss in the lean/rich cross exchanger and increasing lean loading in the stripper bottoms to better
utilize stripping steam.

ION found the optimal performance to be at 0.71 lb/lb, however the SRD remains within 5% of the
minimum in the range of 0.63 to 0.87 lb/lb. This optimal range is reflective of both the ProTreat®
modelling predictions and the design case of the ENTP facility.

9.3.2 SRD Increase with Capture Efficiency
ION varied the capture efficiency at ENTP while choosing optimal L/G ratios to determine minimum
SRD performance at each capture efficiency. The overall impact of the fast kinetics and favorable
thermodynamics of ION’s ICE-31® solvent demonstrate that there is approximately a 6% SRD
penalty when increasing capture from 90% to 98%. The impact on SRD going from 89% capture to
96% capture is minimal (approximately 1.8% increase), indicating that the solvent is reaching a
similar rich loading at these capture efficiencies. However, beyond 96% capture, the solvent did
not reach its optimal rich loading, leading to an increase in SRD at very high capture efficiencies.
With 25% additional absorber packing, ION expects it would be able to capture up to approximately
99% of the CO2 in the flue gas with minimal penalty in SRD.

9.3.3 Long Term Capture Efficiency

ION operated for approximately 2900 hours with an average capture efficiency of 95.8%. In Figure

9.3.5    ICE-31® Emissions
ION continuously monitored solvent emissions (emissions of ICE-31® component(s)) in the outlet
flue gas using a PTR-TOF during the ICE-31® campaign. The PTR-TOF also measured emissions of
ammonia, formaldehyde, and acetaldehyde. Additionally, emissions were analyzed through
extractive sampling performed by a third party, Montrose Environmental. The sampling point was
located on the outlet of the absorber stack, so the outlet emissions measurements were inclusive
of any emissions originating from the base plant.

In addition to solvent emissions, the PTR-TOF also measured other hazardous air pollutants,
including ammonia, formaldehyde, and acetaldehyde. As ammonia and formaldehyde can be
generated in the natural gas turbine, continuous analysis on the inlet flue gas is required to
determine what portion of these emissions is passed through from the inlet flue gas and what
portion is generated by the capture process. However, ION did not have continuous analysis on the
inlet flue gas and thus could not determine the source of these emissions. For the last month of
testing with an aged solvent, emissions of acetaldehyde, the only detected Hazardous Air Pollutant
(HAP), were generally below 2 to 3 ppbv with the highest reading around 7 ppbv. Other VOC
emissions associated with the solvent were generally below 1 ppbv with the highest reading around
3 ppbv. These results demonstrate the excellent emissions performance that can be achieved with
ICE-31® and NGCC flue gas in an ION-designed pilot facility.

Ammonia emissions measured by the PTR-TOF were approximately 1 ppmv, with dips down to
approximately 0.8 ppmv and spikes up to approximately 10 ppmv.

9.3.6 CO2 Product Analysis
ION sampled the CO2 product stream five separate times using sample kits rented from Airborne
Labs International. The kits consist of a sample system and bag that were shipped to Airborne Labs
for analysis. The sample bag was purged at least three times before taking the actual sample. There
was no NOx or SOx detected in any of the samples above the detection limit (0.5 ppmv). The total
non-methane hydrocarbons (TNMHC) value (2 - 3.5 ppmv as CH4) included solvent amine(s) and
there was no breakdown for what specific hydrocarbons or amines were detected. Stripper
overhead temperature did not appear to be correlated to product purity.

9.3.9 Activated Carbon Filtration

During the steady-state portion of the ICE-31® campaign, the solvent was continuously treated with
activated carbon. Each batch of activated carbon was loaded onto the lean side of the solvent
recirculation stream. A rinsing and de-fining process was carried out prior to bringing the activated
carbon on-line. The spent carbon was also rinsed with water prior to unloading to return any
unabsorbed solvent to the main solvent circulation loop. A sample of the spent carbon passed
acceptance testing by the supplier and is not hazardous. ION received a Carbon Acceptance
Number (CAN) under which spent carbon can be sent back to the supplier on a large-scale project.
For the ENTP project, the spent activated carbon was not returned to the supplier and was
disposed of by an appropriate industrial waste disposal service provider.

10 Environmental Health and Safety
The safety and health of both personnel and the environment are paramount to ION. To that end,
ION performed safety assessments throughout the ENTP test campaign including a Pre-Startup
Safety Review, regular safety audits, emergency equipment testing, and rigorous operator training.
Initially, ION performed an EH&S assessment to determine the environmental compatibility of ICE-
31® and to identify safe procedures surrounding both the process and the materials under
consideration. These were carried forward through the pilot campaign to ensure environmental and
safety controls were in place when dealing with hazardous chemicals.

The EH&S guidelines were incorporated into operational procedures and safely demonstrated
during ION’s testing at ENTP. The hazards associated with ION’s technology were eliminated or
minimized throughout the campaign, resulting in zero lost-work safety incidences. This approach
provides a strong basis for EH&S risk management for future scale-up and commercialization of
ION’s solvent capture technology. As a result of this EH&S assessment, ION is confident in the safe
use of its solvent technology.

11 Techno-Economic Analysis
ION was tasked with developing a Techno-Economic Assessment based on the Bituminous
Baseline Study (BBS) cases developed by DOE/NETL. S&L was engaged by ION to prepare the TEA
using performance and process design information specific to the ICE-31® solvent technology. This
TEA was originally prepared in 2020 and has been revised based on operational data of the pilot
facility, including key learnings identified during the DE-FE0031950 project.

The purpose of the TEA is to evaluate the economic feasibility of implementing the ION carbon
capture system at a greenfield power plant equipped with a natural gas combustion turbine, heat
recovery steam generator (HRSG), and steam turbine. Capital costs, operating and maintenance
(O&M) costs, and the cost of electricity (COE) were prepared by the Department of Energy (DOE)
and National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) and published in the BBS in 2019 revision (Rev
4). The objective of the BBS case studies is to determine the comparative costs of electricity at
various types of green-field power facilities. Case 31A is reflective of an F-class NGCC facility
without a carbon capture system; Case 31B is reflective of an F-class NGCC facility utilizing carbon
capture. The carbon capture system chosen for the BBS Case 31B is the Shell Cansolv system,
which is considered a state-of-the-art full-scale application. The ION TEA compares the
incremental increase in COE and cost of capture (in $/tCO2) for the ION system based on the
comparative costs estimated by the DOE between BBS Case 31A to BBS Case 31B. The footprint of
the capture plant, including its balance of plant equipment, is approximately equivalent to the base
NGCC power plant.

This economic evaluation was conducted according to the design basis laid out in the BBS Case
31B to the best extent possible. However, several technical and operational parameters within this
TEA for ION’s carbon capture system are based on the results of the pilot system using ION’s
proprietary advanced solvent, ICE-31®. Furthermore, costs and design elements from a recent
front-end engineering and design (FEED) study were adjusted for the NGCC application. The capital
costs estimate is broken into bare erected costs, total plant cost, and total overnight cost. O&M
costs are broken into variable and fixed O&M. Ultimately the annualized capital costs are
combined with the O&M costs to determine the lifecycle cost of the facility. All costs are reported
in December 2018 dollars. The ION carbon capture TEA shows that:

 • Capital expenditures are 27% lower compared to Case 31B
 • O&M expenditures are 8% lower compared to Case 31B
 • COE is $61.4/MWh, 13% lower compared to Case 31B
 • Cost of capture is $48.7/tCO2, 39% lower compared to Case 31B

12 Conclusion
ION completed a 16-month campaign at its custom-built carbon capture facility known as the
Enterprise Pilot. Over three different solvent campaigns, ION operated for over 7500 hours on a flue
gas slipstream from a market-adaptive NGCC gas turbine (4-5.2% CO2).

The campaign began with 250 hours of parametric testing with MEA to baseline the system with an
industry standard solvent. ION achieved capture efficiencies as high as 89% and captured 76 tCO2
with MEA.

ION performed 350 hours of parametric testing with its ICE-21™ solvent and this was followed by
817 hours of long-term testing. ION achieved capture efficiencies up to 99% with ICE-21™.

ION performed over 2250 hours of parametric testing and nearly 4000 hours of long-term testing
with ICE-31®, capturing ~2500 tCO2. ION demonstrated capture efficiencies as high as 99.4% with
ICE-31®, at which point the outlet flue gas contains a lower concentration of CO2 than ambient air
(~400 ppm). ICE-31® demonstrated excellent solvent stability in the high-oxygen environment that
results from the combustion of NGCC flue gas. This was further shown through the extremely low
emissions of ICE-31®.

Project Enterprise showcased the exceptional qualities of ION capture solvents and demonstrated
ION’s deep understanding of amine-based carbon capture and ability to apply that knowledge in a
functioning industrial setting.

The results confirm and expand ION’s expectations that ICE-31® is an exceptional solvent for flue
gases with high oxidative potential, such as NGCC facilities like LMEC. The results from the ENTP
pilot further demonstrate the readiness of ICE-31® as a viable, commercially deployable carbon
capture technology.
