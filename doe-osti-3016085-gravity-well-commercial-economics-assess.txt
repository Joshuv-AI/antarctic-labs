Executive Summary
This report serves as the final technical report for the CRADA #CRD-22-23269 between NREL
and Renewell Energy. This report summarizes work from two tasks affiliated with the
CRADA—the Gravity Well Revenue Study and the Gravity Well Future Cost Study. The
Gravity Well Revenue Study analyzed the wholesale electricity market and respective potential
revenues that are available based on services that Gravity Wells can provide, including power
capacity, energy capacity, and ancillary services (e.g., frequency/voltage regulation). The
Gravity Well Future Cost Study estimated Gravity Well future costs based on the current
prototype design bill of materials. Future cost prediction used both a learning curve approach
that considers inter- and intra-project learnings and economies of scale.

Gravity Well Revenue Study
In the Gravity Well Revenue Study, we evaluated the potential revenue from Gravity Well
energy storage using historical energy-only electricity prices, forward-looking projections of
hourly electricity prices, and actual reported revenue. This analysis examines the impact of
storage duration and round-trip efficiency, as well as the location of the storage, on storage
revenue within the current and projected U.S. power system.

Figure ES-1 illustrates the modeled revenue for a 1-megawatt (MW) storage system in seven
market regions with durations ranging from 1 hour to 12 hours using historical pricing data. The
historical observations cover hourly energy prices of more than 500 price nodes for each market
region from 2017 to 2021. The results indicate the revenues consistently increase with duration,
though the marginal value of longer duration declines as duration grows. For example, in 2021,
the median revenue increases from $24.7/kW to $63.0/kW with increasing system duration from
1 hour to 6 hours, whereas increasing system duration from 6 hours to 12 hours increases median
revenue by around $6/kW. Additionally, the revenue ranges also widen with increased durations
across years and markets, indicating that longer durations have a greater variation in value than
shorter durations. For example, in 2021, the revenue range for the system with a 12-hour
duration is between $37 and $320/kW, while the range for 1-hour storage is between $11 and
$137/kW.

Figure ES-2 shows the revenue estimation for a 1 MW storage system in seven market regions
with durations from 1 hour to 12 hours using forward-looking electricity prices. The price data
cover hourly energy, operating reserve, and planning reserve prices for 134 regions under 10
different future scenarios of the electricity system. Similar to historical analysis, the revenues
increase with duration, whereas the marginal value of longer duration declines as duration grows.
Moreover, the range of revenue depends on the system’s operational location and the changing
electricity generation mix in future years. For example, in 2050, the revenue range for 2-hour
batteries in CAISO is between $103 and $207/kW, while the revenue range for the same duration
in ERCOT is between $67 and $106/kW. Also, the systems have the highest revenue under
100% decarbonization by 2035, while they have the lowest revenue in the low-natural-gas-price
or low-renewable-energy-cost scenarios.

We also investigated the impact of round-trip efficiency on storage revenue. We found that the
relationship between storage revenue and round-trip efficiency is nonlinear. The value of
improved round-trip efficiency declines as round-trip efficiency increases.

Gravity Well Future Cost Study
In the Gravity Well Future Cost Study, we applied learning curves to predict the future cost
trajectory of Gravity Wells (GrWs). Two types of analysis were implemented. The first was a
bottom-up analysis that used historical learning rates for cost components, such as motors and
gearboxes, and cost categories (e.g., engineering and design) to determine the learning-by-doing-
based single-factor learning curve. The single-factor learning curve (SFLC) expresses the
relationship between the cost of GrW and the number of units deployed (or the cumulative
capacity). In the second analysis, we predicted future GrW costs via a top-down approach. This
approach accounts for historical cost trends in other renewable energy and storage technologies,
which have similarities with GrWs. Using a multi-factor learning curve that accounts for both
intrinsic (cumulative capacity) and extrinsic (the elasticity in the price of steel) factors, we
estimated the future cost of GrWs.

Figure ES-3 shows the resulting learning curves from the bottom-up analysis for the low,
moderate, and high learning rate (LR) scenarios. The learning curves depict normalized costs
decreasing from 100% to 19% in the low LR scenario, 13% in the moderate LR scenario, and
10% in the high LR scenario by Year 10 (i.e., after 33,569 GrW units are deployed). The
resulting overall learning rates are 10%, 12%, and 14% for the low, moderate, and high learning
rate scenarios, respectively. The greatest drop (59% decrease) in cost is expected to come from
purchased parts, revealing the substantial influence of economies of scale on potential cost
decline.

Introduction
As non-baseload energy sources like wind and solar become more prevalent, the need has surged
for efficient energy storage systems to stabilize supply and demand. Gravity-based energy
storage, which relies on moving heavy (solid or fluid) masses between vertical distances to store
and release energy, is emerging as a viable solution for both diurnal and long-duration energy
storage. This technology is considered a sustainable alternative to other storage methods, with a
focus on long-term reliability and lower environmental impact. Although pumped storage
hydropower (PSH) remains the dominant gravity-based storage technology globally, it requires
specific geographical conditions (like mountainous regions) and extensive water resources. Other
above-ground systems that utilize purpose-built cranes have been developed and commercially
demonstrated. However, they require a sizeable surface footprint and are subject to weather
variability. This creates a gap for subsurface gravity storage, which can be implemented using
existing below-ground assets, such as oil and gas (O&G) wells and abandoned mine shafts, with
minimal impacts to existing land use.

An innovative approach to fill this gap is the conversion of inactive (i.e., non-producing and
unplugged) oil and gas (O&G) wells into “Gravity Wells” (GrWs). Renewell Energy is at the
forefront of GrW technology development and is collaborating with the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) on prototype design and testing (Figure 1). By using the vertical
space within inactive wells, GrWs can store potential energy by lifting multi-ton weights. The
stored energy can then be delivered to the grid or behind the meter during a lowering operation
by conversion of the kinetic energy of the moving weight into electrical energy via a surface
bidirectional motor. In GrWs, the well is sealed above the production zone, isolating it from the
reservoir so that no reservoir fluids can flow into the well. This unique reuse for storage could
give a second economic life to millions of inactive wells in the United States while also actively
mitigating fugitive greenhouse gas emissions.

At present, the non-PSH gravity energy storage market is at a relatively nascent stage, and there
are few active players globally. Most of the technology vendors in this market are still
developing their products, testing prototypes, or have just achieved commercial-scale
demonstration. Therefore, current technology readiness levels (TRLs) are between 4 and 8.
Major players in this market are Energy Vault, Gravitricity, Renewell Energy, Gravity Power,
Quidnet Energy, Sage Geosystems, Advanced Rail Energy Storage (ARES), and Gravity Storage
(formerly Heindl Energy) (Table 1). The subsurface gravity storage market is also at a very
nascent stage (TRL 4 to 7). Although some technologies have been developed and piloted
(especially those that rely on reservoir geomechanics), they have not been commercially
demonstrated at scale. Renewell is currently the sole active player in repurposing O&G wells for
gravity storage without interacting with the reservoir. Their technology currently offers reliable
short-duration GrWs that can be readily combined and operated as flexible short- and long-
duration energy storage assets within a single field for both behind-the-meter and grid-connected
applications.

 14

Renewell has established key partnerships in California, where field testing of GrW prototypes is
ongoing. The success of these tests could draw substantial interest from the energy industry and
accelerate the widespread adoption of subsurface gravity-based storage. This innovation taps into
California’s abundant non-producing wells and aligns with the state’s ambitious electricity goals.
Given the state’s push for energy storage to complement intermittent renewable energy
generation, GrW technology could play a pivotal role in California’s energy mix while
addressing the challenge of idle well proliferation. Renewell aims to install over 30,000 GrW
units by 2034, positioning itself at the forefront of the U.S. gravity storage industry.

1.1 CRADA Objectives
Renewell Energy and the NREL have been partnering on the technology development and
demonstration of the Gravity Well over the past 27 months, through the OPEN 2021 grant
funding from the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), Advanced Research Projects Agency-
Energy (ARPA-E)1. NREL’s efforts in that project have been mostly on the design and
development of the electromechanical drivetrain and well data compilation and analysis. This
Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA), funded by the DOE Advanced
Materials and Manufacturing Technologies Office, has supported Renewell’s efforts to
accurately model the economics of their technology in a variety of scenarios. These
investigations analyze revenue potential through all relevant energy storage market mechanisms
and the cost to purchase/build and maintain the equipment that accesses these markets.

1.2 Project Tasks
Two main project tasks were defined in the CRADA: (a) the Gravity Well Revenue Study (Task
1) and (b) the Gravity Well Future Cost Study (Task 2). Task 1 entails a study of the wholesale
electricity market and respective potential revenues that are available based on services that
GrWs can provide, including power capacity, energy capacity, and ancillary services (e.g.,
frequency/voltage regulation). Task 2 involves the estimation of Gravity Well Future Costs
based on the current prototype design BOM. Future cost prediction will be via both a learning
curve approach that considers inter- and intra-project learnings and economies of scale.

Before 2010, nearly all energy storage in the U.S. was PSH. In the 2010s, short-duration (<1-
hour) battery storage emerged to provide ancillary services. With the recent cost declines in
lithium-ion batteries, large-scale lithium-ion systems have been deployed, with most new
deployments having 4 hours of discharge duration since they provide energy and capacity
services (Denholm et al., 2023). The power capacity of utility-scale battery storage systems has
increased from 491 MW to 8,842 MW in the last two years (EIA, 2022). Moreover, 32,466 MW
of capacity is planned to be in operation by 2028 (EIA, 2023b).

Interest in storage development can be gauged in part by storage interconnection requests. An
interconnection request occurs when a project applies for connection to the transmission system.

Long-term planning models of the U.S. power system show storage is likely to continue to grow
over time. For example, the Annual Energy Outlook 2023 projects that storage capacity in 2050
will range from 64 GW to 348 GW (EIA, 2023a), and the 2023 Standard Scenarios project 150
to 600 GW of storage by 2050 (Gagnon et al., 2024).

For storage systems to continue to be deployed, they will need sufficient revenue to offset the
cost of the storage system. That revenue can come from a variety of sources and take many
forms (Mastropietro et al., 2024). For this work, we evaluate the potential revenue from energy
storage using historical energy prices, forward-looking projections of hourly energy prices, and
historical reported revenue. This analysis helps show the trends, revenue ranges, and locational
elements of storage revenue in the current and projected U.S. power system.

2.1 Methodology
This study examines the potential revenue of energy storage systems, using both historical
reported revenue data and price-taker analysis of historical and projected future prices. To
achieve this, we collected price and revenue data and implemented a price-taker model to
determine annual revenue, which is explained in the following sections.

 17

2.1.1 Data Collection and System Assumptions

To analyze reported revenue, we used data reported by the Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC). FERC provides quarterly total transaction charges in its Electric Quarterly
Reports (EQR),3 categorized by product types such as energy, capacity, or regulation and
frequency response. The data are provided at an aggregated level without specific information
about the types of generators the reporting data cover. However, to capture power plants that are
likely to be storage facilities, we extracted any company with “energy storage” in the name,
which resulted in 29 facilities. We cross-checked these company names with the U.S. Energy
Information Administration’s (EIA’s) preliminary monthly electric generator inventory to obtain
location and energy and power capacities for the specific facilities. Facilities’ durations are
calculated based on the ratio of power capacity to energy capacity.

For price-taker analysis using historical energy prices, we used real-time hourly energy price
data from the ABB Locational Marginal Pricing database. The data covers energy prices from
2017 to 2021 across multiple nodes in seven market regions: California Independent System
Operator (CAISO), Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), Midcontinent Independent
System Operator (MISO), Independent System Operator of New England (ISO-NE), New York
Independent System Operator (NYISO), Pennsylvania-New Jersey-Maryland (PJM), and
Southwest Power Pool (SPP). To manage data volume and price-taker model runs, we limited the
number of nodes in each market region to 500. In regions with fewer than 500 nodes, we used
the full set of available nodes. For regions with more than 500 nodes, we applied a selection
process: We first calculated statistical values (maximum, minimum, average, and standard
deviation) for each price node over five years, removed nodes with identical statistical values,
and removed nodes that were missing many observations. If the remaining nodes still exceeded
500, we selected the nodes that had maximum and minimum absolute price points, the maximum
and minimum average prices, and the maximum and minimum standard deviations. The
remaining nodes were randomly chosen until we had a total of 500 for that market region.

Importantly, these historical prices reflect the price formation rules in each market region and are
only the energy price. If a market region also has a capacity market or bilateral capacity
payments, that revenue would not be captured in the price-taker analysis using those historical
prices.

For forward-looking price-taker analysis, we obtained price data from the Cambium Database
(Gagnon et al., 2023). The data contain hourly energy, operating reserve, and planning reserve
prices for 134 model regions of the contiguous United States. We extracted data for 4 years—
2026, 2030, 2040, and 2050—under 10 scenarios. These 134 model regions are matched with 9
regions as shown in Figure 2. The Cambium scenarios include factors such as Inflation
Reduction Act incentives, alternative natural gas prices (low and high natural gas prices),
alternative renewable energy costs (low and high renewable energy costs), carbon policies (95%
reduction in CO2 emissions by 2050 or net zero emissions by 2035), and electrification. The
average price is highest under the net-zero emissions by 2035 and lowest under the low-natural-
gas-price scenario.

2.1.2 Modeling Framework

The price-taker model provides operational decisions for energy storage systems by considering
wholesale electricity prices over a given period. This model assumes that the system does not
influence market prices. As a result, the price-taker model is commonly used in revenue analysis
for single battery systems. In the literature, price-taker models account for the system’s
characteristics, such as energy and power capacity, while market participation of storage can
vary. For example, some models consider only energy market participation (Adebayo et al.,
2018; Arcos-Vargas et al., 2020; Brijs et al., 2019; Connolly et al., 2011; McConnell et al., 2015;
Sioshansi et al., 2009; Zafirakis et al., 2016), while others include ancillary market participation
as well (Berrada et al., 2016; Chazarra et al., 2014; Drury et al., 2011; Guerra Fernandez et al.,
2020; Kazempour et al., 2009; Moreno et al., 2015; Pinto et al., 2011; Staffell & Rustomji, 2016;
Yu & Foggo, 2017).

In this study, we use the Revenue, Operation, and Device Optimization (RODeO) model, a price-
taker revenue optimization model to maximize the net revenue for storage in a given region and
period (Guerra Fernandez et al., 2020). Using hourly electricity market data and technology
characteristics, RODeO determines the optimal hourly charging and discharging schedule and
magnitude with perfect foresight over the course of a year. For instance, Figure 3 shows an
example output from RODeO for a 1-MW, 4-megawatt-hour (MWh) storage system in response
to electricity prices. As shown in the figure, the storage system will charge when prices are
lowest and then discharge when prices are highest to maximize revenue. The storage revenue is
calculated by summing the charging cost and discharging revenue over all hours of the year.
Charging cost is the payment of storage to purchase power from the electricity market, calculated
by multiplying the electricity purchase price with input power. Discharging revenue is the
revenue generated from selling power to the market calculated by multiplying the electricity
purchase price with output power.

For both the historical and forward-looking price-taker analyses, we assumed a 1-MW power
capacity and varied the duration from 1 MWh to 12 MWh with 1-MWh increments. We
normalized revenue results by capacity, so this power capacity assumption was irrelevant. In
addition, we assumed storage round-trip efficiency is 0.75 for historical and forward-looking
revenue analyses. We also conducted sensitivity analysis for round-trip efficiency from 0.35 to
0.85 with 0.10 incrementation and from 0.68 to 0.78 with 0.02 incrementation.

Importantly, because we are taking a price-taker approach, the revenue calculations from
RODeO assume the storage behavior will not impact the electricity price at a given node.

Because of the higher revenues at longer durations, some storage operators might consider
operating their storage device as if it were a longer-duration unit to try to capture some of these
higher revenues. However, unless the operation of the storage impacts the prices, the longer-
duration operation mode would not be able to increase revenue. For example, considering the
average revenue line in Figure 12, the 2-hour revenue amount is $79.93/kW-yr, and the 4-hour

would be operating at half capacity (e.g., a 10 kW/20 kWh device would discharge at 5 kW to
operate for 4 hours). Therefore, it would receive half of the revenue that a 4-hour device
receives, or ½ * $134.74/kW-yr = $67.37/yr. This amount is less than the $79.93/kW-yr it would
receive if it operated as a 2-hour device. Said another way, the slope of the revenue curve (Figure
12) is always declining, which means it is impossible to capture more revenue from operating as
a longer-duration device (again, assuming the storage behavior does not impact the electricity
price).

3 Gravity Well Future Cost Study7
In this study, we used a learning curve analysis to estimate cost reductions and efficiency
improvements over time as technologies are produced and deployed at larger scales. The learning
curve represents a fundamental relationship between cost and capacity increase such that as the
manufacturer produces more of a product, the unit cost of production decreases. The rate of cost
decrease also reduces over time, with a certain cost percent decrease for every doubling of units
produced. In the energy sector, learning curves have been pivotal in tracking the decreasing costs
of technologies like solar photovoltaics (PV), wind turbines, and battery storage. Solar PV and
wind technologies have exhibited significant cost reductions over time due to advances in
manufacturing processes, economies of scale, and technological innovation. Learning curves for
these technologies typically show a progress ratio of around 80%, meaning that costs decrease by
20% for every doubling in capacity. For instance, the price of solar PV modules dropped by 85%
between 2010 and 2020 (Feldman et al., 2021). Energy storage, particularly in the form of
lithium-ion batteries, has followed a similar learning curve. Costs for battery storage systems
have fallen by about 90% since 2010, driven by improvements in battery chemistry, economies
of scale in production, and increased demand due to electric vehicles and grid storage
applications (IEA, 2024). The learning curve for energy storage is crucial in making intermittent
renewable energy sources like solar and wind more reliable by providing flexibility and storage
during periods of overproduction. Similarly, as GrW prototypes progress through testing phases,
learning curve effects are expected to bring down costs. With potentially longer operational
lifespans than lithium-ion batteries, GrWs could become more cost-effective over time.

In the Gravity Well Future Cost Study, we utilized both top-down and bottom-up approaches to
derive the learning rates for GrWs. In the top-down approach, we applied historical learning rates
from other energy generation and storage technologies that are analogues to GrWs. For the
bottom-up approach, we researched historical learning rates for cost categories that comprised
individual components of the GrW based on the bill of materials (BOM) to assess a weighted
average learning rate for the entire GrW unit.

3.1 Bottom-Up Analysis
3.1.1 Methodology

Prototype BOM. The more than 40 capital cost components were categorized into seven cost
categories, namely: engineering and design, sales general and administrative (SG&A), facilities
and equipment, raw materials, purchased parts, manufactured parts, and field assembly. A
learning rate was then assessed for each cost category. The learning curves in this study were
generated by using the single-factor learning rate methodology. Single-factor learning assumes
that cost reductions are primarily driven by the scale of production, such as manufacturing
volume, increased efficiency, standardization, and economies of scale. Therefore, as more GrWs
are deployed, these factors may drive GrW cost reductions. The expression for a single-factor

3.1.2 Results

We estimated the future cost decline of GrWs using the defined learning rates for each cost
category and component represented in Table 3. The resulting learning curves from Year 0 to
Year 10, when the 1st and the 33,569 units are deployed, respectively, for the three learning rate
scenarios are shown in Figure 19. The learning curves show normalized costs decreasing from
100% to 19% in the low LR scenario, 13% in the moderate LR scenario, and 10% in the high LR
scenario after the 33,569 units are deployed. By determining the value of L with Excel-based
power law curve fitting to each curve, we estimated the overall learning rate for each scenario
using the LR =1-2-L relationship. The resulting overall learning rates are 10%, 12%, and 14% for
the low, moderate, and high learning rate scenarios, respectively.

Figure 20 shows the estimated percent cost decline for each cost category between Year 0 (first
unit) and Year 10 (33,569th unit) for the moderate (most likely) scenario. It shows that the
greatest drop in cost would come from purchased parts with a 59% decrease. This is anticipated
to result from improved GrW winch design and increased manufacturing volume. The next
significant drop in cost is expected to emanate from the field assembly with a 19% decrease in
total cost by Year 10. This connotes that as more units get deployed, learning-by-doing will
enable shorter GrW installation timelines, thereby reducing costs.

3.2 Top-Down Analysis
3.2.1 Methodology

The top-down approach applies learning rates from other analogous technologies to estimate the
future cost of GrWs. We endeavored to apply a multi-factor learning curve (MFLC)
methodology for the cost prediction. The SFLC approach in Section 3.1.1 only shows the
relation between cost and cumulative capacity (or, as in this case, the number of deployed units).
It does not account for other factors that may affect technology costs, such as installation
location, resource availability, commodity prices, etc. The MFLC methodology goes beyond the
typical learning-by-doing to account for these additional factors. In this work, we appropriated
the methodology and assumptions defined in the work by Yao et al. (2021) that compared MFLC
across multiple renewable energy technologies. Based on this work, we have determined that
apart from cumulative capacity/installed units (x), the price of steel (Psteel) is an additional
variable that can affect the cost (C) of GrWs. The resulting MFLC equation for GrW is
expressed as:

 log 𝐶(𝑥, 𝑃𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑒𝑙 ) = log 𝑟 + 𝑐 log 𝑥 + 𝛼 log 𝑃𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑒𝑙 (2)

where α is the elasticity index of the steel price, c is the elasticity index for cumulative capacity
(similar to L in Equation (1)) and r is the remaining factor index, which is defined by the
boundary conditions. In this case, in Year 0, r corresponds to the current year’s cost.

We have used assumptions for c primarily from Yao et al. (2021). The value of c for GrWs was
estimated from those of other technologies based on the results from the study by Yao et al.
(2021). These technologies include: (i) PSH, (ii) onshore wind, (iii) offshore wind, and (iv) solar
photovoltaic. We selected PSH because it is the most mature gravity-based storage technology.
Onshore and offshore wind were selected because of their extensive use of motors and gearboxes
(i.e., drivetrain). Solar PV was chosen because it is an inverter-based technology. Table 4 shows
the original elasticity indices and adjusted values of c for each technology. The original values of
the elasticity indices for cumulative capacity were derived from Yao et al. (2021) (offshore wind
and solar PV), Viswanathan et al. (2022) (based on learning rates for PSH), and NREL (2024)
(based on learning rates for land-based wind).

 45

Adjustments were made using similarity factors (0 = no similarity to GrW, 1 = medium
similarity, and 2 = high similarity) and accounting for the influence of technology drivers,
including homogeneity and modularity, technological maturity, capital intensity, production
processes, economies of scale, regulatory and market factors, and research and development
intensity.8 Upward or downward adjustments to the baseline (GrW) relative weights (in Table 5)
with respect to each technology driver were made according to the comparison of each
technology to GrWs. These were used to calculate relative weights for each technology, which
were then used to estimate the adjustment factor. The resulting adjustment factor for each
technology is the sum of the assessed relative weights for each technology. Finally, the adjusted
c values were derived by applying the resulting adjustment factor to the original c values for the
respective technologies. As shown in Table 4, the similarity weighted average c value for GrW
was estimated as -0.203.

4 Conclusions
4.1 Gravity Well Revenue Study
The Gravity Well Revenue Study examined potential storage revenue using reported revenue
data from FERC, historical energy-only electricity prices, and projected bulk electricity system
prices. In conducting this work, we found the following:
 • There is substantial variation in potential storage energy revenue in all three datasets we
 examined. For the historical datasets, this variation is driven by the electricity prices at
 specific nodes or in specific market regions, with some nodes showing much more
 variation in prices than other nodes. For the forward-looking datasets, the variation was
 driven by how the electricity generation mix changes.
 • The variation in prices could dwarf design decisions for energy storage. In other words,
 being able to site a storage device at the “best” node can be more important than having
 the “best” storage device (though the device would still need to meet “good enough”
 standards). However, there is also the potential that siting storage at a high-value node
 might reduce the revenue potential by alleviating congestion or other constraints.
 • The cost of the lowest-price nodes is considerably lower than the current cost of storage,
 indicating substantial cost reductions would be needed for storage to be cost-competitive
 at any location.
 • Round-trip efficiency is an important but second-order driver of revenue, and the
 revenue-efficiency relationship is slightly nonlinear—meaning the value of better round-
 trip efficiency declines as efficiency increases.

Though this work covered the contiguous U.S., more work could be done to further explore the
potential for storage revenue. That work includes capturing ancillary services revenue
opportunities and examining market-specific capacity revenue opportunities.

4.2 Gravity Well Future Cost Study
In the Gravity Well Future Cost Study, we applied both bottom-up and top-down analysis
approaches to assess future costs to install GrWs as percentages of the current cost. In the
bottom-up approach, we assessed learning rates for cost categories and individual components
that define the GrW system. A 12% learning rate was derived from the bottom-up analysis,
which translates to a cost decline to 13% of the original GrW cost (i.e., an 87% cost reduction
from current levels). Most of the decrease in cost (59%) is expected to come from the cost of
purchased parts, suggesting that economies of scale could be a major contributor to GrW’s future
costs.

In the top-down analysis, we applied historical cost elasticity indices from analogous
technologies to derive a multi-factor learning curve for GrWs. Technology analogues include
PSH, offshore wind, onshore wind, and solar photovoltaics. The resulting learning curve for the
moderate scenario shows a similar cost decline as that of the single-factor learning curve from
the bottom-up analysis. However, there is a prediction of a steeper decline in cost within the first
two years in the bottom-up approach compared to the top-down multi-factor learning curve.
