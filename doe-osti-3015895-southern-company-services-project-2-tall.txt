 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Project 2:

 The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) and Southern Company Services (SCS) seek to
 establish an agreement to assess the technical potential for wind energy in the Southeastern U.S.
 SCS has requested that NLR conduct a detailed assessment of system performance, available
 capacity, transmission distance, and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) at turbine hub heights of
 100m to 160m within the SCS service territory. NLR will also provide an assessment of utility
 photovoltaic (PV) technical potential within the SCS service territory. NLR has unique modeling
 capabilities to assist SCS with high spatial and temporal resolution assessment of wind and PV
 technical potential, considering geospatial constraints.

 Project 4:

 NLR and SCS seek to develop a spatially explicit techno-economic assessment for offshore wind
 energy in the Southeastern U.S. The analysis aims to characterize the regional sensitivities of
 offshore wind plant costs and performance, underwater cabling, potential siting constraints, and
 landfall spur transmission costs.

 PROJECT 2:

 Note: The original modification #4 for a topic of low carbon hydrogen was neither funded,
 begun, nor completed, termination of this modification. The project of tall tower wind described
 in this report derives from the replacement modification #4 for Project #2.

 Purpose:

 Based on the modeling needs specified by SCS, NLR will conduct a spatially explicit techno-
 economic assessment, including net capacity factors and LCOE, that aims to characterize the
 regional sensitivities of wind plant costs and performance and transmission costs. NLR will
 examine potential technical sensitivities through a set of wind supply curve scenarios, which will
 be determined in coordination with SCS. Scenarios will be defined by different combinations of
 spatial exclusion assumptions (e.g., terrain slope, land cover, and setback from the coast). Under
 these scenarios, NLR will examine sensitivities to model parameters such as setback thresholds,
 turbine configurations, and different hub heights. NLR will provide time series comparisons
 between wind and PV generation profiles at locations to be determined as representative by SCS.
 NLR will use the renewable energy potential (reV1) model to conduct the analysis and provide
 SCS with spatial outputs as maps and raw data format (specific modeling outputs are specified in
 Section 3).

 PROJECT 2 RESULTS:

 Deployment of wind energy in the Southeastern U.S. has progressed slower than in other regions
 of the country, in part due to lower wind resources at hub heights below 100m. However, recent
 technological improvements and decreasing costs have increased the potential for wind
 deployment in the Southeast. The main technological improvements that have increased interest
 are taller towers and lower specific power turbines, which produce higher capacity factors at
 lower wind speeds and enable more consistent energy production.

 NLR assessed the potential for tall tower wind energy for SCS across Mississippi, Alabama, and
 Georgia. The objective of this study was to provide an updated estimate of technical potential
 and LCOE based on emerging tower and turbine installation technologies and current balance of
 station (BOS) costs appropriate for the region.

 The modeling approach employed high spatial and temporal resolution wind resource data and
 spatially explicit representation of siting constraints. The analysis estimated the potential
 capacity, turbine performance, and LCOE at 120, 140, and 160m hub heights. The potential
 range of available land area for wind projects was explored through a scenario framework that
 aimed to bookend the range of available capacity from lenient to stringent siting constraints.

 Additionally, NLR assessed the distance to transmission infrastructure and the cost for
 transmission interconnection. We evaluated the model sensitivities considering different wind
 plant sizes (in terms of installed capacity) across a range of capital cost assumptions. Finally,
 NLR examined the temporal complementarity of normalized wind and PV generation profiles.

 The study found that under a mid-case scenario of siting constraints, the available capacity
 combined across the three states is 199GW. At a hub height of 160m, there is over 82GW of
 available capacity with greater than 0.4 net capacity factors. Examining relative costs across the
 three hub heights for small versus large wind plant sizes, the lowest LCOE for a 20MW plant
 size was 120m for most sites, whereas for a 150MW plant size, there was more variability
 between which hub height resulted in the lowest LCOE. The sensitivity analysis found that the
 mean LCOE ranged from $48.7/MWh to $52.5/MWh across the different combinations of plant
 sizes, hub heights, and exclusion scenarios. Results showed that there is over 60GW of capacity
 below $42.5/MWh based on a 150MW plant configuration.

 Estimating net capacity factors, available capacity, and LCOE:

 NLR applied the reV model (Maclaurin et al. 2019) with the Wind Toolkit (WTK) time series
 resource data (Draxl et al. 2015) to estimate net capacity factors at hub heights of 120m, 140m,
 and 160m. Estimates were produced for Mississippi, Alabama, and Georgia. NLR examined the
 available capacity within three spatial exclusion scenarios to capture the range of potential land
 availability. Additionally, we evaluated sensitivities to relative rotor spacing and modeled
 associated wake losses for three spacings: 4D, 7D, and 10D (where D is the rotor diameter).
 NLR provided estimates of levelized cost of transmission (LCOT), representing the cost above
 plant installation to connect to the existing transmission system. This was calculated as a
 function of distance and the type of transmission connection (line tie-in, substation, or load
 center). Estimates of LCOE were computed for each scenario at each of the three hub heights.

 The three land-use scenarios examined different levels of spatial constraints to estimate available
 capacity. These are based on the updated 2020 reV model scenarios: Open Access, Reference
 Access, and Limited Access scenarios (Lopez et al., forthcoming). Exclusion categories include
 infrastructure (buildings, roads, etc.), regulatory (siting ordinances, setbacks, etc.), and physical
 (excessive slope, wetlands, etc.). The Open Access scenario aims to simulate a very
 development- friendly future siting regime. The only exclusions are barrier islands, areas directly
 surrounding buildings, roads, transmission lines, and rails, and an aggregated land-use land cover
 layer. The Reference Access scenario adds potential siting restrictions to the set of open access
 layers.

 Setbacks from NEXRAD and short- and long-range radar stations, areas with more than 25%
 slope, and all federal lands are excluded. In addition, to simulate local ordinances, setbacks of
 1.1 times the maximum tip height of a representative turbine are applied to infrastructure, and
 this equates to 228 meters. The Limited Access scenario is the most restrictive, adding distance to
 the infrastructure setbacks and radar buffers. Infrastructure setbacks are calculated as 3 times the
 maximum turbine tip height, and instead of simple distance buffers around radars, a line-of-sight
 exclusion is applied.

 Examining diurnal PV and wind generation complementarity:

 NLR provided a preliminary assessment of the temporal complementarity of utility-scale PV and
 wind generation. Complementarity was evaluated at reference sites on annual and monthly
 diurnal time scales.

 Wind and PV generation are normalized to facilitate comparison based on an assumed DC/AC
 ratio of 1 and the 170W/m^2 turbine as used in this analysis. The assumption of a DC/AC ratio
 of 1 produces a larger variation in PV contribution and an increased DC/AC ratio, as is typical in
 the industry.

 It is worth noting that these plots do not contain demand profiles and the potential for a localized
 ‘duck curve’, or a period where PV generation is decreasing and wind generation is increasing.
 These plots show the combined PV and wind generation drops steeply from approximately 4 to 6
 p.m. which, depending on the generation mix, may suggest that some amount of storage may be
 advantageous as grid penetration of wind and PV increases.

 Additional analysis is needed to investigate long term variability in wind and solar at finer
 timescales.

 Summary Conclusion:

 This study has demonstrated that across Mississippi, Alabama, and Georgia, there is a total
 potential wind capacity of 199GW based on the Reference Access scenario with 7D rotor
 spacing. The high and low estimates for potential capacity were 492GW and 45GW,
 respectively, for the Open Access and Limited Access scenarios with 7D rotor spacing. This wide
 range in potential capacity highlights the substation impact from the stringency of siting
 constraints applied in the model. Much of this difference is driven by setbacks applied to
 structures, roads, railroads, and transmission rights-of-way. The 1.1x max tip height assumption

 11

 in the Reference Access scenario represents setback requirements that are most common among
 jurisdictions with such restrictions. Overall, the Reference Access scenario captures the best
 practices in technical potential exclusion assumptions.

 At the taller hub heights modeled in this study, the estimated wind plant performance shows
 substantial capacity at net capacity factors over 0.40. At 160m hub height, there is over 82GW of
 capacity with greater than 0.4 net capacity factors. At 120m there is still over 60GW above a 0.4
 net capacity factor. The hub height with the lowest LCOE for the 20MW plant size was 120m for
 most sites, whereas for the 150MW plant size there was more variability between which hub
 height resulted in the lowest LCOE. At the reference sites selected by SCS, the lowest hub height
 LCOE was 120m for all 20MW plant sizes and most 150MW plant sizes. There were three sites
 where the 140m hub height had the lowest LCOE for the 150MW plant size.

 The supply curve shows a roughly $8-10/MWh increase in LCOE between the 20MW and
 150MW plant sizes, and that trend is consistent across cumulative capacity. These results
 indicate that for the 20MW plant size, the increased tower cost for 140m or 160m over 120m
 towers is not offset by the increase in capacity factor. However, for the 150MW plant, the
 increased capacity factors do offset the tower costs at more locations, suggesting the 140m and
 160m hub heights have more advantage for larger plant sizes.

 The temporal complementarity of PV and wind is high across the SCS service territory. The
 nighttime diurnal trend of higher wind speeds is complemented well by PV generation. The
 combined PV + 160m wind generation shows a favorable diurnal trend that is consistent across
 all months of the year and holds across all the reference sites.

 PROJECT 4:

 Purpose:

 NLR and SCS seek to develop a spatially explicit techno-economic assessment for offshore wind
 energy in the Southeastern U.S. The analysis aims to characterize the regional sensitivities of
 offshore wind plant costs and performance, underwater cabling, potential siting constraints, and
 landfall spur-transmission costs. In coordination with SCS technical staff, NLR will examine
 supply curve sensitivities across a range of assumptions for near-future technology innovations
 and cost reductions (i.e., anticipated over the next ten years) for offshore wind energy. Specific
 metrics of focus will be net capacity factors, LCOE, interconnection costs, available capacity,
 and areas of potential deployment based on representative offshore lease areas. NLR will
 examine sensitivities to model parameters of turbine configuration (nameplate capacity, rotor
 diameter, hub height) and lower substructure (both-fixed bottom and floating wind turbines).
 Given the current uncertainty of offshore siting constraints, NLR will characterize these metrics
 using spatial layers representing potential stakeholder considerations (e.g., social factors, fishing,
 and wildlife).

 Statement of Work:

 NLR will use the reV model to conduct the analysis and provide SCS with spatial outputs as
 maps and raw data formats. The scope will be determined by the workflow in seven tasks
 detailed below and will be based on the current functionality of the reV model. To appropriately
 represent the offshore-specific challenges and anticipated costs for wind deployment, SCS will
 provide input parameters and assumptions for the reV model.
