 ABSTRACT

The purpose of this Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) was to test two
rooftop commercial heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems supplied by Carrier
Corporation to determine if they met the laboratory test requirements of the United States Department of
Energy (DOE) Commercial Building HVAC Technology Challenge (hereafter noted as “the Challenge”).
Results of the laboratory testing completed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) demonstrated that
the systems met all requirements of the Challenge. Carrier Corporation has successfully completed this
aspect of the Challenge. This CRADA report will briefly describe the Challenge, the test plan created to
measure the success of systems provided by Challenge participants, and the results of laboratory testing of
the Carrier commercial rooftop systems.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

Heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) requires significant energy for commercial businesses
in the US. A common method of providing HVAC to commercial buildings is the use of packaged rooftop
units that combine the functions of heating, cooling, and dehumidifying in one product that is commonly
installed on the building’s roof. The US DOE has challenged HVAC original equipment manufacturers
(OEMs) to develop innovative rooftop systems to meet groundbreaking goals for energy efficiency through
a range of ambient conditions. Successful development of these systems will allow commercial business
and property owners to meet their cost, energy, and reliability needs. Therefore, the objective of this
CRADA was to demonstrate that two prototypes developed by Carrier Corporation meet the targets
specified by DOE through laboratory testing at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). The smaller
capacity prototype was a nominal 10-ton capacity, while the larger was a nominal 15-ton capacity.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE BUILDING TECHNOLOGY OFFICE’S MISSION

This project will supports the mission of the Building Technologies Office (BTO) Commercial Buildings
Integration (CBI) Program, to identify and develop technologies that dramatically reduce commercial
building energy consumption. The specific CBI initiative, the Commercial Building HVAC Technology
Challenge, estimates that high efficiency rooftop units (RTUs) can reduce energy costs by up to 50%
compared with conventional RTUs, but fewer than 15% of commercial buildings in the U.S. currently have
them.

2.1 TECHNICAL SPECIFICATIONS

For the unit under test (UUT), the minimum performance requirements were sub-divided into categories
of General Requirements, Cooling Performance, Heating Performance, and Controls Verification
Procedure (CVP). The details of these categories follow. This specification leverages the standards listed
in order of precedence:
 1. Appendix A1 to Subpart F of 10 CFR 431 (“Appendix A1”) [1]
 2. Air Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration Institute (AHRI) 1340-2024 [2]
 3. American National Standards Institute/American Society of Heating, Refrigerating, and Air-
 Conditioning Engineers (ANSI/ASHRAE) 37-2009.[3]

2.1.1 General Requirements

The prototypes submitted for testing were required to meet all applicable federal and state standards,
regulations, and laws that govern rooftop HVAC systems. The refrigerants that were used in these
systems were to have been approved by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Significant
New Alternatives Policy (SNAP) and they were to comply with the EPA technology transitions program
for light commercial HVAC equipment. The prototypes submitted for testing were to be single-zone
packaged units with air -cooled direct expansion mechanical heating and cooling systems. The heating
capacity for these systems were to be in the range of 10 to 25 tons, defined at an outdoor temperature of
5oF. This range was further divided into two categories, 10 to 14.9 tons and 15 to 25 tons, and prototypes
submitted were to be recognized for meeting/exceeding the requirements in either category. Finally, the
prototype submitted for testing was to be controlled using either of two external control methods. The
first one specified using a thermostat while the second specified the use of a building management system
in which the unit was to use normal communication protocols such as BACnet or Modbus. The latter
required that a communication interface be provided by the manufacturer (e.g., an application to be
operated on a separate laptop computer).

2.1.2 Cooling Performance

The cooling performance was measured using the Integrated Ventilation, Economizing, and Cooling
(IVEC) metric. The minimum requirements were as specified in Appendix A1 [1], which will become
effective for equipment manufactured starting on January 1, 2029. For this CRADA, IVEC for the 10-ton
prototype was to be greater than or equal to 13.4, while IVEC for the 15-ton prototype was to be greater
than or equal to 13.1.

Dehumidification performance was evaluated at the C test conditions with the unit operating at minimum
capacity to demonstrate adequate dehumidification at low cooling loads. This was referred to as the DHC
test. The unit could include dehumidification technologies such as hot gas reheat, desiccants, or others to
provide adequate dehumidification at moderate outdoor air temperatures. Manufacturers were to show
that systems can dehumidify at the 75°F outdoor air temperature condition specified for the IVEC metric
in Appendix A1 [1] and account for cycling due to the low building loads at this outdoor air condition.
Typically, the balance of sensible and latent heat capacity for an air conditioner is measured by the
sensible heat ratio (SHR), which) is a figure that represents the ability of the system to dehumidify. It is
defined as the sensible heat capacity divided by the total of the sensible and latent heat capacities. The
measure of success in this test plan used this measure. The requirement for this portion of the test plan
was that an estimated sensible heat ratio, SHRDHC, based on steady state operation, SHRDHC,SS, and
corrected to account for the performance degradation that naturally occurs during cycling of the
equipment was to be less than or equal to 0.95.

2.2 LABORATORY SETUP

All equipment was tested in environmental chambers, consisting of an indoor (ID) and outdoor (OD)
chamber installed in a side-by-side arrangement. The UUT was installed in the outdoor chamber with
supply and return ductwork communicating with the indoor chamber. Both prototypes were tested in the
downflow airflow configuration. The test configurations followed the requirements specified in ASHRAE
37-2009 [3]. The primary capacity measurement for all systems was the ID air enthalpy method. The
secondary test method was the refrigerant enthalpy method.

2.3.3 Control Verification Procedure

The goal of these tests was to evaluate the ability of the native controls of the UUT to meet building loads
for given target ID and OD test conditions. This testing was conducted in heating mode, and its goals
were to assess the UUT’s capability to complete the following:

 o Reduce its heating capacity, using native controls, to the lowest level when the outdoor
 temperature is 47oF.
 o Maintain heating performance when the outdoor temperature is 5oF and -10oF
 o Manage defrosting operation to minimize peak power demand.
 o Transition smoothly between zone set point temperatures of 70oF and 75oF and prioritize
 mechanical heating when the outdoor temperature is 17oF.
 o Allow backup heat to work in parallel with mechanical heating to meet the building load when
 the outdoor temperature is 5oF.

2.4 RESULTS

Both prototypes successfully met all requirements of the Test Plan. Therefore, Carrier Corporation has
met the lab test requirements of the Commercial Building HVAC Technical Challenge.

This CRADA did not result in any subject inventions. This was not unexpected, as noted in Clause 3 of

 4. COMMERCIALIZATION POSSIBILITIES

The results of the CRADA testing support that the Carrier commercial rooftop systems designated for the
Challenge meet the requirements of the Challenge. Based on the performance of one sample of each
capacity, the products appear to be strong candidates for commercialization.

 5. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

Coincident with testing the Carrier commercial rooftop systems for the Challenge, ORNL researchers
welcomed meeting with Carrier personnel. Their strong engineering abilities were evident during
interactions before and during the short testing period. ONRL would welcome and will actively seek
further collaboration with Carrier on other projects.

 6. CONCLUSIONS

The purpose of this CRADA was to test a nominal 10-ton capacity and a nominal 15-ton capacity rooftop
commercial heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) system supplied by Carrier Corporation to
determine if they met the laboratory test requirements of the United States Department of Energy
Commercial Building HVAC Technology Challenge. Results of the laboratory testing completed at
ORNL demonstrated that the systems met all requirements of the Challenge. Carrier Corporation has
successfully completed this aspect of the Challenge. This CRADA report briefly described the Challenge,
the test plan created to measure the success of heat pump systems provided by Challenge participants, and
the results of laboratory testing of the Carrier commercial rooftop systems.
