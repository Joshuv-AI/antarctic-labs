Executive Summary
Reliable deployment of power-generation infrastructure depends on complex global supply chains for
materials, manufacturing, and specialized equipment. This study evaluates supply-chain exposure
across major power-plant subsystems to identify potential bottlenecks that could affect near-term
deployment timelines. The study covers power-generation supply chains broadly, using natural gas
combined-cycle (NGCC) as the reference for the detailed subsystem assessment because natural
gas accounts for the largest share of U.S. electricity generation and is estimated to remain a major
generation source through 2030 (EIA, 2026). Many of the subsystems, materials, components, and
manufacturing processes evaluated in this study are also relevant to coal, geothermal, hydropower,
and other generation technologies and were captured in the broader system-level screening. The
assessment approach is summarized in Table I-1.

To examine these risks in greater detail, five focus areas were selected for detailed supply-chain
assessment based on criteria described in Section 3.1. These include upstream alloying inputs
such as nickel and chromium; precision manufacturing of turbine blades and vanes; production
of large forgings for rotor shafts, disks, and casings; and custom fabrication of finned-tube heat
exchangers. These areas represent supply-chain stages where supply-chain disruptions could spread
across multiple subsystems and influence power plant construction timelines.
By contrast, many structural components and standard mechanical systems rely on mature indus-
trial supply chains with established domestic or allied manufacturing capacity and are less likely
to constrain near-term deployment.
Priority Mitigation Areas
The analysis identifies mitigation pathways aligned with the critical supply-chain areas examined
in this report (See Table I-3). Across the four focus areas, mitigation priorities emphasize three re-
curring strategies: increasing throughput in existing qualified facilities, strengthening allied supply
partnerships, and stabilizing long-term demand signals to support U.S industrial investment.

Scope and Future Analysis
The subsystem screening intentionally emphasized subsystems relevant across multiple power-
generation technologies, including coal, geothermal, hydropower, and natural gas generation. Nuclear-
specific components were excluded because they are examined in a separate study. While thirteen
components were identified through system-level screening, detailed analysis was limited to five
focus areas selected to align with the sprint scope and timeline.
Additional materials and components, including semiconductor wafers and chips and specialty
electrical components such as transformers, also showed elevated risk signals but were not examined
in detail because they are addressed in other studies.
Future work could extend the analysis to additional components identified during the screening,
including technology-specific equipment for geothermal and hydropower and additional electrical,
control, and advanced-material systems.

1 Power Generation Landscape and
  Supply Chain Scope
The U.S. electric power sector has entered a period of sustained growth after decades of relatively
steady increases in electricity demand (EIA, 2025; EIA, 2026). The rapid expansion of data centers
supporting artificial intelligence (AI), cloud computing, and other digital infrastructure, combined
with the reshoring and expansion of domestic manufacturing, is expected to drive significant in-
creases in electricity consumption over the coming decade. Federal and private investments are
accelerating the development of energy-intensive industries such as semiconductor fabrication, bat-
tery manufacturing, and critical minerals processing, while broader electrification trends across
transportation, buildings, and industrial sectors continue to increase electricity demand. Meet-
ing this growing demand will require substantial additions of generation capacity and supporting
infrastructure while maintaining reliability, affordability, and resilience.

U.S. electricity supply relies on a mix of generation technologies(See Figure 1). In 2025, natural gas
accounted for 40.8% of utility-scale generation, followed by renewables (24.1%), nuclear (17.7%),
and coal (16.6%). While renewable energy deployment is expected to continue to grow rapidly,
natural gas is projected to remain a significant part of the generation mix through at least 2030
(See Figure 2). As utilities and independent power producers expand, modernize, and maintain
generation assets to meet rising demand, securing the supply chains that support these technologies
is becoming increasingly important. The ability of these supply chains to provide critical equipment,
materials, and services in a timely and cost-effective manner will remain important to electric system
reliability, affordability, and resilience.

Given its current and projected role in U.S. electricity generation, NGCC was used as the reference

to include systems relevant across multiple power-generation technologies. Many of the subsystems
and equipment categories evaluated for NGCC are also used in coal, hydropower, geothermal, and

risks across these technologies. Nuclear remains an important source of electricity, but reactor-
specific technologies and supply chains are addressed in a separate study.

2 Power-Plant Subsystems
This section defines the major subsystems that make up power-generation facilities and establishes
the basis for the supply-chain assessment. Power-generation facilities are composed of multiple
interconnected subsystems that perform several functions such as fuel handling, energy conversion,
heat management, power generation, and system control. While configurations vary by technology,
many of these subsystems are shared across natural gas, coal, hydropower, and geothermal systems.

different generation technologies.

The screening framework evaluates subsystems across five criteria that capture key structural
sources of supply-chain risk:

 • Cross-technology use: Components used across multiple power plant types or other sectors

 6

 to assess competition for supply

 • Foreign input dependence: Reliance on imported materials or components

 • U.S. manufacturing constraints: Availability of qualified production capability

 • Critical minerals exposure: Dependence on materials considered critical by U.S. Depart-
 ment of Energy (DOE) assessment (Bauer, Diana, et al. 2023)

 • Manufacturing complexity: Specialized processes, equipment or expertise that may in-
 crease production lead times

Subsystems that scored highly across multiple criteria were prioritized for deeper analysis. The
framework also considered capital equipment requirements associated with key manufacturing pro-
cesses. To manage scope, detailed capital equipment assessment focused on the five priority focus
areas identified through the screening process. This approach allowed the assessment to move from
broad system coverage to a focused evaluation of deployment-critical supply chains.

3.2 Mitigation Pathway Prioritization
To evaluate potential responses to supply-chain risks identified in the assessment, the analysis
applied six mitigation pathway categories. These categories reflect the primary mechanisms through
which supply constraints may be addressed. The mitigation framework includes:

 • Domestic capacity expansion: Increasing U.S. production capability for critical materials
 or components.

 • Capital equipment onshoring and diversification: Expanding domestic manufacturing
 capability for specialized industrial equipment and diversifying supply sources for critical
 production systems.

 • Allied sourcing: Diversifying supply through trusted international partners.

 • Design and qualification flexibility: Reducing constraints on narrowly specified mate-
 rials, components, or processes through design changes, standards updates, or qualification
 reforms.

 • Demand coordination and sequencing: Aligning demand signals across sectors to sup-
 port domestic investment.

 • R&D for alternative materials and processes: Advancing alternative materials, man-
 ufacturing processes, or technologies.

Each focus area is evaluated against these mitigation pathways to identify mitigation options and
highlight where different levers may reduce supply-chain risk. Assessment of the costs associated
with implementing these mitigation options is outside the scope of this study.

7 Upstream Materials: Nickel and
  Chromium
Nickel and chromium serve as foundational inputs for multiple subsystems. These materials enable
stainless steels, specialty steels, and superalloys used in turbines, steam generators, cooling systems,
and balance-of-plant equipment (Nickel Institute, n.d; Bauer et al., 2023).

7.1 Nickel and Chromium: Manufacturing Bottlenecks
For nickel, key bottlenecks occur in smelting and refining. Expanding U.S. refining capacity faces
several challenges, including cost competitiveness and the technical complexity of nickel processing.
Some refining routes require harsh operating conditions and specialized equipment, contributing to
higher processing costs (Li et al. 2025). Box 7.1 below shows how both materials flow from ore
extraction and refining into alloy production and ultimately into multiple plant subsystems.

8.2 Blade and Vanes: Global Supply Concentration and
 Deployment Risk
Direct public data on turbine blade and vane production is limited. As a result, this analysis
relies on proxy indicators including investment casting market share, representative U.S. blade

U.S maintains significant domestic blade and vane production capability with major OEM facilities
operated by GE, Siemens, and Mitsubishi Power performing much of the blade manufacturing
cycle in-house from casting through final inspection and finishing (Gibson 2024, Turboblading
Group n.d.). Also, North America holds a leading share of high-value precision casting capacity
used across aerospace, defense, and industrial turbine applications (Sullivan and Muxlow 2025).
Trade data indicate that turbine parts and components are sourced from allied countries such
as Mexico, Japan, and several European suppliers. Alloy inputs are also largely sourced from
allied producers in Europe and Australia (U.S. International Trade Commission Dataweb, n.d.).
However, casting and heat treatment equipment used in blade manufacturing remains concentrated
among specialized foreign manufacturers, primarily in Europe and Japan. These furnace systems
are highly specialized systems integrated into OEM production processes, limiting the ability to
rapidly expand production capacity. Precision casting capacity and specialized furnace availability
present a greater deployment risk than foreign component sourcing.

9 Heavy Forging: Large Forging
Large forgings are critical structural components used in turbine rotor disk, rotor shaft and casings.
Their size, weight, and metallurgical requirements make them among the most capital-intensive
components in power-generation supply chains.

9.1 Large Forging: Manufacturing Bottlenecks
Production bottlenecks occur primarily at the forging press stage (See Box 9.1). Very large forging
presses are rare, and capital-intensive assets concentrated in a small number of facilities worldwide,
thus limiting throughput and increasing lead times for large forging components (DOW 2018; S&P
Global 2025; World Nuclear Association 2026). These constraints are reinforced by specialized
capital equipment requirements across the production chain. The analysis prioritizes ingot-forming
furnaces – Electroslag Remelting (ESR) and Vacuum Arc Remelting (VAR) and open-die forging
presses. These systems require substantial capital investment and specialized engineering expertise
which present significant barriers to rapid capacity expansion (Credence Research n.d.; Verified
Market Research n.d.; U.S. Department of Energy 2022).

10 Custom Heat-Exchange Fabrication:
 Finned Tubes
Finned tubes are a key heat-transfer component in heat recovery steam generators (HRSGs) used
in combined-cycle power plants. These tubes transfer heat from gas-turbine exhaust gases to
generate steam for additional power production (Jouhara et al. 2018; GE Vernova n.d.). Finned
tubes are typically assembled into tube panels and harp structures, which are then integrated into
larger HRSG modules during plant construction (Buecker n.d). While finned tubes are common
in industrial heat exchange, HRSG systems require a specialized fabrication segment. These units
utilize high-chromium alloys such as Grade 91 alloyed steel to produce T91/P91 tubes, which are
engineered to meet stringent high-temperature and pressure-part requirements under boiler codes
(Swanekamp 2004).

10.1 Finned Tubes: Manufacturing Bottlenecks
Finned-tube supply chains are constrained by both material supply and fabrication capacity (See
Box 10.1). Production of seamless alloy tubes used in high-temperature pressure sections requires
specialized metallurgy and qualified tube mills. Fabrication requires dedicated finning lines, certified
welding procedures, and heat-treatment infrastructure capable of processing large tube panels and
harp assemblies.

11 Conclusion
This study examined supply-chain exposure across major power-plant subsystems to identify sup-
ply chain constraints that could affect power-generation deployment timelines. The system-level
screening confirms that supply-chain risk is present across the power-plant architecture but is con-
centrated in a limited number of material inputs and specialized manufacturing stages.
Across the eleven subsystems evaluated, thirteen components were identified where material depen-
dencies or manufacturing capacity constraints could create potential deployment challenges. These
components include upstream alloy inputs, specialized turbine manufacturing processes, heavy forg-
ing infrastructure, and heat-exchange fabrication systems. While many parts of the power-plant
supply chain rely on mature industrial production networks, these areas represent supply chain
stages where disruptions could spread across multiple subsystems and influence plant construction
schedules.
To examine these risks in greater detail, the analysis focused on five areas selected to align with the
scope and timeline of the sprint: nickel and chromium, turbine blades and vanes, large forgings used
in turbine rotors, and finned tubes used in heat-recovery steam generators. The analysis highlights
how upstream material availability, specialized capital equipment, and manufacturing throughput
are interconnected and influence supply-chain security for power-generation infrastructure.
The findings indicate that strengthening power-plant supply chains will depend less on broad
system-wide interventions and more on targeted efforts at key upstream materials and manufac-
turing stages. Improving throughput at existing facilities, reinforcing supply-chain diversification
through allied partnerships, and supporting investment in specialized manufacturing capacity are
likely to have the greatest influence on near-term deployment timelines.
Future work can expand this analysis to additional subsystems and components identified during
the screening process. Several areas, including electrical systems, control equipment, advanced ma-
terials, and capital equipment supply chains, showed elevated risk signals but were not examined
in detail due to sprint scope constraints. Continued analysis in these areas will help refine under-
standing of system-level vulnerabilities and inform strategies to strengthen industrial capacity and
support reliable power-generation deployment.

A Supply Chain Risk Assessment
  Methodology
Appendix A describes the analytical framework used to evaluate supply-chain risk across power-
plant subsystems and support the detailed screening of the six prioritized subsystems examined in
this report. The methodology evaluates supply-chain exposure using five criteria that capture key
structural sources of risk: cross-technology use, foreign input dependence, domestic manufacturing
capacity, critical mineral dependency, and manufacturing lead times. These criteria were selected
intentionally from many possible indicators because they reflect factors that strongly influence
whether the United States can manufacture or reliably access key power-plant components when
needed.
The analysis was conducted using a two-stage screening framework. The first stage applied an
initial subsystem screening methodology to identify areas with potential supply-chain exposure
across power-plant subsystems. The second stage applied a detailed risk-screening framework to
the six prioritized subsystems to evaluate component-level supply-chain risks.
Table A-1 presents the initial subsystem screening methodology used to flag supply-chain exposure
across subsystem component families.
