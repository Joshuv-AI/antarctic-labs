Transarterial embolization of ruptured distal choroidal artery aneurysm in Moyamoya disease: A case series and literature review
Authors: Daisuke Sato, Satoshi Koizumi, Satoru Miyawaki, Satoshi Kiyofuji, Nobuhito Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Moyamoya disease (MMD) is characterized by the compensatory development of collateral vasculature. Aneurysm formation on collaterals is common. The rupture of these aneurysms is associated with a poor prognosis, which presents significant treatment challenges.Case Description: We encountered two cases of distal choroidal artery aneurysm rupture in MMD. The first case experienced re-rupture and underwent superselective embolization. However, this case was complicated by an intraoperative rupture and subsequent hydrocephalus. The second case underwent parent artery occlusion without complications.Conclusion: These cases highlight both efficacy and the risks involved. Lesion accessibility is crucial. A careful evaluation of collaterals and precise planning are required.

INTRODUCTION

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive steno-occlusive disease that affects the termini of internal carotid arteries (ICAs).[12] Fragile collateral networks bypassing the stenotic lesion develop during disease progression, including periventricular anastomosis, defined by connections between the perforating or choroidal arteries and medullary arteries.[5] Choroidal anastomosis is particularly prone to bleeding and a major site for aneurysm formation and rupture.[4] Aneurysms involving choroidal collateral pathways occur in approximately 0.8–2.0% of patients with MMD.[6,11] Among these, anterior choroidal artery (AChA) aneurysms are reported to be approximately twice as frequent as posterior choroidal artery aneurysms.[11] The 5-year bleeding risk has been reported to be 8.3%,[11] and among ruptured aneurysms, the rebleeding rate may reach 40%.[17] Rupture is associated with poor prognosis. Multiple treatments have been proposed, such as direct clipping, revascularization, endovascular interventions, and even observational strategies.[17] The optimal management approach is still unclear. Here, we describe two cases of distal choroidal artery aneurysm rupture in MMD. Hemostasis was achieved by endovascular techniques, though each case ran through dichotomized long-term outcomes. We discuss pathology and the associated treatment challenges.

CASE PRESENTATION

Patient 1

A 69-year-old woman presented with intense headaches and was diagnosed with an intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) via computed tomography (CT), without evidence of intraparenchymal hemorrhage [Figure 1A]. CT angiography revealed severe ICA terminus stenosis and abnormal collateral vessel development in the basal ganglia, including an aneurysm in the right AChA. She had a second hemorrhage 1 month later. However, she waived undergoing digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Her consciousness suddenly deteriorated 5 days later, and the CT scan revealed a massive IVH with severe hydrocephalus, which implied the third episode of rupture of the right AChA aneurysm [Figure 1B]. The patient underwent external ventricular drainage, and DSA confirmed MMD and aneurysm progression [Figure 1C and D]. Transarterial embolization (TAE) was performed 2 days after the first surgery.

(A) Initial computed tomography (CT) results showed an intraventricular hemorrhage. (B) Following re-bleeding, a distal choroidal aneurysm was identified at the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle (white arrow). (C) Digital subtraction angiography confirmed the presence of an anterior choroidal artery aneurysm. (D) The aneurysm was irregularly shaped and located at the distal portion of the anterior choroidal artery. (E) Coils were used to fill the aneurysm. (F) N-butyl cyanoacrylate was subsequently injected into the ruptured aneurysm. (G) Complete occlusion of the aneurysm was confirmed. (H) Increment of the intraventricular hemorrhage and progression of hydrocephalus were evident from the results of the postoperative CT.

Under general anesthesia, a 6-Fr FUBUKI guiding catheter (ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) was inserted into the right femoral artery. A distal access catheter, Guidepost (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan), was placed at the supraclinoid portion of ICA. A marathon microcatheter (Medtronic, Minnesota, USA) was introduced into the aneurysm with Tenrou 10 (Kaneka, Osaka, Japan) and CHIKAI X10 (ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) microguidewires. Electro-detachable coils were inserted through the microcatheter [Figure 1E], but extravasation was observed after insertion of the ninth coil. Immediately, 0.05 mL of 50% n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) was injected proximal to the aneurysm [Figure 1F]. Total occlusion of the aneurysm and obstruction of the short segment of AChA immediately proximal to the aneurysm were confirmed [Figure 1G].

An IVH increment was revealed via postoperative CT [Figure 1H], although ischemic complications were not evident. Her hydrocephalus exacerbated 1 day after the TAE, which necessitated endoscopic hematoma evacuation. Although the hematoma was removed, and hemostasis was achieved, she suffered from a deep white matter infarction due to damage to the fragile vessels comprising the choroidal anastomosis. She underwent a ventriculoperitoneal shunt 1 month later due to communicating hydrocephalus. Despite a partial recovery, she remained bedridden (modified Rankin scale [mRS] 5) at follow-up 2 months later.

Patient 2

A 57-year-old man was admitted with an intense headache and confusion. He had been diagnosed with MMD in the past and had refused follow-up. On admission, he was in a deep coma with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 (E1V1M1), although his pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light. CT revealed IVH [Figure 2A], and CT angiography showed bilateral ICA terminus stenosis with an aneurysm in the left lateral posterior choroidal artery (LPChA) [Figure 2B]. Endoscopic hematoma removal and ventricular drainage were performed, followed by TAE 29 days later.

(A) An intraventricular hemorrhage was observed via computed tomography at the time of admission. (B) A distal choroidal artery aneurysm was detected along the wall of the body of the left lateral ventricle (white arrow). (C) Digital subtraction angiography revealed a left lateral posterior choroidal artery aneurysm. (D) The aneurysm was located at the distal portion of the left lateral posterior artery. (E) N-butyl cyanoacrylate was injected immediately proximal to the aneurysm. (F) This treatment resulted in complete occlusion.

Under general anesthesia, a 4-Fr FUBUKI Dilator Kit (ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) was inserted from the right radial artery to the right vertebral artery. The LPChA aneurysm was confirmed [Figure 2C and D]. A Guidepost catheter (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was placed at the left posterior cerebral artery. A DeFrictor NANO microcatheter (Medico’s Hirata, Osaka, Japan) was introduced into the left LPChA with Tenrou 10 (Kaneka, Osaka, Japan) and CHIKAI X10 (ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) microguidewires. Subsequently, 0.03 mL of 50% NBCA was injected immediately proximal to the aneurysm [Figure 2E]. Total occlusion of the aneurysm and obstruction of the short segment of LPChA immediately proximal to the aneurysm were confirmed [Figure 2F].

The patient gradually regained consciousness but experienced right hemiplegia and aphasia. Three months later, his neurological functions improved, and he was discharged home with moderate disability (mRS 2). Written informed consent was obtained from the family of each patient for this report.

DISCUSSION

This report highlights the challenges in treating aneurysm rupture in MMD. Hemostasis was achieved, yet outcomes varied, emphasizing early intervention and careful planning. Importantly, TAE of this disease is challenging and is not established as a standard treatment. Therefore, the technical challenges and potential pitfalls of this approach warrant careful consideration.

Lesion access is critical for treating these aneurysms. MMD is characterized by intimal thickening, fragmentation of the elastic lamina, medial attenuation, fibrin deposition, and fibrosis.[18] These changes impact the small caliber, fragile, and tortuous vessel morphologies of MMD, may limit lesion access, and even increase the risk of vessel injury.[18] The choroidal anastomosis, which connects the choroidal and medullary arteries in the periventricular area, can become dilated and more accessible as the aneurysm progresses.[11] However, these vessels are fragile and can bleed easily, requiring careful navigation during TAE.[10] In addition to gentle and cautious microcatheter navigation, excessive guidewire manipulation and forceful contrast injection should be avoided, as these maneuvers may increase the risk of arterial dissection or rupture.

Several TAE cases involving distal choroidal artery aneurysms in MMD have employed small-caliber high-flexibility microcatheters and flow-guided profiles [Table 1].[2,7,8,15,16,19-21] Conversely, it is noteworthy that one of the reasons for treatment failure was access failure [Table 1]. Hence, a preoperative evaluation of the caliber and tortuosity of the target vessel is essential to determine TAE feasibility. The outer diameter of the Marathon microcatheter is 0.51–0.91 mm, and the outer diameter of the DeFrictor NANO microcatheter is 0.43–0.73 mm. These values are smaller than the inner diameter of the target vessel in our cases. Thus, it would be beneficial to compare the target vessel inner diameter with the microcatheter outer diameter to confirm accessibility.

Second, clinicians should choose between selective embolization and parent artery occlusion (PAO). Patient 1 experienced intraoperative rupture during selective embolization, leading to PAO. In contrast, patient 2 underwent PAO directly, avoiding rupture. The fragility of distal choroidal artery aneurysms and the occasional presence of pseudoaneurysms make selective embolization a higher-risk procedure compared to the coiling of true aneurysms affecting major arteries.[1] In contrast, proximal occlusion may avoid direct intra-aneurysmal manipulation and thereby reduce hemorrhagic risk, although it carries a potential risk of ischemia due to the sacrifice of the parent collateral vessel. Thus, the treatment strategy should be individualized by weighing the hemorrhagic risk of endosaccular manipulation against the ischemic risk of proximal occlusion.

Fifteen out of 17 reported cases of TAE underwent PAO without significant ischemic complications, due to the compensatory effect of collateral networks in MMD [Table 1].[2,7,8,15,16,19-21] However, a careful evaluation of collaterals would be essential since extensive artery occlusion may potentially lead to significant ischemia, whereas proximal occlusion without occluding the aneurysm may leave the aneurysm untreated due to collateral flow constraints.[2] Advancing the microcatheter as close to the aneurysm as possible, and short-segment trapping would be ideal.[1] Although several reports have described favorable outcomes with selective embolization, our case resulted in a poor clinical course following the intraoperative re-rupture. Reliance on selective embolization alone may not always be advisable.

Third, the choice of embolization material is also important. Coils are typically used for selective embolization, while NBCA, Onyx, and coils are utilized for PAO.[2,7,8,15,16,19-21] NBCA is a fast-acting adhesive that instantly polymerizes following contact with ionic substances, providing rapid hemostasis.[9,13] The AChA runs along the perimesencephalic cistern before entering the ventricular system and supplies important deep structures.[14] Since the inferior choroidal point is a marker of the entrance to the ventricle,[3] the injection of NBCA proximal to the inferior choroidal point can lead to serious ischemia within critical deep structures. It would be useful to inject high-concentration NBCA from the microcatheter, positioned sufficiently beyond the inferior choroidal point, to prevent the unintended dispersion of embolic material to other areas.

Finally, the timing of intervention is one of the most critical points. Patient 1 experienced re-bleeding before TAE, ultimately resulting in a vegetative state due to severe parenchymal damage. In contrast, patient 2 underwent TAE before experiencing re-bleeding and recovered after intense rehabilitation. This sharp contrast in patient outcomes implies that timely intervention is important to prevent further deterioration of the clinical scenario.

However, some patients do not experience re-bleeding even if observational strategies are opted for. A few aneurysms may even regress spontaneously.[17] Extensive analysis may enable a differentiation between high- and low-risk groups of re-rupture, enabling clinicians to decide whether an early intervention or observational strategy should be adopted. The reasons for TAE failure included inability to access the lesion and deterioration of the condition of the patient due to aneurysm rupture [Table 1]. Considering that re-rupture would potentially result in devastating sequelae, and embolization may be relatively feasible and effective, early intervention to prevent re-rupture would be a reasonable treatment strategy.

CONCLUSION

TAE is an effective treatment for distal choroidal artery aneurysms in patients with MMD. Lesion access is crucial for this procedure. Although PAO with NBCA is a feasible choice, careful evaluation of collateral flow and precise planning of embolization are indispensable. Since re-rupture may result in devastating sequelae, early intervention should be considered. Further research is warranted to better predict whether the lesion is likely to re-bleed or regress.
