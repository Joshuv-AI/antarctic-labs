GRP78/BiP as a novel midbody component induces mitotic exit failure via PLK1 stabilization in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Sanghwa Kim, Yeonhwa Song, Su-Yeon Lee, Kang mo Kim, Haeng Ran Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and is associated with a poor prognosis, even when diagnosed in its early stages. Therefore, identifying novel HCC targets for therapeutic drug development is critically needed. Our proteomic analysis identified Glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) as a potential new target, showing enrichment in HCC spheroids. We observed that post-surgery HCC patients with elevated GRP78 expression in non-tumor tissues had an unfavorable prognosis. Inhibition of GRP78 expression suppressed cell proliferation and reduced viability by controlling the DNA damage response in HCC cells. GRP78 expression peaked during the G2/M phase of mitosis and was predominantly localized to the cytoskeleton. However, its expression decreased as the cell cycle progressed from the G2/M to the G1 phase in HCCs. Overexpression of GRP78 in HCC cells increased the interaction between PLK1 and GRP78, leading to the stabilization of PLK1 in the midbody during cytokinesis. This stabilization resulted in the failure of cells to exit mitosis, producing multinucleated HCC cells. These findings reveal novel functional roles for GRP78 in disrupting mitotic activity through the stabilization of PLK1 at the midbody, thereby fostering polyploidy development in HCC. This suggests that inhibiting GRP78 is a promising approach for enhancing HCC therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is an aggressive malignant cancer with rapid progression and short patient survival times. HCC, a complex disease with many environmental and hereditary risk factors (1), is accompanied by fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver-related metabolic diseases (2). The complicated etiology affects the disease’s course and contributes to a poor prognosis. The incidence of HCC is likely to rise due to increasing alcohol abuse, obesity, and an aging population (3, 4). Most HCC therapy targets VEGF-signaling angiogenesis inhibitors because deregulated angiogenesis is a hallmark of hepatocellular carcinoma (5). However, immunotherapy, either alone or in combination with angiogenesis inhibitors, offers a new approach to managing advanced HCC, highlighting the pivotal role of the immune microenvironment in disease progression (6). To elucidate these molecular changes, we previously characterized the differential global protein response of HCC cells grown in culture as monolayers (2D) or spheroids (3D) (7, 8). Here, we studied glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), a member of the HSP70 family of chaperones. GRP78 is expressed in 3D cultured HCC cells but not in 2D cultured cells. Primarily found in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), GRP78 is essential for the maturation of many cell surface receptor proteins, enabling cancer cells to perceive and respond to growth-promoting signals in their microenvironment (9, 10).

GRP78 also plays a pivotal role in the cellular stress response, acting as a stress sensor and master regulator (11, 12). Thus, GRP78 profoundly influences cellular decision-making, determining whether the cell attempts to survive or undergoes apoptosis. Furthermore, tumors adapt to stress by upregulating GRP78, leading to its overexpression in many types of tumors (13, 14). Animal studies have demonstrated that reduced GRP78 expression is not detrimental to normal cells and organs; instead, it inhibits cancer cell growth and survival. Thus, inhibiting GRP78 may overcome resistance to current cancer therapies (15, 16).

Cell division depends on a meticulously orchestrated sequence of events termed the cell cycle. The failure to accurately transmit duplicated genetic material to both daughter cells can result in cellular transformation, which may promote the development of cancer. Therefore, mammalian cells tightly regulate mitosis, ensuring accurate and reliable segregation of chromosomes. As GRP78 is located in the midbody during mitosis (17), we focused on a novel function of GRP78 as a mitosis regulator. We aimed to identify partner proteins that influence mitotic behavior and subsequently sought to identify GRP78 inhibitors to improve HCC therapy.

RESULTS

HCC patients with high GRP78 expression had an unfavorable prognosis

We analyzed the proteomes of HCC cells grown in 2D monolayers or 3D spheroids using computer-assisted image analysis. We found 102 spots that were differentially overexpressed in HCC spheroids. Among these, HSPA5 (GRP78) and HSP90B1 (GRP94) were uniquely overexpressed in cells from multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTS) compared to cells from monolayer cultures (Fig. 1A). GRP78 and GRP94 were minimally expressed in HCC monolayers but highly expressed in HCC spheroids (Fig. 1B). The expression of other HSPs, including HSP27, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90, was not affected by the culture conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We specifically screened for proteins expressed exclusively in 3D spheroids but not in 2D monolayers. GRP78 and GRP94 were the only 2 genes that strictly met this criterion, which provided the rationale for initially evaluating both candidates.

To further understand the significance of GRP family protein expression in HCC spheroids, we determined the expression of GRP78 and GRP94 in tumor spheroids from five Korean patient-derived HCC lines (SNU449, SNU475, SNU387, SNU389, and SNU898), four Caucasian patient-derived HCC lines (Hep3B, Huh6, HepG2, and PLC/PRF/5), and a normal hepatocyte line (Fa2N-4). Tumor spheroids from the Korean patient-derived HCC lines SNU449, SNU387, and SNU389 showed significantly higher GRP78 levels than spheroids from normal hepatocytes (Fig. 1C). However, similar to normal hepatocytes, GRP78 expression was rarely observed in tumor spheroids from Caucasian patient-derived HCC lines (Fig. 1D). Overall, GRP78 expression was higher in spheroids from Korean patient-derived HCC cell lines than in those from Caucasian patient-derived HCC lines. In contrast, there was no difference in GRP94 expression between HCC cell lines derived from Korean patients, Caucasian patients, and normal hepatocytes (Fig. 1C, D). Recognizing that cultured human cell lines may not fully represent true cell physiology, we analyzed primary HCC cells. We measured GRP78 RNA in tumor spheroids from eight Korean patient-derived primary HCC cells, passaged only 3-6 times, and compared them to normal Fa2N-4 hepatocytes. All tumor spheroids from Korean patient-derived primary HCC cells showed higher GRP78 mRNA expression than spheroids from normal hepatocytes (Fig. 1E).

South Korea has a very high incidence and mortality rate for liver cancer, making it one of the top countries globally in terms of liver cancer burden. Although GRP78 is not prognostic for liver cancer according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) program, we measured GRP78 RNA expression in HCC tissues from 59 Korean patients. GRP78 RNA was overexpressed in HCC tissues compared to non-neoplastic tissues (Fig. 1F, G), but GRP94 RNA was not expressed in HCC tissue (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although we found no correlation between RNA expression of GRP78 and the patients’ 10-year survival rate after resection of HCC (Supplementary Fig. 3), we observed a large reduction in the survival rate of liver cancer patients with high GRP78 expression in non-tumor tissues, constituting about 12% of the experimental group (Fig. 1H). The overall 10-year survival showed no association with GRP94 expression in HCC tissues or surrounding normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4). Thus, HCC patients with high GRP78 expression in non-tumor tissues after surgery may have an unfavorable prognosis.

Based on these results, GRP78, but not GRP94, demonstrated strong clinical relevance in HCC patients; therefore, we selected GRP78 as the primary target for all subsequent functional investigations.

Inhibition of GRP78 resulted in growth inhibition and cell death in HCC

As GRP78 overexpression in non-tumor tissues was associated with an unfavorable prognosis after HCC resection, we determined the effects of altered GRP78 and GRP94 expression on HCC cell growth. GRP family proteins sense cellular stress and play a key role in responding and adapting to it, thereby influencing cell survival or death pathways. In SNU475 HCC cells, GRP78 inhibition by siGRP78 siRNA diminished clonogenic survival, whereas GRP94 inhibition by siGRP94 siRNA, similar to control siRNA (siCont), did not affect clonogenic survival (Fig. 2A). Similarly, siGRP78 reduced clonogenic survival in Huh7 and Hep3B cells (Fig. 2B). In contrast, GRP78 overexpression significantly increased the number of colonies in immortalized human Fa2N-4 hepatocytes (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, GRP78 inhibition by siGRP78 in Huh7 and SNU475 cells increased their doubling times (Fig. 2D).

To determine the role of apoptosis in siGRP78-treated cells, we analyzed apoptosis markers and found increased levels of cleaved PARP and γ-H2AX in Huh7, Hep3B, and SNU475 cells (Fig. 2E). Apoptosis is a secondary response to DNA damage; when we analyzed the DNA damage sensor γ-H2AX in siGRP78-treated HCC cells, we also found increased expression of γ-H2AX (Fig. 2E). As shown in Fig. 2F, GRP78 depletion in Hep3B cells markedly attenuated Rad50 expression and decreased the phosphorylation of key upstream and downstream DDR kinases, including p-ATM (Ser1981), p-CHK2 (T68), and checkpoint kinase (p-CHK, S317), which coordinates the cell cycle checkpoint response (18). Conversely, overexpression of GRP78 in Huh7 cells upregulated Rad50 and increased the phosphorylation levels of ATM, CHK2, and CHK1 (Fig. 2F). Thus, our findings suggest that GRP78 expression modulates DDR signaling and increases apoptosis in HCC cells.

GRP78 is expressed predominantly in the G2/M phase in HCC cells

The role of GRP78 in cell proliferation and its activation of CHK1 after DNA damage suggested that GRP78 expression correlates with the cell cycle. Flow cytometry of Fa2N-4 cells synchronized in the G1 phase by L-mimosine treatment (19) followed by washout showed a peak of mitotic cells from 12 h. There was low expression of GRP78 in cells arrested in G1 by L-mimosine treatment, whereas expression of GRP78 and cyclin B1 increased 12 h after L-mimosine washout when cells in the G2/M phase peaked. This expression was maintained for up to 24 h (Fig. 3A). Similarly, in Huh7 and Hep3B HCC cell lines and primary AMC-H1 and AMC-H2 HCC cells (20), GRP78 expression increased in the G2/M phase (Fig. 3B).

We confirmed oscillations in GRP78 expression during cell cycle progression. Huh7 and SNU475 cells synchronized at the G2/M phase by nocodazole treatment demonstrated high GRP78 expression (Fig. 3C). Morphological analysis of GRP78 expression in Huh7 and SNU475 cells showed significantly increased expression during mitosis (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, the cell cycle distribution changed with the expression level of GRP78: overexpression of GRP78 increased the G2/M population, whereas inhibition of GRP78 by siGRP78 decreased it (Fig. 3E). In addition, phospho-Histone H3 (Ser10) protein, which is a key regulator of gene expression and mitosis, was more predominantly expressed in GRP78 overexpressing HCC cells, conversely, depletion of GRP78 by siRNA in Huh7 cells noticeably reduced the level of phosphor-H3 (Ser10) (Fig. 3F). Therefore, because GRP78 is upregulated during the progression from the G2/M phase to mitosis, it likely plays an important role in the mitotic process.

Aberrant cytokinesis in HCC cells overexpressing GRP78 results in multinucleated cells

By subcellular fractionation, we found GRP78 predominantly in the membranes and cytoplasm of HCC cells, with some presence in the cytoskeletal fractions. However, GRP78 was rarely found in membranes in Huh7 cells synchronized at the G2/M phase by nocodazole treatment (Fig. 4A). Given that mitosis is coordinated by the reorganization of cytoskeletons, including microtubules and actin, we used immunofluorescence to determine positional changes in α-tubulin, F-actin, and GRP78 during mitosis and cytokinesis in HCC cells. We observed that GRP78 co-localized with α-tubulin and F-actin (Supplementary Fig. 5), specifically at the midbody within the intercellular bridge during cytokinesis (Fig. 4B). Since cytokinesis failure can lead to centrosome amplification and the production of polyploid cells, we investigated the role of GRP78 in cytokinesis by analyzing nuclear morphology in HCC cells overexpressing GRP78. We found that these cells yielded a startling number of multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 4C).

Furthermore, we also measured multinucleation and GRP78 expression in patient-derived HCC tissues. To determine the clinical relevance of this morphological change, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on human HCC tissues. We confirmed the prominent presence of multinucleated giant cells exhibiting high GRP78 expression in actual patient tumor tissues (Fig. 4D).

When we quantified the percentage of multiple nuclei in normal liver tissues and HCC tissues, we found less than 1% ≥ 4n cells in normal tissues but more than 68.5% ≥ 4n cells in HCC tissues (Fig. 4E). We found 37.2% ≥ 4n cells in HCC cells with low expression of GRP78 but 52.8% ≥ 4n cells in HCC cells that overexpressed GRP78 (Fig. 4F). Moreover, overexpression of GRP78 caused chromosome mis-segregation in Fa2N-4 cells, which immortalized normal hepatocytes (Fig. 4G). Dysregulation of the protein regulator of cytokinesis 1 (PRC1) is the main cause of cytokinesis failure at the midzone center (21, 22). Overexpression of GRP78 resulted in the mislocalization of PRC1 protein with abnormal cytokinesis in Fa2N-4 cells (Fig. 4H). Thus, GRP78 is essential for cytokinesis and results in increased multinucleated cells in HCC cells.

GRP78 regulation of PLK1 in HCC cells affects the midbody positioning of PLK1

Because GRP78 is mainly expressed in G2/M phase, we determined its role in mitosis and cytokinesis by measuring the expression of major mitotic kinases, including cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1/CDC2), budding uninhibited by benzimidazole 1 (BUB1), polo-like kinase (PLK1), and Aurora kinase B (Aurora B), in Fa2N-4 cells and SNU475 cells overexpressing GRP78. We found that overexpression of GRP78 increased the expression of PLK1 and Aurora B in these cells (Fig. 5A), whereas decreased expression of GRP78 by siGRP78 in HCC cells decreased PLK1 and Aurora B protein levels (Fig. 5B). Further, we determined that PLK1 expression correlated positively with GRP78 protein levels in various HCC cells (Fig. 5C). We also determined the effect of GRP78 on PLK1 protein stability. Following cycloheximide treatment, the half-life of PLK1 increased in GRP78-overexpressing HCC cells (Fig. 5D), whereas attenuation of GRP78 expression reduced the half-life of PLK1 in HCC cells (Fig. 5E). Transcription of PLK1 mRNA was not affected by changes in GRP78 expression in HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). To further elucidate the specific mechanism of PLK1 degradation, we treated the cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. The marked reduction in PLK1 protein levels caused by GRP78 depletion was effectively rescued upon MG132 treatment, indicating that PLK1 undergoes proteasome- dependent degradation (Fig. 5F). Using an anti-FLAG antibody in an IP assay with cells overexpressing GRP78-FLAG, we found that PLK1 interacted with GRP78 in HCC cells (Fig. 5G). These data collectively suggest that GRP78 may bind and stabilize PLK1 in HCC cells by protecting it from proteasome-mediated degradation.

PLK1 regulates mitosis and cytokinesis by its temporal enrichment at three specific subcellular locations—mitotic centrosomes, kinetochores, and the cytokinetic midbody (23). Inhibition of GRP78 led to the loss of PLK1 at the mitotic centrosomes in metaphase (Fig. 5G), and blocked the recruitment of PLK1 at the midbody, which is needed to regulate cytokinesis (Fig. 5H). We also observed co-localization of PLK1 and GRP78 in the midbody within the intercellular bridge in HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 7). Given GRP78’s localization to the midbody and its essential role in cytokinesis, we investigated the effect of GRP78 depletion on key cytokinesis regulators, including mitotic kinesin-like protein (MKLP1), Ras homolog family member A (RHOA), centrosomal protein 55 (CEP55), Anillin, Septin 2, Inner Centromere Protein (INCENP), and Protein Regulator of Cytokinesis 1 (PRC1). Although the expression of these midbody proteins was not dependent on GRP78 expression in HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 8), inhibiting GRP78 expression facilitated the midbody positioning of CEP55 (Fig. 5I) and PRC1 (Fig. 5J) to regulate cytokinesis.

DISCUSSION

HCC is often asymptomatic in its earlier stages, leading to diagnoses only after the disease has advanced. Furthermore, it is difficult to treat, resulting in poor survival rates. In cases of recurrence, few treatments are available. As current therapies have not improved patient outcomes, new drug targets for HCC are needed. Here, we used a 3D HCC cell culture system to identify novel drug targets that may have been overlooked in previous studies due to incomplete recapitulation of the tumor microenvironment. We identified glucose-regulated proteins GRP78 and GRP94, which were uniquely overexpressed in cells from a 3D culture system. However, the absence of GRP94 does not affect cell viability or the general expression of surface protein in isolated mammalian cells (24). Compared to GRP94, GRP78 has a wider variety of client proteins. It plays a pivotal role in the unfolded protein response associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress and serves as an anti-apoptotic and pro-proliferative regulator (25). A notable finding in our study was that overexpression of GRP78 in non-neoplastic tissue after surgery was associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Inhibition of GRP78 expression reduced the growth of HCC cells, whereas overexpression promoted hepatocyte proliferation. The role of GRP78 in cell proliferation is unknown; however, it has been suggested to contribute to growth control. As an increase in cell number is due to cell division—or cytokinesis, the last step of the cell cycle—we considered the role of GRP78 as a cell cycle regulator. The downregulation of GRP78 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells reduces the percentage of cells in the S phase and increases the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase. GRP78 can also influence cell cycle distribution in chondrocyte differentiation. Overexpression of GRP78 enhances S phase cells in chondrogenesis, whereas knockdown of GRP78 inhibits them (26). Moreover, GRP78 is a previously uncharacterized resident midbody protein along with INCENP and PRC1, suggesting that it is a cytokinesis protein (27). Here, we observed that GRP78 expression oscillated with changes in the cell cycle, peaking in the G2/M phase in hepatocytes and HCC cells.

GRP78 is typically found in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum but translocates to the cell surface (csGRP78, membrane GRP78) (28) or nucleus (29) under cellular stress. The csGRP78 interacts with various ligands to initiate signaling pathways that increase cell survival, enhance proliferation, increase angiogenesis, and promote resistance to chemotherapy (30). The nuclear translocation of GRP78/BiP promotes the adoption of an invasive phenotype as a transcriptional regulator in cancer (29).

Here, we identified a new role of GRP78 based on its distribution during the cell cycle. In HCC cells in G2/M arrest, cytoskeletal GRP78 increased, whereas csGRP78 decreased. Cytoskeletal GRP78 localized to midbodies with tubulin and actin during cytokinesis, and GRP78 prevented cytokinesis by interfering with the resident midbody protein PLK1 in HCC cells and hepatocytes.

PLK1, which is well-conserved across eukaryotes (31), associates transiently with several mitotic structures including the spindle poles, kinetochores, the central spindle, and the midbody (32). We found that overexpression of GRP78 led to elevated levels of PLK1 by blocking PLK1 degradation, which resulted in the failure of CEP55 (33) as well as PRC1 (34) recruitment at the midbody and led to mitotic defects such as lagging chromosomes and anaphase bridges. Thus, there were many more multinucleated cells in GRP78-positive cells vs. GRP78-negative cells in patient-derived HCC tissues via stabilization of PLK1 (Fig. 6). Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence that GRP78 is not only a biomarker of poor prognosis in HCC but also a functionally essential driver of tumor cell division and survival. Therefore, targeting GRP78 represents a novel and promising strategy for the treatment of HCC, particularly in overcoming resistance and enhancing the efficacy of existing therapies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and cultures

The HCC cell lines, including SNU449, SNU475, SNU387, SNU398, SNU898, Huh7, PLC/PRF/5, Huh6, and Hep3B, were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank. All HCC cells were maintained in RPMI (Welgene, Korea) or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Welgene, Korea) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (p/s; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). AMC-H1 and AMC-H2 HCC cells, provided by Prof. Kim (Asan Medical Center), were cultured in DMEM. Fa2N-4, a human immortalized hepatocyte cell line, was obtained from Xenotech (Lenexa, KS, USA) and first cultured in a serum-containing plating medium (K4000; Xenotech). To produce tumor spheroids, we seeded HCC cells at a density of 6 × 103 cells/well in 96-well round-bottom ULA microplates (Corning Life Sciences, Corning, NY, USA). All cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37°C.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Samples were provided by 80 patients from the ASAN Medical Center (Seoul, South Korea) with a diagnosis of liver cancer who were treated with follow-up in the clinic. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of ASAN Medical Center (Permit Number: S2014-1412-0008/2014-0897, Approval Date: Aug.14, 2024). The Institutional Review Board of ASAN Medical Center complies with related laws, including those of the International Council on Harmonisation of Technical Requirements, the Korean Good Clinical Practice Committee, and the Bioethics and Safety Act of Korea. Written informed consent for the use of tissues for research purposes was obtained from patients at the time of tumor specimen procurement.

Primary culture of HCC-derived cells

HCC specimens were obtained from liver cancer patients at Asan medical center. A portion of human HCC tumors acquired immediately after surgical resection was immersed in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (Gibco-ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Patient-derived primary HCC cell lines (17S-35372B, 17S-64007, 17S-68354, 17S-21612B, 27273, 101509, 103201, and 116261) were generated from the liver cancer tissues of eight Korean patients.

Proteomic analysis

HCCs were cultured in Monolayer (2D) and tumor spheroid (3D) systems in complete medium for 3 days. The 2D and 3D sample lysates from patient-derived primary cells were separated and analyzed using a nanoACQUITY UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) directly coupled to a Finnigan LCQ DECA ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). The samples were analyzed using an MS/MS spectra system (Thermo Quest, San Jose, CA, USA) and processed using SEQUEST software (Thermo Fisher).

Cell synchronization and drug treatment

For enrichment of cells in mitosis or G2, cells were first synchronized in the S phase for 18 h with 2 mM thymidine (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). The thymidine-containing medium was then removed, and cells were washed four times with fresh medium. To arrest cells in mitosis, we added nocodazole (100 ng/ml) purchased from Thermo Fisher (MA, USA) to cells for 3, 6, 9, and 12 h prior to harvest. To synchronization of cells in G1 phase, the cells were treated using L-minosine from Koa hoals seeds (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) treatment and washed twice with DPBS and released 3, 6, 12 and 24 h in fresh complete medium.

Subcellular fractionation

The cells were collected, washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, MO, USA). The samples were fractionated using the Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit for Cultured Cells (78840, Thermo Fisher, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After fractionation, the samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblot analysis with primary antibodies described in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot assay

Cells from resected tumors were lysed by RIPA buffer (CureBio, Seoul, Korea) for 30 min at 4°C, lysates were collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min, and the protein concentration was measured using the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, Cambridge, MA, USA). Equal amounts of proteins were separated electrophoretically on a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel. The proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using a Bio-Rad transfer system (Hercules, CA, USA) and blocked with 5% skim milk. Details of the primary antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, applied to the blots, and protein bands were detected using the LuminoGraph II system (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunoprecipitation (IP) analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher, Cambridge, MA, USA). The cell extracts (2 mg/500 μl) were immunoprecipitated using a PierceTM Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cambridge, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were incubated with anti-FLAG antibody (F7425, Thermo Fisher) at 4°C overnight, followed by an immunoblot analysis.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), washed in DPBS, and blocked in DPBS plus 10% Normal Goat Serum (NGS) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies, which are described in Supplementary Table 1. The samples were washed with DPBS, and the Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 633 secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, Cambridge, MA, USA) were added to the samples for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were stained using Hoechst 33342 (1:1000) purchased from Thermo Fisher (Cambridge, MA, USA). Washed samples were mounted, and fluorescent images were collected using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM II; LSM710A, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis

Cells were collected and washed once in DPBS, fixed in cold 70% ethanol overnight, and stored at 4°C. For analysis, cells were stained with FxCycleTM PI/RNase Staining Solution (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) and observed and analyzed on a CytoFLEX (Beckman, CA, USA).

Protein stability assays

After the cells were transfected with plasmid or siRNA, cell were treated with 30 ug/ml Cycloheximide;CHX for the indicated times. Then, cells were lysed and prepared for western blot assay. PLK1 levels relative to time point 0 are presented in all graph of CHX experiments.

Transfection with siRNAs

The cells were transfected with 500 nM siRNA/5 × 105 cells using the Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (ThermoFisher, Cambridge, MA, USA) with Opti-MEM, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 48 h. The siRNAs targeting GRP78 were purchased from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO, USA; L-004819-00-0005, SMARTpool: ON-TARGETplus GRP78 and GRP94 siRNA, USA).

Total RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using an RNeasy mini kit (74106, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from reverse transcription-polymerase chain using a cDNA synthesis kit (1708890, Bio-Rad, CA, USA). For real-time PCR, the reaction mixture composed with SYBR green (Applied Biosystems, MA, USA) was used, and the reactions were conducted using a ViiA 7 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). The threshold cycle (CT) is defined as the fractional cycle number at which the fluorescence passes the fixed threshold. CT values were normalized to those of β-actin and calculated according to the mathematical model.

Colony formation assays

For colony formation assays, 1 × 103 cells transfected with siCont and siGRP78 Huh7, SNU475, and Hep3B, and pCMV3 and GRP78-FLAG transfected into Fa2N-4 were transferred to 6-well plates. The medium was replaced every 3 days, starting the day after seeding. The cells were cultured for 14 days, and the plates were washed with DPBS and then stained with crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). The number of colonies was counted and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance between groups was determined using the Student’s t-test with a Newman-Keuls post-hoc test using Prism 5 (GraphPad, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
