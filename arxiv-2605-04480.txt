Geometric Milstein Scheme for Stochastic Differential Equations on SO(n) and SE(n)
Xi Wang, Victor Solo

Abstract
In the paper, we propose a higher-order geometry-preserving numerical method for stochastic differential equations (SDEs) evolving on the Lie groups SO(n) and SE(n). Most existing Lie group integrators rely on Magnus expansion of the exponential map, which makes the construction of higher-order stochastic schemes difficult. To overcome this limitation, we develop a tangent-space parameterization corrected Milstein method (TaSP-CM), extending the tangent space parameterization (TaSP) framework from Lie-group ODEs to the stochastic setting. Although TaSP is a well-established method for Lie ODEs, the extension to SDEs is non-trivial and requires new stochastic corrections that ensure both geometric consistency and higher-order accuracy. We prove that the proposed scheme achieves strong convergence of order 1 under both commutative and non-commutative noise. Numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical results and demonstrate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed method.

Over the past few decades, stochastic differential equations (SDEs) on Lie groups have found increasing application. Among these, the special orthogonal group

The broad range of applications involving SDEs on Lie groups have led to an increasing need for numerical methods that respect the underlying geometric constraints. In this paper, we consider the class of nonlinear autonomous Itô SDEs evolving on Lie groups
G=\mathrm{SO}(n),\mathrm{SE}(n)
, given by

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{X}\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{X}(t))dt+{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{X})dt+\mathbf{X}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{X}(t))dW_{j}(t),

(1)

\mathbf{X}\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{X})

N_{\mathbf{X}}G

1

\delta

\mathbf{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}(t)

When it is reduced to the flat Euclidean setting, i.e., when
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathsf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\equiv 0

When applied directly to Lie groups, where a global vector space structure is absent, these methods fail to preserve the underlying geometry and tend to drift off the manifold even over short time scales.

Some geometry-preserving schemes are proposed for the deterministic counterparts of Lie group SDEs, namely ordinary differential equations (ODEs) on Lie groups

;
23

8

1

\mathrm{SO}(n)

\mathrm{SE}(n)

Several prior works have introduced GPNS for SDEs on Lie groups based on the Magnus expansion. For instance,

2.1
Special Orthogonal Group SO(n)

In this subsection, we introduce the geometric and calculus facts on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
.

2.1.1
Geometry of SO(n)

We define the special orthogonal group

\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}(n)=\bigl\{\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\bigl|\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=I,\det\mathbf{R}=1\bigr\},

as the set of all real
n\times n
rotation matrices. The associated Lie algebra is the collection of skew‑symmetric matrices

\mathfrak{so}(n)=\bigl\{\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\bigl|\mathcal{Z}^{\top}=-\mathcal{Z}\bigr\},

which stands for the infinitesimal rotation and forms a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{so}(n)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the collection of all symmetric matrices

\mathfrak{so}(n)^{\perp}:=\{\mathsf{C}\in R^{n\times n}\mid\mathsf{C}^{T}=\mathsf{C}\}.

For a fixed rotation
\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
, the tangent and normal space are obtained by left‑multiplying the Lie algebra and the orthogonal complement, i.e.,

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{SO}(n)=\{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}\mid\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)\},\qquad N_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{SO}(n)=\{\mathbf{R}\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{C}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)^{\perp}\}.

The orthogonal projection of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
onto the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{SO}(n)
and normal space
N_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{SO}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle\POtg(A)={\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(A-\mathbf{R}A^{\top}\mathbf{R}\bigr),\qquad\POnm(A)={\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(A+\mathbf{R}A^{\top}\mathbf{R}\bigr).

To help readers distinguish geometrical objects, we adopt the following typographic convention throughout the paper: boldface symbols (e.g.,
\mathbf{R}
) represent points on the Lie groups; calligraphic symbols (e.g.,
\mathcal{Z}
) denote elements in tangent spaces; and sans‑serif symbols (e.g.,
\mathsf{C}
) indicate elements in normal spaces.

2.1.2
Calculus on SO(n)

Equipped with the inner product

g(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}):=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{Z}_{2}\bigr),\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}\in\mathfrak{so}(n),

\mathrm{SO}(n)
becomes a embedding Riemannian manifold in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The embedding Riemannian structure allows us to define intrinsic calculus operators on
\mathrm{SO}(n)

6
.

The Levi–Civita connection
\mathcal{\mathcal{L}^{O}}
acts as the intrinsic differentiation on tangent vector fields over
\mathrm{SO}(n)
that is compatible with metric. Formally, let
\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\mathbf{R})
and
\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
be two tangent vector fields over
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. The Levi–Civita connection of
\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}
along
\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}
is defined as the tangent projection of the Euclidean directional derivative,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{O}_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}(\mathbf{R})\bigr):=\POtg\bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}(\mathbf{R})\bigr)\bigr)=\mathbf{R}(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}+{\frac{1}{2}}[\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}]),

(2)

where
\nabla
is the Euclidean directional derivative, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{R}_{rs}}(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2})_{rs}\in T_{\mathbf{R}}{\mathrm{SO}(n)},

and the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{so}(n)
is defined as
[\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}]=\mathcal{Z}_{1}\mathcal{Z}_{2}-\mathcal{Z}_{2}\mathcal{Z}_{1}
.

Equation

2
illustrates that, in addition to the standard directional derivative, a correction term
{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{R}[\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}]
arises due to the non-commutative Lie algebra structure. In contrast, in Euclidean space, where vector fields commute, the differentiation reduces to the usual directional derivative. This contrast highlights a fundamental distinction between differentiation on the curved manifold
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and in flat Euclidean space. With a slight abuse of language, we refer to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{O}_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\bigl(\mathcal{Z}_{2}(\mathbf{R})\bigr):=\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}+{\frac{1}{2}}[\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}]

as the covariant derivative (CovD) of
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
along the direction
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
. Moreover, we also focus on the normal residual of
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\POnm\Big(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathcal{Z}_{2}(\mathbf{R})\bigr)\Big)={\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}\mathcal{Z}_{2}+\mathcal{Z}_{2}\mathcal{Z}_{1}),

where we refer the following bilinear map
\mathsf{S}^{O}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2})(\mathbf{R})={\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}\mathcal{Z}_{2}+\mathcal{Z}_{2}\mathcal{Z}_{1})
as the second fundamental form (SFF)
15
.

2.2
Special Euclidean Group SE(n)

The special Euclidean group
\mathrm{SE}(n)
, the associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{se}(n)
, and the normal component
\mathfrak{se}(n)^{\perp}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SE}(n)=

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\mathbf{E}=(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\Bigm|\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{se}(n)=

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\mathcal{V}\ =(\mathcal{Z},v)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}&v\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\Bigm|\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n),v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{se}(n)^{\perp}=

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\mathsf{U}\ =(\mathsf{C},\mathsf{b})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{C}&0\\
\mathsf{b}^{\top}&a\end{bmatrix}\Bigm|\mathsf{C}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)^{\perp},\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},a\in\mathbb{R}\Bigr\}.

Here
\mathbf{R}
is the rotational component and
\mathbf{p}
the translational component of a rigid-body pose
\mathbf{E}
,
\mathcal{Z}
and
v
give the corresponding infinitesimal rotation and translation. For a fixed pose
\mathbf{E}=(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p})\in\mathrm{SE}(n)
, the tangent and normal spaces are
T_{\mathbf{E}}\mathrm{SE}(n)=\{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}\mid\mathcal{V}\in\mathfrak{se}(n)\}
and
N_{\mathbf{E}}\mathrm{SE}(n)=\{\mathbf{E}\mathsf{U}\mid\mathsf{U}\in\mathfrak{se}(n)^{\perp}\}

Writing a matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix},

the orthogonal projections to the tangent and normal spaces are

\displaystyle\PEtg(A)=\begin{bmatrix}\POtg(A_{11})&\mathbf{a}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\PEnm(A)=\begin{bmatrix}\POnm(A_{11})&0\\
b^{\top}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, equipping
\mathfrak{se}(n)
with the Riemannian metric,
\langle\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2}\rangle={\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{Z}_{2}\bigr)+v_{1}^{\top}v_{2}
, for

\mathbf{E}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SE}(n),\qquad\mathcal{V}_{i}(\mathbf{E})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\mathbf{E})&v_{i}(\mathbf{E})\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad i=1,2,

the Levi–Civita connection on
\mathrm{SE}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{E}_{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{1}}\bigl(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)=\PEtg\bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{1}}\bigl(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)\bigr)=\mathbf{E}\mathcal{D}^{E}_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)

and the CovD on
\mathrm{SE}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{E}_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}[\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}]\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{V}_{1}}v_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}_{1}v_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

(3)

Finally, the SFF reads
\mathsf{S}^{E}(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2})(\mathbf{E}):=(\mathsf{S}^{O}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}),0)
.

2.3
Notation on Time Differences

To better describe the piecewise solution of SDEs on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, we introduce the following time difference notation. For an integer
m
, a step size
\delta
, and time
t>0
, we define the clipped time
t_{{m^{*}}}=\min\{t,m\delta\}
and set

\displaystyle{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t=t_{{(m+1)^{*}}}-t_{{m^{*}}},\quad F_{m}=F(m\delta)

\displaystyle F_{{m^{*}}}(t)=F(t_{m^{*}}),\quad{\Delta^{*}_{m}}F(t)=F_{{(m+1)^{*}}}-F_{{m^{*}}},

for any (deterministic or stochastic) process
F(t)
. In addition, for Wiener process
W_{j}
and
W_{j^{\prime}}
, we denote the double Itô integral within the clipped time as

\displaystyle{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}(t)=\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{s_{1}}dW_{j}(s_{1})dW_{j^{\prime}}(s_{2}).

When
j=j^{\prime}
, the double Itô integral can be simplified as
{\frac{1}{2}}\big(({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})^{2}-{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t\big)
.

Problem Statement

In this section, we formalize the Itô SDEs on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathrm{SE}(n)
. We then define the notion of strong convergence for GPNS and identify key difficulties in designing GPNS schemes on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathrm{SE}(n)
.

3.1
Stochastic Differential Equations on Lie Groups

Let
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
An Itô SDE on
G
can be written in the following Itô form

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{X}\mathcal{B}_{s}(\mathbf{X}(t))dt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\nabla_{\mathbf{X}\mathcal{B}_{j}}\bigl(\mathbf{X}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)\Big)dt+\mathbf{X}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{X})dW_{j}(t),

(4)

where the coefficients
\mathcal{B}_{s},\mathcal{B}_{j}\colon G\to\mathfrak{g}
are smooth
\mathfrak{g}
-valued vector fields and
\{W_{j}(t)\}_{j=1}^{d}
are independent standard Wiener processes. The middle term is the usual Itô correction, expressed as a Euclidean directional derivative
14
. Following the decomposition in
section

2
, we split the Itô correction into its tangent component (in
\mathfrak{g}
) and its normal component (in
\mathfrak{g}^{\perp}
) via the CovD
\mathcal{D}^{G}
and the SFF
\mathcal{S}^{G}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{X}):=\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathbf{X})+{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j}\mathcal{D}^{G}_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)\in\mathfrak{g},\quad\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{X}):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathsf{S}^{G}(\mathcal{B}_{j},\mathcal{B}_{j})(\mathbf{X})\in\mathfrak{g}^{\perp}.

Equation (
4
) then becomes

\displaystyle d\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{X}\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{X}(t))dt+{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{X})dt+\mathbf{X}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{X}(t))dW_{j}(t).

(5)

For
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
with coefficients
\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{R})\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
, we have
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{2}
. Hence,
eq.

5
becomes

\displaystyle d\mathbf{R}(t)=\mathbf{R}\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{R})dt+{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{R}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{2}(\mathbf{R})dt+\mathbf{R}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{R})dW_{j}(t).

(6)

Furthermore, for
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{E}=(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p})\in\mathrm{SE}(n)
with each coefficient splitting as

\mathcal{B}_{j}=(\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{R}}_{j},\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{p}}_{j})
, we have
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{E})=(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{2},0)
. Consequently,
eq.

5
becomes the coupled system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\mathbf{R}(t)=\mathbf{R}(t)\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{R}}_{I}(\mathbf{E})dt+{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{R}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl(\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{R}}_{j}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)^{2}dt+\mathbf{R}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{R}}_{j}(\mathbf{E})dW_{j}(t),\\
d\mathbf{p}(t)=\mathbf{R}(t)\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{p}}_{I}(\mathbf{E})dt+\mathbf{R}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}^{\mathbf{p}}_{j}(\mathbf{E})dW_{j}(t).\end{cases}

(7)

The Itô formulations in
eqs.

4
,
6
and
7
highlight a fundamental distinction between Euclidean SDEs and Lie ODEs. In addition to the tangential drift and diffusion terms, a SDE on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
or
\mathrm{SE}(n)
contains a normal component
{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\mathbf{X})
. The normal drifting term, known as pinning drift
31
, tends to pull the SDE away from
G
and introduces a fundamental difficulty for GPNS on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathrm{SE}(n)
.

3.2
Geometry‑Preserving Numerical Scheme

In this paper, we aim to develop a geometry‑preserving numerical scheme (GPNS) that solve
eq.

5
on
G=\mathrm{SO}(n),\mathrm{SE}(n)
with strong convergence rate
1
. In a GPNS, the interval
[0,T]
is partitioned into
M
equal sub‑intervals of length
\delta=T/M
. Starting from the initial condition
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{0}=\mathbf{X}(0)\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
, a GPNS evaluates the drift and diffusion terms at time
t=m\delta
and constructs a local approximation on the interval
[m\delta,(m+1)\delta]
. To preserve the geometry, the local approximation is required to remain on
G
for every realization of the diffustion noise.

A GPNS is said to achieve strong convergence rate (SCR) of
p
, if there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\delta
, such that the strong error

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(t)-\mathbf{X}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\Bigr]^{1/2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta^{p}),

where
\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

3.3
Main Difficulties

A central challenge in the design of high-order geometry-preserving numerical schemes is to simultaneously enforce the nonlinear manifold constraints and achieve a strong convergence order comparable to that of the corresponding Euclidean methods. The following two representative examples are presented to illustrate this tension.

3.3.1
Euclidean Milstein: Strong Order without Geometry

In Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Euclidean Milstein method achieves
1
SCR for nonlinear SDEs by adding the
3/2
-order Itô-Taylor term to the Euler-Maruyama increment
19
. With step size
\delta
and Wiener increments
{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}(t)=W_{j}(t)-W_{j}(m\delta)
, the update rule of the Euclidean Milstein scheme for
eq.

5
reads

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{m}+\mathcal{B}_{I}(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{m}){\Delta^{*}_{m}}t+{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{m})

Although the Euclidean Milstein scheme preserves the SCR

when applied to Lie SDEs, two ingredients make the algorithm violate the geometric constraint. First, the pinning drift

contains a second-fundamental-form component

3.3.2
Magnus-based Integrators: Geometric without Strong Order

A widely used strategy for preserving group structure is to use the Magnus expansion that pulls the SDE back to the Lie algebra. Writing the state as
X(t)=X_{0}\exp\bigl(\Omega(t)\bigr)
with

\Omega(t)\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\exp

5

\mathfrak{g}
-valued SDE

\mathrm{d}\Omega=A(\Omega)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Gamma_{j}(\Omega)\mathrm{d}W_{j},\qquad\Gamma_{j}=\operatorname{dexp}^{-1}_{-\Omega}(\mathcal{B}_{j}).

Here,

is a drifting matrix that depends on the drift directions
\mathcal{B}_{I}
and
\mathcal{B}_{j}

\operatorname{dexp}_{-\Omega}^{-1}
denotes the inverse differential of the exponential map

\displaystyle\operatorname{dexp}_{-\Omega}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{j})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{k}}{k!}\bigl(\operatorname{ad}_{\Omega}\bigr)^{k}(\mathcal{B}_{j})\qquad\operatorname{ad}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{B}_{j}):=[\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{j}],

(8)

with
\beta_{k}
the Bernoulli numbers. In practice one often approximates the
\mathfrak{g}

appears simultaneously inside the differentiation operator and in the term being differentiated, so finite truncation may undermines the accuracy of the scheme. Consequently, one must keep the entire series when implementing the Milstein method, which is generally intractable.

Taken together, these two examples reveal the core challenge in constructing a geometry-preserving Milstein scheme over Lie groups. To achieve SCR
1

3/2

\mathrm{SO}(n)
a nontrivial problem.

4
TaSP-CM Scheme on SO(n)

To overcome the challenge, we propose a geometric-preserving Milstein method on
\mathrm{SO}(n)

4.1

8
Conclusions

In this work, we study the numerical integration of geometric stochastic differential equations on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathrm{SE}(n)
and focus on a long-standing gap in which no computationally tractable scheme simultaneously preserves the underlying geometry and attains strong convergence order greater than
1/2
. To bridge this gap, we propose the Tangent-Space-Parametrization Corrected Milstein (TaSP-CM) method, which combines a corrected Euclidean Milstein increment with a fourth-order TaSP reconstruction. We show that the TaSP-CM scheme preserves the geometry and achieves strong convergence order
1
under both commutative and non-commutative noise, thereby extending the classical Milstein method to curved manifolds. Numerical experiments further illustrate the theoretical guarantees.

The proposed method expands the class of high-accuracy, structure-preserving integrators for simulation and inference on Lie groups, with potential applications in robotics, computer vision, molecular dynamics, and stochastic control. In future work, we will consider the extensions of our TaSP-CM scheme to other matrix manifolds, including the Stiefel and affine groups. In addition, we will attempt to adapt of the TaSP-CM framework to Riemannian diffusion models and neural SDEs, where higher-order accuracy and strict geometry preservation may enhance training stability and sample quality.

Lemma 12

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{m}+{\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{I,m}}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)+{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{R}_{m}}({\mathcal{B}_{j,m}})^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{j,m}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}(t)+\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}{\mathbf{R}_{m}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\nabla_{j,j^{\prime}}^{m}}{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}+\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}

where
\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}
collects all higher-order residuals.

Compared with
eq.

11
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(t)-\mathbf{R}(m\delta)=\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{\delta}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{\delta}(m\delta)+\sum_{i=1}^{5}A^{(i)}_{m}+\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime},

(23)

where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A^{(1)}_{m}=({\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{I,m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{I,m}}){\Delta^{*}_{m}}t\\
A^{(2)}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}({\mathbf{R}_{m}}({\mathcal{B}_{j,m}})^{2}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}({\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{j,m}})^{2}){\Delta^{*}_{m}}t\\
A^{(3)}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}({\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{j,m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{j,m}})){\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}\\
A^{(4)}_{m}=\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}({\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\nabla_{j,j^{\prime}}^{m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\nabla}_{j,j^{\prime}}^{m}})({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}})\\
A^{(5)}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}({\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\nabla_{j,j}^{m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\nabla}_{j,j}^{m}})(({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})^{2}-{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t).\end{cases}

Summing
eq.

23
over
m
, we have.

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{\delta}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{0}+\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\sum_{i=1}^{5}A^{(i)}_{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle{2}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t)]\leq 6\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(i)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]+6\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}\big\|^{2}\Bigr].

(24)

Step 2: Estimating
A^{(i)}_{m}
.

We first estimate
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(1)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]
. From Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(1)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\|{\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{I,m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{I,m}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\|{\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{I,m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{I,m}}\|^{2}.

where
T
denotes the time horizon.
From
2
,
\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{R})
is Lipschitz continuous, and hence so is
\mathbf{R}\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{R})
.
Therefore, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{R}_{m}}{\mathcal{B}_{I,m}}-{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}}{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{I,m}}\|^{2}\leq C\|\mathbf{R}_{m}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{m}\|^{2}\leq C\|\bar{\alpha}(t_{m})\|^{2}+C\|\bar{\zeta}(t_{m})\|^{2}

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(1)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bar{\alpha}(t_{m})\|^{2}\big]{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)+C\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bar{\zeta}(t_{m})\|^{2}\big]{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)

Since
\bar{\alpha}(t)
and
\bar{\zeta}(t)
are non-decreasing, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bar{\alpha}(t_{m})\|^{2}\big]{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)\leq T\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)],\quad\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bar{\zeta}(t_{m})\|^{2}\big]{\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(1)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}+CT\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)].

(25)

Follow the similar procedure, we can also obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}A^{(i)}_{m}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}+CT\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)],\quad i=2,3,4,5.

(26)

Substituting
eqs.

25
and
26
into
eq.

24
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t)]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta}^{2}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}+C\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)]+\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\|\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}\|^{2}\big].

Applying Grönwall’s inequality (
7
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta^{2}}(t)]\leq e^{CT}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)]+e^{CT}\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq T}\|\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}\|^{2}\big].

(27)

Step 3: Estimating
\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}
.

From
lemma

2
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\bar{\alpha}^{2}(T)]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
. It is sufficient to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})

We prove the above claim in the following proof. For
j\in[d]

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{0}f=\nabla_{\mathbf{R}B_{I}(\mathbf{R})}f+{\tfrac{1}{2}}\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathsf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{R})}f+{\tfrac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{R})[\mathbf{R}\mathcal{B}_{j},\mathbf{R}\mathcal{B}_{j}],\quad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{j}f=\nabla_{\mathbf{R}\mathcal{B}_{j}}f.

and integral differential operators

\displaystyle I_{\alpha,\beta}^{m}(f)=\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{s_{1}}fd\chi_{\beta}(s_{1})d\chi_{\alpha}(s_{2}),

\displaystyle I_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{m}(f)=\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{s_{1}}\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{s_{2}}fd\chi_{\gamma}(s_{1})d\chi_{\beta}(s_{2})d\chi_{\alpha}(s_{3}),

where
d\chi_{0}=ds
and
d\chi_{j}=dW_{j}
. Accoriding to
12
, the residual of the Itô-Taylor expansion
\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}
is written as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}+\varepsilon_{m}^{(2)^{\prime}},\quad\varepsilon_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}=I_{0,0}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{0}\mathfrak{L}_{0}(\mathbf{R})\big)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}^{(2)^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}I_{j,0}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{j}\mathfrak{L}_{0}(\mathbf{R})\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}I_{0,j}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{0}\mathfrak{L}_{j}(\mathbf{R})\big)+\sum_{j=0}^{d}\sum_{j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime}=1}^{d}I_{j,j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime}}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{j}\mathfrak{L}_{j^{\prime}}\mathfrak{L}_{j^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{R})\big).

Under
1
and
2
and the standard moment estimates for multiple Itô integrals
12
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\varepsilon_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}\|\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}),

and the partial sum
\sum_{m}\varepsilon_{m}^{(2)^{\prime}}
is a martingale with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\varepsilon_{m}^{(2)^{\prime}}\|\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}).

Applying
lemmas

11
and
12
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Bigl\|\sum_{m=0}^{{\lfloor\frac{t}{\delta}\rfloor}}\varepsilon_{m}^{\prime}\Bigr\|^{2}\Bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(28)

Substituting
eq.

28
into
eq.

27

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{\zeta^{2}}(t)]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

which completes the proof.
∎

D.1
Moment Estimate of Double Itô integral

Lemma 13

(
12
)
.

For
0\leq t\leq T
and all
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[d]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}(t)]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[({I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}(t))^{2n}\bigr]=\bigl(2(2n-1)e^{T}\bigr)^{2n}(\Delta_{m^{*}}t)^{2n}.

Lemma 14
.

For every index
j\neq j\in[d]
, and
l\in[d]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}(t){\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}(t)]=0,\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

Proof.

If
l\notin\{j,j^{\prime}\}
the claim follows immediately from independence. So, it remains to treat
l=j
or
l=j^{\prime}
. Recall the identity

{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}+{I_{j^{\prime},j}^{{m^{*}}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}\cdot{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}}.

Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}[{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}\cdot{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}}\cdot{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l^{\prime}}]=0.

so
\mathbb{E}[{I_{j^{\prime},j}^{{m^{*}}}}\Delta_{m^{*}}W_{j^{\prime}}]=0
implies
\mathbb{E}[{I_{j^{\prime},j}^{{m^{*}}}}\Delta_{m^{*}}W_{j^{\prime}}]=0
. W.l.o.g, we assume
l=j^{\prime}
.

When
l=j^{\prime}
, we define the process
\Phi_{j,m}(s):=\int_{t_{m}}^{s}dW_{j}=dW_{j}(s)-dW_{j}(t_{m^{*}})
. Conditioning on
W_{j}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}}\cdot{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}\mid W_{j}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}dW_{j^{\prime}}(s_{2})\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\Phi_{j,m}(s_{2})dW_{j^{\prime}}(s_{2})\right].

Applying the Itô isometry (
lemma

5
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}}\cdot{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}\mid W_{j}]=\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\Phi_{j,m}(s)ds.

Finally taking the total expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j^{\prime}}\cdot{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}]=\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{j,m}(s)]ds=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 15
.

For every index
j\neq j\in[d]
,
l\in[d]
, and time
0\leq t\leq T
, we have

1.

\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}(t){\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}^{2}(t)]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})
;

2.

\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}(t){\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}^{4}(t)]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4})
.

Proof.

Adopting the notation
\Phi_{j,m}(s)
from
lemma

14
, we also assume
l=j
, w.l.o.g..

Case (a):
Conditioning on
W_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{2}|W_{j}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}(\Phi_{j,m}(s_{2}))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})dW_{j^{\prime}}(s_{2})\Bigr)^{2}\Big].

Using
lemma

5
again, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{2}|W_{j}]=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}(\Phi_{j,m}^{2}(t))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})^{2}ds.

Taking the total expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{j,m}^{2}(t))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})^{2}\big]ds.

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{j,m}^{2}(s))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}(s))^{2}\big]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{j,m}^{2}(s))(\Phi_{j,m}(s)+W_{j}(t)-W_{j}(s))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3(s-t_{{m^{*}}})^{2}+(s-t_{{m^{*}}})(t-s)\leq 4\delta^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{2}]\leq\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}4\delta^{2}ds\leq 4\delta^{3}.

Case (b):
Similarly, conditioning on
W_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{4}|W_{j}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}(\Phi_{j,m}(s_{2}))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j})^{2}dW_{j^{\prime}}(s_{2})\Bigr)^{2}\Big],

Taking the total expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{4}]=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{j,m}(s))^{2}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}(s))^{4}\big]ds.

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{j,m}^{2}(s))({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}(s))^{4}\big]\leq

\displaystyle 120(s-t_{{m^{*}}})^{2}+24(s-t_{{m^{*}}})(t-s)^{2}\leq 144\delta^{3},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}^{2}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{j}^{4}]\leq\int_{t_{m^{*}}}^{t_{(m+1)^{*}}}144\delta^{3}ds\leq 144\delta^{4}.

Combining cases (a) and (b), we complete our proof.
∎

D.2
Moment Estimate of Approximation Error

Lemma 16
.

For any
h>1
,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{4}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4})
.

Proof.

From
lemma

4
, we know that

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{4}]=\frac{1}{2\pi}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)^{4}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r}\Big(a_{j,r}b_{j^{\prime},r}-a_{j,r}b_{j^{\prime},r}\Big)\Bigr].

Denote
X_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\Big(a_{j,r}b_{j^{\prime},r}-a_{j,r}b_{j^{\prime},r}\Big)
. Since
\{a_{j,r},b_{j,r}\}
are independent normal Gaussian variables, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{r}]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[X_{r}^{2}]=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}[a_{j,r}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[b_{j^{\prime},r}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[a_{j^{\prime},r}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[b_{j,r}^{2}]\bigr)=\frac{2}{r^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{r}^{4}]=\frac{1}{r^{4}}\left(\mathbb{E}[a_{j,r}^{4}b_{j^{\prime},r}^{4}]+\mathbb{E}[a_{j^{\prime},r}^{4}b_{j,r}^{4}]+6\mathbb{E}[a_{j,r}^{2}a_{j^{\prime},r}^{2}b_{j,r}^{2}b_{j^{\prime},r}^{2}]\right)=\frac{24}{r^{4}}.

We define
S_{h}:=\sum_{r>h}X_{r}
. Since
X_{r}
are independent, it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{h}^{4}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[(X_{r})^{4}]+6\sum_{r<s}\mathbb{E}[(X_{r})^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[(X_{s})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\big(\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\frac{24}{r^{4}}+6\sum_{r<s}\frac{2}{r^{2}}\cdot\frac{2}{s^{2}}\big)\leq 24\big(\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{4}}+\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\big(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big)^{2}\big)=\mathcal{O}(1).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{4}]=\frac{1}{2\pi}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)^{4}\mathbb{E}[S_{h}^{4}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}).

∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
h>1
. Then for every index
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[d]
, and
l\in[d]
, we have

1.

\mathbb{E}[\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}]=0
,

2.

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})
,

3.

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{4}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4})
.

Proof.

Case (a):
Because every
a_{j,r}
and
b_{j,r}
is independent of
\Delta_{m^{*}}W_{l}
, the random variables
\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}
and
\Delta_{m^{*}}W_{l}
are independent, therefore

\mathbb{E}[\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}]\mathbb{E}[{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l}]=0.

Case (b):
From the independence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}]\mathbb{E}[({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{2}].

Lemma

4
gives
\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})

, which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}}{\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}){\Delta^{*}_{m}}t=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}).

Case (c):
Using the same independence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{4}]=\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}]\mathbb{E}[({\Delta^{*}_{m}}W_{l})^{4}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})({\Delta^{*}_{m}}t)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}).

Combining cases (a)-(c), we complete the proof.
∎

.

Proof.

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta{I_{j,j^{\prime}}^{{m^{*}}}})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})
. Applying Lemma

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
3

Proof.

To find the normal adjustment, we seek a normal element

\mathsf{U}=(\mathsf{C},\mathsf{b})\in\mathfrak{se}(n)^{\perp}

such that

I+\mathcal{V}+\mathsf{U}=\begin{bmatrix}I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathsf{C}&v\\
\mathsf{b}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SE}(n).

The group constraint imposes conditions that

I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathsf{C}\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\qquad\mathsf{b}^{\top}=0.

Therefore, from
lemma

1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}=\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}^{\top}\mathcal{Z}}-I,

(34)

Hence,

\mathsf{U}=(\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}^{\top}\mathcal{Z}}-I,0)

is the normal adjustment for TaSP over
\mathrm{SE}(n)
.

Now we parametrize the solution of (
7
) as

\mathbf{E}(t)=\mathbf{E}_{0}\bigl(I+\mathcal{V}(t)+\mathsf{U}(\mathcal{V}(t))\bigr).

Differentiating on both sides gives

d\mathbf{E}(t)=\mathbf{E}_{0}\bigl(d\mathcal{V}(t)+d\mathsf{U}(t)\bigr).

Because
\mathsf{U}(t)\in\mathfrak{se}(n)^{\perp}
, projection onto the tangent space

T_{\mathbf{E}_{0}}\mathrm{SE}(n)=\mathbf{E}_{0}\mathfrak{se}(n)
eliminates the normal component

\PEtg(d\mathbf{E}(t))=\mathbf{E}_{0}d\mathcal{V}(t).

Applying the projection operator to the SDE (
7
) yields

\PEtg(d\mathbf{E}(t)\bigr)=\Bigl(\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{E}(t))dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{E}(t))dW_{j}(t)\Bigr).

Left‐multiplying by
\mathbf{E}_{0}^{-1}
gives the dynamics for the tangent motion
\mathcal{V}(t)
, i.e.,

d\mathcal{V}(t)=\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{E}(t))dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{E}(t))dW_{j}(t).

Writing
\mathcal{B}_{I}(\mathbf{E})=(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{E}),\mathcal{B}_{I}^{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{E}))
,
\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathbf{E})=(\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{E}),\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{E}))
for
j\in[d],

we finally obtain the coupled SDEs

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\mathcal{Z}(t)&=\mathcal{B}_{I}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{E}(t))dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{E}(t))dW_{j}(t),\\
dv(t)&=\mathcal{B}_{I}^{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{E}(t))dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{E}(t))dW_{j}(t).\end{cases}

which completes the proof.
