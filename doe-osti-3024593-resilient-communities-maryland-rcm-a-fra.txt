4 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The primary objective of the Resilient Communities, Maryland (RCM) project and its
benefit to the public was to create a community-driven energy resilience framework that
can be replicated across Maryland to enhance public safety and improve preparedness
and recovery during hazardous events and extended power outages. To accomplish
this, the RCM research conducted from February 2023 – July 2025 employed a
Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) framework, grounded in A.C.E
Impact Trailway Methodologies, to understand community perspectives and underscore
the importance of community involvement in energy resilience decisions. Integrating
CBPR approaches (demonstrated in Figures 1 and 2 in Exhibit A1) into energy
resilience planning resulted in greater effectiveness and efficiency in understanding the
impacts of energy infrastructure disruptions in each of the three jurisdictions.

While many people value community engagement, finding the best ways to gather
meaningful community feedback can be difficult. The RCM research and its associated
Framework provide a clear plan that planners and policymakers can use to collect
helpful information for prioritizing resilience efforts. The research is relevant, timely, and
flexible enough to be adapted to different communities, contexts, and concerns, making
it valuable not only for Maryland but also for communities across the United States.
Simultaneously, grounded by the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), the activities
developed are specific enough to offer a coherent and practical structure for community
engagement. The project team has had a rare opportunity to develop a conceptual
research framework and field-test it within a single project, resulting in a stronger
outcome than if the team had tried to combine insights from different projects later. The
work demonstrates how integrating utility electricity planning, community planning, and
research can foster opportunities for community participation in energy resilience
decision-making. This project benefits the public by simplifying decisions about backup
energy for people who are not energy industry experts, encouraging greater public
involvement, and providing additional support for informed decisions once made. It also
promotes the deployment of distributed energy resources (DERs) across the U.S.,
emphasizing its economic benefits such as improving grid efficiency and reliability,
enhancing community energy resilience through reliable backup power, and improving
the quality of local decision-making by encouraging consideration of factors beyond
traditional cost-benefit analysis.

The RCM project contributed to the field by integrating CBPR approaches into energy
resilience planning. The combination of qualitative community-driven data and
quantitative utility outage data improves both the effectiveness and efficiency of
assessing the impacts of disruptions to energy infrastructure. Another important
contribution was the creation of a repeatable framework for evaluating and modeling
energy resilience metrics. Additionally, the RCM research project promoted community
engagement, resulting in more equitable stakeholder participation and improved
decision-making related to siting resilient power infrastructure through microgrid
activities.

5 BACKGROUND
Access to electricity is crucial for economic growth and prosperity (Bie et al., 2017).
Power outages can interrupt access to essential services, including medical care,
childcare, shelter, information, food, and other critical supplies (Clark et al., 2022;
Bawaneh et al., 2019). These disruptions can be especially devastating for individuals
who rely heavily on these services and cannot afford to spend extra time or money to
regain access during an outage (Mitsova et al., 2021; Peterson et al., 2024). Resilience,
as it applies to the RCM research, is defined as “a system's ability to anticipate, prepare
for, and adapt to changing conditions and withstand, respond to, and recover quickly
from disruptions through sustainable, adaptable, and holistic planning and technical
solutions” (Hotchkiss & Dane, 2019, p.8; Clark et al., 2022). Consequently, resilience
energy planning has gained increasing attention in recent years, with distributed energy
resources (DERs)1 offering a means by which to strategically deploy flexible, localized,
and resilient energy solutions that enhance reliability and reduce costs (Cicilio et al.,
2021). Many efforts in the literature have aimed to measure the value of energy
resilience in the utility sector, emphasizing the importance of assessing vulnerability to
disruptions during power outages, supporting risk-based decision-making, guiding
infrastructure planning and operations, and quantifying recovery and restoration
capabilities (Jeffers et al., 2023; Jeffers et al., 2020; Jeffers et al., 2018; Leddy et al.,
2023; Stout et al., 2019). However, while the costs of extended power outages include
compounded community hazards, economic and service disruptions, as well as public
health risks (Ericson et al., 2022), measures by which to quantify the value of avoiding
such outages have yet to be fully standardized.

5.1 Gaps in the Literature
The extant literature has primarily focused on measuring and valuing resilience in the
power sector (at the utility level), including grid modernization, with limited research
conducted on resilience in energy planning at the community level. For instance,
Hotchkiss and Dane (2019) emphasized the need for a systematic approach to energy
resilience that involves community feedback. Besides, the reliability metrics (Table 1 in
Exhibit B) commonly used in the electric utility industry for transmission and distribution
have limitations when assessing resilience (Cicilio et al., 2021). These metrics overlook
key factors such as regional disparities, policy regulations, socioeconomic influences,
system configurations, customer density, and hazard exposure. This is perhaps
because energy reliability – a far more established concept than energy resilience –
primarily addresses routine or short-term issues and the unexpected loss of system
components due to credible contingencies (Cicilio et al., 2021). Additionally, traditional
reliability metrics overlook major outages caused by unforeseen or high-impact events,
such as major event days (MEDs) – i.e., the very scenarios in which the concept of
resilience is most relevant. While resilience metrics are often used to measure

resilience, the two concepts are not synonymous – while a power system may be highly
reliable, it may not necessarily be resilient. These established reliability metrics solely
focus on the performance of the grid and do not reflect the priorities of community
members who depend on the grid to meet their basic needs (Peterson et al., 2023;
Hotchkiss et al., 2019; Day & Simcock, 2016).

Additionally, existing frameworks for combining metrics into actionable resilience
assessments that do incorporate stakeholder engagement tend to focus on stakeholder
engagement with experts rather than community members (Jeffers et al., 2020; Petit &
Vargas, 2020; Anderson et al., 2019). One area in these resilience assessment
frameworks where community participants can offer valuable insights is in metrics that
measure the impacts of power outages. Recent reviews of energy resilience metrics
have highlighted access to services as an emerging energy-related measure that can
inform both infrastructure planning and resilience, ultimately benefiting local
communities. This is because community involvement in energy resilience planning
creates ownership and improves decision-making (Fry et al., 2023; González, 2019). A
community-driven approach to energy resilience emphasizes the role of energy-enabled
infrastructure, as demonstrated by the consistent provision of essential services within a
neighborhood for its residents (McGookin et al., 2021; Ross & Day, 2022). Given that
access to services changes dynamically over time, as various service locations become
available or unavailable, it is crucial to measure the cumulative impact of disruptions at
different service-providing locations at the household or neighborhood level, which can
be aggregated by individual service categories (e.g., medical services) or by all services
(Hart et al., 2024). Addressing routine disruptions and extreme events helps create a
stable and resilient system. However, repairing and restoring systems affected by MEDs
can be more complicated, mainly because outages tend to last longer than those
caused by reliability issues. Resilience approaches to energy planning recognize that
severe impacts on human well-being can result from infrequent but high-impact events
(Peterson et al., 2023). Effective community resilience planning, therefore, must
understand societal needs and ensure these needs are met (Hart et al., 2024; DeSalvo
et al., 2014).

Against this backdrop, the RCM research developed a community-focused energy
resilience framework, which was implemented across three Maryland jurisdictions:
Baltimore (urban), Montgomery County (suburban), and the Eastern Shore (rural). The
research highlights the fact that studies on the interdependence between electricity and
other social, economic, and infrastructure systems have developed metrics, such as
social burden, that better measure the benefits of resilience investments (Wachtel et al.,
2022). Although these metrics incorporate social science methods and community
participation to validate approaches, they have not yet been integrated into community
engagement processes for energy planning. To address this, the project aimed to
connect research with planning by collecting community feedback on hypothetical
energy resilience investments through interactive discussions that can be adapted for
use in many communities. The project focused on critical service access, a metric
designed to help decision-makers identify resilience investments that maximize
community access to essential services (Conway & Stewart, 2019; Conway et al.,

2018). Feedback from community members was used to improve both the interactive
engagement tools and the critical service access metric itself (see Exhibit A2). For
instance, input from rural residents led to expanding the distance radius used to
calculate critical service access, which better reflects the longer distances residents
may travel for services (Conway et al., 2018). Empowering communities involves
transferring decision-making authority and resources to them, highlighting the
importance of community perspectives in energy planning decision-making processes
(Lovell et al., 2024; Dongier et al., 2002). To achieve this, the specific goals for this
project were to:
 • Develop and refine a community-driven framework for measuring community and
 energy resilience.
 • Use this framework to identify priority sites (such as geographic areas, facility
 types, types of services offered by facilities, or other criteria identified through
 research) for implementing community energy resilience across the state.
 • Expand the framework by sharing research results through MEA's energy
 resilience funding program.

5.2 Contributions to the Body of Knowledge
Consistent with the idea that energy resilience is a vital component of societal and
community resilience, our work emphasizes the social benefits of resilient energy
systems, which help maintain essential services such as food, water, and shelter (Bie et
al., 2017). Energy solutions that aim to maximize access to services or ensure
continuous power in accordance with community-defined needs, rather than simply
increasing the total energy supplied, can bolster community resilience and support
distributive services across various communities (Lovell et al., 2024). Engaging
communities in siting these energy solutions ensures that community-specific needs are
considered during the planning process (Clark et al., 2022). This work also contributes
to efforts to measure the connection between community needs and energy system
resilience. By ensuring that critical services—and the electrical infrastructure that
supports them—remain available, communities are better able to meet their essential
needs and maintain resilience.

Commonly cited energy resilience metrics often fail to connect infrastructure
performance with human outcomes in a meaningful way (Peterson et al., 2023; Day &
Simcock, 2016). For instance, the number of customers affected by an outage may
indicate the geographical extent of the impact, while the lost load may indicate how
much electricity those customers typically consume. However, neither of these metrics
distinguish between outages at a grocery store, pharmacy, or library — locations where
outages create different consequences for residents, depending on the services offered
and the proximity of each location to surrounding community members. Likewise, while
outage frequency or duration can highlight areas of the distribution grid that require
repairs or preventive measures, such as tree trimming, these metrics do not offer
specific insight into how outages affect essential services that community members
depend on (Clark et al., 2018; DeSalvo et al., 2014). Additionally, because community

                                       Page 8 of 109

engagement in energy systems is uncommon, this project introduces a new approach to
community involvement in resilience discussions, combining qualitative feedback from
residents with quantitative power outage data to guide infrastructure planning and
policymaking. This work advances the integration of community input beyond traditional
decision-making methods for energy resilience. Ultimately, adopting this approach could
improve how communities nationwide allocate resources for energy resilience
investments.

Designed around a community-centered engagement model (A.C.E. Impact TrailwayTM),
this project aimed to clarify the values that communities prioritize regarding energy
resilience, recognizing community input as a crucial part of any decision-making
process for resilience investments (McGookin et al., 2021; Ross & Day, 2022). This is
because common reliability and resilience metrics only partially reflect community
preferences. We sought to gain a comprehensive understanding of community
members’ experiences before, during, and after power outages, their varying levels of
preparedness, and how the severity of outages within a specific time frame affects their
daily needs and priorities. Through the RCM research project, we aimed to establish
that community participation in energy resilience decision-making requires: 1) a
neighborhood-scale, quantitative measure linking energy and resilience, 2) a process
for including qualitative feedback from residents into that measure, and 3) acceptance
of experts who oversee energy projects. This fills a clear gap in energy resilience
research by connecting community input with quantitative decision support and
providing analyses to inform local, state, and utility actions.

A key contribution involves identifying the best ways to inform investments in energy
resilience infrastructure by integrating CBPR approaches into energy resilience planning
(Ross & Day, 2022; González, 2019). This integration (qualitative community-driven
data and quantitative utility power outage data) enhances2 both the effectiveness and
efficiency of evaluating the consequences of disruptions to energy infrastructure.
Another noteworthy contribution to the field is that the RCM project established a
replicable framework for assessing and modeling energy resilience metrics seen by its
successful use in three different jurisdictions in the state of Maryland. Additionally, the
research facilitated community participation, leading to more equitable stakeholder
involvement and improved decision-making processes related to the siting of resilient
power infrastructure, achieved through microgrid activities.

Ultimately, the RCM project seeks to scale the framework by applying its research
findings through the Maryland Energy Administration’s (MEA) energy resilience funding
program. This approach advances the deployment of distributed energy resources by
integrating community-driven insights into resilience planning, enabling smarter, more
localized energy investment decisions. It also delivers economic value by strengthening

6.1 Budget Period 1 / Year 1 Tasks to be Performed
RCM will develop an initial community energy resiliency framework by studying
performance-based and non-performance-based data for Baltimore City neighborhoods,
relying heavily on community input provided first by a Community Resiliency Council
and then integrated with a broader stakeholder Roundtable, via Step 1 of the A.C.E.
Impact Trailway™ approach that is designed to create equitable partnerships centered
around the community. The project will use the ACA-NET™ framework3 as a guide to
establish equitable partnerships. The project will also subsequently validate and refine
this nascent framework in Montgomery County, Maryland, to identify ways in which the
Baltimore framework can be adapted to fit Montgomery County’s community priorities
and physical infrastructure. The exact structure of community input will first be
established via a Resiliency Council, followed by a broader stakeholder Roundtable.

Regarding the identification of resilience metrics recognized or undervalued for
Milestone 1.2.1, metrics like Resilience Hub Walksheds, Social Burden, and Passive
Survivability are often absent from the literature. While other individual metrics are well-
documented, their combined influence on decision-making and community-driven
processes is overlooked in the literature. Recent reviews of energy resilience metrics
emphasize access to services as a new energy-related measure that can guide both
infrastructure planning and resilience efforts, ultimately helping local communities
(Conway & Stewart, 2019). Critical service access assesses the relative availability of
essential services within a neighborhood for residents (Conway et al., 2018). Access to
services changes over time as various service locations become available or
unavailable and can be measured at the household or neighborhood level, or grouped
by individual service categories (e.g., medical services) or all services. Using a
community-based participatory research approach, we demonstrated how these metrics
could be applied by conducting a series of activities, the results of which are shown in
the appendices of this report.

7.1.4.1 Subtask 4.1: Gather existing information on power outages, natural hazards, and social

To gather existing information on the impact of power outages, utility performance,
energy resilience, and social vulnerability, the project team collected specific data
related to Baltimore, including a review of the City’s Disaster Preparedness Plan. The
Baltimore Office of Sustainability also participated in meetings, offering guidance on
known hazards and risks facing the city. The project team collected ten metrics and
sub-metrics related to social and environmental vulnerability in Baltimore from the White
House Economic Justice Screening Tool (CJEST) and the FEMA Community Resilience
Challenges Index (CRCI). Metrics were overlayed with council member addresses
(which were voluntarily shared and anonymized) and integrated into interactive maps to
allow council members to explore the distribution of community characteristics related to
power outage outcomes, including: energy burden, air pollution, medical electricity
dependency, diabetes prevalence, lack of green space, lack of health insurance,
smartphone access, and mobile home prevalence. Each of the identified metrics above
was then analyzed to produce a custom Heat & Power Outage Vulnerability Index for
Baltimore (see Figure 1 in Exhibit C).

The index was integrated into an interactive map to support discussions with council
members about how aspects of vulnerability can be combined to prioritize areas for
energy resilience. Using NREL’s research on resilience metrics and frameworks
(Peterson, 2023), the project team created a draft baseline survey, which was
distributed to RC participants during the first meeting. This survey served as a pre-
survey, with its results compared to the post-survey conducted at the last RC meeting.
However, while developing the baseline resilience survey, the project team encountered
challenges in structuring the study to demonstrate improvements in the relevance and
applicability of new resilience metrics compared to existing metrics, a milestone for the
three jurisdictions. Given that this was a pre-engagement survey, subjects did not have
an opportunity to learn about the metrics and topics before taking the survey. The
challenge in showing improvement in metric applicability was mainly driven by the need
to educate participants on existing, often technical, metrics, in addition to the research
method involving small, in-depth focus groups. To effectively measure improvement in
the relevance of a resilience metric, the project team would need to survey a much
larger group of individuals. The project team proposed changes to the language and
timing of the milestones to emphasize improvements in understanding metrics rather
than perceived applicability. Furthermore, beginning in the pre-award period and
continuing through Q3 of the project, the RCM team collaborated with BGE, the local
investor-owned utility, to gather information on outage frequency and duration, including
the feeders with the most outages. After numerous discussions and meetings with the
utility on this topic, on 1/26/2024, the project team signed a three-way Non-Disclosure
Agreement (NDA) between Groundswell, BGE, and NREL for access to the utility data.

activity encouraged RC members to evaluate the types of energy-dependent services
their community needs, the locations that provide these services, and how the
significance or importance of services differs across various service categories and
location types. Visualizing the relationship between services and locations as a “scoring
matrix” helps utilities and emergency managers move beyond simply labeling locations
as “critical” or “not critical” towards a more continuous assessment and measurement of
how crucial certain locations are for specific areas. This approach also enables
community members to give practical feedback about their priorities.

The FEMA Lifelines (U.S. Department of Homeland Security, 2025) were utilized to
define initial categories, where each location type could receive a score indicating its
relative importance for services in that category (Figure 1 in Exhibit D). FEMA Lifelines,
however, were designed to support top-down coordination of response and recovery
efforts by emergency managers. The wide range of some FEMA Lifeline categories
makes them challenging to use for prioritizing specific types of service locations,
especially for energy investments. The broad links between many types of locations and
various Lifelines led to a relatively uniform scoring system, where all locations received
high service value. This uniformity made it hard to prioritize sites for specific service
categories effectively. To achieve the goal of helping community members prioritize
services or locations for energy resilience investments, the project team and Baltimore
RC, working in collaboration, significantly enhanced the initial FEMA Lifelines to create
service categories that were more specific to energy provision and better reflected
individual household needs (Figure 2 in Exhibit D). Table 1 in Exhibit D provides more
information about the five themes and associated categories and subcategories that
were used to assign critical service scores to locations across Baltimore, while Tables 2
and 3 show the combined scores of critical service locations under different conditions.

Mapping Neighborhood Accessibility
In a separate activity the project team designed for the Baltimore RC, RC members
were encouraged to think about the places they visit to fulfill their needs as a “service
landscape," where proximity, transportation, service categories, and service quality
interact to create areas of “higher access” and “lower access” to services during normal
conditions—when the power system operates normally. Individual and clustered service
locations each contribute different, measurable amounts to the “service landscape” of a
single home, neighborhood, or city under typical conditions. This activity asked
participants to review a map and a list of prioritized critical service locations, and to
provide feedback on whether the locations, the services offered at those locations, and
their rankings matched their lived experiences. This activity benefited RC members in
visualizing service accessibility across their communities and understanding the ranked
importance of each service location, highlighting which locations, services, or
households might be priorities for energy resilience investments. Neighborhood

                                      Page 26 of 109

Accessibility offers utilities, emergency managers, and community members a richer
context for resilience planning than simple maps or lists of potential priorities.

7.1.4.5 Subtask 4.5: Combine qualitative and quantitative data to create measurable resilience
 metrics
Service Landscapes
Critical service access is determined by all locations within a specific travel time
threshold of a study area. The project team initially used a 20-minute round-trip drive
time to measure critical service access in Baltimore (and subsequently in Montgomery
County). However, feedback obtained from Eastern Shore RC members highlighted the
importance of more distant locations in rural areas and their willingness to travel longer
distances for the services needed. In response, the project team retroactively expanded
the critical service access metric to include all locations within a 60-minute round-trip
travel time. Because the relationship between travel time and service value is inversely
exponential, locations farther from households in the study area have decreasing value
for critical service access. This means that increasing the travel time radius affects
critical service access differently for each study area and household, depending on their
position within the study area and their proximity to transportation infrastructure that
connects them to more distant locations (see Figure 1 in Exhibit E).

Table 1 in Exhibit E displays the additional locations and the increase in critical service
access observed for Baltimore, Montgomery County, and the Eastern Shore when the
drive time threshold expands from 20 minutes to 60 minutes. In each study area, the
number of locations more than doubles, but the increase in critical service access is
much smaller because the value of each location is inversely and exponentially related

                                        Page 27 of 109

to its travel time from households. This means locations farther away have less impact
on critical service access.

Measuring Outage Consequences
Joining community-vetted service locations to utility feeder data allowed the project
team to examine the spatial distribution of critical service access across electrical
infrastructure during both normal conditions and extended power outages. Summarizing
the spatial relationship between the distribution system and the broader social,
economic, and transportation systems it serves can add critical context to utility
decision-making. Utilizing electrical distribution system data, including the spatial
location of feeders and multiple years of outage records for each feeder, provided to the
project team by BGE (the investor-owned utility serving Baltimore City), the project team
developed maps to display service access relative to feeder locations. Critical service
access in Baltimore is mainly located in a ring surrounding the downtown area (see
Figures 1 and 2 in Exhibit F). Such maps can provide an initial indicator of which
components of electrical distribution infrastructure are the highest priority during normal
conditions, using a metric vetted through community engagement.

prioritize site locations in specific neighborhoods for energy resilience investments, the

number of clustered locations to prioritize for such infrastructure, using interactive maps
that display neighborhood access to critical services and estimated backup energy
costs by location. RC members were divided into three groups, corresponding to the
three stages of the microgrid/resilience siting game.
 • Step One: The RC members were asked to select locations where the power
 should stay active during a disruption that causes power outages elsewhere, to
 build a microgrid. The group’s goal was to choose locations that provided the
 most benefits to the majority during a significant outage, while keeping costs low.
 • Step Two: The RC members were instructed to explore possible microgrid sites
 and document information about each one. They were asked to complete an
 online form using a link provided by the project team to record their responses.

                                        Page 33 of 109

 • Step Three: The RC members were directed to determine which microgrids
 would be the best investment for their community. After creating a list of the top
 three microgrids, the project team followed up with questions to clarify each
 option.

The results of this effort for Montgomery County are shown in Figures 1 and 2 in Exhibit
H. The results indicate that RC members identified places of worship, grocery stores,
and schools as the top three facilities for energy resilience investments through
microgrids. A total of nine facilities or service locations were identified as potential
microgrid sites, providing services that were broadly classified into 17 categories. The
key factors for such selections included ease of access (proximity to people and to
transportation), size/capacity to serve many people, physical attributes such as ample
parking and the absence of floodplains, and clustering of multiple services. As identified
by the RCM research, clustering of services is a crucial factor in energy resilience and
microgrid investments. The team has also thoroughly documented the community input
process, producing a series of activity templates and meeting agendas. These
resources can be found in Appendix A to the “RCM Framework Document” delivered to

8 SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS
The primary goal of the RCM project, along with its benefits to the public, was to create
a community-driven energy resilience framework that can be replicated across Maryland
to enhance public safety and boost preparedness and recovery during hazardous
events and prolonged power outages. To achieve this goal, the RCM research
employed the A.C.E. Impact TrailwayTM, a CBPR framework (Exhibit A1) to understand
community perspectives and highlight the importance of local involvement in energy
resilience decisions (Hart et al., 2024; McGookin et al., 2021). This demonstrates not
only the value of incorporating local viewpoints in designing and implementing energy
resilience strategies but also the significance of using CBPR-based approaches to
develop actionable solutions across the state. By engaging community stakeholders in
decision-making, the project laid the groundwork for more community-focused and
effective energy resilience investments. Additionally, the framework developed through
this research provides a scalable model that other regions can adapt to strengthen their
preparedness and recovery against energy infrastructure challenges, ensuring that
community voices stay central to resilience planning and actions.

8.1 Significant Accomplishments
The project team is proud of the work that went into the research. The value of
community engagement is widely recognized, but determining the best ways to elicit
meaningful feedback from community participants can be challenging. The RCM
framework offers a clear roadmap for energy resilience planners and policymakers to
gather information that helps prioritize resilience efforts. It is flexible enough to fit
different communities, contexts, and concerns, making it useful not just for Maryland
communities but for communities across the United States. The activities developed
through RCM are specific enough to guide community engagement efforts. Funding
opportunities to develop a cohesive and practical approach to community engagement
are rare. As a result, most engagement efforts are fragmented. The project team has
had a unique chance to create a framework and field-test it within a single project,
leading to a stronger outcome than if we had tried to consolidate insights from various
disparate projects. Therefore, the value of the RCM project is demonstrated through its
practical applications and implementation, including innovation and usage, as detailed
in the RCM Framework documentation and case studies for each of the three
jurisdictions in this study. The RCM research project facilitated community participation,
leading to more equitable stakeholder involvement and improved decision-making
processes related to the siting of resilient power infrastructure, achieved through
microgrid activities (see Exhibit H). A key takeaway from this research is that the project
team successfully integrated community perspectives into resilience planning
discussions, based on the community-centered, data-driven participatory research
approach, encapsulated by the following remarks:

"It is hard to express just how much we achieved with the RCM research to advance the
field of energy resilience and community engagement driven by decision science. The

                                       Page 43 of 109

project team has developed a framework that facilitates a structured examination of
community norms and preferences regarding energy resilience—a topic that many are
unfamiliar with. Furthermore, we have not only developed a theoretical framework but
also identified best practices and created activities that anyone can use, thereby
lowering barriers for those interested in engaging communities but unsure of how to
begin. It might not be as exciting as saying ‘we have identified a new technology that
improves energy efficiency by 5%.’ However, it is hard to overstate how valuable it is to
know which questions to ask and how to ask them to gain meaningful insights in a
completely new way, unlike traditional frameworks of energy resilience." – Dr. Sara
Peterson (NREL).

The results from the RCM research show that community members can provide
valuable feedback on energy resilience planning, especially when discussions are
interactive and focused on participants’ energy-dependent critical services: their needs,
where they go to meet those needs, when those needs emerge over time, and how
those needs should be prioritized in complex decisions. As a result, the contributions of
the RCM research to the field and the importance of this work include the following
findings: First, the shift from the FEMA Lifelines framework used in Baltimore to the
more effective, community-driven categories used in Montgomery County and the
Eastern Shore was based on discussions with council members, including methods for
mapping and grouping rooted in RCM community engagement (see Figure 2 in Exhibit
D). Co-developing the themes with community members created a classification system
that reflects their real-life experiences.

Second, the data-driven community engagement framework is the most important
innovation in this research. Besides involving community members in deciding how to
approach different types of services, the project team developed a set of activities to
gather community input on various aspects of resilience and prioritization. These
engagement activities helped uncover revealed preferences rather than stated
preferences, which means we now have a structured way to break down the ‘value’ of
different locations into the components that matter most to people. There is value in
directly asking community members what is most important to them or what would be
most important for their community's resilience. However, the various activities
developed in this study can also help clarify why specific responses were given.
Discrepancies between revealed and stated preferences are intriguing—they may
reveal additional data for consideration.

The microgrid activity stands out as the most innovative for several compelling reasons.
First, it uniquely empowers community members to directly engage with and understand
the tradeoffs of resilience investments—a hands-on approach that is rarely seen in
other interactive energy activities. By simplifying real-world microgrid calculations, the
activity bridges technical data with community perspectives, combining rigorous outage
modeling with the lived experiences and values of residents. This integration fosters
consensus and transparency, ensuring that key energy resilience investment decisions
are community-driven, visible, and understandable to everyone. Ultimately, the
microgrid activity transforms abstract planning into practical exploration, enabling

                                      Page 44 of 109

communities to co-design solutions that reflect their specific needs and priorities and
demonstrating a model for how community-driven, inclusive, and transparent processes
can lead to truly resilient energy systems.

Fourth, through the RCM project, we observed that energy resilience metrics often only
measure electricity availability within a utility’s service area (Jeffers et al., 2023; Petit &
Vargas, 2020; Anderson et al., 2019). For example, the number of customers without
power during a major storm or the number of outages lasting longer than a certain
number of hours reflects the experience of the average customer over a year. Although
these baseline metrics are important, they do not highlight other adverse effects of
extended power outages, which are primarily caused by the loss of critical services that
depend on electricity. These services include the acquisition, storage, and preparation
of food, water, and fuel; medical treatments and prescription medications; maintaining
thermal comfort and hygiene; communication with family and officials; and other indirect
services, such as childcare and banking, that support community functions (see Figures
1 through 5 in Exhibit I). Research on the interdependence between electricity and other
social, economic, and infrastructure systems has developed metrics, such as social
burden, which better measure the benefits of specific resilience investments. However,
while these metrics use social science research techniques and involve community
participation to validate methods, they have not yet been integrated into community
participation processes focused on energy planning.

As a result, the project aimed to bridge the gap between research and planning by
examining critical service access, a metric designed to help decision makers identify
energy resilience investments that maximize community access to essential services.
Community feedback collected during the project was used to improve both the
interactive materials used to engage community members and the critical service
access metric itself (see Exhibit A2). For example, input from rural community members
led to expanding the distance radius used to calculate critical service access to better
reflect the longer distances community members may travel for critical services;
feedback from early participants prompted a reduction in the amount of information
provided about the critical service access metric before engaging in activities.
Ultimately, the RCM project helps the public by simplifying decisions about backup
energy for people who are not energy experts. This encourages and facilitates greater
public participation in decision-making and more substantial support for those decisions
once they are made. The project promotes distributed energy resources and economic
benefits in the U.S. by highlighting the advantages of backup power, which are often
difficult to quantify monetarily, and by localizing backup power decisions, increasing the
likelihood that decisions are based on more than just financial cost-benefit analysis.

8.2 Challenges and Mitigating Solutions

The project team encountered and successfully addressed several major challenges
during this research. First, since there was no prior research on the most effective
methods for communicating energy resilience information to community members, we

                                        Page 45 of 109

tested various materials and agenda designs to see which ones best engaged
community participants. Community engagement does not offer a controlled
environment for systematically experimenting with these materials and agendas.
Instead, we observed and assessed their effectiveness as community discussions took
place, then made deliberate adjustments between engagements based on our
observations. This approach formed the basis of the experiential learning method used
in the research. Throughout this process, we learned that it is probably more effective to
start with the simplest version of materials and agendas and gradually add complexity
as needed, rather than beginning with the most detailed and complex versions and
simplifying them later if needed.

Second, the team realized that presenting communities with hypothetical rather than
actual investment decisions diminishes the perceived importance of community
engagement and limits the depth of insights gained from their feedback. This opens the
door for further research to examine how community involvement in real investment
scenarios influences participant engagement and response quality. Future studies could
explore how different levels of participation affect response usefulness and identify best
practices for involving communities in investment research to achieve meaningful
insights and actionable outcomes. Third, the project's focus on both framework
development and its application across various jurisdictions at the same time created
challenges in providing clear guidance during the Baltimore meetings, making them less
targeted than intended. The team tackled this by using a trial-and-error approach,
understanding that creating new frameworks inherently requires time and iteration. This
experience highlighted the need to leave room for learning in the meeting and overall
project schedule, and to adjust expectations accordingly.

Fourth, the project faced a major challenge in tackling a complex question: “How can we
effectively include community perspectives in resilience planning?” Unlike
straightforward questions that yield clear, quantitative answers, such as “a grocery store
can provide 5 times as much food as a convenience store,” this question lacked a
definite endpoint or simple metric. To address this, the project team developed a flexible
and iterative approach that focused on understanding diverse community perspectives.
Instead of searching for a single ‘answer,’ the project team used participatory
engagement techniques that provided nuanced insights. After each jurisdiction, the
team reflected on what it learned and adjusted its approach accordingly. This adaptable
process helped refine our questioning methods and engagement strategies over time.
As a result, the project team created a practical framework for integrating community
input into resilience planning, showing progress even without traditional, clear-cut
answers. This approach shows how embracing complexity and iterative learning can
lead to significant improvements in solving complex, multifaceted questions. The team
saw the absence of traditional ‘answers’ not as a setback but as an opportunity to
develop more contextually rich and flexible strategies, ultimately improving the ability to
incorporate community perspectives effectively.

Finally, although developing community engagement activities required significant time
and effort, the project team used lessons learned from each RC and RT meeting to

                                      Page 46 of 109

refine its approach. As a result, the team created a strong set of materials to support
future community engagement efforts. Many assume that creating community
engagement materials is easier than producing highly technical documents, but the
opposite is true—clarifying concepts and designing activities to gather the right
information is very challenging, yet the RCM team succeeded in doing so. Both the
activities and the analytical methods used in this project could benefit others in the
future.

8.3 Conclusions
Energy resilience planning is complex and multidimensional, making it challenging to
fully evaluate the costs and benefits of resilience infrastructure, including non-financial
benefits such as social factors and public health advantages. Any new infrastructure
investment involves significant capital expenses and/or long-term operational costs.
Traditional planning methods, such as cost-benefit analyses used during utility energy
master planning, may not capture the full benefits of resilience investments. By
analyzing and understanding a community’s access to services—or the lack thereof—
and the impacts of grid outages from a service access perspective, the value of
resilience-focused investments can be better assessed. The RCM project is a step
forward toward a community-informed approach. Consistent with emerging research,
our experience suggests that energy resilience solutions are most successful when they
incorporate community members’ lived experiences and when community input guides
decisions, such as physical infrastructure, power restoration plans, or other outcomes.
Local knowledge of infrastructure, social and economic networks, environmental
resources, and risks should, among other factors, shape strategies for energy
resilience.

9 PATH FORWARD
“The identified gap in the project was the need for a formal, scientific approach to
engaging communities in energy resilience planning. Prior to this research, there was
no formal approach; RCM is the first of its kind, attempting to understand energy
resilience from a community perspective, augmented with quantitative utility power
outage data. Future work could involve scaling the RCM framework through energy
resilience funding programs and applying the framework to identify priority sites for
community energy resilience efforts across other jurisdictions. Significant progress or
research areas that could emerge include integrating social burden and accessibility
with CBPR approaches to improve the efficiency of energy resilience planning.” - Dr.
Erica Holloman-Hill (ASI).

The project team is most proud that we managed to combine a strong quantitative
analysis with meaningful community engagement, while continuously refining both
throughout the project. Most projects focus on only one of these aspects, and few can
improve their methods through iteration. To advance the research, Groundswell is
actively seeking opportunities to apply the RCM Framework, which was developed to

                                       Page 47 of 109

guide actual resilience investments—when concrete funding for such investments is
available or being proposed. For example, a recent award from the MEA will enable us
to conduct technical feasibility studies for prospective resilience hubs in Central and
Eastern Maryland. Elements of the RCM Framework may be used to identify and recruit
suitable candidate sites to participate in this initiative. As a result, it could be beneficial
for the framework to be applied in a community where a real resilience investment
decision is taking place. For example, a community where the city is selecting sites to
include in a grant application for funding a microgrid, or where a state or utility is
assessing proposals for sites to install backup energy. Second, building on the
approach used in RCM, where we connected each service location to the
geographically closest feeder, we could map distribution feeders to the services they
supply with power, provided we have full access to a utility's distribution system model.
This would allow the creation of more accurate maps and insights into the implications
of resilience investments.

Third, the utility feeder analyses we developed provide valuable insights, but the
breadth of the geographic areas limits the actionable information. A potentially more
useful analysis would focus on a smaller geographic region (e.g., a specific
neighborhood within Baltimore City). Theoretically, the applied RCM framework could
help identify the most important feeders in a neighborhood from a service access
perspective, which could then be prioritized for resilient energy infrastructure. Fourth,
future research could incorporate existing utility data on historical outage risk for each
feeder. This addition would add a valuable and insightful dimension to the utility data
sets, offering a more comprehensive view of infrastructure investment decisions.

Fifth, a potential obstacle to the future adoption of the Framework could be the small
number of organizations that choose to implement it, because community input for
energy resilience investments is not (yet) required (from a legal or regulatory
perspective) or incentivized (from a business perspective). Implementation (and
facilitation of RC meetings) requires time, deliberate effort, and knowledge or familiarity
with energy resilience. A few organizations, such as Groundswell, are local, community-
oriented, possess knowledge of energy resilience, and have established relationships
with utilities and other stakeholders that can utilize the information provided by
community residents.

Sixth, another area for future improvement is a more controlled comparison of feedback
from communities that have recently experienced a prolonged power outage and those
that have not. There is also an opportunity to utilize this framework, particularly the
microgrid siting activity conducted at the end of each engagement, to analyze public
decision-making processes more effectively. Siting criteria, such as the number of
microgrids, the duration of outages, the available funding, or the services needed, can
be adjusted to understand how community priorities and collaborative decision-making
evolve when parameters change. Finally, the next step is to put this work into practice
— to be truly valuable, we encourage practitioners to adopt and apply this framework in
their contexts so the impact can be realized.

13.1 Exhibit A1: Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)

The CBPR framework used in this study is a collaborative research approach that
values and engages community residents, practitioners, and academic researchers as
equal partners in the process. As research is increasingly being conducted and led
outside academic institutions, CBPR4 methodology offers a valid way to involve
communities in research. The A.C.E. Impact TrailwayTM proposed by Ayika Solutions,
Inc. (ASI), is a CBPR approach designed to guide organizations (public and private) and
practitioners (community members) through a facilitated process that leverages
resources, activities, and milestones to co-define and develop Social Impact Measures
& Metrics (SIMMs)TM. ASI defines SIMMs as implementable measures and metrics
designed to create measurable, positive social impact (PSI)TM. ASI defines PSI as the
positive, social (and community-driven/led) change desired by organizations and/or
practitioners, guided by the goals of a project or program. In the context of energy
resilience, the barriers that prevent non-energy experts from participating in energy
system decisions (i.e., energy resilience planning) provided an opportunity to apply
ASI’s approach in support of the project’s goals, as shown in Figure 1.
