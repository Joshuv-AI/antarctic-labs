Rare intradural lumbar disc herniation at L1/2: a case report
Authors: Lucia Ledererová, Peter Hudák, Dana Kuniaková, Ján Kozák, Milan Liška
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Intradural disc herniation (IDH) in the lumbar spine is an extremely rare condition, representing only a small percentage of all disc herniations. Despite advanced imaging techniques, IDH is often misdiagnosed as intradural tumor, disc herniation or migrated sequester. Definitive diagnosis is usually established intraoperatively and may be confirmed histologically. This case report describes an L1/2 IDH that was microsurgically removed with the assistance of intraoperative ultrasonography (IOUS) for localization of the lesion. The ventral dural tear, frequently occurring in IDH, was managed using a collagen pad, resulting in an uneventful postoperative course without evidence of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak during follow-up.Case Description We present a 70-year-old female patient with acute back pain, radiating to inner thighs and paraparesis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed intervertebral (IV) disc herniation in the L1/2 segment. Subsequent microdiscectomy assisted by IOUS revealed intradurally migrated fragments, which were carefully removed. The ventral dural tear was identified and treated using an absorbable collagen pad. At both 7-week and 18-month follow-up, the patient remained pain-free and showed no new neurological deficits or signs of CSF leakage.Conclusions Lumbar IDH remains a rare diagnostic challenge, as preoperative imaging is often inconclusive. This case suggests that intraoperative ultrasound may aid localization of intradural pathology, while collagen-pad repair may provide a useful option for managing ventral dural defects when primary closure is technically challenging, although further research is necessary.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Intradural disc herniation (IDH) in the lumbar spine is extremely rare (<1% of all disc herniations) and occurs least frequently at the L1/2 level.

• IDH may mimic other intradural pathologies or conventional disc herniation on preoperative imaging, making diagnosis challenging.

• Careful microsurgical dissection and adequate dural closure are essential to avoid neural injury and cerebrospinal fluid leakage.

What is known and what is new?

• The diagnosis of IDH is frequently established intraoperatively and the associated ventral dural defect requires meticulous management.

• This case highlights the use of intraoperative ultrasonography (IOUS) for localization and confirmation of intradural disc fragments, facilitating targeted durotomy. This case also demonstrates successful management of a ventral dural defect using an intradural underlay collagen pad when primary suturing was considered technically unsafe.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• IDH is rare, but surgeons should be cautious particularly when imaging or intraoperative findings are atypical.

• IOUS may be a useful adjunct in selected cases to improve localization of intradural pathology and assist in guided durotomy.

• When primary repair of a ventral dural defect is technically challenging, collagen-pad-based sealing may represent a feasible management option. However, further comparative research is required to establish collagen pads as equivalent to standard primary suture closure.

Introduction

Intradural disc herniation (IDH) is defined as intervertebral (IV) disc material bulging inside the dural sac. The presence of disc material within the dural sac is a rare occurrence, accounting for less than 1% of all cases (1). The first case of IDH was reported by WE Dandy in 1942 (2). Thohar Arifin et al. state that intradural herniations emerge mostly in the lumbar spine, most commonly in L4/5 segment (55%), followed by L3/4 segment (16%), L5/S1 segment (10%), least frequent in the segment L2/3 and L1/2 (3). Due to unclear findings on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) IDHs may be mistaken for intradural tumors such as neurinomas or meningiomas, or extradural disc protrusions (4).

We present a case of a 70-year-old woman with lumbar IDH in the L1/2 segment that was managed in our department, initially interpreted on preoperative MRI as extradural disc protrusion.

Although L1/L2 IDH has been previously reported, the combination of intraoperative ultrasonography (IOUS)-guided identification of the intradural lesion and collagen-pad repair of the ventral dural defect represents a notable feature and the main contribution of this case, highlighting practical aspects of intraoperative diagnosis and management. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0106/rc).

Case presentation

A 70-year-old female patient was admitted to the department of neurology with disabling back pain lasting approximately one week. Pain was localised predominantly in the thoracolumbar junction, visual analogue scale (VAS) 9/10, radiating to groins and inner thighs bilaterally.

Her medical history included chronic lower back pain and previous spine surgery in 2019 [L4 laminectomy and L4/5 posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF)] (Table 1). Following this procedure, residual paresthesia persisted in the left L5 dermatome.

The neurological assessment demonstrated proximal weakness of both lower extremities and mild paraparesis. Hip flexion was graded as 3/5 and knee extension as 4/5 bilaterally on the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, no saddle anesthesia or sphincter dysfunction, reflexes within physiological range. Posture was steady, prolonged standing and walking, even for a short distance, required assistance. Other neurological findings were within physiological range. Conservative treatment slightly improved the paraparesis and reduced the pain, but only with temporary effect.

Non-contrast MRI revealed a large central L1/2 lesion measuring 7 mm × 14 mm ×10 mm, with cranio-caudal migration and severe spinal canal stenosis (Figures 1,2). The lesion was interpreted as a large disc herniation. No imaging signs suggestive of IDH, such as the Y sign or discontinuity of the posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), were identified. Therefore, contrast-enhanced MRI was not performed, and microsurgical removal of the presumed disc herniation was indicated.

Therapeutic intervention—surgical technique

In the prone position, under general anesthesia, microscopic partial hemilaminectomy was performed in the L1/2 segment, confirmed by fluoroscopy. Following flavectomy, the dural sac and the L2 nerve root were identified and mobilized medially. The spinal canal was meticulously explored, however no protrusion or sequester was found. Based on the mass found on MRI and virtually no disc material found extradurally, the decision was made to explore the intradural space with IOUS. Using IOUS, an intradural mass was localised in the L1/2 segment. Targeted paramedian dorsal durotomy was performed above the lesion. Nerve roots were exposed and carefully mobilised revealing intradural disc fragments. Small portions of the sequester were cautiously dissected from surrounding nerve roots and removed (Figures 3,4). Following disc removal, the intradural space was inspected, revealing a ventral dural defect, visualized in its entirety. Given the anatomical constraints and the proximity of nerve rootlets, direct suturing of the ventral dural defect was considered technically challenging. The defect was therefore repaired using an absorbable collagen pad coated with NHS-PEG (Hemopatch®), placed intradurally as an underlay graft. The dorsal durotomy was sutured in a watertight manner, reinforced with a collagen pad (Hemopatch®) and tissue adhesive. To minimize the risk of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, suction drainage was not used. No signs of CSF leakage were observed postoperatively.

Histopathological examination was not performed. Intraoperatively, the removed fragments had the macroscopic appearance consistent with degenerated disc material, without adherence to the nerve rootlets. The diagnosis of IDH was therefore considered sufficiently established, although the lack of histopathological confirmation represents a limitation of this report.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient remained on bed rest for 48 hours and was subsequently mobilized. Back pain improved substantially, and neurological examination demonstrated improved lower-extremity strength (hip flexion MRC 4/5, knee extension MRC 5/5 bilaterally). Postoperative MRI confirmed complete removal of the intradural disc fragments (Figure 5). No signs of CSF leakage were observed, the patient expressed satisfaction with both the surgical treatment and the recovery process, and was discharged on postoperative day 4. At the 7-week follow-up, the patient was pain-free, with full recovery of muscle strength (MRC 5/5 bilaterally) and no evidence of CSF leakage. At the 18-month follow-up, the patient ambulated independently with no new neurological deficits or signs of CSF leakage. Pre-existing paresthesia in the L5 dermatome (present since the L4/5 PLIF in 2019) remained unchanged.

Discussion

IDHs arise very sporadically, in only about 0.26–0.30%. The lumbar spine is affected in the vast majority of cases, up to 92%, most commonly at the L4/5 level. Involvement of the upper lumbar spine, including L1/2, is exceptionally uncommon. In the thoracic spine the occurrence of IDH is 5% and only 3% in the cervical spine (5-7). Preoperatively, IDH can mimic other entities, such as intradural tumours, therefore definitive confirmation is possible almost exclusively by intraoperative finding (8-10). Cheng et al. report that only a minority of cases (25%) are diagnosed preoperatively (11). Marello et al. state that the percentage of preoperatively diagnosed lumbar IDHs is even lower—only 15,4% (12), Loya et al. claim only 7% (13).

MRI is routinely used for diagnosing spinal pathologies, yet there is no distinctive sign for IDH on imagining studies (11). In 1993 Wasserstrom et al. described a ring enhancement around IDH on gadolinium enhanced MRI that can help distinguish IDH. However, this sign can also be seen in other spinal pathologies (14). Later Ge et al. in 2019 mentioned other signs associated with IDH such as whorl-like mixed intensity on T2-weighted MRI, hawk-beak sign or PLL discontinuity. However, this diagnosis is rare and therefore seldom suspected in advance. Its detection during surgery remains the predominant verification (11). Matsumoto et al. suggested that the radiological diagnosis of IDH may be possible in carefully selected patients (15).

In our case, retrospective review of the preoperative MRI in collaboration with a neuroradiologist revealed none of the characteristic imaging signs of IDH. The lesion remained indistinguishable from a conventional disc herniation. This highlights the diagnostic limitations of preoperative imaging in IDH.

Several theories are debated regarding the etiology of IDH, but the exact pathogenesis remains unclear. Factors such as narrow spinal canal, preexisting tighter adhesions between annulus fibrosus, PLL and dural sac are commonly linked with IDH (16-18). Adhesions can be caused by degenerative changes, congenital anomalies, chronic inflammation or previous surgeries. Also, PLL hypertrophy and ossification can cause vulnerability and fragility of dura (12). In addition, Krajewski et al. state that ventral dura thins with increasing age compared to the dorsal dura (19). Acute traumatic spine injury or chronic disc protrusion can also increase the risk of IDH (20). Additionally, Ge et al. report that 70% of all IDHs occur in male patients (11). Clinical manifestation is usually between 50–60 years of age (4).

Thohar Arifin et al. suggest microsurgical approach by a median or paramedian dural incision for better visibility (3).

IOUS may be useful when an intradural lesion is suspected. It provides a safe and minimally invasive assessment of the intradural space while the dura remains intact (8). In our case, IOUS enabled localization of the lesion and facilitated a targeted durotomy directly above it. Similar to its use in intradural tumor surgery, IOUS may aid intraoperative decision-making in selected cases of IDH. Some authors performed the dural incision based on the location of CSF leak found intraoperatively (17), or preoperative MRI imaging. In some cases tense dural sac with tight adhesion to PLL helped localise the IDH (21). During the intradural part of the procedure it is mandatory to be cautious and follow the standard protocol for intradural tumor resection using microsurgical technique. Intraoperative monitoring should be also considered, if the circumstances allow it.

Ventral dural defect, typically associated with IDH, often requires intraoperative repair, in order to prevent reherniation intradurally or CSF leak (11). Methods for dural defect closure include suturing, fat or fascial grafts, dural substitutes or synthetic sealants (10). Several authors advocate for using sutures to ensure watertight closure (1,22). Hemostatic materials or dural substitutes can be used as well, with reportedly comparable outcomes (8,23). On the other hand, Sasaji et al. state that to avoid nerve root damage, the dural tear can be left untreated (24). In some cases, closure is not feasible because of dense adhesions (13,21). In our instance, the ventral dural defect was repaired using standalone underlay collagen pad with no subsequent CSF leakage. The intradural space should then be inspected for residual fragments and the durotomy closed in a watertight manner.

This report is limited by its single-case design and the lack of histological confirmation. However, the diagnosis was based on characteristic intraoperative findings, including free intradural disc fragments with an appearance consistent with degenerated disc material and identification of the ventral dural defect. Broader conclusions regarding the superiority of specific repair techniques should be supported by additional research or larger case series.

Conclusions

IDH is a rare and diagnostically challenging condition that is often recognized only intraoperatively. When preoperative imaging is inconclusive or no extradural disc material is identified during surgery, IOUS may assist in localizing intradural disc fragments and guiding further surgical management. In cases with an associated ventral dural defect, collagen-pad repair may represent a useful option when primary closure is difficult.
