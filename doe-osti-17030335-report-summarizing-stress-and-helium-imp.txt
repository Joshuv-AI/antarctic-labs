ABSTRACT
This report summarizes FY 2026 activities at Argonne National Laboratory in support of the DOE
Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program
and its Licensing Approach with Ions and Neutrons (LAIN) framework for qualifying additively
manufactured materials for nuclear applications. The work focused on understanding irradiation-
induced defect evolution in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H stainless steel as a function of
processing condition using single-ion and dual-ion irradiation, together with post-irradiation
examination with transmission electron microscope. The LPBF 316H material was examined in the
as-built, stress-relieved, solution-annealed, and cold-worked conditions, with wrought 316H
included for comparison. Single-beam 5 MeV Ni2+ ion irradiation showed that cavity swelling
depends on the initial microstructure, with swelling generally following the order stress-relieved >
as-built > solution-annealed > cold-worked over most of the temperature and dose range studied.
These results indicate that irradiation behavior in LPBF 316H is governed strongly by retained AM
microstructural features.
The FY 2026 work also evaluated the effect of concurrent helium implantation on cavity evolution
and used dual-ion irradiation to estimate neutron-relevant microstructures in LPBF 316H. Helium
implantation generally increased cavity density and reduced cavity size, while reducing the swelling
difference between the solution-annealed and stress-relieved conditions as helium concentration
increased. Guided by rate-theory-based analysis, dual-ion irradiation conditions were selected to
reproduce cavity populations comparable to those observed after neutron irradiation in HFIR at
400°C, and additional experiments were performed to estimate microstructures expected at higher
dose and under lower-helium conditions representative of a harder neutron spectrum. In parallel, a
new capability for ion irradiation under concurrent tensile loading was established and demonstrated.
Preliminary results for ion-irradiated LPBF 709 and LPBF 316H indicate that applied stress
significantly affects cavity evolution, promoting void formation and increasing cavity density while
reducing average cavity size relative to unstressed conditions. These observations are consistent with
prior studies showing that stress can reduce the incubation dose for swelling and enhance void
nucleation. Together, these results provide new experimental data and mechanistic insight needed to
interpret ion irradiation results in a neutron-relevant context and support the broader AMMT
objective of accelerating qualification of additively manufactured structural materials for nuclear
energy systems.

1 Introduction
Additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a transformative fabrication technique that is
reshaping the development of advanced engineering materials. By enabling the production of
components with complex geometries that are difficult or impossible to achieve through
conventional manufacturing, AM has expanded design possibilities while potentially reducing
production time and cost. Although AM alloys generally retain the same nominal chemical
composition as conventionally processed counterparts, the layer-by-layer fabrication process
produces distinctive microstructural features such as porosity, anisotropic grain morphologies,
dislocation cell structures, chemical segregation, and inclusions. Dislocation cell structures in
additively manufactured stainless steel are primarily formed after solidification due to localized
heating/cooling heterogeneities and thermal expansion/shrinkage cycles that generate high
residual stress, which induces localized plastic deformation that causes dislocations to
accumulate and self-assemble into cellular patterns [1]. Because these microstructural features
can strongly influence material properties, comprehensive characterization is essential before
AM components can be confidently deployed in critical applications.
To support the advancement of next-generation manufacturing technologies, the Advanced
Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program within the Department of
Energy's Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) is investigating the suitability of AM materials
for nuclear energy systems. One of the efforts is evaluating how these materials respond to
irradiation, as radiation-induced degradation directly affects the safety and long-term reliability
of reactor components. Similar to any newly developed material for nuclear service, AM
materials must undergo irradiation performance assessments. Particular attention is being given
to microstructural features that are unique to the AM process—including chemical heterogeneity
and dislocation cell structure—which may alter defect evolution and ultimately influence
irradiation tolerance.
Ion irradiation is an experimental approach for investigating the radiation response of materials
under neutron irradiation in nuclear reactors. Compared to neutron irradiation, ion irradiation
offers several practical advantages, including greater accessibility, minimal activation of
irradiated specimens, and precise control over irradiation parameters such as temperature, ion
species, dose, and dose rate. Furthermore, the high damage rates achievable with ion beams
enable the rapid accumulation of displacement damage, substantially shortening the time
required to reach reactor-relevant exposure levels. These advantages make ion irradiation
particularly well suited for efficiently evaluating the effects of different AM processing routes
and post-build treatments on irradiation behavior.
Despite these benefits, ion irradiation cannot fully reproduce the conditions experienced during
neutron irradiation in reactors. Differences in damage rates, penetration depth, surface effect and
injected interstitials are known to produce microstructural evolution deviated from neutron
irradiation to the same dose and at the same temperature [2]. Dual-ion beam irradiation co-
implanting helium provides an avenue to account for the transmutation helium produced by
neutron irradiation. However, the performing ion irradiation with the same He appm/dpa ratio
as neutron would lead to different swelling results because of the much shorter time of ion
irradiation affecting how helium is partitioned between different sinks [3].
As a result, computational modeling and simulation are essential for relating ion irradiation
observations to the expected performance of materials under neutron irradiation. Developing
predictive models requires identifying the irradiation phenomena that are accelerated, modified,

 7

or absent during ion irradiation so that experimental results can be accurately interpreted and
extrapolated to the long-term service conditions encountered in nuclear reactor environments.
The long-term objective of this collaborative effort among Argonne National Laboratory (ANL),
Idaho National Laboratory (INL) and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is to advance the
AMMT program's Licensing Approach with Ions and Neutrons (LAIN) framework [4], which
integrates ion irradiation, neutron irradiation, and predictive modeling to accelerate the
qualification and licensing of additively manufactured materials for nuclear energy applications.
ANL’s main thrust is ion irradiation and associated techniques.
During the fiscal year (FY) 2024 [5], we investigated the irradiation performance of laser powder
bed fusion (LPBF) 316L and LPBF 316H stainless steels (SS), and their wrought counterparts.
Materials were irradiated using in-situ and ex-situ ion techniques at 300°C and 600°C, with doses
ranging from 0.2 dpa to 25 dpa. The characterization of irradiated materials focused on the as-
built condition, with an emphasis on the effects of carbon and pre-existing dislocation cell
structure.
In FY 2025, the research emphasis shifted toward understanding how different processing
conditions influence irradiation-induced defect evolution in a LPBF 316H stainless steel [6]. The
processing conditions investigated included the as-built (AB), solution-annealed (SA), stress-
relieved (SR), cold-worked (CW), and conventionally wrought counterpart. The work pursued
two primary objectives. First, it compared the irradiation behavior of materials produced under
these different processing conditions and identify the microstructural characteristics that govern
their irradiation performance. Second, the study generated high-quality experimental data for the
development and validation of irradiation damage models capable of establishing quantitative
relationships between ion- and neutron-irradiation responses of AM materials [7].
In FY 2026, The research tasks included the following: (1) concluding the heat treatment effect
by fully characterizing the irradiation-induced defects and performing theoretical calculations to
validate the hypothesis interpreting the observations; (2) conducting dual-ion beam irradiation
experiments to investigate the effects of helium on void swelling and how heat treatment affects
the results; and (3) reproducing the void swelling observed in LPBF 316H samples irradiated in
HFIR using dual-ion irradiation, and then experimentally predicting the microstructures of the
same materials (i) after neutron irradiation in HFIR at 400°C to 10 dpa and (ii) after neutron
irradiation at 400°C to 2 dpa in in fast reactors. (4) Setting up a quasi-steady state rate theory
calculation to fit the void swelling results of ion irradiated samples and then extrapolating to
predict neutron results using the same fitted parameters. (5) Initiating the irradiation study with
concurrent uniaxial tensile stress.
Chapter 2 describes the general experimental setup of the single ion irradiation, dual-ion
irradiation and characterization techniques that are employed. The additional experimental setup,
methodology and results of individual tasks above are discussed in the subsequent Chapters 3 to
6, followed by Chapter 7 for Conclusion.

2 Experimental
The general experimental methods and procedures are discussed in this section. Section 2.1
describes the materials and their pre-irradiation heat treatments. Section 2.2 discusses the ex-situ
ion irradiation experiments. Section 2.3 discusses the procedure of post-irradiation sample
preparation and microstructural characterization of ex-situ irradiated samples.
2.1 Materials and pre-irradiation specimen preparation
The primary material investigated in this FY is LPBF 316H, fabricated at ORNL using a GE
Concept Laser M2 system [8, 9]. This material is currently undergoing neutron irradiation
campaigns in HFIR and ATR [10, 11]. In the previous FY report, this material was designated
as LPBF 316H-2. Since it is the only LPBF 316H material examined in the present report, it is
referred to simply as LPBF 316H. In addition to the LPBF material, a wrought 316H alloy was
included as a reference material for comparison.
Table 1 shows the powder composition of LPBF 316H, as provided by the vendor, together with
the corresponding ASTM specification. The powder contains a relatively high carbon
concentration (0.08 wt%), compared with the previously studied LPBF 316H powder (0.04
wt%). In contrast, the silicon concentration (0.07 wt%) is substantially lower than both the
ASTM specification (approximately one-tenth of the specified value) and the previously studied
LPBF 316L and LPBF 316H powders (approximately one-sixth of their silicon concentrations)
[6].

The heat treatment conditions includes as-built (AB), stress relief (SR), solution annealing (SA)
and cold working (CW). The SA at 1100°C for 1 hours and SR at 650°C for 24 hours were
performed at ORNL w in air furnace, followed by water quenching and air cooling, respectively.
A small block of AB LPBF 316H was cold-worked to 20% at University of Illinois, Urbana-
Champaign. The wrought 316H is in the annealed condition.
The printed materials were machined into 3-mm-diameter rods using an electrical discharge
machine (EDM) along build direction. Then, the rods were cut into 0.5 mm-thick disks with
EDM or 0.6-1 mm-thick disks with slow speed saw. Subsequently one side of the disks was
polished with sandpapers down to P4000 grit, polycrystalline diamond suspension down to 1
µm, vibratory polishing using 50 nm colloidal silica polishing suspension, and finally
electropolishing at -30ºC to -40ºC for 20-30 seconds using a Struers Tenupol-5 polisher. The
electrolyte used was a 5% perchloric acid solution in methanol. After electropolishing, samples
were rinsed through 3 batches of methanol at room temperature. Following the last rinse, each
disk was placed on filter paper to quickly drain residual methanol and minimize potential
contamination that could remain upon evaporation. Samples were stored in a desiccator prior to
irradiation.

Figure 1 shows the bright-field (BF) transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of LPBF

Figure 1. BF TEM images of the LPBF 316H stainless sample with different heat treatment
For SR condition, while the dislocation cell structure is retained, the dislocation density was
reduced as compared to the AB condition. M23C6 precipitates were observed decorating the
dislocation cell walls.
For SA condition, the microstructure contained fine grains with sizes comparable to the
dislocation cell structures typically observed in LPBF 316 stainless steel, suggesting that these
grains likely originated from the dislocation cell network. Evidence of limited grain growth was
also observed following heat treatment. In addition, regions of relatively high dislocation density
remain, indicating that the solution annealing treatment did not completely eliminate the
dislocation structure, although the characteristic cellular morphology was no longer present.
Besides, the observations of low angle grain boundaries or dislocation alignment suggest that,
during high-temperature solution annealing, dislocations within the original cell walls
reorganized into lower-energy configurations, resulting in the development of low-angle grain
boundaries. No carbides were detected in SA condition.
For CW condition, cold working introduced deformation feature such as dislocation slip bands,
and noticeably increased dislocation density. No carbides or deformation-induced martensite
were detected in CW condition.

2.2 Ex-situ ion irradiation
Ex-situ ion irradiations were performed using the Tandem accelerator in ANL. The ion beam
was bent through a magnet before entering the specimen chamber. The dosimetry was conducted
with a (rear) Faraday cup behind the specimen and an (front) annular Faraday cup in front of the
specimen. The beam current through a 3-mm diameter aperture was measured by the rear
Faraday cup before, after, and if necessary, during the irradiation if beam conditions changed.
When the ion beam was on target, current was monitored and integrated with the front annular
Faraday cup. Right before an irradiation experiment, the specimen and the holder were cleaned
using a Fischione nanoClean plasma cleaner model 1070 for 5 minutes at the Center for
Nanoscale Materials (CNM) of ANL.
The size of the rear aperture of the annular Faraday cup is 3 mm in diameter. The fluxes of 5
MeV Ni2+ ions was 2.15×1012 ions/cm2/s, which corresponds to current readouts of 46 nA on the
rear Faraday cup. Based on the SRIM calculation, as shown in Figure 2, which was calculated
based on 58Co instead of 58Ni due to an error in the SRIM database of this specific isotope [15],
these fluxes give a dose rate of 10-3 dpa/s at a depth of 600 nm 5 MeV Ni2+ ion irradiations.
During irradiation, beam stability was monitored by the current readout on the annular Faraday
cup. Besides, cryogenic shield (Lake Shore Cryotronics) cooled with liquid nitrogen was placed
upstream near the specimen to further enhance the vacuum as shown in Figure 3.

2.3 The Setup of Ex-situ Ion Irradiation with Concurrent Loading
In this fiscal year, an experimental apparatus was designed to irradiate a sub-sized tensile
specimen (e.g., SS-3) under concurrent mechanical loading. Figure 4(a) shows a photograph of
the apparatus. A dual-loading mechanism was employed: a base load was applied using a
hanging weight of up to 1 kg attached to the bottom of the holder (not shown), while two springs,
each with a spring constant of 3526 N/m, provided additional downward force to achieve the
target total load.

Figure 4. (a) A photo of the holder for irradiating a sub-sized tensile specimen with loading. (b)
A temperature map of the sample as acquired by an infrared camera.
The tensile specimen was thinned to approximately 100 µm using a polishing methodology
similar to that used for 3 mm disc samples. The upper portion of the tensile specimen was
clamped by a rectangular holder, and a thermocouple was inserted between the specimen and the
holder. The lower portion of the specimen was separately attached to an adapter plate connected
to the loading weight.
The same 400 W quartz halogen lamp and heat shield described in the previous section were
employed to heat the specimen. Because heating occurred primarily through conduction from
the region where the tensile specimen contacted the holder, i.e., the upper portion, a temperature
gradient developed along the gauge section. As a result, the temperature measured by the
thermocouple differed from that at the irradiation location, which was approximately at the
center of the gauge section.
As shown in Figure 4(b), the temperature distribution was characterized using an Infratec
VarioCAM HD 900 infrared camera. For a typical specimen geometry and a target temperature
of 600°C, a temperature drop of approximately 50°C was observed between the top of the
specimen and the actual irradiation position. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, an infrared
camera will be used to directly monitor the temperature at the irradiated area, with calibration
performed against the thermocouple measurement.

2.4 Post-irradiation microstructure characterization
Two methods were used to prepare the TEM specimens of ex-situ irradiated samples:
electropolishing and focused ion beam (FIB). The electropolishing procedure has been described
in detail in FY24 report [5]. The FIB lift-out was carried out using a Thermal Fisher Scientific
Helios 5 CX Ga-FIB at CNM. Cross‑sectional specimens prepared by FIB lift‑out were used for
void‑swelling studies on samples irradiated to 5 dpa and 10 dpa. For imaging, the specimens
were first tilted to orientations that minimized overall contrast from the dislocation structures.
Voids were then imaged in bright field (BF) with under-focused and over-focused conditions at
magnifications of 30kx with 1 µm and 5 µm defocus for observing the void distribution across
the entire irradiation range and 100kx with 650 nm defocus to examine greater detail at a depth
of 600 nm from the surface. The diameter of the voids is reported as the size. Conventional BF
imaging was performed using a Hitachi H-9000 TEM at IVEM.
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific
Talos transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV at CNM to obtain elemental maps
for characterizing precipitates and radiation-induced segregation (RIS) at grain boundaries,
voids, and dislocations in the irradiated samples. For RIS characterization, EDS maps were
acquired using a 70 µm C2 condenser aperture and a spot size of 5.
The thickness of the FIB lamellae was measured by electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS)
using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Spectra transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV
at CNM. Thickness maps were acquired from the irradiated regions, and the local foil thickness
at selected depths (e.g., 600 nm from the irradiated surface) was determined by averaging the
thickness values over several adjacent pixels at each depth. For the 3 mm disc specimens, the
foil thickness was estimated using the thickness fringe method.
The neutron irradiated samples were characterized in the Low Activation Materials Development
and Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory at ORNL. TEM lamella was prepared with a FEI Versa
3D FIB and characterized with Thermo-Fisher Talos TEM.

3 Void Swelling in LPBF 316H Irradiated with 5 MeV Ni2+ ions
This chapter summarizes the effect of heat treatment on void swelling in LPBF 316H following
irradiation with 5 MeV Ni2+ ions. The microstructural evolution and swelling behavior of
specimens subjected to different heat-treatment conditions are compared to evaluate how thermal
processing influences void formation.
3.1 Dose Dependence
Figure 5 presents cross-sectional TEM images of AB, SR, SA, and CW LPBF 316H irradiated
at 600 °C as a function of dose. In all images, the top corresponds to the irradiated surface, while
the bottom is near the end of the ion range. Voids are observed in all conditions at the lowest
dose of 5 dpa, except for the CW sample, for which irradiation at 5 dpa was not performed. The
ion range and displacement profile, calculated using SRIM, are overlaid on one of the images to
representatively illustrate the correlation between void formation and damage. Consistent with
the damage profile, void swelling increases with depth; however, it begins to decrease prior to
reaching the peak damage region, likely due to the influence of injected interstitials [16, 17].
The void size as a function of dose is shown in Figure 6. For the SA, SR, and AB conditions, the
average void size increases from 5 dpa to 10 dpa. Between 10 dpa and 15 dpa, the SR condition
shows minimal change, whereas the AB condition continues to increase. In contrast, the SA
condition decreases over this dose interval. For the CW condition, void size increases from 10
dpa to 15 dpa. Across all doses, the void size follows the order SR > SA > AB > CW among the
four heat-treated conditions.
Figure 7 shows void density as a function of dose. The error bars represent an estimated
uncertainty of 20%, accounting for limitations in the EELS measurements. For the AB condition,
the void density decreases from 5 dpa to 10 dpa and remains at a similar level up to 15 dpa. The
SR condition exhibits minimal change in density across the entire dose range. For the SA
condition, the density slightly decreases from 5 dpa to 10 dpa, followed by a significant increase
from 10 dpa to 15 dpa. Similarly, the CW condition shows an increase in density from 10 dpa to
15 dpa, with a comparable rate to that of SA. Across all doses, the void density follows the order
AB > SR > CW > SA among the four heat-treated conditions. For comparison, wrought 316H
irradiated at 600 °C to 10 dpa exhibits a void density of 3.2×1018 m-3 (not shown), which is lower
than that of all LPBF 316H conditions.

For CW condition, voids are present at 600°C and 650°C, but not at 400°C or 500°C. At 600°C,

to have lower densities in regions containing dense slip bands.

Across the four HT conditions, void evolution exhibits a systematic dependence on both
temperature and initial microstructure. Increasing temperature generally promotes void growth
while reducing number density, resulting in peak swelling at intermediate temperatures.
However, the peak swelling temperature varies with condition, occurring near 600°C for AB and
SR, and shifting to higher temperature for SA and potentially to lower temperature for CW.
These differences reflect the distinct starting microstructures influencing defect mobility, void
nucleation, and growth kinetics.
As shown in Figure 12, at 600°C and below, the swelling consistently follows the order SR >
AB > SA > CW, the same trend observed in dose dependence discussed in the previous section.
Above 600°C, the difference among the four conditions becomes less pronounced. In
comparison with LPBF 316H, wrought 316H irradiated to 10 dpa exhibits no observable voids
at 300 °C and 400 °C, while only a very small amount of swelling is detected at 500 °C and 600
°C. Overall, wrought 316H shows lower swelling than all LPBF 316H conditions, except at 500
°C, where a marginal swelling of 0.0086% is measured in wrought 316H, whereas no swelling
is detected in the SA LPBF 316H condition. However, this difference is minimal. The higher
swelling in SA condition than CW condition at 600°C and 650°C is consistent with the well-
established effect of CW suppressing void swelling in 316 stainless steels [21-30].
Literature data for conventionally processed SA and CW 316 stainless steels irradiated with Ni
and C ions to doses up to 25 dpa are also included in Figure 12 for comparison [18-20]. It should
be noted that these studies involve helium co-implantation and higher doses; therefore, their
reported swelling values can be considered an upper bound when compared with the present
single-ion irradiation results at 10 dpa. Overall, the swelling behavior of SA and CW LPBF 316H
in this study roughly falls within the range reported in the literatures. The temperature
dependence of SA LPBF 316H qualitatively aligns with literature. In contrast, the AB and SR
conditions exhibit higher swelling, particularly at temperatures of 600 °C and below. These
results indicate that SA or CW heat treatments effectively reduce the swelling of LPBF 316H to
levels comparable to those of conventional 316 stainless steels.

3.3 Dislocation Density Dependence
The relative swelling among the four heat-treatment conditions generally follows the order SR
> AB > SA > CW over most of the temperature and dose range examined. The primary
distinction among these conditions is their initial dislocation density, which follows the order
CW > AB > SR > SA. Notably, the amount of swelling does not decrease monotonically with
increasing dislocation density. This result is contrary to the initial expectation based on the well-
established literature showing that conventionally CW stainless steels generally exhibit lower
swelling than their SA counterparts [21-30].
Recent ion irradiation studies have similarly shown that AB LPBF 316L and AB LPBF 316H
can exhibit greater swelling compared to their SA or wrought counterparts [12, 31, 32]. Chen et
al. proposed that AB LPBF 316L/H possesses an intermediate dislocation density—higher than
SA but lower than CW—such that a balance is achieved between dislocation sink strength and
void sink strength, i.e., Q-parameter ~ 1 [33, 34], thereby promoting enhanced void swelling
under the irradiation conditions examined [12]. In contrast, an excessively high initial dislocation
density in the CW condition can lead to excessive vacancy annihilation at dislocations, whereas
an insufficient dislocation density in the SA or wrought conditions can cause vacancy–interstitial
recombination to dominate during the early stages of irradiation. In both cases, void nucleation
and growth are delayed or suppressed.
The present study provides further support for this hypothesis by: (1) minimizing the effect of
compositional variation, (2) including CW condition, and (3) validating the interpretation
through rate theory calculation. For (1), previous comparisons between AM 316 and wrought
316 often involve materials with non-negligible compositional differences. Such variations can
affect the swelling behavior. For example, swelling has been reported to increase with increasing
Cr [35, 36], O [37-39] and Si (up to 0.2 wt% [40]), but with decreasing Ni [35]. Swelling
decreases with increasing Mn (up to 1 wt% [36]), but increases with further increases in Mn. Mo
has shown a mixed effect depending on the temperature and the overall composition. Given these
complexities, carefully designed experiments that minimize compositional differences are
required to isolate the role of microstructure.
Among the AB, SA and CW conditions, compositional differences are minimal. They all have a
dilute distribution of aluminum oxides, and no significant difference in those particles is
observed among them. The 20% CW was produced directly from the AB material, so their
compositions are identical. SA dissolves the elemental segregation (Cr enrichment and Fe
depletion) at the cell walls of the AB material, thereby slightly changing the composition in
matrix. However, this redistribution is not expected to have a significant effect because both the
volume fraction of the cell-wall regions and the magnitude of segregation are limited [41].
For SR condition, its aluminum oxide particles are also comparable to other conditions; however,
the effect of M23C6 precipitation on matrix composition needs to be considered. Thermo-Calc
calculation, considering only FCC austenite and M23C6, indicates that equilibrium mole fraction
of M23C6 at 650°C is 1.8%. Under this condition, the matrix Cr and C contents are predicted to
decrease from 17 wt.% and 0.08 wt.% to approximately 16 wt.% and nearly 0 wt.%, respectively
The Cr depletion in the SR condition would be expected to reduce swelling [35, 36]. However,
the SR condition exhibits the highest swelling among all conditions. This suggests that Cr
depletion associated with M23C6 formation is unlikely to be the origin of the observed behavior.

 25

By contrast, carbon depletion is qualitatively consistent with the present observation. Chen et al.
reported lower swelling is in a LPBF 316H (0.04 wt% C) than in a LPBF 316L (0.006 wt%) after
irradiation at 600°C with 4 MeV Ni ions to 10 dpa [12]. Nevertheless, that study did not use
materials specifically designed to isolate carbon as the only variable. Therefore, although carbon
depletion in the SR condition may contribute to the enhanced swelling observed here, its role
remains unresolved. Further investigation using systematically designed alloys in which carbon
content is independently varied is needed to clarify this effect.
For (2), the inclusion of CW condition qualitatively validates the hypothesis. As discussed
earlier, the higher swelling observed in SR and AB conditions is attributed to their intermediate
dislocation density readily creating a balance between dislocation sink strength and cavity sink
strength. Based on this hypothesis, either increasing (Q >> 1) dislocation density or decreasing
it (Q << 1) should lead to reduced swelling, which is consistent with the results shown in Figure
8 and Figure 12. Previous studies have reported the decreased swelling after SA [31, 32], and
the present study provides complementary evidence on the opposite side of the peak swelling
condition through CW treatment.

sink bias required to maximize void growth. The increased absorption of interstitials by
dislocations at lower dose rates correspondingly enhances vacancy survival, resulting in a larger

3.4 Radiation-Induced Segregation
Figure 17 shows elemental maps of AB LPBF 316H irradiated to 10 dpa at 600°C, including the
irradiated region at the top and the unirradiated region below. In the unirradiated region, the
dislocation cell structure remains visible in the BF image, indicating annealing a 600°C for about
2.8 hours did not eliminate the dislocation cell structure, although the density may have been
reduced. Elemental mapping reveals Fe depletion and Cr segregation at the cell walls. However,
unlike previous studies [43], segregation of Mo and Si was not evident. Aluminum oxide
particles are present, but they show no clear association with the dislocation cell structure.
In the irradiated region, the dislocation cell structure and its associated segregation are largely
removed. Instead, voids are observed, with Ni enrichment surrounding them, as highlighted in
the inset of the Ni map. Detailed segregation at voids will be discussed later. The Cr segregation
and Fe depletion characteristic of dislocation cell walls in the unirradiated region were no longer
obvious. Instead, Ni enrichment was detected, likely resulting from irradiation-induced
segregation at pre-existing dislocations. In addition, the Si–Ni-rich particles reported in [43] are
not observed in the present study, likely because of the much lower Si content.

The same EDS analysis was performed for the SR, AB, and CW conditions, and all four
conditions exhibit qualitatively consistent segregation behavior. However, a quantitative
comparison between the conditions is not feasible because of several limitations inherent to the

 35

analysis. First, the void surface may not be oriented edge-on to the electron beam. Second, owing
to the specimen thickness, the measured composition profiles are influenced by out-of-plane
elemental distributions, resulting in averaging of the segregation profiles. Third, beam
broadening causes the X-ray signal to be integrated over a larger lateral volume, leading to
blurring of the void–matrix interface. Finally, EDS measurements are subject to relatively large
uncertainties, particularly for minor and light elements. The first three factors systematically
reduce the apparent segregation magnitude and therefore lead to an underestimation of the peak
enrichment or depletion at the void surface.

5 Experimental Estimation of the microstructure of SR and SA
  LPBF 316H Irradiated with neutrons
LPBF 316H irradiated at 400°C to 10 dpa in HFIR. AMMT irradiated the SR and SA LPBF
316H in HFIR at 400°C to 2 dpa and 10 dpa [10, 11]. To date, only the 2 dpa specimens have
been characterized at ORNL. This section describes the use of dual-ion irradiation to predict swelling
in the two materials irradiated to 10 dpa in HFIR. The methodology first applies rate theory to
estimate the irradiation temperature and helium implantation rate (appm/dpa) required for dual-ion
irradiation to reproduce the microstructure observed in the 2 dpa neutron-irradiated specimens. This
analysis was performed at ORNL using an approach similar to that described in Ref. [3]. Specifically,
the analysis considered the temperature shift needed to compensate for the flux difference between
ion irradiation (10-3 dpa/s) and neutron irradiation (~10-6 dpa/s), as well as helium partitioning and
trapping at dislocations and within the matrix. The recommended irradiation parameters are provided
in Table 4 in the subsection, Simulating Neutron-Irradiated Microstructure in HFIR.
Figure 25(a-b) shows the cavity distributions in dual-beam irradiated SA (525°C to 2 dpa and 50
appm He) and SR (500°C to 2 dpa and 50 appm He) LPBF 316H designed to replicate the
microstructure of the same materials neutron irradiated to 2 dpa at 400°C, shown in Figure 25(c-d).
Tiny cavities were observed in all samples. The larger white features in the neutron irradiated SA
sample are aluminum oxides, as confirmed by EDS mapping. The cavity analysis comparing ion-
and neutron-irradiated samples is presented in Figure 26. Both neutron and ion irradiation produced
a bimodal cavity size distribution. The smaller cavity population corresponds to He bubbles, whose
growth is primarily driven by helium influx, whereas the larger cavity population corresponds to
voids, whose growth is primarily driven by vacancy absorption. He bubbles transition to voids once
their size exceeds the critical radius [45, 46]. For both neutron and ion conditions, the peak He bubble
size is less than 5 nm. In contrast, the neutron-irradiated samples appear to exhibit larger voids and
a higher void-to-bubble ratio than the ion-irradiated samples.

6 Effect of Stress on the Void Swelling in LPBF 709 and 316H
AM 709 in three conditions—as-built, solution annealed, and precipitation treated—was
irradiated with 5 MeV Ni2+ at 600°C, both with and without concurrent loading up to 150 MPa.
TEM characterization indicated that the applied stress enhanced swelling in all conditions. A
complete comparison of all heat-treated LPBF 709 conditions is provided in a separate report
[47].
As an example, the results for solution-annealed LPBF 709 are shown in Figure 29. Compared
with the unstressed condition, the stressed sample exhibited a smaller average void size and a
significantly higher void density. Within the 400–800 nm depth range, the average void size was
40.4 nm (SD = 18.9 nm) for the unstressed sample and 16.6 nm (SD = 10.8 nm) for the stressed
sample. Figure 30(a) shows the void size distributions over the full analyzed depth range. Under
the stressed condition, a bimodal size distribution was observed.

with 5 MeV Ni2+ ions at 600°C to 10 dpa (a) without and (b) concurrent stress of 108 MPa. The
defocus amount is 5 µm. The top edge of the images is the irradiated surface. The loading

Figure 30(b) shows the areal density of cavities as a function of irradiation depth for both
conditions. The stressed sample exhibited a significantly higher cavity density than the
unstressed sample across all depths. In the stressed condition, the void density increased with

of 100 nm and considering only the 400–800 nm depth range, the swelling was estimated to be

Various theories have been studied in regard of the role of stress in the formation and evolution
of defects in materials under irradiation [48-50]. In regard of irradiation-induced dislocation
loops, two mechanisms are proposed: stress-induced preferred absorption (SIPA) [51] is
associated with enhanced growth of prismatic loops, with the rate of preferential absorption

 47

increasing as the resolved normal stress on the corresponding habit planes becomes greater. In
contrast, Stress-induced preferred nucleation (SIPN) [52] describes a process in which radiation-
induced loops preferentially nucleate within displacement cascades as the resolved normal stress
acting on their habit planes increases.
Stress also affects the formation and evolution of irradiation-induced voids. Tensile hydrostatic
stress lowers the critical embryo size required for a void to become stable, thereby reducing the
incubation dose needed for the onset of steady-state swelling. Porter et al. reported that tensile
stress caused swelling to begin at a lower irradiation dose in 316 stainless steel [53]. However,
at a given swelling level, the void size and density, as well as the steady-state swelling rate, were
not significantly affected by stress. Portnykh et al. studied 20% cold-worked
16Cr15Ni2MoTiMnSi steel irradiated in the BN-600 fast reactor at 467°C under hoop stresses
of 0, 100, and 200 MPa [54]. They found that overall swelling increased with increasing applied
stress. In addition, the mean void size decreased while the void density increased with increasing
stress, indicating that stress enhanced swelling primarily by promoting void nucleation. Our
preliminary observations for ion-irradiated LPBF 709 and LPBF 316H (Figure 31 and Figure
30) are consistent with these literature reports. Garner et al. attributed the stress effect to its
changes in interstitial capture efficiency of voids and the changes in the vacancy emission rate
of various microstructural components [55].
On the other hand, stress can also affect the growth of a void. Kohnert and Capolungo
demonstrated that compressive and tensile strains from dislocations can accelerate and decelerate
void growth, respectively [56]. This observation is consistent with our observation in Figure 29
and Figure 31, where the cavity size was smaller in the stressed samples than in the unstressed
samples. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of performing ion irradiation with
concurrent stress and indicate that stress has a significant effect on void formation in additively
manufactured steels. More comprehensive studies are planned for FY27.

7 Conclusion
During FY 2026, work under the DOE-NE Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies
program continued to advance the understanding of irradiation-induced defect evolution in additively
manufactured LPBF 316H stainless steel while further developing experimental capabilities needed
to support the LAIN framework. The work emphasized the role of pre-irradiation processing
condition on cavity evolution under ion irradiation, the effect of concurrent helium implantation, the
use of ion irradiation to estimate neutron-relevant microstructures, and the development of a new
capability for ion irradiation under concurrent mechanical loading.
The single-ion irradiation results show that the swelling response of LPBF 316H is highly sensitive
to pre-irradiation heat treatment and deformation history. Among the conditions examined, the
stress-relieved and as-built materials exhibited higher swelling than the solution-annealed and cold-
worked materials over most of the temperature and dose range studied, while wrought 316H
generally showed lower swelling than the as-built and stress-relieved LPBF conditions. These
observations indicate that the irradiation performance of LPBF 316H is governed not simply by
nominal alloy composition, but also by the specific defect structure retained or introduced during
processing. In particular, the results support the interpretation that the intermediate initial dislocation
densities present in the stress-relieved and as-built conditions are especially favorable for void
nucleation and growth under the present ion irradiation conditions, whereas the lower dislocation
density in the solution-annealed condition and the much higher dislocation density in the cold-
worked condition both act to suppress swelling. The accompanying rate-theory calculations are
consistent with this interpretation and provide a mechanistic basis for the experimentally observed
trend.
The temperature-dependent irradiation results further showed that the peak swelling behavior
depends on the starting microstructure. For the as-built and stress-relieved conditions, swelling
reached a maximum near 600°C, while the solution-annealed and cold-worked conditions showed
reduced swelling and indications of a shifted temperature response. Post-irradiation characterization
also showed that irradiation substantially alters the AM-specific microstructure. In the as-built
condition, the dislocation cell structure and associated elemental segregation observed prior to
irradiation were largely diminished within the irradiated region, while irradiation-induced
segregation developed at voids, grain boundaries, slip bands, and oxide particles. The segregation
behavior observed around voids, including Ni enrichment and Cr depletion, was qualitatively
consistent across the different LPBF conditions and provides additional information for future model
development and validation.
The dual-ion irradiation experiments demonstrated that helium significantly modifies cavity
evolution in LPBF 316H and reduces the difference in swelling behavior between the solution-
annealed and stress-relieved conditions as the helium injection rate increases. In general, increasing
helium content led to smaller cavities and higher cavity densities, although the resulting effect on
swelling depended on the initial microstructure. At lower helium injection rates, the starting
dislocation structure remained a dominant factor in determining swelling behavior, while at higher
helium injection rates the role of helium became increasingly important. These observations
highlight the need to consider both sink structure and helium partitioning when relating ion
irradiation behavior to neutron irradiation conditions.
A key outcome of this work is the continued demonstration that dual-ion irradiation, when guided
by mechanistic modeling, can be used to experimentally estimate neutron-relevant microstructures
in additively manufactured steels. Irradiation conditions selected to approximate HFIR neutron

 49

irradiation at 400°C produced cavity distributions in LPBF 316H that were in reasonable agreement
with the corresponding neutron-irradiated materials characterized to date. On that basis, additional
dual-ion irradiations were performed to estimate the microstructures expected after neutron
irradiation to 10 dpa in HFIR and under lower-helium conditions representative of a harder neutron
spectrum. These experiments provide data that will be useful for future comparison with neutron-
irradiated specimens as they become available, and they represent practical progress toward
quantitatively linking ion and neutron irradiation responses within the LAIN framework.
FY 2026 also included the design and demonstration of an experimental setup for ion irradiation
under concurrent tensile loading. Initial tests confirmed the feasibility of the loading and heating
approach and identified the temperature calibration considerations associated with this specimen
geometry. Preliminary irradiation results for LPBF 709 and LPBF 316H further showed that applied
stress has a measurable effect on cavity evolution. In the stressed samples, cavity size was smaller
than in the corresponding unstressed samples, while the overall response was consistent with stress-
enhanced void formation through increased nucleation. These observations agree with prior literature
indicating that stress can reduce the incubation dose for swelling and modify the balance between
void nucleation and growth. Although this effort remains at an early stage, it establishes an important
new capability for future studies of coupled irradiation and stress effects in additively manufactured
alloys and demonstrates that stress should be considered an important variable in assessing
irradiation performance.
Overall, the FY 2026 results advance the understanding of how processing history influences
irradiation response in LPBF 316H and provide new experimental support for the interpretation of
ion irradiation data in a neutron-relevant context. The work shows that the retained AM
microstructure, especially the initial dislocation structure, plays a notable role in determining
swelling behavior, and that helium can substantially alter this response. The results also show that
concurrent stress significantly affects cavity formation behavior, primarily by influencing void
nucleation and cavity size evolution. Together, these efforts provide a stronger experimental basis
for using ion irradiation, dual-ion irradiation, and rate-theory analysis to bridge ion and neutron
irradiation conditions, while also extending that framework to coupled irradiation-stress conditions
relevant to structural service environments. These advances support the broader objective of
accelerating the qualification of additively manufactured structural materials for nuclear energy
applications.
