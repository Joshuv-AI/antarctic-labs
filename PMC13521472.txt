The Molecular Pathology of Morules in Endometrial Carcinoma: Biological Significance and Clinical Implications
Authors: Makoto Saegusa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMorules were ordinally described as mulberry‐like clusters of endometrial metaplasia with the potential to differentiate toward true squamous metaplasia (SqM). Although both morular and SqM lesions are categorized under “endometrial carcinoma (Em Ca) with squamous differentiation” in the WHO classification, they represent biologically distinct entities. Notably, morules are strongly associated with CTNNB1 (β‐catenin) mutations. This review summarizes the molecular mechanisms driving the differentiation of Em Ca cells toward both morular and SqM phenotypes. Morules are frequently identified in younger Em Ca patients and display a unique immunophenotype characterized by nuclear β‐catenin accumulation, a lack of expression of estrogen‐α and progesterone receptor expression, and a low proliferative index. In Em Ca cells harboring CTNNB1 alterations, the PTEN/β‐catenin axis suppresses cell proliferation and induces epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, promoting morule‐related cancer stem cell (CSC) properties. Conversely, p40 expression serves as the initial signaling event establishing SqM‐related CSC features within the morule‐SqM cascade. Furthermore, the ezrin‐radixin‐moesin‐binding phosphoprotein 50/S100A4/myosin heavy chain 9 axis drives terminal squamous differentiation, particularly under hypoxic conditions. Clinically, histopathological evaluation of morular morphology and nuclear β‐catenin status serves as a valuable tool for predicting progestin therapeutic efficacy in young Em Ca patients.Keywords: β‐catenin, EBP50, morule, myosin 9, PTEN, squamous metaplasia, S100A4

Introduction

Historical Context and Morphological Characteristics

The morphological distinctions between squamoid nodules and true squamous metaplasia (SqM) in the endometrium were reviewed as early as 1930 by Mayer [1]. The term “morule” was subsequently coined in 1959 by Dutra to describe a mulberry‐like endometrial metaplasia that protrudes into the lumens of endometrial glands and/or the surrounding stroma, frequently demonstrating a potential for squamous differentiation [2].

Histologically, morules present as variably sized solid nests consisting of spindled or ovoid cells with ill‐defined cell borders [3, 4]. They exhibit an aberrant neuroectodermal phenotype, characterized by the expression of neuron‐specific enolase, synaptophysin, S100, somatostatin, acetylcholinesterase, and BCL2 [5, 6, 7]. Morules are more frequently encountered in endometrial malignancies and precancers lesions [3, 8, 9]. Interestingly, its precise lineage remains a subject of debate: some studies propose that the morule represents an immature form of squamous differentiation [8, 10, 11, 12], whereas others argue it is a peculiar form of non‐squamous differentiation [13, 14].

Conversely, SqM is composed of the classic cytologic and architectural features typically associated with stratified squamous epithelium [15, 16]. It can be observed in both endometrial carcinoma (Em Ca) and non‐neoplastic conditions, such as chronic endometritis, ichthyosis, and intrauterine device (IUD) usage [17, 18]. Although both morular and SqM lesions are categorized under “Em Ca with squamous differentiation” by the WHO classification [19], they are biologically distinct entities; notably, morules are strongly linked to CTNNB1 (β‐catenin) mutations, which are characteristically absent in conventional SqM [20].

Morules have also been reported in a variety of other human neoplasms, including well‐differentiated fetal adenocarcinoma of the lung, colonic adenocarcinoma and adenoma, cribriform morular thyroid carcinoma, and gallbladder adenoma, which are accompanied by aberrant nuclear β‐catenin accumulation [6, 21, 22, 23, 24]: this indicates that abnormal activation of Wnt signaling pathway may be a common feature during the development of morular lesions [21].

Prognostic and Clinical Implications

Whether squamous differentiation holds prognostic significance in Em Ca remains controversial. While some studies indicate that the presence of squamous differentiation does not correlate with lymph node metastasis or recurrence in Em Ca [25, 26], others suggest a survival impact in specific Em Ca subtypes [27, 28]. In a therapeutic context, however, the presence of morules alone prior to fertility‐sparing treatment is associated with a significantly higher complete response rate. This favorable response is driven by the suppression of Em Ca cell proliferation and the simultaneous induction of morular differentiation within the tumor following progestin therapy [29].

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) introduces a molecular classification that defines four prognostic groups: POLE‐ultramutated (Polymerase Epsilon‐ultramutated), microsatellite instability‐high (mismatch repair [MMR]‐deficient), copy‐number high/p53‐abnormal, and no specific molecular profile (NSMP) [30]. Nuclear β‐catenin identified a higher‐risk subset within the prognostically heterogenous NSMP group [31], whereas aberrant nuclear β‐catenin distribution did not significantly correlate with recurrence in endometrioid‐type Em Ca, nor in the early stage, low grade and NSMP sub‐cohorts [32].

The present review describes the molecular mechanism(s) underlying the development and progression of morule and SqM lesions in Em Ca. The biological significance and the clinical implications of morule development are also discussed.

Clinically Phenotypic Characteristics of Morules

Clinicopathological Features of Morules

Early‐type morules demonstrate island‐like features within the complex glandular lesions of Em Ca, whereas the late‐type morules progressively protrude into the cancerous lumens, eventually assuming a plug‐like appearance [3]. Both morular types exhibit a unique immunophenotype characterized by nuclear β‐catenin accumulation, a lack of expression of estrogen‐α (ESR1) and progesterone receptors (PR) expression, and a low proliferative index [7, 33, 34] (Figure 1A).

Morular lesions were identified in 27 cases (13.6%) out of a cohort of 199 grade (G) 1, G2, and G3 Em Cas [26, 27, 28]. Broken down by grade and patient demographics, morules were present in 27 (21%) of 128 G1 tumors and in 19 (82.6%) of 23 young patients (mean age: 32.2 ± 7.1 years) with G1 tumors who underwent medotoxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) therapy for fertility preservation [33, 34, 35]. In our recent data, 12 (7.2%) cases had pure morules, 32 (19.2%) had coexistence of morules and SqM (morule/SqM), and 36 (21.6%) were classical SqM lesions. These were a subset of the 166 consecutive endometrioid‐type Em Ca case (including 92 grade 1, 43 grade 2, and 31 grade 3) [36].

CTNNB1 Abnormality in Morules

Mutations within the serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) phosphorylation region in exon 3 of CTNNB1 prevent its phosphorylation and subsequent ubiquitination. This leads to an increase in intracellular and nuclear β‐catenin levels, thereby activating β‐catenin‐dependent signaling pathways [37]. CTNNB1 mutations are detected in 16 cases (22.9%) of a mixed‐grade (G1/G2/G3) Em Ca cohort, and in 46 (66.7%) of 70 G1 tumors featuring morules [7, 33, 34, 35], including 7 (78%) of 9 cases with pure morules, 9 (82%) of 11 cases with morule/SqM, and 2 (25%) of 10 cases with SqM (unpublished data). These mutations are also observed in 3 (12.5%) of 24 atypical endometrial hyperplasia lesions [38], suggesting that aberrant β‐catenin signaling is an early event in endometrial tumorigenesis.

In Em Ca cases harboring CTNNB1 mutations, nuclear β‐catenin accumulation is diffusely and intensely observed throughout the morular lesions. In contrast, the surrounding carcinoma (Sur Ca) cells display only sporadic or focal β‐catenin immunopositivity. Given that the identical point mutations are shared between both components [33, 34] (Figure 1B), these findings strongly suggest that additional molecular or microenvironmental factors are required to drive the robust nuclear β‐catenin localization characteristic of morules.

In Em Ca cases with a lack of CTNNB1 mutations, other factors including Adenomatous Polyposis Coli (APC), Axin, and the glycogen synthase kinase‐3 (GSK‐3) complex contribute to activation of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway [39]. For example, activating mutations in CTNNB1 (23%–36%) and loss‐of‐function mutations in Axin (5%–10%) are common in hepatocellular carcinoma [40, 41], and other Wnt pathway mutations are also observed in melanoma, uterine, gastric, and pancreatic ductal carcinoma [41, 42, 43, 44].

Consistent with the presence of CTNNB1 mutations, there was a significant stepwise decrease in nuclear β‐catenin accumulation moving from pure morules, through morule/SqM, to SqM lesions [36], indicating that the majority (~75%) of SqM lesions developed in a de novo manner from the surrounding carcinomas without the mutations, whereas the remaining ~25% arise from tumors with CTNNB1 mutations during a terminal differentiation process moving from morule to a true SqM―collectively termed the morule‐SqM cascade.

Morule Development: Inhibition of Cell Proliferation

Suppression of Em Ca cell proliferation through complex interplays between several signaling pathways is an initial step in the development of morular lesions. These pathways are discussed in the following sections.

The β‐Catenin/TCF4 Axis and Activation of the p14arf/p53/p21waf1 Pathway

In Em Ca cell lines stably overexpressing β‐catenin, the expression of cyclin D1, p14arf, p53, and p21waf1 is upregulated via β‐catenin/TCF4‐mediated activation of the CDKN2A (p14arf) and CCND1 (cyclin D1) promoters. This activation subsequently suppresses cell proliferation and induces senescence‐like features through the direct upregulation of p21waf1. Consistent with these in vitro findings, nuclear β‐catenin accumulation in human morular lesions is positively associated with the expression of cyclin D1, p53, and p21waf1, and inversely correlated with Ki‐67 proliferative status [34]. Furthermore, the overexpression of cyclin D1 contributes to morular differentiation through a mechanism that appears independent of its classical role in cell cycle progression (Figure 2).

Schematic Model of Proliferation Inhibition During Morular Differentiation in Em Ca. The β‐catenin/TCF4 signaling pathway acts as a critical inhibitor of Em Ca cell proliferation via downstream activation of the p14arf/p53/p21waf1 signaling cascade and modulation of cyclin D1 expression. This differentiation process is further amplified by cooperative crosstalk with multiple upstream and parallel regulators, including Egr1, Sox7/Sox4, TCF4, and the Notch pathway.

Upregulation of p21waf1 via β‐Catenin/Cdx2 Signaling Pathway

The mammalian homologues of the Drosophila caudal homeobox genes, Cdx1 and Cdx2, encode nuclear transcription factors that drive cell differentiation during embryonic development [45, 46, 47]. Cdx2 also plays a vital role in regulating cell growth and differentiation within intestinal and colonic tissues [48, 49]. Within endometrial morules, Cdx2 expression directly overlaps with the nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin and p21waf1, while inversely correlating with cell proliferation. This spatial alignment is consistent with the transcriptional activation of the Cdx2 promoter by β‐catenin via a TCF4‐independent mechanism. Furthermore, the overexpression of Cdx2 significantly suppresses cell proliferation by stabilizing p21waf1 mRNA transcripts during morular differentiation [50].

Upregulation of p16ink4a by β‐Catenin in Early‐Type Morules

The p16ink4a/product of the retinoblastoma gene (Rb) pathway plays a critical role in regulating the restriction point at the G1/S cell cycle transition [51, 52]. Notably, p16ink4a is frequently upregulated in early‐type morules, contrasting sharply with its decreased expression in late‐type morules. In Em Ca cell lines, β‐catenin transcriptionally activates the CDKN2A (p16INK4A) promoter via a TCF4‐independent mechanism, leading to increased p16ink4a expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. Mechanistically, active cell proliferation is accompanied by Rb phosphorylation, alongside basal p16ink4a expression. Conversely, growth inhibition via serum starvation decreases the expression of active Rb but does reduce p16ink4a levels, resulting in a high relative ratio of p16ink4a to Rb. Thus, nuclear β‐catenin‐mediated p16ink4a expression, coupled with a concurrent loss of functional Rb, suppresses cell proliferation and drives the differentiation of early‐type morules. Subsequent downregulation of this pathway is associated with the progression to the late‐type morules [53].

A Positive Feedback Loop of TCF4 Expression

The TCF/LEF transcription factor family comprises four core members―TCF1, LEF1, TCF3, and TCF4―which share several structural similarities [54, 55]. Each protein harbors a β‐catenin‐binding domain at its extreme N terminus, while a highly conserved high‐mobility group (HMG)‐box domains―which recognizes the consensus DNA sequence 5'‐CTTTG(A/T)(A/T)‐3'―is located centrally or near the C‐terminus [56]. The nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin and its physical interaction with TCF/LEF factors are the defining events in Wnt/β‐catenin signal transduction [57, 58, 59]. In Em Ca lesions, TCF4 expression frequently overlaps with robust nuclear β‐catenin accumulation within morules. Conversely, TCF4 expression significantly decreases as the morphology transitions from a morular to a SqM phenotype (the morule‐SqM cascade). Mechanically, the TCF4 promoter contains a single, consensus TCF‐binding site that is critical for autoactivation by the β‐catenin/TCF4 complex. Furthermore, the p300 coactivator is required to enhance this β‐catenin‐dependent transcriptional activity in a TCF4‐dependent manner. Collectively, these findings indicate that a positive feedback loop of TCF4 expression, mediated by the β‐catenin/TCF4/p300 transcriptional complex, drives the initial steps of morular differentiation. In contrast, the inactivation of this β‐catenin/TCF4/p300 signaling axis is strongly associated with the induction of the morule‐SqM cascade [60] (Figure 2).

Upregulation of TCF4 via the Sox7/Sox4 Signaling Pathway

The sex‐determining region Y (Sry) box, or Sox, family comprises 20 highly conserved transcription factors that play critical roles in diverse developmental processes across most vertebrates [61, 62, 63]. Depending on the cellular context and their specific interacting partners, Sox factors can act as either activators or repressors of the β‐catenin/TCF signaling pathway [64, 65]. In Em Ca cells, Sox7 transcriptionally upregulates Sox4 expression. However, this cascade not only inhibits Sox4‐mediated β‐catenin/TCF4‐driven transcription but also represses Sox7's own promoter activity, highlighting a highly complex, self‐regulating feedback loop within Sox7‐mediated pathways. Conversely, Sox4 enhances β‐catenin/TCF4‐dependent transcription by directly upregulating TCF4 expression, independent of any direct physical association with β‐catenin. In vitro, cells stably overexpressing Sox4 exhibit significantly decreased proliferation rate, which is accompanied by the concurrent upregulation of TCF4 and p21waf1. In clinical Em Ca samples, Sox4 expression is predominantly pronounced within the morular lesions, correlating positively with β‐catenin, TCF4, and Sox7 status, and inversely with the Ki‐67 proliferative index. Taken together, these data indicate that Sox4 positively regulates morular differentiation by modulating TCF4 expression and suppressing cell proliferation. Sox7 further participates in this differentiation process by transcriptionally driving Sox4 expression, thereby playing a nuanced, regulatory role in tuning the overarching β‐catenin/TCF4 signaling axis [66] (Figure 2).

Upregulation of TCF4 via the Egr1/p300 Complex

Early growth response 1 (Egr1) is a zinc‐finger transcription factor that is rapidly and transiently induced by a diverse array of extracellular stimuli. It binds to a GC‐rich consensus DNA sequence 5′‐GNG(T/G)GGG(T/C)G‐3′ to regulate a broad spectrum of cellular responses, spanning survival, apoptosis, growth arrest, senescence, differentiation, and neoplastic transformation [67, 68, 69]. In vitro, Egr1 mRNA and protein expression is significantly increased following serum and 17‐β‐estradiol stimulation in Em Ca cell lines possessing high baseline ESR1 levels, consistent with observations in the rodent uterus and MCF‐7 breast carcinoma cells [70, 71]. Serum‐stimulated cells concurrently show increased expression of TCF4 and p300, whereas the knockdown of Egr1 via specific siRNA decreases the expression of both molecules. Mechanically, Egr1 transactivates the TCF4 and EP300 (p300) promoters by interacting with specific binding sites, subsequently facilitating the nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin. Conversely, excessive Egr1 overexpression paradoxically inhibits β‐catenin/TCF4/p300‐mediated transcription, likely due to the sequestration (squelching) of limited intracellular pools of the p300 coactivator. While Egr1 promoter activity is elevated by serum, it is unaffected by 17‐β‐estradiol, and its eventual down‐regulation is intricately tied to alterations in the expression of TCF4, p300, and Egr1 itself―underscoring a tightly controlled, muti‐tiered negative feedback loops. In clinical Em Ca tissue sections featuring morules, nuclear Egr1 expression is only sporadically distributed within glandular components. It lacks a direct spatial correction with nuclear β‐catenin or TCF4 localization, a distribution pattern likely maintained by these prominent negative feedback loop. Taken together, these findings suggest that Egr1 serves as an early upstream modulator of the β‐catenin/TCF4/p300 signaling axis, initiating the transcriptional wiring required for morular differentiation before being down‐regulated to allow formal morular maturation [72] (Figure 2).

Interplay Between β‐Catenin and Notch Signaling Pathways

The Notch signaling pathway is a critical contributor to tissue development and cellular homeostasis [73, 74, 75]. In Em Ca cells, the promoters of hairy and enhancer of split 1 (Hes1) and mastermind‐like 2 (MAML2) are directly activated by the Notch intracellular domain 1 (NICD1) rather than β‐catenin, operating through a positive feedback loop sustained between Hes1 and MAML2. Intriguingly, the expression levels of nuclear β‐catenin, Hes1, and MAML2―along with the physical interactions between β‐catenin and either Hes1 or MAML2―are significantly enriched in morular lesions compared to the Sur Ca. Mechanistically, the inhibition of GSK‐3β triggers the activation of this NICD1‐mediated Hes1/MAML2 signaling loop while simultaneously driving the stabilization and nuclear translocation of β‐catenin. This dual action facilitates the assembly of active, multi‐protein transcriptional complexes composed of β‐catenin, Hes1, and MAML2. Ultimately, these cooperative complexes orchestrate β‐catenin‐driven morular differentiation within the tumor [76] (Figure 2).

Crosstalk between NF‐κB and β‐Catenin Signaling Pathways

The nuclear factor κB (NF‐κB)/p65 signaling pathway plays a vital role in determining cell lineage and phenotype by regulating the expression of an expansive network of genes, including the mesenchymal markers Snail and vimentin [77, 78, 79, 80]. In the normal endometrium, nuclear phosphorylated p65 (pp65)―the transcriptionally active form of the NF‐κB subunit―increases significantly during the secretory phase of the menstrual cycle. This increase correlates positively with vimentin and E‐cadherin expression, and inversely with Snail mRNA expression. Mechanistically, p65 directly modulates the transcriptional expression of vimentin, E‐cadherin, and Snail, suggesting a physiological role for NF‐κB signaling in inducing the secretory phenotype. Conversely, during morular differentiation, the levels of nuclear pp65, Snail mRNA, vimentin, and cytoplasmic TNF‐α are profoundly down‐regulated, demonstrating a strong inverse correlation with nuclear β‐catenin accumulation. In vitro, the overexpression of p65 directly suppresses β‐catenin/TCF4‐mediated transcriptional activity, while active GSK‐3β represses TNF‐α‐induced NF‐κB activation. Collectively, these findings indicate that a coordinated molecular shift from NF‐κB‐dependent signaling to the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway―orchestrated by changes in GSK‐3β expression and activity―is crucial for initiating morular differentiation. This pathway transition ultimately results in the transcriptional silencing of Snail and vimentin expression within morular lesions [81].

Hypoxic Signaling and Intersecting Pathways

Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1) serves as a master transcription factor orchestrating the cellular adaptation to hypoxic stress. It regulates an expansive array of genes encoding metabolic enzymes, growth factors, extracellular matrix components, and coagulation factors [82, 83, 84]. The oxygen‐sensitive subunit, HIF‐1α, is primarily regulated post‐transcriptionally by oxygen‐dependent hydroxylation of two proline residues within its oxygen‐dependent degradation domain, targeting it for proteasomal destruction under normoxic conditions [85, 86, 87]. Additionally, HIF‐1α transcription can be upregulated by upstream signaling pathways, including the NF‐κB and phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/AKT axes [88, 89]. In Em Ca cells, long‐term exposure to cobalt chloride (CoCl2)―a well‐established chemical mimetic of hypoxia [90, 91, 92] ―suppressed cell growth and drives the accumulation of HIF‐1α protein, independent of changes in its mRNA levels. Conversely, short‐term exposure triggers a rapid, transient spike in HIF‐1α mRNA expression that coincides with the nuclear stabilization of the NF‐κB subunit p65. Mechanistically, p65 overexpression directly transactivates the HIF‐1α promoter, while elevated HIF‐1α levels simultaneously enhance NF‐κB‐dependent transcription, establishing a reciprocal positive feedback loop between the NF‐κB and HIF‐1α signaling pathways. Furthermore, HIF‐1α physically interacts with the p300 coactivator, indirectly bridging it to nuclear β‐catenin and modestly enhancing both HIF‐1α and β‐catenin‐mediated transcriptional activities. In clinical Em Ca tissue specimens, the levels of nuclear pp65 and nuclear β‐catenin are significantly enriched in HIF‐1α‐positive lesions compared to HIF‐1α‐negative areas. Taken together, these cooperative transcriptional networks involving HIF‐1α, NF‐κB, and β‐catenin/p300 complexes are critical for modulating tumor cell kinetics and survival strategies in response to microenvironmental hypoxia [93].

Inactivation of GSK‐3β in Morules

The GSK‐3β serves as a master negative regulator of the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling cascade [76]. In vitro, treatment of Em Ca cells with lithium chloride (LiCl)―a classic GSK‐3β inhibitor―significantly increases total GSK‐3β levels as well as its phosphorylated (inactive) form at both nuclear and cytoplasmic sites. This inhibition induces a phenotypic shift characterized by a fibroblastic morphology, decreased cellular proliferation, and an attenuated migration rate [76], closely mimicking the baseline characteristics of human morular lesions. The intracellular localization of GSK‐3β is highly dynamic; its nuclear levels increase substantially in G1‐arrested cells, whereas it is sequestered exclusively within the cytoplasm during early S phase [81]. This temporal variation is driven by the fact that GSK‐3β is in constant transit between the mitochondria, nucleus, and cytoplasm [94]. Given the significantly enriched expression of both nuclear and cytoplasmic GSK‐3β (predominantly the inactive, phosphorylated form) in morular lesions compared to the Sur Ca, these findings strongly suggest that the accumulation of inactive GSK‐3β acts as a molecular switch that prevents β‐catenin degradation, thereby facilitating robust nuclear β‐catenin accumulation in morules [76, 95] (Figure 3).

Development of Morules: Cellular Transformation

Following the initial suppression of cell proliferation mediated by the β‐catenin/TCF4 signaling pathway, cellular transformation toward a definitive morular phenotype occurs. This morphological transition is driven by the activation of an epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cancer stem cell (CSC) program, which is coordinated via the ezrin‐radixin‐moesin‐binding phosphoprotein 50 (EBP50)/phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome ten (PTEN)/β‐catenin signaling axis.

A Functional Role of EBP50 in Normal and Malignant Endometrium

EBP50, which is also known as Na+/H+ exchanger regulatory factor‐1 (NHERF1), is a multifunctional scaffold protein that is highly expressed at the apical membrane of polarized epithelial cells [96]. The functional activity of EBP50 is intimately linked to its subcellular localization, which is dynamically regulated by the metabolic state of the cell and the constellation of specific EBP50 protein‐protein interactions [97]. In normal endometrial tissues, robust membranous (Me) EBP50 expression is sustained throughout the menstrual cycles. In contrast, a transient upregulation of cytoplasmic (Cyt) EBP50 occurs specifically during the proliferative phase. This cytoplasmic shift is likely driven by estrogenic stimulation, as the EBP50 promoter contains elements directly activated by ESR1 [98]. In Em Ca, distinct alterations in EBP50 localization correlate with tumor progression. Me‐EBP50 expression shows a significant, stepwise reduction from G1 to G3 tumors, coinciding with the loss of glandular architectures. Conversely, Cyt‐EBP50 levels increase progressively with higher tumor grades. In vitro, EBP50 knockout (KO) induces cellular senescence while reducing cell proliferation and motility. Overexpression (OE) of EBP50 yields the opposite phenotype, significantly accelerating proliferation and migration. Furthermore, the number of focal adhesions―which directly dictate cellular migration capability―is significantly enriched in EBP50‐OE cells and depleted in EBP50‐KO cells [99]. Collectively, these data indicate that EBP50 acts to enhance cell proliferation and migration while simultaneously suppressing cellular senescence within the endometrial epithelium.

The Reciprocal Inhibition Between ESR1/EBP50 and PTEN/β‐Catenin Signaling

The PTEN was originally identified as a tumor suppressor gene [100, 101]. It functions as both an inositol phospholipid phosphatase and a dual‐specificity protein phosphatase capable of dephosphorylating phospho‐threonine, phospho‐serine, and phospho‐tyrosine residues, thereby directly antagonizing the oncogenic PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [102, 103]. In vitro, Em Ca cell lines stably overexpressing PTEN (PTEN‐OE) paradoxically exhibit EMT‐like features, likely driven by β‐catenin/Slug‐mediated transcriptional suppression of E‐cadherin [98, 104]. Furthermore, while PTEN overexpression inhibits cell proliferation, accelerates cellular senescence, and increases apoptotic activity, it simultaneously enhances cell migration capability. Notably, PTEN‐OE cells acquire prominent CSC‐like properties. These properties include the elevated expression of aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1) and CD44s, a significantly expanded ALDH 1high subpopulation, enriched spheroid‐forming efficiency, and the β‐catenin/TCF4‐mediated upregulation of cyclin D2, which is essential for sustained CSC self‐renewal [36]. Consistent with these functional findings, the upregulation of PTEN and key CSC‐related surface markers―such as CD44 and CD133―is characteristically observed within human morular lesions. This expression pattern correlates positively with robust nuclear β‐catenin accumulation and negatively with the Ki‐67 proliferative index. Collectively, these data indicate that PTEN overexpression and concurrent nuclear β‐catenin stabilization cooperatively drive the EMT/CSC programming required for morular differentiation in Em Ca cells. Conversely, ESR1‐mediated EBP50 signaling acts as a powerful negative regulator of morular formation by suppressing both PTEN and active β‐catenin status [98] (Figure 4).

Loss of ESR1/EBP50 axis serves as the initial trigger for the disruption of apical‐basal cell polarity, driving the subsequent subcellular redistribution of PTEN and β‐catenin. This altered PTEN/β‐catenin network is directly responsible for the establishment and maintenance of the morular phenotype and its associated with EMT/CSC characteristics in Em Ca.

Recapitulation of Morule‐Like Structures Using Three‐Dimensional Culture Systems

Three‐dimensional (3D) cell‐based assays allow cells to organize into complex architectures that more faithfully recapitulate the structural hierarchy and microenvironmental interactions found in vivo. Consequently, these systems enable the observation of critical cellular behaviors―such as cell‐cell communication, directional migration, and lineage differentiation―in a highly physiologically relevant context [105]. Using a 3D extracellular matrix culture model, the parental Em Ca cell line Hec6 (which lacks endogenous PTEN expression) characteristically generates well‐defined tubular structures featuring localized EBP50 expression at the apical plasma membrane. Conversely, both EBP50‐KO and PTEN‐OE cells form dense, solid spheroid (morule‐like) structures. These spheroids display an immunophenotypic profile characterized by marked nuclear β‐catenin, CD44, and CD133 upregulation, coupled with a dramatic loss of both Me‐EBP50 and ALDH1 expression [36]. Given that human tissue morules similarly manifest elevated expression of CD44 and CD133 alongside EBP50 down‐regulation [98], these data strongly demonstrate that the 3D culture of PTEN‐OE and EBP50‐KO cells serves as a highly robust, biomimetic in vitro model for studying the morular differentiation.

Terminal Differentiation From Morule to SQM: the Morule‐SQM Cascade

Terminal differentiation from morular to a true SqM (morule‐SqM cascade) is executed through a mesenchymal‐to‐epithelial transition (MET) that restores cell polarity and concurrently suppresses the preceding EMT and CSC programs.

Delta‐N‐p63 (p40) is a SqM‐Related CSC Marker

The TP63 gene encodes p63, the phylogenetically oldest conserved homologue within the p53 family of transcription factors [106, 107]. Through the use of alternative promoters, TP63 yields two primary structural isoforms: the TAp63 isoform, which contains the canonical N‐terminal transactivation domain (TAD), and the Delta‐N‐p63 (p40) isoform, which lacks this full‐length TAD [108, 109]. The p40 isoform is a vital orchestrator of epidermal differentiation and CSC dynamics [110, 111].

Histopathologically, p40 expression is localized precisely at the transition zones where morular cells shift into squamous metaplastic cells, as well as within the basal and parabasal layers of cytokeratin (CK)5/6‐positive squamous metaplastic epithelia (Figure 5A). This distinct spatial distribution indicates that p40 activity drives the phenotypic shift from morular to SqM cell lineages. In vitro, p40‐OE cells upregulate the mesenchymal CSC marker CD44 while downregulating the epithelial CSC marker ALDH1 [112, 113], a shift that enhances cellular migration capability [98]. In clinical tissue samples, a stepwise, significant decrease in CD44 and CD133 expression is observed along the continuum from morular lesions, through SqM components, to the Sur Ca (Figure 5A). Taken together, these findings suggest that p40 acts as a transient molecular gatekeeper: it initially induces SqM‐related CSC properties during the early phase of the morule‐SqM cascade, whereas its subsequent downregulation is required to permit terminal differentiation into mature, fully differentiated SqM phenotypes [36] (Figure 5B). This model is strongly supported by independent evidence demonstrating that p40 activity prevents premature entry into terminal differentiation programs [114].

Interactions between EBP50, S100A4, and Myosin Heavy Chain 9 during SqM Differentiation

Myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9) plays a fundamental role in processes requiring cellular reshaping and mechanical movement, including cell adhesion, cell migration, and cell division. Mechanistically, MYH9 regulates the actin cytoskeleton through its inherent actin cross‐linking and contractile functions, which are tightly governed by both its phosphorylation state and its ability to assemble into functional filaments [115, 116].

Apical Me‐EBP50 stabilizes MYH9 post‐translationally, likely through a direct physical interaction mediated by its PSD‐95/Drosophila discs large/ZO‐1 (PDZ1) domain. At the apical membrane, EBP50 exerts a tumor‐suppressive function by establishing and maintaining epithelial cell polarization via modulation of this MYH9‐dependent cytoskeletal network. Conversely, the loss of Me‐EBP50 expression and subsequent MYH9 dysfunction induces an EMT‐like phenotype, resulting in heightened cellular motility and enhanced CSC characteristics [117].

Parallel to this network, the calcium‐binding protein S100A4 also interacts strongly with MYH9 [118]. Specific chemical inhibition of MYH9 using blebbistatin―a synthetic small molecule that reversibly blocks MHY9 ATPase activity [118] ―accurately phenocopies the biological effects observed in Em Ca cells overexpressing S100A4. This highlights a mechanism whereby S100A4 acts as an effective inhibitor of MYH9 polymerization, thereby driving a fibroblast‐like morphology and promoting EMT/CSC properties [119].

Dynamic interactions between EBP50, S100A4, and MYH9 play an instructive role in driving the morule‐SqM cascade. Notably, MYH9 specifically complexes with EBP50 and S100A4 within SqM components, but not within morular lesions. Structurally, re‐expressed EBP50 promotes the polymerization of MYH9 filaments, facilitating the re‐establishment of epithelial polarity through a MET program. In contrast, S100A4 induces MYH9 depolymerization, reinforcing the maintenance of an EMT‐like morphology. Indeed, disruption of the physical interaction between EBP50 and MYH9 is sufficient to induce morule‐like spheroid formation in 3D‐cultured Em Ca cells [36]. Biochemically, MYH9 phosphorylation at Ser1943 increases in EBP50‐OE cells, but remains unchanged in S100A4‐OE cells. This matches established models showing that MYH9 phosphorylation at Ser1943 sterically inhibits S100A4 binding, thereby protecting MYH9 filaments against S100A4‐mediated depolymerization [120, 121]. Intriguingly, EBP50‐MYH9 binding paradoxically increases in S100A4‐OE cells. This is likely a compensatory, reactive mechanism by the cell to counteract the inhibition of MYH9 polymerization imposed by S100A4. Taken together, these data suggest that a finely tuned monomer‐polymer equilibrium of MYH9, managed by the competing associations of EBP50 and S100A4, dictates the progression and execution of terminal squamous differentiation [36] (Figure 6).

Schematic Representation of the Interplays Between EBP50, S100A4, and MYH9 in the Morule‐SqM Cascade. Primary MYH9 dysfunction and loss of cytoskeletal filament integrity prompt a loss of cellular polarity, driving development toward the morular phenotype. Conversely, the coordinated upregulation of EBP50, S100A4, and MYH9 directs the transition toward a definitive SqM phenotype by balancing MYH9 polymerization dynamics, a process accelerated under localized microenvironmental hypoxic conditions.

Enhancement of the Morule‐SqM Cascade under Hypoxic Conditions

Comedo‐type central necrosis―likely arising from several tissue ischemia―frequently occurs within late‐type morules [3], strongly indicating that morular and SqM lesions develop under profoundly hypoxic conditions. Consistent with this histopathological feature, the HIF‐1α immunohistochemical score is significantly higher in SqM components than in either morular or Sur Ca lesions. Furthermore, this elevated HIF‐1α expression correlates positively with both p40 and CK5/6 scores. In vitro, the expression of p40, MYH9, and S100A4 increases in Em Ca cells subjected to CoCl2‐mediated chemical hypoxia [36]. This hypoxic upregulation ultimately enhances the S100A4‐mediated modulation of the EBP50‐MYH9 interaction network. Given that restricted physiological oxygen tension is well‐known to promote lineage differentiation and structural development across numerous tissue types [66], these findings strongly suggest that localized microenvironmental hypoxia serves as a critical extrinsic trigger accelerating the progression of the morule‐SqM cascade [36] (Figure 6).

Clinical Implications of Morules: Their Relation to Progestin Therapy

Histopathological Evaluation of Em Ca Receiving Progestin Therapy

The gold‐standard treatment approach for Em Ca remains a total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy, frequently supplemented with cytotoxic chemotherapy and pelvic radiation therapy for advanced or recurrent diseases. However, conservative hormone therapy―leveraging the profound maturation‐stimulating and anti‐proliferative effects of progesterone―is a widely accepted fertility‐sparing alternative for young patients wishing to avoid a hysterectomy [122].

To objectively evaluate treatment‐induced morphological alterations following progestin therapy, we implemented a therapeutic efficacy (TE) grading system based on four distinct histopathological parameters:

The final TE grade is determined by calculating the sum of these altered parameters. For example, if a biopsy specimen demonstrates reduced overall cellularity, a transition in nuclear arrangement from stratified to a simple monolayer, and a marked increase in cytoplasmic eosinophilia across more than 50% of the tumor area following progestin treatment, the TE value is defined as Grade 3 (1 + 1 + 1) [123].

Within the high‐response cohort (TE ≧ 3), tumor cells undergo a progressive morphological remodeling during the course of progestin therapy. This is characterized by the rearrangement of nuclei from crowded, stratified layers into organized monolayers, accompanied by an expansion of eosinophilic cytoplasm that drives down both the overall cellular density and the N/C ratio. Concurrently, the nuclear contours of most tumor cells transition from elongated, spindle‐ or club‐like shapes into uniform ovals, though minor cytologic atypia and prominent nucleoli may occasionally persist. Conversely, in the low‐response cohort (TE ≦ 2), the morphological alterations are comparatively negligible, despite a slight reduction in tumor cellularity. Mechanistically, progestin administration primarily suppresses Em Ca cell proliferation without significantly altering the baseline apoptotic index, effectively shifting the overarching tissue kinetics toward a relative dominance of cell deletion overactive growth [123].

Notably, morular cells frequently display multiple intranuclear inclusions with a characteristic pale, homogenous appearances [123]. This phenotypic feature mirrors the morular‐like subnuclear vacuoles observed in normal endometrial glandular cells subjected to high endogenous progesterone stimulation during pregnancy [124]. Furthermore, robust baseline PR expression observed in initial pre‐treatment biopsies drops precipitously immediately after the onset of therapy―occurring well before any visible alterations in tumor morphology manifest―in both the high‐ and low‐response cohorts. This is in sharp contrast to ESR1 expression, which exhibits no apparent immediate down‐regulation in response to progestin administration [123].

Alteration in Nuclear β‐Catenin Expression during Progestin Therapy

Nuclear β‐catenin accumulation increases dramatically during the course of progestin therapy―often within the first 10 weeks of treatment―and its upregulation correlates significantly with higher TE grades. Conversely, following the cessation or interruption of progestin therapy, nuclear β‐catenin expression decreases in most cases, coinciding with a structural reversion of the tumor back to its baseline endometrioid morphology [35] (Figure 7A).

Regarding mutational profiles, an identical CTNNB1 status before and after progestin therapy is maintained in 82.6% of informative Em Ca cases, whereas 17.4% of tumors display discrepant mutational results post‐treatment [35]. In vitro, progestin treatment significantly increases cytoplasmic and nuclear β‐catenin accumulation in Em Ca cell lines harboring CTNNB1 mutations, whereas cell lines with wild‐type CTNNB1 show no such induction [35]. Furthermore, overall morular size is characteristically enlarged in the high‐response progestin therapy group. This explanation is likely driven by enhanced nuclear β‐catenin stabilization resulting from the progestin‐mediated suppression of GSK‐3β function [95] (Figure 7B), a finding consistent with evidence identifying GSK‐3β as a primary downstream target of ovarian steroid hormone signaling [125].

Collectively, these findings indicate that the histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations of nuclear β‐catenin and morular status in baseline and follow‐up biopsy specimens serves as a highly reliable predictive biomarker suite for determining therapeutic efficacy in Em Ca patients undergoing conservative progestin therapy [35, 95, 123].

These data also strengthen patient management strategies through combining genetic sequencing and morphologic assessments in routine pathological practice. For example, the recognition that MELF (microcystic, enlarged, and fragmented) with squamous differentiation is aggressive with a high ratio of lymph node metastasis is extremely relevant in the clinical setting [27]. The presence of either morules or nuclear β‐catenin expression is a useful predictor of whether tumors are likely to harbor POLE mutations [126].

Conclusion

Taken together, the molecular and clinical data reviewed here support a comprehensive model detailing the differentiation of Em Ca cells into morular and SqM phenotypes (Figure 8). In Em Ca cells harboring CTNNB1 abnormalities, hyperactivation of the β‐catenin/TCF4 signaling pathway and the cooperative EBP50/PTEN/β‐catenin axis suppresses cell proliferation while concurrently triggering an EMT/mesenchymal CSC program that initiates morular differentiation. Following this initial step, the p40 acts as the indispensable upstream signal promoting progression along the morule‐SqM cascade. This transition ultimately culminates in terminal squamous differentiation, a process governed by the mechanical monomer‐polymer equilibrium of MYH9 via its competing, highly regulated interactions with EBP50 and S100A4 under microenvironmental hypoxic stress. Beyond the classical stepped cascade, the EBP50/S100A4/MYH9 axis also facilitates a direct, β‐catenin‐independent trans‐differentiation process―an irreversible linage switch from one differentiated cell state directly to another [127] ―converting Sur Ca cells straight into SqM cells.

Comprehensive Schematic Models of Morular and SqM Differentiation Cascade in Em Ca. In Em Ca cells with harboring baseline CTNNB1 mutations, morular‐ and SqM‐related CSCs and mature SqM components develop sequentially through tightly orchestrated EMT and MET programs, a cascade favored under microenvironmental hypoxia. Conversely, in Em Ca cells displaying wild‐type CTNNB1, the tumor cells directly trans‐differentiate into a squamous lineage through a direct β‐catenin‐independent switch managed by shifts in the EBP50/S100A4/MYH9 mechanical axis.

Looking forward, leveraging these insights into the morule‐SqM cascade opens promising avenues for the development of novel, targeted therapeutic strategies. Such approaches could be used to optimize and monitor conservative, fertility‐preserving endocrine therapies, offering a safe and more effective clinical management paradigm for young Em Ca patients.
