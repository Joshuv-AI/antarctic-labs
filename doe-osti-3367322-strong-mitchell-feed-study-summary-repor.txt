 1.INTRODUCTION

1.1 PROJECT OVERVIEW

Sargent & Lundy (S&L) was retained by Heidelberg Materials (Heidelberg) to support a Carbon Capture Front
End Engineering Design (FEED) study for the implementation of a Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) amine-
based carbon dioxide (CO2) capture technology at the Mitchell Cement Plant. This was part of the Department
of Energy (DOE) funding opportunity announcement (FOA) DE-FOA-0002515. The MHI CO2 capture system
(CCS) is followed by a compression system to produce a supercritical CO 2 product for geological
sequestration.

S&L was tasked with preliminary engineering and design of all balance of plant (BOP) components and
providing input and oversight on other project deliverables. The S&L BOP design included utility tie-ins,
interconnecting ductwork/piping, auxiliary steam generation, cooling water system, reagent/chemical supply
and storage, wastewater collection and treatment, structural, civil, substation, electrical and instrumentation
and controls. S&L completed several investigations, summarized in study reports, regarding water quality,
water availability, wastewater treatment, steam and auxiliary power supply, and process cooling options for
the CO2 capture plant. The conclusions of these studies were utilized in the BOP design development.

S&L prepared the overall project design criteria, plot plan and general arrangement drawings, overall process
flow diagram (PFD), water balance, heat & material balance, BOP process and instrumentation diagrams
(P&IDs), single line diagrams, network architecture diagrams, a BOP instrument list, and completed other
critical engineering deliverables or tasks to support the design effort. S&L developed and maintained the FEED
Study project schedule; and prepared the Level 1 project execution which includes the project development
from the next phase FEED study to detailed engineering and design through construction and commissioning.
In addition, S&L supported the development of major equipment datasheets and prepared and solicited
budgetary quotes for select BOP equipment. S&L also participated in a preliminary HAZOP review of the CO2
capture process that was facilitated by MHI.

The overall goal of the project was to develop an Association for the Advancement of Cost Engineering (AACE)
Level 3 Capital Cost Estimate with an accuracy of +30/-20%, with a project definition of 30%, to demonstrate
the economic feasibility of constructing and operating the amine CO2 capture plant at the Heidelberg Mitchell
cement plant. This summary report outlines the scope, engineering approach, and results of the FEED Study.

 2.PROCESS & BALANCE OF PLANT (BOP) DESIGN

S&L was tasked with performing preliminary engineering and design for the BOP scope to support
implementation of an MHI amine-based CCS at the Mitchell Cement Plant. BOP design and engineering was
supplemented by information provided by Heidelberg regarding the operating conditions of Mitchell, existing
electrical layouts, surrounding landscape and facilities, available utilities, and Heidelberg standard practices.

The approach for the preliminary design and engineering for the CO2 capture plant is summarized in the
following sections.

2.1 DESIGN INPUTS

The Mitchell Cement Plant (Mitchell) is a modernized cement production facility rated for a nominal clinker
production rate of 7,000 metric tons per day and incorporates features and technologies to minimize energy
consumption, enable the utilization of alternative fuels and raw materials, and reduces the plant’s greenhouse
gas (GHG) footprint. Mitchell's Kiln 4 includes a preheater/precalciner with an inline vertical roller raw mill. Kiln
4 includes a fabric filter baghouse for particulate control, Selective Non-Catalytic Reduction (SNCR) for NOx
control, Activated Carbon Injection (ACI) for mercury control, and dry sorbent injection (DSI) for SO 2 control.
Flue gas from the preheater has the ability to flow to the raw mill and/or the main baghouse, depending on the
raw mill flow requirements when it’s operating. As such, different operating conditions at the Kiln 4 stack occur
based on the raw mill operating modes (on/off), resulting in two different kiln flue gas compositions. Since the
raw mill off mode is planned at 12 hours per week, and un-planned at 10 hours per week, the design basis is
the raw mill on mode.

Natural gas-fired auxiliary (aux) boilers will be included as part of the project scope to supply steam to the
CCS. The flue gas generated from the aux boilers will be combined with flue gas from the Kiln 4 stack, and
the combined stream will be directed to the new CCS. The new CCS will pre-treat the flue gas, remove CO2
from the flue gas and generate a high purity CO 2 stream that is conditioned and compressed into a pipeline,
which will route the CO2 to a storage field development to permanently sequester the captured CO2 onsite.

2.2.2 Flue Gas Ductwork Tie-in

The CO2 capture plant flue gas ductwork will tie-in to the existing 5600 mm (18.4 ft) diameter ductwork and
transition to 14.5 ft x 14.5 ft rectangular ductwork. The flue gas tie-in is located downstream of the existing kiln
main baghouse ID fans in the combined ductwork header between the stacks. The (existing) round ducting
leading to the existing Kiln 4 stack will be demolished, highlighted in purple below in Figure 2-2, and replaced
with rectangular ducting. The associated round ductwork supports and foundations will also be demolished
and replaced with new supports and new foundations.

Two new single louver isolation dampers will be provided to direct flow to the CO2 capture plant or to bypass
to the existing Kiln 4 stack. This new ductwork arrangement maintains the capability of venting flue gas directly
to the existing stack and allows Kiln 4 to continue to operate in the event the CO2 capture plant is offline.

2.2.3 Flue Gas Ductwork

From the tie-in location, new flue gas ductwork will transfer flue gas from the existing kiln main baghouse
induced draft (ID) fans to the CCS quencher vessel. The ductwork will be sized to maintain adequate flue gas
velocity to avoid drop out, prevent erosion, and minimize overall ductwork pressure drop. See Appendix A for
the ductwork design sketches.

The new carbon steel ductwork will be bottom supported by new structural steel framing. Ductwork supports
will be furnished with flat or spherical slide bearing plates to reduce friction forces during thermal expansion
and contraction cycles. The new flue gas ductwork will be routed across existing plant roads, and 24 m (78.7
ft) above/across the existing limestone/gypsum storage and transport building to the south of Kiln 4. Once the
duct enters the CCS process area, it will combine with the flue gas duct from the aux boilers forming a common
header entering the quencher vessel. Wide flange and channel stiffeners will be designed using ASTM A992
and ASTM A36 materials respectively. Duct plate, base plates, sole plates, turning vanes and miscellaneous
plates will be designed using ASTM A572 Grade 50 materials. Pipes and test ports will be designed using
ASTM A500 Grade C materials.

The new flue gas ductwork will be carbon steel lined with a flake filled novolac epoxy for corrosion resistance.
To prevent condensate collection, the ductwork will be sloped to drains. Sloping configuration, number and
location of drains will be determined during detailed design.

Refer to Appendix A for flue gas ductwork sketches.

2.2.4 Cooling System

Due to the limited raw water resources at the site (discussed further in Section 2.4.1), the CO2 capture plant
cooling will be provided by an integrated hybrid cooling system that consists of a mechanical draft cooling
tower (MDCT) and direct air fin cooler (AFC) implemented upstream of the MHI plate heat exchangers (PHE)
in order to achieve the required cooling temperatures to allow the CCS to operate at a 95% capture rate
throughout the year. The CCS process fluid for these process heat exchangers will first pass through the dry
cooling in the AFCs before passing through the PHE. In this configuration, integrating a direct air (dry) cooling
system in series with an evaporative cooling system would reduce the demand for makeup water. This allows
the MDCT to be sized to provide the balance of heat rejection for these heat exchangers and all other MHI
process heat exchangers not listed.

 8

 Summary Report – Unlimited Rights Version

The closed cooling water system for the process equipment will use an evaporative, four-cell, counterflow
MDCT. For the cooling tower sizing, S&L used the cooling water flow and temperature requested by MHI with
the site weather information to solicit a vendor preliminary budgetary quote. This quote was used in the overall
water balance, wastewater treatment design solution, plant layout, and cooling water system design.

The cooling water system uses three (3) x 50% vertical pumps located in the cooling tower basin. Cooling
water is sent to the main underground supply header leading to the process heat exchangers. Each of the
process heat exchangers connect to this header through a series of stub-ups. Blowdown is sent to the
equalization tank from this supply header as well to control water quality of the cooling water system. After
passing through the process heat exchangers, the cooling water connects to the underground return header
leading back to the cooling tower. Risers connect each cooling tower cell to this underground cooling water
return header. During winter months, the return water can bypass the tower directly into the basin. Makeup
water to the tank will be provided by the water treatment system, treated quencher blowdown. During the
summer months this makeup will be supplemented by the makeup water tank.

2.2.5 Water & Wastewater Treatment System

The water and wastewater treatment system treats various streams recovered from the CO2 capture plant
process to produce treated water, the majority of the produced treated water is recycled back for use as MDCT
makeup and a portion is processed further to produce demineralized water required for the aux boiler steam
cycle makeup and other demineralized water users within the CCS. Reject from the wastewater treatment
system is sent to the Kiln 4 VRM finish mill such that the system operates as a zero liquid discharge (ZLD)
system to prevent further environmental contamination. The water and wastewater treatment system includes
a clarifier, an ammonia scrubber, filtration and softening system, a high efficiency wastewater reverse osmosis
(RO) system, a wastewater permeate RO system and rental mixed beds.

Blowdown from the quencher is first treated by a clarifier and ammonia stripper before being sent for use as
MDCT makeup. Waste solids from the clarifier will be sent to the Kiln 4 calciner for disposal. MDCT blowdown
is sent to the equalization tank along with condensate from the compression dehydration system, aux boiler
blowdown and reject from the wastewater permeate RO system. From the equalization tank water is sent to
both the wastewater recovery system and to the RO reject forwarding tank. Water sent to the wastewater
recovery system first passes through a filtration and softening system before being sent to the high efficiency
wastewater RO system. Solid waste produced from the filtration and softening system will be sent to the Kiln
4 calciner for disposal. The majority of the permeate from the high efficiency wastewater RO system is sent to
the MDCT for use as makeup and the remaining portion is sent to the wastewater RO permeate tank prior to

tank and pumped to the Kiln 4 VRM finish mill. Water from the wastewater RO permeate tank passes through

storage tank to be used as makeup for the aux boiler and supply various demineralized water users within the

2.2.6 Natural Gas Auxiliary Boiler System

Steam supplied to the MHI process will be created by three (3) x 33% natural gas fired boilers. Condensate
from the MHI process will be returned to a deaerator and combined with makeup water. Pegging steam from
the steam header conditions the water before feed pumps send it to the boiler steam drums. Natural gas
feeding the boilers will be regulated from the natural gas conditioning yard. Natural gas conditioning equipment
and tie-in to the natural gas pipeline is not included in this study and will need to be reviewed separately. The
natural gas system supplying the cement plant does not have the additional capacity required to support the
aux boilers. The auxiliary boilers will be provided with a startup stack and interconnecting ductwork between
the boilers and said stack. S&L will provide ductwork between the boiler startup stack and the duct running
between the cement plant and carbon capture quencher.

2.2.7 Quarry/Raw Water System

Raw water will be supplied from Quarry Blue Lake located north of the cement plant using two (2) new
submersible pumps located near the existing lake pumps. A support structure will be erected for these new
pumps and the above ground raw water system lines. Between the pump structure and carbon capture plant,
a 2.2 mile underground line will be installed connecting the plant to the lake. Quarry water will be fed to the
service/fire water storage tank and the makeup water storage tank at east of the main CO2 capture plant site.
Raw water consumption from the lake has been limited by Heidelberg.

2.2.8 Service Water System

The CO2 capture plant service water supply will be provided by the raw water system from the lake. Raw water
fills the shared service/fire water storage tank located next to the administrative building. The service water
take off will be high enough above the fire water take off to provide adequate reserves for the fire water system.
Service water is supplied to the CO2 capture plant tie point and to three BOP buildings using three (3) x 50%

2.2.9 Utility Air System

The CO2 capture plant will be provided with an independent compressed air system consisting of air
compressors, air dryers, and service and instrument air receivers. Instrumentation and valving requiring
compressed air will be supplied off the instrument air receiver, while a second receiver is provided for service
air. Service air supplies air to the water treatment and aux boiler building ring headers. The ring headers allow
easy access to service air along the building walls. Instrument air will be provided to the MHI system, the BOP
air operated valves, and to a nitrogen generator that produces nitrogen for the carbon capture plant.

2.2.10 Drain System

Condensate, blowdown, and washdown is collected throughout the plant in the drain system. The drain system
gravity feeds into the closest of the five building sumps (aux boiler, water treatment, cooling tower aux, admin,
and CO2 compression) located around the site. Each sump uses two (2) x 100% pumps to send drain system
water to the oily water separator located near the wastewater treatment building. Drain system water
processed by the oily water separator is then pumped up to the equalization tank.

2.2.11 Fire Protection System

A fire pump skid comprised of an electric pump and back up diesel powered pump system supplies pressurized
water to the plant fire loop to meet the requirements of NFPA. The fire loop surrounds the perimeter of the
plant underground. Fire hydrants have been located per NFPA 1 to provide coverage to the buildings and plant
equipment where required. The four buildings (admin, aux boiler, water treatment, and CO2 compression)
using a fire water suppression system connect off this fire loop as well. Fire suppression and equipment in the
buildings will be supplied by a fire protection contractor.

2.2.12 Potable Water System

Potable water is provided through a tie-in to the existing plant potable water system. Water is directed to the
admin building for general plumbing and to the potable water tempering skid. The tempering skid provides
heated pressurized water to a loop through the plant. Users off this loop feed eyewash/shower stations. These
users include the water treatment building, aux boiler building, and the MHI system.

2.2.13 Chemical Supply & Storage

Various chemicals will be required for various BOP equipment for maintaining equipment operation.
Anticipated chemicals expected to be included at this time include bleach (sodium hypochlorite), sulfuric acid,
corrosion inhibitor, anti-foam, and non-oxidizing biocide. A chemical treatment program will be finalized during
detailed design with input from chemical suppliers. Bleach required for the quarry lake water storage tank,
service/fire water storage tank, and MDCT to prevent organic growth; will be supplied from a common 4,000-
gallon storage tank and dosing system. Diluted sulfuric acid required for the quencher blowdown ammonia
scrubber and the MDCT to maintain pH will be supplied by a common 4,000-gallon tank and dosing system.
Sulfuric acid will be dosed directly into the MDCT basin through a dedicated mixing trough where it is mixed
with circulating water. The MDCT will also include chemical feed systems for corrosion inhibitor, anti-foam,
and non-oxidizing biocide. These chemicals will be injected directly into the MDCT basin. The corrosion
inhibitor will be supplied from a 1,500-gallon tank and the anti-foam and non-oxidizing biocide will be supplied
from totes. Totes and metering pumps will be located in the in the Water Treatment Building. All totes and
pumps will be placed inside curbed areas sized to contain the entire contents of a spill.

2.2.14 CO2 Capture Plant Buildings

The CO2 compressor, aux boilers, water treatment equipment and electrical equipment will be enclosed in
buildings. The use of these buildings will be special purpose industrial plant equipment enclosures which are
not regularly occupied. The height and area of the buildings are characterized by open space that is dictated
by the equipment, interrelated processes and accommodation for equipment removal and maintenance. All
buildings will be engineered and stick-built to allow for adequate first floor height. Equipment and electrical
rooms on the second floor are supported by elevated concrete slabs.

Interior fire rated partitions will be provided for egress components and area separation as required by
International Building Code (IBC) and as recommended by National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 850.
Personnel doors and stairways will be provided for personnel access and safety. Overhead doors, elevators,
and the use of removable wall panels, where required, will be provided for equipment access and maintenance.
Wall penetrations will be located, as necessary, for all process piping, electrical bus and ducts, and building
louvers. Roof and wall penetrations will be provided and located, as necessary, for HVAC equipment.

2.4.2 Water & Wastewater Treatment Evaluation

S&L completed a water and wastewater treatment study to optimize the amount of recycled process water that
could be reused within the CO2 capture plant. Wastewater in the form of blowdown is generated by the
quencher, the MDCT, and the aux boiler steam cycle. The condensate from the quencher is treated by a
clarifier and ammonia stripper before being sent to the MDCT for use as makeup in order to maintain the
MDCT circulating water quality requirements. The CO2 dehydration system also removes water from captured
CO2. The MDCT and aux boiler blowdown streams and water from the CO2 dehydration system undergo
filtration and softening before being treated in a high efficiency RO process; in which some mineral constituents
are first chemically precipitated and removed by microfiltration (MF), allowing the RO to operate at a high
recovery factor. The majority of the RO permeate is supplied to the MDCT for use as makeup and a smaller
portion is treated further to produce demineralized water required for the steam cycle makeup and various
CCS users. The high efficiency RO reject is sent to the Kiln 4 VRM Finish Mill, allowing the CO2 capture plant
to operate as a zero liquid discharge (ZLD) facility. To produce demineralized water with the required
specification, RO permeate must undergo a second pass RO system and a mixed bed ion exchange. Reject
from this second RO system is recycled back to the filtration and softening system for additional recovery.

The water and wastewater treatment evaluation is discussed in more detail in Section 3.2. Refer to Appendix

2.4.8 WESP Consideration

A Wet ESP (WESP) is considered when there is high concentration of filterable and condensable particulate
matter (FPM/CPM) such as aerosols in the flue gas. Aersols (e.g., SO 3 mist) can act as amine carriers, that
are able to pass through demisters and lead to increased solvent loss and emissions. Based on previous
experience with cement kiln flue gas, higher concentrations of these aerosol forming compounds were
expected. However, initial gas test results did not indicate a need to include a WESP in the initial design for
this phase. Space was reserved in the plot plan to accommodate a WESP in future phases should it become
necessary to include it due to additional flue gas tests or planned future alternative fuels use. A sensitivity
analysis will re-evaluate the need for a WESP during the next phase of the project with updated flue gas
conditions

3.1 STEAM AND ELECTRIC SOURCING STUDY

S&L completed a steam and electric sourcing study, to determine the source of the low-pressure steam
required to heat the MHI solvent in the regenerators to strip the CO2-rich solvent of absorbed CO2 and power
required to operate the MHI process and BOP systems. Because the main CO2 compressor may be coupled
with an electric motor or a backpressure steam turbine drive that would require medium pressure steam and
discharge as low-pressure steam to the CCS reboilers, S&L’s evaluation considered this configuration. This
study summarizes the options for supplying the low or medium pressure steam, pending the CO2 compressor
motor type, and the electricity to the overall CCS facility and establishes the basis of design for sourcing steam
and electricity as part of the CCS FEED study.

3.1.1 Steam

As the Mitchell facility does not have an existing steam supply source, the main options considered were a
natural gas fired auxiliary boiler and a combined heat and power (CHP) system. Several combinations/
configurations of these options were considered, and ultimately the following three options were considered
for the FEED study:

 1. An auxiliary boiler using an electric motor driven compressor

 2. An auxiliary boiler using steam driven compressor

 3. A new CHP using a 7E.03 gas turbine with reduced duct firing and electric driven compressor

While all options provide steam, the CHP has the added benefit of power production to supply the auxiliary
power demands required by the CCS and BOP equipment, offering lower operating costs. However, the CHP
option has a considerably higher capital cost compared to the aux boiler options, and would have other
potential impacts to the CCS when compared to the use of an auxiliary boiler that were not evaluated in detail

3.2 WATER & WASTEWATER TREATMENT STUDY

The water and wastewater treatment study, summarizes the water consumption needs of the MHI CO2 capture
system and BOP systems; it also documents the management and treatment of generated wastewater in order
to recycle and reuse it within the various processes.

MHI provided quality requirements for the circulating water. S&L’s evaluation determined the quencher
blowdown would need to first be treated using a lamella clarifier to remove the total suspended solids (TSS),
then a stripping column to neutralize ammonia, prior to its use as makeup to the MDCT. This treatment would
avoid exceeding the tolerance level provided by the MHI for the circulating water.

As the MDCT requires a significant makeup water flow, the wastewater recovered from the various processes
will be treated and recycled back to offset the amount of lake water required for MDCT makeup. Other
wastewater streams generated including the MDCT blowdown, steam generator blowdown, dehydration
waste, and other miscellaneous wastewater sources will combine in an equalization tank. A portion of the
MDCT blowdown will be sent to the kiln for use in the VRM Finish Mill and Downcomer to minimize the size of
the wastewater treatment system. The rest of the combined wastewater stream from the equalization tank will
first pass through filtration and softening equipment, that will remove solids, as well as hardness, alkalinity,
and silica before treatment by a high-efficiency wastewater RO system to remove total dissolved solids (TDS).
The majority of this RO permeate will be used as MDCT makeup water; the remaining permeate will proceed
to a demineralizer system including an RO followed by rental mixed beds. This will result in the production of
demineralized water for use as steam generator makeup and supply other CCS demineralized water users.
The reject from the demineralizer RO system will be recycled into the equalization tank with other waste
streams for treatment by the high-efficiency wastewater RO system.

The high-efficiency wastewater RO system will generate a highly concentrated liquid waste stream that cannot
be recycled in the CCS. In order to avoid the need to permit a new liquid wastewater discharge stream from
the facility or a secondary wastewater treatment system, the high-efficiency RO reject stream will be sent for
use in the VRM Finish Mill, allowing the CCS to operate as a ZLD facility. This water will be supplemented with
MDCT blowdown, as required.

3.3 COOLING WATER STUDY

S&L completed an extensive evaluation of several cooling water types and combinations due to the limited
raw water available for the CCS use from the Quarry Big Blue Lake and other recovered process water streams
available. Four (4) cooling system configurations were evaluated:

 • 100% dry cooling using an air-cooled heat exchanger

 • 100% fully evaporative cooling system using a mechanical draft cooling tower (MDCT)

 • A hybrid cooling system consisting of a dry cooling system sized for 80% of the total heat rejection
 required dry and a wet cooling system (MDCT) sized for 40% of the total heat rejection required
 (referred to as an 80% dry / 40% wet cooling hybrid system)

 • An integrated hybrid cooling system, using a reduced size MDCT and supplemental direct (amine) air
 coolers on select CCS process streams

A 100% dry cooling system will result in a cooling water supply to the CCS that exceeds an MHI designated
cooling water supply temperature for a significant portion of the year leading to the reduction in CO2 capture
efficiencies. During the hottest times of the year, the cooling water temperature may exceed the maximum
designated temperature, which would not be feasible for operation of the MHI CCS (i.e., at this point the system
must be turned off to avoid complications). As Heidelberg would not be able to achieve close to the 95%
capture rate for operation throughout the year, it was determined than a 100% dry cooling system is not a
feasible cooling option.

A 100% fully evaporative cooling system using a MDCT would be able to reach the MHI designated supply
temperature for the entirety of year thus achieving a 95% capture rate. An MDCT would have the lowest cost,
footprint, and power requirement of the options considered, however, there is not a sufficient amount of make-
up water available to support this option due to the limited site water resources, making it an unfeasible option
at this time.

As neither a 100% dry nor 100% wet cooling system would feasible, S&L evaluated hybrid cooling system
configurations based on optimizing the number of hours that achieve the MHI design cooling water
temperature without exceeding the amount of available makeup water. A water storage tank was introduced
such that during the colder times of the year when the dry cooling system achieves a higher cooling capacity,
excess water can be stored for future use during the warmer months. This resulted in a dry cooling system
80% the size of the hypothetical 100% dry cooling application and a wet cooling system 40% the size of the

 30

 Summary Report – Unlimited Rights Version

hypothetical 100% wet cooling application. A 20-million-gallon closed water storage tank would be needed to
support the hybrid system operation. The 80% dry / 40% wet cooling hybrid system would require a glycol
water mixture to be circulated in the dry cooling system to prevent freezing in the smaller piping. As such, this
would result in two configurations that could be implemented: (1) use of a separate glycol loop for the dry
cooling system that would require an additional heat exchanger on the circulating water loop, further
decreasing heat transfer efficiency, but would send pure water to the CCS and (2) use of a glycol-water mix
for cooling water sent to the CCS, eliminating additional heat exchanger, but will cause an upsize in the CCS
process heat exchangers to achieve the same heat transfer. Because the separate glycol loop will have a
higher approach temperature that risks achieving the desired cooling water temperature, the glycol-water mix
configuration was chosen as the basis for this hybrid cooling system. The 80% dry / 40% wet cooling hybrid
system will achieve a higher capture efficiency compared to the 100% dry cooling system and would avoid
shutdowns due to temperature exceedances. However, there are still times during the year that the required
cooling water temperature for achieving 95% CO2 capture efficiency is not met. The inability to achieve the
maximum annual CO2 capture rate and required CCS process heat exchanger upsizing to accommodate the
glycol-water solution led to the development of the final option considered for the integrated hybrid cooling
system.

The integrated hybrid cooling system will consist of a direct air-cooling system in series with an evaporative
cooling system to reduce the makeup water demand. In this configuration, MHI proposed implementing direct
air fin coolers upstream of cooling water plate heat exchangers on their process steams. The MDCT is sized
to provide the balance of heat rejection for these heat exchangers and all other MHI process heat exchangers.
This integrated hybrid cooling system achieves the required cooling temperatures to allow the CCS to operate
at a 95% capture rate throughout the year. A closed water storage tank will be required to store excess water
available during colder months for use in hotter months for the integrated hybrid option as well, but a
significantly smaller size is needed.

Since the integrated hybrid cooling system can accommodate the current water source limitations, requires a
smaller storage tank, achieves the maximum annual CO2 capture rate, and is the more cost-effective option
compared to an 80% dry / 40% wet hybrid cooling system, the integrated hybrid cooling water system was
selected for the project.

 the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR
 1910.1200). No air or water emissions would be expected for normal storage, handling, and use of

A. General Design Criteria
 1.00 General Description:

 The objective of this project is to lay the conceptual foundation to develop a full-scale carbon capture system at the Heidelberg Materials Mitchell
 cement production site for Kiln #4. The project includes process design of the carbon dioxide (CO 2) capture system by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries
 (MHI), with support from Sargent & Lundy (S&L), and balance of plant (BOP) integration by S&L. The Carbon Capture System (CCS) will consist of a
 1.01 direct contact cooler (DCC) vessel or quencher, booster fan, CO2 absorber vessel, regenerator vessel or stripper, compression technology, and associated
 systems. The train configuration of the CCS is still being investigated, and this document will be revised to reflect the corresponding design approach.
 This document defines the overall design criteria for the project, which will serve as the basis of equipment design and installation of the new CCS. This
 document also summarizes the project design considerations for each major system as well as providing system descriptions for the BOP systems.

 Heidelberg Materials Mitchell facility is a modernized cement production plant rated for a nominal clinker production rate of 7,000 metric tons per day
 and that incorporates features and technologies to minimize energy consumption, enable the utilization of alternative fuels and raw materials, and reduce
 the plant’s greenhouse gas (GHG) footprint. Mitchell's Kiln #4 is a preheater/precalciner with an inline vertical roller raw mill. The preheater gases have
 the ability to flow to the raw mill and the Kiln #4 main baghouse depending on flow requirements in the raw mill while it is running. Kiln #4 includes a
 1.02
 fabric filter baghouse for particulate control, Selective Non-Catalytic Reduction (SNCR) for NOx control, Activated Carbon Injection (ACI) for mercury
 control, and dry sorbent injection (DSI) for SO2 control. A natural gas-fired auxiliary boiler will be included as part of the project scope to supply
 steam to the CCS. The flue gas generated from the aux boiler will be combined with flue gas from the Kiln #4 stack and the combined stream will be
 directed to the new CCS.
 The new CO2 capture system will pre-treat the flue gas, remove CO2 from the flue gas and generate a high purity CO2 stream that is conditioned and
 compressed into a pipeline; which will route the CO2 to a storage field development to permanently sequester the captured CO 2 onsite. The pipeline and
 1.03
 storage are not included in the scope of the first phase of the study (DE-FE0032222), but will be developed during the next phase of the project (DE-
