Abstract
We address the problem of inferring a directed network from nodal measurements generated by linear diffusion dynamics on the sought graph. Observations are modeled as the outputs of a graph convolutional filter, i.e., a polynomial (with unknown coefficients) of a local diffusion graph-shift operator encoding the latent graph topology, excited with an ensemble of independent graph signals with arbitrarily-correlated nodal components. Unlike prior efforts that considered undirected graphs and white signal excitations, here the graph-shift operator and the observations’ covariance matrix are not simultaneously diagonalizable. In this challenging context, we first rely on measurements of the output signals along with prior statistical information on the inputs to identify the diffusion filter. Such system identification problem involves solving a system of quadratic matrix equations, which we show is identifiable under spectral-diversity assumptions on the input covariances. For algorithmic purposes we recast it as a smooth quadratic minimization subject to Stiefel manifold constraints. Subsequent identification of the network topology given the graph filter estimate boils down to finding a sparse and structurally admissible shift that commutes with the given filter, thus, forcing the latter to be a polynomial in the sought graph-shift operator. A joint graph filter and topology identification algorithm is also proposed, which alternates between the aforementioned steps in a mutually reinforcing fashion to offer improved sample complexity. Numerical tests corroborate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms in recovering synthetic digraphs and real-data case studies, and illustrate their potential utility on urban mobility analyses as well as portfolio optimization.



I
Introduction

Machine learning and signal processing over graphs offer a rich toolbox for extracting actionable information from relational data. Upon modeling the data domain as a graph and the nodal observations at hand as graph signals, the graph signal processing (GSP) body of work has contributed innovative models that relate the properties of the signals with those of the graph, along with algorithms that fruitfully leverage graph-induced priors and inductive biases. Arguably, most early GSP efforts dealt with
undirected
networks. Such graphs are equivalently represented by symmetric matrices whose (well-behaved) spectral properties can be used to define transforms, convolutional operators, and even non-linear parametric architectures to learn from graph signals
[
24
,
13
]
. Their scarcer adoption notwithstanding,
directed graph
(digraph) models are more adequate (and, in fact, more accurate) for a number of applications. Information networks such as scientific citations or the Web itself are typically directed, and flows in technological (e.g., transportation, power, communication) networks are oftentimes one-directional. The presence of directionality plays a critical role when the measurements taken in those networks need to be processed to remove noise, outliers and artifacts, and this requires new tools and algorithms that do not rely on symmetric graph operators
[
32
,
20
]
. More abstractly, when the graph encodes (often unknown) relations between observed variables, directionality is vital to identify the nodes representing causes and their effects; see e.g.,
[
27
]
. Beyond causal discovery, there is a need for methodological advances to tackle challenging digraph topology identification problems.

I-A

Recovering the Fundamental Structure of Signals

Consider a network represented as a weighted digraph
{\mathcal{G}}
, with a node set
{\mathcal{N}}
of known cardinality
N
, and an edge set
{\mathcal{E}}
of ordered pairs of elements in
{\mathcal{N}}
. The edge weights
A_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
A_{ij}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
are collected in the (generally non-symmetric) adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
. As a more general algebraic descriptor of network structure, one can define a
graph-shift operator
(GSO)
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
as any matrix having the same sparsity pattern as that of
{\mathcal{G}}

[
32
]
. Accordingly,
{\mathbf{S}}
can be viewed as a local diffusion operator.

Our focus in this paper is on identifying digraphs that explain the structure of a random signal.
Formally, let
{\mathbf{y}}=[y_{1},...,y_{N}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
be a graph signal in which the
i
th element
y_{i}
denotes the signal value at node
i
of an
unknown digraph

{\mathcal{G}}
with shift operator
{\mathbf{S}}
. Further suppose that we are given a zero-mean signal
{\mathbf{x}}
with covariance matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right]
. We say that the sparse digraph
{\mathbf{S}}
represents the structure of the signal
{\mathbf{y}}
if there exists a diffusion process in the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
– modeled as a polynomial graph filter – that generates the observation
{\mathbf{y}}
from the input signal
{\mathbf{x}}
via linear filtering, i.e.,

\textstyle{\mathbf{y}}\ =\ \alpha_{0}\prod_{l=1}^{\infty}({\mathbf{I}}-\alpha_{l}{\mathbf{S}}){\mathbf{x}}\ =\ \big(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}\big)\,{\mathbf{x}},

(1)

for some set of parameters
\{\alpha_{l}\}
, or equivalently
\{\beta_{l}\}
. While
{\mathbf{S}}
encodes only one-hop interactions, each successive application of the GSO in (
1
) percolates
{\mathbf{x}}
over
{\mathcal{G}}
. The justification to say that
{\mathbf{S}}
represents the structure of
{\mathbf{y}}
is that we can think of the edges of
{\mathbf{S}}
as direct (one-hop), directional relations between the elements of the signal. The diffusion described by (
1
) generates indirect relations, for instance, longer-range statistical dependencies in the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\right]
. Our goal is to exploit this model to recover the fundamental relations described by
{\mathbf{S}}
from a set
{\mathcal{Y}}
of independent realizations of a random signal
{\mathbf{y}}
along with prior knowledge of
{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}}
. The required statistical information about
{\mathbf{x}}
is the price paid to accommodate non-stationary processes
{\mathbf{y}}
with respect to (w.r.t.) (possibly) asymmetric GSOs
[
19
,
36
]
.

I-B

Paper Outline and Contributions

In Section
II
, we formulate the problem of identifying a GSO that explains the fundamental structure of a random signal diffused on a digraph. We advocate an innovative two-step approach whereby: i) given independent output observations and prior information on the input statistics, we identify the graph convolutional filter; and ii) given the filter estimate along with structural constraints on the GSO, we recover the topology of the digraph via convex optimization.
In Section
III
, we address the problem of identifying the diffusion filter [cf. i)], which entails solving a system of
quadratic
matrix equations formed using the available information on the input along with output signal measurements. We show that the set of feasible filters is related to the square roots of the observations’ covariance matrix, and that such a set is markedly larger than its counterpart for symmetric filters
[
36
]
. Conditions under which the system identification task admits a unique solution are derived as well (Section
III-A
). Building on these insights, in Section
III-B
we recast the filter-inference problem as a smooth quadratic minimization subject to Stiefel manifold constraints. Such nonconvex problem can be tackled leveraging recent advances for orthogonality-constrained optimization; see e.g.,
[
6
]
. Subsequent identification of a directed GSO given the diffusion filter estimate [cf. ii)] is addressed in Section
IV
. The focus is on finding a
sparse
and structurally admissible shift that commutes with the given filter, thus forcing the latter to be a polynomial in the GSO as in (
1
). In Section
V
we also propose a
joint
graph filter and topology identification formulation and associated provably convergent algorithm, closing the loop between steps i) and ii) to offer improved sample complexity.
A comprehensive experimental evaluation in Section
VI
corroborates the effectiveness of the proposed approach in recovering synthetic graphs in a variety of controlled settings designed to probe different key aspects of the problem. Real data tests hint at the algorithm’s potential impact on urban mobility analyses and portfolio optimization. Concluding remarks with a discussion of limitations along with an outlook towards future work are given in Section
VII
.

]

]
– equivalently implying

; or, colored as in the present paper

. Here instead, we distinctly address the general case of digraphs. Relative to

,

]
that rely on a specific single-pole graph filter stemming from the SEM model, the filter structure underlying (

Relative to the conference precursor
[

, here we consider digraph topology inference via graph filter identification through a unified presentation along with full technical details (including extended discussions, algorithms, and unpublished theoretical results with their proofs), supported by comprehensive numerical experiments. Noteworthy novel pieces in this full-blown journal paper include: (i) the joint digraph and filter identification formulation; (ii) custom-made algorithms for the open- and closed-loop approaches; (iii) theory supporting the feasibility of the asymmetric diffusion filter identification task; and (iv) a comprehensive performance evaluation protocol. II
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

Suppose we observe realizations of a random signal
{\mathbf{y}}
generated through diffusion on
{\mathcal{G}}
of an input
{\mathbf{x}}
, namely via successive applications of a GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
as in (
1
). Since the (statistical) properties of the signal
{\mathbf{y}}
depend on
{\mathbf{S}}
, our goal is to use a set of observations together with available information on the excitation input to infer the digraph topology. In other words, we aim to recover the GSO which encodes pairwise influence between graph nodes, given observable indirect relationships generated by a diffusion process.
To formally state the problem, we elaborate on the diffusion model in (
1
) as well as on the available information from the graph signals.

II-A

Graph Filtering as a Model of Network Diffusion

While the diffusion expressions in (
1
) entail (possibly) infinite-degree polynomials in
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem asserts that they are equivalent to polynomials of degree smaller than
N
. Upon defining the vector of coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}:=[h_{0},\ldots,h_{L-1}]^{\top}
and the asymmetric graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}:=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}

[
32
]
, the model in (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}=\textstyle\big(\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}\big)\,{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}

(2)

for some
{\mathbf{h}}
and
L\leq N
. Most germane to the present paper is that since
{\mathbf{H}}
is a polynomial in
{\mathbf{S}}
,
then
{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}
commute, i.e.,
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{H}}
. We will exploit this identity in Sections
IV
and
V
, which can be interpreted to imply that graph filters are shift invariant. Another upshot of the polynomial relationship is that the eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}
coincide, provided
{\mathbf{S}}
is diagonalizable which we henceforth assume.
Hence, while the diffusion implicit in
{\mathbf{H}}
obscures part of the structure of
{\mathbf{S}}
(its eigenvalues), its eigenvectors remain as templates of the underlying network topology
[
25
,
33
,
36
]
.

II-B

Observations and Prior Information on the Input Signals

We observe
M
network processes
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
on
{\mathcal{G}}
, each one corresponding to a different input zero-mean random signal
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
that is diffused via a common filter
{\mathbf{H}}
. The multiplicity and statistical diversity of input processes will be instrumental towards identifying (uniquely) the diffusion filter. Let
{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}\!:=\!\{{\mathbf{y}}_{m}^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^{P_{m}}
capture the observed output signal realizations associated with the
m
th process, and likewise let
{\mathcal{Y}}\!:=\!\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}
collect all available observations. Regarding the
m
th input process, one could conceivably assume its covariance matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}
, or even realizations of the signal
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^{P_{m}}
are given. Henceforth, the focus will be on the most pragmatic setting whereby only second-order statistics are available
[
36
,
39
,
15
]
. A motivating example can be drawn from opinion formation models in social networks capturing influence among agents. Suppose (e.g., socio-economic or demographic) features for each of the agents are measurable, while initial beliefs on different topics
m=1,\ldots,M
(namely the entries of
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
) are challenging to acquire. It is conceivable that agents with similar features will exhibit correlated beliefs (in a way akin to homophily), hence one could build a model of
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}
based on said features. As a second example, consider financial networks comprising stocks as nodes and their interdependencies as directed links. Publicly-traded stock prices
({\mathbf{y}}_{m})
are known to depend on stock purchases by investors
({\mathbf{x}}_{m})
, whose details are
often hidden from the public for privacy and strategic reasons. On the other hand, each publicly-traded company may broadcast e.g., quarterly statistical summaries of purchases of its stock
({\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m},\>m=1,\ldots,4)
; see for example the setting considered in
[
39
]
and the portfolio optimization test case in Section
VI
.

II-C

Problem Statement

Given observations
{\mathcal{Y}}\!=\!\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}
adhering to the generative model (
2
), where a common filter
{\mathbf{H}}:=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}
diffuses
M
zero-mean inputs
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
with known covariance matrices
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{m}{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\top}\right]
,
m=1,\ldots,M
, the goal is to find the sparsest shift
{\mathbf{S}}
with desirable topological properties (e.g., it is a valid adjacency matrix) that is consistent with the observations. The order
L\leq N
of the convolutional graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
and its polynomial coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
are unknown. We also do not assume a specific structure, say
{\mathbf{H}}=({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{S}})^{-1}
induced by a linear SEM
[
39
,
40
,
15
]
.

Motivated by the discussion following (
2
), our two-step network topology inference approach is to: i) first use realizations of observed signals in
{\mathcal{Y}}
together with side information on the excitation inputs
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
to
identify the diffusion filter

{\mathbf{H}}
; and ii) then use the estimated filter along with prior information on the network topology to
infer the GSO

{\mathbf{S}}
. Step i) is addressed in Section
III
, while ii) is discussed in Section
IV
. A joint graph filter and topology identification algorithm is developed in Section
V
, which alternates between steps i) and ii) in a mutually reinforcing fashion. Refer to Fig.
1
for a schematic depiction of both approaches.

III
Asymmetric diffusion filter identification

Here we study the system identification step, a challenging problem that is of independent interest beyond our topology identification end goal. In a number of applications, realizations of the excitation input
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
may be challenging to acquire, but information about the
statistical
description of
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
could still be available. As in our statement of the problem, assume that the excitation inputs are zero mean and their covariance matrices
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}
are known. As explained in Section
II
, suppose also that for each
m=1,\ldots,M
we acquire observations
{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}=

\{{\mathbf{y}}_{m}^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^{P_{m}}
, which are then used to estimate the output covariance
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{y}}_{m}{\mathbf{y}}_{m}^{\top}]
via sample averaging, that is

{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}=\frac{1}{P_{m}-1}\sum_{p=1}^{P_{m}}{\mathbf{y}}_{m}^{(p)}({\mathbf{y}}_{m}^{(p)})^{\top}.

(3)

Since under (
2
) the output covariance is given by
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}
, the aim is to identify a filter
{\mathbf{H}}
such that
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}
and
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}
are close in some sense. This “covariance matching” idea was first proposed in
[
38
]
for single-pole SEM filters and subsequently generalized for general filters in
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
; see
[
15
]
for a fresh look at the linear SEM case.

III-A

Solving Quadratic Equations for Filter Identification

Assuming for now perfect knowledge of the output signal covariances, the above rationale suggests studying the solutions of the following system of matrix quadratic equations

{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top},\quad m=1,\ldots,M,

(4)

for real-valued and possibly asymmetric
{\mathbf{H}}
. To study the solutions of (
4
), first consider
M=1
and henceforth drop the subindex
m
to focus on the square roots of
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}
.

To that end, recall first that the principal square root of
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
is the only symmetric and positive semidefinite (PSD) matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}
which satisfies
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}
. Such a matrix is given by
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}={\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}
, where
{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
are the eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}
stands for a diagonal matrix with the square roots of the (non-negative) eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
.
With
{\mathbf{U}}
denoting an
orthogonal
matrix (such that
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}={\mathbf{U}}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}={\mathbf{I}}
), one can show that any square matrix factor
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}
such that
{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}
is of the form
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}
. This observation can be leveraged to establish the following result, which characterizes the solution set of each individual quadratic equation in (
4
).

Proposition 1

If
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}
are of full rank, the set
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
containing all the (possibly asymmetric) matrices
{\mathbf{H}}
that solve (
4
) for a particular
m
is given by

\!\!\!\!{\mathcal{H}}_{m}=\left\{{\mathbf{H}}\;{\,\big|\,}\;{\mathbf{H}}\!=\!{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}\;\;\text{and}\;\;{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}={\mathbf{I}}\right\}.

(5)

Proof :
A simple substitution suffices to show that every
{\mathbf{H}}
of the form in (
5
) solves the
m
th equation in (
4
). Conversely, given an
{\mathbf{H}}
that solves (
4
), form the matrix
{\mathbf{U}}={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{1/2}
and observe that if
{\mathbf{U}}
is orthogonal, then
{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
.
Orthogonality of
{\mathbf{U}}
follows since
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{-1/2}={\mathbf{I}}
, where the last equality comes from the fact that
{\mathbf{H}}
is a solution of (
4
).
∎

As expected, the solution to each of the matrix quadratic equations (
4
) is not unique. In fact, Proposition
1
asserts that
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
can be parameterized by means of the orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{U}}
. If the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
(and hence the filter
{\mathbf{H}}
) is symmetric, then
{\mathbf{U}}=\text{diag}({\mathbf{b}})
, with
{\mathbf{b}}=\{-1,1\}^{N}
.
Symmetry removes the rotational ambiguity but
2^{N}
sign permutations remain. This added structure reduces considerably the size of the feasible set in (
5
); see
[
36
]
for details. Still, even in this case it is apparent that
{\mathbf{H}}
is non-identifiable for
M=1

For the general case of

{\mathcal{H}}_{1:M}\!:=\!\bigcap_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{H}}_{m}

shrinks for
M>1

) for

\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}

Define the matrices
\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}},m}

\displaystyle:={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m\!-\!1}^{-1}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2},

(6)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}},m}

\displaystyle:={\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m\!-\!1}^{-1}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{1/2},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{1:M}\!\!=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{m=1}^{M}\left\{{\mathbf{H}}\;{\,\big|\,}\;{\mathbf{b}}_{m}\!\in\!\{-1,1\}^{N}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\right.

(7)

where matrices
\{{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are the eigenvectors of
\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, respectively.

Proof :

Inspection of (
1
) reveals that
M\!=\!2
processes shrink the (continuous) manifold-constrained feasible set to
2^{N}
possible alternatives. Intuitively, each

provides a new set of observations that reduces the original

to

discrete choices. It is thus conceivable that, as

M=4
, even if the covariances

Theorem
1
gives sufficient conditions under which the asymmetric filter
{\mathbf{H}}
is identifiable from the second-order statistics of only
M=4
diffusion processes, arranged in two pairs that share the input eigenbases
{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
(
m=1,2
) and
{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
(
m=3,4
). It complements Lemma
1
by handling a structured case in which inputs partially share eigenvectors, trading spectral diversity
within
each pair for diversity
across
the two input subspaces. Conditions i) and ii) act on the input eigenvalues: i) ensures each input covariance has a well-defined eigenbasis, while the Kruskal-rank condition ii) secures the essential uniqueness of the PARAFAC factorizations used in the proof, pinning down

up to the

sign patterns already exposed by Lemma

{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
and

to be sufficiently different, and iv) requires the filter to be invertible. Taken together, the conditions formalize a simple intuition: sufficient statistical diversity of the excitation, both within and across the two pairs, is what disambiguates an asymmetric filter that a single process leaves undetermined up to a rotation.

III-B

Graph Filter Identification Algorithm

In practice, only empirical covariances (
3
) are available and the equalities in (
4
) must be relaxed. Given estimates
\{{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
obtained with sufficient samples to ensure full-rankness, our digraph filter identification approach is to solve the manifold-constrained least-squares (LS) problem [cf. (
5
)]

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{H}},\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\}}\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|{\mathbf{H}}-{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}_{m}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}\|_{F}^{2}

(8)

s. to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{N},\,\>\>m=1,\ldots,M,

where
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}:=\{{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}\>{\,\big|\,}\>{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}={\mathbf{I}}\}
denotes the Stiefel manifold of
N\times N
real orthogonal matrices. If the accuracy of the empirical covariances
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}
differs significantly across diffusion processes
m=1,\ldots,M
, it may be prudent to introduce non-uniform coefficients to weigh the residuals taking into account those accuracies (possibly as function of the sample sizes
P_{m}
). Indeed, the residuals in (
8
) should vanish in a noiseless setting so the criterion minimizes this discrepancy across the
M
processes considered.

Although the smooth objective

\phi({\mathbf{H}},\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\})\>:=\>\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|{\mathbf{H}}-{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}_{m}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}\|_{F}^{2}

(9)

in (
8
) is jointly convex in the unknowns
{\mathbf{H}}
and
\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, the constraint set
\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{N}^{M}
is not (here
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}^{M}:={\mathcal{U}}_{N}\times\ldots\times{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
denotes the product manifold). We solve (
8
) via an inexact alternating minimization (IAM) algorithm on
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}^{M}
.
Specifically, at each (outer) iteration
k=1,2,\ldots
, given
{\mathbf{H}}^{k}
the problem (
8
) is separable w.r.t. to each of the
\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
. Hence, these subproblems can be tackled concurrently, and we solve them inexactly by running a few (inner) iterations of Riemannian gradient descent (GD)
[
6
]
, while
{\mathbf{H}}^{k}
is fixed to its most up-to-date value and subsequently updated in closed form to complete the cyclic update.

In more detail, the update of
{\mathbf{U}}_{m}
at iteration
k
amounts to running
t=0,1,\ldots,t_{\max}
inner iterations of Riemannian GD on
{\mathbf{U}}^{t}
initialized as
{\mathbf{U}}^{0}={\mathbf{U}}_{m}^{k}
. To this end, one first computes the Riemannian gradient of
\phi({\mathbf{H}},\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\})
w.r.t.
{\mathbf{U}}_{m}
, denoted by
\grad_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi
. Letting
{\mathbf{G}}_{m}^{t}:=\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi(\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\},{\mathbf{H}}^{k}){\,\big|\,}_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}={\mathbf{U}}^{t}}
be the Euclidean gradient,
\grad_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi({\mathbf{U}}^{t})
is the projection of

{\mathbf{G}}_{m}^{t}={\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}\big({\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}^{t}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}-{\mathbf{H}}^{k}\big){\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}

(10)

onto the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{{\mathbf{U}}^{t}}{\mathcal{U}}_{N}

[
6
, pp. 161-162]
. This yields

\grad_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi({\mathbf{U}}^{t})={\mathbf{G}}_{m}^{t}-{\mathbf{U}}^{t}\frac{({\mathbf{U}}^{t})^{\top}{\mathbf{G}}_{m}^{t}+({\mathbf{G}}_{m}^{t})^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}^{t}}{2}.

(11)

Having computed the gradient, a classical descent method consists of taking a certain step in the opposite direction. However, this step likely results in a point outside of the manifold, so we have to project it back to
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
. This projection might be computationally intensive, so the
retraction
alternative is used instead. All in all, the Riemannian GD step is given by

{\mathbf{U}}^{t+1}=\textrm{Retr}_{{\mathbf{U}}^{t}}(-\alpha\grad_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi({\mathbf{U}}^{t})),

(12)

where we adopt the polar retraction
\textrm{Retr}_{{\mathbf{U}}}({\mathbf{W}})={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}

[
6
, p. 161]
, with
{\mathbf{P}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}=\textrm{svd}[{\mathbf{U}}+{\mathbf{W}}]
. Step size
\alpha>0
is chosen via backtracking line search, e.g., using the Armijo rule. A constant step size can also be used, informed by the Lipschitz constant of
\grad_{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}}\phi
. After completing
t_{\max}
gradient updates as in (
12
), the
m
th inner loop returns
{\mathbf{U}}_{m}^{k+1}={\mathbf{U}}^{t_{\max}}
.

To conclude the cycle in outer iteration
k
, we update
{\mathbf{H}}^{k}
. Since (
8
) is an unconstrained quadratic problem w.r.t.
{\mathbf{H}}
, we can update it in closed form as

{\mathbf{H}}^{k+1}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\mathbf{y}},m}^{1/2}{\mathbf{U}}_{m}^{k+1}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{x}},m}^{-1/2}.

(13)

The overall IAM procedure for asymmetric graph filter identification is tabulated under Algorithm
1
.

IV
Digraph topology inference

Given the graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
, our approach to infer the topology of the underlying digraph is to find a GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
that satisfies certain desirable structural properties and is compatible with the convolutional nature of
{\mathbf{H}}
. Focusing on recovery of the sparsest shift operator (i.e., the graph that minimizes the number of direct interactions among nodes), one can solve

\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ \|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{0},\quad\text{s.~to }\>{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{H}},

(14)

where
\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{0}
counts the number of non-zero entries of
{\mathbf{S}}
,
{\mathcal{S}}
is a convex set specifying the type of GSO we want to identify
[
33
]
, and the constraint
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{H}}
forces the filter
{\mathbf{H}}
to be a polynomial in
{\mathbf{S}}
(when all the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{S}}
are simple); see
[
22
,
28
]
and the discussion following (
2
). This last constraint offers an important departure from the (undirected) graph learning algorithms in
[
33
,
25
,
36
]
. These approaches first estimate the
eigenvectors
of
{\mathbf{H}}
, and then constrain
{\mathbf{S}}
to be diagonalized by those eigenvectors in a convex problem to recover the unknown eigenvalues. While in
[
33
,
25
,
36
]
one searches over a lower-dimensional space (
N
versus
N^{2}
here), the formulation (
14
) avoids computing an eigendecomposition and, more importantly, solving a problem over complex-valued variables. For undirected graphs, recent approaches impose the constraint
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}

[
37
,
8
,
23
,
18
]
, a direct consequence of graph stationarity and therefore is not applicable here.

The constraint
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
in (
14
) incorporates a priori knowledge about
{\mathbf{S}}
. If we let
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{A}}
for a digraph with non-negative weights and no self-loops, we can impose
{\mathcal{S}}\!:=\!\{{\mathbf{S}}\,|\,S_{ij}\geq 0,\;\,S_{ii}=0,\;\,\textstyle\sum_{i}S_{i1}\!=\!1\}
. The last condition fixes the scale of the admissible graphs by setting the weighted in-degree of the first node to
1
, and rules out the trivial solution
{\mathbf{S}}\!=\!{\mathbf{0}}
.

IV-A

14
) should be modified to account for model mismatches and imperfect estimates

due to finite-sample effects or local optimal solutions obtained from Algorithm

(15)

The bound

IV-B

Proximal Gradient Algorithm for Topology Identification

Exploiting the problem structure in (
15
), we develop a proximal gradient (PG) algorithm to recover the directed network topology from the graph filter estimate
{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}
; see
[
37
]
. To this end, we dualize
\|{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{S}}-{\mathbf{S}}{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
in (
15
) and write the composite, nonsmooth convex optimization

{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\in\underset{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\text{argmin}}\>\varphi({\mathbf{S}}):=\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1}\!+\!\underbrace{\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{S}}-{\mathbf{S}}{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}\|_{F}^{2}}_{g({\mathbf{S}})}.

(16)

The function
g(\cdot)
is convex and
L_{g}
-smooth [i.e.,
\nabla g(\cdot)
is
L_{g}
-Lipschitz continuous], with
\mu>0
a tuning parameter.

To derive the PG iterations, notice that the gradient of
g({\mathbf{S}})

\nabla g({\mathbf{S}})=\mu\left[{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}^{\top}({\hat{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{S}}-{\mathbf{S}}{\hat{\mathbf{H}}})-({\hat{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{S}}-{\mathbf{S}}{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}){\hat{\mathbf{H}}}^{\top}\right]

(17)

is Lipschitz with constant
L_{g}\!=\!4\mu\sigma_{\max}^{2}({\hat{\mathbf{H}}})
. With
\gamma>0
and
{\mathcal{S}}
a convex set, introduce the proximal operator of a function
\gamma f(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
at matrix
{\mathbf{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
as

{\mathbf{Z}}({\mathbf{D}})=\text{prox}_{\gamma f,{\mathcal{S}}}({\mathbf{D}}):=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left[f({\mathbf{X}})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|{\mathbf{X}}-{\mathbf{D}}\|_{F}^{2}\right].

Using both ingredients, the PG updates with fixed step size
\gamma<\frac{2}{L_{g}}
to solve the digraph topology identification problem (
16
) are given by (once more,
k=1,2,\ldots
denote iterations)

{\mathbf{S}}^{k+1}:=\text{prox}_{\gamma\|\cdot\|_{1},{\mathcal{S}}}\left({\mathbf{S}}^{k}-\gamma\nabla g({\mathbf{S}}^{k})\right).

(18)

Evaluating the proximal operator efficiently is key to the success of PG methods. For
{\mathcal{S}}\!:=\!\{{\mathbf{S}}\,|\,S_{ij}\geq 0,\;\,S_{ii}=0,\;\,\textstyle\sum_{i}S_{i1}\!=\!1\}
and
f({\mathbf{S}})=\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1}
, it follows that
\text{prox}_{\gamma\|\cdot\|_{1},{\mathcal{S}}}({\mathbf{D}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
has entries given by

\hskip-4.55254pt[{\mathbf{Z}}({\mathbf{D}})]_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}0,&i=j\\
\bigtriangleup_{i-1}([D_{21},\ldots,D_{N1}]),&i=2,\ldots,N,\>j=1\\
\max(0,D_{ij}-\gamma),&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(19)

where
\bigtriangleup(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{N\!-\!1}\mapsto[0,1]^{N\!-\!1}
is a projection onto the
N-1
dimensional probability simplex enforcing
\sum_{i}S_{i1}\!=\!1
. Since
\bigtriangleup
is an
N-1
dimensional vector, the notation
\bigtriangleup_{i}
used in (
19
) refers to the
i
th entry of said vector. The projection can be computed in closed form using the method in
[
9
, Alg. 1]
. Beyond the first column of its output, the proximal map nulls the diagonal entries of
{\mathbf{S}}^{k+1}
and applies a non-negative soft-thresholding operator to update the remaining entries. The PG iterations in (
18
) are tabulated under Algorithm
2
, which will also serve as a key building block for the
joint
digraph and filter identification algorithm in Section
V
.

Just like Algorithm

V
Joint digraph and filter identification

The shift-invariance identity
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{H}}
captures a fundamental relationship between the filter and the GSO in the diffusion-based generative mechanism (
2
). While so far only leveraged to recover the graph topology given a filter estimate [cf. the constraint in (
15
)], it is prudent to close the loop and feed back GSO estimates to also aid the initial filter identification step in Section
III

joint
digraph and filter identification framework.

From the insights gained in Sections
III
and

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{H}},\{{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}_{m}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{N},\,\>\>m=1,\ldots,M,

VII
Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work

We studied the problem of inferring the topology of a digraph from observations of signals diffused on the network. Modeling the mapping between the (arbitrarily correlated) excitation inputs and the observed outputs as a generic polynomial graph filter, enabled a two-step topology inference approach whereby: i) we first use (statistical) information on the inputs and outputs to infer the said diffusion filter; and ii) we then combine the filter estimate along with prior structural information on the digraph to recover the network topology. For the system identification step, we assume only second-order statistical information on the inputs is available. While such a problem can be naturally formulated as a nonconvex fourth order optimization, we recast it as a smooth quadratic minimization subject to Stiefel manifold constraints. We established the filter is uniquely identifiable under sufficient spectral richness of the inputs and developed a convergent Riemannian gradient descent algorithm. Regarding the second step, the focus was on finding the sparsest GSO compatible with the estimated filter, namely that both operators (approximately) commute. By feeding back intermediate GSO estimates to boost filter recovery performance, we put forth a joint graph filter and topology identification formulation and associated provably convergent algorithm, closing the loop between steps i) and ii) to offer improved sample complexity. The closed-loop digraph topology inference pipeline was comprehensively evaluated using synthetic and real network data.

The problem is admittedly challenging and our solution requires fairly large sample sizes, multiple input process, and cubic (in the number of nodes) complexity solvers. To bridge these gaps, as future work we plan to investigate lightweight online algorithms, restrictions to specialized graph filter classes, and additional domain-specific constraints.
