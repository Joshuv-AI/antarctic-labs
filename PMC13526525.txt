Stem cell factor regulates endothelial HIF-2α expression during hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis
Authors: Hayoung Jeong, Yang Hee Choi, Yubin Hwang, Minju Kim, June Hyeok Lee, Koung Li Kim, Wonhee Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) act as master regulators of hypoxia-induced pathological angiogenesis, a hallmark of various ischemic ocular diseases. Recent evidence highlights the pivotal role of HIF-2α among HIF isoforms in endothelial cells (ECs) undergoing pathological angiogenesis under hypoxic conditions. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying endothelial HIF-2α expression during hypoxia remain incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrated that stem cell factor (SCF) and its receptor, cKIT, upregulate HIF-2α expression in hypoxic ECs. Using the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model, we observed that HIF-2α was highly expressed in cKIT-positive pathological neovessels in the retina, and SCF was upregulated mainly at astrocytes in the inner retinal layer. Experiments using cKIT mutant mice and anti-SCF neutralizing antibody demonstrated that inhibition of SCF/cKIT signaling significantly reduced retinal HIF-2α expression and pathological angiogenesis in mice with OIR. Moreover, HIF-2α inhibition abolished the SCF-induced increase in the angiogenic activity of human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) under hypoxic conditions. Mechanistic studies in HUVECs revealed that SCF enhanced HIF-2α protein levels without affecting HIF2A mRNA levels, through AKT- and ERK1/2-dependent phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase β-1 under hypoxia. These findings provide novel insights into the regulatory mechanisms controlling HIF-2α expression in angiogenic ECs during hypoxia and suggest that the SCF/cKIT/HIF-2α axis in hypoxic ECs represents a crucial pathway in the regulation of pathological angiogenesis in ischemic ocular diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from preexisting vasculature, is an essential process for tissue growth and organ development. However, it also plays a pivotal role in the development and progression of various pathological conditions. For example, pathological angiogenesis, characterized by excessive formation of immature and dysfunctional neovessels, is a hallmark of ocular diseases such as retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), and wet-type age-related macular degeneration (1).

Hypoxia, caused by vascular occlusion, vascular dysfunction, or increased tissue mass, serves as a key driver of pathological angiogenesis (2). Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), particularly the HIF-1 and HIF-2 isoforms, act as master regulators responsible for the expression of hypoxia-induced angiogenic mediators (3, 4). HIFs are heterodimeric proteins comprising an oxygen-sensitive α subunit (HIF-1α and HIF-2α) and a constitutively expressed, stable b subunit. Under normoxic conditions, prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins (PHDs) hydroxylate specific proline residues on HIF-1α and HIF-2α, targeting them for proteasomal degradation. During hypoxia, PHDs are inactivated, stabilizing HIF-1α and HIF-2α, which then translocate to the nucleus and initiate hypoxia-responsive gene transcription.

While PHD-mediated post-translational regulation of HIF-α subunits is well characterized, emerging evidence highlights alternative pathways regulating HIF-a subunits at transcriptional and translational levels. For instance, HIF1A transcription is stimulated by lipopolysaccharides, angiotensin II, interleukins, and thrombin, while its translation is enhanced by growth factors such as insulin-like growth factor-1 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (5). Additionally, various RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and noncoding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), regulate HIF1A translation via interactions with its untranslated regions (UTRs) (3). Compared to the relatively well-characterized mechanism of HIF-1α regulation, the transcriptional and translational control of HIF-2α under hypoxic conditions remains less understood. Limited studies suggest that hypoxia-associated noncoding RNAs modulate HIF-2α expression in cancer cells under hypoxic conditions. For instance, miR-199a and miR-145, which are downregulated in hypoxia, directly bind to the 3’-UTR of HIF2A mRNA and suppress its translation in ovarian cancer and neuroblastoma under normoxic conditions (6, 7). Furthermore, a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), the suppressing androgen receptor in renal cell carcinoma (SARCC), is differentially regulated by hypoxia and indirectly promotes HIF2A transcription in renal cell carcinoma under hypoxic conditions (8). Although previous studies have enhanced our knowledge of HIF-2α expression regulation in cancer cells, the mechanisms controlling hypoxia-driven HIF-2α expression in endothelial cells (ECs) remain largely unknown.

Previously, we observed that cKIT expression was markedly increased in retinal ECs under hypoxic conditions (9). This upregulation enhanced stem cell factor (SCF)-mediated cKIT signaling in hypoxic ECs, promoting pathological angiogenesis in the eye. Considering the angiogenic role of HIF-2α in ECs during hypoxia, we hypothesized that SCF/cKIT signaling regulates endothelial HIF-2α expression during hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis. In this study, we demonstrate that SCF/cKIT signaling increases HIF-2α protein levels without affecting HIF2A mRNA levels, in hypoxic ECs via AKT and ERK1/2-dependent phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase β-1 (p70S6K). Given the limited understanding of the hypoxia-driven HIF-2α expression pathway in ECs, these findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms controlling endothelial HIF-2α expression during pathological angiogenesis.

RESULTS

HIF-2α expression increases in cKIT-positive ECs of neovascular tufts in the retina of mice with oxygen-induced retinopathy

The oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model is a well-established model that displays hypoxia-induced pathological angiogenesis in the retina and mimics the cardinal features of ischemic retinopathy (ROP and PDR) (10). In the OIR model, the hyperoxic phase from postnatal day 7 (P7) to P12 induces retinal vessel regression. At P12, returning OIR mice to normoxia results in hypoxia relative to the previous 5 days, leading to HIF stabilization and subsequent formation of disordered neovessels, the so-called tufts, in the retina (Fig. 1A, B). Using the OIR mouse model, we first investigated the expression of HIF-2α, SCF, and cKIT in retinas harvested from normal and OIR mice at P17. As we previously reported, retinal cKIT and SCF protein levels in OIR mice were considerably higher than those in normal mice (Fig. 1C) (9). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results consistently confirmed the elevated SCF level in the retina of OIR mice compared to that in normal mice (Fig. 1D). Moreover, retinal HIF-2α protein levels in OIR mice were significantly higher than those in normal mice. The immunohistochemical analysis of transverse retinal sections of OIR mice revealed that cKIT and SCF were highly expressed in CD31-positive neovascular tufts on the inner surface of the retina and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes in the inner retinal layer, respectively (Fig. 1E). Notably, we observed prominent HIF-2α expression in cKIT-positive tufts, suggesting the highly induced co-expression of HIF-2α and cKIT in ECs undergoing hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis in the retina.

Blocking SCF and cKIT suppresses retinal HIF-2α expression in OIR mice

We next investigated whether HIF-2α expression is regulated by the SCF/cKIT pathway during hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis in the retina. OIR models were established in wild-type (WT) and cKit mutant (KitW-sh/W-sh) mice exhibiting a marked decrease in cKIT expression due to an inversion mutation in the 5’ transcriptional regulatory element (11, 12). In the analysis of whole-mounted retinas at P17, cKit mutant mice displayed a significant decrease in tuft formation compared with that in WT mice (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Western blotting data revealed that retinal HIF-2α levels in cKIT mutant mice with OIR were significantly lower than those in WT mice with OIR (Fig. 2C). We next determined whether SCF depletion could also reduce retinal HIF-2α expression in OIR mice. WT mice were intravitreally injected with anti-SCF neutralizing IgG immediately after being returned to normoxia at P12, and their retinal tissues were harvested at P17. SCF/cKIT signaling blockade using anti-SCF neutralizing IgG significantly decreased tuft formation in WT mice with OIR (Fig. 2D, E, Supplementary Fig. 1B). As observed in cKit mutant mice with OIR, HIF-2α protein levels were substantially reduced in OIR mice that received an intravitreal injection of anti-SCF IgG compared with those that received a PBS injection (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these data indicate that SCF/cKIT signaling mediates the hypoxia-driven increase in retinal HIF-2α expression in OIR mice.

HIF-2α mediates SCF-induced in vitro angiogenesis in hypoxic ECs

Next, we explored whether HIF-2α upregulation contributes to SCF-induced angiogenesis in hypoxic ECs using an HIF-2α specific inhibitor, PT2385. PT2385 selectively binds to HIF-2α (Kd = approximately 50 nM), but not HIF-1α, to block HIF-2α/HIF-1β dimerization and has shown potential for treating patients with renal cell carcinoma (13). In vitro angiogenesis data showed that SCF significantly increased wound migration and tube formation in hypoxic human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) (Fig. 3A, B). However, co-treatment with PT2385 significantly abolished the SCF-induced increase in angiogenic activities of ECs in hypoxia. These results demonstrate that SCF promotes in vitro angiogenesis in hypoxia, at least in part, via HIF-2α upregulation in ECs.

SCF-induced activation of AKT and ERK1/2 pathway regulates HIF-2α expression in hypoxic ECs

Given the observation that SCF/cKIT signaling blockade in cKit mutant mice and using anti-SCF neutralizing IgG reduced HIF-2α expression during hypoxia-induced pathological retinal neovascularization, we investigated the mechanism by which SCF/cKIT signaling regulates HIF-2α expression in hypoxic ECs. Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and western blotting analyses showed that SCF treatment did not increase HIF2A mRNA levels, but significantly enhanced its protein levels in HUVECs during hypoxia, whereas no such effect was observed under normoxic conditions (Fig. 4A, B). As we previously reported, this occurs because hypoxia markedly upregulates cKIT expression in ECs, thereby rendering cells highly responsive to SCF and activating the SCF/cKIT signaling pathway (9). We then examined the time course of HIF-2α stabilization using the proteasomal inhibitor (MG-132) in HUVECs during hypoxia. Western blotting data showed that HIF-2a protein levels steadily increased in MG-132-treated HUVECs under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 4C, D). After 4-h treatment with MG-132 under hypoxic conditions, HIF-2α protein levels were significantly higher in SCF-treated HUVECs than those in untreated controls.

Given the previously reported role of PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways in phosphorylating p70S6K, a translation regulatory protein, we investigated whether SCF might increase the HIF-2α protein levels via PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways in HUVECs under hypoxia (14, 15). In hypoxic HUVECs, SCF treatment highly activated its downstream signaling molecules, including AKT and ERK1/2, and increased p70S6K phosphorylation (Fig. 5A, B). Moreover, the blockade of either AKT or ERK1/2 phosphorylation using LY294002 or PD98059, respectively, substantially attenuated the SCF-induced phosphorylation of p70S6K and abolished the SCF-induced increase in HIF-2α proteins in hypoxic HUVECs. Taken together, these data suggest that SCF enhances HIF-2α expression by activating AKT and ERK1/2 signaling pathways in hypoxic ECs.

DISCUSSION

The HIF family has emerged as a master regulator of hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis, controlling the expression of various angiogenic mediators (2-4). Among the HIF isoforms, HIF-1α, the first identified HIF α subunit, is expressed ubiquitously and has been extensively studied in vascular ischemic diseases and cancers (3, 4). Similar to HIF-1α, HIF-2a which shares high homology with HIF-1α, also regulates the hypoxia-induced transcription of angiogenic genes (3, 4). However, HIF-2α is primarily expressed in ECs and is thus also termed endothelial PAS domain protein 1 (16, 17). While HIF-2α has been previously implicated in developmental and physiological angiogenesis, its role in hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis remains incompletely understood (18, 19).

Recent studies by Qin et al. have demonstrated that both HIF-1α and HIF-2α are crucial for retinal pathological angiogenesis (20). In mice with OIR, inhibiting either HIF-1 or HIF-2 effectively suppressed retinal neovascularization. Notably, in hypoxic ECs, HIF-2α upregulates plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), a key angiogenic factor implicated in retinal neovascularization in patients with diabetes (20). Furthermore, several studies have reported that plexiform lesions, a hallmark of pathological disordered angiogenesis in pulmonary arterial hypertension, are attributed to HIF-2α activation in pulmonary vascular ECs (21-23). Genetically modified mice with EC-specific HIF-2α overexpression spontaneously developed severe pulmonary arterial hypertension with plexiform-like lesions (21, 22). Additionally, Dai et al. reported that endothelial-specific PHD2-deficient mice exhibited enhanced endothelial proliferation and angiogenesis in the left ventricle, driven by endothelial activation of HIF-2α due to PHD2 deficiency (24). These in vivo findings are further supported by in vitro studies showing that under hypoxic conditions, HIF-2α plays a pivotal role in enhancing the angiogenic activity of ECs by directly transactivating key angiogenesis-related genes, including VEGF, VEGFR1, VEGFR2, and Tie2 (25, 26).

Given the critical role of endothelial HIF-2α in hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis, understanding the regulatory mechanism underlying HIF-2α expression in ECs under hypoxic conditions is essential. Oxygen-dependent PHD-mediated hydroxylation and subsequent ubiquitination are well-established processes that control the protein stability of both HIF-1a and HIF-2α. Additionally, several studies have identified alternative regulatory mechanisms of HIF-2α expression, involving lncRNAs and miRNAs that modulate HIF2A transcription and translation at hypoxia (27). For instance, Zhai et al. reported that hypoxia-associated lncRNA-SARCC regulates HIF2A transcription through modulating the interaction with androgen receptors in renal cell carcinoma (8). Joshi et al. demonstrated that miRNA-199a controls HIF2A translation in ovarian cancer cells via the direct interaction with the 3’-UTR of HIF2A (6). Furthermore, Zhang et al. identified another miRNA, miR-145, that binds to the 3’-UTR of HIF2A and regulates HIF2A translation in neuroblastoma (7). While these studies have identified PHD-independent regulatory mechanisms of HIF2A expression in various cancer cells, research addressing endothelial-specific regulatory mechanisms of HIF-2α expression is lacking, particularly in the context of hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that SCF/cKIT signaling modulates HIF-2α expression in ECs undergoing hypoxia-driven pathological angiogenesis. Using a murine OIR model, we observed that HIF-2α expression was highly induced in cKIT-positive neovascular tufts in the retina. To directly examine the contribution of SCF/cKIT signaling to HIF-2α expression in angiogenic ECs under hypoxia, we blocked SCF/cKIT signaling using cKit mutant mice and anti-SCF neutralizing IgG in mice with OIR. We found that inhibiting SCF/cKIT signaling significantly suppressed retinal HIF-2α expression and reduced the pathological angiogenesis in the eyes of mice with OIR. Furthermore, in vitro angiogenesis results revealed that treatment with an HIF-2α specific inhibitor significantly abolished the SCF-induced increase in angiogenic activities of ECs at hypoxia, which suggests that HIF-2α upregulation substantially contributes to SCF-induced angiogenesis in hypoxia.

In hypoxic primary human ECs, SCF/cKIT signaling increased HIF-2α expression through AKT- and ERK1/2-dependent phosphorylation of p70S6K, a translation regulatory protein. p70S6K is known to be activated through PI3K and MAPK pathways and regulate cell proliferation and cell cycle progression by inducing protein synthesis (14, 15). In cancer cells, mitogenic stimuli such as growth factors have been shown to phosphorylate p70S6K, which enhances HIF1A mRNA translation independently of hypoxia and promotes tumor growth and angiogenesis (28-31). Nevertheless, other translation-related factors beyond p70S6K could contribute to the SCF-mediated regulation of HIF-2a expression in hypoxic ECs. Additionally, further studies are required to determine whether SCF modulates HIF2A translation by regulating the expression of hypoxia-responsive noncoding RNAs and/or by affecting the interaction between the UTRs of HIF2A mRNA and RBPs such as iron regulatory proteins (32).

Given the emerging critical role of endothelial HIF-2α during pathological angiogenesis, our findings suggest that the SCF/cKIT/HIF-2α axis in hypoxic ECs represents a potentially important angiogenesis regulation pathway. This study provides a rationale for future research into anti-SCF/cKIT therapy as a novel pharmacological strategy for treating ischemic ocular diseases such as PDR and ROP, as well as pulmonary arterial hypertension. Targeting SCF/cKIT signaling may enable the selective suppression of HIF-2α expression in ECs undergoing pathological angiogenesis without affecting its expression in other cell types. Notably, our findings provide novel insights into the regulation of HIF-2a expression in angiogenic ECs under hypoxic conditions, expanding our understanding beyond its relatively well characterized regulation in cancer cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal model of OIR

The C57BL/6 (Orient Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), cKIT mutant (KitW-sh/W-sh) (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, MA, USA), and WT littermate mice used in this study were treated in adherence with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chung-Ang University (reference number: 2020-00060). OIR experiments were performed as previously described (10). Briefly, pups were placed in 75% oxygen at P7 and returned to normoxia on P12. In the SCF depletion study, mice were administered anti-mouse SCF neutralizing IgG (2 μg; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; AF-455-NA) or an equivalent volume of PBS via intravitreal injection. Retinae were harvested at P17 for further analysis. For the surgical procedures, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (6 mg/kg). The adequacy of anesthesia was monitored by the withdrawal reflex from a toe pinch. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation under anesthesia. The pupils of the anesthetized mice were dilated using topical drops of 1% tropicamide (Santen, Osaka, Japan). To visualize retinal neovascularization, retinal flat mounts were stained with Alexa FluorⓇ 594-conjugated IB4 (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA; 1:50 dilution) and imaged using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The area of retinal neovascularization was calculated as the number of pixels in the neovascular tufts divided by the total number of pixels in the entire retina. Two people independently performed the analyses.

Western blotting analysis

Tissue and cell lysates were sonicated and centrifuged, and the insoluble fraction was discarded. The concentration of extracted protein was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay. Protein samples were size-fractionated on Mini Protein Gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-Rad Laboratories). After blocking in 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary IgGs against HIF-2α (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA; NB100-122; 1:1,000 dilution), cKIT (R&D Systems; MAB1356; 0.5 μg/ml), SCF (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; AB64677; 1:1,000 dilution), phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 4060; 1:1,000 dilution), AKT (Cell Signaling Technology; 4691; 1:1,000 dilution), phosphorylated p70S6K (p-p70S6K; Cell Signaling Technology; 9234; 1:2,000 dilution), p70S6K (Cell Signaling Technology; 2708; 1:2,000 dilution), phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2; Cell Signaling Technology; 4370; 1:1,000 dilution), ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology; 4695; 1:1,000 dilution) and β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; SC-47778; 1:5,000 dilution). Subsequently, membranes were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary IgG for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Images captured using a western blot imaging system (Vilber, Marne-la-Vallée, France) were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

ELISA

Murine retinal tissues were digested using the lysis solution supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (CelLyticTM MT; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The murine SCF concentrations were then determined using the SCF ELISA kit (R&D Systems).

Immunohistochemistry

Eyes were enucleated, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA). After blocking with 10% normal horse serum, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary IgGs against HIF-2α (Novus Biologicals; NB100-122; 1:200 dilution), cKIT (R&D Systems; MAB1356; 10 μg/ml), CD31 (R&D Systems; AF3628; 1:500 dilution), GFAP (Abcam; AB53554; 1:1,000 dilution), and SCF (Abcam; AB64677; 1:1,500 dilution). After washing in PBS, tissue sections were incubated with Alexa FluorⓇ 488- or Alexa FluorⓇ 594-conjugated secondary IgGs (Thermo Scientific; A11008, A11005, A11080; 1:500 dilution) at 4°C for 1 day. Thereafter, nuclei were stained with 4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). All images presented are representative of three independent experiments.

Cell culture and treatment

HUVECs (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) were cultured in endothelial growth medium-2 (Lonza) and used from passages three to six. For hypoxic treatment, cells were placed in a modular incubator chamber (MIC-101; Billups-Rothenberg Inc., Del Mar, CA, USA) maintained at 1% O2, 94% N2, and 5% CO2 at 37°C. For experiments with chemical inhibitors, cells were treated with or without recombinant human SCF (rh SCF; R&D Systems), PT2385 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), MG-132 (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), LY294002 (Sigma-Aldrich), and PD98059 (Sigma-Aldrich).

In vitro angiogenesis assays

Tube formation assay was performed by seeding HUVECs in Matrigel (Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA)-coated 24-well plates. Cells were cultured with endothelial basal medium + 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Lonza) with or without rh SCF (50 ng/ml) and PT2385 (10 μM) at 37°C. After 6 h of incubation, tube networks were imaged in four random microscopic fields and quantified by measuring the tube lengths. For the wound migration assay, confluent HUVEC monolayers were scratched using pipette tips and incubated with endothelial basal medium ＋ 1% FBS with or without rh SCF (50 ng/ml) and PT2385 (10 μM) at 37°C for 24 h. Images of the scratches were taken using an optical microscope and cell migration was quantified by measuring the area of cells that had migrated from the wound edges.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells or retinas using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and reverse transcription was performed using a Superscript first-strand synthesis kit (Invitrogen). PCR was performed with 2X Taq PCR MasterMix (Thermo Scientific) and T100 Thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories) using the following primers: 5’-CGGAGGTGTTCTATGAGCTGG-3’ and 5’-AGCTTGTGTGTTCGCAGGAA-3’ for HIF2A; 5’-AACCTGCCAAATATGATGAC-3’ and 5’-TCATACCAGGAAATGAGCTT-3’ for GAPDH. PCR products were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gels and visualized using a SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen). ImageJ software was used to quantify the band intensities.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was evaluated using an unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni’s post hoc multiple comparison test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference between values. The number of samples is indicated by n.
