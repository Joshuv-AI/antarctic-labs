IL-27 shapes NK cell heterogeneity and function in colorectal cancer
Authors: Lorenzo Lucantonio, Andrea Kosta, Ludovica Schiano, Francesca Sozio, Silvia Ruggeri, Giovanna Peruzzi, Giuseppe Pietropaolo, Arianna M Candelotti, Laura Beltrame, Mattia Laffranchi, Rosa Molfetta, Giovanni Bernardini, Silvano Sozzani, Enrico Fiori, Angela Gismondi, Angela Santoni, Helena Stabile, Giuseppe Sciumè, Cinzia Fionda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). While adaptive immunity contributes to tumor control, growing evidence underscores the role of innate lymphocytes, particularly natural killer (NK) cells, in early antitumor surveillance. However, tumor-infiltrating NK cells often exhibit defective maturation and impaired effector functions, whereas the signals regulating NK cell differentiation and activity in CRC remain poorly defined. Interleukin-27 (IL-27) has emerged as a regulator of antitumor immunity, yet its role in modulating NK cell responses in intestinal tumors is largely unexplored.MethodsWe employed an orthotopic transplantation model of genetically engineered colorectal tumor organoids Apc−/−KrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/−Smad4−/− (AKPS) to investigate NK cell heterogeneity, maturation, and function during CRC progression. Tumor-infiltrating innate lymphocytes were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and functional assays. In vitro co-culture systems and in vivo IL-27 blockade were used to assess the impact of tumor-derived IL-27 on NK cell transcriptional programs and effector activity.ResultsSingle-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed marked heterogeneity among tumor-infiltrating NK cells, identifying subsets with different maturation states and functional capacities. Among innate lymphocytes, AKPS tumors were dominated by NK cells displaying reduced activating receptors and diminished cytotoxic and cytokine-producing potential. Phenotypic and adoptive transfer analyses demonstrated that the TME favors persistence of immature CD27+ NK cell subsets while limiting differentiated NK cells. Mechanistically, tumor-derived IL-27 emerged as a critical regulator sustaining NK cell infiltration and activation; IL-27 blockade reduced NK cell accumulation, interferon-γ production, and immature subset frequency. The clinical relevance of these findings was supported by analysis of human CRC single-cell datasets, which revealed elevated IL-27 signaling in intratumoral NK cells, as well as by the ability of patient-derived organoids to enhance NK-cell cytotoxicity, which was reduced by IL-27 blockade.ConclusionsOur study identifies IL-27 as a critical modulator of NK cell differentiation and function in CRC, highlighting its role in sustaining NK cell-mediated immune surveillance within the tumor microenvironment. These findings provide mechanistic insight into NK cell dysfunction in CRC and suggest IL-27 signaling as a promising therapeutic target to restore innate antitumor immunity.Keywords: Colorectal Cancer, Natural killer - NK, Cytokine

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer mortality worldwide,1 driven in part by its ability to shape the tumor microenvironment (TME).2 While adaptive immunity is essential for tumor control,3–5 increasing evidence indicates that innate lymphocytes, particularly natural killer (NK) cells, play a key role in early antitumor defense.6 7 Other innate lymphoid cell (ILC) subsets show context-dependent functions that vary with subset identity type and disease stage.8–10 For example, in the azoxymethane/dextran sodium sulfate (AOM/DSS) model, ILC1-3 acquire inhibitory phenotypes,10 while ILC2 can either restrain or promote CRC depending on the inflammatory milieu.8 11 12 Moreover, reduced tumor-infiltrating major histocompatibility complex-II+ ILC3 correlates with microbiota profiles associated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 resistance.9

Although NK cells can promptly eliminate malignant cells without prior sensitization, they frequently display reduced cytotoxicity and impaired maturation in solid tumors due to the suppressive TME.13–16 Mouse NK-cell differentiation proceeds from bone marrow (BM) to peripheral tissues through CD27-CD11b-KLRG1-defined stages, which correspond to progressive acquisition of immunoregulatory or cytotoxic functions with terminally differentiated CD27⁻CD11b+KLRG1+ NK cells displaying limited functional potential.17–20

Despite established roles in tumor surveillance and immunotherapy, the factors controlling NK-cell functions in TME remain poorly defined.6 7 15 21 22 Mouse studies based on the ApcMin/+ and AOM/DSS models reveal progressive loss of tumor-infiltrating NK cells overtime, and their depletion accelerated tumor growth, underscoring their importance in restraining CRC progression.23 Conversely, NK cells in established tumors rapidly lose effector activity and acquire an ILC1-like phenotype marked by reduced Eomes and induction of ILC1-associated markers.24–26

Current efforts to restore NK-cell function include cytokine-based therapies such as interleukin (IL)-15;24 on the other hand, recent work identifies IL-27 as a key regulator of T-cell responses in CRC with therapeutic potential.27 IL-27 is a heterodimeric cytokine within the IL-6/IL-12 family composed of EBI3 and p28 subunits that signals through the gp130/IL-27Rα receptor complex to activate JAK/STAT1/3 and MAPK pathways.28 29 In the gut, IL-27 is predominantly produced by myeloid cells responding to microbial or inflammatory signals.30 Although IL-27 regulates antiviral and antitumor NK-cell activity,31–35 whether it specifically modulates NK cells within the intestinal mucosa remains unknown. Notably, IL-27Rα is highly expressed by intratumoral T cells and NK cells in CRC,27 suggesting that IL-27 may directly shape NK-cell function within the TME.

Here, using an orthotopic transplantation model of tumor intestinal organoids (Apc−/−/KrasG12D/+/Trp53R172H/−/Smad4−/−; hereafter referred to as AKPS), which rapidly form adenocarcinomas,36 we investigated NK-cell heterogeneity, differentiation, and function during CRC progression.

Integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), flow cytometry, and functional assays, we demonstrate that NK cells develop a hypofunctional phenotype within the TME and that tumor-derived IL-27 sustains NK-cell activity.

These findings highlight IL-27 signaling as a potential target to reinvigorate NK-cell responses in CRC.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J CD45.2 and CD45.1 mice (Jackson Laboratory, stock no. 008449) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All experiments were performed using 6–12 weeks old sex-matched mice. All animal procedures were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health and conducted in compliance with national (D.L. N.116, G.U., suppl. 40, 18-2-1992 and N. 26, G.U. March 4, 2014) and international law and policies (EEC Council Directive 2010/63/EU, OJ L 276/33, 22–09-2010; National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, US National Research Council, 2011). Sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Mouse colon organoids

Apc−/−/KrasG12D/+/Trp53R172H/−/Smad4−/− organoids were generously provided by Professor A Ramírez de Molina (Molecular Oncology Group, IMDEA Food Institute, Madrid, Spain)37 and cultured as previously described.36

For DNA transfection, virus production and in vitro transduction, protein extraction, western blotting and ELISA assay see online supplemental material.

Patient-derived organoids

Tumor organoids were established from biopsies of patients with stage IV CRC following the STEMCELL Technologies protocol. Briefly, biopsies were minced into ~5 mm fragments and incubated in Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent for 60 min at 37°C on a rocking platform. After centrifugation, pellets were mechanically dissociated in ice-cold Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)+1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to release crypts, which were embedded in Matrigel domes and cultured in IntestiCult OGMH Basal Medium. Organoids were maintained at 37°C and passaged three times per week.

Cells

The mouse lymphoma YAC-1 and human K562 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in DMEM (Euroclone, cat# ECB7501L) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum. The murine colon adenocarcinoma MC38 cell line was generously provided by Professor Cecilia Garlanda (IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital, Milan, Italy) and was maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI-1640) (Euroclone) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS). All cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and were Mycoplasma-free (Abcam, cat# ab289834).

Intrarectal and subcutaneous AKPS organoid transplantation

Generation of intrarectal and subcutaneous AKPS tumors was performed as previously described.36 At day 7 post injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and tumors were evaluated for size using caliper and weight. All mice successfully engrafted with organoids were included in analyses.

To deplete NK/ILC1, tumor-bearing mice were treated intraperitoneally with 100 µg of anti-NK1.1 monoclonal antibody (clone PK136; Leinco Technologies, cat#N268) or mouse IgG2a isotype control antibody (Bio X Cell, clone C1.18.4, cat# BP0085) on days −3, +3, and +6 relative to organoid injection.

To block IL-27 pathway, 250 µg of anti-IL-27 (clone MM27.7B1, InvivoMab, cat#BE0326) or IgG2a isotype control antibody (Bio X Cell, C1.18.4, cat# BP0085) was administered via intraperitoneal injection on days 0, +2, +4, +6 of tumor growth.

For histology and immunofluorescence analysis of tumor sections, and for AOM/DSS-induced CRC model see online supplemental material.

Cell isolation, flow cytometry and cell sorting

Cells from intestinal lamina propria and tumors were isolated as previously described.36 Flow cytometry analysis was performed on LSR Fortessa (Becton Dickinson). Cell sorting was performed using FACSAria III (Becton Dickinson); fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted cells were confirmed to be higher than 95% pure with post sorting analysis. Dead cells were excluded by using Ghost dye red 780 Viability (Tombo biosciences, cat# 13–0865 T100). Antibodies used in this study can be found in online supplemental table S1. Data were analyzed using FlowJo_V.10.8.1 (Beckton Dickinson). Dimensionality Reduction: t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was applied for data visualization using FlowJo’s native plugin. For scRNA-seq see online supplemental material.

NK cell isolation

Mouse spleens were dissociated using a pestle and a 70 µm filter to obtain a single-cell suspension. Erythrocytes were lysed with Red Blood Lysis Buffer (Sigma Aldrich, cat# R7757). The remaining splenic cells were counted, and NK cells were isolated using the NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For CD107a detection, NK cells were FACS-sorted from spleen and AKPS tumors as Live/Dead−CD3ε−CD19−CD45+NK1.1+NKp46+. Sorted NK cells were incubated with YAC-1 cell line in a ratio 1:1 in RPMI medium supplemented with murine IL-15 (10 ng/mL) (PeproTech, cat# 210–15), β-mercaptoethanol (20 µM), IL-2 (200 µ/mL) (PeproTech, cat # 200–02) and GolgiStop (1:6000) (BD Bioscience, cat#51-2092KZ) for 4 hours. A fluorochrome-conjugated CD107a antibody was added in the last 2 hours.

For 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) cytotoxicity assay, FACS-sorted NK cells from spleen and AKPS tumors were incubated with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled target cells (MC38) at 8:1 effector/target ratio for 5 hours. CFSE-labeled cells alone were used as control.

For interferon (IFN)-γ production, splenic NK cells and tumor immune infiltrate derived from mice administered with an anti-IL-27 antibody or with an isotype control antibody were cultured for 6 hours in the presence of the phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate/ionomycin-based cell stimulation cocktail (1:500) (eBioscience, cat#00-4970-93) and Brefeldin A (Sigma Aldrich, cat#B7651).

Healthy donors’ peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Lymphoprep (Nycomed) gradient centrifugation as previously described.38 NK cells were negatively selected from PBMCs using the MACS human NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec).

Co-culture experiments and functional assays

AKPS organoids were seeded at a 1:200 ratio with NK cells isolated from the spleens of wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice. Organoids were embedded in 30 µL of Matrigel in pre-warmed 48-well plates and polymerized at 37°C for 20 min. An empty Matrigel dome (30 µL) was used as a control. NK cells were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with murine IL-15 (10 ng/mL) and β-mercaptoethanol (20 µM). CRC patient-derived organoids (PDOs) were seeded with purified NK cells at a 1:200 ratio in pre-warmed 96-well plates.

For blocking experiments, 1 µg of anti-IL-27p28 (anti-mouse IL-27 BD Biosciences, cat# 568409, clone MM27.7B1 or anti-human IL-27 p28 (R&D System, cat# AF2526)) antibody or of isotype control antibody was added to co-cultures and refreshed after 24 hours. Co-cultures were maintained for 24 and 48 hours for flow cytometric analysis and RNA extraction, respectively.

For killing assays, NK cells were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled target cells (MC38 for murine NK cells and K562 for human NK cells) at effector-to-target ratios of 40:1 and 80:1 for 6 hours at 37°C, with 500 target cells per condition. After incubation, cells were stained with 7-AAD for 20 min on ice and analyzed by flow cytometry. CFSE-labeled target cells cultured alone were used to assess spontaneous death. Specific cytotoxicity was calculated as: ((% cell death with NK cells) − (% spontaneous cell death)) / (100 − (% spontaneous cell death)).

For bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) see online supplemental material.

Adoptive transfer experiments

For adoptive transfer experiments, cells were isolated from the spleen or BM of C57BL/6J CD45.1 mice. Splenic NK cells were purified by magnetic separation, using the NK Cell Isolation Kit, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec, cat# 130–115-818). CD27+CD11b+ NK cells were isolated by sorting total BM cells derived from C57BL/6J CD45.1 mice as Live Dead-CD45+CD3−CD19−NKp46+CD49b+CD27+CD11b+ cells. A total of 1×106 purified splenic NK cells and 1×105 BM sorted CD27+ NK cells were intravenously transferred into C57BL/6J CD45.2 recipient mice at day+3 from tumor organoid injection. At day+6, mice were sacrificed and large intestines as well as tumors were collected and digested as described above. Flow cytometry was performed to determine the percentage of CD45.1+ cells in the tumors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends. Unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance were used as appropriate and analyses were performed using Prism V.9.0 software (GraphPad). Data are shown as mean±SD, and p values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

For scRNA-seq analyses, statistical significance was assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. IL-27 expression and overall survival were analyzed by Kaplan-Meier curves using the R2 Genomics platform and publicly available datasets (GSE17538 and Nunes-1063-tpm-ensh38e98). Survival differences were evaluated by log-rank test, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Single-cell RNA sequencing captures NK cell heterogeneity among innate lymphocytes infiltrating AKPS tumors

To investigate the role of NK cells in immune response during the early stages of CRC growth, we used an orthotopic transplantation model of tumor intestinal organoids. These organoids harbor the most common mutations found in sporadic CRC,39 40 including loss-of-function mutations in the Apc, Trp53, and Smad4 genes, as well as the oncogenic KrasG12D mutation, hereafter referred to as AKPS.37 This mouse model of CRC generated adenocarcinomas within a week (online supplemental figure S1A),36 showed infiltration of NKp46+ cells within the tumor mass, as established by immunofluorescence staining of AKPS tumor sections (online supplemental figure S1B) and it was sensitive to depletion of NK1.1 expressing cells. Indeed, anti-NK1.1 administration resulted in an increased tumor burden (figure 1A,B), indicating that NK cells, ILC1 and/or NKT cells may play a role in controlling AKPS tumors.

To characterize CRC-infiltrating NK cells, we FACS-sorted total CD45+ NK1.1+ NKp46+ cells from AKPS tumors and performed scRNA-seq analysis (online supplemental figure S1C and figure 1C). By employing this sorting strategy, we also included cells belonging to the ILC1 fate in the analysis. Unbiased clustering of 3,227 cells revealed nine distinct clusters, including ILC1 and eight NK cell clusters (NKa-h). Each cell cluster was annotated according to the expression of identity genes and functional molecules (figure 1D, online supplemental figure S2 and online supplemental table S2). Bona fide ILC1 were distinguished from NK cells by the expression of Il7r (CD127), Tcf7 (TCF1), Itga1 (CD49a), Cxcr6 and Tmem179a and the absence of Eomes expression. Unlike ILC1, NK cell clusters express Eomes and Itga2, along with the absence of Cd3e and Il7r. Differential abundance analysis showed that NK cells were more abundant than ILC1, with each NK cell cluster represented in similar proportions except for the cycling NKh cluster (figure 1E).

Based on these findings, we focused on NK cells and analyzed their transcriptional profile to gain insight into their function and phenotype. First, we observed that Itgam (encoding CD11b, a marker associated with mature NK cells) was present in all NK cell clusters with its expression being higher in NKa, b and d clusters (figure 1F, upper panel). These clusters also expressed Klrg1, with the highest levels found in the NKd cluster. These findings suggest that these clusters comprise fully mature and more differentiated NK cells (CD11b+KLRG1+). Differently, Cd27 transcript was detected only in NKc and e clusters, which may correspond to cells with a more immature phenotype (CD27+CD11b+). Finally, the remaining cluster, NKf, which expresses only Itgam, resembles mature CD27-KLRG1-CD11b+ NK cells.

Together, these results indicate that AKPS tumors are populated by NK cells which showed distinct NK cell maturation states.

To correlate these profiles with possible differences in NK cell functional activity, we examined the expression of gene encoding for activating/inhibitory receptors, cytotoxic molecules, cytokines/chemokines, chemokine receptors and adhesion molecules (figure 1G). Notably, we revealed that, as compared with the NKd cluster, the other clusters, particularly NKc, e and f, expressed reduced levels of some activating receptors, such as Klrk1 (NKG2D), Cd226 (DNAM1), Cd244a (2B4) and Ncr1 (NKp46), together with increased levels of inhibitory receptors, including Ctla4, Tigit and Ly6e. Accordingly, these three NK clusters displayed also lower levels of transcripts encoding for cytotoxic molecules, such as granzyme B (Gzmb) and Prf1 (perforin), as well as for cytokines or chemokines (Ifng, Ccl3, Ccl4). Again, NKc, e and f clusters differ from the NKd cluster for homing molecules, being characterized by elevated expression of Cxcr3, Cxcr4, Ccr2 and Ccr5, while lacking Cx3cr1 and S1pr5, both highly present in the NKd cluster.

Together, NKc, e and f were endowed with transcriptional profiles resembling impaired effector functions.

Collectively, scRNA profiling of NK cells infiltrating AKPS tumors highlights their pronounced heterogeneity, with more immature NK cell populations displaying reduced functional capacity.

NK cells dominate the innate lymphoid landscape of AKPS tumors yet exhibit impaired effector functions

To validate the transcriptional data, we profiled innate lymphoid populations in tumors and tumor-free large intestinal lamina propria (liLP) at 1 week after orthotopic injection of AKPS organoids using immunofluorescence and flow cytometry.

As shown in online supplemental figure S3 and figure 2A,B, FACS analysis revealed a significant increase in the frequency of total innate lymphocytes (gated as L/D−CD3ε⁻CD19⁻CD45+NK1.1+ and/or CD127+ cells) among CD45+ cells infiltrating liLP and AKPS CRC. ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3, which populated liLP, were almost absent from AKPS tumors. In contrast, NK cells (defined as RORγt−NK1.1+CD49b+ILCs) were scarce in liLP but were abundant in AKPS tumors. Moreover, NK-CD49a+ cells (RORγt−NK1.1+CD49b+CD49a+ILCs) were found exclusively in tumors. Consistently, this subset is known to arise from NK cells in various pathological conditions, including cancer, where transforming growth factor-β is a key driver of their differentiation.15 25

Taken together, these observations confirm that AKPS-infiltrating innate lymphocytes mainly consisted of NK cells.

Next, we evaluated tumor infiltrating NK cell functionality. To this aim, NK cells were FACS-sorted from AKPS tumors and spleen, used as a control, and were co-cultured with tumor target cell lines (figure 2C–E). We found that intratumoral NK cells had a significantly reduced ability to degranulate in response to incubation with YAC-1 cells as well as a reduced cytotoxicity against MC38 cells as compared with splenic NK cells. Moreover, FACS analysis of cytotoxic molecules highlighted that the expression of granzyme A (GZMA), but not GZMB and PRF1, was significantly reduced in tumor-infiltrating NK cells (figure 2F).

In agreement with transcriptomic profiles, these data demonstrate that AKPS tumor infiltrating-NK cells are hypofunctional.

The tumor microenvironment favors the persistence of immature NK cell subsets

Based on our transcriptomic data showing distinct NK cell maturation states, we analyzed tumor-infiltrating NK cells according to CD27 and CD11b expression. Notably, we found that, compared with NK cells from liLP, intratumoral NK cells were significantly enriched in less mature CD27+CD11b+ subset, at the expense of fully mature CD27⁻CD11b+KLRG1+NK cells (figure 3A,B).

This pattern suggests that NK cell maturation is hindered within the TME, leading to the accumulation of less differentiated and functionally restrained NK cell subsets.

To better investigate the relationship between maturation state and tissue residency of intratumoral NK cells, we performed in vivo labeling of blood-exposed cells by injecting tumor-bearing mice with an anti-CD45-PE antibody for 5 min before sacrifice and analyzed CD27 and CD11b expression in NK cells. We found that within AKPS tumors circulating/vascular NK cells, gated among CD45-PE+ leukocytes, showed a more mature phenotype with a higher proportion of the CD27−CD11b+ NK cell subset as compared with tumor resident NK cells (figure 3C,D).

These observations highlight a role for the TME in favoring the access and persistence of the CD27+ NK cell subset.

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the accumulation of the less mature NK cell subsets in AKPS tumors, we performed adoptive transfer experiments (figure 3E; online supplemental figure S4A,B). FACS-sorted CD27+ NK cells (including CD27+CD11b⁻ and CD27+CD11b+ subsets) from the BM and mature CD11b+ NK cells (including CD27+CD11b+ and CD27⁻CD11b+ subsets) from the spleen of CD45.1+ donor mice were transferred into AKPS tumor-bearing CD45.2+ recipient mice. 6 days later, mice were sacrificed and the phenotypes of CD45.1 donor cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. We observed that immature BM NK cells infiltrated AKPS tumors while largely retaining their original CD27/CD11b expression profile. AKPS adenocarcinomas were also infiltrated by CD45.1+ mature splenic NK cells; however, these cells showed a reduced frequency of the more mature CD27⁻CD11b+ subset (figure 3E,F).

Taken together, our data demonstrate that immature NK cells can infiltrate CRC, but within the TME they fail to acquire a more mature phenotype or do not survive on differentiation. Thus, the loss of mature NK cells may result either from reduced survival or fitness within the tumor milieu, or from the preferential recruitment of more immature NK cell subsets.

Inflammation overrides tissue-specific cues in shaping NK cell phenotype in CRC

The colonic microenvironment comprises multiple factors that influence immune cell infiltration and composition, including diet, pathogens, microbiota-derived metabolites, immune cells, and enterocyte-derived signals. To evaluate the impact of the colonic microenvironment on tumor development and innate immune responses, we performed parallel orthotopic and subcutaneous transplantations of AKPS organoids (online supplemental figure S5A). Within 1 week, the organoids developed palpable tumors that were harvested for immune profiling. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that, as observed in orthotopic tumors, the ILC compartment in subcutaneous AKPS implants was largely dominated by NK cells with a similar pattern of maturation markers (online supplemental figure S5A–D).

These findings suggest that the colonic microenvironment is not the primary determinant of ILC distribution and phenotype within tumors derived from AKPS organoids.

The AOM/DSS mouse model is a widely used and valuable tool for studying colitis-associated CRC, with tumor development typically occurring around 10 weeks after treatment.41 We employed this model to investigate how chronic inflammation influences the composition of ILC populations within intestinal tumors (online supplemental figure S5E).

Our analysis revealed that, as observed in AKPS tumors, NK cells represented the predominant ILC population infiltrating AOM/DSS adenomas. However, a comparative phenotypic analysis of NK cells infiltrating AKPS and AOM/DSS tumors revealed differences. Compared with NK cells from AKPS tumors, those from AOM/DSS polyps displayed reduced expression of EOMES and KLRG1, resembling more tissue-resident NK cells42 (online supplemental figure S5F–H).

Taken together, these findings suggest that while the overall composition of ILC populations infiltrating CRC, particularly the predominance of NK cells, is maintained across different tumor settings, chronic inflammatory conditions can modulate the phenotypic characteristics of tumor-infiltrating NK cells.

Tumor-derived IL-27 shapes NK cell transcriptional and functional programs

The TME shapes NK-cell transcriptional programs through immunomodulatory cytokines, lipid mediators, and metabolic constraints. To directly investigate the effect of tumor-derived factors on NK-cell function, we established a reductionist model based on an in vitro co-culture system in which WT splenic NK cells were cultured for 24 hours either with AKPS organoids (AKPS) or with Matrigel domes alone used as a control and processed for bulk RNA-seq (figure 4A). Through this analysis, we identified 615 differentially expressed genes regulated by tumor-derived factors, with 66 upregulated and 86 downregulated in AKPS-conditioned NK cells compared with controls (online supplemental figure S6A,B).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis revealed enrichment of different cytokine-signaling pathways (online supplemental table S3), with IL-27, a cytokine able to regulate the activity of multiple immune cell types, including NK cells, showing one of the highest normalized enrichment scores (figure 4B,C). Consistent with this, our transcriptomic data from scRNA-seq revealed an enrichment of an IL-27 signature27 in NK cells infiltrating AKPS tumors in vivo (figure 4D and online supplemental table S4). Moreover, ELISA demonstrated that both AKPS organoids in vitro and adenocarcinomas derived from implanted organoids in vivo secrete IL-27 (figure 4E). Consistently, histological and immunofluorescence analyses of tumor sections confirmed IL-27 expression in Ki67-positive tumor cells within the tumor bed (figure 4F), supporting the notion that tumor cells represent a relevant source of IL-27 within the TME. As well, flow cytometric analysis showed IL-27Rα expression on intestinal and IL-15 cultured splenic NK cells but not on BM and splenic NK cells (figure 4G–I). Specifically, IL-27Rα expression was detected across all NK populations infiltrating the tumor, whereas the more immature NK subsets (CD27+CD11b− and CD27-CD11b+) expressed comparatively higher levels of the receptor. These results suggest that IL-27 exerts immunoregulatory activity on tumor-infiltrating NK cells, with less mature NK cell subsets being more responsive to IL-27 signaling.

To evaluate the functional relevance of tumor-derived IL-27, splenic NK cells were cultured for 48 hours with either AKPS organoids or Matrigel domes in the presence of IL-15 (5 ng/mL), together with an anti-IL-27 neutralizing antibody or an isotype control. NK-cell effector functions were subsequently assessed. As shown in figure 4K–L, IL-27 blockade abolished the enhanced degranulation and IFN-γ production induced by exposure to tumor organoids following co-culture with YAC-1 target cells, and also reduced NK-cell cytotoxicity against MC38 CRC cells. These findings demonstrate a key role for tumor-derived IL-27 in promoting NK-cell functional activity.

IL-27 signaling sustains NK cell activity in the colorectal tumor microenvironment

To determine the impact of IL-27 signaling on NK cell function in vivo, mice bearing orthotopic AKPS tumors received intraperitoneal injections of an anti-IL-27 antibody every 2 days, starting on the day of organoid transplantation (figure 5A). In line with the antitumoral role of IL-27, we observed that blockade of this cytokine led to a significant increase of tumor volume (figure 5B). Although anti-IL-27 treatment did not alter NK-cell amount in the BM or spleen (figure 5C), it markedly reduced both the frequency and absolute number of NK cells within AKPS tumors and profoundly altered their phenotype, leading to a decreased proportion of CD27+CD11b- and CD27+CD11b+ subsets (figure 5D–G). These effects were specific to tumor-associated NK cells, as neither the frequency nor the phenotype of NK cells residing in the liLP was affected. Functionally, NK cells from anti-IL-27-treated mice displayed a further impairment in IFN-γ production compared with those from untreated AKPS tumors, that were already hypofunctional with respect to splenic NK cells used as a control (figure 5H,I). In contrast, blockade of IL-27 did not affect the cytotoxic capacity of AKPS tumor infiltrating NK cells (figure 5J).

To better investigate the role of tumor-derived IL-27 in these mechanisms, we silenced this cytokine in AKPS organoids by lentiviral infection (figure 5K). We found that IL-27-depleted AKPS organoids generated larger tumors compared with controls (figure 5L). Moreover, the analysis of tumor-infiltrating NK cells revealed that the absence of tumor-derived IL-27 significantly reduced the frequency of NK cells and altered NK cell subset distribution, resulting in an increased abundance of CD27−CD11b+cells (figure 5M,N).

These findings further corroborate the contribution of adenocarcinoma-derived IL-27 to the regulation of the antitumor immune response.

Transcriptional evidence of IL-27-mediated activation in NK cells from patients with CRC

Having established that IL-27 is a key driver of NK cell-mediated immune surveillance in murine CRC, we next investigated whether this cytokine similarly regulates human NK cell responses in human CRC. Interrogation of public CRC datasets revealed that higher IL-27 levels correlate with longer overall survival (figure 6A). Building on these findings, we performed an independent reanalysis of a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of innate lymphoid cells isolated from CRC tumors, matched adjacent non-tumor mucosa, and peritoneal metastases (PM) from 11 patients (GSE302045).43 This analysis resolved eight distinct clusters, including two immature ILC populations, early NK and naïve (n) ILC, ILC3, ILC2, ILC1, and three NK cell subsets (CD16+ NKA, CD16+ NKB, and HLA-DR+ NK) (figure 6B). Consistent with our observations in mice, NK cells were scarce in healthy intestinal mucosa, where ILC3s predominantly patrolled the tissue under homeostatic conditions. In contrast, CRC lesions showed a marked enrichment of NK cells,43 predominantly represented by the HLA-DR+ NK cell cluster (figure 6C).

We then evaluated IL-27 pathway activity across ILC and NK cell subsets by scoring an IL-27 gene signature in different tissue contexts (figure 6D,E and online supplemental table S5). Overall, the IL-27 signature was detectable across all conditions, with higher levels in primary CRC compared with colon and a decrease in PM (figure 6D), in a cell type-dependent manner. Naïve ILCs, ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3 populations were largely stable between healthy mucosa and primary tumor but were strongly affected in the metastatic compartment. In contrast, early NK cells and other NK subsets exhibited clear modulation of IL-27 scores across disease stages, consistent with progressive remodeling from healthy tissue to primary tumor and metastases. Notably, HLA-DR+NK cells showed the most pronounced changes in IL-27 signature activity, with increased scores in CRC and reduced levels in PM, consistent with a highly activated or reprogrammed state in metastatic disease (figure 6E). This subset represents the most abundant NK population within the TME,43 suggesting a central role in shaping antitumor immune responses in CRC.

To functionally validate these findings, we established co-culture systems in which NK cells isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood were cultured around Matrigel domes containing tumor organoids derived from patients with stage IV CRC biopsies (PDO). Under these conditions, NK cell cytotoxicity was significantly enhanced, as assessed by a 7-AAD-based killing assay against K562 target cells. This effect was significantly attenuated in the presence of a blocking anti-IL-27 antibody (figure 6F). Consistently, IL-27 production by PDO was confirmed by both western blot analysis and immunofluorescence microscopy (figure 6G,H), indicating that tumor represents a relevant source of this cytokine within the TME.

Discussion

Here, we dissected the contribution of NK cells to antitumor immunity in CRC using a rapid orthotopic AKPS organoid model that enables early analysis of innate immune responses. Consistent with previous reports of tumor-induced NK cell dysfunction in solid cancers,13–16 transcriptomic and phenotypic profiling revealed a heterogeneous NK-cell infiltrate dominated by immature and functionally restrained subsets. The accumulation of CD27+CD11b+ NK subsets at the expense of KLRG1+ NK cells suggests that the CRC microenvironment prevents the maintenance/differentiation of NK cells at the terminal steps of differentiation. These findings are further supported by adoptive transfer experiments of CD27− and CD27+ NK cell subsets, suggesting that local cues can either inhibit maturation or limit recruitment of more mature subsets.

Comparative analysis across orthotopic, subcutaneous, and inflammation-driven (AOM/DSS) CRC models revealed that, despite the distinct biological contexts, NK cells consistently represent the predominant innate lymphoid population. Notably, their core phenotypic features remain largely conserved across models. However, in the inflammation-driven setting, chronic inflammatory cues impose an additional layer of reprogramming, markedly altering NK-cell phenotype. These observations indicate that inflammatory signals, rather than tissue-specific cues, can shape NK-cell identity and function in CRC, reinforcing the concept that inflammation can redirect innate lymphocytes toward tissue-resident-like states.

Among tumor-derived signals, IL-27 emerged as a critical regulator of NK-cell activation. While previous studies linked IL-27 signaling to colitis-associated cancer and CD8+ T-cell persistence,27 44 our data identify NK cells as additional key targets of IL-27 in CRC.

Several reports have implicated IL-27 as a regulator of NK cell activity in viral infection and cancer. During influenza virus infection, myeloid cell-derived IL-27 promoted early-phase NK cell effector functions enhancing NKG2D-mediated responses,32 with effects linked to NK cell maturation. Indeed, IL-27Ra-deficient mice displayed an enrichment of CD27−CD11b+ NK cells at the expense of CD27+CD11b+ subsets, the main IL-27 responsive population. Conversely, in methylcholanthrene-induced fibrosarcoma and melanoma models, dendritic cell-derived IL-27 regulated CXCL10 production, promoting NK cell recruitment to tumors; accordingly, IL-27p28flox/flox conditional knockout mice showed defective NK cell infiltration.31

Consistent with these observations, we found that in vivo IL-27 blockade reduced NK-cell frequency, altered NK cell subset distribution, and diminished IFN-γ production, underscoring its essential role in sustaining NK-cell effector potential within an otherwise suppressive TME. Although myeloid cells represent a major IL-27 source,27 45 injection of IL-27 silenced AKPS organoids was accompanied by changes in NK cell infiltrate resembled those observed following in vivo IL-27 blockade. Moreover, co-culture assays demonstrated that tumor cells can directly modulate NK cell activity through IL-27 production. Collectively, these findings broaden our understanding of IL-27 as a context-dependent cytokine capable of both promoting and restraining immune responses. Together with IL-15, IL-27 emerges as a promising target for restoring NK cell fitness and enhancing antitumor immunity in CRC. Concordant findings obtained in patients with CRC NK cells and in human tumor organoid-NK cell co-culture systems highlight the translational relevance of IL-27-mediated regulation of NK cell function.

Collectively, by integrating single-cell transcriptomics, functional assays, and in vivo models, we provide a comprehensive view of NK cell differentiation dynamics in CRC and identify IL-27 as a central mediator of their activity. These insights pave the way for therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring NK cell maturation and function, potentially enhancing immunotherapy efficacy in CRC.
