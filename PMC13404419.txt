Cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Denny John, Aparna Ramachandran, Rajul Parikh, Chandrashekar Janakiram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionGlaucoma is an increasing global health problem and the leading cause of irreversible blindness which affects millions of people worldwide. Multiple studies on the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening conducted at community, clinic or hospital in developed and developing countries have been conducted, but evidence from systematic reviews is lacking. Therefore, this systematic review aims to assess the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening among adult populations across various countries and healthcare settings.Methods and analysisA systematic search will be conducted in databases including PubMed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings such as glaucoma and cost-effectiveness analysis along with relevant keywords like screening. Following the predefined inclusion criteria, two reviewers will independently screen the titles, abstract and full text, and any disagreements will be discussed or resolved by a third reviewer. Methodological quality will be assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for economic evaluations. Data will be extracted using the JBI economic evaluation data extraction form with any suitable modifications. JBI Dominance Ranking Matrix for economic evaluations will be used to summarise and compare the results for data synthesis. All reported costs will be converted into a single currency and year prior to synthesis. Cost-effectiveness evidence will be synthesised on the basis of cost per case detected or blindness prevented, cost per quality-adjusted life year gained or cost per disability-adjusted life year averted. Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation assessment will be conducted to evaluate the certainty of economic evidence, such as use of resources and expenditures, and to incorporate the results into the decision-making process.Ethics and disseminationThis systematic review will analyse published peer-reviewed studies and targeted grey literature only and does not require ethics committee approval. Results will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and through conference presentations. Extracted data and supporting materials will be made available on reasonable request.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42017057560.Keywords: Glaucoma, Systematic Review, HEALTH ECONOMICS

Introduction

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases, categorised into two types, that is, closed angle glaucoma and open angle glaucoma (OAG), that induce optic nerve damage and lead to impaired vision and blindness if left untreated.1 Screening for glaucoma is essential to detect, diagnose and treat the patient at earlier stages.2 Population screening for OAG could be viewed as an intervention to minimise blindness by detecting people at earlier stages.3 In the later stages of glaucoma, when visual field impairment is severe in both eyes, health-related quality of life declines significantly for patients.4 Most countries in the world do not have community screening for glaucoma, unlike in the UK, where opportunistic case finding by community optometrists is used to identify or screen for glaucoma.5

In general, glaucoma is an age-related disease that affects older people at higher rates. In 2020, glaucoma was responsible for an estimated 3.61 million cases of blindness and approximately 4.14 million cases of visual impairment globally, accounting for 8.39 of all blindness.6 7 Previous studies estimated that glaucoma is one of the leading causes of blindness, and it is estimated that 111 million people will be affected by 2040.8 The prevalence of glaucoma-related blindness in Africa (15%–20% of affected people) is more than double that of the rest of the world (8%).9

Many studies have assessed the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening in developed countries such as Finland, the UK, and developing countries (China and India).10–14 Some of these studies have been included in previous reviews on the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening. Hernandez et al conducted a Health Technology Assessment review of the cost-effectiveness of screening for OAG.3 Review conducted by Einarson et al and Olawoye et al focused on cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening in Canada and Sub-Saharan Africa.15 16 These systematic reviews have either covered OAG alone,3 or a single country14 or region.15

At a 2008 consensus meeting of the World Glaucoma Association, it was agreed that decisions about implementing glaucoma screening programmes should be guided by the resources and costs available. The justification for screening therefore needs to consider economic feasibility in both developed and developing countries.17 Glaucoma is the third most common cause of blindness and is responsible for 10% of blindness worldwide.18 The majority of people affected by glaucoma live in developing countries and are unaware that they have the disease.19 Glaucoma screening programmes play an important role in raising awareness and in identifying glaucoma suspects and confirmed cases and may help reduce the problem of late presentation.20 Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) for glaucoma screening is based on the premise that it is the wider community interest that is paramount; therefore, additional eyes saved from blindness through the use of a screening programme must be balanced against the extra costs involved in doing so.12 Evidence from cost-effectiveness studies on glaucoma screening provides a rationale for determining interventions that provide the greatest health benefits for the available resources.21

A preliminary search on PubMed, The JBI Evidence Synthesis, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Epistemonikos, and PROSPERO databases identified no current or ongoing systematic review on the topic. The review will help to evaluate the currently available evidence on the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening in the global context across community, clinic or hospital settings. The findings will be crucial towards framing targeted policies to address the growing burden of glaucoma at the global level.

Review question

What is the available evidence of cost effectiveness of glaucoma screening in adults across various countries and settings?

Methods

The suggested systematic review will adhere to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology guidelines for systematic reviews of economic evaluations.22

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review will consider studies that have included individuals aged 18 years or over and of any sex or country. Studies exclusively addressing paediatric/congenital glaucoma will be excluded as they represent distinct clinical pathways.

Interventions

The present systematic review will consider studies evaluating the cost-effectiveness of glaucoma screening delivered by trained eye care professionals (ophthalmologists, optometrists, ophthalmic technicians, ophthalmic nurses) using: optic nerve photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), visual field testing, central corneal thickness and intraocular pressure assessment23 or artificial intelligence (AI)-based screening tools (eg, AI analysis of fundus images/OCT for glaucoma detection)24 Screening interventions conducted using a teleglaucoma screening device or AI-assisted algorithms will also be included.25

Comparators

Studies comparing glaucoma screening interventions against opportunistic case finding (eg, detection by community optometrists or routine eye care) no screening or other active screening strategies (eg, alternative screening test, frequencies or algorithms) will be included. Studies lacking any comparator (eg, single-arm cost descriptions) will be excluded.

Outcomes

This review will include studies reporting full economic evaluations of glaucoma screening that compare both costs and consequences between at least two alternatives (eg, glaucoma screening vs opportunistic case-finding or no screening). Economic outcomes of interest will include incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) such as:

Incremental cost per case of glaucoma detected or blindness averted.

Incremental cost per case of glaucoma detected or blindness averted.

Incremental cost per case of visual impairment or blindness averted.

Incremental cost per case of visual impairment or blindness averted.

Incremental cost per year of visual impairment averted.

Incremental cost per year of visual impairment averted.

Incremental cost per additional quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained.

Incremental cost per additional quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained.

Incremental cost per quality-adjusted year of vision (QAVY).

Incremental cost per quality-adjusted year of vision (QAVY).

Incremental cost per number of cases of glaucoma detected.

Incremental cost per number of cases of glaucoma detected.

Where reported, we will also extract net costs (or net savings) associated with glaucoma screening compared with opportunistic case-finding or no screening from any stated perspective (societal, healthcare payer, provider, patient/household), net monetary benefit (NMB) where sufficient information on incremental costs, incremental effects and relevant willingness-to-pay thresholds is available or can be derived, and the directly linked effectiveness outcome for each ICER or NMB (eg, cases detected, QALYs gained, blindness averted) to support interpretation. Studies reporting only total or average costs without comparative effectiveness data will be recorded but not treated as full economic evaluations.

Context

The review will include studies on adult patients, regardless of gender, with screening conducted in community, clinic or hospital settings. Ethnicity, income and gender will be considered in the context of targeted/selective screening (ie, targeting at-risk ethnic populations).

Types of studies

This review will include full economic evaluations of glaucoma screening that jointly assess both costs and outcomes (as specified in Outcomes), comprising CEA using intermediate or final health outcomes (eg, cases detected, blindness averted), cost-utility analyses (CUA) using summary measures such as QALYs, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) or QAVY, cost-benefit analyses (CBA) where both costs and outcomes are monetised and cost-minimisation analyses (CMA) where equivalence in effectiveness is appropriately justified. Eligible designs include economic evaluations conducted alongside experimental studies (randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies) or observational studies with comparator groups, as well as standalone model-based analyses using decision trees, Markov models, microsimulations or other synthesis methods. Studies may adopt any perspective (societal, healthcare payer, provider, patient/family) and will be included regardless of publication language, though we anticipate most will be in English. We will exclude cost-of-illness studies, cost-description/resource-use studies without comparative effectiveness data, studies reporting only total or unit costs without incremental analysis and economic evaluations of glaucoma treatment that exclude screening and diagnostic components. Studies in languages other than English will be translated using DeepL (DeepL SE, Cologne, Germany), with no further verification due to time and financial constraints.

Exclusion criteria

Articles providing overall costs or treatment costs without comparative evaluation of glaucoma screening will be excluded, as will reviews, editorials, commentaries, methodological studies and previously published systematic reviews (though their bibliographies will be searched for primary studies). Studies reporting cost-effectiveness of glaucoma treatment alone—excluding the screening and diagnostic components of the care pathway from initial screening through diagnosis and treatment referral—will be excluded. Cost-of-illness studies, cost-description or resource-use studies without comparative effectiveness data and studies lacking a comparator group will also be excluded.

Search strategy

A limited preliminary search of MEDLINE (PubMed) (online supplemental material 1 search strategy) will be conducted to find relevant primary studies on the topic. The content of relevant publications, titles, abstracts and keywords will be used to develop a comprehensive search strategy. This search strategy will then be peer-reviewed by the author with health economics expertise (DJ) using CADTH 2015 PRESS guidelines. The search strategy will then be modified and adapted for each included database, incorporating appropriate Medical Subject Headings like Glaucoma, Ocular Hypertension, Cost, Cost Effectiveness. The reference lists of all included studies will be checked for additional studies. The literature search will not have any lower or upper dates. All articles found until the end of the literature search will be screened for eligibility. There will be no restrictions on language. Studies in languages other than English will be translated using DeepL (DeepL SE, Cologne, Germany). Due to time and financial constraints, there will be no further verification of the translations.

Study selection

Following the search, all detected citations will be compiled and uploaded into software (Zotero software: V.6.0.10; Corporation for Digital Scholarship and Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media, Virginia, USA), and duplicates will be removed. A pilot will be undertaken for the first 3–5 studies obtained using our search strategy to assess the clarity and feasibility of our inclusion and exclusion criteria. Once a level of agreement of 80% and above is achieved, the full screening phase will be initiated. After that, the titles and abstracts will go through a screening process that will be conducted by two independent reviewers, who will evaluate them based on the exclusion and inclusion criteria of the review. Reference lists of all included studies will be manually screened (backward citation chasing). Authors of papers will be contacted to request missing or additional data, where required. A non-response will be followed up with an email after 2 weeks; a further 2 weeks of non-response will result in the paper being excluded. The reasons for the exclusion of full-text studies will be included as an appendix in the final systematic review. Any differences that develop between the reviewers during the screening process will be handled through discussion or with the help of a third reviewer) JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment, and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI; JBI, Adelaide, Australia) will be used for the entire screening exercise, and search findings will be presented as per Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses26 flow diagram.

Assessment of the methodological quality

Two reviewers will independently use the JBI checklist for economic assessments to evaluate the methodological quality of the studies that are selected for final inclusion.22 For economic evaluations conducted alongside primary studies, the methodological quality of the underlying clinical studies will also be evaluated using JBI critical appraisal checklists appropriate to their design (eg, RCTs, quasi-experimental studies) or Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) and Risk of Bias In Non Randomized Studies-I (ROBINS-I) tools for experimental and non-randomised studies, respectively.27 28 Disagreements between the reviewers will be solved through conversation or with the assistance of the third reviewer. The outcomes of the critical evaluation will be presented in narrative and tabular form. After screening titles and abstracts and removing duplicates from different databases, selected studies will undergo data extraction and evidence synthesis, irrespective of the quality of their methodology.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers will extract data using modified JBI data extraction forms from JBI SUMARI. The extraction tool used will be independently piloted by the review team before formal use (online supplemental material 2 data extraction instrument). Data extraction will cover: specific details about populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, economic perspective, costs, study design, screening methods/costs, cost-effectiveness method (decision tree, Markov etc), model structure (eg, transitions, utilities), time horizon, discount rate, source of effectiveness data, incremental costs by category (screening tests, diagnostics, treatment referrals, productivity losses) and perspective, ICER sensitivity (deterministic/probabilistic), Net monetary benefit (incremental net benefit=(incremental effects×1–3×GDP/capita Willingness To Pay (WTP) threshold)–incremental costs) and study context. Economic evaluation type will be specified as CEA (eg, cost per case/blindness averted), CUA (cost per QALY/DALY), CBA (monetised net benefits) or CMA (costs under proven effectiveness equivalence). All extracted costs/currencies/base-years converted to 2026 international US$ using Campbell and Cochrane Economics Methods Group and the Evidence for Policy and Practise implementation (CCEMG-EPPI) Converter (GDP-deflators+PPP rates from World Bank). As much as possible, costs will be stratified by socioeconomic status (eg, low/middle/high-income groups) and sex (male/female), where disaggregated in primary studies. They will be tabulated separately in synthesis if ≥3 studies per subgroup, otherwise noted as a limitation. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be solved through conversation or by consulting the third reviewer.

Data synthesis

Data from included studies will be synthesised using structured narrative synthesis supported by tables and figures. All reported costs will be standardised to 2026 international US$ using the CCEMG-EPPI Cost Converter, applying appropriate GDP deflators and purchasing power parity conversions. Where at least two studies report sufficiently homogeneous economic outcomes (population characteristics, screening intervention, comparator strategy, analytic perspective, outcome measure and time horizon) quantitative synthesis will be attempted using random-effects meta-analysis to pool incremental costs and health effects, deriving summary estimates of ICERs or NMB calculated at context-appropriate willingness-to-pay thresholds such as one to three times gross domestic product per capita.

Between-study heterogeneity will be assessed using forest plots, Cochran’s Q statistic (significance level p<0.10), and the I² statistic categorised as low (≤40%), moderate (40%–60%) or substantial (>60%). Where heterogeneity is high (I²>50%) or substantial methodological differences preclude pooling—such as trial-based versus model-based evaluations, short-term versus lifetime horizons, or disparate country income setting statistical synthesis will be avoided in Favour of narrative analysis stratified by key study characteristics, summarised following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis reporting guideline using structured tables, vote counting by direction of effect where appropriate, and summary statistics (medians, ranges) presented by subgroup.29 Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test only if ≥10 studies contribute comparable economic outcomes. Comprehensive strategies, including multi-database searching, citation chasing and author contact, will minimise bias risk.

Subgroup analyses will be conducted where sufficient data are available to explore heterogeneity and effect modification across: (1) country income classification per World Bank criteria (high-income vs upper-middle-income vs lower-middle-income countries); (2) screening delivery strategy (community-based screening vs opportunistic case-finding vs hospital-based or clinic-based screening); (3) screening test algorithms (single-stage vs multi-stage diagnostic pathways); (4) analytic perspective (societal vs healthcare payer vs provider) and (5) time horizon (short-term screening programmes vs lifetime horizon models). Where statistical pooling remains feasible within subgroups, random-effects meta-analysis will be performed and results compared across strata. Results will be tabulated by economic evaluation type (CUA/CEA/CBA/CMA) to facilitate comparison across methodological approaches.

Sensitivity analyses will evaluate the robustness of pooled findings by: (1) excluding studies rated at high risk of bias based on JBI economic evaluation checklist scores below pre-specified thresholds; (2) restricting analyses to studies adopting lifetime time horizons or societal perspectives and (3) varying key cost standardisation parameters such as intervention costs by ±20%, alternative price years, or different purchasing power parity conversions and (4) excluding non-peer-reviewed publications (grey literature) to assess publication bias impact and methodological robustness. These analyses will assess how assumptions regarding study quality, influential observations and cost standardisation affect the direction and magnitude of pooled cost-effectiveness estimates.

For studies reporting complete economic evaluations, the JBI Dominance Ranking Matrix will categorise cost-effectiveness findings as strong dominance (both more effective and less costly), weak dominance (favourable on either cost or effectiveness dimension), or non-dominance (less effective and/or more costly than comparators). All quantitative analyses will employ R V.4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Risk of bias assessments from both underlying clinical studies (Cochrane RoB 2.0 or ROBINS-I) and economic evaluation methods (JBI checklist) will be integrated into synthesis, Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty ratings and interpretation following CCEMG-EPPI-Centre guidelines.

Assessing certainty in the findings

The GRADE30 approach will be used to assess certainty of evidence for key economic outcomes including ICERs per case of glaucoma detected, blindness averted or QALY gained and NMB at relevant willingness-to-pay thresholds. Separate GRADE Summary of Findings tables will be developed: one for clinical effectiveness outcomes linked to economic evaluations (eg, cases detected, visual impairment prevented) and one specifically for economic evidence presenting study numbers and participants, representative ICERs/NMB estimates, resource utilisation considerations (screening programme costs, diagnostic follow-up, treatment referral pathways, productivity losses from blindness), certainty ratings (high/moderate/low/very low) and explanatory footnotes.

For economic evidence, certainty ratings will consider: (1) risk of bias from JBI economic evaluation checklists and underlying clinical study quality (Cochrane RoB 2.0/ROBINS-I); (2) inconsistency across ICER estimates and dominance rankings; (3) indirectness regarding country income settings, screening strategies and healthcare system transferability; (4) imprecision in cost-effectiveness CIs and (5) publication bias risk. Two independent reviewers will conduct GRADE assessments for both clinical effectiveness and economic evidence components, resolving disagreements through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review will analyse published peer-reviewed studies and targeted grey literature only and does not require ethics committee approval. Results will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and through conference presentations. Extracted data and supporting materials will be made available on reasonable request

Study status and dates

Protocol development was completed in February–March 2026. A preliminary MEDLINE search was conducted on 3 March 2026 to refine the search strategy; this pilot search will be updated and re-run as part of the formal searches. The full electronic database searches are planned for June–July 2026, with title/abstract and full-text screening and data extraction scheduled for July–September 2026 and data synthesis and manuscript drafting from October to December 2026. At the time of submission to BMJ Open, no formal study selection, data extraction or data analysis will have been undertaken.
