Flower Delivery: The Emergence of FLOWER (FWE) as a Multifunctional Regulator of Endo‐Lysosome‐Related Organelle Trafficking Across Cell Types
Authors: Justin C Rudd, Laura A Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn humans, the FLOWER (FWE or CACFD1) gene encodes a set of small, alternatively spliced transmembrane protein isoforms (hFWE1‐4). The canonical human isoform, hFWE4, and its various orthologues, which we collectively refer to as FWE for simplicity, assumes a four transmembrane pass structure with cytosolic N‐ and C‐termini. This topology exposes YXXΦ motifs enabling interaction with trafficking machinery and positions highly‐conserved cationic residues for interaction with membrane phospholipids. While identified initially as a mediator of synaptic vesicle endocytosis in presynaptic neurons, FWE is now recognized as a critical mediator of endo‐lysosome related organelle (ELRO) trafficking across diverse cell types including cytotoxic T‐lymphocytes and epidermal keratinocytes. A unifying feature of FWE function appears to be elevation of cytosolic Ca2+ levels. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain elusive. This review synthesizes current literature on FWE‐mediated membrane trafficking, highlighting the evolutionary conservation of its coding sequence and tertiary structure. We propose new structure‐compatible hypotheses for FWE‐dependent cytosolic Ca2+ elevation and subsequent ELRO trafficking, highlighting experimental strategies that may aid testing of these hypotheses. Finally, we examine the distinct localizations and potential functions of non‐canonical FWE isoforms.

Introduction

Endo‐lysosome related organelles (ELROs) are a heterogeneous class of membrane bound organelle that acquire cargo and structural components from endosomes, lysosomes and the trans‐Golgi network (TGN) [1]. The unique composition of these vesicles enables a remarkably broad set of biological functions. These include, among others, synaptic transmission (presynaptic vesicles), immune‐mediated cytotoxicity (lytic granules), and epidermal barrier function (epidermal lamellar bodies) [1, 2]. While the biogenesis and maturation process producing functional ELROs varies significantly across cell types, studies of syndromic genetic disorders affecting this class of vesicle has revealed core molecular machinery that regulates distinct phases of the ELRO life cycle [2]. These include heterotetrametric AP‐1 and AP‐3 adaptor complexes, which sort integral membrane cargo by binding to cytosolic motifs [3]. Also included are multi‐subunit BLOC‐1, ‐2, and ‐3 [4, 5, 6, 7], complexes, which direct endosomal tubulation and contact with maturing ELROs. Finally, there are the HOPS and CORVET complexes [8] which facilitate membrane tethering to allow SNARE‐mediated membrane fusion. For an overview of these established mechanisms, we direct readers to the exceptional review from Bowman and colleagues [2].

It is well appreciated that the membrane fusion events and cargo transport which occur across the endolysosomal system are largely Ca2+ dependent. Similarly, a large body of literature suggests that secretion of many ELROs, including Weibel–Palade bodies [9, 10, 11, 12], multivesicular endosomes [13, 14], and epidermal lamellar bodies [15, 16] often occurs in response to cytosolic Ca2+ alterations. However, the mechanisms that might enable ELROs to alter the local Ca2+ environment remain unclear. One possibility is that, as is true for trafficking machinery, ELROs across cell types share a common method for altering the local Ca2+ microenvironment that can be translated into trafficking and fusion events required for ELRO function. Here, synthesizing evidence from the last 15 years, we propose that the small multi‐pass transmembrane FWE protein alters Ca2+ handling in a manner that is critical for the biogenesis, secretion, and endocytosis of several distinct ELROs. To facilitate evaluation of functional data across metazoan model organisms, we first provide an overview of the genomic and tertiary protein structure for FWE across animals in which it has been studied. We then discuss the molecular function of FWE as a Ca2+‐dependent regulator of ELRO biogenesis, secretion and endocytosis, highlighting both the gaps in our mechanistic understanding of this process and critical experiments needed to move the field forward.

The Canonical FWE Transcript Variant is Highly Conserved Among Animals

To evaluate the ELRO‐related function of FWE proteins, it is critical to consider variations in FWE gene and transcript structure across the model organisms in which it has been studied (Figure 1). In C. elegans, flwr‐1 (WBGene00008973) is located on chromosome X and is predicted to encode only a single transcript composed of four exons that gives rise to a 166aa protein isoform. In Drosophila, fwe (FBgn0261722) is encoded on chromosome 3L and produces three transcript variants that are referred to by various names in the literature: RA/fweubi (FBtr0075542), RB/fweLoseA (FBtr0075541), and RC/fweLoseB (FBtr0075443) (Figure 1a, b). For clarity, we will use fweRA/RB/RC nomenclature for these transcripts, and FwePA/PB/PC for the protein isoforms that they encode. fweRA encodes a 194aa protein isoform, while fweRB and fweRC encode isoforms of 192 and 157aa, respectively. The 5’ UTR and coding sequences (CDS) for amino acids 1–147 in the first four exons are shared across transcript variants, but alternative splicing facilitates inclusion of a variable fifth exon at the 3’ end of the mRNA. As a result of this splicing, fweRB and fweRC share a 3’UTR unique from that contained in fweRA. The unique C‐termini of these splice variants has allowed for convincing validation of endogenous expression at transcript level using C‐terminally directed in situ hybridization [17].

In mouse, the mFwe locus (also Cacfd1 and 5930434B04Rik) is located on chromosome 2 and generates six protein coding transcript variants, producing five distinct protein isoforms known as mFwe1‐5 (Figure 1c). As of CCDS release 23, only three of these transcripts (mFwe1‐3) exhibit high quality, consistent annotation across the Ensembl and NCBI annotation pipelines. The amino acid sequence of the canonical isoform, mFwe2, is encoded by two transcript variants (ENSMUST00000114006 and ENSMUSTO0000114007) that differ only in 3’UTR. The exonic structure of mFwe transcript variants displays a more complex pattern of alternative splicing than that observed in Drosophila as evidenced in (Figure 1b). Notably, mFwe4 utilizes an alternative transcription start site, and mFwe5 exhibits inclusion of a second exon that is not shared by any other variant. This unique transcript structure has allowed for detection of specific transcript variants using qPCR [18].

In humans, the hFWE locus (also CACFD1 and C9orf7) is located on chromosome 9 and generates five predicted protein coding transcript variants. However, only four (hFWE1‐4) are consensus CDS as of CCDS release 24. Expression of hFWE is detected across all human tissues with minimal specificity [19]. One recent report has suggested that expression is modulated by STAT3 binding within the promoter region [20]. However, little else is known about regulation of hFWE expression. Control of alternative splicing at the hFWE locus is also poorly understood. hFWE1‐4 share exons 1, 2, and 4, while exons 3 and 5 are alternatively spliced into select transcripts (Figure 1d). A recent report suggests that recruitment of the splicing factor SRSF3 to exon 3 can lead to its exclusion [20]. However, control over splicing of exon 5 remains undefined. Interestingly, inclusion of exon 5 results in a +1 bp frameshift in the exon 6 open reading frame, generating a unique C‐terminal amino acid sequence in hFWE1 and hFWE2.

While the CDS of the canonical transcript variant is highly conserved across animals (Figure 1e), non‐canonical transcript variants produced from the mouse and human FWE locus deviate significantly from the canonical transcript structure. This is not the case in Drosophila where all isoforms share a common initial CDS encoding the first 147aa. Analysis of publicly available short‐ and long‐read RNA‐seq data suggests that human transcripts encoding non‐canonical protein isoforms are expressed at significantly lower levels relative to the canonical variant. Long‐read sequencing indicates that hFWE4 accounts for 77% of all transcripts across tissues from the GTEx repository, while hFWE3 and hFWE2 account for 19.5% and 3.5%, respectively, while hFWE1 is not detected [19]. Short‐read sequencing shows negligible usage of exon 5 which characterizes hFWE1 and hFWE2 [19]. Notably, most non‐canonical transcript variants in mouse and human lack internal sequence that encode portions of a highly conserved structural domain found in their canonical counterparts. This core structural domain positions N‐ and C‐termini of canonical isoforms within the cytosol to allow for protein–protein and protein–phospholipid interactions and may also facilitate direct Ca2+ channeling – functions that enable FWE to mediate diverse ELRO trafficking events.

The Canonical FWE Isoform Forms a Four TM Structure With Highly Conserved Functional Motifs in the N‐terminus

The ELRO‐related function of FWE is highly dependent on the structure of the canonical isoform. While N‐ and C‐terminal structures vary slightly across animals (Figure 2), all canonical FWE isoforms, and all three Drosophila isoforms, harbor a highly conserved four alpha helical domain termed Cg6151‐P (Figure 2). This domain is also found in a putative fungal orthologue of FWE, TVP‐18, a Golgi‐resident protein that is purported to regulate membrane trafficking [21, 22, 23]. The presence of Cg6151‐P containing proteins in the endomembrane network of distant eukaryotes may indicate that this is a conserved site of action. Early hydrophobicity modeling in Drosophila by two separate groups suggested that the Cg6151‐P domain in FWE produces either a three or four‐transmembrane (TM) structure [17, 24]. The four TM structure was predicted to position the N‐terminus, C‐terminus and a helical loop motif between TM2 and TM3 of FWE in the cytosol [24], while the three TM structure positions the C‐terminus in a vesicle lumen or in the extracellular space [17].

Several independent groups have since provided experimental evidence supporting the cytosolic positioning of N‐ and C‐termini and have identified several other functional motifs in these regions conserved across animals. Perhaps most critical are FWE's several phosphatidylinositol‐ 4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2) interacting residues in the N‐ and C‐termini [25]. Among other functions, PIP2 can directly activate ion channels [26] and membrane trafficking machinery [27], as well as liberate Ca2+ stores through generation of metabolites like inositol 1,4,5‐triphosphate (IP3) [28]. In Drosophila, experimental evidence suggests that cationic residues in both the N‐ (K29/R33) and C‐terminus (K146/K147) of FwePB (Figure 2) interact with PIP2 at the cytosolic face of the plasma membrane [25]. Recent in silico modeling of FLWR‐1, the canonical isoform in C. elegans, similarly predicted a four‐pass TM structure that positioned K27/K31 at the cytosolic plasma membrane leaflet to facilitate PIP2 interaction [29]. Across model organisms, AlphaFold models demonstrate a high degree of conservation in the positioning of these cationic residue pairs at both N‐ and C‐termini (Figure 2).

Work that further validated the cytosolic positioning of both N‐ and C‐terminus in mouse mFwe2, also identified the presence of YXXΦ (where Φ represents a bulky hydrophobic residue) motifs within these cytosolically exposed regions. In many transmembrane proteins, cytosolically exposed YXXΦ motifs are recognized by the medium subunits of AP‐1 and AP‐2 adaptor complexes [30]. Subsequent binding of clathrin by the beta subunit in AP‐1 or AP‐2 complexes facilitates formation of a clathrin scaffold around the recognized cargo [30]. As AP‐1 localizes to the trans‐Golgi network (TGN) and recycling endosomes while AP‐2 localizes to the plasma membrane, this enables sorting of select cargo to unique endomembrane compartments [30]. Co‐immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that N‐terminal (YRWL) and C‐terminal (YARI) motifs in mFwe2 allow interaction with both AP‐1 and AP‐2 adaptor complex subunits [31].

We subsequently combined in silico modeling with live imaging assays to validate the four TM structure of the canonical human isoform, hFWE4 [19]. In HEK293 cells, cytosolic intrabodies could bind N‐terminal, C‐terminal, and the TM2‐TM3 loop motif, while only TM1‐TM2 and TM3‐TM4 loop motifs could be detected by extracellular antibody. We also demonstrated functional conservation of the N‐ and C‐terminal YXXΦ motifs. Interestingly, there appears to be more robust conservation of the N‐terminal YXXΦ motif, which is present at the start of TM1 in human (YRWL), mouse (YRWL), and fly (YLKY), than of the C‐terminal YARI motif which is only present in human and mouse. Enhanced conservation of both the YXXΦ motif and PIP2 interacting residues in the N‐terminus likely owe to their larger functional significance which we will discuss in coming sections.

One significant outstanding question regarding FWE structure is the functional significance of a highly conserved glutamate residue in the second TM segment. In their seminal report, Yao and colleagues suggested that FwePB formed homo‐multimers to generate a Ca2+ permeable pore gated by a glutamate‐alanine‐proline (EAP) selectivity filter in the second TM helix [24] (Figure 2). Recently, Ravichandran and colleagues used non‐denaturing SDS‐PAGE to confirm that the canonical FWE isoform in human, mouse and fly formed oligomeric structures with slightly variable stoichiometry across animals [32]. While this work did not interrogate the position of the conserved glutamate in these oligomers, in recently generated structural predictions from Seidenthal and colleagues, homotetramerization of FLWR‐1 in C.elegans, does not position the E74 residue within the central pore [29]. Indeed, OmegaFold [33] based predictions for homotetramers of the canonical isoform across model organisms position this residue away from the pore (Figure 3A). Ravichandran and colleagues have recently demonstrated that several non‐canonical isoforms, which exhibit much poorer structural conservation and typically lack the Cg6151‐P domain (Figure 3B), do not properly oligomerize [32]. Definitive structural determination for both monomeric and oligomeric FWE, as well as determination of oligomer stoichiometry in situ will be critical to further advance understanding of molecular function. While small membrane proteins pose challenges for x‐ray crystallography and traditional cryo‐electron microscopy (cryo‐EM), recent work has shown that antibody fragment‐based scaffolds [34, 35] significantly improve cryo‐EM based structural determination of such proteins. While burdensome, applying these experimental strategies to FWE would be beneficial to the field.

Despite these unresolved structural questions, there has been much progress made in our understanding of the biological function of FWE in the last 15 years. Central to this progress is the developing consensus that canonical isoforms of FWE, unified by inclusion of the Cg6151‐P domain, assume a four TM structure. A growing body of literature suggests that when integrated into membranes along the secretory pathway, the cytosolic positioning of N‐ and C‐terminal tails, enabled by the four TM structure, allows for interaction of the conserved motifs described above with vesicular trafficking machinery as well as bioactive membrane phospholipids. These interactions appear to allow FWE to participate in many different trafficking events that facilitate the biogenesis, secretion, and endocytosis of several unique ELROs across diverse cell types.

FWE is Critical for Synaptic Vesicle Endocytosis in Worm and Fly

Recycling of synaptic vesicles has been an invaluable model for studying dynamic membrane trafficking events. Following release of their contents at the synapse, synaptic vesicles can be regenerated through at least three distinct mechanisms: clathrin mediated endocytosis (CME), ultrafast endocytosis, and activity‐dependent bulk endocytosis (ADBE). These mechanisms enable recycling of synaptic vesicle components and ensure homeostasis of membrane tension at the presynaptic terminal. In CME, adaptor complexes, including AP‐2, bind PIP2 at the plasma membrane and subsequently recruit an outer clathrin coat at sites of synaptic vesicle retrieval [36]. Alternatively, in ultrafast endocytosis, large vesicles are initially derived from the plasma membrane in a clathrin‐independent manner. These vesicles are delivered to synaptic endosomes, from which synaptic vesicles are then generated in a clathrin‐dependent manner. Similarly, following high intensity stimulation, ADBE facilitates clathrin‐independent invagination of large plasma membrane patches. This invagination is dependent on extracellular Ca2+ influx that activates Ca2+‐sensitive endocytic machinery [36]. Subsequently, Ca2+ release from bulk endosomes [37] facilitates clathrin‐dependent regeneration of synaptic vesicles. The mechanism by which Ca2+ entered the presynaptic terminal following neuronal stimulation was unknown until seminal work implicating the FWE protein.

FWE was originally identified as a synaptic vesicle membrane protein during an ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis screen interrogating regulators of synaptic transmission in Drosophila [24]. In this work, it was demonstrated that homozygous mutation of fwe results in a significant impairment of synaptic vesicle (SV) endocytosis, functional deficits in synaptic transmission at the neuromuscular junction, and produces structural aberrations resulting in ‘flowery’ presynaptic boutons [24]. Yao and colleagues demonstrated that the FwePB isoform localizes to SVs and and is deposited at the plasma membrane upon exocytosis. Ultimately, this allows Ca2+ flux from the synaptic cleft to stimulate subsequent SV endocytosis. Biochemical assays in reconstituted proteoliposomes suggested that FWE directly channels this Ca2+, a function enabled by the formation of higher‐order FWE oligomers with glutamate residues facing a central pore as part of an EAP Ca2+ selectivity filter. However, Xue et al. later challenged this Ca2+ channel function, showing that while Fwe is deposited at the plasma membrane upon SV exocytosis in rat calyx of Held nerve terminals, this does not generate detectable calcium currents [38].

Subsequent work from Yao and colleagues has suggested that this discrepancy in Ca2+ channel activity may have to do with the intensity of stimulus provided. While low intensity stimulation does not elicit FWE‐dependent Ca2+ flux it does elicit clathrin‐mediated endocytosis in an FWE‐dependent manner [39]. This suggests that FWE may be able to recruit adaptor complexes such as AP‐2 to YXXΦ motifs even in the absence of direct Ca2+ channeling (Figure 4A). More recent data has demonstrated that high‐intensity stimulus elicits FWE‐mediated Ca2+ flux to enable ADBE [25, 39]. ADBE is impaired in FWE‐deficient neurons and cannot be rescued by expression of the E79Q Ca2+ selectivity filter mutant. Mechanistically, it has been proposed that this FWE‐dependent Ca2+ flux during ADBE serves to facilitate calcineurin‐mediated deposition of PIP2 at the presynaptic membrane. Subsequently, PIP2 binds to cytosolic‐facing lysine and arginine residues in the N‐terminus, C‐terminus, and loop motif between second and third TM segments of FWE. This in turn, enhances Ca2+ flux through the channel to recruit additional PIP2 to the membrane. Calcineurin‐mediated dephosphorylation of endocytic enzymes localized to PIP2 microdomains formed as a result of this positive feedback loop then drive the actin polymerization required for membrane budding and formation of bulk endosomes from which FWE‐containing synaptic vesicles are eventually regenerated [25] (Figure 4B).

Recently, a third model of FWE‐dependent endocytosis has been proposed. Working in C. elegans motor neurons, Seidenthal and colleagues showed that FLWR‐1‐dependent recruitment of PIP2 to the plasma membrane facilitates endocytic recycling of synaptic vesicles [29]. Intriguingly, FLWR‐1 deficiency actually increases cytosolic Ca2+, suggesting that endocytosis and PIP2 recruitment are not the result of FLWR‐1 channeling Ca2+ into the cytosol. Instead, the authors propose that FLWR‐1 contributes to synaptic vesicle recycling by recruiting PIP2 to the plasma membrane where it may stabilize or increase the abundance of the plasma membrane Ca2+ ATPase (PMCA) homolog, MCA‐3 [29]. It was also suggested, based on bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiC) assays, that physical proximity of FLWR‐1 with MCA‐3 may contribute to MCA‐3 activity and plasma membrane abundance. Theoretically, plasma membrane‐associated MCA‐3 could then contribute to the clearance of excess cytosolic Ca2+, a process previously demonstrated to facilitate CME [40] (Figure 4C). Importantly, the authors note that their findings are not sufficient to rule out Ca2+ channel functionality of FLWR‐1. In fact, using higher intensity stimulus to excite body wall muscles, voltage clamp assays reveal a reduction in post synaptic currents in FLWR‐1 mutant worms. This finding could be explained by a reduction in Ca2+ influx directly through FLWR‐1, though alternatively could indirectly result from impaired endocytosis of large quantities of MCA‐3 deposited at the membrane during high intensity stimulation.

Several lines of evidence now point to a critical role for PIP2‐FWE interaction at the plasma membrane in eliciting downstream molecular events required for synaptic vesicle endocytosis. This is in line with the high degree of conservation of N‐ and C‐terminal PIP2‐binding residues in canonical FWE isoforms. It is also possible that FWE serves to enhance PIP2 synthesis at specific membrane domains. As Seidenthal and colleagues note [29], the PIP2 interacting motif in the N‐terminus of FWE ( R F L A K ) bears resemblance to the PIP kinase I gamma (PIPKIγ) binding motif in synaptotagmin‐1 ( RL KK K ). This interaction between PIPKIγ and synaptotagmin‐1 promotes PIP2 synthesis and AP2‐dependent endocytosis in neurons [41]. FWE similarly appears to increase local PIP2 abundance. Whether subsequent FWE‐PIP2 interaction facilitates direct Ca2+ channeling through FWE [24] or indirectly influences Ca2+ flux through nearby channels like MCA‐3 [29] is not yet clear. However, prior to addressing these mechanisms of regulation, there is a critical need for convincing experimental supporting or refuting direct Ca2+ channeling through FWE. In our estimation, single‐channel patch clamp assays in HEK293 engineered to express high levels of FWE could provide sensitive measurement of Ca2+ flux through FWE at the plasma membrane. Additionally, split proximity biotinylation systems that allow for selective labelling of proteins proximal to FWE‐associated PIP2 microdomains could provide insight into downstream effectors across cell types. Such studies may help elucidate whether FWE differentially associates with plasma‐membrane channels that move Ca2+ from cytosol to extracellular space (such as MCA‐3) or from extracellular space to cytosol, depending on cell type. It is also critical to consider that the plasma membrane is not the sole subcellular region where FWE influences Ca2+ homeostasis. Recent work by our group and others has suggested that FWE exerts control over the trafficking of other ELROs in diverse cell types potentially by altering the release of intracellular Ca2+ stores.

FWE is Essential for Lytic Granule Endocytosis in Murine Cytotoxic T‐Lymphocytes

Building upon work performed in presynaptic neurons of Drosophila, murine mFwe was later implicated in the endocytosis of lytic granules by cytotoxic T‐lymphocytes (CTLs). This unique ELRO undergoes polarized exocytosis to deliver cytotoxic cargo to virally‐infected or cancerous target cells at the immunological synapse [42]. To enable serial killing, lytic granule components must subsequently be recovered from the plasma membrane. Much like CME of synaptic vesicles discussed in the previous section, this relies on recruitment of the AP‐2 adaptor complex and formation of a clathrin coat [43]. With little other molecular details known at the time, Chang and colleagues demonstrated FWE is a critical intermediary in this process [31]. They found that the canonical mFwe2 isoform localizes to intracellular vesicles, which upon CTL contact with a neighboring dendritic cell (DC), rapidly polarize to the immunological synapse. Interestingly, these vesicles are not lytic granules themselves but instead represent an undefined vesicle population that fuses at the synapse prior to lytic granule arrival. While FWE deficiency only modestly delays arrival of lytic granules at the synapse [31], FWE‐deficient CTLs are unable to recover lytic granule components following fusion [31, 32]. Intriguingly, this phenotype occurs without alterations to intracellular Ca2+ but is rescued by elevating extracellular Ca2+.

Mechanistically, Chang and colleagues further demonstrated that FWE functions in lytic granule endocytosis by recruiting AP‐2 adaptor complex subunits to N‐ and C‐terminal YXXΦ motifs (Figure 5). Recent work has suggested that of these, the N‐terminal motif is most critical for this process [32]. It remains unclear whether FWE modulates intracellular Ca2+ handling to facilitate AP‐2 recruitment. While FWE depletion does not alter cytosolic Ca2+ alterations in stimulated FWE‐deficient CTLs, endocytosis is rescued in these cells by raising extracellular Ca2+. Relatedly, expression of an E74 Ca2+ selectivity filter mutant FWE fails to fully rescue the endocytic phenotype. Both findings suggest Ca2+ is critical to the endocytic function of FWE in CTLs.

While not yet experimentally supported, two models have been proposed for how FWE might affect Ca2+ handling during lytic granule endocytosis. In the first, FWE containing vesicles adjacent to the plasma membrane may release Ca2+ (Figure 5a). Alternatively, Ca2+ could enter the cytosol either directly through FWE or unidentified FWE‐associated channels at the plasma membrane (Figure 5b). In both models, Ca2+ elevation could then activate an unknown Ca2+ sensor to act on local AP‐2 subunits, enabling their binding to the YXXΦ motif in the N‐terminus of FWE, allowing subsequent assembly of the clathrin coat. In synaptic vesicles, the Ca2+ sensor synaptotagmin directly engages the μ2 subunit of AP‐2 complexes to facilitate CME [45, 46]. However, it is unknown whether a similar mechanism contributes to lytic granule endocytosis. An additional question is whether Ca2+ release from FWE containing vesicles facilitates their trafficking to or fusion at the synapse. We recently found evidence for this kind of event during FWE‐dependent trafficking of epidermal lamellar bodies.

Why does FWE localize directly to an ELRO in neurons but to a vesicle that indirectly affects ELRO function in CTLs? One intriguing possibility is that since the YXXΦ motifs of FWE may recruit multiple adaptor complexes, differential availability of AP complex subunits and their varied accessory proteins across different cell types could sort FWE into unique compartments. Supporting the notion that differential AP complex interaction leads to cell‐type specific localization, while co‐immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry data from Chang and colleagues identified association of FWE with AP‐2 complex subunits in CTLs, preferential association with AP‐1 complex subunits was observed in brain lysates [31]. Classically, the AP‐1 complex facilitates trafficking between early/recycling endosomes and trans‐Golgi network, while AP‐2 complexes traffic cargo from plasma membrane to the early endosome [30]. It is possible that these kinds of cell‐type specific interactions may also enable FWE to participate in exocytic trafficking events such as those recently described by our group and others.

FWE is Required for Exocytic Trafficking of Epidermal Lamellar Bodies and Astryocyte‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles

Previous work in CTLs and presynaptic neurons motivated our group to investigate the function of FWE during epidermal maturation. This process relies heavily on Ca2+‐dependent trafficking of a unique ELRO, the epidermal lamellar body (LB). LBs carry lipids and protein cargo critical to the formation of the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the epidermis. Apical secretion of LBs is stimulated by release of intracellular Ca2+ stores [47]. However, the molecular events that regulate their transport remain poorly understood. While recent work has implicated Rab3d [48], Rab11a [49, 50], Rab27b [48] and Myosin 5b [51], little is known about the Ca2+‐dependency of this process.

We show that FWE localizes to LBs in the epidermis and that loss of FWE produces a significant defect in barrier function [52]. In cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas, tumors derived from epidermal keratinocytes, FWE deficiency similarly leads to LB dysregulation and abnormalities in keratinization [53]. In cultured keratinocytes, ectopic expression of the canonical hFWE4 isoform facilitates plasma membrane trafficking of cargo contained within LB‐like vesicles. Chelating intracellular Ca2+ abrogates this trafficking, suggesting a Ca2+ dependent effect of FWE [52]. Proteomic profiling and super‐resolution microscopy suggest that these vesicles originate from TGN46‐positive, trans‐Golgi network (TGN) derived vesicles. FWE‐positive vesicles also engage an array of secretory trafficking machinery, including many Rab GTPases, and cognate v‐SNARES, t‐SNARES and SNARE regulatory machinery. Many of these have previously been reported to either mediated vesicle docking at the plasma membrane (VAMP8, VAMP3, STX4) [44, 54] or facilitate fusion between TGN and other intracellular compartments (VAMP4, STX6, STX7) [55]. We hypothesize that FWE‐dependent release of vesicular Ca2+ stores enables recruitment or activation of this trafficking machinery to facilitate LB transport to the plasma membrane and/or regulate vesicle fusion events required for LB maturation (Figure 6). Additional experiments will be required to identify Ca2+ sensing proteins that could mediate these effects.

Hypothesized mechanisms of FWE‐dependent lamellar body (LB) trafficking. FWE localizes to TGN46 positive TGN derived carriers that, through an undefined process, receive membrane and cargo from lysosomes, early and recycling endosomes (EE/RE) to produce mature LBs which are targeted to the apical surface in the last viable keratinocyte layers of the epidermis. FWE may facilitate Ca2+ release from several different stores to subsequently facilitate either apically polarized trafficking of mature LBs, or membrane fusion events that enable LB biogenesis. To create a local Ca2+ rich domain enabling apical membrane targeting of mature LBs, FWE could (1A) directly permit Ca2+ flux from the LB, (1B) facilitate Ca2+ flux out of the LB through an unknown channel, or (1C) activate Ca2+ transport ATPases such as SPCA1 to load the LB, thus priming it for Ca2+ release. Alternatively, (1D) FWE could release Ca2+ stores from the TGN itself, or crosstalk with neighboring organelles to facilitate release of Ca2+ from these compartments. This Ca2+ rich environment could be translated into Rab11‐Myo5b dependent trafficking toward the apical membrane and also facilitate assembly of requisite SNARE machinery for fusion at the plasma membrane. FWE‐dependent Ca2+ release could similarly regulate the tethering and fusion between FWE‐positive TGN and lysosome, EE or RE.

Intriguingly, experiments from this work done in nominally Ca2+ free media demonstrated that ectopic hFWE4 expression significantly potentiates release of Ca2+ from intracellular stores sensitive to thapsigargin, GPN and ionomycin. These data do not exclude the possibility that FWE impacts Ca2+ influx at the plasma membrane. However, they do suggest that in keratinocytes FWE predominantly facilitates Ca2+ release from intracellular stores. This is consistent with data from our group and others that has demonstrated the majority of FWE resides on intracellular membranes. While still lacking direct experimental evidence, we hypothesize that FWE could elicit this response through several potential mechanisms; by serving directly as a Ca2+ permeable channel on the intracellular compartment membrane, or by positively regulating channels that either release Ca2+ into the cytosol or load it into FWE‐positive compartments (Figure 6). It is also possible that FWE facilitates organelle crosstalk to elicit Ca2+ release from other intracellular stores such as lysosomes, endosomes or endoplasmic reticulum. Given the recent report of C. elegans FLWR‐1 positively regulating the PMCA homolog MCA‐3 [29], the idea that FWE might similarly regulate Ca2+ transport ATPases like SPCA and SERCA channels to load intracellular compartments, priming them for release, is particularly interesting. Determining the precise manner in which FWE alters intracellular Ca2+ handling will require development of genetically‐encoded calcium indicators (GECIs) targeted to the lumen of FWE‐positive vesicles, or alternatively, chemically enlarging these vesicles to allow direct patch clamp recording [56].

Recent work by Tu and colleagues in murine astrocytes has also proposed a role for FWE in exocytosis of extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing membrane‐bound full‐length and soluble fragmented FWE [57]. While expression of the canonical mFwe2 isoform increases secretion of these EVs, the non‐canonical isoform mFwe1, which has a unique C‐terminus, minimally enhances secretion. While the mechanism for this FWE directed secretion was not described, it is possible that astrocytes engage FWE‐dependent secretory trafficking machinery similar to that utilized by lamellar bodies. One hypothesis is that these FWE containing EVs are derived from multi‐vesicular endosomes (MVEs), an organelle also considered an ELRO [1]. However, Tu and colleagues propose that EV‐localized FWE positions both termini into the extracellular space. Inward budding of the endosomal membrane that occurs during classical EV biogenesis would position these regions into the EV lumen [58]. Direct shedding of plasma membrane‐associated FWE into ectosomes would also be expected to maintain normal membrane orientation [58]. An alternative hypothesis is that these FWE‐positive EVs are generated during autophagosome‐dependent unconventional protein secretion which has been demonstrated to invert the topology of membrane proteins such as caveolin 1 [59]. While several reports suggest that secretion of EV containing MVEs [13, 14] and unconventional secretion of autophagosome‐derived EVs rely on intracellular Ca2+ alterations [59], whether FWE contributes to these processes is currently unknown.

Interestingly, it was also proposed by Tu and colleagues that secretion of the N‐terminal fragment of FWE can elicit apoptotic death in neighboring astrocytes [57]. While the precise mechanism remains undefined, this apoptotic fate can be prevented by ectopic expression of either full length or a C‐terminal fragment from canonical mFwe2, but not by mFwe1 expression. Surface plasma resonance experiments revealed that the N‐terminal epitope shared by all FWE isoforms physically binds the C‐terminal epitope of the canonical mFwe2 isoform to confer this protective effect in neighboring cells. It remains unclear whether expression of non‐canonical mFwe1 plays an active role in this non‐autonomous cell death, or if competition between mFwe2 and mFwe1 expressing cells is solely established by differential expression of canonical FWE and availability of the protective C‐terminal fragment.

Poorly Conserved Non‐canonical FWE Isoforms in Mouse and Human are Aberrantly Localized and Fail to Rescue Function in FWE Deficient CTLs

While the canonical FWE isoform is now appreciated as a critical regulator of several distinct vesicular trafficking processes, the function of non‐canonical isoforms remains unclear. Important work by Ravichandran and colleagues investigated the ability of various isoforms to rescue the endocytic defect observed in mFwe KO CTLs [32]. While expression of the canonical FwePA, mFwe2, and hFWE4 isoforms effectively rescues the phenotype, hFWE2 was the only non‐canonical isoform to produce a complete rescue [32]. Of note, hFWE2 contains the complete Cg6151‐p motif and differs from hFWE4 only in the C‐terminus. mFwe1 and mFwe3 provide partial phenotype rescue, while other non‐canonical isoforms with large deletions, including mFwe4, hFWE1 and hFWE3 along with Drosophila isoforms FwePB and FwePC do not provide any rescue [32]. As they were performed in mouse CTLs, it is possible that these experiments are complicated by expression of non‐mouse isoforms in a heterologous system. Suggestive of this is the finding that defects in synaptic vesicle endocytosis observed in fwe mutant Drosophila neuromuscular junction boutons are rescued by ectopic expression of the non‐canonical FwePB [39]. It will be important to determine whether non‐canonical isoforms can rescue trafficking function in homologous systems and across diverse FWE dependent trafficking events, including synaptic vesicle endocytosis and exocytosis of epidermal lamellar bodies.

One potential explanation for the lack of trafficking function in non‐canonical isoforms is that they exhibit aberrant subcellular localization. We previously demonstrated that hFWE3 is retained in the ER [19], while Ravichandran and colleagues similarly demonstrated that mFwe1, mFwe3 and mFwe4 exhibit predominantly ER localization [32]. From an evolutionary perspective, it is curious that these non‐canonical isoforms in vertebrate animals appear to have arisen through alternative splicing rather than through paralogous expansion which diversified similar trafficking machinery [60]. Whether these isoforms functionally contribute to FWE‐dependent trafficking or other yet‐undescribed FWE functions is unknown. One speculative, yet attractive hypothesis is that ER‐localized non‐canonical isoforms may facilitate contact of the ER with vesicles containing the canonical isoform through direct FWE‐FWE interaction in order to allow communication between these subcellular compartments. Complicating the ability to address these kinds of questions is the fact that, at least in human and mouse, these non‐canonical isoforms are expressed at exceedingly low levels across cell types. Our analysis of GTEx data suggested that hFWE4 transcript is ∼4x more abundant than hFWE3, and ∼20x more abundant than hFWE2, while hFWE1 transcripts are not detectable [19]. At protein level, non‐canonical isoforms remain undetectable as evidenced by our recent work showing that only a single protein product corresponding to hFWE4 is detectable when an epitope tag is knocked into the C‐terminus shared by hFWE3 and hFWE4 [52]. Sophisticated genetic strategies will be required to create reliable reporters for these non‐canonical isoforms.

Unlike in Drosophila, where protein isoforms utilize unique C‐terminal reading frames enabling their detection through selective knock‐in of epitope tags [61], detection of endogenous non‐canonical protein isoforms in mouse and human has not been achieved. Despite this, they have been attributed function based on the consequences of their overexpression. Their major attributed function is the ability to mediate competitive interactions between adjacent cells. This is proposed to involve cell surface presentation that enables isoforms to expose unique C‐terminal epitopes to the extracellular space where they can be recognized through an undefined mechanism [62, 63]. It has been proposed that cells overexpressing ‘winning’ isoforms, for example hFWE2 or hFWE4 in humans, facilitate cell death in neighbors overexpressing ‘losing’ isoforms hFWE1 or hFWE3 [20, 64]. As data from our group and others suggest that the C‐terminal epitopes of all FWE isoforms are positioned in the cytosol, and that non‐canonical isoforms in human and mouse are retained in the ER, it appears unlikely that this competitive interaction is mediated by extracellular presentation of C‐terminal FWE epitopes. Instead, mechanisms like those described by Tu and colleagues 57] where the canonical isoform facilitates trafficking of soluble or cell surface cargo that can mediate intercellular communication are more consistent with existing understanding of FWE topology and trafficking function. Future experiments will be required to determine if non‐canonical isoforms are necessary for this process, or whether it is entirely mediated by differential secretion or surface presentation driven by the canonical isoform.

Conclusions and Future Directions

In the last 15 years, growing evidence has positioned FWE as a multifunctional regulator of ELRO biogenesis, secretion, and endocytosis across cell types. While we now appreciate that some of these events are dependent upon PIP2 and adaptor complex interactions with the highly conserved N‐terminal domain, much work remains to more completely understand the molecular function of FWE. Generating robust structures of FWE monomers and oligomers in situ, defining their direct Ca2+ channeling ability, and compiling more comprehensive FWE interactomes across cell types using sophisticated proteomic approaches is essential to this effort. Such experiments promise to provide significant insight into mechanisms by which FWE contributes to diverse ELRO trafficking events required for homeostatic tissue function. They may also help to identify novel intracellular compartments where FWE exerts function. One particularly intriguing compartment is the secretory lysosome, which undergoes Ca2+ regulated exocytosis to facilitate plasma membrane repair [65, 66], cytosolic pH regulation, and even intercellular signal transduction [66]. While we have not yet identified FWE in conventional lysosome populations [19, 52], investigation of additional cell types under conditions that elicit lysosome exocytosis may be informative.

In addition to these efforts devoted toward developing a more complete model of homeostatic FWE function, investigating FWE dysregulation as a contributor to diseases affecting ELRO biology is worthwhile. While pathological mutations in FWE have not yet been identified in rare diseases that affect ELROs across tissue types, analysis of the FWE locus could be included in targeted sequencing efforts for novel rare syndromic disorders that manifest ELRO‐related phenotypes. Similarly, in studies using targeted sequencing approaches in larger patient cohorts to investigate the genetic basis of more common ELRO‐related phenotypes such as albinism (melanosomes), or ichthyosis (epidermal lamellar bodies), the FWE locus could be included as a target. Finally, recent work suggests that FWE may direct secretion of specialized vesicles that mediate intercellular communication between astrocytes [57]. Might activating mutations or other methods of potentiating FWE function allow this process to be co‐opted in diseases such as cancer? Directed secretion of ELROs such as melanosomes has been proposed to remodel the tumor microenvironment to create a permissive niche for tumor growth [67, 68]. This could help to explain why FWE abundance appears increased in several different epithelial cancers [20, 64], though the impact on selection of FWE activated cells would likely depend on cell type and its resident ELRO. Continuing to study the function of FWE in these varied disease states may well illuminate new therapeutic targets to prevent ELRO‐related pathogenesis.
