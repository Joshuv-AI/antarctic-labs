ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing (AM) processes such as selective laser sintering (SLS) inherently produce
surface roughness and geometric deviations that can influence thermal performance, particularly
in heat transfer applications. Conventional surface roughness measurement techniques require
direct access to internal surfaces, often necessitating destructive sectioning of test articles.
Computed tomography (CT) offers a non-destructive alternative for internal surface
characterization, however, its application in metrology remains unstandardized and sensitive to
machine resolution and operator technique.
This study investigates the feasibility of using CT scanning to quantify areal surface roughness in
metal AM heat exchanger tubes with three distinct internal geometries: ribbed, discrete W, and
featureless. CT-derived surface parameters were extracted using custom Python scripts and
compared to measurements obtained from a focus variation microscope calibrated against a known
standard.
Results show that CT-based roughness measurements closely matched microscope values for the
discrete W specimen, which exhibited higher surface roughness (7.67-8.15 µm). Deviations
between CT and microscope measurements were minimal—less than 1 µm—indicating reliable
reconstruction. In contrast, the ribbed and featureless specimens, with lower roughness values
(5.45-6.84 µm), showed greater discrepancies, with CT overestimating roughness by up to 3.3 µm
in some cases. These differences are attributed to limitations in voxel resolution and post-
processing artifacts.
The findings suggest that CT scanning can be a viable non-destructive metrology tool for AM parts
with surface roughness above approximately 8 µm. For smoother surfaces, current CT capabilities
may not provide sufficient accuracy, highlighting the need for resolution-aware workflows and
further standardization in CT-based surface metrology.

1. INTRODUCTION
Additive manufacturing (AM) has become a preferred method for producing complex or
unconventional geometries that are difficult to fabricate using traditional techniques. Among AM
methods, selective laser sintering (SLS) is widely used for metal components, where a high-
powered laser fuses powdered material layer by layer to form the desired shape. However, SLS
inherently introduces surface roughness and geometric deviations due to the nature of powder
fusion and variability in laser energy distribution.
These deviations—such as rounded corners, uneven surfaces, and unintended particulate
formations—can significantly affect the thermal and fluid performance of components, especially
in heat exchanger applications. For example, surface roughness has been shown to enhance the
Nusselt number by up to 60% at high Reynolds numbers (Re) in supercritical CO2 (sCO2) flows
(Roy et al., 2022). While frictional losses may be secondary in some regimes, the mismatch
between as-designed and as-manufactured geometries can lead to discrepancies between
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations and experimental results.
Quantifying surface roughness and geometric deviation is essential for validating performance
models and improving design fidelity. However, most metrology tools—such as stylus
profilometers or optical microscopes—require direct access to the surface of interest. For internal
features, this often necessitates destructive sectioning, rendering the test article unusable for
further experimentation or quality assurance.
Computed tomography (CT) offers a non-destructive alternative by enabling internal geometry
capture through X-ray imaging. CT systems rotate the sample or emitter-detector array to collect
projections, which are reconstructed into three-dimensional (3D) volumes. While promising, CT-
based metrology is limited by voxel resolution and lacks standardized workflows, making accuracy
highly dependent on machine capability and operator expertise.
Given these challenges, this study explores whether CT scanning can reliably quantify surface
roughness in metal AM parts. Leveraging the CT infrastructure and expertise at the National
Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL) Morgantown campus, three previously fabricated heat
exchanger tubes—featuring ribbed, discrete W, and featureless internal geometries—were scanned
using a Tescan DynaTOM system. Surface roughness parameters were extracted from CT
reconstructions and compared to measurements obtained via a calibrated Olympus DSX510 focus
variation microscope.
This effort aims to assess the feasibility of CT-based surface metrology for AM components,
establish an in-house workflow, and determine the resolution threshold below which CT
measurements may become unreliable.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
CT has emerged as a powerful non-destructive technique for capturing internal geometries of
complex components, particularly those produced via AM. By measuring the attenuation of X-
rays as they pass through a material, CT enables volumetric reconstruction of internal and external
features. The fidelity of these reconstructions is governed by a range of parameters, including beam
type (cone or fan), detector resolution, voxel size, and post-processing algorithms (Kruth et al.,
2011). Despite its widespread use in industrial inspection, CT metrology remains largely
unstandardized, with significant variability introduced by machine configuration and operator-
dependent settings (Horvatic Novak and Runje, 2017).
Efforts to quantify CT measurement uncertainty have revealed the challenges inherent in this
modality. The “CT Audit” study, comprised of 15 different participant groups across Europe, the
United States, and Asia, evaluated dimensional accuracy across CT systems using standardized
artifacts. It was found that only a minority of participants could rigorously declare measurement
uncertainty, and even fewer could relate it to formal procedures (Carmignato, 2012). This
underscores the need for robust, repeatable workflows and highlights the limitations of CT as a
metrological tool in its current state.
Surface roughness, a critical parameter in both manufacturing quality and functional performance,
presents additional challenges for CT-based measurement. Traditional tactile profilometry, such
as stylus-based devices, suffers from mechanical filtering effects due to probe geometry, which
limits the ability to resolve fine surface features (Salzinger et al., 2016). Similarly, CT
reconstructions are sensitive to grayscale thresholding, particularly at material boundaries where
voxel intensity gradients are steep. The selection of threshold values directly influences the
perceived edge location, introducing uncertainty in surface characterization (Salzinger et al.,
2016).
Studies have proposed general guidelines for CT-based roughness measurement, suggesting that
dimensional uncertainty typically falls within 10-20% of the voxel size under optimal conditions
(Kruth et al., 2011). Danzl et al. (2011) further demonstrated that CT can reliably capture surface
roughness above approximately 38 µm, but exhibits significant deviation for features below 19
µm due to reconstruction unsharpness. These findings are particularly relevant for AM
components, which often exhibit fine-scale surface textures that challenge the resolution limits of
CT systems.
Beyond metrology, surface roughness plays a pivotal role in the thermal and fluid performance of
AM parts. In high Reynolds number regimes (Re > 100,000), rough surfaces can substantially
enhance convective heat transfer. Roy et al. (2022) showed that surface roughness in AM channels
can increase the Nusselt number by up to 60% compared to smooth-walled geometries in sCO2
flows. While frictional losses may be secondary in such regimes, the thermal performance factor
remains favorable, making surface roughness a key design variable in heat exchanger applications.
Given the functional importance of surface texture and the limitations of conventional stylus
measurement tools, alternative approaches are needed to characterize internal surfaces for
comparison to CT obtained data. Focus variation microscopy offers a validated optical method for
high-resolution surface metrology. This is accomplished by capturing sequential images at varying
focal depths, filtering out light of regions not in focus utilizing a pinhole or other method and
reconstructing a full-depth, 3D surface. This technique avoids the mechanical filtering inherent in
stylus devices and provides superior reproducibility. Danzl et al. (2011) reported deviations of less

 3

than 3 µm between focus variation and stylus measurements, with significantly lower standard
deviation across repeated scans. This study showed standard deviation of less than 1 nanometer
over 25 measurements for the focus variation microscope, whereas the standard deviation of the
stylus-based device was seen to be almost 5 nanometers over 30 measurements (Danzl et al., 2011).
Furthermore, when comparing the two tools line-based surface roughness (Ra) was found to be
within 3 nanometers of one another (Danzl et al., 2011).
In this study, areal surface roughness (Sa) was selected as the primary metric due to its robustness
against outliers and its ability to represent the overall surface texture more comprehensively than
Ra. Sa values derived from CT reconstructions were compared against those obtained via focus
variation microscopy, which serves as a benchmark for evaluating CT accuracy. This comparison
aims to assess the feasibility of CT-based surface metrology for AM components and to identify
resolution thresholds below which CT measurements may become unreliable.

3. METHODS
This study utilized three AM heat exchanger tube specimens, each featuring distinct internal
geometries: a smooth-walled “featureless” tube, a “ribbed” tube with vertical fins, and a “discrete
W” tube with staggered chevron features. These geometries were previously employed in NETL’s
Heat Exchange and Experimental Testing (HEET) Rig. All specimens shared similar external
dimensions and internal channel sizes, as detailed in Table 1, and are visually depicted in Figure
1.
Due to the original tube lengths exceeding the capacity of the CT system, representative segments
were sectioned from each tube. These segments were scanned using the Tescan DynaTOM
industrial CT system at the NETL Morgantown facility. Each specimen underwent two
independent CT scan and reconstruction cycles to assess repeatability and artifact behavior.

3.2 CT SCAN POST-PROCESSING METHODOLOGY
Post-processing of CT data was conducted using Fiji/ImageJ (ImageJ, 2025). Raw image stacks
were screened for detector anomalies, motion artifacts, and beam-hardening effects (Boas and
Fleischmann, 2012; Ketcham and Carlson, 2001). Corrupted slices were removed to prevent
distortion during segmentation. The image stacks were rotated to align the tube axis vertically
within the voxel space, improving edge fidelity during thresholding (Maire and Withers, 2014).
Noise reduction was applied using a 3D Gaussian filter followed by a 3D median filter to suppress
speckle and ring artifacts while preserving structural boundaries (Li et al., 2020). Intensity
normalization and contrast adjustments were performed to stabilize grayscale distribution across
the volume (European Society of Radiology, 2024). Global thresholding was used to segment the
metal structure from surrounding air, followed by outlier removal and fill operations to ensure
watertight binary masks suitable for meshing.
This standardized workflow was applied uniformly across all specimens to ensure consistency in
reconstruction quality and facilitate comparative analysis. Artifact behavior and segmentation
fidelity are further discussed in Appendix A.

3.3 ESTABLISHING WORKFLOW WITH A FOCUS VARIATION MICROSCOPE,
 UTILIZING CALIBRATED SPECIMEN
To establish a reliable and reproducible measurement protocol for surface roughness, a calibrated
milled specimen was selected for preliminary scans using the Olympus DSX510 focus variation
microscope (ATEC, 2025). The specimen, manufactured by Starrett and sourced from Grainger
(2025), reported an Ra of 5.81 µm, likely measured using a stylus-based profilometer. As stylus
devices inherently apply a mechanical low-pass filter due to probe geometry, it is important to
keep in mind this low-pass mechanical filter is likely present in the calibrated specimen Ra.
An iterative scanning approach was employed to optimize microscope settings. Multiple scans
were performed at varying vertical (z-axis) ranges to assess the impact of scan depth on data
fidelity. It was observed that insufficient z-range could truncate surface peaks, while excessive
range introduced unnecessary noise. Both filtered and unfiltered datasets were analyzed to
understand their influence on roughness metrics.
Initial scans were conducted using a 50x objective lens, which provided high-resolution detail but
limited the scan area, potentially reducing representativeness. To address this, a 5x objective lens
was used in conjunction with the microscope’s built-in optical zoom, enabling larger scan areas
while maintaining sufficient resolution. The results of these scans are summarized in Table 3,
showing close agreement with the reported Ra value—within 5%—thus validating the selected
configuration.

3.4 METHODOLOGY TO OBTAIN SURFACE ROUGHNESS OF SPECIMENS
 WITH A FOCUS VARIATION MICROSCOPE
Having developed an accurate process to obtain line-based surface roughness values verified
against a calibrated specimen, measurements were taken of each of the three test articles
(featureless, ribbed, and discrete W) to obtain Sa values. Scans were repeated three times to
demonstrate reproducibility. Area of interest for these scans were bounded for the ribbed and
discrete W cases such that the cooling geometry features would not be included in the
measurement, additionally for these test articles secondary scans were taken at secondary locations
between features to ensure values from initial scans were not disproportionally affected by outlier
features. The general steps for the process to obtain these areal surface roughness measurements
are outlined in Table 4.

 OBTAINING CT-BASED SURFACE ROUGHNESS
Following CT scanning and post-processing (as described in Section 3.2), each test article was
reconstructed into a stack of grayscale images representing cross-sectional slices. These slices
were used to generate surface meshes for roughness analysis. Representative slices from the ribbed
test article are shown in Figure 7, illustrating the clean binary contrast between the metal structure
(white) and surrounding air (black).

 Figure 7: Examples of post processed tif image stack from CT scans for the ribbed test

To convert these image stacks into 3D surface meshes, the Fiji/ImageJ “3D Viewer” plugin was
used (ImageJ, 2025; 3D Viewer, 2025). The plugin applies the marching cubes algorithm
(Lorensen and Cline, 1987) to extract surface geometry based on user-defined grayscale
thresholds. The resulting mesh was exported in STL format for further analysis.
Each STL file was imported into a custom Python script using the Open3D module (Open3D,
2025). Since STL files retain relative dimensions but lack real-world scaling, the mesh was scaled
to microns using bounding box dimensions and known tube diameters. The user then selected four
corner points to define the area of interest, which was isolated from the full mesh. These
coordinates were saved in a JSON file and hardcoded into the script for repeatability. Surface
roughness parameters were calculated using definitions from International Organization for
Standardization (ISO) 25178-2 (ISO, 2021). The primary metric, Sa, was computed using Equation
1, which calculates Sa as a function of area of interest and surface z height at each discrete point
in x and y, also derived from ISO 25178-2 (ISO, 2021).

During analysis, it was observed that the internal surfaces exhibited slight curvature. When using
a flat plane, a central ridge was consistently elevated above the fitted plane. Therefore, curved
plane fitting was used for all final calculations. The choice of two million sampling points was
validated by comparing results with four million points. Sa values differed by less than 1% in four
out of six cases, and by less than 0.3 µm in all cases.

4.1 OBSERVED ARTIFACTS AND IMPERFECTIONS IN CT-SCANNED
 GEOMETRIES
High-energy CT imaging of dense metallic components frequently produces localized
reconstruction defects due to acquisition instability, detector saturation, and geometric occlusion.
Although the majority of each scan exhibited stable grayscale behavior, the initial and terminal
regions of the CT stacks for all three HEET tube specimens displayed systematic image
degradation. These regions were removed prior to filtering and segmentation to prevent corrupted
voxel values from influencing downstream grayscale gradients and geometry-dependent analyses.
The following slice ranges were excluded from each dataset:
 • Featureless specimen: Slices 1-52 and slices 3,242-3,293
 • Ribbed specimen: Slices 1-30 and slices 3,303-3,416
 • Discrete W specimen: Slices 1-30 and slices 3,515-3,628
The defective slices exhibited several characteristic artifact modes. At the beginning and end of
each scan, incomplete projections and truncated detector readings resulted in partial-frame
shadows and intensity discontinuities. These are consistent with transient X-ray flux imbalances
during rotation startup and shutdown. Final slices also showed streaking, nonuniform brightness
fields, and edge-warping patterns indicative of detector saturation and geometric misalignment.
These distortion patterns are well-documented in high-density CT imaging, particularly when
scanning components with thick cross-sections or internal cavities (Boas and Fleischmann, 2012;
Ketcham and Carlson, 2001). A representative example of these artifacts is shown in Figure 9,
which highlights the defective regions in each geometry.

These observations confirm that internal geometry strongly influences artifact expression.
Smooth-wall samples are prone to threshold-induced gradient loss, vertical ribs enhance local
over-segmentation, and angled ribs exaggerate attenuation gradients, resulting in the greatest
surface deviation.
While these artifacts are important to document, they largely do not affect the surfaces of interest
used for surface roughness analysis. The primary limitation in accurate roughness quantification
appears to be voxel resolution. Features significantly below the sub-voxel detection threshold may
be missed or distorted, regardless of post-processing quality.

5. CONCLUSIONS
This study compared Sa values obtained through two distinct methods: experimental
measurements using a calibrated Olympus DSX510 focus variation microscope, and
computational analysis of CT scan reconstructions processed via a custom Python workflow. The
comparison across three AM test articles—ribbed, discrete W, and featureless—revealed key
insights into the capabilities and limitations of CT-based surface metrology. The results of these
data gathering efforts are categorized in Figure 17.

Several trends are evident from this comparison:
 • Microscope measurements consistently yield narrower parameter ranges, reflecting the
 precision and repeatability of the calibrated optical system.
 • CT-derived values show greater variability, which can be attributed to post-processing
 artifacts, segmentation sensitivity, and limitations in voxel resolution.
 • The discrete W specimen, which exhibited the highest surface roughness, showed the
 closest agreement between CT and microscope measurements. Differences in Sa were
 minimal (≤0.7 µm), and Sz values were within ~0.2 µm.
 • In contrast, the featureless and ribbed specimens, with lower roughness values, showed
 larger discrepancies—up to 2 µm in Sa and over 100 µm in Sz—highlighting the challenge
 of resolving fine surface features below the CT system’s resolution threshold.

These findings reinforce the importance of resolution-aware workflows in CT-based metrology.
With a voxel size of approximately 10 µm, and sub-voxel edge detection potentially improving
resolution by 3-5 µm, CT scanning is best suited for surfaces with roughness values exceeding 8-
10 µm for this physical system and workflow. Below this threshold, reconstruction accuracy
diminishes, and results may be influenced by noise and segmentation instability.

In conclusion, CT scanning can serve as a viable non-destructive tool for surface roughness
characterization in metal AM parts—particularly when roughness exceeds the resolution limit of
the imaging system. For smoother surfaces, complementary methods such as focus variation
microscopy remain essential. Future efforts should focus on refining post-processing techniques,
improving segmentation fidelity, and establishing standardized protocols to enhance the reliability
of CT-based surface metrology.

APPENDIX A: DETAILED BREAKDOWN OF POTENTIAL ARTIFACTS IN CT POST-

A.1 POST-PROCESS EXAMPLE
Appendix A focuses on the discrete W test sample to illustrate the effect of the standardized
preprocessing workflow, which combines a rectangular flow channel with staggered 45° chevron
turbulator strips and therefore represents the most geometrically complex test specimen. The raw
CT stack for this tube contained multiple slices with detector instability and reconstruction
streaking at both the leading and trailing ends of the scan. These slices were removed (slices 1-30
and slices 3,515-3,628) to prevent severe grayscale outliers from biasing subsequent filtering and
thresholding, a practice consistent with recommendations to exclude corrupted projections in high-
density CT datasets (Figure A1).
A representative interior slice (slice 188) of the discrete W tube before preprocessing shows clear
rib geometry but also pronounced beam-hardening (dark gray to black areas) along the interior
corners and slight radial intensity gradients across the field of view (Figure A1, panel A). These
features are typical for CT scans of metal, where long path lengths through angled or thickened
regions attenuate low-energy X-rays and depress voxel intensities in corner zones (Boas and
Fleischmann, 2012; Ketcham and Carlson, 2001).

 Figure A1: Representative CT slice showing beam-hardening artifacts in the test article.
 Darkened regions along the interior corners and rib transitions reflect increased attenuation
 where X-ray paths are thickest, while the bright outer ring represents edge-hardening
 caused by preferential transmission of higher-energy photons. Both effects are characteristic
 of polychromatic CT imaging of dense metallic components.

Clearing voxels outside the tube body isolates the region of interest and removes external air and
fixture materials from the grayscale histogram (Figure A1). In the discrete W test article, this step
tightens the dynamic range around the metal-air contrast, making corner darkening and rib-related
gradients more visually prominent but also simplifying subsequent filter response.
Rotating the stack by 20.5° to align the tube axis with the voxel grid further improves
interpretability (Figure A1, panel B). After rotation, ribs and channel walls intersect the grid along

near-orthogonal directions, reducing aliasing and stepped edges, which are known to complicate
segmentation and quantitative dimensional analysis (Maire and Withers, 2014). In the 45° tube,
this alignment also decouples geometric rib orientation from the reconstruction angle, helping to

A.2 3D GAUSSIAN FILTER
In the Gaussian-filtered slice, the interior channel appears noticeably more uniform, with the fine
granular speckle seen in the raw reconstruction largely suppressed (Figure A3). The ribs display
smoother grayscale transitions, and the abrupt pixel-level variations present along their edges in
the unfiltered image are reduced, resulting in a cleaner and more continuous appearance of the rib
surfaces. Subtle ring-like shading and small radial intensity fluctuations visible across the raw slice
are diminished, producing a more even tonal field across the full cross-section. Despite this
smoothing, the primary geometric features—rib boundaries, channel walls, and corner regions—
remain clearly defined, with no loss of bulk structure. Beam-hardening zones, particularly in the
interior corners and near rib bases, remain visible but appear less mottled, reflecting a more
coherent grayscale gradient following noise reduction.

A.3 3D MEDIAN FILTER
In the median-filtered slice, the smoothing previously applied by the Gaussian filter became more
structurally coherent, with small, isolated bright and dark speckle remnants largely removed. The
interior channel, which already appeared uniform after Gaussian smoothing, shows an even cleaner
grayscale field with fewer pixel-level fluctuations. Along the ribs and rib–wall transitions, the
median filter sharpened the local definition compared to the slightly softened appearance left by
the Gaussian pass, reducing scattered noise without blurring the rib edges (Figure A4).
Additionally, the residual streaking or faint ring artifacts visible after Gaussian smoothing are
further diminished, producing a more stable tonal distribution across the cross-section. Overall,
the median-filtered result retains the major geometry while presenting a more consistent and
artifact-reduced grayscale representation than the Gaussian-only image.

A.4 COLOR BALANCE AND BRIGHTNESS/CONTRAST TUNING
A CT slice of the discrete W test article after sequential intensity normalization steps is shown in
Figure A5. The initial color-balance adjustment (-5,527/51,085) reduces the uneven gray shading
across the field, producing a more neutral and evenly distributed background while making the rib
features appear less muted than in the median-filtered image. Following this, the
brightness/contrast setting (57,409/65,535) enhances the tonal separation between the metal walls
and the surrounding air gap, revealing clearer rib outlines and more discernible gradients near the
beam-hardened corner regions. Together, these adjustments yield a more visually stable and evenly
illuminated slice while maintaining the underlying geometry.

A.5 GLOBAL THRESHOLDING, OUTLIER, AND FILL REQUIREMENT
After thresholding (Figure A6, Panel A), the tube geometry is captured as a continuous binary
silhouette; however, several small irregularities remain along the rib transitions and wall
boundaries, where the grayscale gradients were steepest (Figure A5). These manifest as slight
contour distortions and thin gaps near the upper and lower edges. Following outlier removal
(Figure A6, Panel B), the mask becomes noticeably cleaner, with isolated black specks and fringe
artifacts eliminated from the background. The boundary of the tube appears smoother and more
cohesive, indicating that non-structural noise has been successfully removed from the segmented
volume. After applying the fill operation (Figure A6, Panel C), the remaining voids within the
binary region and the most persistent disconnected particles are corrected, producing a uniformly
solid representation of the tube interior. The fill step resolves small discontinuities that
thresholding alone cannot reconcile, especially near complex, ribbed zones. Together, these three
stages yield a cohesive binary geometry in which structural boundaries are continuous, internal
gaps are closed, and non-physical particles have been eliminated.
