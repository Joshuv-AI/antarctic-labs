Signal Intensity versus Spatial Resolution: Divergent MPI Performance of SPIONs in Mesenchymal Stromal Cell Labelling
Authors: Serbay Ozkan, Elena Ureña Horno, Reilteann Niamh Saul, Mahon L Maguire, Harish Poptani, Marco Giardiello, Patricia Murray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimMagnetic Particle Imaging (MPI) is a highly sensitive modality for non-invasive cell tracking; however, imaging performance is strongly influenced by nanoparticle properties and intracellular behavior following labelling. In this study, four commercially available superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) - ProMag, VivoTrax, SynoMag-D, and Ferumoxytol - were evaluated for labelling efficiency and cytocompatibility in mouse mesenchymal stem/stromal cells, followed by MPI characterization of biocompatible candidates.MethodsSPION labelling efficiency and cytocompatibility were systematically evaluated across a range of iron concentrations and incubation durations using Prussian blue staining, and ATP-based viability assay respectively. Based on these outcomes, biocompatible candidates were selected for further analysis. Cells labelled with ProMag or VivoTrax for 2-hour underwent MPI characterization to evaluate signal properties, alongside transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine intracellular nanoparticle localization and distribution.ResultsProMag achieved >90% labelling efficiency at 20 µg Fe/mL, whereas VivoTrax required ≥240 µg Fe/mL to reach >75% efficiency. SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol required transfection agents that induced cytotoxicity and were excluded. MPI analysis demonstrated a linear correlation between signal intensity and cell number for both SPIONs. ProMag-labelled cells exhibited a 2.33-fold increase in total signal intensity, closely matching the ~2.3-fold increase in intracellular iron loading, confirming iron content as the primary determinant of signal strength and detection sensitivity (limit: 7,812 cells). In contrast, VivoTrax-labelled cells produced higher maximum signal per unit iron and significantly improved spatial resolution, indicated by reduced full width at half maximum, suggesting that spatial resolution is primarily governed by particle-specific physicochemical properties and their intracellular behavior following uptake. TEM revealed ProMag predominantly as dispersed particles, whereas VivoTrax formed aggregates within endosomal structures.ConclusionIntracellular iron loading dictates MPI signal strength, whereas particle-specific characteristics govern spatial resolution. Optimizing SPION selection is therefore essential to balance sensitivity and image quality for preclinical cell tracking applications.Keywords: mesenchymal stromal cells, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, labelling, magnetic particle imaging, signal intensity, spatial resolution

Introduction

Stem cell therapies show promise for treating various diseases due to their regenerative potential.1 Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) are particularly promising due to their accessibility, multipotency, immunomodulatory capacity, and regenerative secretome.2 Pre-clinical studies support their therapeutic potential in conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, renal dysfunction, and autoimmune disorders.1 However, clinical translation of MSC therapies remains limited, with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) currently only approving MSC use for graft-versus-host disease.3 A critical barrier to translation is the incomplete understanding of MSC fate post-transplantation, particularly their migration, survival, and integration.4

Tracking MSC biodistribution is crucial for assessing therapeutic safety and efficacy.5 Common imaging techniques used for cell tracking, such as bioluminescence imaging (BLI), nuclear imaging, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), each have limitations.6–8 BLI is sensitive but lacks spatial-resolution and quantitative precision, as signal intensity depends not only on cell number but also on factors like substrate kinetics and tissue depth.9 Nuclear imaging allows deep tissue penetration but poses radiotoxicity risks and has a short detection window.10 MRI with superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) offers better spatial resolution but suffers from low sensitivity and negative contrast in T2*-weighted images, making it difficult to quantify and image SPION-labelled cells in the lungs, liver and spleen.9 Magnetic particle imaging (MPI) has emerged as a promising alternative, offering superior sensitivity and quantification without ionizing radiation.11 MPI signal correlates linearly with iron content, enabling accurate cell quantification.12 When combined with BLI and anatomical imaging (eg MRI or CT), MPI enhances real-time tracking of labelled cells.13

The selection of specific SPIONs as labelling agents is pivotal for the efficiency and reliability of cell tracking.14,15 Due to their biocompatibility and strong T2* relaxation effects, certain SPIONs, that have been used for MRI-based cell tracking can also be adapted for MPI signal detection.14–16 Among commercially available formulations, ProMag, VivoTrax, SynoMag-D, and Ferumoxytol were selected in the present study because they represent distinct classes of SPIONs with differing core structures, coatings, hydrodynamic sizes, and reported MPI performance characteristics.17 In addition, these nanoparticles have been widely utilized in preclinical MRI and MPI studies for cell tracking applications, making them clinically and experimentally relevant candidates for direct comparison.18 Transfection agents (TAs), like protamine sulphate (PS), can facilitate SPION uptake.12

Despite the commercial availability of SPIONs, systematic comparisons of their labelling efficiency for MPI in MSCs have focused on signal performance, while data about intracellular processing and biocompatibility remain limited. Importantly, differences in intracellular localization, aggregation, and degradation of SPIONs can substantially influence MPI signal behaviour; however, these aspects are often overlooked in comparative studies, limiting accurate interpretation of MPI performance and hindering further signal optimization. Thus, optimization of both SPION formulation and labelling conditions is essential to achieve an appropriate balance between efficient cell labelling, MPI sensitivity, spatial resolution, and preservation of stem cell health. Moreover, optimizing the labelling protocol, particularly the incubation period, is crucial to balancing effective SPION internalization with minimizing impact on cell viability. Identifying the shortest incubation time that still ensures efficient labelling is essential to reduce cellular stress and preserve stem cell function, especially in clinical or time-sensitive experimental settings. Therefore, this study investigated the labelling efficiency of commercially available SPIONs in relation to their intracellular fate and their effects on MSC viability, with the aim of identifying suitable candidates for safe and effective MPI-based cell tracking. In addition, we aimed to analyse how MPI signal intensity and spatial resolution are influenced by intracellular iron loading and nanoparticle-specific physicochemical properties, respectively, thereby providing insight into how both cellular iron uptake and intrinsic nanoparticle characteristics contribute to overall MPI performance. Ultimately, this work seeks to inform strategies to improve MPI signal performance through biologically informed labelling approaches in stem cell research and therapeutic applications.

Material and Method

MSC Culture

Immortalized mouse bone marrow MSCs (D1-ORL-UVA, CRL-12324; ATCC, VA, USA) were cultured in DMEM/F12+GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). Cells at passages 10–15 were used for labelling.

Cell Labelling

Cells were seeded on 24 well plates in growth medium at a density of 1×105 per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The next day, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) without Ca+2 and Mg+2. Separate cohorts of cells were labelled with one of the SPION labelling solutions [ProMag (Bang Laboratories, PMC1N, IN, USA), VivoTrax (Magnetic Insight, CA, USA), SynoMag-D (Micromod, Partikeltechnologie GmbH, Rostock, Germany) and Ferumoxytol (AMAG Pharmaceuticals, MA, USA); manufacturer-provided physicochemical properties are summarized in (Table 1)]. SPION solutions, prepared across a defined range of iron (Fe) concentrations based on previously reported effective labelling doses for each SPION formulation8,12,19,20 (Table 2), were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following initial tests, combination of TAs, 8 IU/mL heparin (5000 IU/mL, Wockhardt, Mumbai, India) and 240 ug/mL PS (Sigma-Aldrich, P4020, MO, USA), were added to the labelling media of SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol SPIONs.21

Optimization of Incubation Periods

To determine the minimum effective incubation time for SPION labelling, preliminary experiments (n = 3) were performed with incubation periods of 18, 4, and 2 hours for all labelling solutions and controls. The results indicated that a 2-hour incubation achieved labelling efficiencies comparable to longer durations across all tested concentrations. The 2-hour labelling condition was validated across five independent experiments and was therefore applied in all subsequent analyses.

Prussian Blue Staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA), stained with potassium ferrocyanide and HCl (Sigma Aldrich, HT20, MO, USA) for 30 minutes, and imaged in five random fields at 60× magnification using a Leica DFC420C digital camera. Cell Viability

Cell viability was assessed using a bioluminescent adenosine triphosphate (ATP) assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 1×104 cells per well in 100 µL of culture medium in a 96-well plate and incubated overnight. The next day, media was replaced with defined concentration of SPION labelling solution in Table 2. Control groups for ProMag and VivoTrax consisted of medium only, without SPIONs. For SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol, two control conditions were included: medium with TAs but without labelling solution, and medium alone. To detect potential background, cell-free wells were supplemented with defined concentrations of the labelling solutions. After a two-hour incubation at 37 °C, the labelling solution was aspirated, and the cells were washed thoroughly. CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, G7571, WI, USA) was then added, and luminescence was measured in triplicate using a microplate reader (Fluostar Omega, Ortenberg, Germany). Cell viability was expressed relative to control cells incubated in medium without SPIONs or TAs. To assess potential delayed cytotoxic effects, the same experimental setup was repeated to evaluate cell viability 24- and 48-hours post-labelling, with all measurements performed in three technical repeats.

Magnetic Particle Imaging

Cells (2.5×106) were labelled with ProMag (20 µg/mL) or VivoTrax (240 µg/mL) for 2 hours, washed, trypsinised, and counted. Samples were prepared with a constant cell concentration by sequentially halving the total cell quantity and corresponding volume (from 1×106 to ~2×103 cells) and imaged individually in a 7-well custom holder using a MomentumTM MPI system (45 kHz drive field, 3 T/m selection gradient; Magnetic Insight, CA, USA). The applied drive field amplitudes were set at 20 and 26 mT along the x- and z-axes, respectively. Imaging was performed with a field of view of 120×60 mm2, and each acquisition lasted 2.2 minutes. Each sample was imaged individually to avoid potential signal contamination from adjacent samples.

Image Analysis

Regions of interest (ROI) were defined using a 5×SD background threshold.22 Two key sensitivity metrics were analyzed: Maximum Intensity and Total Intensity. Maximum Intensity was defined as the highest recorded signal within the ROI, while Total Intensity was calculated as the sum of the MPI signal within the ROI. The profile of the MPI signal through the center of the sample along the x- and z-axes was fitted with a Lorentzian function, and the spatial resolution was assessed using the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of fitted curves (Further detail provided in supplementary method 1). The number of labelled cells corresponding to the lower detection limit was assessed using MPI measurements of sequentially halved cell numbers. The detection limit was defined as the lowest number of cells below which the fitting of the Lorentzian function failed to converge.

Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) Iron Content Analysis

Following MPI, cell pellets were digested in 0.54 mL hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) for 24 hours, then diluted with deionized water to obtain a final concentration of 2% HCl and analyzed using an Agilent Technologies 5110 ICP-OES system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

Transmission Electron Microscopic (TEM) Evaluation

Cells were seeded onto a 6-well culture plate and labelled with ProMag or VivoTrax SPIONs as described above. Cells were washed three times, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2.-7.4) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, secondary fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) was performed for 1 h. Then, the cells were further processed using standard sample preparation procedures, as detailed in supplementary method 2. The samples were examined using TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Bio-TWIN) and imaged with a Gatan Rio 16 CMOS camera.

Analysis of Cellular Uptake Mechanisms of SPIONs

To investigate the mechanisms of SPION internalization, two complementary assays were performed: low-temperature inhibition, and pharmacological blockade of endocytic pathways in the same experimental setup used for labelling analysis. For low-temperature inhibition, MSCs were pre-chilled at 4 °C or maintained at 37 °C before exposure to ProMag (20 µg/mL) or VivoTrax (240 µg/mL) SPIONs.23 Following a 2-hour incubation, cells were washed, fixed, and stained with Prussian blue to assess iron uptake. For pharmacological inhibition, MSCs were seeded on 24-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The next day, cells were washed with HBSS (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) and pre-treated for 1 hour with either 100 µM amiloride hydrochloride (AH; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) or 3 µM chlorpromazine hydrochloride (CH; ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA), inhibitors of macropinocytic and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, respectively.24 Cells were then incubated with ProMag or VivoTrax SPIONs for 2 hours in the continued presence of the inhibitors, followed by Prussian blue staining to determine labelling efficiency. All experiments were performed in three technical repeats for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.5.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparisons test or the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cell Labelling and Prussian Blue Staining

Firstly, the cells were incubated for 18-hour with the defined concentrations of SPIONs. Under these conditions, 20 µg Fe/mL ProMag, 240 µg Fe/mL VivoTrax, and 125 µg Fe/mL SynoMag-D labelled more than 90% of the cells, while the highest concentration of Ferumoxytol (400 µg Fe/mL) labelled only 40% of MSCs (Supplementary Figure 1). For Ferumoxytol and SynoMag-D, cell labelling required the presence of transfection agents (TAs). Specifically, labelling efficiency >70% was achieved using 240 µg/mL protamine sulphate (PS) and 8 IU/mL heparin, whereas labelling efficiency remained below this threshold in the absence of TAs or at lower TA concentrations (60 µg/mL PS and 2 IU/mL heparin). To determine whether this level of labelling efficiency could be achieved in a shorter timeframe, the incubation time was reduced to 4 hours. Under these conditions, higher SPION concentrations were required to reach >90% labelling efficiency: SynoMag-D required 250 µg/mL, whereas ProMag maintained >90% efficiency even at the lowest tested concentration. VivoTrax labelling efficiency decreased with 4-h incubation, reaching only 75% at the highest tested concentration, compared to 93% after 18 hours. Ferumoxytol showed minimal labelling, peaking at 24% efficiency at 100 µg/mL (Supplementary Figure 2).

When the incubation time was further reduced to 2 hours, ProMag achieved 97% labelling efficiency at 20 µg/mL, whereas VivoTrax, SynoMag-D, and Ferumoxytol reached 72%, 73%, and 31% at 240, 500, and 200 µg/mL, respectively (Figure 1a–d). At this time point, control groups of SynoMag-D (Figure 1c) and Ferumoxytol (Figure 1d) in the presence of TAs exhibited a bubbly appearance, likely due to complexes formed between PS and heparin covering the cell surface. This appearance was not observed in the absence of TAs (Figures 1e and f). At the highest concentration of SynoMag-D (500 µg Fe/mL) and Ferumoxytol (400 µg Fe/mL) in the absence of TAs, no labelling was observed (Figure 1e and f).

Cell Viability

ATP-based assays showed that at all tested concentrations and post-labelling time points, VivoTrax-labelled cells showed no loss of viability (Figure2a). In contrast, ProMag generally showed dose-dependent toxicity, with highest viability at 20 µg/mL (90%, 71% and 84% at 0, 24h and 48h post labelling, respectively) (Figure 2b). At 24- and 48-hours post-labelling, significant reductions in viability were observed at all tested doses, except for 20 ug/mL at 48h. These data indicate that increasing ProMag concentration can lead to significant loss of viability in the post-labelling period. The presence of TAs significantly reduced cell viability for SynoMag-D in all groups, including the TA-only control (Figures 2c). Similarly, Ferumoxytol exhibited a significant reduction in cell viability in the presence of TAs, except for the highest nanoparticle concentrations at the 0-hour time point (Figures 2d).

MPI and ICP Iron Content Analysis

Due to the reduced cell viability associated with the use of TAs required to enhance cellular internalization of SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol, only ProMag (20 µg/mL) and VivoTrax (240 µg/mL) were included in subsequent MPI experiments. Prussian Blue staining confirmed labelling efficiencies of 93% for ProMag and 76% for VivoTrax following a two-hour incubation period (Supplementary Figure 3). Labelled cells were aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes in decreasing numbers using two-fold serial dilutions, and 2D MPI projection images were captured for each dilution (Figure 3a and b). The detection limit was defined as the minimum number of cells at which the Lorentzian fit no longer converged, which happens when the signal amplitude is too similar the noise amplitude. Using this criterion, the minimum detectable cell numbers were 7,812 for ProMag and 15,625 for VivoTrax. The FWHM values of VivoTrax were found to be significantly shorter than the ones of ProMag (X-FWHM: 11.04 mm vs 9.82 mm and Z-FWHM: 7.99 mm vs 6.68 mm) indicating superior spatial resolution for VivoTrax (Figure 3c and d).

At the highest tested cell number (1 × 106), total iron content was 11.6 µg (11.60 pg/cell) for ProMag and 5.03 µg (5.03 pg/cell) for VivoTrax. For MPI signal analysis of both ProMag and VivoTrax, the maximum intensity (r2=0.99 for both) and total intensity (r2=0.99 for both) were linearly correlated with the number of labelled cells (Figure 4a and b). ProMag-labelled cells exhibited significantly higher maximum and total MPI signal intensities compared with VivoTrax-labelled cells (Figure 4a and b), reflecting greater overall MPI detectability of the cells driven primarily by higher intracellular iron loading. Linear regression analysis of total MPI signal intensity versus cell number showed slopes of 0.0525 for ProMag-labelled cells and 0.0225 for VivoTrax-labelled cells. Accordingly, ProMag-labelled cells generated approximately 2.33-fold higher total signal intensities per unit cell than VivoTrax-labelled cells. Notably, this ratio closely matches the measured ratio of intracellular iron content per cell (~2.3), strongly supporting the conclusion that MPI detectability is predominantly driven by intracellular iron loading. In contrast, when MPI signal was normalized to the amount of internalized iron, VivoTrax-labelled cells generated a higher maximum MPI signal per unit of iron (Figure 4c), indicating superior signal efficiency and higher intrinsic sensitivity per unit of iron despite lower total iron uptake. Analysis of Cellular Uptake Mechanisms of SPIONs

Cellular uptake of both ProMag and VivoTrax was significantly reduced at 4 °C relative to 37 °C by 81.5% and 69.3%, respectively (Figure 5a). At 37 C, pharmacological inhibition with either AH or CH did not significantly affect the labelling efficiency of ProMag. In contrast, treatment with CH and AH reduced VivoTrax labelling efficiency at 37 °C by approximately 39% and 7% respectively. (Figure 5b). Although these reductions did not reach statistical significance, this is likely a reflection of the limited statistical power in this analysis.

TEM analysis of VivoTrax-labelled cells showed aggregated particles were inside the endocytotic vesicles. VivoTrax SPIONs were also detected in the cytoplasm, either freely dispersed or localized within various cellular compartments such as mitochondria and the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (Figure 6a). TEM analysis of ProMag particles showed that they were substantially larger than the VivoTrax particles. While not necessarily aggregated, they were often found in close proximity to each other and were predominantly observed in the cytoplasm, typically opposite the nuclear region (Figure 6b).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated four commercially available SPIONs—ProMag, VivoTrax, SynoMag-D, and Ferumoxytol—for labelling mMSCs for MPI. ProMag and VivoTrax emerged as the most promising candidates due to their high labelling efficiency and minimal impact on cell viability. ProMag generated a higher MPI signal per cell compared to VivoTrax, indicating greater sensitivity for detecting small cell populations. In contrast, VivoTrax exhibited superior spatial resolution, a critical factor for accurate signal separation and precise determination of the biodistribution of labelled cells. SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol required TAs to promote uptake into MSCs but the TAs compromised cell viability making these SPIONs unsuitable for further analysis.

Since prolonged SPION exposure can impair MSC viability and function, short incubation times are preferable, particularly for clinical applications requiring rapid and scalable protocols. Initial experiments at 18-, 4-, and 2-hour revealed that ProMag consistently achieved >90% labelling efficiency at the lowest concentration (20 µg Fe/mL), regardless of incubation duration. In contrast, VivoTrax required ≥240 µg Fe/mL to achieve comparable labelling efficiency after an 18-hour incubation, and this efficiency decreased with shorter incubation times. SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol showed no detectable labelling without TAs (PS and heparin). SPION uptake is influenced by physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles, particularly surface coatings that modulate colloidal stability and cellular interaction.23,25 ProMag, with its carboxyl-functionalized polystyrene coating and large hydrodynamic diameter (~1 µm), may benefit from enhanced sedimentation and increased surface contact with cells, thereby facilitating endocytosis.20 Conversely, SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol labelling improved only in the presence of TAs following a prolonged 18-h incubation. PS, a cationic TA, can reverse the net negative surface charge of SPIONs, promoting electrostatic interactions with the cell membrane and leading to the formation of iron–PS nanocomplexes.12 Under light microscopy, these complexes had a turbid appearance (cloudy appearance to naked eye) (Figure 1c and d). Such complexes may adhere to the cell surface or remain extracellular, with cellular internalization being more likely to occur during extended incubation. However, the increased hydrodynamic size and aggregation of SPION-TA complexes may impair MPI signal due to restricted Brownian motion, as previously reported in cancer cells.12,21

Cell viability of SPION-labelled MSCs was evaluated longitudinally at 0, 24, and 48 h post-labelling to assess delayed cytotoxic effects of intracellular iron, which is particularly relevant given that MSC-mediated therapeutic effects often occur hours to days after administration.26 VivoTrax labelling did not result in any significant loss of viability across all tested concentrations or time points, indicating excellent cytocompatibility. ProMag labelling showed no significant viability loss immediately after labelling (0 h); however, a concentration-dependent reduction in viability became evident at higher Fe concentrations after 24 and 48 h, suggesting delayed effects of intracellular iron load. In contrast, SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol induced significant reductions in MSC viability at all time points when used with transfection agents (TAs), including control conditions containing TAs alone. These findings indicate that the observed cytotoxicity is primarily TA-mediated rather than SPION-specific, consistent with previously reported TA-induced membrane disruption.27,28 Notably, in iron-free controls, unbound TAs may interact more freely with cell membranes, increasing cytotoxicity. At the highest Ferumoxytol concentration (400 µg Fe/mL), the lack of a significant viability reduction further suggests partial TA saturation by SPIONs, limiting membrane interaction. Accordingly, ProMag and VivoTrax preserved acceptable viability thresholds (>70% and >90%, respectively) at the lowest effective concentrations of 20 µg Fe/mL for ProMag, and 240 µg Fe/mL for VivoTrax following 2 h incubation, underscoring the need to balance labelling efficiency with cytocompatibility to maintain MSC function.

Based on the labelling efficiency results, SynoMag-D and Ferumoxytol required TAs to achieve effective mMSC labelling; however, this requirement was associated with increased cytotoxicity and the potential for altered MPI signal characteristics. Consequently, both SPIONs were excluded from subsequent analyses, including MPI evaluation. MPI characterization confirmed that there was a linear correlation between the number of labelled cells and total signal intensity for both ProMag and VivoTrax as it was shown in previous studies.8,21,29 ProMag-labelled cells generated higher MPI signal intensity and detection sensitivity than VivoTrax-labelled cells, attributable to greater intracellular iron loading (~11.6 vs ~5.03 pg/cell), which closely matched the corresponding signal increase (2.30-fold vs 2.33-fold). However, when normalized to iron content, VivoTrax showed higher peak MPI signal per unit iron, while total signal per iron was comparable between both SPIONs. This indicates that ProMag enhances overall sensitivity via higher iron uptake, whereas VivoTrax produces a more concentrated signal with improved spatial resolution, as confirmed by significantly better FWHM. Overall, these findings highlight a trade-off between sensitivity (ProMag) and spatial resolution (VivoTrax). This might be ascribed to physicochemical characteristics of the iron core and its MPI field response, which can be influenced by aggregation state, compartmentalization, and intracellular viscosity, all of which affect Néel and Brownian relaxation mechanisms.30 The minimum cell detection limits for the MSCs labelled with ProMag and VivoTrax were determined as 7,812 and 15,625 cells, respectively. Similarly, a recent comparative study using human adipose stromal cells reported that ProMag generated higher MPI signal intensity and sensitivity, but at the expense of reduced spatial resolution relative to VivoTrax.18 In another study, detection limits of 4T1 cancer cells labelled with VivoTrax was measured as 8000 cells with 2D MPI analysis.31 This variation could be related to phagocytic activity and intracellular fate of the internalized nanoparticles and indicates the labelling efficiencies of the cells varies greatly depending on cell type.

SPION internalization by cells is predominantly mediated by endocytosis, a temperature-dependent process that is effectively inhibited below 10.23,32 Consistent with this, our low-temperature assays confirmed that both ProMag and VivoTrax are internalized via active, temperature-sensitive pathways. Endocytosis occurs through several mechanisms, including clathrin-mediated, caveolae-mediated, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis.28 While no single uptake pathway can be universally assigned to nanoparticles, particle size, surface chemistry, and the presence of targeting ligands collectively influence the dominant internalization route. The specific endocytic pathway is particularly relevant for magnetic nanoparticles, as macropinocytosis is often associated with reduced lysosomal trafficking, whereas clathrin-mediated endocytosis typically leads to lysosomal fusion and potential particle degradation, which may compromise magnetic properties and MPI performance.28 Therefore, identifying the uptake mechanism is critical for preserving intracellular magnetization. To investigate the internalization routes, pharmacological inhibition was performed using amiloride hydrochloride (AH, 100 µM) to block macropinocytosis and chlorpromazine hydrochloride (CH, 3 µM) to inhibit clathrin-mediated endocytosis.24 Neither inhibitor significantly reduced ProMag uptake, suggesting that its internalization does not rely on macropinocytosis or clathrin-mediated pathways. Given the relatively large size of ProMag particles (~1 µm), phagocytosis represents a plausible uptake mechanism, consistent with reports that particles >200 nm are preferentially internalized via phagocytic pathways.28,33 In contrast, VivoTrax internalization was reduced by approximately 39% following chlorpromazine treatment, indicating partial dependence on clathrin-mediated endocytosis. However, incomplete inhibition suggests the involvement of additional uptake mechanisms. Further studies employing inhibitors of caveolae-mediated endocytosis and phagocytosis are therefore warranted to fully delineate the internalization pathways of these SPIONs.

TEM evaluation of labelled cells showed that both particles were mostly localized in endosomes, reinforcing the interpretation that our labelling strategy is achieved by active transport rather than passive absorption. However, TEM revealed distinct intracellular localization and aggregation patterns. ProMag particles were mainly present as individual endosomal particles, whereas VivoTrax showed a higher degree of aggregation within endocytic vesicles and distribution across compartments including mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum. These differences may influence MPI performance, as larger aggregates in VivoTrax could restrict rotational freedom and attenuate signal. In line with this, several studies have shown that the MPI signal tends to decrease after internalization, despite strong signals from free SPIONs.12,20,29,30,34 This highlights the importance of considering not only uptake but also intracellular behavior when selecting SPIONs for MPI applications.

The commercial SPIONs compared in this study have been used for MPI tracking in previous studies;20,35 however, they were originally developed for other purposes, such as MRI contrast agents or as medicinal supplements like Ferumoxytol, rather than for MPI, which operates based on different physical principles to MRI. Therefore, this comparison of SPIONs provides a valuable starting point for designing new SPIONs that can fully exploit the potential of MPI in future studies. A limitation of this study is that all experiments were performed using a non-human cell line. Since SPION uptake, intracellular processing, and labelling efficiency may vary across species and cell types, the findings may not fully represent the behaviour of clinically relevant human cells. Future studies should therefore validate these results in human-derived cells intended for clinical cell-tracking applications to better assess the translational potential of the identified SPION candidates. In addition, this study focused primarily on the cytocompatibility of the tested SPIONs. The effects of SPION labelling on key biological properties, including stem cell phenotype, differentiation potential, migration capacity, immunomodulatory function, and antioxidant activity, were not evaluated. As these characteristics are critical for the therapeutic application of labelled cells, their assessment represents an important area for future investigation. Furthermore, special attention is required for their in vivo application, which presents unique challenges such as signal reduction after administration due to cell dispersion, positional variability, or changes in the tissue environment.20 Moreover, in vivo imaging performance may not fully reflect in vitro results due to differences in aggregation, compartmentalization, or degradation pathways. Thus, the lack of in vivo analysis can be considered one of the limitations of our study. Furthermore, the removal of unbound SPIONs after labelling is critical. Larger particles, such as ProMag, tend to adhere more strongly to the cell membrane than do smaller particles, which may result in their detachment post-transplantation and uptake by host tissue cells. Therefore, an effective method for washing off unbound particles must also be carefully considered.

Conclusion

Optimizing SPION labelling is essential to fully exploit MPI for cell tracking, balancing signal intensity and spatial resolution. Among the evaluated SPIONs, ProMag achieved higher labelling efficiency, intracellular iron uptake, and MPI signal per cell, making it well suited for sensitive detection of low cell numbers, with signal largely driven by iron content. In contrast, VivoTrax showed higher signal per unit iron and superior spatial resolution, highlighting the influence of particle-specific properties and intracellular behaviour on signal quality. These findings highlight the need to balance signal intensity and spatial resolution in SPION selection. Accordingly, the choice of SPION should depend on whether sensitivity or spatial resolution is prioritized. Preliminary mechanistic studies suggest that ProMag internalization likely occurs via phagocytosis and/or caveolae-mediated endocytosis, whereas VivoTrax uptake is partially dependent on clathrin-mediated endocytosis; however, these pathways require further investigation. A deeper understanding of SPION-specific internalization pathways and intracellular fate is essential for optimizing magnetic relaxation and overall MPI performance. Long-term in vivo evaluation of labelled cells is also needed to translate these findings into practical applications. Overall, this work highlights a central trade-off in MPI-based cell tracking: intracellular iron loading determines signal strength, whereas particle-specific properties dictate spatial resolution. Achieving an optimal balance between sensitivity and image quality will require joint consideration of both factors, and will inform the design of next-generation SPION tracers for improved cellular imaging.
