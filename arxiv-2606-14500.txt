Local convergence of Sussmann’s infinite product for analytic vector fields
Jérémy Le Borgne, Frédéric Marbach

Abstract
In 1986, Sussmann proposed an expansion of the Chen series as an infinite product of exponentials of Hall basis elements multiplied by coefficients defined through a simple induction. He proved that this product converges locally in time for bilinear control systems associated with matrices or bounded operators. In this note, we prove that Sussmann’s product also converges locally in time for control-affine systems driven by analytic vector fields. The difficulty is that one must keep track of the structure of the involved Lie brackets, as the natural decay of the explicit coefficients is not enough to counterbalance the natural growth of the iterated Lie brackets.



1
Introduction

1.1
Sussmann’s infinite product

Sussmann’s infinite product is a factorization of the Chen series into an ordered product of exponentials indexed by a Hall basis.
Its interest is twofold: it preserves the Lie structure of the dynamics and its coefficients are given by an explicit induction.
We recall the construction briefly, referring to
[
3
, Section 2]
for the basic definitions and more details.

Let
I
be a finite set, and let
X=\{X_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be a finite alphabet of non-commuting indeterminates.
We denote by
\mathcal{A}(X)
the free associative algebra of non-commutative polynomials in
X
, and by
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
its completion, consisting of formal non-commutative power series.
Similarly,
\mathcal{L}(X)
denotes the free Lie algebra generated by
X
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(X)
its completed counterpart.

We consider the following differential equation on
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
(see e.g.
[
24
, Section 3]
):

\dot{S}(t)=S(t)\left(\sum_{i\in I}u_{i}(t)X_{i}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad S(0)=1,

(1.1)

where
u=(u_{i})_{i\in I}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{I})
is the input of the system.

The most direct representation of its solution is the Chen series
[
7
,
8
]
, obtained by repeated integration of (
1.1
):

S(t)=\sum_{\omega\in I^{*}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}u_{\omega}\right)X_{\omega}

(1.2)

where
I^{*}
is the free monoid on
I
, and, for any
\omega=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in I^{*}
,
X_{\omega}:=X_{i_{1}}\dotsb X_{i_{k}}
and

\int_{0}^{t}u_{\omega}:=\int_{0<\tau_{1}<\dotsb<\tau_{k}<t}u_{i_{1}}(\tau_{1})\dotsb u_{i_{k}}(\tau_{k}),

(1.3)

with the convention that
X_{\emptyset}=1
and
\int_{0}^{t}u_{\emptyset}=1
.
In rough paths theory, this object is known as the
signature
of the bounded-variation path
U(t):=(\int_{0}^{t}u_{i})_{i\in I}
(see e.g.
[
18
, Ch. 2]
or
[
9
, Exercise 2.1]
).
An advantage of (
1.2
) is that the coefficients (
1.3
) are easy to compute.
Its drawback is that it is written in the associative algebra and therefore does not display the Lie structure of the equation.

The Magnus expansion
[
19
]
gives a complementary point of view.
Given a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{L}(X)
, one can prove that there exist coefficients
\zeta_{b}(t,u)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

S(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\zeta_{b}(t,u)b\right).

(1.4)

This representation is Lie-theoretic and much sparser than the Chen series.
However, one does not know easy formulas for the
coordinates of the first kind

\zeta_{b}
.

A classical alternative, introduced by Wei and Norman
[
28
]
in the finite-dimensional setting, is to choose an order on
\mathcal{B}
and seek a factorization

S(t)=\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{b\in\mathcal{B}}}\exp(\xi_{b}(t,u)b),

(1.5)

where the
\xi_{b}(t,u)\in\mathbb{R}
are called
coordinates of the second kind
.
In finite dimension this is a local product of finitely many exponentials.
In the free Lie algebra, the product is infinite.
Sussmann proved in
[
26
]
that, when
\mathcal{B}
is a particular type of basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
called a Hall set (see
Definition

2.1
), this infinite product is meaningful at the formal level and the coefficients
\xi_{b}
are given by a very nice explicit inductive formula (see
Definition

2.6
).

We refer to
[
3
,
14
,
15
]
for more detailed comparisons of the above expansions.

1.2
Convergence for real-analytic vector fields

We now pass from formal series to control systems driven by vector fields.
Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
be real-analytic vector fields defined in a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We consider the control-affine system

\dot{x}(t)=\sum_{i\in I}u_{i}(t)f_{i}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad x(0)=p,

(1.6)

where
u=(u_{i})_{i\in I}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{I})
is a control. This class includes systems with drift by singling out an index
0\in I
and setting
u_{0}\equiv 1
.
Whenever it is well-defined, the unique absolutely continuous solution to (
1.6
) is denoted by
x(t;u,p)
.

Representation formulas for the nonlinear system (
1.6
) can be derived from formal formulas for the linear abstract system (
1.1
); see
[
3
, Section 4.1]
for a short tutorial.
This technique was introduced in
[
1
]
and is also used for the design of numerical splitting methods (see e.g.
[
5
]
).
However, this passage involves convergence issues, which are sometimes delicate.
The purpose of this note is to prove that Sussmann’s product does converge locally for real-analytic vector fields.
This gives a positive answer to the question raised in
[
3
, Open Problem 135]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
I
be a finite non-empty set.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open neighborhood of
0
and
(f_{i})_{i\in I}\in(C^{\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{I}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Hall set on
X=\{X_{i}\mid i\in I\}
.
There exists
\eta>0
such that, for any
T>0
,
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{I})
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|p|\leq\eta
and
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\eta
, and for any
t\in[0,T]
,

x(t;u,p)=\left(\overset{\longrightarrow}{\prod_{b\in\mathcal{B}}}e^{\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}}\right)(p),

(1.7)

where
\xi_{b}(t,u)\in\mathbb{R}
is a coefficient defined in
Definition

2.6
,
f_{b}\in C^{\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an iterated Lie bracket defined in
Definition

3.4
, and
e^{\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}}
denotes the time-one flow of the autonomous vector field
\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}
(which is well-defined under the given smallness assumption).
The infinite product is defined as a net over finite subsets and converges uniformly on a small ball (see
Section

5.1
).

Remark 1.2
.

The directions of the ordered products in (
1.5
) and (
1.7
) are opposite because their is an inversion in the passage from formal series and linear operators to compositions of flows of nonlinear vector fields.

Example 1.3
.

Take
I=\{0,1\}
and
d=2
.
Consider the following real-analytic vector fields

f_{0}(x):=\left(0,\frac{x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}(x):=(1,0).

(1.8)

To illustrate the computations, take controls
u_{0}(t)=1
and
u_{1}(t)=2t
.
Then

\dot{x}_{1}(t)=2t,\qquad\dot{x}_{2}(t)=\frac{x_{1}(t)}{1-x_{1}(t)}.

(1.9)

Starting from
p=(0,0)
, explicit integration gives, for
0\leq t<1
,

x_{1}(t)=t^{2},\qquad x_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{s^{2}}{1-s^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{2k+1}=\operatorname{artanh}(t)-t.

(1.10)

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a length-compatible Hall set with
X_{1}<X_{0}
(as in
[
11
,
12
]
, see
[
2
, Section 6]
), and set

h_{k}:=\operatorname{ad}_{X_{1}}^{k}(X_{0}),\qquad k\geq 1.

(1.11)

Then
h_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
for all
k\geq 1
, and
X_{1}<X_{0}<h_{1}<h_{2}<\dotsb
up to possible interleaving of Hall brackets of the same length.
The relevant Sussmann coefficients (see
Definition

2.6
) are

\xi_{X_{0}}(t)=t,\qquad\xi_{X_{1}}(t)=t^{2},\qquad\xi_{h_{k}}(t)=\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{t}s^{2k}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)k!}.

(1.12)

On the vector-field side, one has

f_{0}=\frac{x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}},\qquad f_{1}=\partial_{x_{1}},\qquad f_{h_{k}}=\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{k}(f_{0})=\frac{k!}{(1-x_{1})^{k+1}}\partial_{x_{2}},\qquad k\geq 1.

(1.13)

All other Hall brackets give zero vector fields: the vertical fields

f_{0},f_{h_{1}},f_{h_{2}},\ldots
commute with each other, and bracketing with

f_{1}
only differentiates in
x_{1}
.

Hence, after removing identity factors, the truncation of Sussmann’s product at length
N+1
is

G_{N}(t)=e^{t^{2}f_{1}}\circ e^{tf_{0}}\circ e^{\xi_{h_{1}}(t)f_{h_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ e^{\xi_{h_{N}}(t)f_{h_{N}}}.

(1.14)

Since the factors
e^{\xi_{h_{k}}(t)f_{h_{k}}}
are applied at
x_{1}=0
and

f_{h_{k}}(0)=k!\partial_{x_{2}}
, while
f_{0}(0)=0
, we obtain

G_{N}(t)(0,0)=\left(t^{2},\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\right).

(1.15)

Therefore, for
0\leq t<1
,

\left(\overset{\longrightarrow}{\prod_{b\in\mathcal{B}}}e^{\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}}\right)(0,0)=\left(t^{2},\operatorname{artanh}(t)-t\right),

(1.16)

which equals (
1.10
).

1.3
Known results and main difficulty

The elements
b
of a Hall set
\mathcal{B}
have a length
|b|\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
; see
Section

2
. For each
n\geq 1
, there are at most
|X|^{n}
elements of length
n
.

Sussmann proved
Theorem

1.1
in the linear case, namely when
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x
for matrices
A_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, and more generally for bounded operators on a Banach space
[
26
]
.
In that setting the convergence mechanism is straightforward.
If
M_{A}:=\max_{i\in I}\lVert A_{i}\rVert
, then a direct induction gives

\lVert A_{b}\rVert\leq(2M_{A})^{|b|}.

(1.17)

Moreover, the inductive definition of
\xi_{b}
(see
Definition

2.6
) yields

|\xi_{b}(t,u)|\leq\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}^{|b|}.

(1.18)

Together with the bound on the number of brackets of a fixed length, these estimates imply that

\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\xi_{b}(t,u)A_{b}\rVert<+\infty

(1.19)

for small enough
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}
, which entails the convergence of the infinite product (see
[
3
, Section 5.4.2]
).

This smallness assumption cannot be removed. Sussmann constructed a counterexample, recalled in
[
3
, Proposition 132]
, showing that convergence may fail for large controls or large times.
This counterexample relies on a Hall set within which there exists a sequence
b_{k}
such that
|b_{k}|\to\infty
and
\xi_{b_{k}}(t,u)\geq c_{0}(t/t_{0})^{|b_{k}|}
and matrices such that
\lVert A_{b_{k}}\rVert\geq c_{1}^{|b_{k}|}
.
Hence the products
\xi_{b}(t,u)A_{b}
do not even tend to
0
.

Such an example might hint towards a non-convergence result for real-analytic vector fields (even under appropriate smallness assumptions).
Indeed, for nonlinear vector fields, the associated differential operators are unbounded, so one cannot even hope for a geometric bound for
f_{b}
.
For example, it is known that the Magnus expansion (
1.4
) does not converge, even locally, in very weak senses, for real-analytic vector fields (see
[
3
, Section 5.2.3]
).
As we mentioned in
[
3
, Section 5.4.3]
, the best bound one can hope based solely on the length of the bracket is of the form

\lVert f_{b}\rVert\leq C^{|b|}|b|!.

(1.20)

Thus the proof of
Theorem

1.1
cannot rely on length alone.
It must exploit the Hall decomposition of each bracket.
The main difficulty of this paper is to show that the decay of Sussmann’s coefficients and the growth of the corresponding analytic Lie brackets compensate each other once the combinatorial structure of the Hall set is kept throughout the estimates (see in particular
Propositions

2.7
and
3.5
and
Section

4.3
, which contain the main arguments of this paper).

1.4
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.

•

In
Section

2
, we recall Hall sets and prove estimates for Sussmann’s coordinates
\xi_{b}
.

•

In
Section

3
, we establish analytic estimates for the iterated Lie brackets
f_{b}
.

•

The balance between the decaying coefficients and the growing brackets is encoded in
Section

4
by a logarithmic sequence indexed by the Hall set, for which we prove a linear estimate.

•

In
Section

5
, we combine these bounds to prove convergence of the infinite product of flows.

2
Hall sets

We recall the definition and basic properties of Hall sets which will be used in the sequel.
We define and estimate the coordinates of the second kind
\xi_{b}
.

We follow Viennot’s convention of
[
27
]
for Hall sets, but other conventions exist (e.g. in
[
21
]
).

2.1
Definitions

Let
X
be a set.
Let
\operatorname{Br}(X)
denote the free magma over
X
.
Elements of
\operatorname{Br}(X)
can be represented as binary trees, e.g.
(X_{0},(X_{0},X_{1}))
, when
X_{0},X_{1}\in X
.
For
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
,
|b|\geq 1
denotes its length, defined by
|b|=1
for
b\in X
, and
|(a,b)|=|a|+|b|
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Hall set
is a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\operatorname{Br}(X)
endowed with a total order
<
such that

•

X\subset\mathcal{B}
,

•

for all
a,b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
,
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}
iff
a,b\in\mathcal{B}
,
a<b
and (
b\in X
or
b=(b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})
with
b^{\prime}\leq a
),

•

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}
then
a<(a,b)
.

Note that, in
[
26
]
, Sussmann used a stronger definition of Hall sets, closer to the original works of
[
11
,
12
]
, where one requires that the order is compatible with length (i.e. where
a<b\Rightarrow|a|\leq|b|
).
This simplifies some arguments, but we proved in
[
3
]
that his infinite product is still well-defined for non-length-compatible Hall sets, as suggested by
[
13
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Hall set on
X
.
For any
b\in\mathcal{B}
, either
|b|=1
and
b\in X
, or
|b|>1
and there exists a unique tuple
(a,c,m)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
b=\operatorname{ad}^{m}_{a}(c)
,
a<c
and either
c\in X
or
c=(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})
with
c^{\prime}<a
.
Since
|b|=m|a|+|c|
with
m\geq 1
, one has
|a|<|b|
and
|c|<|b|
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Definition

2.1
, up to iterating the decomposition until the right factor is either in
X
or satisfies the strict inequality
c^{\prime}<a
(not just
c^{\prime}\leq a
).
∎

The fact that length is strictly decreasing in
Lemma

2.2
allows to use such a decomposition as a well-founded induction to define functions on a Hall set
\mathcal{B}
inductively.

This induction process is linked with Lazard elimination
[
16
]
and the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

A
Lazard set
is a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\operatorname{Br}(X)
, with a total order
<
and such that, for every
L\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the set
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}=\{b_{1}<\dotsb<b_{k+1}\}
of elements of
\mathcal{B}
with length at most
L
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &b_{1}\in Y_{0}:=X,\\
&b_{2}\in Y_{1}:=\{\operatorname{ad}_{b_{1}}^{m}(v);m\in\mathbb{N},v\in Y_{0}\setminus\{b_{1}\}\},\\
&\dots\\
&b_{k+1}\in Y_{k}:=\{\operatorname{ad}_{b_{k}}^{m}(v);m\in\mathbb{N},v\in Y_{k-1}\setminus\{b_{k}\}\}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

and
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}\cap Y_{k}=\{b_{k+1}\}
(or, equivalently,
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}\cap Y_{k+1}=\emptyset
).

One has the following equivalence
[
27
, Corollary 1.1]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

A totally ordered subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\operatorname{Br}(X)
is a Hall set if and only if it is a Lazard set.

The main interest of Hall sets or Lazard sets is that they allow to construct bases of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
Indeed, there is a natural map
\textnormal{{e}}:\operatorname{Br}(X)\to\mathcal{L}(X)
defined by induction by
\textnormal{{e}}(x):=x
for
x\in X
and
\textnormal{{e}}((a,b)):=[\textnormal{{e}}(a),\textnormal{{e}}(b)]
.
This leads to the following result
[
27
, Prop. 1.1 and Theorem 1.1]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Hall set on
X
.
Then
\textnormal{{e}}(\mathcal{B})
is a basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Different choices of orders on
\operatorname{Br}(X)
lead to different Hall sets, and thus different bases of
\mathcal{L}(X)
, sometimes with very different properties (see for example
[
2
]
where we give many examples of such orders, and study the structure constants of the associated bases).

2.2
Coordinates of the second kind

We recall the explicit formula defining coordinates of the second kind indexed by a Hall set
\mathcal{B}
on a finite set
X
, due to Sussmann in
[
26
, equation (19)]
, and we prove an inductive bound.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Hall set on
X=\{X_{i}\mid i\in I\}
.
Let
T>0
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{I})
.
We define the associated coordinates of the second kind by induction by

\xi_{X_{i}}(t,u):=\int_{0}^{t}u_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in I\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi_{b}(t,u):=\frac{1}{m!}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{a}^{m}\dot{\xi}_{c}

(2.2)

when
b=\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{m}(c)
is given by the unique representation of
Lemma

2.2
.

For each
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
\xi_{b}(\cdot,u)
is absolutely continuous in time and
\dot{\xi}_{b}(\cdot,u)
exists almost everywhere.

These coordinates satisfy the following estimates.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
T>0
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{I})
.
For
t\in[0,T]
, define

\Xi(t):=\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t)}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i\in I}|u_{i}|.

(2.3)

For any
b\in\mathcal{B}
, one has

\displaystyle|\dot{\xi}_{b}|

\displaystyle\leq e^{\Gamma_{b}}\Xi^{|b|-1}\dot{\Xi}\qquad\text{a.e.\ on }(0,T),

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\xi_{b}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{\Gamma_{b}}}{|b|}\Xi^{|b|}\qquad\quad\text{ on }[0,T],

(2.5)

where
\Gamma:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by setting
\Gamma_{X_{i}}:=0
for
i\in I
and

\Gamma_{b}:=m\Gamma_{a}+\Gamma_{c}-m\ln|a|-\ln(m!)

(2.6)

when
b=\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{m}(c)
is given by the unique representation of
Lemma

2.2
.

Proof.

For each fixed
b\in\mathcal{B}
, (
2.5
) is obtained by integrating (
2.4
) from the initial condition
\xi_{b}(0)=0
.
For
i\in I
,
\dot{\xi}_{X_{i}}=u_{i}
by
Definition

2.6
so (
2.4
) holds.
By
Definition

2.6
for
b=\operatorname{ad}^{m}_{a}(c)
and the induction hypothesis, one has

|\dot{\xi}_{b}|\leq\frac{|\xi_{a}|^{m}}{m!}|\dot{\xi}_{c}|\leq\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{e^{\Gamma_{a}}}{|a|}\right)^{m}e^{\Gamma_{c}}\Xi^{m|a|+|c|-1}\dot{\Xi},

(2.7)

which proves (
2.4
) provided one sets
\Gamma_{b}
as in (
2.6
).
∎

3
Estimates of Lie brackets of analytic vector fields

In this section, we prove estimates on Lie brackets of real-analytic vector fields which we will need to prove the convergence of Sussmann’s infinite product.
Here,
\Omega
denotes an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.1
Analytic norms and elementary estimates

Given
r>0
, we define the space
C^{\omega,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{q})
as the subset of
C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{q})
for which the following norm is finite

\lVert f\rVert_{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{r^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\sup_{\Omega}|\partial^{\alpha}f_{i}|.

(3.1)

The following standard properties of the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{r}
are proved in
[
3
, Lemmas 70 and 71]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Algebra property)
.

For any
r>0
and
f,g\in C^{\omega,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, one has

\lVert fg\rVert_{r}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{r}\lVert g\rVert_{r}.

(3.2)

Lemma 3.2

(Gradient)
.

For any
r>0
,
\rho>0
,
f\in C^{\omega,r+\rho}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
and
1\leq j\leq d
,

\lVert\partial_{j}f\rVert_{r}\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\lVert f\rVert_{r+\rho}.

(3.3)

Given two vector fields
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define their Lie bracket
[f,g]\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

[f,g]:=Dg\cdot f-Df\cdot g=\sum_{j=1}^{d}f_{j}\partial_{j}g-g_{j}\partial_{j}f.

(3.4)

We also define the linear map
\operatorname{ad}_{f}:g\mapsto[f,g]
, for which we have the following estimate.

Lemma 3.3

(Lie brackets)
.

For any
r>0
,
\rho>0
,
f,g\in C^{\omega,r+\rho}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{m}_{f}(g)\rVert_{r}\leq\left(\frac{2m}{\rho}\right)^{m}\lVert f\rVert_{r+\rho}^{m}\lVert g\rVert_{r+\rho}.

(3.5)

Proof.

For
m=1
, the estimate is a consequence of
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
and the choice of norm (
3.1
) which allows to sum over the dimensions.
For
m>1
, one applies the
m=1
estimate
m
times with a radius loss
\rho/m
at each step.
This yields

\begin{split}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{m}_{f}(g)\rVert_{r}&\leq\left(\frac{2m}{\rho}\right)\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{m-1}_{f}g\rVert_{r+\frac{\rho}{m}}\lVert f\rVert_{r+\frac{\rho}{m}}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{2m}{\rho}\right)^{m}\lVert g\rVert_{r+\rho}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lVert f\rVert_{r+j\frac{\rho}{m}},\end{split}

(3.6)

which concludes the proof because the norm (

4.1
A length-based estimate is not enough

A natural strategy to estimate the growth of

This leads to the following estimate, which is insufficient for our purpose, but which we give as an illustration of why such a strategy fails.

Lemma 4.3

For all

,
W_{b}\leq\ln(|b|!)
.

Proof.

From

4.1

W_{b}\leq\Lambda_{|b|}
where

and, for

\Lambda_{n}:=\max\left\{m\Lambda_{a}+\Lambda_{c}+m\ln\frac{n}{a}-\ln(m!)\mid a,c,m\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\enskip ma+c=n\right\}.

Let us prove by strong induction that

Given

((a-1)!)^{m}c!\leq(n-m)!

Moreover, for

(4.4)

Combining (

(a!)^{m}c!\frac{n^{m}}{a^{m}m!}\leq n!

Moreover, equality is achieved in these estimates when

Hence, for all

.
∎

4.3
A universal linear bound

In this section,
X
is finite and nonempty and
\mathcal{B}
is an arbitrary Hall set over

To control the growth of

, we will perform estimates on a kind of “partition function” depending on a regularity parameter

For

\mathrm{P}_{Z}(\beta):=\sum_{b\in Z}p_{b}(\beta)\quad\text{where}\quad p_{b}(\beta):=\frac{1}{|b|}\exp\big(W_{b}-\beta|b|\big).

(4.9)

The natural induction strategy within a Hall set is called “Lazard elimination”.
Let
a,c\in\mathcal{B}
such that
a<c
and (
c\in X
or

The key idea of this section is that, up to small loss in the regularity parameter

from

Proposition 4.7
.

Let

),

Thus, by (

\begin{split}\mathrm{P}_{a^{*}c}(\beta+p_{a}(\beta))&=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m|a|+|c|}e^{W_{\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{m}(c)}}e^{-(\beta+p_{a}(\beta))(m|a|+|c|)}\\

Lemma 4.8

3.1
), we thus have

\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}\rVert_{C^{0}(B_{3\delta})}\leq\delta\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}\rVert_{C^{1}(B_{3\delta})}\leq\frac{\delta}{r}<+\infty.

(5.10)

Hence, for any
t\in[0,T]
, the infinite product of (
1.7
) converges on
B_{\delta}
by
Lemma

5.3
.

We shall also use a similar estimate for the differentiated coordinates.
The derivative estimate in
Proposition

2.7
and the Lie-bracket estimate in
Proposition

3.5
give

\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{\xi}_{b}|\|f_{b}\|_{3r}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq e^{\Gamma_{b}+F_{b}}\left(\frac{2e}{r}\right)^{|b|-1}M_{f}^{|b|}\int_{0}^{t}\Xi^{|b|-1}\dot{\Xi}=\frac{r}{2e}\frac{1}{|b|}e^{W_{b}}\left(\frac{2eM_{f}}{r}\eta\right)^{|b|}.

(5.11)

The right-hand side is summable over
b\in\mathcal{B}
by (
5.7
) and (
5.6
).

Step 2: We prove that the trajectory is defined on
[0,T]
and remains in
B_{\delta}
.

Indeed, as long as the state remains in
B_{2\delta}
, using (
5.6
),

|x(t;u,p)|\leq|p|+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i\in I}|u_{i}|\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{C^{0}(B_{\delta})}\leq|p|+M_{f}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\delta.

(5.12)

The usual continuation argument shows that
x(t;u,p)
is defined on
[0,T]
and remains in
B_{\delta}
.

Step 3: We reduce the result to the convergence of bounded length products.

Let
G(t)
denote the infinite product of the right-hand side of (
1.7
).
For
L\geq 1
, let
G_{L}(t)
be the finite ordered product over
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}
.
Since the finite sets
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}
are cofinal among finite subsets of
\mathcal{B}
, the convergence of
G(t)
already proved implies, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
G_{L}(t)\to G(t)
in
C^{0}(B_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
L\to+\infty
.
Hence it suffices to prove that
G_{L}(t)(p)\to x(t;u,p)
.

Step 4: We construct auxiliary systems by Lazard elimination.

Let
L\geq 1
.
By
Lemma

2.4
,
\mathcal{B}
is a Lazard set.
Using the notations of
Definition

2.3
, write
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}=\{b_{1}<\dotsb<b_{k+1}\}
and let
Y_{0},\dotsc,Y_{k+1}
be the associated Lazard elimination sets.
We use the standard property of Lazard elimination for Hall sets that each
Y_{j}
is a subset of
\mathcal{B}
.
In particular,
Y_{k+1}\subset\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}\cap Y_{k+1}=\emptyset
, hence

Y_{k+1}\subset\{b\in\mathcal{B}:|b|>L\}
.

Define successively

z_{0}(s):=x(s;u,p),

(5.13)

and, for
1\leq j\leq k+1
,

z_{j}(s):=e^{-\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}}\bigl(z_{j-1}(s)\bigr).

(5.14)

Since
x(s;u,p)\in B_{\delta}
and
\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\xi_{b}(s,u)f_{b}\rVert_{C^{0}(B_{3\delta})}\leq\delta
, all these curves remain in
B_{2\delta}
.
Indeed, during each inverse flow the displacement is bounded by the
C^{0}(B_{3\delta})
norm of the corresponding vector field, and the cumulative displacement is controlled by (
5.10
).
In particular, the intermediate flow trajectories remain in
B_{3\delta}
, so the following computations are legitimate.

We claim that, for every
0\leq j\leq k+1
,

\dot{z}_{j}(s)=\sum_{b\in Y_{j}}\dot{\xi}_{b}(s,u)f_{b}(z_{j}(s)),\quad\text{and}\quad z_{j}(0)=p,

(5.15)

for a.e.
s\in(0,T)
, where the series is absolutely convergent in
L^{1}((0,T);C^{1}(B_{2\delta}))
.

For
j=0
, this is exactly the original differential equation (
1.6
), because
Y_{0}=X
and
\dot{\xi}_{X_{i}}=u_{i}
.
Assume (
5.15
) holds at step
j-1
.
From (
5.14
), the chain rule gives, for a.e.
s\in(0,T)
,

\dot{z}_{j}(s)=-\dot{\xi}_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}(z_{j}(s))+\sum_{v\in Y_{j-1}}\dot{\xi}_{v}(s,u)\left(e^{-\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}}\right)_{*}f_{v}(z_{j}(s)).

(5.16)

The term
v=b_{j}
cancels the first term, because the flow of
f_{b_{j}}
preserves
f_{b_{j}}
.
Moreover, the bounds above ensure that
\lVert\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}\rVert_{C^{0}}<\delta
and
\lVert\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}\rVert_{3r}<r/6
.
Thus, by
Lemma

5.4
, for every
v\in Y_{j-1}\setminus\{b_{j}\}
,

\left(e^{-\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)f_{b_{j}}}\right)_{*}f_{v}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\xi_{b_{j}}(s,u)^{m}}{m!}f_{\operatorname{ad}_{b_{j}}^{m}(v)}

(5.17)

with convergence in
C^{\omega,2r}
.
Substituting (
5.17
) in (
5.16
) and recalling
Definition

2.6
and the definition of
Y_{j}
proves (
5.15
).
The absolute convergence required in the claim follows from (
5.11
), since each
Y_{j}
is contained in
\mathcal{B}
.

Step 5: We prove the claimed convergence.

We now apply (
5.15
) at the final step
j=k+1
.
Since
z_{k+1}(0)=p
, (
5.15
), the inclusion
\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}\cap Y_{k+1}=\emptyset
, and (
5.11
) yield

|z_{k+1}(t)-p|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{b\in Y_{k+1}}|\dot{\xi}_{b}|\|f_{b}\|_{C^{0}(B_{2\delta})}\leq\frac{r}{2e}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathcal{B}\\
|b|>L\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|b|}e^{W_{b}}\left(\frac{2eM_{f}}{r}\eta\right)^{|b|}.

(5.18)

The last expression tends to
0
as
L\to+\infty
, as the tail of a convergent positive series.

Finally, the finite product
G_{L}(t)
has a Lipschitz constant bounded independently of
L
.
Indeed, by (
5.10
),

\operatorname{Lip}(G_{L}(t))\leq\exp\left(\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\leq L}}\|\xi_{b}(t,u)f_{b}\|_{C^{1}(B_{3\delta})}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\delta}{r}\right).

(5.19)

By construction,
z_{k+1}(t)
is obtained by applying the inverse finite product to
x(t;u,p)
. Hence

x(t;u,p)=G_{L}(t)\bigl(z_{k+1}(t)\bigr).

(5.20)

Therefore, by (
5.18
) and (
5.19
),

\displaystyle|x(t;u,p)-G_{L}(t)(p)|=|G_{L}(t)(z_{k+1}(t))-G_{L}(t)(p)|\leq\exp\left(\frac{\delta}{r}\right)|z_{k+1}(t)-p|

(5.21)

This proves that
G_{L}(t)(p)\to x(t;u,p)
, which concludes the proof of (
1.7
).
∎

Appendix A
Proof of the Lyndon length lemma

In this section, we prove
Lemma

4.6
of
Section

4.2
.
Let
\varepsilon
denote the empty word.

We shall use the following standard facts about Lyndon words.
First, a nonempty word
f
is Lyndon if and only if it is strictly smaller than each of its nonempty proper suffixes.
Second, if
u,v\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
and
u\prec v
, then
uv\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
v,w\in X^{*}
and
x\prec z\in X
.
If
\ell=vxw\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
, then
vz\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.

Proof.

If
v=\varepsilon
, then
vz=z\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.
We may therefore assume that
v\neq\varepsilon
.

Let
s=v_{2}z
with
v_{2}\in X^{*}
be a nonempty proper suffix of
vz
, and write
v=v_{1}v_{2}
with
v_{1}\in X^{+}
.

Since
v_{2}xw
is a nonempty proper suffix of
\ell
and
x\prec z
, we have
\ell\prec v_{2}xw\prec v_{2}z=s
.

Since
v
is a proper prefix of
\ell
, we have
v\prec\ell
, and therefore
v\prec s
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
vz\succeq s
.
Then
v\prec s\preceq vz
.
Since
vz
is obtained from
v
by appending the single letter
z
, this forces
v
to be a strict prefix of
s
.
Hence
|v|<|s|
.
But
|v|=|v_{1}|+|v_{2}|\geq|v_{2}|+1=|s|
, a contradiction.

Thus
vz\prec s
for every nonempty proper suffix
s
of
vz
, and therefore
vz\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\ell\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
, and let
\ell_{1}
be its longest proper Lyndon prefix.
Write
\ell=\ell_{1}\ell_{2}
.
Assume that
\ell_{1}
is not a prefix of
\ell_{2}
.
Then
\ell_{2}=\bar{\ell}_{1}z
for some possibly empty strict prefix
\bar{\ell}_{1}\in X^{*}
of
\ell_{1}
and some letter
z\in X
.

Proof.

Recall that
\ell_{2}\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
, and that
\ell_{1}\prec\ell_{2}
.
Let
p
be the shortest prefix of
\ell_{2}
such that
\ell_{1}\prec p
.
Such a prefix exists because
\ell_{1}\prec\ell_{2}
.

Since
\ell_{1}
is not a prefix of
\ell_{2}
, the comparison
\ell_{1}\prec p
occurs at the first letter where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
differ.
Hence there exist a possibly empty strict prefix
\bar{\ell}_{1}\in X^{*}
of
\ell_{1}
, letters
x,z\in X
, and a word
r\in X^{*}
, such that
\ell_{1}=\bar{\ell}_{1}xr
,
p=\bar{\ell}_{1}z
,
x\prec z
.
By
Lemma

A.1
,
p=\bar{\ell}_{1}z\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.

If
p
were a proper prefix of
\ell_{2}
, then
\ell_{1}p
would be a proper prefix of
\ell
.
Moreover, since
\ell_{1},p\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
and
\ell_{1}\prec p
, the word
\ell_{1}p
would be Lyndon.
This contradicts the maximality of
\ell_{1}
as the longest proper Lyndon prefix of
\ell
.
Therefore
p=\ell_{2}
, and the claim follows.
∎

The following result can be deduced from
[
20
, Lemma 7.14]
, the formulation given here is better suited for our purpose of proving
Lemma

4.6
.

Corollary A.3
.

Let
\ell\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
, and let
\ell_{1}
be its longest proper Lyndon prefix.
Then there exist an
m\geq 1
, a possibly empty strict prefix
\bar{\ell}_{1}\in X^{*}
of
\ell_{1}
, and a letter
z\in X
such that
\ell=\ell_{1}^{m}\bar{\ell}_{1}z
.

Proof.

Write the standard factorization as
\ell=\ell_{1}L_{1}
, with
L_{1}\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.

We shall use the following observation.
Let
W\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
have longest proper Lyndon prefix
\ell_{1}
, and write
W=\ell_{1}R
with
R\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
.
If
R
has
\ell_{1}
as a prefix, then
\ell_{1}
is the longest proper Lyndon prefix of
R
.
Indeed, any proper Lyndon prefix
q
of
R
with
|q|>|\ell_{1}|
has
\ell_{1}
as a strict prefix, hence
\ell_{1}\prec q
; therefore
\ell_{1}q\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
, contradicting the maximality of
\ell_{1}
in
W
.

Starting with
L_{0}=\ell
, repeat the standard factorization
L_{i-1}=\ell_{1}L_{i}
as long as
L_{i}
has
\ell_{1}
as a prefix.
The observation shows that the hypothesis that
\ell_{1}
is the longest proper Lyndon prefix is preserved at each step.
Since lengths strictly decrease, the process terminates.
Thus, for some
m\geq 1
,
\ell=\ell_{1}^{m}L_{m}
where
L_{m}\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
,
L_{m}
does not have
\ell_{1}
as a prefix, and
\ell_{1}
is the longest proper Lyndon prefix of
L_{m-1}=\ell_{1}L_{m}
.

Applying
Proposition

A.2
to
L_{m-1}
gives
L_{m}=\bar{\ell}_{1}z
for some possibly empty strict prefix
\bar{\ell}_{1}
of
\ell_{1}
and some
z\in X
.
Hence
\ell=\ell_{1}^{m}\bar{\ell}_{1}z
, as claimed.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}\setminus X
, and let
\ell\in\mathrm{Lyn}(X)
be such that
b=\pi(\ell)
.
Let
\ell_{1}
be the longest proper Lyndon prefix of
\ell
.
By
Corollary

A.3
, there exist
m\geq 1
, a possibly empty strict prefix
\bar{\ell}_{1}\in X^{*}
of
\ell_{1}
, and a letter
z\in X
such that
\ell=\ell_{1}^{m}\bar{\ell}_{1}z
.
By the definition of the Lyndon Hall basis, this corresponds to the decomposition
b=\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{m}(c)
, where
a=\pi(\ell_{1})
and
c=\pi(\bar{\ell}_{1}z)
.
Therefore

|c|=|\bar{\ell}_{1}z|=|\bar{\ell}_{1}|+1\leq|\ell_{1}|=|a|,

(A.1)

because
\bar{\ell}_{1}
is a strict prefix of
\ell_{1}
.
∎
