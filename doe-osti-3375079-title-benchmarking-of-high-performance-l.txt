Title: Benchmarking of High-performance Low-temperature Plasma Particle-in-cell Codes for
Modeling Magnetized Plasmas

RESEARCH PERFORMANCE PROGRESS REPORT
 1. What are the major goals and objectives of this project?

The major goals and objectives of this Collaborative Research project was to provide graduate
student and some faculty support to investigate and formulate our theoretical and computer
algorithmic development of magnetized plasmas of direct interest to the Department of Energy
through close coordination telecons and visits to PCRF. The UIUC CHAOS code will also be
ported to clusters at PPPL to provide benchmarking for their PIC codes as well. The
fundamental questions that we will address in this project include understanding what magnetic
field and number density plasma conditions allow one to model electron cooling using polytropic
laws. We will analyze how the polytropic index for expanding magnetic plasma flows depends
on the magnetic field strength and cross-field diffusion mechanisms. Finally, the role of electron
- ion and electron - neutral particle collisions in magnetic plasma flows will be assessed.

 2. What was accomplished under these goals?

During this short project we studied the application of a DC magnetic field to magnetic nozzle
flows. The development and validation of a fully kinetic three-dimensional (3D) code, Cuda-
based Hybrid Approach for Octree Simulations, CHAOS, for magnetized plasma plume
simulations were investigated. The CHAOS code, initially designed for unmagnetized plumes,
now incorporates magnetic field effects, allowing for comprehensive simulations of magnetized
plasma jets in both in-space and in-chamber conditions. Studying 3D simulations of magnetized
plumes is crucial for understanding the intricate processes within magnetic nozzles, including
both ion and electron dynamics—particularly for capturing the electrons’ individual orbital motion
along magnetic field lines and their collective non-Maxwellian behavior during the plume
expansion process. The Boris scheme and a charge-conserving energy-based (CCE) boundary
condition (BC) were implemented to move charged particles under combined electric and
magnetic fields, and to address outflow boundary challenges and achieve steady-state solutions
within finite computational domains. Nanbu’s collision algorithm was also implemented to
accurately simulate Coulomb scattering, particularly in high-density plasma regimes. The new
version of the CHAOS code was tested in collisionless and collisional conditions and validated
against experiments. The effect of injected ion beam profile and inclusion of Coulomb collisions
in the simulation results presented new insights into an understanding of different experimental
results of Little and Choueiri (2016, “Electron cooling in a magnetically expanding plasma,” Phys
Rev Lett 117:225003). In particular, we investigated the role of Coulomb collisions at plasma
densities higher than those reported in the experiment and demonstrate the impact of increased
collisionality through analyses of 3D electron trajectories and electron velocity distribution
functions (EVDFs).

 3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project
 provided?

 The physics of low temperature, magnetized plasmas is complex and not well
 understood. This project permitted the training of a Ph.D graduate student, Mr. Davut
 Vatan Vatansever, in low temperature plasmas through his interactions with his research
 advisor, Dr. Deborah Levin, and Dr. Igor Kaganovich of PCRF/PPPL. In particular,
 because electrons, in contrast to ions, are strongly magnetized their behavior can lead to
 large ExB drifts, ionization, and large density gradients that are still difficult to model in
 large particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations. The main goal of the research was to advance
 the understanding of magnetic nozzles and magnetic lenses by extending a three-
 dimensional, fully kinetic particle-in-cell simulation tool, CHAOS, to magnetized plasmas.
 To achieve this, the student had to learn the key elements of GPU computing and
 parallelization as well as apply the new particle boundary conditions that prevent
 spurious numerical instabilities.

 4. How have the results been disseminated to communities of interest?

 The student has presented his work at conferences and published a journal paper. Two
 more journal papers will most likely be published as well and will be a major part of his
 Ph.D thesis. Deborah Levin presented an invited paper at the 2024 APS/GEC.

V. IMPACT:

a. These are the ﬁrst magnetic nozzle time-accurate PIC simulations to be performed in
three-dimension with a full electron kinetic treatment. The modiﬁcation of the CHAOS
code which includes both PIC/DSMC and PIC/MCC with Coulomb collisions and ionization
will enable modeling where magnetic ﬁelds are important in gridded-ion thruster discharge
chambers and Hall-eEect thrusters. It will also enable direct modeling of electron
cyclotron resonance devices.
b. The ability to develop magnetic nozzles may save mass and cost for small vehicle space-
based propulsion. The use of such population, in turn, provides lower cost access to space for
both commercial and defense applications.
