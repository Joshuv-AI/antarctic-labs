  title: Progress towards a fully 3D, thermodynamically-consistent, constitutive model for volume

 Abstract
 A thermodynamically-consistent constitutive model for semicrystalline polymers has been
 developed that is capable of representing volume strains from crystallization and melting.
 The new model is targeted at representing the thermal strain behavior of polymeric photo-
 voltaic module polymer encapsulation which may undergo crystallization and melting in their
 service environments. In the model, a homogenized material point is represented by a Gibbs
 free energy incorporating contributions from the crystalline phase, the amorphous phase, and
 mixing terms. The crystalline phase is represented using a linear thermoelastic free energy
 and the amorphous phase is represented using a nonlinear thermoviscoelastic free energy.
 Both phases are isotropic, and so to is the homogenized response of the material point. The
 crystalline volume fraction is included as an internal state variable with a temperature and
 rate-dependent evolution law. Volume strains from crystallization and melting are enabled
 by a mixing enthalpy term in the Gibbs free energy. The model has been added to the Li-
 brary of Advanced Materials for Engineering (LAMÉ) [1] as the polymer crystallization
 model, and will be available for use in the next release of Sandia’s Sierra/SolidMechanics
 finite element code [2] (Next release: 5.26). Using data found in the literature, the model was
 parameterized for poly(ethylene-vinyl acetate) (EVA), a common photovoltaic module en-
 capsulant. During calibration, the crystal evolution law was successfully fit to experimental
 data that approximately obeyed non-isothermal Avrami kinetics. The as-calibrated model
 captured qualitative features of a complex thermal strain curve of EVA undergoing crystal-
 lization and melting. Despite these successes, the present model is a prototype. Avenues for
 future development are enumerated.

1 Introduction
Photovoltaic (PV) modules often include a polymer encapsulation layer that provides struc-
tural support and protection from moisture, while also offering high transparency to light [3].
The most common encapsulation material is poly(ethylene vinyl acetate) (EVA) [4], which
is a copolymer of polyethylene and vinyl acetate. Polyolefin elastomer (POE) and mixed
POE/EVA are also common encapsulation materials [5]. However, thermal strain mis-
matches between the polymer encapsulation and surrounding parts drives the development
of residual stresses within PV cells. These residual stresses contribute to the growth of
cracks within the silicon that were initially introduced during manufacturing, shipping, or
installation. In service, the residual stress state changes as the PV models are subjected to
thermal excursions, which further drives PV cell cracking. Thus, thermal mismatch strain
between the polymer encapsulation and surrounding PV cell materials is a major driver of
PV cell and module degradation [6].

Predicting thermally-driven residual stresses in PV modules depends on accurate models of
the complex thermal-strain behavior of the polymer encapsulation. EVA exhibits viscoelastic
behaviors [7], so its thermal strains are time-dependent [8] and therefore also temperature
dependent through the time–temperature superposition principle [9, 10]. EVA is also a
semicrystalline polymer; the polyethylene comonomer can exist in a crystalline or amorphous
state [11]. In hot climates, the temperature changes seen by the PV module during a day–
night cycle may be sufficient to induce melting and crystallization of EVA [12], which has a
melting temperature in the range of 40 ◦ C–80 ◦ C, depending on the vinyl acetate content [13].
For comparison, Ref. [14] recommends 85 ◦ C as an upper limit for qualification testing. Since
the crystalline phase is significantly denser than the amorphous phase, crystallization and
melting can result in linear thermal strains on the order of 1 % [12].

In recent work [15], thermal strain curves of EVA on melting and crystallization were di-
rectly input into finite element simulations. The results showed that the interplay between
thermoviscoelasticity and strains from melting and crystallization have a pronounced effect
on predictions of residual stress through a day–night cycle. As a side note, the importance
of strains from polymer crystallization are not specific to EVA in PV modules. Crystalliza-
tion is an important consideration in additive manufacturing, where large thermal gradients
can cause warping as cooler regions shrink due to crystallization while melted polymer is
continuously deposited at the frontier of the print.

Numerous constitutive models for semicrystalline polymers are concerned with how the
macroscopic thermo-mechanical behavior depends on the crystallinity. In these models,
the crystal volume fraction is taken as a constant material parameter which is used to cal-
culate effective macroscopic properties by homogenization of the amorphous and crystalline
properties (e.g., Refs. [16–21]). In Felder et al. (2020) [22], a two-stage decoupled approach
was used to model crystallization during 3D printing. In the first stage, the model pre-
dicts crystallization from the relaxed (zero-stress) melt, which allowed for a prediction of
crystallinity as a field variable throughout the additively manufactured part. In the sec-
ond stage, the thermo-mechanical behavior of the solidified polymer is predicted for com-

plex stress states, but additional changes to the crystallinity are not considered. Lion and
Johlitz (2016) [23] developed a one-dimensional thermodynamic model for crystallization
and melting in semicrystalline polymers. The model homogenizes a linear thermoelastic
crystalline phase, a thermoviscoelastic amorphous phase, and a rigid amorphous phase that
only contributes through thermal terms. The crystalline volume fraction is introduced as
an internal state variable that can increase or decrease (crystallize or melt) depending on
the chemical potential. However, the Lion and Johlitz (2016) model neglects volume strains
induced by changes in the crystallinity. More recently, Sreejith et al. (2023) [24] developed
a thermodynamics-based model for the additive manufacturing of semicrystalline polymers
that accounts for phase change on both heating and cooling and the accompanying changes
in volume. The model was implemented in Abaqus/Standard as a User Material (UMAT)
Subroutine [25]. Ren et al. (2025) [26] developed a finite element model for warping during
additive manufacturing due to phase change through multiple thermal cycles. These ef-
forts to predict volume changes from polymer crystallization have been targeted at additive
manufacturing applications. In contrast, models applied towards PV modules are lacking.

Finite element codes usually come equipped with models capable of capturing the viscoelas-
ticity of EVA [1, 7, 27] and, as previously mentioned, there are numerous models in the
literature for semicrystalline polymers that model the influence of a constant crystallinity
of the effective mechanical behavior. However, models for semicrystalline polymers that
account for phase change and the strains produced from the phase change are not readily
available. Therefore there is currently no capability to accurately assess the role of crys-
tallization and melting within EVA on the degradation of PV cells for standard thermal
environments.

In this paper, a thermodynamically-consistent model is developed for a semicrystalline poly-
mer in which viscoelasticity, crystallization and melting, and volume strains from changes in
crystallinity may all be simultaneously active. The model form extends the one presented in
Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23], with key changes being (i) incorporation of nonlinear viscoelas-
ticity for the amorphous phase, (ii) addition of a mixing enthalpy term to predict volume
strains from phase transformation, and (iii) extension to a 3D framework for future use in
finite element analysis of PV modules. EVA is used as a representative material for PV
module encapsulants, but the model formulation is applicable to semicrystalline polymers in
general. The model form developed in this document represents the simplest set of features
that capture the phenomenon relevant to modeling residual stresses in PV modules. The
current iteration of the model was developed within a small project with focused objectives,
and should therefore be regarded as a prototype.

The report is organized as follows. The model theory is described in Section 2. Then,
the numerical scheme to time-integrate the model in Sandia’s Sierra/SolidMechanics finite
element code [2] is described in Section 3. Additional equations used in the numerical
implementation are shown in Appendix A. The ability of the model to predict volume strains
from crystallization and melting is demonstrated in Section 4 using a series of isothermal
holds. A preliminary model calibration for EVA is built from literature data in Section 5. The
input syntax to use the calibrated model in Sierra/SolidMechanics is shown in Appendix B.

2 Model Theory
The model theory draws heavily from the 1D model of Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23], but the
present model is extended and modified in several key ways, including an extension to 3D
for use in finite element codes. The model represents the thermo-mechanical behavior of a
semicrystalline polymer using a Gibbs free energy (defined per unit mass in the reference
configuration) that includes contributions from the crystalline phase, the amorphous phase,
and mixing terms. The crystalline phase is represented using a linear thermoelastic Gibbs
free energy and the amorphous phase is represented using a viscoelastic Gibbs free energy.
Viscoelasticity is modeled using a hereditary integral framework to enable implementation
of the nonlinear simplified potential energy material clock [27, 28]. This is a departure from
the Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23], which used viscoelasticity based on internal state variables.
Since the model is based on a Gibbs free energy, creep functions (i.e., retardation functions)
are deployed rather than relaxation functions, which are used when starting from a Helmholtz
free energy. The creep function–based hereditary integral model form draws heavily from
the prior work of Lester and Long (2021) [29], where a model was developed for coupled
viscoelasticity and phase transformation of glass-ceramics. The Gibbs free energies of each
phase are combined using a mixture rule that is weighted by the crystal mass fraction, which,
for brevity, is referred to here as the crystallinity. Combining Gibbs free energies through a
linear mixture rule is equivalent to a stress-shared homogenization of the distinct phases.

The crystallinity, is treated as an internal state variable that changes through the the
thermodynamically-consistent evolution law proposed in Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23]. Whether
the evolution law predicts crystallization or melting is dictated by the chemical potential; the
drive for phase transformation is to minimize the Gibbs free energy. The rate of phase trans-
formation is governed by the difference between the current crystallinity and the equilibrium
crystallinity, as well as the viscoelastic shift factor.

Two mixing terms are also included in the Gibbs free energy: the mixing entropy and the
mixing enthalpy. The mixing entropy term contributes to the heat produced during phase
transformation and also contributes to the thermodynamic drive for phase transformation.
On the other hand, the mixing enthalpy term produces a volume strain from phase trans-
formation due to the mismatched densities of the two phases. The phase transformation–
induced volume strain was neglected in Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23], where the thermal
response during phase transformation was the main focus. On the other hand, the mixing
enthalpy is key for predicting the thermal strain behavior of EVA through crystallization
and melting.

In the model of Lion and Johlitz (2016) [23], a rigid amorphous phase is also considered,

deformation and increases stiffness. In the Lion and Johlitz model, it is assumed that the

that the amount of rigid amorphous phase is proportional to the amount of crystalline phase.
This assumption is used to limit the maximum crystallinity, as once the non-rigid amorphous
phase is completely converted to crystalline phase, the residual rigid amorphous phase cannot
crystallize.

maximum crystallinity is limited because polyethylene is the only crystallizable comonomer [11].

3 Numerical Implementation
The scheme to time-integrate the model is described here. Given a known state at time tn ,
the goal is to solve for the state at time tn+1 . Here, it is assumed that the finite element
code will pass the strain and temperature to the constitutive model. Therefore, the stress
σn+1 needs to be computed and returned to the mechanical solver. For separate scaling of
hydrostatic and shear stresses, the stress tensor is separated into its volumetric and deviatoric
parts: σkk,n+1 and sn+1 . Also, the internal state variables an+1 , xn+1 , and xeq,n+1 need to be
computed. The stress and the internal state variables will be determined through an implicit
scheme necessitating the solution of nonlinear equations. The nonlinear equations will be
solved using an initial guess that is iteratively updated until convergence tolerances are met.

Additionally, the hereditary integrals require an update. It is computationally prohibitive to
store the histories of stress and temperature for each integration point for direct computation
of the integral at each time step. Instead, restrictions are placed on the creep functions so
they can be represented as state variables that are incrementally updated. The state variables
representing the hereditary integrals can be updated explicitly for a known value of an+1 and
σn+1 or θn+1 .

The time-integration procedure is as follows. For the set of state variables included in
the implicit scheme ({σkk,n+1 , sn+1 , log an+1 , xn+1 , xeq,n+1 }), initial guesses are set based on
values from the previous time step. Next, the hereditary integrals are explicitly updated.
Then xeq,n+1 is updated using the bisection method. Since the gradients of xeq,n+1 are very
steep at the edges of its domain, its solution method is separate from the other variables
requiring an implicit solve. After solving for xeq,n+1 , the remaining implicit state variables
{σkk,n+1 , sn+1 , log an+1 , xn+1 } are updated through a Newton-Raphson scheme. The three
step loop of (i) updating hereditary integrals, (ii) solving for xeq,n+1 using bisection, (iii) up-
dating {σkk,n+1 , sn+1 , log an+1 , xn+1 } using Newton-Raphson is repeated until all convergence
tolerances are met. This scheme is described in more detail in the following sections.

4 Demonstration of Volume Strains from Crystallization or Melt-

A key feature of this model is its ability to predict volume strains from phase transformation.
This capability is demonstrated here for a series of stress-free, isothermal holds that induce
either melting or crystallization. The parameters used for the demonstration are listed
in Tables 1 and 2. For these parameters, the stress-free equilibrium crystallinity versus
temperature is plotted in Fig. 7. The initial crystallinity is set to x0 = 0.3 with xmax =
0.6. When αc ̸= α∞ and θ ̸= θref , the model will predict a volume strain during phase
transformation because the effective CTE changes with x. This effect is eliminated by
setting all CTEs to zero. This ensures that all strains in the simulation are produced by
the mixing enthalpy term κ(x). For all simulations shown in this section, the model was
initialized under equilibrium conditions at the temperature of the isothermal hold. Therefore,
no thermal history is present in the simulations, thus all hereditary integrals are identically
zero. This also means that the creep functions and glassy values do not contribute to the
simulation results.

Results from the isothermal holds are shown in Fig. 8. The evolution of crystallinity during
the isothermal hold is shown in Fig. 8a. For isothermal holds at 450 K, 400 K, and 375 K,
the crystallinity melts to zero. At higher temperature, melting is faster due to the presence
of the shift factor a in crystallinity evolution law, Eq. (35). At 350 K, 0 < xeq < x0 , so the
model predicts melting, but not all the way to x = 0. Likewise, at 325 K, x0 < xeq < xmax ,
so the model predicts crystallization, but not all the way to x = xmax . For isothermal holds
at 300 K and 250 K, the model predicts complete crystallization to x = xmax . Again, the
phase change is faster at higher temperatures.

5 Preliminary Model Calibration for EVA
To further demonstrate the model’s capabilities, an initial calibration was developed for EVA
using data found in the literature. The parameters for the calibrated model are shown in
Tables 3 and 4. For brevity, Prony series for the creep functions do not appear in these
tables, but they can be found in Appendix B. The procedure used to generate the model
parameters is described here. To simplify obtaining parameters from the literature, it was
assumed that, when applicable, the properties of the crystalline phase are identical to the
glassy properties of the amorphous phase, i.e., Kc = Kg , µc = µg , αc = αg , cc = cg . Prior
work at Sandia [12] characterized the thermal strain behavior of EVA with approximately
30 % vinyl acetate. For comparisons with that work, literature data on EVA with similar
vinyl acetate content is preferred, whenever possible. When data for EVA was not available,
data for polyethylene (PE) was used. Given these simplifying assumptions, the variety of
sources used for calibration, and the early stage of development of the model, the calibration
developed here should be regarded as preliminary.

5.1 Linear Viscoelastic Parameters

The linear viscoelasticity of EVA was characterized in Bosco et al. (2020) [7]. The parameters
defining the linear viscoelastic shift factor (θref , C1 , C2 ) were taken directly from Bosco et
al. (2020) [7]. Likewise, the glassy and rubbery shear moduli (µg and µ∞ ) were also taken
from Bosco et al. (2020) [7]. The viscoelasticity of the bulk modulus was neglected, with a
constant K = Kg = K∞ calculated from µg , assuming a Poisson’s ratio of ν = 0.49. When
Kg = K∞ , the bulk creep function j1 has no impact on the constitutive model.

Bosco et al. (2020) [7] also characterized the shear relaxation spectrum for EVA using a Prony
series. However, the shear Prony series in Bosco et al. (2020) [7] requires conversion from
a relaxation Prony series to a creep (i.e., retardation) Prony series. This was accomplished
using the open source software PSI (Prony Series Interconversion) [34]. Unfortunately, PSI
suffers from numerical issues when the input Prony series spans 24 decades or more, and
the Prony series in Bosco et al. (2020) [7] spans 45 decades. Therefore, the longest Prony
times from Bosco et al. (2020) [7], which also had the lowest weights, were truncated to
facilitate conversion using PSI. The original and truncated Prony series for the shear re-
laxation spectrum are compared in Fig. 9a. On a log-log plot, the differences between the
original and truncated Prony series are apparent around f2 (t) ≈ 0.01. On a semilog plot,
the two spectrum are essentially indistinguishable. Therefore, truncation of the Prony series
is justified. The truncated relaxation Prony series is plotted with the converted creep Prony
series in Fig. 9b.

Densities and heat capacities of purely amorphous and crystalline polyethylene (PE) were
used as approximations for the densities and heat capacities of amorphous and crystalline
EVA. The densities of amorphous and crystalline PE were taken from Refs. [35, 36]. The
heat capacities for purely amorphous and crystalline PE were taken from Ref. [36], for the
temperature 240 K, as this temperature is closest to the reference temperature in the present
calibration. Temperature dependence of the heat capacity is neglected.

5.2 Nonlinear Viscoelastic Parameters

The nonlinear viscoelastic clock used in this model (see Section 2.5) is a simplified potential
energy clock, and as such, the clock temperature variable N (see Eq. (26)) is derived following
essentially the same procedure used in the SPEC [28] and SPECtacular models [27]. The
clock temperature is derived starting from an expression of the configurational energy, also
called the non-bonded potential energy. Then, only certain integral terms are kept to form
a simplified expression for the potential energy. The simplified expression is divided by
−(cg − c∞ ) to define N , which has units of temperature. The main difference between the
derivation for Eq. (26) and the form used for SPEC is the form of the free energy used to
derive the model; SPEC starts from a Helmholtz free energy, while the present model starts
from a Gibbs free energy. Starting from the Gibbs free energy results in additional terms
in the potential energy, as the enthalpy has to be subtracted to transform to the internal
energy potential. These additional terms (all multiplied by stress) were neglected, since the
established simplification procedure used the internal energy at the reference state, which is
stress-free.

After simplification, three hereditary integrals remain in the expression for N . The strength
of two of the integral terms are governed by the nonlinear viscoelastic parameters N22 and
N3h , which introduce stress coupling into the material clock. This allows for nonlinear
viscoelastic predictions, e.g., when creep is accelerated at large applied stresses [8]. In this
report, the model is only exercised under stress-free conditions. Therefore, N22 and N3h
have no influence on the simulation results. However, their values are estimated according
to the full potential energy clock theory to prepare for future investigation of this model

A definite value for the maximum crystallinity, xmax , was not obtained. However, in Bis-

Crystallization and melting of EVA was studied in Shi et al. (2008) [13]. EVA with differing

5.4 Thermal Strain Simulation

The model development is, in part, motivated by the path-dependent thermal strain behavior
of EVA identified in SAND2021-12670C [12]. The experimental data, measured on a thermo-
mechanical analyzer using a ramp rate of 1 K/min, is reproduced in Fig. 11a. During the
first heat, the slope of strain with temperature is lower than subsequent temperature sweeps.
It is assumed that the lower apparent CTE on the first heating cycle is due to a higher
crystallinity, although without knowing the prior history of the specimen, the exact reason
for differences is not knowable. Above 323 K (50 ◦ C) in heat 1, the crystalline phase melts,
causing a sudden increase in the thermal strain. On the first cooling cycle, the specimen
crystallizes below 323 K (50 ◦ C), and the volume contracts. The remainder of the cooling
sweep is approximately linear. On the second heat, the trace from the first cooling is followed
from 223 K (−50 ◦ C) to 298 K (25 ◦ C). Above 298 K (25 ◦ C), the thermal strain curve shows
a hysteresis, with the increasing strain from melting on heat 2 being more diffuse than the

decreasing strain on crystallization from cool 1. Nevertheless, cool 1 and heat 2 form a closed
loop, indicating that the phase transformation from cool 1 is reversed by heat 2.

to improve quantitative agreement.

6 Conclusion and Future Work
This report describes a new 3D constitutive model for semicrystalline polymers that couples
nonlinear viscoelasticity and phase transformation. Through a mixing enthalpy term, the
model captures volume strains induced by crystallization and melting, making it suitable for
modeling EVA encapsulation in PV modules, which may undergo phase transformation in
normal service environments. A preliminary model calibration was developed for EVA using
data and parameters from the literature. The model calibration was able to qualitatively
capture the complex, path-dependent thermal strain behavior measured in experiments.

However, the model was developed within a limited time-frame, and should regarded as a
capability that is still in development. First, the model would benefit to robustness im-
provements in the time-integration scheme. When the temperature crosses the melting tem-
perature, the appropriate time step to resolve crystalline evolution may be relatively small.
Therefore, a material time-step should be defined to help integrate the model through melting
and crystallization, without forcing an overly small time step at other temperature regimes.

Second, the model form should be extensively verified. For example, during verification work
towards the end of the project lifecycle, it was determined that the effective behavior may
not limit to the correct crystalline behavior at x = 1 or the amorphous behavior at x = 0.
This issue arises because of how ρref is calculated. Presently, it is calculated from the initial
crystallinity (see Eq. (43)) and constant thereafter, i.e., ρref = ρref (x0 ). However, under this
approach, terms in Eq. (40) will not, in general, limit to the pure-phase behavior. To address
this, ρref should be a function of the current crystallinity, i.e., ρref = ρref (x). This issue is
illustrated in Fig. 13 for the CTE and shear modulus, which reveals that the discrepancy is
quite pronounced for the CTE where αc and α∞ are the same order-of-magnitude. On the
other hand, the discrepancy is much less noticeable for the shear modulus where µc and µ∞
are separated by several orders-of-magnitude. Additional verification will help identify and
eliminate other such inconsistencies and bugs.

Finally, a focused validation effort should compare the model predictions to experimental
data. Thoughts for future validation and incorporation of additional physics include the
following.

 1. To our knowledge, the coupling of nonlinear viscoelasticity and phase transformation is
 novel, but the implications of this coupling have not been fully explored. For example,
 at low temperatures, the equilibrium crystallinity is xmax , but a is large enough to
 potentially “freeze out” crystallization so that x < xmax and ẋ ≈ 0. However, under
 shear deformation, the material time is increased by the nonlinear clock, potentially
 allowing predictions of strain-induced crystallization [37–39]. However, shear force can
 also reduce the melting temperature, causing xeq = 0 even at low temperatures. Such
 complex emergent behaviors from the model should be compared to experimental data.

 2. Simplified potential energy clocks have been used to represent nonlinear viscoelasticity,
 but only within Helmholtz free energy–based models [27, 28]. It is an open question

 if the Gibbs free energy–based model form is equivalent, or if the simplification of the
 potential energy retains the same predictive capability as it does for the Helmholtz free

  3. Avrami kinetics [40, 41] are often used to represent polymer crystallization (e.g.,
 Ref. [22]). If the present crystal evolution law cannot produce a wide range of Avrami
 crystallization behaviors, then the success of the present model form will be limited,

  5. Thermal terms could be implemented for calculating heat capacities. This would

  6. Currently, the crystalline phase is represented using a simple thermoelastic free energy.
 In reality, the stress-free state of the crystalline phase depends on the temperature
 at which it forms. Plasticity could also be incorporated into the crystalline phase
 to predict yield, with more advanced plasticity formulations enabling predictions of
     double yield [43–45].
