Abstract

This project, conducted by TDA Research, Inc. in collaboration with GE Research under DOE
Award DE-FE0032261, developed and demonstrated advanced structured sorbent-coated
contactors for the high-performance Direct Air Capture (DAC) of CO₂. The work focused on
integrating TDA’s thermally stable amine-functionalized sorbent with commercially available
tube-in-plate heat exchanger structures to achieve a low pressure drop, high heat-transfer rates,
and rapid thermal swing adsorption (TSA) cycling. Across two budget periods, the team optimized
the sorbent formulations, coating processes, and fin geometries; evaluated coupon-, sub-bench-
, and bench-scale contactors; and successfully demonstrated long-term durability, reproducible
performance, and scalable fabrication.

Sub-bench-scale modules achieved sorbent productivities exceeding 10 g CO₂/kg-sorbent-hr,
air-side pressure drops below 2.5 kPa, and stable operation over more than 5,000 cycles with
<5% capacity fade per 1,000 cycles. Bench-scale slit-fin contactors met targets of 10–50 g CO₂/hr
capture and >0.2 kg CO₂/m³-hr productivity, though performance degradation after ~300 cycles
was attributed to steam condensation effects. Based on experimentally validated
thermal-hydraulic models, a full-scale DAC contactor module was designed to carry out the
removal of 100,000 MT CO₂/year from ambient air.

The techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) indicated strong potential
for cost-effective DAC deployment. The base-case cost of capture is approximately $251/tonne
CO₂ (2019$) excluding transport and storage; under optimized conditions, the cost decreases to
$153/tonne CO₂, with projected energy-related emissions as low as 0.034 kg CO₂ per kg captured
when powered by low-carbon electricity. This work advanced the technology from TRL 3 to TRL
4 and demonstrates a viable pathway toward scalable, low-cost DAC using structured
sorbent-coated heat-exchange contactors.

2. Introduction

Growing concern over climate change has spurred interest not only in stopping the production of
anthropogenic CO2 but also reversing the effects through direct air capture (DAC) (Martin 2007,
Stolaroff 2006, Lackner 2001). DAC is challenging due to the very low partial pressure of CO2 in
air (c.a. 400 ppmv). Recently, adsorption-based DAC systems employing supported amine
sorbents have shown the potential to achieve high CO2 uptake and alleviate the issues related to
chemical stress and attrition observed in metal oxides/ hydroxides that remove CO 2 by forming
carbonate or bicarbonate bonds. These sorbents are regenerated at modest temperatures (80-
120°C) to recover the CO2 at high pressure and purity. However, the first generation of supported
amine sorbents suffered from the poor stability of the amine groups under thermal swing. This
led several researchers to explore tethering the active amine groups on high surface-area
supports such as cellulose (Sehaqui 2015, Gebald 2013), porous polymer networks (Lu 2013),
and porous silica materials (Kumar 2015, Kulkarni 2012, Chaitkittisilp 2011), unfortunately these
methods met with only limited success.

Recently, in several DOE, NASA and DOD SBIR projects (DE-SC-00020846, 80NSSC18C0135,
N00178-18-C-8009) TDA developed a new supported amine sorbent where a highly reactive
TEPA molecule was tethered to a mesoporous silica support such as SBA-15, as well as to much
lower cost fumed silica supports (e.g., AerosilTM from Evonik) used in DAC and various life support
applications (e.g., removing low concentrations of CO2 from a spacesuit or from the cabin air of
130-crew Virginia Class Submarine). In these sorbents, the TEPA strongly interacts with the
surface groups of the support, ensuring very high stability through repeated high temperature
regeneration cycles. In the Navy project we showed stable sorbent operation for 10,000 cycles
in a vacuum-assisted TSA process. The sorbent achieved a high working capacity for CO2, 8 to
10% wt. CO2 (~2.0 mmol/g) under DAC conditions when cycled between 0.4 mbar CO2 (400 ppm
CO2 at 1 atm) at ambient temperature and regenerated at 90oC, recovering CO2 as a pure product.

3. Project Objectives

TDA Research, Inc., in collaboration with GE Research, developed a structured cell for Direct Air
Capture (DAC) of CO2 where the active sorbent phase was coated on the surfaces of tube-in-
plate heat exchangers that can be economically produced in large-scale. The sorbent layer
thickness and the plate spacing and geometry were optimized to reduce the pressure drop and
improve the heat transfer rate (and hence the CO2 productivity). The structured gas-solid
contactor design reduces the associated parasitic energy loss for circulating large volumes of air
to the system. While the adsorbent was developed in an earlier project and the heat exchangers
used in this work are commercially available, the use of sorbent coated heat exchangers for CO2
removal in a thermal swing application had not been previously demonstrated. Key metrics
include an improved volumetric CO2 productivity, a decreased pressure drop, reduced capacity
fade and a lower cost, scalable fabrication process. The material system developed here
demonstrated significant progress towards meeting the goal of $100/MT CO2 removed for DAC.

3.1 Work Plan

The proposed work was split into 2 budget periods. The details of the work plan for the two budget
periods are summarized below:

In BP1 of the project, we optimized the sorbent paste to make it suitable for printing, including
adjustments to viscosity and binder content. GE then coated the plates using TDA’s sorbent
formulations. The coated test plates (1”×1”×1”) were evaluated individually and as stacks at the
bench scale under representative conditions. We optimized the paste properties based on their
physical characteristics, mechanical stability, CO₂ adsorption, pressure drop, and surface area.
Three types of fin-structure plates were tested at the individual coupon level (Task 3). One
baseline (flat-fin) and one alternative fin (slit-fin) structure were down-selected as a result of this
optimization (Task 3). In Task 4, the baseline and alternative fin structures were tested at the
sub-bench scale, defined as a stack achieving a DAC CO₂ removal productivity of 0.5 to 1.0 g
CO₂/hr. An Initial Techno-Economic Assessment (TEA) and Life Cycle Analysis (LCA) were
completed (Task 5).

In BP2, we further optimized the coating paste at the sub-bench scale as described in Subtask
6.1. Two sub-bench-scale heat exchangers and one larger heat exchanger—representing bench
scale, defined as achieving a DAC CO₂ removal productivity of 10 to 50 g CO₂/hr—were coated
using the optimized paste formulation and coating process. In Subtask 6.2, we tested the
contactors’ performance under DAC conditions in TDA’s automated test rigs. A 5,000 cycle test
was conducted to demonstrate the life of the structured sorbent in a sub-bench-scale contactor
operating with compressed air. Additionally, a 500 cycle test was completed with the bench-scale
contactor to demonstrate the concept at ambient conditions. Based on the results, we designed
a full-scale gas-solid DAC contactor, identifying key parameters such as tube size, length and
spacing, plate type, spacing and depth, and material selection needed to support 100,000
MT/year of CO₂ capture from air (Task 7).

In Task 8, we prepared the final TEA and estimated the cost of CO₂ captured with the new
technology. A final Life Cycle Analysis (LCA) was also carried out to fully quantify the
carbon-footprint reduction achievable with the new technology (Task 9). The project was
completed over two Budget Periods of 12 months each. Over the course of the work, the
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of the technology advanced from 3 to 4.

We successfully met all of our objectives, on schedule and on budget.

5.3.1 Task 6.1 Sub-bench Scale Testing at TDA (BP2)

TDA received the down selected sub-bench scale module from GEVAR from the BP1 work and
assembled an automated system to test it for extended cycles (5,000 cycles). TDA updated an
existing apparatus for this extended sub-bench scale testing (Figure 10). For the purposes of
this project, the sub-bench scale is defined as achieving DAC CO2 removal productivity of 0.5 to
1.0 g CO2/hr.

Figure 10. Sub-bench modules (left) and bench-scale test apparatus (right).

For the sub-bench scale testing, TDA received four identical sub-bench contactors coated with
the TDASSSA-7A sorbent and the GE binder formulation. These were arranged in series and
installed as shown in Figure 11. The flow region for each individual unit is 3” wide by 2.12” high
(with 16 fins evenly spaced) and close to 1.75” deep. Life and durability tests are currently being
performed for 10-minute cycles (5-minute adsorb and 5-minute regen). Adsorb conditions are 300
SLPM flow, 500 ppmv CO2 inlet concentration and 40% relative humidity (20 °C). During
adsorption, the contactors are cooled with water at 15 °C flowing through the heat exchange
tubes. A short vacuum purge, 30 seconds, occurs at the end of each adsorb cycle before switching
over to regen. During regeneration, the contactor is held under vacuum pressure, 1 psia, and
heated using 90 °C water in the heat exchanger tubes. At the end of regeneration, the bed is
brought back up to pressure for 30 seconds before fully switching to adsorb and repeating this
cycle sequence.

The following results were collected under baseline cycling conditions unless otherwise stated.
The baseline adsorption conditions are 5 minutes in duration with 40-50% relative humidity lab air
flow at 300 SLPM containing 500 ppmv inlet CO2 concentration. The baseline regeneration
conditions are 5 minutes in duration at 1 PSIA vacuum, and 100 °C heat exchange fluid in the
heat exchanger/ sorbent contactor.

5.4 Task 8. Life Cycle Analysis

TDA Research, Inc., in collaboration with GE Research, has developed a structured cell for Direct
Air Capture (DAC) of CO2 where the active sorbent phase will be coated on the surfaces of tube-
in-plate heat exchangers that can be economically produced in large-scale. The sorbent layer
thickness and the plate spacing and geometry were optimized to reduce the pressure drop and
improve the heat transfer rate (and hence the CO2 productivity). The structured gas-solid
contactor design will also reduce the associated parasitic energy loss for circulating large volumes
of air to the system. While the adsorbent was developed in an earlier project and the heat
exchangers are commercially available, the use of sorbent coated heat exchangers for CO 2
removal in a thermal swing application was not previously demonstrated. In this project we
demonstrated coated heat exchangers for DAC with contactors that are in sub-bench and bench
scale sizes. Key metrics included an improved volumetric CO2 productivity, a decreased pressure
drop, reduced capacity fade and a lower cost, scalable fabrication process. The material system
fabricated in the project demonstrated significant progress towards meeting the goal of $200/net
Tonne CO2 removed for DAC.

The study goal was to determine the environmental impact of capturing CO2 using TDA’s DAC
plant including the sorbent plant. The scope included a DAC plant and sorbent plant taking
account any emissions during production of the sorbent and procurement of raw materials,
contactor fabrication and steel and concrete used for the construction of the DAC plant and
sorbent production plant. This LCA was a cradle-to-gate analysis determining CO2 consumption
and release from both direct sources (e.g. CO2 used as the feedstock for the sorbent system) and
indirect sources (e.g. CO2 released from fuels used for transportation) throughout the life of the
product. Since the CO2 will be stored underground permanently, the LCA used the boundary of
the DAC facility.

The functional unit of the Proposed Product System is 1 kg of CO2, separated but not compressed
or sequestered. This LCA details the inputs and outputs to the system from the cradle (raw
materials), through system use, to the gate (separation of 1 kg CO2). To determine transportation
emissions, we placed the DAC plant near our facility in Golden, Colorado. The DAC system
manufacturing facility and sorbent plant were placed next to the DAC operation. The DAC plant
is assumed to have a lifetime of 30 years. The sorbent lifetime is assumed to be 4 years with the
used contactor metals being recycled back into new contactors. Major drivers that impact the
environmental result are the source of the electricity used to drive the DAC process,, amine
sorbent production and aluminum production. After these flows, the next major categories are
copper production, steel production, and Portland cement production (Portland cement is a
component of concrete). For solar electricity, the global warming potential (GWP) impact is low,
but when coal generated electricity is used, the GWP impact is higher. In general, solar and
nuclear had the lowest values when comparing energy emissions followed by the grid power and
coal-based power. A summary of the LCA findings is given below, while the full details are
included in Appendix B.

The LCA model showed that the solar-powered DAC system led to the emission of 0.0340 kg
CO2 eq for each 1 kg CO2 separated. The Life Cycle 45Q Displacement Factor (LC 45Q DF) is
933.96. The Coal-based DAC model is identical to the Solar-based DAC model except for the
source of electricity. This allows us to show the impact of the electricity sources on the DAC
process. For GWP, the highest emissions were shown for coal > grid > nuclear > solar but nuclear
and solar were nearly identical. We showed that electricity source is one of the key modeling
parameters that influence the study results, and the results are affected by the sensitivity of that
the electricity estimate which comes from the TEA.
