1.
introduction

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field. The generalized Kronecker quiver
K(n)
is just the quiver with two vertices
1
,
2
, and
n
arrows
\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{n}:

1\rightarrow 2
. It is popular to study the representations of
K(n)
when studying finite groups, moduli spaces, quiver Grassmannians and many other aspects of mathematics (cf.
[
14
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
8
]
). Let
\rep_{k}(K(n))
denote the category of the finite-dimensional representations of
K(n)
and let
\mathcal{K}_{n}=kK(n)
, the
path algebra
of
K(n)
(cf.
2.2
). If we use
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
to denote the category of finite-dimensional modules over
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, then there exists an equivalence between the categories
\rep_{k}(K(n))
and
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. We will identify these two categories frequently.

When
n\leq 2
, it is known how to classify the indecomposable modules in
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}

[
13
, XIX.3]
. When
n\geq 3
, however, it is hopeless to classify all the indecomposable modules since the representation type is wild in this case (cf.
[
13
, XVIII]
,
[
6
, 1.3]
). Hence it is desirable to find invariants in the category
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. J. Worch introduced the invariant
width

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})
for a regular component
\mathcal{C}
of
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
in 2013, and she showed that
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0

[
14
, Section 3]
. C. M. Ringel found two invariants:
the smallest radius
and
the number of flow modules
for the covering quiver of
K(n)
in 2018 (cf.
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
4
]
). Then it is natural to ask what the width of a regular component
\mathcal{D}
of the covering is.

From now on, we let
n\geq 3
for
K(n)
. Let
M\in\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Then we have a linear operator
x^{M}_{\alpha}=\alpha_{1}M(\gamma_{1})+\alpha_{2}M(\gamma_{2})+\cdots+\alpha_{n}M(\gamma_{n}):M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}
for every
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in k^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus we can define two full subcategories of
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
with the equal kernels property and with the equal images property (cf.
[
14
, Definition 2.1]
):
\EKP_{n}:=\{M\in\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}\mid\forall\alpha\in k^{n}\setminus\{0\}:x^{M}_{\alpha}\text{ is injective}\}
and
\EIP_{n}:=\{M\in\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}\mid\forall\alpha\in k^{n}\setminus\{0\}:x^{M}_{\alpha}\text{ is surjective}\}.

One shows that
\EIP_{n}\cap\EKP_{n}=(0)
, and the regular Auslander–Reiten (AR) components of the category
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
are of the form
\mathbb{Z}A_{\infty}
(cf.
[
11
]
). Let
M\in\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
be an indecomposable module and let
\tau
be the Auslander–Reiten translation
[
2
, IV.2.3]
. Then a module
M
is said to be
regular
if
\tau^{m}(M)\neq 0
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose that
M\in\EKP_{n}
is regular. Then there exists some positive integer
m
such that
\tau^{m}M\in\EIP_{n}
(cf.
[
14
, Section 3]
). Hence it suggests us to find an invariant connecting
\EIP_{n}\cap\mathcal{C}
and
\EKP_{n}\cap\mathcal{C}
for every regular AR component
\mathcal{C}
of
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Then we can define a
width

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})(\geq 0)
of
\mathcal{C}

[
14
, Section 3]
. In this note, we investigate the width from another aspect using covering theory.

Let
(T(n),\Omega)
be the universal covering of
K(n)

[
3
, Section 7]
, i.e.,
T(n)
is an
n
-regular tree with a bipartite orientation
\Omega
(see
2.2
). Let
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be the category of finite-dimensional representations of
(T(n),\Omega)
. Then
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
also admits AR–sequences (cf.
[
4
, 2.2]
). Similarly, we have widths for the regular components
\mathcal{D}
of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
. C. M. Ringel defined an invariant
b(\mathcal{D})
(the number of flow modules) for the components
\mathcal{D}
in 2018 (cf.
[
12
]
). We study the components
\mathcal{D}
and show that
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})>0
.

2.
preliminaries

Throughout,
k
will denote an algebraically closed field.

2.1.
Trees

A
graph

G=(G_{0},G_{1})
consists of two sets:
G_{0}
(the set of
vertices
) and
G_{1}
(the set of
edges
). Two vertices
a,b\in G_{0}
are called
neighbours
if
\{a,b\}
is an edge in
G_{1}
. For a graph
G
, by an
orientation
we mean a map
\Omega

:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{0}\times G_{0}
such that
\Omega(\{a,a^{\prime}\})
is either (
a,a^{\prime}
) or (
a^{\prime},a
). We call
(G,\Omega)
an
oriented graph
. We write
a\rightarrow a^{\prime}
if
\Omega(\{a,a^{\prime}\})=(a,a^{\prime})
and call
a
the
start
,
a^{\prime}
the
target
,
a\rightarrow a^{\prime}
the
arrow
. Meanwhile we call
a
a
sink
(or a
source
) if it is not a start (or the target, respectively) of any arrow. If any vertex is a sink or a source, then the orientation
\Omega
will be called
bipartite
. A
subgraph
of a graph
G=(G_{0},G_{1})
is a graph
G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}_{0},G^{\prime}_{1})
such that
G^{\prime}_{0}\subseteq G_{0},G^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq G_{1}
. A
walk
of length
t\geq 0
with
start

a_{0}
and
target

a_{t}
in a graph
G
is a finite sequence

l_{t}=(a_{0}|\{a_{0},a_{1}\},\{a_{1},a_{2}\},\cdots,\{a_{t-1},a_{t}\}|a_{t})

such that
a_{i-1}
and
a_{i}
are neighbours for
1\leq i\leq t
, and
a_{j-1}\neq a_{j+1}
for
1\leq j<t
. We sometimes use
(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{t})
to denote the walk
l_{t}
if there only exists one walk connecting
a_{0}
and
a_{t}
. The graph
G
is called
finite
if
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are finite sets, and
G
is said to be
connected
if for every two vertices
a,b\in G_{0}
, there always exists a walk that connects them. A graph
G
is said to be a
tree
if it is connected and there only exist walks of length
0
that connect a vertex with itself. Moreover, for any two different vertices
a,b
in a tree
G
we can only find one walk that connects them. Suppose that
G
is a finite tree. Then we call the walks of the maximal possible length the
diameter walks
of
G
, and we use
d(G)
to denote their length. Let
l_{t}=(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{t})
be a walk in
G
. If
t=2r
, then we call
a_{r}
or
\{a_{r}\}
the
center
of
l_{t}
and
r
its
radius
. If
t=2r+1
, then we call
\{a_{r},a_{r+1}\}
the
center
of
l_{t}
and
r
its
radius
. In fact, all diameter walks of a finite tree
G
have the same center
[
12
, 2.1]
. Hence we take the center and the radius of diameter walks of a finite tree
G
as the center and the radius of the
G
itself and we use
C(G)
and
r(G)
to denote them, respectively.

2.2.
Quivers

A
quiver
is a quadruple
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
, where
s,t:Q_{1}\rightarrow Q_{0}
. The elements in
Q_{0}
are called
vertices
or
points
, and the elements in
Q_{1}
are called
arrows
, respectively. For every arrow
\alpha\in Q_{1}
, we call the vertex
s(\alpha)
its
start
, and the vertex
t(\alpha)
its
target
. Note that an oriented graph
(G,\Omega)
can be seen as a quiver without loops and multiple arrows.

Let
Q
be a quiver, and let
x\in Q_{0}
. We define two sets

x^{+}:=\{y\in Q_{0}\mid\exists\alpha\in Q_{1}:t(\alpha)=y\text{ and }s(\alpha)=x\}
,

x^{-}:=\{y\in Q_{0}\mid\exists\alpha\in Q_{1}:s(\alpha)=y\text{ and }t(\alpha)=x\}.

The
neighbourhood

\mathcal{N}(x)
of
x
is
x^{+}\cup x^{-}.
We say that
x
is a
leaf
, provided
x
has at most one neighbour. A sequence
(a\mid\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{r}\mid b)
in
Q
is called a
path
with start
a
and target
b
if
s(\alpha_{1})=a,t(\alpha_{r})=b
, and
t(\alpha_{k})=s(\alpha_{k+1})
for each
1\leq k<r
. Moreover, we take each vertex
x\in Q_{0}
as a
trivial path
of length
0
. The quiver
Q
is said to be
locally bounded
if for every
x\in Q_{0}
the neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}(x)
is finite and there exists a number
m_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that each path in
Q
which starts or ends in
x
is of length
\leq m_{x}
. In this note, we always assume that it is locally bounded when we take about a quiver
Q
.

Let
\delta_{a,b}
be the Kronecker delta, and let
l_{1}=(a_{0}|\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{p}|a_{p}),l_{2}=(b_{0}|\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{q}|b_{q})
be two paths. We define the product of the paths
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
by
l_{1}l_{2}=\delta_{a_{p},b_{0}}(a_{0}|\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{q}|b_{q})
. Then we can get a
k
-algebra
kQ
of the quiver
Q
consisting of
k
-linear span of all paths of length
l\geq 0
(cf.
[
2
, II.1]
). We call
kQ
the
path algebra
of
Q
. When
Q_{0}
is finite, the
kQ
is an associative and finite-dimensional
k
-algebra. A finite-dimensional representation
M=((M_{x})_{x\in Q_{0}},(M(\alpha))_{\alpha\in Q_{1}})
of
Q
consists of vector spaces
M_{x}
and
k
-linear maps
M(\alpha):M_{s(\alpha)}\rightarrow M_{t(\alpha)}
such that
\dim_{k}M:=\sum_{x\in Q_{0}}\dim_{k}M_{x}
is finite. A
morphism

f:M\rightarrow N
between two representations is a collection of
k
-linear maps
(f_{z})_{z\in Q_{0}}
, such that for each arrow
\alpha:x\rightarrow y
, we have
f_{y}\circ M(\alpha)=N(\alpha)\circ f_{x}
. We often use
\rep_{k}(Q)
to denote the category of finite-dimensional representations of
Q
over
k
. If we use
\modd kQ
to denote the category of finite-dimensional left modules of
kQ
, then the categories
\modd kQ
and
\rep_{k}(Q)
are equivalent (cf.
[
2
, III 1.6]
).

Let

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that

is a regular indecomposable module. Then

(a)

M
is a sink module and
d(M)\geq 4
.

(b)

Any leaf of
T(\sigma M)
is a sink. In particular,
\sigma M
is a sink module.

Proof.

According to the definition, every map is injective in
M
, that is, every leaf of
T(M)
is a sink, whence
M
is a sink module. Note that
\sigma M
is also indecomposable. Suppose that there exists a source
b_{0}
being a leaf in
T(\sigma M)
. Let
\alpha:b_{0}\rightarrow b_{1}
be an arrow of
T(\sigma M)_{1}
and the
k

\varphi=(\sigma M)(\alpha):(\sigma M)_{b_{0}}\rightarrow(\sigma M)_{b_{1}}
be non-trivial. Since
\sigma^{-}(\sigma M)\cong M
, there exists a map
\varphi^{\prime}=M(\alpha^{\prime}):M_{b_{1}}=(\sigma M)_{b_{1}}\rightarrow M_{b_{0}}=\coker{\varphi}
that is not injective in

, this is a contradiction. Hence all leaves of
T(\sigma M)_{0}
are sinks and
\sigma M
is a sink module. Moreover,

is complete and

,
C(\sigma M)=C(M)

[

Since

is a sink and regular module, it follows that

, then we have

, that is,
\mid T(\sigma M)_{0}\mid=1
, so that
\sigma M
is not regular, a contradiction.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a regular component of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
. Then

\mid\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})-\mathcal{W}(\sigma\mathcal{D})\mid\leq 1

\frac{b(\mathcal{D})+1}{2}\leqslant\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})

Proof.

Since

, we have
(\sigma^{2})^{(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})+1)}M_{\mathcal{D}}=W_{\mathcal{D}}

2(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})+1)+\iota(M_{\mathcal{D}})=\iota(W_{\mathcal{D}})
, that is,
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})=\frac{\iota(W_{\mathcal{D}})-\iota(M_{\mathcal{D}})}{2}-1

(1)

\begin{split}\frac{\iota(W_{\mathcal{D}})-1-(\iota(M_{\mathcal{D}})+1)}{2}-1\leq\mathcal{W}(\sigma D)=\frac{\iota(W_{\sigma\mathcal{D}})-\iota(M_{\sigma\mathcal{D}})}{2}-1\leq\frac{\iota(W_{\mathcal{D}})+1-(\iota(M_{\mathcal{D}})-1)}{2}-1.\end{split}

Thus

(2)

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})-1\leq\mathcal{W}(\sigma\mathcal{D})\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})+1.

According to Lemma
3.1
and Corollary

\sigma
-orbit of the module

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a regular component of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
. Suppose that
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{W}(\sigma\mathcal{D})
. Then
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})\geq\frac{b(\mathcal{D})+2}{2}
.

Proof.

We define two sets

\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{D}\setminus(\Inj_{n,\Omega}\cup\Sur_{n,\Omega})
, and
\mathcal{M}(\sigma):=\sigma\mathcal{D}\setminus(\Inj_{n,\sigma\Omega}\cup\Sur_{n,\sigma\Omega})
.

If
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})\leq\frac{b(\mathcal{D})+1}{2}
, then we have
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})=\frac{b(\mathcal{D})+1}{2}

3.7
. Hence we get
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{W}(\sigma\mathcal{D})=\frac{b(\mathcal{D})+1}{2}

quasi-simple modules contained in the set

3.1
and Corollary
3.2
. Hence there are at least
b(\mathcal{D})+2
quasi-simple modules contained in the set
\mathcal{M}\cup\mathcal{M}(\sigma)
, and this is a contradiction.

∎
