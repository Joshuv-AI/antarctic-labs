 ABSTRACT

 High temperature gas-cooled reactors using tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuels currently suffer from
limited fissile densities of common fuels that are used. UN or UC-based fuels exhibited higher fuel
densities, although challenges exist in generating high-purity, dense UN or UC kernels with retained
spherical morphology. This milestone report outlines initial efforts to understand the role that gas
atmospheres play in the kernel conversion process to form advanced ceramic fuel kernels of spherical
morphology.
 Kernels comprised of a 2UO3(NH3)●3H2O and carbon produced through internal gelation were
processed under inert, reducing and nitriding atmospheres to produce UN kernels. High purity UN kernels
were produced by calcining C/U = 2.675 green kernels under 685 sccm Ar-6%H2 at 3°C/min to 1900°C,
followed by nitriding under 685 sccm N2-5%H2 for 20 hours at 1900°C. This resulted in kernels with a
composition of UC0.11N0.89 (0.53 wt% C) with trace amounts of U2SN2 (0.6 wt%), and 76% TD with respect
to phase-pure UN. Carbon contents measured for these kernels by combustion analysis indicated lower
carbon contents of 0.41 wt% C. Significant differences in the composition of the kernels processed under
different routes were observed through x-ray diffraction.
 Kernel cracking during calcination was found to be sensitive to heating rate and gas atmosphere.
Reducing gas atmospheres during calcining improved kernel morphology and reduced cracking. Calcining
at a ramp rate of 3°C/min also mitigated cracking. Fired kernel densities were insensitive to gas atmosphere.
A density gradient of fine porosity was observed, with decreasing porosity near the kernel exterior,
suggesting an additional high temperature isothermal hold is necessary for improving densities.
 Impurity removal was driven by the presence of H2 in the gas stream, batch size and isothermal hold
length, with smaller batch sizes and longer holds improving kernel purity. UO2 and U2SN2 impurities were
both reduced to trace amounts under N2-5%H2 for 20 hours. Net C removal rates were calculated as 0.0225
wt%C/hr with an increase in isothermal hold length from 10 to 20 hours, suggesting sluggish C removal
kinetics compared to literature. This implies a significant role of furnace gas flow path on the purification
and densification of UN kernels for advanced gas reactors.
 Fabrication of UCO kernels was also investigated for green kernels of different C/U ratios heated to a
12 hour hold at 1800°C at 10°C/min under vacuum. Mass losses greater than 90% were observed for C/U
= 2.1 and 2.2 kernels, with 73% mass loss observed for C/U = 2.675 kernels. Significant cracking and
damage to kernels was observed under these conditions with UC and UO2 present after conversion.

 1. Introduction
 Tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuels are a promising fuel design for advanced reactor systems such as
high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs) owing to their robust triple-layered structure.1 TRISO
particles are comprised of an enriched uranium compound as the core (UO2, or UC/UC2 + UO2, abbreviated
UCO) encapsulated by a lower density carbon buffer layer to accommodate fuel swelling and an inner
pyrolytic carbon shell. The inner pyrolytic carbon shell is itself surrounded by a SiC layer, acting as a
gaseous/metallic fission product barrier, which is surrounded by a dense, outer pyrolytic carbon shell.
Kernels are then embedded in a graphite powder/phenolic resin matrix and fabricated into either a right
cylindrical rod (prismatic reactor) or a sphere (pebble-bed reactor), which gets implemented into the
requisite reactor design. TRISO compacts in both designs have demonstrated excellent resistance to
abnormal operating conditions, such as excursions for long durations at 1600°C, with minimal kernel
degradation and little-to-no release of fission products.2 Despite these advantages, TRISO fuels suffer from
issues such as low fissile density, as the packing fraction of spheres within the compacts is low (~0.4), and
the spheres possess a high volume fraction of non-fissile encapsulation material. Because of this, high assay,
low-enriched uranium (HALEU) is typically used for TRISO-type fuels to improve fuel density and
burnup.3 Other avenues for increasing the magnitude of fissile material in the system are through the use of
higher particle size fuels, and blends of varying particle sizes of fuels to improve the packing fraction of
kernels in the system beyond the current low limits.3 In addition, changing the fuel material from UCO to
a material with a higher uranium content, such as UN (~13.5 g/cc uranium density), or UC (13.0 g/cc
uranium density) instead of the UCO fuel mixture, can enable higher fissile densities. Endemic to all these
approaches is a need to understand the role of processing on the fabrication and performance of fuels at
sizes larger than what is currently produced, and how to adapt processing routes to ensure that the
morphology and integrity of the fabricated kernels is maintained from the beginning to the end of the
process.
 Fabrication of high-purity UN is routinely performed through a carbothermal reduction and nitridation
(CTRN) process, followed by thermally cycling the nitrided material repeatedly, forcing the UN to undergo
a rapid phase transformation to U2N3, resulting in a large volume expansion (~20%) that causes “chemical
milling” of the material to occur.4 This exposes fresh interfaces and surfaces allowing for extended removal
of C and conversion to high-purity UN. Because the large volume expansion of the UN > U 2N3 phase
transformation causes fracturing of the particles, this approach is unlikely to be useful for production of

 1

high-purity UN kernels, as kernels will likely fracture. Therefore, alternative approaches must be
considered, such as purely gas-phase reactions to reduce carbon impurities through the process, which can
lead to detrimental fuel particle swelling.5

 The goal of this milestone is to report on the initial progress in understanding processing-property
relationships for the fabrication of non-oxide fuel kernels for advanced reactors through gas atmosphere
control and includes a summary of the results-thus far on the conversion and densification of UO3+C kernels
to form high-purity UN kernels, as well as initial investigations to convert UO3+C kernels into UCO kernels.

 2. Experimental Methods
 Green kernels were produced by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in three different C/U molar
ratios (C/U = 2.1, 2.2 and 2.675) through an internal gelation method using a chilled mixture of acid-
deficient uranyl nitrate doped with the required amount of carbon black to achieve the target C/U ratio,
hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) and urea. The solution was dripped into hot silicone oil to initiate
gelation of the droplets, followed by aging of the microspheres, a washing step and subsequent drying to
remove any adsorbed moisture or cleaning products from the process.6
 X-ray diffraction was performed using a Bruker D2 Phaser x-ray diffractometer. All samples analyzed
were crushed in a mortar and pestle in an inert atmosphere Ar glovebox to form a powder, then dispersed
onto a zero background Si XRD sample holder coated with vacuum grease to hold the powder in place and
minimize texturing that may occur. A poly(methyl methacrylate) air-tight, anti-scattering dome with a
knife-edge was used for all samples. Scans were performed using Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation in a θ/2θ
geometry, with a beam current and accelerating voltage of 10 mA and 30 kV, respectively. Scan parameters
used in all scans were as follows: dwell time of 2s, step sizes of 0.02° and ranges of 10-90°2θ. Standards
for lattice parameter determination were not used, so compositions calculated from the lattice parameter
are estimates.
 Carbon content was measured on samples through combustion analysis in a LECO C844 with high
purity iron chip and copper accelerator used as fluxing agents. Samples were prepared for analysis using a
mortar and pestle to grind kernels into powder. Samples were tested in triplicate after correcting for blank
crucible runs and calibrating with LECO CRM 502-930 for green kernels and LECO CRM 502-702 for
converted kernels.
 Initial calcination screening studies were performed using a NETZSCH simultaneous thermal analyzer
(STA) 449 F3 with a Pt/Rh heater and an Al2O3 crucible. Kernels were heated to 700°C using ramp rates
of 1.5, 3 and 5°C/min under 200 sccm UHP Ar, or a blend of 190 sccm UHP Ar with 10 sccm Ar-6%H 2.
Measurements were corrected for drift by subtracting the mass vs. temperature curve for a blank baseline
crucible prior to measuring materials.
 Carbothermal reduction and nitridation (CTRN) to produce UN kernels was performed using a Centorr
tungsten element, top-loading furnace contained within an inert atmosphere glovebox. Gas atmospheres on
the furnace were controlled through electronically actuated solenoid valves and mass flow controllers. Gas
was introduced into the furnace through a hole located at the bottom of the furnace, below a standoff on
which the crucible was placed. Below 1100°C, temperature was monitored with a type-C thermocouple,
and above 1100°C, a single-color pyrometer was used to monitor temperature. Kernels were heated under
a thermal profile of 3°C/min to 1400°C, 1°C/min to 1900°C, with isothermal holds of 10 or 20 hours,

 3

followed by cooling at 20°C/min. Gas atmospheres used throughout the run included UHP Ar or Ar-6%H 2
during heating, and UHP N2 or N2-5%H2 during the isothermal hold. Cooling was performed under UHP
Ar in all instances. A Yokogawa UP55A controller was used to control the furnace profile and gas
atmospheres. Samples were placed in W crucibles lined with W foil on the bottom of the crucible. The side
wall of the crucible had 8 machined holes along the perimeter which was not lined with foil to improve gas
flow over the kernels. Kernel masses tested typically were either 350 mg or 750 mg, depending on the scope
of the experiment. Post-furnace run, kernels were counted, weighed and diameters were measured using an
optical microscope. Average kernel masses and diameters were used for calculation of the fired bulk density
(FBD) of kernels subjected to different furnace profiles. In some cases, kernels were mounted in epoxy
resin and polished to a 1 µm finish using diamond polishing compound.

 Carbothermal reduction to produce UCO kernels was performed on each C/U ratio using a tungsten
(W) mesh furnace contained in a glovebox. For each run, the kernels were loaded into a W crucible, which
was placed at the top of a three-crucible stack with its open face oriented upward and positioned near the
furnace thermocouple to ensure the samples were within the furnace hot zone (Figure 1a). This
configuration provided consistent positioning of each batch relative to the thermocouple for reproducibility
between furnace runs. After loading, the furnace chamber was evacuated, and the samples were heat treated
under vacuum according to the furnace profile shown in Figure 1b. The vacuum system was controlled
through three digital-output channels: DOut_04 actuated the vacuum valve, DOut_05 controlled the
roughing pump, and DOut_06 controlled the turbo pump. During the initial pump-down, the roughing pump
was activated first, followed by the turbo pump. The vacuum valve was then opened at the beginning of the
heating cycle and remained open during heat treatment and cooling. The furnace was heated to 900°C and
subsequently to 1800°C at 10°C/min, held at 1800°C for 12 h, and cooled at a programmed rate of 10°C/min
under vacuum.

 3. Results and Discussion

 3.1 Characterization of Green Kernels
 X-ray diffraction (XRD) plots highlight the phase assemblage of as-received, green kernels in Figure
2 below. For all C/U ratios received, only a single phase, 2UO3(NH3)●3H2O (PDF #00-044-0469), was
identified. A contribution from graphitic carbon is expected near 2θ ≈ 26°, corresponding to the graphite
(002) reflection; however, this position overlaps with an intense uranium-bearing reflection. Despite
reported C/U molar ratios as high as 2.675, no graphite or C-containing phases were observed in the
diffraction pattern owing to the significant difference in atomic scattering factors of C when compared to
U-containing phases. The U-containing phase identified in the diffraction pattern is consistent with that
produced through the precipitation of ammonium-uranates through the addition of ammonia to an acid-
deficient uranyl nitrate solution.7 The precipitation reaction is reported to become complete near pH 4, with
changes in the resulting uranate phases occurring as the ammonia concentration and pH increase.
Significant peak broadening is observed for the 2UO3(NH3)●3H2O phase in the diffraction pattern,
indicating either substantial microcrystallinity or uniform microstrain within the system. Because there is
no crystallographic structural data (atomic positions, site occupancy, etc.) for this phase in the powder
diffraction file, Rietveld refinement could not be performed to quantify the magnitudes of microcrystallinity
and microstrain to the observed full width at half-maximums (FWHM) in the diffraction patterns.
 Cordfunke identified several hydrated ammonium uranate compositions in the NH₃–UO₃–H₂O system
and demonstrated that these phases exhibit distinct XRD patterns, with the precipitate formed from uranyl
nitrate and ammonia dependent on precipitation conditions. 8 In particular, precipitation from uranyl nitrate
with ammonia was reported to produce different uranium-bearing phases as a function of pH, including a
Type-II ammonium uranate phase over approximately pH 4–7 and Type II/III mixtures at higher pH. 7 The
close correspondence among the present diffraction patterns therefore indicates that adjustment of the
carbon loading from C/U = 2.1 to 2.675 did not measurably perturb the dominant uranium-bearing precursor
structure or precipitation chemistry.
 Elemental analysis of the green kernels is shown in Table 1 below. Kernels showed excellent agreement
with reported C contents; the largest observed percent error in the mean C content was 3.34% for kernels
with a C/U ratio of 2.2 while the smallest observed percent error was -0.65% for kernels with a C/U ratio

 3.3 Preliminary Kernel Conversions in Tungsten Furnace to form UN
 To assess the feasibility of using a CTRN furnace profile developed at LANL previously, a handful of
C/U = 2.2 kernels were fired to 1600°C following a thermal profile like that described in US Patent
10562771B1.4 Kernels that were subjected to this treatment were severely damaged, with fissures on the
surface of most of the kernels, weakening their structural integrity. Figure 10 shows the damaged kernels
after the heat treatment. XRD results in Figure 11 reveal the presence of a significant fraction of UO2 and
UN1.7 (closely corresponding to U2N3+x) alongside the UN phase. UN1.7 is likely carried over from the
CTRN cycling process, as it was designed to take advantage of the large molar volume difference between
U2N3 and UN to “chemically mill” the material by conferring large stresses to powder particles undergoing
the face change during thermal cycling. The large amount of UO2 detected by XRD in the kernels after
CTRN is related to the initial starting C/U ratio of the kernels. For every mole of UO3, 0.5 moles of C are
required for calcination to UO2. Because the starting C/U ratio used was 2.2, only 1.7 moles of C remain
per mole of UO2, resulting in an insufficient amount of C to carbothermally reduce the UO2. Even though
the UO2 content could likely be solved by using a higher C/U ratio in the starting kernels, it appears that
this type of processing method is inadequate to produce kernels with a controlled morphology and minimal
defects, as the volume change associated with the U2N3 to UN phase change is too large. Reaction pathways
that avoid the formation of U2N3 must be used to produce high purity UN kernels.

 3.4 Control of Kernel Properties Through Gas-Phase Reactions
 The following sections describe efforts to control kernel morphologies, densities and carbon contents
through experiments utilizing different gas atmospheric conditions within the furnace. For all runs, a
standard furnace profile of 3°C/min ramp to 1400°C, 1°C/min ramp to 1900°C followed by various lengths
of isothermal holds at 1900°C and a 20°C/min cool to room temperature was used while varying gas
atmospheres during different profile segments. Only C/U = 2.675 molar ratio kernels were used for these
trials because of preliminary investigations that revealed insufficient C contents for carbothermal reduction
post-calcination. Fired bulk densities reported values should be treated as lower estimates, as the maximum
Feret diameter determined from ImageJ particle analysis was used for the diameter of particles, and
spherical geometries were assumed.

3.4.1 Initial Studies to Assess the Conversion of Kernels at Short Times

 Kernels were calcined under 1 SLM UHP Ar while ramping to 1900°C, then nitrided at 1900°C for 1
hour under 1 SLM UHP N2. The general morphology of kernels is shown in Figure 12, where distinct
fissuring/cracking and dimpling on the surface is observed. A slight, darker discoloration is observed on
the surface of the kernels in places, likely remnant UO2 leftover from an insufficient reaction time. Dimpling
on the surface of the kernels is possibly a result of locally low green density from internal porosity that
shrinks more than the surrounding material. Optical microscopy of polished cross sections (Figure 13)
shows two distinct phases are present, a darker UO2, and a lighter UN-based phase. A variety of defects
from the initial gelcasting process are observed, as well as a mostly uniform distribution of porosity within
the material, although the size distribution and aspect ratios of the porosity both appear to be non-uniform.
Geometric measurements of kernels subject to these conditions result in an average max Feret diameter of
909 ± 36 µm, resulting in a FBD of 10.89 g/cc. This density is likely a lower estimate, as the maximum
Feret diameter will overestimate calculated volumes. Ignoring the contribution to bulk density of the UO 2
present in the microstructure, this corresponds to approximately 76% TD with respect to pure UN.
 XRD analysis of the converted material shown in Figure 14 indicates two distinct phases, UO2 and a
rocksalt cubic phase, UCxNy, which is a solid solution between UC formed during the carbothermal
reduction process and UN formed during the subsequent nitridation segment. Rietveld refinement of the
diffraction pattern indicates 8 wt% UO2 and 92 wt% UCxNy. Refinement of the rocksalt cubic UCxNy
structure resulted in a lattice parameter of 4.926 Å. Assuming complete solid solution between UC and UN
to form UCxNy allows for the application of Vegard’s Law (Equation 2) to estimate the phase fractions of
UC and UN that would have to be in an ideal solid solution to result in an overall UCxNy composition that

3.4.2 Improvement in degree of conversion with increased isothermal hold

 Calcination of kernels under 1 SLM UHP Ar followed by a 10 hr nitriding step at 1900°C under 1 SLM
UHP N2 resulted in kernels whose morphology is shown in Figure 15. Clear regions of discoloration are
observed on the surface of a few kernels. This discoloration is thought to be unreacted UO 2 on the surface,
possibly due to inadequate exchange of gas at that surface of the kernels. Most of the heat treated kernels
possess gross defects such as cracking, although dimpling on the surface appeared to be less prominent.
The average kernel diameter from this furnace run was calculated to be 918 ± 16 µm, resulting in a fired
bulk density of 10.47 g/cc, or 73% of the theoretical density of UN. The 95% confidence interval reported
for the average kernel diameter was smaller for kernels tested at 10 hours rather than 1 hour, implying that
tighter dimensional control requires longer isothermal hold lengths. Reduced densities at longer isothermal
hold lengths could be related to the increased presence of cracks in the material. Analogous to the
calcination investigations reported earlier, the presence of cracks is thought to be related to oxidation of the
sample during calcination, which appeared to cause cracks to occur during the initial calcination stage to
remove binder.
 XRD analysis of kernels in Figure 16 reveal the presence of two phases, a major constituent rocksalt
cubic phase with lattice parameter larger than phase-pure UN, and a smaller secondary phase, indexed to
be U2SN2. The lattice parameter of the rocksalt cubic phase was calculated through Rietveld refinement to
be 4.906 Å. Using Vegard’s Law as described earlier leads to a calculated composition of UC0.26N0.74.
Assuming first-order, linear rate kinetics for C removal from the UCxNy, changing carbon contents from
UC0.6N0.4 (2.86 wt% C) at t = 1 hr to UC0.26N0.74 (1.23 wt% C) at t = 10 hr leads to an estimated C removal
rate of 0.1811 wt% C/hr under this gas atmosphere at 1900°C.

 The presence of sulfur in the sample that would react with UN to form U2SN2 is thought to originate
from the gelcasting process, as dispersion of carbon black within the initial broth required the addition of a
dispersant/surfactant like TAMOL SNTM, which possesses sulfonate functional groups.6, 11 The presence of
UO2 within the sample cannot be precluded within this sample; however, as interactions with the sample
holder and the x-ray beam led to increased background intensities at low 2θ’s.

3.4.5 Impact of isothermal hold length on purity and density of UN kernels and
 recommendations for improvement

 750 mg of kernels were additionally calcined under Ar-6%H2 at 685 sccm and nitrided for 20 hours
under N2-5%H2 at 685 sccm. This resulted in kernels with similar morphologies to those nitrided for 10

 Kernel diameters and densities showed no statistically significant difference with changing isothermal
hold lengths from 10 to 20 hours indicating that the net densification rate at these temperature is insufficient
for complete densification of kernels; differences in densities between tests primarily appear to arise from
uncertainty in both the average kernel mass and in the determination of the kernel diameter from image

volume). For kernels subjected under these gas conditions to a 20 hour isothermal hold at 1900°C, the
calculated relative fired bulk density with respect to phase-pure UN changes from 76% to 84% solely
depending on whether the maximum or minimum Feret diameter is used in calculation. As a reference, the
average maximum Feret diameter for this kernel set was calculated as 903 ± 12µm, whereas the average
minimum Feret diameter was calculated as 871 ± 12 µm. Clearly, a more effective estimator must be used
for the determination of the volume of kernels in the assessment of fired bulk densities in these materials.
A potential avenue to remedy this is an average volume determination via xCT, since this provides a non-
destructive true volume for each kernel in the sample of kernels tested, whereas volume derived from optical
microscopy calculations are prone to errors in the assumption of the kernel geometry. If kernels maintain
perfect sphericity throughout the conversion process, then optical microscopy is suitable as an analysis
technique; this is rarely ever the case. Because the effective kernel diameters for every gas atmosphere and
isothermal hold length tested show little-to-no difference within the measurement error, the shrinkage (and
fired bulk density) of kernels appears to be largely driven by the thermal history of the sample. A second
isothermal hold at elevated temperatures (>2000°C) is recommended to sinter fine porosity present within
the sample and increase fired bulk densities, although a more in-depth determination of the densification
behavior of the UCxNy phase as a function of C content is required in order to develop a robust thermal
profile capable of densifying UCxNy phases of varying C contents to target densities. Kernel grain size
analysis through EBSD or image analysis under SEM must be performed to support these studies in the
future.
 Kernel densities can be improved through additional testing of the ramp rates and gas atmospheres
through the earlier calcination steps. Care must be taken on the low temperature calcination, as the kernels
appear to be the most sensitive at that stage; however, improvements in both final carbon content and
density by optimization of the carbothermal reduction step (1400°C) are likely possible through the
implementation of a vacuum hold, which would drive the carbothermal reduction reaction forward through

3.4.6 UCO Kernel Fabrication Efforts
 Following CTR heat treatment, no recoverable C/U = 2.1 kernels or residual bulk product remained in
the W crucible, preventing post-conversion characterization. Figure 26 shows the crucible before and after
heat treatment. The initial kernel mass was 1.1832 g, whereas only approximately 7.5 mg of residual
material could be recovered and measured after treatment, corresponding to a mass loss of approximately
99.4%. At elevated temperature, reaction between carbon and oxygen-bearing uranium phases is expected
to produce CO. Stoops and Hamme reported significant CO evolution from U–C–O materials during
heating and demonstrated that continued CO removal under vacuum at 1800 °C can shift the system toward
uranium-rich compositions and metallic U formation.15 They also experimentally observed uranium loss by
evaporation during high-temperature treatment. The complete loss of the C/U = 2.1 batch is therefore
consistent with combined volatile loss during binder/precursor decomposition and extensive carbothermal
conversion under prolonged high-temperature vacuum, potentially followed by uranium evaporation;
however, the specific reaction pathway cannot be confirmed because no final material was recovered.

 The C/U = 2.2 batch exhibited substantial mass loss during CTR conversion, with the kernel mass

the original kernel geometry. These observations, together with the 96.8% mass loss, indicate extensive
transformation of the starting kernels during heat treatment. The large material loss is consistent with binder
and precursor decomposition, carbothermal reduction, and removal of volatile reaction products under
vacuum. Continued CO removal at 1800 °C may additionally shift the U–C–O system toward uranium-rich
compositions and promote uranium loss by evaporation.

 4. Conclusions
 Kernels comprised of a 2UO3(NH3)●3H2O phase and carbon produced using an internal gelation
method were processed under varying atmospheres to investigate the carbothermal reduction and nitridation
process to fabricate kernels of advanced fuels with retained geometry and morphology. Fabrication of UN
kernels was accomplished by calcining C/U = 2.675 green kernels under either ultrahigh purity Ar or Ar-
6%H2 to 1900°C, followed by nitriding under either ultrahigh purity N2 or N2-5%H2 for varying isothermal
hold lengths. Standardless lattice parameters of rocksalt cubic phases present in the diffraction patterns
determined from Rietveld refinement for kernels ranged from 4.926Å to 4.897Å, for kernels calcined under
ultrahigh purity Ar and nitrided for 1 hour under ultrahigh purity N2 and kernels calcined under Ar-6%H2
and nitrided for 20 hours under N2-5%H2, respectively. These lattice parameters correspond to an overall
composition of UC0.6N0.4, and UC0.11N0.89, respectively, although they are likely overestimates compared to
actual carbon contents measured through combustion analysis.
 Retention of kernel geometry was found to be driven by initial heating rates of the furnace profile, with
3°C/min resulting in the smallest magnitude of kernel cracking. Reducing gas atmospheres were found to
be effective in mitigating crack formation further on the initial heating stages, enabling the retention of
kernel geometries through the furnace profile. Regardless of gas atmosphere used, kernels exhibited the
same magnitude of fired bulk density, around 76% with respect to pure UN. Optical microscopy of polished
cross-sections revealed the presence of fine porosity within the bulk, decreasing near the exterior of the
kernel, implying that an additional isothermal hold at elevated temperatures is necessary under these
conditions to increase the density of kernels.
 The furnace gas atmosphere and isothermal hold length during nitriding was found to be key in reducing
the magnitude of impurity phases present, such as UO2 and U2SN2, which were both reduced to trace
amounts under N2-5%H2 for 20 hours. Additionally, reduction in C content of the fabricated UN kernels to
0.41 wt% C was achieved under the same conditions. Net C removal rates calculated from LECO elemental
analysis were 0.0225 wt%C/hr on increasing the isothermal hold length from 10 hours to 20 hours,
suggesting sluggish C removal kinetics compared to literature, implying a significant role of furnace gas
flow path on the purification and densification of UN kernels for advanced gas reactors.
 Fabrication of UCO kernels was also investigated for C/U = 2.1, 2.2 and 2.675 green kernels heated to
a 12 hour hold at 1800°C at 10°C/min under vacuum. Mass losses greater than 90% were observed for C/U
= 2.1 and 2.2 kernels, with 73% mass loss observed for C/U = 2.675 kernels. Significant cracking and
damage to kernels was observed under these conditions, possibly due to uranium evaporation or increased
concentrations of CO in the furnace. Both UC and UO2 were observed through x-ray diffraction after
conversion, indicating that complete vacuum heating is inadequate to produce UCO kernels.
