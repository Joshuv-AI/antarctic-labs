ABSTRACT
Nondestructive determination of uranium enrichment is a core capability for nuclear material
accounting and control (NMAC) and safeguards verification measurements; however, traditional
gamma spectroscopy-based techniques for enrichment measurement rely on significant
assumptions of material composition and geometry, precluding their use in scenarios where a
heterogeneous spatial distribution of enrichments is encountered. As an alternative, we are
developing a technique to use delayed neutron temporal signatures for the measurement of
uranium enrichment. Each uranium isotope has unique delayed neutron group yields, resulting in
a unique delayed neutron decay time profile which can be analyzed to determine enrichment
without the need for calibration sources. As part of this effort, we performed a series of
measurement campaigns in which we used an active well coincidence counter (AWCC) retrofitted
with commercial D-D and D-T generators to evaluate the operational characteristics of this
method in response to a set of uranium enrichment and mass standards, as well as representative
diversion scenarios in which either “concealed” enriched uranium is shielded by depleted uranium
or declared enriched uranium is “hollowed out” and replaced with a central region of depleted
uranium. A standard operating procedure and best practices were compiled to facilitate the use of
delayed neutron-based enrichment measurements for international safeguards inspections.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Project Overview

The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has identified a need for instrumentation to
verify nuclear material in heterogeneous matrices, particularly for waste and scrap material, and
detect highly-enriched (HEU) production at declared low-enriched uranium (LEU) facilities,
which delayed neutron-based measurements are well-positioned to address due to their
penetrating nature.
The technique explored in this effort differs from traditional gamma spectroscopy-based methods
for enrichment measurement in that it does not require any a priori information regarding the
material composition, geometry, shielding, or detector efficiency. Consequently, the delayed
neutron technique is capable of enrichment assay in situations where any of these parameters are
unknown or suspect. Specifically, gamma-based methods are unreliable in scenarios involving
heterogeneous distribution of enrichments, e.g., an item with an HEU core and depleted uranium
(DU) skin would appear as simply DU in a gamma spectroscopy measurement. In contrast,
delayed neutron measurements are sensitive to the fissionable isotopic content of the entire
volume of an item in aggregate.
The delayed neutron enrichment meter developed under this effort may enable accurate
nondestructive assay of previously inaccessible materials in the nuclear fuel cycle. As we
demonstrate, the IAEA could retrofit existing neutron-based safeguards inspection equipment with
commercial neutron generators to comprise such an enrichment meter. While this system would
not replace traditional gamma-based assay for well-defined, homogeneous nuclear materials, it
could be routinely used to measure uranium enrichment in facilities where irregular and/or
heterogeneous material is expected.
Regardless of any specific system design, there are several operational parameters which need to
be optimized. Selection of the irradiation period can improve or degrade contrast between 235 U
and 238 U, and candidate numerical techniques for time profile analysis whose performance will be
compared. To develop the physical system, prior work under NA-241 NGSI in FY16 is being
leveraged, which demonstrated the replacement of AmLi neutron sources with a commercial
deuterium-deuterium (D-D) neutron generator in a large-volume active well coincidence counter
(LV-AWCC) [1] at ORNL. In this effort, we use the existing system to demonstrate delayed
neutron-based uranium enrichment measurement over a wide range, from depleted to highly
enriched (>90% 235 U). Alongside this previously-developed system, higher-output D-T and D-D
neutron generators available at LLNL and SNL/CA will be used in place of the original D-D
generator in conjunction with an ordinary AWCC to investigate the advantages and drawbacks of
modifying the system to incorporate one of these alternative generators.

1.2. Background and Theory

Discrimination of 235 U from 238 U [3] and 233 U [4, 5] by their delayed neutron emission time
profiles has been demonstrated in the laboratory setting numerous times. This is made possible

because 235 U and 238 U have distinct fission fragment distributions, and thus have distinct delayed
neutron precursor yields. Delayed neutron precursors decay with half-lives ranging from about
0.5 s to 60 s, such that the delayed neutron emission time is much longer than the prompt die away
time for typical neutron generators, reducing the active background associated with delayed
neutron measurements to effectively zero. 2.45 or 14.1 MeV neutrons can easily penetrate large
volumes of shielding and SNM compared to uranium passive gamma signatures (186 keV), and
the induced delayed neutrons are similarly immune to shielding and self-absorption. The goal of
this project is to utilize existing high-efficiency neutron counters (e.g., AWCCs) in conjunction
with commercial neutron generators to develop a robust system to induce and measure this
signature on a variety of uranium enrichment and mass standards, as well as a standard, optimized
analysis method to correlate the results with uranium enrichment. Since the delayed neutron time
profiles of 235 U and 238 U depend solely on irradiation time and their delayed neutron group yields,
enrichment can be measured in this way without the need for calibration. The delayed neutron
8-group yields for fission of 238 U and 235 U are shown in Table 1-1.
It should also be noted that throughout this work, an assumption is made that other uranium
isotopes (notably 234 U and 236 U) undergo negligible fission in comparison to 235 U and 238 U, such
that the enrichment is equivalent to the ratio of 235 U to (235 U + 238 U).

2. PROCEDURAL AND ANALYTICAL CONSIDERATIONS

2.1. Neutron detectors

The selection of a neutron detector for measurement of the delayed neutron time profile is
relatively straightforward in comparison to other neutron measurements which may rely on
coincidence/multiplicity measurements and/or neutron spectroscopy, as the statistical quality of a
delayed neutron measurement is determined by the total delayed neutron count rate measured
above background alone. Delayed neutrons have a relatively soft spectrum compared to prompt
fission neutrons [10]; consequently, recoil-based fast neutron detectors (e.g., organic scintillators
or 4 He-based detectors) are typically unsuitable, and capture-based detectors are preferred in
conjunction with a moderator.

2.3. Fit-based analysis

The first approach to determining enrichment from a measured delayed neutron decay time profile
is to simply fit the time profile to a superposition of the 235 U and 238 U time profiles, with the
relative contribution of each isotope corresponding to the enrichment:

3. SIMULATIONS

3.1. Interrogating neutron spectrum

Each AWCC/LV-AWCC configuration tested in this effort has a unique interrogating neutron
spectrum, due to changes in the detector geometry (i.e., AWCC vs. LV-AWCC), neutron generator
(i.e., DD vs. DT), or cadmium shielding. Understanding the characteristics of these interrogating
neutron spectra is vital to interpreting delayed neutron measurements involving both 235 U and
238 U, as these isotopes have radically different fission cross-sections, as shown in Figure 3-1.

 Figure 3-1 Fission cross-sections for 235 U and 238 U[2].

To assess the relative sensitivity to 235 U and 238 U of each AWCC/LV-AWCC configuration, a
series of simulations using MCNPX-PoliMi were performed to quantify both the probability of
fission in each isotope for a given configuration and the spectrum of neutrons inducing fission in
each isotope. Uranium enrichment standards were used as targets in the simulations to assess the
validity of assumptions made for the various signature modes discussed in this work.
Of particular interest is the spectrum of neutrons which induce fission in 235 U and 238 U for each
configuration of detector, neutron generator, and cadmium. While neutrons that moderate in the
HDPE around the neutron generator are not expected to impact the probability of fission in 238 U,
which is fissionable but not fissile, these slow neutrons are expected to dramatically increase this
probability for 235 U and the associated sensitivity of this technique to 235 U. It is also important to
note that due to their high cross-section for fission in 235 U, thermal neutrons are much more likely
to be self-shielded in large and/or highly-enriched items. This self-shielding would lead to

 23

nonlinearity in the expected delayed neutron response with respect to enrichment, and should be
strongly considered for its impacts on operations with this technique. Consequently, the selection
of neutron generator and cadmium shielding will correspond to different use cases for the
technology, e.g., different declared enrichments and uranium masses.
The spectra of neutrons inducing fission in 235 U and 238 U for the LV-AWCC using a DD neutron
generator in the case of no cadmium, partial cadmium, and full cadmium are shown in Figure 3-2.
These spectra demonstrate the phenomenon discussed above: cadmium has little impact on the
probability of fission in 238 U, but thermal neutron-induced fission is massively curtailed in 235 U as
cadmium is added to the cavity lining.

4. EXPERIMENTAL EFFORTS

The aims of this work can be distilled to the application of delayed neutron signature analysis for
two broad scenarios: assay of uranium enrichment and flagging the presence of undeclared
heterogeneously enriched uranium. For each scenario, an experimental campaign was conceived,
as detailed in the following section.

4.1. Measurement of homogeneous uranium enrichment standards

A two-week measurement campaign was executed at ORNL as part of this project, to obtain a
large dataset of delayed neutron time profiles measured using the LV-AWCC-DD generator
system, representing a set of uranium enrichment standards as well as select heterogeneously
enriched representative items.
In this campaign, a ThermoScientific MP320 D-D neutron generator, which emits 106 n/s at full
power, was used as the neutron source. This generator is digitally controlled, and can be operated
via command line. For the campaign, the project team developed a python-based script for
operating the generator which met our specific needs. The script allows a user to specify a number
of irradiation cycles, an irradiation time, and a decay time. Then, the generator will autonomously
go through its startup process, emit neutrons at full power for the irradiation time, shut off, and
wait for the decay time before starting a new cycle, repeating for the set number of cycles. For this
measurement, the generator is set to operate in pulsed mode, where the neutrons are emitted in
discrete bursts of 103 at 1000 Hz: in this mode, a logic signal can be taken from the generator
control electronics for every pulse, which serves as an indication that the generator is running for
data acquisition system. Neutron pulses from the LV-AWCC and these output pulses from the
MP320 were recorded on a CAEN DT5730 digitizer.

4.2. Measurement of heterogeneously enriched uranium

4.2.1. Measurement equipment configurations

A Jomar AWCC was used in a second set of measurements at LLNL, where the primary focus was
the measurement of heterogeneously enriched uranium. The AWCC was modified in a similar
way to the LV-AWCC in the previous measurement in order to accommodate neutron generators,
with a few notable differences in the setup and expected response. First, the AWCC has a narrower

 32

cavity than does the LV-AWCC; consequently, the thickness of HDPE surrounding the neutron
generator is smaller, and a harder interrogating spectrum is expected. Second, the Jomar AWCC
model available for the measurements has a non-removable cadmium liner on the cylindrical sides
of the cavity, so only the cadmium on the end-plugs could be removed. Third, Thermo Scientific
P383 neutron generators were used to interrogate the uranium samples, using both DD and DT
reactions. The DD and DT generators used have nominal outputs of 1 × 107 and 3 × 108 ,
respectively. When the DT generator is used, an even harder spectrum is expected, not only
because of the higher initial neutron energy of 14.1 MeV, but also because the cross-section of
these neutrons in HDPE is smaller, reducing the coupling of the neutron generator to the
moderator and the resultant intensity of thermal neutrons. The detector electronics, readout, and
neutron generator control were the same as used in the previous set of measurements.

Four configurations of the detector system were used to irradiate and measure the delayed neutron
signature of heterogeneously-enriched test objects. In these measurements, “partial cadmium”
refers to the configuration in which the sides and top of the cavity have their cadmium lining, while
the bottom HDPE end plug surrounding the neutron generator is not lined with cadmium. “Full
cadmium” refers to the configuration in which all surfaces of the cavity are lined with cadmium.

A set of 235 U mass standards in the form of 93% HEU U3 O8 was available for this set of
measurements, in addition to a small 93% HEU metal plate, and several DU metal objects. The
individual uranium objects used are described in Table 4-3, and their heterogeneous arrangements
are described in Table 4-4. The “effective enrichment” shown is the ratio of 235 U to total U in the
heterogeneous arrangement. Note that the large DU annulus DU-AN has an internal diameter
sufficiently large to fit the HEU mass standards inside.

5. DISCUSSION AND RECOMMENDED OPERATING
 PROCEDURE

From the measurements detailed in the previous section, it is clear that in general,
delayed-neutron-based assay of uranium enrichment is most powerful and accurate for uranium
enriched ⪅ 20%, with more highly-moderated interrogating spectra further limiting the sensitive
domain. The reasoning for this is logical: 235 U always has a significantly higher fission cross
section than does 238 U, with this difference increasing with decreasing neutron energy.
Consequently, 235 U and 238 U will have equal contributions to the delayed neutron signal at low
enrichment, and at high enrichment, the 235 U signal can be many orders of magnitude more
intense, such that deviations in enrichment have little impact on the shape of the time profile. This
effect can be considered 235 U saturation, and is a very important consideration for operations, as it
means that different high enrichments may be indistinguishable. 235 U saturation will manifest in
the fit-based method as a degeneracy between the 𝑥 and 𝐶 parameters, as a marginal increase in
enrichment at high enrichment is indistinguishable from simply an increase in overall intensity. In
the ratio-based method, it manifests as very high uncertainty in 𝑥, as the uncertainty 𝛿𝑥 is
 𝑑𝑥
proportional to 𝑑𝑅  , which has a large magnitude at values of 𝑅 corresponding to high
enrichment.

It is also clear that a high statistical quality is required for precise measurement of uranium
enrichment using either the fit- or ratio-based analysis methods. Consequently, implementation of
this technique for timely results will require high output neutron generators, likely ⪆ 1 × 107 n/s.
The measurements at ORNL which used a 1 × 107 n/s DD generator only produced time profiles
with high statistical quality when the measurement ran for ≥ 10 hours, which is prohibitively long
for routine use. The measurements at LLNL with a 1 × 107 n/s DD generator provided time
profiles with sufficient statistical quality in ≤ 1 hour, furthermore, when a 3 × 108 n/s DT
generator was used, high-quality results could be achieved in a single cycle of ⪅ 5 minutes. This
is a strong motivation for the use of commercially-available DT generators for this technique, or
otherwise high-output DD generators.

In additional to statistical considerations, use of a DT neutron generator is also motivated by the
fact that at 14 MeV, the ratio of 235 U fission cross-section to that of 238 U is lower than at any lower
energy, which reduces the degree of 235 U saturation as discussed previously.

Assessing the quality of the analysis methods, the fit-based method appears to be generally more
reliable, but the ratio-based method will continue to be investigated for further refinement. From
the measurements in this effort, different uranium enrichments clearly produce statistically
different calculated ratios, however, the model developed to relate ratio to enrichment did not yield
useful results. If it can be further developed and shown to be reliable, the ratio-based method
requires far less computational resources for implementation in an integrated electronics system.

5.1. Concept of operations for generic, independent assay of uranium
 enrichment

To perform a measurement of uranium enrichment when there is no declared enrichment, it is most
convenient to configure the detector and neutron generator with the hardest possible spectrum,
using the full cadmium lining of the well chamber, such that a constant 𝜎 ¯ 𝑓 can be assumed for
both 235 U and 238 U. When a fast interrogating neutron spectrum is used, no assumptions about
enrichment or uranium mass and their associated self-shielding effects are necessary, and the
sensitivity of the detector to 235 U and 238 U are similar, within an order of magnitude.
With all this in mind, the values of 𝜎¯ 𝑓 in Table 3-1 and 𝑐 1 -𝑐 4 in Table 3-2 can be used as constants
in the fit- and ratio-based analyses in §2.3-2.4, respectively. The resultant values of enrichment
can then be used as an estimate which is effectively immune to gamma shielding and variations in
thickness or material matrix.

5.2. Concept of operations for flagging undeclared heterogeneous
 enrichment

To confirm a suspected undeclared heterogeneously-enriched uranium object or verify a declared
enrichment in general, a slightly different procedure is used. In this case, a cadmium configuration
would be chosen based on the declared enrichment, e.g., no or partial cadmium for depleted,
natural, or low-enriched uranium; or full cadmium for highly-enriched uranium. Then, for the
chosen cadmium/neutron generator, the values of 𝜎 ¯ 𝑓 for 235 U and 238 U would be taken from a
look-up table or correlation (to be developed in ongoing work) corresponding to the declared
enrichment and mass. The measured delayed neutron time profile would then be analyzed using
these values, and the resultant value of enrichment is compared to the declared enrichment: a
statistically significant discrepancy indicates that the uranium object is not the same mass and/or
enrichment as declared.

6. TECHNOLOGY OUTLOOK

Based on the results from the measurements using various configurations of a LV-AWCC and
AWCC with DD and DT neutron generators, there is clear feasibility of this technique for
international safeguards NDA measurements in multiple scenarios. Consequently, this work will
progress as a future effort to integrate the hardware associated with the measurement technique,
and unify the neutron generator control software with the output data analysis in a user-friendly
graphical user interface (GUI). Specifically, this will entail a single electronic device which could
supply high- and low-voltage power to the AWCC, acquire list-mode pulse trains from the AWCC
and neutron generator, and interface with the user’s computer. Integrated hardware platforms with
similar requirements incorporate System-On-Chip Field Programmable Gate Arrays (SoC
FPGAs) which excel at acquiring data, controlling hardware through specified protocols,
performing real time analysis, and providing user interaction. A SoC FPGA platform would run a
server locally connected over ethernet to an operator’s browser displaying the GUI; removing
requirements of specific hardware, operating systems, or installed software while allowing the
hardware platform to be adaptable to changes such as new neutron generators or well counters.
Additionally, a SoC FPGA would measure the digital states of the neutron pulses and generator
without needing a power consuming digitizer, enabling the use of small battery for portable
operation. The GUI would allow the user to specify the irradiation and decay times and the
number of neutron generator cycles, while at the same time perform both fit- and
integral-ratio-based analyses of the measured time profiles, updating after each irradiation cycle.
Such an integrated hardware and software system could then be transferred to the IAEA and
deployed for international safeguards use, adding a new tool for the measurement of uranium
enrichment in objects where gamma spectroscopy is impractical.
In parallel to the hardware development effort, the fit- and ratio-based analysis algorithms will be
further refined to incorporate more detailed effects of changing uranium enrichments and masses,
based on additional simulations and measurements.
