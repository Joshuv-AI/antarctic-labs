ABSTRACT
Highly pixelated organic scintillator detectors for nuclear nonproliferation applications require
compact, low-cost readout of hundreds to thousands of silicon photomultiplier (SiPM) channels,
each providing deposited energy, sub-nanosecond timing, and neutron/gamma pulse shape dis-
crimination (PSD). This report documents an investigation of two field-programmable gate array
(FPGA)-centered readout methods that avoid full waveform digitization: delta-sigma modulation
(DSM), which encodes the SiPM pulse as a high-rate one-bit stream, and time-to-digital converter
(TDC)-based methods, which timestamp threshold crossings of the pulse. Simulations show that
one-bit DSM can preserve PSD information for fast organic scintillators only at oversampling rates
near 1 GHz; FPGA input/output latency limits FPGA-in-the-loop modulators to roughly 100 MHz
to 150 MHz, so viable DSM designs require external high-speed feedback logic with an associated
power and component cost, and DSM was not carried to hardware. For the TDC approach, a
four-phase-clock TDC and a tapped-delay-line TDC with tens-of-picosecond precision were im-
plemented on Xilinx 7-series devices, and custom SYZYGY front-end boards were developed to
compare fixed DC threshold, dynamic (RC) threshold, and periodic (sine wave) threshold time-
over-threshold (TOT) readout on a common platform. Experimental measurements with a stilbene
scintillator, a 6 mm SiPM, and Na-22 and AmBe sources demonstrate energy calibration and
clear neutron/gamma separation using two DC thresholds, with emulated RC thresholds showing
improved separation at higher energies, and the periodic TOT method capable of sampling SiPM
waveforms up to 240 MS/s. These results indicate that comparator-plus-TDC readout is a promising
path toward scalable FPGA-based SiPM array readout with PSD capability.

SUMMARY

Scalable readout of large SiPM arrays coupled to fast organic scintillators is a barrier to compact,
interaction-resolving neutron/gamma detection systems. Conventional waveform digitizers do not
scale to thousands of channels, and application-specific integrated circuits are costly to develop
and inflexible. This project evaluated whether FPGA-centered readout could deliver the three
quantities needed per channel: deposited energy, event timing, and neutron/gamma pulse shape
discrimination (PSD). Two approaches were considered: delta-sigma modulation (DSM) and time-
to-digital converter (TDC) methods based on time over threshold (TOT).
DSM (Chap. 3). Waveform simulations of stilbene coupled to a 6 mm SiPM established that PSD-
preserving readout requires performance equivalent to roughly a 50 MHz, 8-bit ADC. Three one-bit
DSM designs were developed with state-space methods and verified in PSpice. A second-order
design with the FPGA in the feedback loop was limited by FPGA I/O latency (6.5 ns to 10 ns) to
a 100 MHz oversampling clock and could not reproduce the prompt or delayed pulse components
needed for PSD. Designs using an external high-speed flip-flop in the feedback path at 1 GHz and
350 MHz preserved PSD information in simulation, but at the cost of hundreds of mW per channel
or reduced bandwidth, respectively. Because these tradeoffs reduce the advantage of DSM over
TDC methods, the DSM approach was not carried through to experimental characterization. The
key finding is that FPGA-in-the-loop DSM is not an attractive option for fast scintillators on the
family of FPGAs considered; future DSM readout must place the loop in external logic or use
devices with substantially lower I/O latency.
TDC implementation (Chaps. 4 to 6). Two FPGA TDCs were implemented: a four-phase-clock
TDC (0.833 ns nominal bin, ∼1 ns RMS) used for early demonstrations, and a calibrated tapped-
delay-line TDC using CARRY4 chains (17.8 ps nominal bin, ∼37 ps effective bin, 30 ps to 50 ps
measured precision) used for final measurements. A series of SYZYGY peripheral boards for the
Opal Kelly Brain-1 (Zynq-7012S) host culminated in a common front-end supporting fixed DC,
RC dynamic, and periodic sine wave thresholds with TLV3800-family comparators. Firmware
packaged the TDC as an AXI4-Stream block with FIFO buffering, six-TDC readout for three DC
thresholds, and a ping-pong TDC architecture supporting up to 240 MS/s of crossings for the
periodic method. Calibration procedures for DAC voltage drop, inter-TDC timing offsets, and
rising/falling group offsets were developed and validated against oscilloscope measurements.
Results (Chaps. 7 and 8). Using stilbene, a 6 mm SiPM, and the tapped-delay-line TDC, a
self-consistent TOT-to-pulse-height calibration was obtained using the three DC channels, and
estimated pulse heights from different thresholds aligned for Na-22 spectra. Energy calibration
using Compton edges followed. With an AmBe source, pairs of DC thresholds produced clear
neutron and gamma bands, with figures of merit that improve at lower energies for lower threshold

 13

pairs. Emulated RC dynamic thresholds derived from 2 GS/s digitized data showed PSD separation
improving with energy, suggesting a favorable tradeoff for one additional FPGA pin and passive
components. The periodic threshold method reconstructed injected waveforms with a baseline-
based phase estimation that removes the need for absolute inter-TDC calibration. The periodic
threshold method was able to successfully resolve SiPM waveforms, despite an introduced source
of reconstruction error that was not identified. An exploratory HDBSCAN clustering method
demonstrated unsupervised neutron/gamma separation for the three-threshold simulated data using
all thresholds at once.
Conclusion. Comparator-plus-TDC readout on commodity FPGAs is a practical, low-component-
count path to energy, timing, and PSD for SiPM-coupled organic scintillators. DSM remains
theoretically capable but is constrained by FPGA I/O latency. The work presented indicates that
FPGA-based readout of SiPM is a viable pathway, and additional work is required to advance the
technology.

1. INTRODUCTION

Advanced radiation detection systems for nuclear nonproliferation and related applications increas-
ingly rely on highly pixelated scintillator detectors [1, 2]. In these systems, large numbers of
photodetector channels are used to infer the deposited energy, interaction time, particle type, and
position of each radiation interaction. Silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) are attractive photodetec-
tors for these applications because they are compact, mechanically robust, insensitive to magnetic
fields, and available in dense commercial arrays with high photon detection efficiency and ex-
cellent timing performance. When coupled to fast organic scintillators, SiPM arrays can enable
compact radiation detection systems, neutron/gamma correlated timing systems, and interaction-
resolving detector concepts such as neutron and gamma-ray imaging systems and high-resolution
radiographic detector panels.
The principal technical challenge addressed in this report is the readout of large SiPM arrays. Sys-
tems of interest can require hundreds to thousands of readout channels, with each channel ideally
providing sub-nanosecond timing, deposited-energy estimation, and neutron/gamma pulse shape
discrimination (PSD). Conventional high-speed waveform digitizers provide excellent information
content but are generally too large, power hungry, and expensive to scale to very high channel counts
in compact deployable systems. Application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) can provide com-
pact, low-power, channel-dense readout, but they require long and costly development cycles and
typically implement a fixed signal-processing architecture. Existing commercial ASICs developed
for applications such as positron emission tomography provide many useful capabilities, but they
are not generally optimized for organic scintillator neutron/gamma PSD, wide dynamic range, or
the full set of requirements associated with interaction-resolving neutron detectors [3–5].
Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) provide an alternative path toward scalable, flexible
SiPM readout. Modern FPGAs offer large numbers of digital input/output pins, high-speed internal
logic, clock-management resources, embedded processors in system-on-chip devices, and in some
families, multi-gigabit transceivers. FPGA-based readout can potentially reduce cost, footprint,
and power consumption relative to full waveform digitization, while retaining more flexibility
than an ASIC-based approach. Firmware can be modified to accommodate different scintillators,
SiPMs, detector geometries, dynamic ranges, timing requirements, or event-processing algorithms,
all without redesigning an ASIC. Because FPGA technology continues to improve, successful
FPGA-based readout architectures may also benefit from future increases in logic density, speed,
and power efficiency.
This report documents work performed to investigate two FPGA-centered readout approaches for
SiPM signals from organic scintillators:

 1. Delta-sigma modulation (DSM), also commonly referred to as sigma-delta modulation
 (SDM), in which an analog SiPM pulse is converted into a high-rate one-bit or few-bit

 17

 digital stream. The density of high values in the bitstream encodes the input signal am-
 plitude, and subsequent digital filtering or windowed bit counting can be used to estimate
 deposited energy and PSD-related pulse-shape information.
 2. Time-to-digital converter (TDC)-based readout, in which FPGA-implemented TDCs measure
 the times at which an analog SiPM signal crosses one or more thresholds. These time
 measurements can be used to estimate pulse time, pulse amplitude through time over threshold
 (TOT), and pulse shape through multiple threshold or dynamic threshold measurements.

These methods were investigated as part of a broader project on flexible and low-cost FPGA-
based SiPM array readout. The broader project also included a separate photon-counting bitstream
approach targeted at monolithic scintillator detectors, in which individual scintillation photon
arrivals are converted to digital bitstreams and processed using FPGA gigabit transceivers. That
photon-counting work is not the primary subject of this report. Instead, this document focuses on
the DSM and TDC methods intended primarily for optically segmented detector geometries, where
each scintillator element or pixel is read out by one or a small number of SiPM channels.
The technical goal of the DSM and TDC work was to determine whether these methods can provide
the essential quantities needed for neutron/gamma detection with organic scintillators:
 • an estimate of deposited energy or detected scintillation light;
 • a timestamp suitable for sub-nanosecond or better event timing;
 • a PSD observable sufficient to distinguish neutron and gamma interactions;
 • an implementation path compatible with compact, low-cost, high-channel-count FPGA-based
 electronics.

2. OVERVIEW OF READOUT METHODS

The readout approaches considered in this report convert the analog SiPM waveform into one or
more digital representations that are well matched to FPGA resources. Delta-sigma modulation
converts the waveform into a high-rate digital bitstream whose local density represents the analog
signal amplitude, similar to pulse-width modulation. TDC-based approaches convert threshold-
crossing times into digital timestamps, from which timing, pulse amplitude, and pulse-shape
observables can be inferred. In the case of DSM, most of the complexity is in the external analog
filtering circuits, while for the TDC the complexity is contained in the implementation of the TDC
within the FPGA.
The target signal class is the output of an SiPM coupled to a fast organic scintillator. Organic
scintillators such as stilbene or EJ-276 produce scintillation pulses whose temporal profile differs
for gamma-ray and neutron interactions. Gamma-ray interactions are dominated by faster scintil-
lation components, whereas neutron interactions exhibit a relatively larger slow component. Pulse
shape discrimination exploits this difference. In conventional waveform processing, PSD is often
performed using a charge-comparison metric, such as

with a high-speed analog-to-digital converter (ADC).
The two approaches described below represent different points in the design space. DSM is most
naturally viewed as an alternative ADC architecture that can preserve integrated charge and pulse-
shape information if sufficient oversampling bandwidth is available. TDC-based methods are most
naturally suited to precise timing, but can also provide amplitude and PSD observables through
time over threshold or related threshold-crossing techniques.

2.1. DSM methods

Delta-sigma modulation (DSM), also called sigma-delta modulation (SDM), is an oversampling
analog-to-digital conversion technique in which an analog input signal is converted into a high-rate
stream of one-bit or few-bit digital samples. Rather than representing the instantaneous signal
amplitude directly with a multi-bit value, a DSM encodes the input amplitude in the density of
high bit values in the bitstream. Over a given time interval, a larger analog input produces a larger
fraction of ones in the bitstream, while a smaller input produces a smaller fraction. A digital

A basic first-order DSM consists of an integrator, a comparator, and a feedback digital-to-analog
conversion path (Fig. 2-1). The input signal is compared against the feedback representation of the
quantized output. The difference is integrated, quantized by the comparator, and fed back to the
summing node. This feedback loop shapes the quantization noise, pushing much of it to higher
frequencies. A subsequent low-pass or decimation filter suppresses the high-frequency quantization
noise and recovers the signal band of interest.
For FPGA-based SiPM readout, DSM is attractive because its one-bit representation is well suited
for the digital I/O of an FPGA. The FPGA itself at a minimum only needs to implement a flip
flop clocked at the sample rate to record 𝑦[𝑛]. The conversion and processing applied on 𝑦[𝑛] can
be implemented using FPGA input logic and digital filters. Previous work in radiation detection
demonstrated FPGA-assisted one-bit DSM for SiPM and photomultiplier tube (PMT) signals in
medical-imaging-like contexts, with promising energy-measurement performance and compact
electronics [6, 7]. The central question in this project was whether similar methods could be
extended to fast organic scintillators and, in particular, whether DSM bitstreams could support
neutron/gamma PSD.
At the same time, DSM imposes demanding timing requirements. The useful signal bandwidth
is determined by the oversampling frequency, modulator order, and acceptable signal-to-noise
ratio. If the oversampling rate is too low, the recovered pulse shape is distorted or too noisy for
PSD. Organic scintillator pulses are faster than many inorganic scintillator pulses used in medical
imaging, and neutron/gamma PSD depends on resolving relatively subtle differences in the pulse
tail. Therefore, the DSM implementation must provide adequate effective bandwidth and resolution
over the timescales relevant to PSD.

A key distinction for the DSM work is whether the FPGA is inside or outside the feedback
loop. In the original concept, the FPGA would provide the quantizer and feedback logic directly.
This is attractive because it minimizes external components, but the FPGA input/output latency
becomes part of the DSM loop delay. For fast scintillator readout, this loop delay can limit the
stable oversampling frequency. Alternative architectures remove the FPGA from the high-speed
feedback loop by using an external flip-flop or comparator stage, with the FPGA recording the
resulting bitstream. This can recover high oversampling rates but shifts some complexity and
power consumption back into external circuitry.
This report considers several DSM-related concepts:
 1. First-order DSM with FPGA in the loop. This is the simplest architecture and provides a
 useful baseline, but its performance is limited by oversampling rate and first-order noise
 shaping.
 2. Second-order DSM with FPGA in the loop. A second-order modulator can provide improved
 in-band noise performance for the same oversampling rate, at the cost of additional analog
 circuitry and stability considerations.
 3. External high-speed DSM feedback. The FPGA can be removed from the feedback loop, with
 an external high-speed flip-flop or related logic providing the fast quantization and feedback
 path. The FPGA then records and processes the bitstream.
The DSM work described in later chapters therefore serves two purposes. First, it evaluates whether
DSM can theoretically provide adequate energy and PSD performance for SiPM-coupled organic
scintillators. Second, it identifies the practical implementation constraints that determine whether
FPGA-centered DSM is feasible with current devices and reasonable per-channel circuitry.

2.2. TDC methods

TDC-based readout takes a complementary approach. Rather than trying to reconstruct the ana-
log waveform through oversampled amplitude encoding, the readout records the times at which

 23

the waveform crosses one or more voltage thresholds. These threshold-crossing times are then
used to infer pulse timing, pulse amplitude, and pulse-shape information. This approach is well
matched to FPGAs because modern devices can implement high-resolution TDCs using internal
clocking resources and fast carry-chain delay elements to realize tapped delay lines for sub-clock
resolution.
The simplest TDC-based observable for amplitude estimation is time over threshold. For a fixed
threshold voltage 𝑉th , the time over threshold is

 𝑡TOT = 𝑡fall − 𝑡 rise ,

where 𝑡rise is the time at which the pulse crosses the threshold on the rising edge and 𝑡fall is the
time at which it crosses the same threshold on the falling edge. For pulses with a consistent shape,
larger pulses remain above threshold longer, so TOT is correlated with pulse amplitude or deposited
energy.
For fast scintillator pulses, a low threshold can provide good leading-edge timing and sensitivity to
low-energy events. However, the falling-edge crossing at a low threshold occurs late in the pulse,
where the slope is shallow and the signal is more susceptible to baseline noise. For the falling edge
this means that the comparator may transition at a later time than for the rising edge transition.
These timing uncertainties can degrade the amplitude estimate and introduce timing jitter. A
higher threshold can improve the robustness of the falling-edge measurement and provide stronger
sensitivity to the prompt component of the pulse, but it increases the minimum pulse amplitude
required for an event to be measured. Thus, a single fixed threshold involves a tradeoff among
timing, energy threshold, dynamic range, and PSD performance. Several TDC-based architectures
were considered to address these tradeoffs.

2.2.1. DC Threshold TOT

In the DC threshold TOT method, one or more constant DC voltage thresholds are compared against
the SiPM signal. A single threshold can provide event timing and an amplitude estimate. Multiple
thresholds provide information about the pulse shape. For example, a low threshold is sensitive
to the total duration of the pulse, including the slow scintillation tail, while a higher threshold is
more specifically sensitive to the prompt component. Comparing the TOT from one threshold
against the TOT from another threshold can produce neutron/gamma separation analogous to
charge-comparison PSD.

The advantages of fixed-threshold TOT are simplicity, low component count, and direct compati-
bility with FPGA TDC inputs. The disadvantages are nonlinearity, threshold-dependent dynamic
range, and sensitivity of the falling-edge crossing to noise.

2.2.2. Dynamic-threshold TOT

Dynamic time over threshold replaces the fixed threshold with a threshold that changes in time after
a trigger or threshold crossing. The threshold may be a linear ramp, an RC ramp, or other shaped
waveform. The purpose is to improve the relationship between measured time and pulse amplitude
and to reduce the time spent in regions where small voltage noise produces large timing uncertainty
due to comparator propagation delay uncertainty.

In a dynamic-threshold implementation, the SiPM pulse is compared to a reference waveform
𝑉ref (𝑡) rather than a constant 𝑉th . If 𝑉ref (𝑡) is chosen appropriately, the crossing time can be made
more nearly linear with pulse amplitude over a desired dynamic range. Previous work has shown
that dynamic TOT can improve energy linearity relative to simple TOT [8, 9]. In the present
application, using two dynamic thresholds or two ramp shapes may also provide PSD sensitivity
by probing different portions of the pulse.

A practical dynamic-threshold circuit can be implemented with a comparator, FPGA-generated
control signal, and RC or active feedback network that produces the time-varying reference. The
FPGA records the resulting crossing times with a TDC. Compared with fixed-threshold TOT, this
method adds analog complexity but may improve energy response and reduce falling-edge noise
sensitivity.

2.2.3. Periodic threshold waveform reconstruction

A more information-rich TDC-based method uses a periodic reference waveform, such as a sine
wave, as the comparator threshold. The SiPM signal is compared against this known periodic
waveform. Each crossing time provides a point at which the unknown SiPM voltage equals the
known reference voltage. If the reference waveform is known and the crossing times are measured
accurately, an input waveform can be reconstructed from the set of crossings [11–13].
A scope trace representing the oscillating-threshold concept can be seen in Fig. 2-3. The lower
orange signal would be fed into one or more FPGA-based TDCs, and the resulting time stamps
would permit reconstruction of the full waveform.
This method is conceptually intermediate between TOT and full waveform digitization. It does
not sample the waveform at uniform time intervals with a multi-bit ADC. Instead, it obtains
nonuniform samples determined by intersections of the pulse and the oscillating reference. The
vertical precision is related to the TDC timing precision and the slope of the reference waveform at
each crossing. A faster reference oscillation yields more crossings and potentially more waveform
information, but it also increases the demands on the comparator, TDC, and signal integrity.
For PSD applications, oscillating-threshold reconstruction is attractive because it can recover
enough pulse-shape information to support conventional or near-conventional PSD algorithms. Its
disadvantages are greater circuit complexity, the need for a stable and well-characterized reference
waveform, higher data volume, and potentially more complicated reconstruction algorithms.

3. DSM METHODS

Delta-sigma modulation was investigated as a potential low-cost, low-power, FPGA-compatible
method for extracting deposited energy and pulse-shape information from SiPM signals. The
objective was to determine whether a DSM-based readout could preserve enough of the time
profile of organic scintillator pulses to support neutron/gamma PSD, while using substantially less
external circuitry than a conventional high-speed multi-bit ADC.
The DSM work was motivated by prior demonstrations of FPGA-assisted DSM readout for radiation
detectors, including one-bit DSM readout of PMT and SiPM signals and compact multi-channel
nuclear-imaging readout systems [6, 7]. Those demonstrations focused primarily on energy mea-
surement in scintillators relevant to medical imaging. The present work considered a more demand-
ing case: SiPM readout of fast PSD-capable organic scintillators, where the readout must preserve
differences in the scintillation temporal profile between gamma-ray and neutron interactions.
The design goals for the DSM work were:
 • preserve pulse integral information for deposited-energy estimation;
 • preserve sufficient pulse-shape information for PSD;
 • minimize the number of FPGA I/O pins per channel;
 • minimize external analog components and per-channel power;
For these reasons, the work focused on one-bit DSM architectures. Multi-bit DSM architectures
can improve in-band signal-to-noise ratio for a given oversampling frequency, but they require
additional comparators, more complex feedback digital-to-analog conversion to preserve linearity,
and more FPGA I/O resources [14]. Since the target application is high-channel-count SiPM
readout, one-bit designs were the most attractive starting point.

3.1. Initial simulation results

The first step in evaluating DSM for SiPM readout was to determine the effective waveform-
sampling performance required for energy estimation and PSD. A simulation framework was
developed to generate representative SiPM waveforms for gamma-ray and neutron interactions in
stilbene coupled to an OnSemi J-series 6 mm SiPM. The simulated waveforms were then sampled by
model ADCs with different sampling rates and effective numbers of bits as shown in Fig. 3-1. This
allowed the required signal bandwidth and resolution to be estimated independently of any particular
DSM implementation. These limits then bound acceptable OSR and ENOB and modulator order
𝐿 for a candidate DSM architecture to be viable for neutron/gamma discrimination.

The simulated gamma and neutron waveforms differed primarily in the relative amount of delayed
scintillation light, based on time profiles found in the literature [15, 16]. Gamma-ray interactions
produced pulses with a larger prompt component, while neutron interactions produced pulses
with a larger slow component and therefore a longer tail. A PSD-capable readout must preserve
these differences well enough that a tail-to-total or equivalent metric can separate the two particle
classes.

The initial ADC-equivalent simulations showed that acceptable PSD performance requires both
adequate sampling bandwidth and adequate vertical resolution. In the simulated conditions, perfor-
mance comparable to a conventional charge-comparison PSD measurement required an effective
Nyquist-rate ADC with sampling frequency of order tens of MHz and vertical resolution of at least
six bits. In particular, an ADC with approximately 50 MHz sampling rate and approximately 8
effective bits was found to be a useful target for preserving PSD information in the simulated SiPM
pulses. Lower sampling rates or lower effective resolution degraded the PSD separation, especially
for lower-light-output events. This study is shown in Fig. 3-2, where the number shown indicates
the acceptable order of 1-bit DSM ADC that could be used to realize the same signal bandwidth
and ENOB.

This ADC-equivalent requirement was then translated into DSM requirements. For a one-bit DSM,
the in-band signal-to-noise ratio depends on the oversampling frequency, the modulator order, and
the signal bandwidth. Higher oversampling frequency and higher modulator order both improve the
achievable in-band resolution. Initial calculations and simulations indicated that a first-order DSM
operating at sufficiently high oversampling frequency could retain some PSD sensitivity, but that a
second-order DSM would provide substantially better waveform fidelity for the same oversampling
rate.

A representative conclusion from this early phase was that a one-bit DSM operating near 1 GHz
could plausibly provide sufficient information for PSD if the modulator architecture and feedback
loop could be implemented with sufficiently low delay. However, the feasibility of this approach
depends critically on the total loop delay between the quantizer and feedback DAC. If the loop

This delay constraint became the central limitation for FPGA-in-the-loop DSM architectures. Xilinx

3.2. Proposed circuits

Based on the initial simulations, three DSM circuit concepts were investigated. They were chosen to
span the trade space between minimum external circuitry and sufficient oversampling performance;
their principal characteristics are summarized in Table 3-1.

The first concept (Design 1, Fig. 3-3) retained the FPGA inside the DSM feedback loop: the FPGA
receives the quantizer output, clocks the one-bit value, and drives the feedback signal. The appeal
of this approach is its simplicity and scalability, since only the analog integration stages and level
shifting lie outside the FPGA. Its limitation is that the FPGA I/O latency is part of the feedback
loop. DSM theory requires this delay to be less than one oversampling clock period or explicitly
compensated, which restricts the clock to roughly 100 MHz. Design 1 therefore used a higher-order
modulator with additional external integration stages to improve performance at this reduced rate,
and a direct feedback path to compensate a loop delay modeled as a fraction of the clock period.
Such compensation improves stability, but it cannot recover the oversampling performance lost
when the clock rate is an order of magnitude below the desired 1 GHz scale.

The second and third concepts (Designs 2 and 3, Fig. 3-4) removed the FPGA from the high-speed
feedback loop. An external high-speed D flip-flop performs the clocked one-bit quantization and
drives the feedback path, while the FPGA supplies or synchronizes to the clock and records the
bitstream. Because the loop delay is then dominated by the flip-flop and associated analog circuitry
rather than by FPGA I/O, oversampling rates of several hundred MHz to 1 GHz are possible. The
cost is additional external circuitry and higher per-channel power. Design 2 operated at 1 GHz
using LVPECL-class logic, which was estimated to consume hundreds of mW per channel in the
high-speed feedback element. Design 3 was an intermediate design at 350 MHz using lower-power

supply voltages, and delay compensation was included where appropriate. Circuit-level implemen-
tations were then created in PSpice to verify that realistic analog components could reproduce the
idealized state-space behavior as shown in Fig. 3-5.

3.4. DSM summary

The DSM investigation identified both a potential path and a practical limitation for DSM-based
SiPM readout. Simulations indicate that one-bit DSM can preserve energy and PSD information if
the oversampling rate is sufficiently high, which in practice requires implementing the high-speed
feedback loop outside the FPGA. The original goal of placing the FPGA directly in the DSM
feedback loop is limited by FPGA I/O latency; for the fast organic scintillator pulses considered
here, that latency restricts the oversampling rate to a regime where waveform reconstruction and
PSD performance are inadequate. External-feedback architectures remain technically promising,
but their power consumption and component count reduce their advantage over other readout
strategies, particularly TDC-based methods, and the DSM approach was not carried forward to
experimental characterization within this project. The results nevertheless provide design guidance
for future DSM readout efforts: either the feedback loop must be implemented with external
high-speed logic, or an FPGA or device technology with substantially lower I/O latency must be
used.

4. TDC REALIZATIONS ON FPGA

The TDC-based readout methods described in Chap. 2 require accurate measurement of the crossing
times of an input signal to the FPGA transitions between logic states. In this work, an external
comparator sits between the analog signal and the FPGA, and the comparator output is a digital
pulse whose rising and falling edges correspond to the times when the analog SiPM signal crosses
a selected threshold. The TDC implemented in the FPGA measures these edge times, and the
difference between them gives the time over threshold. For multi-threshold methods, independent
TDC channels measure the crossing times associated with multiple comparator outputs and their
times are correlated together after accounting for timing offsets due to differences in signal trace
lengths and delays within the FPGA fabric.
Two FPGA TDC implementations were investigated. The first was a relatively simple multi-
phase-clock TDC, implemented initially to demonstrate the basic readout concept and to provide a
low-complexity timing measurement. The second was a tapped-delay-line TDC using FPGA carry-
chain resources, pursued to achieve substantially finer timing resolution. The multi-phase-clock
implementation provided a useful first demonstration and was sufficient for early spectroscopy tests
with emulated detector pulses, especially using a slower inorganic scintillator. The tapped-delay-
line implementation provided timing resolution in the tens of picoseconds, better matched to the
final threshold-based PSD measurements with fast organic scintillators.

4.1. Four-phase TDC

The simplest method of implementing a TDC on an FPGA is to count the number of clock periods
for which the input signal remains high. This simple counter allows for calculating input pulse
length based on the known period of the clock the counter is based on. While this method is
straightforward to implement on board, it is severely limited by the maximum possible clock speed
supported by the internal fabric of the FPGA. The fastest Artix-7 FPGAs can support clocks up to
628 MHz providing a time resolution of 1.59 ns [19]; however, increasing the FPGA clock speeds
leads to increasing instability and counters are also limited by additional delays from the routing
of signals between FPGA elements.
One method of increasing the TDC clock speed while reducing instability and jitter is by using mul-
tiple clocks below the maximum speed but with different phases [20]. By using 𝑁 clocks separated
in phase, the nominal fine timing bin is reduced by a factor of 𝑁. In this TDC implementation, a
set of four 300 MHz clocks with phases of 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270° were used to create an effective
1200 MHz clock, providing a theoretical time resolution of 0.833 ns. The pulse length of the input
signal is calculated by using a counter based on the in-phase 300 MHz clock and by recording
the state of each clock on the rising and falling edge of the input signal. The counter provides a

measured pulse length. Due to the four different phases, any combination of two clocks will always
be high on the rising and falling edge of the clocks while the other two low. The edge of the input

of the three out-of-phase clocks are synchronized to the in-phase clock to keep the entire design
synchronous for metastability. This is done by using a chain of flip-flops (FF) for each clock where
the length is 𝑁 number of clocks (16 FFs total when using four clocks). The clock state upon the
rising or falling edge of the input signal is fed into the chain of FFs where the FF output is fed into
the FF input of the chain the next phase clock. This means that the clock state of the 270° clock
is fed into the first FF in its respective chain, then synchronized to the next FF of the 180° clock,
then the next FF of the 90° clock, and finally the next FF of the in-phase clock. This scheme is
shown in Fig. 4-1. This results in a unique identifier for the start and stop times of the input signal
which can then be related to the delay in time between the start of the input signal and the start of
the counter.

The design of the multi-phase-clock TDC is separated into two components, the TDC and the data

4.2. Tapped delay line TDC

To improve timing resolution beyond the four-phase-clock implementation, a tapped-delay-line
TDC was adopted from an open source design by Michel Adamič [24]. Carry-chain TDCs are
commonly used in FPGA-based time measurement because the dedicated carry logic provides
propagation delays smaller than the period of any clock. Instead of resolving time only by clock
phase, the input edge propagates through a chain of carry elements normally used for binary
addition, and the state of the chain is sampled on a system clock edge. The number of taps reached
by the propagating edge measures how long the edge has been travelling, and therefore how far
ahead of the sampling clock edge it arrived. This gives a fine-time measurement within one clock
period.

clock edge, a larger tap count corresponds to an earlier arrival: an edge arriving just after a clock

4.3. Comparison of TDC implementations

The two TDC implementations served different purposes in the project. The four-phase TDC was
straightforward to implement and provided an early demonstration that FPGA-based time-over-
threshold measurements could recover amplitude-correlated pulse-width information. Its nominal
0.833 ns bin width and measured ∼1 ns RMS timing precision were adequate for initial testing with
pulse generators and detector-emulator spectra, but not ideal for final PSD characterization.

 41

The tapped-delay-line TDC required more careful implementation and calibration, but provided
much finer timing resolution. Its use of carry-chain resources enabled tens-of-picoseconds timing
precision, shifting the limiting factors from the FPGA TDC to the analog readout chain and
comparator threshold crossings. For this reason, the tapped-delay-line TDC was the preferred
implementation for the final multi-threshold and dynamic-threshold measurements.
A concise comparison is given in Table 4-1. The final TDC-based readout results presented later
in this report use the higher-resolution tapped-delay-line approach where possible. The four-phase
TDC remains useful as a low-resource implementation and as a demonstration of the underlying
time-over-threshold concept, but the tapped-delay-line TDC better supports the timing precision
required for robust SiPM threshold-crossing measurements.

5. ELECTRONICS

This chapter describes the development of printed circuit board (PCB) designs which were manu-
factured and evaluated. Only the time-to-digital portion was realized on final hardware boards, with
the delta-sigma methods explored in simulation as described in Chap. 3. This work made extensive
use of the SYZYGY open standard of high-speed peripheral boards for rapid prototyping [26]. This
section gives an overview of the electronic design, a progression of prototype circuits developed,
and a description of the final circuit design used to obtain the results in Chap. 7.

5.1. FPGA Host Board and SYZYGY Interface

To support rapid prototyping of proposed circuit designs it is preferable to separate the circuit
elements supporting the FPGA, external power, and data transfer from the portions of the circuit
relating to SiPM readout. Several peripheral interfaces are common with electronic development
boards such as Pmod and FPGA Mezzanine Card (FMC) [27, 28]. Pmod interfaces are simple but
are bandwidth limited. FMC and variants support multi-gigabit data transfer, but the connector
itself is large and supports many more pins than would be used in our designs focused on single
SiPM readout.
A third peripheral standard is the open SYZYGY interface by Opal Kelly, which has a standard
variation that supports up to 32 custom pins with bandwidths of up to 500 MHz in a small size [26].
Additionally, SYZYGY compliant boards support SmartVIO, which allows each peripheral board
to request a specific supply voltage value. This decouples the supply voltage of the FPGA and the
I/O pins, which allows exploring low-voltage differential signaling (LVDS), low-voltage CMOS
(LVCMOS), Stub Series Terminated Logic (SSTL), and other I/O standards without hardware
changes.
The last consideration for the FPGA is analysis of the data acquired and post-processing of it.
While all desired functionality could be implemented in firmware, it is less work to instead transfer
data out of the FPGA and into a central processing unit (CPU) for post-processing and analysis.
Additionally, it is necessary to send control signals from a user to the FPGA to begin taking data
and monitor status conditions. A common variant of FPGAs are System-on-Chip FPGAs (SoC
FPGAs) which package an FPGA together with an embedded processor. The Xilinx Zynq FPGA
SoC family embeds an Artix 7-Series FPGA with an ARM Cortex-A9 processor, which share an
AMBA AXI4 high-speed interconnect. This allows FPGA firmware to easily write large amounts of
data directly to shared DDR memory, and for a CPU to access that data. General purpose registers
are implemented using AXI4 which are shared between the CPU and FPGA, allowing integrated
data acquisition that can be controlled by from the CPU using a script. Xilinx Vivado supports this
design flow using the block diagram development model and provided IP blocks.

5.2. TDC Circuit Design Iterations

The TDC-based readout approach requires an analog front end to convert the SiPM current pulse
into a voltage waveform suitable and large enough for comparison against one or more reference
thresholds. The output of each comparator is routed to an FPGA input and timestamped by the TDC
firmware described in Chap. 4. The circuit design therefore has three primary requirements:
 1. Provide a stable voltage representation of the SiPM pulse with sufficient amplitude and
 bandwidth;
 2. Generate one or more threshold-comparison signals suitable for energy and PSD estimation;
 3. Produce digital outputs compatible with the FPGA and TDC timing requirements.
Secondary requirements are the generation of any supply voltages to the board, inclusion of test
points, and complying with the the SYZYGY standard by including a microcontroller that specifies
the I/O voltage requested. Initial benchtop work used commercial evaluation boards and discrete
wiring to explore the basic signal chain before committing to a custom PCB.

avoid stubs to ensure signal integrity of the input SiPM waveform. The resulting board is shown
in the left of Fig. 5-4, and was made with a four-layer stackup to reduce costs. The main purpose
of this board was to ensure no other large problems remained, and to provide a board to begin
finalizing the FPGA firmware.

5.2.3. Final TDC Board

With the second prototype completed, a final version of the board was made which would support
all three TDC readout methods of Sec. 2.2. To remove any possibility of introduced noise, it was
decided to instead couple the SiPM signal to ground in a non-inverting opamp configuration to
keep the noise as low as possible on the SiPM waveform itself. To do this, the evaluation boards
were restored to their original configuration where the SiPM output is fed through a 10 Ω shunt
resistor and the opamp operates in a fixed 7x amplification gain configuration. Tying the SiPM
signal to ground required that the high speed comparators are provided ±2.5 V in a split-supply
configuration. Small separate test circuits for the generation of the ±2.5 V rails using the supplied
+3.3 V and +5 V rails were made to verify the generated voltages were correct and did not propagate
any supply voltage noise.
This board also included the footprint to generate an RC ramping threshold to support the third
TDC readout method. A prototype board was manufactured to verify the design as shown in
Fig. 5-5. The fixed value RC circuit was included on the final TDC board, with the resistor and

6. FPGA FIRMWARE, ANALYSIS SOFTWARE, AND SIMULATIONS

This chapter describes the FPGA digitial design (firmware) implemented for the DC threshold and
periodic threshold methods, and the analysis software applied before SiPM relevant information
such as energy and PSD can be obtained from the recorded timestamp information.

6.1. FPGA Firmware

This section briefly describes the FPGA firmware designs which were built around the TDC
described previously in Sec. 4.2. The original TDC implementation [24] was packaged as an IP
block which was configured to generate embedded block memory (BRAM) to store timestamp
hit data. The first modification was to abstract the interface so each TDC instance provides an
AXI4-Stream interface that can write to any other AXI4-Stream receiver including DDR memory.
To avoid requiring that the memory end point be ready at all times, each TDC is configured with a
small first-in, first-out (FIFO) buffer such that many timestamps can accumulate until the memory
destination is ready to receive data.

The readout of the DC threshold method of Sec. 2.2.1 and the oscillating threshold method of
Sec. 2.2.3 were realized using separate FPGA firmware. However, several aspects were shared
between them, including

 • SPI interface to interact with the 8-channel DAC and setting of DC voltages

 • Conversion of the input LVDS signals to asynchronous rising and falling edge digital signals

 • Inclusion of an external low-jitter 312.5 MHz clock to drive the TDC logic

 • A coarse binary counter driven by the 312.5 MHz clock

 • The ZYNQ7 Processing IP, System Reset IP, and associated AXI interconnect IP modules
 for transferring data between the FPGA fabric and the neighboring A7 ARM processor

A given firmware design is loaded into the FPGA from a Python Jupyter notebook running on the
embedded ARM processor. The notebook also parses the generated hardware handoff file which
describes the addresses of the registers present and wraps them in simple python function calls to
drive the acquisition from a web browser on a separate computer connected to Opal Kelly Brain-1
over Ethernet.

6.1.1. DC Threshold Firmware

For the DC threshold method, six TDCs total are required. Each of the three DC thresholds needs
a separate pair of TDCs to accurately measure the rising and falling edges of the SiPM waveform
with respect to each threshold. Since the AXI interface was already made the main interface of the
TDC, and combined with the internal FIFO, adding these TDCs was straightforward, as the AXI
interconnect and AXI4-Stream Switch IPs handle the readout from all the TDCs automatically.
These IP modules then pass each TDC AXI4 data stream into a shared embedded block memory
space of 128 KB. Each data packet stores the TDC channel ID, and the ordering of the timestamps
is handled post-acquisition using software.
The control of the FPGA firmware is fairly simple. An 8-bit register controls the six TDCs
independently, and also includes a flag to reset the coarse counter. After each run the block ram is
rewritten with zeros from software, and at the start of the next run the 8-bit register is set to 0xFF to
start acquisition. After a set period of time the acquisition is ended by writing 0x00 to the control
register and the data is transferred from the FPGA block ram to the CPU. All of these aspects of
the design are shown in Fig. 6-1.

6.1.2. Periodic Threshold Firmware

The firmware design for the periodic threshold differs in several ways to support recording hundreds
or thousands of threshold crossings for each SiPM waveform.
First, to obtain a reliable trigger, one of the the comparators is used to indicate whether the SiPM
waveform exceeds a DC threshold. This is only used to indicate a pulse has arrived. This is used

 50

 Figure 6-2. The block diagram of the FPGA design for the Periodic Threshold readout method.

with a TDC Control Block, which then fans out enable signals to all of the TDC modules. The
Control Block also prevents the trigger signal from propagating if any of the TDCs are not ready to
begin acquisition, in addition to keeping track of how many triggers were read out or missed.

The second change is the modification of the FIFO within the TDC to store hundreds of crossings
in a round-robin fashion. As the TDC is continuously recording crossing times, we need a way to
keep several of the crossing times prior to the DC trigger firing. A round robin readout therefore
continuously overwrites the oldest timestamp present. When a trigger is fired, the oldest timestamp
is redirected into an AXI Direct Memory Address module, which will write the timestamps to the
512 MB of on board DDR memory.

However, due to the delay line needing to be reset between each hit, each TDC has a maximum
readout rate of about 70 MS/s, limiting the total readout rate to about 140 MS/s when recording
both the rising and falling edges. To increase the readout speed, two TDCs are used for each of the
rising and falling edges in an asynchronous ping-pong architecture. A steering module acts as a
multiplexer, connecting one of two TDCs to the input signal and alternating on the falling edge of
the signal. In this design, we can support up to an average of 240 MS/s of threshold crossings.

The other aspects of the firmware are similar to the DC threshold firmware. Control signals interface
to the TDC Control Block to begin or stop acquisition, after each run the DDR memory is set to 0
and the coarse counter is reset. The block diagram for this firmware design is shown in Fig. 6-2.

6.2. Analysis software

Each of the three methods of Sec. 2.2 require different approaches to process and analyze the data
acquired. For the simple threshold methods only a handful of timestamps are acquired, while for
the periodic threshold method thousands of timestamps are acquired per SiPM pulse. The DC
threshold method needs to be carefully calibrated to compensate for timing offsets and delays; the
RC method was explored using simulation; and the periodic threshold method is treated more as
a digitization method. An overview of the analysis required for each method is described with
discussion of their relevance for SiPM readout reserved for Chap. 7

6.2.1. DC Threshold Analysis Software

The DC Threshold method relies on accurate timing performance with respect to a specific DC
voltage value. While the calculation of the fine time measurement easily yields the sub-clock cycle
resolution using the look up table shown in Fig. 4-7, each of the six TDCs have their own timing
offset due to the PCB trace length and internal placement of the TDC carry chain within the FPGA
resources.

First, the DC voltage is calibrated to ensure that the voltage output from the DAC is exactly the
voltage applied onto the comparator input pin. As there is a 500 Ω protection resistor between
them, and the comparator draws a few µA of current, there is a drop of several mV between them.
A calibration was performed by iteratively writing DC voltage levels from software and comparing
to the value measured by an accurate multimeter at the comparator pins. The resulting data series
was fitted to
                              𝑉drop = −(𝑎 + 𝑏(1 − 𝑒 −𝑉set /𝑡 )) + 𝑐𝑉set ,
where 𝑉set is the DAC set voltage and 𝑉drop is the difference between the measured voltage and
𝑉set . The resulting calibration points and fitted curves for each of the three thresholds are shown in
Fig. 6-3.

Next is correlating the timestamps after calibration by accounting for the offsets between the six
TDCs. This is done by using a DG535 pulse generator as the input signal, setting all of the DC
thresholds equal to 450 mV, and measuring the offsets among the rising edge TDCs and the falling
edge TDCs separately. This is shown in Fig. 6-4 for two of the rising edge TDCs.

The process is repeated for all pairs of rising edge TDCs and all pairs of falling edge TDCs. The
earliest rising edge TDC and the earliest falling edge TDC are given an offset of zero, and the other
two TDCs for the riding and edge groups are assigned a relative timing offset such that the mean of
the timing difference distributions of Fig. 6-4 is zero.

The remaining offset to be calculated is between the rising and falling edge TDC groups. The
average crossing time of the DG535 pulse at 0.45 V is calculated by acquiring multiple waveforms
using a 5 GS/s oscilloscope with a bandwidth of 1 GHz and averaging the time over threshold in
software. A single offset is then applied to all of the pairs of rising and falling edge TDC crossing
times, which then yields time distributions with the correct mean as shown in Fig. 6-5.

7. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

7.1. DC threshold TOT

In order to begin creating estimated energy spectra and plots demonstrating neutron and gamma
discrimination using the DC threshold methods, a function to map TOT to pulse height is required.
Initially, this was estimated using the synthetic generation of TOT values from the acquired digitized
data using the DRS4. However, the energy spectra between different thresholds did not match. For
an Na-22 source the peaks did not align with their correct energy values. While the analog portion
of the readout is the same between the DC threshold and DRS4 digitized data, the specific values
of TOT between the two methods did not agree. Instead of resolving the discrepancy, likely due
to not accounting for the dependence of the TOT value on the overdrive voltage applied on the
comparator, an alternative method was then developed.
Instead of using external acquisition methods to correlate pulse height and DC TOT, the three DC
TOT channels were used both to estimate the SiPM pulse height and to measure the DC TOT value
and different DC voltages. This method proceeded as follows
 1. Set DC1 to the desired DC voltage to obtain the DC TOT vs Pulse Height mapping
 2. Select a pulse height voltage to measure, 𝑉ph
 3. Set DC2 to 𝑉ph + 2.5 mV and DC3 to 𝑉ph − 2.5 mV
 4. Conduct repeated acquisition runs of fixed time where 𝑉ph is set between 𝑉DC,1 and 1.0 V
 5. In each run, filter out DC1 TOT events where DC2 was triggered and DC3 was not
 6. Create a scatter plot of the filtered DC1 TOT events vs 𝑉ph and fit a functional form to obtain
 the TOT to Pulse Height curve
Assuming the DC TOT is identical between the three channels, which they are expected to be after
the timing offset corrections, obtaining the DC TOT Pulse Height (DTPH) Curve is only required
for different voltages, not different TDC channels. The results of this method are shown in Fig. 7-1.
Each scatter plot is fit to a functional form of 𝑉ph = 𝑏 exp(𝑎𝑡 2 /(𝑡 + 𝑐)) where 𝑏 is fixed as the DC
TOT voltage.
With the DTPH curves obtained for the four considered DC voltage values, one group of (50 mV,
100 mV, 150 mV) and another of (50 mV, 100 mV, and 200 mV), the next step is to obtain a
mapping of estimated pulse height and deposited energy.

fitting the two peaks at 511 keV and 1275 keV. For each of the four DC voltages, the TOT and

As discussed in Sec. 6.2.2, the analysis was applied only to emulated TOT values derived from

that of Sec. 7.1.

Fig. 7-7 and Fig. 7-8 show the summarized results. As can be seen in this limited dataset, unlike
the two DC threshold method having one of the thresholds be a fed back RC curve yields improving
PSD capability with increasing deposited energy. The reason for this is the same as the increase in
the FOM for the two DC threshold case at low energy. When one threshold time correlates closer
to the energy (via the pulse height), and one threshold is more dependent on the pulse shape, the
capability to discriminate between neutrons and gammas increases. However, for the RC curve of
Fig. 6-7, the upper threshold tracks closer to the energy information regardless of pulse height. If a
clean bias voltage is present on the board, the RC threshold can easily be realized using only passive
components and another FPGA I/O pin. This is a worthwhile tradeoff to obtain an improved PSD
capability.

7.3. Periodic Threshold Results

Following the analysis and firmware development described in Sec. 6.2.3, the periodic threshold
method was first evaluated using the same synthesized trapezoidal signal used for the DC threshold
calibration, with sine wave thresholds at several frequencies. After the phase estimation of Eq. (6.3)
was applied independently to each of the four TDC channels, waveforms were reconstructed from
the recorded crossing times. Representative results are shown in Fig. 7-9. The trapezoid is clearly
recovered at both 60 MHz and 120 MHz, and the higher frequency yields correspondingly denser
sampling of the signal.

Two artifacts were observed. First, as shown in Fig. 7-10, a low-frequency oscillation near 400 kHz
is superimposed on the reconstructed waveform. Because the input is a generated signal, neither
the injected trapezoid nor the SiPM analog front end, which was not connected, can be responsible.
The remaining candidates are the sine wave source and the readout electronics. A harmonic or
spurious tone on the sine wave could produce such an oscillation, but inspection of the sine wave
with a spectrum analyzer showed no component at 400 kHz, leaving the peripheral board or the
FPGA itself as the likely source.
The peripheral board is a six-layer design with solid ground pours on the inner layers. Signal
integrity principles were followed in the layout, but a detailed analysis of return-current paths was
not performed, and power-supply ripple coupling into the comparator inputs cannot be excluded.
Alternatively, FPGA-implemented delay-line TDCs are known to be sensitive to fluctuations in the
FPGA core supply voltage, which modulate the carry-chain propagation delay [32, 33]. Supply
variations producing fine-time shifts of 50 ps to 100 ps are plausible and would appear in the
reconstruction as a slow modulation of the recovered voltage. Confirming this would require
probing the board and host supplies for a 400 kHz component; if the FPGA supply is the source,
the practical remedy is a custom host board with lower supply noise.
Second, although each of the two rising-edge TDCs and each of the two falling-edge TDCs
individually produces a smooth reconstruction, the rising- and falling-edge reconstructions do not
overlap when plotted together (Fig. 7-9, bottom). A constant time offset between the two groups
does not resolve the discrepancy, and its origin was not identified within the project. A rising-to-
falling propagation delay asymmetry in the comparator, or a dependence of that delay on the slope
of the sine wave at the crossing, are candidate explanations. In the present analysis it is therefore
cleaner to treat the rising-edge and falling-edge data as two independent reconstructions of the same
signal rather than to merge them.
The method was then applied to SiPM pulses from the same stilbene scintillator, 6 mm SiPM, and
analog front end used in Sec. 7.1, with a Na-22 source and a 120 MHz sine wave. A reconstructed
event is shown in Fig. 7-11. The pulse shape is recovered with the fast leading edge, peak, and
slow tail all visible, and it is consistent with the DRS4-digitized pulses of Fig. 1-1. Several features
specific to SiPM pulses are worth noting.
The sampling is inherently nonuniform. Each half-period of the sine wave yields at most one
crossing per TDC group, so the leading edge of the pulse, which rises in a few nanoseconds, is
sampled by only a small number of points, whereas the slowly decaying tail is sampled densely.
This is favorable for PSD, since the tail carries the neutron/gamma information, but it means the
peak amplitude is not directly sampled and must be inferred by interpolation or fitting.
The vertical precision of each reconstructed point depends on the slope of the sine wave at the
crossing. Points near the sine wave extrema, where the slope approaches zero, are the most sensitive
to timing error and are where the 400 kHz oscillation and TDC quantization are most visible. The
usable dynamic range is set by the sine wave amplitude and offset; pulses exceeding the sine wave
peak produce no crossings during the portion of the pulse above the peak, which appears as a gap
in the reconstruction.

on the baseline and tail, and the misalignment between rising- and falling-edge reconstructions is

8. PSD CHARACTERIZATION TECHNIQUES

Each TOT method, or combination of methods, must be evaluated for how well it separates
neutron and gamma-ray events. In conventional waveform processing, this is done with the charge-
comparison statistic of Chap. 2. For TDC-based readout, in contrast, the TOT analogs introduced
in Sec. 2.2.1 are used: an amplitude-correlated quantity derived from one threshold (the TOT of
the lower threshold, or the estimated pulse height obtained from it as in Sec. 7.1) is plotted against
a shape-sensitive ratio of TOT values, such as the lower-to-upper ratio for DC thresholds or the
slow-to-fast ratio for the linear and RC ramping thresholds.

8.1. Figure of merit

Separation is quantified with the standard figure of merit (FOM),

 FOM = (8.1)
 FWHM𝑛 + FWHM𝛾
where 𝜇𝑛 and 𝜇𝛾 are the means of the neutron and gamma-ray PSD distributions and FWHM𝑛 and
FWHM𝛾 their full widths at half maximum, evaluated as a function of energy. For simulated data,
where the particle type of each event is known, the two populations are fit separately using Locally
Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (LOWESS) to obtain a rolling mean and standard deviation of
each band. For experimental data, where labels are not available, double-Gaussian fits in energy
slices are used instead, as shown in Fig. 7-5. Both procedures yield the FOM-versus-energy curves
of Figs. 7-6 and 7-8.

8.2. Simulated comparison of TOT methods

Before the hardware of Chap. 5 was available, the three TOT methods were compared in a Python
simulation of the TDC readout. Simulated stilbene gamma-ray and neutron pulses from the model
of Sec. 3.1 were passed through simulated DC, linear-ramp, and RC-ramp thresholds. The ramping
thresholds begin as a DC level and start their ramp a fixed delay after the rising-edge crossing,
with the RC ramp following Eq. (6.1). TDC timing resolution was modeled by adding Gaussian
jitter with the measured TDC standard deviation to each crossing time, and front-end electronic
noise by adding 5 mV RMS Gaussian noise low-pass filtered at 300 MHz to each waveform before
thresholding. The resulting PSD distributions are shown in Fig. 8-1. Generally they predict better
performance than seen in PSD plots created from acquired data, which is not unexpected as they
don’t account for any timing behavior of the comparator aside from hysteresis. The propagation
delay and jitter of the comparator output is dependent on the voltage overdrive, which is not
accounted for in this simulation.

8.3. Multi-dimensional PSD with HDBSCAN

The FOM procedure above is inherently two-dimensional. The experimental results of Sec. 7.1
suggest that different threshold pairs perform best in different energy ranges, which motivates
using three or more thresholds simultaneously; the PSD observables then form a three- or higher-
dimensional space, and the bands can no longer be characterized by a single fitted curve. As
an exploratory study, unsupervised clustering was evaluated as an alternative way to separate the
populations in this case.
Several common methods are poorly matched to the problem. K-means requires the number of
clusters in advance and assumes roughly spherical clusters, whereas the neutron and gamma-
ray bands are curved and energy dependent. DBSCAN assumes a single density scale, but the
bands vary strongly in density with energy. Supervised methods such as support vector machines
require a labeled training set, which would be specific to each detector and threshold configuration.
Hierarchical DBSCAN (HDBSCAN) [34] is an unsupervised method that accommodates clusters
of varying density and shape without a specified cluster count, and was therefore selected using the
implementation in the open source hdbscan library [35].
HDBSCAN was applied to simulated data generated with three DC thresholds. Each event was
represented as a point in three dimensions: the simulated number of scintillation photons as an
energy proxy, the ratio of the middle- to lower-threshold TOT, and the ratio of the upper- to lower-
threshold TOT. The gamma-ray and neutron datasets were combined and passed to HDBSCAN with
no labels. Figure 8-2 compares the truth-labeled data with the HDBSCAN output. The algorithm
recovered two principal clusters corresponding to the neutron and gamma-ray bands, plus a small
outlier class (cluster −1), which HDBSCAN always produces, populated by events at the fringes of
both bands where they begin to merge.
This result indicates that as thresholds are added to improve low-energy PSD, clustering can provide
event labels without detector-specific training data. In an experimental workflow it could also be
used to define initial neutron and gamma-ray populations for the fits of Sec. 7.1. HDBSCAN was
not applied to the experimental TDC data within this project, and its behavior in the presence of
real noise, pileup, and low-energy band merging remains to be evaluated.

9. DISCUSSION

9.1. Implications of the results

The central question of this work was whether FPGA-centered readout can provide deposited
energy, event timing, and neutron/gamma PSD for SiPM-coupled organic scintillators without
full waveform digitization. The results of Chap. 7 answer this affirmatively for the TDC-based
approach. Two DC thresholds, two comparators, and a calibrated FPGA TDC were sufficient to
produce energy spectra with correctly aligned Na-22 features and clear neutron/gamma separation
for an AmBe source. The emulated RC threshold results indicate that replacing one DC threshold
with a passive RC ramp improves separation at higher energies at the cost of one additional FPGA
pin, and the periodic threshold method demonstrated that near-full waveform information can be
recovered with the same comparator-plus-TDC hardware when it is needed.

The DSM investigation reached a more qualified conclusion. DSM is capable in principle of
preserving PSD information for fast scintillators, but only at oversampling rates near 1 GHz, which
cannot be reached with the FPGA in the feedback loop on the 7-series devices considered. The
external-feedback designs that do reach adequate rates carry a per-channel power and component
cost that removes much of the original motivation for DSM relative to TDC methods. This result
redirected the project toward the TDC approach and is, in itself, useful design guidance.

A recurring observation across the TDC results is that once the tapped-delay-line TDC is calibrated,
the FPGA is no longer the limiting element. The effective bin width of ∼37 ps and the measured
∼14 ps standard deviation between two TDC channels (Fig. 6-4) are small compared with the
timing uncertainty introduced by comparator propagation delay, threshold noise on the shallow
falling edge of the pulse, and front-end analog noise. Future improvements in energy resolution
and PSD performance should therefore be sought in the analog chain and threshold design rather
than in the TDC.

9.2. Limitations of the present implementations

DSM. The DSM designs were evaluated only in state-space and PSpice simulation. Board
parasitics, clock jitter, comparator noise, and SiPM baseline behavior were not included, and the
reported bandwidth and ENOB figures in Table 3-1 should be regarded as upper bounds.

 73

DC threshold calibration. The TOT-to-pulse-height (DTPH) mapping had to be measured
directly with the TDC hardware because TOT values emulated from DRS4 digitized data did not
agree with measured TOT. The most likely cause is the dependence of comparator propagation delay
on overdrive voltage, which is not captured by simple threshold crossing of a digitized waveform.
This discrepancy was worked around rather than resolved, and the DTPH curves are specific to the
threshold voltage, comparator, and front-end gain used. While this is not itself a limitation, the
dependence on overdrive voltage of the timing output of the comparator should be characterized in
more detail.

Dynamic threshold. The RC threshold results are entirely emulated from 2 GS/s digitized data
using the model of Eq. (6.1). The RC footprint on the final board was not populated, so the effects
of the real Schmitt-trigger drive, the FPGA-induced holdoff, and hysteresis were not measured.

Periodic threshold. The periodic threshold method recovered injected and SiPM waveforms,
but a residual source of reconstruction error was observed and not identified within the project. We
believe this may be due to several factors. The sine wave used was created by a signal generator
using Direct Digital Synthesis. While the listed bandwidth was 1 GHz, there may be a small
harmonic that is introducing the error in the reconstruction. Additionally, TDCs are very sensitive
to supply voltage variations. If the observed oscillation is correlated to a supply voltage rail, that is
a likely explanation. Lastly, while signal integrity principles were implemented in the PCB design,
an extensive study was not performed to determine if other parts of the peripheral board including
voltage supply generation may be affecting the input signal integrity. The maximum crossing rate is
set by the delay-line reset time of each TDC, and the data volume per event is orders of magnitude
higher than for the DC threshold methods. To avoid sending excessive amounts of data to be
analyzed offline, a real-time method akin to traditional uniform sampling should be explored which
implements a trigger, timing, energy, and PSD estimation per event.

Electronics. Several iterations were required to reach a usable front end. Supply-rail transients
coupled from the host board through the SYZYGY connector corrupted the pulse baseline in the
first prototype; SMA and trace stubs acted as low-pass filters; the transimpedance configuration of
the OPA858 oscillated with the SiPM capacitance; and a schematic error required board rework.
The final board relies on a commercial OPA858 evaluation board for the analog stage rather than
an integrated on-board front end. A next iteration should attempt to improve the analog front end
with a lower bandwidth but more stable amplifier and revisit the transimpedance configuration in
addition to implementing the analog readout on the board as originally intended, instead of relying
on a commercial evaluation board.

Scope of measurements. Results were obtained with a single small stilbene crystal, a single
6 mm SiPM, and one FPGA channel. Coincidence timing resolution between two detectors, which
motivates the sub-nanosecond timing goal, was not measured. The Dynamic threshold method and
HDBSCAN analysis was applied only to simulated data. More thorough data acquisition of all the

 74

methods with comparisons to commercial digitizing solutions would be beneficial to illustrate the
benefits and drawbacks of each method.

9.3. Scalability considerations

The per-channel FPGA cost of the TDC approach is two TDC instances (rising and falling edge)
per comparator, so the three-DC-threshold configuration uses six TDCs and six LVDS input pairs,
and the periodic configuration uses four TDCs plus a trigger input. Each tapped-delay-line TDC
spans 179 taps, or roughly 45 CARRY4 blocks, plus the adder tree, FIFO, and AXI4-Stream logic.
The actual resources used within the FPGA were modest, and the Zynq FPGA can be replaced
with higher resource versions that can support hundreds to thousands of channels on one chip. The
external component count per channel is modest: an amplifier, one to three comparators, DAC
channels for thresholds, and optionally a passive RC network.
Three scaling concerns emerge from this work. First, calibration burden: each TDC requires a
code-density calibration, each pair of TDCs an offset calibration, and each threshold voltage a
DTPH curve. These procedures were performed manually here and would need to be automated
and made robust to temperature drift for a many-channel system. Second, data throughput: the DC
threshold methods produce a handful of timestamps per event and scale straightforwardly, whereas
the periodic threshold method produces thousands and would require on-FPGA reconstruction or
feature extraction before scaling beyond a few channels. Third, front-end integration: the noise
coupling and layout issues encountered on single-channel prototypes will be more severe on a dense
multi-channel board and argue for a dedicated analog board with its own clean supplies rather than
a host-powered peripheral.

9.4. Future work

Addressing the concerns listed previously is a substantial amount of follow on work that can
be explored. Additional work is needed to combine the analog and digital electronics together
as originally intended in a small footprint area that can easily scale to hundreds of channels.
Additionally, other FPGAs such as those offered by Lattice Semiconductor have substantially
lower pin-to-pin delays and FPGA package sizes that can be placed much closer to the SiPM.
This reduction of net delay could make the DSM approach more viable by allowing increased
oversampling frequency without requiring power-intensive discrete flip-flops. Lastly, while the
motivation of this work has been to find a solution that can scale to hundreds or thousands of
channels, the DC and Dynamic Threshold methods can also support PSD capable readout that
targets ultra-low power applications. Several FPGAs such as the iCE40 series by Lattice have
extremely low static power consumption. When combined with a TDC design that does not require
actively running fast clocks, the same method can be applied while allowing the FPGA to remain
in a static low power state.

10. CONCLUSION

This report investigated two FPGA-centered approaches for reading out SiPM signals from fast
organic scintillators without full waveform digitization: delta-sigma modulation and time-to-digital
converter methods based on time over threshold. The goal was to determine whether either approach
can deliver deposited energy, event timing, and neutron/gamma pulse shape discrimination with
electronics compatible with compact, high-channel-count systems.
Simulations established that one-bit DSM can preserve PSD information for stilbene pulses only
at oversampling rates near 1 GHz. Because FPGA input/output latency on the 7-series devices
considered limits FPGA-in-the-loop modulators to roughly 100 MHz to 150 MHz, the original
concept of minimal external circuitry is not viable for fast scintillators. External-feedback designs
recover the required rate but at a power and component cost that reduces their advantage over TDC
methods, and the DSM approach was not carried through to a hardware implementation.
The TDC approach was carried through firmware, electronics, and experiment. A calibrated tapped-
delay-line TDC achieved an effective bin width of ∼37 ps, shifting the limit on timing precision
from the FPGA to the analog front end. A common SYZYGY front-end board supporting DC, RC,
and periodic thresholds was developed, and calibration procedures for threshold voltage, inter-TDC
offsets, and TOT-to-pulse-height mapping were established. With a stilbene scintillator and a 6 mm
SiPM, two DC thresholds produced calibrated energy spectra and clear neutron/gamma separation
for an AmBe source. Emulated RC thresholds showed improved separation at higher energies for
the cost of one additional FPGA pin, and the periodic threshold method demonstrated waveform
reconstruction from threshold crossings using the same hardware.
These results show that comparator-plus-TDC readout on commodity FPGAs is a practical path to
energy, timing, and PSD for SiPM-coupled organic scintillators. The remaining work to reach a
deployable multi-channel system lies primarily in front-end integration, automated calibration,
on-FPGA data reduction, and experimental validation of the dynamic and periodic threshold
methods.
