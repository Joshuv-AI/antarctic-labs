 ABSTRACT

The Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) has initiated the Advanced Reactor Cladding (ARC) irradiation
campaign to generate irradiation performance data for candidate fuel cladding concepts. The campaign
includes a diverse set of ferritic/martensitic steels, oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) alloys, FeCrAl-
based alloys, coated materials, and welded cladding specimens produced through multiple US Department
of Energy (DOE) programs and international collaborations. Three complementary experimental thrusts
comprise the campaign: tensile testing (ARC Tensile) to rapidly screen candidate alloys, fracture toughness
testing (ARC Fracture) to evaluate irradiation effects on crack resistance, and tubular weld testing (ARC
Weld) to quantify irradiation-induced changes in the mechanical performance of end cap welds.

This report documents the irradiation campaign design, including the selected materials, specimen types,
irradiation matrix, and capsule designs for irradiation within the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). A total
of 14 irradiation capsules were developed to achieve target irradiation temperatures between 300°C and
600°C and doses up to 30 dpa. Thermal analyses were performed using finite element methods to establish
capsule geometries capable of achieving the desired specimen temperatures while accommodating
differences in specimen geometry and material properties. The resulting capsule designs provide the basis
for irradiation of the AFC-ARC experimental matrix and subsequent post-irradiation examination to assess
the effects of neutron irradiation on advanced cladding materials.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) has initiated a comprehensive irradiation campaign to help address
many of the top priorities identified by the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE),
which include (1) keeping existing plants open, (2) building advanced reactors, (3) fostering international
nuclear cooperation, and (4) securing and sustaining the nuclear fuel cycle. For current reactors, the lack of
materials that can be resilient to the time-at-temperature phenomenon or that can enable operation to higher
fuel burnups may limit the lifetime of the current fleet. For the deployment of advanced reactors, key
degradation phenomena still remain unresolved for ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steels. To achieve American
energy dominance, AFC is collaborating with its international partners to compare US Department of
Energy (DOE)-produced materials with those of its collaborators. Finally, to sustain the nuclear fuel cycle,
advanced fuel cladding and coating candidates that can enable the realization of higher operating
temperatures and new fuel systems are necessary.

A range of Fe-based alloys using various compositions and processing techniques are currently being
considered for deployment in advanced light-water reactor (LWR) concepts and for advanced reactor
applications. Both applications require materials that can perform under extreme environments, including
elevated temperatures, high displacement damage (> 10 dpa), and corrosive conditions. Unfortunately,
achieving the displacement damage necessary to screen advanced cladding concepts is a challenging
endeavor, as the research reactors within the US are limited in their capacity and achievable fluxes.
Fortunately, the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) provides the highest steady-state neutron flux available
in the nation and has been previously used to achieve doses as high as 50 dpa within candidate cladding
and structural materials [1].

This irradiation campaign, titled the Advanced Reactor Cladding (ARC) campaign, comprises three thrusts:
the ARC Weld (ARC-W) campaign to provide post-irradiation strength and ductility data on HT9 end cap
welds produced by commercial partners, the ARC Tensile (ARC-T) campaign to rapidly screen potential
new alloys for inclusion in future AFC-funded research initiatives, and the ARC Fracture (ARC-F)
campaign to evaluate the effect of processing and/or composition on the crack resistance of advanced
cladding candidates.

This report describes the materials that have been selected for inclusion within the AFC-ARC irradiation
campaign, provides design details pertaining to specimen types and capsule configurations, and summarizes
the irradiation matrix and timeline.

 2. MATERIALS OF INTEREST

This irradiation campaign consists of a total of 24 unique materials that were produced for fuel cladding
applications, as listed below in Table 1. Each material is enumerated and identified using the generic
alloy ID, the heat designation, and with any noted composition or processing information provided by the
supplying organization. In short, these materials can be discretized into three categories: (1) cladding
materials produced by the AFC program for near- and longer-term fast reactor cladding applications,
(2) forward-looking material combinations investigated by the Innovative Nuclear Materials (INM)
program, and (3) materials supplied in collaboration with Nippon Fuel Development (NFD) for comparison
with AFC-produced alloys.

The highest-priority sample in this matrix, and the predicate for this irradiation campaign, is HT9. The AFC
program aims to provide data to close current knowledge gaps for the UZr/HT9 fuel/cladding system, and
a key aspect of this initiative is the understanding of heat-to-heat variation in material properties. Material
1 is a heat of HT9 previously demonstrated to exhibit low irradiation hardening following low-temperature
irradiation in the Advanced Test Reactor [2]. This heat of material has also recently been selected for
inclusion in an upcoming collaborative JOYO irradiation as part of the Civil Nuclear Working Group
(CNWG). This HT9 plate was produced as part of a 300 lb heat produced by Metalwerks following NQA-
1 quality control, and it is colloquially identified as the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) heat of HT9 to be
consistent with prior presentations on the material. The other key HT9 materials in this irradiation campaign
include Materials 19 and 20. Material 19 is a heat of HT9 thin-walled tubing produced as a 200 lb heat of
material and then drawn by Veridiam down to final dimensions of 6.86 mm outer diameter (OD) × 0.56
mm thickness. This year, Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) collaborated with EWI and Oklo to
perform end cap welds, designated as Material 20.

In addition to these HT9 reference materials, next-generation cladding materials, including higher-
vanadium Grade 92 (Gr92V, Material 2) and low-impurity oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) alloy
14YWT (Material 3), were also included, as these materials are also to be irradiated in the collaborative
JOYO irradiation tentatively scheduled for calendar year 2028. There are two other variants of 14YWT that
were produced via hot rolling to achieve different grain and dislocation structures.

In addition to new F/M and ODS FeCr alloys, recent work has suggested that FeCrAl-ODS materials may
perform better in impure sodium than FeCr alloys [3]. In addition, advanced small modular reactors (SMRs)
and boiling water reactors (BWRs) need oxidation-resistant and high-strength cladding that is impervious
to irradiation-induced hardening, properties have been recently demonstrated for some FeCrAl-ODS
variants [4]. As such, this irradiation campaign includes select FeCrAl-ODS alloys produced for the AFC
program (Material 6), the INM program (Materials 7 and 8), and from NFD (Materials 15–18, 22). These
alloys all have different compositions, nanoprecipitates, and processing steps. Reference FeCrAl alloy
C26M is also included as a baseline for FeCrAl irradiation performance.

Other alloys that may show promise in advanced fast reactor conditions have also been provided by INM.
These alloys include intermetallic dispersion strengthened (IDS) steels developed by Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) (Materials 9–11), nanolayered coatings on HT9 and Zircaloy-4 developed by Argonne
National Laboratory (ANL, Materials 12 and 13), and friction-extruded and pilgered tubing from Pacific
Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL, Material 21). This latter tubing will be compared with stress-
relieved and recrystallized variants of high-pedigree 12Cr-ODS alloy OFRAC previously developed by
ORNL as part of the AFC program [5].

 3. IRRADIATION TEST MATRIX

The irradiation test matrix is schematically shown in Figure 1. It consists of a total of 14 irradiation capsules
(also known as HFIR rabbits). The schematic shows an icon delineating fracture toughness (ARC-F)
rabbits, tensile (ARC-T) rabbits, and tubular cross weld (ARC-W) rabbits. Each icon delineates one HFIR
rabbit, the x-axis delineates the target irradiation temperature, and the y-axis provides the target irradiation
dose. In short, there are a total of 4 ARC-W rabbits, 4 ARC-F rabbits, and 6 ARC-T rabbits. The majority
of rabbits, due to the focus of this campaign being primarily on fast reactor operating temperatures, will be
irradiated between 350°C and 600°C. Tubular specimens of HT9 cross welds will target 350°C and 550°C
to provide data on end cap weld microstructural evolution. Conversely, tensile specimens will aim for
slightly higher irradiation temperatures of 400°C and 600°C to be more consistent with target irradiation
temperatures within the JOYO irradiation matrix. Select 300°C capsules are included to compare the
evolution of various FeCrAl-ODS rabbits at advanced SMR/BWR operating temperatures.

The four specimen types to be machined and tested as part of this campaign include the SSJ2 (0.5 mm thick)
subsized tensile specimen [6], the MPC2 coupon specimen (rectangular specimen of dimensions 16 mm ×
4 mm × 0.5 mm) used for coating work, the MBS-1 fracture toughness specimen [7], and the Tubular
Tensile Specimen (TTS), which comprises 4 parallel curved SSJ2 specimens harvested from the monolithic
tube and/or end cap weld. The number and type of each material are provided for reference in Table 2.

 4. CAPSULE DESIGN AND THERMAL ANALYSIS

4.1 GENERAL THERMAL ANALYSIS DETAILS

All capsules described in this report are intended for irradiation within the HFIR flux trap region.
Experimental capsules are inserted into target rod holders, which are positioned within the flux trap. Each
target contains seven discrete axial irradiation positions. Position 4 is located at the core midplane and
experiences the most symmetric neutron flux and gamma heating profile, while positions above and below
the midplane experience progressively less heating and more asymmetric irradiation conditions.

The irradiation position is an important input parameter for thermal analysis because local neutron and
gamma heating directly influence the volumetric heat generation rates within the capsule components and
specimens. Consequently, the irradiation position must be considered when dimensioning the capsule
internal, gas gap, and other thermal design parameters.

All thermal analyses were performed as steady-state finite element simulations using ANSYS Mechanical.
Internal heat generation rates were assigned to each capsule component based on neutronics calculations
for the corresponding material and irradiation position within the HFIR flux trap. As a result, heat
generation varied among capsule components according to both material composition and local irradiation
conditions.

The thermal boundary conditions were defined using established HFIR flux trap coolant conditions. Heat
removal from the capsule was modeled through convection at the outer capsule housing surface,
representing the coolant flow within the surrounding flux trap cooling channels. Consequently, specimen
temperatures were governed by the balance between internal heat generation, thermal conduction through
the capsule components and gas gaps, and convective heat removal to the reactor coolant.

4.2 ADVANCED REACTOR CLADDING TENSILE (ARC-T) DESIGN

4.2.1 ARC-T Capsule Design

The GENTEN capsule design selected for this experimental matrix is a standard configuration that has been
used extensively in previous irradiation campaigns for tensile specimens. The capsule consists of an outer
housing separated from three internal sample holders by thimble tabs. Each holder contains 12 specimens
and 4 SiC thermometry specimens, which are held in place by friction using a central retaining pin.
Illustrations of the capsule internals are provided in Figure 2 [8]. Specimens may consist of SSJ2 “dogbone”
tensile specimens, which are paired with chevron pieces that fill the gaps between the specimens and the
inner holder walls, or rectangular MPC2 “blank” specimens, which do not require chevron pieces..

The temperature of the specimens is primarily controlled by the gap between the outer housing and the
sample holder, in combination with heat generated within the holder and specimens during irradiation. This
thermal resistance establishes the operating temperature of the specimens contained within each holder. For
300 series steels, a response surface was previously developed that maps average specimen gauge
temperature as a function of holder diameter for various holder materials and gas gap compositions, as
shown in Figure 3 [8].

4.2.2 ARC-T Thermal Analysis

Three target operating temperatures were selected for the tensile specimen capsules in this irradiation
campaign: 300°C, 400°C, and 600°C. For each target temperature, a thermal analysis was performed using
the ANSYS Mechanical finite element software package. The previously developed response surface was
used to select the holder diameter, gas composition, and filler material required to achieve the desired
specimen temperature. These design parameters were subsequently verified using detailed ANSYS thermal
models.

The specimens included in this irradiation campaign span a range of compositions and crystal structures,
from FeCrAl-based alloys to 300 series stainless steels. As a result, slight variations exist in both thermal
conductivity and heat generation rates among the specimens. However, these differences were determined
to be sufficiently small that all specimens could reasonably be represented using the thermal properties of
304 stainless steel. Because 304 stainless steel was used in the development of the response surface and has
served as the basis for numerous previous capsule thermal analyses, this assumption is considered justified.

The thermal analyses showed that specimens located within the same holder experienced nearly identical
temperatures regardless of their orientation relative to the central pin. Therefore, the average gauge

4.3 ADVANCED REACTOR CLADDING FRACTURE (ARC-F) DESIGN

4.3.1 ARC-F Capsule Design

The MINBEN capsule design was selected for the fracture specimens in this irradiation campaign. Similar
to the tensile capsule design, the MINBEN configuration has been used extensively in previous irradiation
experiments, making it a proven and robust choice. The capsule utilizes a gas gap between the outer housing
and the internal holder to establish the operating temperature of the specimens.

Unlike the tensile capsule design, which contains three specimen holders, the MINBEN capsule employs a
single holder. This holder contains six fracture specimens and four thermometry specimens, as illustrated
in Figure 7 [7]. To ensure effective thermal coupling, spring specimens and shims are incorporated to
maintain good contact between the thermometry specimens and fracture specimens as well as between the
fracture specimens and the holder walls. As with the tensile capsule design, thimble tabs are positioned on
either side of the holder to maintain the desired gas gap between the holder and the capsule housing.

4.3.2 ARC-F Thermal Analysis

As with the tensile specimen irradiation matrix, three target operating temperatures were selected for the
fracture specimen capsules: 300°C, 400°C, and 600°C. However, the fracture specimens more closely
resemble FeCrAl alloys than 300 series stainless steels in terms of their thermal properties. As a result, the
previously developed response surface for this design for 300 series steels was not directly applicable for
determining the final capsule geometry.

The primary objective of the thermal design process was to achieve the desired temperature at the specimen
fracture plane, which represents the region of interest for subsequent mechanical testing. The location of
the fracture plane within the capsule geometry is illustrated in Figure 8.

To establish the desired specimen temperatures, three optimization studies were performed using ANSYS
Mechanical. The capsule housing inner diameter was held constant at 9.519 mm, and a 100% helium gas
environment was assumed for all capsules. FeCrAl material properties were assigned to the fracture
specimens, while the shims were modeled using 304 stainless steel properties. Holder materials were
constrained to Mo or Al.

Although the response surface developed for 300 series steels was not used directly for the final design, it
provided a useful initial estimate for the thermal configuration. Parameters such as gas composition,
irradiation location, and holder material were initially selected based on the response surface results and
previous capsule designs. With these parameters fixed, the holder outer diameter was varied in the ANSYS
optimization studies until the desired fracture plane temperature was achieved for each target operating
condition.

4.4 ADVANCED REACTOR CLADDING WELD (ARC-W) DESIGN

4.4.1 ARC-W Capsule Design

The ARC-W capsules employ a variation of a previously utilized specimen geometry known as TTS. In
this design, four tensile specimens are machined directly out of the wall of a cylindrical cladding-like
geometry. The specimen profile is designed such that the projected silhouette of each curved specimen
closely matches that of a conventional tensile specimen (SSJ2), similar to those used in the standard tensile
capsules.

Additionally, the geometry is configured so that the gauge section of each tensile specimen remains flat
despite being machined from a tubular specimen. The tubular specimens were designed with an outer

 15

diameter of 6.66 mm and an inner diameter of 5.66 mm, resulting in a wall thickness of 0.5 mm. This
thickness was selected to match that of conventional SSJ2 tensile specimens, thereby maintaining
consistency with established specimen geometries and testing methodologies. This feature preserves a well-
defined testing region while enabling mechanical characterization of material in a geometry representative
of cladding applications. Although TTSs have been used in previous irradiation campaigns, the tubular
specimens employed in the present campaign have a smaller diameter than those used previously.
Consequently, custom capsule housings with increased wall thickness were required to accommodate the
reduced specimen diameter while maintaining the desired capsule outer dimensions and specimen-to-
housing gas gap.

Each ARC-W capsule contains three TTSs, with each tubular specimen incorporating four curved SSJ2
tensile specimens. The center TTS contains welded material and is positioned between two standard,
unwelded TTSs to provide a direct comparison of weld and base material performance under nearly
identical irradiation conditions. This configuration was selected to facilitate direct comparison of irradiated
weld and base material performance during post-irradiation examination (PIE).

As with the other capsule designs, specimen temperature is controlled through a gas gap; however, in the
ARC-W configuration, the gap is located between the outer surface of the specimen and the inner wall of
the capsule housing. Heat is generated within both the specimen and an internal filler component located
inside the tubular specimen. SiC thermometry specimens are positioned within notches machined into the
filler to provide post-irradiation temperature measurements.

Thimble tab components are located at both ends of each tubular specimen to maintain the desired gas gap
between the specimen and capsule housing. The internal configuration of the capsule is illustrated in Figure

 5. CONCLUSION

The AFC-ARC irradiation campaign was developed to generate irradiation performance data for advanced
fuel cladding materials through a coordinated series of tensile, fracture toughness, and tubular weld
irradiation experiments in HFIR. The experimental matrix encompasses 24 candidate materials representing
AFC-developed alloys, INM concepts, and international collaboration materials, with irradiation conditions
selected to address both fast reactor and advanced LWR operating environments. This irradiation campaign,
when successful, will provide essential performance data necessary to downselect future cladding
candidates for research within the AFC program in the coming years.

This report summarizes the materials selected for irradiation, the experimental matrix, and the capsule
designs developed for each specimen geometry. Finite element thermal analyses were performed for each
capsule configuration to verify that the desired specimen operating temperatures could be achieved through
appropriate selection of holder geometry, gas gap dimensions, and irradiation position.

The capsule designs and irradiation matrix described herein establish the foundation for execution of the
AFC-ARC irradiation campaign. Following irradiation, PIE will provide mechanical property data needed
to evaluate candidate cladding materials, quantify the influence of processing and welding on irradiation
performance, and support future material downselection and qualification efforts for advanced nuclear
energy systems. All materials have been received and machined, and capsule parts have been procured so
that a January 2027 target insertion date in HFIR is feasible.
