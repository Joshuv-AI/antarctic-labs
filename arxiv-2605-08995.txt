Semiparametric Elliptical Mixture Clustering for High-Dimensional Data
Long Feng 1 and Dan Zhuang 2 1 Nankai University, 2 Fujian Normal University

Abstract
. Clustering high-dimensional data is especially challenging when cluster distributions are heavy tailed and only approximately elliptical. Existing high-dimensional methods are largely built for Gaussian or other light-tailed models, whereas classical robust elliptical procedures are mostly low dimensional or rely on fully parametric radial families. We propose a semiparametric elliptical mixture clustering framework with cluster-specific centers, an unknown common radial generator, and a common sparse precision-shape matrix, together with a data-driven rule for selecting the number of clusters. A generalized expectation–maximization (GEM) algorithm is developed by combining transformed-radius estimation of the radial generator, radial-score center updates, and a Tyler–POET–GLASSO update for the common precision-shape matrix. The method avoids specifying a parametric radial family and remains computationally feasible in high dimensions. We establish high-dimensional consistency for the estimated model components and the excess misclustering error. Simulation studies and a handwritten-digit application demonstrate the competitive performance and robustness of the proposed method, particularly in heavy-tailed elliptical settings.

Clustering is a central tool for uncovering latent heterogeneity in modern scientific data, but it becomes particularly difficult when the ambient dimension is comparable to, or much larger than, the sample size. Gene-expression

In this regime, irrelevant coordinates may dominate distance-based criteria, covariance or shape estimation is often unstable, and high-dimensional noise can obscure weak cluster separation. These challenges are further amplified by heavy tails, non-Gaussian radial behavior, and nontrivial feature dependence, motivating clustering methods that can accommodate these features simultaneously.

This paper studies this problem through a semiparametric elliptical mixture model. Conditional on the latent class
Z=k
, an observation
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has density

\displaystyle f_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})=|\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}|^{1/2}g^{*}\!\left\{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*})\right\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.

The centers
\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}
are cluster specific, while the radial generator
g^{*}
and the precision-shape matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}
are common across clusters; the matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}
is assumed to have a sparse precision structure after an appropriate shape normalization.
This specification retains the interpretable location-shape-radial structure
of elliptical models while avoiding the need to pre-specify a parametric radial
or tail form. The model is therefore parametric in the cluster locations and sparse precision-shape structure, but nonparametric in the common radial behavior.

If the population parameters were known, classification would be straightforward. Since the determinant term is common across components, the Bayes rule assigns
\bm{x}
to

\displaystyle G^{*}(\bm{x})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\left[\log\pi_{k}^{*}+\log g^{*}\!\left\{(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*})\right\}\right].

In practice, however, the class labels are unobserved, the radial generator
g^{*}
is unknown, and the common precision-shape matrix must be estimated in high dimensions. This naturally suggests an expectation–maximization-type strategy, in which posterior responsibilities serve as soft labels. However, the usual Gaussian-mixture Expectation–Maximization (EM) updates are not directly applicable, because the radial generator is unspecified and conventional moment-based updates for centers and shape can be unstable under heavy-tailed radial variation. These considerations motivate a generalized Expectation–Maximization (GEM) framework tailored to the semiparametric elliptical mixture model.

To close this gap, the proposed method builds on robust high-dimensional tools that target different parts of the problem. Tyler’s
M

estimation, high-dimensional dependence regularization, and sparse precision-shape estimation.

We integrate these ingredients through a semiparametric generalized EM algorithm. The E-step computes posterior responsibilities under the current elliptical-mixture fit. The transformed-radius block estimates the unknown common generator
g
by reducing the nonparametric component of the problem to one-dimensional smoothing of weighted transformed Mahalanobis radii. The resulting radial score is then used to update the component centers robustly, so that center estimation is driven by the fitted elliptical geometry rather than by raw Euclidean averages. Finally, a Tyler–POET–GLASSO block updates the common sparse precision-shape matrix: Tyler-type reweighting controls heavy-tailed radial effects, POET stabilizes high-dimensional dependence estimation, and graphical lasso enforces sparse inverse-shape structure. The procedure is a GEM algorithm because these blocks preserve the posterior-weight logic of EM while replacing unavailable exact maximizers with stable semiparametric and penalized updates.

The main contributions are threefold. First, we propose a high-dimensional semiparametric elliptical mixture clustering framework with cluster-specific centers, an unknown common radial generator, and a common sparse precision-shape matrix. Second, we develop an implementable GEM algorithm that combines transformed-radius estimation, radial-score center updates, and a Tyler–POET–GLASSO common-shape update within a single posterior-responsibility iteration. Third, we establish high-dimensional theory for the resulting estimator. The analysis controls the mixing proportions, centers, precision-shape matrix, radial generator, and excess misclustering error, thereby linking componentwise estimation consistency to clustering performance under heavy-tailed elliptical sampling.

2.
Model and Method

2.1.
Semiparametric elliptical mixture with fixed
K

Let
Z\in\{1,\dots,K\}
be a latent class label with

\mathbb{P}(Z=k)=\pi_{k}^{*},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{*}=1,\qquad\pi_{k}^{*}>0.

Conditional on
Z=k
, the observation
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has density

f_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})=|\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}|^{1/2}g^{*}\!\left(\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right),\qquad\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})=(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}),

where
\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}\succ 0
is a common precision-shape matrix, and
g^{*}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an unknown common radial generator, and satisfying

\frac{\pi^{p/2}}{\Gamma(p/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{p/2-1}g^{*}(u)\,du=1
.
The observed marginal density is therefore

f^{*}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{*}|\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}|^{1/2}g^{*}\!\left(\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right).

The parameter is

z^{*}=(\bm{\pi}^{*},\bm{\mu}_{1}^{*},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{K}^{*},\mathbf{\Omega}^{*},g^{*}).

Because the generator is unknown,
(\mathbf{\Omega}^{*},g^{*})
is identifiable only up to scale. Throughout the paper we impose the trace normalization

(2.1)

\mathrm{tr}\{(\mathbf{\Omega}^{*})^{-1}\}=p.

Write

\ell^{*}(u)=\log g^{*}(u),\qquad\omega^{*}(u)=-\frac{d}{du}\ell^{*}(u).

Since the determinant term is common to every component, the oracle Bayes rule is

G^{*}(\bm{x})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\Bigl\{\log\pi_{k}^{*}+\ell^{*}\!\left(\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)\Bigr\}.

Under the
0
–
1
loss, the corresponding Bayes risk is denoted by
R(G^{*})=\mathbb{P}\{G^{*}(\bm{X})\neq Z\}
.

2.2.
Oracle EM map

The class labels are latent, so an EM-type construction is the natural starting point for model-based clustering under the mixture model. For a given candidate parameter, the E-step replaces the unobserved labels by posterior responsibilities, and the M-step updates the model blocks conditionally on those responsibilities. In the present semiparametric elliptical setting, however, the generator
g
is unknown and the common precision matrix must be regularized when
p
is large, so a literal closed-form EM maximization is neither available nor numerically stable. We therefore define an oracle update map that preserves the exact posterior-weight structure of EM and couples it with a robust high-dimensional common-shape routine. The oracle map below follows the same block order as the practical algorithm.
For a generic candidate parameter

z=(\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{K},\mathbf{\Omega},g),

let

\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)=(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}),\qquad a_{k}(\bm{x};z)=\log\pi_{k}+\log g\!\left(\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)\right).

The oracle E-step computes posterior responsibilities

r_{k}(\bm{x};z)=\frac{\pi_{k}g\!\left(\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)\right)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}g\!\left(\delta_{\ell}(\bm{x};z)\right)}=\frac{\exp\{a_{k}(\bm{x};z)\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp\{a_{\ell}(\bm{x};z)\}}.

The oracle transformed radius is

q(u)=\log(1+u),\qquad Y_{k}(\bm{x};z)=q\!\left(\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)\right).

Let
f_{Y,z}
be defined by

\int h(y)f_{Y,z}(y)\,dy=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)h\{q(\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z))\}\right]

for all bounded measurable functions
h
.
The inverse change of variable from the transformed scale back to the radial scale is

\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f)(u)=c_{p}\,u^{1-p/2}\frac{f\{q(u)\}}{1+u},\qquad u>0,

where
(1+u)^{-1}
is the Jacobian factor associated with
q(u)=\log(1+u)
,
u^{1-p/2}
converts the one-dimensional radius density back to the elliptical generator scale under the
p
-dimensional radial measure, and
c_{p}
is the corresponding normalizing constant.
The oracle
generator update is obtained by composing this inverse transform with the shape-normalization
projection,

G(z)=\mathcal{P}\bigl[\mathcal{A}^{-1}\{f_{Y,z}\}\bigr],\qquad w_{z}(u)=-\frac{d}{du}\log G(z)(u).

The oracle mixing update is

\Pi_{k}(z)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)\bigr].

The center proposal is

\widetilde{U}_{k}(z)=\frac{N_{k}(z)}{D_{k}(z)},\qquad N_{k}(z)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)w_{z}\!\left(\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z)\right)\bm{X}\Bigr],\qquad D_{k}(z)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)w_{z}\!\left(\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z)\right)\Bigr],

and the oracle damped center update is

(2.2)

U_{k}(z)=(1-\eta_{\mu})\bm{\mu}_{k}+\eta_{\mu}\widetilde{U}_{k}(z),\qquad 0<\eta_{\mu}\leq 1.

The oracle residual after the center update is

\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)=\bm{X}-U_{k}(z).

The population analogue of the common-shape block follows the same sequence of robust factor adjustment, Tyler reweighting, and sparse precision regularization as the empirical algorithm. We do not maximize the raw complete-data scatter directly, because that route is unstable when
p
is large and the tails are heavy. Instead, we begin with a weighted bounded-radial pilot, which is the population analogue of a spatial-sign scatter matrix in the sense of
42
, use POET to separate a leading factor part from an idiosyncratic remainder, run a Tyler fixed-point update, and then apply POET once more before the sparse inverse step. The weighted spatial-sign pilot is

\mathbf{H}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)\frac{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)^{\top}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\vee\varepsilon_{r}}\right].

Let
m(z)
be the factor count selected by the eigenvalue-ratio rule used by the algorithm, and let
\lambda_{u}(z)
be the corresponding POET threshold. The first POET pilot is

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pss}}(z)=\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u}(z),m(z)}\{\mathbf{H}(z)\}.

Define the weighted Tyler map

\mathcal{T}_{z}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\Biggl[\frac{p}{\mathrm{tr}\!\Bigl\{(1-\rho_{T})\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{z}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+\rho_{T}\mathbf{I}\Bigr\}}\Bigl\{(1-\rho_{T})\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{z}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+\rho_{T}\mathbf{I}\Bigr\}\Biggr],

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{z}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=p\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{k}(\bm{X};z)\frac{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)^{\top}}{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\vee\varepsilon_{r}}\right],

\rho_{T}\in(0,1)
is the Tyler ridge parameter, and
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}
denotes projection onto the positive-definite cone. The oracle Tyler scatter
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)
is the fixed point of
\mathcal{T}_{z}
. The second POET step is

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pt}}(z)=\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u}(z),m(z)}\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)\}.

The sparse precision proposal is

\widetilde{T}(z)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\mathbf{\Omega}\succ\bm{0}}\Bigl\{\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pt}}(z)\bigr)-\log\det(\mathbf{\Omega})+\lambda_{\Omega}(z)\left\lVert\mathrm{off}(\mathbf{\Omega})\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr\}.

The final damped and renormalized oracle precision update is

(2.3)

T(z)=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\Bigl(\mathcal{N}\bigl[\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\{(1-\eta_{\Omega})\mathbf{\Omega}+\eta_{\Omega}\widetilde{T}(z)\}^{-1}\bigr]^{-1}\Bigr),\qquad 0<\eta_{\Omega}\leq 1,

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=p\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
is the trace-normalization operator.
The oracle EM map is therefore

M(z)=\bigl(\Pi_{1}(z),\dots,\Pi_{K}(z),U_{1}(z),\dots,U_{K}(z),T(z),G(z)\bigr).

By construction
M(z^{*})=z^{*}
.

2.3.
The empirical generalized EM algorithm

We now describe the empirical counterpart of the oracle map.
The procedure is a generalized EM algorithm because the latent-label block is updated through posterior responsibilities, while the generator and common precision blocks are updated through nonparametric and penalized surrogate maps rather than by closed-form likelihood maximization. Each outer iteration has four statistically distinct parts: an E-step for posterior weights, a one-dimensional transformed-radius update for the common generator, a radial-score update for the centers, and a robust high-dimensional common-precision update based on Tyler’s
M
-equation, POET regularization, and graphical lasso. We write every block explicitly, because the theoretical analysis in Section
3
and Appendix
A
tracks the same sequence of operators.

The current iterate is written as

z^{(t)}=\bigl(\bm{\pi}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{K}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t)},\hat{g}^{(t)}\bigr),\qquad\hat{\ell}^{(t)}=\log\hat{g}^{(t)}.

The algorithm uses a robust sparse
K
-median initialization, then repeats an E-step, a transformed-radius generator update, a radial-score center update, and a TME–POET–GLASSO common-precision update. For definiteness, the numerical stability constants are denoted by

\varepsilon_{r}>0,\qquad\rho_{T}\in(0,1),\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pd}}>0,

where
\varepsilon_{r}
prevents division by zero in radial normalization,
\rho_{T}
regularizes the Tyler iteration, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pd}}
is used in positive-definite projection. The generator update uses a lower radius cut-off
\varepsilon_{u}>0
and clips the empirical radial score to an interval
[\omega_{\min},\omega_{\max}]
. These constants are fixed and do not affect the asymptotic rates as long as they are kept within compact admissible ranges.

Auxiliary operators and regularization maps

Several symbols used in the algorithm denote deterministic numerical operators rather than standard probability or optimization notation. We define them explicitly here so that every later update is mathematically unambiguous.

For any interval
[a,b]
with
a\leq b
, the clipping operator is

\operatorname{clip}_{[a,b]}(x)=\min\{\max(x,a),b\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{M}
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{M}
be real numbers. The linear-interpolation operator
\operatorname{LinInterp}(\{(u_{m},v_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M};u)
is defined by

\operatorname{LinInterp}(\{(u_{m},v_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M};u)=\begin{cases}v_{1},&u\leq u_{1},\\[4.0pt]
v_{m}+\dfrac{u-u_{m}}{u_{m+1}-u_{m}}(v_{m+1}-v_{m}),&u_{m}<u\leq u_{m+1},\ 1\leq m\leq M-1,\\[8.0pt]
v_{M},&u\geq u_{M}.\end{cases}

Thus the grid values are extended piecewise linearly on the interior intervals and constantly outside the grid range.

For a real symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
, write the eigendecomposition of its symmetrized version as

\frac{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{\top}}{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})^{\top},\qquad\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{A}).

The positive-definite projection used throughout the algorithm is

\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}(\mathbf{A})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\{\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A})\vee\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pd}}\}\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}.

This is exactly the eigenvalue-thresholding rule implemented in the computation: the matrix is first symmetrized, every eigenvalue below
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pd}}
is replaced by
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pd}}
, and the matrix is reconstructed from the modified spectrum.

For a matrix
\mathbf{A}=(A_{jj^{\prime}})
, define the off-diagonal soft-thresholding operator by

[\mathcal{T}_{\lambda_{u}}(\mathbf{A})]_{jj^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}A_{jj},&j=j^{\prime},\\[4.0pt]
\operatorname{sgn}(A_{jj^{\prime}})(\left|A_{jj^{\prime}}\right|-\lambda_{u})_{+},&j\neq j^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Following the POET construction of
17
, the regularization map used in this paper is

\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u},m}(\mathbf{A})=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}+\mathcal{T}_{\lambda_{u}}\!\left[\mathbf{A}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})\bm{v}_{j}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\right]\right),

with the convention that the leading-eigenvalue sum is zero when
m=0
. The first term is the retained low-rank factor part, the second term is the thresholded principal orthogonal complement, and the final projection restores positive definiteness.

6:

Return
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\bigl(\mathbf{L}_{m}(\mathbf{A})+\mathcal{T}_{\lambda_{u}}\{\mathbf{R}_{m}(\mathbf{A})\}\bigr)
.

Robust sparse
K
-median initialization

The empirical GEM iteration is initialized by a robust sparse
K
-median criterion. This initialization is used only to place the algorithm in a stable basin; it is not the final clustering model. Its design is motivated by the high-dimensional median literature: componentwise medians are robust to heavy tails in the sense emphasized by
23
, feature selection in
K
-median clustering was introduced by
44
, and related nonparametric feature-screening ideas for very high-dimensional clustering were developed by
12
. The hard-thresholding form below is also consistent with the recent sparse prototype-clustering literature, where exact variable exclusion is often preferred to continuous shrinkage for interpretability
(
35
)
.

For a threshold value
\tau>0
, let
\widetilde{c}_{kj}
be the current coordinate-wise median of cluster
k
in coordinate
j
, and define

\bar{c}_{j}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{c}_{kj},\qquad D_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\widetilde{c}_{kj}-\bar{c}_{j}\right|,\qquad S_{\tau}=\{j:D_{j}\geq\tau\}.

D_{j}
measures the between-cluster separation of coordinate
j
on the median scale. If
S_{\tau}
is empty, all coordinates are retained. Given the current medians and selected coordinate set, observations are assigned by the
L_{1}
rule

c_{i}(\tau)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\sum_{j\in S_{\tau}}\left|X_{ij}-\widetilde{c}_{kj}\right|.

The coordinate-wise cluster medians are then recomputed, and the assignment and median updates are alternated until convergence. The corresponding objective is

Q_{\tau}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leq k\leq K}\sum_{j\in S_{\tau}}\left|X_{ij}-\widetilde{c}_{kj}\right|.

The threshold
\tau
is chosen from a data-dependent finite grid. Let
\widetilde{x}_{j}
be the overall coordinate-wise median and let
\widetilde{x}_{kj}
be the fitted cluster median. The between-cluster
L_{1}
dispersion is

B_{\tau}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}\sum_{j\in S_{\tau}}\left|\widetilde{x}_{kj}-\widetilde{x}_{j}\right|.

For each candidate
\tau
, a reference statistic is obtained by independently permuting each column of the data matrix and refitting the same sparse
K
-median criterion. If
B_{\tau,b}^{\mathrm{perm}}
denotes the statistic from the
b
th permuted data set, the selected threshold is

\hat{\tau}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\left\{\log B_{\tau}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log B_{\tau,b}^{\mathrm{perm}}\right\}.

The resulting fitted medians provide the initial centers
\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(0)}
, and the initial hard memberships define an
n\times K
responsibility matrix
\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(0)}\in\{0,1\}
.

Posterior responsibilities and mixing proportions

At iteration
t
, first compute the Mahalanobis radii

\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}=(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(t)})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t)}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(t)}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\quad 1\leq k\leq K.

The posterior responsibility of component
k
for observation
i
is

\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\pi_{k}^{(t)}\hat{g}^{(t)}\!\left(\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}\right)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}^{(t)}\hat{g}^{(t)}\!\left(\Delta_{i\ell}^{(t)}\right)}.

The mixing proportion update is

\pi_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)},\qquad k=1,\dots,K.

For later use, define the normalized stacked weights

\widetilde{w}_{ik}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}}{\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{K}\hat{\tau}_{uv}^{(t+1)}}=\frac{\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}}{n}

and the effective sample size

n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}=\frac{\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\{\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\}^{2}}=\frac{n^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\{\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\}^{2}}.

Since each row of the responsibility matrix sums to one and
K
is fixed,
n\leq n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}\leq Kn
.

Transformed-radius generator update

The generator is estimated from the same radii
\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}
and the updated responsibilities
\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}
. Define the transformed radii

Y_{ik}^{(t+1)}=\log\bigl(1+\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}\bigr).

When a deterministic bandwidth is not prescribed, the plug-in choice is

h_{t+1}=\max\left\{h_{\min},\ 1.06\,\widehat{\sigma}_{Y,t+1}\,n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}^{-1/5}\right\},

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{Y,t+1}^{2}
is the weighted variance of
Y_{ik}^{(t+1)}
under the weights
\widetilde{w}_{ik}^{(t+1)}
and
h_{\min}>0
is a numerical lower bound. A grid
y_{1}^{(t+1)}<\cdots<y_{M}^{(t+1)}
is placed over the empirical transformed-radius range with a bandwidth-dependent buffer, and

u_{m}^{(t+1)}=\max\{\exp(y_{m}^{(t+1)})-1,\varepsilon_{u}\},\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

The weighted Gaussian kernel estimator of the transformed-radius density is

(2.4)

\widehat{f}_{Y,z^{(t)},h_{t+1}}(y)=\frac{1}{h_{t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{w}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\phi\!\left(\frac{y-Y_{ik}^{(t+1)}}{h_{t+1}}\right),

where
\phi
is the standard normal density. The relationship between the transformed-radius density and the elliptical generator gives the raw log-generator values

\widehat{\ell}_{\mathrm{raw},m}^{(t+1)}=\left(1-\frac{p}{2}\right)\log u_{m}^{(t+1)}+\log\widehat{f}_{Y,z^{(t)},h_{t+1}}\!\left(y_{m}^{(t+1)}\right)-\log\bigl(1+u_{m}^{(t+1)}\bigr)-\log\widehat{C}_{t+1},

with numerical normalizer

\widehat{C}_{t+1}=\int\frac{\widehat{f}_{Y,z^{(t)},h_{t+1}}\{\log(1+u)\}}{1+u}\,du.

In computation, this integral is approximated over the radius grid

[u_{1}^{(t+1)},u_{M}^{(t+1)}]
by the trapezoidal rule.
The normalizing constant is evaluated on the radius grid by the trapezoidal rule,

\widehat{C}_{t+1}=\sum_{m=2}^{M}\frac{u_{m}^{(t+1)}-u_{m-1}^{(t+1)}}{2}\left[\frac{\widehat{f}_{Y,z^{(t)},h_{t+1}}\!\left(y_{m}^{(t+1)}\right)}{1+u_{m}^{(t+1)}}+\frac{\widehat{f}_{Y,z^{(t)},h_{t+1}}\!\left(y_{m-1}^{(t+1)}\right)}{1+u_{m-1}^{(t+1)}}\right].

Here
\widehat{C}_{t+1}
serves as a numerical normalizer on the radius scale; multiplicative constants that do not affect the subsequent radial score are absorbed into the generator normalization convention.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}}
denote the cubic smoothing-spline operator on the transformed-radius scale with a fixed smoothing parameter
\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}>0
. The smoothed log-generator curve is

\widehat{\ell}^{(t+1)}(y)=\mathcal{S}_{\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}}\bigl[\{(y_{m}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\ell}_{\mathrm{raw},m}^{(t+1)})\}_{m=1}^{M}\bigr](y),

and its derivative on the transformed-radius scale is

\widehat{d}^{(t+1)}(y)=\frac{d}{dy}\widehat{\ell}^{(t+1)}(y).

For later interpolation back on the radius scale, define the grid values

\widehat{\ell}_{m}^{(t+1)}=\widehat{\ell}^{(t+1)}\!\left(y_{m}^{(t+1)}\right),\qquad\widehat{d}_{m}^{(t+1)}=\widehat{d}^{(t+1)}\!\left(y_{m}^{(t+1)}\right),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

The updated log-generator is obtained by interpolation,

(2.5)

\log\widehat{G}_{n,h_{t+1}}(z^{(t)})(u)=\operatorname{LinInterp}\Bigl(\{u_{m}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\ell}_{m}^{(t+1)}\}_{m=1}^{M};u\Bigr).

The radial score used in the next center update is

\widehat{\omega}_{m}^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{clip}_{[\omega_{\min},\omega_{\max}]}\left(-\frac{\widehat{d}_{m}^{(t+1)}}{1+u_{m}^{(t+1)}}\right),

again extended to all radii by linear interpolation.

Damped radial-score center update

Given the updated responsibilities and radial score, define the center proposal

\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\widehat{\omega}^{(t+1)}\!\left(\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}\right)\bm{X}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\widehat{\omega}^{(t+1)}\!\left(\Delta_{ik}^{(t)}\right)}.

The accepted center update is damped,

(2.6)

\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta_{\mu})\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(t)}+\eta_{\mu}\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{k}^{(t+1)},\qquad 0<\eta_{\mu}\leq 1.

The residuals for the common-shape update are then recomputed as

\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)}=\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(t+1)}.

TME–POET–GLASSO common-precision update

The common-shape block is designed to remain stable under heavy-tailed elliptical sampling and high dimensionality. The residuals
\{\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\}
are stacked over
i
and
k
, with weights
\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}
. A weighted analogue of the spatial sign covariance matrix
(
42
)
is used only as a bounded-radial pilot. Its purpose is to stabilize the first POET factor adjustment and to place the subsequent Tyler fixed-point iteration inside a numerically regular local region. The final scatter estimator is not this pilot; it is the output of the weighted Tyler
M
-equation
(
40
)
, followed by the second POET regularization
(
17
;
46
)
. The pilot itself is

(2.7)

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{n}(z^{(t)})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\,\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)\top}/\bigl(\left\lVert\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\vee\varepsilon_{r}\bigr)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}}.

This matrix is not the final scatter estimator; the final robust scatter is obtained from Tyler’s equation. Let
d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{p}
be the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{n}(z^{(t)})
. The factor count is selected by the eigenvalue-ratio rule

V_{j}=\sum_{\ell=j}^{p-1}d_{\ell},\qquad\mathrm{GR}_{j}=\frac{\log(1+d_{j}/V_{j})}{\log(1+d_{j+1}/V_{j+1})},\qquad 1\leq j\leq M_{\mathrm{kr}}\wedge(p-2),

and
\hat{m}_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{j}\mathrm{GR}_{j}
. If not specified otherwise, the POET threshold has order

\lambda_{u,t+1}=c_{u}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}}}.

The first POET regularization is

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{pss}}^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u,t+1},\hat{m}_{t}}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{n}(z^{(t)})\right).

This is exactly the matrix map described above and summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Starting from the trace-normalized pilot

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(0,t+1)}=\mathcal{N}\!\left\{\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{pss}}^{(t+1)}\bigr)\right\},\qquad\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})=p\mathbf{A}/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}),

the weighted Tyler iteration computes, for
\ell=0,1,2,\dots
,

q_{ik}^{(\ell,t+1)}=\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)\top}\bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(\ell,t+1)}\bigr)^{-1}\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)},

c_{ik}^{(\ell,t+1)}=\frac{p\,\hat{\tau}_{ik}^{(t+1)}}{\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{K}\hat{\tau}_{uv}^{(t+1)}}\cdot\frac{1}{q_{ik}^{(\ell,t+1)}\vee\varepsilon_{r}},

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(\ell+1,t+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{ik}^{(\ell,t+1)}\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\bm{r}_{ik}^{(t+1)\top}.

The next iterate is

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1,t+1)}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\Bigl[\mathcal{N}\bigl\{(1-\rho_{T})\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(\ell+1,t+1)}+\rho_{T}\mathbf{I}\bigr\}\Bigr].

The iteration is run until a prescribed relative Frobenius tolerance is reached, or until a fixed maximum number of inner iterations has been completed. Its output is the weighted Tyler scatter
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Ty}}^{(t+1)}
. A second POET regularization yields

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u,t+1},\hat{m}_{t}}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Ty}}^{(t+1)}\right).

The sparse precision proposal is obtained from graphical lasso. If the base penalty is not prescribed, set

\lambda_{\Omega,t+1}^{\mathrm{base}}=c_{\Omega}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}}},

and form a finite multiplicative grid
\Lambda_{t+1}
around this value. For every
\lambda\in\Lambda_{t+1}
, solve graphical lasso with input covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(t+1)}
and choose the minimizer of

(2.8)

\mathrm{EBIC}_{t+1}(\lambda)=-n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}\,\ell_{\lambda}+\log\bigl(n_{\mathrm{eff},t+1}\bigr)\,df_{\lambda}+4\gamma_{\mathrm{ebic}}\log(p)\,df_{\lambda},

where
\ell_{\lambda}
and
df_{\lambda}
are the Gaussian profile log-likelihood and the number of selected off-diagonal edges. Denote the selected precision proposal by
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(t+1)}
. The accepted precision update is

\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t+1/2)}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\Bigl\{(1-\eta_{\Omega})\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t)}+\eta_{\Omega}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(t+1)}\Bigr\},\qquad 0<\eta_{\Omega}\leq 1,

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}=\mathcal{N}\Bigl\{\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\bigl((\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t+1/2)})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr\},\qquad\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathrm{PD}}\bigl((\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(t+1)})^{-1}\bigr).

Thus the positive-definite projection, damping, and trace normalization preserve the shape convention
\mathrm{tr}\{(\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t+1)})^{-1}\}=p
.

Empirical map and final classifier

The empirical one-step map is

M_{n,h_{t}}(z^{(t)})=\bigl(\bm{\pi}^{(t+1)},\bm{\mu}_{1}^{(t+1)},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{K}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Omega}^{(t+1)},\widehat{G}_{n,h_{t+1}}(z^{(t)})\bigr).

The algorithm iterates

(2.9)

z^{(t+1)}=M_{n,h_{t}}(z^{(t)}),\qquad t=0,1,\dots,T-1,

until the maximum of the center change, relative Frobenius precision change, and mixing-proportion change is below a prescribed tolerance.
At the end of the outer iterations, the generator and responsibility matrix are recomputed once using the final centers and precision matrix. The final clustering rule is the plug-in Bayes rule

(2.10)

\widehat{G}(\bm{x})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\Bigl\{\log\widehat{\pi}_{k}+\log\widehat{g}\!\left(\widehat{\delta}_{k}(\bm{x})\right)\Bigr\},

where

\widehat{\delta}_{k}(\bm{x})=(\bm{x}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{k})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\bm{x}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{k}).

If multiple random starts are used, the selected run is the one with the largest fitted semiparametric pseudo-loglikelihood

\mathcal{L}^{(s)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{(s)}\widehat{g}^{(s)}\!\left(\Delta_{ik}^{(s)}\right)\right\}.

2.4.
Choosing the number of clusters by a gap-LSE rule

The preceding algorithm assumes a fixed value of
K
. In applications
K
is usually unknown, and we choose it by an outer gap-statistic layer applied to the fitted GEM clustering. This layer is separate from the statistical analysis in Section
3
, which is conditional on a fixed true number of clusters, but it is the default data-driven rule used in our implementation.

Let

\mathcal{K}=\{K_{\min},K_{\min}+1,\dots,K_{\max}\}

be a finite grid of candidate cluster numbers. For each
K\in\mathcal{K}
, run the full GEM algorithm described above using the same tuning conventions and the same number of random starts. Denote the fitted quantities by

\widehat{z}_{K}=\bigl(\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{K},\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{1,K},\dots,\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{K,K},\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K},\widehat{g}_{K}\bigr),\qquad\widehat{\tau}_{ik,K},\qquad\widehat{C}_{i,K}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\widehat{\tau}_{ik,K}.

The fitted Mahalanobis radius of observation
i
to component
k
under the
K
-component fit is

\widehat{\Delta}_{ik,K}=(\bm{X}_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{k,K})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K}(\bm{X}_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{k,K}).

Because the proposed method is designed for heavy-tailed elliptical components, we do not use the raw squared Mahalanobis radius as the within-cluster loss. Instead we use the transformed radial loss

\rho(u)=\log(1+u),

which is the same transformation used in the nonparametric generator block. The observed within-cluster dispersion is

(2.11)

W_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{i\widehat{C}_{i,K},K}\bigr).

The soft responsibility analogue is

W_{K}^{\mathrm{soft}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\tau}_{ik,K}\rho(\widehat{\Delta}_{ik,K}),

and may be used interchangeably when posterior responsibilities are nearly degenerate. In the numerical implementation reported below, the hard-label form (
2.11
) is used.

The reference distribution in the gap statistic should destroy clustering structure while preserving the marginal scale and tail behavior of each coordinate. We therefore use the coordinate-permutation reference common in high-dimensional sparse clustering. For
b=1,\dots,B
, generate a reference data matrix
\mathbf{X}^{0,b}
by independently permuting each column of the observed data:

X^{0,b}_{ij}=X_{\sigma_{b,j}(i),j},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,\quad j=1,\dots,p,

where
\sigma_{b,1},\dots,\sigma_{b,p}
are independent random permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. This construction preserves the empirical univariate distribution of every coordinate, including heavy tails, but breaks the joint cluster structure. It is in the same spirit as the reference resampling used in the original gap statistic of
38
and in sparse clustering criteria such as
45
.

For every reference data set
\mathbf{X}^{0,b}
and every
K\in\mathcal{K}
, fit the same GEM algorithm and compute the corresponding reference dispersion

W^{0,b}_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}^{0,b}_{i\widehat{C}^{0,b}_{i,K},K}\bigr).

Define

\overline{L}_{K}^{0}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log W_{K}^{0,b},\qquad s_{K}=\sqrt{1+B^{-1}}\left\{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bigl(\log W_{K}^{0,b}-\overline{L}_{K}^{0}\bigr)^{2}\right\}^{1/2}.

The gap value is

(2.12)

\operatorname{Gap}(K)=\overline{L}_{K}^{0}-\log W_{K}.

Large values of (
2.12
) indicate that the fitted
K
-cluster partition reduces radial dispersion substantially more than would be expected under the permutation reference distribution.

There are two natural rules. The maximum-gap rule is

\widehat{K}_{\max}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Gap}(K),

with ties resolved in favor of the smaller
K
. In high-dimensional clustering this rule can be too aggressive because the empirical gap may keep increasing when additional small artificial clusters are introduced. We therefore use the lower-complexity one-standard-error rule, denoted by
gap-lse
in the implementation. Write the ordered candidate grid as
K_{1}<\cdots<K_{L}
. The selected number of clusters is

(2.13)

\widehat{K}_{\mathrm{LSE}}=\min\left\{K_{j}:1\leq j<L,\operatorname{Gap}(K_{j})\geq\operatorname{Gap}(K_{j+1})-s_{K_{j+1}}\right\},

with the convention that
\widehat{K}_{\mathrm{LSE}}=K_{L}
if the set in (
2.13
) is empty. This is the usual one-standard-error principle: choose the smallest model whose gap is statistically indistinguishable from the improvement obtained by moving to the next larger candidate. In our numerical experiments the
gap-lse
rule is more stable than
\widehat{K}_{\max}
, and it is therefore the default rule used to choose
K
.

3.
High-dimensional theory

3.1.
Oracle, smoothed, and empirical maps

The algorithm in Section
2
has three natural versions. The first is the oracle population map
M
, in which every empirical average is replaced by its expectation and the generator block uses the unsmoothed transformed-radius density. The second is the smoothed population map
M_{h}
, in which the generator block is still population based, but the transformed-radius density is first convolved with the Gaussian kernel of bandwidth
h
. The third is the empirical map
M_{n,h}
defined by the empirical update in (
2.9
). Throughout the theory section the bandwidth sequence
h=h_{n}
is treated as a deterministic input. Data-adaptive bandwidth choices can be analyzed by conditioning on the selected bandwidth or by adding a preliminary bandwidth-selection argument.

For

z=(\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{K},\mathbf{\Omega},g),\qquad\widetilde{z}=(\widetilde{\bm{\pi}},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{K},\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}},\widetilde{g}),

write
\ell=\log g
,
\widetilde{\ell}=\log\widetilde{g}
,
w=-\ell^{\prime}
, and
\widetilde{w}=-\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}
. The distance used in the theory is

(3.1)

\displaystyle d(z,\widetilde{z})

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}}\Biggl[\left\lVert\bm{\pi}-\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\infty}\vee\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{k}-\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\sigma(k)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\vee\left\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}-\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}\left|\ell(u)-\widetilde{\ell}(u)\right|\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}(1+u)^{1/2}\left|w(u)-\widetilde{w}(u)\right|\Biggr].

where
U_{0}>0
is the fixed radius cut-off used in the theoretical analysis, and
\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}_{\sigma}=(\widetilde{\pi}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\widetilde{\pi}_{\sigma(K)})
. Let

\mathcal{B}(r)=\{z:d(z,z^{*})\leq r\}.

The minimum pairwise Mahalanobis separation is

(3.2)

\Delta_{*}^{2}=\min_{1\leq k<\ell\leq K}(\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}(\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell}^{*}).

For the sparse precision matrix write

S_{\Omega}=\{(a,b):\Omega_{ab}^{*}\neq 0\},\qquad s_{\Omega}=\max_{1\leq a\leq p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}\bm{1}\{(a,b)\in S_{\Omega}\}.

The weighted Tyler, POET, and GLASSO blocks produce the precision error scale

(3.3)

\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{Ty}}=\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}},\qquad\zeta_{n}=s_{\Omega}\Biggl(\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{Ty}}+\rho_{n,p}+\lambda_{\Omega,n}\Biggr),

where
\rho_{n,p}
is the POET approximation error and
\lambda_{\Omega,n}
is the GLASSO penalty level. The full one-step statistical error is

(3.4)

\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}=h^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{nh}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\zeta_{n}.

The term
h^{2}
is the smoothing bias. The term
\sqrt{\log n/(nh)}
is the stochastic fluctuation of the one-dimensional kernel block. The term
\sqrt{\log p/n}
comes from the mixing-proportion and center blocks. The term
\zeta_{n}
is produced only by the common TME–POET–GLASSO precision block.

3.2.
Assumptions

The next assumptions are used throughout Section
3
and Appendix
A
. They are stated once and then reused in all subsequent theorems.

Assumption 3.1

(Semiparametric elliptical mixture and separation)
.

The number of components
K
is fixed. The data follow the common-shape elliptical mixture

\mathbb{P}(Z=k)=\pi_{k}^{*},\qquad\bm{X}\mid Z=k=\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*1/2}R\bm{U},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

where
\bm{U}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
,
R\geq 0
is independent of
(Z,\bm{U})
, and
\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}=(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})^{-1}
. The trace normalization
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})=p
holds, and the radial variable satisfies

\mathbb{E}(1+R)<\infty.

In addition,

\min_{1\leq k\leq K}\pi_{k}^{*}\geq c_{\pi}>0,\qquad 0<m_{\Omega}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{*})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{*})\leq M_{\Omega}<\infty,

and

\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{\mu},\qquad\Delta_{*}^{2}\geq\Delta_{0}^{2}.

This condition specifies the fixed-
K
common-shape elliptical mixture that motivates the entire method. The finite first radial moment is used only in the local contraction argument to control the overlap-weighted perturbation term; it still allows heavy-tailed radial laws but rules out infinite-mean radial variables in the population EM theory. The stochastic representation, trace-normalized shape matrix, and component separation are standard in elliptical mixture and semiparametric clusterwise elliptical analyses; see
19
,
24
, and
37
.

Assumption 3.2

(Generator regularity and score decay)
.

The true generator
g^{*}
is positive, strictly decreasing, and twice continuously differentiable on
[0,U_{0}]
. Writing
\ell^{*}=\log g^{*}
and
w^{*}=-(\ell^{*})^{\prime}
, there exist constants
M_{w}
,
L_{w}
,
m_{w}
,
u_{0}
, and
c_{0}
such that

(3.5)

\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}(1+u)^{1/2}w^{*}(u)\leq M_{w},\qquad\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}(1+u)^{3/2}\left|w^{*\prime}(u)\right|\leq L_{w},

and

(3.6)

\inf_{0\leq u\leq u_{0}}w^{*}(u)\geq m_{w},\qquad\mathbb{P}(R^{2}\leq u_{0})\geq c_{0}.

The transformed radial variable
Y=\log(1+R^{2})
has density
f_{Y}^{*}
on
[0,\log(1+U_{0})]
, and

(3.7)

\sup_{y\in[0,\log(1+U_{0})]}\left|f_{Y}^{*\prime}(y)\right|+\sup_{y\in[0,\log(1+U_{0})]}\left|f_{Y}^{*\prime\prime}(y)\right|<\infty.

The smoothness of
g^{*}
controls both the stability of posterior weights and the second-order bias of the one-dimensional kernel estimator for transformed radii. Comparable radial smoothness and shape constraints are commonly imposed in semiparametric elliptical likelihood arguments and in kernel-smoothed nonparametric estimation; see
37
and
20
.

Assumption 3.3

(Local parameter neighborhood)
.

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that every
z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
satisfies

\pi_{k}\geq c_{\pi}/2,\qquad m_{\Omega}/2\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Omega})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Omega})\leq 2M_{\Omega},\qquad\max_{k\leq K}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2M_{\mu},

and its generator
g
obeys the same bounds as in (
3.5
)–(
3.6
). The score function of every
z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
is denoted by
w_{z}
.

This is a local basin condition rather than a tail or moment assumption on the data. Such neighborhoods are standard in finite-mixture EM theory, where population contraction and empirical concentration are proved only near the target parameter; see
6
and
11
.

Assumption 3.4

(Factor structure for the common scatter)
.

The common scatter matrix admits the decomposition

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathbf{B}^{*}\mathbf{B}^{*\top}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{u}^{*},

where
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=m_{*}
is fixed. The eigen-gap condition

(3.8)

\lambda_{m_{*}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})-\lambda_{m_{*}+1}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})\geq c_{\mathrm{gap}}>0

holds, and the sparse remainder obeys

\left\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{u}^{*}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq M_{u},\qquad\rho_{n,p}\to 0.

The thresholding rule used in the two POET steps is the entrywise soft-thresholding rule. In addition, the POET map is locally stable in entrywise norm: there exist constants
\epsilon_{\mathrm{P}}>0
and
C_{\mathrm{P}}<\infty
, independent of
(n,p)
, such that for any symmetric matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
satisfying

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\right\rVert_{\max}\vee\left\lVert\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{P}},

one has

(3.9)

\left\lVert\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u},m_{*}}(\mathbf{A})-\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u},m_{*}}(\mathbf{B})\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{\mathrm{P}}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{\max}+C_{\mathrm{P}}\rho_{n,p}.

This max-norm stability is imposed directly; the proof below does not use the crude inequality
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{2}\leq p\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{\max}
to control POET. If the factor rank is chosen by the eigen-ratio rule in the algorithm, then

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{m}=m_{*})\to 1.

The low-rank-plus-sparse structure is exactly the regime for which POET regularizes high-dimensional scatter matrices by thresholding the principal orthogonal complement. The explicit entrywise stability condition in (
3.9
) is included to make the high-dimensional max-norm argument dimension-free; without such a condition, a spectral-norm Davis–Kahan step would introduce an unwanted factor of
p
. The same structure under elliptical symmetry appears in robust POET-type analyses such as
17
,
18
, and
46
.

Assumption 3.5

(Tyler equation and local nonsingularity)
.

For
z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}\succ\bm{0}
with
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=p
, define

(3.10)

\Psi(\mathbf{\Sigma},z)=\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathcal{N}\Biggl[(1-\rho_{T})p\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left\{r_{k}(\bm{X};z)\frac{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)^{\top}}{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)\vee\varepsilon_{r}}\right\}+\rho_{T}\mathbf{I}\Biggr],

where
\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{X};z)=\bm{X}-U_{k}(z)
and
\rho_{T}\in(0,1)
is the Tyler ridge constant. The equation
\Psi(\mathbf{\Sigma},z)=\bm{0}
has a unique solution
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)
in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
, and the Jacobian

\mathbf{J}_{T}=D_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\operatorname{vec}\Psi(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*},z^{*})

is nonsingular with

\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{J}_{T})\geq c_{T}>0.

The assumption states that the population Tyler shape equation is locally identifiable and differentiable. This is the analogue of a nonsingular estimating-equation Jacobian in classical
M
-estimation and allows the Tyler block to be controlled through the implicit function theorem; see
40
.

Assumption 3.6

(Sparse precision regularity)
.

Let

\mathbf{\Gamma}^{*}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}.

The standard graphical-lasso incoherence and inverse-Hessian bounds hold:

(3.11)

\left\lVert\mathbf{\Gamma}^{*}_{S_{\Omega}^{c}S_{\Omega}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}^{*}_{S_{\Omega}S_{\Omega}})^{-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1-\alpha,\qquad\left\lVert(\mathbf{\Gamma}^{*}_{S_{\Omega}S_{\Omega}})^{-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{\Gamma},

for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
M_{\Gamma}<\infty
. The row sparsity
s_{\Omega}
may depend on
(n,p)
, but

s_{\Omega}\lambda_{\Omega,n}\to 0,\qquad\lambda_{\Omega,n}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}.

The incoherence and inverse-Hessian bounds are the standard local regularity conditions under which graphical-lasso solutions are stable in max norm and support pattern. Closely related assumptions are used in sparse inverse covariance estimation and graphical-model selection, for example by
22
and
34
.

Assumption 3.7

(Local empirical-process complexity)
.

For each fixed
h\in(0,1]
, the classes generated by the local ball
\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
are considered on a deterministic transformed-radius grid
y_{1},\dots,y_{M}
. The theory allows
M=M_{n}
to grow with
n
provided
\log M_{n}\lesssim\log n
and the smoothing-spline and interpolation operators on this grid sequence have uniformly bounded
L_{\infty}
operator norms; a fixed grid is the special case
M_{n}\equiv M
. With this convention,

\mathcal{F}_{\pi,k}=\{\bm{x}\mapsto r_{k}(\bm{x};z):z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})\},

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,k,j,h}=\Bigl\{\bm{x}\mapsto r_{k}(\bm{x};z)w_{h,z}\bigl(\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)\bigr)x_{j}:z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})\Bigr\},

\mathcal{F}_{K,m,h}=\Bigl\{\bm{x}\mapsto r_{k}(\bm{x};z)K\Bigl(\frac{y_{m}-q\{\delta_{k}(\bm{x};z)\}}{h}\Bigr):z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0}),\ 1\leq k\leq K,\ 1\leq m\leq M\Bigr\},

and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ty},a,b}=\Bigl\{\bm{x}\mapsto r_{k}(\bm{x};z)\frac{r_{k,a}^{+}(\bm{x};z)r_{k,b}^{+}(\bm{x};z)}{\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{x};z)^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{r}_{k}^{+}(\bm{x};z)\vee\varepsilon_{r}}:z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0}),\ \mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\Bigr\},

with

\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}=\{\mathbf{\Sigma}\succ\bm{0}:\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=p,\ m_{\Sigma}\mathbf{I}\preceq\mathbf{\Sigma}\preceq M_{\Sigma}\mathbf{I}\},

are VC-type classes with entropy bound

(3.12)

\sup_{Q}\log N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_{2}(Q)},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(Q)\bigr)\leq A_{0}+v_{0}\log(1/\varepsilon)+C_{0}\log p,\qquad 0<\varepsilon\leq 1,

for envelopes
F
that are uniformly bounded in
(n,p)
. The kernel class has bounded envelope of order
h^{-1}
and variance of order
h^{-1}
.

This empirical-process condition is used only to invoke uniform Bernstein-type bounds over the local function classes generated by the update map. The fractional Tyler class is included explicitly in the assumption: the regularized denominator in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ty},a,b}
is bounded away from zero by
\varepsilon_{r}
, and the spectral bounds on
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
give a bounded envelope, but the VC-type entropy of this matrix-inverse fractional class is not inferred from ordinary coordinatewise VC closure alone. Thus Theorem
3.5
should be read as conditional on this stated local entropy bound. Such VC-type entropy assumptions are standard in high-dimensional
M
-estimation and empirical-process theory; see
41
.

Assumption 3.8

(Initialization and tuning)
.

The initial iterate produced by the sparse
K
-median block satisfies

d(z^{(0)},z^{*})\leq r_{0}/2

with probability tending to one. The bandwidth and penalties satisfy

(3.13)

h=h_{n}\downarrow 0,\qquad\frac{nh_{n}}{\log n}\to\infty,\qquad\lambda_{\Omega,n}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}},\qquad\rho_{n,p}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}.

The initialization requirement is the usual local-start condition for nonconvex EM-type algorithms. In our implementation the sparse median initializer is used because median-based high-dimensional procedures are less sensitive to heavy tails, as emphasized by
23
, while the local-basin role mirrors the initialization conditions in
11
.

3.3.
Population contraction

The first result shows that the oracle map is locally contractive. The proof is not based on an abstract Lipschitz assumption. Instead, the required local smoothness is derived from Assumptions
3.1
–
3.5
.

Proposition 3.1

(Local regularity of the oracle blocks)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.5
hold. Then there exist
r_{1}\in(0,r_{0}]
and finite constants
L_{\pi}
,
L_{G}
,
L_{\mu}
,
L_{\mathrm{Ty}}
, and
L_{\Omega}
, depending only on the constants in Assumptions
3.1
–
3.5
, such that for all
z,\widetilde{z}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{1})
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Pi(z)-\Pi(\widetilde{z})\right\rVert_{\infty}\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}\left|G(z)(u)-G(\widetilde{z})(u)\right|

\displaystyle\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}(1+u)^{1/2}\left|w_{z}(u)-w_{\widetilde{z}}(u)\right|\leq(L_{\pi}+L_{G})d(z,\widetilde{z}).

(3.15)

\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\lVert U_{k}(z)-U_{k}(\widetilde{z})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq(1-\eta_{\mu}+\eta_{\mu}L_{\mu})d(z,\widetilde{z}),

and

(3.16)

\left\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(\widetilde{z})\right\rVert_{\max}\leq L_{\mathrm{Ty}}d(z,\widetilde{z}),\qquad\left\lVert T(z)-T(\widetilde{z})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\eta_{\Omega}L_{\Omega}d(z,\widetilde{z}).

Theorem 3.2

(Population contraction)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.5
hold. Then there exist constants
r\in(0,r_{1}]
and
\Delta_{0}^{*}<\infty
such that, whenever
\Delta_{0}\geq\Delta_{0}^{*}
in Assumption
3.1
, the oracle map satisfies

(3.17)

d\bigl(M(z),z^{*}\bigr)\leq\kappa\,d(z,z^{*}),\qquad z\in\mathcal{B}(r),

for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
. More explicitly,

(3.18)

\kappa=\max\Bigl\{L_{\pi}^{\dagger},L_{G}^{\dagger},1-\eta_{\mu}+\eta_{\mu}L_{\mu}^{\dagger},\eta_{\Omega}L_{\Omega}\Bigr\}+C_{r}r,

where
L_{\pi}^{\dagger}
,
L_{G}^{\dagger}
, and
L_{\mu}^{\dagger}
decrease to
0
as
\Delta_{0}\to\infty
.

3.4.
Kernel bias and empirical concentration

The smoothed map
M_{h}
differs from
M
only through the generator block and the center block that uses the smoothed score. The next proposition isolates the deterministic smoothing bias.

Proposition 3.3

(Kernel bias)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.2
hold, and let
K
be the Gaussian kernel used in the generator update. Then there exists
C_{h}<\infty
such that

(3.19)

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}(r)}d\bigl(M_{h}(z),M(z)\bigr)\leq C_{h}h^{2}.

The next theorem controls the empirical generator, mixing-proportion, and center blocks. Here
\bm{U}_{k,h}(z)
denotes the smoothed population center update obtained from
U_{k}(z)
by replacing
w_{z}
with the kernel-smoothed score
w_{h,z}
, and
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{k}(z)
denotes its empirical counterpart. The rate contains two terms. The factor
\sqrt{\log p/n}
comes from the finite-dimensional mixing and center blocks. The factor
\sqrt{\log n/(nh)}
comes from the one-dimensional kernel estimator on the transformed-radius scale.

Theorem 3.4

(Uniform concentration of the non-precision blocks)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.7
hold. Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Biggl[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}(r)}\Biggl\{\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\pi}}(z)-\bm{\Pi}(z)\right\rVert_{\infty}\vee\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{U}}_{k}(z)-\bm{U}_{k,h}(z)\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}\left|\widehat{G}_{n,h}(z)(u)-G_{h}(z)(u)\right|\vee\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}(1+u)^{1/2}\left|\widehat{w}_{n,h}(z)(u)-w_{h}(z)(u)\right|\Biggr\}

\displaystyle>C\Biggl\{\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{nh}}\Biggr\}\Biggr]\leq Cp^{-c}+Cn^{-c}.

The next theorem treats the common covariance block. The statement is now written directly for the weighted Tyler
M
-estimator and the resulting precision matrix. Here
\mathbf{\Omega}_{h}(z)
denotes the population GLASSO precision output associated with the smoothed population map, and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{h}(z)
denotes the empirical GLASSO output. The weighted spatial-sign pilot is used only inside the proof to place the Tyler iteration inside the local basin.

Theorem 3.5

(Weighted TME–POET–GLASSO concentration)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.8
hold. Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

(3.21)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}(r)}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{Ty}}(z)\right\rVert_{\max}>C\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{Ty}}\right]\leq Cp^{-c},

and

(3.22)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}(r)}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(z)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pt}}(z)\right\rVert_{\max}>C\bigl\{\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{Ty}}+\rho_{n,p}\bigr\}\right]\leq Cp^{-c}.

Moreover, the common sparse precision estimator produced by the GLASSO step obeys

(3.23)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}(r)}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{h}(z)-\mathbf{\Omega}_{h}(z)\right\rVert_{1}>C\zeta_{n}\right]\leq Cp^{-c}.

3.5.
Main recursion, parameter consistency, and misclustering error

The next theorem combines Theorem
3.2
, Proposition
3.3
, Theorem
3.4
, and Theorem
3.5
.

Theorem 3.6

(One-step recursion and parameter consistency)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.8
hold. Let

e_{t}=d(z^{(t)},z^{*}).

Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that, for every deterministic sequence
T_{n}\lesssim\log n
,

(3.24)

\mathbb{P}\Biggl[e_{t+1}\leq\kappa e_{t}+C_{1}h^{2}+C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{nh}}+C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+C_{4}\zeta_{n},\qquad 0\leq t<T_{n}\Biggr]\geq 1-Cp^{-c}-Cn^{-c}.

Consequently,

(3.25)

e_{t}\leq\kappa^{t}e_{0}+\frac{C_{1}h^{2}+C_{2}\sqrt{\log n/(nh)}+C_{3}\sqrt{\log p/n}+C_{4}\zeta_{n}}{1-\kappa},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T_{n},

with the same probability. If
T_{n}\asymp\log(1/\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta})
, then

(3.26)

d(z^{(T_{n})},z^{*})=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}\right)\qquad\text{in probability.}

In particular,

(3.27)

\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{\pi}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{k}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}\right\rVert_{1}+\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}\left|\widehat{g}(u)-g^{*}(u)\right|=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}\right).

Corollary 3.7

(Canonical rate)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.8
hold and

h\asymp\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/5},\qquad\rho_{n,p}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}},\qquad\lambda_{\Omega,n}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}.

Then

(3.28)

d(z^{(T_{n})},z^{*})=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{2/5}+(1+s_{\Omega})\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right)\qquad\text{in probability.}

For the clustering error define the oracle and plug-in scores

\eta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})=\log\pi_{k}^{*}+\ell^{*}\bigl(\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\bigr),\qquad\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\bm{x})=\log\widehat{\pi}_{k}+\log\widehat{g}\bigl(\widehat{\delta}_{k}(\bm{x})\bigr),

and the oracle margin

(3.29)

\mathcal{M}^{*}(\bm{x})=\eta_{G^{*}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{x})-\max_{\ell\neq G^{*}(\bm{x})}\eta_{\ell}^{*}(\bm{x}).

The final assumption is a standard margin condition adapted to the multiclass elliptical rule.

Assumption 3.9

(Margin condition)
.

There exist constants
C_{m}>0
and
\alpha\geq 0
such that, almost surely with respect to the radial variable
R
,

(3.30)

\mathbb{P}\{0<\mathcal{M}^{*}(\bm{X})\leq t\mid R\}\leq C_{m}t^{\alpha},\qquad t>0.

In addition, the radial variable satisfies

(3.31)

\mathbb{E}(1+R)^{\alpha+1}<\infty.

This is a radius-conditional Tsybakov margin condition for the posterior separation between the best and second-best component labels. It is a mild strengthening of the usual unconditional margin assumption and is convenient here because the plug-in score error is proportional to
(1+R)
under elliptical symmetry. Related margin conditions are standard in classification and high-dimensional mixture analysis; see
39
and
11
.

Theorem 3.8

(Excess misclustering error)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.9
hold. Then

(3.32)

R(\widehat{G})-R(G^{*})=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}^{\alpha+1}\right)\qquad\text{in probability.}

In the Lipschitz-margin case
\alpha=1
,

(3.33)

R(\widehat{G})-R(G^{*})=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}^{2}\right)\qquad\text{in probability.}

Theorem
3.8
translates the high-dimensional parameter rate in
Theorem
3.6
into a clustering-risk rate. The quantity

\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}
controls the uniform error of the fitted posterior scores,
whereas the margin exponent
\alpha
determines how much probability mass lies
near the oracle decision boundary. Hence a smaller score error produces an
excess misclustering error of order
\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}^{\alpha+1}
rather
than merely
\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}
. In particular, under the canonical tuning
in Corollary
3.7
,

R(\widehat{G})-R(G^{*})=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left[\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{2/5}+(1+s_{\Omega})\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right]^{\alpha+1}\right),

and this becomes a quadratic rate in the common Lipschitz-margin case

\alpha=1
.

4.
Simulation studies

We examine the finite-sample performance of the proposed semiparametric GEM procedure under simulation settings aligned with the high-dimensional elliptical model considered in the paper.
The simulations have two purposes.
First, fixing the true number of clusters at
K=3
, we compare the clustering performance of GEM with several high-dimensional and robust competitors.
Second, we evaluate the gap-LSE rule for selecting the number of clusters when
K
is unknown. Unless stated otherwise, the sample size is
n=300
, and we consider two ambient dimensions,
p=100
and
p=200
. The common scatter matrix is the autoregressive matrix

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}_{ab}=0.5^{|a-b|},\qquad 1\leq a,b\leq p.

The component probabilities are equal. The centers differ only in the first six coordinates:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\delta,\delta,\delta,0,0,0,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=(-\delta,0,0,\delta,\delta,0,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{3}^{*}

\displaystyle=(0,-\delta,\delta,-\delta,0,\delta,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},

with
\delta=1.5
. Thus the clustering signal is sparse in the coordinate basis, whereas the within-cluster shape is correlated through
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be the symmetric square root of
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
, so that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}
, and let
\bm{U}
be uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. Each component is generated in the elliptical form

\bm{X}=\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}+R\mathbf{S}\bm{U},\qquad k=1,2,3,

where
R\geq 0
is independent of
\bm{U}
. To match the simulation design exactly, let
Q\sim\chi_{p}^{2}
,
G\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu_{t}}
,
E\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)
, and
V\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)
, all mutually independent and also independent of
\bm{U}
. The four radial laws are then

R_{\mathrm{Gauss}}=Q^{1/2},\qquad R_{t}=\left\{\frac{(\nu_{t}-2)Q}{G}\right\}^{1/2}\quad\text{with }\nu_{t}=5,

Let
c_{i}^{0}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
denote the true class label and let
\widehat{c}_{i}
denote the estimated cluster label. Since cluster labels are identifiable only up to permutation, we report the label-invariant clustering accuracy

\operatorname{Acc}(\widehat{\bm{c}},\bm{c}^{0})=\max_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma(\widehat{c}_{i})=c_{i}^{0}\},

where

Accuracy measures the fraction of correctly assigned observations after the best relabeling. The adjusted Rand index measures pairwise agreement after correcting for chance, and is also invariant to label permutations.

6.
Conclusion

This paper develops a semiparametric GEM procedure for high-dimensional clustering under heavy-tailed elliptical mixture models. The method combines a nonparametric update of the common radial generator with robust TME–POET–GLASSO estimation of a common sparse precision-shape matrix, and it is equipped with high-dimensional consistency theory for parameter estimation and plug-in clustering. Simulations and the Optdigits analysis show that the proposed procedure is especially effective when the data are non-Gaussian but retain approximate elliptical geometry.

Several extensions are natural. The most direct one is to relax the common-shape assumption and allow component-specific shape matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}
or sparse precision matrices
\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
. A statistically stable version would likely require a joint graphical penalty, shared support restrictions, or low-rank-plus-sparse coupling across clusters, because unrestricted component-specific shapes are difficult to identify and estimate when
p
is large. A second direction is to allow component-specific generators
g_{k}
, which would accommodate clusters with different tail behavior; however, this extension requires additional identifiability conditions separating radial variation from shape variation, since changes in
g_{k}
and rescalings of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}
can otherwise produce observationally similar densities. A third direction is to develop a full theory for data-driven cluster-number selection under the proposed gap-LSE rule and to compare it with semiparametric information criteria. Related tuning questions include adaptive bandwidth selection, factor-rank selection, and graph-penalty selection under heavy-tailed elliptical sampling. Finally, minimax lower bounds for high-dimensional semiparametric elliptical mixtures remain open and would clarify whether the rates obtained here are optimal.

Lemma A.1

(Bernstein inequality for bounded empirical classes)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a VC-type class with entropy bound (
3.12
), envelope
F
, and variance bound
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Var}\{f(\bm{X})\}\leq\sigma^{2}
. If
\|F\|_{\infty}\leq B
, then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that for every
t>0
,

(A.1)

\mathbb{P}\Biggl[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{f(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}f(\bm{X})\}\right|>C\Biggl\{\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log p+t}{n}}+B\frac{\log p+t}{n}\Biggr\}\Biggr]\leq e^{-ct}.

The inequality is a standard consequence of Bousquet’s version of Talagrand’s inequality for suprema of bounded empirical processes combined with VC-type entropy bounds; see
8
and
41
.

Lemma A.2

(Elliptical geometry inside the local ball)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
hold. Then there exists
C<\infty
such that for every
z\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
and every
1\leq k\leq K

(A.2)

C^{-1}(1+R^{2})\leq 1+\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z)\leq C(1+R^{2})\qquad\text{almost surely,}

whenever

(A.3)

w_{z}\bigl(\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z)\bigr)\,\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C,

\left\lVert\frac{(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}}{(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C.

Proof.

Fix

and condition on
Z=k

\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*1/2}R\bm{U}+(\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}-\bm{\mu}_{k}).

Since
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Omega})\geq m_{\Omega}/2
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Omega})\leq 2M_{\Omega}

\frac{m_{\Omega}}{2}\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\delta_{k}(\bm{X};z)\leq 2M_{\Omega}\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*1/2}R\bm{U}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{k}^{*}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})^{1/2}R+p^{1/2}r_{0},

and similarly

\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})^{1/2}R-p^{1/2}r_{0}.

Because

which proves (
A.3
). Finally, for
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}

(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma})}\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

so

\left|\frac{(X_{a}-\mu_{k,a})(X_{b}-\mu_{k,b})}{(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k})}\right|\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\frac{\left|(X_{a}-\mu_{k,a})(X_{b}-\mu_{k,b})\right|}{\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\leq C.

Taking the maximum over
a
and
b
gives (
A.4
).

Lemma A.3

(Softmax derivative)
.

For
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{K})^{\top}
, define

s_{k}(\bm{a})=\frac{\exp(a_{k})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp(a_{\ell})}.

Then

(A.5)

\frac{\partial s_{k}(\bm{a})}{\partial a_{\ell}}=s_{k}(\bm{a})\{\bm{1}(k=\ell)-s_{\ell}(\bm{a})\}.

Consequently,

(A.6)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\left|\frac{\partial s_{k}(\bm{a})}{\partial a_{\ell}}\right|\leq 2s_{k}(\bm{a})\{1-s_{k}(\bm{a})\}\leq 2\max_{1\leq u\leq K}s_{u}(\bm{a})\{1-s_{u}(\bm{a})\}.

Differentiate the quotient definition of

\frac{\partial s_{k}(\bm{a})}{\partial a_{\ell}}=\frac{\bm{1}(k=\ell)e^{a_{k}}\sum_{u=1}^{K}e^{a_{u}}-e^{a_{k}}e^{a_{\ell}}}{(\sum_{u=1}^{K}e^{a_{u}})^{2}}=s_{k}(\bm{a})\{\bm{1}(k=\ell)-s_{\ell}(\bm{a})\}.

Summing over
\ell

\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\left|\frac{\partial s_{k}(\bm{a})}{\partial a_{\ell}}\right|=s_{k}(\bm{a})\Bigl\{1-s_{k}(\bm{a})+\sum_{\ell\neq k}s_{\ell}(\bm{a})\Bigr\}=2s_{k}(\bm{a})\{1-s_{k}(\bm{a})\},

which is (
A.6
).

Lemma A.4

(POET perturbation)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.4
holds. There exist
\epsilon_{\mathrm{P}}>0
and
C_{\mathrm{P}}<\infty
, independent of
(n,p)

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\right\rVert_{\max}\vee\left\lVert\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{P}},

one has

\left\lVert\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u},m_{*}}(\mathbf{A})-\operatorname{POET}_{\lambda_{u},m_{*}}(\mathbf{B})\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{\mathrm{P}}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{\max}+C_{\mathrm{P}}\rho_{n,p}.

Proof.

This is precisely the local entrywise stability condition (

Lemma A.5

(GLASSO perturbation)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.6
holds. Then there exist constants
\epsilon_{G}>0
and
C_{G}<\infty
such that whenever

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\epsilon_{G},

and both matrices are positive definite, the corresponding graphical-lasso solutions satisfy

(A.8)

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega}\right\rVert_{1}\leq C_{G}s_{\Omega}\Bigl(\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}\right\rVert_{\max}+\lambda_{\Omega,n}\Bigr).

Proof.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker system for the graphical-lasso problem is

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}+\lambda_{\Omega,n}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}=\bm{0},\qquad\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}+\lambda_{\Omega,n}\mathbf{Z}=\bm{0},

where
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
and
\mathbf{Z}
are subgradient matrices with
\widehat{Z}_{ab}=\operatorname{sgn}(\widehat{P}_{ab})
and
Z_{ab}=\operatorname{sgn}(P_{ab})
on the support, and entries in
[-1,1]
off the support. Subtracting the two equations gives

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}-\lambda_{\Omega,n}(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}-\mathbf{Z}).

Vectorizing and expanding
\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}
around
\mathbf{\Omega}
yields

\operatorname{vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma})=-\mathbf{\Gamma}^{*}\operatorname{vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega})+\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{R})-\lambda_{\Omega,n}\operatorname{vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}-\mathbf{Z}),

with a quadratic remainder
\mathbf{R}
. By the incoherence condition (
3.11
), the standard primal–dual witness argument yields

\left\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega})_{S_{\Omega}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{\Gamma}\Bigl\{\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}\right\rVert_{\max}+\lambda_{\Omega,n}\Bigr\},

and the off-support coordinates are dominated by the same quantity. Converting the vector bound to the matrix
\ell_{1}
norm and using the row sparsity
s_{\Omega}
gives (
A.8
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

Work on the high-probability event from Theorem

, on which (

) holds. Fix
\bm{x}
and define

If
\widehat{G}(\bm{x})\neq G^{*}(\bm{x})
, then by definition of the oracle margin,

\eta_{G^{*}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{x})-\eta_{\widehat{G}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{x})\leq\left|\widehat{\eta}_{G^{*}(\bm{x})}(\bm{x})-\eta_{G^{*}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|+\left|\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{G}(\bm{x})}(\bm{x})-\eta_{\widehat{G}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|.

Hence

(A.31)

\mathcal{M}^{*}(\bm{x})\leq 2\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left|\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\bm{x})-\eta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|\qquad\text{on }\{\widehat{G}(\bm{x})\neq G^{*}(\bm{x})\}.

Next,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\bm{x})-\eta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{\pi}^{-1}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{\pi}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\sup_{0\leq u\leq U_{0}}\left|\widehat{\ell}(u)-\ell^{*}(u)\right|

(A.32)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sup_{\xi\in[0,U_{0}]}w_{\xi}\,\left|\widehat{\delta}_{k}(\bm{x})-\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|,

where
w_{\xi}
denotes the score at an intermediate radius. By the quadratic expansion used in (
A.11
),

\left|\widehat{\delta}_{k}(\bm{x})-\delta_{k}^{*}(\bm{x})\right|\leq C\{1+\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\infty}\}^{2}\Bigl(\max_{u\leq K}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{u}-\bm{\mu}_{u}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr).

Conditioning on
Z=k_{0}
, write
\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}_{k_{0}}^{*}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*1/2}R\bm{U}

\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C(1+R)

3.5
) yields

\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left|\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\bm{X})-\eta_{k}^{*}(\bm{X})\right|\leq C(1+R)\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}\qquad\text{almost surely on the event of Theorem~\ref{thm:main_recursion}.}

Combining (
A.31
) and (

) gives

(A.34)

\{\widehat{G}(\bm{X})\neq G^{*}(\bm{X})\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{M}^{*}(\bm{X})\leq C(1+R)\varepsilon_{n,h,\zeta}\}.

\bm{x}

\displaystyle R(\widehat{G})-R(G^{*})

3.9

3.9
