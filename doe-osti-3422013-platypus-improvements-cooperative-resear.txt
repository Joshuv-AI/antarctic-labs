 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 PLatypus is a field photoluminescence system under development at NLR. This project will
 cover improvements to PLatypus that enhance the current prototype's ability to efficiently do
 field imaging campaigns.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 Summary of research results:

 Task 1: Implement improvements to NLR’s Platypus - These improvements include a portable
 power supply, automation of image collection, and mechanical improvements for deploying and
 operating PLatypus in the field.

 • Most PV systems have limited or no AC power available for test equipment. Truck-based
 generators are available, but only sometimes and often not with adequate power for PL
 imaging. A portable battery power supply surmounts these obstacles and broadens the
 scope of systems that PLatypus can be used on. We will build upon the existing battery
 power supply to improve its robustness. This includes adding connections for a
 supervisory controller, safety lights, and any other enhancements that field trials require.
 • PLatypus relies on coordinated lighting control and image acquisition between several
 modular camera nodes. We will continue improving this coordination to minimize
 thetotal cycle time for characterizing a module. This will increase the amount of PV
 system area that can be covered in a single evening imaging campaign. This includes
 adding and modifying existing control software to improve the efficiency of field
 campaigns.
 • PLatypus is mechanically modular and can be field assembled from easily portable
 components. We will continue developing the components that interface the imaging
 system with the assembled PV system. This will reduce the time and effort needed to
 mount and unmount PLatypus on successive portions of the PV system under test. This
 includes implementing multiple or adjustable structures for different system
 configurations or module sizes.
 • Components which are purchased under this agreement and integrated into DOE property
 will be deemed DOE property.

 • Deliverable: Results from a field trial of the improved prototype. Estimated completion

 A proof-of-concept prototype of PLatypus was built as part of a Duramat Spark project. Near the
 end of this Spark project, this CRADA began supporting improvements to the prototype. We
 implemented these improvements prior to a Duramat-funded field demonstration of the prototype
    in March 2023.

 Portable power supply

 PLatypus is powered by a 36-volt lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery. In this CRADA, we
 investigated a problem with some of PLatypus’s illumination LEDs flickering during use. We
 traced the problem to fluctuating outputs from some of the per-LED buck converters. The
 converters are intended to keep every LED operating in a constant-current mode. While the
 unbranded buck converter modules were sold as having 40-volt maximum input, the actual
 maximum input of the switching regulator used in these buck converters was 36 volts. Because
 36-volt LFP batteries charge to approximately 40 volts when full, the buck converter modules
 were running inside the specified input range, but outside the actual range allowed by their
 components. Some modules tolerated this and some responded with output fluctuations leading
 to flicker.

 We partially solved the flickering problem by (a) not fully charging the LFP battery and (b)
 running the LEDs for a few seconds, allowing the battery voltage to temporarily drop, before
 collecting an image.

 A permanent solution to the flickering problem would rely on redesigning the power supply for
 the illumination system, either replacing buck converters, changing battery voltage, or changing
 the electrical input requirements of the LEDs themselves.

 Automation of image collection

 Images are collected by each PLatypus camera node and need to be collected in a central location
 for processing. The original prototype was configured to transfer each image to a shared storage
 device, where a controller could access them for processing. This transfer added about five
 seconds to each image acquisition. In this CRADA, we investigated the source of this delay. We
 ruled out wireless communication and the speed of the storage medium as bottlenecks. We
 identified the speed of the storage controller, built into a portable network router, as the
 bottleneck. By transferring images directly to the supervisory computer, in this case a laptop
 computer, we avoided this bottleneck and shortened the acquisition process by several seconds.

 Mechanical improvements

 The original PLatypus design consists of eight nodes, arranged in a 1 m by 2 m grid to cover a 2
 m^2 PV module. Each module contains one camera, four high-power LEDs, and support
 electronics. During an assembly trial, we identified that this configuration, pictured below, was
 too heavy to be practically used with high throughput.

 3

 Reducing the weight of a future prototype is a priority for a future project. In this CRADA, we
 developed a temporary solution. We modified the existing prototype to operate with half of the
 modules, in a 0.5 m by 2 m grid, as shown below.

 4

 At the cost of cutting throughput in half, this cut the weight in half. It required fabricating
 adjustable metal legs to replace the original fixed-position cellulose legs and fabricating half-
 length wheel bars. These elements are visible below.

 We also identified that the approach angle when PLatypus is first mounted can sometimes cause
 metal parts to contact the face of the module. We added wheels at the top of the structure, visible
 in the photo above, to eliminate the possibility of scraping the module during the approach.

 Camera size reduction

 The original PLatypus prototype is based on the Raspberry Pi HQ camera. This is the
 configuration used for the final Duramat-funded field trial, pictured below (photo by Werner
 Slocum / NLR).

 In this CRADA, we investigated switching to the Raspberry Pi camera module 3. This is a much
 smaller camera with integral lens, eliminating substantial weight and complexity. We designed
 and fabricated a mounting structure for testing camera module 3 inside the original PLatypus
 structure, shown below.

 7

 Unlike the HQ camera, the camera module 3 cannot be easily disassembled to insert a longpass
 filter. The fixture shown above includes a mount to place the original longpass filter outside of
 the lens. But because this interference filter has a strong angular dependence, we found this
 configuration was inadequate. In this CRADA we developed a bonded-filter design, where the
 interference filter alone is replaced with the interference filter bonded to a new colored glass
 filter. This configuration is shown below and had satisfactory spectral and angular performance
 to show that the camera module 3 is a viable replacement for the HQ camera.

 8

 Illumination design study

 A major contributor to the weight of the original PLatypus device is optics and heat removal
 hardware for the illumination LEDs. The camera module 3, discussed above, will allow for
 reduced working distance, making the entire platypus assembly lower-profile when used in the
 field. Reduced working distance makes the existing LED optics unnecessary. In this CRADA,
 we tested PL imaging at shorter working distance with bare LEDs. An example image with a
 bare configuration of 625 nm (red) LEDs, is shown below.

 9

 This configuration eliminates glass optics on the LEDs, representing a major weight savings
 compared to the original prototype. But the image suffers from weak PL emission and strong
 specular reflections of the unfiltered LEDs, problems we address below. Similar problems
 occurred when we tried 525 nm (green) LEDs.

 We explored further weight reduction potential by mounting LEDs directly to an aluminum
 plate, eliminating the original prototype’s heavy finned aluminum heatsink and fan on each LED.
 We monitored the heatsink temperature during a series of rapid-fire PL exposures. Even without
 forced convection, the temperature did not exceed 65°C, as shown in the upper-right spot
 measurement in the image below. This shows that a planar heatsink may be a viable lightweight
 replacement for a future prototype.

 10

 We considered making the heat load on the planar heatsink more uniform, further reducing its
 peak temperature. This is possible by replacing the original prototype’s four high-power LEDs
 with a larger number of lower-power LEDs. The original prototype used a shortpass filter in
 front of each LED to prevent its infrared tail from interfering with the PL signal of interest. With
 a large number of low-power LEDs, individual shortpass filters are no longer practical. We
 considered using a large-area shortpass filter, a panel of low-emissivity glass, but its stopband
 performance was inadequate.

 We evaluated various wavelengths of LEDs to identify one where PL performance was adequate
 even when unfiltered. While 625 nm (red) and 525 nm (green) LEDs had the problems shown
 above, 470 nm (blue) LEDs showed promising results, with strong PL signal and only a faint
 specular reflection of the LEDs when unfiltered (example image below).
