Construction of a risk model based on folate metabolism-related genes to predict prognosis and immunological characteristics of stomach adenocarcinoma
Authors: Liu He, Lilei Zhuang, Shenbao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the substantial disease burden of stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD)—the dominant and lethal subtype of gastric cancer—and the pivotal role of folate metabolism reprogramming in malignancy and treatment response, this study aimed to construct a prognostic model utilizing folate metabolism-related genes (FMRGs). Such a model is urgently required to supplement traditional TNM staging and to guide personalized precision medicine.MethodsInterrogation of TCGA and GEO transcriptomic profiles enabled the identification of differentially expressed FMRGs. These genes subsequently facilitated the construction of a prognostic signature via LASSO-Cox regression, which was then subjected to external validation. We further evaluated the clinical relevance of this risk signature by exploring its correlations with the tumor immune microenvironment, immunotherapy efficacy, and drug sensitivity, employing CIBERSORT and ssGSEA analytical frameworks.ResultsA five-gene prognostic model established from 220 FMRGs demonstrated moderate prognostic performance in identifying high-risk stomach adenocarcinoma patients with poorer survival. The high-risk group exhibited immunosuppressive microenvironments with stromal activation, while the low-risk group demonstrated “hot” tumor phenotypes characterized by higher immunogenicity and superior responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy. Consequently, this model serves as a robust independent prognostic indicator.ConclusionThis five-gene folate metabolism signature shows moderate prognostic value in STAD and may help inform future investigations of the tumor immune microenvironment and therapeutic stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05648-z.Keywords: Stomach adenocarcinoma, Folate metabolism-related genes, Immunological characteristics, Risk model

Introduction

Histopathologically, stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) is defined as a neoplasm derived from the gland-forming epithelial cells of the gastric mucosa. Accounting for more than 90% of all cases, it holds an overwhelmingly dominant position among gastric cancer pathological types, hence serving as the most common and representative form [1, 2]. Globally, its disease burden is substantial. According to data from the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research on Cancer, gastric cancer was the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer death worldwide in 2020, with the vast majority of these cases being STAD [3]. The incidence of this disease shows significant geographical variation, with high-incidence areas including East Asia (particularly China, Japan, and South Korea), Eastern Europe, and parts of South America [4]. This distribution is closely related to factors such as dietary patterns and Helicobacter pylori infection rates [5, 6]. In terms of disease progression, the early symptoms of STAD are very subtle, potentially manifesting only as indigestion or mild upper abdominal discomfort, and are easily overlooked. Consequently, a significant proportion of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, missing the optimal window for treatment [7]. Treatment and prognosis are highly dependent on pathological staging. Early-stage STAD holds the potential for cure through endoscopic resection or surgical intervention, while advanced cases require a comprehensive strategy primarily based on systemic drug therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy), with an overall relatively poor prognosis [8–10]. While genetic predisposition constitutes a significant determinant of patient outcomes [11, 12], the distinct molecular profiles of tumors underlie the heterogeneity observed in their responses to therapeutic agents [13].Therefore, the traditional TNM staging system alone is increasingly insufficient to meet the demands of precision medicine, creating an urgent need to uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms of STAD.

Folate serves as a core carrier of intracellular one-carbon metabolism, playing an irreplaceable role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [14, 15]. Primarily by mediating the transfer of one-carbon units, it drives the de novo synthesis of purines and thymidylate, providing essential nucleotide precursors for DNA replication and cell proliferation, while also regulating the generation of S-adenosylmethionine to maintain the stability of epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation [16–18]. Given that tumor cells not only require large amounts of nucleotides to sustain their uncontrolled proliferation but also rely on NADPH generated by one-carbon metabolism to counteract oxidative stress, the reprogramming of the folate metabolic network is considered a hallmark event in cancer development [19, 20]. In the pathological progression of STAD, dysregulation of this pathway is particularly prominent. On one hand, insufficient folate intake or functional polymorphisms in key metabolic enzymes (such as MTHFR) can lead to genomic DNA hypomethylation in gastric mucosal cells, subsequently inducing genomic instability and activating oncogenic signaling, thereby promoting tumorigenesis [21]. On the other hand, established STAD tissues often exhibit a high dependency on folate metabolic enzymes. This not only correlates closely with aggressive tumor progression and poor prognosis but also constitutes the pharmacological basis for antifolate chemotherapeutic agents, such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), in clinical practice [22, 23].

Given the critical role of folate metabolism in tumor progression and its clinical translational potential in STAD, this study constructed and validated a prognostic model for STAD based on folate metabolism-related genes (FMRGs) using large-scale transcriptomic data. In recent years, transcriptome-based prognostic signatures have been widely developed in solid tumors, highlighting the feasibility of omics-driven risk modeling for survival stratification and biological interpretation. For example, a recent LUAD study constructed a novel prognostic model based on sialylation-related lncRNAs, further supporting the value of transcriptomic signatures in cancer prognosis research [24]. The study not only assessed the independent prognostic value of the model but also systematically elucidated the associations between risk scores and the tumor immune microenvironment, response to immunotherapy, and sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs. Collectively, the research aims to provide a scientific basis for risk stratification management and the development of precise treatment strategies for STAD patients through this comprehensive scoring system.

Materials and methods

Data download

Transcriptomic RNA-Seq data for STAD (375 tumors, 32 normals) and corresponding clinical information with copy number variation data were acquired from UCSC Xena (TCGA-STAD cohort) and TCGA GDC Data Portal (project ID: TCGA-STAD), respectively, forming the training cohort after excluding patients without survival data. For independent validation, microarray data GSE15459 (192 tumors) were obtained from the GEO accession GSE15459. Additionally, 2265 FMRGs were retrieved from the GeneCards Human Gene Database by searching the keyword‘folate metabolism’, and no further filtering was applied.

Analysis of FMRGs

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), transcriptomic profiles of normal and STAD tumor samples were compared using the “limma” package, with a |logFC| > 1 and adj.P.Val < 0.05 set as significance cutoffs. The intersection of DEGs and FMRGs yielded differentially expressed FMRGs (DEFMRGs). These DEFMRGs were subsequently subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the “clusterProfiler” package for GO and KEGG pathways. After excluding samples with survival times < 30 days from the clinical data to minimize the influence of very early deaths and potential non-tumor-related confounding factors, univariate Cox regression (P < 0.05) was performed via the “survival” package to pinpoint prognosis-related DEFMRGs. For these significant genes, their expression levels in normal versus tumor groups were compared, and intergenic correlations were computed. Finally, their precise chromosomal locations were visualized using the “RCircos” package.

Screening prognostic-related features to construct a prognostic model

Leveraging genes identified from univariate screening (P < 0.05), we applied LASSO regression to refine the gene set and construct a parsimonious prognostic model. To mitigate overfitting, candidate genes were subjected to LASSO regression via the “glmnet” package, where an optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was selected through cross-validation to eliminate redundant features and simplify the model. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression (“survival” package) was applied to these filtered genes to establish the final model. A risk score for each patient was computed by combining the expression levels of the model’s genes with their respective regression coefficients. Using the median score as a cutoff, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. For both the training and validation sets, we generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves, time-dependent ROC curves (with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUC values), and visualizations of risk score distribution and survival status. Additionally, the expression patterns of the characteristic genes were assessed through box plots comparing tumor vs. normal tissues and high- vs. low-risk groups, and their individual prognostic impact was illustrated via K-M curves in the training cohort.

Enrichment analysis

Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on individual risk scores calculated from the prognostic model, using the median score as the threshold. Subsequently, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, version 4.3.2) was employed to functionally characterize this classification. In addition, a differential expression analysis was carried out between the risk groups. The biological relevance of the differentially expressed genes was then interrogated via GO and KEGG enrichment analyses implemented in the “clusterProfiler” package.

Independent prognostic analysis

We first conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to determine whether the prognostic risk score, alongside clinical variables, held independent predictive value for survival, presenting the results in forest plots. We then proceeded to develop a comprehensive nomogram (using the “rms” package) to visually predict patient survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years. The nomogram underwent rigorous validation via calibration curves, along with a decision curve analysis (employing the “ggDCA” package) to evaluate its clinical utility. These analyses collectively tested the model’s suitability as an independent prognostic factor.

Risk model analysis based on clinicopathological characteristics

To assess the association between the prognostic risk score and clinical features in STAD patients, correlation analyses were conducted and visualized using violin plots across relevant clinical subgroups. Patients were stratified based on their clinical characteristics as well as their high- or low-risk classification. Specifically, subgroups were defined by age (≤ 65 vs. >65 years), gender, tumor stage (I-II vs. III-IV), T stage (T1-2 vs. T3-4), N stage (N0 vs. N1-3), and M stage (M0 vs. M1). Subsequently, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed within each of these stratified subgroups to evaluate survival differences.

Immune landscape analysis

We profiled the tumor immune landscape in the training cohort using multiple computational approaches. First, ssGSEA was used to generate infiltration scores for 29 immune cell types and pathways, with differences between risk groups displayed in box plots. We then leveraged the “estimate” package to compute and compare microenvironment scores (Immune, Stromal, ESTIMATE) and Tumor Purity. Immune cell composition was further interrogated via the CIBERSORT algorithm, while differential expression of immune checkpoints was statistically tested and plotted. To assess predicted immunotherapy response, we obtained Immunophenoscore (IPS) data from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA, https://tcia.at) and computed TIDE scores (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), visualizing both as violin plots. Finally, we performed Pearson correlation analysis to explore associations between the model’s signature genes and immune cell fractions derived from CIBERSORT.

Prediction of immunotherapy response and analysis of tumor mutational burden

Given the central role of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in immunotherapy, we assessed our risk score’s ability to predict such therapeutic response. Transcriptomic and clinical data from the anti-PD-L1-treated IMvigor210 cohort were acquired via the “IMvigor210CoreBiologies” package. Applying our prognostic model, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. For TCGA-STAD samples, we calculated Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) scores from mutation data, comparing distributions between risk groups with violin plots. Additionally, the mutational landscape of the top 20 frequently mutated genes was statistically summarized and visualized using waterfall plots generated by the “maftools” package.

Drug sensitivity prediction

To discover novel therapeutic targets and agents, we leveraged the CellMiner database (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/) to identify targeted and anti-tumor drugs whose sensitivity profiles showed significant correlation with our prognostic signature genes. Subsequently, the “pRRophetic” package (tissueType = “all”, batchCorrect = “eb”, selection = 1, dataset = “cgp2016”) was employed to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of these drugs in the high- and low-risk patient groups. Lower predicted IC50 values signify greater potential drug efficacy.

Identification of subtypes based on folate metabolism-related model characteristic genes

Following consensus clustering of STAD samples based on the model’s characteristic genes using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package (maxK = 9, reps = 100, pItem = 0.8, pFeature = 1, clusterAlg = “km”, distance = “euclidean”, seed = 123456), survival analysis (“survival” package) and comparative expression visualization (box plots) were performed across the identified subtypes. Differential expression analysis between subtypes (criteria: |logFC| > 0.585, adj.P.Val < 0.05) identified DEGs, which were subsequently subjected to GO enrichment analysis via “clusterProfiler”. The tumor microenvironment of each subtype was then profiled using multiple approaches: the “estimate” package was used to calculate Immune, Stromal, ESTIMATE scores and Tumor Purity; ssGSEA assessed immune function and cell infiltration; and immune cell quantification was performed using both the CIBERSORT and MCP-counter (from the “IOBR” package) algorithms, with results displayed in comparative box plots.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all statistical computations and data visualizations using R (version 4.4.0). Group comparisons for continuous data employed the Wilcoxon test, whereas survival differences were analyzed via Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. We assessed correlations using either Spearman or Pearson tests. The median value was designated as the threshold for risk stratification. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with the following notation: * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), **** (P < 0.0001), and ‘ns’ for non-significant results (P > 0.05).

Results

Analysis of DEFMRGs in STAD patients

Comparative transcriptomic analysis between tumor and normal tissues revealed 1,149 DEGs (719 upregulated, 430 downregulated; Fig. 1A). Integration of these DEGs with the folate metabolism-related gene set yielded 220 DEFMRGs (Fig. 1B). Functional annotation of this core set showed significant enrichment in GO terms related to hemostasis and wound healing, and in KEGG pathways including the cell cycle and lipid metabolism (Fig. 1C-D). From these, univariate Cox regression (P < 0.05) screened 31 genes with prognostic significance (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table 1), whose differential expression was confirmed (Fig. 1F). The complex co-expression patterns and specific chromosomal locations of these 31 prognostic genes were further illustrated by a correlation heatmap and a circos plot, respectively (Fig. 1G-H).

Model construction commenced with the 31 prognosis-related genes from univariate analysis. Predictive accuracy of the model in the training cohort was shown by AUCs of 0.681, 0.734, and 0.710 at 1, 3, and 5 years, indicating good performance (Fig. 2D). Patients stratified into high-risk groups exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (Fig. 2E), a trend visually supported by risk score distribution plots showing increased mortality with higher scores (Fig. 2F). External validation using the GSE15459 dataset confirmed generalizability, yielding comparable AUCs (0.678, 0.697, 0.699) and replicating the adverse prognosis associated with high-risk stratification (Fig. 2G-I). Notably, all five signature genes were differentially expressed between tumor and normal tissues, with SERPINE1, RNF43, and F5 significantly upregulated in tumors (Fig. 2J). Consistent with this, their expression was also dysregulated between risk groups (Fig. 2K), and Kaplan-Meier analysis individually confirmed each gene’s significant correlation with patient prognosis (Fig. 2L).

Enrichment analysis of high- and low-risk groups

To elucidate the biological basis of the risk stratification, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This uncovered a distinct functional divergence: the high-risk group was predominantly associated with processes like Head Morphogenesis and Regulation of Cell Spreading, whereas the low-risk group showed enrichment in mitotic and RNA metabolic processes (Fig. 3A-B). To further characterize these differences, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO annotation pinpointed significant involvement in actin cytoskeleton organization and vascular development (Fig. 3C), while KEGG analysis confirmed enrichment in key pathways such as vascular smooth muscle contraction and muscle cell cytoskeleton (Fig. 3D). These findings collectively suggest that the prognostic model captures intrinsic differences related to cytoskeletal dynamics and the vascular microenvironment.

Analytical validation of prognostic independence

The prognostic signature derived from the five characteristic genes proved to be an accurate and reliable independent indicator for patient outcome assessment. This conclusion was supported by a comprehensive validation framework. Cox regression analyses, both univariate and multivariate, were performed with clinical covariates. riskScore emerged as a significant prognostic factor in univariate analysis alongside age and stage (Fig. 4A), a finding corroborated by multivariate analysis where riskScore remained independently significant (Fig. 4B). To generate a practical clinical tool, we constructed a nomogram that integrated the riskScore with key clinical variables, enabling the quantification of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 4C). The nomogram’s practical value was evidenced by high net benefits on Decision Curve Analysis (Fig. 4D) and excellent predictive calibration (Fig. 4E), underscoring its promising clinical applicability.

Subgroup analysis of the risk model based on clinical characteristics

To further explore the correlation between the riskScore and clinicopathological characteristics, we compared the distribution of riskScore across different clinical subgroups (Fig. 5A). Violin plot analysis revealed significant differences in riskScore across M stage, N stage, and overall clinical stage (Stage), whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in terms of age, gender, or T stage. Subsequently, to validate the applicability and stability of this prognostic model across diverse populations, we stratified patients based on clinical characteristics such as age (≤ 65 years vs. > 65 years), gender, Stage (I + II vs. III + IV), T stage (T1 + 2 vs. T3 + 4), N stage, and M stage, and performed Kaplan-Meier subgroup survival analyses (Fig. 5B). The results demonstrated that in the vast majority of clinical subgroups, patients in the high-risk group had significantly lower overall survival rates compared to those in the low-risk group. This confirms that the risk scoring model, as an independent prognostic factor, exhibiting robust predictive ability and generalizability across patient populations with varying clinical features. Immune analysis

To further investigate the relationship between risk scores and the characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) as well as the response to immunotherapy, we first evaluated immune cell infiltration and immune functions using the ssGSEA and CIBERSORT algorithms. The ssGSEA algorithm revealed that the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher levels of infiltration by mast cells, neutrophils, and helper T cells, while the low-risk group showed a greater abundance of NK cells and Th2 cells. In terms of immune-related functions, the high-risk group had significantly elevated scores for functions such as cytokine receptors, parainflammation, and Type II IFN Response. In contrast, the low-risk group demonstrated more active expression of functions related to APC co-inhibition and MHC class I (Fig. 6A). Further analysis using the ESTIMATE algorithm indicated that the high-risk group displayed significantly higher Stromal Scores and ESTIMATE Scores, along with lower tumor purity (Fig. 6B). The CIBERSORT algorithm showed that patients in the low-risk group exhibited significantly higher levels of infiltration by T cells CD4 memory activated and T cells follicular helper. Conversely, the tumor microenvironment of patients in the high-risk group had significantly increased abundances of monocytes and mast cells activated (Fig. 6C). Analysis of immune checkpoints revealed significant differential expression, including CD274, between risk groups (Fig. 6D). This immunogenic disparity was predictive of therapeutic response: the low-risk group exhibited significantly higher Immunophenoscore (IPS), suggesting greater potential benefit from immunotherapy (Fig. 6E). This prediction was validated in the IMvigor210 anti-PD-L1 cohort, where low-risk patients experienced significantly better survival (Fig. 6F) and a higher objective response rate (27.5% vs. 18.1%; Fig. 6G). Consistently, responders had lower transcriptomic risk scores than non-responders (Fig. 6H), and the low-risk group demonstrated lower TIDE scores, indicating a reduced likelihood of immune evasion (Fig. 6I). Collectively, these findings underscore the model’s clinical relevance, which is further elucidated by significant correlations between the five signature genes and immune cell infiltration levels (Fig. 6J).

Tumor mutation analysis and drug sensitivity analysis

To further investigate the genomic variation characteristics underlying different risk groups and potential drug treatment strategies, we first analyzed TMB based on TCGA data. A waterfall plot displayed the distribution of the top 20 most frequently mutated genes in the high- and low-risk groups, revealing higher mutation frequencies in genes such as TP53, TTN, and MUC16 (Fig. 7A). Further quantitative analysis indicated that patients in the low-risk group had significantly higher TMB levels than those in the high-risk group, which is generally associated with better responses to immunotherapy (Fig. 7B). In terms of drug sensitivity, analysis using the pRRophetic algorithm showed that the estimated IC50 values for 5-Fluorouracil, Cetuximab, Docetaxel, and Paclitaxel were significantly lower in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group, suggesting that low-risk patients are more sensitive to these conventional chemotherapeutic drugs (Fig. 7C). Additionally, gene-drug association analysis conducted using the CellMiner database further revealed significant correlations between the expression of characteristic genes and drug sensitivity. For example, high expression of GAMT was associated with low IC50 values (i.e., high sensitivity) to Paclitaxel, while the expression of RNF43 and F5 showed positive correlations with sensitivity to Cisplatin (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Table 4). These findings provide a potential biological basis for personalized precision medicine in clinical practice.

Subtype identification and immune analysis

To identify molecular subtypes, we employed the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package to perform consensus clustering on the training cohort, using the expression profiles of the five model characteristic genes. By integrating the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF), and Delta area curve, k = 2 was determined as the optimal number of clusters, dividing the patients into two distinct molecular subtypes (Group 1 and Group 2) (Fig. 8A-C). Although the CDF and Delta area curves suggested that k = 2, 3, and 4 were all plausible clustering solutions, k = 2 was ultimately selected because it showed better cluster stability and separation in the consensus matrix, whereas k = 3 and k = 4 exhibited relatively poor clustering performance (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Analysis of survival outcomes demonstrated a markedly poorer prognosis for Group 2 relative to Group 1 (Fig. 8D). Subsequent differential analysis (Fig. 8E) and GO enrichment analysis of the two subtypes showed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in biological pathways such as “regulation of integrin-mediated cell adhesion,” “chemokine activity,” and “digestive system process” (Fig. 8F). A distinct expression pattern was observed for the characteristic genes across subtypes. Notably, all genes except MTTP were expressed at higher levels in Group 2 (Fig. 8G). Further evaluation of tumor microenvironment characteristics revealed that Group 1, which had a better prognosis, displayed significantly higher Immune Scores and ESTIMATE Scores, along with lower tumor purity (Fig. 8H). The ssGSEA algorithm revealed that in terms of immune cells, Group 1 exhibited significantly higher infiltration levels of anti-tumor effector cells, including CD8 + T cells, B cells, NK cells, DCs, and TILs. Regarding immune functions, Group 1 also demonstrated a more active immune status, with significant upregulation in key immune response pathways such as cytolytic activity, checkpoint inhibition, MHC class I, and T cell co-stimulation (Fig. 8I). The CIBERSORT algorithm delineated contrasting immune landscapes: notably higher infiltration of CD8 + T cells and activated CD4 + memory T cells in Group 1, whereas Group 2 showed a predominance of resting CD4 + memory T cells and M0 macrophages (Fig. 8J). MCP-counter analysis further confirmed that Group 1, with a better prognosis, displayed significantly higher abundances of immune and stromal cell infiltration. Notably, the significant enrichment of anti-tumor effector cells, such as T cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, and NK cells, revealed a more active immune microenvironment in Group 1 (Fig. 8K).

Discussion

This study successfully constructed and validated a robust prognostic model based on folate metabolism-related genes, which may complement the traditional TNM staging system in individualized survival assessment. As a prognostic indicator independent of clinicopathological features in this retrospective cohort, the model not only showed moderate prognostic performance for patient survival outcomes but also provided preliminary insights into the intrinsic link between folate metabolism abnormalities and the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. By integrating immune profiling analysis and drug sensitivity prediction, this scoring system may provide a potential basis for identifying populations likely to benefit from immunotherapy and for exploring more precise chemotherapy regimens. However, its clinical utility and translational value still require further validation in large-scale prospective cohorts and real-world clinical studies.

Recent studies have increasingly employed pan-cancer analytical frameworks to identify prognostic and immune-related biomarkers, followed by validation in specific cancer types, highlighting the value of integrating large-scale genomic data with tumor-specific investigations [25–28]. Consistent with this research paradigm, our study systematically explored the prognostic significance, immune characteristics, immunotherapy response, and drug sensitivity associated with folate metabolism-related genes in STAD. Collectively, these findings further support the potential of metabolism-based molecular signatures for risk stratification and therapeutic guidance in cancer, while emphasizing the need for future prospective and experimental validation before clinical implementation.

This study identified five key genes (SERPINE1, MTTP, GAMT, RNF43, F5) that not only constitute a robust model for predicting poor prognosis in STAD, but also reveal the underlying mechanisms by which tumor cells drive malignant progression by remodeling folate metabolism networks and related signaling pathways. Among these genes, SERPINE1, MTTP, and GAMT are mainly associated with metabolic reprogramming and tumor-promoting signaling events. SERPINE1 (serpin family E member 1) is a key gene regulating the fibrinolytic system, extracellular matrix remodeling, and the tumor microenvironment [29]. High expression of SERPINE1 significantly enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion of gastric cancer cells, and promotes metastasis by regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related genes such as FN1, MMP2, and TIMP1 [30]. Furthermore, SERPINE1 continuously drives malignant phenotypes of tumor cells by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and the NFATC2/SERPINE1/JAK3/STAT3 feedback loop [31]. The MTTP (microsomal triglyceride transfer protein) gene encodes an endoplasmic reticulum-localized lipid transfer protein that catalyzes the transfer of triglycerides, phospholipids, and cholesteryl esters to nascent apolipoprotein B [32]. Compared to normal gastric tissue, both mRNA and protein levels of MTTP are generally upregulated in gastric cancer tissues, and high expression is associated with more advanced TNM stage [33].The GAMT (guanidinoacetate N-methyltransferase) gene encodes a key metabolic enzyme that plays an indispensable role in maintaining energy homeostasis in high-energy-demand tissues such as the brain and muscles [34]. Studies have shown that GAMT promotes gastric adenocarcinoma cell proliferation and invasion by maintaining intracellular redox and energy homeostasis and inhibiting disulfide death [35].

RNF43 and F5 are more closely linked to signaling regulation, genome stability, and immune modulation. RNF43 (Ring Finger Protein 43) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that negatively regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by ubiquitinating and degrading the Wnt receptor Frizzled, thereby maintaining stem cell homeostasis and suppressing tumorigenesis [36]. Research indicates that RNF43 enters the nucleus to ubiquitinate γH2AX, activating the DNA damage response; its loss of function leads to impaired DDR, genomic instability, and thus accelerates gastric carcinogenesis [6]. The F5 gene encodes a glycoprotein primarily synthesized in the liver and megakaryocytes [37]. Its activated form serves as the core cofactor of the prothrombinase complex, catalyzing factor Xa to convert prothrombin to thrombin, thereby amplifying the coagulation cascade [38]. Patients in the high F5 group have a significantly shorter overall survival [39]. Studies suggest that tumor cell-derived F5 in gastric cancer can inhibit CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and enhance metastatic capacity, while patients with low F5 expression respond better to neoadjuvant immunotherapy [40]. Therefore, the five key genes identified in this study not only construct a robust predictive model for poor prognosis in STAD, but also may be involved in biological processes related to STAD malignant progression, including metabolic reprogramming, signaling pathway dysregulation, microenvironment remodeling, and immune modulation.

We identified two contrasting immune phenotypes through a comparison of immune cell infiltration and function between the risk groups. Patients in the low-risk cohort exhibited an activated anti-tumor immune environment, which correlates with their improved clinical outcomes and greater therapeutic response. This favorable immune milieu is shaped by NK cells, which function by recognizing tumor cells with reduced MHC-I expression. Their anti-tumor action is dual: inducing apoptosis via cytotoxic mediators like perforin and granzymes, and secreting cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α to inhibit tumor proliferation [41]. Meanwhile, Tfh cells migrate to tumor TLS via CXCR5-PD-1, stimulate B cells through CD40L-CD40 binding and IL-21, and promote germinal center formation, leading to the production of high-affinity anti-tumor antibodies and memory B cells, thereby enhancing humoral immune responses [42]. The predominance of these cellular components suggests that the TME in the low-risk group possesses stronger immune surveillance and killing potential. The low-risk group also demonstrated higher activity in MHC class I molecules and APC co-stimulatory functions. Effective expression of MHC class I molecules is a prerequisite for CD8⁺ T cells to recognize tumor antigens [43]. Combined with the group’s higher IPS scores, this indicates that tumors in the low-risk group have higher immunogenicity, making them more easily recognized and attacked by the immune system, consistent with the characteristics of “hot tumors” [44].

The high-risk group exhibits pronounced immune suppression and stromal remodeling features, which are closely associated with its poor prognosis. Despite expressing higher levels of immune checkpoint molecules, the high-risk group shows limited responsiveness to immunotherapy, likely due to complex immune evasion mechanisms. The high-risk group demonstrates significant enrichment of mast cells, neutrophils, and monocytes. Neutrophils suppress the proliferation and function of CD8⁺ T cells by secreting reactive oxygen species, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [45]. Monocytes secrete factors such as Arg-1, IL-1β, and VEGF, which inhibit T cell function while promoting angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastasis [46]. These innate immune cells often adopt pro-tumor phenotypes within the tumor microenvironment. Importantly, the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy is influenced not only by immune-cell infiltration but also by complex interactions among cytokines, stromal components, and tumor-associated factors within the TME. Recent studies have demonstrated that immunosuppressive cytokine networks and stromal remodeling can restrict antitumor immune responses [47], while tumor-intrinsic programs, including cancer stem cell–related regulatory pathways, may further promote immune evasion and therapeutic resistance [48]. Therefore, the poor immunotherapy responsiveness observed in the high-risk group may result from the combined effects of immune suppression, stromal activation, and tumor-associated regulatory mechanisms within the TME rather than from immune checkpoint expression alone.

Notably, the immunologic consequences of folate-metabolism dysregulation appear to be context dependent rather than uniformly pro- or anti-tumorigenic. One-carbon/folate metabolism has been reported to support T-cell activation, differentiation, and effector function, suggesting a potential role in sustaining antitumor immunity in some settings [49]. However, other studies have shown that folate receptor β (FRβ) marks immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages/myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and tumor-specific activation of FRβ can further reinforce an immunosuppressive myeloid phenotype [50, 51]. In addition, a recent folate-metabolism-associated signature in colorectal cancer was linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and differences in immunotherapy response [52]. Taken together, these inconsistent findings suggest that the immune effects of folate metabolism vary by tumor type and cellular context. In STAD, our data indicate that the low-risk group is characterized by a more inflamed, hot-tumor-like immune phenotype, whereas the high-risk group exhibits a more immunosuppressive microenvironment.

This study has several limitations, primarily due to its foundation in retrospective data from public databases (TCGA and GEO). The model achieved acceptable prognostic performance; however, the AUC values were only moderate, indicating room for further improvement. This may reflect the retrospective nature of the study, cohort heterogeneity across public databases, and the limited number of prognostic features included. Incorporating multi-omics data and more comprehensive clinical variables may further optimize model performance and improve its clinical utility. In addition, although we screened multiple publicly available GEO cohorts for external validation, only GSE15459 yielded a stable and statistically significant validation result consistent with the training cohort. Therefore, future studies are warranted to validate the model in large-scale prospective clinical cohorts, thereby excluding potential data selection bias and cohort heterogeneity. Moreover, the immunotherapy-related analysis was validated only in the public IMvigor210 cohort of metastatic urothelial carcinoma; therefore, this represents a cross-cancer exploratory validation rather than disease-specific confirmation in STAD, and the generalizability of the immunotherapy findings remains to be further verified in real-world STAD immunotherapy cohorts. Another limitation is that the study only predicted associations between the characteristic genes and the immune microenvironment as well as drug sensitivity through bioinformatics algorithms. Wet-lab experiments, such as in vitro cell assays or in vivo animal models, have not yet been conducted to validate the specific biological functions and molecular mechanisms of these five key genes. Future studies should therefore include cell-based drug-response assays, such as viability and apoptosis assays after treatment with the predicted compounds, to experimentally validate the drug sensitivity findings. Accordingly, the clinical translational potential and precise molecular mechanisms of this model require further confirmation through future multicenter clinical studies and foundational experiments. In particular, qRT-PCR and Western blotting can be used to verify their expression patterns, while loss-of-function and gain-of-function assays can assess their effects on cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion. In vivo studies and mechanistic investigations will also be necessary to clarify their roles in disease progression and in shaping the immune microenvironment.

Conclusion

The five-gene folate metabolism risk signature developed herein provides a predictive tool for patient prognosis and is associated with tumor immune microenvironment dynamics and drug sensitivity. This model may offer a potential framework for risk stratification and for exploring individualized immunotherapy or chemotherapy strategies; however, its clinical utility requires further validation in prospective cohorts and real-world clinical settings.
