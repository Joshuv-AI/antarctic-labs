Identification of the R2R3-MYB gene family in wild jujube (Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa) and analysis of its expression under drought stress
Authors: Yuejia Liu, Kexin Zhai, Xin Zhao, Hongrui Zhang, Rui Liang, Shengjun Dong, Jingli Yang, Quangang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundR2R3-MYB gene family serves as a pivotal regulatory factor in plant growth, development, and responses to environmental stresses. To investigate its function in the drought stress response of wild jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill. var. spinosa), a typical eco-economic forest species, this study performed genome-wide identification and relevant analyses of R2R3-MYB genes.ResultsA total of 91 R2R3-MYB genes (designated as ZjMYB1 to ZjMYB91) were identified, which were unevenly distributed across 12 chromosomes. These genes mainly encode hydrophilic and unstable proteins, 97.8% of which are localized in the nucleus. Phylogenetic analysis classified these genes into 25 clades, showing evolutionary conservation and species-specific divergence with the R2R3-MYB protein family. The expansion of the ZjMYB family is mainly characterized by segmental duplication, and all duplicated gene pairs have undergone purifying selection. ZjMYBs are widely involved in plant growth and development as well as abiotic stress responses, with the highest expression level particularly in leaf tissues; a total of 13 genes were specifically annotated as water deficit response-related genes in drought stress and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways. Integrating the above analyses together with transcriptome data and qRT-PCR validation results revealed that ZjMYB5, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57 and ZjMYB85 function as core drought-responsive genes, which display both tissue-specific and time-dependent expression patterns under drought stress.ConclusionsThis study systematically elucidated the functional characteristics and regulatory network of the R2R3-MYB gene family in wild jujube, providing critical genetic resources and a theoretical basis for dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance in wild jujube and breeding drought-resistant cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09609-0.

Background

In recent years, the problem of climate change has become increasingly severe, with extreme weather events occurring frequently, and drought has emerged as a key abiotic stress factor restricting plant growth and development, attracting extensive attention from the academic community [1]. Drought stress can induce cell dehydration and plasmolysis in plants, damage the structural stability of cell membranes and enzyme systems, further trigger metabolic disorders in plants, and ultimately alter their growth and development trajectories [2, 3]. To resist the damage caused by adverse environments such as drought, plants have evolved multiple defense mechanisms during the long evolutionary process. They can alleviate the adverse effects of drought stress through physiological approaches such as promoting root growth, increasing the root-shoot ratio, degrading photosynthetic pigments, reducing photosynthetic efficiency, and slowing down plant growth rates [4]. Meanwhile, plants can achieve precise responses at the molecular regulatory level by inducing or repressing the expression of relevant genes, which include both functional genes directly involved in drought stress responses and transcription factors that regulate their expression levels; these two types of genes jointly constitute the molecular regulatory network for plant drought resistance [5].

Transcription factors (TFs) are a class of regulatory proteins that bind to cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of target genes to activate or repress the transcription of specific genes. As core regulators of gene expression, they are widely involved in various biological processes and stress responses in plants [6, 7]. According to the characteristics of their DNA-binding domains, TFs can be divided into multiple gene families, among which the MYB gene family is one of the largest TF families in plants [8]. The proteins encoded by this family contain a highly conserved MYB DNA-binding domain, which consists of 1 ~ 4 imperfect repeat sequences. Based on this, MYB proteins can be classified into four subfamilies: 1R-MYB/MYB-related, 2R-MYB/R2R3-MYB, 3R-MYB/R1R2R3-MYB and 4R-MYB, with the R2R3-MYB subfamily being the most abundant and widely distributed type in plants [9]. With the development of plant genome sequencing technology, researchers have completed genome-wide identification of the MYB gene family in various plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, rice, watermelon, and potato, and found consistent conclusions [10–13].

Previous studies have confirmed that plant R2R3-MYB genes are not only involved in the regulation of specific physiological processes such as growth and development and secondary metabolism, but also play a key role in abiotic stress responses [14]. Many genes can enhance plant tolerance to drought, low temperature, and salt stress by regulating plant architecture, ion homeostasis, reactive oxygen species balance and other pathways [15]. Multiple R2R3-MYB subgroups in A. thaliana have been proven to be associated with abiotic stress responses [11]. Under drought conditions, AtMYB2, AtMYB44, AtMYB96 and AtMYB15 can activate the transcription of dehydration response genes or inhibit the expression of PP2C through ABA-dependent pathways, thereby improving plant drought resistance [16]. The stress-resistant functions of R2R3-MYB genes have also been successively elucidated in economic crops such as apple [17], cotton [18], soybean [19], and tomato [20], indicating that R2R3-MYB genes have conserved and diverse regulatory functions in plant abiotic stress responses.

Wild jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill. var. spinosa), also known as sour jujube is an endemic species of China belonging to the genus Ziziphus in the Rhamnaceae family, and is also the primitive ancestral species of common jujube (Ziziphus jujuba). It has extremely high edible, medicinal and ecological values. Its fruits, flowers, leaves and stone shells all have important development and utilization potential, and its mature seeds, commonly known as Suanzaoren, are a precious traditional Chinese medicinal herb widely used in disease treatment and functional food development [21]. At the same time, wild jujube has extremely strong adaptability to adverse environments, especially showing prominent drought and barren soil tolerance [4]. It is a preferred tree species for afforestation of barren hills, windbreak and sand fixation, and soil and water conservation, and is also often used as root stock for grafting and seedling raising of common jujube trees, occupying an important position in jujube cultivation and ecological restoration [22]. As a typical drought-tolerant woody plant, the molecular mechanism of drought stress response in wild jujube has not been systematically elucidated, and the regulatory role and molecular mechanism of its MYB gene family, especially the R2R3-MYB, in drought stress responses are still lack of in-depth research.

In this study, genome-wide identification and drought-resistance functional analysis of the MYB gene family in wild jujube were performed, and key R2R3-MYB genes involved in drought stress responses were identified. This work is of great significance for revealing the molecular basis of drought tolerance in wild jujube, enriching the gene regulatory networks underlying drought stress responses in woody plants, and providing valuable candidate gene resources for stress-resistance molecular breeding and germplasm improvement of jujube.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of R2R3-MYB genes in Z. jujuba var. spinosa

The R2R3-MYB gene family of wild jujube was identified via a combination of 126 A. thaliana R2R3-MYB protein sequences, conserved MYB-binding domain motifs, and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) search with the PF00249 domain. A total of 91 R2R3-MYB genes were identified in the wild jujube genome and designated as ZjMYB1-ZjMYB91 according to their chromosomal localization order.

Physicochemical property analysis of the 91 ZjMYBs revealed significant sequence and structural diversity (Table S1). The amino acid lengths ranged from 194 (ZjMYB38) to 1,059 (ZjMYB51), with theoretical isoelectric points (pI) spanning 4.34 (ZjMYB9) to 9.74 (ZjMYB58); among them, 59 proteins were acidic (pI < 7) and 32 were basic (pI > 7). The relative molecular weights varied from 22.0 kDa (ZjMYB38) to 116.39 kDa (ZjMYB51). All ZjMYBs had a grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) value < 0 and a lipid index below 100, confirming their hydrophilic nature, with ZjMYB53 being the most hydrophilic (GRAVY = -1.10). Based on the instability index, only ZjMYB40 and ZjMYB74 (index < 40) were predicted as stable proteins, while the remaining 89 ZjMYBs showed high instability (index > 40). Subcellular localization prediction indicated that 89 ZjMYBs were localized to the nucleus, and only ZjMYB45 and ZjMYB85 were targeted to chloroplasts, consistent with the core functional characteristics of transcription factors in regulating nuclear gene expression (Table S1).

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic classification of ZjMYBs

Multiple sequence alignment of the 91 ZjMYBs confirmed the typical R2R3 structural domain characteristic of the R2R3-MYB subfamily, with highly conserved tryptophan (W) residues in the repeat regions (WebLogo analysis). The R2 repeat contained three conserved W residues, while in the R3 repeat, the first W residue was frequently substituted by hydrophobic amino acids (phenylalanine F, isoleucine I, leucine L), and the second and third W residues remained highly conserved. The conserved W residues marking the R2R3 domain were labeled with yellow stars, and the variable first W residue site in the R3 repeat was labeled with colorless stars in the WebLogo diagram (Fig. 1).

Chromosomal localization, replication events and synteny analysis of ZjMYBs

Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that the 91 ZjMYBs were distributed highly unevenly across the 12 chromosomes of wild jujube (Fig. 3). Chromosome 5 harbored the largest number of ZjMYBs, with 13 members accounting for approximately 14.3% of the entire gene family. In striking contrast, several chromosomes carried only a small number of family members, indicating a pronounced distribution bias. Further gene density analysis showed that ZjMYBs were mainly concentrated in chromosomal regions with high overall gene density. Together, these observations illustrate a non-random and biased distribution pattern of the ZjMYB family in the wild jujube genome.

Gene duplication patterns, including tandem duplication and segmental duplication, were systematically characterized across all ZjMYB genes of wild jujube using the standard MCScanX analytical pipeline (Fig. 4). After comprehensive pairwise collinearity scanning and strict parameter filtering, we identified only one tandem duplicated gene pair (ZjMYB55 and ZjMYB56) within the ZjMYB gene family. Besides this tandem duplication pair, a total of 17 independent segmental duplication events distributed on distinct chromosomes were uncovered, covering 29 ZjMYB family members in total. These collinearity results clearly illustrated that the scattered gene clusters in the wild jujube genome originated from diverse gene duplication events at different evolutionary stages. During the long-term evolutionary process of the ZjMYB gene family, tandem duplication events occurred at a very low frequency and barely contributed to gene family expansion. By contrast, widespread segmental duplication events dominated the genome and acted as the major driving force promoting the expansion and functional differentiation of the ZjMYB gene family.

To evaluate the selection pressure acting on homologous ZjMYB genes, the synonymous substitution rate (Ks), non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka), and Ka/Ks ratio of each duplicated gene pair were calculated. The Ka/Ks values of segmental duplicated gene pairs ranged from 0.041 to 0.403 (Table S2). Excluding six homologous gene pairs for which valid Ka/Ks values were unavailable, the Ka/Ks ratios of all remaining homologous gene pairs were less than 1, demonstrating that these gene pairs underwent purifying selection.

To explore the evolutionary relationships of the ZjMYB gene family, we performed synteny analysis between wild jujube (Z. jujuba var. spinosa) and four representative plant species, including the model plant A. thaliana, the woody plant Populus trichocarpa, and two closely related Prunus species (Prunus sorotina and Prunus salicina), as well as cultivated jujube (Z. jujuba) (Fig. 5).

The results revealed extensive collinearity between the genomes of wild jujube and cultivated jujube, with a total of 132 collinear ZjMYB gene pairs identified and a high degree of conserved syntenic blocks across all chromosomes, indicating a close evolutionary relationship and limited genomic rearrangement between the two jujube types. Wild jujube shared the largest number of collinear gene pairs with P. sorotina (232 pairs), followed by P. trichocarpa (202 pairs) and P. salicina (124 pairs). By contrast, the collinearity between wild jujube and A. thaliana was relatively limited, with only 98 syntenic gene pairs detected, reflecting their distant phylogenetic relationship. These findings suggest that wild jujube shares the closest evolutionary relationship with cultivated jujube, followed by P. sorotina, P. trichocarpa and P. salicina, while it is more distantly related to A. thaliana. Notably, several ZjMYBs were located in conserved syntenic blocks across different species, implying that these genes may have retained conserved functions during evolution. Collectively, the synteny analysis provides valuable insights into the evolutionary history and conservation of the MYB gene family in wild jujube.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of ZjMYBs

Exon-intron structure analysis revealed substantial structural diversity across the ZjMYB family (Fig. 6A). Several genes clustered on the same branch had entirely intron coding sequences, while most other members exhibited canonical exon-intron alternating architectures. Across the family, exon counts ranged from 1 to 8, with three-exon structures representing the most prevalent configuration.

Conserved motif analysis using MEME identified a suite of conserved motifs in ZjMYB proteins (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. S1). Motif 1, Motif 2, Motif 3, and Motif 4 were widely distributed across most family members. Notably, the universal presence of Motif 1 and Motif 2 in all identified ZjMYBs underscored the high evolutionary conservation of core structural features within the family.

Conserved domain analysis further confirmed that Motif 1, Motif 2, and Motif 3 constitute the signature motifs of the ZjMYB family (Fig. 6C). Individual genes exhibited distinct motif combinations, reflecting functional divergence among different family members.

Cis-acting element and gene ontology (GO) annotation analysis of ZjMYBs

To investigate the regulatory characteristics of the ZjMYBs, cis-acting elements in the 2000 bp promoter region upstream of the coding sequences of ZjMYBs were predicted using the Plant CARE database (Fig. 7, Table S3). The identified cis-acting elements were categorized into three groups: those related to plant growth and development, hormone responses, and abiotic stress responses. Focused analysis on abiotic stress-related elements revealed that all ZjMYBs promoters contained at least one abiotic stress response element, with ABA-responsive elements being the most distributed. This indicates that the ZjMYB gene family is mainly involved in regulating plant abiotic stress responses.

Among the hormone-responsive elements, 69 ZjMYBs contained 193 ABA-responsive elements, 63 contained 123 light-responsive elements, and 53 contained salicylic acid-responsive elements. 40 ZjMYBs harbored cold-responsive elements. Among the ZjMYBs containing drought-responsive (MBS) elements, ZjMYB46 possessed the largest number of MBS elements (3), followed by ZjMYB10, ZjMYB14, ZjMYB15, ZjMYB45, ZjMYB64 and ZjMYB82 (each containing 2 MBS elements). ZjMYB46 had the highest total number of cis-acting elements, and both ZjMYB42 and ZjMYB46 contained up to 10 types of cis-acting elements.

GO annotation analysis of 69 ZjMYBs via the EggNog database revealed clear functional annotations in three categories (cellular component, molecular function, biological process) (Fig. 8, Table S4). In the cellular component category, all 69 ZjMYBs were annotated to the nucleus, consistent with the subcellular localization prediction results. In the molecular function category, the main annotations were transcription regulation and nucleic acid binding, which is the core functional characteristic of transcription factors. In the biological process category, 42 ZjMYBs responded to endogenous stimuli (GO:0009719), 39 to xenobiotic stimulus (GO:0009410), and 18 to hormonal stimuli (GO:0032870).

Protein-Protein interaction (PPI) network prediction of ZjMYBs

To dissect the potential regulatory interactions among ZjMYB transcription factors in wild jujube, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) prediction network using the STRING database, based on the evolutionary homology between ZjMYB proteins and their orthologs in the model plant A. thaliana (Fig. 9, Table S5). All nodes in the network are labeled with their corresponding Arabidopsis gene names, facilitating functional inference based on published studies in Arabidopsis. Functional homology analysis revealed that several ZjMYB members share clear homology with well-characterized stress-responsive MYB transcription factors. Specifically, ZjMYB23 and ZjMYB42 show high sequence similarity to the drought-inducible gene AtMYB96, suggesting they may play analogous regulatory roles in drought signal transduction. Meanwhile, ZjMYB45 is homologous to the key ABA signaling component AtMYB44, implying its potential involvement in the ABA response pathway in wild jujube. Analysis of the overall network topology showed that AtMYB44 acts as the most highly connected hub node, with predicted interactions with 12 ZjMYB proteins, indicating its potential role as a critical regulatory hub in this network. These homology-based PPI predictions provide a preliminary framework for understanding how ZjMYB family members may form a complex regulatory network via protein interactions to collectively mediate drought stress responses in wild jujube.

Transcriptome analysis of ZjMYBs under drought and heat stress

Transcriptome data analysis of wild jujube under different durations of drought and heat stress revealed tissue-specific and time-dependent differential expression patterns of ZjMYBs (Fig. 10).

Under heat stress, ZjMYB13 showed a progressive upregulation pattern, with fold changes of 2.09, 3.17, 2.64, and 2.59 at 1 h, 3 h, 5 h, and 7 h of recovery, respectively, indicating its sustained activation in the early response to heat stress. Under drought stress, ZjMYB75 was strongly induced at multiple time points: its expression increased by 1.26-fold at 6 h, 1.29-fold at 12 h, 1.57-fold at 6 h under heat-drought combined stress, and peaked at 4.03-fold after 3 days of recovery, suggesting a critical role in both drought adaptation and post-stress recovery. Notably, ZjMYB53 exhibited a striking upregulation of 4.17-fold at 5 days of recovery, implying its potential function as a late-stage effector in stress adaptation. Conversely, some genes such as ZjMYB25 and ZjMYB69 showed significant downregulation (up to -9.01-fold and − 8.78-fold, respectively) at specific recovery stages, indicating their possible roles as negative regulators. Overall, these fold-change patterns reflect a dynamic and diverse regulatory network of ZjMYB family members responding to drought and heat stress in wild jujube.

Tissue expression pattern and drought stress response of ZjMYBs

Combined with transcriptome data, cis-acting element and promoter analyses, we screened 21 representative genes that are more sensitive to drought stress. The qRT-PCR was used to detect the expression characteristics of these candidate ZjMYB genes in roots, stems and leaves of wild jujube. The results showed that these genes exhibited obvious tissue-specific expression patterns (Fig. 11A).

The 21 ZjMYB genes exhibited divergent tissue expression profiles and were classified into three groups according to their organ-preferential expression patterns. The first group contained nine leaf-preferential genes (ZjMYB5, ZjMYB27, ZjMYB40, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57, ZjMYB83, ZjMYB84, ZjMYB85 and ZjMYB88). All of these genes displayed markedly higher transcript abundance in leaves than in other tissues, showing strong leaf tissue specificity. Specifically, the expression levels of ZjMYB5, ZjMYB27, ZjMYB40, ZjMYB53 and ZjMYB83 in leaves were 2.4-, 1.7-, 2.0-, 1.8- and 1.9-fold higher than those in roots, respectively, further verifying their outstanding leaf specificity. The second group included two root-dominant genes (ZjMYB64 and ZjMYB69). Among them, the transcript abundance of ZjMYB64 in roots was 20-fold higher than that in stems, reflecting an extremely strong root-specific expression trait. The third group consisted of two stem-preferential genes, ZjMYB56 and ZjMYB87. The expression level of ZjMYB87 in stems was 2.0-fold higher than that in roots.

Subsequently, pairwise correlation analysis was performed on the 21 ZjMYB genes (Fig. 11B). The correlation matrix revealed distinct co-expression patterns. Firstly, nine leaf-preferential genes (ZjMYB5, ZjMYB27, ZjMYB40, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57, ZjMYB83, ZjMYB84, ZjMYB85 and ZjMYB88) formed a large continuous dark-red block in the matrix, with pairwise correlation coefficients mostly ranging from 0.95 to 1.0, indicating a group of highly synergistic co-expressed genes. Secondly, two stem-dominant genes ZjMYB56 and ZjMYB87 constituted an independent red block with a strong positive correlation coefficient of 0.83 between them. Finally, of the two root-preferential genes (ZjMYB64 and ZjMYB69), ZjMYB64 showed the most striking antagonistic expression pattern, as it was negatively correlated with most of the other genes.

MYB transcription factors exert crucial regulatory functions in plant responses to drought stress. To characterize the expression patterns of ZjMYB genes potentially involved in drought response, qRT-PCR was performed to quantify the transcript levels of 21 ZjMYB genes at a series of drought treatment time points (0 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h and 24 h). Significant expression differences were detected among the 21 ZjMYB genes, and the majority of them exhibited high transcript abundance at 1 h and 24 h under drought stress (Fig. 12A). Among these genes, seven members (ZjMYB5, ZjMYB7, ZjMYB56, ZjMYB64, ZjMYB70, ZjMYB75 and ZjMYB83) were markedly upregulated at 1 h of drought treatment. On the other hand, six ZjMYB genes (ZjMYB4, ZjMYB5, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB70, ZjMYB83 and ZjMYB88) reached their expression peak at 24 h under drought stress. Notably, ZjMYB53 and ZjMYB85 showed the strongest induction after 24 h and 9 h of drought treatment, respectively, with their expression levels approximately 50-fold higher than those of the untreated control group.

Correlation analysis of gene expression at different drought stress time points showed that most ZjMYB genes were significantly positively correlated (Fig. 12B). ZjMYB53 and ZjMYB85 showed the strongest correlation with ZjMYB31, with a correlation coefficient of 0.99, while ZjMYB40 and ZjMYB84 showed the most notable negative correlation with a coefficient of − 0.73. These results reveal that these genes are coordinately or antagonistically expressed at different stages of drought stress. Notably, the cis-acting element prediction results of ZjMYB5 contained abundant ABRE cis-acting elements. Meanwhile, GO annotation results indicated that ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57 and ZjMYB85 participate in the regulatory process of stimulus response, demonstrating that these genes may play central roles in the drought resistance mechanism of Z. jujuba var. spinosa and can serve as candidate genes for subsequent gene function validation.

Discussion

TFs are a class of protein molecules that specifically bind to cis-acting elements in the promoter region and possess the function of regulating mRNA expression [23], playing a crucial role in modulating plant growth and development as well as stress responses [24, 25]. Based on differences in DNA-binding domains, transcription factors can be divided into multiple gene families, mainly including the MYB, AP2/ERF, EIN3, bZIP, bHLH, WRKY and NAC families [7]. Of these, the MYB family represents one of the most extensive and broadly distributed transcription factor families across the plant kingdom [26], and numerous studies have completed systematic analyses of the MYB family in different plant species at the whole-genome level [27]. As the most prevalent subgroup within the MYB family, R2R3-MYB proteins play crucial regulatory roles in plant growth, development, and stress tolerance, and this gene family has been characterized and investigated across numerous plant taxa [28]. In wild jujube, 91 ZjMYBs is fewer than the number reported in Arabidopsis (125) [29], pear (122) [30] and walnut (136) [31]. Although multiple tandem and segmental duplication events were identified in the ZjMYB family (typical driving forces for gene family expansion), the limited number of ZjMYBs may be due to the relatively small genome size of wild jujube and the loss of some duplicated genes during evolution. This indicates that the ZjMYB family has undergone moderate gene amplification events, but the amplification scale is smaller than that of other plant species. Greatly differences were observed in the amino acid sequence length, molecular weight and isoelectric point of ZjMYBs. The diverse physicochemical features suggest that these genes may function differently during plant growth and stress response. (Table.S1). All ZjMYBs exhibited positive GRAVY scores, suggesting that all were hydrophilic in nature. Such hydrophilic properties help maintain the stability of protein secondary and tertiary structures against deleterious alterations, while also safeguarding cellular constituents from damage induced by water-deficit conditions [32]. Within the ZjMYB family, ZjMYB51 has the longest coding sequence and largest molecular weight, which is distinctly higher than the family average. It is speculated that this gene may be involved in more diverse biological pathways and exert extensive regulatory effects in wild jujube. Approximately 97.8% of the ZjMYBs were predicted to localize to the nucleus, implying that they primarily function in nuclear transcriptional regulation. The remaining two ZjMYBs (ZjMYB45 and ZjMYB85) were predicted to be chloroplast-localized, indicating their potential involvement in modulating the transcription and expression of genes associated with chloroplast biogenesis or photosynthesis. A phylogenetic tree constructed based on the motif functions of wild jujube, the classification of the 126 AtMYB and the 91 ZjMYBs contained 25 subgroups (Fig. 2). TFs encoded by genes in the same subgroup may have similar structures and functions, and those with higher similarity in the phylogenetic clades also tend to have greater functional similarity [33]. In A. thaliana, AtMYB96 was found to be a key regulator of plant drought stress responses by integrating ABA and auxin signaling pathways [34]. In this study, Overexpression of TbA02G027080.1 in Taraxacum mongolicum enhances drought tolerance by increasing sensitivity to exogenous ABA [35], from which it can be inferred that ZjMYB27 (the ortholog of TbA02G027080.1) may also enhance drought tolerance through the same mechanism. In summary, these identified orthologs in A. thaliana may provide clues for the potential functions of R2R3-MYB genes in wild jujube. Meanwhile, we found that differences in conserved motifs between different subgroups imply that they may be involved in different biological processes.

Gene duplication drives functional differentiation of genes and profoundly promotes the expansion and evolutionary diversification of plant gene families [36, 37], and functions as a major driver of evolutionary innovation. In pear, 37 gene pairs were detected to have undergone segmental duplication events [12]. A study on strawberry also showed that the R2R3-MYB family had a high frequency of gene duplication events [13]. Segmental duplication events have also been proven to play a crucial role in the expansion of the MYB family in cucumber [38]. In this investigation, throughout the evolutionary process of the R2R3-MYB gene family in wild jujube (Fig. 4), 17 gene pairs were implicated in duplication events, among which 92% arose from segmental duplication. Furthermore, the Ka/Ks values for all duplicated gene pairs were determined to be below 1 (Table. S2). Collectively, these findings indicate that segmental duplication events likely constitute the primary driving force underlying the evolution of the ZjMYB gene family. All gene duplications occurred within the same subgroup. After segmental duplication, all transcription factors retained comparable structural features and motif organizations, enabling them to preserve conserved functions and execute analogous regulatory roles.

Inter-species synteny analysis facilitates the exploration of evolutionary connections and genetic homology across diverse plant species [39]. The synteny analysis in this study revealed key evolutionary features of the ZjMYB gene family in wild jujube. The high level of collinearity between the genomes of wild jujube and cultivated jujube indicates a relatively recent divergence, with the chromosomal locations and functions of the MYB gene family largely conserved during domestication. Wild jujube showed more collinear gene pairs with Prunus species (especially P. sorotina) but weaker collinearity with A. thaliana, which is consistent with their phylogenetic positions within Rosaceae and supports the evolutionary conservation of the ZjMYB gene family.

Conserved ZjMYB members located in cross-species syntenic blocks suggest that these genes may play core and stable regulatory roles in stress responses of woody plants, providing key candidates for subsequent functional validation. Collectively, these results provide fundamental data for understanding the origin and evolution of the MYB family in wild jujube, as well as evolutionary clues for deciphering its functions in drought stress responses. Multiple sequence alignment and WebLogo analysis revealed that the R2R3 domains of wild jujube MYB proteins were highly conserved. Three conserved tryptophan residues were stably distributed in the R2 domain. In the R3 domain, the first tryptophan residue was replaced by phenylalanine, isoleucine or leucine, which was consistent with the structural characteristics of R3 domains in other plant species [40]. Analysis of the gene structure of the MYB family in wild jujube indicated that the number of exons and introns contained in ZjMYBs ranged from 1 to 8 (Fig. 6A), All MYB of wild jujube contained the conserved Motif1 and Motif2, and a total of 20 conserved motifs were identified. Phylogenetic analysis divided the MYB members of wild jujube into 25 subgroups. Some ZjMYB members had similar distribution abundance and number of motifs, indicating the diversity and conservation of the ZjMYB family (Fig. 6B, S1). Domain analysis showed that ZjMYB members possessed six types of domains, and some members shared the same domains, suggesting the diversity and similarity of their functions. Many MYB are involved in hormone signaling to potentially regulate plant stress responses [41]. The upstream promoter regions of ZjMYBs contain numerous potential regulatory cis-elements responsive to ABA, with ABA-responsive elements being the most abundant. ABA has been proven to be a key regulator of plant responses to abiotic stresses [42]. In addition, cis-acting elements involved in MBS (MYB binding site for drought induction) were identified in 28 ZjMYBs (Fig. 7). GO annotation of ZjMYBs showed similar results (Fig. 8), with most genes annotated to be associated with ABA signaling.

From the results of protein-protein interaction prediction, AtMYB44 was found to have the most extensive interaction with ZjMYBs in this study (Fig. 9). Previous studies have demonstrated that AtMYB44 enhances abiotic stress tolerance in Arabidopsis by regulating endogenous abscisic acid (ABA) levels [43]. GO analysis of ZjMYB84 annotated it as a gene responsive to drought stress, indicating that this TF may also regulate the production of cuticular wax to resist external stresses.

Plants orchestrate the differential expression of numerous regulatory genes across distinct tissues and in response to various abiotic stresses, thereby constructing intricate regulatory networks [27]. Transcriptome sequencing serves as a powerful approach to quantify gene expression profiles and facilitate the identification of candidate functional genes [44]. Studies on A. thaliana have shown that AtMYB16 and AtMYB106 (orthologs of three ZjMYBs: ZjMYB24, ZjMYB40 and ZjMYB41) regulate cuticle formation in reproductive organs and trichomes, thereby protecting plants from external damage [45]. Transcriptome analysis in this study revealed that the expression of most ZjMYB genes exhibited varying degrees of changes at different time points under drought stress, indicating that these transcription factors may regulate plant cuticle biosynthesis to enhance drought resistance, which is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies [46].

Drought caused by uneven precipitation or excessively high temperatures can hinder the growth and development of wild jujube, thereby affecting the yield of wild jujube fruits and economic benefits [15]. This study explored the expression characteristics and potential regulatory relationships of the ZjMYB family in wild jujube under normal growth and drought conditions, which lays a certain theoretical foundation for further studying drought resistance mechanisms of wild jujube. In the present study, 95% of the selected ZjMYBs were upregulated under drought stress (Fig.12), which is similar to the results reported previously in A. thaliana [47] and apple [17]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the majority of family members are induced by drought stress. A previous study on poplar demonstrated that PeMYB119 was suggested to improve drought tolerance of seedlings under drought stress by regulating genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis or ABA biosynthesis [48]. In this study, the expression of ZjMYB5 and ZjMYB53, the homolog of PeMYB119, was rapidly induced in wild jujube following drought stress. Therefore, it can be hypothesized that ZjMYB5 and ZjMYB53 also enhances drought tolerance by regulating genes related to flavonoid biosynthesis or ABA biosynthesis. Functional analysis of grape rootstock genes confirmed that VhMYB15, could significantly enhance drought tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis through ABA-dependent stomatal closure and ROS scavenging pathways [49]. In the present study, ZjMYB57 (a homolog of VhMYB15), is speculated to improve drought resistance via ABA induction as well. A previous study on walnut demonstrated that JrMYB100, could improve drought tolerance of seedlings under drought stress by regulating genes involved in cuticular wax biosynthesis or ABA signal transduction [50]. In the present study, ZjMYB85 (an ortholog of JrMYB100) may also enhance drought resistance via modulating the expression of related functional genes. Thus, they will serve as important candidate genes for our further investigation into the drought resistance functions and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of ZjMYBs.

Notably, our study differs from the research by Qing et al. [51] in both research objects and focuses. Qing et al. focused on the MYB superfamily in cultivated jujube (Z. jujuba) and analyzed its expression during developmental stages, whereas this study focuses on the R2R3-MYB subfamily in wild jujube (Z. jujuba var. spinosa) and its expression under drought stress. A key advantage of our study is that we identified core drought-responsive ZjMYB genes (ZjMYB5, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57 and ZjMYB85) by integrating bioinformatics, transcriptome, and qRT-PCR technology, which provides a basis for breeding drought-tolerant wild jujube varieties.

Conclusion

The R2R3-MYB gene family is a key group of regulators involved in different tissues, and responses to abiotic stresses, among which drought stress is a major environmental constraint limiting the growth and yield of wild jujube. The main purpose of this study was to screen core candidate genes associated with drought resistance in wild jujube and provide a theoretical basis for its molecular breeding. To achieve this goal, the present study systematically identified R2R3-MYB family members in wild jujube and conducted a comprehensive analysis of their gene characteristics. Furthermore, we combined bioinformatics analysis, transcriptome sequencing and qRT-PCR assays to explore their expression patterns and potential functions in different tissues and under drought stress. This multi-dimensional analysis allowed us to preliminarily predict the potential functions of ZjMYBs in drought stress responses and ultimately identify four core candidate genes (ZjMYB5, ZjMYB53, ZjMYB57 and ZjMYB85) that may play pivotal roles in regulating drought resistance in wild jujube. In summary, our findings not only deepen the fundamental understanding of the functional characteristics of the R2R3-MYB gene family in wild jujube but also provide a theoretical framework for dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance. More importantly, the identified candidate genes serve as key targets for future functional validation and molecular breeding, laying a solid genetic foundation for developing new wild jujube varieties with enhanced drought resistance, high yield, and superior quality.

Method

Identification and physicochemical properties of R2R3-MYB genes in Z. jujuba var. spinosa

Known protein sequences of R2R3-MYB transcription factors from A. thaliana were retrieved from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR, http://www.arabidopsis.org/) database [52]. Using these sequences as queries, the online BLASTP program (E-value ≤ 1e − 5) was utilized to screen and identify R2R3-MYB family members within the wild jujube genome dataset [53]. After manually removing redundant protein sequences of wild jujube, candidate protein sequences were verified for the presence of complete MYB domains (PF00249) using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) [54]. Sequences meeting the criteria were designated as the final R2R3-MYB protein sequences, and these wild jujube R2R3-MYB genes were renamed ZjMYBs. The characteristics of ZjMYB protein sequences were analyzed using the online ExPASY tool (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/), while their subcellular localization was predicted with the CELLO program (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/) [55].

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic classification of ZjMYBs

MEGA software (version 11.0) and Jalview software (version 2.11) were employed to visualize the conserved domain sequences of ZjMYB proteins. The results of multiple sequence alignment were submitted to the WebLogo online tool (http://weblogo.berkeley.edu/logo.cgi) [56] to generate a sequence logo of the R2R3-MYB domain. ClustalX software (version 2.1) was used for multiple sequence alignment of the full-length R2R3-MYB protein sequences from wild jujube and A. thaliana. Subsequently, phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA software, where the neighbor-joining (NJ) algorithm was implemented with the following parameters: the Poisson correction model, pairwise deletion of gaps, and 1000 bootstrap replications to evaluate the statistical robustness of the resulting tree. The visualization of the phylogenetic tree was optimized using the iTOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/) [57]. Subfamily classification of ZjMYBs from wild jujube was performed with reference to the classification system of R2R3-MYB genes in A. thaliana.

Chromosomal localization, replication events and synteny analysis of ZjMYBs

sequences containing complete R2R3-MYB domains were retained and named ZjMYB1 to ZjMYB91 according to their chromosomal locations from top to bottom. TBtools software combined with the wild jujube genomic database was used to identify and map the chromosomal locations of ZjMYBs. The rates of nonsynonymous substitutions (Ka) and synonymous substitutions (Ks) among homologous gene pairs were quantified utilizing the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator integrated within TBtools software, thereby enabling the assessment of evolutionary selection pressures acting upon these gene pairs. Furthermore, the divergence time of candidate gene pairs was estimated according to the formula T = Ks/2r, wherein Ks denotes the number of synonymous substitutions per site, and the synonymous substitution rate (r) for dicotyledonous species was predefined as 1.5 × 10⁻⁸ substitutions per site per year [58, 59]. The GFF files and genomic data of A. thaliana (Assembly TAIR10, NCBI link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_000001735.4/), P. trichocarpa (Assembly v4.1, NCBI link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_000210455.5/), P. sorotina (Assembly Prunus_sorotina_v1.0, NCBI link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_963583255.1), P. salicina (Assembly P_salicina_v1.0, NCBI link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly) and Z.jujuba (Assembly ID: GCF_031755915.1 Link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_031755915.1) were all retrieved from the NCBI Genome Database. TBtools software was employed to construct syntenic analysis maps between wild jujube and other representative plants [60].

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of ZjMYBs

TBtools software was used to analyze the structural characteristics of ZjMYBs, and the conserved motifs of ZjMYBs were identified via the online tool MEME (http://meme-suite.org) [61]. Based on the full-length genomic DNA sequences of ZjMYBs, the sequence information of each ZjMYB was extracted using TBtools software.

Cis-acting element and GO annotation analysis of ZjMYBs

To identify putative cis-regulatory elements, the 2000 bp promoter fragments upstream of the ATG start codon of ZjMYBs were subjected to in silico analysis using the PlantCARE web server (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [62], and TBtools software was used to draw the distribution maps of these cis-acting elements. EggNOG database (http://eggnog5.embl.de/) was utilized for Gene Ontology (GO) annotation of ZjMYBs [63], and the annotation results were visualized with the online tool WeGo (https://wego.genomics.cn/) [64].

PPI network prediction of ZjMYBs

All ZjMYBs sequences were uploaded to the STRING database (Version 11.0, http://string-db.org) [65], with A. thaliana designated as the reference organism. Following BLAST sequence alignment, the A. thaliana orthologous genes with the optimal alignment scores were chosen to establish the protein-protein interaction network of ZjMYBs.

Transcriptome analysis of ZjMYBs under abiotic stress

Based on the identified ZjMYBs, transcriptome data from drought-treated (GEO: GSE175581) [66] and high-temperature-treated (GEO: GSE136047) [67] diploids and tetraploids of Z. jujuba were downloaded from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Chip plots were used to visualize differentially expressed levels, as well as the transcriptome data from wild jujube subjected to drought treatment (GEO: GSE180449) [68]. Chip plots were utilized to construct heatmaps for visualizing differential expression levels.

Tissue expression pattern and drought stress response of ZjMYBs

Experimental materials of wild jujube were collected from the National Forest Germplasm Resource Repository for Wild Jujube, Shenyang Agricultural University, which is located in Kazuo County, Chaoyang City, Liaoning Province, China (40°47′–41°33′ N, 119°24′–120°23′ E). One-year-old wild jujube seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots with specifications of 17 cm in height, 17 cm in top diameter and 12 cm in bottom diameter. The weighing method was adopted to control soil water content during drought stress treatment. Two groups were set up in the experiment, including the control group and the moderate drought stress group. The soil water content of the control group was maintained at 60%–80%, while that of the drought treatment group was kept at 40%–45%. Leaf samples were collected at 0 h (as the control), 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, and 24 h after the initiation of drought stress. Three biological replicates were established for both sampling and stress treatment processes. All samples were initially preserved in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Beijing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix Perfect Real Time (Takara, Beijing, China), and the resulting cDNA was stored at − 80 °C. Gene-specific primers were designed with Primer 5.0 software (Table S6) and synthesized by SYNBIO Technologies (Suzhou, China). The ZjActin of wild jujube was used as the internal reference gene [69]. QRT-PCR amplification was performed on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The QRT-PCR reaction conditions were set as follows: pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s. The 2−ΔΔCT method was employed to calculate the relative expression levels of ZjMYBs [70]. Finally, Origin 2024b software was used to perform correlation analysis on the expression levels among genes and generate the corresponding plots [71].
