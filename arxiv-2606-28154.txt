A Weakly Nonlinear Theory of Zonal-Flow Forcing in Gyrokinetic Turbulence
Georgia Acton, Eduardo Rodríguez, Gareth Roberg-Clark, Alessandro Zocco

Abstract
The forced generation of zonal flows by microinstability-driven turbulence is investigated within the framework of local gyrokinetic theory far from marginality. We use a numerically and physically informed three-wave truncation scheme, which allows the prediction of the zonal-flow k ψ k_{\psi} -spectrum during the early phase of nonlinear gyrokinetic simulations. The model reproduces the known 2 γ \gamma growth rate resulting from nonlinear beating of linearly unstable primary modes, in line with previous results, without any marginal stability point. The phase-space structure of such zonal flow is strongly constrained by that of the driving fluctuations, which is essential to understand its behaviour in the region of validity. It is shown that this leads to an enhanced residual spectrum compared to the classic Rosenbluth–Hinton calculation.

Introduction

Zonal flows (ZFs) have become a major subject of investigation in the field of magnetically confined fusion plasmas (see e.g.,

12

24

They are defined as self-organised, turbulence-driven flows that are largely constant over flux surfaces, while remaining radially sheared. For a magnetic field of the form

(
5

13

28
;
46
;
47

12
;
24

The seminal work of Rosenbluth and Hinton
(
41
)
remains a first simple reference point for understanding ZFs. It uses linear, radially local, electrostatic gyrokinetic theory
(
14

to analytically derive how much of an initial ZF remains at late times. What remains is the long-wavelength, non-Landau damped component, commonly referred to as the
ZF residual
. This remanent level is often used to characterise ZFs (and the quality of the magnetic field which supports it), partly because it measures how
“resilient”

29

36

49
;
31
;
43
;
36

However, one may ponder about the initial generation of ZFs. Two main generation mechanisms have been identified: generation through secondary (or modulational
(
44
)
) instabilities (see e.g.,
40
;
42
;
35
;
32

(
40
;
9

. It is often appropriate to model the underlying non-zonal turbulence as streamers
(
11
)
;
i.e.

k_{\psi}=0

(
40

in a gyro-fluid, slab model. They showed that the ZF grows super-exponentially, akin to a Kelvin-Helmholtz instability. Recently this work has been extended by
32

They emphasise the importance of the radial component of the magnetic drift, which leads to an additional zonal growth path through the toroidal secondary mode (TSM). Both of these descriptions pertain to a strongly nonlinear regime of the turbulence evolution, where only the initial streamer behaves linearly. Other treatments of this nonlinear generation mechanism do not necessarily consider the streamer assumption, and use a finite drift wave frequency for it
(
9

The second mechanism of ZF generation is the more direct, purely driven scenario. In this direct drive the growing non-zonal modes beat together to nonlinearly drive the ZF. This has been repeatedly observed by various authors as a
“
2\gamma

, but it is often considered a lesser effect

2
Gyrokinetic Framework

We consider turbulence associated with fluctuations of the electrostatic potential
\phi(\boldsymbol{r},t)
and the distribution function
\delta\!f_{s}(\boldsymbol{r},\mu,\mathcal{E},t)
. Here, we have introduced the phase-space coordinates
\{t,\mathcal{E},\mu,\vartheta,\boldsymbol{r}\}
, corresponding to time, energy per mass
\mathcal{E}=v^{2}/2
, the first adiabatic invariant
\mu=v_{\perp}^{2}/(2B)
, gyrophase, and particle position, respectively. The full distribution function is decomposed as

f_{s}=F_{0s}+\delta\!f_{s}\equiv F_{0s}\!\left(1-\frac{q_{s}\phi(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{T_{0s}}\right)+h_{s}(\boldsymbol{R}_{s},\mu,\mathcal{E},t)+\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}),

(1)

for species
s,
with charge
q_{s},
and temperature
T_{0s}
. Here,
F_{0s}
is the equilibrium distribution function, and is taken to be a Maxwellian with total density
n_{s}=\int d^{3}\mathbf{v}f_{s}
. We also define
\rho_{*}=\rho_{i}/L\ll 1
to be the ratio of the thermal ion gyroradius to the system size. The above satisfies the following gyrokinetic (GK) ordering

\frac{\delta\!f_{s}}{F_{0s}}\sim\frac{q_{s}\phi}{T_{0,s}}\sim\frac{k_{\parallel}}{k_{\perp}}\sim\frac{\omega}{\Omega_{s}}\sim\rho_{*}\ll 1,

(2)

where
k_{\parallel}
and
k_{\perp}
are wavevectors parallel and perpendicular to the equilibrium magnetic field. We also introduce a characteristic frequency of fluctuations,
\omega
, and the gyrofrequency,
\Omega_{s}=q_{s}B/m_{s}c
, with
B
the magnetic field magnitude, and
m_{s}
the species mass. Here,
h_{s}
denotes the non-adiabatic part of the distribution function, evaluated at the gyro-centre position
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
. The gyro-centre and particle positions are related by
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}=\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta)
, where
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta)=\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\times\boldsymbol{v}/\Omega_{s}

is the velocity-dependent gyroradius, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\equiv\boldsymbol{B}/|B|
is the unit vector in the direction of the magnetic field. Note that the electrostatic potential is evaluated at the particle position, while
h_{s}
is evaluated at the gyro-centre. Together, the potential and distribution function satisfy the quasineutrality condition
(
14
;
6
;
1
)

\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\left[\langle h_{s}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{r}}-\frac{q_{s}}{T_{0s}}F_{0s}\phi\right]=0,

(3)

where the velocity integral is evaluated at constant particle position,
\boldsymbol{r}
. The angle brackets denote a gyroaverage, which we define for a generic function,
g
, as

\displaystyle\langle g(\boldsymbol{r})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{R}_{s}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle g(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{R}_{s}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g\!\left(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta,

(4a)

\displaystyle\langle g(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{r}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle g(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{r}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g\!\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{s}(\vartheta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta,

(4b)

where
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are held fixed, respectively, during the

\vartheta
integration. The non-adiabatic distribution function
h_{s}
satisfies the nonlinear
GK equation
(
14
)

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{\parallel}\nabla_{\parallel}+\mathbf{v}_{d,s}\cdot\nabla\right)h_{s}=\frac{q_{s}F_{0s}}{T_{0s}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\langle\phi\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{R}_{s}}\\
-\frac{c}{B}\hat{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla\left\langle\phi\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{R}_{s}}\times\nabla F_{0s}-\frac{c}{B}\hat{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla\left\langle\phi\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{R}_{s}}\times\nabla h_{s},

(5)

where
\gradient_{\parallel}=\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\gradient
indicates a derivative along fieldline, and
\mathbf{v}_{d,s}
the magnetic drift.

Leveraging the ordering in equation (
2
), a WKB representation is used to treat the perpendicular directions in equations (
3
) and (
5
). We define the perpendicular wave-vector
\boldsymbol{k}_{\perp}=k_{\alpha}\nabla\alpha+k_{\psi}\nabla\psi
, with the local perpendicular wavenumbers
\{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}\}
constant. This yields the working equations for quasineutrality

\sum_{s}\frac{1}{n_{0s}}\int d^{3}\mathbf{v}\,J_{0s,\boldsymbol{k}}h_{s,\boldsymbol{k}}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{T_{0s}}\,,

(6)

and the GK equation

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,h_{s,\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{q_{s}F_{0s}}{T_{0s}}J_{0s,\boldsymbol{k}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=i\partial_{t}+iv_{\parallel}\nabla_{\parallel}-\boldsymbol{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{d,s}

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=i\partial_{t}-c\frac{k_{\alpha}T_{0s}}{q_{s}}\frac{F_{0s}^{\prime}}{F_{0s}}

(8b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=ic\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\left(k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}^{\prime}-k_{\alpha}k_{\psi}^{\prime}\right)J_{0s,\boldsymbol{k}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}h_{s,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}

(8c)

where the gyroaverages have given Bessel
functions
(
2
, Chapter 9)
,
J_{0s,\boldsymbol{k}}=J_{0}(\sqrt{2b_{\boldsymbol{k}}}v_{\perp}/v_{\mathrm{th,s}}),
with
b_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{k}^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}/2
, where we have dropped the perpendicular subscript on
\boldsymbol{k}
for ease of notation. We do likewise with the species subscript from here on, and consider electrons to adopt a modified Boltzmann response (see e.g.,
13
Eq. (5);
20
;
1
). For this the electron distribution function is taken as
\delta f_{e}=e(\phi-{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\psi})/T_{e}
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\psi}\equiv 1/V\int dz\int d\alpha\cdot/(\hat{\bf{b}}\cdot\nabla z)
indicates a flux-surface average, with
V
the volume of the flux-surface.

The main difficulty of the GK equation, Eq. (
7
), is the presence of the nonlinear term
\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, related to the advection of perturbations by the perturbed electrostatic potential. In the modal language of this paper, this nonlinear term links the evolution of a mode
\boldsymbol{k}
to all other pairs satisfying the convolution condition,
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=\boldsymbol{k},
for two given wavenumbers
\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}
excluding self-interactions. This complexity prevents one from explicitly solving such an equation in its full form.

To make progress towards understanding evolution and behaviour of the zonal mode, we start by considering a reduced number of modes. Such minimal-models are capable of reproducing key numerical results with good accuracy in specific regimes, as we will demonstrate later and has been seen by others (see e.g.,
18
;
40
).
The absolute miminimal number necessary for capturing the nonlinear dynamics is clearly three and their complex conjugates; zonal (
k_{\psi}\neq 0,\,k_{\alpha}=0
), primary
(k_{\psi}=0,\,k_{\alpha}\neq 0)
, and sideband
(k_{\psi}\neq 0,\,k_{\alpha}\neq 0)
(see Figure
1
). Their associated electrostatic potentials are denoted by
\phi_{k_{\psi}}
,
\phi_{k_{\alpha}}
, and
\phi_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}
respectively. The minimal form of the truncated problem is three equations of the form of Eq. (
7
) (one for each mode), plus quasineutrality for each.

The dynamics of the ZF are determined by the structure of these equations, which in turn depends strongly on the relative importance of the linear and nonlinear terms. Different relative orderings imply distinct dominant dynamics, and can therefore be described as different
regimes
of ZF evolution. The most basic is a fully
linear regime
: the nonlinear terms in all equations play a subdominant role. This regime is illustrated as region (i) in Figure
1
: the initial phase in a typical evolution of a nonlinear GK simulation. When the zonal mode evolves nonlinearly, but the other two modes evolve linearly, then a
weakly nonlinear regime
ensues. The occurrance of such a regime is illustrated as region (ii) in Figure
1
. Finally,
strongly nonlinear
and
fully nonlinear regime
s are here defined as a system for which two or all three mode types are strongly influenced by their nonlinear terms respectively. The latter is illustrated in region (iii) in Figure
1
. We now discuss these regimes as they pertain to zonal flows.

3.1
Linear Regime

In the linear regime, every mode is taken to evolve linearly. The linear evolution of the zonal mode is a well studied problem, corresponding to setting
\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}}=0
in Eq. (
7
). It is within this regime that the classic Rosenbluth-Hinton residual is derived
(
41
)
.

To argue when this scenario is dominant we need to consider the ordering of different terms in the nonlinear GK equation. The linear term is ordered as
\mathcal{L}_{k}\sim\omega_{k}^{\mathrm{lin}}
, where
\omega_{k}^{\mathrm{lin}}
represents a linear time scale. We do not distinguish at this level between the different terms that make up the linear operator. With that, a linearly evolving zonal flow requires

\omega_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{lin}}h_{k_{\psi}}\gg\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}}.

(9)

To make the ordering of the different terms more explicit, let us introduce additional simplifications and define adimensional quantities. It is convenient in this context to take
h\sim(q\phi/T)F_{0}
, such that the above ordering can be written as,

\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\gg\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}},

(10)

where
\tilde{\phi}\sim\rho^{-1}_{*}(q\phi/T)
,
\tilde{\omega}=\omega a/v_{\text{th},s}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\sim B_{0}k_{\psi}\rho_{i}k_{\alpha}\rho_{i}
, with
B_{0}
a reference magnetic field. For the linear regime to hold, we thus need the amplitude of the zonal mode to be sufficiently large compared to the product of the primary and sideband.

However, the inequality given in Eq. (
10
) is bound to be violated given linearly unstable non-zonal modes. Linearly, the zonal modes cannot grow. In the absence of parallel streaming, under the local approximation, the linear modes are all damped on a time scale
\sim\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}
. In the nonlocal limit, if one retains the effects of parallel streaming, one expects the system to damp geodesic acoustic modes (GAMs)
(
51
;
38
)
via Landau damping. In fact, all components of the ZF decay except the residual
(
41
)
. Meanwhile, finite
k_{\alpha}
-modes can grow linearly, as they can tap into equilibrium gradients as sources of free energy (
e.g.
, the ion temperature gradient driven instability, ITG).
Hence, even if initially the system is fully linear, at some time
t_{0}
the non-zonal modes will eventually become sufficiently large to drive the nonlinear term.

3.2
Weakly Nonlinear Regime

Following the linear discussion above, it is natural to consider the weak regime as one in which the zonal mode comes to being nonlinearly dominated, while the sideband and primary remain linear. This corresponds to region (ii) in Figure
1
.

To model this regime we adopt the minimal three-mode (triadic) model, see Figure
1
a, with the following simplified nonlinearities in the GK equations, Eq. (
7
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\alpha}}&=0,\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}&=0,\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}}&=ick_{\psi}k_{\alpha}\left[J_{0,k_{\alpha}}\,\phi_{-k_{\alpha}}\,h_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}-J_{0,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\,\phi_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\,h_{-k_{\alpha}}\right].\end{cases}

(11)

To understand when this regime is valid, we must once again consider the orders of the various terms in the GK equation. The non-zonal modes evolve linearly, and for simplicity we take them to do so on the same time scale,
\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\sim\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
, although this assumption is not essential. By contrast, the characteristic time scale of the ZF is set by the nonlinear drive, which imposes its own timescale. Since the nonlinear drive is generated by modes each growing like
\phi_{k_{\alpha}}\sim\exp(i \gamma_{\ky}^\mathrm{lin})
, the nonlinear forcing goes like
\phi_{k_{\alpha}}\phi_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}\sim\exp(2 i \gamma_{\ky}^\mathrm{lin})
, meaning that the ZF evolution time scale is ordered
\omega_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{NL}}\sim\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
.

For the nonlinear term to dominate we need,

\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\ll\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}},

(12)

in which case we can order
\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}/\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
. We still need the nonlinear terms in the primary and sideband equations to remain small, and thus insist the following, (invoking
\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\sim\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\sim\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
directly)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\gg

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\sim\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\right)^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\gg

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\sim\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{2}}{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}},

from which it follows that,

\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}},\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}},\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\ll\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{=}}\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}},

(14)

where
\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}
, resulting from the balance of the linear time scale of the primary and the nonlinear interaction, sets a critical perturbation magnitude below which the weak regime is valid. As in the linear regime, this inequality cannot be satisfied
ad infinitum
, and at some time
t_{1}
the growing
\tilde{\phi}
will reach the constant
\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}
threshold. The weakly regime will be readily visible so long as the orderings can be consistently satisfied over a time window
\Delta t\sim 1/\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
.

3.3
Strongly Nonlinear Regime

We now consider the immediate extension to the above, in which both the zonal and sideband modes evolve nonlinearly, while the primary mode is the only one to remain linear. The minimal ingredients for defining the strongly nonlinear regime are

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\alpha}}&=0,\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}&=ick_{\psi}k_{\alpha}\left[J_{0,k_{\psi}}\,\phi_{k_{\psi}}\,h_{k_{\alpha}}-J_{0,k_{\alpha}}\,\phi_{k_{\alpha}}\,h_{k_{\psi}}\right],\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}}&=ick_{\psi}k_{\alpha}\left[J_{0,k_{\alpha}}\,\phi_{-k_{\alpha}}\,h_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}-J_{0,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\,\phi_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\,h_{-k_{\alpha}}\right].\end{cases}

(15)

To assess this regime we must once again consider the orderings. The characteristic time scales of the zonal and sidebands are determined by consistently satisfying
\omega_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{NL}}\approx\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{NL}}-(\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}})^{*}
and
\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{NL}}\approx\omega_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{NL}}+\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
. Taking the nonlinear complex frequencies and growth rates to be comparable,
\omega\sim\gamma
, the essential requirement is that the zonal and sideband dynamics occur on a time scale much faster than that of the primary mode

\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\ll\omega_{k_{\psi}}\sim\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}.

(16)

Following from this, it can be shown that the zonal and sideband amplitudes should be comparable,
\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\sim\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}
.

For the primary to remain linear, we insist that over a time window of interest,
\Delta t\sim 1/\gamma_{k_{\psi}}
, the nonlinear correction to the primary must remain small. Estimating this contribution by integrating the nonlinear term over
\Delta t
, we obtain the scaling requirement

\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\gg\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\tilde{\gamma}_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{NL}}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}.

(17)

On the other hand, for the zonal and sideband equations to exhibit dominantly nonlinear behavior, the primary must be sufficiently strong. This yields the constraints

\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}\gtrsim\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}}{\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}},\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}{\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}}.

(18)

This scenario set by Eq. (
16
), corresponds to the standard secondary–instability set-up in which the primary (streamer) magnitude is frozen in time. In this regime fall both the treatments of RDK
(
40
)
and of
32
. If, like RDK, one neglects geometric effects from the linear contributions to the zonal and sideband equations, one finds

\tilde{\omega}_{k_{\psi}}^{\mathrm{NL}}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{N}}|\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}|.

(19)

This corresponds to the celebrated super-exponential RDK secondary growth of the ZF. To retain consistency with the condition in Eq. (
17
), the primary must be sufficiently strong,
\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\alpha}}^{2}\gg\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}}\tilde{\phi}_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}
, and must be so over a sufficiently long time window. We present in Appendix
A
a more detailed comparison to the original gyrofluid work by RDK.

Including the geometry for the ZF and sideband brings us closer to the work of
32
. In Appendix
B
we prove the equivalence between the original work, and our truncated modal approach, under the same subsidiary ordering
k_{\alpha}\gg k_{\psi}
. A crucial point in establishing this equivalence is the need to include an additional mirror mode to the minimal triadic model; namely, the mode at finite
k_{\psi}
and negative but finite
k_{\alpha}
. We must correspondingly add an extension to Eqs. (
15
) (see Figure
8
and details in Appendix
B
).

4
Weakly Nonlinear Regime

For the remainder of this paper we shall focus on ZF evolution in the weakly nonlinear regime, described in Section
3.2
. The most straightforward way to extract the dominant behaviour of the ZF in this regime is to treat the problem as a forced system. For this, we first invoke the triadic model, which we repeat here for clarity

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,h_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{qF_{0}}{T_{0}}J_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{for}\quad\boldsymbol{k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle(0,k_{\alpha})\\
\displaystyle(k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}),\end{cases}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,h_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{qF_{0}}{T_{0}}J_{0,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{for}\quad\boldsymbol{k}=(0,k_{\psi}).

(22)

We further assume that Eq. (
21
) admits a single unstable normal mode solution,
\phi_{k_{\alpha}}\sim\exp(-i\omega_{\ky} t)
, and
\phi_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}\sim\exp(-i \omega_{\kx\ky} t)
. From wavenumber matching, the temporal behaviour of
\mathcal{N}_{k_{\psi}}
is

\omega_{k_{\psi}}=\omega_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}-\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*},

(23)

which then drives the ZF at that same frequency. Assuming that the primary and sideband both behave similarly with growth rate
\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}
(which is the case in the limit of
k_{\psi}\rightarrow 0
, or
\omega_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}\sim\omega_{k_{\alpha}}\gg\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}
) this gives

\omega_{k_{\psi}}=\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}-\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}\approx 2i\gamma_{k_{\alpha}}.

(24)

Thus, for strong nonlinear drive, and
k_{\psi}\ll k_{\alpha},
the zonal flow is bound to be purely growing, and to grow with a rate twice that of the primary mode
(
48
)
. This, in fact, is a special case of the convolution condition in Eq. (
23
), which has been verified numerically. Note that this is a purely driven response, with no marginal points, and largely independent of the initial ZF condition. Consequently, provided the primary mode is unstable, this behaviour will inevitably be observed if the system is evolved for a sufficiently long time. Generally, as one goes to higher
k_{\psi}\rho_{i}
the sideband will become linearly stabilised, and thus this mechanism will become subdominant. One should therefore frame much of this analysis to be primarily relevant for
k_{\psi}\rho_{i}<1
.

4.2
Analysis of Weakly Nonlinear Regime

Given the simple set-up in the weakly nonlinear regime, it is possible to directly analyse the structure of the ZF as it is driven. We derive this structure assuming the dominant Fourier analysis considered at the beginning of Section
4
. For this, we start with the quasineutrality condition for the zonal component of the distribution function

\frac{1}{n_{0}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}J_{0,k_{\psi}}h_{k_{\psi}}=\left(\frac{1}{\tau}+1\right)\frac{e\phi_{k_{\psi}}}{T_{0e}}-\frac{e}{T_{0e}}\frac{1}{\tau}\langle\phi_{k_{\psi}}\rangle_{\psi},

(25)

where we have taken the electrons to have a modified adiabatic response,
J_{0,e,\boldsymbol{k}}\sim 1
, and
\tau=T_{0,i}/T_{0,e}
.
We then use the nonlinear Eq. (
22
) for
h_{k_{\psi}}
, and the linear Eqs. (
21
) for
h_{k_{\alpha}}
and
h_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}
(dropping the streaming terms), to yield the following amplitude for the ZF,
\phi_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}
,

\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}\phi_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}=-i\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{NL}}}\phi_{-k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}},

(26)

with

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\frac{F_{0i}}{n_{0}}J_{0,k_{\psi}}^{2}\frac{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}{\omega_{k_{\psi}}-\hat{\omega}_{d,k_{\psi}}}-1,

(27a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{NL}}^{-1}=

\displaystyle-\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\frac{F_{0i}}{n_{0}}\frac{J_{0,k_{\psi}}J_{0,k_{\alpha}}J_{0,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}{\omega_{k_{\psi}}-\hat{\omega}_{d,k_{\psi}}}\mathcal{I}

(27b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}-\hat{\omega}_{*,k_{\alpha}}}{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}-\hat{\omega}_{d,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}-\frac{\omega_{-k_{\alpha}}-\hat{\omega}_{*,-k_{\alpha}}}{\omega_{-k_{\alpha}}-\hat{\omega}_{d,-k_{\alpha}}},

(27c)

where
\omega_{\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}}=ck_{\psi}k_{\alpha}\phi_{k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}
,
\hat{\omega}_{*,k_{\alpha}}=\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}\left[1+\eta(\hat{v}^{2}-3/2)\right]
,
\eta=\mathrm{d}(\ln T)/\mathrm{d}(\ln n)
,
\hat{\omega}_{d,\lambda}=\omega_{d,\lambda}\left(\hat{v}_{\parallel}^{2}+\hat{v}_{\perp}^{2}/2\right)
, and
\hat{v}=v/v_{\text{th},s}
indicates the normalised velocity coordinate. Here
\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}
represents the linear zonal response, whilst
\Omega_{\mathrm{NL}}^{-1}
expresses the nonlinear driving. It should be stressed that Eq. (
26
) should not be interpreted as a dispersion relation, but instead as an equation for the amplitude of
\phi_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}
in terms of linear primary amplitudes, with
\omega_{k_{\psi}}
already determined by the complex frequency matching from Eq. (
23
).

A more careful approach employing the Laplace transform yields the same dominant behaviour provided the non-zonal modes are strongly unstable (which is generally the case for small
k_{\psi}\rho_{i}
). The Laplace approach helps setting up the necessary integrals involved in quasineutrality properly, and is more careful to not disregard any potentially important time response of the ZF. This analysis is performed in Appendix
C
, demonstrating the lack of marginal stability point and highlighting the fundamentally driven nature of the system (see Figure
4
).

The nonlinear integral in Eq. (
27b
) can be written in terms of more manageable functions

\Omega_{NL}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}-\omega\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}}\left[\omega_{d,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}I_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}-\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}I_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}-\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}I_{-k_{\alpha}}^{-\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}}\right],

(28)

where we define,

I_{\beta}^{\omega}\left(\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}\right)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\frac{F_{0i}}{n_{0}}\frac{\omega-\hat{\omega}_{*,k_{\alpha}}^{T}}{\omega-\hat{\omega}_{d,\beta}}J_{0,k_{\psi}}J_{0,k_{\alpha}}J_{0,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}.

(29)

These integrals are derived in Appendix
D
, and a closed form in terms of plasma dispersion functions presented when dropping FLR effects
(
4
;
55
;
25
)
. In that limit
\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}=I_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}-1
also holds.

To further understand this amplitude response and its implications, let us now consider the limit for which
k_{\psi}\rightarrow 0
.
Retaining the FLR effects and expanding both sides in
\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}/\omega_{k_{\psi}}\sim k_{\psi}|\nabla\psi|\rho_{i}\ll 1
and
\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}/\omega_{k_{\alpha}}\sim k_{\alpha}|\nabla\alpha|\rho_{i}\ll 1
, with
\omega_{k_{\psi}}\sim\omega_{k_{\alpha}}
, we find

\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}\approx\frac{\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}}{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}-b_{k_{\psi}},

(30)

and

\Omega_{\text{NL}}^{-1}\approx\frac{1}{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}(\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}+\omega_{k_{\psi}})\omega_{k_{\psi}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(b_{k_{\psi}}+b_{k_{\alpha}}+b_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}\right)\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}^{T}\omega_{k_{\psi}}\right.\\
\left.+\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}\omega_{k_{\psi}}-\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}-\frac{\omega_{*}^{T}}{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}}\frac{\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}\omega_{k_{\psi}}+\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}(2\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}\omega_{k_{\psi}}+\omega_{k_{\psi}}^{2})}{(\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}+\omega_{k_{\psi}})}\right].

(31)

Note that all terms in Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) are proportional to either
b_{\boldsymbol{k}}
or
\omega_{d,\boldsymbol{k}}
. The dependence of
\epsilon_{k_{\psi}}
in particular results from the compression response of the zonal arising from either geodesic curvature or FLR effects.

In the long wavelength limit with finite geodesic curvature, this shows that one always gets a finite zonal response. Further assuming
\real(\omega_{\boldsymbol{k}})\ll\imaginary(\omega_{\boldsymbol{k}})
,

\phi_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}\approx 2i\frac{c}{\gamma/k_{\alpha}}\frac{\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}/k_{\alpha}}{\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}/k_{\psi}}k_{\alpha}\phi_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}\left(\phi_{k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}}\right)^{*}

(32)

to leading order,
\mathcal{O}(1)
. Assuming that
\gamma\propto k_{\alpha}
, the zonal amplitude is larger when the primary has a shorter length scale, as is shown by the linear dependence on
k_{\alpha}
. The geometry of the field also plays an important role in controlling the strength of the zonal response. Both the normal and geodesic curvatures are involved, balancing each other to give a finite response at long wavelengths. Configurations with a reduced geodesic curvature will therefore see a very strong growth of large scale zonal-flows. This is an enhancement that aligns with the geometric considerations following the ZF residual in stellarators
(
36
;
39
)
.

This behaviour invites us to consider the limit of vanishing geodesic curvature, as is the case in a
Z
-pinch (
\omega_{d,k_{\psi}}\rightarrow 0
and
k_{\psi}\rho_{i}\ll 1
). In this limit we find

\phi_{k_{\psi}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}}}\approx-i\frac{c}{k_{\psi}}\phi_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}}\left(\phi_{k_{\alpha}}^{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}}\right)^{*}\frac{1}{(\gamma/k_{\alpha})^{2}}\left[(b_{k_{\psi}}+b_{k_{\alpha}}+b_{k_{\psi},k_{\alpha}})\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}^{T}/k_{\alpha}+2\omega_{d,k_{\alpha}}/k_{\alpha}\right]\propto\frac{1}{k_{\psi}}.

(33)

That is, the zonal amplitude is large (in fact, diverges) for small
k_{\psi}
. This suggests that in geometries with small but finite geodesic curvature there will be a rapid growth of large-scale zonal modes through the
2\gamma
-mechanism that leads the system to quickly leave the weakly nonlinear regime and transition into a strongly nonlinear or fully saturated state.

5
Appearance of a Large Residual

The discussion about the zonal response in the weakly nonlinear regime thus far has focused on the electrostatic potential amplitude. Little was said about its velocity-space structure, which is however important to fully understand the nature of the ZF. In particular, the level of the ZF residual is known to depend on the precise form of its distribution function
(
30
)
. In this section we study this.

Let us start our study of the phase-space structure of the zonal mode by considering a simplified zonal distribution function. As will be shown later, this structure naturally arises in the limit of a strongly driven toroidal ion-temperature gradient (ITG), in which the drift contributions to the ZF can be neglected,
\omega_{*}\gg\omega_{d,\boldsymbol{k}},\omega_{k_{x}}
(see e.g.
26

4

\eta\gg 1

h_{k_{\alpha}}\sim q/TJ_{0,k_{\alpha}}\hat{\omega}_{*,k_{\alpha}}/\omega_{k_{\alpha}}F_{0}\phi_{k_{\alpha}}
from Eq. (
21
), Eq. (
22
) yields,

\displaystyle\langle\delta f_{k_{\psi}}\rangle_{\mathbf{R}}=\hat{\mathcal{N}}J_{0,k_{\alpha}}J_{0,k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}W_{N}F_{0},

(34a)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}=-ik_{\alpha}k_{\psi}c\frac{q}{T}\phi_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}\phi_{k_{\psi}}\frac{\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}^{T}}{\omega_{k_{\alpha}}^{*}\omega_{k_{\psi}k_{\alpha}}},\quad W_{N}=\hat{v}^{2}-\frac{3}{2}.

(34b)

The velocity-space dependence is inherited from that of the diamagnetic frequency, explicitly
\hat{\omega}_{*,k_{\alpha}}=\omega_{*,k_{\alpha}}^{T}(\hat{v}^{2}-3/2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nT}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}J_{0k_{\psi}}v^{2}\langle\delta\!f_{k_{\psi}}\rangle_{\mathbf{R}}=\frac{3}{2}\hat{\mathcal{N}},

6
Conclusion

We have investigated the origin and structure of zonal flows in the weakly nonlinear regime, where the zonal flow is purely driven by unstable linear modes. We introduce an appropriate ordering in amplitudes and time scales in which the so-called
2\gamma
behaviour is dominant, and consider its minimal description. We compare this directly to nonlinear, electrostatic, gyrokinetic simulations, showing agreement that confirms the validity of its essential features.

Within this framework, it is highlighted that the apparent onset of the zonal flow growth in simulations is an artifact. This distinguishes it from other growth mechanisms, such as secondary instabilities, which we also contextualise on similar terms and orderings. Provided the primary mode is linearly unstable, the
2\gamma
zonal response will inevitably emerge given sufficient time, as a consequence of nonlinear forcing. Moreover, it will always be present, even if subdominant in amplitude. An expression for the zonal-mode amplitude is derived and shown to be large in magnetic fields with small geodesic curvature.

The phase-space structure of the zonal response is closely tied to that of the driving primary. In the case of ITG drive, this contributes a large temperature perturbation, resulting in a larger zonal flow residual than in the classic Rosenbluth-Hinton scenario. The residual sensitivity to the exact form of the velocity-space structure calls into question its physical relevance. The analysis nevertheless shows that there is a large component of the zonal flow that is not visible through the electrostatic potential, and motivates a broader diagnostic when considering the effects of zonal flows including in the regime of fully developed, saturated turbulence.

In more complicated geometries, parallel streaming may become important, and thus a natural extension of this work would include its effects, which we have neglected in this paper. Beyond this weak regime, in saturation, it remains to be seen if any remnants of this driven mechanism persists. It is conceivable that the long-wavelength zonal flow would be continuously fed via this mechanism, even when it may not be the dominant behaviour. Future work will aim to address both of these questions.

Appendix E
Comparison to
10

In this Appendix, we briefly compare our work to that of
10
. In
10
, they combine the driven (beat-driven) and modulational instability mechanisms to describe the generation of ZFs.

In the same way that we do in this paper, the authors rely on a truncated mode description. They decompose the perturbed electrostatic potential into a non-zonal (drift-wave) component and a zonal component, with zonal modes defined by vanishing parallel and binormal wavenumbers. This equates to a small set of interacting modes, consisting of a primary drift wave, its radial sidebands (including the mirror mode discussed in Appendix
B
), and a zonal mode.

Their
“primary”
treatment differs in that they retain a zero-frequency drift wave component. This leads to fundamental differences in the nonlinearities responsible for driving the zonal mode. In
10
, the zonal flow is driven by the interaction between a constant component of the drift wave and a component growing at the zonal growth rate, as discussed in the analysis preceding their Eq. (22). A
2\gamma
-driven interaction would exist within their model, however this occurs at
k_{\psi}
that lies outside their truncation, and is thus ignored.

A second point of difference is in the time scale ordering assumed. In deriving their
(
10
, Eq. 15)
the authors assume
\omega_{r}\gg\gamma
for the primary drift wave, whereas the analysis presented in this paper takes
\omega_{r}\sim\gamma
. This leads to some differences in the region of validity and the resulting ZF behaviour.
