Arrangements of Consecutive Numbers in Mallows Permutations
Katarzyna Rybarczyk

Abstract
We study the random variable that counts the number of specific arrangements of clustered consecutive numbers in permutations under the Mallows distribution. We provide an asymptotic expression for the expected value of this random variable. This result extends and tightens the previously known result by Pinsky (2022) concerning clustered consecutive numbers in Mallows permutations. Moreover, we identify a range of parameters for which the distribution of the number of arrangements of clustered consecutive numbers in Mallows permutations is close to a Poisson distribution.

Introduction

Let
\mathbf{S}_{n}
be the family of all permutations of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in(0,\infty]
, the Mallows distribution
\Mallow(n,q)
samples a random permutation

according to the probability measure

\forall_{\sigma\in\mathbf{S}_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(\varPi_{n}=\sigma\right)=\frac{q^{\text{inv}(\sigma)}}{a_{n,q}},

where

\text{inv}(\sigma)=|\{(i,j):1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n,\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}|,

and
a_{n,q}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{n}}q^{\text{inv}(\pi)}
is a normalising constant.

[

,

[

[

[

, logical limit laws
[

We investigate the clustering of consecutive elements under the Mallows distribution. This problem, for Mallows and
p
–shifted distributions, has been considered by Pinsky in
[
29
]
. For a permutation
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
, Pinsky analyzes the random variable counting the number of intervals
\{j,j+1,\ldots,j+\ell-1\}\subseteq[n]
whose images under
\varPi_{n}
form consecutive natural numbers. Moreover, it is noted in
[

that, with some additional work, these results can be extended to more general arrangements of consecutive elements. Our objective is to formulate our results at the highest possible level of generality. To this end, we introduce a precise definition of arrangements of clustered consecutive numbers in a permutation.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\ell\geqslant 2
be a constant integer and
\mathcal{S}
,
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbf{S}_{\ell}
, be a family of permutations of
[\ell]=\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
.
Moreover let
\varPi_{n}\in\mathbf{S}_{n}
,
n\geqslant\ell
,
0\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant n-\ell
, and
0\leqslant k_{2}\leqslant n-\ell
. There is an
(\mathcal{S},k_{1},k_{2})
–arrangement in
\varPi_{n}
if

\exists_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}\forall_{i\in[\ell]}\varPi_{n}(k_{2}+i)=k_{1}+\sigma(i).

For any ,
0\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant n-\ell
,
0\leqslant k_{2}\leqslant n-l
, an
(\mathcal{S},k_{1},k_{2})
–arrangement we call an
\mathcal{S}
–arrangement.

In other words, in an
(\mathcal{S},k_{1},k_{2})
–arrangement in
\varPi_{n}
, the consecutive numbers
k_{2}+1,\ldots,k_{2}+\ell
take values in the set
\{k_{1}+1,\ldots,k_{1}+\ell\}
in the order determined by a permutation
\sigma
from
\mathcal{S}
.
When we set
\mathcal{S}=\mathbf{S}_{\ell}
, the presence of an
(\mathcal{S},k_{1},k_{2})
–arrangement in
\varPi_{n}
implies that the values
\varPi_{n}(k_{2}+1),\varPi_{n}(k_{2}+2),\ldots,\varPi_{n}(k_{2}+\ell)
are
\ell
consecutive numbers
k_{1}+1,\ldots,k_{1}+\ell
(in any possible order), which is exactly the case considered in
[
29
]
.
Given
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbf{S}_{\ell}
, denote by
\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\varPi_{n})
the number of
\mathcal{S}
–arrangements in
\varPi_{n}
. Then the random variable studied in
[
29
]
is
\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathbf{S}_{\ell}}(\varPi_{n})
.

The Mallows distribution satisfies a duality relation. Let the reverse of a permutation
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
be defined by

\sigma^{rev}=(\sigma_{n},\ldots,\sigma_{1}).

For
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
and
\varPi_{n}^{\prime}\sim\Mallow(n,1/q)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\varPi_{n}=\sigma\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\varPi_{n}^{\prime}=\sigma^{rev}\right).

Therefore, all theorems concerning
\mathcal{S}
–arrangements for
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
with
q\in(0,1)
can be reformulated in terms of
\mathcal{S}^{rev}
–arrangements, where
\mathcal{S}^{rev}=\{\sigma^{rev}\in\mathbf{S}_{\ell}:\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\}
, and
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,1/q)
. Consequently, without loss of generality, the analysis may be restricted to the regime
q\in(0,1)
.

Moreover, for
q\in(0,1)
, the Mallows distribution assigns higher probability to permutations with fewer inversions, whereas for
q>1
it favours permutations with a larger number of inversions. The case
q=1
corresponds to the uniform distribution on
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. A particularly interesting situation arises when
q=q_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, capturing a limiting transition toward uniformity.

The following theorem was shown in
[
29
]

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 1.1
[
29
]
)
.

Let
q=q_{n}=1-c/n^{\alpha}
, for
c>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\ell\geqslant 2
be a constant integer, and

\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
,

\displaystyle\frac{((\ell-1)!)^{2}\ell!}{(2\ell)!}\leqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\frac{\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathbf{S}_{\ell}}(\varPi_{n})}{n(1-q)^{\ell-1}}}

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\frac{\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathbf{S}_{\ell}}(\varPi_{n})}{n(1-q)^{\ell-1}}}\leqslant(\ell-1)!.

Corollary 1.4

(Corollary 1.2
[

)
.

Let
q=q_{n}=1-c/n^{\alpha}

,

,

\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
,

\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\varPi_{n})
,
\ell\geqslant 3
,
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)

We use the following notations.
For
t_{1}\leqslant t_{2}
,
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}

.

\Lambda(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}=\sigma(j)-|\sigma([j-1])\cap[\sigma(j)-1]|\right).

(1)

\Lambda(\mathbf{S}_{\ell})=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(1-q^{j}).

(2)

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\ell\geqslant 2
be a constant integer,
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbf{S}_{\ell}
,
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)

If moreover

then

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\varPi_{n})=(1+o(1))|\mathcal{S}|\frac{[(\ell-1)!]^{2}}{(2\ell-1)!}n(1-q)^{\ell-1}.

Therefore by (
2

1.2
(Theorem 1.1
[

]
).

.

be a constant integer,
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)

q=q(n)\in(0,1)

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{\mathbf{Arr}}_{\mathbf{S}_{\ell}}(\varPi_{n})=(1+o(1))\frac{\ell![(\ell-1)!]^{2}}{(2\ell-1)!}n(1-q)^{\ell-1}.

In the next result we analyse the distribution of

for

Let
\ell\geqslant 3
be a constant integer,
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbf{S}_{\ell}

\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)

Relations between Mallows permutations and the Mallows infinite process

The results in this section are crucial to the proofs of Theorem
2.1

\bar{\mathbf{\Pi}}\sim\Mallows(\mathbb{N},q)
and those concerning
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)

3.1
Mallows sequence

Due to the method of the proof, it will be convenient to consider, instead the Mallows permutation
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
, its sequence of preimages, that is

\bar{\varPi}_{n}:=(\bar{\varPi}_{n,1},\ldots,\bar{\varPi}_{n,n}),\text{ where }\ \bar{\varPi}_{n,i}=\varPi_{n}^{-1}(i),\ i\in[n],\text{ for }\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q).

We will denote by

\Mallows(n,q)

the distribution of the sequence of preimages of
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
, and we will write
\bar{\varPi}_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)
. Note that, there is a natural, straight forward one–to–one correspondence between
\mathcal{S}
–arrangements in
\varPi_{n}\sim\Mallow(n,q)
and arrangements of consecutive elements in
\bar{\varPi}_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)

Remark 3.1
.

For
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbf{S}_{\ell}

\exists_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}\forall_{i\in[\ell]}\bar{\varPi}_{n,k_{1}+i}=k_{2}+\sigma^{-1}(i).

(6)

Proof of Theorem

(19)

where
i
represents the number of times that
Z_{j}>\ell
before in the Mallows infinite process one chooses the first number in
[\ell]
. By convention we set
\prod_{j=1}^{0}a_{j}=1
.

.

\mathbf{B}_{k}
we start with the value of
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}[k,0]\right)
. By the properties of the geometric distribution

&=q^{k\ell}\Lambda(\mathcal{S}).\end{split}

(20)

\mathbf{B}_{k}
. As before,

counts the number of times the geometric random variable

,

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k\ell}\Lambda(\mathcal{S})}{1-q^{\ell+k}}+\frac{(1-q^{k})}{1-q^{k+\ell}}\mathbf{B}_{k-1},

(21)

where we used (

) in the last line.

) into two.

Now we assume that
