 Brookhaven National Laboratory, Upton, NY 11973‐5000.

 Several radiation-induced incidents, including damage to labels within the 27-ID HEX FE and
 the systematic failure of electronic devices in its FOE, were reported in 2025. To investigate the
 underlying cause of these events and mitigate their impact in the beamline’s vicinity, a set of Monte
 Carlo calculations using the FLUKA code were carried out, complemented by dosimetric evaluations,
 and its findings are hereby reported.

 I. INTRODUCTION

 The 27-ID High Energy Engineering X-ray Scattering (HEX) beamline, commissioned in 2024, operates within
 the Hard X-ray Scattering and Spectroscopy program at Brookhaven Lab’s National Synchrotron Light Source II
 (NSLS-II). The high energy spectrum at the HEX beamline extends up to several hundred keV, making it well suited
 for applications such as imaging large engineering samples, including fully assembled batteries, fuel cells, etc. [1, 2]
 In the last quarter of 2025, a checkpoint label downstream of the fixed mask at the Front End (FE) of the HEX
 beamline was cut during normal accelerator operation (see Figure 1 (left)). A discolored label was also observed at
 the same location, above the downstream lead collimator (Figure 1 (right)). The radiation resulting from this incident
 was contained within the storage ring shielding and therefore had no impact on the exposure to personnel. To confirm
 this, a radiation survey was carried out outside of the shielding in the vicinity of the 27-ID area under equivalent
 operational conditions, promptly after the issue was detected [3].

 Extensive thermal monitoring near the leakage area determined that temperatures were still relatively low. Thus,
 the leaking radiation was deemed incapable of affecting the integrity of the downstream lead collimator which, due to
 the high power of HEX, could become a potential radiological concern. It was still capable of damaging other non-
 shielding structures and components and therefore it was found worthy of investigation and mitigation. Simulations
 with the Monte Carlo particle transport and interaction code FLUKA [5, 6] were therefore carried out to investigate the

 4

cause of this incident by modeling the beamline elements near the fixed mask and using the corresponding operational
parameters at the time of the occurrence. A partial view of the 27-ID beamline FE shown in Figure 2 illustrates the
elements relevant to the described issue, which were implemented in the FLUKA geometry. In addition, a dosimetric
evaluation was conducted at the same location and compared with the simulation results, as described below.
 Because of the short distance between the FE components and the storage ring, an evaluation of beam losses was
also performed in the vicinity of the fixed mask using an active gamma irradiation monitor in situ. The different
beam contributions to radiation levels in the studied area were assessed by measuring the dose rate under different
beam configurations, as discussed in the following section.
 Finally, periodic radiation-induced failures of smoke detectors near the ceiling of the First Optical Enclosure (FOE)
of the same beamline were also reported. Dose measurements at the smoke detector locations were carried out and
are presented in this report.

 II. INSTRUMENTATION & METHODS

  The approach adopted to address the incidents reported in the HEX FE and FOE was threefold:
 • perform FLUKA simulations to investigate the issue registered at the level of the bellows downstream of the
 fixed mask. Since other occurrences were identified in the vicinity of bellows around the machine, an in-depth
 Monte Carlo calculation was carried out to accurately explain the radiation leakage mechanism and propose
 mitigation actions;
 • measure radiation levels at the bellows location using active and passive dosimetry, primarily to complement and
 validate the simulations, including the assumptions for the loss pattern and incident power on the fixed mask.
 Additional dose measurements were undertaken near the ceiling of the FOE to quickly evaluate the radiation
 levels to which the electronics in this location are exposed, allowing an estimate of the Mean Time Between
 Failures (MTBF) if no protective measures are implemented;
 • due to the proximity of the issue reported in the HEX FE to the storage ring, and since the latter was not
 modeled in the simulated geometry, the main contributor to the measured radiation levels had to be identified
 to validate the geometry implemented in the simulation.
  Each of the aforementioned approaches is further described in the following subsections, while the results are
presented in the corresponding section.

 1. Dosimetric Instrumentation & Auxiliary Diagnostics

  Active and passive dosimeters were used for the reported dose measurements, namely:
 • GIM 201KTM (Mirion Technologies) – active gamma area monitor measuring dose rates from photons over the
 range of 10−1 to 106 mrem/h (10−3 to 104 mSv/h), with energy range of 50 keV to 7 MeV [7]. The gamma
 monitor, kindly provided by the NSLS-II Radio Frequency (RF) group, was located at two different locations:
 – one closer to the beamline, at 15–30 cm from the vacuum chamber, on the outboard side of the temporary
 lead shielding installed around the bellows, as shown in Figure 3 (left);
 – and the other, at 57–72 cm from the vacuum chamber above the downstream collimator (Figure 3 (right)),
 farther from the beamline to allow measurements both with and without the lead shielding, without harming
 the detector.
 • HarshawTM 8800 Plus (Thermo ScientificTM ) – passive Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) (LiF:Mg,Ti) for
 absorbed dose from photons (> 1 keV), electrons (> 200 keV) and neutrons (< 100 MeV) with a range of 10 to
 105 mrad (0.1 mGy to 1 Gy), and up to 106 mrad (tens of Gy) when applying additional corrections [8, 9]. The
 TLDs, supplied by the Radiological Control Division (RCD), were positioned near the vacuum chamber and at
 a greater distance, just below the gamma monitor, as shown in Figure 4.
 Fluorescence screens were also used as qualitative indicators of radiation leakage along the fixed mask and down-
stream elements (see, e.g., Figure 3 (left)):
 • PFG (MCI Optonix) – green emitting fluorescence screen (ZnCdS:Ag) with absorption peaks around 9.66 and
     26.70 keV [10].

  For the present analysis, simulations were performed with pencil photon beams sent to hit four different points
around the central branch beamline, just before the downstream flange of the fixed mask. Of the total power entering

 4. Beam Loss Evaluation

 The main contributor to the radiation levels near the fixed mask was evaluated by requesting specific beam con-
figurations in the HEX beamline and storage ring in close collaboration with the NSLS-II Accelerator Division. For
each beam configuration, dose rate measurements were performed using the active gamma monitor GIM 201k, located
above the downstream collimator, as shown in Figure 3 (right). Specifically, the contribution of the electron beam
losses in the storage ring was compared to that of the synchrotron beam in the HEX FE, as well as evaluating the
impact of injection losses during Top-Off operation vs. losses from natural decay only.

 3. FLUKA Outputs

 The results depicted in Figure 9 compare the ambient dose equivalent rate (dH ∗ /dt) in the simulated region, with
and without shielding around the bellows. The gamma monitor location is indicated by the square in the top–right
corner of the dose maps. As shown in Figure 9 (top), there is significant synchrotron radiation transmission through
the thin walls of the bellows. As a result, the calculated dose rate at the gamma monitor location is of the order of
105 mrem/h, in good agreement with the measured value of 1.7×105 mrem/h (measurement #5 of Table I).
 The presence of a 1/4-inch-thick layer of lead around the bellows suppresses the propagation of synchrotron radiation
in air. Consequently, the dose rate at the position of the gamma monitor was reduced by one order of magnitude: 104
mrem/h (see Figure 9 (bottom)), again in good agreement with the measured values of 4.2×104 and 5.2×104 mrem/h
at the same location (measurements #2 and #3 of Table I)

 IV. DISCUSSION

 1. HEX FE

 After identifying the damaged label downstream of the fixed mask and confirming that the issue was radiation-
related rather than thermal, the fluorescence screens, used with and without the lead shielding around the bellows,
provided evidence that most of the radiation was leaking from the bellows. The simulations presented in this work
clarified the actual radiation leakage mechanism. Specifically, due to the wide horizontal slit beneath the three beam
apertures, part of the synchrotron radiation spreads downstream in the horizontal plane. The resulting fan-shaped

 11

beam impinges on the thin walls of the bellows slightly below the centerline, precisely at the location of the damaged
label.
  Figure 11 shows a detailed view of the wide slit below the beam apertures at the downstream end of the fixed mask
and of the significant increase in dose with a band-shaped pattern at the bellows. This result explains the burn marks
observed slightly below the centerline on the fluorescence screens (faintly visible in Figure 3 (left)) and supports the
explanation given above.

 The 1/4-inch-thick layer of lead installed around the bellows suppresses the propagation of synchrotron radiation in
air, reducing the dose rate in the surroundings by a factor of 10, except in the forward region (see Figure 9 (bottom))
due to a suboptimal shielding height. Therefore, the proposed mitigation is to maintain the thickness of the current
local lead shielding while reducing its height to better fit the bellows.
 All dose rate measurements performed in the FE (Table I) agreed well with the FLUKA simulations, validating
the approach followed. Moreover, the beam loss tests under different beam configurations (Table III) revealed the
following:
 • the dose rates measured near the bellows originate almost entirely from scatterings of synchrotron radiation
 rather than from electron beam losses in the storage ring, with a difference of 4 orders of magnitude between
 the two contributions;
 • at the gamma monitor location, top-off vs. natural decay operation modes are equivalent in terms of radiation
 levels, resulting in similar dose rates and showing only brief and slight increases during top-off refills.
 • since the impact of gas bremsstrahlung on dose rates is much smaller than that of synchrotron radiation, the
 latter was confirmed as the main contributor to radiation levels near the fixed mask.

 2. HEX FOE

  The dose measurements near the ceiling of the HEX FOE indicated values of approximately 2×103 – 3×103 rad
(20 – 30 Gy) per operation cycle (8 days) at 500 mA (Table II). Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) electronics, even
on custom boards, are known to withstand up to 104 rad (100 Gy) [15], resulting in an expected MTBF of only a few
weeks, which corroborates the reported smoke detector failures after several weeks to a few months of exposure.

 12

 In the meantime, the smoke detectors were removed from the FOE and replaced by the Very Early Smoke Detection
Apparatus (VESDA) system for remote detection.

 V. CONCLUSION

 Radiation-induced incidents were reported in 2025 at the 27-ID HEX beamline (FE and FOE), motivating the
present work. The Monte Carlo calculations performed in this study, complemented by dose measurements and beam
loss tests, confirmed the main source of radiation leakage. Owing to the wide horizontal slit beneath the three beam
apertures at the downstream end of the fixed mask, part of the synchrotron radiation spreads in the horizontal plane
and exits through the thin walls of the bellows. The temporary 1/4-inch-thick lead shielding installed around the
bellows significantly reduces the propagation of synchrotron radiation in air, lowering the surrounding dose rate by
a factor of 10. A minor adjustment of the shielding height is recommended to better fit the bellows geometry and
reduce the observed radiation leakage in the forward region.
 Dose measurements performed near the ceiling of the HEX FOE indicated values on the order of 103 rad per
operation cycle (8 days) at 500 mA, showing that electronic devices used in the FOE are exposed to significant radiation
levels and therefore must be carefully chosen and/or protected to avoid systematic failures. These measurements
explained the periodic failures of the smoke detectors after a few weeks/months of exposure.
