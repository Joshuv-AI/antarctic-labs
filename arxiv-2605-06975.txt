Efficient symplectic integrators for cubic and quartic potentials
Alejandro Escorihuela-Tomàs

Abstract
We present a set of new, efficient high-order symplectic methods designed for Hamiltonian systems with cubic or quartic potentials. By demonstrating that polynomial potentials require fewer order conditions, we develop schemes that outperform both standard symmetric compositions of second-order methods and existing RKN splitting methods. Numerical results confirm their improved efficiency over state-of-the-art alternatives found in the literature.



1
Introduction

We are interested in constructing symplectic integrators for Hamiltonian systems of the form:

H(q,p)=T(p)+V(q),\quad\text{with}\quad T(p)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{2},

(1)

where
q_{i},p_{i},m\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\dots,d
, with
d
denoting the dimension of the system,
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})
,
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})
and
V(q)
is a polynomial potential.
For Hamiltonian systems, the equations of motion are given by:

\dot{q}_{i}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\qquad\dot{p}_{i}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}},

which, in the case of a quadratic kinetic energy as in (
1
), can be rewritten as:

\ddot{q}_{i}=-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{i}}.

(2)

This second-order system corresponds to an ordinary differential equation of the form:

\ddot{y}=g(y),

(3)

where, if
V(q)
is a polynomial of degree
n
, the function
g(q)
is a polynomial of degree
n-1
. For equations of these form, specially tailored numerical methods can be constructed.
In this work, we focus on such equations with the additional assumption that
g(q)
is a polynomial of degree up to 3, which corresponds to quartic potentials.
Furthermore, we require our methods to preserve the geometric structure of the original problem;
in particular, we search symplectic integrators.

The defining feature that distinguishes symplectic integrators from other numerical methods is that each integration step corresponds to the application
of a symplectic transformation. As a consequence, such integrators preserve phase-space volume and ensure that the energy error remains bounded.
In particular, even over very long integration times, no systematic growth of the energy error is observed
[
5
]
.

In the next section, we introduce splitting methods, which will be used to construct our symplectic schemes.
In Section 3, we derive the order conditions that the coefficients of a splitting method must satisfy in order to achieve a prescribed order of accuracy.
Section 4 presents the newly developed integration methods, while in Section 5 we compare their performance with that of existing symplectic
integrators reported in the literature. Finally, Section 6 provides some concluding remarks.

2
Splitting methods

One efficient approach to constructing symplectic integrators for Hamiltonian problems is through the use of splitting methods
[
3
]
. We begin by reviewing this technique, followed by a section on the derivation of order conditions. We then present new integrators and provide numerical experiments to compare their performance against the most efficient schemes currently available in the literature.

Splitting methods are applicable when the system
\dot{x}=f(x)
can be decomposed into two or more components, each of which can be integrated exactly. In our setting, we consider a two-part splitting:

\dot{x}=f(x)=f_{A}(x)+f_{B}(x),\quad\text{with}\quad x(t_{0})=x_{0},

(4)

where the flows corresponding to each subproblem
\dot{x}=f_{i}(x)
admit exact solutions denoted by
\varphi_{t}^{[i]}(x_{0})=x(t_{0}+t)
for
i=A,B
. By composing these flows with appropriately chosen coefficients, we can construct an integrator for the full problem:

\psi_{t}=\varphi_{a_{0}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{0}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{1}t}^{[B]}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{a_{s-1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{s-1}t}^{[B]},

where
s
denotes the number of stages, with each stage corresponding to a evaluation of
\varphi_{t}^{[A]}
and
\varphi_{t}^{[B]}
. Denoting by
\varphi_{t}(x_{0})
the exact solution, the integrator is said to be of order
r
if:

\psi_{t}(x_{0})=\varphi_{t}(x_{0})+\mathcal{O}(t^{r+1}).

It is well known that compositions of symplectic maps yield symplectic. Since the flows arising in Hamiltonian splitting are themselves symplectic, the overall integrator inherits this property. Splitting schemes preserve the qualitative features of the dynamical system and exhibit favorable long-term energy behavior
[
8
]
. One of the most widely used second-order splitting schemes is the Strang splitting:

\mathcal{S}_{t}^{[2]}=\varphi_{t/2}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{t/2}^{[A]}.

(5)

In this study, we focus on splitting methods with palindromic sequence of coefficients:

\psi_{t}=\varphi_{a_{0}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{0}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{1}t}^{[B]}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{a_{s-2}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{s-2}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{s-1}t}^{[A]},\qquad\text{with }a_{j}=a_{s-j-1},\ b_{j}=b_{s-j-2},

(6)

since such schemes are symmetric in time, i.e.,
\psi_{-t}\circ\psi_{t}\equiv\mathrm{Id}

[
3
]
, where
\mathrm{Id}
denotes the identity transformation.
In other words, advancing by a step
t
and then by a step
-t
returns us to the initial point, thereby preserving, by construction, one of the properties of the exact flow.

Problems of the form
\ddot{y}=g(y)
(of which our system is a particular case) can be treated via splitting by introducing an auxiliary variable
v=\dot{y}
and rewriting the system as:

\dot{x}\equiv(\dot{y},\dot{v})^{\top}=(v,0)^{\top}+(0,g(y))^{\top}=f_{A}(y,v)+f_{B}(y,v),

(7)

where the flows admit exact solutions:

\varphi_{t}^{[A]}(y,v)=(y+tv,v)^{\top},\qquad\varphi_{t}^{[B]}(y,v)=(y,v+tg(y))^{\top}.

As mentioned above, for this kind of problems (
\ddot{y}=g(y)
), specially adapted numerical methods can be constructed.
For instance, Runge–Kutta methods tailored to such equations are known as Runge–Kutta–Nyström (RKN) methods
[
9
]
; by analogy, splitting methods adapted to this class of problems are commonly referred to as RKN splitting methods
[
6
]
.
Splitting schemes of up to sixth order, and up to eighth order in the RKN framework, have been constructed (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
).
However, one of the main challenges in high-order methods is the exponential growth in the number of order conditions required
[
14
]
.
To address this, a highly effective alternative is to construct high-order symplectic integrators using symmetric composition techniques
based on a symmetric second-order method, such as the Strang splitting (
5
):

\mathcal{SS}_{t}=\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{0}t}^{[2]}\circ\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{1}t}^{[2]}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{s-1}t}^{[2]},\qquad\text{with }\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{s-j}.

(8)

This strategy has led to the development of efficient high-order symplectic integrators
[
19
]
, which we use as benchmarks to evaluate the performance of the new methods proposed in this article.

Having introduced the class of problems under consideration and the general framework of splitting methods, we now proceed to derive the order conditions required to construct high-order schemes.

3
Order conditions

Splitting methods for problems of the form
\ddot{y}=g(y)
require fewer order conditions to achieve a given order compared to general splitting methods.
Furthermore, when
g(y)
is a polynomial function, an additional reduction in the number of order conditions occurs.
In this work, we consider the cases where
g(y)
is either quadratic or cubic, corresponding to cubic and quartic potentials, respectively.

As shown in
[
3
,
5
]
, each splitting integrator can be associated with a formal series
\Psi(t)
of differential operators.
In the case of the time-symmetric splitting integrator introduced in the previous section (see equation (
6
)), we have:

\Psi(t)=e^{a_{s-1}t\mathcal{L}_{A}}e^{b_{s-2}t\mathcal{L}_{B}}\cdots e^{b_{0}t\mathcal{L}_{B}}e^{a_{0}t\mathcal{L}_{A}},

where
\mathcal{L}_{A}
and
\mathcal{L}_{B}
denote the Lie derivatives associated with the functions
f_{A}
and
f_{B}
, respectively
[
2
]
. In the case considered here namely,

\ddot{y}=g(y)
with the splitting introduced previously, these operators take the form:

\mathcal{L}_{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{L}_{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}.

Associating a differential operator series
\Psi(t)
to the numerical integrator allows us to apply the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula
[
21
]
, expressing the composition
\Psi(t)
as the exponential of a single operator, i.e.,
\Psi(t)=\exp(Z(t))
, where:

Z(t)=\sum_{i\geq 1}t^{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{i,j}E_{i,j},

(9)

with
E_{i,j}
denoting a basis of independent commutators of order
i
resulting from the application of the BCH formula, where
E_{1,1}=\mathcal{L}_{A}
and
E_{1,2}=\mathcal{L}_{B}
. The integers
n_{i}
represent the number of independent commutators at order
i
, and the coefficients
\omega_{i,j}
are polynomial functions of the splitting coefficients
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
.
Thus, in order for the numerical integrator to be of order
r
, the operator
Z(t)
must satisfy

Z(t)=t(\mathcal{L}_{A}+\mathcal{L}_{B})+\mathcal{O}(t^{r+1}),

which is achieved by setting
\omega_{1,1}=\omega_{1,2}=1
and cancelling all other functions
\omega_{i,j}
for
i\leq r
.
These functions at low orders are of the form
[
3
,
5
]

\displaystyle\omega_{1,1}=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}a_{i},\quad\omega_{1,2}=\sum_{i=0}^{s}b_{i}=1,\quad\omega_{2,1}=\frac{1}{12}\omega_{1,1}\omega_{1,2}-\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq s}b_{i}a_{j}

\displaystyle\omega_{3,1}=\frac{1}{12}\omega_{1,1}^{2}\omega_{1,2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq k\leq s}a_{i}b_{j}a_{k},\quad\omega_{3,2}=\frac{1}{12}\omega_{1,1}\omega_{1,2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{0\leq i\leq j<k\leq s-1}b_{i}a_{j}b_{k}.

Here, the time-symmetry of the integrator implies that all coefficients
\omega_{2k,j}
vanish automatically
[
3
]
.
The choice of commutators
E_{i,j}
is not unique. Below, we list the commutators used up to order 4 in the present work:

E_{11}=\mathcal{L}_{A}
;

E_{12}=\mathcal{L}_{B}
;

E_{21}=[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
;

E_{31}=[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
;

E_{32}=[\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
;

E_{41}=[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
;

E_{42}=[\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
;

E_{43}=[\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{A}]
;

Here, the basic commutator is defined as
[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=\mathcal{L}_{A}\mathcal{L}_{B}-\mathcal{L}_{B}\mathcal{L}_{A}
, and nested commutators are evaluated from right to left, for example,
[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]\equiv[\mathcal{L}_{A},[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]]
. For higher orders, the first element at each level is constructed as
E_{k,1}=[\mathcal{L}_{A},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
with
\mathcal{L}_{A}
appearing
k-1
times.
Table
1
lists the number of independent commutators arising at each order
r
for the general splitting case, denoted by
n_{r}
.

In the next section, we illustrate how the number of order conditions is reduced in the case
\ddot{y}=g(y)
, and how further simplification occurs when
g(y)
is polynomial.

3.1
Reduction of order conditions in
\ddot{y}=g(y)

Using the Lie derivatives introduced earlier, the commutator
[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
takes the form
[
3
]
:

[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=\sum_{j,i=1}^{d}v_{j}\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}.

With this result, the next nested commutator can be computed as
[
3
]
:

[\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=2\sum_{j,i=1}^{d}g_{j}\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}.

As can be seen, this commutator does not depend on the variables
v_{i}
.
Consequently, higher-order nested commutators such as
[\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{B},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
vanish
[
3
,
5
]
,
and many of the terms appearing in expression (
9
) cancel automatically.
These results can be seen in Table
1
, where we list the number of independent commutators that remain at order
r
, denoted by
l_{r}
. In Table
2
,
we show the corresponding minimal number of stages
L_{r}
required by a symmetric numerical integrator when taking advantage of this structural property.

3.2
Reduction of order conditions in
\ddot{y}=g(y)
with polinomical
g

The reduction in the number of order conditions for cubic potentials was first introduced in
[
15
,
12
]
. We now derive the expressions for the nested commutators that make this simplification possible.
Using the expression for
[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]
, we compute:

[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=\sum_{k,j,i=1}^{d}v_{k}v_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}g_{i}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}-2\sum_{j,i=1}^{d}v_{j}\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}},

which corresponds to the element
E_{3,1}
in our notation. Proceeding to compute the next element,
E_{4,1}
:

[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=\sum_{l,k,j,i=1}^{d}v_{l}v_{k}v_{j}\frac{\partial^{3}g_{i}}{\partial y_{l}\partial y_{k}\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}-3\sum_{k,j,i=1}^{d}v_{k}v_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}g_{i}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}.

From these expressions, it is straightforward to generalize the formula for the commutator
E_{n+1,1}
as follows:

[\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{A},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{A}}_{n\text{ times}},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=\sum_{i_{n+1},\dots,i_{1}=1}^{d}v_{i_{n+1}}\cdots v_{i_{2}}\frac{\partial^{n}g_{i_{1}}}{\partial y_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\partial y_{i_{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i_{1}}}-n\sum_{i_{n},\dots,i_{1}=1}^{d}v_{i_{n}}\cdots v_{i_{2}}\frac{\partial^{n-1}g_{i_{1}}}{\partial y_{i_{n}}\cdots\partial y_{i_{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i_{1}}}.

(10)

From this general expression, one can immediately conclude that if
g(y)
is a polynomial of degree
n
, say
g(y)=P_{n}(y)
, then:

g(y)=P_{n}(y)\rightarrow E_{n+3,1}=[\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{A},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{A}}_{n+2\text{ times}},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=0.

(11)

This vanishing occurs because the
(n+1)
-th derivatives of
g(y)
in expression (
10
) are identically zero.
In this work, we are interested in constructing numerical schemes for the cases
n=2,3
. We now examine these two specific cases in detail:

•

. A cubic potential
V(q)=P_{3}(q)
leads to an equation of motion
\ddot{q}=g(q)
where
g(q)=Q_{2}(q)
, with
P_{3}
and
Q_{2}

denoting polynomials of degree 3 and 2, respectively. According to (
11
), the first commutator that vanishes due to this condition is
E_{5,1}=[\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}]=0
.
This results in a reduction in the number of order conditions, in addition to that already induced by the second-order structure
\ddot{y}=g(y)
.
In Table

, the number of independent surviving commutators at order
r
is denoted by
l_{r}^{3}
, while in Table

,
the corresponding minimum number of stages required for a symplectic splitting integrator is indicated by

.

Thus, from (

4
New methods

In the case of general splitting, the flows
\varphi_{t}^{[A]}
and
\varphi_{t}^{[B]}
are interchangeable.
However, in the specific setting considered here, the choice of splitting type (
7
)
and the resulting cancellation of certain commutators require us to distinguish between two types of compositions
[
3
]
.
On the one hand, we consider compositions of type ABA:

\psi_{t}=\varphi_{a_{0}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{0}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{1}t}^{[B]}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{a_{s-1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{s-1}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{s}t}^{[A]},\qquad\text{with }a_{j}=a_{s-j},\ b_{j}=b_{s-j-1},

and on the other hand, compositions of type BAB:

\psi_{t}=\varphi_{b_{0}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{0}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{1}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{1}t}^{[A]}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{b_{s-1}t}^{[B]}\circ\varphi_{a_{s-1}t}^{[A]}\circ\varphi_{b_{s}t}^{[B]},\qquad\text{with }a_{j}=a_{s-j-1},\ b_{j}=b_{s-j}.

The number of stages is counted using the FSAL (First Same As Last) property
[
6
,
4
]
, which allows the last flow of one integration step to be reused as the first flow of the next.

We denote the newly constructed methods using a two-letter code. The first letter is either
\mathcal{C}
or
\mathcal{Q}
, indicating whether the method is designed for cubic or quartic potentials, respectively. The second letter specifies the composition type:
\mathcal{A}
for ABA and
\mathcal{B}
for BAB compositions.

We have selected those methods that showed the best efficiency; additional methods and simulation data are available at the following link:

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19772477

Interestingly, the most efficient methods we obtained were of ABA type.

To solve the order conditions, we employed Python libraries
[
20
]
, specifically
numpy
and
scipy

[
10
,
22
]
. The latter provides wrappers for MINPACK routines
[
16
]
, which implement Powell’s hybrid algorithm
[
18
]
. Among the solutions obtained, we selected those with the smallest
\ell_{1}
norm of the coefficients, defined as:

\ell_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{s}|a_{i}|+\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}|b_{i}|\qquad\text{(ABA)}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\ell_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}|a_{i}|+\sum_{i=0}^{s}|b_{i}|\qquad\text{(BAB)}.

In cases where more stages than the theoretical minimum (see Table
2
) were used,
the additional degrees of freedom were exploited to minimize the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coefficients using
an arc pseudo-continuation technique as described in
[
1
]
.

5.4
Schrödinger equation with quartic potential

Finally, we apply the new 8th-order integrator to the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation (with
\hbar=1
):

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\phi(x,t)+V(x)\phi(x,t),

where
\phi(x,t)
is the wave function describing the physical system, and the potential is given by:

V(x)=-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{20}x^{4}.

Discretizing the spatial variable using a Fourier spectral collocation method yields an
N
-dimensional linear differential equation:

i\dot{u}(t)=Hu(t)=(T+V)u(t),\qquad\text{with }u(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{N},

where
T
is the differentiation matrix resulting from the discretization of the kinetic energy operator,
and
V
is a diagonal matrix representing the potential, with
V_{nn}=V(x_{n})
.
The vector
u
approximates the wave function:
u_{n}(t)\approx\phi(x_{n},t)
.
The action of the matrix
T
on
u
is implemented using forward and backward discrete Fourier transforms
[
13
]
.

shows an efficiency diagram for this test case, comparing our method

\mathcal{SS}

, where
\sigma

The spatial domain is discretized over the interval
x\in[-10,10]
using
N=256
grid points, and time integration is performed up to

.

6
Concluding remarks

In this work, we have shown, using the Lie formalism, why the number of order conditions can be significantly
reduced for Hamiltonian problems with polynomial potentials. This theoretical insight has allowed us to construct
new integrators that outperform the most efficient methods currently available in the literature for both cubic and quartic potentials.

These new integrators should be regarded as highly competitive alternatives when the goal is to integrate
cubic or quartic potentials while preserving the geometric structure of the original system.

The methods presented here can be seen as a natural continuation of the work by
[
12
]
,
and they demonstrate that designing specialized integrators for structured problems can yield efficiency gains of several orders of magnitude.
