A Framework to Model Stellar Irradiated Disks with Frequency-dependent Absorption and Scattering Opacities in Athena++ Athena++ ( 78 ) , DSHARP Mie-Opacity Library ( 6 ) , Matplotlib ( 34 ) , NumPy ( 30 ) , RADMC-3D ( 23 ) , SciPy ( 85 )
Stanley A. Baronett, Yan-Fei Jiang (姜燕飞), Zhaohuan Zhu (朱照寰), Shangjia Zhang (张尚嘉), Philip J. Armitage

Abstract
The frequency dependence of opacity is crucial for determining the thermal structure of protoplanetary disks, which in turn influences disk dynamics and planet formation. Yet many disk models adopt simplified thermodynamics, and common radiation-hydrodynamic approaches often use gray opacities, ignore scattering, and yield inaccurate results in regions with intermediate optical depth. We present a comprehensive framework that models stellar irradiation with frequency-dependent absorption and scattering across all optical depths using the Athena++ finite-volume code, extended with multigroup radiation transport and newly implemented radial rays to more accurately represent the stellar flux. To calibrate this framework, we focus exclusively on hydrostatic disk models, allowing us to isolate radiative effects and evaluate the method without additional dynamical complexity. Because dust opacity increases strongly with frequency, ultraviolet stellar irradiation heats the tenuous disk atmosphere while the optically thick midplane remains cooler. This vertical temperature gradient is captured more accurately when more frequency bands are used or when scattering is included. Our hydrostatic models achieve equilibrium temperatures that differ from Monte Carlo radiative-transfer benchmarks on average by 2–5% with 64 frequency bands and 7–11% with 3 bands. Reducing the number of bands lowers computational cost by at least an order of magnitude while increasing the maximum possible temperature deviation only from 8% to 19%. This calibration demonstrates the accuracy and efficiency of the framework and provides a solid foundation for future self-consistent studies of irradiated protoplanetary disks, including fully dynamical simulations and applications involving chemical processes and time-dependent stellar luminosity.

Introduction

Stellar irradiation is the primary heating mechanism that predominantly sets the temperature throughout a protoplanetary disk
(
26
)
.
This includes complex vertical thermal structures
(
49
;
29
)
recently revealed by observations of molecular-line emission from a variety of sources
(
66
;
80
)
.
Radiation and the resultant structure directly affect complex hydrodynamics, including thermodynamically sensitive instabilities driven by self-gravity, baroclinicity, and buoyancy effects (see
54
and
50
for recent reviews).
Radiation can also influence various aspects of planet formation and migration (see
18
and
67
for recent reviews).

A significant amount of stellar spectral energy is absorbed by dust, whose spatial distribution results from a balance between sedimentation and turbulence
(
21
)
.
Meanwhile, as the grain size distribution evolves
(
22
;
5
)
, so does its overall frequency-dependent opacity
(
6
)
.
Together, these affect the temperature structure
(
13
;
26
;
74
)
and chemical evolution of disks, which involves numerous sublimation regions that result in a complex radial distribution of volatiles
(
48
)
whose condensation and subsequent photochemistry occur on grain surfaces
(
65
)
, which also affects opacities.

Theoretical and computational models require radiation transport to properly account for heating and cooling processes.
Compared to simplified moment equations, the complete angular and frequency dependence of radiative transfer strongly influences the vertical temperature structure
(
25
)
.
By individually tracking an immense number of photon packets and their interactions through a static medium, Monte Carlo methods have accurately reproduced the spectral energy distributions, dust continuum emission, and scattered light observations of many disks.
These achievements have established them as reliable benchmarks against alternative methods
(
69
;
70
)
.

Unfortunately, combining Monte Carlo radiative transfer with hydrodynamics in a self-consistent way poses major conceptual and computational challenges.
Although the review by
63
) summarizes several approaches for mitigating the numerically stiff energy exchange produced by radiative interaction terms—stiffness that likewise limits deterministic (i.e., non–Monte Carlo) transport schemes—practically none of the Monte Carlo codes used for low-energy or nonrelativistic applications is readily compatible with hydrodynamics
(
58
, e.g.,)
.
As a result, other radiation-hydrodynamic methods have provided more effective attempts to investigate self-consistent radiative effects on disk dynamics
(
61
)
, disk instabilities
(

(

;
3
)
.
However, these methods often trade accuracy for performance.

In this radiative transfer approximation, the typically gray (i.e., frequency-independent) flux follows either a free-streaming or diffusion limit in optically thin or thick regions, respectively.
Consequently, it is well known to give inaccurate solutions where optical depths transition from thin to thick

Moreover, due to excessive diffusivity in the free-streaming limit, it is unable to cast shadows

The M1 closure

Unlike flux-limited diffusion or M1, the discrete ordinate method avoids these limitations by solving the complete transfer equation without assuming closure relations
(

The time-dependent equation can be solved implicitly
(
36

to avoid explicit time-step constraints by the speed of light, e.g., in the nonrelativistic environments of protoplanetary disks.
The method can also be extended by a multigroup approach
(
37

to capture the inherent frequency dependence of radiation and opacities, including scattering.
Its accuracy and advantages, as implemented in the open-source astrophysical magnetohydrodynamic code Athena++

Extending the multigroup discrete‑ordinates implementation of

Written in C++ as a successor to Athena
(
77
)

Domain decomposition can also be parallelized for strong scaling

on high-performance-computing resources.
Moreover, the code is modular and supports various physics, including multigroup radiation transport via discrete ordinates
(
37
)
, which can be solved explicitly or implicitly
(

)
.

II.4
Irradiative–Thermal Equilibrium

The temperature dependence of Planck’s law requires careful consideration when used as a weighting function for mean opacities under a multigroup framework.
For a wide band
f
(e.g., where
\nu_{f+1}/\nu_{f}\gtrapprox 3
), the mean opacities defined by equations (
8
) through (
10
) can vary by an order of magnitude or more as the frequency at which
\max[B_{\nu}(T)]
occurs
(cf.
73
, eq. 1.56a)
,

\nu_{\mathrm{peak}}\approx\frac{2.82}{h}k_{\mathrm{B}}T,

(12)

displaces across
f
as a linear function of temperature.
This can be seen for
\kappa_{\mathrm{p},f}(T)
,
\kappa_{\mathrm{r},f}(T)
, and
\kappa_{\mathrm{s},f}(T)
in the left panel of Figure
2
for bands
1\leq f\leq 3
.
Given this potentially immense range in mean opacities for a wide band (e.g., when using a limited number of frequency bands
N_{f}
), we check for
irradiative–thermal equilibrium
to help ensure those used in optically thin regions (e.g., the rarefied yet irradiated disk atmosphere, where the local thermal temperature may be hundreds of Kelvin) are not under weighted by Planck’s law (e.g., evaluated significantly below the effective stellar temperature at thousands of Kelvin).

Irradiative–thermal equilibrium is met when the frequency at the spectral peak of the radiation field is close to
\nu_{\mathrm{peak}}
for the local thermal temperature
T_{\mathrm{therm}}
.
In other words,
T_{\mathrm{therm}}
should be similar to the
color temperature

T_{\mathrm{c}}
which describes an indeterminate blackbody source of unknown absolute scale or distance
(e.g., the host star;
73
, p. 26)
.
Note that local thermodynamic equilibrium neither guarantees irradiative–thermal equilibrium (
T_{\mathrm{c}}\approx T_{\mathrm{therm}}
) nor is guaranteed by it
(
73
, p. 32)
.

To assess irradiative–thermal equilibrium in a given cell, our Athena++ problem generator (Appendix
A
) compares the frequency bands containing the spectral peaks of the specific radiation energy density
E_{\mathrm{r},f}/\Delta\nu_{f}
and a localized blackbody at
T_{\mathrm{therm}}
(i.e.,
f_{\mathrm{irrad}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}f_{\mathrm{therm}}
).
A subroutine determines
f_{\mathrm{irrad}}
as the band
0\leq f<N_{f}-1
that contains
\max(E_{\mathrm{r},f}/\Delta\nu_{f})
, where the radiation energy density
(cf.
37
, eq. 7)

E_{\mathrm{r},f}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\mathrm{ang}}-1}I_{f}(\hat{\mathbf{{n}}})w_{n}

(13)

for the specific intensity along each angle
I_{f}(\hat{\mathbf{{n}}})
, and where the bandwidth
\Delta\nu_{f}=\nu_{f+1}-\nu_{f}
(Section
II.1
).
The highest frequency band
f=N_{f}-1=[\nu_{N_{f}-1},\infty)
is excluded from the search, as
E_{\mathrm{r},N_{f}-1}/\Delta\nu_{N_{f}-1}
always evaluates to zero.
To identify
f_{\mathrm{therm}}
, the Wien displacement law [equation (
12
)] is used to compute
\nu_{\mathrm{peak}}(T_{\mathrm{therm}})
before another subroutine identifies finds
f
where
\nu_{f}<\nu_{\mathrm{peak}}(T_{\mathrm{therm}})<\nu_{f+1}
.

If
f_{\mathrm{irrad}}=f_{\mathrm{therm}}
, which typically occurs in optically thick regions [e.g., the dense disk midplane shielded from stellar irradiation; Section
III.1
and equation (
19
)], the code interpolates the absorption opacity coefficient
\kappa_{\mathrm{p},f,\mathrm{e}}
[of the second mean radiation energy density
J_{0,f}
on the right-hand side of equation (
6
)] at
T_{\mathrm{therm}}
, as usual (Section
II.3
).
However, if
f_{\mathrm{irrad}}\neq f_{\mathrm{therm}}
, the code interpolates
\kappa_{\mathrm{p},f,\mathrm{e}}
at
T_{\mathrm{c}}
instead (e.g., closer to the effective stellar temperature
T_{*}
).
We estimate
T_{\mathrm{c}}
by solving equation (
12
) for
T
but use the arithmetic center of
f_{\mathrm{irrad}}
as
\nu_{\mathrm{peak}}=(\nu_{f_{\mathrm{irrad}}}+\nu_{f_{\mathrm{irrad}}+1})/2
.
As the error in estimating
T_{\mathrm{c}}
scales with the bandwidth
\Delta\nu_{f_{\mathrm{irrad}}}
, the overall condition becomes less important for narrow bands or higher
N_{f}
since

\lim_{N_{f}\to\infty}\begin{cases}\kappa_{\mathrm{p},f(,\mathrm{e})}=\kappa_{\mathrm{a},f}=\kappa_{\nu}^{\mathrm{abs}},\\
\kappa_{\mathrm{s},f}=\kappa_{\nu}^{\mathrm{sca}}\end{cases}

(14)

[see also Section
III.2
and equation (
22
)].

IV.1.1

To compare the equilibrium dust temperatures of stellar irradiated disk models (Section

and

V
Discussion

This section discusses our multigroup radiation transport framework (Section
II
) and our stellar-irradiated disk model (Section
III.1
) in the broader context of protoplanetary disk modeling.
Section
V.1
contrasts this framework with alternative radiation-hydrodynamic approaches and implementations.
Section
V.2
outlines various applications of our framework to self-consistently investigate the hydrodynamic and thermodynamic properties of stellar-irradiated disks.

V.1
Alternative Approaches

In an attempt to account for the frequency dependence of stellar radiation,
45
combined flux-limited diffusion (Section
I
) with ray tracing in the magnetohydrodynamics code PLUTO
(
59
)
.
Specifically, stellar irradiation was modeled with a first-order ray-tracing routine with frequency-dependent absorption opacities [cf. equation (
28
)], while dust thermal reemission was approximated by flux-limited diffusion with gray opacity.
However, scattering is neglected, as it makes the radiative transfer equation integrodifferential
(
73
, § 1.7)
.

Similar to Section
IV.3.1
,
45
compared the temperature profiles of their static models with those from the predecessor of RADMC-3D (Section
IV.1.1
), RADMC
(
24
;
21
)
.
Along an optically thick disk midplane, they reported relative errors of up to 11.1% for their hybrid method with frequency-dependent irradiation but up to 38.4% with gray irradiation.
They also showed qualitative agreement of meridional (i.e., polar) profiles between their hybrid method with frequency-dependent irradiation and RADMC but only at
r=2\,\mathrm{au}
.
However, in an extended follow-up comparison of their frequency-dependent hybrid method and RADMC,
46
found temperatures along an even more optically thick midplane deviated as much as 48% as
\tau
approaches
10^{3}
and remained above 35% for
10^{3}\lesssim\tau\lesssim 10^{6}
(cf. Table
2
and Figure
10
).

Following
45
,
42
implemented this hybrid method into the hydrodynamic code TRAMP
(
38
)
.
However, similar to
91
, they used only mean (i.e., gray) opacities for both the dust thermal reemission and the ray-traced stellar irradiation, each evaluated at the respective local dust and effective stellar temperatures
(
42
, eqs. 3 and 4, respectively)
, instead of frequency-dependent opacities and flux for the latter.
Although they assumed a global dust-to-gas mass ratio of 1% in TRAMP, they used the grid cell temperatures resulting from their radiation-hydrodynamic snapshots to adjust the local dust densities
(
42
, eq. 12)
in their comparative hydrostatic models produced with the Monte Carlo radiative transfer code Mol3D
(
64
)
.
Despite this a posteriori modification to improve the consistency of absorption coefficients
(i.e.,
\rho\kappa
;
73
, eq. 1.22)
between models, they found relative differences as high as 33%
(
42
, § 3.1.1 and Figs. 3, 4)
.

In a recent attempt to address the artificial interactions between crossing beams inherent to the M1 closure method (Section
I
),
57
implemented into PLUTO a half-moment closure method
(
20
)
, which integrates the specific intensity over hemispheres instead of all solid angles.
Neglecting scattering, their M1 and half-moment disk models with 22 frequency groups disagree with RADMC-3D by up to 21% and 6%, respectively, or with 3 groups by up to 23% and 8%, respectively.
However, the half-moment method cannot be generalized to all radiative interactions, as allowable beam crossings are restricted only to a preselected direction
(
57
, Fig. 2)
.

V.2
Potential Applications

Using the larger Athena++ hydrodynamic framework (Section
II
), our multigroup framework can facilitate radiation hydrodynamics investigations into a variety of protoplanetary disk models with frequency-dependent stellar irradiation, absorption, and scattering.
To compare with hydrostatic models from Monte Carlo radiative transfer methods, we set
\rho_{0}
(Section
III.1
) so the density field defined by equation (
15
) represents only dust with the size distribution specified in Section
III.2
.
However, depending on the dust-to-gas mass ratio,
\rho_{0}
and the dust opacities can be adjusted when evolving the gas disk (cf. Section
III.3
), which RADMC-3D cannot do.

Our current model (Section
III.1
) straightforwardly applies to disks with large-scale substructures, e.g., depleted gaps.
Although it was designed to mitigate challenges to the implicit solver from an optically thick inner radial boundary to stellar irradiation, our radially softened density profile, defined by equation (
20
) and shown in Figure
3
(blue curve), can effectively model a disk outside the orbit of a planet that carved a gap at or within
10\,\mathrm{au}
.
In this case, equations (
3
) and (
5
) could be adjusted to more accurately capture the prior attenuation of stellar radiation by dust inside the orbit (i.e.,
r<r_{\mathrm{min}}
).

On the other hand, since equations (
3
) and (
5
) assume
R_{*}<r<r_{\mathrm{min}}
is optically thin, our current flux of stellar irradiation through the radial boundary readily models a transitional disk
(
27
)
with an inner dust-depleted cavity
(
84
)
.
Despite having truncated dust distributions, transitional sources exhibit active accretion, indicating the presence of gas in the cavity.
In the hydrodynamic case, this dust truncation radius, including this optically thin inner region of gas, can be modeled by softening the opacity field, instead of the (gas) density as in equation (
20
).
As suggested in Section
III.1
, softening the opacity coefficients [equations (
8
)–(
10

Since Athena++ evolves models in time (Section

Similarly, stellar irradiated circumbinary disks can be studied by making these equations periodic.

Our framework can also be used to study disk chemistry (Section

Frequency-dependent radiative transfer has previously been combined with various (photo)chemical and physical processes to compute hydrostatic disk models and interpret gas emission lines
(

Moreover, established thermochemical codes already use Monte Carlo methods

(

Thus, the flexibility of our framework could complement or even be combined with thermochemical codes to simultaneously evolve the distribution, phases, and interactions of molecules relevant to planet formation.
As disk dynamics affects temperature, which affects chemistry, which affects ionization, which affects disk dynamics, this coupled feedback loop should be studied self-consistently, which cannot be done in RADMC-3D.

VI
Conclusions

We introduce a comprehensive framework for developing accurate, self-consistent irradiated disk models with frequency-dependent absorption and scattering for radiation hydrodynamics in Athena++ (Section
II
).
We added radial rays to the multigroup radiation transport module (Section
II.1
) to capture stellar irradiation (Section
II.2
and Figure
1
) and systematically evaluate band-mean opacities (Section
II.3
and Figure
2
) at the local or color temperature depending on irradiative–thermal equilibrium (Section
II.4
).
To demonstrate our framework in the hydrostatic limit (Section
III
), we formulate an axisymmetric disk model (Figures
3
,
4
, and
5
), irradiated by a sunlike star (Section
III.1
);
and using the dust opacities adopted by the DSHARP project (Section
III.2
), we hydrostatically evolve the radiation field (Figure
7
) until it reaches thermal equilibrium (Section
III.3
and Figure
6
).

We thoroughly study the accuracy of our framework by comparing the equilibrium temperatures of hydrostatic models (Section
IV
) primarily against the Monte Carlo radiative transfer code RADMC-3D (Section
IV.1.1
).
Compared to an analytic solution for purely attenuated ray tracing (Section
IV.2.1
), the radial flux is slightly higher, but the relative error decreases linearly overall with increasing radial resolution (Figure
8
).
Across at least 13 orders of magnitude in optical depth that can reach above
10^{4}
(Table
2
), our models with 64 frequency bands differ from RADMC-3D (with 150 wavelength points) by less than 6% on average and at most 11% for gray opacity (Section
IV.2.2
), but similar on average and at most 8% for frequency-dependent absorption (Section
IV.3.1
) and with scattering (Section
IV.3.2
).
Compared to gray models, the difference between atmospheric and midplane temperatures is at least three times greater with frequency-dependent opacities (cf. Figures
9
,
10
, and Figure
11
).
Moreover, the difference between isotropic and anisotropic scattering is less than 4% on average and at most 9% (Figure
12
).

We also study the convergence and performance of our framework with respect to key resolution parameters in the radiation module (Section
IV.4
).
In general, increasing the number of frequency bands (Section
IV.4.1
) or angular rays (Section
IV.4.2
) increases the accuracy (Figures
13
and
14
) but also linearly increases the total computational time (Section
IV.4.3
and Figure
15
).
However, reducing 64 logarithmically uniform bands to just 3 optimized bands (Figure
16
) reduces the computation time by at least an order of magnitude while only increasing potential inaccuracies by two to three times (Section
IV.5
and Figure
17
).

We discuss our framework in the broader context of protoplanetary disk modeling (Section
V
).
We review the limitations of alternative approaches to radiation hydrodynamics (Section
V.1
), including hybrid methods using frequency-dependent ray-tracing and flux-limited diffusion (cf. Section
IV.1.2
) as well as a half-moment variation of the M1 closure.
For the former, ray tracing is unable to incorporate scattering effects, and comparisons with Monte Carlo methods showed deviations as high as 48% with increasing optical depth.
Since radiative energy scales with
T^{4}
, such temperature deviations can produce substantial errors in the estimated energy budget, reinforcing the need to accurately capture radiative heating.
For the latter, the half-moment closure allows radiation beams to cross but only in one preselected direction.

Lastly, we outline potential applications of our framework to examine the hydrodynamic properties of stellar-irradiated disks (Section
V.2
).
Our current model already applies to transitional disks or can be tuned for those with large-scale substructures, e.g., planet-carved gaps.
As the Athena++ hydrodynamic code evolves systems in time, our flexible framework can model dynamic irradiation (e.g., from accretion outbursts or binary host stars) or compliment thermochemical codes.
We maintain and make available our open-source resources in a public repository (Appendix
A
).

Appendix C
Hybrid Ray-Tracing Method

The hybrid ray-tracing scheme computes the stellar irradiation as the sum of wavelength-dependent fluxes, whereas the disk thermal radiation is treated in the single-frequency approximation, following the implementation of
45
.
Compared to the two-temperature approach (one temperature for the star and one for the disk) used in
91
, this method improves the accuracy of the temperature calculations.
This is because longer-wavelength stellar photons, which exhibit lower optical depths, can penetrate deeper into the disk and heat regions closer to the midplane.
In other words, the surface where
\tau=1
is no longer a sharp boundary.
Instead, each frequency has its own surface, where
\tau_{\nu}=1
, and longer wavelength infrared photons have
\tau=1
surfaces closer to the midplane.

To calculate the heating from stellar irradiation, we compute the radial flux in each frequency bin as [cf. equation (
28
)]:

F_{*}(\nu_{i})=e^{-\tau_{\nu_{i}}^{\mathrm{abs}}}\frac{c}{4}\int_{(\nu_{i-1}+\nu_{i})/2}^{(\nu_{i}+\nu_{i+1})/2}B_{\nu}(T_{*})\mathrm{d}\nu,

(C1)

where the optical depth to absorption at frequency
\nu
is given by [cf. equation (
19
)]:

\tau_{\nu}^{\mathrm{abs}}(r,\theta,\phi)=\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r}\rho(r^{\prime},\theta,\phi)\kappa_{\nu}^{\mathrm{abs}}\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}.

(C2)

The total stellar irradiation flux is then the sum over all frequency bins:

F_{*,\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i}F_{*}(\nu_{i}).

(C3)

If the opacity is wavelength independent, this expression reduces to equation (
28
).
We use the same monochromatic absorption opacity specified in Section
III.2
with 200 logarithmically uniform wavelength bins, sampling between 0.1
\mu
m and 10 cm.
