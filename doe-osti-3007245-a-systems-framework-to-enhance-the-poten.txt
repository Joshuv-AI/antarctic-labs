Executive Summary
 To alleviate the dependency of the United States on fossil fuels, particularly from foreign
sources, the transition to biofuel crops has long been proposed as a crucial part of the long-term
solution. Camelina sativa has emerged as one of the most promising oilseed crops for domestic
biofuel and bioproduct production due to its low agronomic input requirements, natural resilience
to a wide range of biotic and abiotic stresses, and the successful testing and approval of its oil-
based blends for use in liquid transportation fuels.
 Despite these advantages, broader adoption of Camelina is limited by its modest yield. Efforts
to improve productivity have been constrained by an incomplete understanding of the gene
regulatory networks that control plant growth and environmental responses, as well as by the
limited insight into the extent of genetic diversity and gene content across Camelina accessions.
This project directly addresses these knowledge gaps by generating foundational genomic
resources and developing tools that will enable more efficient breeding and engineering of
resilient, high-yielding cultivars.
 Over the course of this project, we sequenced the genome of Suneson, one of the most widely
used Camelina sativa varieties, and produced the first Camelina pangenome. These analyses
allowed us to identify the dominant subgenome, providing an opportunity to leverage information
from diploid ancestors for genome-selection strategies. We also built a comprehensive Camelina
transcription factor open reading frame collection (TFome), which is already facilitating the
identification of protein–DNA interactions that shape gene expression programs.
 To make these resources broadly accessible, we enhanced the CamRegBase
knowledgebase with interactive network-visualization tools and began integrating chromatin
accessibility profiles with mRNA accumulation data during seed development and under water-
deprivation stress. These datasets are now enabling the characterization of Conserved
Noncoding Sequences and other important cis-regulatory elements. Leveraging these insights,
we developed a suite of promoters capable of driving cell- and tissue-specific expression,
providing valuable tools for trait engineering.
 Finally, as part of our effort to strengthen the Camelina research community, we helped
organize the inaugural International Camelina Conference in 2024 and contributed to establishing
its next meeting in 2026.
 Together, these accomplishments provide essential genomic knowledge, powerful molecular
tools, and a strengthened research community, resources that will collectively accelerate the
development of more resilient and higher-yielding Camelina cultivars in support of U.S. bioenergy
goals.

Project Objectives
 The goal of this project has been to develop and centralize tools for Camelina research and
fill the knowledge gap of gene regulatory networks to improve the potential of developing
Camelina as an oilseed crop for biofuel and agricultural use. By employing the following
objectives, we provide community-accessible resources to study Camelina genes that confer
important agronomic traits.

Objective 1: Characterize the genetic variation in spring and winter Camelina varieties
Objective 2: Map the accessible chromatin space in different organs, varieties and conditions
Objective 3: Build a transcription factor (TF) open reading frame collection (TFome)
Objective 4: Use the TFome to map genome-wide protein-DNA interactions (PDIs) and integrate
with gene expression and genome accessibility into a systems network.
Objective 5: Develop a promoter collection to deploy genes with temporal/spatial definition.

Accomplishments
Objective 1: Characterize the genetic variation in spring and winter Camelina varieties
 To accomplish this objective, we first needed to generate a high-quality genome for Camelina
sativa cv. ‘Suneson’, which is a mid-season variety. This new assembly recovered an additional
5,326 genes compared with the previous DH55 genome. Additional information about the genome
assembly can be found in Brock et. al., 20241. Annotated genome and associated data files are
available in CamRegBase (https://camregbase.org/), and raw data on NCBI-SRA under
accession numbers SRX25825928-SRX25825946, SRX25826254, SRX25827247, and
SRX25827248.

 Our team published the first Camelina pangenome2, a study initiated in the Edger lab, in part
supported by this grant, and led by former graduate student, Dr. Kevin Bird. The study was
conducted in collaboration with the US Department of Energy Joint Genome Institute and the
resulting pangenome comprises twelve additional genomes spanning both spring and winter
varieties. In addition to the genome assembly, this study explores subgenome expression
dominance across tissue types and genetic diversity between subgenomes, as shown in Figure
1. We found that Subgenome 3 unique orthogroups were most enriched for processes including
defense response, response to auxin, and response to biotic stimulus. Abiotic and biotic stress
resistance are crucial traits for developing resilient and high-yielding crop varieties. Further
investigation of these Subgenome 3-specific genes could reveal valuable candidates for breeding
programs aimed at enhancing stress tolerance in crops.
 The loss of Conserved Noncoding Sequences (CNSs), which harbor crucial cis-regulatory
elements (CREs), is a significant mechanism driving gene expression divergence among
homoeologs in polyploid species. The discovery of CNSs across the Brassicaceae family was
initially accomplished by identifying deeply conserved sequences through whole-genome
alignments, which were then functionally linked to regulatory elements associated with genes 3.
The Edger lab was involved in this foundational study, and as part of this grant, we have been
further analyzing these CNSs in Camelina sativa, moving beyond their initial discovery into their
functional characterization in the context of polyploidy and to identify unknown drought responsive
elements and genes. The elimination of these CNSs is frequently associated with notable
changes in gene expression, often resulting in the loss of specific spatial or temporal expression
patterns. We have leveraged this phenomenon to study and compare the evolution of drought-

responsive genes and pathways in Camelina relative to its diploid progenitors. Specifically, we
are using the targeted loss of CREs as a strategy to functionally annotate these regulatory
elements. This approach permits us to identify and characterize novel regulatory elements
currently not characterized in Camelina, as well as other related Brassicaeae crops, or even well-
studied model organisms like Arabidopsis.
 Our next focus turned to Camelina neglecta as a diploid model for functional validation.
Growth conditions were optimized for greenhouse and growth chambers. We developed a
transformation protocol for Camelina neglecta, an Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip method
modified from that used for Camelina sativa which resulted in comparable transformation
efficiencies to Camelina sativa of 0.1% positive transformants, as determined by red fluorescence
provided by the seed-specific expression of the DsRed visual marker. Putative positive
transformants were grown and genotyped for confirmation. This transformation protocol was then
used to target a transcription factor using CRISPR-Cas9, with mutant lines already identified for
further analysis. Camelina neglecta was also grown for gene expression analyses of 41-day old
plants green tissue and roots under well-watered and water deprivation conditions. Analysis of
this data is currently underway.

Objective 2: Map the accessible chromatin space in different organ, varieties and conditions
 We mapped the accessible chromatin space for embryos from five seed developmental stages
ranging from 15 days post-anthesis (DPA) to 30 DPA using ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-
Accessible Chromatin sequencing), and integrated chromatin accessibility results with gene
expression data obtained from RNA-seq. Early stages of seed development show accessible sites
for genes related to fatty acid biosynthesis and lipid metabolism, and later stages show more
accessible site for genes related to seed dormancy. Of the expressed genes, only 37% show
accessible chromatin regions near transcription start sites and transcription termination sites. For
the genes identified to have differential accessibility regions within these regulatory domains, 18%
also displayed differential expression.
 To functionally validate the regulatory elements associated with drought-responsive gene
expression, we integrated ATAC-seq data with RNA-seq data and our analysis of CNSs. The
ATAC-seq data provided genome-wide maps of chromatin accessibility, indicating open,
regulatory regions likely to contain active CREs. By correlating changes in chromatin accessibility
(from ATAC-seq) with corresponding changes in gene expression (from RNA-seq) under both
mild and extreme drought conditions, we were able to significantly refine and further validate the
 specific regulatory elements influencing
 drought-responsive expression patterns
 (Manuscript in preparation).

Impact
 This project was designed to have impact for the entire camelina research community. The
tools and resources developed throughout this grant are publicly available through
camregbase.org, and include 1) the new Suneson genome, 2) the Camelina sativa TFome, 3)
protocols for transformation of the diploid Camelina neglecta, 4) RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data
during seed development, and of Camelina sativa and Camelina neglecta plants subjected to
water deprivation, 5) expansion of CamRegBase and incorporation of the PDI interface.
CamRegBase has logged over 1.8 million views since its development.
 Dr. Edger and Dr. Brock worked in collaboration with the US Department of Energy Joint
Genome Institute on the first Camelina Pangenome.
 Dr. Grotewold helped develop, raise funds and served as Chair of the scientific committee for
the first International Camelina Conference held on July 19-20, 2024 in Lincoln, Nebraska.
 This grant has provided the opportunity for training of 26 undergraduate students, including
the completion of senior research thesis projects for two of these students. Training was also
provided to one post-baccalaureate scholar, two PhD students, and four postdoctoral fellows who
have moved on to various academic and industry positions.
