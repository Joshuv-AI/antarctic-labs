Toward a Candidate Endoscopic Spine Surgery Registry Framework: Lessons From Global Spine Registry Models
Authors: Vit Kotheeranurak, Jin-Sung Kim, Inbo Han, Hyeun-Sung Kim, Thanapol Ultarnpatumros, Chinati Puinongpo, Javier Quillo-Olvera, Lei Jiang, Christoph Siepe, Worawat Limthongkul, Weerasak Singhatanadgige, Surachat Jaroenwareekul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndoscopic spine surgery (ESS) has expanded rapidly, but existing spine registries do not consistently capture ESS-specific technical variables, ultra-early recovery trajectories, selective imaging validation, or dissemination-related learning-curve effects. This review synthesizes established spine registry models, contemporary consensus initiatives, and digital follow-up approaches to propose a candidate ESS-specific registry framework and preliminary Minimum Data Set (MDS) for future validation. MethodsA structured narrative review was conducted on national and multinational spine registries, relevant consensus initiatives, and digital follow-up models. Sources were reviewed with emphasis on governance, data architecture, workflow design, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) integration, follow-up structure, data linkage, validation strategies, and implementation feasibility. ResultsEstablished registries provide a methodological foundation for benchmarking, complication surveillance, longitudinal PROM capture, and real-world evidence generation. However, ESS requires modular augmentation of existing registry infrastructures to capture approach-specific operative details, early recovery, selective imaging phenotypes, and governance-controlled maturity indicators. The proposed candidate MDS is organized into 4 domains: (1) baseline case-mix and phenotyping, (2) procedure-specific technical and perioperative variables, (3) selective quantitative imaging and morphometric validation, and (4) structured longitudinal outcome surveillance. The framework prioritizes an essential core dataset while separating recommended, optional, research-oriented, and governance-controlled modules, including digitally enabled early follow-up, opioid-related outcomes, radiation exposure, advanced imaging, and learning-curve variables. ConclusionA candidate ESS-specific registry framework may support harmonized data capture, real-world evidence generation, quality feedback, and future registry-based research. Formal Delphi consensus, multicenter feasibility testing, and staged integration into existing spine registry infrastructures are required before broader implementation.

INTRODUCTION

Clinical registries have become central to value-based spine care by enabling real-world evidence generation, outcome benchmarking, and longitudinal follow-up across heterogeneous patient populations [1-3]. Over the past 3 decades, established spine registry models have standardized outcome collection for conventional open and microscopic procedures, particularly through validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). Collectively, these registries provide an important methodological foundation for contemporary spine outcomes research [2-17].

Concurrently, endoscopic spine surgery (ESS) has expanded as a technology-intensive procedural model with technical and recovery characteristics that are not consistently represented in conventional registry structures [18-21]. ESS-specific variables include access corridors, optical configurations, fluoroscopy-related exposure, anesthesia pathways, conversion to open surgery, and endoscopic procedure-specific complications [2,21-24]. In addition, potential advantages of ESS, such as early pain relief, early ambulation, and reduced short-term analgesic requirements, may occur before conventional registry timepoints [25,26]. The recognized learning-curve sensitivity of ESS further supports structured surveillance of procedural maturity and volume-outcome relationships during dissemination [27-29].

Several contemporary initiatives have already addressed overlapping aspects of ESS outcome standardization and registrybased outcome measurement [30-32]. The International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) Low Back Pain Standard Set has provided a basis for PROM harmonization across multiple spine outcome systems, while the Neurocore-SENSED Delphi consensus has advanced expert-derived standardization for ESS-specific reporting and data elements [30,31]. In parallel, prospective digital follow-up initiatives such as the Endoscopic Spine Research Group (ESRG) SpineHealthie platform have demonstrated the feasibility of high-frequency postoperative PROM capture during the early recovery period [26,32,33]. Building on these efforts, the present review is not intended to replace ICHOM or Neurocore-SENSED, but to address the remaining registry-implementation gap: how ESS-specific technical, temporal, imaging, workflow, and governance variables can be stratified, captured, and integrated into existing registry infrastructures without creating a competing standard [30-33].

Standardized PROM collection should remain central to ESS registry development, while selected objective imaging measures may complement PROM-based outcomes in specific contexts, including research-oriented cohorts, symptomatic or revision cases, and instrumented or fusion procedures [2,8,31,34,35]. Although emerging comparative evidence has begun to clarify short- to midterm outcomes of endoscopic decompression strategies, longer-term durability, reoperation patterns, opioid-related outcomes, and safety during dissemination and learning-curve phases remain incompletely characterized [25,27,29,36,37]. These aims are best supported by registry infrastructure that minimizes manual data-entry burden through electronic PROM capture, mobile health interfaces, automated reminders, and linkage with electronic patient records (EPRs) where available [15,17,32,33,38,39].

This narrative review examines established spine registry models alongside contemporary consensus and digital follow-up initiatives to identify transferable design principles for ESS registry development. On this basis, the review proposes a tiered, interoperable, and governance-aware candidate ESS-specific framework and preliminary Minimum Data Set (MDS), structured around an essential core dataset with optional researchoriented modules. This framework is intended to support future Delphi-based consensus development, multicenter feasibility testing, and staged integration into existing spine registry infrastructures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This structured narrative review synthesizes established spine registry models, relevant consensus initiatives, and digital follow-up approaches to inform development of a candidate ESSspecific registry framework. A registry-focused approach was selected because ESS outcomes and safety are influenced by approach-specific technical factors, early postoperative recovery profiles, procedural maturity, and learning-curve effects that remain inconsistently captured in conventional spine registry structures. The primary aim was to identify transferable registry design principles and propose a conceptual framework rather than comprehensively map all available evidence. Accordingly, the proposed MDS should be interpreted as candidate variables pending formal Delphi-based consensus, stakeholder validation, and prospective feasibility testing. The review was planned and reported in accordance with the SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) [40].

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library for relevant publications available through January 2026; selected technical standards and registry specifications were additionally checked for current versions during manuscript revision. Search terms used Boolean combinations across 4 concept domains: (1) registry methodology and infrastructure; (2) major benchmark spine registries, including Swespine, NORspine, DaneSpine, Spine Tango, the American Spine Registry (ASR), Canadian Spine Outcomes and Research Network (CSORN), Japanese Orthopedic Association National Registry (JOANR), and FinSpine; (3) ESS-related outcomes methodology, including endoscopic techniques, PROM methodology, and minimal clinically important relative change (MCIRC); and (4) contemporary consensus and digital follow-up initiatives relevant to ESS outcome standardization, including ICHOM, Neurocore-SENSED, ESRG, SpineHealthie, smartphone-based PROM capture, and remote patient monitoring. Exact PubMed search strings are provided in Supplementary Material 1. To capture operational details not uniformly described in the indexed literature, backward citation tracking was performed along with a review of nonindexed official registry reports, data dictionaries, governance documents, and platform-related publications. No language restrictions were applied.

Sources were selected according to their relevance to registry architecture, data governance, data ownership, data dictionaries, collection workflows, PROM integration, follow-up reporting, validation strategies, digital implementation and registry-based methodological applications rather than comparative treatment effects. Extracted features were synthesized using a deductive thematic approach and iteratively reviewed by the authors to identify transferable design principles, feasibility constraints, and areas of overlap with existing consensus or operational initiatives. This synthesis informed the derivation of a candidate ESS framework comprising 4 interoperable domains: (1) baseline case-mix and phenotyping, (2) procedure-specific technical and perioperative variables, (3) selective imaging and morphometric validation, and (4) structured longitudinal outcome surveillance. No formal Delphi process, stakeholder survey, society endorsement, or prospective pilot implementation was performed as part of this review; these steps are proposed as future validation pathways. 1. Evolution and Current Landscape of Spine Registries

Established spine registries have evolved from local observational databases into national and multinational platforms for outcome benchmarking, safety surveillance, health policy evaluation and real-world evidence generation [1,2,9,17]. These systems differ in governance, architecture, data-entry workflow, PROM integration, and follow-up structure, as summarized in Table 1 [2,3].

Several registry models provide transferable lessons for ESS. Scandinavian and Nordic registries illustrate the value of national coverage, longitudinal PROM capture, long-term follow-up, case-mix adjustment, and linkage with national health infrastructure [10,14,15,17,41]. Multinational and society-led registries, including Spine Tango, British Spine Registry, CSORN, and DWG, highlight the importance of modular architecture, professional governance, multicenter benchmarking, audit-based verification, and research-oriented data structures [6,9,11,13,42]. More recent digital and procedural registry models, including ASR and JOANR/Japanese Society for Spine Surgery and Related Research Database, emphasize scalable electronic infrastructure, institutional participation, and procedural capture [3,7,43].

Taken together, these models suggest that ESS-specific registry development should proceed as modular augmentation rather than wholesale registry replacement. Added ESS fields should focus on procedure-specific technical capture, digitally enabled early recovery assessment, and governance-controlled maturity indicators for dissemination-phase safety [21,23,26,27,29,33]. This approach preserves the strengths of established registries while ensuring that ESS-specific granularity can be collected with sufficient completeness, interpreted consistently, and governed appropriately across participating centers.

2. Methodological Considerations in Registry-Based ESS Research

The methodological validity of registry-based ESS research depends on 3 interrelated safeguards: data completeness, standardized outcome interpretation, and risk-adjusted comparability. Because registry data are observational, these safeguards are essential to reduce attrition bias, outcome heterogeneity, treatment-selection bias, and learning-curve confounding during cross-center or cross-technique comparisons. Governance is particularly important when added ESS-specific granularity includes surgeon-, center-, or maturity-related variables.

1) Data completeness and attrition control

Longitudinal attrition remains one of the principal threats to registry validity because missing follow-up data may distort estimates of effectiveness, complications, and reoperation risk [1,2]. International registry experience demonstrates substantial variability in case completeness and PROM follow-up, and a followup rate of approximately 75%–80% is often considered a pragmatic benchmark for robust registry analysis [2,3,10]. Moderate loss to follow-up does not necessarily introduce major bias when baseline variables are comprehensively captured and appropriately adjusted; however, ESS registries may require particular attention to retention during early adoption phases, when safety signals and learning-curve effects are most relevant [17,27,29,41,44]. Therefore, any candidate ESS registry framework should pair essential outcome collection with predefined retention and missing-data monitoring procedures, together with transparent reporting of follow-up completeness [3,13,15,17].

2) Outcome standardization and MCID/MCIRC interpretation

Standardized outcome measurement is foundational for cross-registry comparability. Core PROMs such as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), Neck Disability Index, pain visual analogue scale/Numerical Rating Scale (VAS/NRS), and Euro-Qol-5 dimension; (EQ-5D) remain widely used across major spine registries and should anchor ESS outcome assessment [2,8,17]. Interpretation of postoperative change should primarily retain established absolute-change metrics such as the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), particularly because MCID thresholds for lumbar spine surgery vary by population, pathology, and PROM instrument [8]. Relative-change metrics, including the MCIRC, may provide complementary information by accounting for baseline symptom severity and may improve comparability across heterogeneous baseline scores [45,46]. Relative change can be calculated as: (follow-up score − baseline score)/baseline score×100, with the direction of improvement interpreted according to each PROM scale. However, current MCIRC thresholds are derived mainly from cervical or non-ESS cohorts, and percent-improvement thresholds in lumbar spine surgery should not be assumed to be directly transferable to ESS [45,46]. Therefore, in lumbar ESS registries, MCIRC should be considered exploratory until ESS-specific thresholds are derived and externally validated. Candidate registries should collect baseline and follow-up PROMs in a format that permits future evaluation of both absolute and relative improvement metrics without treating MCIRC as a finalized benchmark. Accordingly, MCIRC should be reported, if used, as a secondary exploratory analytic metric rather than as a primary registry benchmark until lumbar ESS-specific thresholds are established.

3) Risk adjustment, propensity-based modeling, and learningcurve confounding

Because registry data are observational, risk adjustment is essential to mitigate treatment-selection bias, institutional heterogeneity, and case-mix imbalance [1,2,17]. Standard baseline variables, including age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification grade, revision status, diagnosis, operated level, and baseline PROM severity, support fairer cross-center and cross-technique comparisons [1,3,8,17]. Propensity score matching, weighting, or related propensity-based approaches may strengthen comparative-effectiveness analyses by improving balance in measured covariates between treatment groups, particularly when comparing ESS techniques with conventional microscopic or minimally invasive approaches [1,17]. However, these methods require adequate sample size, standardized variable definitions, and high baseline data completeness, and they cannot fully address unmeasured expertise-related confounding.

ESS introduces additional sources of residual confounding, including procedural maturity, surgeon experience, center volume, and evolving technology [21,27,29,44]. These factors may influence operative time, complications, conversion, recurrence, and early recovery, but they require careful governance if captured at surgeon or center level [27,29,44]. Accordingly, learning-curve and maturity indicators should be treated as optional governancecontrolled analytical variables rather than universal public-reporting fields. DISCUSSION

1. Proposed Candidate ESS-Specific Registry Framework

Building on the registry design principles and methodological safeguards outlined above, this review proposes a candidate modular ESS-specific registry framework for future consensus development and feasibility testing. It is conceived as an interoperable, additive module rather than a competing standalone registry, with the goal of reducing data fragmentation across registry platforms.

The preliminary MDS, summarized in Table 3, is organized across 4 interdependent domains: (1) baseline case-mix and patient phenotyping, (2) procedure-specific technical and perioperative variables, (3) selective imaging and morphometric validation, and (4) structured longitudinal outcome surveillance. To balance procedural granularity with implementation feasibility, variables are stratified into an essential core dataset and recommended, optional, or research-oriented modules.

The comparative review found recurring ESS-relevant gaps in existing registry systems, especially in procedure-specific technical variables, ultra-early recovery capture, selective imaging validation, opioid-related outcomes, and governance-con-trolled learning-curve indicators. Detailed comparative coverage ratings are provided in Supplementary Table 1, with grouped columns used only to preserve readability. These ratings should be interpreted as a public-source-based comparison rather than definitive evidence of absence, because registry data dictionaries vary in public availability, local implementation, and platformspecific internal fields.

1) Domain I: baseline case-mix and patient phenotyping

Domain I defines the baseline case-mix required to support risk adjustment, cross-center comparability, and interpretation of longitudinal outcomes in ESS registry research [1-3,17]. Within the essential core of the candidate MDS, baseline variables should include age, sex, BMI, smoking status, ASA physical status classification grade, primary diagnosis, symptom duration, operated level, laterality, and primary versus revision status [1,3,8,17]. Primary versus revision surgery should be explicitly recorded because recurrence risk, technical complexity, and outcome interpretation may differ substantially between primary and revision ESS [1,3,29].

Baseline PROM collection should remain standardized and parsimonious, using validated generic and condition-specific instruments aligned with existing spine registry and outcomestandardization initiatives [2,17,30,31]. For lumbar ESS, the essential PROM set would typically include ODI, leg and back pain VAS/NRS, and EQ-5D; for cervical ESS, NDI and arm/neck pain scores should be selected according to anatomical region and pathology [8,17,31]. These baseline PROMs provide the reference for longitudinal outcome interpretation, MCID assessment, and future exploratory evaluation of relative-change metrics [8,45,46].

Employment status, baseline analgesic use, and opioid exposure expressed as morphine milligram equivalents per day may be included as recommended or optional value-based variables, particularly in registries with reliable prescription linkage or standardized medication capture [3,15,25,31]. In this structure, Domain I functions as the risk-adjustment and baseline-phenotyping foundation of the candidate MDS, while socioeconomic and medication-related variables remain expandable according to local data availability and registry capacity [2,3,15].

2) Domain II: procedure-specific technical and perioperative variables

Domain II defines the procedure-specific technical and perioperative variables required to classify ESS procedures consistently, support cross-technique comparison, and contextualize safety outcomes [21,23,30]. Within the candidate MDS, these variables should be organized into an essential technical core and feasibility-dependent or optional fields, preserving procedural granularity while limiting unnecessary data-entry burden.

Within the essential technical core, procedure-specific variables should include access corridor, operative anatomical target, endoscopic platform, procedural intent, and anesthesia modality, linked to the spinal region and operated level captured in Domain I. Access corridor may be categorized as interlaminar, transforaminal, or other defined approaches, while operative anatomical target should specify the principal zone of pathology, such as central canal, lateral recess, foraminal, or extraforaminal disease. Endoscopic platform should distinguish uniportal, biportal, and other emerging configurations, and procedural intent should specify discectomy, decompression, foraminotomy, endoscopic fusion, or combined pathology-specific objectives [21,23,30]. Anesthesia modality is retained in the essential core because it is a low-burden categorical variable that may influence patient selection, intraoperative neurological assessment, early recovery, and cross-center comparability; detailed anesthesia protocols, drug regimens, block type, or sedation depth should remain optional rather than essential registry fields [21,47]. To maintain interoperability with existing ESS terminology, procedure-specific fields should be mapped to the AOSpine working-channel endoscopic nomenclature where applicable, including spinal region, approach corridor, anatomical target, and procedural intent. Structured extensions may be added for platform-specific variables not fully captured by this nomenclature, such as uniportal versus biportal configuration. Where relevant, these registry variables should also remain compatible with parallel society-led ESS education, reporting, or consensus initiatives to avoid creating a competing terminology standard [23,30].

Essential perioperative variables should include operative time, estimated blood loss, intraoperative adverse events, and conversion to open or microscopic surgery [21,22]. Intraoperative events should be captured using structured categories, including dural tear, neural injury, vascular injury, irrigation-related complication, and conversion, with an “other/specify” option for uncommon procedure-specific events. Radiation exposure is clinically relevant in fluoroscopy-dependent ESS, but because dosimetry metrics vary across equipment and institutions, it should be treated as a recommended feasibility-dependent field when standardized fluoroscopy time, dose-area product, or cumulative dose metrics are available rather than as a universal essential requirement [24]. Additional technical adjuncts, such as drill use, radiofrequency probes, laser, neuromonitoring, navigation, robotics, or visualization adjuncts, may be recorded as optional structured fields when they materially influence workflow, safety, or interpretation of outcomes [21,22,48].

Learning-curve effects are not unique to ESS, but they are particularly relevant during ESS dissemination because outcomes may be influenced by approach-specific technical demands, visualization platform, fluoroscopy dependence, and procedural maturity [21,27,29,44]. Accordingly, maturity-related variables should be treated as optional governance-controlled analytical fields rather than essential public-reporting items. Potential variables include pseudonymized surgeon and center identifiers, cumulative ESS case number, annual surgeon and center ESS volume, and broad procedural maturity phase. Where implemented, these variables should be pseudonymized, access-restricted, and used only for quality improvement or approved research purposes, not for punitive surgeon-level ranking. Because thresholds for defining procedural maturity remain uncertain, maturity-phase categories should be considered provisional until standardized through future consensus [27,29,44]. Their activation should therefore depend on appropriate consent, ethics approval, registry governance, and predefined data-use agreements.

3) Domain III: selective imaging and morphometric validation

Domain III is intended as a selective imaging and morphometric validation module rather than a universal registry requirement. Although objective imaging can complement PROMbased assessment by confirming decompression adequacy, anatomical phenotype, and structural preservation, routine morphometric measurement or postoperative imaging is not universally feasible or clinically justified for all ESS patients [28,34,35]. Therefore, imaging variables within the candidate MDS should be stratified according to clinical indication, research purpose, and local infrastructure, with quantitative morphometry reserved primarily for advanced or research-oriented implementations.

Within Domain III, the essential imaging component should be limited to structured documentation of the imaging-derived anatomical phenotype rather than mandatory quantitative measurement. Using preoperative imaging obtained as part of routine clinical care, the candidate MDS should record 3 key elements: the imaging modality supporting the surgical indication, the imaging-confirmed pathology linked to the operated level and laterality captured in Domain I, and the principal zone of compression, such as central canal, lateral recess, foraminal, or extraforaminal disease [49,50]. Relevant structural modifiers, including spondylolisthesis, instability, recurrent disc herniation, or fusion-related pathology, may also be recorded as structured categorical fields when applicable. This approach preserves essential anatomical documentation while avoiding additional imaging acquisition solely for registry entry.

Quantitative morphometric assessment should therefore remain beyond the essential documentation core as an advanced or research-oriented module. In such settings, morphometric variables may be selectively captured when imaging is clinically obtained or prospectively justified, as summarized in Table 4. For central canal decompression, dural sac cross-sectional area (CSA) measured on axial T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at the level of maximal stenosis may provide an objective marker of neural decompression [28,34,35]. For foraminal pathology, foraminal height and width may be recorded to assess exiting nerve root decompression, particularly in transforaminal approaches [49]. Disc height, segmental angulation, and sagittal translation may be included when structural preservation, instability, or instrumented fusion is clinically relevant [35,51]. Standardized categorical systems, such as the Michigan State University disc herniation classification or simplified MRI grading frameworks, may also support anatomical phenotyping and intercenter comparability [50,52].

Postoperative imaging should be handled as a clinically indicated or research-oriented component rather than a routine requirement for all ESS cases. Routine 12-month MRI or dynamic flexion-extension radiographs should not be considered universal registry requirements for asymptomatic decompressiononly patients, given practical constraints related to reimbursement, radiation exposure, cost, and clinical justification. Instead, postoperative MRI may be selectively captured in symptomatic recurrence, revision cases, research cohorts, or comparativeeffectiveness studies [53,54]. Standing radiographs, dynamic flexionextension views, or CT should be reserved primarily for instrumented or fusion procedures, suspected instability, hardware assessment, or equivocal fusion status [51,55,56]. By separating essential anatomical documentation from optional morphometric analysis, Domain III preserves the value of objective imaging validation while limiting unnecessary imaging, cost, and measurement burden. This tiered structure may also support future registry-linked imaging workflows without making manual morphometric measurement a universal requirement.

4) Domain IV: structured longitudinal outcome surveillance

Domain IV defines a structured longitudinal surveillance strategy for capturing both early recovery and longer-term durability after ESS. Because excessive follow-up burden can reduce compliance and compromise registry validity, the candidate MDS should distinguish essential benchmark assessments from recommended, optional, and research-oriented timepoints. The essential core should prioritize clinically meaningful, feasible, and cross-center comparable endpoints, while ultra-early, highfrequency, and extended follow-up should remain modular components tailored to local infrastructure, procedure type, and research objectives [1,2,17,41].

The essential longitudinal core should focus on safety, effectiveness, and failure endpoints that can be captured reliably across most centers. Safety surveillance should capture intraoperative and postoperative complications, unplanned readmission, and unplanned reoperation at standardized 30- and 90-day intervals. Patient-centered effectiveness should be assessed using the same core PROM instruments collected at baseline, with 3 months serving as an early functional recovery benchmark and 12 months as the principal effectiveness and durability benchmark. Recurrence, persistent or recurrent symptoms, revision surgery, and reason for reoperation should also be recorded as structured failure endpoints to support comparable outcome interpretation [1-3,8,17,29].

Ultra-early recovery assessment, such as postoperative day (POD) 1–2 or daily first-week evaluations, should be treated differently. These data may capture ESS-related advantages that occur before conventional 3- and 12-month follow-up, but they should remain digitally enabled recommended or optional modules rather than universal essential requirements. The ESRG/SpineHealthie experience supports the feasibility of high-frequency early postoperative PROM capture, while also reinforcing that such assessment should be implemented through lowburden digital workflows, with escalation to registry staff reserved for nonresponse or safety alerts rather than routine manual follow-up [26,32,33]. Practical delivery through mHealth applications, automated short message service (SMS)/email links, or patient portals is detailed in the digital follow-up section below [33,38,39].

Within such digitally enabled early recovery modules, data collection should distinguish recommended low-burden recovery and safety indicators from optional medication- or activityrelated measures. Recommended indicators may include early pain trajectory, new or worsening neurological symptoms, and unplanned healthcare utilization, because these can be captured through brief patient-facing surveys and are directly relevant to early recovery and safety surveillance. Wound-related concerns or other patient-reported red flags may be included as optional safety-alert fields where digital monitoring and triage workflows are available. Feasibility-dependent variables may include time to ambulation, return to basic activity, length of stay, and analgesic or opioid consumption. Opioid use is particularly relevant for value-based assessment, but detailed morphine milligram equivalent tracking should be treated as optional and ideally captured through prescription linkage or standardized medication fields where available; otherwise, simplified patient-reported analgesic use may be collected [3,15,25,32,33].

Table 5 summarizes suggested longitudinal assessment windows for the candidate ESS registry framework. Baseline, 3-month, and 12-month PROMs serve as the core outcome anchors, while POD 1–2 pain VAS/NRS is retained as an ESS-specific early recovery measure. Safety endpoints should be captured at standardized 30- and 90-day intervals. Additional week 2, month 1, month 6, opioid-related, imaging, and ≥24-month assessments may be added according to clinical indication, procedure type, infrastructure, and research objectives [1-3,17,29].

2. Digital Platforms and Technical Implementation

1) Registry architectures, workflow integration, and data linkage

Implementation should account for heterogeneity across national, multinational, and society-based registry architectures. Rather than requiring a new standalone platform, the candidate ESS module could function as an add-on data layer within existing registries. Core variables already captured by established systems—such as demographics, baseline PROMs, complications, and standard follow-up—should be mapped to existing fields rather than duplicated. ESS-specific variables, including access corridor, endoscopic platform, anatomical target, radiation metrics, early digital recovery items, selective imaging fields, and governance-controlled maturity indicators, could then be incorporated as structured modular extensions using a shared ESS data dictionary that defines each item as a reusable registry variable rather than a site-specific free-text field. This dictionary should specify each variable’s definition, response format, timing, source system, tier, and linkage pathway [1-3,17,42].

Data capture should follow the natural sequence of care so that information is collected where it is generated rather than entered retrospectively into a parallel system. Preoperative registry entry can link baseline clinical data and PROMs obtained during routine care; intraoperative ESS-specific variables can be captured through structured operative templates using predefined fields; and discharge or post-discharge outcomes can be populated through EPRs, automated PROM workflows, administrative linkage, or targeted chart review. This workflow reduces manual entry, limits free-text dependence, and supports more consistent capture across centers [2,3,57].

Advanced modules should be activated only when reliable source systems exist, allowing resource-limited sites to begin with the essential core while mature centers implement broader data capture. Opioid-related analyses should prioritize simplified, low-burden data capture, such as current analgesic use, opioid use status, or prescription linkage where available, rather than manual morphine milligram equivalents (MME) calculation [3,15,57]. Selective morphometric modules should use picture archiving and communication system (PACS) or imaging-metadata linkage where available, with postoperative imaging captured only when clinically indicated or prospectively justified. Maturity-related fields should be technically supported as restrictedaccess variables, while access and reporting safeguards are addressed separately. Thus, interoperability requires shared definitions, structured point-of-care capture, reliable source-data linkage, and registry-compatible export into a common ESS data structure rather than a single shared software platform [3,57,58].

2) Patient-facing digital follow-up and PROM capture

Patient-facing digital tools should be used primarily to reduce follow-up burden, improve completion, and enable time-sensitive safety monitoring rather than expand manual data entry. Electronic PROM systems, mHealth applications, automated SMS/email links, and patient portals can deliver brief recovery surveys at module-specific timepoints, track completion, and trigger automated reminders for nonresponse [33,38,39]. For ultraearly recovery modules, surveys should remain short and focused on high-yield items such as pain trajectory, new or worsening neurological symptoms, and unplanned healthcare utilization, with escalation to registry staff reserved for nonresponse or safety alerts. As noted above, the ESRG/SpineHealthie experience supports this workflow [26,32,33]. Medication capture should be simplified, focusing on analgesic category or opioid use status. Detailed MME calculation should be used only where reliable prescription, pharmacy, or EPR linkage is available. As with other optional modules, implementation should depend on sourcedata reliability and local workflow capacity [3,57].

3) Governance of surgeon, center, and maturity indicators

Surgeon-, center-, and maturity-related indicators may support risk adjustment, quality improvement, approved research, and dissemination-phase safety monitoring, but their value depends on clear rules for use and access. Because these data can become sensitive when linked to identifiable performance, they should be used to contextualize outcomes and support learning rather than to generate punitive surgeon-level rankings or unrestricted public reporting [3,19,29,44].

In practice, governance should begin with pseudonymized surgeon and center identifiers, with any re-identification key stored separately from the analytical registry dataset. Access to identifiable or re-identifiable data should be role-restricted and limited to authorized registry personnel, institutional qualityimprovement committees, or approved research teams under predefined data-use agreements. Reporting should preferentially use aggregated outputs by center type, volume category, or maturity phase, with suppression of small-cell results when re-identification is possible. Local requirements for consent, ethics approval, data-sharing, and privacy regulation should guide implementation across jurisdictions [3,13,17]..

These safeguards are particularly important for maturity indicators. Cumulative ESS case number, annual surgeon or center ESS volume, and broad maturity phase may help contextualize outcomes, but thresholds defining early adoption, intermediate experience, or established practice are likely to vary by procedure, platform, and case complexity. Until these thresholds are refined through future consensus and empirical validation, maturity indicators should remain provisional governancecontrolled analytical variables rather than definitive quality benchmarks [27,29,30,44].

3. Future Directions: Research on Registries

1) Consensus validation and pilot implementation

The proposed MDS should be viewed as a candidate framework requiring staged refinement before broad implementation. Future validation would most appropriately begin with a Delphi-based or modified stakeholder consensus process involving ESS surgeons, registry methodologists, outcomes researchers, radiologists, digital-health specialists, data-governance experts, and patient representatives [30,59,60]. This process would refine the classification of variables into essential core, recommended feasibility-dependent fields, optional research-oriented modules, and governance-controlled administrative fields, while ensuring alignment with ICHOM outcome standards, Neurocore-SENSED consensus items, AOSpine endoscopic nomenclature, and ESRG/SpineHealthie operational experience [23,26,30-32].

Consensus refinement should then be followed by prospective multicenter pilot testing across centers with different levels of registry infrastructure. The purpose of this phase would be to determine whether the essential core can be captured reliably during routine care, and whether recommended or optional modules can be implemented without excessive data-entry burden [1-3,17,61]. Feasibility assessment could focus on data completeness, PROM response, missing-data patterns, data-entry burden, ultra-early follow-up completion, opioid-data capture, imagingmodule uptake, and acceptability of surgeon- and center-level governance procedures [15,26,32,33,41,61].

Findings from this pilot phase should guide iterative refinement before broader registry integration. Variables with low yield, poor reliability, inconsistent definitions, or excessive burden could be revised, reclassified, or removed. Through this staged pathway, the framework could progress from a conceptual candidate MDS toward a consensus-informed and feasibility-tested ESS registry module suitable for integration into existing spine registry infrastructures [2,3,59-61].

2) Later-stage applications: R-RCTs, quality feedback, and AI-enabled automation

After consensus refinement, feasibility testing, and early implementation, a mature ESS registry could support later-stage real-world evidence generation through registry-based randomized controlled trials (R-RCTs) and pragmatic comparative studies. By embedding randomization within established registry infrastructure, R-RCTs may reduce trial-specific cost and administrative burden, improve follow-up efficiency, and generate evidence across a broader range of surgeons, centers, and healthcare settings. In ESS, this approach could be particularly useful for unresolved areas of clinical equipoise, including uniportal versus biportal platforms, approach selection for foraminal or recurrent disease, selected indications for endoscopic fusion, and comparisons with microscopic decompression. These applications would require validated data definitions, reliable follow-up workflows, adequate case volume, and governance structures capable of supporting randomization, monitoring, and data-quality assurance [62-66].

In parallel, a mature ESS registry could support continuous quality feedback rather than function only as a passive data-collection system. Risk-adjusted aggregate summaries of PROM improvement, complications, readmission, reoperation, recurrence, early recovery, and opioid-related outcomes could be returned to participating centers as benchmarks for local quality improvement. The practical value lies in a cycle of measurement, feedback, targeted practice refinement, and remeasurement over time. Consistent with the governance principles outlined above, feedback should remain aggregate, nonpunitive, and directed toward learning rather than surgeon-level ranking [1-3,17,67,68].

Artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled automation should be viewed as a later-stage support tool rather than a prerequisite for the initial candidate MDS. Once standardized data definitions, linkage pathways, and governance safeguards are established, automation may help reduce manual workload, improve data completeness, and support scalable implementation. A practical downstream application is registry-linked imaging, where validated semiautomated or AI-assisted measurement could facilitate optional morphometric modules by extracting parameters such as dural sac CSA, foraminal dimensions, disc height, and segmental alignment from linked MRI, computed tomography, or radiographic data. These tools should be implemented only after validation, auditability assessment, and human verification, and should support registry operations rather than replace clinical judgment or generate punitive performance rankings [57,58,69-71].

4. Limitations

This review should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, although the search strategy was structured, the study was designed as a narrative review rather than a systematic or scoping review. The synthesis therefore emphasizes conceptual integration and registry-framework development rather than exhaustive evidence mapping. Second, the proposed ESSspecific framework and preliminary MDS were derived deductively and have not yet undergone Delphi-based consensus, stakeholder validation, society endorsement, or prospective pilot implementation. Accordingly, the proposed variables should be regarded as candidate elements for future refinement rather than established registry standards.

Third, the cross-registry comparison was based on published literature, public registry reports, and available data dictionaries; consequently, it may not fully capture unpublished platform updates, local implementation practices, or internally maintained registry fields. Finally, the feasibility of several proposed elements, ultra-early follow-up, opioid tracking, radiation exposure, quantitative imaging, and maturity indicators, carry known feasibility constraints discussed within each domain above. These elements require staged implementation, local adaptation, and prospective feasibility testing before broader adoption. Thus, the framework is intended to guide future consensus development and validation rather than prescribe immediate universal implementation across all ESS settings.

CONCLUSION

Existing spine registries provide a strong methodological foundation for real-world outcome surveillance, but they do not consistently capture the technical, temporal, imaging, and dissemination-related features that are particularly relevant to ESS. This review proposes a candidate ESS-specific registry framework and preliminary MDS as an implementation-oriented extension to existing registry infrastructures, rather than as a competing standalone standard.

The framework balances a lean essential core with feasibilitydependent, optional, research-oriented, and governance-controlled modules. This structure preserves practical feasibility while allowing selective capture of early recovery trajectories, opioid-related outcomes, imaging validation, and learning-curve or maturity indicators under appropriate governance. Delphibased consensus refinement, multicenter feasibility testing, datadictionary harmonization, and staged integration into existing registry platforms are required before broader adoption.
