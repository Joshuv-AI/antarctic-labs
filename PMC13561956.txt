VT-SCC: Visual-text semantic alignment via multimodal guidance for unified staple crop organ counting
Authors: Lanying Wang, Zhengbo Liu, Xinyu Dong, Hainan Wang, Wei Guo, Yuanyuan Xiao, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate counting of yield-related plant organs is fundamental to crop breeding and management. However, scalable counting remains challenging because crop morphology varies widely and field environments are complex. Current specialized models lack flexibility, whereas generic few-shot counters often exhibit feature ambiguity arising from their reliance on visual exemplars, leading to frequent misidentification in dense scenes. To address these challenges, we developed VT-SCC, a visual-text unified staple crop organ counting framework that incorporates semantic guidance into visual representation learning. VT-SCC integrates a Scale-Adaptive Feature Aggregator (SAFA), which uses a multi-branch structure to adaptively capture targets with large size variation, and a Semantic-Guided Channel Modulator (SGCM), which uses category text prompts to calibrate visual features dynamically and suppress background interference. In extensive experiments on a diverse dataset comprising wheat (Triticum aestivum), maize (Zea mays), rice (Oryza sativa), and soybean (Glycine max), VT-SCC achieved a mean absolute error(MAE) of 3.28 and a root mean square error(RMSE) of 4.48, outperforming existing generic counters while remaining competitive with specialized models. VT-SCC also generalized effectively in few-shot transfer learning tasks, adapting to unseen domains with minimal data. These observations demonstrate the feasibility of VT-SCC as a unified agricultural counter and highlight its potential as a scalable, robust solution for high-throughput phenotyping. The code and dataset are publicly available at http://vt-scc.samlab.cn.Keywords: Agricultural vision, Crop organ counting, High-throughput phenotyping, Multimodal prompts

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum), maize (Zea mays), rice (Oryza sativa), and soybean (Glycine max) provide approximately 60% of human caloric intake [1]. Ensuring the productivity of these staple crops through accelerated breeding is vital for global food security. With the global population projected to reach 9.7 billion by 2050 [2], the current rates of crop production are insufficient to meet future needs; global demand for crops increases at a rate of 2.4% annually while genetic yield gain averages only 1.3% [3]. To address this deficit, breeders have been focusing on optimizing yield components such as the number of reproductive organs per unit area [4]. However, population-scale trait screening using traditional manual sampling is labor-intensive and prone to human error [5,6]. While unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and high-throughput phenotyping (HTP) platforms have been used to collect high-resolution imagery for improved screening efficiency [7], robust counting algorithms are required to translate these data into reliable phenotypic evaluations under complex field conditions.

Deep learning methods have been extensively explored for automating field counting and fall into three categories: regression-based counting, instance segmentation, and object detection. Regression-based approaches are effective for processing dense scenes and reducing annotation requirements. Examples include WheatNet [8], the UAV-based density algorithm developed by Wu et al. [9], and CSNet [10]. While these methods are effective for automated field counting in crowded scenes, they lack individual bounding boxes, limiting the localization of specific plant organs. Instance segmentation methods aim to outline the boundaries of crop organs. Examples include AS-SwinT [11] and a tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) counting model proposed by Afonso et al. [12]. However, despite providing detailed shape information, the high cost of high pixel-level annotation hinders the scaling of these methods for large agricultural datasets. Object detection is frequently used for phenotyping because it balances the need for localization with low annotation costs. YOLO and Faster R-CNN are widely used due to their ability to balance accuracy and speed [13]. For specific applications, TasselLFANet [14] and the Panicle-Cloud platform [15] demonstrate superior performance in instance-level quantification. Despite these advancements, most existing models share a common limitation: they were designed for use with specific crops. For instance, wheat models often perform poorly for maize or soybean due to morphological differences. This species-specific dependency limits cross-species generalization, hindering the development of a unified counting paradigm for multiple crops.

Few-shot counting (FSC) addresses the scalability bottlenecks of the "siloed paradigm" (Fig. 1A) by easing the constraints imposed by fixed-parameter models with static feature spaces (Fig. 1D (1)). FSC shifts the focus toward task-adaptive inference. To date, most agricultural studies using FSC have used the visual exemplar-based approach, which uses a few cropped images as reference patterns. For instance, Zhao et al. [16] and Ye et al. [17] used this approach to perform the multi-species tasks shown in Fig. 1B. More recently, TasselNetV4 [18] introduced a unified plant-counting model that counts classes across species through multi-branch box-aware counters and token-level local regression. However, while these methods reduce retraining requirements, they primarily rely on an "extract-and-match" mechanism using visual exemplars. Because visual exemplars often include irrelevant background elements, background noise can interfere with target features and create visual ambiguity, as illustrated in Fig. 1D(2), resulting in false positives. In addition, multi-granularity adaptation remains a persistent challenge. Because crop organs differ substantially in physical size and geometric structure, the model must process features at multiple levels.

To overcome these limitations, we developed VT-SCC, a visual-text framework for directable staple-crop counting (Fig. 1C). VT-SCC builds on Grounding DINO [19] while adapting it to the scale variation, morphological diversity, and background ambiguity of agricultural scenes. A Text-Guided Semantic Field (Fig. 1D(3)) aligns multi-granularity visual tokens into a shared representation, while two modules provide the key adaptations: the Scale-Adaptive Feature Aggregator harmonizes crop features across species and target sizes, and the Semantic-Guided Channel Modulator uses task instructions to suppress non-target interference. Together, these components move beyond prompt matching in multimodal counters such as CountGD [20], enabling robust counting of multiple staple crops within a single architecture.

Results

Experimental details

The proposed VT-SCC framework was built on the Grounding DINO architecture. During training, the Swin Transformer backbone and BERT text encoder were frozen to maintain representational stability. The model was optimized using the AdamW algorithm with a global initial learning rate of 1e-4 and a weight decay of 1e-4. We used a StepLR scheduler to reduce the learning rate every 10 epochs over a total of 50 training epochs. The loss function was a weighted combination of Focal Loss and L1 Loss, synergistically optimizing target classification and bounding box regression.

We used a multi-scale augmentation strategy for image processing during the training phase, dynamically scaling the shortest side of the input images between 480 and 800 pixels. By contrast, the architecture supports unrestricted input resolutions during the inference phase, allowing flexible processing of diverse images from various field sensors. Finally, we utilized the curated M2-Crop dataset for unified multi-species training, while reserving the MTC dataset for out-of-distribution evaluation to verify the model's generalization capability.

Comparison with specialized methods on individual crops

We conducted these experiments to establish a performance baseline and to verify the feature perception capability of the proposed architecture within specific crop domains. In this setting, the model operates as a crop-specific counter and undergoes individual training on datasets for wheat, soybean, rice, and maize. We benchmarked our method against a comprehensive set of baselines: general detectors such as Faster R-CNN [21], SSD [22], Mask R-CNN [23], FCOS [24], EfficientDet [25], and YOLO variants [[26], [27], [28], [29]]; density-regression models such as MCNN [30] and CSRNet [31]; and domain-optimized counters such as CSNet [10], WheatLFANet [32], TasselLFANet [14], YOLO POD [33], PoDNet [34], and SPCN [35]. This crop-wise evaluation isolated dataset-specific training effects to test whether the method consistently addressed shared phenotyping challenges, such as background ambiguity and instance occlusion.

First, the proposed method achieved superior results for the GWHD 2021 wheat dataset, with an MAE (mean absolute error) of 3.38 (Fig. 2A). This performance was better than that of the domain-optimized CSNet [10] and general detectors such as Faster R-CNN [21], SSD [22] and DETR [36], as well as the transformer-based baseline TransCrowd-GAP [37]. This improvement indicates that the learned representation effectively suppresses confusion caused by complex foliage and textural similarities between wheat heads and backgrounds.

Second, our model reported an MAE of 2.65 and an RMSE of 4.12 for the YOLO POD dataset (Fig. 2E), which features dense soybean pod clusters with heavy overlap. The consistent advantage over dataset-oriented baselines such as YOLO POD [33], PoDNet [34], and SPCN [35], as well as general models including Mask R-CNN [23], Swin-S Mask R-CNN [38], FCOS [24], EfficientDet [25], and YOLO variants [[26], [27], [28], [29]], confirms that the proposed features remain separable under severe adhesion and occlusion.

In addition, the VT-SCC framework demonstrated high reliability in small-target aerial scenarios using the DRPD dataset (Fig. 2B), achieving the lowest available MAE of 1.64. Compared to specialized models such as PlantBiCNet [17], P2PNet [39], TasselLFANet [14], Panicle-Cloud [15], and LKNet [40], our method maintained competitive accuracy without relying on strong assumptions tied to a particular viewpoint or repetitive background textures.

Furthermore, using the MrMT dataset (Fig. 2C), our method achieved an MAE of 2.58, surpassing crop-oriented baselines such as TasselNetV3-Seg [41] and TasselLFANet-HE [14]. Despite substantial changes in maize appearance across developmental stages and interference from leaves, the lower MAE obtained using our method confirms that the representation remained consistent across morphological shifts, outperforming general detectors such as SSD [22], RetinaNet [42], CenterNet [43], and Yolov7-tiny [44].

Finally, we conducted a cross-domain evaluation of the unseen MTC dataset (Fig. 2D) to assess the robustness of the VT-SCC framework under a distribution shift and to determine whether the learned representations capture intrinsic morphological invariants. By training exclusively on the MrMT source domain and performing direct transfer inference on the target domain, our model achieved the lowest MAE of 4.84. This performance surpassed that of established baselines such as TasselNetV2 [45], TasselNetV2+ [45], BCNet [46], and CHCNet [47], confirming that the captured features remained informative despite variations in sensors, illumination, and background statistics, thereby validating the strong generalization capacity of our unified perception paradigm.

Analysis of category-encoding strategies

To evaluate the need for multimodal semantic guidance and to determine whether pre-trained textual embeddings provide unique advantages for the SGCM module, we conducted a comparative experiment involving three encoding strategies: One-hot Encoding, which assigns fixed 4-dimensional IDs to the crop categories; Learnable Embedding, which utilizes a randomly initialized layer to learn representations during training; and BERT Embedding, which leverages pre-trained semantic priors to generate high-dimensional feature vectors. The results using the M2-Crop dataset are summarized in Table 2(B).

BERT Embedding achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE, outperforming the non-semantic encoding methods. This result suggests that pre-trained linguistic knowledge serves as a stable semantic anchor for the SGCM, helping the model distinguish target organs from visually similar background clutter. Learnable Embedding exhibited the highest error rates across all metrics, indicating that without the structural constraints of linguistic knowledge, the Prompt Query (Q) suffers from optimization instability during training. In the absence of semantic priors, the learnable parameters fluctuate significantly, making it difficult for the modulator to consistently activate target-relevant feature channels in dense field environments. In addition, One-hot Encoding provided a lower error rate than Learnable Embedding because its fixed, orthogonal IDs offer a rigid task definition that prevents optimization drift. However, its error rate was worse than that of the BERT-based approach, as it lacks the semantic relationships needed to handle the visual ambiguity inherent in agricultural scenes. In summary, these results confirm that BERT embeddings are not redundant but function as essential semantic filters, bridging the gap between low-level visual patterns and high-level counting intentions across diverse crops.

Contributions of the proposed modules

To isolate the contributions of our proposed modules from the pre-trained foundation, we first established the performance ceiling of the adapted backbone. Deploying Grounding DINO without task-specific adaptation yielded an MAE of 41.93, confirming that generic multimodal weights cannot inherently bridge the agricultural domain gap. We then adopted a strong baseline that fully adapted the Grounding DINO architecture for counting under the frozen-backbone protocol described in Section 2.1. This baseline achieved an MAE of 3.91 (Table 2(C)), representing the maximum performance attributable to architecture adaptation and task alignment alone. The further reduction to 3.28 reflects the incremental gain produced by the SAFA and SGCM modules.

To quantify the individual and collective contributions of the SAFA and SGCM modules, we conducted a stepwise ablation study on the unified test set. We evaluated the counting accuracy of the baseline, individual modules, and the integrated framework to verify the collaborative logic of the core components in addressing multi-granularity and visual ambiguity. The results are shown in Table 2C.

First, incorporating the SAFA module into the baseline reduced the MAE by 12.5%, validating its capability to handle multi-granularity challenges arising from diverse morphological structures and harmonize heterogeneous features. This improvement enhances perceptual robustness across varying scales, constructing a more discriminative feature space at the structural level. Subsequently, adding only the SGCM module reduced the MAE to 3.35, confirming the efficacy of channel-wise semantic modulation in suppressing environmental interference. This improvement reflects SGCM's ability to construct a dynamic semantic field via text instructions, fundamentally mitigating the challenges related to visual ambiguity. In addition, the full configuration achieved optimal performance, demonstrating the complementarity between structural representation refinement and semantic guidance. This synergy stems from a hierarchical optimization process in which the SAFA refines the feature representations, and the SGCM precisely directs attention to ensure stability across heterogeneous morphologies. In summary, the experiment confirmed that integrating the SAFA and SGCM modules is essential for high-performance counting. The unified VT-SCC framework therefore shows greater robustness and representational depth than traditional single-structure architectures in complex agricultural scenarios.

Few-shot transfer experiments

We conducted few-shot transfer learning experiments to assess the adaptability of the proposed framework under data-scarce conditions and domain shifts. The evaluation followed a two-stage protocol: pre-training on the generic FSC-147 dataset followed by fine-tuning on a minimal subset of the target agricultural dataset. Specifically, a standardized 3-shot protocol was implemented, in where three support samples per crop category were used for target domain adaptation. To rigorously evaluate the framework under non-idealized real-world conditions, these support samples were acquired via a random selection strategy from the target test distribution, bypassing any manual pre-screening or morphological intervention. Each randomly selected support instance was annotated with human-verified bounding boxes to serve as visual exemplars. During the few-shot inference phase, the confidence gating threshold τ was strictly set to 0.23 to align with the primary evaluation protocol. Cross-modality task guidance was consistently provided through predefined text prompts to ensure stable semantic field calibration under data-scarce conditions. We benchmarked our framework against various few-shot counters, including CounTR [51], SAFECount [49], CACViT [48], and VLCounter [50], with the data distribution and performance results shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

In this study, we developed VT-SCC, a unified framework designed to overcome the scalability limitations of specialized models for crop organ counting. This tool integrates SAFA and SGCM to process features across crops including wheat, maize, rice, and soybean. This framework captures cross-species visual invariants and adapts to morphologies ranging from node-distributed pods to branching tassels without the need for retraining. VT-SCC achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.28, demonstrating its potential for automated phenotyping.

VT-SCC demonstrates robustness in field backgrounds due to its use of semantic constraints to resolve visual ambiguities. The optimization between structural refinement and semantic alignment ensures strong performance across different stages of plant growth. This framework uses a text-guided deep alignment strategy instead of background segmentation because target organs and foliage often share identical spectral and textural characteristics. Due to these similarities, segmentation models can fail to define geometric boundaries. Furthermore, segmentation-based approaches require pixel-level inference and annotation costs, whereas this framework uses category-specific text prompts as a cognitive prior to filter background interference based on identity. The effectiveness of the SGCM relies on the semantic alignment between text prompts and target features. In high-density environments, the disambiguation capability depends on the specificity of the task instructions.

The M2-Crop dataset generated in this study serves as a bridge between generic few-shot counting and specialized agricultural tasks. M2-Crop encompasses variations in target granularity, sensor altitude, and illumination conditions. Specifically, as shown by statistical analysis Supplementary Fig. S2B), the Nearest Neighbor Distance (NND) distribution of the dataset encompasses a wide range, from dense clusters to sparsely distributed targets. This variance in spatial density mitigates the risk of overfitting to a single density pattern and functions synergistically with the SAFA. The transition from dense to sparse features provides diverse scenarios that train the dynamic adjustment of multi-scale receptive fields. By adapting to varying levels of crowdedness, this statistical diversity guides models toward learning universal geometric representations rather than relying on textural memorization. This could explain why the framework effectively generalizes to unseen domains and maintains high counting precision, provided that the target distribution falls within the learned density range. The inclusion of multimodal annotations encompassing text, exemplars, and points supports the development of interactive counting paradigms. We plan to expand the framework for use with additional crop species and cultivation environments.

Materials and methods

Previously constructed datasets and new dataset construction

Traditional agricultural counting datasets usually focus on a single species. The fragmented nature of these datasets hinders the development of scalable cross-species counting models. Moreover, developing four independent datasets requires repetitive data collection, cleaning, and validation workflows, which significantly increase the annotation burden. The primary motivation for constructing a multimodal, staple crop benchmark is to bridge these isolated data domains and enable the model to learn universal visual invariants across different crops. However, the construction process involves several significant technical challenges. First, standardizing heterogeneous data requires a complex workflow to unify multiple annotation formats into a single detection setting. Second, establishing multimodal pairs requires generating and verifying category-specific text prompts to provide accurate semantic guidance. Third, ensuring data quality across disparate sources requires rigorous curation and screening to exclude low-quality samples that contain insufficient target instances. Finally, supplementing bounding box annotations for datasets that contain point labels to support cross-domain evaluation requires a laborious manual effort that is essential to the construction workflow.

We constructed the M2-Crop dataset, also known as the Multimodal and Staple Crop Counting dataset. This benchmark integrates four representative datasets: GWHD 2021 [52] for wheat, MrMT [14] for maize, DRPD [15] for rice, and YOLO POD [33] for soybean. To address inconsistent data structures, we unified heterogeneous labels from the original sources, including XML, JSON, and point-only labels, into a standardized detection schema with bounding box coordinates. For the multimodal configuration, we associated images of wheat, maize, rice, and soybean with the text prompts “Wheat head.”, “Maize tassel.”, “Rice panicle.”, and “Soybean pod.”, respectively. Simultaneously, we randomly sampled three ground-truth bounding boxes per image as visual exemplars, while the remaining targets were annotated with center points using fixed-size pseudo-boxes. Following this curation, the M2-Crop dataset comprised 13,474 images, which were partitioned into training, validation, and test sets at an approximate ratio of 8:1:1. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, M2-Crop encapsulates the intrinsic challenges of field phenotyping, such as extreme scale variations and heavy occlusion. Additionally, the MTC [53] dataset for maize is reserved exclusively for cross-domain testing. We manually added the required bounding box annotations for visual exemplars across all images in this dataset to evaluate the model's generalization to previously unseen data distributions.

Dataset unification and multimodal configuration

Supplementary Table S3, compares the technical attributes of the proposed M2-Crop dataset with several established plant-counting benchmarks. Specialized datasets, such as GWHD 2021 [52] and YOLO POD [33], provide essential high-quality annotations for specific crops in a single domain. The construction of the PAC-105 and PAC-Somalia datasets [18] represents a notable advancement in enabling cross-species counting. Specifically, PAC-105 encompasses 105 categories across 64 plant species, while PAC-Somalia includes 32 unique species native to Somalia, collectively establishing a data foundation for class-agnostic plant counting.

While vision-based studies have achieved broad species coverage, M2-Crop was developed to address the demand for greater informational depth for core staple crops. Within its target domains, M2-Crop provides an extensive collection of 13,474 images, establishing a dense cross-species data foundation for robust feature learning. As highlighted in Supplementary Table S3, a key technical distinction is the transition from “visual-only exemplars” to multimodal support. Unlike earlier approaches that rely on visual similarity, M2-Crop integrates “Box + Text” annotations, enabling models to leverage category-specific semantic guidance to resolve visual ambiguities common in complex field environments. By adopting a multi-task detection paradigm, the dataset provides the structured support required for unified frameworks that combine precise spatial localization with deep semantic alignment.

VT-SCC framework architecture

We developed a unified staple crop counting framework, as shown in Fig. 6A. The Swin Transformer backbone extracts multi-scale image representations, whereas the Scale-Adaptive Feature Aggregator (SAFA) dynamically recalibrates these feature channels to adapt to the intra-class scale variance of different crops and reduce scale-confused responses. Simultaneously, text descriptions are encoded into semantic embeddings using a BERT model, while visual exemplars are transformed into visual tokens via RoI Align to collectively provide explicit task definitions. To resolve semantic ambiguity, these multimodal prompts guide the SGCM, which functions as an alignment mechanism to selectively activate the feature channels that correspond to the specific counting target. Finally, the cross-modal decoder regresses the aligned features into bounding boxes to generate an accurate count. In the following subsections, we discuss the implementation principles of each part of the framework in detail.

Visual-text deep alignment

The SAFA module establishes a robust representation that adapts to varying target sizes. However, precise counting in complex field environments requires more than just size adaptation; robust discriminative power is needed to distinguish target organs from background elements with highly similar spectral or textural characteristics. In dense agricultural scenes, the visual signals of target organs are often entangled with those of foliage and stems, creating semantic ambiguity that cannot be resolved by representations focused primarily on geometric scale. To provide the model with “directable” cognitive capabilities and bridge the gap between low-level visual patterns and high-level counting intentions, we developed a visual-text deep alignment framework. This framework functions as a task-specific semantic filter, ensuring that the model selectively focuses on the intended crop category while suppressing interference from visually similar distractors.

Cross-modal query decoding and instance-aware counting

Generating reliable phenotypic data requires the precise localization of individual crop organs, yet this process is often hindered by the extreme density and heavy occlusion inherent in field environments. In typical agricultural scenarios, such as clustered soybean pods or overlapping wheat heads, the visual boundaries between targets become blurred, making it difficult for traditional regression-based models to resolve entangled instances into discrete individuals. This lack of instance-level resolution often leads to inaccurate phenotypic evaluations when dealing with complex crop morphologies.

Evaluation metrics

To quantitatively evaluate the counting performance of the unified framework across diverse agricultural scenarios, we defined the final predicted count yˆ as the total number of valid bounding boxes exceeding a confidence threshold τ during inference. We adopted four metrics for assessment: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), the Coefficient of Determination (R2), and the Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE). Specifically, the MAE intuitively reflects the average magnitude of deviation between predicted and ground-truth counts. The RMSE serves as a measure of robustness, particularly in extreme counting cases, by penalizing larger prediction errors more heavily. R2 is utilized to assess the goodness of linear fit between the predicted distribution and the ground truth, where a value closer to 1 indicates superior explanatory power. Furthermore, the NRMSE provides a scale-independent assessment of accuracy across diverse crop types with varying target densities. By normalizing the error against the population mean, the NRMSE facilitates a fair performance comparison between high-density and low-density scenarios.
