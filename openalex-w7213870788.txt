Abstract
The immune system protects the body against harmful agents and is composed of several molecules that perform this
function. However, when this system attacks the body’s own cells, autoimmunity is established, as it becomes unable
to distinguish self from non-self, which characterizes autoimmune diseases. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an
autoimmune disease that can present symptoms ranging from mild to severe. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells
(MDSCs) are cells responsible for immunosuppression; however, under pathological conditions, they may expand and
become dysfunctional. In this context, the present study aimed to explore the contribution of these cells to the
pathophysiology of systemic lupus erythematosus. This study consisted of a literature review, with searches conducted
in the PubMed and BVS databases, resulting in the inclusion of 15 articles. Based on the results obtained, a relevant
involvement of MDSCs in the pathophysiology of SLE was observed, as in some contexts they contribute to
inflammation and disease progression, whereas in others they may exert a regulatory effect. It is concluded that
MDSCs play a relevant role in the pathogenesis of SLE and that further studies may reveal new therapeutic targets.
Keywords: MDSC; Autoimmunity; Inflammatory response; Disease progression; Immunosuppression.

1. Introduction
The immune system is composed of different cells, chemical substances, and proteins that work together to defend the
body against harmful substances, such as microorganisms, tumor cells, and other antigens, without attacking what belongs to
the body itself. Autoimmunity occurs when this system loses its ability to distinguish between “self” and “non-self” in specific
regions of cell-surface molecules, known as epitopes, which are recognized by B cells (which produce antibodies) and T cells
(Abbas, 2023).
The process that occurs in the thymus may determine the presence of these autoreactive cells. The thymus performs
positive and negative selection to determine which cells may remain in circulation. During positive selection, the thymus
presents the T lymphocyte with MHC (major histocompatibility complex); if the T cell fails to recognize it, it should be
eliminated, whereas if it recognizes it with moderate affinity, it proceeds to negative selection. During this process, the thymus
presents self-antigens to the lymphocyte and, if the lymphocyte recognizes and attacks them, it should be eliminated. However,
when this does not occur, autoimmunity may be established. In this context, autoimmune diseases develop when autoimmunity
persists over time, leading to the generation of autoreactive T cells and autoantibodies that attack and damage tissues (Abbas,
2023; Song et al., 2024).
It should also be recognized that the autoimmune process tends to remain active in the absence of adequate control, as
the altered and misguided immune response may further stimulate the production of defective cells and antibodies, with the
formation of new autoantigens and the recruitment of additional autoantibodies. This inappropriate attack may lead to chronic
inflammation, progressive organ destruction, and alterations in the physiological functioning of different body systems (Song
et al., 2024).
Accordingly, once induced, autoimmune diseases tend to be progressive. This occurs, in part, because the immune
system possesses intrinsic processes originally employed to allow a limited number of responsive cells to overcome infection.
However, these same mechanisms may ultimately amplify the autoreactive response, favoring its maintenance and expansion.
Furthermore, autoimmune diseases are characterized by an attack against self-epitopes, with the emergence of autoantibodies;
when these responses spread, susceptibility to autoimmune diseases increases. Variations in the possible epitopes that comprise
autoantigens normally do not come into contact with T lymphocytes during the induction of immune tolerance. However, if
they become recognizable, they may induce persistent autoimmunity. The underlying immune response directly influences the

clinical and pathological profile of a given autoimmune condition (Kumar et al., 2023).
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a prototypical systemic autoimmune disease characterized by a large number
of autoantibodies, particularly antinuclear antibodies (ANAs). Environmental factors contribute to increased apoptosis and the
consequent release of nuclear antigens, which form immune complexes capable of activating immune system cells (Kumar et
al., 2023). The clinical presentation of the disease encompasses a wide range of symptoms, from mild to catastrophic,
involving multiple organs and posing a risk of organ failure with a high probability of mortality (Fonseca-Zuluaga et al., 2025).
The global prevalence of SLE in the population has been estimated at 43.7 cases per 100,000 individuals,
corresponding to approximately 3.41 million individuals (Robbins & Cotran, 2023). The four countries with the highest
estimated prevalence rates of SLE in the general population were the United Arab Emirates, Barbados, Cuba, and Brazil (Tian
et al., 2023). The incidence ratio between males and females is 1:9, respectively, with an average of 1 in 700 women of
reproductive age being affected (Kumar et al., 2023; Kiriakidou et al., 2020). Moreover, prevalence is two to three times higher
among Black and Hispanic populations than among White populations (Tian et al., 2023).
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) constitute a population of immature myeloid precursors that expand under
pathological conditions. These cells accumulate in the bone marrow, lymphoid tissues, peripheral blood, and tumor
microenvironments. They exert potent immunosuppressive activity by inhibiting innate and adaptive immune responses
(Nagaraj and Gabrilovich, 2012; Gabrilovich and Nagaraj, 2009). MDSCs are classified into different subtypes according to
cell-surface markers. In mice, MDSCs are divided into two major subsets: monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and
polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs), also referred to as granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs). M-MDSCs are defined as
CD11b⁺ Ly6ChighLy6G−, whereas PMN-MDSCs are characterized as CD11b⁺ Ly6ClowLy6G⁺ in mice (Bronte et al., 2016). In
humans, MDSCs are classified into three main subtypes: monocytic MDSCs (mMDSCs), characterized by HLA-DR−/low
CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD14⁺; polymorphonuclear or granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs), identified by Lin − HLA-DR−/low CD33⁺
CD11b⁺ CD15⁺; and early-stage MDSCs (eMDSCs), which are Lin− (including CD3, CD14, CD15, CD19, and CD56) and
HLA-DR−/low CD33⁺, reflecting their phenotypic and functional diversity in inflammatory environments (Bronte et al., 2016).
MDSCs are involved in various aspects of immune regulation in diseases characterized by chronic inflammation
including cancer (Gabrilovich and Nagaraj, 2009; Pereira et al., 2026), autoimmune diseases (Abbas, 2023; Bekić et al., 2023),
viral infections (Norris et al., 2013), bacterial infections (Lai et al., 2014), fungal infections (Kaminski et al., 2025), and
pregnancy (Köstlin et al., 2016; Fragoso et al., 2026).
Considering SLE, MDSCs may exhibit pathogenic potential depending on their phenotype in patients (Wu et al.
2016). In viral infections, according to a study by O'Connor et al. (2017) myeloid-derived suppressor cells play a role during
viral infections by suppressing CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell proliferation and modulating T-helper cell polarization, among other
mechanisms, thereby affecting the response of these cells during infection (O'Connor et al., 2017). During bacterial infections,
another study demonstrated the involvement of MDSCs in the suppression and reduction of T-cell infiltration at sites
containing bacterial biofilms, which was shown to affect the infection process (Heim et al., 2014). According to Umansky et
al. (2016), MDSCs play a fundamental role in tumor progression by exerting immunosuppressive effects that affect the
immune response to tumor cells (Umansky V et al., 2016). Regarding MDSCs in fungal infections, fungi are capable of
evading the immune system, interfering with the effectiveness of immune responses. Consequently, MDSCs may be recruited
and exert immunosuppressive effects, reducing T- and NK-cell proliferation; however, their effects during these infections are
highly dependent on the pathogen, as MDSCs may exert protective effects against some pathogens and detrimental effects
against others (Rieber et al., 2015; Kaminski et al., 2025). Together, these data show how diverse and important are the role of
MDSCs in different pathological contexts.

However, MDSCs are not involved exclusively in pathological processes; their presence can also be observed under

increase in GR-MDSCs and a smaller increase in M-MDSCs occur during pregnancy. These cells are involved in the

the maternal immune system. Collectively, these processes contribute to the establishment of maternal-fetal tolerance during
pregnancy (Köstlin-Gille et al., 2020; Fragoso et al., 2026).
Although the role of MDSCs is better understood in cancer research, their role in systemic lupus erythematosus
remains incompletely understood (Ji et al., 2019). Thus, this review aims to explore the contribution of MDSCs to the
pathophysiology of SLE and their immunomodulatory mechanisms.

2. Methodology
This study is an integrative literature review (Page et al., 2021; Snyder, 2019), employing a quantitative approach to
describe the selected articles and a qualitative approach to analyze and discuss the role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells
(MDSCs) in the pathogenesis and immunopathological mechanisms of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (Risemberg et al.,
2026; Pereira et al., 2018). The study selection and reporting processes were informed by the recommendations of the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020), with the aim of promoting
transparency in the identification, screening, and inclusion of studies.
Scientific articles were identified through searches conducted in the PubMed and Virtual Health Library (BVS)
databases. The following search strategies were employed: in PubMed, ("Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells"[Mesh]) AND
"Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic"[Mesh]; and in BVS, ("MDSC" OR "myeloid-derived suppressor cells" OR "myeloid
suppressor cells") AND ("SLE" OR "systemic lupus erythematosus"). Articles published in English or Portuguese were
included, with a primary focus on MDSCs and SLE.

3. Results
The database search identified a total of 67 articles, including 23 retrieved from PubMed and 44 from the Virtual
Health Library (BVS). After removing six duplicate articles, 61 articles remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage,
21 articles were excluded because they did not focus on myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in systemic lupus
erythematosus (SLE), leaving 40 articles for full-text assessment.
Following full-text assessment, 25 additional articles were excluded because they were review articles, resulting in a
final sample of 15 articles that met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review. Of these, eight articles were
retrieved from PubMed and seven from BVS which are summarized in Table 1 and further discussed in the Discussion section.

4. Discussion
The findings of this review indicate that MDSCs play a complex and context-dependent role in the pathophysiology of
SLE. Although these cells are classically recognized for their immunosuppressive properties, the studies analyzed here
demonstrate that MDSCs may also acquire pro-inflammatory functions in SLE, contributing to disease progression through
distinct cellular and molecular mechanisms. This functional heterogeneity appears to be influenced by MDSC subset, signaling
pathways, disease context, and the surrounding immune microenvironment.
Several studies support a pro-inflammatory role for MDSCs in SLE. In a murine model, M-MDSCs increased in
frequency and were associated with activation of CD4⁺ IFN-γ⁺ T cells, suggesting that these cells may contribute to
inflammatory responses during lupus progression (Uribe et al., 2024). Similarly, MDSCs have been implicated in TLR7
pathway activation through an S100A8/9-dependent mechanism, further supporting their involvement in inflammatory
signaling in lupus (Yang et al., 2024). MDSCs may also contribute to the imbalance between Treg and Th17 cells, with
alterations in these populations favoring inflammatory responses and disease progression (Ji et al., 2016). In addition, the
NEAT1-BAFF axis in G-MDSCs has been associated with activation of type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling in B cells,
providing another mechanism through which MDSCs may contribute to lupus pathogenesis (Dong et al., 2020). Together,
these findings indicate that, under specific conditions, MDSCs may contribute to the amplification of dysregulated immune
responses rather than effectively suppressing them.

The association between MDSCs and disease activity is also supported by studies in human SLE. Circulating MMDSCs were increased in newly diagnosed patients with SLE and positively correlated with disease severity, while their
immunosuppressive activity was dependent on iNOS (Wang et al., 2019). Similarly, MDSCs were expanded in patients with
rheumatic diseases, including SLE, and their frequencies were associated with inflammatory activity and joint manifestations
(Zhu et al., 2018). These findings highlight an important aspect of MDSC biology in SLE: increased abundance may coexist
with either pathogenic or regulatory activity, depending on the phenotype and functional state of the cells. Thus, MDSC
frequency alone may not adequately reflect their biological effect and should ideally be interpreted together with phenotypic
and functional characteristics.
The functional heterogeneity of MDSCs may be partly explained by alterations in intracellular signaling and
epigenetic regulation. Tan et al. (2025) demonstrated that the ALKBH5/FoxO1/Met axis regulates M-MDSC-associated effects
on B-cell proliferation, indicating that RNA modification and transcriptional regulation may influence the interaction between
MDSCs and adaptive immune responses. Likewise, MDSC-derived polyamines were shown to promote miR-542-5p
expression and enhance Th17-cell differentiation, contributing to inflammation in SLE (Hu et al., 2023). Notch1 signaling has
also been implicated in MDSC expansion, as its inhibition reduced MDSC accumulation and disease progression in a murine
model (Li et al., 2023). These findings suggest that the functional state of MDSCs is determined not only by their abundance
but also by specific molecular pathways that regulate their differentiation and activity.
The Dectin3/FoxO1/LOX-1 axis provides another example of the molecular regulation of MDSC function. Dectin3
deficiency promoted FoxO1-mediated apoptosis of MDSCs and reduced the accumulation of LOX-1⁺ M-MDSCs, which were
associated with enhanced Th17-cell differentiation and SLE progression (Li et al., 2021). These findings suggest that Dectin3
signaling may contribute to the maintenance of pathogenic M-MDSC populations, whereas FoxO1-mediated mechanisms may
restrict their accumulation. The identification of these molecular pathways reinforces the possibility of selectively targeting
pathogenic MDSC populations rather than treating MDSCs as a homogeneous cell population.
The therapeutic implications of these findings are particularly relevant because the available evidence supports
different, and sometimes apparently opposing, strategies for targeting MDSCs in SLE. Approaches aimed at reducing MDSC
accumulation include Notch1 inhibition and hydroxychloroquine treatment. Inhibition of Notch1 signaling reduced MDSC
expansion and disease progression, whereas hydroxychloroquine increased CD81 expression in MDSCs, promoting apoptosis
and reducing their levels (Li et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2022). These findings suggest that reducing specific MDSC populations
may be beneficial when their expansion is associated with pathogenic activity.
Conversely, other studies support the therapeutic potential of preserving or enhancing the immunoregulatory
properties of MDSCs. PD-L1-expressing MDSCs exhibited enhanced immunoregulatory activity and ameliorated lupus-like
disease in murine models, promoting regulatory immune responses and reducing disease manifestations (Park et al., 2021).
Similarly, the infusion of MDSCs promoted regulatory B-cell expansion and reduced autoantibody production and renal
inflammation in SLE models (Park et al., 2016). Therefore, therapeutic strategies may need to distinguish between pathogenic
and regulatory MDSC populations, with the objective of eliminating or suppressing the former while preserving or enhancing
the latter.
Sex and hormonal factors may represent additional determinants of MDSC behavior in SLE. Jones et al. (2022) found
that G-MDSC frequencies were increased in both male and female patients with SLE, regardless of sex and sex hormone
levels. This finding is relevant because it suggests that differences in MDSC frequency alone do not explain the marked sex
bias observed in SLE. Conversely, the immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs may vary according to sex and disease context,
as demonstrated by Davison et al. (2021), in which MDSCs exerted a protective immunosuppressive effect in male NZBWF1

Taken together, the evidence reviewed here indicates that MDSCs are not a functionally homogeneous population in
SLE. Their effects may range from immunosuppression and disease control to promotion of inflammatory responses,
depending on the MDSC subset, molecular pathway, disease stage, sex, and immune microenvironment. Importantly, the
coexistence of pathogenic and regulatory functions may explain some of the apparently contradictory findings across studies.
Consequently, MDSCs should not be regarded simply as either beneficial or detrimental in SLE; rather, their phenotype and
functional state should be considered when interpreting their role in disease pathogenesis and when designing therapeutic
interventions.
Future studies, particularly those involving well-characterized human SLE cohorts, should prioritize the simultaneous
assessment of MDSC frequency, phenotype, and suppressive or pro-inflammatory activity. Such approaches may help identify
clinically relevant MDSC subsets and determine whether these cells can be reliably used as biomarkers of disease activity or
treatment response. More importantly, a better understanding of the mechanisms governing MDSC functional polarization may
enable the development of targeted strategies capable of selectively suppressing pathogenic MDSCs while preserving or
enhancing their immunoregulatory functions.

5. Final Considerations
This review aimed to synthesize the available evidence on the role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in
systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The studies analyzed indicate that MDSCs exhibit context-dependent functions in SLE,
participating in both the amplification and regulation of inflammatory responses. This functional heterogeneity highlights the
importance of considering factors such as MDSC subset, phenotype, molecular pathways, and biological context when
interpreting their role in disease pathogenesis. Further investigation of the mechanisms underlying MDSC function may
contribute to the identification of reliable biomarkers and the development of more targeted and effective therapeutic strategies
for SLE.
