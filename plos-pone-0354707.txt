Prediction of oxygen uptake at peak exercise in patients with inflammatory bowel disease using submaximal cardiopulmonary exercise testing and the steep ramp test as a practical field test

Karlijn Demers, Sander M J van Kuijk, Marieke J Pierik, Daisy M A E Jonkers, Laurents P S Stassen, Bart C Bongers

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To develop predictive equations for oxygen uptake at peak exercise (VO2peak) measured during a cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) for patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) using submaximal CPET-derived parameters and the steep ramp test, along with demographic and clinical characteristics. Methods: This study included patients with IBD in remission or with mild-to-moderate disease activity who performed a CPET and a steep ramp test within 14 days. Submaximal CPET-derived parameters included oxygen uptake (VO2) at the ventilatory anaerobic threshold (VAT) and oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES), while the outcome of the steep ramp test was the work rate achieved at peak exercise (WRpeak). Results: Predictive regression equations with submaximal VO2 at the VAT and with OUES, when combined with age and body fat percentage, explained >89% of the variance in VO2peak (optimism-adjusted R2 = 0.895 and 0.938). Predictive capacity increased when both VO2 at the VAT and OUES were combined in a predictive regression equation, together with age, body fat percentage, and clinically active disease (optimism-adjusted R2 = 0.951). Steep ramp test WRpeak explained 89% of the variance in VO2peak (optimism-adjusted R2 = 0.892). Conclusion: The highly predictive equations developed in this study support the use of the submaximal CPET and the steep ramp test as alternatives for assessing VO2peak in patients with IBD in remission or with mild-to-moderate disease activity in clinical and research settings.

Introduction Cardiorespiratory fitness, representing the body’s capacity to deliver and utilize oxygen during large muscle physical activity, is a well-established indicator of overall health and is strongly associated with morbidity and mortality in the general population [ 1 ]. In individuals with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), improved cardiorespiratory fitness may positively influence subjective well-being, mitigate mucosal inflammation, or reduce the risk of adverse postoperative outcomes, but evidence remains limited [ 2 ]. Routine assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness is crucial to understand its clinical implications in IBD and identifying potential therapeutic avenues that could enhance patient outcomes through targeted physical exercise interventions. The gold standard test for assessing cardiorespiratory fitness involves quantifying oxygen uptake at peak exercise (VO 2peak ) during a cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET), requiring maximal exertion. However, in certain patients with IBD, maximal exertion may be constrained by disease-specific factors, such as disease activity, abdominal pain, fatigue, arthralgia, or psychological barriers such as lack of motivation or the fear of exacerbating symptoms [ 3 , 4 ]. In such cases, submaximal CPET-derived metrics, including the oxygen uptake (VO 2 ) at the ventilatory anaerobic threshold (VAT) and the oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES), may provide valid alternatives for evaluating cardiorespiratory fitness [ 5 ]. Despite CPET being the most accurate method for evaluating cardiorespiratory fitness, its use in clinical settings is often constrained by relatively high costs, time requirements, and the need for specialized personnel and equipment [ 1 ]. The steep ramp test, a practical, short maximal exercise test performed on a cycle ergometer that does not require respiratory gas analysis, could serve as a less demanding alternative [ 6 ]. In a previous study, a very strong correlation was found between the outcome of the steep ramp test, i.e., the work rate achieved at peak exercise (WR peak ), and VO 2peak attained during CPET [ 7 ]. To evaluate the possibility to use the submaximal CPET and the steep ramp test in clinical and research settings for patients with IBD in remission or with mild-to-moderate disease activity, and to enable comparisons with population-based normative data, the objective of this study was to develop predictive equations for VO 2peak measured with CPET, using (1) submaximal CPET-derived parameters ( i.e., VO 2 at the VAT, OUES) and (2) steep ramp test performance (WR peak ), both combined with demographic and clinical characteristics. Methods Study design and participants Adult patients (≥18 years) with Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC) were recruited for this cross-sectional study from the gastroenterology outpatient clinic between August 17, 2022, and November 11, 2023. Detailed in- and exclusion criteria are provided elsewhere. In brief, eligible patients were required to be in remission or have mild-to-moderate clinical disease activity (Harvey Bradshaw index ≤16 or simple clinical colitis activity index ≤11) [ 8 , 9 ]. Patients were excluded if they had injuries or comorbidities potentially affecting study evaluations, or if they were pregnant, lactating, or competitive/elite athletes. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee. All patients provided written informed consent. Data collection Demographic and clinical data from all participants was collected during the study visit and from electronic patient records, including age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index, smoking status, disease duration, medication use, and prior intestinal resections. Active clinical disease at time of inclusion was defined as a Harvey Bradshaw ≥5 for patients with CD or a simple clinical colitis activity index of ≥3 for patients with UC. Faecal calprotectin values, if available within 8 weeks of inclusion, were collected, with levels below 250 µg/g indicating biochemical remission. Anthropometry and exercise testing Body weight and height were measured barefoot and wearing light clothing to calculate body mass index (BMI). Body fat percentage was estimated by calculating the cumulative sum of four skinfold thicknesses ( i.e., biceps, triceps, subscapular, and suprailiac) and applying it to the age- and sex-specific prediction equation by Durnin and Womersley [ 10 ]. The CPET and steep ramp test were conducted on two separate days, with a maximum interval of 14 days between tests. Detailed testing protocols are described elsewhere [ 7 ]. Resting baseline values were recorded before each test, and rate of perceived exertion was evaluated pre- and post-test using the 6–20 Borg scale [ 11 ]. The CPET on a cycle ergometer (Lode Corival CPET, Lode BV, Groningen, the Netherlands) started with a 3-minute warm-up of unloaded cycling, followed by an incremental increase in work rate (i.e., resistance or cycling load, expressed in Watts ) tailored to the participant's estimated fitness level, aiming for maximal effort within 8–12 minutes. Participants were instructed to maintain a pedaling frequency of 70–80 rpm, with the test terminating when frequency fell below 60 rpm, indicating the point of maximal exertion. Peak exercise parameters were averaged over the final 30 seconds before this point. During the test, participants wore an oro-nasal facemask connected to a calibrated ergospirometry system for respiratory gas analysis (Vyntus CPX four, Vyaire Medical, Mettawa, IL, USA). Heart rate and rhythm were monitored using a 12-lead electrocardiogram (Vyntus ECG 12-lead PC-ECG, Vyaire Medical, Mettawa, IL, USA). SpO 2 was continuously measured, and blood pressure (Tango M2 Stress Test Monitor, SunTech Medical Inc., Morrisville, NC, USA) was measured every 2 minutes. VO 2peak was considered (near-)maximal when the respiratory exchange ratio (RER peak ) exceeded 1.10 and/or when heart rate at peak exercise (HR peak ) surpassed 85% of the predicted maximum ( i.e., 208 – 0.7 × participant’s age ). Additionally, participants had to show subjective signs of maximal effort. The VAT was defined as the point of an increase in carbon dioxide production relative to the oxygen uptake (VO 2 ) using the V-slope method. This point was confirmed by marking where the ventilatory equivalent for oxygen and partial end-tidal oxygen tension began to consistently increase after reaching a minimum, while the ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide and partial end-tidal carbon dioxide tension remained constant. The OUES was calculated based on the linear relationship between VO 2 and the logarithm of minute ventilation throughout exercise, using all available test data from the beginning of the work rate increments to the point of test termination or up to a clear VO 2 plateau, when applicable. CPET interpretation was conducted by a trained and experienced clinical exercise physiologist (BB) and subsequently verified by a medical doctor trained in clinical exercise physiology (KD) using a systematic, guideline-based approach [ 12 ]. Any discrepancies were reviewed and discussed until a consensus was reached. The steep ramp test, performed on a cycle ergometer started with a 3-minute warm-up of unloaded cycling, followed by an incremental work rate increase of 25 W every 10 seconds (2.5 W/s). Participants cycled until exhaustion at a pedaling frequency of 70–80 rpm. WR peak , normalized to body mass, was defined as the work rate at the point at which the pedaling frequency dropped below 60 rpm despite strong verbal encouragement. Heart rate and peripheral oxygen saturation were continuously recorded. Statistical analyses Categorical variables were presented as absolute number and percentage, while continuous variables were described as median with the first and third quartile (Q1, Q3). Differences between the CPET and steep ramp test values were assessed using the paired-samples t-test for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon signed-rank test when normality assumptions were violated, or outliers were present. To develop predictive equations for VO 2peak , univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were used. Initially, predictive regression equations were developed including test outcomes only. Subsequently, demographic and clinical factors were added to the model to assess if predictive accuracy improved. Univariable linear regression analyses were used to assess the association between VO 2peak and individual demographic and clinical factors. Variables with a p < 0.10 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models, along with submaximal CPET-derived parameters ( i.e., VO 2 at the VAT and OUES) and steep ramp test WR peak . Backward elimination on the Wald test was used for the multivariable models was used. The predictive equations were derived from the final models, and the apparent-performance was quantified as R 2 to assess the models’ explanatory power. To estimate performance in future patients ( i.e., patients who were not used to derive the formulae), the predictive models were internally validated through bootstrapping with 1000 bootstrap samples to calculate the optimism-adjusted performance. This optimism-adjusted R 2 provides a more realistic estimation of the models’ performance when applied to new patients from the same underlying population. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics and R statistical software. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The current findings showed that both submaximal CPET-derived parameters, when combined with age, body fat percentage, and the presence of clinically active disease, and the steep ramp test, showed strong predictive accuracy for estimating VO 2peak , explaining over 89% of the variance. Submaximal CPET parameters, such as VO 2 at the VAT and OUES, are well-established indicators of cardiorespiratory fitness and have demonstrated strong correlations with VO 2peak , especially in populations where achieving maximal effort may be constrained [ 5 , 13 – 16 ]. VO 2 at the VAT reflects the transition from mainly aerobic metabolism to a significant contribution of anaerobic metabolism as an additional energy source, whose relative contribution to total energy production increases with further increasing exercise intensity [ 5 , 17 ]. Hence, the VO 2 at the VAT provides a physiologically meaningful threshold related to cardiorespiratory fitness. The OUES represents the ventilatory efficiency with respect to VO 2 during progressive exercise [ 18 ]. In this study, the combination of VO 2 at the VAT and OUES, when combined with age, body fat percentage, and the presence of clinically active disease, accounted for 95% of the variance in VO 2peak. This high predictive accuracy underscores the suitability of these measures for assessing cardiorespiratory fitness, particularly in patients whose performance may be limited by factors other than cardiorespiratory deficiency. Potential arguments for avoiding maximal exertion in patients with IBD may include fatigue, joint pain, severe disease activity, prior to a surgical intervention, or psychological concerns, such as fear of symptom exacerbation during maximal effort. While one patient was unable to achieve maximal VO 2peak in the current study, all participants successfully reached the VAT. The high success rate of achieving VO 2peak in the current population could be explained by the fact that the cohort consisted of patients in remission or with only mild-to-moderate disease activity, excluding those with severe disease activity. Furthermore, patients with significant joint complaints, such as active arthritis, were excluded. In outpatient or resource-constrained settings, the steep ramp test could serve as a practical alternative to the CPET in patients with IBD, in which the achieved WR peak explained 89% of the variance in VO 2peak. High reliability of the steep ramp test was also found in other populations [ 6 ]. A key advantage of the steep ramp test is that it does not require expensive respiratory gas analysis and specialized personnel, making it more accessible for routine clinical practice or research settings. Furthermore, the current findings highlight that the steep ramp test imposes a significantly lower burden on patients compared to CPET, with shorter test durations, lower heart rates at peak effort, and a smaller change in rate of perceived exertion after the test. These characteristics may make the steep ramp test also appropriate for use in patients with more advanced disease activity of severe fatigue. However, its applicability in patients with severe disease activity requires further investigation. Further, the addition of demographic and clinical variables to the regression model did not significantly enhance its predictive accuracy, indicating that the steep ramp test alone provides sufficient accuracy for estimating VO 2peak . Although a large body of evidence highlights the importance of cardiorespiratory fitness as a critical health indicator, its potential role as a modulator of disease and postoperative outcomes in patients with IBD remains poorly evaluated. This study addresses an important gap by contributing to the integration of less-invasive routine assessments of cardiorespiratory fitness into clinical practice and research initiatives for patients with IBD. The developed regression equations for estimating VO 2peak using CPET-derived parameters and the steep ramp test facilitate comparisons with normative values from healthy populations, offering a method to identify cardiorespiratory deficiencies in patients with IBD. Future research should focus on identifying effective therapeutic strategies to improve cardiorespiratory fitness in patients with IBD and investigate whether these improvements contribute to better disease control, including reduced inflammation and improved patient-reported outcomes, as well as improved postoperative outcomes. A limitation of the study is the relatively small sample size of 53 patients, which may limit the precision of its regression coefficient estimates. Although the regression models demonstrated strong predictive accuracy after internal validation, larger, multicenter studies are needed to externally validate these equations in more extensive and diverse IBD populations. Moreover, the exclusion of patients with severe disease activity may lead to differences in the predictive accuracy of the models when applied to patients with severely active disease. Another limitation is the potential variability in the determination of the VAT. While previous research indicates that the inter-observer variability of VAT measurement is acceptable for experienced clinicians, it is important to note that the VAT can sometimes be challenging to identify [ 19 – 21 ]. Conclusion In this study, we developed highly predictive equations for estimating VO 2peak in patients with IBD in remission or with mild-to-moderate disease activity using submaximal CPET-derived parameters and the steep ramp test, combined with demographic and clinical characteristics. These methods offer more feasible and practical alternatives to the traditional maximal CPET, making the assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness more accessible in clinical and research settings for patients with IBD. This could improve the identification of patients with low cardiorespiratory fitness levels and aid in the development of personalized physical exercise training interventions, potentially contributing to better disease management and enhanced quality of life for individuals with IBD.
