Goals of the project
The use of external magnetic fields in ICF has been identified as a promising way to assist ignition. In this
relatively new area of research, the magnetic field transport during compression and related magnetized
plasma dynamics must be studied. Cylindrical geometry provides a simpler testbed compared to spherical
implosions for exploring and isolating magnetized phenomena in high-energy-density (HED) plasmas. Our
main goal is to develop a platform for the OMEGA-60 laser facility to study MHD effects in cylindrical
implosions at regimes of large magnetic pressure and magnetization. 2-D simulations using the MHD
code GORGON predicted that a seed B-field of 30 T can be compressed to ~30 kT owing to flux
compression when imploding the cylindrical target. As a result, the characteristic conditions of the
compressed core are expected to be modified by the large magnetization and magnetic pressure thereby
reached at maximum compression. On a few previous magnetized cylindrical compressions at the OMEGA-
60 facility, the effect of the compressed B-field was investigated using neutron measurements only.
However, the neutron yield in cylindrical implosions is rather low (on the order of 108-109) and e.g. a small
target variation can induce very large yield fluctuations, therefore strongly limiting a clear interpretation of
the results. Alternatively, we propose using Ar/Kr dopant(s) in the D2 gas. As the core conditions are
modified by the compressed magnetic field, we expect to observe systematic changes in the dopant emission
spectra between unmagnetized and magnetized shots. Carrying out such magnetized implosion experiments
will advance the modeling of B-field compression and diffusion, and the benchmarking of atomic kinetics
and line shape calculations in magnetized plasmas relevant to complex ICF-related experiments with
embedded B-fields. Indeed, the design and interpretation of magnetized experiments and applications
strongly rely on magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) codes. Simulations must include extended-MHD effects,
which represent the transport of energy and magnetic flux in a plasma. Magnetized plasmas are thought to
exhibit complex behavior in the electron population and, above resistive-MHD, extended-MHD
additionally accounts for temperature gradient-driven transport—such as the Nernst term moving magnetic
fields down electron temperature gradients—and electric-current-driven transport—such as the Hall term
moving magnetic fields with the flow of charge. In this context, our goal is to obtain clear experimental
data that will facilitate benchmarking of MHD codes and consequently help the modeling of
magnetized HED experiments. Moreover, the interpretation of spectral emission measurements from
complex magnetized experiments requires novel and accurate modeling of the detailed atomic and
radiation transport physics. In this project, we are investing significant efforts to mature the spectroscopy
diagnostic techniques for magnetized HED experiments.

A second goal of the project arises from the perspective of increasing further the magnetic pressure and
magnetizations in cylindrical implosions. In the regime of high magnetic pressures, the imploding shell
decelerates on the magnetic field, converting kinetic energy into magnetic. For instance, the measurement
of lower overall core pressure when a large magnetic field is applied would indicate that the magnetic
pressure has become significant. The highly compressed magnetic field induces large electrical currents in
the hot-spot; finite plasma resistivity results in Ohmic dissipation, diffusing the magnetic field and heating
the electron population. The large induced electrical currents can also cause Hall physics to be important,
which has not been reported in laser-driven implosions to date. There are two main paths to increase the
compressed B-field. The first one is to increase the initial (seed) B-field. The second one is to increase
the convergence ratio of the implosion, which is the ratio of the initial target radius to the compressed core
radius. Note that dopants added to the imploding fuel can also be used to radiatively cool the hotspot,
allowing the magnetic field to be more compressible, but at the cost of a lower magnetization. It is worth

mentioning as well that in the frozen-in-flow approximation, the magnetic field is amplified quadratically
with the convergence ratio of the cylindrical implosion. Increasing the convergence ratio is obtained
through higher laser drive energy, thinner shell thicknesses or lower gas fills. However, this method tends
to an implosion that is more susceptible to perturbation growth and instabilities. Concerning the seed B-
field, the team at the Laboratory for Laser Energetics (LLE) hosting the OMEGA laser facility has
developed a pulsed-power system capable of delivering B-fields up to ~30T over the volume requirements
of a cylindrical implosion. This B-field generation technique can be readily used for our experiments.
Increasing the seed B-field being a promising way to reach extreme magnetization regimes, our team aims
at exploring the prospects of a relatively new technique to generate static seed magnetic fields using
laser-driven coils (LDC), which are debris-free and potentially stronger than what can currently be
achieved with pulsed-power devices. LDCs have shown very promising results on mid-scale laser
facilities generating several MG fields in a cubic mm. The scaling with OMEGA (or NIF) laser beams to
drive the LDCs is not yet fully explored, but LDCs can have the potential to produce B-fields of several
tens of Tesla, with almost no debris and fewer obstruction concerns than MIFEDS. We determined that a
seed B-field of ~50T on cylindrical implosions at OMEGA would lead to a hot-spot with a magnetic
pressure on the same order as the thermal pressure.

experiments carried out at the OMEGA laser facility during the last funding period, which were published
in Physical Review Research [10]. When applying a 30 T seed axial B-field to the implosions (using

magnetization effects in cylindrical implosions were, for the first time, reproducibly demonstrated from the

To understand the origin of these temperature and density differences, we post-processed the MHD
simulation output to produce synthetic spectra. The calculation procedure is conceptually identical to the
one followed in the spectroscopic model used for diagnosis, with the only difference that now the core
temperature and density spatial profiles are those produced by the simulation at every time step. In Fig. 5c-
d, we show the comparison between experimental and synthetic spectra for both the case with a 30 T seed
B-field (c) and the case with no applied B-field (d). The synthetic spectra are time integrated from
simulation outputs until matching experimental peak compression. A good agreement is found between the
synthetic and experimental spectra. Interestingly, the respective core-averaged temperature and density
predicted at these times are close to the values inferred from the previous independent quantitative
spectroscopic analysis. The agreement between synthetic and experimental spectra firmly indicates that the
differences in the temperature and density conditions obtained in the magnetized case are caused by the
~10 kT compressed B-field predicted in the simulation.
These results provide a first demonstration of the suitability of dopant spectroscopy as a reliable
technique to unravel the effects of high magnetization in plasma conditions. Moreover, spectroscopic
analysis allows us to build a bridge with the underlying extended-MHD physics, as the results align well

with the impact of a ~ 10 kT compressed magnetic field predicted in simulation. The 50% mass-averaged
core temperature increase from ~1 keV to ~1.5 keV is due to the magnetic suppression of heat conduction,
and to our knowledge, this is the highest relative temperature increase reported in a magnetized implosion
experiment, exceeding the ~40% theoretical limit derived for spherical implosions [18,19].

Discovery Science proposal for a new magnetized implosion platform on NIF (FY23-FY24)
 With this new platform design, we aim to diagnose plasma magnetization using two independent
techniques, namely secondary deuterium-tritium neutron spectra [20,21] and dopant K-shell
spectroscopy [22,23]. The experimental results are to be compared with MHD predictions. Note that the
criteria for tritons confinement is akin to alpha particles’ confinement, making the proposed study also
relevant to magnetized ICF studies [23,24,25]. Demonstrating triton confinement is new in laser-driven
implosions and gives the opportunity to unambiguously show the impact of resistive diffusion losses [26]
and other loss mechanisms [27] on B-field compressibility. Using metrics established by our collaborator
[18], our first objective will be to show that the ratio of primary DD to secondary DT neutron yield and
secondary neutrons’ spectra can be used to infer the compressed B-field and its topology [28]. This
technique complements the more indirect inference of compressed magnetic field from increases in
temperature, obtained from dopant spectroscopy and primary neutron spectra, which will also be measured
in the experiment.
