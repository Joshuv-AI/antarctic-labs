 ABSTRACT

This report details the responsibilities, outcomes, and project details of a summer R&D
internship at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). LANL is a multidisciplinary laboratory
that focuses on current cutting-edge research in many fields such as national security,
engineering, materials science, computational modeling, and advanced manufacturing. The goal
of the internship project was to work with lab engineers and resources to develop an energy
absorbing structure for high-velocity impact applications. The successful development of this
technology and methodology would not only positively impact future project funding but also
contribute to the laboratory's commitment to solve national security challenges through
simultaneous excellence. Such devices would also support efforts surrounding the research and
development of energy absorbing structures and would provide new vital information backed by
experimentation. Different computational and modeling methods were used to design these
structures, in addition to qualitative background information provided by past literature. The
resultant designs were successfully tested, and the test results were successfully quantified. From
these results, new computational methods were developed through python programming and
modeling to predict ideal materialistic properties for these structures given a sufficiently defined
application.

I. INTRODUCTION
The United States has 16 national laboratories across the country each dedicated to solving the
nation’s most pressing scientific problems, inventing and innovating, and pushing world-wide
scientific advancement forward. Los Alamos National Laboratory is one of these historic and
prestigious laboratories. Based in New Mexico, the lab was established in 1943 during World War
II as a secret facility with the sole purpose of developing the world’s first atomic bomb for the
Manhattan Project. While the lab’s initial mission was narrowly focused, over the decades LANL
has become a multi-disciplinary facility that houses diverse leading-edge research all the way from
microbiology to nuclear deterrence. The lab is comprised of roughly 18,000 employees, each
assigned to their own specialized group. The project outlined in this report takes place within one
of the many engineering division groups responsible for the R&D of new technologies. The sole
purpose of this project is to additively manufacture an energy absorber to mitigate damages
associated with high-speed impact applications.

The analysis and design of high-performance structural technologies is a complex focus in
engineering that requires the use of principles ranging from solid mechanics to signal processing.
Such technologies require heavy computation and simulation, as basic static and uniform
considerations are often not applicable. This requires the utilization of CAD, programming, and
FEA software. For this project initial predictions and calculations were heavily reliant on a mix of
theory, calculation, and simulation. This was done with Ansys LS Dyna [5] and a python program
developed with Anaconda’s Jupyter software [2]. After the design specifications were quantified
and selected, the basic shape was modeled in SolidWorks [1]. It is important to note that given
certain geometric and dynamic constraints, simplistic uniform geometries are not always ideal in
such applications. To accommodate this, the original CAD model was converted to a STEP file
that was fed into nTop [3], a modeling tool for complex geometries and optimization. Currently
these types of structures are on the forefront of innovation due to their incredible performance
capabilities, and because they cannot be fabricated using standard manufacturing methods. This
calls for the use and development of additive manufacturing methods. To manufacture the final
designs, the file was converted to STL where it was then fed into the Bambu Studio slicer [4] for
Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) printing.

The development of optimized energy absorbers with complex geometries is a vital focus as
engineers continue to create more robust high-energy systems than ever seen before. As such, the
general goal of the work detailed above is to successfully learn how to design and produce
structures for these kinds of applications. To meet this goal, the structure must be able to withstand
the dynamic loading it will be subjected to by absorbing a substantial amount of energy. It must
also simultaneously remain compliant and not too rigid, so that shocks will not be transmitted to
more sensitive components. This design is reliant on a few different variables which are
interconnected, making the process of defining these variables a challenging one. Because of this,
more advanced and less tried computational approaches are also developed through this project.
This project additionally relies on new manufacturing methods, which creates a push to improve
current additive manufacturing technology. Overall, the success of this project broadly contributes
to LANL’s commitment to scientific advancement, as well as national security.

II. EXPERIMENTAL
Foremost, this project requires sufficient understanding of energy absorbing structures, past
research conducted, and computational methods for design. The beginning of this summer term
was used reviewing past literature and computational approaches to determine how to apply these
principles to support critical design decisions. These topics include, but are not limited to, solid
mechanics, kinematics, differential equations, signal processing, linear algebra, and materials
science.

Once the necessary approach was determined, calculations were done using python programming.
This set-up required information from prior simulation data that was taken from Ansys LS-DYNA
with post-processing done in LS-PrePost. There were many unknowns when characterizing the
structure, so certain simplifications were made. Additionally, given time constraints with testing,
manufacturing methods were limited to additive manufacturing processes on-site. This outlined
the materials available to produce the structures with. All work in this phase was completed with
company-provided computers, and office equipment.

During the design phase of this project, initial computer-aided design modeling was done using
SolidWorks. The dimensioning of the structure was determined by specified experimental
conditions which defined the region available to work with. To optimize energy absorption, further
design took place in nTop, where more complexities were introduced into the design. From a
SolidWorks STEP file, the design was altered in nTop and finally converted to an STL. This final
file was then sliced in Bambu Studio, which allowed the designs to then be printed with available
filaments on a Bambu X1E fused deposition modeling 3D printer.

After manufacturing was complete, physical testing took place on July 6 th. These tests returned
several physical qualities and results that were able to be further analyzed and understood. Using
optical and physical measurement instruments, these meaningful quantities were extracted. Once
this was completed, the rest of the summer term was spent analyzing these findings further.

Characterization and conclusions were drawn through a mix of numerical, modeling, and
simulation efforts. The initial python program used in outlining specific quantities, such as
stiffness, were compared with the physical results to assess the prediction accuracy. Additionally,
nTop was also used again to homogenize the model to better understand points of different stress
concentrations and elastic modulus. Finally, the experimental set up was recreated in Ansys LS
Dyna with real data from the testing and compared to the initial simulations utilized.

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Based on experimentation and testing results, this project successfully pushed forward progress
with developing computational and modeling methods to design an effective energy absorber. A
major initial obstacle with designing the samples was determining fit and tolerancing based on the
boundary conditions defined for the application. Additionally, there were concerns with being able
to pass light through the structures due to their geometry, which was the mode by which the optical
measurement system would provide necessary data. In testing, the findings showed that all samples
successfully met this baseline criterion that were necessary to obtain meaningful data. From this
data, homogenization, time-series analysis, and further modeling were able to be conducted. This
allowed for characterization of physical findings, which will provide framework for future designs
and testing. However, the findings also revealed that the three samples were not ideal energy
absorbers. The chart below details the subjective performance quality of each of the three samples
based on past literature/research, as well as prior computation.

observed by separate testing on a different project. Sample 2 demonstrated that although a structure

IV. CONCLUSION
This summer R&D internship at Los Alamos National Laboratory successfully advanced the
development and understanding of energy absorbing structures for high-velocity impact
applications. Through a combination of computational modeling, additive manufacturing, and
physical testing, the project demonstrated both the challenges and opportunities inherent in
designing optimized energy absorbers. While all samples met baseline criteria, their performance
highlighted the critical importance of material selection and structural design in achieving ideal
energy absorption and with safe failure modes. The findings reinforced established principles
from past literature such as the correlation between relative density and stiffness. Although none
of the tested structures were fully ideal, the results offer a valuable framework for future
improvements and underscore the significance of continued innovation in this field. The work
completed contributes meaningfully to the laboratory’s mission of scientific advancement and
national security and lays the groundwork for further research and development in energy
absorbing technologies.
