Nursing Students' Evaluation of a Team‐Based Clinical Learning Environment and Mentoring—A Mixed Methods Study
Authors: Tytti Karjalainen, Ashlee Oikarinen, Kristina Mikkonen, Pirkko Sivonen, Taina Kärsämänoja, Camilla Strandell‐Laine, Jonna Juntunen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe clinical learning environment of nursing students is increasingly shifting from individual one‐on‐one mentoring to a variety of team‐based mentoring models. As the mentoring model changes, it is important to pay attention to students' mentoring experiences to ensure the quality of mentoring and a positive learning experience. The aim of this study was to explore nursing students' experiences and evaluation of a team‐based clinical learning environment and mentoring, and to identify associated factors.DesignA mixed methods study design was applied. Data were collected from an online Healthcare feedback survey using a modified Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher instrument (CLES + T) from nursing students (n = 397) who received team‐based mentoring in Oulu University Hospital during 2019–2020. The instrument included both quantitative items and qualitative open‐ended questions. Quantitative data were analysed using the K‐means clustering algorithm, and qualitative data were analysed using content analysis. Convergent mixed methods analysis was applied to compare the results of cluster analysis, defining student profiles, and inductive content analysis of students' evaluation of the clinical learning environment and mentoring.ResultsThree student profiles were identified in total, showing that students rated the clinical learning environment and mentoring more highly when mentors supported their professional development and when learning goals were discussed with the mentor and achieved. Content analysis corroborated these findings, highlighting the importance of enhancing preparation for clinical practice, enabling a well‐designed and unified pedagogical learning environment with committed, well‐educated and goal‐oriented mentors.ConclusionMost students were satisfied with team‐based clinical practice. In order to support the achievement of students' learning goals and their professional development, the team‐based clinical learning environment should be thorough, consistent, safe and motivating in terms of the structures of the pedagogical environment and in mentoring as well as in the preparation of students for team‐based learning.ImpactThe results of this research can be used to further develop team‐based mentoring models of clinical practice and to develop training for mentors.Patient or Public ContributionNo Patient or Public Contribution.

Introduction

Nursing students' competence consists of various areas of expertise, such as comprehensive critical thinking, decision‐making, and cooperation skills that are learned mainly in clinical settings through real patient contact (Rusch et al. 2019; Panda et al. 2021). Traditionally, nursing students' clinical learning takes place in various healthcare settings under individual mentoring from registered nurses (Saukkoriipi et al. 2020; Tuomikoski et al. 2020). During mentoring, mentors facilitate learning, supervise, and assess nursing students to ensure patient safety (Flott and Linden 2016). Mentoring facilitates learning in clinical practice and promotes professional development. Under individual mentoring, nursing students evaluate their clinical learning environment and mentoring more highly when they have had a named mentor, the student and mentor have discussed learning goals, there is a final assessment of the clinical placement, and the nurse mentor's mentoring skills support student learning in the clinical placement (Pitkänen et al. 2018; Saukkoriipi et al. 2020).

Recently, there has been a global need to increase educational provisions to develop such competence due to ageing populations and the loss of nursing staff through retirement or nurses leaving the profession. Hence, there have been increases in the number of students admitted to nursing programs and pressure to develop alternative clinical practice models to ensure there are sufficient clinical placements (Beech et al. 2019; Markowski et al. 2021). Potential solutions include enabling students to complete clinical placements working in pairs or small groups and to take the lead in patient care under the mentoring of healthcare professionals (Markowski et al. 2021). Numerous studies have shown that providing students with this type of collaborative, team‐based clinical practice can help them develop clinical skills, cooperation, and an understanding of responsibility in patient care (Crawford et al. 2018; Ekstedt et al. 2019; Markowski et al. 2021). However, there are known challenges in creating a learning culture that promotes these skills (Antohe et al. 2016). When moving from individual mentoring to a team‐based model, there is a growing need to understand the factors that support and impede students' learning experiences. This study was interested in the nursing students' perception of one Finnish university hospital where this shift has been promoted in many units. This study aimed to acquire and analyse nursing students' evaluations of the team‐based clinical learning environments and mentoring, and to then identify associated factors.

Background

In Finland, licences to practice nursing are granted to graduating bachelor's degree nurses, midwives, public health nurses and paramedics. Nursing students spend at least half of their education in clinical practice under the supervision of healthcare professionals, that is, mentors (European Parliament 2013). High‐quality learning environment and individual mentoring during clinical practice have been found to be an important factor in nursing students' professional growth and development of professional identity (Gusar et al. 2020; Tuomikoski et al. 2020), and have been consistently shown to affect how and what nursing students learn during their clinical practice (Saukkoriipi et al. 2020; Yaas et al. 2023). Factors known to influence the quality of clinical practice units as learning environments include characteristics of the physical environment, psychosocial interactions, organisational culture, and mentor training programs (Flott and Linden 2016). An effective clinical learning environment requires a caring, trusting student‐mentor relationship that encourages students to think and act like nurses (Rosli et al. 2022). Key elements in this relationship are mentors' clinical competence and mentoring abilities (Tuomikoski et al. 2020), as they act as professional role models by integrating theory into practice in safe patient care (Flott and Linden 2016).

Clinical practice has traditionally been organised on an individual basis, with mentoring provided in a personalised one‐on‐one relationship between a student and a designated mentor (Saukkoriipi et al. 2020). Team‐based models for organising clinical practice involve mentoring between a mentor and two to four students at once, with students being encouraged to take an active and independent role as a team to take care of their assigned patients. In team‐based mentoring models, the mentor's role is to help students understand their responsibility, encourage cooperation and support them in making appropriate nursing decisions (Manninen et al. 2015).

Antohe et al. (2016) found that individualised mentoring has advantages over team‐based models in terms of students' satisfaction with clinical practice. Ekstedt et al. (2019) compared these two models of mentoring and found that, although students had positive experiences in both groups overall, students who provided team‐based mentoring had more positive experiences of cooperation and relationships among the student, mentor, and nurse teacher. Oosteroma et al. (2019) reviewed the literature on interprofessional training wards in which students work in teams to provide patient care. The results were promising, showing a positive trend in patient satisfaction rates and student outcomes; however, additional research is needed given the methodological quality of most studies (Oosteroma et al. 2019). Nursing students' clinical practice has been identified as a significant factor in teaching students the crucial aspects of nursing, such as time management, prioritisation, medical competence, and handling multiple patients during shifts (Rusch et al. 2019). Team‐based models can provide such experience and thereby enhance critical thinking, problem‐solving skills (Ekstedt et al. 2019), and interprofessional cooperation (Oosteroma et al. 2019).

Finnish clinical practice units have commonly been rated by nursing students as high‐quality learning environments (Pitkänen et al. 2018; Saukkoriipi et al. 2020). Saukkoriipi et al. (2020) found that students' satisfaction with their clinical practice was positively associated with support provided during their learning. However, Pitkänen et al. (2018) found that students' satisfaction is related to students having one‐on‐one time with their mentors, which is less easy to arrange in team‐based mentoring. It is also known that team‐based mentoring can be challenging, as it requires a clear execution plan, pedagogical understanding, continuous evaluation and development (Manninen et al. 2015), as well as education for both students and mentors (Oosteroma et al. 2019; Tuomikoski et al. 2020). Due to the complexities of the factors involved, there is a clear need for detailed mixed analyses to characterise and understand the issues related to students' assessment of team‐based mentoring (Gusar et al. 2020). To distinguish these connections, there was interest in grouping students' assessments based on their similarities in satisfaction with team‐based clinical practice (Munro 2005). By creating these different profiles, the factors related to both students' backgrounds and the implementation of team‐based mentoring could be compared. Thus, this was the approach adopted in our study, as described in the following section.

Objectives

The aim of this study was to explore nursing students' experiences and evaluation of a team‐based clinical learning environment and mentoring, and identify associated factors.

The following research questions were specifically addressed: What kinds of distinct nursing students' profiles can be identified relating to nursing students' evaluation of the clinical learning environment and mentoring in team‐based clinical practice?What types of experiences have nursing students described regarding team‐based clinical learning environments and mentoring?

Methods

Design

A convergent mixed methods design was applied to enhance the overall understanding of Finnish team‐based mentoring by analysing quantitative and qualitative data separately and integrating the findings during the interpretation phase (Grove and Gray 2019; Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). The data were obtained from nursing students' responses to multiple‐choice and open‐ended questions in an online Healthcare feedback survey, using a modified Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher (CLES+T) instrument. There was an interest in both being able to distinguish factors related to students' evaluation from the statistical data and gaining a deeper understanding from the qualitative responses (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018), as suggested by Gusar et al. (2020). During convergent mixed‐methods analysis, the quantitative and qualitative data are analysed separately, after which their similarities and differences are discussed. This was used to maximise the advantages and compensate for the weaknesses of both quantitative and qualitative analysis and increase the reliability of the results (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018.) To perform this, cluster analysis was applied to group responses based on similarities across multiple‐choice questions, after which statistically significant factors were identified by comparing the formed profiles with background factors (Munro 2005). Inductive content analysis was used to broaden the understanding of nursing students' assessment (Mikkonen and Kyngäs 2020). Finally, findings from both quantitative and qualitative data were considered in an integrated fashion in the discussion section of the article (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018).

Participants and Settings

Participants in this study were nursing, paramedic, public health nursing, and midwifery students who had completed a clinical practice in team‐based mentoring units at Oulu University Hospital during the calendar years 2019 and 2020.

Data Collection and Study Sample

This study utilised pre‐existing data obtained from the Oulu University Hospital Healthcare Student Feedback Survey. At this hospital, students' feedback on their clinical practice environment is collected electronically through an anonymous Webropol link provided upon completion of their clinical practice. Students direct their feedback to the relevant clinical units and mentoring models they have experienced. Consequently, the data used in this study were pre‐existing, and the researchers had no control over their content or structure (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). The dataset was delivered to the researchers by a hospital clinical nurse educator in a password‐protected file.

Data were drawn from responses collected during the calendar years 2019 and 2020 (n = 2935). The overall response rate for the survey was 63.0% in 2019 and 57.1% in 2020, yielding a combined average of 60.1%. Participants representing the target population (n = 421) were identified based on their self‐reported completion of a clinical practice in a team‐based mentoring model during the survey. The exact size of the target population could not be determined, and thus, a reliable response rate could not be calculated. This was due to organisational changes in clinical units, variability in the implementation of team‐based mentoring, disruptions caused by the COVID‐19 pandemic, and voluntary participation based on self‐reported clinical placement type.

Instrument

In Finland, the quality of clinical learning environments and mentoring is monitored by the widely used anonymous online Healthcare student feedback survey. It includes the use of the modified CLES+T scale, a valid and reliable tool used internationally to evaluate the quality of mentoring in healthcare (Saarikoski et al. 2008; Gustafsson et al. 2015). The present study is based on data collected using the 2019 and 2020 versions of this national survey (Meretoja et al. 2018). At the end of their clinical practice, students can voluntarily complete a survey to evaluate the learning environment and mentoring in their current placement.

The modified CLES+T (Saarikoski et al. 2008) instrument consists of five sub‐dimensions: pedagogical atmosphere (7 items), premises of care (4 items), premises of learning (7 items), supervisory relationship (8 items), and role of the nurse teacher (9–11 items, varied by different years). Each item presents Likert‐type responses with a scale from 1 (fully disagree) to 10 (fully agree). Higher scores indicate more positive evaluations of the clinical learning environment and mentoring. In addition, there were altogether 21 separate questions covering the clinical practice (length of the clinical practice in weeks, the method of mentoring and how it was conducted, discussions held with the mentor about learning goals and how those set goals were accomplished, how the mentoring and the education supported the professional development, questions about interim and final assessment and overall assessment) and the student's background (age, previous and ongoing education, academic year, study language, degree programme and educational institution). Students are also invited to share their personal experiences through voluntary open‐ended questions.

However, there were some differences between the 2019 and 2020 surveys, which led the researchers to exclude some data before analysis. In 2020, the Likert scale from 1 (fully disagree) to 10 (fully agree) was extended to include a point labelled XX (does not concern me), resulting in missing data in the aforementioned sub‐dimensions. One item in the first four sub‐dimensions of the CLES+T scale had a missing data rate of 5.3% (the ward manager of the clinical unit supported my learning). This was accepted because the others' rates were < 1%. In 2020, the “nurse teacher” sub‐dimension of the CLES+T scale was extended, but the new items had not yet been validated (Strandell‐Laine 2019). Moreover, the missing data rate was highest for the “nurse teacher” sub‐dimension (9.1%–11.6%), and only six of the sub‐dimension's questions were the same as those in the 2019 survey, so this sub‐dimension was excluded from the analysis. The exclusion was necessary to ensure comparability between the two survey years and to avoid including non‐validated items. In the present study, the internal consistency of the four sub‐dimensions included in the analyses was good to excellent, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.81 to 0.97.

Also, the 2019 survey included three open‐ended questions: “What was the most meaningful learning experience?”, “How would you develop your clinical practice?” and “What else would you like to tell us about your recently ended clinical practice?”. Only the last question was included in the 2020 survey. Student responses to the 2020 version of the survey covered the same theme as the second and third questions in the 2019 version. Therefore, the responses to these two questions were seen as meaningful.

It should also be noted that the instrument used in this study is based on the Finnish national Healthcare Student Feedback Survey, in which the CLES+T scale has been modified over time by the organisations responsible for its administration, in cooperation with the developers of the instrument. It should also be noted that the instrument has undergone several changes over the years, including those observed between 2019 and 2020. Compared to the validated 2008 version (Saarikoski et al. 2008), nine out of 26 items have been slightly altered, four new items have been added to the first four sections of the instrument, and three have been removed. In the present study, only items that were identical across both years were included in the analysis to ensure consistency.

Data Analysis

The first step in analysing the acquired data was separating the quantitative and qualitative data. A total of 421 responses were initially collected. Responses directed to other clinical departments (n = 5) and to other educational programs (n = 15) were excluded. To ensure that the results were not distorted by partial completion of the survey, responses with more than 50% missing data (n = 1) and responses lacking answers to all items within at least one of the first four sub‐dimensions of the CLES+T instrument (n = 3) were also excluded. Following these exclusions, the quantitative analysis was based on responses from 397 nursing students.

Quantitative data obtained from these responses were analysed with SPSS Statistics (version 26, IBM Corporation). Since the instrument was changed between 2019 and 2020, separate background questions that were the same in both years (16 items), items from the first four sub‐dimensions of the CLES + T instrument (26 items) and question 9 (Occurrence of supervision), which was changed to question 11 in 2020 (What would best describe the occurrence of supervision?) were included.

Cluster analysis was applied to participants' responses by combining CLES+T items to identify distinct profiles. The number of clusters was determined based on statistical criteria, including a post hoc Bonferroni correction (p < 0.001) and a rule that no cluster should comprise less than 5% of the total sample (Munro 2005). The identified clusters were compared with background variables using cross‐tabulation, and differences between groups in sub‐dimensions were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (Munro 2005).

The survey also included an open‐ended question, which was voluntary and intended to provide complementary qualitative insights. A total of 261 participants responded to the open‐ended question and were included in the qualitative data analysis. Open‐ended responses were not included in the criteria for survey completeness, as non‐response to this item did not affect inclusion in the quantitative analyses. Qualitative data were formed from responses to two of the open questions: the one that was the same in both years and the question “How would you develop your clinical practice?” This was included only in the 2019 survey, but students also provided developmental ideas in 2020, so the question was included. Inductive content analysis was applied to describe the phenomenon using the data students provided and to identify the key features affecting nursing students' team‐based clinical practice experience (Kyngäs 2020; Mikkonen and Kyngäs 2020). Using this, it was possible to deepen students' understanding of their perceptions and to find explanations for the qualitative results (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). Inductive content analysis was performed by reading the data several times to form a general understanding, identifying relevant items, grouping items according to similarities, and finally creating upper and main categories through abstraction (Kyngäs 2020; Mikkonen and Kyngäs 2020).

Finally, a convergent mixed methods approach was applied by comparing the findings in a narrative discussion (Grove and Gray 2019; Creswell and Plano Clark 2018).

Ethical Considerations

The target university hospital granted permission to conduct research in January 2021, and a detailed data protection risk impact assessment was conducted. Permission to use data collected with the CLES+T instrument was received from the copyright holder of the scale. The copyright holder granted the university hospital, collecting data from students, permission to use the CLES+T instrument. The Finnish Advisory Board on Research Integrity guidelines (TENK 2023), covering issues such as honesty, diligence, accuracy, accountability and transparency, were followed throughout the study. According to the TENK (2023) guidelines and the Finnish Medical Research Act, separate ethical review was not required because the study involved competent adult participants, posed no psychological or physical risk, and did not include any intervention. Nursing students answered the survey voluntarily and anonymously after their clinical practice. Information about the possibility of using the data for research purposes is presented at the beginning of the survey. Individual students cannot be identified. The clinical areas, units, and any mentions of staff members or fellow students in the open answers were coded into general terms, and their anonymity was strongly preserved (Grove and Gray 2019). Data were stored on a password‐protected personal computer and hard drive, and the results were compared openly and with respect to other studies (TENK 2023). All relevant stipulations of the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (European Parliament 2016) were rigorously followed.

Results

The final study sample consisted of 397 participants whose responses were included in the quantitative analyses. Of these, 261 provided written responses to the voluntary open‐ended question and were included in the qualitative analysis. Team‐based mentoring was implemented in 26.0% of the hospital's clinical units in 2019 and in a further 11.1% in 2020. Study participants were enrolled in seven educational institutions. The majority were nursing students (63.7%), followed by midwifery (21.7%), public health nursing (14.4%), and paramedic nursing students (0.3%). Overall, 59.9% of the participants reported having previous education. Most participants were aged 20–24 years (44.6%) and were predominantly second‐year students (61.2%).

Quantitative Results

K‐means clustering identified three distinct student profiles based on their perceptions of the clinical learning environment and mentoring across the first four sub‐dimensions of the CLES+T instrument. These profiles differed significantly from each other (p < 0.001). Profile differences were observed consistently across all four CLES+T sub‐dimensions (pedagogical atmosphere, premises of nursing care, premises of learning, and supervisory relationship), indicating distinct patterns in students' experiences of the clinical learning environment and mentoring. As shown in Table 1, students in Profile 1 (n = 252) reported the highest scores across all four CLES+T sub‐dimensions, with mean scores ranging from 9.08 (SD 0.66) to 9.30 (SD 0.59). Students in Profile 2 (n = 116) reported moderate scores across all sub‐dimensions, with mean scores ranging from 7.24 (SD 0.97) to 7.70 (SD 0.89). Students in Profile 3 (n = 29) reported the lowest scores across all sub‐dimensions, with mean scores ranging from 3.57 (SD 1.19) to 6.34 (SD 1.72). The largest differences between profiles were observed in the supervisory relationship and pedagogical atmosphere sub‐dimensions, where Profile 3 students reported markedly lower scores compared to Profiles 1 and 2 (Table 1).

Overall, Profile 1 can be characterised by a highly supportive pedagogical atmosphere, well‐functioning learning and care environments, and strong supervisory relationships. Profile 2 reflects a moderately positive but less consistent experience across these dimensions. In contrast, Profile 3 represents a clearly less favourable learning environment, particularly in relation to supervision and pedagogical atmosphere.

Almost half (46.4%) of Profile 1 students were 20–24 years old, and most were nursing (66.7%) or midwifery (21.4%) students in their second academic year (61.1%). A significantly higher share (79%) discussed their learning goals with their mentor than students of the other profiles (p < 0.001), they scored achievement of their learning goals most highly (mean 4.49, SD 0.57, p < 0.001) and felt most satisfied with their perceived mentoring in relation to professional development (mean 4.69, SD 0.48; p < 0.001).

In line with the high CLES+T sub‐dimension scores, these students also reported the most positive evaluations of supervisory practices and learning support, suggesting alignment between structured mentoring processes and perceived quality of the learning environment.

Of the Profile 2 students in the profile, 38.8% were 30 years old or younger, 62.9% had prior education, and most (87.1%) completed their clinical cycle in 4–5 weeks. Of these students, 57.8% discussed their learning goals with their mentor (p < 0.001), but they rated their achievement of these goals lower (mean 4.08, SD 0.64; p < 0.001) than profile 1 students. Their moderate CLES+T scores suggest that while the basic structures of the learning environment were in place, the consistency and quality of supervision and pedagogical support were less optimal than in Profile 1.

Most Profile 3 students were 20–24 years old (62.1%). Compared to the other profile groups, this group had the highest proportion of public health nursing students (24.1%) and the lowest proportion with prior education (51.7%). Most (72.4%) were in their second academic year. More than half (58.6%) discussed their learning goals with their mentor (p < 0.001), but they rated the achievement of their learning goals lowest (mean 3.86, SD 0.92; p < 0.001).

Consistent with their low scores across all CLES+T sub‐dimensions, particularly supervisory relationship (mean 3.57, SD 1.19) and pedagogical atmosphere (mean 4.09, SD 1.4)—these students appear to have experienced significant challenges in mentoring, support, and the overall learning environment (Table 1).

There were significant differences in students' overall evaluations of the clinical placement and mentoring (p < 0.001). Profile 1 students rated it most highly (mean 9.18, SD 0.32), profile 2 students gave intermediate ratings (mean 8.2, SD 0.66), and profile 3 students were least satisfied (mean 6.34, SD 1.23). These overall evaluations were consistent with the pattern observed across the CLES+T sub‐dimensions, reinforcing the distinction between the three profiles.

Another important element of the pedagogical learning environment was the staff's efforts to secure goal‐oriented learning in team‐based mentoring. Students felt that mentors' support in setting and achieving individual learning goals was important. They needed opportunities to acquire competence both with their assigned patients and in other situations that may occur in the unit. A major cause of challenges in mentoring relationships in the team‐based learning environment was the constant turnover of mentors. This made it difficult for mentors to get to know the students, form trusting relationships, recognise competences, and ultimately help students achieve learning goals. The third main category, “Mentor's team‐based mentoring competence”, encompassed three upper categories. One mentor's team‐based mentoring skills included intelligence and patience in helping students identify issues that need attention in patient care and in allowing them to perform nursing tasks that matched their level of competence, which were seen as rewarding. Students expressed a desire to understand patient‐related matters independently. The final upper category was supporting students' professional development. Students described mentors' support in learning practical skills, advancing knowledge, and outlining comprehensive nursing care as some of the most significant elements. Comprehensive nursing care was easier to perceive when mentors provided grounds for their own actions among students, colleagues, patients and family members and urged students to do the same. Discussion

This convergent mixed‐methods study examined nursing students' experiences of team‐based clinical learning environments and mentoring by combining quantitative CLES+T profiles with qualitative descriptions of those experiences. The quantitative results identified three distinct profiles reflecting high, moderate, and low evaluations across the four CLES+T sub‐dimensions (pedagogical atmosphere, premises of nursing care, premises of learning, and supervisory relationship). The qualitative findings further explained these differences by highlighting factors such as preparation for mentoring, consistency of pedagogical practices, and mentors' competence. Together, the findings indicate that positive student experiences are associated with pedagogically consistent environments, strong supervisory relationships, and well‐prepared mentors, whereas challenges in these areas are reflected in lower evaluations across the CLES+T dimensions. This study highlights nursing students' generally positive experiences of team‐based clinical learning environments and mentoring and identifies variation in how these environments are perceived. The findings suggest that team‐based mentoring can provide a supportive framework for achieving learning goals, but its effectiveness depends on the consistency and pedagogical quality of the clinical learning environment. The results also emphasise the important role of mentors in supporting students' professional development. The results also indicate that well‐structured team‐based mentoring may help address the growing demand for clinical placements.

The quantitative and qualitative findings support the study objectives by providing a convergent overview of nursing students' perspectives on the clinical learning environment, mentoring in team‐based clinical practice, and associated factors. The findings show that a good team‐based learning environment is pedagogically consistent. This is particularly reflected in Profile 1's high scores across all CLES+T sub‐dimensions, especially the pedagogical atmosphere and supervisory relationship. These were qualitatively explained by students' experiences of clear mentoring practices, consistent expectations, and supportive staff collaboration. In addition, the mentors in this study were well prepared for students' arrival, interested in students' learning goals, and supported students' professional development in several ways.

Satisfaction with clinical practice is expected if students are properly integrated into their working community and their personal needs are individually supported (Ekstedt et al. 2019; Saukkoriipi et al. 2020). Accordingly, we found that profile 1 students, who were highly satisfied with their clinical practice, were the most satisfied with the unit's learning premises. The respondents felt that a caring atmosphere, with social, practical, and theoretical support, provided a good start for team‐based clinical practice. Despite variations in preparation across the clinical units, students felt welcome and respected, and believed their mentors were taken seriously. Their high evaluations across the CLES+T sub‐dimensions were supported by qualitative findings describing well‐organised orientation, goal‐oriented mentoring, and a strong commitment by clinical staff to students' learning. Profile 1 students also reported discussing their learning goals with their mentors, a practice whose importance has already been recognised (Pitkänen et al. 2018; Saukkoriipi et al. 2020). This suggests that opportunities to comprehensively review the clinical unit's layout and to understand students' needs are significant in team‐based mentoring.

Since acquiring nursing competence in a team‐based clinical practice involves practising independence and taking responsibility for patient care (Ekstedt et al. 2019), it might be most suitable for final‐year students (Pitkänen et al. 2018). In this study, the majority of profile 3 students (who were least satisfied with their team‐based clinical practice) were between 20 and 24 years old, suggesting that this age group may require more thorough support and guidance. However, the profile 3 group is smaller (n = 29), and almost half of the profile 1 students (the most satisfied) were in the same age group, so the same conclusion cannot be drawn from this study. We found a slightly higher‐than‐average representation of profile 3 respondents among public health students (who tend to work independently), while midwifery students were more satisfied than average with team‐based mentoring. Therefore, the suitability of team‐based mentoring should always be considered in relation to students' learning goals (Oosteroma et al. 2019).

Team‐based peer learning models are recognised as valuable for students' learning experience in clinical practice (Markowski et al. 2021). Accordingly, profile 1 students most frequently reported achieving their learning goals and were the most satisfied with the pedagogical atmosphere and the premises of nursing care. This may indicate that their team‐based learning environment was well‐designed and that nursing tasks matched their competence (Flott and Linden 2016). Students felt that the entire clinical unit and staff needed to be pedagogically committed to team‐based mentoring in accordance with nursing guidelines. Inconsistencies in mentors' nursing principles or in expectations of students' competence prevented some students from participating in nursing procedures, creating an incomplete picture of comprehensive nursing care. These challenges help explain the lower scores observed, particularly in the pedagogical atmosphere and premises of learning sub‐dimensions among Profile 2 and Profile 3 students. Students also listed several issues, including staff turnover and mentors being overloaded with other duties, which can disrupt mentoring continuity and, therefore, hinder learning (Panda et al. 2021). Challenges affecting student‐mentor relationships in team‐based mentoring have also been well documented in a previous study (Gusar et al. 2020).

Yaseen Fathi and Ibrahim (2023) identified a lack of mentoring as a major challenge in clinical learning environments. In our study, students highlighted the importance of mentors' competence in promoting students' professional growth, a finding well supported in the literature (Tuomikoski et al. 2020). Profile 1 students reported feeling supported and rated their supervisory relationship the highest. This aligns with their highest scores in the supervisory relationship sub‐dimension. Profile 3 students' low scores in this dimension were reflected in qualitative descriptions of mentor turnover, lack of continuity, and limited individual support. Similar correlations have also been detected in individual mentoring (Antohe et al. 2016; Saukkoriipi et al. 2020; Yaseen Fathi and Ibrahim 2023), where getting to know students and their learning goals individually is easier (Crawford et al. 2018). However, mentors' attitudes can have a greater impact on students' perceptions of their clinical practice than the number of mentors (Bhurtun et al. 2019). Corroborating previous studies (Flott and Linden 2016), we also found that mentors' attitudes affect students' competence. Students described needing mentors' support in realising the connections between theory and practice, making judgement calls and understanding the level of their own competence. Thus, the possibility that mentors may supervise several students at once in team‐based units may cause stress and feelings of insufficiency, negatively affecting the student‐mentor relationship (Gusar et al. 2020). Our respondents felt they learned most from a mentor who was encouraging, provided additional information, provided grounds for nursing procedures, and offered constructive feedback. Therefore, although team‐based mentoring has been shown to promote students' independence, responsibility in nursing and cognitive skills (Crawford et al. 2018; Ekstedt et al. 2019), students also need to be included in nursing and supported in their learning journey by competent mentors (Tuomikoski et al. 2020). Mentors, therefore, need to be well‐prepared and educated in how to be mentors (Pitkänen et al. 2018; Tuomikoski et al. 2020) in any implementation of team‐based mentoring (Oosteroma et al. 2019), as well as in units that use individual mentoring.

The qualitative findings provide clear explanations for the quantitative profile differences, demonstrating that variations in pedagogical atmosphere, learning opportunities, and supervisory relationships are closely linked to how mentoring is organised, implemented, and experienced in team‐based clinical practice.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several limitations. Most importantly, it reflects the perceptions of students who completed their clinical placements within a single Finnish hospital between 2019 and 2020. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalising the findings beyond the study context. However, the study was conducted in a large university hospital encompassing a wide range of clinical specialities and learning environments, with data collected across multiple clinical units. This diversity broadens the range of perspectives represented in the data and may enhance the applicability of the findings to similar healthcare settings. In addition, some sub‐dimensions of the CLES+T scale were not included, which may have influenced the conclusions drawn from the cluster analysis. Nevertheless, the findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies, supporting the credibility of the results.

Reliability of qualitative and quantitative data is considered separately (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). The CLES+T instrument has been found to be valid and reliable in previous studies (Saarikoski et al. 2008; Gustafsson et al. 2015). The researchers had no control over the data content, as the target hospital and mentors were responsible for encouraging students to provide feedback on their clinical practice (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). However, some aspects may affect students' perceptions. Clinical practice is usually over by the time the feedback is given. The reliability of the data obtained may be influenced by the student's experiences during the last days of the clinical practice, the time of day, mentors' personal attributes, future studies or placements. The evaluation mentors give students following the clinical practice may also influence the feedback the students give.

For the statistical analysis, widely used and reliable SPSS software was used. The methods and analytical process were described in detail to ensure the study's reliability and reproducibility (Polit and Beck 2012). The analysis of qualitative data was thoroughly described to ensure quality, and feedback was also guided by a strict focus on understanding what the student wanted to say (Polit and Beck 2012; Kyngäs 2020; Mikkonen and Kyngäs 2020). Students' feedback was not deleted or modified, except when the student named a person or clinical unit. The study's qualitative data were reduced to ensure a clear link between the raw data and the categories. In addition to the headings and subcategories, the original expressions were used to describe experiences (Kyngäs 2020).

The possibility of bias existed, as the researcher had been working as a mentor at the target hospital at the time of the study. Challenges related to this aspect, as well as those with developing and implementing team‐based clinical practice environments, were well known prior to the study. To maintain an objective point of view, the analysis steps were carefully documented (Mikkonen and Kyngäs 2020). The statistical analysis was conducted by a team of three researchers. During inductive content analysis, the analysis path was continually introduced to the corresponding author, who was also familiar with the steps taken in the convergent mixed‐methods analysis.

Implications for Education

Given the key role of mentors in team‐based clinical practice, healthcare organisations should ensure mentors have adequate resources and sufficient time to support students' learning. The transition from individual to team‐based mentoring can be challenging, as mentors must supervise multiple students simultaneously, build trust, and manage complex learning processes. Based on the findings of this study, structured team‐based mentoring training is recommended to support mentors in establishing and sustaining pedagogically coherent, goal‐oriented clinical learning environments. In addition, close collaboration between clinical practice settings and educational institutions appears essential to the successful implementation of team‐based mentoring models.

Future Research

Further research is needed to explore nurse mentors' perceptions of their competence and experiences in team‐based mentoring and to examine differences between degree programmes in relation to team‐based clinical practice. Additional studies using mixed methods and comprehensive measurement instruments, such as the full CLES+T scale, are warranted to confirm and extend this study's findings. Future research could also investigate variations in team‐based mentoring practices and compare them with traditional models across clinical units in other hospitals and healthcare facilities to better understand their impact on students' learning experiences.

Conclusions

Nursing students participating in this study from the target university hospital were overall satisfied with their team‐based clinical practice and reported achievement of their learning goals, indicating that team‐based mentoring provides an effective and high‐quality clinical learning environment. The findings suggest that team‐based mentoring may be a viable approach to addressing the increasing number of students in clinical placements while maintaining the quality of clinical mentoring. Successful team‐based clinical practice appears to be supported by a well‐prepared, pedagogically consistent learning environment, as well as by mentors who play a central role in students' professional development.
