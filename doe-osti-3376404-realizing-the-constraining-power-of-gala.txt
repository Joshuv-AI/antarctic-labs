1. What are the major goals of the project?

The project includes three major goals:
1. Address the systematics in current Dark Energy Survey (DES) cluster data, including optical selection
 bias and the low lensing signals observed in low-richness clusters.
2. Develop the infrastructure for Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) cluster
 analyses and incorporate the lessons learned from DES data.
3. Develop methods for joint analyses with LSST and the Roman Space Telescope.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

Across the reporting periods, the team made progress on DES cluster systematics, LSST mock-analysis
infrastructure, Roman/LSST spectroscopic methods, and multi-wavelength cluster calibration.
Optical selection bias and DES cluster lensing
The PI completed a thorough investigation of cluster lensing selection bias using DES synthetic
observations (Buzzard). The work concluded that optically selected clusters, such as those used in DES
cluster cosmology, suffer 10-20% selection bias in the lensing signals. The first-author DES paper
"Optical selection bias and projection effects in stacked galaxy cluster weak lensing" was published in
Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society in September 2022.
The team also made a breakthrough in understanding the DES Year 1 (DES-Y1) cluster key project
results, which suffered from unknown systematics in low-mass systems. The DES-Y1 data were re-
analyzed using a simulation-based model and found to be consistent with Planck cosmology. This work
was carried out with Dr. Andres Salcedo, published in Physical Review Letters.
Forward modeling of cluster selection bias
The team developed a forward model of cluster selection bias that incorporates N-body simulations, Halo
Occupation Distributions (HODs), and counts-in-cylinder mock cluster finding. The model is flexible
enough to cover different galaxy models and to predict cluster observables self-consistently. The team
built emulators over a wide range of HOD and projection-depth parameters, used these tools to interpret
DES-Y1 cluster lensing results, and began extending the framework to cluster clustering, DES-Y3, and
cosmological parameter constraints.
This effort included collaborations with Dr. Andres Salcedo, Dr. Chun-Hao To, and Dr. Ananth Tenneti to
apply galaxy-halo connection models to N-body simulations and to create synthetic catalogs for
redMaPPer clusters and redMaGiC galaxies.

Self-calibration of cluster selection bias
The PI supervised Chenxiao Zeng at Ohio State on self-calibrating cluster selection bias by combining
auto- and cross-correlation functions of shear, clusters, and galaxies. The work used Abacus simulations
to demonstrate the feasibility of the analysis for large-scale correlations and developed a method to
recover unbiased nuisance parameters. This work formed a major part of Dr. Zeng's Ph.D. dissertation,
published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.
Multi-wavelength cross-calibration of optical selection bias
The PI supervised Conghao Zhou at UC Santa Cruz on using multi-wavelength data to constrain optical
cluster selection bias. The team created synthetic catalogs for joint optical and SZ observations, derived
the formalism for the analysis, and built extensive software for end-to-end tests. The framework
demonstrated that splitting DES-selected clusters by SPT detection versus non-detection can constrain the
correlated residual between cluster richness and lensing. The resulting manuscript was published in
Physical Review D.
Spectroscopic calibration of projection effects
The PI supervised Andy Lee at Boise State on using SDSS spectroscopic data to quantify cluster
projection effects. The team developed simulations of projection effects and compared model predictions
with the SDSS redMaPPer cluster sample with spectroscopic members, including abundance and redshift-
scanning richness tests. The data complement lensing measurements and can discriminate between
models for projection effects. Mr. Lee graduated from Boise State and joined the Physics Ph.D. program
at Ohio State in fall 2023; manuscript preparation continued afterward.
The team also recruited Dr. Lei Yang and began developing methods for using sparse spectroscopic
galaxy samples to study projection effects. These methods are designed for DESI and Roman
spectroscopic data, which are expected to be highly incomplete in cluster regions, and for combination
with photometric cluster samples from DES, DECaLS, and LSST.
Cluster lensing mass calibration and computational speed
The PI supervised Titus Nyarko Nde on cluster lensing mass calibration. The project explored machine-
learning methods to predict galaxy cluster density profiles and theoretical uncertainties, developed
simulation-based inference approaches that are comparably precise and at least ten times faster than
Markov Chain Monte Carlo, and tested statistical and systematic uncertainties in stacked cluster lensing
signals using N-body simulations and mock catalogs. End-to-end tests used the Mini Uchuu N-body
simulation and the Cardinal mock catalogs. The team also collaborated with Dr. Andrius Tamosiunas on
machine-learning methods for uncertainty quantification.
LSST mock analyses and projection effects
The PI supervised Gladys Muthoni Kamau on cluster lensing selection bias and projection effects using
LSST's CosmoDC2 simulations. This project was approved by the LSST DESC and received computing
time at the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center. The work later expanded to
CosmoDC2, SkySim5000, and Cardinal simulations to understand the sensitivity of projection effects to
galaxy modeling and cluster-finding choices. The PI also supervised Dr. Narayan Khadka on mock cluster
likelihood analyses using CosmoDC2 and SkySim5000 and on the impact of cluster lensing selection bias
on cosmological constraints.
Cluster-galaxy cross-correlations in cluster cosmology
The team recruited Dr. Shulei Cao and began incorporating cluster-galaxy cross-correlations in cluster
cosmology analyses. The approach builds on the simulation-based model above, with the goal of
modeling cluster and galaxy data vectors using unified HODs. The team collaborated with Dr. Chun-Hao
To to use the Cardinal simulation for end-to-end tests of the cosmology pipelines.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?

Graduate students
• Titus Nyarko Nde (Boise State Data Science): cluster lensing modeling, mass calibration, statistical and
 systematic uncertainties, and computation speed.
• Gladys Muthoni Kamau (Boise State Data Science): cluster selection bias and projection effects in
 LSST's CosmoDC2, SkySim5000, and Cardinal simulations.
• Chenxiao Zeng (Ohio State Physics): self-calibrating cluster selection bias using correlations of shear,
 clusters, and galaxies; Ph.D. awarded in December 2022.
• Conghao Zhou (UC Santa Cruz Physics): cross-calibration of optical cluster selection bias using DES x
 SPT data.
The Boise State Data Science Ph.D. students took data science and physics courses and passed their
comprehensive exams in fall 2022. The PI met with students weekly or more often, including fixed group
meetings and on-demand research appointments for complex problem solving.

Postdoctoral fellows and other early-career scientists
• Dr. Ananth Tenneti (Boise State, May 2022-February 2023): cluster galaxies in IllustrisTNG
 hydrodynamic simulations, HOD-pipeline validation, and emulator construction; subsequently
 moved to quantum information at Carnegie Mellon University.
• Dr. Narayan Khadka (Bellarmine University): impact of cluster lensing selection bias on cosmological
 constraints and mock cluster likelihood analyses using CosmoDC2 and SkySim5000; later moved
 to Stony Brook University.
• Dr. Shulei Cao (Boise State): cluster-galaxy cross-correlations in cluster cosmology; joined in June
 2023 after receiving a Ph.D. from Kansas State University.
• Dr. Lei Yang (Boise State): sparse spectroscopic surveys in cluster analyses; joined in August 2023 after
 a postdoctoral position at Yunnan University and a Ph.D. from Shanghai Jiaotong University.
• Dr. Andrius Tamosiunas, Dr. Andres Salcedo, and Dr. Chun-Hao To: external collaborators mentored
 and engaged through regular project meetings.

Undergraduate students
• Andy Lee: spectroscopic calibration for cluster projection effects; joined the Physics Ph.D. program at
 Ohio State in fall 2023.
• Kaitlyn Linder: intrinsic alignment of cluster galaxies; presented a poster at the 2023 Undergraduate
 Research Showcase at Boise State.
• Antone Chacartegui: machine-learning regression for cluster lensing signals.
• Alessia Molino: cluster richness in IllustrisTNG simulations; later joined the Geophysics Ph.D. program
 at UC Santa Cruz.
• Rosana Lenhart: cluster triaxiality in IllustrisTNG simulations.
• Sean Halford: assembly bias in forward modeling of cluster observables.
• Joseph Migchelbrink: photometric-redshift uncertainties in cluster and galaxy two-point correlation
 functions.

The PI typically supervised two to three undergraduate students at a time, training them in Python, data
analysis, literature presentations, poster presentations, and graduate-school preparation.
Meetings, workshops, schools, and courses
The team met at least weekly, often twice a week, in small groups, one-on-one meetings, and hybrid
journal clubs. The journal club began in 2023, focused on galaxy cluster cosmology with a strong
pedagogical emphasis, and involved collaborators across institutions.
Team members attended or planned to attend the Penn State Summer School in Statistics for Astronomers,
DES collaboration meetings, La Serena School for Data Science, the Michigan Cosmology Summer
School, LSST DESC Collaboration Meetings, and the DES Collaboration Meeting in Barcelona.
The project established a yearly cluster-workshop tradition. The first workshop was held at Boise State in
June 2022 with 9 participants, including 3 external participants. The second workshop was held at the
University of Arizona in June 2023 and used a project-sprint format. A third workshop was planned at the
University of Arizona in May 2024.
The PI taught two Boise State courses relevant to the project: Scientific Computing (PHYS 325) and
Cosmology (PHYS 406). These courses introduced students to computational physics, cosmological
research, and current experimental results, and helped recruit undergraduate researchers into the project.
