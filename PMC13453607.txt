The role of the kynurenine pathway in the pathogenesis of pain and affective disorders after traumatic brain injury
Authors: Itzick Nahmoud, Karli Monahan, Eric A Woodcock, Alana C Conti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI) alters neuroimmune physiology resulting in an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cascades. Whereas acute inflammation serves as an adaptive defense mechanism of the innate immune system to promote healing and restore homeostasis after injury, unresolved pro-inflammatory cascades have been shown to contribute to long-term sequelae of TBI. An important, yet understudied, neuroimmune signaling pathway affected by TBI is the kynurenine pathway (KP). Kynurenine is a product of tryptophan metabolism by indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase in the central nervous system, a metabolite downstream of kynurenine, e.g., quinolinic acid and kynurenic acid, as products of kynurenine 3 monooxygenase and kynurenine aminotransferase, respectively. TBI patients exhibit disrupted levels of KP metabolites, including kynurenine and quinolinic acid, in plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, and brain. Alterations in KP metabolite levels have been reported in both Major Depressive Disorder and pain-related conditions, including headaches, fibromyalgia, and neuropathic pain, thereby suggesting a role of the KP in progression of prevalent pathological TBI sequalae, that include depression and chronic pain. This narrative review outlines the KP, evaluates its alterations following TBI in relation to sequelae such as chronic pain and depression, and discusses potential KP directed therapeutic targets within the TBI framework.Keywords: Kynurenine pathway, Traumatic brain injury, Pain, Depression, Microglia, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) impacts approximately 1.4 million Americans each year and is the leading cause of mortality and morbidity among individuals under 40 years old (Gironda et al., 2009; Sayer et al., 2009; Choi et al., 2022; Jorge et al., 1994, 2004; Singh et al., 2018). Among the sequelae of TBI, psychotropic complications most commonly manifest as pain and depression. Meta-analyses indicate that chronic pain and depression symptoms are highly prevalent after TBI, occurring in 40-60% of moderate to severe TBI patients (Gironda et al., 2009; Sayer et al., 2009; Choi et al., 2022; Jorge et al., 1994, 2004; Singh et al., 2018). Pain and depression symptoms are highly comorbid in the general population (Walker et al., 2014; Campos et al., 2020), and remain closely linked and highly prevalent in the context of TBI (Walker et al., 2014; Adams et al., 2020; Jammoul et al., 2024; Sullivan-Singh et al., 2014; Hammond et al., 2015). Following TBI, these conditions can persist for decades contributing substantially to long-term morbidity (Hammond et al., 2015; Harrison-Felix et al., 2024). For instance, a longitudinal prospective observational cohort study including more than 2000 patients over a 9-week period in an inpatient rehabilitation setting found that 70% of TBI patients remained on antidepressants and 40% continued to receive narcotics at week 9 (Walker et al., 2014; Campos et al., 2020; Adams et al., 2020; Jammoul et al., 2024; Sullivan-Singh et al., 2014; Hammond et al., 2015). Concerningly, TBI victims are ten times more likely than the general population to die from accidental overdoses, 70% of those involving opioids (Hammond et al., 2020).

Systematic literature reviews indicate that TBI often results in a neuroinflammatory phenotype, including microglial activation, which likely influences the development of both pain and depressive symptoms that often accompany TBI (Walker et al., 2014). This inflammatory response is characterized by sustained elevations in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which can disrupt synaptic function and neural circuit integrity involved in the spinothalamic (Berger et al., 2025) and limbic pathways (Bodnar et al., 2018), which regulate pain and depression. Importantly, the persistence of this neuroinflammatory state beyond the acute injury phase suggests a mechanistic link to chronic symptomatology rather than a transient response.

However, the relationship between neuroinflammation and clinical outcomes is complex. Major depressive disorder (MDD) and chronic pain are highly heterogeneous conditions that arise from multiple biological mechanisms rather than a single pathophysiological process. Similarly, although neuroinflammation is a common consequence of TBI, not all individuals develop chronic pain, depression, or persistent inflammatory abnormalities following injury (Berger et al., 2025; Rowland et al., 2005). Indeed, there is ongoing debate as to whether inflammation should be viewed as merely an epiphenomenon or as a causal driver of depression (Jha et al., 2025). With respect to TBI-pain, pain itself is recognized as one of the five cardinal signs of acute inflammation, alongside redness, heat, swelling, and loss of function, and it is both a consequence and remains a contributor to chronic pain states (Woodcock et al., 2013). While not all individuals with chronic pain or depression exhibit inflammatory abnormalities (or vice versa), converging evidence suggests that immune and inflammatory mechanisms contribute to symptom development in biologically distinct subgroups of patients (Enthoven et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2025; Pariante, 2017). Accordingly, distinguishing such subgroups may help identify patients less likely to respond to standard pharmacotherapies alone, while also informing the selection of anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory treatment strategies (Jha et al., 2025; Omoigui, 2007). This inflammation-associated phenotype has become a major focus of investigation in both pain and depression research.

Emerging frameworks such as unalamation further suggest that mediators traditionally labeled as “inflammatory” may also participate in homeostatic immune surveillance, highlighting the context-dependent nature of immune signaling and the distinct cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying acute and chronic inflammatory states (Maddipati, 2020). Accordingly, chronic disease may reflect not only excessive inflammatory signaling but also dysregulation of processes that normally maintain immune homeostasis. In parallel, a growing body of preclinical work has utilized microglial depletion strategies to elucidate the complex interplay between immune signaling and affective states (Yu et al., 2021; Bollinger et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that microglia-related neuroimmune dysfunction represents a shared biological substrate linking post-TBI pain and depression in a subset of patients.

An understudied neuroinflammatory signaling pathway that may hold importance in TBI is the kynurenine (KYN) pathway (KP). The KP is constitutively active throughout the periphery but in the brain it is largely inducible, becoming prominently engaged under conditions of severe stress or injury (Kwidzinski et al., 2007). In the brain, the first and rate-limiting enzymatic step in the KP is the metabolism of tryptophan (TRYP) by the enzyme, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1) (Lashgari et al., 2023). IDO1 expression, and thus KP metabolic flux, is bidirectionally responsive to immune signaling molecules: upregulated by proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin [IL]-1β, interferon-γ [IFNγ]) and downregulated by anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, IL-4) (Lashgari et al., 2023). Accordingly, KP metabolic rate is modulated by the neuroimmune state. In TBI, prior research has shown an increase in microglial activation, increased IDO1 expression, and increased expression of downstream metabolites in the KP compared to healthy controls (Zhang et al., 2018, 2022; Zakhary et al., 2020; Harper et al., 2019). Similarly, in pain and depression, numerous studies have shown KP dysregulation (de Barros et al., 2026; Ou et al., 2023; Serafini et al., 2017; De La Torre Canales et al., 2024).

We present a narrative review examining KP dysregulation after TBI, possible KP medication targets, and clinical approaches to investigate the KP dysfunction among patients after TBI. Further, we describe the potential contribution of KP dysregulation to the development and progression of two prevalent, chronic outcomes following TBI: pain and depression symptoms. We included primary literature from both animal and human studies and note PubMed search terms, along with the number of articles yielded, in the legend of each table. The guiding research question for this narrative review was: what is the current evidence describing alterations in the KP following TBI and are these changes plausibly linked to pain and depression symptoms observed after TBI?

Methods

A narrative review design was employed to enable an integrative synthesis of the emerging literature on mechanistic KP dynamics in pain and depression, with particular emphasis on studies contextualizing these processes in TBI, a condition frequently associated with these sequelae. The current evidence describing alterations in the KP following TBI and their potential links to pain and depression-like symptoms observed after TBI was examined; LPS, a known pro-inflammatory agent, was also considered. Two authors (IN and KM) independently screened and reviewed articles retrieved from PubMed using predefined search descriptors.

Overview of the kynurenine pathway

The KP is part of the broader system of TRYP metabolism which can shunt TRYP away from serotonin and melatonin production. In humans, L-TRYP is an essential amino acid that can only be supplied by diet and is transported to the brain via the large amino acid transporter (LAT1). Approximately 95% of free TRYP is metabolized along the KP, whereas the remaining ∼5% is substrate for serotonin and melatonin production (Lashgari et al., 2023). Downstream of KYN are neuroactive metabolites, including quinolinic acid (QUIN) and kynurenic acid (KYNA), that modulate immune signaling and neurotransmission (Fig. 1). The relative balance between QUIN and KYNA can influence neuronal plasticity, development, cognitive function, recovery after injury, and specific aspects of behavior such as pain perception (Ciapala et al., 2023; Rojewska et al., 2018a). It is important to note that in vitro findings regarding IDO1 activity and downstream metabolites such as KYNA and QUIN may be influenced by the simplified nature of isolated systems and may not fully reflect in vivo conditions; therefore, in vivo cell- and tissue-specific studies are critical for contextualizing these observations. Below is a summary of the critical enzymes and metabolites in the KP and a description of factors that regulate their expression and activity.

Kynurenic acid (KYNA) and quinolinic acid (QUIN) synthesis along the kynurenine pathway, and their modes of action. The catabolism of tryptophan (TRYP) by the enzyme indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) represents the rate-limiting step in the KP. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid, derived from the diet, and 95% of free tryptophan is metabolized into kynurenine (KYN). Kynurenine can be further processed through three distinct pathways to form either KYNA, anthranilic acid (AA), or QUIN. Kynurenic acid (KYNA) is formed by the irreversible transamination of kynurenine via kynurenine aminotransferases (KAT I–IV) (Lashgari et al., 2023). On the other hand, quinolinic acid (QUIN) is produced after a series of enzymatic reactions, either including kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO), kynureninase (KYNU), and 3-hydroxyanthranilate 3,4-dioxygenase (3-HAAO) or KYNU, anthranilate 3-monooxygenase (AMO), and 3-hydroxyanthranilate 3,4-dioxygenase (3-HAAO) to produce 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA) and 2-amino-3-carboxymuconic-6-semialdehyde (ACMS), which is then spontaneously converted to QUIN. IDO1: The rate-limiting step of the KP is the conversion of TRYP to N-formylkynurenine, catalyzed by tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenases (IDO1 and IDO2), with IDO1 notably expressed across multiple neural cell types, including neurons, microglia, and astrocytes (Murakami et al., 2013). Numerous studies indicate that IDO1 expression levels, in the presence of immune stimulation, are elevated in neuronal, microglial, and astrocytic cultures compared to low or undetectable IDO1 levels in respective unstimulated cultures (Guillemin et al., 2005; Saito et al., 1993). Together, these data indicate that IDO1 is upregulated in response to immune activation and inflammation (Guillemin et al., 2005; Saito et al., 1993). In a study by Murakami and Saito, IDO1 activity was highest in stimulated monocytes (420 × 103 pmol/h/mg), followed by stimulated astrocytes and neurons (260 × 103 pmol/h/mg), with minimal activity in unstimulated cells (<0.1 pmol/h/mg) (Murakami et al., 2013). Immune signaling molecules, including IFN-γ, Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), pathogen- or damage-associated molecular pattern molecules (PAMPs or DAMPs), and arachidonic acid, upregulate IDO1 expression in microglia (Lashgari et al., 2023). Specifically, IFN-γ induces IDO1 via the Janus kinase/Signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) or nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathways. TNF-α is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that can activate NF-κB to stimulate IDO1 expression, whereas PAMPs and DAMPs can lead to the activation of IDO1 via the Toll Like Receptor – Myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (TLR-MyD88) pathway. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a product of the arachidonic acid pathway, can also stimulate IDO1 activity. In the absence of IFN-γ, c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNK) activation or cluster of differentiation 80 (CD80) and Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) interactions with cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) can induce IDO1 upregulation (Lashgari et al., 2023). Beyond cytokines, KYN itself can promote IDO1 production by binding to an intracellular aryl-hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which is a transcription factor (Lashgari et al., 2023). Through these possible avenues, IDO1 catalyzes KYN production, which is a substrate for divergent pathways, one that directly produces KYNA, considered a ‘neuroprotective’ metabolite, and the other that produces 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK), 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA), and QUIN, which are considered ‘neurotoxic’ metabolites (Lashgari et al., 2023).

KYNA: KYNA is neuroprotective and may be anti-inflammatory. KYNA exerts neuroprotective effects via several mechanisms. First, KYNA acts as a glutamate receptor antagonist, which can prevent excitotoxicity and cell lysis (Leeson et al., 1991). In neuronal cell cultures, KYNA prevented activation of alpha-7 nicotinic cholinergic receptors (nAChR) by epibatidine, an nAChR agonist, measured via patch-clamp electrophysiology (Hilmas et al., 2001). Second, KYNA is a competitive inhibitor of the glycine co-agonist site of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAr) with an IC50 of 8-15 μM (Hilmas et al., 2001) and KYNA derivatives have an IC50 of 32 nM (Leeson et al., 1991). More recently, KYNA activity at nAChRs has been contested, and a review by Stone et al., concludes that the NMDAr is the primary site of action, not nAChRs (Stone, 2020). Indeed, Dobelis et al. directly demonstrated that bath application of KYNA to brain slices from adult mice completely blocked spontaneous glutamatergic excitatory postsynaptic potentials in a rapid and reversible manner, yet KYNA had no effect on choline evoked currents (Dobelis et al., 2012). Third, multiple reports indicate that KYNA inhibits α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid (AMPA) receptors (IC50 10–500 μM) (Mok et al., 2009) which can also reduce excitotoxicity. Fourth, in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that KYNA can scavenge hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and other free radicals (Lashgari et al., 2023). Huitron et al., showed that ferrous sulfate (FeSO4) stimulated reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation in vitro, which was blunted by KYNA even in NMDAr-free cell cultures, and that acute infusion of KYNA in vivo reversed FeSO4 induced ROS (Lugo-Huitron et al., 2011). Fifth, and most controversial, KYNA may exert anti-inflammatory effects by activating two key receptors, G protein-coupled receptor 35 (GPR35) and AhR, thereby initiating signaling cascades that lead to the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10), and inhibition of immune cell migration (Wirthgen et al., 2017).

QUIN: QUIN, on the other hand, activates NF-κB and is a glutamate receptor agonist that can promote excitotoxicity (Lashgari et al., 2023). Stone and Perkins found that 20–50 mM injection of QUIN in vivo reliably induced excitatory postsynaptic potentials (Perkins et al., 1983). Chiarugi et al. also demonstrated that, at micromolar concentrations, the cytotoxic effects of QUIN can be recapitulated in primary cortical neuronal cultures (Chiarugi et al., 2001). Although endogenous QUIN concentrations in vivo are substantially lower, typically reported in the nanomolar range, chronic exposure to nanomolar levels has still been shown to induce apoptotic signaling in experimental systems (Behan et al., 1999).

In vivo QUIN is upregulated in psychiatric disorders, including MDD, and neurological disorders, including TBI, pain, cancer, epilepsy, Huntington's Disease, Multiple Sclerosis, meningitis and more (Steiner et al., 2011; Meier et al., 2016, 2020; Yan et al., 2015; Sugisawa et al., 2022; Barjandi et al., 2020; Chugani et al., 2011). However, these findings are not uniform across the literature. Importantly, studies have measured QUIN across different biological compartments, including blood, cerebrospinal fluid, peripheral tissues, and, less commonly, brain tissue, which may contribute to variability in reported findings and interpretation. Consistent with this heterogeneity, meta-analytic and systematic reviews indicate that QUIN levels may be unchanged or even reduced in psychiatric conditions (Ou et al., 2023; Marx et al., 2021). Notably, QUIN measurements in biological fluids are technically challenging due to its low abundance, often approaching the lower limits of quantification of commonly used assays (including LC–MS/MS), making results highly sensitive to methodological factors such as sample handling, analytical platform, and laboratory-specific validation.

Among KP metabolites, QUIN has emerged as a particularly compelling therapeutic target due to its well-established roles in driving neuroinflammation, excitotoxicity, and oxidative stress. Given that these same pathophysiological processes are chronically disrupted following TBI, QUIN is strongly implicated as a mechanistic link between TBI and the development of pain and depression-like symptoms. Lifestyle factors, including sleep (Cabrera et al., 2000), as well as dietary components such as sulforaphane, selenium, curcumin, and L-theanine (Lashgari et al., 2023), have been reported to reduce peripheral QUIN levels and may mitigate QUIN-associated neurotoxicity through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. At the molecular level, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-1β can increase QUIN production (Laumet et al., 2017; Garrison et al., 2018). Extracellularly, QUIN can also participate in redox cycling with ferrous iron, promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species, lipid peroxidation, and cellular damage (Perez-De La Cruz et al., 2012). In vitro and in vivo studies further demonstrate that IFN-γ–stimulated monocytes are major contributors to QUIN production, exhibiting up to ∼8000-fold increases compared with unstimulated cells (Guillemin et al., 2005).

Cell-type differences in kynurenine pathway enzyme expression also contribute to QUIN regulation. Kynurenine monooxygenase (KMO), a key enzyme directing kynurenine metabolism toward the QUIN branch of the pathway, shows higher activity in neurons (∼3 pmol/h/mg) compared to macrophages or microglia (∼0.5 pmol/h/mg) and is absent in astrocytes (0 pmol/h/mg) (Sathyasaikumar et al., 2022). Consistent with this, Espey et al. reported negligible QUIN production in stimulated astrocytic cultures (0 nmol/mg) compared to robust production in microglial cultures (∼1.4 nmol/mg) (Espey et al., 1997). Pharmacologically, several agents target KMO as a rate-limiting enzyme in QUIN biosynthesis, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target (see supplementary table).

The kynurenine pathway after TBI

The KP is rapidly activated as part of the innate immune response following TBI (Zakhary et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018). Although the exact cause of KP activation after TBI is not fully understood, it is likely driven by a combination of factors, including DAMPs released from lysed cells that activate TLR4, along with other pro-inflammatory pathways involving PD-1, IFN, and PGE receptors on microglia and astrocytes (Lashgari et al., 2023). These microglia and astrocytes work in pathways that produce inflammatory cytokines like IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, which contribute to localized brain inflammation and the activation of the KP (Fig. 2). Chemokines are also released, which recruit innate immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages to the site of injury, enhancing the inflammatory response and KP activation. During the innate immune response, pro-inflammatory cytokines can activate the KP by upregulating IDO1, leading to increased QUIN production, while also reducing KYNA synthesis through inhibition of KATII (Lashgari et al., 2023; Zakhary et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018).

Although numerous reports highlight distinct, cell-specific roles in regulating the KP response, mechanisms underlying this response remain poorly understood. Under pro-inflammatory conditions, macrophages and activated microglia express the full complement of KP enzymes and can drive KYN metabolism toward the neurotoxic branch, leading to increased QUIN production via IDO/TDO, KMO, KYNU, and 3-HAAO. In contrast, astrocytes lack KMO and are thus incapable of producing QUIN (Murakami et al., 2013; Sathyasaikumar et al., 2022; Espey et al., 1997). Astrocytes instead contribute to the neuroprotective branch of the pathway by producing KYNA through KAT II and degrading QUIN via quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRTase). Under baseline conditions, radiolabeled tracer studies by Robert Schwarcz et al. demonstrated that KYN readily crosses the blood–brain barrier, whereas downstream metabolites, including AA, 3-HAA, KYNA, and QUIN, exhibit comparatively limited permeability (Fukui et al., 1991). Furthermore, a systematic review found moderate to strong concordance for KYN between peripheral (blood) and central (CSF or brain) concentrations across psychiatric disorders (Skorobogatov et al., 2021). However, TBI often compromises the blood-brain barrier, that can allow peripheral influx of QUIN precursors - such as KYN, 3-HK, AA, and 3-HAA - to a higher degree which may overwhelm astrocytic capacity to regulate KP metabolism. Further compounding neurotoxicity, QPRTase activity is quickly saturated, resulting in QUIN accumulation (Braidy et al., 2011). Despite these insights, several critical gaps remain. The relative contributions of KP metabolites from peripheral macrophages versus resident microglia following TBI are not well defined. It is also unclear why QUIN production in astrocytes does not proceed through KYNU despite the presence of upstream substrates. Additionally, whether microglial KAT II activity is diminished after TBI - and how this may impact local KYNA availability - remains to be determined. These unresolved questions underscore the need for further investigation into how cell-type-specific regulation of the KP shapes the balance between neurotoxic and neuroprotective responses after TBI.

Clinical studies consistently show that TBI patients exhibit higher than normal QUIN concentrations in the blood, CSF, and brain tissue, which were linked to excitotoxicity, poorer neurological outcomes, and heightened mortality risk. Specifically, Meier et al., showed an acute and chronically sustained elevation of serum QUIN in patients with repeated sports related concussions and that 3-HK, a QUIN precursor, was inversely correlated with hippocampal volume (Meier et al., 2016, 2020). Singh et al., showed elevated plasma QUIN and reduced KYNA concentrations in football players with prior concussion compared to those without, at acute and chronic timepoints, and that QUIN correlated with worse mood symptoms at chronic timepoints (Singh et al., 2016). Sinz et al. (1998) and Bell et al. (1999), in adult and pediatric patients, respectively, showed a strong association between time after severe TBI and increased QUIN concentration in patients’ CSF, which peaked by day 3 post-injury and showed significantly higher QUIN elevation in non-survivors compared to survivors (Sinz et al., 1998; Bell et al., 1999). Yan et al., demonstrated in post-mortem severe TBI victims, the highest concentration of cells expressing IDO1 were detected in the cortex of patients acutely after TBI-induced death compared to tissues from non-TBI patients (Yan et al., 2015). The findings highlight the pivotal role of KP dysregulation in TBI, emphasizing its potential as a target for therapeutic intervention and a biomarker for pathological progression and outcomes.

Preclinical models of applied CNS injury show upregulated KP activity, consistent with clinical studies. Blight et al., showed that spinal cord injury - mechanistically distinct from TBI, though both are CNS injuries that can co-occur in certain injury contexts - resulted in increased KYN (∼2-fold), QUIN (∼10-fold), and IDO1 activity (∼2-fold) at the injury site (T13) 5 days post-laminectomy compared to an uninjured region (C2) (Blight et al., 1993); however, the translational relevance of these findings to TBI should be interpreted with caution. Saito et al., demonstrated that transient carotid artery occlusion for 10-min in gerbils led to higher IDO expression and QUIN levels in the hippocampus, striatum, cortex, and thalamus, compared to unmanipulated control gerbils (Saito et al., 1993). Post-hoc analyses revealed that hippocampal and striatum levels of QUIN were significantly higher than the cortex or thalamus. In a mouse model of intracerebral hemorrhage, brain KYN peaked 24-72 h post-injury and was associated with lower neurological scores and higher oxidative stress and apoptosis within the hippocampus (Ren et al., 2022). Furthermore, Ren et al. demonstrated that, in comparison to control animals, blast-related TBI in rodents was associated with apoptosis in hippocampal CA1 neurons and greater KMO and KYNU expression in surviving neurons three and seven days post-injury (Zhang et al., 2022). Ren et al. also demonstrated that daily administration of a KMO inhibitor for 6 consecutive days reversed apoptosis and ameliorated behavioral deficits measured in an elevated plus maze on day 7 post-injury (Zhang et al., 2022). Consistent with Ren et al., Li et al. demonstrated that mice subjected to controlled cortical impact exhibited increased hippocampal expression of IDO1 and KMO at 28 days post-injury compared to sham controls (Li et al., 2026). Additionally, in a surgical brain injury model - mechanistically distinct from TBI - Zakhary et al. found that KMO inhibition within 6 h after frontal lobe injury reduced edema and apoptosis, decreased perilesional brain QUIN, increased brain KYNA, and improved motor function at acute (24 h) and chronic (4 weeks) timepoints post injury - effects reversed by KAT2 inhibition (Zakhary et al., 2020), although the translational relevance to TBI should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, Smith et al. reported KYNA injections yielded comparable benefits in a TBI impact model (Smith et al., 1993). Kannan et al. demonstrated that controlled cortical impact in juvenile rabbits was associated with greater expression of IDO1 mRNA in the perilesional cortex, detectable as early as 6 h post-injury and persisting for at least 21 days compared to sham rabbits. They also observed IDO1 expression within IBA-positive microglia and higher brain KYN levels compared to uninjured sham or naïve animals (Zhang et al., 2018). Sadek et al. used a rat fluid percussion injury (FPI) model of TBI, which resulted in impairments in navigation, learning, memory, and increased anxiety - assessed using the Barnes maze and open field tests. Injured rats showed elevated KYN levels in the cortex and hippocampus three days post-injury compared to uninjured controls (Sadek et al., 2024). Behavioral deficits between 25 and 28 days post injury was diminished in rats treated with a 14-day regiment of an oral IDO1 inhibitor compared to sham rats (Sadek et al., 2024). Zakhary et al. demonstrated that surgical brain injury (SBI; partial right frontal lobectomy) in rodents was associated with higher levels of both QUIN and KYNA within the injured hemisphere 24-72 h post injury compared to uninjured controls (Zakhary et al., 2020). Extending these studies, our group has recently shown that a modified closed-skull non-contusive impact model of TBI, elevated both acute and chronic cortical pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, etc.) and QUIN levels in injured mice compared to sham controls, as measured by enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) and liquid chromatography tandem-mass spectrometry, respectively (Nahmoud et al., 2025).

TBI-related pain and depression

Chronic pain, defined as pain lasting for at least 3 months, is a frequent consequence of TBI. Chronic pain rates have been shown to range from 30% to 75% for civilians (Nampiaparampil, 2008) and from 40% to 80% for service members/veterans with TBI (Gironda et al., 2009; Sayer et al., 2009). These rates are higher than the rate of 20% in the US adult general population (in the absence of TBI) (Yong et al., 2022). The prevalence of pain symptoms after TBI and their association with poor outcomes, such as MDD (discussed below), highlight chronic pain management as an important treatment target for TBI patients. Importantly, there are currently no clinical practice guidelines for chronic pain treatment after TBI. In addition to high rates of TBI-induced pain, about half of all people with TBI are affected by depression within the first year after injury, and depression hazard ratio scaled with TBI severity (Choi et al., 2022; Jorge et al., 1994, 2004; Singh et al., 2018). Even more (nearly two-thirds) are affected within seven years after injury (Singh et al., 2018). By comparison, in the general population, depression affects fewer than one in 10 people over a 1-year period (Goodwin et al., 2022). There is also an increased risk of suicide subsequent to TBI with two independent studies noting that nearly 25% of TBI patients reported suicidal ideation within 1-year post-TBI, and 12% attempted suicide within five years (Mackelprang et al., 2014). A meta-analysis stratified to randomized controlled trials found no significant benefit of antidepressant treatment - whether administered weeks, months, or years after injury - in patients with traumatic brain injury across all severity levels (Kreitzer et al., 2019).

TBI is a heterogeneous condition in which time after injury, injury location and severity may influence depression or pain risk; however, evidence directly linking TBI severity or lesion location to post-TBI depression or pain is limited. A targeted PubMed search (“TBI” AND “depression/pain” AND “lesion/injury location”) identified only a small number of relevant studies with mixed findings. For example, Rowland et al. (2005), and Finset et al. (2000), reported that lesion location was not associated with depression across TBI severities in the acute to subacute period (days to weeks post-injury), although symptom trajectories were positively correlated with time after injury; notably, Finset et al. focused on severe TBI and similarly observed that lesion location was not associated with depression months after injury. In contrast, Kwak et al. (2020), did identify an association between lesion location and depressive outcomes months after injury, reporting that patients with bilateral frontal lesions exhibited greater executive dysfunction and higher levels of depression compared to those with unilateral frontal lesions, highlighting the need for further investigation of depression in the chronic phase of TBI. Consistent with variability across clinical outcomes, Couch et al. (2016) found no association between TBI severity and headache pain years after initial injury, highlighting variability across clinical outcomes. In theory, location of TBI should impact risk of post-TBI sequalae related to pain and depression. However, the nature of the injury, biomechanical forces, and numerous other factors also contribute to the clinical presentation. Further, standard of care is structural imaging (CT/MRI) which will not capture changes to biochemical signaling pathways, i.e., KP, nor neuroinflammatory processes (microglia activation/proliferation, peripheral macrophage infiltration, etc.). PET imaging of the KP can begin to address some of these questions and may inform our understanding between injury location and clinical sequalae (see ‘Imaging the Kynurenine Pathway in Patients). Xue et al. (2022) provide additional insight by demonstrating widespread gray matter volume loss across limbic cortical and subcortical regions following TBI, with these changes associated with cognitive impairment and psychiatric symptoms, suggesting that post-traumatic depression and pain is more likely driven by diffuse network disruption rather than focal injury.

Independent of TBI, pain and depression are also highly comorbid conditions. Chronic pain is a significant risk factor for depression: 30-60% of individuals with clinical pain have comorbid depression (Arnow et al., 2009). Conversely, idiopathic pain is common among patients with MDD. For instance, 75% of subjects in the Sequenced Treatment Alternative to Relieve Depression study reported pain (Husain et al., 2007). According to a meta-analysis by Liu et al., approximately 55% of cases with MDD report having painful physical symptoms (Liu et al., 2021). One explanation for high comorbidity of pain and depression is they share common neurobiological underpinnings, including neuroinflammation and KP dysregulation (discussed below). A meta-analysis by Walker et al. investigated the shared mechanisms linking neuroinflammation, pain, and depression, focusing on the role of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Walker et al., 2014). They concluded that these pro-inflammatory cytokines contribute to central sensitization, a process by which neurons in pain pathways become hyperactive, and to the development of depressive symptoms by impairing neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus. Walker et al., highlighted the involvement of activated microglia and astrocytes in amplifying neuroinflammatory responses, which perpetuate both pain and depression (Walker et al., 2014). A meta-analysis by Campos et al. reviewed data showing that chronic pain induces glial activation and cytokine release, which disrupt neural circuits in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, exacerbating depressive symptoms (Campos et al., 2020). Neuroinflammation can affect monoaminergic pathways by shunting TRYP metabolism toward KP activation thereby reducing serotonin availability and worsening mood disorder symptoms. Finally, clinical studies reliably show elevated inflammatory markers in patients with comorbid pain and depression, suggesting that plasma cytokine levels could serve as biomarkers.

The kynurenine pathway in pain and depression: converging evidence from clinical, preclinical, and cellular studies

Clinical research has demonstrated a link between KP activity and pain, including neuropathic pain, temporomandibular disorders, and fibromyalgia (Barjandi et al., 2020; Groven et al., 2021; Kuc et al., 2024; Hazrati et al., 2024; Kortesi et al., 2024; Rosa, 2023). Consistent with these findings, a meta-analysis reported lower circulating tryptophan levels in patients with generalized musculoskeletal pain compared with pain-free controls; however, insufficient data on kynurenine concentrations precluded a quantitative analysis of that metabolite (De La Torre Canales et al., 2024).

Clinical research has also demonstrated a link between KP activity and depressive symptoms (Rosa, 2023), including suicidality (Serafini et al., 2017). Evidence from several meta-analyses suggests that mood disorders are associated with altered KP activity (de Bartolomeis et al., 2025; Sales et al., 2023; Almulla et al., 2022). For example, Bay-Richter et al. found that individuals who had survived a suicide attempt showed higher CSF QUIN and lower CSF KYNA compared to healthy controls, with these alterations persisting for up to 18 months post-attempt (Bay-Richter et al., 2015). Steiner et al. further reported that individuals who died by suicide exhibited increased QUIN expression in microglia within the anterior cingulate cortex relative to controls (Steiner et al., 2011). Numerous reports also link TBI-induced KP dysregulation to anxiety and depressive symptoms (Singh et al., 2016; Meier et al., 2025). While these findings broadly support KP involvement across pain and depression, emerging evidence indicates that KP alterations are heterogeneous and influenced by inflammatory state, biological compartment, and sex. In a large MDD cohort, Milaneschi et al. (2021), observed no overall case-control differences in KP metabolites; however, inflammatory stratification revealed significant increases in KYN and QUIN in high C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and high-TNF subgroups. In addition, Youn et al. (2026), reported that blood QUIN levels were negatively correlated with bilateral caudate volumes of MDD patients, linking peripheral KP activity to structural brain alterations. Furthermore, sex-stratified analyses in related studies suggest differential vulnerability, including reduced plasma KYNA and KYNA/QUIN ratios predominantly in females with MDD (Nikkheslat et al., 2025), as well as sex- and suicide-specific alterations in postmortem anterior cingulate cortex, including reduced KYNA and altered KAT2 expression (Brown et al., 2024).

Collectively, these findings suggest that KP dysregulation in pain and depression is not uniform but is shaped by inflammatory context, sex, and compartment-specific dynamics. Elevated metabolism along the “neurotoxic” branch of the KP in both mood disorders and pain conditions has led to the hypothesis that upregulated QUIN production may represent a shared pathophysiological mechanism underlying their high comorbidity (Dehhaghi et al., 2023). Building on this framework, preclinical studies have begun to explore KP-targeted interventions, demonstrating promising effects in reducing pain, depressive-like behaviors, and neuroinflammation.

Preclinical, animal, models of pain sensitivity and depression-like behaviors have demonstrated that the KP (i.e., IDO1, KMO, and KAT2) is not only involved in the development of these symptoms but also represents a promising therapeutic target pathway for their treatment.

IDO1

Given its role as the rate-limiting enzyme driving inflammation-induced activation of the KP, IDO1 has been extensively investigated in preclinical models of pain and depression.

For instance, Ciapala et al. (2023) and Rojewska et al. (2018a) reported that sciatic nerve ligation in rodents led to a marked increase in spinal microglia (7-fold), proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β (30–50-fold), interferon (3-fold), and reactive oxygen species (150-fold) compared to non-injured controls. Interestingly, these proinflammatory responses were normalized by treatment with an IDO inhibitor. Their studies also revealed 2–4-fold increases in neurotoxic KP enzymes in the spinal cord (KMO, KYNU, and 3-HAAO), which correlated with reduced tactile and thermal pain thresholds-an effect reversed by administration of an IDO1 inhibitor (Ciapala et al., 2023; Rojewska et al., 2018a). Consistent with these findings, virally induced IDO1 expression in the lungs and lymphoid tissue was reported to elicit pain hypersensitivity, an effect absent in IDO1 knockout mice but restored following intravenous KYN administration (Huang et al., 2016). Similarly, Kim et al. demonstrated that hindpaw Complete Freund's Adjuvant (CFA) injection in rats decreased mechanical and thermal pain thresholds, as assessed by Von Frey and Hargreaves tests, and that administration of an IDO1 inhibitor - either peripherally or into the hippocampus - significantly attenuated this pain sensitivity (Kim et al., 2012). Furthermore, Sugisawa et al. demonstrated that IDO1 within cultured microglia can be activated by a pain receptor-derived hormone (Reg3γ) (Sugisawa et al., 2022).

Parallel studies link KP activity to depression-like behaviors. Li et al. exhibited that Mice which developed depressive-like behavior following TBI exhibited elevated hippocampal IDO1 expression, with no significant change in KAT2, suggesting a shift of kynurenine metabolism toward the neurotoxic branch of the pathway (Li et al., 2026). Bansal et al. exhibited that surgical removal of the olfactory bulb in rodents - a model for depression - resulted in increased KYN production in the cortex and hippocampus, alongside behavioral phenotypes resembling depression (Bansal et al., 2022). Heisler et al. found that LPS-treated IDO1 knockout mice spent more time exploring novel objects compared to wild-type LPS-treated controls - an effect reversed by peripheral KYN injection (Heisler et al., 2015). Kim et al. showed that CFA injections increased immobility time in both the tail suspension test (TST) and forced swim test (FST) - effects attenuated by IDO1 inhibition (Kim et al., 2012). Additional work by O'Connor et al. (O'Connor et al., 2009) and Lawson et al. (2013) demonstrated that peripheral or central LPS injections in mice increased brain KYN levels, heightened immobility in the TST and FST, and decreased sucrose preference, all of which were reversed by IDO1 inhibition or genetic knockout (O'Connor et al., 2009; Lawson et al., 2013). Lawson et al. exhibited that intracerebroventricular administration of an IDO1 inhibitor reduced anhedonic behavior - reflected in higher sucrose preference and reduced immobility in the TST - following LPS exposure (Lawson et al., 2013). In a study designed to parse the role of IDO1 in pain versus depression, Kim et al. demonstrated that IDO1 inhibition decreased immobility in the FST and increased foot withdrawal latency in the hot-plate test - indicating reductions in both depression-like behavior and pain sensitivity (Kim et al., 2012). Interestingly, mice subjected to chronic restraint stress - a model of anhedonia - exhibited greater immobility in the FST and heightened pain sensitivity compared to animals that received only acute insults (Kim et al., 2012). Together, these data demonstrate a dual role for IDO1 in mediating both pain sensitivity and depression-like symptoms, highlighting the KP as a shared and targetable mechanism in these comorbid conditions.

KMO

Downstream of IDO1, KMO regulates the neurotoxic branch of the KP. Related to pain, Ciapala et al. (2023) and Rojewska et al. (2018a) demonstrated that SNI mice or rats administered competitive or noncompetitive KMO inhibitors have higher mechanical or thermal pain thresholds than untreated SNI mice, and that co-administration with an opioid such as morphine elicited a higher pain threshold in an additive manner compared to either morphine or KMO inhibitor administered alone (Ciapala et al., 2023; Rojewska et al., 2018a). Similarly, Cheng et al. (2025) showed that spared SNI induces chronic microglial proliferation in the rat hippocampus, increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, and elevated microglial KMO expression. Furthermore, KMO inhibition reduced hippocampal levels of quinolinic acid (QUIN), pro-inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis, and decreased immobility in the forced swim test, but did not affect mechanical hypersensitivity (paw withdrawal) (Cheng et al., 2025).

Related to affective symptoms, in vivo, KMO deletion or inhibition is linked to altered depression-like responses. In the absence of an inflammatory challenge, KMO deletion is associated with both depressive-like (Mori et al., 2021) and antidepressant-like phenotypes (Tashiro et al., 2017). Laumet et al. showed that intracebroventricular injection of an IL-1 antagonist in SNI (sciatic nerve injury/transection) mice is associated with lower KMO expression in the contralateral hippocampus and decreased immobility in the FST; similar effects were observed with icv KMO inhibition (Laumet et al., 2017). Parrott et al. (2016) and Larsson et al. (2016) showed that peripheral LPS infection in mice activates the KP in blood and brain, upregulating KMO and increasing immobility in the TST. Additionally, peripheral injection of 3-HK in naïve mice elevated brain 3-HK levels, increased immobility, and reduced open field exploration (Larsson et al., 2016; Erhardt et al., 2017). Furthermore, Heisler et al., reported that KMO deletion is associated with higher levels of novel object exploration time compared to wild-type mice (Heisler et al., 2015). Similar to Zhang et al., who showed that blast-induced TBI affects hippocampal neuroplasticity, apoptosis, and KMO expression in surviving cells, Tao et al. demonstrated that peripheral LPS administration induces hippocampal cell apoptosis, elevates both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus and cortex, increases microglial recruitment and 3-HK levels in the hippocampus and cortex, and leads to greater immobility in the TST (Tao et al., 2020).

Studies by Cheng et al. (2025), Zhou et al. (2015), Kim et al. (2012), and Laumet et al. (2017) attempt to parse pain and depression apart and conclude that while the KP is involved in pain conditions such as neuropathic and LPS-induced pain, its primary functional impact appears to be on pain-associated depressive-like behaviors (Laumet et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2012; Cheng et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2015). However, pain symptoms and depression symptoms are difficult to study in animal models, and therefore, the role of KP in pain sensation cannot be ruled out.

In BV2 microglial cultures, KMO inhibition reduced TNF-α and nitrite levels without affecting NOS, IL-6, or IL-1β (Garrison et al., 2018). KMO inhibition also reduced neuroinflammation in rodent models of infection (Larsson et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2022). Knockout studies in microglial cultures and LPS-treated mice showed increased KYNA and decreased TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 with KMO deletion (Mori et al., 2021; Erhardt et al., 2017; Giorgini et al., 2013; Bondulich et al., 2021).

KAT2

Four kynurenine aminotransferases (KAT 1-4) are able to catalyze the formation of KYNA, with KAT2 accounting for most (∼75%) of the brain KYNA production (Szabo et al., 2025).

Studies have also demonstrated that KYNA can reduce inflammatory pain and diminish nociception in different pain models (Pineda-Farias et al., 2013). Moreover, a low KYNA to QUIN ratio has been suggested as a potential biomarker for chronic pain (Auyeung et al., 2023). In a rodent neuropathic pain model, intrathecal Zaprinast, a GPR35 agonist similar to KYNA, persistently decreased mechanical and thermal pain sensitivity, decreased microglial activation in the spinal cord, and enhanced the analgesic effect of morphine (Rojewska et al., 2018b). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as Diclofenac, increased tactile threshold and blood KYNA levels compared to untreated controls in a sciatic nerve ligation model for neuropathic pain in rodents (Debbag et al., 2023). KYNA administered into the periaqueductal gray matter, a critical region involved in pain perception, potentiates the effect of morphine (Morgan et al., 2009). Pineda-Farias et al. confirmed that intrathecal administration of KYNA alleviated allodynia in a rodent model of neuropathic pain. Notably, co-administration of KYN with probenecid, an inhibitor of KYN efflux from the CNS, increased spinal KAT2 protein expression and KYNA levels, effectively reversing allodynia in a dose-dependent manner, whereas neither compound alone produced the same effect (Pineda-Farias et al., 2013). Yaksh et al., showed that 4-chlorokynurenine administered in the peritoneum, crosses the blood brain barrier, and is converted to KYNA by astrocytes in the spinal cord and brain to ultimately decrease thermal hyperalgesia and tactile allodynia in rodents that received carrageenan, a potent pro-inflammatory agent, compared to saline controls (Yaksh et al., 2017).

Genetic deletions of KAT2 are less studied, however, one investigation by Potter et al., revealed that KAT2 deletion improves novel object recognition, and contextual memory (Potter et al., 2010). KYNA has been shown to have modulatory effects on pain processing and neuroinflammation (Szabo et al., 2023). Recent research by Szabo et al., exhibits decreased KYNA and increased hydroxykynurenine expression in the plasma and urine of KAT2 knockout mice, which correlated with a higher immobility time in FST as well as less time spent in the center of an open field (Szabo et al., 2025). In vitro and in vivo studies of KYNA (Szabo et al., 2023) revealed that this metabolite is anti-inflammatory, activating microglia.

Together, these studies suggest that IDO-KMO activity promotes a pro-inflammatory milieu through the generation of downstream neurotoxic metabolites, whereas IDO-KAT2 activity appears to exert reciprocal, anti-inflammatory (i.e., neuroprotective), effects. This balance may contribute to neuroinflammatory processes implicated in pain and depression in a subset of patients (Pariante, 2017).

Imaging the kynurenine pathway in patients

While neuroimaging modalities, such as MRI or CT, are optimal for localizing and quantifying structural and functional changes in response to injury, Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging allows for investigation of mechanistic, molecular, and neuroreceptor changes following TBI. Because of the wide portfolio of validated radiotracers, PET imaging is a powerful tool, therefore, for quantifying molecular and neuroreceptor changes in patients with TBI. In the context of the KP, several PET radiotracers exist for probing pathway-specific changes, including the widely used radiotracer, α-[11C]-methyl-L-tryptophan ([11C]AMT), and a newer F18 labeled counterpart, [18F]-fluoroethyl-tryptophan ([18F]FETrp). Both are radiolabeled forms of TRYP that cross the BBB via LAT-1 like endogenous TRYP. Unlike endogenous TRYP, both [11C]AMT and [18F]FETrp become trapped inside cells, including both neurons and glia, after metabolism by IDO1. Calculation of the unidirectional rate constant (K-complex) reflects radiotracer uptake and metabolism and is proportional to IDO1 expression in vivo (Muzik et al., 1997, 2024; John et al., 2020). To date, there have been no studies that have specifically investigated KP metabolism in the post-TBI brain using PET imaging.

However, PET [11C]AMT imaging has revealed KP dysfunction in other clinical conditions that exhibit a neuroinflammatory phenotype. For example, [11C]AMT PET studies have shown high sensitivity and nearly 100% specificity in detecting precise epileptic foci in patients with tuberous sclerosis, allowing for better targeted interventions for intractable seizures (Chugani et al., 2013; Kumar et al., 2011). Prior research by our colleagues at Wayne State University has linked KP dysfunction and depression symptomatology among patients with epilepsy (John et al., 2021; Bosnyak et al., 2015). Specifically, they showed higher [11C]AMT uptake rate in cortical and thalamic brain regions correlated with higher Beck Depression Inventory-II scores, suggesting a linear relationship between IDO1 expression and depression symptom severity (John et al., 2021; Bosnyak et al., 2015). These findings underscore the utility of [11C]AMT PET imaging in detecting KP activation and its relevance to neuroinflammatory and mood-related conditions.

[18F]FETrp, a newer TRYP analog with a longer half-life, improves accessibility for hospital and imaging centers without an on-site cyclotron thereby expanding potential clinical impact (Jiang et al., 2023). Further, [18F]FETrp may provide a more sensitive measure of IDO1 expression than [11C]AMT thereby yielding improved visualization and quantification (Jiang et al., 2023; Henrottin et al., 2015, 2016; Michelhaugh et al., 2017). Given its recent development, relatively few clinical applications of [18F]FETrp have been published to date, but initial findings in cancer are promising (Muzik et al., 2024; Michelhaugh et al., 2017).

PET imaging has advantages over blood and CSF measurements that may lend it an important future role in monitoring neuroimmune and inflammatory pain and depression related to the KP. PET imaging facilitates precise spatial measurements of KP metabolic activity (on the order of ∼3 mm isotropic) thereby facilitating investigation of focal dysfunction and investigation of links between affected brain tissue and clinical symptomology. Conversely, blood and CSF are systemic measures and cannot be localized to affected brain tissue [Visser et al.]. In addition, PET imaging provides insight to tissue processes behind the BBB, whereas blood markers are distal to brain processes and limited by BBB permeability and transport kinetics (Visser et al., 2011). PET imaging is a minimally invasive, in vivo neuroimaging technique that can be applied repeatedly with high reproducibility, making it ideal approach for quantifying tissue dysfunction and monitoring disease progression or treatment response (Dobson et al., 2004). Identifying and tracking specific changes to central KP activity using PET imaging can add to the current limited knowledge base regarding mechanisms of brain pathophysiology and provide strong support for further research and development of more targeted therapies.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several important gaps remain in our understanding of KP dynamics following TBI. First, the extent to which individual tryptophan catabolites (TRYCATs) traverse the blood-brain barrier (BBB) under physiological versus post-injury conditions remains incompletely defined. While compounds such as kynurenine are known to cross the intact BBB, the permeability of downstream metabolites (e.g., kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid) is more restricted under basal conditions (Lashgari et al., 2023). However, TBI-induced BBB disruption likely alters this compartmentalization in the acute setting, potentially permitting greater peripheral-central exchange of neuroactive metabolites. In mice and rats, extravasation of IgG, dyes, or synthetic tracers peak within 1-3 days and return to baseline levels within 7 days of TBI (Lowing et al., 2014; Kawoos et al., 2021; Adelson et al., 1998; Li et al., 2016; Hue et al., 2016), similar timespans have been described in humans (Jungner et al., 2016). Rodent and human TBI studies with extended follow-up have demonstrated persistent BBB permeability disruptions months to years after injury (van Vliet et al., 2020; Hay et al., 2015), indicative of a potential biphasic response; indeed, the chronic time course of TBI-associated BBB disruption remains poorly understood. Future studies aimed at more precisely characterizing the pharmacokinetics of KP metabolites, using approaches such as ex vivo mass spectrometry-based quantification of brain metabolite levels or longitudinal in vivo PET in humans and animal models, are needed to elucidate how dynamic changes in BBB integrity regulate TRYCAT flux following injury.

Second, although KP activation is well characterized for innate immune cells such as microglia and macrophages, its interaction with the adaptive immune system in TBI remains underexplored. Emerging evidence indicate that T cells can infiltrate the injured brain and contribute to beneficial, reparative processes (Bao et al., 2014; Kilgore et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2021). In pro-inflammatory contexts, not yet shown in TBI models, IDO1 has been shown to exert immunosuppressive effects through KYN- and KYNA-mediated activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a xenobiotic-sensing receptor that promotes the metabolism of environmental toxins, found in T cells, leading to reduced lymphocyte proliferation and promotion of regulatory immune phenotypes (i.e., immune tolerance) (Alam et al., 2020; Stone et al., 2023; Grishanova et al., 2024) -- a negative feedback loop. Whether similar mechanisms link KP activation to adaptive immune responses in TBI, and whether the KYN-AhR feedback loop is perturbed in TBI, remains unclear and represents an important area for future investigation. Adding to these complexities, the KP metabolite, KYNA is a key agonist of the G protein-coupled receptor 35 (GPR35) which is also found in immune cells, such as T-cells, to modulate immune cell signaling and also influence nociceptive processing (Cosi et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2023). However, the precise mechanisms linking KYNA to GPR35 activation to coordinated immune and pain responses is an area of active investigation.

Third, the temporal dynamics of KP activation following TBI are not yet sufficiently resolved. To date, three manuscripts have been published on measuring endogenous KP dynamics after TBI as stated earlier – including work by Kannan et al. (Zhang et al., 2018), Reid et al. (Sadek et al., 2024) and Ren et al. (Zhang et al., 2022) – with some evidence that IDO1 and KMO levels are still elevated in the perilesional site 7 (Zhang et al., 2022) to 21 (Zhang et al., 2018) days post TBI. The temporal and regional dynamics of KP enzymes, alongside metabolite levels across the brain remain poorly defined. A more systematic characterization of KP metabolite profiles across acute, subacute, and chronic phases of injury would improve our understanding of how KP signaling contributes to the evolution of secondary injury and may help identify time-sensitive therapeutic windows.

Fourth, the cellular specificity of KP metabolites is under investigation, primarily QUIN and KYNA. Several influential reviews of the KP, including those by foundational figures such as Schwarcz et al. (2012) and Guillemin et al. (2001) among others, present framework schematics suggesting divergent cell-type specificity of KP metabolites, particularly associating KYNA (and its synthesizing enzyme, KAT2) with astrocytes and QUIN (and its upstream enzymes, KMO and KYNU) with microglia. However, emerging evidence demonstrate that QUIN is present in neurons, both in vitro (Guillemin, 2012) and in vivo (Laumet et al., 2017; Sathyasaikumar et al., 2022). Advances in viral vector technology and gene editing now enable selective tagging and silencing of key KP enzymes within specific cellular populations, offering a more rigorous approach to defining cell-type specificity. In parallel, developments in cellular isolation techniques, along with single-cell transcriptomics and metabolomics, will be essential for disentangling these contributions and clarifying how cell-specific KP activity shapes neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative outcomes following TBI.

Fifth, the efficacy of therapeutic modulation of the KP in humans has primarily focused on peripheral disease. Data supporting KP enzymatic inhibition in the CNS as an effective therapeutic are scarce, with the TBI setting remaining essentially unexplored. For this reason, preclinical and clinical studies in peripheral tissues can serve as proxy measures to demonstrate the potential efficacy and clinical utility of inhibiting key KP enzymes centrally. Pharmacologically altering various enzymatic check points has demonstrated reductions in pro-inflammatory KP metabolites in peripheral tissues. It has also been found that therapy often needs to be tissue-specific, due to the varying roles of the KP and interaction with tissue-specific metabolites. These preliminary data provide rationale for the investigation of similar centrally-acting therapeutics that alleviate pain and depressive symptoms associated with pathologic post-TBI KP dysregulation. The supplementary (S1) summarizes existing literature investigating the KP as a therapeutic target in peripheral tissues.

Lastly, KP metabolites modulate circuit level hubs. In terms of neurogenesis, KAT2 is enriched in proliferative zones, such as the rostral migratory stream - extending as a continuum from the olfactory bulbs to the hippocampal formation - supporting a circuit-level organization of KP metabolism with potential implications for interregional neuromodulation of neurogenesis via KYNA (Song et al., 2018). Investigating this pathway in TBI can elucidate the connection between the KP and neurogenesis. In terms of neurotransmitters, GABAergic (Beggiato et al., 2014), glutamatergic (Schwarcz, 2016), dopaminergic (Nilsson et al., 2006), and serotoninergic (Sun et al., 2020) signaling are all modulated by KP metabolism. Further, the involvement of KP dynamics in modulating specific brain circuits in TBI has not been investigated, either. The intersection between TBI and the KP remains nascent and is ideally suited for investigation at the level of circuit dynamics, leveraging the neuroscience toolbox to elucidate how this pathway shapes neuronal activity, connectivity, and network function.

Conclusion

A robust body of preclinical and clinical evidence implicates dysregulation of the KP as a central mechanism in the pathogenesis of TBI-associated neuropsychiatric symptoms, particularly chronic pain and depression. Preclinical models of CNS injury, including spinal cord injury, intracerebral hemorrhage, and controlled cortical impact, consistently demonstrate elevated KP activity, with increased levels of neurotoxic metabolites such as QUIN and 3-HK, heightened expression of enzymes like IDO1 and KMO, and associated behavioral and histological deficits. Importantly, pharmacologic inhibition of these enzymes has been shown to inhibit neuronal apoptosis, reduce inflammation, and improve mood-related behaviors in animal models. Clinical studies mirror these findings, showing elevated KP metabolites and enzymes in the CSF, blood, and brain tissue of TBI patients, with correlations to excitotoxicity, hippocampal atrophy, pain, depressive symptoms, and poorer neurological outcomes. Furthermore, elevated IDO1 expression in post-mortem TBI cortex samples and sustained increases in KP metabolites across acute and chronic timepoints underscore the pathway's persistent role in TBI pathology.

KP dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a common mechanism underlying both pain and depression conditions that frequently co-occur after brain injury. Elevated KP activity has been reported in individuals with neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, temporomandibular disorders, and in patients with depressive symptoms, including suicidality. These findings support the hypothesis that upregulated QUIN production along the neurotoxic branch of the KP may represent a common pathophysiological mechanism linking chronic pain and mood disorders. Emerging preclinical studies have begun to validate this model, demonstrating that KP-targeted treatments can attenuate both pain and depressive-like behaviors by restoring balance between neurotoxic and neuroprotective KP metabolites. Coupled with the advent of PET imaging using tracers like [11C]AMT and [18F]FETrp to quantify in vivo KP activity noninvasively, the field is now poised to translate these mechanistic insights into precision diagnostics and therapeutics, positioning the KP as a compelling dual-purpose biomarker and treatment target for TBI and its neuropsychiatric sequelae.
