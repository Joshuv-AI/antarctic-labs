Case Report: Neonatal lethality caused by novel compound heterozygous missense variants of SLC6A9 gene

Jianbin Liu, Ke Wu, Yan Ni

Abstract

AbstractBackgroundThe homozygous or compound heterozygous missense variants in the SLC6A9 gene (OMIM*601019) are associated with autosomal recessive glycine encephalopathy with normal serum glycine (OMIM#617301). Fewer than ten SLC6A9 variants have been reported to date. Neonates harboring pathogenic variants in the SLC6A9 gene exhibited a mortality rate of up to 75% during the first 7 months of life. We reported the first documented case of neonate death in China attributable to compound heterozygous missense SLC6A9 variants.MethodsTrio-based whole-exome sequencing (trio-WES) was performed on genomic DNA (gDNA) of amniotic fluid cells and parental peripheral blood samples. Two mutant SLC6A9 expression vectors, namely pCMV-Flag-Neo(EGFP)-SLC6A9-m1 and pCMV-Flag-Neo(EGFP)-SLC6A9-m2 (SLC6A9-m1/m2), and wild-type pCMV-Flag-Neo(EGFP)-SLC6A9-wt (SLC6A9-wt) mammalian expression vectors were constructed. All vectors were subsequently transformed into HEK293T cells. Assays measuring the relative expression of SLC6A9 gene mRNA and SLC6A9 protein were performed. Concurrently, we conducted a systematic review of previously reported SLC6A9-related cases with accessible clinical data, encompassing relevant genetic testing findings and prenatal/postnatal clinical manifestations.ResultsTrio-WES identified novel compound heterozygous missense variants c.859C > T (p.Arg287Trp) and c.1120T > C (p.Cys374Arg) in the SLC6A9 gene (NM_201649.4). In vitro functional studies demonstrated that neither variant caused a reduction in the expression of mutant mRNA, but both led to a significant decrease in the expression of mutant SLC6A9 protein. After reviewing the current literature, we found that fetuses with SLC6A9 gene variants commonly presented with increased nuchal translucency (NT), polyhydramnios and talipes equinovarus (clubfoot). Pathogenic SLC6A9 variants exhibited complete penetrance and a relatively consistent phenotypic spectrum characterized by developmental delay, facial dysmorphism, clubfoot, and a high neonatal mortality rate. The characteristic phenotype in affected infants was characterized by elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) glycine concentrations, with or without concurrent elevation in serum glycine levels.ConclusionPreliminary evidence from in vitro functional studies suggested that the pathogenic mechanism of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy may be associated with the loss-of-function (LoF) effect caused by SLC6A9 gene variants. This study also expanded the mutational spectrum of the SLC6A9 gene.Keywords: clubfoot, facial dysmorphism, glycine encephalopathy, neonatal lethality, SLC6A9 gene

Introduction

Empirical evidence indicates that 30%–40% of neonatal deaths are associated with monogenic genetic disorders (, ). Herein, we report an extremely rare case of neonatal lethality caused by novel compound heterozygous missense variants of SLC6A9 gene in China. The homozygous or compound heterozygous missense variants in the SLC6A9 gene (OMIM*601019) are associated with autosomal recessive glycine encephalopathy with normal serum glycine (OMIM#617301). Between 2003 and 2005, multiple independent studies reported findings regarding the Slc6a9 gene knockout animal model. The Slc6a9 knockout mouse exhibited severe growth restriction, respiratory failure and early postnatal lethality. It was not until 2016 that Majid Alfadhel et al. () reported the first documented human case of a homozygous SLC6A9 gene variant associated with a distinct neurodevelopmental phenotypes, characterized by neonatal respiratory distress, global delay, hypotonia, congenital talipes equinovarus, and elevated glycine concentrations in both plasma and cerebrospinal fluid. Fewer than ten SLC6A9 variants have been reported to date. Pathogenic SLC6A9 variants exhibited complete penetrance and a high neonatal mortality rate. Here, we also performed a series of functional studies on these two variants, which preliminarily suggested that the pathogenic mechanism of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy may be associated with the loss-of-function (LoF) effect induced by SLC6A9 gene variants.

Materials and methods

Clinical features

A 19-year-old woman (gravida 3, para 0) sought genetic counseling due to a neonatal death. The first pregnancy terminated by medical abortion; the second pregnancy resulted in induced labor at 19 weeks due to fetal bilateral clubfoot, without fetal genetic analysis. The present pregnancy constituted her third gestation. At 18 weeks of gestation, ultrasound examination revealed bilateral choroid plexus cysts. At 19 weeks, ultrasound showed bilateral clubfoot. Follow-up ultrasounds at 20, 22, 26, and 35 weeks confirmed the persistent bilateral clubfoot. At 19 weeks, amniocentesis was performed. The chromosomal karyotype analysis and copy number variations (using low pass genome sequencing) of the amniotic fluid sample revealed no abnormalities. At 35 weeks, the fetal Doppler ultrasound scan revealed the following fetal findings: biparietal diameter: 81 mm (−1.6 SD); head circumference: 304 mm (−1.0 SD); abdominal circumference: 310 mm (0 SD); femur length: 62 mm (−1.0 SD). The estimated fetal weight (EFW) was 2,291 ± 334 g (−0.6 SD). A female infant was delivered vaginally at 39 weeks and 5 days of gestation. The birth weight was 3,150 g, the birth length was 50.0 cm, the head circumference was 31.5 cm (−1.3 SD). The APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min were 5 and 7, respectively. The newborn exhibited profound hypotonia, absence of cry, respiratory distress, recurrent apneic episodes, hypotonia, episodic rhythmic limb tremors, arthrogryposis (bilateral clubfeet, bilateral flexion contractures of the elbows, hip dislocation, and clenched fists), absence of suck reflex, and feeding difficulties. At 3 h postpartum, no abnormalities were detected on neonatal brain ultrasound; peripheral blood glycine concentration was 699.00 μmol/L (reference range: 150–700 μmol/L), and other metabolic screening tests showed no abnormalities. The newborn presented with fair and loose skin, dysmorphic facial features (low-set ears, micrognathia, depressed nasal bridge, hypertelorism, retrognathia, deep prominent philtrum, glossoptosis, high-arched palate), bilateral talipes equinovarus and overriding toes (). Upon admission, the newborn was diagnosed with necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, and anemia, and died from respiratory failure on the fourth day after birth.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from centrifuged amniotic fluid cells using the TIANGEN Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Parental gDNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples using Genome Column Medium Extraction Kit (Kangweishiji, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted gDNA samples were subjected to quality control using a Qubit 2.0 fluorimeter and electrophoresis with a 0.8% agarose gel for further protocols. Trio-based whole-exome sequencing (trio-WES) was performed on fetal and parental gDNA. Quality control (QC) of the DNA library was performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer System (Agilent, USA). DNA nanoball (DNB) preps of clinical samples were sequenced on ultra high throughput DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China) with paired-end 150 nt strategy following manufacturer's protocol.

Bioinformatic analysis

Sequencing data were analyzed according to our in-house procedures. Raw data were processed to remove adapters and filter out low-quality reads. The trimmed reads were then mapped to the University of California at Santa Cruz (UCSC) GRCh37/hg19 reference genome using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software (version 0.7.17). The Genome Analysis ToolKit (GATK) software (version 4.2.1.0) was used for single nucleotide polymorphisms/variants (SNPs/SNVs) and short insertions/deletions (INDELs) (<50 bp) calling. Samtools (version 1.22.1) and Picard software (version 2.22.1) packages were used to generate high-quality binary alignment map (BAM) data by removing duplicate reads. Variants were annotated for analysis using the single nucleotide polymorphism database (dbSNP, version 5.3, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/), gnomAD exomes database (version 4.1.0, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and Chigene in-house minor allele frequency (MAF) database. Tools of pathogenicity prediction like REVEL (Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner) and AlphaMissense were used for predicting possible impact of variants. As a prioritized pathogenicity annotation to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://www.omim.org/), Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD, https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/) and ClinVar databases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) were used as conferences of pathogenicity of every variant.

Variants classification

As per the guidelines of ACMG for interpreting sequence variants, variants were classified. Classification considered the position of the variant in the human genome, MAF, the pathogenicity prediction of variants, disease mechanism, clinical phenotypes, literature evidence, evolutionary conservation.

Variants verification

Fetal and parental gDNA samples was used for the verification of this variant. All reactions were carried out in a total volume of 20 μL using the Taq DNA polymerase. After confirmation of the size of the amplicons, the PCR products were purified by standard protocol. The reaction system was: 2 μL DNA (10 ng/μL), 1 μL forward primer, 1 μL reverse primer, 10 μL PCR Master Mix, 6 μL free RNA H2O. The following protocol of PCR: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were electrophoresed on a 2.0% agarose gel and were sequenced. Sanger sequenced using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (ABI 3730 Applied Biosystem, USA) for the verification of candidate variants. The identification of this variant was performed using Chromas software (version 2.2.6, Technelysium, Australia).

Construction of the SLC6A9 variants in vitro and transfection

Two mutant SLC6A9 expression vectors pCMV-Flag-Neo(EGFP)-SLC6A9-m1/m2 (SLC6A9-m1/m2), one wild-type pCMV-Flag-Neo(EGFP)-SLC6A9-wt (SLC6A9-wt) and one empty vector negative control mammalian expression vectors were constructed and purchased from Bioeagle Biotech Company Ltd (Wuhan, China). Human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293T cells were transfected with the above four expression vectors in separate tubes. The constructed expression vectors were verified using Sanger sequencing. Then, 6 h after the transfection, we gently removed the lipofectamine-containing medium, replaced the medium with Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and incubated the slide at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator until day 2. On day 3, the samples were collected and used in a reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to quantify the SLC6A9 mRNA expression and the Western blot (WB) assay to quantify the SLC6A9 protein expression. Each cell line underwent three independent replicates of the RT-PCR and WB assays. The RT-PCR and Western blotting experiments were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Wild-type (SLC6A9-wt), mutant (SLC6A9-m1/m2) and negative control expression vectors were respectively transfected into HEK293T cells. All samples were collected at 48 h post-transfection for subsequent analysis. The transfected cells were collected by centrifugation, followed by total RNA extraction using the Trizol method. Total RNA concentration and quality were checked using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The cDNA concentration was about 1,200 ng/μL. The total reaction system was 20 μL, with 500 ng of total RNA used as a template. Quantitative PCR was performed using the Maxima SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix (2×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative SLC6A9 mRNA expression levels were determined using 2−ΔΔCt method, and the ACTB gene was used as the reference gene. The experimental values were normalized to the empty-48 h condition, designated as the internal control. The qRT-PCR reaction was performed in triplicate, and mean values and standard deviations were calculated from the obtained data.

Western blot (WB)

Wild-type (SLC6A9-wt), mutant (SLC6A9-m1/m2) and negative control expression vectors were separately transfected into HEK293T cells. All samples were collected at 48 h post-transfection for subsequent analysis. Western blot analysis was subsequently performed using standard protocol. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in a tris buffered saline with tween-20 (TBST) solution for 2 h at room temperature. Anti-Flag (MBL, Japan, dilution ratio is 1:1000) and anti-GAPDH dilutions were added respectively and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. GAPDH served as the loading control and internal reference protein. The loading amount of WB protein was 10 μg/well. Blot bands were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (ImageJ2). Protein levels were normalized to GAPDH. The relative SLC6A9 protein expression levels are quantified by the fold change (FC) in the ratio of the grayscale between the SLC6A9 protein band and the internal reference protein band.

Statistical analysis

Multiple comparisons across these experimental groups were performed using the Holm-Šídák multiple comparisons test in Prism software (GraphPad Prism 10.6.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Literature review

We systematically searched the PubMed database for relevant published studies on the research topic using “SLC6A9 gene” as keywords. The search time was from the establishment of the databases to June 30, 2026. We reviewed SLC6A9-related cases with availability of clinical data including relevant genetic testing results, prenatal/postnatal manifestations.

Results

Genetic analysis and confirmation

Trio-WES identified novel compound heterozygous missense variants c.859C > T (p.Arg287Trp) and c.1120T > C (p.Cys374Arg) in the SLC6A9 gene (NM_201649.4) These variants were further verified by Sanger sequencing, confirming that the heterozygous variant c.859C > T (p.Arg287Trp) was inherited from the mother and the heterozygous variant c.1120T > C (p.Cys374Arg) was inherited from the father (). 1 m1 (c.859C > T): The allele frequency of the heterozygous variant c.859C > T was 0.000002543 in the gnomAD (v4.1.1). However, it was not observed in East Asian populations, nor were homozygous carriers observed. The score of REVEL for this variant, an ensemble method for predicting the pathogenicity of missense variants based on a combination of scores from 13 individual tools, was 0.71. 2 m2 (c.1120T > C): The allele frequency of the heterozygous variant c.1120T > C was 0 in the gnomAD (v4.1.1). The score of REVEL for this variant was 0.73. As per the interpretation guidelines by ACMG (), both variants were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) (PM2_Supporting + PP3_Supporting). We have submitted these variants to a public database [Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD)], and the accession number can be found at the following URL: https://databases.lovd.nl/shared/individuals/00484148.

Functional analysis of the missense variants

Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the full-length SLC6A9 gene cDNA in both wild-type and mutant vectors. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the wild-type and mutant expression vectors were successfully constructed (). To evaluate the effect of these SLC6A9 variants, our functional studies revealed that these SLC6A9 variants didn't markedly decreased mRNA expression of SLC6A9 gene (), but leading to a reduction of mutant SLC6A9 protein () compared to the SLC6A9-wt groups. Discussion

Solute carriers (SLC) are a superfamily of transporter proteins that play a crucial role in the transport of substances (such as ions, amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleotides, vitamins, and drugs) across cellular membranes. They are involved in various physiological functions, including nutrient uptake, metabolic regulation, and waste expulsion (). The SLC6A9 protein is a member of the SLC transporter protein superfamily. The SLC6A9 gene (solute carrier family 6 member 9, OMIM*601019) is located on chromosome 1p34.1. The transcript of SLC6A9 gene (NM_201649.4) has 14 exons with the transcript length of 2,330 base pairs. Its translation product consists of 706 amino acid residues. The precursor SLC6A9 protein undergoes preliminary partial N-glycosylation modifications (hypo-glycosylated) in the endoplasmic reticulum to form an intermediate product of approximately 60 kDa. This hypo-glycosylated SLC6A9 protein is further processed by the Golgi apparatus (with addition of complex oligosaccharide chains) to yield the fully glycosylated (hyper-glycosylated) mature membrane glycine transporter 1 (GlyT1), weighing about 80 kDa (). The mature GlyT1 is trafficked and localized to the plasma membrane, it exhibits full substrate binding and transport activity. GlyT1 is widely distributed in the central nervous system and peripheral organs. In neurons (both excitatory and inhibitory) and glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and ependymal cells), the sodium- and chloride-dependent GlyT1 clears glycine from the synaptic cleft, regulates inhibitory glycine signaling in the central nervous system, and maintains dynamic glycine homeostasis ().

From 2003 to 2005, several researchers successively reported findings on Slc6a9 gene knockout animal models. Homozygous Slc6a9 knockout mice exhibited disrupted respiratory rhythms, severe respiratory distress, and generalized hypotonia after birth, ultimately dying within 12 h of birth (); heterozygous Slc6a9 knockout mice survived and exhibited normal phenotypes in adulthood, showing no difference from wild-type mice (). The slc6a9 gene mutant zebrafish displayed motor behavior deficits during the first 2 days of development, such as diminished escape responses upon tactile stimulation and the absence of swimming (). It was not until 2016 that Majid Alfadhel et al. () and Kurolap et al. () reported six documented cases of three homozygous human SLC6A9 gene variants associated with neonatal respiratory distress, hypotonia that rapidly transformed into hypertonicity with pronounced startle-like clonus jerks, arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, congenital talipes equinovarus, elevated CSF glycine concentrations with or without elevated plasma glycine concentrations, and neonatal lethality.

After a literature review, we found that a total of 14 documented cases with SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy from 7 pedigrees have been reported in six literature to date. There were four homozygous missense and three homozygous nonsense SLC6A9 variants in these 14 cases (). The case reported in this study was the first genetically confirmed case with compound heterozygous missense SLC6A9 variants. Consanguineous marriages were identified in six families. Five of 14 cases were fetuses who underwent pregnancy termination, and the remaining nine were live-born infants, of whom five were delivered preterm (before 35 weeks' gestation). The specific phenotype of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy was characterized by elevated CSF glycine concentrations alongside normal or elevated serum glycine concentrations. Common clinical manifestations included: death due to respiratory failure within 7 months after birth (78%, 7/9), a phenotypic transition from hypotonia to hypertonia, startle-clonic seizures, and epileptiform episodes within 1–2 weeks after birth (67%, 6/9), cranial and facial dysmorphism (89%, 8/9, such as microcephaly, macrocephaly, trigonocephaly, broad forehead, esotropia, thick or sparse eyebrow, long eyelashes, wide-set eyes, hypertelorism, cleft palate, low-set ears, posterior rotated ears retrognathia, deep philtrum, anteverted nostrils, collapsed nasal bridge, ptosis), arthrogryposis (67%, 6/9), congenital talipes equinovarus (56%, 5/9); as well as non-specific phenotypes such as other limb or femoral deformities (e.g., high-arched feet, thoracic kyphosis, joint dislocations/contactures, scoliosis, knee hyperextension, overlapping fingers/toes, absence of patellae, decreased palmar wrinkling, single palmar creases). Additional abnormal phenotypes may include cryptorchidism, bilateral hydrocele, micropenis, inguinal hernia, atrial septal defect, hypertension, hypotension, hearing impairment, hirsutism, motor and sensory polyneuropathy, abnormal visual evoked responses, and cutaneous hyperpigmentation. We found that no significant differences in clinical phenotypes were observed between individuals harboring missense SLC6A9 variants and those with nonsense SLC6A9 variants. The penetrance of missense/nonsense in the SLC6A9 gene appeared to be complete. All 12 cases with available prenatal ultrasound records exhibited nonspecific prenatal phenotypic features. The most frequently observed abnormalities were increased NT (6/12, 50%), polyhydramnios (5/12, 42%), talipes equinovarus (6/12, 50%), and arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (5/12, 42%). This newborn reported by us was the first documented neonatal fatality linked to the compound heterozygous missense SLC6A9 variants in China. In addition to the aforementioned clinical findings (recurrent apneic episodes, respiratory distress after birth, cranial and facial dysmorphism, a phenotypic transition from hypotonia to hypertonia, talipes equinovarus), this neonatal case also presented with necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, anemia (not previously described in the literature), and died from respiratory failure on the fourth day after birth. This family had a documented history of one adverse pregnancy outcome and the postpartum clinical presentations closely matched the clinical phenotypes of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy in the literature. Fetal talipes equinovarus was observed in two pregnancies within this family, suggesting that this phenotype may represent one of the characteristic manifestations of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy during the prenatal stage and warranted clinical attention.

Based on three cases with SLC6A9 nonsense variants and Slc6a9 gene knockout animal models, we postulated that the pathogenic mechanism of SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy may be associated with the loss-of-function (LoF) effect of SLC6A9 variants. The gene dosage sensitivity curation from Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) database does not record the score of haploinsufficiency of SLC6A9 gene. The “gene2phenotype” database records that the pathogenic mechanisms of SLC6A9 gene variants may be associated with the loss-of-function (last updated: 2025-01-28). To validate the hypothesis proposed in this study, we conducted in vitro cellular function assays on two identified missense variants in this case. By constructing two mutant SLC6A9 expression vectors and transfecting them into human HEK293T cells, we measured the mRNA and protein expression levels of the SLC6A9 gene. This study results demonstrated no significant difference in mRNA expression levels between the two mutant groups compared to the wild-type group, but a marked reduction in SLC6A9 protein expression was observed. These findings suggested that these two missense variants may impair SLC6A9 protein expression, compromising glycine transport efficiency and activity. This finding aligned with the hypothesis of a “LoF pathogenic mechanism” for SLC6A9 gene variants.

Currently, direct functional evidence for the pathogenicity of SLC6A9 LoF variants remains extremely limited, and the number of well-documented cases is also limited. Nonetheless, emerging experimental evidence suggests that certain SLC6A9 variants may indeed exert the LoF effects. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the potential pathogenic mechanisms underlying SLC6A9 LoF variants, aiming to establish a coherent theoretical foundation, and guide future functional validation studies and classification of SLC6A9 variants. 1 For SLC6A9 nonsense variants (): Nonsense variants introduced a premature termination codon (PTC), causing the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD). Truncated proteins exhibited precluded normal N-glycosylation, thereby compromising structural stability and rendering them susceptible to rapid degradation by the intracellular ubiquitin-proteasome system (e.g., p.Lys310Phefs*31); some truncated proteins may undergo incomplete N-glycosylation and subsequently form dimers or oligomers, leading to their retention in the endoplasmic reticulum and failing to traffic to the cellular membrane (e.g., p.Gln573*); owing to the loss of essential structural or functional domains, some truncated variants may completely lack glycine transport activity. Nonsense variants resulted in the loss of GlyT1 function via the aforementioned molecular mechanism. 2 For SLC6A9 missense variants: missense variants (e.g., p.Val118Met, p.Ser407Gly) did not alter SLC6A9 protein expression levels, post-translational modification patterns, or subcellular localization to the plasma membrane; however, they significantly diminished GlyT1 substrate binding affinity and transport efficiency, thereby compromising glycine uptake capacity and leading to a functional loss of GlyT1 activity (). Our study elucidated an alternative pathogenic mechanism underlying missense SLC6A9 variants. Specifically, these variants do not alter SLC6A9 mRNA expression levels but lead to a significant reduction in SLC6A9 protein expression (including both fully and partially N-glycosylated forms). This phenomenon was similar to prior observations reported by Wang et al. () (e.g., p.Y206F, p.F207Y, p.R333H, p.E338K, p.E446K, p.R643H, p.R662W). The underlying pathogenic mechanism is proposed as follows: missense variants may reduce the stability of SLC6A9 protein (e.g., by impairing its N-glycosylation modification or three-dimensional structure), thereby activating intracellular proteasome and lysosome degradation pathways, which decreased the total amount of functional GlyT1 on the cellular membrane (); missense variants may form heterodimers or polymeric complexes with wild-type GlyT1, which couldn't be transported to the cellular membrane via the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi trafficking pathway but instead accumulated and were degraded intracellularly, leading to a significant reduction in functional GlyT1 levels on the cellular membrane (). In summary, SLC6A9 gene variants may impair the quantity or function of GlyT1 on the cellular membrane via multiple molecular mechanisms, including reduced structural stability, disruption of essential structural domains, aberrant post-translational modifications, and diminished substrate affinity or transport efficiency, thereby exerting a loss-of-function effect. Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, the study is limited by a small sample size, as the compound heterozygous variants were identified in only a single family; therefore, the pathogenicity of these variants and phenotypic spectrum warrant further validation through additional familial and cohort-based investigations. Second, in vitro cellular assays inherently lack the physiological complexity of the in vivo environment; consequently, the functional consequences of the missense SLC6A9 variants require corroboration in more physiologically relevant models, such as animal models or patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC). Third, the rapid clinical deterioration observed in the affected proband precluded timely collection of CSF, thereby limiting the assessment of CSF glycine concentrations. Ultimately, owing to funding constraints, experimental evidence supporting a LoF mechanism underlying these SLC6A9 missense variants remains incomplete. Complementary functional investigations into these SLC6A9 missense variants still necessitate additional experimental validation, including N-glycosylation analysis, subcellular localization assays, and electrophysiological characterization of glycine-induced GlyT1.

In conclusion, this study identifies and functionally validates novel compound heterozygous missense variants in the SLC6A9 gene through comprehensive analysis of a family with adverse obstetric outcomes. To our knowledge, We presented the first reported neonatal lethality caused by compound heterozygous missense variants in the SLC6A9 gene and comprehensively delineated the prenatal and postnatal phenotypic spectrum associated with SLC6A9-related glycine encephalopathy, thereby offering novel molecular insights to support prenatal diagnosis and informed genetic counseling. Also, this study integrated in vitro cellular functional assays with evidence from published case reports, SLC6A9 gene knockout animal models, and independent cellular functional studies reported by other research groups, thereby providing preliminary mechanistic evidence that the pathogenic mechanism underlying SLC6A9-associated glycine encephalopathy may be attributable to the LoF effect conferred by pathogenic variants in the SLC6A9 gene.
