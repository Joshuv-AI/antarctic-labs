1. Background & Scientific Rationale

 Low frequency plasma wave generation in space is important for both scientific and practical
applications. One of the most promising techniques for doing this is to directly inject whistler waves into
the space environment from an antenna onboard one or more satellites. This technique has been
discussed for years, but there are still open questions about the best way to generate plasma waves. So
far, most theoretical [Kondrat92], lab based [Pribyl2010, Stenzel2016] and space-based experiments
[DSX] have focused on studying the generation of whistler waves from an electric dipole antenna.
However, a dipole antenna is very inefficient because it puts a lot of energy in waves that are not
effective for most applications. Theoretical [Kondrat92] and lab experimental [Stenzel2016] results
indicate that a loop antenna is much more efficient at generating whistler waves than a dipole antenna.
 A satellite experiment will need to be developed to demonstrate that whistler waves can be
generated from a loop antenna in the space environment. The challenge is that to efficiently transmit
whistler modes in the natural plasma environment of space, the loop antenna will have to be very large.
For example, at L=2 (one earth radius away from the surface of the earth) a loop antenna would need a
radius on the order of ~200 m to radiate efficiently, as shown in fig. 1, left. The antenna size and
complexity would require a prohibitively large and expensive satellite mission.

Our proposed innovation is to exploit the fact that the characteristic wavelength of whistler waves
decreases in more dense plasma, which reduces the size needed for an antenna to radiate efficiently.
Fortunately, a technique already exists for enhancing the local plasma density in space, called a plasma
contactor [Kovaleski2001]. A plasma contactor can be used to create a local environment where the
plasma density is enhanced around the satellite, which in turn reduces the size of an antenna that is
needed to radiate efficiently (Fig. 1, right).

An additional benefit of this concept is that it allows for an additional diagnostic of whistler wave
generating efficiency in a deployed satellite (or rocket) experiment. In previous experiments involving an
antenna on a satellite such as the Demonstration Science Experiment (DSX) [DSX], wave generation
efficiency is measured in 2 ways: through direct measurement of radiation resistance at the antenna
[DSX2] , and through measurement of waves propagating into space through boomerangs [DSX3] and
conjunctions [DSX]. Neither of these methods are perfect: direct radiation resistance measurements
suffer because it is difficult to tell how much energy is being dissipated as radiation vs. plasma heating
[DSX2], and boomerang and conjunction measurements are rare, and in most cases do not yield
measurements of antenna radiation resistance or radiation generating efficiency [DSX, DSX3].

However, in the concept shown in Fig. 1, there is a plasma density gradient close to the antenna, and
some of the radiated whistler waves will reflect back to the satellite from the density gradient. These
reflected waves will provide an additional diagnostic that does not rely on conjunctions or boomerangs
and can separate out the actual radiation resistance at the antenna from the resistance from plasma
heating.

Thus, there are 2 main benefits for using a plasma contactor in addition to a loop antenna: Benefit 1: the
enhanced local plasma density significantly reduces the size of loop antenna needed to radiate efficiently
Benefit 2: the density gradient introduces wave reflections that can be used as an additional diagnostic
of wave generating efficiency.

Current state of the project

The development of this concept is currently funded by a DOE Laboratory Research and Development
(LDRD) grant at Los Alamos National Laboratory. The project is a three-year project, and it is in its second
year. There are three main goals for this funded project: Task 1 Demonstrate that the concept shown in
Fig. 1 works through numerical simulation. Task 2 Demonstrate experimentally that whistler waves can
propagate across a density gradient and explore the possibility of measuring the reflected return waves,
and Task 3 Develop a strawman design for a future space experiment to explore this concept.

Expected Results

At the end of the project, in September 2027, we expect to have several peer reviewed papers submitted
related to the idea of using a plasma contactor to allow for efficient generation of whistler waves from a
loop antenna. These publications will include numerical results as well as experimental results from the
ICPD experiments. In addition, we plan to publish an Instruments paper related to our antenna concept.

2. Experiment Design
2.1 Experimental Setup and Diagnostics

This experiment will take place at the Inductively Coupled Plasma Device (ICPD) [ICPD2]. Data will be
taken during the afterglow of the plasma discharge, at times ranging from 0.5 to 10 ms after the plasma
source is turned off. The neutral density before plasma turn-on will range from 10^12 - 10^13 particles
per m^3, and will consist of Argon gas. The nominal magnetic field will range from 10 Gauss to 100
Gauss. Measured density under these plasma conditions ranges from 10^10 - 10^11.

Parallel plasma density gradients will be established in 2 ways: by either raising the neutral pressure in
the plasma, or by flaring the magnetic field away from the plasma source. Both methods have already
been demonstrated at the ICPD, with plasma density gradients measured with interferometry and
Langmuir probes. For the flared magnetic field experiments, the magnetic field will be set to 90 Gauss at
the location of the plasma source and will drop to 20 Gauss away from the plasma source. When plasma
gradients are established by raising the neutral pressure, the pressure will be brought up from 0.5 mTorr
to 1.5 mTorr of Argon.

 The diagnostics will be the same ones that have been used to measure whistler waves in the
ICPD with a constant density plasma. The circuits attached to the whistler antenna itself are used to
measure the total radiation resistance [3], while a movable 3-axis B-dot probe is used to measure the
amplitude of whistler waves in the plasma. In addition, Langmuir probes and interferometry are used to
measure the density variation in the plasma. A B-field probe is used to measure the magnetic field
external to the plasma, and a python code exists to determine the internal magnetic field from this data.

2.2 Experimental Run plan
There are two main goals for our experimental run at the ICPD next year. Goal 1 is to measure whistler
propagation across a parallel density gradient, over a range of values of density change, density special
gradient, magnetic field gradient, and whistler frequency. These values will be systematically varied and
compared to LANL’s numerical calculations [SPS]. Goal 2 will be to demonstrate the advanced antenna
circuit that was developed at LANL. The circuit has already been tested at ICPD but needs to be further
characterized by testing it with different loop antenna diameters. In addition, a second-generation circuit
is currently being fabricated at LANL, that will be tested next year. These antennas will be tested in and
outside of the plasma. The measured stray impedances will be compared to simulations with LTspice
[SPICE] and CST Studio Suite [CST].
