Don’t Get Your Kroneckers in a Twist: Gaussian Processes on High-Dimensional Incomplete Grids
Mads Greisen Højlund, August Smart Lykke-Møller, Henry Moss, Ove Christiansen

Abstract
We introduce CUTS-GPR, a new method for performing numerically exact Gaussian process regression in high-dimensional settings. The key component of CUTS-GPR is an extremely fast kernel matrix-vector product, which exhibits near-linear or even linear scaling with the amount of training data, N N , and low-order polynomial scaling with dimensionality, D D . This is obtained by combining an additive kernel with an incomplete grid and exploiting the resulting structure of the kernel matrix. We demonstrate the scalability of the matrix-vector product by running benchmarks with billions of data points and thousands of dimensions. Full GPR calculations, including hyperparameter optimization, are completed in a matter of hours for N = 447 265 N=$447\,265$ and D = 24 D=24 . We demonstrate that our CUTS-GPR enables Bayesian modeling of high-dimensional potential energy surfaces – a longstanding challenge in computational chemistry.



1
Introduction

Gaussian process regression is a powerful nonparametric, probabilistic machine learning method that enables
predictions with quantified uncertainty and modeling of complex data.
For many applications in science and engineering – where accuracy, interpretability,
and reliable uncertainty estimates are essential – Gaussian process regression is highly attractive.
It is particularly well suited to settings where data is expensive and error control is critical.
However, the high computational cost of Gaussian process regression remains a major limitation.
For
N
training points, conventional Gaussian process regression implementations require
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
operations and
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
storage, which becomes prohibitive for large datasets.
The computational cost of Gaussian process regression becomes particularly severe in high-dimensional spaces. As the dimensionality,
D
, increases,
the data and computation requirements quickly compromise practicality – this is the
curse of dimensionality
.
Gaussian process regression could potentially see much broader adoption if (near-)linear scaling with
N
and low scaling with
D

were achievable at the same time.

Near-linear scaling with
N
can in fact be obtained if the training
data is located on a complete Cartesian product grid
(
34
;
42
;
17
;
14
;
25
)
, but

N
in turn scales exponentially with
D
and the curse of dimensionality remains.
In many scientific applications (such as molecular
simulations and materials modeling)
D
can be large,
so existing grid-based Gaussian process regression methods are not sufficient.
With CUTS-GPR, we show that grid structure can be exploited to drastically lower computational cost
even though the grid is not complete.
Incomplete grids are useful for sampling high-dimensional functions such as potential energy surfaces, which in turn play a central role in understanding and predicting chemical processes.
CUTS-GPR thus paves the way for truly high-dimensional
applications in science and elsewhere.
In particular, we make the following contributions:

(i)

We introduce a class of structured, incomplete grids suitable for high-dimensional applications.
The grid includes a reference point and a set of low-dimensional
subgrids or cuts (see Figure
1
). In the simplest case, we include all subgrids of order
0,1,2,\ldots,\alpha
,
in which case the amount of training data scales as
\mathcal{O}(D^{\alpha})
.

(ii)

Combining this type of grid with an additive kernel, we derive and implement a
kernel matrix-vector product with computational complexity only

\mathcal{O}(n\alpha N)
(assuming
n
grid points per dimension). Focusing on
D
, the complexity is
only
\mathcal{O}(D^{\alpha})
.
The key to achieving this remarkable performance is the careful use of structure in the kernel matrix.
No approximation is involved in the kernel matrix-vector product.

(iii)

The availability of a fast, scalable matrix-vector product in turn
allows predictions and hyperparameter optimization using
iterative numerical techniques. This enables high-dimensional Gaussian process regression with
(near-)linear scaling with
N
and polynomial scaling with
D
,
which is a major breakthrough for many applications of Gaussian process regression.

A substantial literature has sought to address the

Sparse inducing-point methods, such as FITC
(

(

(

;

2.1
Gaussian process regression

The training data is a set of
D

\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
along with the
corresponding noisy outputs, which are collected in a vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The task is now to predict the unknown outputs at a set of test points

\mathcal{X}_{*}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N_{*}}
,
and for this purpose we need the training–training, training–test and test–test
covariance matrices, which are defined in terms of the kernel function,
k
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=k(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}),\quad\mathbf{K}_{*}=k(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{*}),\quad\mathbf{K}_{**}=k(\mathcal{X}_{*},\mathcal{X}_{*}).

(1)

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}

the weights,
\bm{\upalpha}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{y}

\bm{\mu}=\mathbf{K}_{*}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{K}_{*}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\bm{\upalpha},\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{K}_{**}-\mathbf{K}_{*}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{*}.

(2)

Any hyperparameter,

maximizing the marginal log-likelihood,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{y}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\bm{\upalpha}+\log{|\mathbf{C}|}+N\log{2\pi}\right).

2.2
Complete grids

Throughout the paper, we are concerned with
D
-dimensional inputs
\mathbf{x}
that are located on a grid.
We will start by defining a
complete
grid, by which we mean
a Cartesian product grid like

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}^{(D)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(4)

Each
\mathcal{X}^{(d)}
is a 1D grid (not necessarily regular) of size
n_{d}
, and the full grid
has size
N=|\mathcal{X}|=n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{D}
. It is natural in this setting to index
each 1D grid by an index set
\mathcal{I}^{(d)}=\{0,1,\ldots,n_{d}-1\}
and each multidimensional grid point
by a multi-index
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{D})\in\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{I}^{(D)}
.
It is well known
that the combination of a complete grid and a separable kernel,

\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{m=1}^{D}k^{(m)}\big(x^{(m)},\tilde{x}^{(m)}\big),

(5)

enables Gaussian process regression with near-linear complexity
(
34
;
42
;
17
;
14
)
.
This is due to the fact that the kernel matrix obtained by evaluating (
5
) over a complete grid
is a Kronecker product. Specifically, one finds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{K}^{(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{K}^{(D)},\quad\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times n_{m}},

(6)

where the matrices
\mathbf{K}^{(m)}
are called base matrices.
The key properties of Kronecker products are outlined in Appendix
B
,
along with some implications for low-scaling Gaussian process regression.
One can, for instance, compute inverse kernel matrix-vector products with a computational cost of only

\mathcal{O}\big((n_{1}+\cdots+n_{D})N\big)=\mathcal{O}(nDN)
,
assuming 1D grids of identical size
n
.
In spite of the attractive computational complexity, there
are two major downsides to this approach: (1) The separable kernel is often a poor choice in high dimension; and (2)
the
N
required to preserve modelling accuracy scales exponentially with the number of dimensions, thus limiting applications to
D<10
or so (depending on
n
).

2.3
Additive kernels

A more attractive kernel format for high-dimensional applications is the
additive
kernel
(
12
;
11
;
29
)
,
which is typically defined by a maximum interaction order,
\omega
:

\displaystyle k=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\sum_{m}k^{(m)}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\sum_{\mathclap{m<m^{\prime}}}k^{(m)}k^{(m^{\prime})}+\cdots+\sigma_{\!\omega}^{2}\sum_{\mathclap{m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{\omega}}}k^{(m_{1})}k^{(m_{2})}\cdots k^{(m_{\omega})}.

(7)

The hyperparameters
\sigma_{k}^{2}
are called order variances and control
the relative importance of different interaction orders.
In addition, the 1D kernels (base kernels) may each depend on one or more hyperparameters.
For definiteness, we will assume that the 1D kernels
each depend on a
length scale
,
\ell^{(m)}
.
If
\omega=D
, the kernel is
saturated
and includes all possible interactions.
Such a kernel is highly descriptive, but it is usually not possible or necessary
to include high-order terms, as shown by
29

who introduced the orthogonal additive kernel.
Compared to a generic additive kernel, the orthogonal additive kernel approach includes an
extra step where the 1D kernels are orthogonalized or centered
(
11
)
,
which ensures an identifiable model
(
29
)
.

25
introduce
a more general and flexible class of additive kernels, which we write as

\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}k^{(m)}\big(x^{(m)},\tilde{x}^{(m)}\big)

(8)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}
lists the interaction terms included in the kernel.
In addition, they argue that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}

should be
hierarchical
, but not necessarily of the simple form implied by (
7
).
Comparing with Section
2.2
, it is clear that (
8
) evaluates to a sum of Kronecker products
when using a complete grid, but it is not at all obvious that this kind of structure can be exploited.
However,
25
prove the surprising fact that (
15
)
can be diagonalized in closed form (assuming centered base kernels).
Their approach allows GPR with near-linear scaling and a flexible additive kernel for complete-grid datasets.
However, the exponential scaling with
D
remains as a serious limitation.

3
Incomplete grids

To address the curse of dimensionality and motivated by our target applications in computational chemistry, we need a version of grid-based GPR that allows
sampling only a subset of the full grid, referred to as an
incomplete
grid. We
consider
\widehat{N}
inputs located on an incomplete grid
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
indexed by the multi-indices
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
We will start by introducing a specific type of incomplete grid and later describe how it
relates to a more general class of grids.
The incomplete grid used in this paper is defined with respect to
a
reference point
that we label by the zero multi-index,
\mathbf{i}=(0,0,\ldots,0)
.
The reference point can be chosen freely according to the problem at hand.
In addition to the reference, the grid includes a set of low-dimensional
subgrids
or
cuts
.

Example 3.1.

Consider an incomplete grid including the reference, all 1D subgrids and all 2D subgrids.
With
D=3
this construction includes the points corresponding to
the multi-indices

0D:

\displaystyle(0,0,0)

1D:

\displaystyle(a,0,0),\;\;

\displaystyle(0,b,0),\;\;

\displaystyle(0,0,c)

2D:

\displaystyle(a,b,0),\;\;

\displaystyle(a,0,c),\;\;

\displaystyle(0,b,c)

for all
0<a<n_{1}
,
0<b<n_{2}
and
0<c<n_{3}
(see also Figure
1
).

Index sets like Example
3.1
occur frequently in the tensor literature
(see e.g.
27
)

and in vibrational structure theory
(
10
)
.
In both of these fields,
the pertinent dimensions are called
modes
, which is a terminology that we
will use in the following.
Given a set of 1D grids, a subgrid is uniquely defined by its mode combination,
which is simply the list of modes with non-zero indices.
An incomplete grid is in turn defined by its mode combination range,
which is simply the list of mode combinations. Example
3.1
thus corresponds to the mode combination range

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}=\{(),\;(1),\;(2),\;(3),\;(1,2),\;(1,3),\;(2,3)\}.

(10)

A formal definition of subgrids and incomplete grids is given in Appendix
C
.
In the following, we exclusively consider incomplete grids
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}

whose mode combination range is
hierarchical
or closed under taking subsets:

Definition 3.2.

The set
\mathcal{M}
is closed under taking subsets (CUTS) if

\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\subset\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}
implies
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
.

The mode combination range framework is flexible and can be used to put more emphasis
on certain dimensions and less on others. However, it is often useful
to define a
simple
mode combination range that includes all cuts of order
0,1,2,\ldots,\alpha
.
This construction is obviously closed under taking subsets and allows definite computational
complexities to be derived and stated concisely.
Assuming for simplicity that
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{d}=n
, we find that an incomplete
grid with maximum cut level
\alpha
has size

\displaystyle\widehat{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\binom{D}{k}(n-1)^{k}\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\binom{D}{\alpha}(n-1)^{\alpha}\right)\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}n^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\right).

(11)

Importantly, we see that
\widehat{N}
grows polynomially with
D
,
in contrast to the exponential growth of the complete grid. Thus, with
\alpha<D
,
this data format allows us to mitigate the curse of dimensionality.

A cut-based grid
with an mode combination range that it closed under taking subsets
is just one example of a grid that is closed under decrements:

Definition 3.3.

A set of multi-indices
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
is closed under decrements (CUD) if

\mspace{10mu}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d},\ldots,i_{D})\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}},\;i_{d}>0\;\Rightarrow\;(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}-1,\ldots,i_{D})\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}.\mspace{10mu}

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
is also said to be downward closed.

It turns out that being closed under decrements is the crucial property
that enables a fast matrix-vector product.
Moreover, the notion of closed under decrements can be generalized compared to Definition
3.3

(
22
)
(see also Appendix
D
), which opens
the possibility of scalable Gaussian process regression with other types of incomplete grids.

4
Combining incomplete grids and additive kernels

We now combine the additive kernel with the incomplete grid from Section
3
. To that end, we introduce the following convenient notation:

Definition 4.1.

Consider the square matrices
\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times n_{m}}
for
m=1,\ldots,D
and let
N=n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{D}
.

(i)

We define the Kronecker product

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

via
A_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}=A^{(1)}_{i_{1}j_{1}}A^{(2)}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots A^{(D)}_{i_{D}j_{D}}

for the multi-indices
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
and
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{D})
.

(ii)

A one-mode bracket matrix is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\![m]}=\mathbf{I}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{I}^{(D)}

with identity matrices in all positions except one.

(iii)

A many-mode bracket matrix is defined as

\mathbf{A}^{\![\mathbf{m}]}=\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{A}^{\![m]}=\bigotimes_{m=1}^{D}\mathbf{B}^{(m)},\quad\mathbf{B}^{(m)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\quad}l@{}}\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)}&\text{if }m\in\mathbf{m},\\
\mathbf{I}^{(m)}&\text{if }m\notin\mathbf{m}.\end{array}\right.

Introducing the all-ones matrices,
\mathbf{J}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times n_{m}}
, the complete-grid additive kernel matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\mathbf{K}^{[\mathbf{m}]}\mathbf{J}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}.

(15)

The notation
\neg\mathbf{m}
means the set of modes
m\notin\mathbf{m}
(the complement of
\mathbf{m}
).
The kernel matrix over the incomplete grid,
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widehat{N}\times\widehat{N}}
, can be formally obtained
from the complete-grid matrix,
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, by appropriately deleting rows and columns. This can
be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{\Gamma}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{[\mathbf{m}]}\mathbf{J}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}\mathbf{\Gamma},

(16)

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\widehat{N}}
is a
chopping
matrix (the columns of
\mathbf{\Gamma}

are the standard basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
).
Equation (
16
) is thus a sum of chopped Kronecker products.
It would seem that chopping destroys the structure of the complete-grid kernel,
but we will see that chopping can in fact be handled
efficiently and very elegantly if
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
is closed under decrements.
Chopped Kronecker products have been used extensively for solving the vibrational
Schrödinger equation
(
44
;
45
;
37
)
,
and the mathematical aspects have been formalized by
Holzmüller and Pflüger
(
22
)
to whom we refer for details and additional references.
We will use the following theorem:

Theorem 4.2.

Let the index set
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
be closed under decrements and let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
In addition, let
\mathbf{M}^{[m]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

be an arbitrary one-mode bracket matrix (see Definition
4.1
).
Using hats to denote chopping, e.g.
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Gamma}
,
we then have

(i)

If
\mathbf{A}
is lower triangular or
\mathbf{B}
is upper triangular,
then
\widehat{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
.

(ii)

If
\mathbf{A}
is lower (upper) triangular and invertible, then
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
is lower (upper) triangular
and
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}
is invertible with
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}=\widehat{\mathbf{A}^{-1}}
.

(iii)

The matrix-vector product
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\widehat{\mathbf{w}}

can be computed with a complexity of at most
\mathcal{O}(n_{m}\widehat{N})

without referencing the multi-indices
\mathbf{i}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
.

Proof.

See
22
.
∎

Theorem
4.2
(ii)
actually
allows a separable kernel to be inverted directly over an incomplete grid,
but only in the noiseless case (see Appendix
E
).
Our initial investigations
in this direction were not promising:
(i) The complete lack of noise leads to very ill-conditioned numerics;
(ii) the
D
-complexity is higher than CUTS-GPR; and
(iii) a separable kernel is not attractive for the applications we have in mind.

The details of how to compute the matrix-vector product in Theorem
4.2
(iii)

are covered by Theorem
E2
and Appendix
F
.
The crucial aspect of
Theorem
4.2
(iii)

is that it only ever references the multi-indices of the incomplete grid.
The remaining multi-indices of the underlying complete grid,
\mathbf{i}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
, are never referenced, not even
in intermediate steps.
Staying on the incomplete grid at all times is a
necessary
condition for breaking the curse of dimensionality, but
the complexity offered by Theorem
4.2
(iii)
is not
sufficient
in itself
due the large number of terms in the additive kernel.
This problem can, however, be resolved by careful use of structure

.

5
Low-scaling implementation

5.1
Fast matrix-vector products

Our task is now to apply the chopping framework to the
kernel matrix in (
15
).
Before proceeding, we observe that the all-ones matrix
can be factorized like

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{(m)}=\mathbf{L}^{(m)}\mathbf{R}^{(m)}\mathbf{U}^{(m)}

(17)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{(m)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&0&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{R}^{(m)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{U}^{(m)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}.

(18)

The matrix
\mathbf{R}^{(m)}
is symmetric
and idempotent and hence a projection.
It is, more specifically, the matrix that projects
onto the reference point for mode
m
.
Using (
17
), a short derivation (see Appendix
G.1
)
shows that (
15
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{L}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}\right]\mathbf{U}\equiv\mathbf{L}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{U},\quad\mathbf{M}^{(m)}=\big(\mathbf{L}^{(m)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\big(\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\big)^{-1},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{L}^{(D)},\quad\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{U}^{(D)}.

(20)

Each term in (
19
)
has a lower triangular factor to the left (
\mathbf{L}
),
a non-triangular factor in the middle (
\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}
)
and two upper-triangular factors to the right (
\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}
and
\mathbf{U}
).
We can therefore apply
Theorem
4.2
(i)

thrice to get

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{K}}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}\right]\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}.

(21)

Using the factorized form above, we can rephrase the matrix-vector product with
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}
as a sequence of three simpler
matrix-vector products. The matrices

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}^{[1]}}\widehat{\mathbf{L}^{{[2]}}}\cdots\widehat{\mathbf{L}^{{[D]}}}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[1]}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{{[2]}}}\cdots\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{{[D]}}}

are evidently very sparse in addition to being highly structured.
One can show that each factor touches only a small part of the vector,
with the result that matrix-vector products by
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}

have a complexity of only
\mathcal{O}(\alpha\widehat{N})
(see Appendix
G.2
).
This should be compared to the naive complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nD\widehat{N})
obtained by repeatedly
applying Theorem
4.2
(iii)
without utilizing the special structure of
the 1D matrices
\mathbf{L}^{(m)}
and
\mathbf{U}^{(m)}
.

The matrix-vector products with
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
is much more challenging, simply because of the large number of terms.
However, we demonstrate in Appendix
G.3
that
each term is extremely sparse due to
the presence of the projector
\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}
.
This leads to a complexity of only
\mathcal{O}(n\alpha\widehat{N})
, which
is also the complexity of the full matrix-vector product by
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}
.
What we have obtained is a factorization of
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}
into three factors that exhibit
a highly structured kind of sparsity that allows an extremely fast matrix-vector product.

5.2
The quadratic term and overall cost

Computing the quadratic term of (
2.1
),
\bm{\upalpha}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}(\partial\widehat{\mathbf{C}}/\partial\theta)\bm{\upalpha}
,
is not a difficult task on its own, and the cost for each individual hyperparameter is usually not problematic.
Consider, however, the task of computing
all
length scale quadratic terms.
A typical implementation consists of a derivative matrix-vector product,

(\partial\widehat{\mathbf{C}}/\partial\ell^{(m)})\bm{\upalpha}
, followed by a dot product with
\bm{\upalpha}
.
Even neglecting the cost of the matrix-vector product, this leads to a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D\widehat{N})

due to the dot products alone. If the grid mode combination range is simple with cut level
\alpha
, this implies
a
D
-complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D^{\alpha+1})
compared to
\mathcal{O}(D^{\alpha})
in the rest of the code.
Appendix
G.5
shows how to maintain a
D
-complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D^{\alpha})

by leveraging the factorized form of
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}
in
(
21
).

Appendix
K
gives an overview of the code and the cost of the various steps.
The most expensive parts are the hyperparameter optimization and predictive variances
with complexities

\mathcal{O}\big(N_{\mathrm{opt}}N_{\mathrm{probe}}N_{\mathrm{CG}}(n\alpha\widehat{N}+k\widehat{N})\big)

and

\mathcal{O}\big(\widehat{N}_{*}N_{\mathrm{CG}}(n\alpha\widehat{N}+k\widehat{N})\big)
,
respectively. The symbols
N_{\mathrm{opt}}
,
N_{\mathrm{probe}}
,
N_{\mathrm{CG}}

and
\widehat{N}_{*}

denote the number of optimization cycles, probe vectors (for trace estimation),
conjugate gradient iterations and test points, respectively, while
k
is the preconditioner rank.

First, we study the convergence of the hyperparameter optimization in CUTS-GPR. Figure

7
Conclusions, limitations and extensions

We have introduced CUTS-GPR, a new approach for doing numerically exact Gaussian process regression
with large datasets in high-dimensional settings.
CUTS-GPR is based on the combination of two attractive components: (i) Additive kernels and (ii) structured, incomplete grids.
Careful analysis shows the surprising fact that this combination implies a highly structured kernel,
which in turn allows a scalable kernel matrix-vector product.
The matrix-vector product offers low-order polynomial scaling with
D

and
near-linear or even linear scaling with
N
,
something that we demonstrate theoretically and empirically.
Without approximating the kernel matrix, CUTS-GPR enables Gaussian process regression is previously
inaccessible settings, potentially with billions of data points
and thousands of dimensions – well beyond what is feasible with current methods.

In its current form, CUTS-GPR assumes a particular data format that may be too restrictive for some applications.
Some restrictions can, however, be lifted with relative ease.
Accidental missing data
points can, for example, be handled by introducing an extra layer of chopping.
Writing
\tilde{N}<\widehat{N}
for the actual number of data points, the kernel matrix-vector product
then reads as

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=\tilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\tilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}

with
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{N}\times\tilde{N}}
.
In contrast to the chopping inside
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
, which is handled
implicitly
,
the vector
\tilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widehat{N}}
should be constructed
explicitly

followed by fast multiplication with
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
.
We have also mentioned that the notion of an incomplete grid can be generalized by using the flexibility of
the closed under decrements property (Definition
3.3
). This potentially allows a wider range of grid-type data
sets to be addressed. One could also interpolate non-grid data onto an incomplete grid, similar
to the idea of KISS-GP
(
43
)
whose applicability in high
dimension is limited by the use of a complete grid.

Although the reliance on a particular data structure may be viewed as a limitation it should
be remembered that, very often, the user controls the
sampling of data and hence the data structure.
In applications such as potential energy surface fitting, which we have considered in this paper,
an incomplete grid structure is a very natural choice.

Computing predictive variances for very large test sets is a bottleneck in our current implementation.
In the future, we plan on combining CUTS-GPR with Lanczos variance estimates (LOVE)
(
32
)

to enable much faster computation of variances.

A.1
Conjugate gradient

Linear systems like
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
can be solved
iteratively using the well-known conjugate gradient algorithm
(
21
;
30
;
33
)
.
Since the overall computational effort scales linearly with the number of conjugate gradient
iterations, it is important to accelerate convergence as much as possible.
This can be achieved by using a preconditioner,
i.e. a symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbf{P}\approx\mathbf{C}

that allows fast solves,
\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{v}
.
The preconditioner is formally introduced into the conjugate gradient algorithm
by using its Cholesky
decomposition
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}

to write an equivalent linear system,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{E}^{-\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}})(\mathbf{E}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{b})

(A1)

This system has the same solution as the original system, but
convergence depends on the conditioning of the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{E}^{-\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}

rather than
\mathbf{C}
itself. If
\mathbf{P}
approximates
\mathbf{C}
well
then
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\approx\mathbf{I}

and convergence can be much improved. This is often summarized in terms
of condition numbers:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{precond}}=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\tilde{\mathbf{C}})}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\tilde{\mathbf{C}})}\ll\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathbf{C})}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{C})}=\kappa_{\mathrm{orig}}.

(A2)

The Cholesky factor
\mathbf{E}
is not needed in the actual
implementation of the algorithm and serves only
as a formal device. All that is needed is matrix-vector products with the inverse
preconditioner,
\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{v}
.

Any convenient
preconditioner can be used, but we will rely on a rank-
k
pivoted Cholesky
decomposition
(
5
;
19
;
8
)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\approx\mathbf{K},\quad\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times k}

(A3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}\approx\mathbf{C}.

(A4)

From the matrix inverse and matrix determinant lemmas
we get the following simple expressions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\right),

(A5)

\displaystyle\log{|\mathbf{P}|}=\log{|\mathbf{Q}|}+(N-k)\log(\sigma^{2}),

(A6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{Z}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}.

(A7)

The matrix
\mathbf{Q}
and its Cholesky factorization can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}N)

and
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
time, respectively. Once this is done,
the cost of
\log{|\mathbf{P}|}
is only
\mathcal{O}(k)
while
\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{v}

has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(kN)
.

The pivoted Cholesky algorithm evaluates the main diagonal and
k
columns of
\mathbf{K}
at a cost of

\mathcal{O}(\rho_{\mathrm{diag}})
and
\mathcal{O}(k\rho_{\mathrm{col}})
, respectively (
\rho_{\mathrm{diag}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{col}}
depend on the structure of the kernel; see Appendices
H
and
I
).
In addition, the algorithm expends
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}N)
arithmetic operations
and requires
\mathcal{O}(kN)
storage
(
8
)
.

A.2
Lanczos

The (non-preconditioned) Lanczos algorithm
(
28
;
7
)
can be described very informally as follows:
Running
\ell
iterations of the Lanczos algorithm with a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{C}

and starting vector
\mathbf{b}
builds an approximation

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\approx\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}^{\phantom{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{T}_{\ell}^{\phantom{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}},\quad\mathbf{T}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell},\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\ell}.

(A8)

The matrix
\mathbf{T}_{\ell}
is symmetric tridiagonal
and the columns of
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
(the Lanczos vectors) are
orthonormal (in exact arithmetic) with the first column being
\mathbf{q}_{0}=\mathbf{b}/\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{e}_{0}\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert.

(A9)

Lanczos can be used to approximate quadratic forms involving matrix functions,
a technique known as Lanczos quadrature
(
7
)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}f(\mathbf{C})\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{b}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}^{\phantom{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}}f(\mathbf{T}_{\ell}^{\phantom{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}})\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}f(\mathbf{T}_{\ell})\mathbf{e}_{0}.

(A10)

Although the equality due to (
A9
) only holds in exact arithmetic,
one should always use the latter expression
(
31
)
.
This has the added benefit that the Lanczos vectors need not be stored.

The Lanczos algorithm (with starting vector
\mathbf{b}
)
and the conjugate gradient algorithm (with right-hand side
\mathbf{b}
and zero starting guess)
are intimately related,
and the quantities of one can be obtained from the other
(
30
;
33
)
.
With a slight (and very inexpensive) modification of conjugate gradient
(
16
)
we can thus obtain
the Lanczos tridiagonal matrix
\mathbf{T}_{\ell}
and compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\mathbf{C})\mathbf{b}\approx\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\mathbf{T}_{\ell})\mathbf{e}_{0}.

(A11)

The matrix logarithm of
\mathbf{T}_{\ell}
can be computed
by diagonalization, which has a cost of
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})
since

\mathbf{T}_{\ell}
is symmetric tridiagonal.

If a preconditioner is used,
the equations above must be modified slightly.
In this case the Lanczos algorithm (and the modified conjugate gradient algorithm)
build an approximation
of the preconditioned matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{E}^{-\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}

and the formal starting vector is the preconditioned vector

\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
(rather the user-supplied vector
\mathbf{b}
).
Comparing with (
A11
) we thus find that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\tilde{\mathbf{C}})\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\approx\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\rVert^{2}\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{\ell})\mathbf{e}_{0}

(A12)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{E}^{-\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\tilde{\mathbf{C}})\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\approx(\mathbf{b}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{b})\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\log(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{\ell})\mathbf{e}_{0}.

(A13)

Importantly, the right-hand side of (
A13
) does
not refer to
\mathbf{E}
, which can once again be considered as a formal device.

Appendix B
Properties of Kronecker products

The following theorem states a few important properties of the Kronecker product.

Theorem B.1.

Let
\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)},\mathbf{B}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times n_{m}}
for
m=1,2,\ldots,D
.

(i)

The Kronecker product is linear in each factor (multilinear):

\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes(\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)}+\mathbf{B}^{(m)})\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\begin{aligned} t]{2}=\;&\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(m)}&&\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\\
+\;&\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{B}^{(m)}&&\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}.\end{aligned}

(ii)

The Kronecker product is compatible with the ordinary matrix product (mixed-product property):

\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\big)\big(\mathbf{B}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{B}^{(D)}\big)=\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\mathbf{B}^{(1)}\big)\otimes\cdots\otimes\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\mathbf{B}^{(D)}\big).

(iii)

A Kronecker product is invertible if each factor is invertible. In that case

\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\big)^{-1}=\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\big)^{-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}\big)^{-1}.

(iv)

Matrix-vector products with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}

can be written as a sequence of one-index contractions:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\big)_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}}A_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}v_{\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}}A^{(1)}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\sum_{j_{2}}A^{(2)}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots\sum_{j_{D}}A^{(D)}_{i_{D}j_{D}}v_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{D}}.

(v)

Matrix-vector product with a one-mode bracket matrix can be written as a single one-index contraction:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{A}^{\![m]}\mathbf{v}\big)_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}}A^{[m]}_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}v_{\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{m}}A^{(m)}_{i_{m}j_{m}}v_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m-1}j_{m}i_{m+1}\cdots i_{D}}.

(vi)

Assume that the matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(m)}
are diagonalizable as

\mathbf{A}^{(m)}=\mathbf{Q}^{(m)}\mathbf{D}^{(m)}\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(m)})^{-1}
. Then the Kronecker product

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\!(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\!(D)}
is also diagonalizable:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} t]{3}\mathbf{Q}&=\mathbf{Q}^{(1)}&&\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{Q}^{(D)}&&,\\
\mathbf{D}&=\mathbf{D}^{(1)}&&\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{D}^{(D)}&&.\end{aligned}

(vii)

The bracket matrices commute in the sense that

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{A}^{\![m]},\mathbf{A}^{\![m]}\big]

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{if}\quad m\neq m^{\prime},

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{A}^{\![\mathbf{m}]},\mathbf{A}^{\![\mathbf{m}^{\prime}]}\big]

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbf{m}\cap\mathbf{m}^{\prime}=\varnothing.

Proof.

The statements (i)–(v) are easily verified using Definition
4.1
(i). Statements (vi) and (vii) follow directly
from (ii).
∎

Appendix D
A generalized notion of CUD

It turns out that the version of the closed under decrements property given in the main text (Definition
3.3
)
can be generalized considerably, as described by
22
.
Here, we state a few selected definitions from their work that indicate how
our GPR approach can be extended to other types of grids.
The framework of
22
relies heavily on the notion of a preorder:

Definition D.1.

A preorder is a binary relation
\leq
on a set
\mathcal{S}
that satisfies (i) and (ii) for all
i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}
:

(i)

Reflexivity:
i\leq i
(every element is related to itself).

(ii)

Transitivity:
i\leq j
and
j\leq k
implies
i\leq k
.

(iii)

Antisymmetry:
i\leq j
and
j\leq i
implies
i=j
(no two distinct elements precede each other).

(iv)

Strong connectedness:
i\leq j
or
j\leq i
(all pairs of elements are related).

A partial order satisfies (i)–(iii), while a total order (or linear order)
satisfies (i)–(iv).

Having defined the concept of a preorder, we consider an index set
\mathcal{I}

and a subset
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
which is closed under decrements in the following, generalized sense:

Definition D.2.

The subset
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathcal{I}
is closed under decrements
if
i\leq j
implies
i\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}

for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
j\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
.

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
is also said to be downward closed with respect to
\leq
.

We also need a generalized notion of triangularity
(see
22
, Definition 1)
:

Definition D.3.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an index set equipped with a preorder,
\leq
.
In addition, let
\mathbf{A}
be a square matrix of size
|\mathcal{I}|
.
We define
\leq
-triangularity in the following way:

(i)

\mathbf{A}
is called lower
\leq
-triangular if
A_{ij}\neq 0
implies
j\leq i
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
.

(ii)

\mathbf{A}
is called upper
\leq
-triangular if
A_{ij}\neq 0
implies
i\leq j
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
.

In the context of grids, it is natural to introduce 1D index sets
\mathcal{I}^{(d)}
equipped
with preorders
\leq_{d}
(note that the 1D indices need not be integers).
The corresponding multi-indices are defined as in the main text:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{I}^{(D)}.

(D1)

The 1D preorders in turn induce a preorder
\leq
on
\mathcal{I}

(see
22
, Definition 2)
:

Definition D.4.

For multi-indices
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{I}
, we define
\mathbf{i}\leq\mathbf{j}

if and only if
i_{d}\leq_{d}j_{d}
for all
1\leq d\leq D
.

With these definitions in place, Theorems
E1
and
E2
hold as they stand

(see
22
, Theorems 1 and 3, for proofs and formal statements)
.
These theorems allow kernel matrix-vector products to be computed without reference to the underlying complete grid, thus eliminating
the curse of dimensionality. The concrete computational complexity will, however, depend on the details of the grid.

One grid type of interest is the classical sparse grid
(
6
)
, whose
hierarchical structure makes it closed under decrements.
Sparse grids are discussed in detail by
22
, so
we will not consider them any further apart from mentioning their
potential use in a Gaussian process regression context.

In the main text we considered the 1D index sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(m)}=\{0,1,\ldots,n_{m}-1\},

(D2)

and we tacitly assumed a particular preorder,
namely the standard order on the integers. A matrix that is triangular
with respect to the standard order is simply triangular in the ordinary sense.
It is, however, possible to choose a different preorder under which

\mathcal{I}^{(m)}

Let

.

.

G.2
Complexity of MVPs with
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}

In order to determine the complexity of the matrix-vector product

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\big(\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[1]}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[1]}}\cdots\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[D]}}\big)\widehat{\mathbf{v}}

(G6)

we start by considering a single one-mode contraction
\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
.
The underlying 1D matrix
\mathbf{U}^{m}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
0&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\dots&0\\
0&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\dots&1\\
0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{I}^{(m)}+\mathbf{S}^{(m)}

(G7)

which implies

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\big(\widehat{\mathbf{I}^{[m]}}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}^{[m]}}\big)\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}.

(G8)

The only non-zero block of the matrix
\mathbf{S}^{(m)}
is the down-block,
so the second term in (
G8
) only involves down-contractions in mode
m
.
This means that only mode combinations containing
m
are touched.

For definiteness, we consider an mode combination range,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
, defined by a maximum cut level,
\alpha
.
The mode combination range is dominated by the
\alpha
-cuts, which will thus determine the complexity. The number of
\alpha
-cuts
containing the given mode
m
is

\displaystyle\binom{D-1}{\alpha-1}\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{(D-1)^{\alpha-1}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha-1}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right).

(G9)

Assuming
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{D}=n
for simplicity, each down-contraction has an operation count
of
n^{\alpha}
, so the complexity of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha-1}n^{\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right).

(G10)

Equation (
G6
) involves
D
matrix-vector products with this complexity, so the overall
cost of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}n^{\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\alpha\widehat{N}).

(G11)

The complexity of
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
can be established in much the same way. To start with, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{m}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\dots&0\\
1&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&0&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\dots&0\\
0&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\dots&0\\
1&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{I}^{(m)}+\mathbf{D}^{(m)}

(G12)

which implies

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{L}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\big(\widehat{\mathbf{I}^{[m]}}+\widehat{\mathbf{D}^{[m]}}\big)\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}+\widehat{\mathbf{D}^{[m]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}.

(G13)

This time, the second term involves up-contractions in mode
m
, so only mode combinations excluding
m
are touched.
Due to the chopping we only need
to consider those up-contractions that remain in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
. Doing an up-contraction on
an
\alpha
-cut will for example produce a result of order
\alpha+1
, which is outside
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
.
The dominating up-contractions are thus those that touch mode combinations of order
\alpha-1
that do not contain the given mode
m
.
The number of such mode combinations is

\displaystyle\binom{D-1}{\alpha-1}\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{(D-1)^{\alpha-1}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha-1}}{(\alpha-1)!}\right)

(G14)

while the cost of each up-contraction is
n^{\alpha}
. In conclusion, we find that
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
has the same complexity
as
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
, namely
\mathcal{O}(\alpha\widehat{N})
.

G.3
Complexity of MVPs with
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}

We will now address the complexity of the matrix-vector product with the matrix

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}.

(G15)

For simplicity, we will start by considering
\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}

and then
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}

before summing up the terms.
The key component is the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}
,
which projects onto the subspace where all modes
m\notin\mathbf{m}
are in the reference
(equivalently, this is the subspace where only the modes
m\in\mathbf{m}
are allowed to be
displaced from the reference). This subspace can generally be characterized by a set of multi-indices

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{m})\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
, but for our purposes it is more useful
to consider the corresponding mode combination range,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
.
Due to the action of the projector (as described above), we must have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})=\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\,|\,\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m},\,\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}\}.

(G16)

In our implementation we currently assume
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
,
so we always have
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
. But
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
is closed under taking subsets,
so in this case (
G16
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})=\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\,|\,\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}\}=\mathrm{powerset}(\mathbf{m}),

(G17)

which has size
|\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})|=2^{|\mathbf{m}|}
.

In addition to being a projector,
\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}
commutes with
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}

so

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}=\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}\widehat{\mathbf{R}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}},

(G18)

which shows that the action of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}
does not produce any terms outside the subspace

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})
. We conclude that each term in
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
touches only a small part of the vector,
which is in fact the key to the performance of the matrix-vector product.
The actual multiplication by
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}
can be carried out using
the LU-based machinery (Theorem
E1
and Corollary
E3
) with the only modification that the code should only
access the sub-vector defined by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})
, rather than the full vector.
If
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
(as in our current implementation),
the subspace
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})
can be viewed as a complete grid over the modes
\mathbf{m}
.
This means that
\widehat{\mathbf{M}^{[\mathbf{m}]}}
can be carried out as a sequence of ordinary
one-mode contractions (no LU decomposition is necessary).

To derive a definite complexity, we consider identical kernel and grid mode combination ranges (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
)
defined by a maximum interaction order or cut level,
\alpha
.
The matrix-vector product is dominated by the highest-order kernel terms (the
\alpha
-terms), and each such term
is dominated by the forward contractions.
Consider an
\alpha
-term indexed by
\mathbf{m}
. Every non-empty subspace mode combination,
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{m})
,
can be forward-contracted
k=|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|
times, and each of those forward contraction has an operation count of
(n-1)^{k+1}

(assuming
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{D}=n
). For a single
\alpha
-term, this yields an operation count of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{k}k(n-1)^{k+1}=\alpha n^{\alpha-1}(n-1)^{2}<\alpha n^{\alpha+1}.

(G19)

The first equality can be proved by differentiating the binomial theorem,

n^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{k}(n-1)^{k}
, with respect to
n

and multiplying the result by
(n-1)^{2}
on both sides.
The number of
\alpha
-terms is

\displaystyle\binom{D}{\alpha}\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\right),

(G20)

so the overall complexity of the matrix-vector product by
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\left(n\alpha\frac{D^{\alpha}n^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n\alpha\widehat{N}).

(G21)

Appendix H

\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}

The first part of the matrix-vector product is now expressed without chopping,

can be viewed as a rank-one tensor.
To be specific, we make the association
\mathbf{i}\cong(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a})

(see Definition

) and write

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{i}}=\bigotimes_{m=1}^{D}\mathbf{v}^{(m)},\quad\mathbf{v}^{(m)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\quad}l@{}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}&\text{if }m\notin\mathbf{m},\\

\mathbf{v}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}}

equal to one and the remaining elements equal to zero.
The symbols
\mathbf{r}^{(m)}
and

\mathbf{a}^{(m)}

(

)
and
m\in\mathbf{m}
(

).
To further simplify notation we reorder the factors of the Kronecker product, which
corresponds to a permutation of the elements of
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{i}}
. To
compensate, we multiply by the inverse permutation,
\mathbf{P}
, and rewrite (
H
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{i}}=\mathbf{P}\bigg[\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg].

(H5)

We stress that the only purpose of this reordering is to make the equations more readable.
No permutation is necessary in the actual computation.

Before proceeding we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{(m)},

(H6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\mathbf{a}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{(m)}+\mathbf{a}^{(m)},

(H6b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{(m)}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{(m)},

(H6c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{(m)}\big(\mathbf{r}^{(m)}+\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{(m)}.

(H6d)

Using (
H6a
) and (
H6b
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bigotimes_{m=1}^{D}\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\bigg)\mathbf{P}\bigg[\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\bigg[\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{U}^{(m)}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\bigg[\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}}\big(\mathbf{r}^{(m)}+\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\big)\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg].

(H7)

Substituting this into (
H2
)
and applying (
H6c
) and (

) then yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{i}}

{}\mspace{200mu}\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\setminus\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg]\Bigg)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\widehat{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\Bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\sigma^{2}_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\bigg[\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{M}^{(m)}\big(\mathbf{r}^{(m)}+\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\big)\bigg)\\
{}\mspace{200mu}\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\setminus\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{M}^{(m)}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg]\Bigg)\end{multlined}

{}\mspace{200mu}\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\setminus\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{M}^{(m)}_{:0}\bigg)\otimes\bigg(\bigotimes_{m\notin\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\bigg)\bigg]\Bigg).\end{multlined}

The order of the Kronecker factors may change at the third equality, corresponding to
a new permutation
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
. The notation
\mathbf{M}^{(m)}_{:j}
means the
j
th column
of
\mathbf{M}^{(m)}
. In practice, the computation proceeds as follows: For each kernel term

\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}
, a Kronecker product of
matrix columns is formed according to (
H
).
The Kronecker product is then partitioned according to mode combinations and added
to an intermediary vector. If
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}

then the Kronecker product may generate out-of-space mode combinations, which should
be skipped during computation due to the presence of the chopping matrix
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}
.
Finally,
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
is applied to the intermediary vector to obtain the result.

To derive a definite complexity, we consider identical kernel and grid
mode combination ranges (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
)
defined by a maximum interaction order or cut level,
\alpha
.
We also assume, for simplicity, that
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{d}=n
.
Each of the dominating terms in (
H
) consists of
a Kronecker product of
\alpha
vectors of size
n
, which has a
cost of no more than
\alpha n^{\alpha}
. The number of
\alpha
-terms is

\displaystyle\binom{D}{\alpha}\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{D^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\right)

(H14)

so the overall complexity of computing the sum is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha\frac{D^{\alpha}n^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\right)=\mathcal{O}\big(\alpha\widehat{N}\big),

(H15)

which is also the complexity of the subsequent matrix-vector product by
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}

(see Appendix
G.2
). We conclude that a single column of
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}

can be computed at a cost of
\mathcal{O}\big(\alpha\widehat{N}\big)
.

Appendix I
Computing the diagonal of
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}

The pivoted Cholesky algorithm (see Appendix
A.1
) requires the main diagonal
of the kernel matrix.
We start by recalling the complete-grid
kernel as written in (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}|}^{2}\mathbf{K}^{[\mathbf{m}]}\mathbf{J}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}]}.

(I1)

The diagonal of
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}
consists of the
elements
\widehat{K}_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{i}}=K_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{i}}

for
\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}
.
It is convenient to rewrite each multi-index as
\mathbf{i}\cong(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a})

(see Definition
C.2
), in which case

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\mathbf{K}^{[\mathbf{m}^{\prime}]}\mathbf{J}^{[\neg\mathbf{m}^{\prime}]}\right]_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}}K^{(m)}_{a^{m}a^{m}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\setminus\mathbf{m}}K^{(m)}_{00}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}}\frac{K^{(m)}_{a^{m}a^{m}}}{K^{(m)}_{00}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}K^{(m)}_{00}\Bigg).

(I2)

We have assumed
K^{(m)}_{00}\neq 0
.
The intersection
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}
of

\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}
and

\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grid}}
plays a central role in the following.
The crucial idea is to group the terms in (
I
) according to
\mathbf{s}
.
We start by noting that the possible intersections are exactly the subsets of
\mathbf{m}
,
i.e.
\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}
. Next, we define
the set of kernel mode combinations with a prescribed intersection as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}=\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}\;|\;\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{s}\}.

(I3)

With this notation,

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{s}}\frac{K^{(m)}_{a^{m}a^{m}}}{K^{(m)}_{00}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}K^{(m)}_{00}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{s}}\frac{K^{(m)}_{a^{m}a^{m}}}{K^{(m)}_{00}}\Bigg)\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}K^{(m)}_{00}\Bigg).

(I4)

The outer sum is manageable given that
|\mathbf{m}|
is small, while the inner sum involves only products of reference base kernel elements,
K^{(m)}_{00}
.
As it stands, however, the inner sum must be computed for every combination of
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{s}
, which would be costly.

To get started, we define some notation for the inner sum:

\displaystyle X^{(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}P^{(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})},

(I5)

\displaystyle P^{(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})}=\sigma_{|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}|}^{2}\Bigg(\prod_{m\in\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}K^{(m)}_{00}\Bigg).

(I6)

The computational problem is the simultaneous dependency on
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{s}
.
In order to eventually remove this problem, we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{S(\mathbf{s})}=\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}\;|\;\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\},

(I7)

which is the set of all kernel mode combinations containing
\mathbf{s}
.
The idea is now to group the elements of
\mathcal{S(\mathbf{s})}
in a convenient way.
To that end, choose a fixed
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{grid}}
such that
\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}

and observe that

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m},\,\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}.

(I8)

On the other hand,
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}\subseteq\mathbf{m}
so

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\subseteq(\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m})\subseteq\mathbf{m}.

(I9)

We conclude that the intersection
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}
is at least
\mathbf{s}
and at most
\mathbf{m}
.
This implies the following partitioning of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{s})
for
\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{s})=\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}\;|\;\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}\{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{kernel}}\;|\;\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}).

(I10)

In turn, we find that

\displaystyle Y^{(\mathbf{s})}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{s})}P^{(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})}=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}P^{(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}X^{(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})},

(I11)

does not depend on
\mathbf{m}

Let
\mathbf{m}=(1,2,3)

\displaystyle X^{(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s})}=Y^{(\mathbf{s})}-\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subset\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{m}}X^{(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}.

M.2
Kernel centering

Generally speaking, the base kernel centering depends on the distribution of input locations in
the training set.
(
29
;
25
)

Using the
empirical distribution, which is discrete, one finds
that centering amounts to the following inexpensive modification of the training–training,
training–test and test–test base matrices:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(m)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{K}^{(m)}-\frac{\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}}{(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}},

(M15a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{*}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{*}-\frac{\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{*}}{(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}},

(M15b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{**}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{**}-\frac{(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{*})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{*}}{(\mathbf{w}^{(m)})^{\mspace{-0mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}^{(m)}}.

(M15c)

The vector
\mathbf{w}^{(m)}
contains the frequencies with which
the 1D grid points for mode
m
occur in the training set.
The first element of
\mathbf{w}^{(m)}
is largest,
since the reference is the most frequent point for every mode.

M.4
Priors over hyperparameters

Initial experiments showed that the order variances,
\sigma^{2}_{k}
, tend to become very
large if the optimization is run without constraints.
This leads to deterioration of the
condition number of the kernel, which in turn reduces the overall computational efficiency.
At the same time, we observed no significant gain in accuracy when the order variances were allowed to grow freely.
To avoid this situation, we
place
\mathrm{Gamma}(1.0,0.1)
priors on the order variances, similar to
29
.

To avoid overfitting, we place a log-normal prior on each length scale
as described by
24
.
Since the average distance between two randomly sampled points in a

D
-dimensional hypercube scales as
\sqrt{D}
, they recommend a
prior of the form

\displaystyle\ell^{(m)}\sim\mathrm{Lognormal}\left(\mu_{0}+\log(\sqrt{D}),\sigma_{0}\right)

(M22)

whose mean and mode also scale as
\sqrt{D}
.
In our setup, the points are confined to low-dimensional subgrids/cuts rather
than being distributed over the entire hypercube.
Assuming a cut level of
\alpha
, the effective dimensionality turns out to be

2\alpha<D
(because two points on two non-overlapping
\alpha
-cuts differ along
exactly
2\alpha
dimensions). We therefore use

\displaystyle\ell^{(m)}\sim\mathrm{Lognormal}\left(\mu_{0}+\log(\sqrt{2\alpha}),\sigma_{0}\right)

(M23)

with
\mu_{0}=\sqrt{2}
and
\sigma_{0}=\sqrt{3}
.

To maintain the regularizing effect as the
number of training points increases, we normalize the marginal log-likelihood
before adding the prior terms. The objective function
we optimize is thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\widehat{N}}+\text{prior terms}

(M24)

M.5
SVGP settings

All calculations were run with the same priors and noise as CUTS-GPR, though inputs were standardized to the range
[0,1]
.
Hyperparameters were initialized with default values and optimized using Adam with a learning rate of
0.1
(
\omega=2
) or
0.01
(
\omega=3
).
The optimization was run until the change in objective function was less that
0.1
over
10
iterations.
All other settings were left as default.
