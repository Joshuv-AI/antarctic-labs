Authorship, Disclosures, and Conflicts of Interest in Papers Related to the Use of Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Jaeryung Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As artificial intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI, is being actively introduced and utilized in medical research and manuscript writing, new challenges are emerging in academic publishing, specifically regarding author attribution, transparency, and conflicts of interest. This review examines the current status of AI use in medical publishing by focusing on three key areas: author attribution, disclosure methods regarding AI usage, and conflicts of interest. The prevailing view to date is that AI cannot be recognized as an author because it lacks the capacity to assume the responsibility that is a core requirement of authorship. Therefore, AI contributions are generally disclosed in the acknowledgment section. As AI becomes more deeply involved in the analysis and manuscript writing processes, it is expected that discussions regarding the attribution of intellectual contributions and the boundaries between tools and contributors will become more active in the future. The transparency of reporting AI usage depends on how the AI contributes to the research or manuscript. While AI used for purposes such as grammar or spelling correction is often exempt from disclosure requirements, if AI contributes more substantially to the content of the paper—such as text generation, data analysis, or code development—it is necessary to report this by indicating such details explicitly within the paper. However, stances on the level of disclosure vary among journals, such as whether to reveal all details like model specifications or prompts. Nevertheless, when generative AI is used in the research itself, detailed reporting is increasingly emphasized to ensure the reproducibility and scientific validity of the findings. Furthermore, AI adds a new dimension to conflicts of interest. This includes financial interests related to AI development, data ownership, and potential biases inherent in training datasets and algorithms. Since these factors can influence research results in subtle ways, more transparent and comprehensive disclosure of conflicts of interest in AI-based research is crucial.

BACKGROUND

As research and the writing of papers utilizing artificial intelligence (AI) increase, the publication of such papers is also on the rise.1 Consequently, controversy persists regarding how to establish principles for the publication of research involving AI-related technologies. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) has begun to incorporate issues concerning the publication of AI-assisted papers into its existing guidelines for the publication of medical papers.2 However, the specific details regarding how to handle these principles are left to the editors of individual journals.

While the issue of authorship began to be resolved relatively early,3 it is now time to discuss the underlying reasons why AI cannot be an author and to determine future directions. When subdivided, the use of AI in publishing encompasses various scenarios. In the case of writing papers, AI can be utilized throughout the entire process, ranging from idea generation and reference tracing to translation, spell-checking, drafting, abstract writing, analysis, coding, and data generation.4 While some journals clarify exactly when AI is permitted in all these cases, it remains unclear whether such specific cases need to be specified in other instances.5 Nevertheless, a relatively large number of academic journals disclose details such as whether the AI was used for analysis or writing.6 As the use of AI in medical journals increases, discussions regarding conflicts of interest are being raised again. These include discussions on AI bias and the conflicts of interest between researchers involved with AI and the AI systems being used.

This review discussed authorship issues, the method of ensuring transparency in disclosures, and conflicts of interest related to the use of AI in medical research and publishing. It aimed to critically synthesize current policies and suggest directions for solutions.

PRINCIPLES AND IMPLICATIONS OF AUTHORSHIP

Authorship in academic papers

In the writing of research papers, the role of generative AI extends beyond assisting with research components such as analysis or data generation to contributing to the writing process itself. Beyond simply translating or clarifying meanings, there are instances where new text is created or text is rewritten. Furthermore, its scope is so broad that it may be involved in many aspects of the writing process. In such cases, a significant portion of the actual text may be the product of generative AI. Unlike papers in the humanities, the content of scientific papers, particularly in the medical field, is often considered more important than the written text itself. Therefore, unlike other fields, a broader tolerance is applied regarding duplication or self-plagiarism in specific areas such as methodology. This line of thinking might lead one to consider whether it is acceptable to entrust a substantial portion of the paper to AI and grant it authorship if it contributes to the writing or even to analysis to such an extent. The debate regarding AI and authorship is not so much a debate about the capabilities of AI, but rather a debate about the definition of authorship itself in academic papers. Traditionally, authorship has been understood as the recognition of intellectual contribution to scientific discovery. From this perspective, anyone who has made a substantial contribution to the conception of ideas, the interpretation of research results, and the drafting of the manuscript is entitled to recognition as the author, regardless of the specific tools used. As generative AI becomes increasingly involved in hypothesis generation, literature synthesis, statistical programming, and even the writing of scientific papers, it is becoming increasingly difficult to argue that AI makes no intellectual contribution to academic research. However, unlike general writing, authorship in academic papers is subject to strict scrutiny. Researchers who have contributed to the research and the writing of the paper are listed as authors upon publication. Not everyone who contributes to research and publication becomes an author; authorship is granted only when all of several conditions are met. The issue is whether generative AI can possess all of these criteria for authorship. According to the ICMJE Recommendations,7 authorship was granted only if all three conditions were met: 1) substantial contributions to conception and design, acquisition of data, or analysis and interpretation of data; 2) drafting the article or revising it critically for important intellectual content; and 3) final approval of the version to be published. Under these conditions, there may be controversy as to whether current generative AI could be recognized as an author. However, the ICMJE revised in 2013 added a fourth clause: Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved.8 In other words, it began requiring agreement to assume responsibility. Such responsibility included not only the parts in which one participated but also the identification of specific other parts of the work. In addition, authors should have confidence in the integrity of the contributions of their co-authors. According to these recommendations, it is generally accepted that current generative AI does not qualify as an author. In other words, they cannot be listed as authors because they cannot be held accountable regardless of the extent of their contribution to the paper. Following the recommendation that contributors who do not qualify as authors should be listed in the acknowledgment section, the principle of listing AI as a contributor in the acknowledgment section when it has assisted in writing is becoming widespread.

Implications of authorship

Participating as an author of an academic paper carries significance far beyond merely identifying the writer. It indicates who is credited with the research findings presented in the paper, as well as who assumes responsibility for its content. The publication of a research paper is a process that continues even after the paper is published. Subsequent researchers evaluate the results, assess whether they can be reproduced, and build upon the findings in their own research, thereby extending the scholarly record. Sometimes, research results are later refuted, while at other times, they are widely cited in derivative studies. At times, authors must respond to and address various inquiries regarding the research findings. Authors continue to undertake these roles even after publication. Therefore, the author’s role in research publication is not merely completed upon publication; rather, it involves ongoing responsibilities that persist thereafter. In other words, it means assuming responsibility not only for the truthfulness of the research paper but also for handling post-publication responses. In the scientific community, research papers are often composed of multiple co-authors rather than a single author. This reflects the reality that research in the field is frequently conducted by dividing roles among contributors, rather than being completed by a single individual. In this context, each author is required to acknowledge that they have reviewed and agreed to the entire paper, which was jointly authored through their participation in the research, rather than being held responsible only for the specific experiments they performed. They must then confirm and sign this information when submitting the paper. In research and manuscript writing, such a signature signifies a responsibility for verifying comprehensive information, including not only the content of the paper but also details regarding the participating research team. Occasionally, authorship issues arise after publication, known as authorship disputes. In such cases, the paper may be revised or retracted. It is difficult for editors to ascertain the actual roles of authors in research and publication. Therefore, most editors request information regarding the authors' roles upon submission. Through this, editors can better understand the contributions of each author. Consequently, the judgment regarding authorship is made not merely as the editor’s responsibility, but through the authors themselves confirming whether they meet the criteria. For the editor, this not only confirms that each author participated in the submitted work but also demonstrates that all authors have communicated and agreed upon their respective roles. While the corresponding author may sometimes provide information regarding such facts on behalf of all authors, it generally implies that the authors collectively confirm and assume responsibility for their participation and roles. This plays a crucial role in the event of future disputes. Issues may arise when an author is listed despite not having participated, or when an author is qualified but not listed. In such cases, the documentation regarding the authors’ participation provided at the time of initial submission serves as important evidence for the editorial decisions. Even if an author's qualifications or role are incorrectly stated, if the integrity of the paper is not significantly affected, some journals may rectify the error through an author correction procedure. However, papers may be retracted if the integrity of the work is significantly impacted, such as when a specific author is incorrectly listed but the research content they claimed to have handled actually does not exist. Unfortunately, AI is not in a position to sign off on such authorial responsibilities. This means that it cannot participate responsibly in tasks such as verifying the contributions of co-researchers or agreeing to share accountability for the results.

The role of an author bearing such responsibility is currently impossible for AI, as it is not considered to possess consciousness. If so, could the developer of the AI assume responsibility in place of the AI? The answer to this is negative. Not only is it realistically difficult to accept a developer participating as an author, but developers also typically transfer responsibility for the use of AI-generated outputs to the user as a condition of using generative AI systems. Therefore, participating as an author implies not only participating in the research and writing of the paper, but also reviewing detailed matters such as the content of the paper and information regarding the authors, and acknowledging shared responsibility. There is an issue that requires deeper consideration regarding the current qualifications of an author, namely intellectual contribution and responsibility. This is the fact that even when human contribution contributes more to assuming responsibility and actual significant intellectual contribution becomes extremely small, it is possible for humans to become the sole authors. In this case, there is a significant difference from the classical concept of contribution. The discussion regarding the two concepts of authorship—namely, the recognition of intellectual contribution and the assignment of responsibility—has previously arisen in the context of ghost authors or gift authors. There were cases, like ghost authors, where individuals actually contributed to a paper but were not listed as authors to avoid liability; conversely, there were cases, like gift authors, where individuals were listed as authors and held accountable after publication, yet did not actually contribute to the paper. However, this leads to situations where AI makes the majority of the intellectual contribution, while the human author bears only the responsibility for it. The controversy regarding AI’s qualification as an author should be understood as a conflict between two opposing concepts regarding the definition of an author: namely, the qualification of an author as an intellectual contribution and the qualification of an author as a responsibility. Current editorial policies generally prioritize the latter. This distinction reveals a significant conceptual contradiction within current authorship policies. If authorship policy is primarily a reward for intellectual contribution, future AI systems capable of generating original hypotheses or designing analytical frameworks would be able to meet a significant portion of traditional academic contribution criteria. Conversely, if authorship policy primarily represents accountability, it would become a mechanism for assigning responsibility rather than a reward for creativity.

Authorship in the age of AI

When generative AI is used for analysis, the issue of authorship typically does not arise. This is because it is relatively clear that AI was used as a tool, so it is sufficient to disclose its use in the conventional processing manner. However, controversy may arise regarding how to handle cases where creative content is included in the research and writing processes. There is an argument that as AI continues to develop, emergent properties may arise.9 While this may be attacked based on the principle that “there is nothing new under the sun,” AI can generate content that is newly revealed or creative—beyond what is naturally obtained from a universal human perspective—and thus may be considered meaningful. In such cases, controversy may arise regarding to whom credit should be attributed: can human authors claim such credit, or should such contributions be explicitly disclosed? In this situation, there may be debate over whether it is necessary to distinguish such creative content and disclose it as AI-generated. One argument is that the intellectual achievement lies with the humans who identify and validate whether the content is truly emergent. Regardless of the direction of such discussions, there is currently no clear method to attribute the credit directly to AI. This is because it is generally considered sufficient to acknowledge AI involvement in the preparation of the manuscript. There is no established mechanism for formally citing AI as a source. The ICMJE does not permit the inclusion of AI as a reference. This means that AI should not be cited as a source of information. In other words, AI-generated content cannot be formally cited as an independent source. This is consistent with the idea that AI cannot bear responsibility for the content it produces. Even if an emergent idea is generated, it implies that no accountable agent exists to assume responsibility for that idea. Currently, there appears to be no consensus that creative ideas obtained during the preparation or writing process of a paper must be disclosed specifically as originating from generative AI rather than human authors. While existing policies address the issue of liability, the issue of intellectual contribution remains unresolved. As AI systems increasingly acquire autonomous scientific reasoning capabilities, the question may be how to acknowledge the intellectual contributions generated by AI without undermining the principle that ultimate responsibility lies with human researchers.

The status of future AI and changes in authorship

AI is advancing, and there is much debate regarding its status.10 To date, it does not appear likely that generative AI will attain a legal status similar to that of humans. Recently, there has also been discussion regarding the possibility of AI being recognized as a legal entity.11 Despite these discussions, it appears that the possibility of human involvement in some form remains, even if the actual subject is AI rather than humans. While some believe that such an era may arrive sooner than expected, there is also considerable opinion suggesting that it may be difficult to realize within our generation. This is related to the definition of consciousness.1213 This is because there is controversy regarding whether consciousness can be possessed, whether it truly exists, and whether humans themselves possess it. However, regardless of whether the subject possesses consciousness, the controversy over the exclusive rights of humans as authors has long been a subject of philosophical discourse, dating back to before the age of AI.1415 In any case, when the time comes for the status of AI to change from what it is now, there may also be corresponding changes in its eligibility for authorship. However, as of now, generative AI remains a tool, and tools cannot be authors. The emergence of generative AI has shown that contribution and responsibility, previously considered inseparable, are actually different dimensions of authorship. This suggests the need to separate intellectual contribution and responsibility in future publishing systems. Future publishing ethics may need to distinguish between “copyright,” which signifies responsibility, and “intellectual contribution,” which signifies creative participation. While human authors must bear responsibility for the integrity of their works, we can consider ways to transparently document significant intellectual contributions generated by AI through standardized disclosure mechanisms.

METHOD FOR DECLARING AI-RELATED SPECIFICATIONS

From the initial controversy regarding the extent of AI use, there has been discussion on the specific ways in which AI is used and how such use should be disclosed.1617 It has become known that generative AI can be utilized throughout the entire process, ranging from brainstorming18—such as generating ideas and searching for previously reported facts—to simple translation, summarization, and analysis. While the authorization of its use is important, the specific method of disclosure when such authorization is granted can vary by journal, and the format of such disclosure also differs. Although AI can be used at various stages of the paper writing process, this can be broadly divided into two phases: the research process itself and the preparation of the paper.5

In other words, the issues that arise when AI is used for research purposes may differ somewhat from those that occur when it assists in the writing process-that is, when it is used to prepare a paper to report the results. When assistance is obtained during the writing process, the main issues are ethical matters such as plagiarism or copyright infringement, whereas when it is used in the methodology, the primary issue concerns accuracy and validity. Currently, human authors bear the responsibility for these matters, but transparent disclosure of such use is essential to ensure the integrity and reproducibility of research. However, there are differences among academic journals regarding how to disclose such usage and the extent of disclosure required in each case. For example, while there are recommendations to promote transparency in reporting AI use, there are no uniform regulations specifying exactly how this should be disclosed or to what extent it must be reported.

For preparing the manuscript and writing assistance

There is a growing trend of utilizing generative AI in the process of preparing research papers following the completion of research. In such cases, AI is often used to assist with writing, and the acceptance of such usage is increasing. Examining the meaning of “writing assistance,” it can be categorized into various areas, ranging from simple spelling and grammatical corrections to rewriting in appropriate language and summarizing the text. However, the specific extent to which editors permit such processes may vary by academic journals. It is important to note, however, that this does not encompass “writing itself.” In other words, it implies that AI should be used strictly as a writing assistance and not as a substitute for the entire writing process. The dictionary definition of “assistance” is “act of helping or aiding; help given.”19 This implies that AI is not the primary agent of the writing execution. Since the subject of writing is ultimately human, human authors in this case are being required to transparently disclose the AI that assisted in the writing process—that is, in the preparation of the manuscript. Regarding this, The Lancet states the following: While acknowledging that AI can be used to support manuscript preparation, it clarifies that it cannot be used to replace human “critical thinking, expertise, and evaluation.”20 It explains specific methods for using AI under human control.

Representative and simple examples of AI assistance in writing include tasks such as grammar and spell-checking. The ICMJE states that using AI to spell-check and correct grammar is generally viewed as acceptable. Such simple editing has a positive aspect in that it can reduce the limitations arising from language barriers. JAMA states that there is no need for disclosure regarding the use of AI when it is used for such simple tasks (Table 1).21 Cell follows Elsevier’s publication policy, which specifies that there is no need to disclose the use of AI in tasks such as grammar, spelling, and punctuation.22 According to the editorial guidelines of Springer Nature, to which Nature belongs, they have defined AI-assisted copy editing and suggest that the use of AI in this process does not need to be disclosed.23 Here, AI-assisted copy editing is defined as “AI-assisted improvements to human-generated texts for readability and style, and to ensure that the texts are free of errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation and tone.”23 They express caution by stating that while the concept of “wording and formatting changes to the texts” is included, it does not include “generative editorial work and autonomous content creation.”23 This is interpreted to mean that anything beyond basic editing that involves sentence generation must be disclosed. Science, which maintains a somewhat conservative stance on the use of AI, also suggested that there is no need to disclose the use of AI when it is used for “refinement, correction, editing, or formatting to improve clarity of language.”5 Regarding simple translation, the principles have not yet been clearly presented in many academic journals. However, Science specifies that the use of AI in translation is not an exception to AI-related disclosures and states that it must be disclosed just like drafting or text generation.5

A common example of using generative AI for writing is the direct drafting of a manuscript or the generation of sentences for submission. Regarding this, New England Journal of Medicine, a leading medical journal, has declared that it adopts the policy of the ICMJE.24 Among the ICMJE's recommendations is the requirement to disclose details regarding the use of generative AI in this manner within the acknowledgment section,25 and many academic journals have adopted this approach.6 Traditionally, acknowledgments have been listed in cases where a contributor has contributed to the research but lacks sufficient qualifications to be an author. While AI is listed in this section because it contributed to the paper but did not meet authorship requirements, it also aims to contribute to the integrity of the paper by transparently disclosing the use of AI to readers. Depending on the journal, details regarding the use of AI may be listed in the methods section without specifying whether it was used for writing or analysis, or, in cases where there is no methods section, the details should be listed in an appropriate location.

Some journals also recommend disclosing details in the cover letter as well as the acknowledgment when using AI for writing.5 A cover letter serves as an important means of presenting key information, such as the title and content of a submitted paper, to editors, thereby demonstrating the value of the paper and its suitability for the target journal. In particular, it is used as a guarantee by indicating that the submitted paper adheres to the journal's major policies. Therefore, disclosing the use of AI in the cover letter—in addition to the main body of the paper—provides editors with more transparent information regarding AI usage and serves as a public guarantee that the author will comply with the journal's AI policies. Requiring the disclosure of AI usage to editors plays a crucial role in determining whether to accept the paper for initial review or reject it, depending on the editor's policy. This can streamline the review process or assist in determining the appropriate procedures required for reviewing papers that utilize AI. Another prominent journal, Science, also requires this information to be disclosed in the cover letter.5

Regarding the scope of disclosure, some journals, such as Science and Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, specify the model name and version number, while others, like Cell, require the name of the AI tool used, the purpose of its use, and the extent of their oversight.526 It is not common for academic journals to specifically require the disclosure of prompts when used in the writing process. This is because it is considered sufficient to confirm that authors bear primary responsibility for issues of plagiarism or copyright arising during the writing process. This is likely because the writing itself is not being reproduced for scientific verification. However, The Lancet states in a separate section regarding the AI used in the preparation of papers that “prompts should be provided where applicable, in addition to the name and version of the AI tool used, the purpose of the use, and the extent of their oversight.”20 While this may be perceived as requiring disclosure of even minute details, it appears to be because they believe it supports transparency and plays an important role in supporting trust among those involved in publication, including authors, readers, editors, and reviewers.

For the research from literature search to data analysis

Generative AI is also used for research. It is used in the early stages of research, such as finding ideas and comparing and searching literature, and it can also be used to generate and analyze data. Principles regarding the disclosure of its usage have been established. Science states that there is no need to disclose usage for literature searches. However, it requires disclosure when it is used for research, such as generating and analyzing data.

Editorial organizations such as the ICMJE also present principles regarding how to disclose the use of AI in research. The ICMJE recommends providing sufficient information to enable such research to be replicated, stating that such information includes the tool used, version, and prompts where applicable.25 World Association of Medical Editors also requires that when AI is used for analysis or code writing, the full prompt used to generate results must be disclosed for scientific verification, such as confirming reproducibility, and that the name of the AI used, its version, and the date and time of the query must also be provided.27 New England Journal of Medicine has adopted the ICMJE recommendation, and other journals also require that such content be provided in the Methods section.24 In particular, JAMA requires detailed information, such as the name of the platform or program, tool, version, and manufacturer; specify dates and prompts used and their sequence and any revisions to prompts in response to initial outputs.21 These demands likely stem from the fact that they serve as an important means for the scientific verification of research, as well as addressing issues regarding plagiarism or copyright in the writing process. Research utilizing AI has been conducted even prior to the emergence of generative AI, and there are already papers reporting on the performance of AI. In particular, due to the recent development of generative AI, there is growing interest in the reporting methods for research utilizing it. Unlike when AI is used in writing, the primary concern when AI is employed in research methods is an issue related to truthfulness, such as the reliability and utility of the research results. Many editors believe that papers utilizing AI can simply be disclosed according to existing reporting guidelines, just like other papers. Existing reporting guidelines include CONSORT, STARD, STROBE, PRISMA, and MOOSE; however, with the recent increase in the use of AI, guidelines such as CONSORT-AI, DECIDE-AI, TRIPOD+AI, CLAIM, and STARD-AI are being proposed to disclose this.28 Through these measures, the aim is to provide the crucial information necessary to assess the performance and validity of AI. Even in the case of analysis using AI, researchers are often required to provide verification in a manner understandable to humans, such as through classical methods like statistical analysis. This may stem from issues of trust between humans and AI, such as the potential for AI hallucination29 and recent examples where AI solves problems in ways that are difficult for humans to understand or are entirely different, yet responds to humans that it solved the problem in the way humans desired. Such problems can extend to trust issues between humans—specifically among authors, reviewers, readers, and editors—and can affect the integrity of research. Measures had to be devised to avoid undermining this trust, and to date, verification and assurance by human authors through classical approaches—as well as explainable AI—are being applied to research utilizing AI.

CONFLICTS OF INTEREST IN THE AGE OF AI

Conflicts of interest are important in the publication of research papers because they aim to maintain the integrity of the research. They are reflected in the peer review process during publication and in the authors’ evaluations after publication. While interests may exist, a conflict of interest arises only when such interests actually influence the research and publication. Therefore, the mere existence of an interest does not always result in a conflict. However, transparently disclosing such interests can prevent conflicts from occurring and help peer reviewers and readers understand the content of the paper. Most academic journals require the disclosure of interests prior to the review process. If undisclosed interests are determined to have compromised the integrity of the paper, the manuscript may be retracted.

Conflicts of interest can also arise within similar categories in research utilizing AI. As in general cases, authors of research papers may have interests with companies possessing specific AI, such as equity ownership or research funding; they may also overestimate the performance of the technology or conceal its weaknesses. In particular, many doctors and researchers are participating not only in research using AI but also in its development. Furthermore, they are publishing results regarding the performance of AI currently under development. This presents a different pattern from traditional research, which involves testing the efficacy of equipment already developed or being developed by pharmaceutical or medical device companies. This is because the doctors or researchers themselves are participating as developers of the AI being reported. This implies the possibility of different types of interests than before, as human interests may be more prevalent than in the past. This is because such AI can be commercialized, which may lead to future financial interests. Hospitals also provide large amounts of data and desire to secure intellectual property rights regarding the provision of that data. This issue holds particular significance in the healthcare system, as collaboration between hospitals and AI companies has established itself as a key model for medical AI development. In many projects, hospitals not only provide clinical validation but also actively contribute their own medical datasets, expert annotations, and clinical knowledge, while industry partners develop and commercialize algorithms. The intellectual property rights generated in this way will be shared through joint patents, technology transfer agreements, license agreements, etc. Therefore, conflicts of interest can extend beyond individual researchers to the interests of the institution. Even if the doctor does not make a direct financial investment, the affiliated hospital can benefit from successful commercialization through technology transfer revenue, patent royalties, enhanced institutional reputation, and opportunities to secure future research funding. Therefore, an institution’s interests regarding medical data ownership and intellectual property rights must be considered a significant factor in AI-related conflicts of interest. After development, interests such as technology transfer may also exist. Unlike the evaluation of software developed simply, it seems necessary to disclose financial interests that may arise when a researcher participates in the development process. It is necessary to disclose participation regarding ownership of data or models. Moreover, AI-related conflicts of interest are not limited to financial relationships. Non-financial conflicts of interest can also become equally important. Researchers who dedicate years to developing AI models may have a strong academic, professional, or reputational motivation to prove the excellence of their models. The success of technology can impact career advancement, securing future research funding, academic leadership, or the institution’s reputation. Such intellectual and professional commitment can lead to subtle biases that are more difficult to recognize than existing financial conflicts of interest. Therefore, medical AI research by doctors creates a situation where developers simultaneously play the roles of user, evaluator, and author of the same technology, thereby prompting a reconsideration of the traditional distinction between financial and non-financial conflicts of interest.

Current policies regarding conflicts of interest in the medical field have primarily focused on addressing relationships between researchers and external commercial entities, such as pharmaceutical or medical device companies. However, medical AI research is blurring these boundaries by integrating researchers, hospitals, data resources, and commercial partners into a single innovation ecosystem. Disclosures of stock ownership or research funding alone may no longer adequately reflect the true causes of potential bias. Therefore, future disclosure frameworks need to be expanded beyond traditional financial interests to include participation in AI model development, ownership or management of training datasets, participation in algorithm design, intellectual property rights, technology transfer agreements, and the institution’s commercial interests. Through such disclosure, editors, reviewers, and readers will be able to better assess the potential impact of the interpretation and publication of AI-related research findings. The general principle regarding the handling of conflicts of interest is to handle them transparently. While it is questionable whether this principle can resolve all conflicts of interest in research using AI, transparent disclosure in accordance with that principle is required to date. In particular, it appears necessary for researchers or authors to review the principles regarding conflicts of interest during the submission and publication process of papers and to disclose their interests more transparently compared to other research.30 Ultimately, the challenge posed by AI lies not in creating entirely new conflicts of interest, but in revealing the limitations of the existing concept of conflicts of interest itself. The key issue is no longer simply whether financial interests exist, but whether the various roles of developers, evaluators, authors, and institutional stakeholders are transparent enough to enable an objective scientific evaluation. Therefore, publishing ethics in the age of AI will require a broader and more comprehensive concept of conflicts of interest than was applied to existing biomedical research.

CONCLUSION

The number of papers related to AI, including generative AI, is steadily increasing. While it is expected that the contribution of AI to these papers will continue to grow, generative AI currently fails to meet the accountability required of academic authors. Consequently, it falls short of being listed as an author and is regarded as a contributor rather than an author. We have examined the discussions to date regarding the scope of disclosure related to the principle of transparency, which has become a key issue in research and publication utilizing AI, particularly generative AI. Major principles regarding usage during the research process (analysis) and the preparation process (writing) are becoming somewhat established. Furthermore, a consensus is being reached regarding the methods of such disclosure. In most cases, there is a tendency not to require disclosure even when AI is used merely to clarify the meaning of the text. However, when AI is used during the research process or for writing—such as preparing a paper—transparent disclosure is required at varying levels depending on the journal and the specific content. Along with the frequent use of AI in medical papers, issues such as conflicts of interest are being discussed again. However, despite these issues, the principles that the scientific community has adhered to regarding the publication of academic papers have not changed significantly. It would be reasonable to view this merely as the addition of more specific behavioral guidelines related to AI.
