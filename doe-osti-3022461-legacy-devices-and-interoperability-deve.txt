Executive Summary
The Legacy Device Challenge
Legacy devices across grid, transportation, and building systems present complex modernization challenges that
extend far beyond simple equipment obsolescence. Pulling from presentations and break-out group discussions from
this workshop on legacy devices across all three sectors, this report examines the interconnected factors driving
replacement decisions, documents successful modernization initiatives from recent years, and provides strategic
guidance for coordinating infrastructure transformation across these critical sectors.
Legacy devices, defined herein as existing equipment superseded by newer technologies but still operational, face
mounting challenges related to maintenance, integration, and security. While they may function adequately in isola-
tion, they increasingly constrain system-wide capabilities. A 1970s transformer reliably converts voltage but lacks
monitoring features for managing distributed energy resources. Building controls from the 1990s cannot participate
in demand response programs. Transportation sensors designed for traditional traffic cannot support connected vehi-
cle applications. Replacement patterns vary substantially across sectors. Grid infrastructure may operate 40-50 years
before encountering critical limitations, while transportation and building systems experience more rapid degra-
dation (5-15 years) due to environmental exposure. Understanding these lifecycle patterns enables more effective
infrastructure modernization planning and resource allocation.

Key Replacement Drivers
Five primary factors drive legacy device replacement decisions:
 • Supply Chain Constraints: Legacy devices depend on obsolete components, discontinued manufacturing
 processes, and sufficient knowledge transfer to new members of the workforce. Replacement parts may re-
 quire months to procure or custom fabrication. As equipment ages and manufacturers exit markets, supply
 vulnerabilities intensify, eventually forcing replacement even when devices remain functional.
 • Physical and Performance Degradation: Equipment degrades through weather exposure, insufficient main-
 tenance, wear and tear (especially batteries), and technical deprecation (obsolete protocols, discontinued
 software). Degradation rates vary dramatically by sector and application.
 • Enhanced Features: Modern devices offer capabilities legacy equipment cannot provide—real-time mon-
 itoring, dynamic control, system integration, and security features. While some equipment accepts retrofits
 (thermostats, sensors), others resist modification (commercial HVAC, electrical infrastructure). Compatibility
 requirements and evolving standards complicate integration.
 • Funding Cycles: Financial constraints fundamentally shape replacement timing. Public organizations rely
 on grants with limited availability, causing deferred maintenance and phased upgrades. Some lower-income
 households may replace equipment more slowly to save up for a high-quality replacement. Others replace it
 faster due to breakage (often caused by insufficient maintenance due to the high price) and select less capable
 or lower quality models which are more affordable. Upfront capital requirements create barriers even when
 lifecycle economics favor replacement.
 • Public perception also influences decisions, with communities prioritizing visible improvements (traffic,
 safety) while sometimes opposing infrastructure changes due to aesthetic, environmental, or resource con-
 cerns.

1 Introduction
1.1 Workshop Overview
The goal of this workshop was to map legacy devices across the transportation, building, and power grid sectors to
better understand the common challenges and opportunities for improving interoperability, scalability, and life cycle
management of aging infrastructure. Legacy devices were defined as existing devices that remain operational but
are outdated—whether superseded by newer models, lacking modern features like connectivity or remote control, or
incompatible with contemporary systems. As sensors and controllers proliferate throughout building management
systems, traffic networks, and power grids, the diverse ecosystem of devices creates significant challenges due to
varying communication protocols, data formats, and controllability features.
To establish a foundation for productive cross-sector dialogue, three speakers presented on the current state of legacy
devices in transportation, grid, and building systems. These presentations provided essential context about sector-
specific challenges, existing infrastructure constraints, and emerging solutions before participants engaged in the
workshop’s primary focus: interactive breakout sessions designed to identify specific legacy devices, transferable
strategies and uncover common patterns across different domains. This section summarizes the foundational presen-
tations from Session 1, while the remainder of this report synthesizes the insights and conclusions drawn from the
breakout group discussions and plenary session conducted in Sessions 2 and 3.

1.3 Summary of Workshop Presentations
1.3.1 “Works in Theory”: Mobility Angle (Jibonananda Sanyal, NLR)
In this presentation, Jibo Sanyal from NLR’s Energy Conversion and Storage Systems center presented on build-
ing observability at scale for urban mobility systems, focusing on the challenges and opportunities presented by
legacy transportation infrastructure. Sanyal outlined the CTwin project–a "Digital Twin" (AMPO 2021) for regional
mobility in Chattanooga, Tennessee–which demonstrates how real-time data feeds can be integrated with simula-
tion, modeling, and machine learning to create situational awareness and enable cyber-physical control actions at a
regional scale (Sanyal 2020).

 1

The CTwin system aggregates over 500 primary data streams from seven proprietary vendor systems across three
institutions, including data from the Chattanooga, Tennessee, and Georgia Department of Transportation (DOT),
as well as technology companies, TomTom and Wejo. The infrastructure includes approximately 285 signalized
intersections equipped with fisheye lens cameras that use computer vision for traffic detection, along with 214 Radar
Detection Sensors (RDS) positioned about every half-mile that collect lane occupancy, speed, and vehicle count
data every 30 seconds (Xu, Berres, et al. 2023). Traffic signal phase and timing data is collected every tenth of a
second using the National Transportation Communications Protocol for Intelligent Transportation Systems (NTCIP)
standard (Lakin 2021).
Sanyal highlighted significant challenges inherent to working with legacy transportation systems. These include
older controllers with unknown firmware versions, varying levels of connectivity (some standalone, others net-
worked), and inconsistent settings across different controllers. The partial observability of current sensor networks
creates gaps in coverage, particularly for sidewalks and broader area views, while maintenance issues and the high
cost of upgrading to newer Lidar-based sensors present ongoing operational challenges.
During the discussion, Sanyal addressed questions about weather impacts on sensor accuracy, cybersecurity vulner-
abilities in industrial control systems, and the resistance of some manufacturers to protocol updates. He emphasized
the critical need to move away from legacy protocols and implement systems that can be remotely controlled and
updated, noting that many current traffic sensors still require physical site visits for maintenance and updates, which
is inefficient and potentially insecure in modern operational environments.

1.3.2 Legacy Device Experience in AI-Driven Smart Community Control for Accelerating PV Adoption and Enhancing
 Grid Resilience (Rajendra Adhikari, NLR)
In this talk, Rajendra Adhikari from NLR’s Building Technologies and Science group shared an overview of a Net
Zero Energy (NZE) community project and the challenges that had to be addressed. Many NZE communities have
a high proportion of photovoltaic (PV) generation, which can cause issues like overvoltage, voltage flickers, and
degraded power factors within the electrical distribution system. Existing solutions had an insufficient understanding
of behind-the-meter systems. The project team developed and validated a hierarchical community-scale solution to
resolve these grid issues by connecting Artificial Intelligence (AI)-driven control with a Home Energy Management
System (HEMS) and an aggregator (Jin et al. 2022).
In a pilot study in Basalt Vista, an affordable housing community in Basalt, Colorado, a cloud-based HEMS was
deployed to manage four homes. It interfaced with a nearby energy distribution feeder, the home’s PV generation
and battery storage systems, and the home’s Internet of Things (IoT) assets. A Next Unit of Computing (NUC) was
deployed in each home to serve as a bridge between household and cloud. Each home was equipped with eGauge
powerline adapters (eGauge 2025) to facilitate ethernet-over-powerline connections (Sagar 2011) for the home’s
energy assets. Additional technology was deployed to connect legacy devices: water flow sensors to measure water
consumption, copper gateways to connect the utility meters, and SkyCentrics controllers (SkyCentrics 2025) to
control the water heaters. The web-based HEMS provided real-time monitoring and control for the homeowners, and
AI-based autonomous control to optimize energy use.

Echoing sentiments from Sanyal’s transportation presentation, Adhikari noted that real-world deployment brings
new risks and challenges which cannot be anticipated or tested in a laboratory environment. While some of these
challenges of field deployment could be derisked with a reconnaissance trip, it was crucial to have local personnel
with site access to help solve unexpected issues. Remote access to smart plugs for power cycling and TeamViewer
(TeamViewer 2025) on the NUC for monitoring and remote support were extremely helpful in addressing issues as
they came up. Snowfall presented another hurdle as the snow cover on solar panel reduced generation to well below
the forecast generation based on weather data. Overall, the team found that they had to balance the tradeoff between
optimal grid control and homeowner satisfaction.
During the discussion, Adhikari shared that building and testing the setup in a laboratory setting prior to deployment
greatly sped up the field deployment. With these prior tests, local setup in the community only took one day. Utility-
based control was simulated through the system, but not integrated at the time of the project.

1.3.3 Legacy Devices and Interoperability: Grid (Barry Mather, NLR)
In this talk, Barry Mather from NLR’s Power Systems Engineering Center illustrated the pervasive nature of legacy
systems in power grids by opening with a compelling historical example from Germany in 2011. At that time, Ger-
many had installed 14,000 megawatts (MW) of solar PV capacity, with 70% connected to the low-voltage grid across
400,000 systems larger than 10 kW. A critical vulnerability was discovered: all solar generation would simultane-
ously trip offline if grid frequency reached 50.2 hertz (Hz), potentially causing a nationwide blackout. This scenario
exemplified how legacy control systems designed for simple maintenance procedures failed to anticipate the massive
penetration of distributed solar resources, ultimately requiring a costly retrofit of all affected control systems.
Mather emphasized that nearly everything in the electrical grid represents legacy infrastructure, from the fundamen-
tal 120 Vac utilization voltage and 60 Hz frequency standards in the United States to transformer configurations and
voltage regulation philosophies established decades ago. These design decisions, some made 50-100 years ago, are
now "locked" into what has become the world’s largest engineered system operating on the continental scale. The
presentation highlighted how these legacy choices create ongoing challenges as the grid undergoes rapid transforma-
tion in generation resource mix, load characteristics, and societal expectations for reliability and performance.
The core technical analysis focused on a comprehensive study of legacy distributed PV systems in the Western
Interconnection, examining voltage ride-through capabilities under various fault scenarios. Using the Heavy Sum-
mer 2023 planning case with high levels of utility-scale (17%) and distributed (5%) renewable sources, Mather’s
team modeled three-phase fault scenarios across all Western Interconnection paths. The study revealed that under
legacy Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 1547-2003 standards (IEEE 2003), approximately
4,000 MW of distributed generation would be lost during fault conditions, compared to only 1,500 MW under the
most advanced IEEE 1547-2018 Category III standards (IEEE 2018), demonstrating the critical importance of updat-
ing legacy protection standards (Basso 2014; Kenyon and Mather 2020).
During the discussion, Mather addressed questions about recent grid blackouts, noting that while the February
2021 blackouts in Texas (FERC and NERC 2021; King et al. 2021; Flores et al. 2023) may not be the best example
for renewable-related issues, events in April 2025 in the Iberian Peninsula (Lauby 2025; Lara et al. 2025) likely
involved both renewable and conventional generation controls contributing to system instability. He emphasized
that blackouts typically result from configuration and programming issues rather than the inherent capabilities of
energy resources themselves. Regarding the need for device updates, Mather acknowledged that while some legacy
devices cannot be upgraded, the scale of legacy infrastructure in the United States may not require the massive
retrofit undertaken in Germany, as natural equipment turnover provides opportunities for gradual modernization,
though managing interactions between old and new devices remains a significant challenge.

1.3.4 Commercial Buildings Research Infrastructure and Behind-the-Meter Storage : Flexible and Modular Platforms
 for Building and Grid Research (Donald Price, NLR)
In this presentation, Donald Price from NLR’s Building Technologies and Science center outlined the Commercial
Buildings Research Infrastructure (CBRI) (NREL 2025) Behind-the-Meter Storage (BTMS) platforms designed
to address the growing complexity of building-grid interactions. Research has emphasized that buildings represent
the largest electrical load on the grid, and with the increasing installation of Distributed Energy Resources (DER)
like Electric Vehicles (EV) and PV within buildings, there is a critical need for integrated research platforms to
investigate intelligent efficiency, advanced controls, interoperability, demand flexibility, and energy storage systems.
The CBRI platform provides a flexible research infrastructure with electrical capacity for three commercial buildings
(400 Amps (A)/326 kilowatts (kW) each), utilizing Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) functionality to create a modular
system that connects building simulation with buildings hardware such as Building Management Systems (BMS),
Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) equipment, and various DERs. The platform features sophis-
ticated emulation capabilities including up to 2-MW generation source emulation, dynamic air-side and water-side
condition modeling for HVAC equipment, and connections to electric vehicle chargers exceeding 350 kW. A key in-
novation is the HIL network that serves as a data streaming service connecting hardware and simulation components,
enabling researchers to test grid-interactive efficient building technologies in controlled yet realistic environments.
The BTMS component focuses on optimal design and control of behind-the-meter storage systems, combining ther-
mal storage (560 kilowatt-hour (kWh) thermal capacity) with electrochemical storage (40 kWh lithium-ion battery)
to investigate the optimal combination of these technologies. The research addresses fundamental questions about

 3

connecting disparate control systems from different storage resources to achieve system-level optimal control, while
validating whether simplifying assumptions in building and grid models adequately predict the impact of com-
bined electrochemical and thermal energy storage systems. Integration experiments use HIL simulation with actual
laboratory hardware to calibrate models and implement supervisory controls in realistic operational environments.

During the discussion, Price addressed significant cybersecurity concerns, confirming multiple instances of power
grid hacking by both state and non-state actors. The discussion revealed that many existing building control systems,
some only 6-7 years old, already face obsolescence issues with outdated software and hardware lacking adequate
cybersecurity protections. Price advocated for abandoning legacy protocols in favor of systems that can be securely
updated remotely, noting that while larger manufacturers tend to be more accepting of protocol changes, the industry
faces ongoing challenges with unreliable cybersecurity in control systems, potentially necessitating the development
of proprietary secure protocols or exclusive use of verified trustworthy systems.

2 Legacy Devices
For the context of this paper, we define legacy devices as existing devices which are outdated and have been su-
perseded by newer models or entirely new technology. These devices are still working, but they often have fewer
features, diminished efficiency, manufacturer support is phased out, or they are incompatible with newer devices in
their local device ecosystem.
This poses challenges for maintenance, integration with other systems, and security. However, the lifecycle of these
devices varies significantly.

Legacy devices often lack major features that newer devices have. These features include:
 1. Observability: Legacy devices that are not observable are replaced with new devices which offer this as a
 new feature. If the legacy device is already observable, a newer device might enable access in real-time, or
 integration with other systems.
 2. Controllability: Legacy devices that are not controllable are replaced with new devices which support con-
 trol. If control was already possible, a newer device might offer additional control options and better control
 mechanisms.

2.1 Building System Devices
Legacy devices can be grouped into a few different categories. Many of these devices are specific to a subset of
building types as building size and purpose influence the choice of devices.

2.1.1 Thermal Equipment
Thermal equipment is responsible for regulating the indoor air and water temperature. It encompasses HVAC equip-
ment and water heaters. Each of these device types has a variety of subtypes, which are suited to different environ-
ments and building types.
 • HVAC devices include a wide breadth of equipment: furnaces, boilers, heat pumps, radiant heaters, Air Han-
 dling Units (AHU), Energy Recovery Ventilators (ERV), Heat Recovery Ventilators (HRV), exhaust fans and
 make-up air units, air conditioners, chillers, cooling towers, packaged Rooftop Units (RTU), Variable Refriger-
 ant Flow (VRF) systems, and Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems (DOAS). These devices are generally controlled
 by thermostats. The control complexity can range from simple on/off operation for single stage devices, to
 more complex controls that specify speeds or modulate output for multi-stage or variable speed equipment.
 Modern HVAC equipment can also integrate with Building Automation Systems (BAS) to enable energy-
 efficient operation, demand response, and optimization strategies that adjust performance based on occupancy,
 utility pricing, or grid conditions.

 • Water heaters for both residential and commercial buildings can be either tankless or tank-based. Tankless
 water heaters provide hot water on demand and typically require minimal control, though the desired out-
 put temperature can be set. Tank-based water heaters store hot water and are traditionally set to a single
 temperature, with potential setback temperatures when hot water needs are low. Modern commercial models,
 however, can support more advanced controls such as custom time-of-day setpoints, or strategies that priori-
 tize heating water to the maximum temperature in the configured temperature when electricity prices are low,
 improving energy efficiency and operational flexibility.
 • Temperature Control is managed through thermostats, which regulate heating and cooling setpoints to main-
 tain occupant comfort and energy efficiency. Smart thermostats expand this functionality by supporting
 custom time-of-day and day-of-week setpoints, allowing for temperature setbacks–automatic adjustments dur-
 ing unoccupied or low-activity periods to reduce energy without sacrificing comfort when spaces are occupied.
 In commercial buildings, these thermostats can integrate with building automation systems or operate within a
 network of zone-level controls to optimize HVAC performance across multiple spaces. When coordinated with
 occupancy sensors or scheduling software, smart thermostats enable fine-grained control strategies that lower
 operating costs, reduce peak demand, and contribute to overall building energy management goals. In addition
 to the individual building system equipment, some campuses or districts of buildings rely on district heating

 5

 systems to provide both space and water heating. These centralized systems distribute thermal energy from
 a central plant to multiple buildings and can improve overall efficiency, reduce on-site fuel use, and simplify
 maintenance. However, they are a big investment, and they can lock in conventional energy production for
 decades.

2.1.2 Large and Small Appliances
With the rise in IoT devices, appliances are not exempt. While many of the features are centered around convenience,
they can add safety and increase savings by taking advantage of variable energy pricing. They also allow users to
monitor and start devices via apps. One disadvantage is that it can force some building owners into brand loyalty if
they want to integrate all their devices into one system.

 • Smart fridges can show their contents on a display outside (so users do not have to open the door unneces-
 sarily), keep track of shopping lists, and send notifications if the fridge door is left open. Even high-efficiency
 refrigerators without smart features may include audible warnings for doors left open for too long or warnings
 when the water filter needs to be changed.
 • Smart stoves can notify the user when the oven is done pre-heating, or when they forgot to turn off the stove.
 They can also be started or turned off remotely. This can increase home safety by reducing fire hazards, but
 they could also pose new hazards due to potential cybersecurity risks.
 • Smart laundry, dryer, or dishwasher can be started remotely or scheduled to begin running when energy
 prices are lowest.

 • Smaller appliances like robot vacuums are able to discover the layout of the home, and clean independently
 on a user-defined schedule or on command. However, they can compromise customer privacy and safety as
 home layouts, sound, and imagery are analyzed through cloud-based AI (White 2025).
 • Smart outlets and smart light bulbs can be operated remotely, or configured to turn on or off under certain
 conditions (timer, occupancy, etc). This can save energy if they are set up to reduce the time that energy is
 used. It can also increase safety, e.g. break-ins are less likely if the lights are turned on.

Unlike these smart devices, legacy devices are not able to integrate with a building control system, or be operated
remotely on-demand. A minimum level of observation and control can be achieved by adding smart plugs to legacy
devices, but most devices do not automatically start tasks immediately upon being plugged in.

2.1.3 Control Systems
Building control systems can monitor and control building loads. With these systems, buildings are kept within a
specified range of parameters for occupant comfort and energy efficiency, and it is possible to take advantage of
energy price incentives. In case of major grid events like high demand or outages, control systems can support the
grid by implementing load management and serving as a flexible load.
 • Home Automation Systems can enable flexible controls in residential buildings by tying smart appliances
 together. These systems can connect to utility programs and enable coordination of loads across a district of
 buildings.
 • Building Management and Automation Systems in commercial buildings, also called BMS or BAS, provide
 the capability to integrate distributed sensing (e.g. temperature, occupancy, etc.) to monitor and control the
 time and amount of energy expended by major building loads, such as HVAC, and lighting. As commercial
 buildings are larger and more complex, these systems have to be able to manage a large number of devices.
 For instance, a large building typically has multiple thermal zones, but due to airflow between them, the
 system has to coordinate control across zones to achieve the desired temperature in each area. In addition,
 it may have to respond to varying factors, such as occupancy levels, infiltration rates, and solar heat gain,
 impacting parts of the building differently throughout the day.
 • Industrial Building Controls have the biggest variation in systems as each industry has its own specific plug
 and process loads, and schedule requirements to account for. Some industrial processes can be more sensitive

 6

 to interruptions in power supply than others, and building operators may opt to include or exclude some or all
 of their loads from grid responsive load flexibility.
Legacy building control systems may have some, but not all, of the capabilities found in modern smart systems.
They may have outdated or proprietary equipment, or components that are incompatible with newer devices or
communication protocols, limiting integration and advanced control strategies.
In contrast, most newer, smart devices can share a common concern: cybersecurity. The rapid proliferation of con-
nected technologies, combined with little standardization across products, creates challenges for building operators
and Information Technology (IT) teams. Without consistent security protocols or interoperability guidelines, it can
be difficult to implement a comprehensive cybersecurity regimen. Effective strategies often require a combination of
device-level security features, network segmentation, regular software updates, and integration with broader building
or enterprise cybersecurity polices.

2.1.4 Building-Grid Interface Equipment
Grid interface devices serve as the critical connection points between building systems and the electrical grid. While
these devices are part of the broader grid infrastructure (discussed further in Section 3.2), their integration with build-
ing management systems presents unique challenges and opportunities from a building perspective. As buildings
become more energy-aware and seek to optimize their grid interactions, the capabilities of these interface devices
become increasingly important for building operations.
 • Legacy Meters can require manual reading by utility personnel and provide no real-time information to build-
 ing operators. This limits a building’s ability to monitor energy consumption patterns, identify inefficiencies,
 or respond to time-of-use pricing signals. Without access to granular consumption data, building management
 systems cannot optimize operations based on actual energy usage or costs.
 • Smart Meters can be read remotely and enable real-time monitoring of electricity consumption, allowing
 building systems to respond dynamically to usage patterns and pricing signals. Advanced smart meters can
 provide interval data that helps building operators understand load profiles and optimize equipment schedul-
 ing. However, integration capabilities vary significantly, and many smart meters still provide limited direct
 communication with building management systems, requiring additional gateway devices or software inter-
 faces.
 • Legacy Inverters for on-site renewable generation (primarily solar PV systems) often function as simple
 power conversion devices with minimal communication capabilities. From a building energy management per-
 spective, these legacy inverters provide no visibility into generation performance, system health, or real-time
 production data. Building operators cannot integrate solar generation information into their energy manage-
 ment strategies, potentially missing opportunities to optimize self-consumption or coordinate generation with
 building loads.
 • Smart Inverters provide real-time generation monitoring, system performance analytics, and integration capa-
 bilities with building automation systems. Smart inverters can enable buildings to maximize self-consumption
 of on-site generation, coordinate solar production with controllable loads, and even provide grid services while
 optimizing building energy costs. Some smart inverters support energy storage integration, allowing buildings
 to further optimize their grid interactions through strategic charging and discharging of battery systems.
The evolution from legacy to smart grid interface devices represents a fundamental shift in how buildings can inter-
act with the electrical grid and other utilities. Legacy devices create a barrier between building operations and grid
conditions, while smart devices enable buildings to become active participants in grid management through demand
response, peak shaving, and distributed energy resource coordination. However, the integration of these smart de-
vices with existing building control systems often requires careful planning to ensure compatibility and realize the
full potential of enhanced grid-building interactions.

2.2 Grid Devices
The electrical grid presents a complex landscape of aging infrastructure that spans multiple decades of technological
development. Workshop participants identified a comprehensive inventory of legacy equipment currently deployed
across grid operations, revealing the diverse ecosystem of devices that must interoperate despite varying ages, capa-
bilities, and communication protocols. These devices can be categorized based on their primary functions within the
grid infrastructure.

2.2.1 Generation and Power Conversion Equipment
Legacy generation and power conversion equipment forms the backbone of electricity production and conditioning
across the grid.
 • Power Plant Controllers manage the operation of conventional generators, controlling startup sequences, load
 dispatch, and safety systems. Older controllers often rely on proprietary protocols and may lack integration
 capabilities with modern grid management systems. The transition to more flexible, responsive generation
 requires upgrading these controllers to support faster response times and better communication with grid
 operators.
 • Conventional Generators include older coal, natural gas, and nuclear plants that were designed for baseload
 operation rather than the flexible, responsive generation needed in modern grids. These legacy generators often
 have longer startup times, minimum operating levels, and limited ramping capabilities compared to newer,
 more flexible generation resources (DOE OE 2021).
 • Photovoltaics (PV) Inverters represent a rapidly evolving technology where equipment quickly becomes
 outdated. Older photovoltaic inverters may lack advanced grid support functions such as voltage regulation,
 frequency response, and ride-through capabilities during grid disturbances. They often cannot participate in
 grid services that newer smart inverters can provide, limiting their contribution to grid stability and resilience.
 • Power System Stabilizers are control systems for synchronous generation that help maintain grid stability.
 Legacy stabilizers may use outdated control algorithms and lack the sophisticated coordination capabilities
 needed for modern grid operations with distributed resources and varying load patterns.

As renewable energy and distributed generation reshape electricity production, upgrading these legacy controllers
and generators becomes important not just for improved performance, but to enable the flexible, responsive genera-
tion capabilities that modern grids fundamentally require.

2.2.2 Transmission and Distribution Infrastructure
The physical infrastructure for power delivery faces a dual challenge: expanding access to remote and rural areas
while simultaneously modernizing decades-old equipment across both sparsely and densely populated regions (DOE
GDO 2023). Much of the existing transmission and distribution infrastructure contains legacy components that were
designed for unidirectional power flow and simpler operating conditions, often installed 30-50 years ago when grid
requirements were fundamentally different. Rural and remote areas may have particularly outdated infrastructure
due to lower investment priorities, while urban areas face the challenge of upgrading aging systems without service
interruptions to dense populations (McKenna et al. 2025).

 • Transformers represent some of the longest-lasting assets in the grid, with some units operating for 40-50
 years or more. Legacy transformers lack monitoring capabilities that provide real-time information about load-
 ing, temperature, and condition. Without this observability, utilities must rely on periodic manual inspections
 and conservative operating practices, potentially underutilizing capacity or missing early warning signs of
 equipment failure.

 • Transmission Lines and associated infrastructure were designed for traditional grid operations and may
 require upgrades to support new transmission patterns, increased loading from renewable integration, or
 enhanced monitoring capabilities for better situational awareness.

 8

 • Voltage Regulators in legacy systems often operate independently with limited coordination capabilities.
 Modern grid operations require more sophisticated voltage control that can coordinate across multiple devices
 and respond to rapidly changing conditions from distributed generation and dynamic loads.

The challenge of modernizing decades-old transmission and distribution infrastructure is compounded by its es-
sential role: upgrades must enhance capabilities without compromising the continuous, reliable service that users
depend on daily.

2.2.3 Protection and Grid Management Systems
Grid protection and control systems ensure safe and reliable operation, but legacy versions often lack the speed,
coordination, and communication capabilities needed for modern grid operations.

 • Protection Devices include relays, circuit breakers, and other equipment designed to isolate faults and protect
 grid infrastructure. Legacy protection systems often operate with fixed settings and limited coordination capa-
 bilities. They may not be able to adapt to bidirectional power flows, rapid changes from renewable resources,
 or the need for more selective protection schemes that minimize outage impacts.

 • Programmable Logic Controllers (PLC) and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) Con-
 trols form the communication and control backbone of grid operations. Legacy systems often use proprietary
 protocols, have limited cybersecurity features, and may not support the real-time communication needed for
 advanced grid applications. The integration of distributed resources and the need for faster grid response times
 challenge these older control systems.

 • Electrical Sensors and Phasor Measurement Units (PMU) provide critical grid monitoring capabilities.
 Legacy sensors may lack the precision, communication capabilities, or real-time data provision needed for
 advanced grid applications like wide-area monitoring and control.
The shift from unidirectional, centralized power flows to bidirectional, distributed generation fundamentally changes
grid dynamics, making the upgrade of these protection and control systems essential for safe, reliable operations
rather than optional improvements.

2.2.4 Load Management and Power Quality Equipment
Equipment designed to manage power quality and loads often requires upgrades to support bidirectional power flows
and more dynamic grid conditions.
 • Capacitor Banks (both fixed and switchable) provide reactive power support for voltage regulation and
 power factor correction. Legacy capacitor banks often operate with simple local controls and may not co-
 ordinate effectively with other voltage control devices or adapt to changing grid conditions from distributed
 generation.
 • Filters are used to manage harmonic distortion, which occurs when non-linear electrical loads create addi-
 tional frequencies beyond the standard 60 Hz power frequency, distorting the electrical waveform. Harmonics
 can cause equipment overheating, interference, and reduced system efficiency. Legacy filters were designed for
 traditional loads like motors and resistive heating that created predictable harmonic patterns. Modern power
 electronic devices, including solar inverters, Light-Emitting Diode (LED) lighting, variable frequency drives,
 and EV chargers, generate different and more complex harmonic profiles that legacy filters may not adequately
 address.

 • Variable Frequency Drives (VFD) are power electronic devices that control motor speed by varying fre-
 quency and voltage, allowing motors to adjust power consumption based on demand. Large Industrial Loads
 without VFD represent significant inflexible demand that cannot respond to grid conditions or participate in
 demand response programs.
 • EV Charging Stations represent a rapidly growing category of grid-connected load equipment. Legacy
 charging infrastructure consists of simple, unmanaged charging points that draw power at fixed rates without

 9

 coordination with grid conditions. Smart charging stations can communicate with grid operators, adjust charg-
 ing rates based on grid conditions, and even support Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) applications where vehicles can
 discharge power back to the grid.

 With electric vehicles, solar inverters, and other distributed resources creating unprecedented complexity in
 power flows and grid interactions, modernizing load management equipment transitions from a convenience to
 a necessity for maintaining grid stability and power quality.

2.2.5 Monitoring and Forecasting Systems
Legacy monitoring and forecasting systems often lack the sophisticated capabilities needed for modern grid opera-
tions with high renewable generation and dynamic conditions.

 • Legacy Meters require manual reading and provide limited information about electricity usage patterns.
 Smart meters can be read remotely and may integrate with building control systems, but the transition from
 legacy meters is still ongoing in many areas.
 • Weather Sensors and Air Monitoring Sensors support grid operations by providing environmental data
 that affect both generation (particularly renewable resources) and load patterns. Legacy sensors may lack the
 communication capabilities or data quality needed for advanced forecasting applications.
 • Forecasting Systems for load, solar, and wind prediction are critical for modern grid operations but legacy
 systems often lack the sophisticated algorithms, data integration capabilities, and real-time updating needed for
 reliable operation with high renewable generation.

 • Old Servers and Workstations supporting grid operations may lack the processing power, security features,
 and reliability needed for critical grid applications, while also presenting cybersecurity vulnerabilities that
 could compromise grid operations.
Accurate, real-time monitoring and forecasting capabilities are foundational to modern grid operations. Without
upgraded systems that can process vast amounts of data and predict renewable generation patterns, grid operators
cannot effectively manage the variability and complexity of today’s distributed energy landscape.

2.3 Transportation System Devices
2.3.1 Environmental Monitoring
The transportation system is affected by the environment, and it can affect the environment itself. Therefore, environ-
mental monitoring is included in many traffic applications.
 • Air quality sensors play an important role, especially in metropolitan areas. Both the environment (wild-
 fires, high pollen counts, high ozone) and the transportation system (emissions) can result in low air quality.
 Many metropolitan areas issue air quality alerts asking drivers to minimize driving, and advising medically
 vulnerable populations to minimize exertion and time spent outdoors.

 • Fog Sensors: Fog and smoke can lead to dangerous low-visibility conditions on roadways. In areas where
 naturally occurring very dense fog can be extremely dangerous, fog sensors have been deployed. In 1990,
 dense fog led to a deadly car pile-up of 99 vehicles on I-75 near Calhoun, Tennessee (Local 3 News 2015).
 These sensors, along with a human-in-the-loop (state trooper assigned to this area), were put in place to trigger
 fog gates to shut down the highway when extremely low visibility necessitates it.

 • Weather Sensors: During inclement conditions, some autonomous vehicle functions may be deactivated; e.g.
 dynamic cruise control and lane assistants, which depend on distance sensors and camera-based lane detec-
 tion. For legacy vehicles and (partially) autonomous vehicles alike, the loss in traction can pose a safety issue.
 Infrastructure can also be affected by snow and rain. Camera-based traffic sensors rely on good visibility to
 function. Many northern US regions have infrastructure to shut down highways during major snow storms us-
 ing snow gates, similar to fog gates. While some of these gates may be equipped with sensors and automated,
 others are legacy devices which depend entirely on human intervention.

 10

2.3.2 Traffic Monitoring
Traffic monitoring consists of a variety of different sensor options which support different monitoring purposes.
 • Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV) cameras exist for surveillance throughout cities. Depending on place-
 ment, they could simply be installed for public safety. However, if placed strategically, they can support traffic
 operations. For instance, cameras placed along highways can be used by traffic operation centers for real-time
 traffic monitoring (Brogan and Berres 2023). Beyond this, they can also be used to obtain traffic counts and
 classify vehicles using object detection models such as You-Only-Look-Once (YOLO) (Zhang, Liang, and
 Wang 2025). However, they also have a significant drawback: during inclement weather and at night, their
 detection is a lot less reliable due to a decrease in image quality (Kaur et al. 2023).
 • Radar sensors are widely used for traffic on land, in the air, and on the water. They support tracking and rout-
 ing of vessels, especially in air traffic and marine traffic, where routes are less constrained by physical features.
 In road traffic, radar sensors are used for traffic counts, occupancy, and speed monitoring. Depending on the
 model and configuration, they can differentiate different vehicle sizes and lanes. This data is traditionally used
 to understand longer-term traffic patterns, but as real-time sensor access emerges, they can be used to support
 traffic control (Wang et al. 2022), autonomous vehicles (Xu, Yuan, et al. 2023), or perform real-time analysis
 and prediction tasks, such as crash detection (Moriano et al. 2024).

 • Light Detection and Ranging (Lidar) sensors are traditionally less commonly used in transportation, but
 their capability to obtain 3-dimensional data makes them invaluable for (semi-)autonomous vehicles on the
 road and in the air (unmanned aerial vehicles).
 • Toll systems are placed along express lanes or entire highways, and they have largely replaced staffed toll
 booths. They collect vehicle data (license plate number or transponders) and charge drivers according to their
 road use. Some toll systems operate for all vehicles whereas others may only track and charge freight vehicles
 or vehicles using express lanes.
 • Loop detectors (Mid-Atlantic Entry Systems Virginia 2015) are placed in the roads at signalized intersections.
 They serve as inputs for traffic signal controllers, letting them know when vehicles are present. Some loop
 detectors have trouble detecting smaller traffic participants such as bicyclists. Furthermore, when a loop
 detector breaks, the only way to replace it is to dig up the road, which is expensive, time-consuming, and
 unpleasant for traffic participants.
 • Camera-based detectors are increasingly replacing loop detectors as they are less expensive to install, easier
 to replace and maintain, and much more customizable than a loop detector. A single camera can detect ve-
 hicles in a large number of lanes using virtual detectors, as long as it has a good view of the road. Using the
 camera data, it could be possible to implement prioritization for specific types of vehicles (e.g. buses). De-
 pending on the model of camera and software, these detectors can detect lane occupancy, vehicle turn counts,
 speeds, and other traffic data. Like CCTV cameras, these detectors are affected by light and weather conditions
      (Shao et al. 2022).

 • Bluetooth-based travel time systems use roadside sensors to capture the Media Access Control (MAC)
 addresses from Bluetooth devices on the passing vehicles and the associated timestamp. By comparing the
 timestamps of the same MAC address from different roadside sensors, we can derive the travel time between
 the locations where the roadside sensors are located.

2.3.3 Traffic Control
Traffic signals are arguably the most noticeable control mechanism in transportation. In addition, there are various
other control mechanisms.
 • Traffic signal controllers control the traffic lights at an intersection. Traditionally, the signal timing is de-
 termined by a time-of-day schedule which is periodically updated based on observed traffic counts. A signal
 controller with access to loop detectors can extend the duration of green periods given the existence of vehi-
 cles. With the advent of camera-based sensing and other real-time data sources, it is possible to use real-time
 traffic information at a larger scale to optimize traffic conditions (Wang et al. 2022).

 11

 • Traffic cabinets house the traffic signal controllers and other processing units and devices, connect to sensors
 and traffic lights, distribute electrical power to them, and provide an internet connection for remote monitoring
 and configuration.

 • Ramp metering limits vehicle flow from a ramp onto highways. Like signal control, it can be based on a fixed
 schedule, or be more responsive to traffic conditions.
 • Variable speed limits are electronic speed limit signs which can change the displayed speed. These changes
 could be based on a schedule, automatically update based on real-time traffic or environmental conditions
 (light/dark, heavy rain, snow, fog, etc.), or via manual input from humans. Due to their variability, they provide
 much more flexibility than traditional signs. However, they are much more expensive, and their dependency on
 power to function may limit where they can be implemented.
 • Dynamic messaging signs provide a method for transportation authorities to convey important information
 to drivers, such as weather warnings, travel times, or road closures. They can be used to direct traffic to an
 alternate route.

2.3.4 Vehicles and Fleets
In vehicles, sensing and logging of vehicle data can occur. While this is of particular importance to fleets such as
freight or public transit, it can also be used for personal vehicle sensing and diagnostics. When personal vehicle
locations are tracked, special care has to be taken to protect the driver’s privacy, such as home address.
 • Fleet telemetrics track the operational status (driving, stopped, ignition status, etc.), driving statistics (speed,
 fuel use, etc.), and work hours for each vehicle and driver. They are used to ensure compliance with safety
 regulations and have largely replaced manual record-keeping by drivers on paper. Furthermore, they can also
 be used to analyze vehicle efficiency and how it relates to routing, time of day, and other factors.
 • Vehicle logging can log similar information to telemetrics at an individual vehicle level. The information from
 connect Electronic Control Units (ECU) flowing in the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus can be access
 through On-Board Diagnostics II (OBD-II). It can also store vehicle malfunction information which can be
 read out by mechanics.
 • Vehicle sensing include some sensors that are of direct use to the driver even in old vehicles, such as fuel
 gauge and oil levels. Newer vehicles may have distance sensors for parking, blind spot detection, or rear-view
 cameras for parking. The newest vehicles have multiple sensors for features like lane detection, more elaborate
 distance sensors for adaptive cruise control, or additional cameras for parking.

 • EV have different proprietary charging ports and communication protocols to negotiate the charge rate with
 charging stations. While Level 2 charging has a common port, fast charging is dominated by proprietary ports.
 As most Level 3 ("fast") charging stations only have a single plug type, EV owners have to find a charger
 which matches the ports in their car.

3 Legacy Device Lifecycle
Legacy device replacement decisions involve multiple interconnected factors beyond simple technical obsolescence.
Analysis of replacement patterns across grid, transportation, and building systems reveals four primary drivers:
supply chain constraints that limit continued operation, physical and performance degradation over operational
lifespans, availability of enhanced features in newer technologies, and funding cycles that govern capital investment
timing.
Replacement trigger timing varies substantially across sectors and equipment types. Grid infrastructure may operate
for decades before encountering supply chain limitations, while transportation and building infrastructure experience
more rapid degradation due to environmental exposure. Understanding these lifecycle patterns enables more effective
infrastructure modernization planning and strategic resource allocation across these critical systems.

3.1 Supply Chain Issues
The lifecycle management of legacy devices presents unique supply chain challenges that differ significantly from
modern equipment procurement and maintenance. Legacy devices often rely on obsolete components, discontinued
manufacturing processes, and specialized materials that are no longer in regular production. Replacement parts can
take much longer to procure than modern devices, or they may have to be custom-ordered and produced.

3.2 Equipment Degradation
There are different causes of equipment degradation in device lifecycles. Large equipment typically has an expected
lifecycle end, e.g. 20 years for HVAC systems. However the equipment lifespan can be affected by a variety of
factors.
 • Weather: Equipment that is exposed to the elements may degrade faster than it might in a different environ-
 ment. This issue can affect everything from above-the-ground sensors, loop detectors (breakage in the road
 damages loops), or a build-up of dirt. Distribution transformers are sensitive to heat.
 • Insufficient Maintenance: Most equipment requires regular maintenance but for some equipment this is es-
 pecially urgent. For instance, HVAC systems require regular filter changes, as well as a seasonal maintenance
 of other components, such as clearing dirt and debris from outdoor components. Similarly, water heaters ac-
 cumulate build-up from particulate matter in the water which reduces their performance and can lead to their
 eventual failure.
 • Wear and Tear: Some components degrade from extended use or old age. Batteries are a good example
 of such degradation in a wide range of devices. While they are easy to change for some devices (e.g. ther-
 mostats), they are very difficult and expensive to replace for others (e.g. EVs).
 • Deprecation: This can affect hardware and software alike. For communications hardware, telephone lines and
 serial port have shifted to fiberoptic, ethernet, and wireless communications.

3.3 New Features
In some cases, the introduction of new features is the main driver of equipment lifecycle decisions.
 • Retrofit limitations: In some cases, retrofitting updated equipment, or components, is a possibility, e.g. it is
 relatively easy to change thermostat settings in a building, and it is possible to add blind spot alerts to a vehicle
 through after market modifications. But other equipment is more challenging to retrofit, such as replacing
 large, commercial HVAC equipment, upgrading electrical infrastructure, or increasing building insulation.
 • Compatibility requirements: A new technology may require specific features (e.g. a specific communication
 protocol) to operate properly in its environment. Older devices may not have compatible features to integrate,
 or integrate easily, with the new technology.
 • Standards evolution: While standards aim to establish a unifying set of expectations for specific technologies,
 they can change over time. This is especially relevant for emerging technologies in sectors with very fast-
 paced innovation, such as autonomous vehicles.

 13

3.4 Budget
Budgetary concerns influence decisions about equipment, maintenance, and upgrades at every scale. These finan-
cial constraints often shape choices that affect the efficiency, durability, and interoperability of systems over their
lifecycle.

3.4.1 Funding Cycles
Organizations in the public sector often rely on grants to upgrade their infrastructure. These grants can be limited in
available funds and may not be sufficient to upgrade all equipment at the same time. As a result, these organizations
can experience deferred maintenance and a slower timeline to upgrade equipment due to the timing of secured grant
funding.

3.4.2   Income
Lower income households may upgrade their devices at a slower rate than higher income households, and may
choose to go with less advanced but more affordable models of devices.

3.5 Public Perception
Public perception can be a driver for upgrades in technology. For example, cities may prioritize optimizing traffic
corridors with big delays. To support these improvements, equipment upgrades may be necessary.
Safety is also a very major incentive for decision-makers on many issues. Additional monitoring equipment can be
helpful to support faster responses to safety issues (traffic accidents, crime, etc.) and increase perceived safety.

In addition, public perception can slow down equipment upgrades. Some upgrades to public infrastructure may
experience opposition from the community due to aesthetic or environmental reasons, or worries about resources.

4 Conclusion
Legacy device replacement across grid, transportation, and building systems represents a complex challenge that
extends far beyond simple equipment obsolescence. As this analysis has demonstrated, replacement decisions are
driven by interconnected factors including supply chain constraints, physical degradation, evolving feature require-
ments, budgetary limitations, and public perception. Understanding these drivers and their interactions is essential
for developing effective modernization strategies that balance technical performance, economic feasibility, and
stakeholder acceptance. The replacement patterns identified across sectors reveal significant differences in lifecycle
timelines and degradation mechanisms. Grid infrastructure often operates for decades before encountering critical
limitations, while transportation and building systems face more immediate pressures from environmental exposure
and evolving technological standards. These varying timelines necessitate sector-specific approaches to infrastructure
planning and resource allocation.

4.1 Pathway Forward
Effective legacy device modernization requires coordinated action across multiple stakeholders and decision-making
levels. The following priorities can help guide strategic planning and implementation:
 • Coordinated Infrastructure Planning: Utilities, municipalities, and building operators must work collab-
 oratively to align grid upgrades with building and transportation system improvements. This coordination is
 particularly critical as DERs, EVs, and advanced building controls increase the complexity of grid-building
 interactions. Joint planning efforts can identify opportunities for shared infrastructure investments and avoid
 costly misalignments between system capabilities.
 • Strategic Incentive Programs: Targeted incentive programs for building upgrades can accelerate the adop-
 tion of smart technologies while addressing equity concerns. These programs should prioritize cost-effective
 improvements that deliver multiple benefits—such as energy efficiency, grid flexibility, and enhanced com-
 fort—while ensuring that lower-income households are not left behind in the transition to modern infrastruc-
 ture.
 • Cross-Sector Research and Development: Continued research is needed to address persistent challenges in
 legacy device integration, cybersecurity, interoperability standards, and lifecycle management. Priority areas
 include:
 – Development of secure communication protocols that can bridge legacy and modern systems.
 – Standardization efforts to reduce compatibility barriers across equipment generations.
 – Advanced forecasting tools to better predict equipment degradation and optimize replacement timing.

 – Cost-benefit frameworks that account for the full value of modernization, including reliability, flexibility,
 and resilience benefits.
 • Proactive Supply Chain Management: Organizations should develop strategies to mitigate supply chain
 risks for both legacy equipment maintenance and new technology deployment. This includes maintaining
 strategic parts inventories, cultivating relationships with specialized suppliers, and planning replacement
 timelines that account for procurement lead times.

4.2 Looking Ahead
The transition from legacy to modern infrastructure is not merely a technical challenge but a fundamental trans-
formation in how our energy, building, and transportation systems operate and interact. As renewable generation,
electric transportation, and smart building technologies become increasingly prevalent, the limitations of legacy
devices become more acute. Equipment that once served reliably within isolated systems may now constrain the
performance and capabilities of interconnected networks.

This transformation creates both urgency and opportunity. The urgency stems from growing misalignments between
aging infrastructure and emerging operational requirements—particularly in grid management, cybersecurity, and

 15

real-time system coordination. The opportunity lies in the potential for modernized systems to deliver unprecedented
levels of efficiency, flexibility, and resilience.
Success will require sustained commitment to infrastructure investment, continued innovation in integration tech-
nologies, and thoughtful policies that balance modernization goals with economic and social considerations. By
understanding the multifaceted drivers of legacy device replacement and developing coordinated approaches to
infrastructure modernization, stakeholders across sectors can navigate this transition effectively and build the founda-
tion for more capable, sustainable, and resilient systems.
