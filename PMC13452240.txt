Hypoxia-induced immortalization of Helicoverpa armigera pupal ovarian cells
Authors: Wenyi Jin, Yan Tong, Xuan Li, Qian Meng, Lin Guo, Huixian Shi, Qin Kang, Jihong Zhang, Qilian Qin, Huan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEstablishing continuous cell lines from lepidopteran insects is notoriously inefficient, with success rates typically below 5% and often below 1%, severely limiting research and biopesticide development for pests such as Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae). We present a simple, reproducible method, hypoxia-induced immortalization that dramatically improves this efficiency. By applying a brief hypoxic stimulus (5% O2, 7 days) during primary culture of pupal ovary cells, we achieved an overall success rate of 17.8% across four independent batches, more than 3-fold higher than conventional normoxic methods. This high efficiency enabled the generation of eight continuous cell lines from the pupal ovaries, allowing systematic comparative analysis of early-passage dynamics. As a proof of concept, the IOZCAS-Ha-8 cell line exhibits high susceptibility to both homologous and heterologous baculoviruses. Hypoxia-induced immortalization provides a reproducible, high-throughput platform for generating well-characterized insect cell lines, facilitating studies on insect-virus interactions and biological control.

Introduction

Since Grace established the first insect cell line in 1962,1 continuous cell lines have been derived from over 170 insect species, mainly Lepidoptera and Diptera (the Expasy database, https://web.expasy.org/cellosaurus/). It is worth noting that around the same time, Prof. Shangyin Gao (Gaw Z.Y.) and colleagues also successfully established cell lines from another insect species.2 These lines have become indispensable tools in insect physiology, virology, and recombinant-protein production.3 They form the foundational platform for baculovirus expression vector systems (BEVSs) used for high-yield production of high-value proteins (e.g., Sf94 and High Five cells (Trichoplusia ni, BTI-TN5B1-4)5), and serve as scalable bioreactors for baculovirus-based biopesticides.6 Although Sf9 and High Five remain the gold standards for baculovirus research, Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) lacks a comparable high-performance model, limiting transformative studies on its homologous nucleopolyhedroviruses.

Ovarian tissue has long been the preferred source for establishing insect cell lines due to its good developmental synchrony and low contamination risk.1 Pupal ovaries are particularly suitable for large-scale culture and offer operational convenience. However, to date, no highly virus-susceptible cell lines derived from H. armigera pupal ovaries have been reported, with the most recent comprehensive survey dating back to 200810 (see also Table 1). To address this critical gap in baculoviral pesticide development and host-pathogen research,11 we previously demonstrated that a hypoxic culture strategy facilitates the establishment of cell lines from other lepidopteran pests, including the common armyworm Mythimna separata12 and the fall armyworm Spodoptera frugiperda.13 Based on these findings, we developed a 5% O2 hypoxic induction strategy during primary culture and successfully established eight immortalized cell lines from H. armigera pupal ovaries. Notably, IOZCAS-Ha-8 (hereinafter referred to as Ha-8) demonstrated an infection rate of 54.73% with Helicoverpa armigera nucleopolyhedrovirus (HearNPV), providing a robust H. armigera-baculovirus research system. This study reports for the first time the use of hypoxic pretreatment to overcome the immortalization bottleneck of H. armigera ovarian cells and the establishment of a cell line (Ha-8) with exceptional virus susceptibility.

Results

Establishment of continuous cell lines and analysis of early passage dynamics

Primary cultures were initiated from pupal ovaries of H. armigera, with detailed operational procedures, timelines, and hypoxic induction steps shown in Figure 1A. After tissue dissection and surface sterilization, the ovarian explants were placed into culture flasks for adherent culture (designated as day 0). Within 2 to 14 days, numerous cells migrated out from the ovariole. By day 21 of culture, a 7-day hypoxic induction at 5% O2 was carried out on the cells, followed by the restoration of normoxic on day 28. After continued culture under normoxia for 30 days, numerous cell aggregates became visible under the microscope. The first passage (P1) was performed once a sufficient number of cell aggregates had formed, and subculturing was continued as long as the passaged cells maintained their proliferative capacity. During the early passage stages, cell proliferation was slow, resulting in long passage intervals. From passage 10 onwards, the passage intervals gradually shortened and stabilized. Thereafter, cells were routinely subcultured once per week at a standardized ratio of 1:5 (cell suspension to fresh culture medium). Out of 45 independently initiated primary cultures, eight continuously growing cell lines were successfully established across four independent batches. These lines were designated Ha-5, Ha-7, Ha-8, Ha-9, Ha-10, Ha-11, Ha-12, and Ha-13 (hereinafter referred to as Ha-5, Ha-7, Ha-8, Ha-9, Ha-10, Ha-11, Ha-12, and Ha-13), yielding an overall immortalization success rate of 17.8% (Table 2). Preliminary optimization experiments using other oxygen concentrations (1%, 10%) and treatment durations (3 days, 14 days) in Spodoptera exigua resulted in lower or no immortalization success (data not shown). Therefore, the 5% O2/7 days condition was selected as the most effective and was used throughout this study. Batch-specific success rates ranged from 11% to 29%, with a single batch (batch 1) generating three cell lines. This high success rate represents the first systematic comparative analysis of early passage dynamics across multiple independent cell lines derived from the same tissue origin. Significant differences were observed in the time required for P1 of the eight cell lines (Figure 1B). Ha-11 had the longest initial passage interval of 311 days, followed by Ha-13 (278 days) and Ha-12 (277 days). The first passage times for Ha-8, Ha-7, and Ha-9 were considerably shorter, at 56 days, 57 days, and 61 days, respectively, with Ha-5 being intermediate at 184 days. To quantitatively assess the adaptation kinetics, we monitored the passage interval of each cell line from P1 to P14. All eight lines exhibited a progressive reduction in passage interval over successive passages, reflecting gradual adaptation to in vitro conditions. However, the rate of decline and the number of passages required to reach stability varied markedly. Ha-8 and Ha-7 adapted most rapidly, with their passage intervals decreasing by 87.5% and 84.2% from P1 to P6, stabilizing before P9 with stable intervals not exceeding 7 days. In contrast, Ha-11 and Ha-13 adapted slowly, requiring 12–14 passages to stabilize at 7–10 days. Ha-5 showed an intermediate pattern, with a sharp drop after P2 followed by gradual stabilization from P8 onward. Based on these early passage dynamics, the eight cell lines could be divided into three groups. The first group, fast adapting, comprised Ha-7 and Ha-8, characterized by initial passage intervals of less than 60 days, rapid stabilization within 6–9 passages, and final stable intervals of 4–7 days. The second group, intermediate adapting, included Ha-5 and Ha-9, with initial intervals of 61–184 days and stabilization by P8–P10. The third group, slow adapting, consisted of Ha-10, Ha-11, Ha-12, and Ha-13, which had initial intervals of 110 days or more, slower adaptation rates, and required 10–14 passages to reach stability. In the later passages (P10–P14), we further assessed the stability of passage intervals by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV). All eight lines had CV values below 15%, confirming consistent growth kinetics suitable for routine maintenance and experimental applications. Notably, Ha-8 showed the highest stability with a CV of 6.8%, while Ha-10 exhibited slightly higher variability (CV = 12.3%), indicating minor differences in growth consistency among the established cell lines. Taken together, these data reveal substantial heterogeneity in the in vitro adaptation potential of cell lines derived from the same tissue origin. At the time of submission, most cell lines had been passaged to 25 passages and cryopreserved, while Ha-7 and Ha-8 were further propagated to passage 50, as no obvious phenotypic changes were observed beyond that point.

Hypoxic treatment reduces early apoptosis after subculture

To explore the mechanism by which hypoxic treatment facilitates cell line establishment, early apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) double staining (Figure 2A). Primary cultures were either continuously cultured under normoxia conditions or subjected to a 7-day hypoxic induction (5% O2) from day 21 to day 28 and then returned to normoxia, continuing culture until day 120. However, upon P1 of the surviving cells in each group, a dramatic reversal was observed. Cells derived from hypoxia-pretreated culture (HaP1-Hypoxia group) showed a markedly reduced early apoptosis rate, ranging from 10.4% to 15.3%, whereas those from normoxic cultures (HaP1-Normoxia) maintained high apoptosis levels, ranging from 78.6% to 86.2% (Figure 2C), and the difference between the two groups was extremely significant (p < 0.0001). These results suggest that although hypoxic exposure induced sustained apoptosis in primary cells, it also selected for or conditioned a subpopulation of cells capable of tolerating the passage process, thereby facilitating successful cell line establishment.

Primary culture characteristics and cellular origin

Each culture flask was inoculated with five sets of ovarian tissue. The anatomical structure of the ovaries of mid-pupal H. armigera is shown in Figure 3A. A large number of cells migrating from the ovarioles was observed within 48 h post-inoculation (Figure 3B). DAPI staining combined with fluorescence microscopy revealed that the migrating cells originated predominantly from the luminal layer derived from ovarian somatic cells, as nuclear staining further confirmed their cellular origin (Figure 3C). Initially, cells grew densely around the explants and gradually migrated to peripheral areas, eventually covering the entire bottom of the flask. The migrated primary cells exhibited a relatively uniform morphology initially but showed increasing diversity after proliferation, primarily displaying fibroblast-like or hemocyte-like morphologies. Overgrown cells formed multilayered aggregates or detached from the adhered substrate and became suspended in the culture medium. Morphological characteristics and size comparison of established cell lines

During serial passaging, the morphology of the cells was observed throughout the cell line establishment process. In the Ha-7 cell line, the proportion of round cells fluctuated considerably at P5, ranging from 44.0% to 90.8% (mean ∼61.3%); it decreased to 24.9%–42.6% (mean ∼31.4%) by P10; and then increased to 53.4%–69.8% (mean ∼60.2%) by P20, indicating that the cell morphology stabilized over long-term passaging, predominantly round cells (Figures 4A and 4C). In the Ha-8 cell line, spindle-shaped cells predominated at P5, ranging from 70.5% to 91.5% (mean ∼82.7%). By P10, the proportion of round cells increased while the spindle-shaped cells decreased to 36.7%–58.7% (mean ∼50.3%). By P20, the proportion of spindle-shaped cells returned to 83.7%–94.0% (mean ∼88.5%), re-establishing spindle-shaped cells as the dominant morphology (Figures 4B and 4D). These morphological data further suggest that during the first 20 passages of cell line establishment, the cell population underwent intense selection and adaptation. Both Ha-7 and Ha-8 displayed marked fluctuations in morphological proportions between P5 and P10, reflecting that a large number of cells were eliminated during in vitro adaptation. The early-passage cells lost in this process (e.g., at P5 and P10) contain rich information on adaptation and elimination. If cryopreserved in a timely manner during the cell line establishment process, they would represent valuable resources for studying the mechanisms of in vitro adaptation and immortalization of insect cells. Moreover, this method successfully established cell lines in multiple independent batches, indicating that the hypoxia induction strategy has good reproducibility. Further scaling up of the establishment efforts would enable the development and utilization of even more cell line resources with diverse characteristics.

The proliferation characteristics of six established cell lines (Ha-5, Ha-7, Ha-8, Ha-9, Ha-12, and Ha-13) were evaluated by measuring cell density over a 7-day culture period (Figure 4F). All six lines exhibited typical S-shaped growth curves, with an initial lag phase followed by an exponential proliferation phase, although the stationary phase was not clearly observed due to the limited measurement period. Among them, Ha-8 displayed the fastest growth, reaching a maximum cell density of approximately 3.13 × 106 cells/mL by day 7 and has been stably maintained for over 50 passages, while Ha-5, Ha-7, Ha-9, Ha-12, and Ha-13 exhibited slower growth kinetics. Based on the exponential growth phase data, population doubling times were calculated using Kuchler’s formula (1977)14: 73.57 h (Ha-5), 55.81 h (Ha-7), 34.61 h (Ha-8), 78.00 h (Ha-9), 62.70 h (Ha-12), and 93.88 h (Ha-13). These results demonstrate that all six cell lines possess stable and predictable growth kinetics, with Ha-8 being the fastest growing and thus particularly suitable for large-scale in vitro applications.

To clearly verify the species origin of the newly established cell line, mitochondrial DNA molecular marker technology was employed. The full-length COX 1 gene (1,471 bp) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from H. armigera pupal ovarian tissue (HaPOT) and the Ha-8 cell line, followed by Sanger paired-end sequencing. Sequence alignment showed that the target sequences of Ha-8 and HaPOT shared over 99% homology with the reference mitochondrial genome of H. armigera conferta isolate M0237 (GenBank: MG437194.1), conclusively demonstrating that the cell line originates from H. armigera. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S2, the COX 1 gene sequences are in Data S1, and the sequence alignment results are in Figure S1.

IOZCAS-Ha-8 exhibits high susceptibility to homologous virus and supports efficient viral replication

The susceptibility of Ha-8 to homologous and heterologous baculoviruses was evaluated. Five days after infection with HearNPV, numerous occlusion bodies appeared in Ha-8 cells (Figure 5A). Ha-8 was also permissive to heterologous AcMNPV and MbNPV, displaying typical cytopathic effects and visible polyhedra formation at 7 days post-infection (Figures 5C and 5D). The infection rate of HearNPV in Ha-8 was 54.73%, which was lower than that in the reference cell line BCIRL-Hz-AM1 (Hz-AM1)15 (68.44%) (Figures 5B–5E). The occlusion body (OB) yield in Ha-8 was 2.29 × 107 OBs/mL (average 22.9 OBs per cell), also lower than that in Hz-AM1 (4.83 × 107 OBs/mL; average 48.3 OBs per cell) (Figure 5F). Discussion

In this study, by applying brief hypoxic stimulus (5% O2, 7 days) during the early phase of primary culture, eight cell lines derived from pupal ovaries of H. armigera were successfully established, and their biological characteristics and viral susceptibility were systematically evaluated. This approach achieved an overall immortalization success rate of 17.8% across four independent batches, significantly higher than that of conventional normoxic culture methods (typically below 5%). This figure is based on both published data (Lynn, 2001)5 and our own empirical observations over two decades of insect cell line establishment. The consistent success across all four batches, with batch-specific rates ranging from 11% to 29%, demonstrates the good reproducibility of this method.

The beneficial effect of brief hypoxic stimulation on cell immortalization is consistent with the known role of hypoxia in cellular adaptation. Hypoxia induces the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), which promotes cell survival, metabolic reprogramming, and resistance to apoptosis.16,17 HIF-1α has been shown to orchestrate adaptive responses to low oxygen, including the shift from oxidative to glycolytic metabolism and the induction of anti-apoptotic proteins.18 These pathways may help primary cells overcome the proliferative crisis that typically precedes immortalization. Our observation that hypoxic preconditioning reduces early apoptosis after subculture further supports this interpretation, suggesting that transient hypoxia may precondition cells to withstand the stress of passaging.

The difficulty in establishing continuous cell lines is not unique to insects. For marine invertebrates, despite decades of efforts since the 1960s, few continuous cell lines has been successfully established. Notable exceptions include the Bge cell line derived from the freshwater snail Biomphalaria glabrata19 and, more recently, the first continuous marine sponge cell line.20 Previous attempts to immortalize molluscan cells using heterologous oncogenes (e.g., ras, myc) or viral oncoproteins (e.g., SV40 LT) have consistently failed.19,21,22 Moreover, the limited success of growth-factor-supplemented media in invertebrate cell culture has been well documented.19,23

In contrast to conventional normoxic methods (which typically yield success rates below 5%), approaches relying on exogenous gene introduction (e.g., c-myc)24 or complex supplement cocktails and growth-factor-based strategies,23 our brief physical hypoxic stimulus (5% O2, 7 days) offers several distinct advantages. It avoids the risk of genetic modification and cell-cycle dysregulation associated with oncogene transfection. It requires no complicated additives, making it simpler and more reproducible. It achieves an overall success rate of 17.8%, more than 3-fold higher than traditional normoxic approaches. Moreover, compared to growth-factor-supplemented media, our strategy relies solely on oxygen tension modulation, eliminating batch-to-batch variability of biological additives. This comparison highlights that a simple, non-invasive physical stimulus can be more effective than chemical or genetic interventions for inducing insect cell immortalization.

Unlike most studies on insect cell-line establishment, which typically yield only one or two cell lines per batch and often require multiple batches to obtain even a single cell line, our hypoxia-induction strategy generated up to three cell lines from a single batch and succeeded in all four independent batches. This high efficiency enabled us, for the first time, to obtain multiple independently derived cell lines from the same tissue source and to perform a systematic comparative analysis of their early-passage dynamics. While the earliest passages in a single cell line establishment process are of limited value on their own, comparing multiple cell lines from several batches revealed patterns and insights that could not be obtained from any single line, thereby turning them into a valuable research resource.

We re-evaluated the relationship between adaptation phenotype and viral susceptibility. Although Ha-7 and Ha-8 both belong to the fast-adapting group, Ha-7 was non-permissive to HearNPV while Ha-8 was highly susceptible. Among the slow-adapting lines, Ha-11 showed moderate susceptibility (infection rate ∼10%), whereas Ha-10, Ha-12, and Ha-13 were essentially non-permissive (Figure S2). These results indicate that there is no simple linear correlation between early passage adaptation rate and viral susceptibility. Viral permissiveness is likely governed by multiple factors, including the expression levels of viral receptors, the status of innate immunity, metabolic profiles, and stochastic genetic or epigenetic changes that occur during cell line establishment. Therefore, when screening for highly susceptible cell lines, viral susceptibility should be assessed directly rather than inferred from adaptation kinetics. Nevertheless, the diverse panel of cell lines (Ha-5 to Ha-13) generated in this study, which exhibit marked variation in adaptation rate, morphology, and viral susceptibility, provides a valuable resource for future investigations into host factors that influence baculovirus infection.

Ha-8 is a highly baculovirus-permissive cell line established in this study, providing a readily scalable in vitro platform for recombinant protein production, nucleopolyhedrovirus biopesticide manufacturing, and related basic research. The absence of CO2 in the hypoxic culture system used in this study is technically justified, as insect cell culture media typically employ phosphate or organic buffering systems (e.g., HEPES) rather than the carbonate-bicarbonate buffers used in mammalian cell culture. This design choice eliminates the potential confounding effects of CO2-mediated pH fluctuations on cellular hypoxic responses, allowing precise control of oxygen tension as the sole experimental variable. In summary, this study demonstrates that a hypoxia-induction strategy can effectively improve the efficiency of establishing cell lines from H. armigera pupal ovaries, providing a reproducible and generalizable approach for developing continuous cell lines from non-model insects. Among the eight cell lines established, Ha-8 combines rapid proliferation with high viral susceptibility, making it an ideal platform for baculovirus research and biopesticide development.

The hypoxia-induced immortalization strategy established here could be extended not only to other insect species but also to other H. armigera tissues from which few cell lines have been derived, such as larval/adult nervous system tissues and larval midgut. These underrepresented tissues are of particular interest for insecticide discovery programs, as they represent key targets for neurotoxic and digestive toxins. Moreover, the development of “next-generation” insect cell lines from diverse species, tissues, and developmental stages—as advocated by Reall et al. (2019)25—is essential for advancing pest management research and expanding the toolkit for insect toxicology, virology, and biotechnology. Whether this approach can be extended to other insect species remains to be further explored.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted.

First, the hypoxia-induced immortalization method was optimized using a single oxygen concentration (5% O2) and duration (7 days). Systematic exploration of different hypoxic conditions was performed in S. exigua (beet armyworm), but not in H. armigera. The oxygen concentration and treatment duration used for H. armigera were adopted based on the optimal results obtained from S. exigua. Other conditions may yield different outcomes, and the current condition may not be the absolute optimum for H. armigera.

Second, the newly established cell lines (Ha-5 to Ha-13) have been authenticated by COX1 sequencing to confirm their species origin as H. armigera. However, their tissue origin has not been further validated by molecular methods. On one hand, standardized techniques for insect tissue-specific identification are not yet well established, and we relied on morphological observations during culture to infer that the cells originated from the ovariole lumen layer. On the other hand, because the cellular microenvironment changes dramatically during immortalization, gene expression patterns in continuous cell lines may differ from those in the original tissue, making direct molecular confirmation challenging. Therefore, the conclusion regarding tissue origin requires further validation using more direct approaches.

Third, the mechanistic link between hypoxic preconditioning and reduced apoptosis after subculture remains hypothetical; direct evidence (e.g., HIF-1α pathway manipulation) is needed to establish causality.

Fourth, while Ha-8 shows high susceptibility to several baculoviruses, the underlying molecular determinants (e.g., viral receptor expression, innate immune status) have not been investigated.

Finally, the generalizability of this method to other lepidopteran or non-lepidopteran insects requires further testing.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study did not involve human participants, live vertebrates, human embryos, or plants. The experimental models used include insects (Helicoverpa armigera) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), insect cell lines, and baculoviruses.

Insects (H. armigera)

The insect species used in this study was H. armigera. Pupae were obtained from a laboratory colony reared on an artificial diet under aseptic conditions at 25°C with a 14:10 h light:dark photoperiod. The colony has been maintained for over 10 years since the original insects were field-collected in 2014. Mid-stage female pupae (characterized by dark-red eye-disc pigmentation) were selected for ovarian dissection. No sex-based analysis was performed because only female pupae were used as the tissue source for cell line establishment. The larvae used for baculovirus propagation were reared under the same conditions. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for insect rearing.

Cell lines

The following cell lines were used in this study:

Newly established cell lines (IOZCAS-Ha-5, Ha-7, Ha-8, Ha-9, Ha-10, Ha-11, Ha-12, Ha-13): These were derived from pupal ovaries of H. armigera (female origin). They were cultured in INSECT-XPRESS medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, non-heat-inactivated) at 27°C in a normoxic atmosphere (21% O2) or during primary culture (i.e., the cell line establishment process) under hypoxic conditions (5% O2) as described in the method details. The sex of these cell lines is considered female because they originated from female tissue; no further authentication was performed beyond species identification by COX1 sequencing.

Reference cell lines: BCIRL-Hz-AM1 (Hz-AM1, from Helicoverpa zea pupal ovaries, female origin, obtained from Prof. Zhang Zhifang at the Biotechnology Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences),15 IOZCAS-Myse-1 (Myse-1, from Mythimna separata pupal ovaries, female origin, established in our laboratory),12 IOZCAS-Spex-12 from Spodoptera exigua pupal ovaries, female origin, established in our laboratory),26 IPLB-Sf9 (Sf9, from Spodoptera frugiperda pupal ovaries, female origin, purchased from Invitrogen in 2009),4 and IPLB-Sf21 (Sf21, from S. frugiperda pupal ovaries, female origin, purchased from Invitrogen in 2009)3 were obtained from established collections. All reference cell lines were cultured in the same medium and under the same conditions (27°C, 21% O2) as the newly established lines. Their species identities have been confirmed by our laboratory through COI sequence alignment and then deposited; no re-authentication was performed.

Baculoviruses

The following baculoviruses were used:

HearNPV (Helicoverpa armigera nucleopolyhedrovirus): Wild-type and recombinant GFP-tagged (HearNPV-GFP) strains were used. Viruses were propagated in H. armigera larvae or in IOZCAS-Ha-8 cells as described in the method details.

HearNPV was originally isolated from H. armigera larvae in Beijing, China, in 1984 and is maintained in our laboratory stock collection. Viruses were propagated in H. armigera larvae or in IOZCAS-Ha-8 cells as described in the method details.

AcMNPV (Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus): A laboratory-maintained strain was used. This strain was originally obtained as a virus suspension from Prof. W. G. Yendol (USA) and propagated in our laboratory. The natural host of AcMNPV is the cabbage looper, Trichoplusia ni. The strain is maintained in our laboratory stock collection. Viruses were propagated in S. exigua larvae or in Sf9 cells as described in the method details.

MbNPV (Mamestra brassicae nucleopolyhedrovirus): A laboratory-maintained strain was used. This train was originally obtained from S. exigua in Neimenggu, China, in 1980, and is maintained in our laboratory stock collection. Viruses were propagated in S. exigua larvae or in IOZCAS-Spex1226 cells as described in the method details.

No specific publication describes these isolates; however, they have been maintained in our laboratory for many years. No ethical or biosafety restrictions apply beyond standard laboratory practices for baculovirus handling.

Cell authentication and RRIDs

The newly established cell lines were authenticated by mitochondrial COX1 gene sequencing (see Data S1 and Figure S1). They have not been deposited in a public cell bank yet. No RRIDs are available for these newly generated lines. For commercially or publicly available reference cell lines, RRIDs were not obtained as they are not required by the journal for this type of resource; however, source information is provided in the key resources table.

Sex and gender considerations

All cell lines used in this study originated from female pupal ovaries; therefore, the cells are of female origin. No analysis of sex or gender influence on the results was performed because the study focuses on in vitro cellular responses and viral susceptibility, which are not expected to be influenced by sex or gender in a cell line context. This limitation is discussed in the “limitations of the study” subsection of the Discussion.

Method details

Cell line initiation and maintenance

Ovarian explants were dissected from mid-stage female pupae reared on artificial diet under aseptic conditions (characterized by dark-red eye-disc pigmentation). The pupae were surface sterilised by immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 10 min and then blotted dry with sterile filter paper. Sterile forceps were used to remove the ovarian duct from the pupal abdomen and, keeping the structure intact, the transparent ovarian tissues containing the ovarioles were carefully extracted. (Figure 1A).

The excised tissues were sequentially washed three times with calcium-containing and magnesium-free Ringer’s solution (NaCl 8.05 g/L, KCl 0.42 g/L, CaCl2 0.18 g/L) to remove hemolymph contaminants, and then immersed in initial culture medium [INSECT-XPRESS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS,non-heat-inactivated) and 10% phenylthiourea-saturated INSECT-XPRESS solution to inhibit melanization]. The ovarioles were transferred to a T-25 cm2 culture flask (Corning, NY, USA) containing 1.0 mL of initiation culture medium to maximize the adherent area of the matrix. The culture flasks were sealed and incubated statically at 27°C.

After incubating for 12–15 h (to encourage initial attachment), 1.0 mL growth medium (phenylthiourea-free) was gently introduced. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of medium was added every 3–4 days until the total volume reached 5 mL. On Day 21, the plug seal cap was replaced with a vent cap, and the cultures were transferred to a triple-gas incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Forma 3131) set at 5% O2 balanced with N2 (0% CO2). As INSECT-XPRESS medium employs a phosphate buffering system independent of CO2, supplemental CO2 was unnecessary. Oxygen levels were monitored by the incubator’s oxygen sensor and maintained by N2 displacement of ambient air. After 9 days of the hypoxic induction (5% O2), the culture medium was replaced with 5 mL fresh growth medium under aseptic conditions. The flasks were then resealed with plug seal cap and incubated for an additional 30 days.

Primary cultures were passaged by gently pipetting the culture medium over the semi-adherent cell monolayer to detach the cells. The resulting cell suspension 50% (v/v) was then transferred to new T-25 flasks and adjusted to 5.0 mL with fresh complete medium. The parental flask was maintained at the same working volume of 5 mL. Subsequent passages were performed every 7–10 days by replacing 70–80% of the culture medium. The formation of cell aggregates was monitored weekly using an inverted phase-contrast light microscope (Leica, Germany, DM2000).

Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis assay

To distinguish between normal cells, early apoptotic cells, and late apoptotic/necrotic cells, the Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining method was performed. The principle is that in early apoptotic cells, phosphatidylserine on the inner side of the cell membrane flips to the outer side and can be specifically recognized and bound by Annexin V-FITC. Moreover, propidium iodide (PI) cannot penetrate an intact cell membrane and only enters late apoptotic or necrotic cells with compromised membrane integrity to bind nucleic acids.

To evaluate the effect of hypoxia treatment on early apoptosis during primary culture and after the first passage, primary ovarian cells of H. armigera were cultured under either normoxic (21% O2) or hypoxic (5% O2 for 7 days followed by return to normoxia) conditions as described above. Cells were collected for testing at two time points: (1) after 21 days of primary culture followed by 7 days of hypoxia induction or an equivalent period of normoxia culture, denoted as HaPC-Normoxia group and HaPC-Hypoxia group, respectively; and (2) after the first passage (P1) of the surviving cells, designated as HaP1-Normoxia group and HaP1-Hypoxia group, respectively.

For cell collection, the culture medium was first removed. The semi-adherent cells were detached by gentle pipetting, and the cell suspension was transferred to centrifuge tubes. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed once with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, without Ca2+ or Mg2+) and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Annexin V-FITC and PI working solutions were added, and the cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by three washes with PBS. Images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope (FITC: excitation 488 nm, emission 530 nm; PI: excitation 488 nm, emission 615 nm).

Primary culture characterization

For nuclear staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 min at room temperature. DAPI was applied only to fixed cells and was not used on live cells that were to be continued in culture; therefore, it did not affect cell viability. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (excitation 350 nm, emission 470 nm).

Passage interval monitoring and kinetic analysis

The passage interval for each newly established cell line was recorded from the first passage (P1) to the 14th passage (P14). The passage interval was defined as the number of days between two consecutive subcultures. To characterize cell adaptation dynamics,the percentage decrease in passage interval from P1 to P6 was calculated using the formula: (P1 interval-P6 interval)/P1 interval × 100%. Additionally, the number of passages required for each cell line to reach a stable passage interval was also determined, with the stable interval defined as three consecutive passages with an interval ≤10 days and a coefficient of variation less than 15%. Based on the combined parameters of initial passage interval, adaptation rate, and stabilization time, the eight cell lines were categorized into three groups (fast-adapting, intermediate-adapting, and slow-adapting) to facilitate comparative analysis.

Authentication, imaging and growth kinetics of IOZCAS-Ha-8

DNA was isolated from the 20th passage of IOZCAS-Ha-7, IOZCAS-Ha-8, and pupal ovarian tissue of H. armigera. PCR amplification of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COX 1) gene was performed using primers listed in Table S2, yielding a 411 bp (partial length) or 1471 bp (full length) amplicon. Reaction setup, thermal cycling parameters, sequencing procedures, and sequence alignment methodologies followed established protocols (Tong et al., 2024).

Microscopic imaging of primary cultures and established cell lines was performed on an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) equipped with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera system (TOUPCAM, Suzhou, China, E3CMOS). Cell dimensions were quantified based on calibrated magnification scales, with 30 randomly selected cells measured per replicate across three independent experiments.

Growth curves were established using cells in logarithmic phase (passage 20), and population-doubling times were calculated according to previously published procedures (Tong et al., 2024).

Budded virus production and susceptibility assay

Budded virus (BV) inocula were prepared by feeding late-third-instar H. armigera larvae with artificial diet contaminated with HearNPV, AcMNPV, or MbNPV occlusion bodies (OBs) at 1 × 107 OBs/mL. Hemolymph was collected 3 days post-infection, clarified by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to remove hemocytes (blood cells), filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane, and diluted 1:100 before use. HearBV titers were determined by plaque assay as described below. For susceptibility testing, logarithmic-phase cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells/mL and allowed to attach for 24 h. Cells were then inoculated with 0.1 mL of BV stock (MOI ≈0.1) and incubated at 27°C for 3 h with gentle agitation every 15 min. After adsorption, the inoculum was removed and replaced with fresh medium. For GFP-tagged virus experiments, cells were infected with HearNPV-GFP at MOI 0.1, and GFP fluorescence was measured at 96 h post-infection using a microplate reader (Ex/Em 485/528 nm). Three independent infections were performed for each cell line. The infection rate was determined by counting OB-positive cells in at least 10 random microscopic fields per well (three wells per condition).

HearNPV growth kinetics analysis

Log-phase Ha-8 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells/mL and allowed to attach for 24 h. Cells were then infected with HearNPV at MOI 0.1. After adsorption (27°C, 3 h with shaking every 30 min), cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in fresh medium. Culture supernatants were harvested by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to remove cell debris at 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 144 h post-infection (hpi). BV titers were determined by plaque assay using Ha-8 cells as indicators. Briefly, supernatants were serially diluted 10-fold (10−1 to 10−6) and added to confluent Ha-8 monolayers in 24-well plates (4 × 105 cells per well). After 1 h adsorption at 27°C, the cells were overlaid with medium containing 2% low-melting-point agarose and incubated at 27°C for 5–7 days until plaques appeared. Plaques were counted, and titers were calculated as PFU/mL. Three independent growth curves were generated for each condition, and each dilution was tested in duplicate. No data were excluded.

Stability assessment

For each cell line, the coefficient of variation (CV) of passage intervals from P10 to P14 was calculated using the formula: CV = (standard deviation/mean) × 100%. A CV value of less than 15% was considered indicative of stable growth behavior. This assessment was based on the actual passage records of each cell line maintained in routine culture; no additional replicates were performed because the data were derived from the routine maintenance records of the established lines.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified (see each method subsection). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was used. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but the sample sizes chosen are similar to those generally employed in the field. Randomization and blinding were not applicable for most assays because the measured parameters were objective (e.g., cell counts, fluorescence intensity). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All statistical details, including exact p values and the number of replicates, are reported in the corresponding figure legends.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. The definition of center is the arithmetic mean, and dispersion is measured by SD. Normality of data distribution was assessed visually by Q-Q plots; no formal statistical tests for normality were applied due to the small sample sizes, but parametric tests were used because the data were approximately normally distributed. No data were excluded from the analyses, and no methods were used to determine whether the data met the assumptions of the statistical approach beyond visual inspection.

Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. This test was used for the following comparisons: early apoptosis rates between HaPC-Normoxia and HaPC-Hypoxia (Figure 2B), and between HaP1-Normoxia and HaP1-Hypoxia (Figure 2C); GFP fluorescence intensity between Ha-8 and Hz-AM1 (Figure 5G); and growth curve parameters where appropriate. The exact n (number of independent biological replicates) and the definition of n are provided in the corresponding figure legends (e.g., for Figure 2B, n = 4 represents four independent primary culture flasks; for Figure 2C, n = 3 represents three independent first-passage flasks; for Figure 5G, n = 3 represents three independent infections). The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. Exact p values are reported in the figures or in the text when possible (e.g., p < 0.05, p < 0.0001).

Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. This was applied for comparisons among more than two cell lines in growth curve analyses (Figure 4F) and for comparing morphological proportions across passages (Figures 4B and 4D). The exact n values and what they represent are indicated in the figure legends (e.g., for growth curves, each point represents the mean of three independent culture flasks). No stratification or randomization was applied to group allocation because all cell lines were established independently and compared directly; blinding was not applicable as the measurements were objective.

Sample size estimation was not performed using formal statistical methods. The sample sizes (n values) were chosen based on standard practices in the field of insect cell culture and virology: typically three to four independent replicates were used to ensure reproducibility. No data were excluded from any analysis.

Randomization and blinding were not applied in this study because most measurements were objective (e.g., cell counts, fluorescence readings, passage intervals) and did not involve subjective scoring. For infection rate counts, at least ten random microscopic fields were selected per well, and the counting was performed without knowledge of the sample identity; however, formal blinding was not implemented because the differences between groups were large and unambiguous.

Location of statistical details in the manuscript: All statistical parameters (exact n values, definition of n, SD, p values, and test names) are reported either in the corresponding figure legends or within the Results section text. For example, the statistical comparisons in Figures 2, 4F, and 5G are detailed in their respective legends.
