Serine Ameliorates Cadmium‐Induced Testicular Injury by Regulating the PGAM1/PHGDH Signaling Pathway
Authors: Xueru Wang, Na Luo, Yonggang Ma, Hui Zou, Xishuai Tong, Jiaqiao Zhu, Ruilong Song, Hongyan Zhao, Zongping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCadmium (Cd) is a widespread environmental contaminant that impairs male reproductive function; however, the metabolic mechanisms underlying Cd‐induced testicular toxicity remain incompletely understood. This study investigated whether serine supplementation could alleviate Cd‐induced testicular injury and explored its association with phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1)‐ and phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH)‐related serine metabolism. Forty male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to four groups and received ultrapure water, serine (50 g/L), Cd (50 mg/L), or Cd plus serine in drinking water for 18 weeks. Testicular morphology, hormone levels, ion homeostasis, oxidative stress, inflammation, glycolytic metabolism, fibrosis, apoptosis, and the expression of PGAM1/PHGDH‐related proteins were evaluated. Cd exposure induced marked testicular injury, characterized by reduced testicular weight and index, disrupted reproductive hormone levels, histopathological damage, ion imbalance, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and decreased expression of blood–testis barrier‐associated proteins. Cd also suppressed glycolysis and serine biosynthesis, as evidenced by reduced activities of glycolytic enzymes, decreased lactate and pyruvate production, lower serine levels, inhibited PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH expression, and altered PGAM1 expression. These changes were accompanied by increased fibrosis and germ cell apoptosis. Serine supplementation substantially ameliorated these abnormalities, restoring metabolic homeostasis, improving antioxidant capacity, reducing inflammation, and attenuating fibrosis and apoptosis. In conclusion, serine exerts a strong protective effect against Cd‐induced male reproductive toxicity by counteracting metabolic dyshomeostasis, highlighting its potential therapeutic value as a beneficial additive against heavy metal‐associated reproductive damage.Keywords: blood‐testis barrier, cadmium, glycolytic flux, serine, testicular injury

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is a representative environmental heavy metal toxin that easily accumulates in the body and is difficult to degrade. It can enter the body through multiple routes, including the respiratory tract, digestive tract, and skin, causing adverse effects on both animal and human health [1]. Previous studies have shown that Cd exposure can induce liver and kidney dysfunction, immune suppression, bone damage, nervous system disorders, and reproductive toxicity [2, 3]. Among these, the reproductive system is particularly susceptible to Cd, which can lead to infertility and sterility. The testes are central organs in the male reproductive system, and the blood–testis barrier (BTB), established by Sertoli cells (SCs), plays a critical role in supporting normal development and differentiation of germ cells by limiting the penetration of toxic substances [4]. However, exposure to toxic agents has been shown to damage the BTB, resulting in reduced fertility. Jiang et al. demonstrated that Cd induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, leading to BTB dysfunction via activation of the ROS/NLRP3 axis in sheep testes [5]. Foote et al. reported that Cd inhibits proliferation and differentiation of testicular cells, disrupts BTB integrity, and reduces sex hormone levels and semen quality in rabbit testes [6]. Similarly, Zhu et al. found that Cd exposure causes abnormal testicular morphology and structure and decreases sperm counts in duck testes [7]. Arafa et al. revealed that Cd disrupts testicular ion homeostasis, promotes inflammation and fibrosis, and impairs sperm quality in mice [8]. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying Cd‐induced testicular toxicity and the role of key metabolic regulators such as phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) and phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) remain largely unexplored.

Glycolytic metabolism refers to the process by which intracellular glucose is converted into pyruvate under anaerobic conditions and subsequently to lactic acid. PGAM1 is a key enzyme in glycolysis that catalyzes the conversion of 3‐phosphoglycerate (3‐PG) to 2‐phosphoglycerate (2‐PG), thereby facilitating glucose catabolism to pyruvate. PGAM1 is also involved in the serine synthesis pathway (SSP) by regulating intracellular 2‐PG and 3‐PG levels. SCs utilize glycolysis to produce lactic acid, providing energy to developing germ cells and ensuring normal spermatogenesis [9]. PGAM1 is also expressed in various cancers, where it regulates cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [10, 11]. In testicular tissue, PGAM1 is highly expressed in spermatogenic cells (spermatogonia and spermatocytes) and SCs, but weakly in Leydig cells, with a positive expression rate of approximately 90% in individuals with normal spermatogenic function, decreasing to around 10% in patients with severe oligozoospermia. Schrade et al. confirmed that PGAM1 participates in glucose metabolism in testicular support cells [12]. Siu et al. demonstrated that PGAM1 downregulation exacerbates oxidative stress, promotes spermatogenic cell apoptosis, increases BTB permeability, permits toxic substance infiltration into seminiferous tubules, and aggravates testicular injury [13]. In SCs, PGAM1 overexpression reduces cell proliferation while increasing expression of glycolytic genes, pyruvate, and lactic acid levels in Tibetan sheep [14]. Additionally, PGAM1 overexpression elevates TGF‐β levels, promoting non‐small cell lung cancer progression [15], whereas PGAM1 deletion alleviates inflammation and fibrosis after myocardial ischemia in mice [16]. PHGDH, the first enzyme in the glycolysis‐linked serine de novo biosynthesis pathway, is often overexpressed in cancers, and its inhibition reduces glycolysis and tumor growth [17]. In the testis, PHGDH is predominantly expressed in SCs and moderately in spermatogenic cells, regulating energy metabolism, resisting oxidative stress, maintaining BTB integrity, and inhibiting germ cell apoptosis [18]. PHGDH expression is high in individuals with normal spermatogenesis but significantly reduced in patients with oligozoospermia, asthenozoospermia, and infertility, correlating closely with spermatogenic dysfunction. Clinical studies show PHGDH downregulation in testes of patients with varicocele, leading to SC metabolic disorders, decreased lactate, increased oxidative stress, and BTB disruption, ultimately impairing spermatogenesis [19]. Furthermore, reduced PHGDH expression increases BTB permeability, exacerbating testicular toxicity induced by environmental toxins. Collectively, low PHGDH levels may serve as a diagnostic biomarker for male infertility and represent a potential therapeutic target [19].

Cd directly inhibits de novo serine synthesis in the testis via PHGDH and PGAM1, disrupting one‑carbon metabolism, which represents a core endogenous target of Cd‐induced testicular toxicity. Conventional antioxidants merely scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cannot restore Cd‐induced metabolic dysregulation. In contrast, the serine pathway plays a dual role in both metabolic recovery and endogenous antioxidant defense. Spermatogenic cells are particularly reliant on serine one‑carbon metabolism for maintaining physiological homeostasis. This pathway exhibits higher tissue specificity and mechanistic relevance to Cd‐induced reproductive damage compared to general metabolic or antioxidant mechanisms, which is why it was prioritized in this study. With the rapid advancement of industrialization, Cd's harmful impact on human and animal reproductive health has become increasingly evident. However, the effects of serine on Cd‐exposed mammalian testes and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored. In the present study, we investigated whether PGAM1/PHGDH‐mediated glycolysis is involved in Cd‐induced testicular injury and sought to elucidate the associated mechanisms. Our findings demonstrate that Cd disrupts ion homeostasis and structural function in the testis, manifested by compromised BTB integrity, hormonal imbalances, inflammation, fibrosis, and ultimately germ cell apoptosis in mice. Mechanistically, Cd exposure suppresses serine synthesis and inhibits testicular glycolysis, accompanied by altered PGAM1 and PHGDH expression, highlighting a feedback loop through the SSP that may mitigate testicular injury.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Reagents

All animal procedures were approved by the Yangzhou University Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: SYXK‐SU‐2022−0044). Cadmium chloride (CdCl2; Cat. No. 10108‐64‐2) and l‐serine (Cat. No. 56‐45‐1) were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China) and MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), respectively.

Experimental Design

Forty healthy male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 weeks, were purchased from the Comparative Veterinary College of Yangzhou University. Following a 1‐week acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 per group): Control, Serine, Cd, and Cd + Serine. Cd (50 mg) and/or serine (50 g) were dissolved in ultrapure water and provided ad libitum. Standard laboratory feed and water were also available freely. The experimental period lasted 18 weeks. Dosages of Cd and serine were selected based on previous rodent studies using the same species and treatment duration [20, 21]. The concentration of serine used did not induce any observable systemic toxicity, supporting the rationale and safety of the applied supplementation under the current experimental conditions.

Sample Collection

At the end of the experimental period, mice were fasted for 12 h, weighed, and recorded. Mice were anesthetized with 3% pentobarbital sodium (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) prior to blood collection via cardiac puncture. Serum was prepared from the collected blood according to standard protocols. Following blood collection, mice were euthanized, and testes were rapidly excised, rinsed with normal saline, photographed, weighed, and recorded. The testicular index was calculated as the ratio of testicular weight to body weight. Each testis was then sectioned into multiple portions and stored under appropriate conditions for subsequent histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses.

Measurement of Sex Hormones

Serum testosterone (T; nmol/L, Cat. No. ml001948), luteinizing hormone (LH; IU/L, Cat. No. ml063366), and follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH; IU/L, Cat. No. ml001910) levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Mlbio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP‐MS)

Testicular tissues were dried to a constant weight at high temperature, homogenized into powder, accurately weighed, and digested with concentrated nitric acid at 95°C for 12 h. After complete digestion, samples were diluted to a final volume of 25 mL with ultrapure water and analyzed by ICP‐MS. Standard solutions were used to generate calibration curves, and elemental concentrations were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves.

Histopathological Analysis

Testicular tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin‐embedded tissues were sectioned at 5 μm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome according to standard protocols. Histopathological changes were examined and photographed using a light microscope (DM2000 LED; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Quantitative analysis of spermatogenic cells and interstitial spacing between seminiferous tubules was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Four spermatogenic cell populations were identified and quantified, including spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, secondary spermatocytes, and spermatids. Spermatogonia were identified as the smallest germ cells located adjacent to the basement membrane of seminiferous tubules. Primary spermatocytes, the largest and most abundant cells within the seminiferous epithelium, were located immediately above the spermatogonial layer. Secondary spermatocytes were less frequently observed because of their relatively short lifespan. Spermatids were located near the tubular lumen and displayed stage‐dependent morphological characteristics; late spermatids appeared as radially arranged thread‐like structures. The number of each cell type was manually counted in individual cross‐sections of seminiferous tubules.

For assessment of interstitial spacing, three independent linear measurements were obtained across the interstitial region between two intact and well‐preserved adjacent seminiferous tubules within each microscopic field. The mean interstitial width was calculated and expressed in micrometers (μm).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Testicular tissues were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde followed by 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a transmission electron microscope (SU‐8010; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) to evaluate ultrastructural alterations in testicular tissue.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6; Cat. No. ml063159), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β; Cat. No. ml106733), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α; Cat. No. ml002095), and interleukin‐10 (IL‐10; Cat. No. ml037873) in testicular tissues were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Mlbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Detection of Glycolysis‐Related Indices

The levels of lactate and pyruvate, as well as the activities of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Cat. No. A020‐2‐2), hexokinase (HK; Cat. No. A077‐3‐1), phosphofructokinase (PFK; Cat. No. A129‐1‐1), and pyruvate kinase (PK; Cat. No. A076‐1‐1), were determined in testicular tissues using commercially available assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of Oxidative Stress and Antioxidant Indices

Oxidative stress and antioxidant parameters in testicular tissues were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The evaluated indices included catalase (CAT; Cat. No. A007‐1‐1), glutathione/oxidized glutathione ratio (GSH/GSSG; Cat. No. A061‐5‐1), total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD; Cat. No. A001‐3‐2), malondialdehyde (MDA; Cat. No. A003‐1‐2), and total antioxidant capacity (T‐AOC; Cat. No. A015‐2‐1).

Immunofluorescence

Testicular sections (5 μm) were subjected to drying, dehydration, and pretreatment, followed by antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer. Sections were then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, sections were incubated with appropriate primary antibodies, followed by incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 20 min. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DM2000 LED; Leica Microsystems).

TUNEL Staining

Apoptosis in testicular tissue was evaluated using a TUNEL staining kit (Cat. No. C1086; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and permeabilized with proteinase K for 10−30 min. Sections were then incubated with TUNEL working solution at 37°C for 1 h, followed by incubation with POD solution for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were visualized with DAB, after which sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and examined under a fluorescence microscope (DM2000 LED; Leica Microsystems). Apoptotic rates were calculated as the percentage of TUNEL‐positive nuclei relative to the total number of DAPI‐stained nuclei.

Western Blotting

Total proteins were extracted from testicular tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Sigma‐Aldrich). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Western blotting was performed following the method described by Pu et al. [22]. Briefly, proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, blocked, and incubated with the appropriate primary and secondary antibodies. Specific protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). Detailed information for all antibodies used in this study is provided in Table 1. Subsequent mentions of antibodies in the manuscript refer solely to the company.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). ImageJ was used for image quantification where applicable. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among multiple groups were assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Serine Ameliorated Testicular Damage and Sex Hormone Disturbances in Cd‐Exposed Mice

Organ coefficient is a widely used, objective index in toxicological studies, while histopathological changes provide one of the most direct indicators of organ injury [2]. In Figure 1A–C, testes from the Cd group appeared darker, firmer, and hyperemic, with significantly lower testicular weight and index compared to the control group (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001). To further assess Cd‐induced testicular injury, histomorphology and ultrastructure were examined using H&E staining and TEM, respectively. H&E staining revealed normal seminiferous tubule architecture and orderly spermatogenic cell arrangement in controls, whereas Cd exposure caused varying degrees of testicular damage, including disrupted seminiferous epithelium, reduced spermatogenic cell numbers, widened tubular spacing, and abnormal intercellular vacuoles in the treated groups (Figure 2A). TEM analysis corroborated these findings, showing irregular, fractured, and vesiculated cellular structures, nuclear deformation, dilated endoplasmic reticulum, and disrupted mitochondrial cristae in Cd‐treated testes (Figure 3). Cd exposure also reduced testicular Glu levels compared to controls (Figures 1D and 2C). Masson's trichrome staining further indicated collagen fiber accumulation in the Cd group (Figure 2B). Hormonal analysis revealed significantly elevated FSH and LH levels (p < 0.05 or p < 0.001) and decreased T levels (p < 0.05) in Cd‐exposed mice relative to controls (Figure 1E–G). Importantly, serine supplementation mitigated these histological, ultrastructural, and hormonal alterations, suggesting a protective effect against Cd‐induced testicular toxicity. These results demonstrate that Cd disrupts testicular architecture and BTB integrity, while serine partially restores testicular structure and function.

Serine Effectively Restored Cd‐Induced Disturbances in Testicular Ion Homeostasis, Oxidative Stress, and Inflammatory Responses

Ion homeostasis is essential for maintaining normal physiological functions, and its disruption can compromise tissue health. As shown in Figure 4A–J, Cd exposure significantly elevated testicular Cd, Cr, Mg, and Pb levels (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001), while reducing Zn, Mn, Ca, Fe, and Cu concentrations (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001). Regarding oxidative stress parameters (Figure 5A–E), the GSH/GSSG ratio, T‐SOD, CAT, and T‐AOC levels were markedly decreased in Cd‐treated mice compared to controls (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001), whereas MDA content was significantly increased (p < 0.05). Furthermore, ELISA analysis revealed that Cd markedly upregulated pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IL‐18 in the testis (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001; Figure 5F–J). These results indicate that Cd induces ion imbalance, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in testicular tissue. Importantly, serine supplementation significantly ameliorated these disturbances, restoring ion homeostasis, antioxidant capacity, and reducing pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels.

Serine Rescued Cd‐Induced Impairment of BTB Integrity

To further assess the impact of Cd on BTB integrity, the expression levels of BTB‐associated proteins were analyzed by western blotting. Cd exposure significantly decreased the levels of ZO‐1, Occludin‐1, Claudin‐1, and CX43 compared with controls (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01; Figure 6A–C), indicating disruption of the BTB. Notably, serine supplementation partially restored the expression of these proteins, suggesting a protective effect on BTB integrity under Cd‐induced stress.

Serine Restored Cd‐Suppressed Testicular Glycolysis in Mice

To evaluate the effects of Cd on testicular glycolysis, key glycolytic enzymes, metabolites, and related proteins were analyzed. As shown in Figure 7A–F, Cd exposure significantly reduced the activities of LDH, PFK, HK, and PK compared with the control group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001). In parallel, the levels of the glycolytic end products lactate and pyruvate were also significantly decreased (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). To further characterize glycolytic alterations, the expression of glycolysis‐related proteins was examined. Cd exposure significantly decreased the expression of GLUT1, GLUT3, GLUT8, LDHA, LDHB, HK2, PFK, and PKM2 (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01; Figure 7G–K). These findings indicate that Cd suppresses glycolytic activity in the testis, leading to reduced lactate and pyruvate production. Notably, serine supplementation effectively reversed these changes, suggesting that serine alleviates Cd‐induced metabolic dysfunction by restoring glycolytic capacity.

Serine Ameliorated Cd‐Induced Testicular Fibrosis in Mice

To determine whether Cd exposure induced testicular fibrosis, the expression of fibrosis‐related proteins was assessed by Western blotting. As shown in Figure 8A,B,H, Cd exposure significantly increased the expression levels of α‐SMA, TGF‐β, Collagen I, and fibronectin compared with the control group (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). In contrast, serine supplementation markedly attenuated the Cd‐induced upregulation of these fibrotic markers. These results indicate that Cd promotes fibrotic remodeling in the testis, whereas serine exerts a protective effect by alleviating testicular fibrosis.

Serine Alleviated Cd‐Triggered Apoptosis in Mouse Testes

Testicular apoptosis was evaluated using TUNEL staining. As shown in Figure 8C, apoptotic cells were identified by green fluorescence, whereas nuclei were counterstained blue. The number of TUNEL‐positive cells was markedly increased in the testes of Cd‐exposed mice compared with the control group, indicating enhanced germ cell apoptosis. Consistent with these findings, western blot analysis revealed that Cd exposure significantly increased the expression of the pro‐apoptotic proteins Caspase‐3 and Bax (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01), while significantly decreasing the expression of the anti‐apoptotic protein Bcl‐2 (p < 0.05; Figure 8D–G). Notably, serine supplementation attenuated these alterations, as evidenced by reduced apoptotic signals and normalization of apoptosis‐related protein expression. These results suggest that Cd promotes apoptosis in testicular tissue, whereas serine exerts a protective effect against Cd‐induced apoptotic injury.

Altered PGAM1 and PHGDH Expression Was Associated With Cd‐Induced Testicular Injury and Serine‐Mediated Protection

To explore the potential involvement of PGAM1 and PHGDH in Cd‐induced testicular injury and serine‐mediated protection, the expression of proteins related to the SSP was examined. As shown in Figure 9A,B, Cd exposure was associated with increased PGAM1 expression and decreased PHGDH expression in testicular tissue. Similarly, the expression of the PHGDH‐related SSP proteins PSAT1 and PSPH was significantly reduced, accompanied by a decrease in testicular serine levels (Figure 9C–F). These findings indicate that Cd exposure is associated with suppression of the SSP and reduced serine biosynthesis.

To further evaluate the potential role of serine in this process, mice received serine supplementation throughout the experimental period. Serine administration markedly alleviated Cd‐induced testicular injury, as reflected by improvements in oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, glycolytic activity, fibrosis, and apoptosis. Importantly, these protective effects were accompanied by partial restoration of PGAM1, PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH expression, as well as recovery of serine levels. These results suggest that altered PGAM1 and PHGDH expression is associated with suppressed SSP activity and aggravated testicular injury following Cd exposure, whereas serine supplementation reverses these alterations and confers protection against Cd‐induced testicular toxicity.

Discussion

Recently, the detrimental effects of environmental pollutants on reproductive health have received increasing attention. Cd, a well‐characterized toxicant, has been shown to impair liver and kidney function in both aquatic organisms and mammals [23]. However, the mechanisms by which serine may mitigate Cd‐induced testicular toxicity remain poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the protective effects of serine supplementation on Cd‐exposed male mouse testes.

Testicular histological alterations are a key indicator of reproductive tissue injury. Consistent with prior reports, Cd exposure disrupted testicular architecture and altered serum reproductive hormone levels, largely due to damage to Leydig and SCs [23]. Dysfunction of these somatic cells impairs the negative feedback regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, resulting in disordered secretion of testosterone, FSH, and LH [24]. Beyond hormonal disturbances, Cd also perturbs testicular ion homeostasis, which is recognized as an upstream driver of oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [25]. Previous studies have shown that Cd exacerbates inflammation and promotes internalization of tight junction proteins, ultimately compromising BTB integrity [26]. The BTB, established by SCs, is composed of tight junctions, adherens junctions, and gap junctions. Tight junctions, the predominant form, consist of proteins such as Claudin and Occludin linked to the actin cytoskeleton via ZO‐1, which is critical for maintaining cell polarity and barrier function. Gap junctions are plasma membrane channels between adjacent cells, with connexin‐43 as the main protein; its expression affects BTB dynamics and spermatogenic integrity. In line with previous findings, our results demonstrate that Cd downregulates key BTB‐associated proteins and impairs barrier function, contributing substantially to testicular pathology [5, 27].

PGAM1 is a key metabolic enzyme that regulates glycolytic flux, and its aberrant activation has been reported to exacerbate tissue inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis through modulation of downstream TGF‐β signaling [28]. In this study, we observed marked upregulation of PGAM1 in Cd‐injured testes, accompanied by increased expression of fibrosis‐related proteins and enhanced germ cell apoptosis, consistent with the functional roles of PGAM1 reported in other organ injury models [16, 29]. Metabolically, PGAM1 mediates the interconversion between 2‐PG and 3‐PG and influences PHGDH activity, the rate‐limiting enzyme of the SSP [30]. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated PGAM1 impairs SSP flux, suppresses endogenous serine production, and alters the expression of glucose transporters and glycolytic rate‐limiting enzymes, thereby inhibiting glycolysis in testicular tissue [19]. GLUT1, the most widely distributed glucose transporter [31], along with GLUT3, highly expressed in energy‐demanding cells such as spermatids [32], and the recently identified testicular GLUT8 [33], have all been reported to decrease following Cd exposure [34, 35]. LDH, a pivotal glycolytic enzyme, exists as two isoforms: LDHA, which converts pyruvate to lactate, and LDHB, which catalyzes the reverse reaction [36]. Increased lactate production is a hallmark of enhanced glycolysis [37]. HK2, PFK1, and PKM2 are rate‐limiting enzymes in glycolysis [38], and upregulation of HK2 and PKM2 has been shown to promote aerobic glycolysis [39]. PFK1 functions as a major glycolytic pacemaker and exists in three isoforms: PFKM, PFKP, and PFKL, contributing to tissue‐specific glycolytic regulation. Supporting this concept, Traditional Chinese medicine, such as Qingzao Jiufei Tang, has been reported to inhibit glycolysis and cell proliferation by targeting HK2, PFK2, and PKM2 [40]. Our data indicate that Cd disrupts glycolysis in the testis by modulating the PGAM1/PHGDH axis, suppressing SSP‐related proteins, and reducing serine production, which further exacerbates testicular functional impairments.

Importantly, our intervention study preliminarily demonstrated that exogenous serine supplementation effectively mitigates Cd‐induced testicular oxidative stress, inflammation, and ion homeostasis disruption. At the molecular level, serine partially restrains aberrant PGAM1 activation, restores SSP metabolism, and reverses glycolytic inhibition, thereby preserving BTB structural integrity and alleviating testicular fibrosis and germ cell apoptosis. Zhou et al. reported that PGAM1 knockdown triggers apoptosis in spermatogenic cells via the P53/Caspase‐3/Caspase‐6/Caspase‐9 signaling axis [41]. Co‐immunoprecipitation studies further indicate that PGAM1 directly interacts with TGF‐β in vitro. Beyond its role in fibrosis, TGF‐β participates in NF‐κB pathway activation, influencing inflammation and cell death [42]. Zhao et al. demonstrated that high‐temperature stress upregulates PGAM1 expression in mouse testes and impairs glycolysis, whereas Luo et al. confirmed that glycolytic flux regulates serine synthesis [43, 44]. Notably, serine acts as an activator of PKM2 during the final step of glycolysis; under serine‐deficient conditions, PKM2 activity decreases, redirecting glycolytic intermediates toward SSP [45]. Taken together, these studies indicate that PGAM1 can interact with TGF‐β to modulate NF‐κB‐mediated inflammatory signaling, while serine can activate PKM2 to balance glycolytic and serine biosynthetic pathways. These published metabolic and molecular findings strongly support the regulatory network proposed in our study, providing a mechanistic basis for the protective effects of serine against Cd‐induced testicular toxicity.

Collectively, our findings suggest that Cd exposure disrupts serine one‐carbon metabolism and is associated with excessive oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impairment of BTB integrity in testicular tissue. As a key amino acid involved in GSH synthesis and metabolic homeostasis, serine supplementation enhances endogenous antioxidant capacity, restores the expression of PGAM1 and PHGDH, and re‐establishes the balance between glycolysis and serine biosynthesis. These metabolic improvements are accompanied by preservation of mitochondrial function and BTB integrity, ultimately alleviating Cd‐induced testicular injury and reproductive toxicity. Therefore, modulation of serine metabolism may represent a promising strategy for protecting against Cd‐associated reproductive damage.

Limitations

This study primarily evaluated the phenotypic improvements and preliminary molecular effects of serine at the tissue level in mice. Further cellular studies and targeted gene interventions are needed to clarify the direct interactions and precise regulatory mechanisms between serine and the PGAM1/PHGDH pathway. Additionally, the current research focused on testicular histopathology and hormonal profiles, without assessing key functional reproductive endpoints such as sperm quality and fertility outcomes. Long‐term metabolic disturbances and potential reproductive safety risks to offspring resulting from low‐dose Cd exposure also warrant more comprehensive and systematic investigation in future studies.

Conclusion

Cd induces male reproductive toxicity by disrupting ion homeostasis, triggering oxidative stress and inflammation, impairing BTB integrity, and altering hormonal profiles. Mechanistically, these effects are associated with dysregulated PGAM1 and PHGDH expression, suppressed serine synthesis, and impaired glycolysis, which collectively promote testicular fibrosis and germ cell apoptosis. Serine supplementation exerts protective effects against Cd‐induced testicular injury, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy to mitigate heavy metal‐associated male reproductive dysfunction.
