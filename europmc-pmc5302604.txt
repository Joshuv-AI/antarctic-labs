Whilst the injection is being made, it is necessary so to
move the patient that the lipiodol will tend to gravitate towards
the particular field under investigation. Thus if the lesion we
wish to demonstrate is in the centre or inferior part of, say>
the right lung, the patient is placed in the recumbent position
slightly inclined towards the right with the head moderately
raised. In such an attitude the fluid flows into the middle and
inferior lobes of the right lung. If, on the contrary, we wish
to investigate the left upper lobe, the patient is placed lying
slightly towards the left side and in a modified Trendelenburg
position, care being taken not to exaggerate this position lest
the lipiodol gravitate into the pharynx. The actual course
and position of the lipiodol can be ascertained by screening the
patient before actually making the photograph. If too long
an interval be allowed to elapse between making the injection
and taking the photograph, the minutiae of the bronchial
system, especially of the trachea and large bronchi, are to a
large extent lost, hence it is desirable to photograph as soon
as possible after the lipiodol has gravitated to the desired site.
In our experience between two and three minutes gives the
best results.
Complications.?In the vast majority of cases, if reasonable
precautions are taken, complications are not likely to arise.
A few mishaps, however, have been reported, and it is wise to
bear in mind that although the operation is, in itself, compart'
tively simple, accidents may occur.
As a result of faulty technique the tracheal mucosa may
be injured, thereby leading to haemoptysis of greater or less
extent. Castex and Lanari report haemoptysis following the
lipiodol injection in a case of pulmonary isyphilis. We have
seen a slight streaking of blood in the sputum immediately
