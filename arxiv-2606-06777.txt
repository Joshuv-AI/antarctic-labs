Systole, inradius and rigidity of cusped hyperbolic 3 3 -manifolds
Stéphane Sabourau

Abstract
. We establish optimal inequalities relating the systole and the inradius to the volume of finite-volume hyperbolic 3 3 -manifolds. In the cusped orientable case, we refine a theorem of Gendulphe by proving a sharp systole–volume inequality whose unique extremal manifold is the figure-eight knot complement. Excluding the figure-eight knot complement, we obtain a stronger inequality whose extremal manifold is the sister of the figure-eight knot complement. We also establish analogous optimal systole–volume inequalities for closed orientable hyperbolic 3 3 -manifolds, where the extremal manifolds are the Weeks–Matveev–Fomenko manifold, the manifold Vol ​ 3 {\rm Vol}3 , and the Meyerhoff manifold. In the second part of the article, we study the inradius. We prove optimal inradius–volume inequalities for orientable and nonorientable cusped hyperbolic 3 3 -manifolds, identifying respectively the sister of the figure-eight knot complement and the Gieseking manifold as the extremal cases. We also prove that the Gieseking manifold is the unique cusped hyperbolic 3 3 -manifold of minimal inradius, thereby completing a result of Gendulphe, who had previously established the corresponding lower bound.

1.
Introduction

The geometry of finite-volume hyperbolic
3
-manifolds
M
is strongly constrained by the interaction between volume and other geometric invariants.
In this article, we study two such invariants: the
systole

\sys(M)
, defined as the length of the shortest (noncontractible) closed geodesic, and the
inradius

{\rm R}(M)
, defined as the radius of the largest embedded ball.
Our goal is to establish optimal inequalities relating these quantities to the volume and to characterize the manifolds realizing the extremal cases.

A fundamental result in this direction, due to Gendulphe
[
21
]
, asserts that every cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
satisfies

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{13}}{4V}\vol(M),

(1.1)

where
V
is the volume of a regular ideal tetrahedron in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
, with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the Gieseking manifold.
Here, the multiplicative constant is approximately
1.13443...

He also obtained a stronger inequality outside the equality case.
Recall that the Gieseking manifold is nonorientable and realizes the smallest volume among cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.
Non-optimal systolic–volume inequalities in higher dimensions can be found in
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
.

Our first objective is to refine this result in the orientable case.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a noncompact orientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold of finite volume.
Then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{13}}{8V}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement.
The multiplicative constant is approximately
0.56721...

Furthermore, if
M
is not isometric to the figure-eight knot complement, then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{7}}{8V}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the sister of the figure-eight knot complement.
The multiplicative constant is approximately
0.53916...

Theorem
4.7
provides a better bound away from the extremal manifolds.
These inequalities improve the constants appearing in Gendulphe’s theorem and isolate the precise extremal orientable manifolds.
This result allows us to recover (
1.1
) and strengthen the main result of
[
21
]
; see Corollary
4.9
.

For cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds
M
of large volume, the systolic inequality

\sys(M)\lesssim\frac{4}{3}\log\vol(M)

proved by Lakeland and Leininger
[
26
]
provides a better bound than the previous estimates.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the geometry of maximal cusps and on the analysis of the Adams–Reid isometry associated with tangent horoballs.
This isometry is obtained by composing a parabolic translation preserving one horoball with an isometry exchanging the two tangent horoballs.
The proof then splits into two cases depending on whether the resulting isometry is loxodromic or parabolic.
The main ingredients include cusp-density estimates, lower bounds on the waist size of maximal cusps due to Adams, and geometric estimates on flat tori.
Together, these tools allow us to control the translation length of suitable loxodromic elements in terms of cusp geometry and volume.

We also investigate the analogous problem for closed orientable hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.
In this setting, the extremal manifolds turn out to be the three closed orientable hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of smallest volume: the Weeks–Matveev–Fomenko manifold, the Meyerhoff manifold, and the manifold
{\rm Vol}3
.
More precisely, we prove optimal systole–volume inequalities characterizing these manifolds.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be a closed orientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold.
Then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq C_{1}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the Weeks–Matveev–Fomenko manifold.

Furthermore, if
M
is not isometric to the Weeks–Matveev–Fomenko manifold, then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq C_{2}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to
{\rm Vol}3
.

Moreover, if
M
is isometric neither to the Weeks–Matveev–Fomenko manifold nor to
{\rm Vol}3
, then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq C_{3}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the Meyerhoff manifold.

Note that the extremal manifolds do not appear according to increasing volume.
Approximate values for the optimal multiplicative constants
C_{i}
can be found in Theorem
3.2
, together with sharper estimates outside the equality cases.

The proof combines volume estimates for embedded balls and tubes with the classification of small-volume hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of Gabai, Haraway, Meyerhoff, Thurston and Yarmola
[
17
]
.

The existence of closed or cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds with arbitrarily small systole follows from Thurston’s hyperbolic Dehn surgery; see
[
29
, §5.8]
.
In dimension
2
, the analogous statement is immediate.
In dimension
4
, a similar result was proved by Agol
[
6
]
using an inbreeding construction, and was later extended in higher dimensions by Belolipetsky and Thomson
[
9
]
; see also
[
10
, last remark]
.

In the second part of the article, we turn to the inradius.
We first establish optimal inequalities relating the inradius to the volume for both orientable and nonorientable cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.
In the orientable case, we prove the following optimal inradius–volume inequality.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
be a noncompact orientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold of finite volume.
Then

\cosh{\rm R}(M)\leq C\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the sister of the figure-eight knot complement.

Furthermore, if
M
is not isometric to the sister of the figure-eight knot complement, then

\cosh{\rm R}(M)\leq\frac{\sqrt{22}}{8V}\vol(M).

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement.

An approximate value for the optimal multiplicative constant
C
, together with sharper estimates outside the equality cases, are given in Theorem
5.5
.

A similar sharp inradius–volume inequality holds in the nonorientable case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
M
be a noncompact nonorientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold of finite volume.
Then

\cosh{\rm R}(M)\leq\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2V}\vol(M)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the Gieseking manifold.

A stronger inequality outside the equality cases is established in Theorem
5.7
.

The proofs of these results rely on explicit geometric decompositions of the figure-eight knot complement and its sister into regular ideal tetrahedra, together with a detailed analysis of orbit distances in the universal cover allowing us to identify the extremal configurations.
We also use rigidity results for cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of small volume.

We also investigate the opposite problem, namely the existence of a universal lower bound on the inradius of cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.
Gendulphe
[
21
]
proved that every cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
satisfies

\cosh{\rm R}(M)\geq\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}

with equality if
M
is isometric to the Gieseking manifold.
He left open the characterization of the equality case; see
[
21
, Remarque 3.2]
.
We revisit this inequality and prove a rigidity result for the cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold of minimal inradius; see Theorem
1.6
.

A central role in our analysis is played by the waist size of maximal cusps.
The waist size
w(C)
of a maximal cusp
C
in a cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
is defined as the systole of the flat torus or Klein bottle corresponding to the cusp boundary
\partial C
; see Definition
2.5
.
Adams
[
3
]
proved that every maximal cusp
C
in an orientable hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
satisfies

w(C)\geq 1

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement.

We extend this rigidity theorem to orientable cusps in nonorientable hyperbolic
3
-manifold.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
M
be a noncompact complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold of finite volume, possibly nonorientable.
Let
C
be an orientable maximal cusp of
M
.
Then

w(C)\geq 1

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement.

Using these waist-size rigidity results, we prove the following characterization theorem.

Theorem 1.6
.

Every cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
satisfies

\cosh{\rm R}(M)\geq\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the Gieseking manifold.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
proceeds through a detailed analysis of tangent horoball configurations associated with maximal cusps.
We first show that equality forces the existence of additional tangencies between certain horoballs in the universal cover under the action of parabolic isometries.
This imposes strong restrictions on the cusp geometry and, in particular, forces the existence of maximal cusps of minimal waist size.
The argument then splits into three cases according to the orientability of the cusp and of the ambient manifold.
When the manifold is orientable, Adams’ theorem implies that the manifold must be the figure-eight knot complement, for which we explicitely compute the inradius.
When the cusp is orientable but the manifold is nonorientable, our extension of Adams’ waist-size rigidity theorem yields the same conclusion.
Finally, when the cusp itself is nonorientable, we analyze the geometry of flat Klein bottle cusp sections and show that equality forces the manifold to be isometric to the Gieseking manifold.
Combining these three cases yields the sharp lower bound together with the characterization of the equality case.

The minimum and maximum values of the inradius on the moduli space of hyperbolic surfaces have been determined; see
[
30
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
15
]
.

We refer to
[
21
]
for a more comprehensive treatment of systolic and inradius estimates for hyperbolic manifolds with an extensive bibliography.

2.
Preliminaries

In this article, we identify the hyperbolic space
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
with Poincaré’s upper half-space model

{\mathbb{H}}^{3}=\{(x,y,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\mid t>0\}

equipped with the hyperbolic metric

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dt^{2}}{t^{2}}.

The
t
-coordinate will be referred to as the height.
We will also identify
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
by writing
(x,y,t)=(z,t)
where
z=x+iy
.
The sphere at infinity is the Riemann sphere

\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}=\hat{{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}.

Geodesics in this model are vertical lines and Euclidean semicircles orthogonal to the horizontal plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\{t=0\}
.
Horoballs are the regions above the horizontal planes
\{t=t_{0}\}
with
t_{0}>0
or the Euclidean balls tangent to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

An ideal regular tetrahedron in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
is a hyperbolic tetrahedron whose four vertices lie in
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
which admits
S_{4}
as its symmetry group.
It is unique up to isometry and can be identified with the standard ideal tetrahedron with vertices
0
,
1
,
e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}
,
\infty
.
It is the unique ideal tetrahedron of maximal volume in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
, up to isometry.
Its volume
V
is approximately equal to
V=1.01494...

Its dihedral angles are equal to
\frac{\pi}{3}
.

2.1.
Möbius group and isometry group

The group of Möbius transformations
{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})={\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})/\{\pm\textrm{Id}\}
acts on
\hat{{\mathbb{C}}}
by homographies:

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

This action extends uniquely to an isometric action on
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
via Poincaré’s extension formula

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot(z,t)=\left(\frac{(az+b)(\overline{cz+d})+a\bar{c}t^{2}}{|cz+d|^{2}+|c|^{2}t^{2}},\frac{t}{|cz+d|^{2}+|c|^{2}t^{2}}\right).

(2.1)

The resulting map is an orientation-preserving isometry of
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
, and every such isometry arises in this way.
Hence,

\Isom^{+}({\mathbb{H}}^{3})\simeq{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}).

The full isometry group is obtained by adjoining complex conjugation.
Hence, it identifies with the semidirect product

\Isom({\mathbb{H}}^{3})\simeq{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}.

Here, the generator of
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
acts on
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
by an orientation-reversing isometry sending
(z,t)
to
(\bar{z},t)
.

Nontrivial isometries of
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
are classified according to their fixed points in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}\cup\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
:

•

elliptic elements fix a point in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
;

•

parabolic elements have exactly one fixed point on
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
and no fixed point in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
;

•

loxodromic elements have exactly two fixed points on
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
and no fixed point in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.

A loxodromic isometry preserves the geodesic of
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
joining its two fixed points at infinity, called its axis.
Along this axis, it acts as a translation by a positive distance called its translation length.

2.2.
Hyperbolic distance

In the upper half-space model, the hyperbolic metric
d
and the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
are related by the following Euclidean–hyperbolic distance formulas; see
[
28
, Theorem 4.6.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
p,p^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
with
p=(z,t)
and
p^{\prime}=(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
,

d(p,p^{\prime})=\arcosh\left(1+\frac{|p-p^{\prime}|^{2}}{2tt^{\prime}}\right).

(2.2)

In particular, if
p
and
p^{\prime}
lie in the same vertical line, then

d(p,p^{\prime})=\left|\log\left(\frac{t^{\prime}}{t}\right)\right|.

(2.3)

If
p
and
p^{\prime}
lie in the same horizontal plane of height
t
, then

d(p,p^{\prime})=2\arsinh\left(\frac{|z-z^{\prime}|}{2t}\right).

(2.4)

We will also need the following classical distance–height estimate.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two horoballs with disjoint interiors in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
, centered at
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with heights
h_{1},h_{2}>0
.
Then

|z_{1}-z_{2}|\geq\sqrt{h_{1}h_{2}}.

(2.5)

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are tangent.

Proof.

The horoball
H_{i}
centered at
z_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
of height
h_{i}
is a Euclidean ball of radius
\frac{h_{i}}{2}
centered at
(z_{i},\frac{h_{i}}{2})
.
The Euclidean distance between the center of the two balls is

\sqrt{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+\left(\frac{h_{1}-h_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}}.

Since the interiors of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are disjoint, this distance is at least the sum of the radii.
That is,

\sqrt{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+\left(\frac{h_{1}-h_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}}\geq\frac{h_{1}+h_{2}}{2}.

Squaring both sides, expanding and rearranging the terms, we obtain

|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}\geq h_{1}h_{2}.

∎

2.3.
Hyperbolic
3
-manifolds

A complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold is a quotient

M={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma

by a discrete torsion-free subgroup
\Gamma\leqslant\Isom({\mathbb{H}}^{3})
acting freely on
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.
By the Mostow–Prasad rigidity theorem, if
M
has finite volume, then the subgroup
\Gamma
is unique up to conjugation.

Closed (oriented) geodesics of
M
correspond to conjugacy classes of loxodromic elements of
\Gamma
.
More precisely, if
\gamma\in\Gamma
is loxodromic, its invariant axis in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
projects to a closed geodesic in
M
whose length equals the translation length (or minimal displacement)

\ell_{\gamma}=\inf_{p\in{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}d(p,\gamma.p)

which is attained on the axis of
\gamma
.

The translation length of a loxodromic element can be expressed in terms of the trace; see
[
21
, Lemma 5.1]
.
(Recall that the trace of an element of
{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is defined only up to sign.)

Lemma 2.3
.

The translation distance
\ell_{\gamma}
of a loxodromic isometry
\gamma\in{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
is given by the trace formula

2\cosh\left(\frac{\ell_{\gamma}}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\left|\frac{1}{2}\tr(\gamma)-1\right|+\left|\frac{1}{2}\tr(\gamma)+1\right|&\text{ if }\gamma\text{ is orientation-preserving,}\\
\sqrt{\tr(\gamma^{2})+2}&\text{ if }\gamma\text{ is orientation-reversing.}\end{cases}

(2.6)

2.4.
Cusps of noncompact hyperbolic
3
-manifolds

A complete hyperbolic
3
-manifolds
M={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma
of finite volume is noncompact if and only if
\Gamma
has parabolic elements (and not just loxodromic elements).
In this case, there are finitely many conjugacy classes of parabolic stabilizers

\Gamma_{\xi}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma.\xi=\xi\}

where
\xi\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
is the fixed point of a parabolic element of
\Gamma
.
These conjugacy classes correspond to the ends of
M
, which are represented geometrically by cusps.

Geometrically, a cusp
C
of
M
is obtained as the quotient of a horoball
H_{\xi}
centered at a parabolic fixed point
\xi
by its stabilizer:

C=H_{\xi}/\Gamma_{\xi}.

Up to conjugation in
\Isom({\mathbb{H}}^{3})
, we may assume that
\xi=\infty
.
Then the parabolic subgroup
\Gamma_{\xi}
acts cocompactly by Euclidean isometries on the horosphere
\partial H_{\xi}=\{t=t_{0}\}
with the induced Euclidean metric.
The boundary
\partial C
of the cusp, which coincides with the quotient
\partial H_{\xi}/\Gamma_{\xi}
, is therefore either a flat torus or a flat Klein bottle.

The cusp
C
is isometric to the warped product
\partial C\times[t_{0},\infty)
with the metric
\frac{1}{t^{2}}(g_{\rm eucl}+dt^{2})
.
In particular, we have the following area–volume relation for cusps

\vol(C)=\frac{1}{2}\area(\partial C).

(2.7)

Using Böröczky’s horoball packing density theorem
[
11
]
, the following cusp density estimate was obtained in
[
27
]
for a single cusp and extended to multiple cusps in
[
2
]
.
As customary, when counting cusps, we count the number of disjoint cusps, that is, the number of ends.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
be
m
cusps of
M
with pairwise disjoint interiors.
Then

\vol(M)\geq\frac{2V}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\vol(C_{1})+\cdots+\vol(C_{m})\right)

(2.8)

where
V
is the volume of an ideal regular tetrahedron in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.

In the universal cover, the cusps of
M
correspond to
\Gamma
-orbits of horoballs centered at parabolic fixed points.
Maximal cusps correspond to horoball systems whose interiors are pairwise disjoint but such that some of them are tangent.

In particular, a maximal cusp
C
is self-tangent.
In this case, we denote by
\partial C
the quotient
\partial H_{\xi}/\Gamma_{\xi}
of a horoball
H_{\xi}
covering
C
.
With this convention, maximal cusp boundary
\partial C
is a flat torus or a Klein bottle without self-intersection.

2.5.
Waist size of maximal cusps

Let
M={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma
be a noncompact complete hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of finite volume.
Let
C
be a maximal cusp in
M
.
By maximality, there exist two tangent horoballs in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
covering
C
.
After a possible conjugation in
\Isom({\mathbb{H}}^{3})
, we will generally assume that these two horoballs coincide with

H_{\infty}=\{t\geq 1\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad H_{0}=\{x^{2}+y^{2}+(t-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\}

which are centered at
\infty
and
0
, and are tangent at
p_{0}=(0,0,1)
.

The notion of waist size was introduced in
[
3
]
for orientable cusps.

Definition 2.5
.

The
waist size
of a maximal cusp
C
, denoted by
w(C)
, is defined as the systole of the flat torus or Klein bottle corresponding to the cusp boundary
\partial C
.
That is,

w(C)=\sys(\partial C)=\min_{p\in\partial H}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{H}\setminus\{\id\}}d_{\partial H}(p,\gamma.p)

where
H
is any horoball covering
C
and
\Gamma_{H}\leqslant\Gamma
is the (parabolic) stabilizer of
H
in
\Gamma
.

If
\partial C
is a flat torus, it is enough to take

w(C)=\sys(\partial C)=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{H}\setminus\{\id\}}d_{\partial H}(p,\gamma.p)

where
p
is any point in
\partial H
.

The waist size of
C
provides a lower bound on the volume of the cusp.
Indeed, Minkowski’s inequality asserts that

\area({\mathbb{T}}^{2})\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}{\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}^{2}

(2.9)

with equality if and only if
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
is a hexagonal torus.
Similarly, for every flat Klein bottle
K

\area(K)\geq{\sys(K)}^{2}

(2.10)

with equality if and only if
K
is a square Klein bottle.
Combining these inequalities with (
2.7
) yields the waist–volume estimates

\vol(C)=\frac{1}{2}\area(\partial C)\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}w(C)^{2}\quad\mbox{ if }C\mbox{ is orientable}

(2.11)

and

\vol(C)=\frac{1}{2}\area(\partial C)\geq\frac{1}{2}w(C)^{2}\quad\mbox{ if }C\mbox{ is nonorientable}.

(2.12)

Adams proved that the waist size of a maximal cusp admits a uniform lower bound and characterized the inequality case; see
[
3
]
for the first estimate and
[
4
]
for the second estimate.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
M
be a noncompact complete hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of finite volume.
Let
C
be a maximal cusp of
M
.
Then

w(C)\geq 1

(2.13)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement (and
C
is its unique maximal cusp).

Furthermore, if
M
is not isometric to the figure-eight knot complement, then

w(C)\geq\sqrt[4]{2}

(2.14)

with equality if and only if
M
is isometric to either the
5_{2}
knot complement or the manifold obtained by
(2,1)
surgery on the Whitehead link.

The argument giving the waist size lower bound (
2.13
) also applies to a collection
(C_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
of cusps where each cusp
C_{i}
is maximal in
M\setminus(\cup_{j\neq i}C_{j})
.
In this case, either
C_{i}
is self-tangent (that is,
C_{i}
is a maximal cusp) or
C_{i}
is tangent to
\cup_{j\neq i}C_{j}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
M
be a noncompact complete hyperbolic
3
-manifolds of finite volume.
Let
(C_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
be a collection of cusps where each cusp
C_{i}
is maximal in
M\setminus(\cup_{j\neq i}C_{j})
for every
1\leq i\leq m
.
Then,

w(C_{i})\geq 1.

Proof.

By maximality, for every
1\leq i\leq m
, there exists a horoball
H_{i}
covering
C_{i}
which is tangent to a horoball
H_{j}
covering
C_{j}
for some
j=1,\dots,m
(possibly with
j=i
).
After a possible conjugation in
\Isom({\mathbb{H}}^{3})
, we can assume that
H_{i}
coincides with the horoball
H_{\infty}
centered at
\infty
of height
1
.
In this case, the horoball
H_{j}
, which is tangent to
H_{i}=H_{\infty}
at some point
p\in\partial H_{\infty}
, is also of height
1

Now, every parabolic isometry

4.
Systole of noncompact orientable hyperbolic
3
-manifolds

In this section, we establish an optimal systole–volume estimate for
orientable
cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds, which extends Gendulphe’s systolic inequality.

4.1.
The Adams–Reid isometry

Let
M={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma
be a noncompact orientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold.
Fix a maximal cusp
C
in
M
.
By maximality, there exist two tangent horoballs in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
covering
C
.
After a possible conjugation in
\Isom(M)
, we can assume that these two horoballs coincide with the horoballs
H_{\infty}
and
H_{0}
centered at
\infty
and
0
that are tangent at
p_{0}=(0,0,1)
.

By construction, there exists an isometry
\gamma\in\Gamma
which sends
H_{0}
to
H_{\infty}
.
Observe that
\gamma
does not lie in the parabolic stabilizer
\Gamma_{\infty}\leqslant\Gamma
of
H_{\infty}
,
i.e.
,
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{\infty}
.
The isometry
\gamma
takes
H_{\infty}
to a horoball
H_{\omega}
centered at some point
\omega\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.
Note that
\omega
is different from
0
and
\infty
.
The horoball
H_{\omega}
is tangent to
H_{\infty}
at
\gamma.p_{0}
.
Thus, both
p_{0}
and
\gamma.p_{0}
lie in the horizontal Euclidean plane
\partial H_{\infty}
of height
1
.

As observed in
[
3
]
, the horoballs
H_{0}
and
H_{\omega}
lie in different
\Gamma_{\infty}
-orbit.
Otherwise, there would exist
\tau\in\Gamma_{\infty}
such that
\tau.\omega=0
.
The product
\gamma\tau
would then switch
H_{0}
and
H_{\omega}
, and thus would fix their tangent point, which is impossible for a nontrivial element of
\Gamma
.
Thus, the points
p_{0}
and
\gamma.p_{0}
project to two different points in the maximal cusp boundary
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\partial C=\partial H_{\infty}/\Gamma_{\infty}
.

Since the horoballs
H_{0}
and
H_{\omega}
have disjoint interiors and are of Euclidean diameter
1
, it follows from the previous observation that the flat torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
contains two disjoint open disks of radius
\frac{1}{2}
centered at the projections of
p_{0}
and
\gamma.p_{0}
.
In particular, we have

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(\gamma.p_{0},p_{0})\geq 1.

Replacing
\gamma
with
\tau\gamma
for some
\tau\in\Gamma_{\infty}
if necessary, we can further assume that
\gamma.p_{0}
is closer to
p_{0}
than to any other point of
\Gamma_{\infty}.p_{0}
for the Euclidean metric
d_{\partial H_{\infty}}
on
\partial H_{\infty}
(and also for the hyperbolic metric
d
on
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
).
That is,

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(\gamma.p_{0},p_{0})=d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(\gamma.p_{0},\Gamma_{\infty}.p_{0}).

This implies that

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(\gamma.p_{0},p_{0})=d_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\pi(\gamma.p_{0}),\pi(p_{0}))\leq\diam({\mathbb{T}}^{2})

(4.1)

where
\pi:{\mathbb{H}}^{3}\to M
is the canonical projection and
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\partial C=\partial H_{\infty}/\Gamma_{\infty}
is the maximal cusp boundary of
C
.

We will refer to this isometry
\gamma
as the
Adams–Reid isometry
of
\Gamma
, since it was first introduced in
[
5
]
to bound the systole of cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.

4.2.
Systole, diameter and area of a flat torus

We will need the following straightforward result about a flat torus.

Lemma 4.1
.

The systole, the diameter and the area of a flat torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
satisfy the following inequality

{\diam({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\,{\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\area({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}{\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}\right)^{2}

Proof.

Every flat torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
can be written as a quotient
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
, where
\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}u+{\mathbb{Z}}v
is a lattice of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
u=(s,0)
,
v=(t,h)
and
s=\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
Every lattice point has the form
(ms+nt,nh)
, where
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Take any point
(a,b)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Choose
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
so that
|b-nh|\leq\frac{|h|}{2}
.
Then choose
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
so that
|a-(ms+nt)|\leq\frac{s}{2}
.
Thus, for every
(a,b)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, there exists a lattice point at distance at most

\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{s^{2}+h^{2}}

from
(a,b)
.
Note that
A=sh
, where
A=\area({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
Since the flat metric on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
is invariant by translations, this shows that

\diam({\mathbb{T}}^{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{s^{2}+\left(\frac{A}{s}\right)^{2}}.

∎

There are

,

,

,

In the loxodromic case, we have the following estimate.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
M
is different from the figure-eight knot complement and its sister.
Suppose that the Adams–Reid isometry
\gamma
is loxodromic.
Then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq 0.44647\,\vol(M).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, we can assume that
\vol(M)\geq 3.07
.

Since
\gamma
is loxodromic, we derive from the formula (
2.2
) and the inequality (

) that

\cosh(\sys(M))\leq\cosh(d(p_{0},\gamma.p_{0}))=1+\frac{1}{2}\,d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p_{0},\gamma.p_{0})^{2}\leq 1+\frac{D^{2}}{2}

where
D=\diam({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we have

D^{2}\leq\frac{s^{2}}{4}+\frac{A^{2}}{4s^{2}}

where
s=\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
and
A=\area({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
Therefore,

\cosh(\sys(M))\leq 1+\frac{s^{2}}{8}+\frac{A^{2}}{8s^{2}}.

By the second waist size inequality (
2.14
) – assuming that
M
is different from the figure-eight knot complement and its sister – and Minkowski’s inequality (
2.9
), we obtain

\sqrt[4]{2}\leq s\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt[4]{3}}\,\sqrt{A}.

On this interval, the function
f
defined by
f(s)=1+\frac{s^{2}}{8}+\frac{A^{2}}{8s^{2}}
is first decreasing, then increasing.
It follows that

\cosh(\sys(M))\leq\max\left\{f(\sqrt[4]{2}),f\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt[4]{3}}\,\sqrt{A}\right)\right\}.

We need to consider the following two cases.

Suppose that
\cosh(\sys(M))
is bounded by
f(\sqrt[4]{2})
.
By the area–volume cusp relation (
2.7
), we obtain

\cosh(\sys(M))\leq 1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}+\frac{A^{2}}{8\sqrt{2}}\leq 1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}+\frac{3}{8\sqrt{2}\,V^{2}}\,\vol(M)^{2}.

Using the relation
\cosh(2t)=2\cosh(t)^{2}-1
, we derive

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{16}+\frac{3}{16\sqrt{2}V^{2}}\vol(M)^{2}}\leq 0.44647\,\vol(M)

where the last inequality holds for
\vol(M)\geq 3.07
.

Suppose that
\cosh(\sys(M))
is bounded by

.
As previously, by the area–volume cusp relation (
2.7
), we obtain

\cosh(\sys(M))\leq 1+\frac{7}{16\sqrt{3}}\,A\leq 1+\frac{7}{16V}\,\vol(M).

Thus,

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{7}{32V}\vol(M)}\leq 0.41989\,\vol(M)

where the last inequality holds for
\vol(M)\geq 3.07
.
∎

4.4.
Parabolic case

For the rest of this section, we will assume that the Adams–Reid isometry
\gamma
is parabolic.
In this case, the isometry
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{\infty}
satisfies
\gamma.0=\infty
,
\gamma.\infty=\omega
and
\tr(\gamma)=\pm 2
.
Therefore, it can be identified with the following matrix of
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}2&-\frac{\omega}{2}\\
\frac{2}{\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}.

By Poincaré’s extension formula (
2.1
), the isometry
\gamma
sends
p_{0}=(0,0,1)
to

\gamma.p_{0}=\left(\omega,\left|\tfrac{\omega}{2}\right|^{2}\right)\in\partial H_{\infty}.

This implies that
|\omega|=2
.
Observe also that
\frac{\omega}{2}
is the fixed point at infinity of
\gamma
.

Consider a (nontrivial) parabolic isometry
\sigma\in\Gamma_{\infty}
of minimal translation distance on
\partial H_{\infty}
.
That is,

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p,\sigma.p)=\min_{\tau\in\Gamma_{\infty}\setminus\{\id\}}d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p,\tau.p)

for any
p\in\partial H_{\infty}
.
Note that the translation distance
d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p,\sigma.p)
does not depend on the point
p\in\partial H_{\infty}
.
This means that the parabolic isometry
\sigma
realizes the waist size of
C
(
i.e.
, the systole of the flat torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\partial C=\partial H_{\infty}/\Gamma_{\infty}
).
That is,

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p,\sigma.p)=\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2}).

(4.2)

The parabolic isometry
\sigma\in\Gamma
satisfies
\sigma.\infty=\infty
,
\tr(\sigma)=\pm 2
and
d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(x,\sigma.x)=s
, where
s=\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
Therefore, it can be identified with the following matrix of
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&\xi\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that the products

\sigma^{\pm 1}\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}2\pm 2\frac{\xi}{\omega}&-\frac{\omega}{2}\\
\frac{2}{\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}

cannot both be parabolic.

We consider the following dichotomy: both products are loxodromic or one of them is parabolic.

In the first case, we have

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
\gamma
is parabolic and one the products
\sigma\gamma
or
\sigma^{-1}\gamma
is also parabolic.
Then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{3}{8V}\vol(M).

The multiplicative constant is approximately
0.36947...

Proof.

Replacing
\sigma
with its inverse if necessary, we can assume that
\sigma\gamma
is parabolic.
In this case,

\tr(\sigma\gamma)=2+2\,\frac{\xi}{\omega}=-2.

That is,
\xi=-2\omega
and
s=|\xi|=4
.

It follows that

\sigma^{-1}\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}6&-\frac{\omega}{2}\\
\frac{2}{\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}

has trace
6
.
By the trace formula (
2.6
), we derive

\cosh\left(\frac{\ell_{\sigma^{-1}\gamma}}{2}\right)\leq 3.

Since
\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})=s=4
, we deduce from the cusp density estimate (
2.8
) and the waist–volume inequality (
2.11
) that

\vol(M)\geq\frac{V}{2}s^{2}=8V.

Hence,

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{3}{8V}\vol(M).

∎

In the second case, we have

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that
\gamma
is parabolic and both products
\sigma\gamma
and
\sigma^{-1}\gamma
are loxodromic.
Then

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq 0.52086\,\vol(M).

Proof.

Recall that

\sigma^{\pm 1}\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}2\pm 2\frac{\xi}{\omega}&-\frac{\omega}{2}\\
\frac{2}{\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}.

where
|\omega|=2
.
Since both
\sigma\gamma
and
\sigma^{-1}\gamma
are loxodromic, we obtain from the trace formula (
2.6
) that

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\cosh\left(\frac{\ell_{\sigma^{\pm 1}\gamma}}{2}\right)=\frac{s}{4}+\left|1\pm\frac{\xi}{2\omega}\right|

where
s=|\xi|=\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.

Applying the inequality

\min|a\pm b|\leq\sqrt{|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}}

which can be derived by measuring the distance from
0
to
a\pm b
in the triangle

with an acute angle at

, we deduce that

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{s}{4}+\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{s}{4}\right)^{2}}.

(4.3)

Now, recall that the isometries
\gamma
and
\sigma
are defined from the horoballs covering a maximal cusp
C
of
M
, where the tangency points between the horoballs correspond to the self-tangency points of

.
We will consider the following three cases.

Case 1.

Suppose that
M
has a single cusp.
We follow the argument of
[

.
As previously observed, the point
\frac{\omega}{2}
is the (only) fixed point of the parabolic isometry of
\gamma
.
This means that there is a horoball
H_{\omega/2}
centered at

By uniqueness of the cusp, this maximal cusp is necessarily

.
Now, since
H_{\omega/2}
and
H_{\infty}
have disjoint interiors, the height of
H_{\omega/2}
is at most
1
.
Thus, the horoballs

of height

and

, contains

.
Therefore, the translation distance of
\gamma
on
\partial H_{\omega/2}
is bounded by that on
\partial H^{+}_{\omega/2}

By definition, the parabolic isometry
\gamma
takes the tangent point

and
H^{+}_{\omega/2}
to the tangent point

of

and

.

(and the horoballs

By Lemma

Combined with (

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2}{V}\vol(M)-1}+\sqrt{\frac{15}{16}+\frac{1}{8V}\vol(M)}.

(4.4)

By
[
7
]
, the volume of an orientable complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold with two cusps is at least four times Catalan’s constant.
(Recall that Catalan’s constant
\beta(2)
, where
\beta
is the Dirichlet beta function, is the alternating sum of the reciprocals of the odd square numbers.)
Thus,

\vol(M)\geq 4\beta(2)=3.66386...

Combined with (
4.4
), we derive

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq 0.49182\,\vol(M).

Case 3.

Suppose that

has at least three cusps.
First, choose a cusp
C^{\prime}
which is maximal in

, then choose another cusp
C^{\prime\prime}
which is maximal in

.
By Lemma

and the waist–volume inequality (

), the volume of

is at least
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
.
As previously, it follows from the cusp density estimate (

) that

\vol(M)\geq\frac{2V}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\vol(C)+\vol(C^{\prime})+\vol(C^{\prime\prime})\right)\geq\frac{2V}{\sqrt{3}}\vol(C)+V.

Therefore,

s^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\area(\partial C)\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}\vol(C)\leq\frac{2}{V}\vol(M)-2.

Combined with (
4.3
), this yields

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2}{V}\vol(M)-2}+\sqrt{\frac{7}{8}+\frac{1}{8V}\vol(M)}.

Now, by Lemma
4.2
, we can assume that
\vol(M)\geq 3.07
.
In this case, we derive

\cosh\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sys(M)\right)\leq 0.52086\,\vol(M).

∎

Conclusion

Combining Lemma
4.2
, Proposition

Let

be a noncompact orientable complete hyperbolic

5.1.
Injectivity radius, inradius and orbit distances

Recall that the
inradius

{\rm R}(M)
of a complete Riemannian manifold
M
without conjugate points is defined as the radius of the largest embedded ball in
M
.
That is,

{\rm R}(M)=\sup_{x\in M}\inj(x)

where
\inj(x)
is the injectivity radius of
M
at
x
.

Let us show the following well-known characterization of the injectivity radius and inradius in terms of orbit distances.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold without conjugate points.
Denote by
\Gamma
the deck transformation group of
M
acting on the universal cover
\bar{M}
.
For every
x\in M
and every
p\in\bar{M}
projecting to
x
, we have

\inj(x)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p).

In particular,

{\rm R}(M)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{p\in\bar{M}}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p).

Proof.

Denote by
\pi:\bar{M}\to M
the canonical projection.

Let
r>\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p)
.
Then there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\id\}
with
d(p,\gamma.p)<2r
.
Hence the balls
B(p,r)
and
B(\gamma.p,r)
intersect.
Choose
q
in the intersection.
By construction,
q
and
\gamma^{-1}.q
both lie in
B(p,r)
and have the same image under
\pi
.
Thus,
\pi
is not injective on
B(p,r)
.
Therefore,
\inj(x)\leq r
.
Hence,

\inj(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p).

Let
r<\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p)
.
Then for every
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\id\}
, the balls
B(p,r)
and
B(\gamma.p,r)
are disjoint.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in B(p,r)
with
\pi(q_{1})=\pi(q_{2})
.
Then there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
q_{2}=\gamma.q_{1}
.
Since
q_{1}\in B(p,r)
, we have
q_{2}\in B(\gamma.p,r)
.
Thus,
q_{2}\in B(p,r)\cap B(\gamma.p,r)
.
This forces
\gamma=\id
and hence
q_{2}=q_{1}
.
Therefore
\pi
is injective on
B(p,r)
.

Since
M
has no conjugate points, the injectivity radius at
x
coincides with the largest radius of a ball centered at
p
on which the covering map is injective.
Thus,
\inj(x)\geq r
.
Hence,

\inj(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}d(p,\gamma.p).

∎

6.
Inradius rigidity

In this section, we characterize cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifolds with minimal inradius.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let

\cosh({\rm R}(M))\geq\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}

The proof of this rigidity result is carried out in several steps.
We begin by characterizing manifolds of minimal inradius in terms of horoball tangencies; see Theorem

Next, we extend Adams’ waist size rigidity theorem to orientable maximal cusps in nonorientable manifolds; see Theorem

We then address the case of nonorientable cusps; see Theorem

We conclude the section with several elementary estimates regarding the inradius of flat Klein bottles and hyperbolic cusps that are used throughout the proof.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let

be a noncompact complete hyperbolic

-manifold of finite volume.

Moreover, if equality holds, then there exists a parabolic isometry

where

and

Proof.

The
\Gamma

is formed of tangency points between horoballs covering

.
The height of these points is either

, when they lie in

The tangency points between

and two full-size horoballs are at Euclidean distance at least

on
\partial H_{\infty}
.
In particular, the minimal distance between
p_{0}
and the points of
\Gamma.p_{0}

H_{\infty}
and a full-size horoball tangent to

By the hyperbolic–Euclidean formula (

), this minimal distance equals

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\Gamma.p_{0}\cap H_{\infty}\\
p\neq p_{0}\end{subarray}}d(p_{0},p)=\arcosh\left(\frac{3}{2}\right).

(6.5)

Similarly, the minimal distance between
p_{0}
and the points of
\Gamma.p_{0}

of its

∎

For the sake of completeness, we prove the following straightforward result.

Lemma 6.17
.

The distance between the center of a regular ideal octahedron
\mathcal{O}
of
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
and its faces is equal to

\arcosh\left(\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\right).

Proof.

In
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
, we can realize
\mathcal{O}
as the ideal octahedron with vertices
0
,
\infty
,
\pm 1
,
\pm i
.
By symmetry, its center is the point
p_{0}=(0,0,1)
.
The nearest-point projection of
p_{0}
to the face with ideal vertices
1
,
i
,
\infty
lies in the vertical geodesic given by
q_{t}=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},t)
.
By the Euclidean–hyperbolic distance formula (
2.2
), we obtain

d(p_{0},q_{t})=\arcosh\left(\frac{t^{2}+\frac{3}{2}}{2t}\right).

This distance is minimal for
t=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}
, and the corresponding minimal distance is
\arcosh\left(\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\right)
.
∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
6.10
.
∎

Putting all these results together, we now proceed to the proof Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Let
M
be a noncompact, nonorientable, complete hyperbolic
3
-manifold of finite volume.
By Theorem
6.2
, we may assume that
M
satisfies the equality case in the inradius lower bound.
Namely,

\cosh({\rm R}(M))=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Fix a maximal cusp
C
of
M
.

Suppose first that
C
is orientable.
As noted in Remark
6.3
, its waist size is equal to
1
.
By the equality case in Theorem
6.4
, it follows that
M
is isometric to the figure-eight knot complement.
In this situation, Proposition
5.2
yields

\cosh({\rm R}(M))=\frac{\sqrt{22}}{4}

which contradicts the previous assumption on the inradius of
M
.

Therefore, we conclude that
C
is nonorientable.
In this case, Theorem
6.10
implies that
M
is isometric to the Gieseking manifold.
∎

6.4.
Inradius of Klein bottles and cusps

The maximal radius of a Euclidean open disk embedded in a flat torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
is equal to
\frac{1}{2}\sys({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.
We extend this result to flat Klein bottles as follows.

Every flat Klein bottle is isometric to the quotient
K(a,b)
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by the group
\Gamma_{a,b}=\langle u,v\rangle
generated by

\displaystyle u:(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x+a,-y)

\displaystyle v:(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x,y+b)

where
a>0
and
b>0
.
The parameters
(a,b)
are uniquely defined.

Proposition 6.18
.

The Klein bottle
K(a,b)
contains an embedded Euclidean open disk of radius
r
if and only if

a\geq r,\quad\frac{b}{2}\geq r,\quad a^{2}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2}\geq 4r^{2}.

Thus, the maximal radius of a Euclidean open disk embedded in
K(a,b)
is

\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{2a,b,\sqrt{a^{2}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2}}\right\}.

Proof.

The Klein bottle
K(a,b)
contains an embedded Euclidean open disk of radius
r
if and only if there exists
p=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{a,b}\setminus\{\id\}}d(p,\gamma(p))\geq 2r.

The orientation-preserving transformations of
\Gamma_{a,b}
are of the form
u^{2n}v^{m}
with
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is,

u^{2n}v^{m}:(x,y)\mapsto(x+2na,y+mb).

Thus,

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{+}_{a,b}\setminus\{\id\}}d(p,\gamma(p))=\min\{2a,b\}

(6.12)

where
\Gamma^{+}_{a,b}\leqslant\Gamma_{a,b}
is the subgroup of orientation-preserving transformations.

The orientation-reversing transformations of
\Gamma_{a,b}
are of the form
\gamma=u^{2n+1}v^{m}
with
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is,

u^{2n+1}v^{m}:(x,y)\mapsto(x+(2n+1)a,-y+mb).

Thus,

d(p,\gamma(p))=\sqrt{(2n+1)^{2}a^{2}+(mb-2y)^{2}}

and

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{a,b}\setminus\Gamma^{+}_{a,b}}d(p,\gamma(p))=\sqrt{a^{2}+d(2y,b{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}}.

Unlike (
6.12
), this expression depends on the
y
-coordinate of
p
.
It is maximal when
y\equiv\frac{b}{4}\,\text{mod}\,\frac{b}{2}
.

In this case,

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{a,b}\setminus\{\id\}}d(p,\gamma(p))=\min\left\{2a,b,\sqrt{a^{2}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2}}\right\}.

This minimum is greater or equal to
2r
if and only if

a\geq r,\quad\frac{b}{2}\geq r,\quad a^{2}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2}\geq 4r^{2}.

∎

The following result relates the inradius of a cusp to the inradius of its boundary.

Proposition 6.19
.

Let
C
be a cusp (orientable or nonorientable) of a complete finite-volume hyperbolic
3
-manifold.
Suppose that the cusp boundary
\partial C
contains an embedded Euclidean disk of radius
r
.
Then
C
contains an embedded hyperbolic open ball of radius at least

\frac{1}{2}\log(1+2r).

Proof.

We may assume that
C
is covered by the horoball
H_{\infty}
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
and that the embedded Euclidean disk of radius
r
in
\partial C
lifts to a disk centered at
p_{0}=(0,0,1)\in{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.
By assumption,

d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p_{0},\gamma.p_{0})\geq 2r

for every
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}
.

Define
p_{t}=(0,0,e^{t})\in H_{\infty}
for
t\geq 0
.
The cusp
C
contains an embedded open ball of radius
R
centered at the projection of
p_{t}
provided

d(p_{t},\partial H_{\infty})\geq R\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\setminus\{\id\}}d(p_{t},\gamma.p_{t})\geq 2R.

By the Euclidean–hyperbolic distance formulas (
2.3
) and (
2.4
),

d(p_{t},\partial H_{\infty})=t

and

d(p_{t},\gamma.p_{t})=2\arsinh\left(\frac{d_{\partial H_{\infty}}(p_{0},\gamma.p_{0})}{2e^{t}}\right)\geq 2\arsinh(re^{-t})

since
p_{t}
and
\gamma.p_{t}
have the same height
e^{t}
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}
.

Hence
C
contains an embedded open ball of radius

R_{t}=\min\{t,2\arsinh(re^{-t})\}.

Now,
R_{t}
is maximal when
t=2\arsinh(re^{-t})
.
That is, when

t=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+2r),

which is also the maximal value of
R_{t}
.
∎

Remark 6.20
.

If the cusp boundary of
C
contains an embedded Euclidean disk of radius

r>\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{5}\right)=0.80901...

then

{\rm R}(M)>\arcosh\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}\right).

We will also need the following elementary result from planar Euclidean geometry.

Proposition 6.21
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=ABCDEF
be a centrally symmetric hexagon in the Euclidean plane with all sides of length
1

Assume that the angles at
B,C,E,F
are equal to
\theta\in[\pi/2,\pi]
.
Then the maximal distance from a point
P\in\mathcal{H}
to the set of vertices of
\mathcal{H}
is

R=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{\theta}{2}}&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta\leq\frac{2\pi}{3}\\
\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\sin^{2}\theta}&\text{if }\frac{2\pi}{3}\leq\theta\leq\pi.\end{cases}

Moreover, for
\theta<\frac{2\pi}{3}
the maximum is achieved at exactly two symmetric points, whereas for
\theta\geq\frac{2\pi}{3}
it is achieved uniquely at the center of symmetry
O
of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be the perpendicular bisector of
[B,C]
.
By symmetry with respect to the axis
(AD)
, it is enough to consider the trapezoid
ABCD
.
By symmetry with respect to
\Delta
and by convexity of the function

P\longmapsto\min\{PA,PB,PC,PD\},

the maximum of this function over
ABCD
is attained on
\Delta
.

If
\theta\leq\frac{2\pi}{3}
, the perpendicular bisectors of
[A,B]
,
[B,C]
,
[C,D]
intersect at a point
P\in\mathcal{H}
.
This point is the unique maximizer of the distance function to the set of vertices within
ABCD
.
Clearly,

PA=PB=PC=PD=\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{\theta}{2}}.

If
\theta>\frac{2\pi}{3}
, the perpendicular bisectors of
[A,B]
,
[B,C]
,
[C,D]
intersect outside
\mathcal{H}
.
In this case, the center
O
of
\mathcal{H}
is the unique maximizer.
The point
O
lies at distance
\sin\theta
from
[B,C]
, and we obtain

OA=OB>OB=OC=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\sin^{2}\theta}.

∎
