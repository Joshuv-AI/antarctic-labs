Physics-Informed Neural PDE Solvers via Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow
Hanru Bai, Yuncheng Zhou, Difan Zou

Abstract
Deep learning paradigms, such as PINNs and neural operators, have significantly advanced the solving of PDEs. However, they often struggle to capture the continuous integral nature of physical systems, relying either on pointwise residuals that ignore the integral perspective or on pre-discretized temporal grids. Drawing inspiration from MeanFlow, a continuous-time integrator recently developed to efficiently solve generative ODEs, we introduce Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow, which functions as a novel PDE solver learning the finite-interval evolution of physical states. By substituting the generative velocity field with the physical PDE operator, we transform multi-step numerical integration into an efficient prediction with a freely controllable integration length. Crucially, we extend the original MeanFlow constraint from the temporal to the spatio-temporal domain, coupling time evolution with spatial consistency. This yields a unified framework naturally accommodating both time-dependent and stationary PDEs. Comprehensive experiments on benchmarks demonstrate that our approach achieves superior accuracy and inference efficiency over representative baselines. Furthermore, the proposed integral constraint enables excellent generalization to out-of-distribution initial conditions and varying spatial resolutions.

Introduction

Solving partial differential equations (PDEs) efficiently and accurately is fundamental to modeling complex physical phenomena across science and engineering, such as shock wave propagation and fluid turbulence
(
Bandrauk et al. 2007
;
Madhavi 2025
;
Glimm 1991
)
. Fundamentally, this mathematical process is a continuous integration process. For example, for a time-dependent PDE governed by
\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}=\bm{f}[\bm{u},\nabla,\bm{x},t]
, the evolution of the state field
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
across the continuous spatial domain from time
\tau
to
t
is the integral:

\textstyle\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{x},\tau)+\int_{\tau}^{t}\bm{f}[\bm{u}(\bm{x},s),\nabla,\bm{x},s]\,\mathrm{d}s.

(1)

While the two dominant deep learning paradigms for PDE solving, namely Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
(
Cuomo et al. 2022
)
, which embed governing equations as pointwise loss penalties, and neural operators like FNO
(
Li et al. 2020a
)
, which learn data-driven mappings between function spaces, have achieved remarkable success, they struggle to fully capture the continuous integral process defined in Eq. (
1
). Specifically, FNO relies heavily on pre-discretized time slices and treats the integration interval as a fixed hyperparameter, essentially functioning as a discrete, step-by-step integrator. Conversely, PINNs rely solely on the pointwise PDE formula itself, bypassing the integral perspective entirely. Grounded in the formulation of Eq. (
1
), we argue that a more straightforward approach is to directly train a neural integrator capable of operating flexibly in continuous domain.

A similar challenge has been extensively studied in generative modeling, such as diffusion models
(
Croitoru et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Luo 2022
)
and flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
, where the inference process can also be formulated as an ordinary differential equation (ODE). Consequently, several works strive to develop efficient methods to solve this generative ODE
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2023
)
. One notable model is MeanFlow
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
, which directly learns the average velocity over a finite interval using neural networks. By doing so, it essentially functions as a continuous-time integrator whose core advantage lies in bypassing repeated velocity evaluations, transforming multi-step integration into an efficient, single-step prediction. This provides a powerful intuition: can we adapt this analogous mechanism to construct a continuous-time neural integrator dedicated to serving PDE solving?

Inspired by this, we adapt the continuous integral formulation of MeanFlow to PDE
solving. Specifically, we replace the generative velocity field

\bm{v}
in the MeanFlow identity with the PDE right-hand side

\bm{f}
, and use the resulting identity to parameterize the
finite-interval evolution of PDE solutions. The original MeanFlow constraint
characterizes temporal evolution alone. For PDEs, however, generalization also
depends critically on spatial consistency. We therefore extend the MeanFlow
loss from the temporal domain to the spatio-temporal domain, introducing a
spatio-temporal MeanFlow loss tailored to PDE solving that strengthens the model’s
ability to generalize across spatial locations and resolutions. Furthermore,
this formulation naturally accommodates static PDEs by specializing to a
purely spatial MeanFlow loss, providing a unified framework for both
time-dependent and stationary PDEs. By transcending purely
data-driven learning, the model achieves superior generalization to
out-of-distribution initial conditions and varied spatial resolutions.
Our contributions are:

•

A MeanFlow-inspired PDE solver framework
: We develop a novel PDE solver that enables efficient inference by transforming conventional multi-step
numerical integration into finite-interval predictions,
with the integration length freely controllable at inference time.

•

A novel spatio-temporal MeanFlow loss
: We introduce a
physics-informed constraint that spatio-temporal significantly enhances solving accuracy
and generalization under out-of-distribution initial conditions and different spatial resolutions.

•

Comprehensive experimental validation
: We conduct extensive evaluations on canonical PDE benchmarks and a real-world dataset. As shown in Fig.
1
, our method achieves an optimal accuracy-efficiency trade-off across synthetic and real-world PDEs. Furthermore, extensive ablations validate our core components, demonstrating the integration-step flexibility of our continuous formulation and showing how the spatio-temporal MeanFlow losses drive robust generalization to higher resolutions and out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions.

Methodology

This section presents the Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow framework. Sec.
3.1
and
3.2
formulate the problem via integration paths and derive physics-informed governing constraints. To mitigate temporal-spatial scale mismatches, Sec.
3.3

3.1
Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow Formulation

We consider the general partial differential equation (PDE) system defined by Eq.
3
.
To relate the states between a starting point
(\bm{\xi},\tau)
and a target point
(\bm{x},t)
, we define a spatio-temporal flow path
\mathfrak{p}:[0,1]\mapsto D\times[0,T]
such that
\mathfrak{p}(0)=(\bm{\xi},\tau)
and
\mathfrak{p}(1)=(\bm{x},t)
(Fig.
2
A).
We then define the spatio-temporal MeanFlow function
\bm{m}
as the integral average of the state evolution along path
\mathfrak{p}
:

\textstyle\bm{m}[\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau),\mathfrak{p},\bm{a}]\triangleq{\int_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{u}(\mathfrak{p})}\penalty\ /\penalty\ {\int_{\mathfrak{p}}\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\mathfrak{p}\|_{2}}.

(6)

For a convex domain
D
, we consider a straight path
\mathfrak{p}(s)\triangleq[\bm{\xi}+s(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}),\tau+s(t-\tau)]
and define path length
l(\bm{\xi},\tau,\bm{x},t)\triangleq\int_{\mathfrak{p}}\|\textrm{d}\mathfrak{p}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}+(t-\tau)^{2}}
which is the total spatio-temporal Euclidean distance.
Following the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals, Eq.
6
satisfies

\textstyle l(\bm{\xi},\tau,\bm{x},t)\cdot\bm{m}[\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau),\bm{\xi},\tau,\bm{x},t;\bm{a}]=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau).

(7)

Note that using a straight path requires
D
to be convex in classical analysis, as it might temporarily exit the physical boundary of non-convex domains (e.g., fluid flow around obstacles). Fortunately, parameterized neural networks act as global continuous functions, hence implicitly perform a
smooth analytic continuation
of the physical state field into the convex hull of
D
.

In our formulation, the proposed path integration and differentiation operators rely on two fundamental mathematical prerequisites: 1) The state variable
\bm{u}
is sufficiently smooth. Specifically, we require
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(D\times[0,T])
for first-order PDEs, and
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
for systems involving second-order spatial derivatives. This guarantees that the Jacobian-based operators (e.g.,
\bm{K}
in Sec.
3.2
) are well-defined; 2) The domain is topologically connected to ensure the physical meaningfulness of bridging any two states.

3.2
Physics-informed MeanFlow Constraints

By differentiating the fundamental identity Eq. (
7
) with respect to the initial coordinates
(\bm{\xi},\tau)
, we derive the physics-informed constraints for our framework, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textstyle l_{t}\cdot\bm{m}=l\bm{K}\bm{f}+\gamma l^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau},

(8)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \textstyle l\cdot\bm{K}\cdot\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)&=\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}},\\
\textstyle l\cdot\bm{K}\cdot\Delta\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)&=-(\bm{\alpha}+2\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\beta}),\quad\cdots.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

Here,
\bm{\alpha}\triangleq n\bm{m}-(l_{s}^{2}\mathbin{/}l^{2})\cdot\bm{m}+2l^{2}{\Delta}\bm{m}(\bm{\xi},{\tau})
and
\bm{\beta}\triangleq-\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})-(l_{s}^{2}\mathbin{/}l)\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bm{m}
, with
l_{t}=t-\tau
being the time step,
l_{s}=\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}
the spatial distance, and
\bm{K}=\bm{I}+l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}
the Jacobian-based correction operator. Specifically, Eq.
8
is derived by differentiating Eq. (
7
) with respect to time
\tau
, while Eq.
9
are obtained by taking spatial derivatives (first-order, second-order, and so forth) with respect to
\bm{\xi}
. Prop.
D.1
, Appendix
D
provides the detailed derivation. Consequently, the spatio-temporal MeanFlow loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{ST-MeanFlow}}\triangleq\|\textstyle l_{t}\cdot\bm{m}-l\bm{K}\bm{f}-{\gamma}l^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial{\tau}}\|_{2}^{2},

(10)

with the gradient terms in
\bm{f}
substituted by Eqs. (
9
).

3.3
Space-Time Decoupling Strategy

In many physical systems, temporal evolution and spatial variations occur at vastly different magnitudes (e.g., fast chemical reactions in large-scale reactors)
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Krishnapriyan et al. 2021
)
. When these dimensions are tightly coupled in a single loss function, the optimization landscape often exhibits pathological curvature, characterized by a highly ill-conditioned Hessian matrix
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
with large off-diagonal cross-coupling terms (Fig.
2
B). To solve the challenge of inherent
spatio-temporal scale mismatch
, we decompose the spatio-temporal objective into two independent first-order components:

Temporal MeanFlow Loss.

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{T-MeanFlow}}\triangleq\left\|\bm{m}-\bm{K}\bm{f}-{\gamma}l_{t}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}
constrains the network to satisfy the temporal evolution predicted by the Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow identity along the time-line.

Spatial MeanFlow Loss.

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{S-MeanFlow}}\triangleq\left\|l_{s}\bm{K}\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}-\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}+l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
enforces consistency between predicted Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow’s spatial derivatives and numerical spatial gradients.

Thm.
3.2
, Sec.
3.5
ensures that the decoupling provide a theoretical reference for the overall Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow loss.
The core advantage of this decoupling is the removal of explicit spatio-temporal cross-derivatives in the residual definitions. Mathematically, this transformation reshapes the Hessian into a nearly block-diagonal structure, reducing its condition number and enabling faster, more robust convergence (Thm.
D.4
, App.
D.4
). This decoupling is theoretically supported by the
Space-Time Decoupling Bound
presented in the following section, which ensures that minimizing these independent losses effectively constrains the global spatio-temporal error.

3.4
Detailed Implemention

Training.

We parameterize the MeanFlow predictor
\bm{m}
using a neural network, denoted as
\bm{m}_{\theta}
, to approximate the true
\bm{m}
. For time-dependent PDEs, the network is designed to map the concatenated input
[\bm{u}(\bm{x},\tau),\tau,\bm{x},t,\bm{a}]
, where
\tau
and
t
are randomly sampled time pairs during training. The model outputs the average integral from time
\tau
to
t
. According to the fundamental identity (Eq. (
7
)), the target state at time
t
is explicitly constructed as
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{x},\tau)+(t-\tau)\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}
.
The total objective function is defined as the weighted sum of the data loss (measuring the discrepancy between the predicted solution at time
t
and observational data) and the physics-informed decoupled spatio-temporal MeanFlow losses:

\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Data}}+\lambda_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{T-MeanFlow}}+\lambda_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{S-MeanFlow}},

(11)

where
\lambda_{t}
and
\lambda_{s}
are the weighting coefficients.

Conversely, when
\gamma=0
, the system degenerates into a static PDE. In this scenario, temporal variables are omitted. The model instead takes the analytically extended boundary conditions
\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x})
(where
\bm{u}_{0}\rvert_{\partial D}=\bm{u}\rvert_{\partial D}
) and the coefficient field
\bm{a}
as inputs. The solution over the interior domain
\bm{x}\in D\setminus\partial D
is constructed as
\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x})+\bm{m}_{\theta}
, where
\bm{m}_{\theta}
represents the network output. The temporal loss naturally vanishes, and the training objective simplifies to:

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Data}}+\lambda_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{S-MeanFlow}}.

Note that in our practical implementation, considering that the benchmark PDEs evaluated in this work are defined on regular grids, we maintain fixed spatial coordinates (
\bm{x}
) across inputs and outputs. Consequently, our current network design predominantly emphasizes continuous-time evolution. However, our fundamental mathematical formulation inherently supports continuous spatial evolution as well, where the anchor point
\bm{\xi}
and the target point
\bm{x}
can vary continuously. The detailed algorithms for the fully generalized spatio-temporal framework are provided in App.

Inference.

We first focus on the one-step inference scenario. For time-dependent PDEs, standard deep learning-based solvers typically take the initial condition and physical coefficients as inputs to predict the solution over the spatial domain at a target time
t
. Following this paradigm, our algorithm takes
[\bm{u}(\bm{x},0),0,\bm{x},t,\bm{a}]
as input during inference to predict the average integral from time
0
to
t
, denoted as
\bm{m}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
. The final solution at time
t
is then recovered via

\textstyle\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{x},0)+t\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}(\bm{x},t).

(12)

For static systems, the general objective is to derive the global solution given specific boundary conditions and coefficient fields. Maintaining consistency with our training phase, the model simply takes the analytically extended boundary conditions and the coefficient field as inputs and then get the solution across the spatial domain through
\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x})+\bm{m}_{\theta}
. Furthermore, for time-dependent PDEs, since our framework models the continuous integral over arbitrary time intervals, it naturally extends to multi-step inference. The detailed procedure for multi-step prediction is provided in Alg.
2
.

3.5
Theoretical Results

The proposed framework is supported by two core theoretical pillars: a-posteriori error bounds for the predicted state (Thm.
3.1
), and effectiveness of the decoupled loss terms (Thm.
3.2
). App.
D.2
-
D.4
provide proofs for them, with the improved optimization landscapes through decoupling (Thm.
D.4
).

For simplicity, we define
\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\triangleq l_{t}\bm{m}-l\bm{K}\bm{f}-{\gamma}l^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial{\tau}}
with higher-order spatial gradients in
\bm{h}
substituted by spatial MeanFlow Eqs. (
9
),
\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}\triangleq\bm{m}-\bm{K}\bm{f}-{\gamma}l_{t}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial{\tau}}
, and
\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\triangleq l_{s}\bm{K}{\nabla}^{\top}\bm{u}-\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}+l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}
as the residual vectors for different MeanFlow losses.

(A-posteriori Error Estimate)
.

Assume the MeanFlow predictor
\bm{m}_{\theta}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-continuous. If the total training loss bounds the squared path residuals such that
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\leq\epsilon
, then the pointwise error between reconstructed state
\bm{u}_{\theta}
and true solution
\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}
at any target point

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs).

While CFO shares a similar continuous integration modeling philosophy with our approach, our framework additionally introduces a physics-inspired spatio-temporal MeanFlow loss, significantly enhancing its generalization capabilities. 6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a physics-informed Neural PDE Solver via Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow. Functioning as an arbitrary-interval continuous integrator, it enables the computational efficiency of flexible integration steps. Furthermore, by enforcing physical consistency via a novel spatio-temporal loss, our method achieves enhanced generalization capabilities. Extensive evaluations demonstrate its superior accuracy, efficiency, and generalization.

Limitations and Future Work.

Although our underlying mathematical formulation natively supports continuous spatial evolution, our current empirical implementation focuses on regular grids. Activating this continuous spatial modeling to tackle irregular domains represents the future step to further maximize the framework’s generalization capabilities. Furthermore, extending our spatio-temporal loss to real-world datasets lacking analytical PDEs addresses a ubiquitous bottleneck in physics-informed learning. Finally, evolving our inverse modeling proof-of-concept into a unified architecture that seamlessly co-learns coefficient fields and solutions will further unleash the framework’s potential.

Impact Statement

Our Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow framework accelerates complex physical simulations by transforming traditional multi-step numerical integration into efficient, finite-interval predictions. This enhanced computational efficiency has the potential to significantly reduce the energy consumption and carbon footprint associated with large-scale scientific modeling, such as fluid dynamics, meteorology, and climate forecasting. Furthermore, its demonstrated versatility in handling real-world datasets and inverse problems opens up promising downstream applications in environmental monitoring and engineering design.

Despite these positive implications, the deployment of neural PDE solvers in safety-critical domains (e.g., aerospace design or nuclear engineering) requires careful consideration. Due to the inherent black-box nature of deep learning, neural solvers may fail unpredictably on extreme anomalies, even with improved out-of-distribution generalization. Therefore, we strongly recommend utilizing our framework to accelerate iterative design and optimization phases, while continuing to rely on mathematically guaranteed numerical methods for final safety certifications.

Appendix B
Extended Related Work

B.1
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs): Optimization Challenges in PINNs

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
(
Raissi et al. 2019
)
represent a foundational class of deep learning-based PDE solvers. They parameterize the solution manifold by embedding the governing physical laws into the training objective as soft constraints. Despite their widespread success across diverse physical domains (ranging from fluid dynamics to power systems), PINNs suffer from severe scalability bottlenecks due to their inherent reliance on instance-specific, iterative optimization. Furthermore, a prominent challenge in training PINNs is their notoriously pathological optimization landscape, caused by severe stiffness among competing boundary, initial, and residual loss terms.

To address this problem, subsequent works have developed sophisticated adaptive schemes. Beyond standard uncertainty weighting
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
and gradient pathology mitigation
(
Kendall et al. 2018
)
, Wang et al.
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
proposed an adaptive learning rate annealing strategy along with an improved neural architecture (IA-PINN) to balance fitting and regularization. Concurrently, methods like IAW-PINN advanced uncertainty weighting by controlling the upper bounds of weights, offering more flexible balancing among distinct loss terms. Recognizing that singular approaches are often limited by network structure, recent efforts have integrated these strengths (such as I-PINN
(
Niu et al. 2025
)
, which unifies both IA-PINN’s architecture and IAW-PINN’s weighting strategies).

B.2
Neural Operators: Advancements in Neural Operator Architectures

Neural operators learn a direct mapping between infinite-dimensional function spaces. Architectures like Fourier Neural Operators (FNO)
(
Li et al. 2020a
)
and DeepONet
(
Lu et al. 2019
)
enable rapid, zero-shot inference for new initial or boundary conditions. However, purely data-driven operators often struggle with out-of-distribution generalization and may violate physical laws. To address this, Physics-Informed Neural Operators (PINO)
(
Li et al. 2024
)
explicitly incorporate PDE residuals into the operator training objective, blending fast inference with physical constraints. However, when applied to time-dependent systems, these models typically rely on autoregressive (AR) schemes, which are prone to error accumulation over long horizons and restricted by uniform temporal discretization.

To address these limitations, the Continuous Flow Operator (CFO)
(
Hou et al. 2026
)
was introduced as a novel class of flow-based operators. CFO shares our core conceptual motivation: rather than treating flow matching as a simple generative sampler, it repurposes the framework to learn the underlying differential dynamics (the physical right-hand side of the PDE) directly. By fitting temporal splines to trajectory data, CFO constructs continuous velocity fields that grant the model inherent time-resolution invariance. However, despite this shared perspective, its formulation is purely temporal; it captures the continuous evolution over time but lacks explicit constraints to ensure physical consistency across the spatial domain, which is critical for PDE generalization. Our proposed temporal-spatial MeanFlow framework strategically overcomes this. By expanding the integral constraints to the spatio-temporal domain, we explicitly enforce physical consistency across spatial resolutions.

Furthermore, a wide array of other architectural extensions have been proposed to overcome the geometric constraints of standard neural operators. For instance, while original FNOs rely heavily on uniform grids, methods such as Geo-FNO
(
Li et al. 2023
)
and multipole graph neural operators
(
Li et al. 2020b
)
have been developed to accurately map and compute solutions across highly irregular physical geometries. Similarly, the Galerkin Transformer
(
Cao 2021
)
and CViT
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
introduce robust mechanisms for handling non-uniform or irregularly sampled observational data.

To capture complex, overlapping physical phenomena, models like the Wavelet Neural Operator (WNO)
(
Tripura & Chakraborty 2022
)
integrate multi-scale structural processing. Finally, architectures such as U-NO
(
Rahman et al. 2022
)
address the depth limitations of traditional operators, employing U-Net-like structures to enable deeper, more expressive feature extraction without sacrificing computational efficiency. While these methods successfully expand the expressivity and geometric flexibility of operator learning, our approach uniquely pivots toward using generative flow matching as a direct, physical equation solver to achieve rapid, single-step integration.

B.3
Generative Modeling for PDE Simulation

Generative modeling offers a novel paradigm for PDE simulation by treating solution fields as samples from learned distributions. Early advancements in this domain heavily relied on diffusion models. For example, DiffusionPDE
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
applies EDM-style diffusion combined with physics guidance during the sampling phase, while CoCoGen
(
Jacobsen et al. 2025
)
extends score-based models to ensure physically consistent generation. Although these conditional diffusion models achieve high fidelity, they typically require hundreds of stochastic steps at inference, severely limiting their practical utility for fast simulation.

To address this severe latency, researchers have increasingly explored flow matching and rectified flows. Armegioiu et al.
(
Armegioiu et al. 2025
)
introduced a rectified flow surrogate that learns a time-dependent conditional velocity field, effectively transporting input-to-output laws along nearly straight trajectories. By leveraging a curvature-aware sampler, this approach drastically accelerates inference, reducing the required ODE evaluations to as few as eight steps while matching the Wasserstein statistics of standard diffusion models. In parallel, to improve physical fidelity within these generative frameworks, methods like PBFM
(
Baldan et al. 2025
)
have been developed to explicitly integrate physical constraints directly into flow matching training through conflict-free gradient updates. Furthermore,
(
Zhang & Zou 2025
)
proposed a post-hoc distillation method to enable more accurate and fast generation for PDE problems, achieving remarkable acceleration and improvements over many PDE-related problems.

Despite these significant advancements in inference speed and physical consistency, the core objective of these models remains unchanged: they are designed to map initial noise distributions to target solution manifolds. They fundamentally operate on a noise-to-sample generation paradigm. Our approach diverges completely from this statistical sampling formulation. Rather than generating solutions from noise, we adapt the continuous-time modeling capabilities of flow matching purely from the perspective of direct equation solving. By replacing the unconstrained generative velocity field with the physical right-hand side of the PDE, our framework functions strictly as an efficient, deterministic neural integrator.

D.1
The Construction of Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow Constraints

In this section, Prop.
D.1
conclude and show how the MeanFlow constraints being constructed.

Proposition D.1

(MeanFlow Constraints.)
.

For MeanFlow function
\bm{m}
satisfying

l(\bm{\xi},{\tau},\bm{x},t)\cdot\bm{m}[\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},{\tau}),\bm{\xi},{\tau},\bm{x},t,\bm{a}]=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},t),

(15)

we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{t}\cdot\bm{m}=l\bm{K}\bm{f}+{\gamma}l^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial{\tau}},\\
&l\cdot\bm{K}\cdot{\nabla}^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},{\tau})=\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}},\\
&l\cdot\bm{K}\cdot{\Delta}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},{\tau})=-(\bm{\alpha}+2\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\beta}),\end{aligned}\right.

where
\bm{\alpha}=n\bm{m}-(l_{s}^{2}\mathbin{/}l^{2})\cdot\bm{m}+2l^{2}{\Delta}\bm{m}(\bm{\xi},{\tau})
and
\bm{\beta}=-\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})-(l_{s}^{2}\mathbin{/}l)\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bm{m}
.

Proof.

We enforce a perturbation on
{\gamma}
if
{\gamma}=0
and let
{\gamma}\to 0
in the end phase. This technique allows us to regard parameter
{\gamma}
as a non-sigular value.

We compute the derivatives of
\bm{m}
given that
\bm{\xi},\bm{x},t,\bm{a}
are constants w.r.t.
\tau
and
\tau,\bm{x},t,\bm{a}
are constants w.r.t.
\bm{\xi}
, leading to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{m}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\tau}=&\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}=\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma}+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau},\\
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{m}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{\xi}}=&\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}=\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

The derivatives w.r.t.
\tau
of both sides of Eq. (
15
) are

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\text{LHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\tau}=&\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}l}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\tau}\bm{m}+l\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{m}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\tau}=\frac{-(t-\tau)\bm{m}}{l}+l\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma}+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right),\\
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\text{RHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\tau}=&-\frac{\partial\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\partial\tau}=-\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma},\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

where
\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)\triangleq\bm{f}[\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau),\nabla,\bm{\xi},\tau,\bm{a}]
.
Notably, the notations
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\penalty\ \bm{\cdot}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\penalty\ \bm{\cdot}}
and
\frac{\partial\penalty\ \bm{\cdot}}{\partial\penalty\ \bm{\cdot}}
both represent the Jacobian matrices, whereas
\nabla\bm{f}
represent spatial gradients, which is the transpose of Jacobian matrix.

Consequently, we have the ground-truth approximation

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{\text{gt}}=

\displaystyle\frac{l}{t-\tau}\left[l\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma}+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right)+\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma}\right],

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{l}{t-\tau}\left[\bm{K}\cdot\frac{\bm{f}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\gamma}+l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right],

where
l\triangleq\sqrt{(t-\tau)^{2}+\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}}
and
\bm{K}=\bm{I}+l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}
.
This coheres to the final result in conventional
temporal MeanFlow
. The
spatial
part is derived as follows.

The derivatives of Eq. (
15
) w.r.t.
\bm{\xi}
, on the other hand, result in

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\text{LHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{\xi}}=&\frac{\bm{m}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x})^{\top}}{l}+l\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right),\\
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\text{RHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{\xi}}=&-\frac{\partial\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\partial\bm{\xi}}=-\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau),\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

and thus

\bm{K}\cdot\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)=\frac{1}{l}\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}.

(20)

Note that this result may substitute the
\nabla\bm{u}
’s of
\bm{K}\bm{f}
in (
18
).

There are more PDEs containing two or more order of derivatives, e.g. the Navier-Stokes equations with
\Delta\bm{u}
within considerations.

For PDE with higher order in space, say order-
2
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}^{2}\text{LHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{2}}=&\frac{\bm{m}\bm{1}^{\top}}{l}-\frac{1}{l^{3}}\bm{m}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x})^{\top}\text{diag}\penalty\ (\bm{\xi}-\bm{x})+l\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{\xi}^{2}}+2\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}^{2}}\right)\\
&+\frac{2}{l}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right)\text{diag}\penalty\ (\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}),\\
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}^{2}\text{RHS}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{2}}=&-\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{z}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\partial\bm{\xi}^{2}},\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

where the matrices
\left[\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}^{2}\bm{z}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{x}^{2}}\right]_{ij}\triangleq\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}^{2}\bm{z}_{i}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{x}_{j}^{2}}
and
\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{z}}{\partial\bm{x}^{2}}\right]_{ij}\triangleq\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{z}_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}^{2}}
, then,

\displaystyle-\bm{K}\Delta\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau),

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{K}\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\partial^{2}\bm{\xi}^{2}}\bm{1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big(\bm{I}+l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\Big)\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)}{\partial^{2}\bm{\xi}^{2}}\bm{1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{l}\bm{m}-\frac{\|\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}{l^{3}}\bm{m}+2l\Delta\bm{m}(\bm{\xi},\tau)+\frac{2}{l}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{l}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bm{K}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{l}\bm{m}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x})^{\top}+l\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right)(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\alpha}}{l}-\frac{2}{l^{2}}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}\|\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}+2\left[\frac{\bm{K}}{l}-\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\right]\bm{K}^{-1}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}),

hence

\bm{K}\Delta\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)=-\Big(\frac{\bm{\alpha}}{l}+\frac{2}{l}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\beta}\Big),

(23)

where
\bm{\alpha}\triangleq n\bm{m}-\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}}{l^{2}}\bm{m}+2l^{2}\Delta\bm{m}(\bm{\xi},\tau)
and
\bm{\beta}\triangleq-\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})-\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}}{l}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bm{m}
, in which
n
represents the number of spatial dimensions.
Consequently,
-l\bm{K}^{2}\Delta\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)=\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}+2\bm{\beta}
.
∎

D.2
Error Estimate

Theorem D.2

(A-posteriori Error Bound, manuscript)
.

Assume the PDE operator
\bm{f}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to the state variable with constant
L
, and continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{1}
) such that its Jacobian
\nabla_{\bm{u}}\bm{f}
is bounded locally. Further, assume the MeanFlow predictor
\bm{m}_{\theta}
and the true physical state
\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-continuous. If the total training loss bounds the squared path residuals such that
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\leq\epsilon
, then the pointwise error between the reconstructed state
\bm{u}_{\theta}
and the true solution
\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}
at any target point
(\bm{x},t)
is bounded by:

\|\bm{u}_{\theta}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}(\bm{x},t)\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2}),

(24)

where
C
is a constant depending on the spatio-temporal path length
l
and the Lipschitz constant
L
.

Proof.

Let the straight spatio-temporal path
\mathfrak{p}
connecting the starting point
(\bm{\xi},\tau)
and the target point
(\bm{x},t)
be parameterized by a scalar
s\in[0,1]
. The predicted state along the path is linearly modeled as
\bm{u}_{\theta}(s)=\bm{u}(\bm{\xi},\tau)+s\cdot l\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}
, implying its path derivative is precisely
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{u}_{\theta}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}=l\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}
.

We define the pointwise state error as
\bm{v}(s)=\bm{u}_{\theta}(s)-\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}(s)
. Assuming the starting state is exact (i.e., anchored by data constraints such that
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{0}
), the evolution of the error along the path is governed by the difference in their derivatives:

\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{v}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}=\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{u}_{\theta}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}-\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}=l\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}-l\cdot\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}(s)).

(25)

To bridge the predicted MeanFlow and the true physical operator, we explicitly add and subtract the term
l\cdot\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\theta}(s))
:

\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{v}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}=l\cdot\left[\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\theta}(s))-\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}(s))\right]+l\cdot\left[\bm{m}_{\theta}-\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\theta}(s))\right].

(26)

The first bracketed term is governed by the Lipschitz continuity of the PDE operator
\bm{f}
with constant
L
, bounded by
L\|\bm{v}(s)\|_{2}
. The second bracketed term measures the deviation between the constant MeanFlow vector and the continuous local PDE evaluation. By Taylor expanding
\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\theta}(s))
around the starting point
s=0
, we relate it to the global MeanFlow residual
\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}
evaluated by the network:

l\cdot\left[\bm{m}_{\theta}-\bm{f}(\bm{u}_{\theta}(s))\right]=\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}+\bm{r}(s),

(27)

where
\bm{r}(s)\sim s\cdot l^{2}\nabla_{\bm{u}}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{m}_{\theta}
is the first-order Taylor remainder along the path. Because the state derivatives and the Jacobian
\nabla_{\bm{u}}\bm{f}
are assumed bounded, the remainder satisfies
\|\bm{r}(s)\|_{2}\leq Msl^{2}=\mathcal{O}(l^{2})
, with
M
being a local bounding constant.

Taking the
L_{2}
norm on both sides and applying the triangle inequality yields a rigorous differential inequality:

\left\|\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{v}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}\leq l\cdot L\|\bm{v}(s)\|_{2}+\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2}).

(28)

Applying Grönwall’s inequality to integrate this bound from
s=0
to
s=1
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}\left\|\bm{v}(s)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{v}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}\leq l\cdot L\|\bm{v}(s)\|_{2}+\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2}),

(29)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s}\left[\left\|\bm{v}(s)\right\|_{2}\textrm{e}^{-lLs}\right]\leq\left[\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2})\right]\textrm{e}^{-lLs},

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}(1)\|_{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left[\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2})\right]\exp\left[lL(1-s)\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s.

The integral linearly separates into two components. For the first component involving the trained residual, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to relate it to the squared loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\leq\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}\exp\left[lL(1-s)\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}}\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left[2lL(1-s)\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(30)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon},

where
C=\sqrt{\frac{\exp(2lL)-1}{2lL}}
. For the second component, the integration of the
\mathcal{O}(l^{2})
term over
s\in[0,1]
simply scales the exponential factor, strictly preserving the
\mathcal{O}(l^{2})
magnitude order.

Superimposing these integrated bounds directly yields the final pointwise error bound:

\|\bm{u}_{\theta}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}(\bm{x},t)\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(l^{2}).

(31)

This establishes that the global error is bounded by the optimization loss and a strict structural truncation error, concluding the proof.
∎

D.3
MeanFlow Error Bounded by Decoupled Losses

Theorem D.3

(Thm.
3.2
, manuscript)
.

Assume the MeanFlow predictor
\bm{m}_{\theta}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-continuous. If the total training loss bounds the squared path residuals such that
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\leq\epsilon
, then the pointwise error between the reconstructed state
\bm{u}_{\theta}
and the true solution
\bm{u}_{\textrm{True}}
at any target point
(\bm{x},t)
is bounded by:

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\leq c_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Temp}}+c_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Spac}}+{\varepsilon},

(32)

where the coefficients are defined as
c_{1}=3l_{t}^{2}
and
c_{2}=3\left(\frac{l-l_{t}}{l_{s}}\|\bm{g}\|_{\textrm{op}}\right)^{2}
. Here,
{\varepsilon}
represents the structural coupling error and
{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{2})
.

Proof.

We begin with the spatio-temporal residual vector
\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}
:

\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}=l_{t}\bm{m}-l\bm{K}\bm{f}-\gamma l^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}.

(S1)

From the definition of the temporal residual
\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}
, we isolate the ground-truth term:

\bm{m}=\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+\bm{K}\bm{f}+l_{t}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}.

(33)

Substituting this into Eq. (S1) yields:

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}=

\displaystyle l_{t}(\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+\bm{K}\bm{f}+l_{t}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau})-l\bm{K}\bm{f}-\gamma l^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau},

(S2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l_{t}\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+(l_{t}-l)\bm{K}\bm{f}+(l_{t}^{2}-\gamma l^{2})\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}.

Given the linear gradient PDE structure
\bm{f}=\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{g}+\bm{h}
, and the spatial residual
\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}
, we substitute the spatial gradient
\bm{K}\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}
into the expression for
\bm{K}\bm{f}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}=l_{t}\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+(l_{t}-l)\bm{K}({\nabla}^{\top}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{g}+\bm{h})+(l_{t}^{2}-\gamma l^{2})\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau},

(S3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l_{t}\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+\frac{l_{t}-l}{l_{s}}(\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}+\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}})\cdot\bm{g}+(l_{t}-l)\bm{K}\bm{h}+(l_{t}^{2}-\gamma l^{2})\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau},

Applying the generalized triangle inequality for squared
L_{2}
norms (or Jensen’s inequality for quadratic function, i.e.,
\|\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bm{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|\bm{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
) and the operator norm bound
\|\bm{v}\cdot\bm{g}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{g}\|_{\textrm{op}}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}
, we obtain:

\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3l_{t}^{2}\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}\|_{2}^{2}+3\left(\frac{l-l_{t}}{l_{s}}\|\bm{g}\|_{\textrm{op}}\right)^{2}\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\|_{2}^{2}+3\|\bm{r}\|_{2}^{2}

(34)

where

\bm{r}=\underbrace{\frac{l_{t}-l}{l_{s}}\left[\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right]\cdot\bm{g}}_{\textrm{Boundary or Higher-Order Discretization Errors}}+\underbrace{(l_{t}-l)\bm{K}\bm{h}}_{\textrm{Source Term Error}}+\underbrace{(l_{t}^{2}-{\gamma}l^{2})\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial{\tau}}}_{\textrm{Temporal Scaling Mismatch}},

(35)

collects the remaining structural terms. Therefore, as
\|\bm{K}\|_{\textrm{op}}\leq{\rho}
and the state variables are sufficiently smooth such that their norms are bounded, we have, applying generalized triangle inequality for squared
L_{2}
norms again,

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}=3\|\bm{r}\|^{2}_{2}\leq

\displaystyle 9(l_{t}-l)^{2}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{l_{s}^{2}}\cdot\left\|\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}_{2}+{\rho}^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{l_{t}^{2}-\gamma l^{2}}{l_{t}-l}\right)^{2}\left\|\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}9\left\|\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}_{2}+9l_{s}^{2}\cdot{\rho}^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2},&\text{if }{\gamma}=0,\\
9(l_{t}-l)^{2}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{l_{s}^{2}}\cdot\left\|\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}_{2}+{\rho}^{2}\|\bm{h}\|_{2}^{2}+(l_{t}+l)^{2}\left\|\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],&\text{if }{\gamma}=1,\end{array}\right.

where
\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}\triangleq\left[\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}-l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right]\cdot\bm{g}
. Here, the result for
\gamma=0
follows from the fact that
l_{t}=0
and
l=l_{s}
for a steady-state system implied by
\gamma=0
.

Note that the higher-order discretization error
\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\bm{r}_{\bm{g}}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}\bm{g}\|_{2}+\left\|l_{s}^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\bm{g}\right\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{m}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{g}\|_{\textrm{op}}+\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{2}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{m}\|_{2}\cdot l_{s}\cdot\|\bm{g}\|_{\textrm{op}}=\mathcal{O}(l_{s}).

Consequently, since the bracketed term in the
\gamma=1
case is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)
and the temporal scaling factor satisfies
l_{t}-l=\frac{l_{t}^{2}-l^{2}}{l_{t}+l}=-\frac{l_{s}^{2}}{l_{t}+l}=\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{2})
, the asymptotic bound for the structural coupling error yields:

{\varepsilon}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{2}),&\text{if }{\gamma}=0,\\
\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{4}),&\text{if }{\gamma}=1.\end{array}\right.

This concludes the proof.
∎

D.4
Hessian Condition Number Analysis

Theorem D.4

(Hessian Condition Number Analysis)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}}
be the Hessian matrices of the coupled spatio-temporal loss and the disentangled loss with respect to the network parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Under inherent physical scale-mismatches between spatial and temporal dimensions, the condition numbers satisfy:

\kappa(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}})\ll\kappa(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}}).

(37)

Proof.

Consider the coupled loss optimized over the neural network parameters
\bm{\theta}
. Based on the linear decomposition in Eq. (S3), Thm.
D.3
, the coupled residual can be abstractly expressed as
\bm{r}_{\textrm{MeanFlow}}(\bm{\theta})\approx\alpha\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}(\bm{\theta})+\beta\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}(\bm{\theta})
, where
\alpha\sim\mathcal{O}(l_{t})
and
\beta\sim\mathcal{O}(l_{s}^{-1})
denote the characteristic temporal and spatial scales. The coupled loss is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Coupled}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\alpha\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}+\beta\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\|_{2}^{2}

(38)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\|\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Decoupled}}(\bm{\theta})}+\underbrace{\alpha\beta\langle\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}},\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Cross}}(\bm{\theta})}.

Let
\bm{J}_{T}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{r}_{\textrm{Temp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
\bm{J}_{S}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{r}_{\textrm{Spac}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be the Jacobian matrices of the temporal and spatial residuals, respectively. Applying the Gauss-Newton approximation (neglecting second-order residual terms), the Hessian of the decoupled loss is a well-conditioned sum of two positive semi-definite (PSD) matrices:

\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}}\approx\alpha^{2}\bm{J}_{T}^{\top}\bm{J}_{T}+\beta^{2}\bm{J}_{S}^{\top}\bm{J}_{S}.

(39)

Conversely, the Hessian of the coupled loss introduces explicit cross-Jacobian interactions:

\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}}\approx\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}}+\underbrace{\alpha\beta\left(\bm{J}_{T}^{\top}\bm{J}_{S}+\bm{J}_{S}^{\top}\bm{J}_{T}\right)}_{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Cross}}}.

(40)

In stiff physical systems, temporal variations and spatial gradients evolve at vastly mismatched scales, leading to highly skewed singular values between
\bm{J}_{T}
and
\bm{J}_{S}
. The matrix
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Cross}}
is highly indefinite and dense. According to Weyl’s inequality for Hermitian matrices, the minimum eigenvalue of the coupled Hessian is bounded by:

\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}})\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}})+\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Cross}}).

(41)

Because
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Cross}}
is indefinite and dominated by the scale mismatch
\mathcal{O}(\alpha\beta)
, its extreme eigenvalues perturb the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}}
violently. This interference dramatically suppresses
\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}})
toward zero (or creates pathological ravines in the optimization landscape) while inflating
\lambda_{\max}
, thereby severely escalating the condition number:

\kappa(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Coupled}})}\gg\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}})}=\kappa(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Decoupled}}).

(42)

By optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Temp}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Spac}}
independently, the decoupled strategy explicitly annihilates the pathological cross-term
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Cross}}
, ensuring a geometrically stable and well-conditioned gradient flow.
∎

Appendix E
Examples of Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow Losses

Example 1: d’Alembert’s equation
The proposed method applys to the equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bm{c}(\bm{u})=\bm{0},\\
&\bm{u}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}),\end{aligned}\right.

the variables in our method are
\nabla^{\top}\bm{u}=\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{x}},\bm{f}=-\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bm{c}(\bm{u})
, hereby Eq. (
18
) obtains

\displaystyle(t-\tau)\bm{m}-\left(-\bm{m}(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}+l^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\right)\bm{c}(\bm{u})-l^{2}\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\left[t-\tau+(\bm{x}-\bm{\xi})^{\top}\bm{c}(\bm{u})\right]\bm{m}-l^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\bm{c}(\bm{u})+\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial\tau}\right)=\bm{0},

where
l^{2}=(t-\tau)^{2}+\|\bm{x}-\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}
and the equation allows the construction of a MeanFlow loss just as the original MeanFlow framework did.

Example 2(a): Navier-Stokes equations
The Navier-Stokes equations are,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\bm{w}(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\bm{w}(\bm{x},t)}{\partial\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)+v\Delta\bm{w}(\bm{x},t)+\bm{f}(\bm{x}),\\
&\bm{w}(\bm{x},t)=\nabla\times\bm{u}(\bm{x},t),\\
&\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=0,\\
&\bm{w}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{w}_{0}(\bm{x}),\forall x\in D.\end{aligned}\right.

(43)

We can eliminate
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
by solving

\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\bm{w}(\bm{y},t)\times(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{3}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\textrm{d}\bm{y},

using the Biot–Savart integration under the condition that
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0
.

F.2
Details of the Real-world HYCOM Dataset

We use a real-world ocean dynamics dataset derived from HYCOM for evaluating the scalability and robustness of the proposed method. HYCOM, the Hybrid Coordinate Ocean Model, is a global ocean analysis and forecasting system. Different from purely synthetic PDE benchmarks, HYCOM products are generated by combining numerical ocean model dynamics with real observations through data assimilation. The assimilated observations include satellite measurements and in-situ ocean observations. Therefore, the resulting fields reflect realistic ocean variability, while also containing practical complications such as observation noise, assimilation artifacts, imperfect boundaries, and possible mismatch between the observed dynamics and idealized PDE assumptions.

Data source.

The data are obtained from the public HYCOM GLBy0.08 product through the THREDDS/OPeNDAP service:

\texttt{https://tds.hycom.org/thredds/dodsC/GLBy0.08/latest}.

We use the variable
water_temp
, which represents three-dimensional ocean water temperature. The original HYCOM product provides global ocean fields over longitude, latitude, depth, and time. In our experiment, we select a local ocean region and use the first 8 depth levels to construct a three-dimensional spatio-temporal prediction task.

Selected region and variables.

We extract a local spatial block from the HYCOM grid using latitude indices
[1100,1260)
and longitude indices
[2900,3060)
. This corresponds approximately to latitudes from
-36.0^{\circ}
to
-29.64^{\circ}
and longitudes from
232.0^{\circ}
to
244.72^{\circ}
in the HYCOM convention, i.e., approximately
128.0^{\circ}
W to
115.28^{\circ}
W. The selected depth levels are

0,\ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ 10,\ 12,\ 15
meters.

The raw extracted block has shape

32\times 8\times 160\times 160,

corresponding to time, depth, latitude, and longitude dimensions, respectively.

Preprocessing.

We normalize the extracted temperature field using the mean and standard deviation computed over the selected raw block. The statistics used in our processed dataset are

\mu=23.01293796527723,\hskip 18.49988pt\sigma=1.3310423861925285.

The raw temperature values in the selected block range approximately from

18.8700\text{ to }26.1490.

After normalization, we construct local spatio-temporal samples using a sliding-window procedure. Each sample contains 9 time steps on a spatial grid of size

32\times 32\times 8.

The spatial stride is set to 4 along the two horizontal dimensions. This produces a total of

26136

Dataset split.

We split the processed samples into training, validation, and test sets using an 80/10/10 split:

20908\text{ training samples},\hskip 9.24994pt2614\text{ validation samples},\hskip 9.24994pt2614\text{ test samples}.

Prediction task.

Given the initial three-dimensional temperature field and the spatio-temporal coordinates, the model predicts the future temperature evolution over the 9-step temporal window. Unlike idealized synthetic PDE data, the HYCOM data are not generated from a single clean closed-form PDE with perfectly specified boundary conditions. Instead, they are data-assimilative ocean fields influenced by numerical model dynamics, observations, and assimilation procedures. F.6
Compared Methods

We compare our method with baselines from three representative paradigms: physics-informed solvers, neural operator solvers, and generative PDE solvers.

PINN.

Physics-informed neural networks directly parameterize the solution function with a neural network and train it by minimizing initial-condition, boundary-condition, and PDE residual losses. PINNs do not require paired simulation data, but their optimization can become unstable when different loss terms have conflicting scales or gradients.

I-PINN.

I-PINN improves the vanilla PINN framework by combining an enhanced feed-forward architecture with adaptive loss weighting under upper-bound constraints. This design aims to mitigate gradient-flow stiffness and reduce gradient-related training failures. Compared with PINN, I-PINN improves convergence and accuracy without introducing substantial extra computational complexity.

FNO.

Fourier Neural Operator is a data-driven neural operator that learns mappings between function spaces. It uses Fourier-domain integral operator layers to model global dependencies and can be evaluated on different spatial discretizations. In our experiments, FNO serves as a standard data-driven operator-learning baseline.

DeepONet.

DeepONet is a neural operator architecture designed to learn nonlinear operators. It uses a branch network to encode the input function sampled at sensor locations and a trunk network to encode output coordinates. The two representations are combined to predict the output function value. Therefore, DeepONet is categorized as an operator-learning method rather than a standard pointwise neural network.

PINO.

Physics-informed Neural Operator combines supervised operator learning with PDE residual constraints. PINO is built on the FNO framework and imposes physics losses, potentially at higher resolutions than the supervised data. It is a hybrid baseline between data-driven operator learning and physics-informed training.

CFO.

Continuous Flow Operator learns continuous-time PDE dynamics using a flow-matching objective. Instead of autoregressively predicting future states on a fixed time grid, CFO fits temporal splines to trajectory data and trains a neural operator to predict analytic velocity fields. During inference, the learned continuous vector field is integrated with an ODE solver. We classify CFO as a continuous-time neural operator baseline.

DiffusionPDE.

DiffusionPDE formulates PDE solving as generative modeling under partial observation. It models the joint distribution of solution and coefficient fields, allowing it to infer missing information and solve forward or inverse PDE problems. This makes it a representative diffusion-based generative PDE solver.

Rectified Flow.

Rectified Flow learns a conditional velocity field that transports input laws to output solution laws along nearly straight trajectories. Compared with diffusion models that often require many stochastic denoising steps, rectified-flow surrogates use deterministic ODE sampling and can reduce the number of inference steps substantially.

PBFM.

Physics-Based Flow Matching is a physics-constrained generative flow-matching method. It addresses the conflict between distributional fidelity and physical consistency by enforcing physical constraints during training with conflict-free gradient updates. Unlike methods that rely on inference-time correction, PBFM incorporates physics constraints into the generative training process.
