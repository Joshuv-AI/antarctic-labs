Executive Summary
As part of the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale consortium, the Next-Generation Profiles (NextGen Profiles
[NGP]) project presents analysis and results from the characterization of high-power conductive
and wireless charging infrastructure. High Power Charging equipment is capable of recharging
electric vehicle traction batteries at power levels of 200KW and above.

EVSE characterization was conducted over a wide range of DC output current and DC voltage
charging for nominal and off-nominal test conditions. Test plans and procedures were developed
and refined to define the test configurations and requirements, measurement parameters, and test
procedures used throughout NextGen Profiles testing. The EVSE Characterization: A NextGen
Profiles Project Report details test data, outcomes and evaluation related to EVSE performance
characterization activities conducted through September 2023, which covers performance of
EVSE2_H1 and performance of a polyphase WPT system at powers up to 100KW. This report
builds and supplements the 2023 publication by summarizing all activities conducted to date and
presents new data and results collected between October 2023 and September 2024. This report
adds off-nominal temperature performance of EVSE2_H1, characterizes performance of
EVSE1_H1 and the polyphase WPT system on target vehicle platforms at 100KW and 270KW
levels and concordant comparison and analysis.

Key Findings:
 • Conductive Power Transfer:
 o Output Regulation: Both EVSEs accurately track commanded output currents.
 However, ripple performance varies significantly. EVSE1_H1 shows up to 20%
 ripple at light load conditions.
 o Efficiency and Power Factor Trends: Efficiency improves significantly beyond 15%
 rated load for both EVSEs, with the AC-DC conversion stage being the primary
 source of losses. Power factor follows a similar trend, exceeding 95% at higher
 loads. Specifically, EVSE1_H1 achieves this for AC input power transfers exceeding
 100 kW and EVSE2_H1 surpasses 95% above 50 kW AC input.
 o Load management: Power sharing strategies between the two power cabinets impact
 performance, emphasizing the trade-off between centralized and distributed loading
 for both efficiency and long-term reliability.
 o Temperature Sensitivity: Both hot (40°C) and cold (-7°C) ambient conditions limit
 output power. High ambient temperatures lead to rapid connector heating and power
 limiting, while low temperatures appear to reduce coolant effectiveness, contributing
 to derating even when cable temperature limits are not exceeded.
 o Grid Resilience: Both EVSEs maintained uninterrupted operation under off-nominal
 grid conditions, including voltage and frequency deviations and harmonic injections,
 indicating robust grid compatibility.

 o Smart Energy Management (OCPP): All curtailment commands were successfully
 executed, with EVSE responses typically within ±7% of the requested power level.
 Differences in how manufacturers implement curtailment (AC vs DC side) are
 notable for demand response stakeholders. Ramp rates between curtailed and
 restored states varied widely, highlighting design dependent differences.
 • Wireless Power Transfer (WPT):
 o The 100 kW WPT system maintained ~94.4% efficiency and was robust to lateral,
 longitudinal, and angular misalignments.
 o The 270 kW WPT system maintained 95.4–95.56% efficiency at nominal conditions
 and showed minimal efficiency loss under misalignment. Efficiency dropped to
 ~93.5% at a 200 mm ground clearance due to decreased coupling.

These findings from EVSE characterization of high-power charging infrastructure presented in
this report provide data and insight for use by numerous entities including modeling and simulation
organizations, policy makers, fleet planners and industry stakeholders among others involved with
the development and deployment of electrified transportation technologies. Additional high-power
conductive and wireless charging results are anticipated in future publications in support of the
U.S. DOE EVs@Scale consortium NextGen Profiles project.

EVSE Characterization: A NextGen Profiles Project Report

1 EVSE Characterization Introduction
This report provides data and results from the conductive and wireless charging Electric Vehicle
Supply Equipment (EVSE) characterization study performed for the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale
NextGen Profiles project, which aligns with other reports from this project: EVs@Scale NextGen
Profiles: High Level Analysis and Procedures Report (Davidson, Kogalur, Tolbert, Watt, &
Meintz, 2024), EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - EV Profile Capture 2023 (Thurston & Wells,
2023) and EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - Fleet Utilization 2023 (Wells & Thurston, 2023). This
report updates and complements EVSE Characterization, A Next-Gen Profiles Project Report
(Carlson & Onar, 2023).

The high-power charging infrastructure characterization is performed on production and future
charging equipment which includes conductive and non-conductive (wireless power transfer,
WPT) EVSE systems. Using EV emulators, EVSE can be tested under repeatable and controlled
conditions to simulate, isolate and characterize charging behavior in Direct Current (DC) fast
charging systems. This report focuses on EVSE power and system efficiency performance under
varying conditions. Interoperability and reliability are outside the scope of this report.

EVSE operational performance is characterized depending on system type, (conductive or non-
conductive) under varying system conditions (identified as nominal or off-nominal for NGP testing
purposes) and across the full range of system operating DC voltages and currents. Conductive
EVSE characterization aims to explore performance across boundary conditions pertaining to
ambient temperature, alternating current (AC) grid input conditions, and Smart Energy
Management system curtailment requests. Additionally, high-utilization testing was conducted to
quantify the EVSE performance during quick succession, short-duration charge sessions at full
power. For WPT, nominal cases for the previous conditions are used; however, an additional set
of WPT-specific boundary conditions are explored pertaining to misalignment and airgap
scenarios between the EVSE and EV emulator coils.

The nomenclature used in this report enables cross-report comparison. For example, EVSE2_H1
refers to EVSE#2 operating on the CCS-1 liquid-cooled cable, which is the first of two charge
dispenser cables. EVSE2_H2 refers to EVSE#2 operating on the second cable, which is
CHAdeMO. EVSE7 refers to the EVSE#7 operating via a WPT coil. Characterization of the
CHAdeMO charging connector is not evaluated in the NextGen Profiles project.

1.1 Conductive EVSE Characterization Hardware Configuration
EVSE characterization testing focuses on single port charging performance. Multi-
port/simultaneous-session testing was not evaluated. Figure 1 depicts the configuration for EVSE
characterization, including the EVSE power cabinets, EVSE kiosk (dispenser), and an EV
emulator load. EV emulator use enables repeatable testing across a wide range of test conditions
and accelerates testing by obviating time required for battery discharge.

1.3.1 Conductive EVSE Measurement Parameters

Approximately 30 parameters from Table 1 are collected using power analyzers and other
laboratory-grade sensors to quantify the system operation for EVSE characterization. A majority
of the collected parameters are also collected during EV profile capture as described in the EV
Profile Capture report. A few vehicle-specific parameters, such as battery temperature, are omitted

recorded to quantify system performance. The EVSE Unique ID, firmware, and software version
parameters have been omitted, or altered, for anonymity. The Energy Management Source
Curtailment Request is only recorded for sessions that are performed during Smart Charge
Management (SCM) tests.

measurements across multiple EVSEs. Measurement locations are consistent for parameters that
are collected in both EV charge profile tests and the EVSE characterization tests. Figure 6 depicts

The AC electrical input to the HPC EVSE is measured at the input to each power cabinet

touch panels among other loads. These measurements are made at the EVSE auxiliary power
source location. The DC output and the AC auxiliary power measurement locations at the kiosk

Cable and connector temperatures are obtained from the cable manufacturer’s installed thermistors

inside the CCS connector adjacent to the DC pins in the CCS connector. The EVSE power
cabinet’s internal air temperature is obtained from a temperature sensor installed inside each of the
power cabinets.

2.1 Test Conditions
2.1.1 Conductive EVSE Test Conditions

During nominal condition EVSE characterization, emulated vehicle traction battery DC voltage is
set to one of several predefined values listed in Table 4 and DC current is stepped in 10A intervals
from 50A up to the maximum available EVSE DC charging current to capture the entire EVSE
operating performance envelope. For off-nominal EVSE characterization tests, EVSE
performance is collected under conditions where ambient temperature, grid input voltage
characteristics, OCPP Smart Charge Management commands, and EVSE utilization duty-cycle is
varied to collect system performance under a full set of expected real-world operating conditions.

2.2.1 EVSE Power Transfer Characterization at Nominal Conditions

During nominal condition EVSE characterization, emulated vehicle traction battery DC voltage is
set to one of several predefined values listed in Table 4 and DC current is stepped in 10A intervals

shorter than 180 seconds to allow the EVSE operating condition to stabilize into a quasi-steady
state condition. The test procedures and requirements for the quasi-steady-state testing are as
follows:
 • The EVSE will soak at the ambient temperature (23°C ±2°C) for a minimum of four

 temperature, day/time.

 • Each quasi-steady-state DC current and DC voltage test point is held for a minimum of
 180 seconds. This is necessary to ensure the EVSE reaches steady-state operation.
 o Steady state is defined as +/- 0.2 ADC and +/-1 VDC over the final 30 seconds of the

3.1.2 Efficiency Results

As the number of EVs on the road grows, EVSE manufacturers face the challenge of ensuring their
systems support a wide voltage range while maintaining high efficiency. The conversion of AC to
DC power across varying load conditions and DC output voltages can be challenging.

The efficiency curves for EVSE1_H1 and EVSE2_H1 are presented in Figure 12. EVSE2_H1
exhibits consistently higher efficiency than EVSE1_H1 across various power levels. For
EVSE2_H1, peak efficiency occurs at 750VDC between 125KW and 225KW AC input power.
EVSE1_H1 hits peak efficiency at 400V at higher loads.

Both EVSEs show a common trend in Figure 12: efficiency is lower under light load conditions
but improves significantly once the load gets higher than approximately 50KW or 15% of EVSE
nameplate power. This low efficiency at light loads may be due to the loss of zero-voltage
switching which increases losses and reduces overall performance.

One potential strategy to improve efficiency could be to vary the number of power modules in
operation depending on the load. Since power cabinets typically contain multiple parallel power
modules, activating only the necessary number of modules can help maintain operation closer to
the rated power of each module thereby enhancing the system efficiency. EVSE2_H1 utilizes this
coordinated power module control strategy to utilize only the necessary power modules at the
highest power level and efficiency for the given power transfer condition, which is discussed in
more detail in Section 3.1.7 Power Sharing Strategy between Cabinets.

Another slight drop in performance occurs at higher power levels which is particularly noticeable
in EVSE2_H1. Contributors to this loss are increased power electronics conductance losses and
increased resistive losses in the small gauge liquid-cooled CCS cable at high DC current.

Identifying the specific causes of efficiency variations between manufacturers can be challenging
as different power conversion topologies and control methods may be employed. To maximize
efficiency, it is essential that EVSE OEMs design converters with a deep understanding of user
needs and system characteristics so that they operate most efficiently at the power and voltage
levels where they are used most frequently.

Characterization results during nominal operation show the AC-to-DC power transfer efficiency
for the 350kW conductive EVSE1_H1 is 96.0% peak and is >78.3% when the AC power is at least
50KW.

3.1.7 Power Sharing Strategy between Cabinets

As mentioned in Section 1.1 Conductive EVSE Characterization Hardware Configuration, the
EVSE consists of two components: two power towers that convert AC into DC and a dispenser
featuring a CCS connector which connects to the electric vehicle's charging inlet. The EVSE can
distribute supplied power to an EV between the two power towers based on the logic implemented
by the EVSE manufacturer. These towers feature a modular converter structure that can share
power in such a way that the converters operate at their maximum efficiency points and improve
overall system efficiency.

Figure 17 illustrates the power sharing between the two towers for EVSE1_H1 and EVSE2_H1
across various power levels. It is observed for EVSE2_H1 that a single tower taking on full load
share at lower delivered power levels can exhibit higher performance compared to power sharing
that causes lower individual power levels for each tower and thereby lower performance. However,
this approach carries a critical caveat: concentrating the load on a single tower can potentially
increase its operating stress and impact long-term reliability.

In Figure 17, EVSE1_H1 power distribution between the two towers is unequal, maintaining a 4:1
power sharing ratio, where the primary tower transfers more energy than the secondary tower. In

4.1 Nominal Operating Conditions Test Results
Wireless power transfer (WPT) EVSE characterization includes metadata, system specifications
and the test results parameters. Metadata includes ground-side coil dimensions (x, y, or diameter
in mm), vehicle-side coil dimensions (x, y, or diameter in mm), vehicle-side weight if known (kg),
and standby active/reactive power consumption with no vehicle present (W/VAr), and the standby
EM-field with no vehicle present (µT). Test results parameters include (1) the DC output power
(kW) in perfectly aligned condition at nominal airgap, (2) operating frequency, (3) DC input to
DC output efficiency, (4) DC input to DC output efficiency, (5) efficiency at or near nominal
power (%), and (6) EM-field at the edge of the vehicle body in vehicle integrated systems (closest
approach boundary).

For nominal operating conditions, nominal DC output power and efficiency are recorded, and
efficiency is characterized as a function of the output power in 10KW increments. The output uses
resistive loads reflecting the nominal voltage range of the battery packs of the target vehicle
platforms as well battery emulators (constant-voltage loads) reflecting the voltage of the battery at
test conditions. Performance characterization for the off-nominal conditions involve several
misalignment scenarios, including the Δx = ±7.5 cm longitudinal and Δy = ±10 cm lateral
misalignments; angular misalignment conditions, including roll angle (ΔΨ = ±2°), pitch angle (Δθ
= ±2°), and yaw angle (Δφ = ±3°) misalignment conditions; and testing the system at another
airgap condition that is different than the nominal airgap.

Results show that the system efficiency is around 94.4% below nominal conditions at peak power
of 100kW. Under nominal conditions, the wireless EVSE demonstrated similar performance in
lateral and longitudinal misaligned conditions, or their combination for up to 4 inches in any
direction, with no significant drop in efficiency or power transfer (about 0.6% to 0.7% maximum
drop in efficiency). Yaw, pitch, and roll angle misalignments also maintained the same power
transfer rate and efficiency while the yaw angular misalignment slightly increased the efficiency.
The electromagnetic and electric field emissions are also presented at required distances from the
center of the couplers.

Metadata including the coil dimensions and weight and the standby EM-field with no vehicle
present (µT) are presented for the ORNL-developed 270KW WPT EVSE characterization. For the
nominal test conditions, the WPT system’s DC-to-DC efficiency is characterized with output
power in 10KW increments with resistive load conditions reflecting typical battery voltages at the
lower end of the state-of-charge and with constant voltage output condition emulating a battery-
based load with 25KW increments. Off-nominal conditions include test results at increased airgap
above the nominal airgap as well as misalignment conditions as defined in SAE J2954 Standard
including Δx = ±7.5 cm longitudinal and Δy = ±10 cm lateral misalignments. The 270KW system
was also tested for angular misalignment conditions, including roll angle (ΔΨ = ±2°), pitch angle

 41

EVs@Scale: NextGen Profiles: EVSE Characterization 2024

(Δθ = ±2°), and yaw angle (Δφ = ±3°) and the system efficiency and output power did not change
compared to nominal conditions. The system output power and the efficiency are mapped in 3D
plots for x and y axis misalignments up to 7.5cm and 10cm respectively. At nominal conditions
with resistive loads, the 270kW WPT system’s efficiency is between 95.4% and 95.56%,

to be 95.36% with reduced efficiency at light-load conditions in constant voltage output.

5 Conclusion
EVSEs were operated under varied real-world conditions comprising a battery of nominal and off-
nominal tests. This report complements the EVSE Characterization: A NextGen Profiles Project
Report with additional test data, outcomes and evaluation related to EVSE performance by
summarizing all activities conducted to date and presents new data and results collected between
October 2023 and September 2024. This report adds off-nominal temperature performance of
EVSE2_H1, characterizes performance of EVSE1_H1 and the 270KW polyphase WPT system
and concordant comparison and analysis.

Under nominal conditions, both EVSE1_H1 and EVSE2_H1 demonstrated high accuracy in
regulating output current with minimal steady-state error. However, differences emerged in ripple
content, with EVSE1_H1 showing significantly higher ripple – up to 20% – at light loads which
may affect power quality and vehicle battery health. Efficiency performance for both EVSEs
followed a consistent trend, remaining lower at light loads and improving significantly once the
delivered power exceeded approximately 15% of the rated capacity. Similarly, power factor
improved with load, exceeding 95% at higher power levels. EVSE1_H1 achieved this threshold
above 100 kW AC input, and EVSE2_H1 achieved it above 50 kW.

Power sharing strategies among modular converters were found to significantly influence system
performance. Operating power modules within the primary and secondary cabinets at higher load
levels can improve efficiency but also introduces increased thermal and electrical stress on
individual modules, which may impact long-term reliability. These findings underscore the
importance of intelligent load allocation strategies in modular EVSE architectures to balance
performance and durability.

Under off-nominal characterization, thermal testing revealed derating behavior in both hot and
cold conditions. At high ambient temperatures, connector temperatures rose rapidly, triggering
power limitations and sometimes preventing the secondary cabinet from engaging. At low ambient
temperatures (−7°C), derating was observed even when cable temperatures were within safe limits,
likely due to changes in coolant flow properties or viscosity.

Further, grid disturbance testing showed that both EVSEs maintained uninterrupted operation
under all tested off-nominal conditions, including input voltage and frequency deviations and
harmonic injection, indicating good grid robustness.

Power factor behavior was found to be more sensitive to system power level than to input voltage
or ambient temperature. At lower output currents (e.g., 150 A), deviations in AC input voltage had
a more pronounced effect on power factor than at higher currents.

Key observations on OCPP curtailment requests were drawn from the smart charge management
tests. The OCPP 1.6J smart charging commands showed successful execution across all test
scenarios, with power reduction and restoration occurring promptly. However, manufacturer
implementation differences were observed – EVSE1_H1 curtailed current limits on the DC side,

 62

EVs@Scale: NextGen Profiles: EVSE Characterization 2024

while EVSE2_H1 applied control on the AC side. This distinction can lead to different responses
than intended and is important for charging station operators or aggregators so they can
appropriately respond to grid management programs. Ramp rates for transitions varied
significantly across systems, from −566 A/s to +712 A/s, under laboratory conditions where no
ramp limits were implemented in the EV emulators. However, it will likely have a slower response
with commercially available EVs due to dictated current demand slew from the EVCC or
constraints defined by standard charging protocols on the EVSE side.

ORNL’s wireless power transfer characterizations also demonstrated strong nominal performance.
The 100 kW WPT system maintained ~94.4% efficiency at full load and showed resilience to
lateral and angular misalignments up to 4 inches, with minimal efficiency drop. The 270 kW WPT
system achieved 95.4–95.56% efficiency at nominal conditions and exhibited minimal sensitivity
to angular misalignments. However, increasing the ground clearance from the nominal 125 mm to
200 mm resulted in reduced performance, with efficiency decreasing to approximately 93.5% and
output power declining to 207 kW.

Overall, these comprehensive findings from both conductive and wireless high-power charging
systems highlight important performance attributes of the next-generation EVSE technologies.
The results underscore the critical role of testing under several boundary conditions in
understanding system behavior in real-world applications. As EV charging moves toward higher
power levels and broader deployment, optimizing system-level design for reliability, thermal
performance, and grid responsiveness will be essential. Additional high-power conductive and
wireless charging results are anticipated in future publications in support of the U.S. DOE
EVs@Scale consortium NextGen Profiles project.
