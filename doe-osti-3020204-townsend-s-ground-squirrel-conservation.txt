 1.0 INTRODUCTION

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), Hanford Field Office (HFO) conducts ecological
monitoring on the DOE-managed portion of Hanford Site (hereby referred to as Central Hanford)
to collect and track data needed to ensure compliance with an array of environmental laws,
regulations, and policies governing HFO activities. Ecological monitoring data provides baseline
information about the plants, animals, and habitat under HFO stewardship on the Hanford Site,
which is required for accurate ecological impact assessment decision making under the National
Environmental Policy Act and the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and
Liability Act. In addition, ecological monitoring helps ensure that HFO, its contractors, and other
entities conducting activities on the Hanford Site are in compliance with DOE/EIS-0222-F, Final
Hanford Comprehensive Land Use Plan Environmental Impact Statement. HFO places priority
on monitoring plant and animal species or habitats that have specific regulatory protections or
requirements; that are rare and/or declining (federal or state listed endangered, threatened, or
sensitive species); or are of significant interest to federal, state, or tribal governments or to the
public.
DOE/RL-96-32 (2017), Hanford Site Biological Resources Management Plan, is identified by
DOE/EIS-0222-F (1999) as the primary implementation control for managing and protecting
natural resources on the Hanford Site. DOE/RL-96-32 provides a mechanism for assuring
compliance with laws protecting biological resources; provides a framework for ensuring that
appropriate biological resource goals, objectives, and tools are in place to make the DOE an
effective steward of the Hanford biological resources; and implements an ecosystem
management approach for onsite biological resources. DOE/RL-96-32 provides a comprehensive
direction that specifies DOE biological resource policies, goals, and objectives.
DOE/RL-96-32 ranks wildlife species and habitats (Levels 0 to 5) based on the level of concern
for each resource, providing a graded approach to monitoring biological resources. The
Townsend’s ground squirrel (Urocitellus townsendii) is ranked as a Level 3 resource in
DOE/RL-96-32. The management goal for Level 3 resources is conservation, requiring a
moderate level of status monitoring.

1.1 BACKGROUND AND LIFE HISTORY

Townsend’s ground squirrels are found in Washington State only within the Columbia Basin and
only west of the Columbia River. Taxonomical classification of Townsend’s ground squirrels
located on the Hanford Site is historically complex and has changed repeatedly over the years as
ground squirrels of North America were continuously shifted between species, and sub-species
and genus names were changed. Townsend’s ground squirrels were first described in 1839
(Bachman) as Spermophilus townsendii. Over the years, classification of Townsend’s ground
squirrels was revised to Citellus townsendii and then eventually changed back to S. townsendii
(Howell et al. 1938; Nadler 1968).
Starting in the late 1960’s, the Townsend’s ground squirrels located west of the Columbia River
and north of the Yakima River, including the Hanford Site squirrels, were classified as S.
townsendii nancyae, based off karyotype differences from S. t. townsendii squirrels that were
also located west of the Columbia River, but south of the Yakima River (Nadler 1968).

 1-1

The most recent taxonomical reassignment maintained the species delineation that included
categorizing sub-species nancyae with townsendii under S. townsendii. However, this
reassignment considered morphological differences that many of the western ground squirrels,
including nancyae and townsendii, had from old world ground squirrels of the Spermophilus
genus, and shifted these squirrels to the Urocitellus genus where they remain (Helgen et al.
2009).
U.t. nancyae is found north and east of the Yakima River and includes the Hanford Site squirrels.
U.t. townsendii occurs south and west of the Yakima River and is listed by Washington
Department of Fish and Wildlife (WDFW) as a State Candidate species (WDFW 2013; WDFW
2024; Yensen and Sherman 2003). The predicted distribution of Townsend’s ground squirrels
from the Washington State Gap Analysis Project (Johnson and Cassidy 1997) demonstrates that
the Hanford Site represents a significant portion of the expected distribution of the U.t. nancyae
(Figure 1-1).
Ground squirrels are important to the shrub-steppe ecosystem for many reasons. They serve as a
food source for many mammals and birds such as badgers (Taxidea taxus), coyotes (Canis
latrans), hawks (Accipitridae family), falcons (Falconidae family), and owls (Strigidae and
Tytonidae families). The ground squirrel’s diet consists of a variety of foods, including seeds,
which contributes to native plant seed dispersal. The process of digging burrows helps to aerate
the soil and provides burrows for other species, including burrowing owls (Athene cunicularia;
Sato 2012). There has been a regional decline to ground squirrel populations, for multiple
reasons, including the loss of suitable habitat, isolation of their communities through
fragmentation, and control programs involving poisoning and shooting (WDFW 2013).
Ground squirrels are known to rely heavily on grasses for their diet. While the bulk of ground
squirrel diet, approximately 60%, consists of the above ground parts of grasses, stomach contents
analysis has found that ground squirrels also consume forb leaves and seeds, shrub leaves, and
insects at 21%, 16%, and 2%, respectively (Smith and Johnson 1985). In one study, ground
squirrels on the Hanford Site were found to consume mostly Sandberg bluegrass (Poa secunda),
followed by a variety of forbs, including western tansymustard (Descurainia pinnata), lupine
(Lupinus spp.), and long-leaf phlox (Phlox longifolia; Rogers and Gano 1980). Invasive species,
such as cheatgrass (Bromus tectorum), were not found to be significant parts of ground squirrel’s
diets; however, native species dominated the landscape at the time of that study and may have
been preferred or invasives may not have been available in significant quantities. Observations of
current ground squirrel colonies show that they readily eat invasives, such as cheatgrass, at
colonies where it is present. Ground squirrels in the Snake River Birds of Prey area in
southwestern Idaho have been found to use exotic invasive species, such as cheatgrass,
tumbleweed (Salsola tragus), and tumblemustard (Sisymbrium altissimum) as forage (Yensen
and Quinney 1992).

Townsend’s ground squirrels’ life cycle consists of several seasonal components (Figure 1-2).
Typically, during mid- to late January, male ground squirrels begin to emerge from their burrows
after hibernation followed shortly after by females (Smith and Johnson 1985). On this project,
generally a few males begin emerging in mid to late January and continue to emerge into early
February, while females are typically seen emerging within approximately a week of each other
in mid-to late February (HNF-71002; HNF-72656;). However, male squirrels have been seen
active the first week of January on the Hanford Site during particularly mild winters and both
male and female squirrels have emerged as late as the end of February (HNF-72656).
Literature suggests females breed soon after emergence in January and February (Rickart 1987;
Smith and Johnson 1985; Svilha 1939). However, for this project, females have not been
detected prior to the first week of February. The breeding season does not appear to be
synchronous immediately after female emergence, as pups have been recorded to have been born
in the same year between February 19 and March 25, after an approximate gestation period of
23-24 days, putting the breeding season between late January to late-February or early March.
(Michener 1980; Shaw 1925; Svilha 1939). The young typically become active outside the
burrow, shortly after eyes open at three weeks of age, as early as late March and early April and
typically in mid to late April (Smith and Johnson 1985; Svilha 1939). Juveniles on the Hanford
Site have been observed to first emerge from burrows between the first week of April to late
April, and occasionally, even into the first weeks of May. Back dating birth of young by
emergence observations and using an estimated 24-day gestation period, breeding activity for
this project varies substantially by individuals and is assumed to occur between mid-February
and mid-March, with litters born between mid-March and mid-April.
The squirrels become dormant again starting from late May to late June, entering a type of torpor
called estivation that is used to avoid the hot and dry portion of the year (Svilha 1939;
WDFW 2013). Hanford Site observations of squirrels have shown adult males activity
decreasing substantially by late May, with adult females following a few weeks later. Juveniles
have been active longer, likely needing the time to gain enough weight to survive the winter,
with juveniles first observed decreasing activity and entering estivation in early June, but
regularly seen active through late June for some (HNF-71002; HNF-72656). The timing of this
cycle likely varies across the Townsend’s ground squirrel range and may also vary annually due
to local weather conditions. In poor forage years after estivation, ground squirrels may emerge
from torpor and spend late September and October foraging in preparation for over-winter
hibernation (Alcorn 1940; Rickart 1987). Activity in the fall has only been observed in a small
number of squirrels at one time at a single colony on the Hanford Site, although the colonies are
not heavily monitored during this period and prevalence of activity during this time may not be
fully captured (HNF-69169).

In addition to understanding seasonal activity, documenting activity patterns within a day is also
important to knowing when to monitor ground squirrels. Detection data was collected from all
the colony locations from an array used to monitor and identify squirrels (described in 4.1.2) and
combined into a single dataset. To assess activity windows all partial survey days were removed
so that only days with 24 hours of monitoring were included in the analysis. All surveys were
standardized to hours after sunset and 1,897 unique detections occurred. The interquartile range
of squirrel activity occurs between 3.9 and 7.7 hours after sunrise. Figure 1-3 shows the
distribution of activity in relation to hours after sunrise. No squirrel scans occurred between
sunset and sunrise, indicating that Townsend’s ground squirrels are strictly diurnal. This
knowledge has been particularly useful for trapping squirrels. Since they are completely diurnal,
traps are not left overnight to avoid incidental non-target trapping of other species. Additionally,
since activity sharply declines by mid to late-afternoon, trapping activities are focused between
mid-morning and early afternoon during peak activity.

1.2 HISTORY ON THE HANFORD SITE

Although the Hanford Site represents a significant portion of the predicted range of the
U.t. nancyae species (Figure 1-1), it appears to be declining in this area, and ground squirrels are
now largely absent. Reasons for the observed declines on the Hanford Site, and across the region,
are unknown, but contributing factors could be habitat fragmentation, genetic
isolation/inbreeding, disease, and recent extreme weather events. February 2019 was the
snowiest February on record on the Hanford Site, totaling 64.2 cm (25.3 in.) of snowfall with
over 35.6 cm (14.0 in.) of snow accumulation that sustained on the ground for an extended
period of time (HMS 2022). Anecdotal information from landowners and agency personnel in
the area suggests that year’s extreme weather coincided with the disappearance or severe
diminishment of ground squirrel colonies across the region. That year, squirrels would have
emerged from hibernation to snow covered ground with no vegetation available to help rebuild
fat reserves. Conversely, spring 2021 was extremely dry with some of the driest months on
record occurring during that period; only 0.15 cm (0.06 in.) of rain were recorded from March
through May. Drought is known to impact ground squirrels through reduced body mass,
decreased overwinter survival (particularly of juveniles), and delayed onset of breeding or even
failure to produce young within the drought year (Smith and Johnson 1985; Van Horne et al.
1997). These extreme weather events may have further suppressed the already-vulnerable
remaining colonies of Townsend’s ground squirrels.
Historically, multiple colonies existed across the Hanford site (HNF-53075; HNF-56374). Since
CY 2013, no additional colonies have been detected despite surveys of quality ground squirrel
habitat (HNF-68254), and known colony occupation has declined dramatically, with only five

 1-6

active colonies (the Vineyard, Clay Cliff, Goose Egg, Gator, and 300 Area; Figure 1-4) in CY
2015 (HNF-59911) and just three in CY 2017 (Vineyard, Goose Egg, and Gator; HNF-68254).
During spring 2021, prior to the ground squirrel estivation period, surveyors navigated to the
centroid of three colonies documented as active in CY 2017 (Goose Egg, Gator, and Vineyard),
as well as all the colonies previously described as inactive, to survey for squirrels. Results from
surveys of ground squirrel colonies on the Hanford Site showed a drastic decline in remaining
colonies, with only a single active colony found in CY 2021 (Gator; HNF-68254). With only one
colony remaining on the Hanford Site, there was a significant risk that a single catastrophic event
(e.g., fire, disease, drought) would eliminate the colony. In CY 2021, a plan was initiated to
select a site and design and construct an enclosure with artificial burrow systems (ABS) on the
Hanford Site to support translocation of ground squirrels. A potential donor colony (i.e., a colony
considered as pests on private land) of Townsend’s ground squirrels, within the range of the U.t.
nancyae, was discovered in agricultural land near Sunnyside, Washington.
In CY 2022, construction was completed on an enclosure located on the Hanford Site to house
ground squirrels, and two new colonies of Townsend’s ground squirrels were established on the
Hanford Site through translocation (HNF-69169). The first colony (Holocek) was comprised of
individuals trapped and translocated from the previously identified donor colony and released via
soft release methods (i.e., providing temporary shelter and some supplemental foods) to a site
near the White Bluffs bank. The second colony (Army Loop Enclosure) was also comprised of
individuals trapped and translocated from the same donor colony and released via soft release
(i.e., semi-permanent shelter and protection from predators by a fence, and fully supplemented
foods) to the newly completed enclosure.
Due to the discovery in CY 2021 that only a single colony remained on the Hanford Site (Gator),
the colony was monitored throughout CY 2022 for signs of activity and only a single squirrel
was identified that year – an adult female in post-lactating condition. It was determined that, due
to the low number of squirrels expected to still occupy this location, providing support for this
remaining colony would be necessary to allow for population recovery via a method termed
“genetic rescue” (Frankham 2015). Genetic rescue involves introducing new genetics to an
isolated population to improve diversity which can result in higher survival rates and improved
long-term viability. Therefore, in CY 2023, squirrels were translocated from the same donor
population used to establish the two translocated colonies to supplement the remaining native
squirrels and help re-establish the colony with a healthy population (HNF-71002).
Since that time, all three colonies have been closely monitored and received additional
translocated squirrels (HNF-72656). Ground squirrels are adept at kin recognition and are known
to avoid breeding with close relatives (Holmes and Sherman 1982; Mateo 2003); therefore, it is
essential to keep movement of outside individuals coming into the colony. This is especially
important to a dispersing species, where significant numbers of squirrels may disperse in a given
year to avoid inbreeding and search for mating opportunities. Due to the isolation of Hanford
Site colonies, ongoing releases are intended to simulate natural immigration that would occur
between adjacent colonies, helping to avoid inbreeding and improve genetic diversity. The focus
of monitoring and translocation is to preserve and grow the single remaining historic colony
onsite and continue working towards all translocated colonies becoming self-supporting.

1.3 OBJECTIVES

The primary objective in CY 2025 for the Army Loop Enclosure colony was to provide a
foundational shift from colony establishment coupled with supplemental support (started in
2022) to a self-supporting colony by the end of 2026. This objective is accomplished through
extensive monitoring and population modeling of colonies established through translocation
while weaning colonies off supplemental care. However, additional opportunistic translocations
of squirrels to augment the newly established colonies allows simulated immigration of new
squirrels into the colony to boost reproduction and keep the population genetically diverse.
Due to a depredation even in CY 2024 where a badger attacked the Holocek colony during the
critical time prior to vulnerable juveniles emerging from the natal burrows, objectives for that
colony were slightly different. Goals included monitoring to understand the full extent of loss
from the depredation event and additional translocations to help re-build the colony.
In CY 2025, the primary objective for the Gator colony was to evaluate the longer-term benefits
to the population from the translocation of new individuals to the site in CY 2023. Other
objectives included more translocations to continue boosting the population and, as previously
described, simulate immigration of new squirrels to the colony, since no nearby colonies exist
that would allow for dispersal in between.
Additional objectives included conducting burrow counts at existing populations, surveying to
see if squirrels are dispersing from colonies and surviving in new locations, and providing
supplemental support to all colonies on the Hanford Site, as necessary.
These objectives will contribute to a comprehensive understanding of factors that may affect the
stability of Hanford Site ground squirrel colonies. Ecological monitoring results will provide the
necessary biological information for adaptive management and provide important information to
offsite resource managers who make region-wide management decisions concerning Townsend’s
ground squirrels.

 2.0 ENVIRONMENTAL CONDITIONS

Environmental conditions at each of the colony sites impact the activity, behavior, and health of
the ground squirrels residing there. Temperature and precipitation can significantly impact
post-winter emergence dates, subsequently altering the breeding season and ultimately, the
reproductive output of a colony. Temperature and precipitation can also impact the quality and
quantity of available vegetative food sources for the colony.
In addition to yearly fluctuations in vegetation due to environmental factors, vegetation varies at
each ground squirrel colony in both available species and abundance. The Holocek and Gator
sites had natural vegetation prior to squirrel releases, thus, they are expected to have adequate
vegetation to support a ground squirrel colony. Conversely, the Army Loop Enclosure site is
sparsely vegetated due to revegetation efforts in CY 2022 that were deemed unsuccessful in CY
2023. Revegetation efforts were supplemented in CY 2024.

2.1 TEMPERATURE AND PRECIPITATION

On average, CY 2025 was cooler during January and February, slightly warmer during March,
and close to 10-year average temperatures from April through July. The 2025 water year saw
above average amounts of precipitation compared to the 10-year average (Figure 2-1 and Figure
2-2). The beginning of the calendar year is a key time in the breeding season of Townsend’s
ground squirrels, and warmer and wetter environmental conditions earlier in the active season
may contribute to earlier emergence/litters and better forage availability, which could contribute
to higher juvenile and adult weights entering estivation. Conversely, hot and dry conditions later
in the active season may lead to squirrels entering estivation early and with lower body weight.
Additionally, there is evidence that squirrels will forgo breeding during drought years (Smith and
Johnson 1985; Van Horne et al. 1997).

2.2 AVAILABLE VEGETATION

In CY 2025, vegetation surveys were conducted at the Gator and Army Loop Enclosure sites
(Appendix A). Additionally, subjective vegetation abundance and quality were periodically
assessed at each colony site to ensure that vegetation is available in sufficient quantities to
support the colonies.
Army Loop Enclosure – At the Army Loop Enclosure site, the colony resides completely within a
revegetated area. The area outside of the revegetated area is dominated by cheatgrass. Within the
revegetated area in CY 2025, the most abundant species were Russian thistle, Sandberg’s
bluegrass, cheatgrass, tumblemustard, and western tansymustard. Squirrels are often seen
foraging on any available grasses and dragging clippings back to their burrows. In addition to
grasses, squirrels were also observed foraging on available vegetation, including young Russian
thistle, and sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata) and gooseberryleaf globemallow (Sphaeralcea
grossulariifolia) that occur inside the enclosure but were not picked up in plot frames used to
estimate vegetative coverage and frequency.
Gator – At the Gator site, the colony resides adjacent to a revegetated area. Therefore,
revegetation monitoring results describe area next to the actual colony, but within range of
potential burrows or movement of squirrels into the area to forage. This revegetation area has
been mostly taken over by cheatgrass and Russian thistle, but Sandberg’s bluegrass is present.
Within the actual colony, vegetative composition is different than the revegetation area and
squirrels are frequently seen foraging on cheatgrass and fiddleneck (Amsinckia lycopsoides), and
both species are regularly seen clipped and drug into burrows. Other common species seen in the
area of the colony that are likely to be consumed include Siberian wheatgrass (Agropyron

Russian thistle. Like the Army Loop Enclosure, not all species utilized by squirrels were
captured in the plot frames used to estimate vegetative cover and frequency.

 3.0 COLONY MAINTENANCE

Existing, artificial habitat resources exist at all three colonies on the Hanford Site. These
resources are designed to provide shelter and protection from predators during the transition
from translocation of individuals to establishment of the individual at the site. In addition to
aiding in the settlement of translocated squirrels, these resources provide the same shelter and
protection for squirrels born onsite at these locations. Maintenance improvements are made to
these resources each year.
Another element of colony maintenance is supplemental feeding. Supplemental feeding was
originally planned to aid the transition of the squirrels translocated from agricultural fields to the
Hanford Site colonies. The ground squirrels translocated from vineyards are accustomed to a
different diet (e.g., irrigated agricultural crops and weeds) than the natural habitats where they
are being released, and they may require a larger proportion of supplemental food and a longer
transition period to the native vegetation available onsite. Project objectives aim to reduce and
eliminate supplemental feeding to shift the colonies towards self-sustainment once translocated
squirrels have established site fidelity; however, the unavailability of natural food sources at the
Army Loop Enclosure site has extended the time supplemental feeding is needed.

3.2 SUPPLEMENTAL FEEDING

Revegetation efforts have been conducted to some degree at both the Army Loop Enclosure and
Gator sites, to provide a greater abundance of preferred native food items. In the case of the
Army Loop Enclosure site, the revegetation effort included the removal of all vegetation at the
site and the seeding and planting of native vegetation, resulting in a need to supplement the
squirrels at that site with food until native vegetation could be established.
Supplemental feeding is commonplace in ground squirrel translocation actions and was
performed during a USFWS project with food items provided in late winter supplemental
feeding. Supplemental feeding included black oil sunflower seeds distributed weekly from
February to March and peanuts, carrots, and black sunflower seeds distributed weekly from June
to early July (Whitfield 2019). The original supplemental feeding plan for this project consisted
of a timothy hay-based, pelleted rabbit diet from Mazuri,® 1 carrots, and black oil sunflower seeds
on an as-needed basis, typically post-translocation.
Since translocations of squirrels to the Hanford Site began in CY 2022, some form of
supplemental feed has taken place at each of the three colonies. The need for supplemental
feeding is evaluated continuously, and adjustments are made yearly and within the year.

 4.0 POPULATION MONITORING AND SUPPLEMENTATION

Monitoring is key to understanding the status and population dynamics of ground squirrel
colonies. Townsend’s ground squirrels are underground for much of the year for hibernation and
estivation, making it crucial to monitor during the correct times of year. The critical window to
observe and monitor these ground squirrels is between mid-January, when the first males emerge
from hibernation, and late June when the last juveniles enter estivation. This period is typically
when all activity for the year occurs.

4.1 METHODS

Monitoring of the Hanford Site ground squirrels is conducted between emergence and estivation
and is categorized into passive and active monitoring. Each type of monitoring provides different
kinds of information and when combined produces data that describes the status and dynamics of
each colony’s population. Additionally, ongoing monitoring allows for in-season adaptive
management, such as supplementing the population through translocation. For example, a
smaller than expected number of squirrels seen early in the season could trigger a translocation
of adults, particularly pregnant females, to help boost juvenile output at that site. Alternatively,
monitoring juvenile output at each colony later in the season may help determine what
proportion of late season translocated squirrels should go to each site. Monitoring and
translocations go hand in hand for building stable colonies.

4.1.1 Passive Monitoring

Passive ground squirrel monitoring conducted at each colony is categorized as observational.
Visual site surveys and camera traps are employed each year and can be done as frequently as
needed and without disruption to the squirrels. Burrow counts are conducted once per year at
each colony to document the extent of activity and are paired with intuitive surveys searching for
dispersed squirrels. The benefit of passive monitoring includes the collection of information
without stress or disturbance to the squirrels, with less overall effort than active monitoring.
Limitations of passive monitoring include the inability to identify individuals, and lack of
specific data collection, such as weight data, and inaccurate population estimates.
Site Surveys – Site surveys began early in the year to determine when emergence from
hibernation occurred and when the colony became active for the year and continued through the
active season. Routine visual surveillance took place when a site was visited (e.g., during
revegetation work or supplemental feeding). Visual observations were made with binoculars
from the road, approximately 15 to 30 m (50 to 100 ft) away from the colonies. If squirrels were
seen, the total count was noted. In general, the ground squirrels would seek shelter in burrows
when we arrived, so counts were usually done immediately upon arrival or after the work activity
was completed, when observers returned to the road and as the ground squirrels would re-
emerge. The date, time, and total number of adult and juvenile squirrels were recorded at each
visit.
When no squirrels were observed during a site visit, surveyors conducted walk downs of the
colony looking for sightings of individuals while also listening for alarm calls. During the walk
downs, burrows identified the previous year were examined for signs of activity, including fresh

 4-19

digging and absence of spider webs covering the entrances, and inspections were completed
looking for newly constructed burrows.
Camera Traps – Camera traps were used to capture photographic evidence of live ground
squirrels during important periods such as emergence from hibernation and juvenile emergence
(Figure 4-1). The cameras used for this project were the Reconyx® 2 Ultrafire XP9S and the
PC900 HyperFire™ professional trail cameras. These cameras took color photos during the day
and used an invisible infrared flash for unintrusive photographing at night. The camera triggered
when an object with a different temperature than the ambient temperature moves through the
camera frame. Cameras were generally set on the high trigger sensitivity, taking three photos per
trigger, 24 hours per day. No interval was used between photos (i.e., the Rapidfire setting), and
no quiet period was selected between trigger events. Each photo was time and date stamped.

 Figure 4-1. Camera Trap Setup to Monitor Ground Squirrel Activity.
Burrow Counts – Burrow counts for each Hanford Site colony and the Sunnyside Vineyard
donor colony were conducted during the breeding season, after male and female squirrels had
emerged from hibernation and prior to juvenile emergence. At Army Loop and Holocek, the
centroid coordinates of the colonies were loaded onto a Trimble® 3 Global Positioning System
(GPS) unit with sub-meter accuracy along with buffers placed 100 m (328 ft) from the centroids.

4.1.2 Active Monitoring

Active monitoring centers around the initial capture of a squirrel, the implantation of a passive
integrated transponder (PIT) tag, and subsequent recapture or detection of the squirrel via the
PIT tag. Trapping occurs each year at various times, typically post-winter emergence, during
translocations, and post-juvenile emergence from the natal burrows. Benefits of active
monitoring include the collection of large amounts of high-quality data, such as individual
survival and weights and the ability to make more accurate population level estimates.
Limitations include the need to have many initial captures to tag each animal for ongoing
monitoring, the large effort it takes to recapture individuals, and the need to continuously move
equipment used to monitor tagged individuals in order to cover the entire site of each colony.
Trapping – Live animal traps, such as Tomahawk or Havahart® 4 cage style traps or Sherman® 5
folding traps were used for trapping. Open sections of PVC pipe 7.6 cm in diameter by 1.5 m in
length (3.0 in. in diameter by 5.0 ft in length) were also used to capture ground squirrels. Ground
squirrels use the pipes as refuge, even when un-baited, and surveyors can tip the pipes up on end
and capture the ground squirrels inside a pillowcase covering one end of the pipe. Traps were set
near active burrow entrances and baited with carrots and black oil sunflower seeds. Each trap
was checked regularly throughout the day and closed each night due to the squirrel’s strictly
diurnal activity. Trapping was avoided if temperatures were elevated to avoid heat stress.
Actively trapping individual squirrels allow for the collection of demographic data, such as age,
sex, weight, reproductive status, and the implantation of the AVID® 6 PIT tag during the initial
capture. Subsequent recaptures provide updated data on weight and reproductive status, as well
as ongoing data on survival and colony structure.
Array – In addition to trapping squirrels, a PIT tag scanning array (referred to as the array) was
used to detect specific squirrels without the need for handling after first capture. Since all
captured ground squirrels are implanted with a microchip PIT tag during their initial capture, an
array consisting of a Biomark Small-Scale Monitoring System with an ASR650 reader and a
Bluetooth® 7 low-energy data logger with six sensing wands was deployed to facilitate
identification of squirrels with embedded PIT tags. Array wand heads have openings of
7.6 cm (3 in.) or 15.2 cm (6 in.) and 15 m (50 ft) wire cables that can be placed at burrow
entrances or over PVC pipes to read the PIT tags of the ground squirrels as they move around
their colony site. Protective sleeves were placed over the wire cables to prevent squirrels from
chewing on the cables. Proprietary software, Biomark Device Manager v.1.2.29, is used to
connect to and interact with the array. The array data is downloaded every time the unit is moved
to a new location. The array provides an additional tool to passively monitor the squirrels
without further disturbance after the initial capture.

4.3 POPULATION RETENTION AND ESTIMATES

Information collected during array deployment and at trapping events at different times of the
year helps us to understand demographics of the squirrels retained over winter and through the
breeding season. The terms “retained”, and “retention” are used versus “survived” and “survival”
since it cannot be determined if squirrels are absent because of mortality or dispersal.
Retention rates of squirrels of different age classes are used to build an estimated adult
population size at each colony for the following year. Creating a rough estimate of the population
size assists in building monitoring plans and determining early season management activities for
each colony for the next year.
CY 2025 Estimates vs. Actual – Population estimates for CY 2025 (HNF-72656) were compared
to actual numbers in CY 2025 (Table 4-8). The expected population at the Army Loop Enclosure
was overestimated, with only 9 adult squirrels seen during the breeding season. The actual
number seen, particularly less than expected males, was possibly due to limited early season
monitoring, and present males may have dispersed before they could be counted. Additionally,
there may have been higher than anticipated dispersal from competition over resources from
other species.
Conversely, the population of the Gator colony was underestimated, due to higher-than-expected
retainment of yearling females between CY 2024 and CY 2025. High female retainment may
have been influenced by available unoccupied burrows and other resources from the previously
small population at that site. Like the Army Loop Enclosure colony, limited early season
monitoring may have prevented detection of some males at the site. Significant early season
monitoring is important to establishing an accurate picture of the breeding population at each
site, which could influence management decisions, such as translocating additional breeding
individuals.

each colony. Total squirrels for each cohort were calculated and an estimated breeding
population for each colony for CY 2026 was estimated (Table 4-11). With these estimates, we

Actual retention may vary from the averages if unforeseen events occur, such as a predation
event or heavy dispersal due to lack of resources, which could result in fewer retained
individuals. The ability to determine the accuracy of these estimates in CY 2026 will depend on
the level of monitoring effort at each site early in the year. Lower levels of effort may result in
missed identification of individuals residing in the colony, especially those that may breed and
then disperse. As more data is collected, estimate accuracy should increase along with the ability
to apply estimates more specifically to specific demographic groups.

 5.0 MANAGEMENT IMPLICATIONS

Due to a significant decline in Townsend’s ground squirrel populations on both the Hanford Site
and in the region, efforts were made to preserve the single remaining colony onsite, the Gator
colony, and to establish two additional colonies, the Army Loop Enclosure and the Holocek
colonies, through translocation. Each colony receives various levels of support determined by
need and with the goal of transitioning them into self-supporting populations. Monitoring each
colony provides information to assess activities and adjust management plans to provide this
support. The extent of support that the Army Loop Enclosure colony will require to become self-
supporting and whether the Gator colony will need additional support to stabilize is yet to be
determined but will require ongoing monitoring and likely additional supplemental
translocations. Further, monitoring allows evaluation of achievements and deficiencies and
advances adjustments to management of these populations. Based on monitoring activities in CY
2025, the following ongoing monitoring and management activities should continue in CY 2026:
 • Ongoing colony maintenance and improvement through repairs of artificial structures and
 relocation of resources within and between colonies. Addition of new resources for the
 colonies, such as additional PVC pipes and drill-dug burrows following additional
 translocation efforts.
 • Ongoing supplemental feeding on an as-needed basis, dependent on the success of
 ongoing revegetation efforts and resulting natural food availability for the Army Loop
 Enclosure colony. The Gator site contains adequate vegetation to support a ground
 squirrel colony, and supplemental feeding is not conducted at these sites. If extreme
 weather (e.g., drought, late snow accumulations) occur, supplemental feeding would
 occur at these two locations to prevent starvation until the extreme weather event
 subsides.
 • Burrow count surveys at active Hanford colonies to map distribution of burrows at each
 site and compare with other population monitoring data to look for relationships between
 the number of burrows and the population size. Continue burrow count surveys at the
 Sunnyside Vineyard donor colony to monitor for impacts to the donor population from
 ongoing translocations and to determine whether the colony remains a sufficient source
 for those translocations.
 • Dispersal is suspected to be a significant source of the loss of squirrels at the primary
 colony locations. Intuitive surveys looking for potential dispersed squirrels or burrows
 along likely travel routes away from onsite colonies and in areas with habitat that would
 support ground squirrels within dispersal distance of the active colonies. Finding
 dispersed squirrels could help determine whether translocated squirrels and squirrels born
 onsite are emigrating from the established colonies, surviving elsewhere, and creating
 new colonies.
 • Monitoring throughout the active season of each onsite colony to determine population
 estimates, demographics, biometric data, and overall population health. Continue
 post-juvenile emergence to examine juvenile recruitment, collect biometric data, and
 implant PIT tags. Ongoing implantation of PIT tags in juveniles is necessary to accurately
 monitor the population and conduct passive monitoring through the array. Collectively,

 5-44

 monitoring information helps determine success of the translocations; retainment of
 individuals at each site; and ultimately, survival of the colonies.
 • Continued supplemental releases of translocated squirrels from the Sunnyside Vineyard
 donor colony. Both Hanford ground squirrel colonies function as genetic islands due to
 their disjunct distribution (greater than 10 miles apart), which prevents dispersal between
 the colonies. This type of isolation can result in increased levels of inbreeding and
 genetic drift and is known to cause reduced genetic diversity over time (Frankham 2015).
 Additionally, squirrels are known to disperse from colonies to avoid mating with close
 relatives; with no other colonies to go to, these dispersed squirrels risk isolation and
 predation away from the colony, both of which result in decreased populations.
Adaptive management is used for ground squirrel conservation efforts on the Hanford Site.
Information obtained from other agencies, academic research, and additional sources was used to
create an initial management plan for Townsend’s ground squirrels on the Hanford Site. As new
data is collected or unexpected events occur, plans are adapted accordingly. Adaptive
management depends on continued monitoring and is essential to capitalize on early successes
and achieve healthy, self-supporting populations on the Hanford Site.
Monitoring in CY 2025 showed that efforts to rescue the few remaining squirrels in the Holocek
colony after the previous year’s badger depredation event, had failed. Efforts were conducted in
CY 2024 to re-populate the site with a large number of translocated squirrels, however,
monitoring showed only two males remained in CY 2025, and the site was declared inactive.
This event shows the importance of having multiple established colonies onsite to minimize the
impact of catastrophic events. This type of event has the potential to reduce an early-stage colony
to a low enough population so that natural recovery would be improbable without mitigation
efforts such as additional animals being translocated.
After establishing a colony of squirrels at the Army Loop Enclosure in CY 2022, the site has
seen an abundance of ancillary wildlife, including predators, such as badgers, coyote, and
raptors, and other species that may benefit from the squirrel’s presence, especially their extensive
burrow systems, such as black-tailed jackrabbits and burrowing owls. Burrowing owls have even
established residency and began nesting at the Army Loop Enclosure since it was established.
The introduction of squirrels to that site and the subsequent ecological recovery to that specific
area exemplifies the importance of squirrels to the ecosystem. However, the Army Loop
Enclosure colony, while previously maintaining a stable population size, was found to have
declined substantially in CY 2025. While it is unclear if this is due to dispersal, mortality, or a
combination of the two factors, there is some evidence that the decline is at least partially
influenced by an increased presence of burrowing owls at the site. Burrowing owls were first
observed at the Enclosure in CY 2023 using above ground drainpipes and have increased since
then including occupying ABS sites at the Army Loop Enclosure. The decrease in squirrel
population and decline in total active squirrel burrows, notably in the area around the owl-
occupied ABS sites, has coincided with the increased burrowing owl presence. It is unclear if the
owls are preying on juvenile squirrels or if the increased presence of burrowing owls and their
occupancy of ABS structures in the enclosure is utilizing enough resources intended for squirrels
that they are triggering increased dispersal away from the enclosure. In coming years, it will be
important to identify and address the cause of population decline at the Army Loop Enclosure in
a way to grow the population to a point that it can sustain its size, allowing the colony to thrive
while balancing their unique ecological niche that supports these other species.

 5-45

Following a moderately successful translocation of squirrels to the Gator colony in CY 2023 and
breeding season in CY 2024, the Gator colony saw substantial growth in CY 2025 with better
than anticipated retention of adults and high juvenile output. With the growing population, new
burrows popped up spreading outward from the colony center and both badger and burrowing
owls were seen at the site for the first time. While this colony is still recovering and is not fully
self-supporting, the larger size of the population and spread of the colony provides some
protection and makes the colony less sensitive to disturbances. Continued monitoring of both
badgers and burrowing owls and their impact on the colony, as well as new management actions
if needed, will ensure the colony successfully balances its survival while fulfilling its ecological
role.
Based on new information learned from CY 2025 monitoring and management activities, certain
key efforts should be reinforced, and other new management activities should be implemented.
Suggested focused monitoring and management activities and suggested new activities in CY
2026 include:
 • Evaluate the need for additional ground squirrel colonies on the Hanford Site. The loss of
 the Holocek colony exemplifies the need to have multiple colonies on Site to mitigate
 colony-eliminating catastrophic events. Additionally, with both Hanford Site colonies
 isolated geographically, the long-term goal of self-supporting colonies may rely on new
 colonies or satellite colonies being established within dispersal distance.
 • Identify alternate donor colony locations to source squirrels for translocations. With only
 a single donor colony identified, there is risk of losing access to a source of squirrels for
 ongoing simulated immigration or as a means of supplementing the Hanford Site colonies
 if the population numbers drop from unforeseen events, such as a predation event.
 Additionally, with low population numbers and colonies in the growing phase, new donor
 sources could provide genetic diversity which could be beneficial to a small population.
 • Post-winter emergence monitoring of the Army Loop Enclosure and Gator colonies to
 focus on a combination of trapping and passive monitoring of squirrels via the array that
 records PIT tags that pass within a short distance of attached wands. Trapping will allow
 the processing, including insertion of a PIT tag, for any yearlings that were missed during
 juvenile captures in CY 2025. Monitoring via the array should throughout the active
 season would identify the continued presence of individuals and the arrival or departure
 of other individuals.
 • Conduct radio tracking of collared squirrels to obtain dispersal, retention, and survival
 data. Not all chipped squirrels that are active in a colony each year are trapped or
 detected on the PIT tag array after winter emergence the following year during
 monitoring. The largest data gap for ground squirrels on the Hanford Site is determining
 if squirrels that are not detected again are experiencing mortality over the winter or if
 they are dispersing prior to entering summer estivation or soon after post-winter
 emergence, and if so, where are they dispersing. Determining the outcome of dispersal
 attempts by squirrels is critically important to understand how dispersal will influence
 establishment of self-supporting colonies on site. Tracking dispersing squirrels with radio
 transmitters would help to determine dispersal distances, locations, and outcomes.
 • Modify or establish new artificial resources at the Army Loop Enclosure to support
 multiple species residing at the site. The addition of resources specifically to support

 5-46

 burrowing owls nearby, but outside of the enclosure, could help squirrels regain needed
 burrow structures. Additional artificial burrows, such as sunken drainpipes, could
 encourage squirrels to establish their own burrow systems at the enclosure rather than
 dispersing elsewhere and adding ABS outside of the enclosure may help naturally
 relocate burrowing owls and free up the ABS in the enclosure for squirrels to occupy.
 Monitor the Gator colony site to determine if there is need to provide additional artificial
 resources to support the arrival of burrowing owls to the site.
• Evaluate burrowing owl pellets to see if they’re eating ground squirrels. While pellets are
 routinely scanned for PIT tags, burrowing owls would likely only be able to prey on
 small juvenile squirrels, which often do not have PIT tags as they are generally only
 captured and processed once they are a bit older.
• Monitor colony sites for the presence of badgers during the breeding season when
 juvenile squirrels would be helpless underground. Chain link fencing over burrows was
 determined to be an effective badger deterrent with no observed impact to the squirrels
 and could be deployed on short notice at the arrival of a badger.
• Begin annual vegetation surveys to characterize available forage at each of the colony
 sites, including species and general abundance, to determine what vegetation is available
 to the squirrels and whether sufficient natural vegetation is available to sustain the
 populations without supplemental feeding. Vegetation surveys, as opposed to
 revegetation monitoring, would provide more applicable data on vegetation availability
 and use by the squirrel colonies.
