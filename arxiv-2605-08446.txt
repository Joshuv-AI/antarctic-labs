Direct Bethe Free Energy Minimization for Bayesian Neural Network
Pavel Prochazka

Abstract
We propose training Bayesian neural networks by directly minimizing the Bethe free energy rather than maximizing a variational lower bound. On tree-structured factor graphs the Bethe free energy is exact; deterministic layers drop out of the objective and are trained by standard backpropagation, so the framework accommodates any mixture of probabilistic and deterministic subgraphs without modification. Restricting the weight posterior to a last-layer Gaussian yields analytically tractable losses: for a Gaussian likelihood the Bethe loss equals the exact marginal likelihood, and for a probit likelihood it reduces to a closed form via the probit–Gaussian convolution. Both objectives sit strictly between MAP and the ELBO ( ℒ MAP ≤ ℒ Bethe ≤ ℒ ELBO \mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} ), removing the structural Jensen gap that no choice of variational family can close. The Z-consistent prior formulation makes the prior precision a differentiable parameter, enabling empirical Bayes — joint optimization of weights, covariance, and hyperparameters — in a single gradient pass, with no cross-validation or outer loop. All variants admit a closed-form predictive at MAP-equivalent inference cost, in contrast to ensemble and sampling-based methods. On 8 UCI regression and 12 UCI classification benchmarks evaluated under a single shared hyperparameter regime, Bethe is competitive with standard reference methods at single-pass cost. Independently, joint single-pass empirical Bayes matches grid-search cross-validation of the prior precision on essentially all dataset–variant combinations, eliminating the outer hyperparameter loop without measurable cost. Isolated optimization gaps on a few datasets reflect numerical rather than principled limitations of the framework.



1
Introduction

Bayesian neural networks are typically trained by maximising the evidence
lower bound (ELBO)—a lower bound on the log-evidence obtained by Jensen’s
inequality.
The gap is structural: it closes only when the approximate posterior
q

equals the exact posterior, a condition never met in practice.
A different objective arises from asking whether beliefs at neighbouring
variables in the model agree—a local consistency condition.
Beliefs satisfying this condition minimise the Bethe free
energy
(
12
;
8
)
.
On tree-structured factor graphs, local consistency is not merely a useful
proxy: it is sufficient for exactness, the Bethe optimum is the true
posterior, and the Jensen gap disappears.

What direct Bethe minimisation unlocks.

The Bethe free energy is usually treated as the objective that belief
propagation implicitly minimises, accessible only through iterative message
passing.
We make it the explicit training loss and minimise it by gradient descent.
For tree-structured graphs with last-layer Gaussian posteriors and Gaussian
or probit likelihoods, the objective admits a closed form, and three
practical consequences follow in a single gradient pass:
(i)
empirical Bayes
—joint optimisation of weights, covariance, and
prior hyperparameters with no outer cross-validation loop, because the
Z-consistent prior renders hyperparameters differentiable;
(ii)
MAP-equivalent inference cost
—the predictive is closed-form,
so a single deterministic forward pass replaces MC sampling, ensembling, or
Hessian inversion;
(iii)
a tighter optimisation geometry
—the objective sits strictly
between MAP and ELBO,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}
,
removing the structural Jensen gap that no choice of variational family can.

The gap in current approaches.

Variational inference carries a Jensen gap that no choice of
q
removes;
post-hoc methods (Laplace, BLL, NNGP) shape features at MAP without
probabilistic feedback; and sampling methods (MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles)
require multiple forward passes at inference.
Belief propagation
(
12
)
does minimise the Bethe objective,
but only via iterative message passing—not end-to-end differentiable,
hence unsuitable as a training loss.
No existing method delivers all three properties above in a single
training-and-inference pass.
Section
6
gives a per-method comparison.

This paper.

We minimise the Bethe free energy by gradient descent on factor graphs whose
final layer carries a Gaussian weight posterior

q(w)=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
over a deterministic backbone

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{H}
.
For Gaussian likelihoods, the Bethe objective equals the
exact

marginal likelihood (Theorem
1
); for probit likelihoods, the
probit–Gaussian convolution gives a closed-form loss
(Theorem
2
).
Figure
1
illustrates the qualitative effect:
on the Two-Moons benchmark, Bethe produces calibrated uncertainty that
expands away from data, while MAP is overconfident and Laplace is
poorly scaled—all three at the same architecture and inference cost.

Contributions:

Direct Bethe minimisation as a training objective.

A gradient-based framework for minimising the Bethe free energy in
BNNs (Section
3
), bypassing iterative belief
propagation and covering any mixture of probabilistic and deterministic
subgraphs.

Single-pass empirical Bayes.

The Z-consistent prior renders prior precision (and other structural
hyperparameters) differentiable, so empirical Bayes happens in the same
gradient pass that trains the model—no cross-validation, no
marginal-likelihood approximation, no outer loop.
Empirically, EB matches grid-search CV on
full-covariance deep networks across regression and classification
benchmarks.

Closed-form losses with single-pass inference.

Under a last-layer Gaussian posterior, the Bethe regression objective
equals the exact marginal likelihood (Theorem
1
).
The classification objective combines the closed-form data term

-\log\Phi(t_{n})
(
\Phi
: standard normal CDF) from the
probit–Gaussian convolution (Theorem
2
) with the
Z-consistent prior term.
Both predictives are closed-form, giving MAP-equivalent inference cost
across all covariance variants.

Empirical Results

On 8 UCI regression and 12 UCI classification benchmarks, Bethe at
single-pass cost matches or exceeds MAP, MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles,
Laplace, and VBLL on the majority of NLL and calibration
metrics (6/8 regression, 6/8 classification datasets best or tied for best).
Multi-pass refinements (ensembling, MC predictive, post-hoc Laplace)
are orthogonal to the Bethe objective and apply on top, making Bethe a
stronger single-pass building block rather than a replacement for
existing BNN techniques.

2
Background

2.1
Gaussian message convolutions

Two convolution identities underpin the regression and classification
objectives.

Gaussian–Gaussian.

Marginalising a Gaussian likelihood

\mathcal{N}(y;f,\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2})
(observation noise variance)
over a Gaussian message
\mathcal{N}(f;\mu_{f},v_{f})
gives

\int\mathcal{N}(y;f,\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2})\,\mathcal{N}(f;\mu_{f},v_{f})\,\mathrm{d}f=\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{f},\,\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{f}).

(1)

This identity underlies the GP regression
predictive
(
11
)
; Section
4
uses it as the
exact regression loss (Theorem
1
).

Probit–Gaussian.

The convolution of a probit likelihood
p(y|f)=\Phi(yf/c)
(scale
parameter
c>0
) with a Gaussian message
\mathcal{N}(f;\mu_{f},v_{f})

yields the closed form

\int\Phi\!\left(\frac{yf}{c}\right)\mathcal{N}(f;\mu_{f},v_{f})\,\mathrm{d}f=\Phi\!\left(\frac{y\mu_{f}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+v_{f}}}\right).

(2)

This identity is well known from GP
classification
(
11
;
7
)
, where it gives the
predictive distribution at test time.
Section
4
uses it as a
training loss
: the
integrand is treated as a per-sample loss whose gradient drives end-to-end
learning of the feature map and posterior, rather than as a closed-form
predictive over a fixed kernel.

2.2
Evidence lower bound

Variational inference maximises the ELBO, a lower bound on the log-evidence:

\log p(y)\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{q(w)}\!\left[\log p(y\mid w)\right]-\mathrm{KL}(q(w)\|p(w)),

(3)

obtained by Jensen’s inequality applied to

\log p(y)=\log\mathbb{E}_{q}[p(y,w)/q(w)]
.
The bound is tight when
q(w)=p(w\mid y)
.

2.3
Bethe free energy

On any tree-structured factor graph, the Bethe free energy at its optimum
equals the exact negative log-partition function
(
12
)
:

F_{\text{Bethe}}(q^{*})=-\log Z.

(4)

On tree-structured graphs, stationary points of
F_{\text{Bethe}}

coincide with both belief-propagation fixed points and EP fixed
points
(
8
)
: any iterative consistency-finding algorithm
reaches the same optimum that we minimise directly by gradient descent in
Section
3
.

3
Direct Bethe Free Energy Minimisation

Standard belief propagation finds stationary points of the Bethe free
energy by iterative message passing; on loopy graphs convergence is not
guaranteed and the procedure is not end-to-end differentiable.
We minimise
F_{\text{Bethe}}

directly
by gradient descent.
On tree-structured factor graphs this is exact—no approximation is
introduced by the inference procedure itself, only by the choice of
q

and by the shared-cavity construction described below.

Tree factor graph.

Consider a factor graph whose variable nodes are weights
\{w_{l}\}
,
intermediate activations
\{h_{n,l}\}
, and outputs
\{f_{n}\}
(latent pre-likelihood scores), with inputs

\{x_{n}\}
and observations
\{y_{n}\}
fixed (conditioned on).
Factor nodes represent prior, transition, and observation factors.
Any feedforward network without skip-connections induces such a graph and
is therefore a tree; Equation (
4
) applies.

Local consistency and the shared-cavity construction.

Minimising
F_{\text{Bethe}}
is equivalent to finding local
beliefs—one per factor, one per variable—that are mutually consistent
at every edge: the marginal of each factor belief at a connected variable
equals that variable’s belief.
On a tree-structured graph, exact local consistency requires each factor to
receive a per-edge
cavity message
.
For the weight node
w
shared across
N
data plates, the exact cavity
message to plate
n
is
q_{(-n)}(w)=q(w)/\beta_{n}(w)
, where

\beta_{n}(w)\propto\int p(y_{n}|f_{n})\,q(f_{n})\,\mathrm{d}f_{n}
is plate
n
’s
backward contribution.
This per-plate cavity is exact but yields
N
distinct outgoing messages
from
w
.

We adopt the standard simplification of using the shared
q(w)
itself as
the outgoing message to every plate—equivalent to assuming

\beta_{n}(w)\approx 1
per plate, exact in the
N\to\infty
limit and tight
when no single observation dominates the posterior.
Because this approximation conflates per-plate cavities with the full
posterior, all training in this paper uses full-batch gradients: each
update consistently reflects every plate, whereas mini-batching would
optimise the shared
q(w)
against subsets of plates and amplify per-plate
cavity error.
Under the shared-cavity construction, the Bethe free
energy (
4
) decomposes over factors as

F_{\text{Bethe}}(q)=\sum_{l}(-\log Z_{w_{l}})+\sum_{a}(-\log Z_{a})+\sum_{n}(-\log Z_{n}),

(5)

where

\displaystyle Z_{w_{l}}

\displaystyle=\int q(w_{l})\,p(w_{l})\,\mathrm{d}w_{l},

(6)

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle=\int p(y_{n}\mid f_{n})\,q(f_{n})\,\mathrm{d}f_{n},

(7)

and
Z_{a}
is the local partition function of intermediate factor
a
under
the shared-cavity construction (in a feedforward network, these are the
activation transition factors
p(h_{n,l}\mid h_{n,l-1},w_{l})
).
No distributional family is assumed for any belief; the integrals must
only be tractable.

Remark 1

(Deterministic subgraphs)
.

When activation factors are deterministic and weight beliefs at those
layers are point masses, the corresponding
Z_{a}
reduce to feasibility
constraints (
-\log Z_{a}=0
on consistent configurations); the Bethe
loss then contains only prior and observation terms, with
deterministic-layer parameters trained by standard backpropagation.
Appendix
A.2
gives the explicit reduction.

Remark 2

(Z-consistent Bethe vs. ELBO)
.

The ELBO mixes two algebraic forms—a KL prior term and an
expected-log-likelihood data term—while (
5
)
treats every factor uniformly through
-\log Z
(hence

Z-consistent
).
The data terms differ by exactly Jensen’s inequality
(Proposition
1
); the prior treatment makes
hyperparameters of
p(w_{l})
differentiable through
\log Z_{w_{l}}
.

Proposition 1

(Bethe–ELBO inequality)
.

For any
q(f_{n})
and any observation factor
p(y_{n}\mid f_{n})
:

-\log Z_{n}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{n})}\!\left[-\log p(y_{n}\mid f_{n})\right].

(8)

Proof.

Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave logarithm:

\log Z_{n}=\log\mathbb{E}_{q}[p(y_{n}|f_{n})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(y_{n}|f_{n})]
.
Negating both sides gives (
8
).
∎

Summing (
8
) over
n
gives

\sum_{n}(-\log Z_{n})\leq\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}_{q}[-\log p(y_{n}|f_{n})]
,
i.e. the Bethe data term is dominated by the ELBO data term; combined
with the analogous prior identity (Remark
2
), this
gives
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}
globally.

Remark 3

(Bethe and MAP)
.

The point-belief limit
q(w)\to\delta(w-w^{*})
collapses the Bethe loss
to the full negative log-joint, recovering ridge-MAP as a special case
under fixed
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}

What this gives us.

Three properties follow from (
5
) and motivate the
rest of the paper: deterministic subgraphs drop out of the loss
(Remark
1
), so probabilistic and deterministic
layers combine freely on any backbone; the data term is strictly tighter
than ELBO (Proposition
1
), removing the structural
Jensen gap; and prior hyperparameters enter
-\log Z_{w_{l}}

differentiably, enabling single-pass empirical Bayes without an outer
loop.
Section
4
instantiates these properties under a Gaussian
last-layer posterior.

Empirical Bayes and special cases.

The objective (
5
) spans a spectrum of inference
regimes depending on how the prior factors
-\log Z_{w_{l}}
are treated.
Dropping them entirely recovers maximum-likelihood training with no prior
on weights.
Retaining them with fixed hyperparameters gives standard Bayesian
inference under the Z-consistent prior, recovering MAP as beliefs
concentrate.
Treating the hyperparameters themselves—such as prior precision

\alpha
—as differentiable parameters and optimising jointly with the
beliefs is Type-II maximum likelihood
(
7
)
: empirical Bayes
without cross-validation, with closed-form fixed-point conditions
following from (
6
) (Section
4
).

4.1
Gaussian Regression

For Gaussian likelihood, the Bethe free energy evaluates exactly to a
heteroscedastic negative log-likelihood plus the prior term
(Theorem
1
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}(\mu,\Sigma,\sigma_{\text{obs}},\alpha)=-\log Z_{w}+\sum_{n}\!\left[\frac{(y_{n}-\mu^{\top}\!\psi_{n})^{2}}{2\,V_{n}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log V_{n}\right],\quad V_{n}=\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{n}(\Sigma).

(14)

Theorem 1

(Regression Bethe is exact)
.

For Gaussian likelihood

\mathcal{N}(y_{n};\mu^{\top}\psi_{n},\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2})
with deterministic
features
\psi_{n}
, the Bethe predictive

\mathcal{N}(y_{n};\mu^{\top}\psi_{n},\,\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+\psi_{n}^{\top}\Sigma\,\psi_{n})

is the
exact
marginal likelihood over the last-layer posterior

q(w)
—the Bethe loss equals the correct marginal.

Proof.

Each observation factor contributes

-\log\!\int\mathcal{N}(y_{n};f,\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2})\,\mathcal{N}(f;\mu^{\top}\!\psi_{n},v_{n})\,\mathrm{d}f=-\log\mathcal{N}(y_{n};\mu^{\top}\!\psi_{n},\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{n})

by Equation (
1
); expanding
-\log\mathcal{N}
gives the
data terms in (
14
).
Adding
-\log Z_{w}
from (
13
) completes the Bethe
objective (
5
).
No approximation is introduced since the factor graph is a tree
(Equation (
4
)).
∎

Observation noise and variance starvation.

Jointly optimising
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
alongside
\mu
and
\Sigma

triggers a variance-attribution degeneracy:
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
and

v_{n}(\Sigma)
compete to explain residual variance in
V_{n}
, and
gradient descent tends to collapse
\Sigma\to 0
while

\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
absorbs all predictive uncertainty.
The Bethe-FS variant fixes
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{obs}}^{2}
to its MAP
estimate before optimising
\mu
and
\Sigma
as a partial mitigation;
we report empirical consequences in Section
5
and
a diagnostic signature in Appendix
B
.

4.2
Probit Classification

For probit classification, the strict Bethe free energy evaluates to a
closed-form observation-level loss plus the shared prior term:

Theorem 2

(Closed-form strict Bethe loss (T1))
.

Under the probit likelihood and prior
p(w)=\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha^{-1}I)
,
the strict Bethe free energy evaluates to

\mathcal{L}_{\text{class}}(\mu,\Sigma,\alpha)=-\log Z_{w}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\log\Phi(t_{n}),\qquad t_{n}=\frac{y_{n}\,\mu^{\top}\!\psi_{n}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+v_{n}(\Sigma)}}.

(15)

Proof.

The prior factor contributes
-\log Z_{w}
(Equation (
13
)); each
likelihood factor contributes

-\log\!\int\Phi(y_{n}f/c)\,\mathcal{N}(f;\mu^{\top}\!\psi_{n},v_{n})\,\mathrm{d}f=-\log\Phi(t_{n})
by Equation (
2
); summing over
n

gives (
15
).
∎

Prior and probit scale.

The prior precision
\alpha>0
acts as a global weight regulariser;

\alpha^{-1/2}
is the expected weight magnitude.
The probit scale
c>0
plays the structural role of observation noise:

c^{2}+v_{n}
is the total predictive uncertainty in the forward message,
paralleling
V_{n}=\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{n}
in regression.
Unlike
\sigma_{\text{obs}}
,
c
is fixed (typically
c=1
) rather than
optimised: the latent variance of a binary observation is unidentifiable
from the labels, and a free
c
would race to zero to inflate the
signal-to-noise ratio
t_{n}
.
This makes classification simpler than regression—the
variance-attribution degeneracy of Section
4.1
has no
analogue here, since only
v_{n}(\Sigma)
is free to vary.

4.3
Multiclass Classification

Two strategies extend the binary Bethe formulation to
K
classes,
both sharing the deterministic backbone

\psi_{n}=\mathrm{NN}_{\theta}(x_{n})
and all covariance variants (V1–V3):
(i) one-versus-all (OvA), which trains
K
independent binary probit
heads and normalises at prediction time; and
(ii) ordinal probit, which thresholds a single shared latent

f_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n},v_{n})
by
K{-}1
learned boundaries to
produce class probabilities via differences of
\Phi
.
For
K=2
the ordinal model reduces exactly to the binary loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{class}}
(Theorem
2
).
Full derivations and loss expressions for both models are in
Appendix
A.3
.

Hyperparameter treatment.

In the empirical Bayes default,
\mu
,
\Sigma
, the backbone parameters

\theta
, the prior precision
\alpha
, the regression observation noise

\sigma_{\text{obs}}
, and the ordinal thresholds
\tau
are all
differentiable parameters optimised jointly via gradient descent.
The probit scale

is fixed at 1; the numerical-stability constant

\varepsilon
is fixed at

6
Related Work

We position the contribution by explicit contrast rather than a survey
of Bayesian deep learning.

Last-layer Bayesian neural networks.

The closest baselines—VBLL
(
5
)
,
Laplace Redux
(
3
)
, and
Rich-BLL
(
2
)
—all combine a deterministic
backbone with a Bayesian last layer.
VBLL trains an ELBO with a fixed regularisation weight; Laplace
approximates the posterior post-hoc from the MAP solution; Rich-BLL
uses an NTK approximation for richer last-layer expressiveness.
Bethe differs in optimising a strictly tighter, closed-form objective
with joint empirical Bayes on the prior precision in a single
gradient pass, requiring no MC sampling or post-hoc Hessian
estimation.

Tighter variational bounds.

IWAE
(
1
)
tightens the ELBO by importance-weighted
sampling; Laplace Redux
(
3
)
and
VBLL
(
5
)
rely on per-step or post-hoc sampling.
The Bethe objective is exact for Gaussian likelihoods
(Theorem
1
) and admits a closed form for probit
(Theorem
2
), removing the Jensen gap structurally rather
than asymptotically.

Probit and GP classification.

The probit–Gaussian convolution that gives our closed-form
classification loss is well known from GP
classification
(
11
;
7
)
, where it provides the
predictive distribution at test time.
We use it as a
training
loss with end-to-end optimisation of
the feature map and posterior, rather than as a closed-form
predictive over a fixed kernel.

Empirical Bayes in deep learning.

Type-II maximum likelihood for neural network priors

(
7
;
10
)
typically requires an outer
optimisation loop or marginal-likelihood approximation.
Z-consistency in our Bethe construction makes the prior precision a
differentiable parameter of the loss, eliminating the outer loop.

Belief propagation and EP.

Bethe stationary points coincide with belief-propagation and EP fixed
points
(
8
;
12
)
, both reached by iterative
message passing.
We instead minimise the same objective by gradient descent, allowing
end-to-end training jointly with the feature map.

Sampling-based predictive uncertainty.

Deep Ensembles
(
6
)
and MC
Dropout
(
4
)
require multiple forward passes at inference
and do not target consistency.
Bethe provides a closed-form predictive at MAP-equivalent cost;
multi-pass refinements remain orthogonal and applicable on top.

7
Conclusion

We presented direct minimisation of the Bethe free energy as a
training objective for Bayesian neural networks.
The framework is general: on any tree-structured factor graph with
tractable per-factor partition functions, the Bethe loss is exact
and can be optimised end-to-end by gradient descent, accommodating
any mixture of probabilistic and deterministic subgraphs.
For the practical case of a Gaussian last-layer posterior with
Gaussian or probit likelihoods, the loss admits a closed form;
empirical Bayes optimisation of the prior precision happens in the
same gradient pass that trains the model, predictives are
closed-form (MAP-equivalent inference cost), and the resulting
objective sits strictly between MAP and ELBO.
On UCI regression and classification benchmarks, this single-pass
instantiation matches or exceeds reference methods on a substantial
fraction of NLL metrics; specific optimisation challenges and their
diagnostic signatures are documented in
Appendix
B
.

Limitations.

Our concrete instantiation is restricted to last-layer Gaussian
posteriors over deterministic backbones with Gaussian or probit
likelihoods, and to UCI-scale benchmarks.
The optimisation challenges documented in
Appendix
B
—
\alpha
-runaway, variance starvation
in regression, validation-NLL early-stopping suboptimality, the OvA
per-class
\alpha
pathology, and the backbone-depth
interaction—are open questions about the joint EB optimisation
landscape rather than fundamental limitations of the Bethe framework
itself.
Probabilistic intermediate layers, non-Gaussian likelihoods, richer
message families (particles, flows), and behaviour at larger scales
remain to be addressed.

Appendix A
Proofs and derivations

A.1
The MAP-Bethe-ELBO ordering

The general inequality
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}

(Proposition
1
) follows from Jensen for any likelihood
and any posterior
q
with tractable
-\log Z_{n}
integral.
This appendix establishes the complementary direction

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}
on a localised basis:
under the Bethe parametrisation, the loss increases as
v_{n}
grows from
zero on samples where the model fits well relative to the predictive
uncertainty.

Probit classification.

At
v_{n}=0
,
t_{n}=y_{n}\mu^{\top}\psi_{n}/c
is the standard probit margin
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}
.
For
v_{n}>0
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{n}}{\partial v_{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{h(t_{n})\,t_{n}}{c^{2}+v_{n}},

where
h(t)=\phi(t)/\Phi(t)
is the inverse Mills ratio.
On correctly-classified samples (
t_{n}>0
), both factors are positive,
so
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}
exceeds
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}
as

v_{n}
grows.

Gaussian regression.

Similarly, at
v_{n}=0
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}
.
For
v_{n}>0
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{n}}{\partial v_{n}}=\frac{1}{2V_{n}}\left(1-\frac{(y_{n}-\mu_{f})^{2}}{V_{n}}\right),

where
V_{n}=\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{n}
.
This is positive when the squared residual is smaller than
V_{n}

(samples the model fits well relative to the predictive variance).

Caveat.

On poorly-fit samples (
t_{n}<0
in classification, large residuals in
regression),
\partial\mathcal{L}_{n}/\partial v_{n}
may be negative, so

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}
locally.
The aggregate inequality
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bethe}}

holds in expectation over reasonably-fit predictions; it is sharp only
on per-sample bases for well-classified or well-fit data.

A.2
Why
Z_{a}=1
at feasible configurations

We consider an activation factor
a
corresponding to a deterministic
transition
h_{n,l}=g(h_{n,l-1},w_{l})
.
Assume that all messages flowing into and out of this factor are point
masses:

q(w_{l})=\delta(w_{l}-\hat{w}_{l})
,

q(h_{n,l-1})=\delta(h_{n,l-1}-\hat{h}_{n,l-1})
,
and
q(h_{n,l})=\delta(h_{n,l}-\hat{h}_{n,l})
.
This holds, for example, when the input
x_{n}
is observed (a fixed
dataset point), all upstream activation factors are deterministic, and
all upstream weights are deterministically estimated.
The local partition function under the shared-cavity construction is
then

Z_{a}=\int p(h_{n,l}\mid h_{n,l-1},w_{l})\,q(h_{n,l-1})\,q(w_{l})\,q(h_{n,l})\,\mathrm{d}h_{n,l-1}\,\mathrm{d}w_{l}\,\mathrm{d}h_{n,l},

which collapses to evaluating
p(\hat{h}_{n,l}\mid\hat{h}_{n,l-1},\hat{w}_{l})
.
For deterministic
p=\delta
, this is
1
when

\hat{h}_{n,l}=g(\hat{h}_{n,l-1},\hat{w}_{l})
(a feasible
forward-pass-consistent configuration) and
0
otherwise.
At any feasible configuration,
-\log Z_{a}=0
.
At infeasible configurations
-\log Z_{a}=+\infty
, precluding
inconsistent states from optimisation.

The Bethe free energy (
5
) therefore reduces to

F_{\text{Bethe}}^{\text{det}}(q)=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{P}}(-\log Z_{w_{l}})+\sum_{n}(-\log Z_{n})+\mathrm{const},

where
\mathcal{P}
indexes the probabilistic layers only.
Probabilistic intermediate layers (where
q(h_{n,l})
is non-Dirac)
require the integral above to be evaluated explicitly and are outside
the scope of this paper.

A.3
Multiclass probit extensions

Both multiclass strategies share the deterministic backbone

\psi_{n}=\mathrm{NN}_{\theta}(x_{n})
and all covariance variants (V1–V3)
from the binary case.

A.3.1
One-versus-all (OvA)

The OvA strategy trains
K
independent binary probit heads, with
per-class last-layer weights
\mu_{2,k}
and posteriors
q_{k}(w_{k})
.
Binary labels are
y_{n,k}=+1
if
y_{n}=k
, else
-1
.
The joint loss sums
K
instances of

\mathcal{L}_{\text{class}}
(
15
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{OvA}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!\left[-\log Z_{w,k}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\log\Phi(t_{n,k})\right],\qquad t_{n,k}=\frac{y_{n,k}\,\mu_{2,k}^{\top}\psi_{n}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+v_{n,k}}}.

(16)

Each head is an independent binary probit, so Theorem
2

applies per class with shared prior
p(w_{k})=\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha^{-1}I)
.
Predictive probabilities are obtained by normalising:

\hat{p}_{n,k}\propto\Phi(\mu_{2,k}^{\top}\psi_{n}/\sqrt{c^{2}+v_{n,k}})
.
This normalisation is a prediction-time heuristic; each head is trained
on an independent binary loss with no joint normalisation constraint, so
probabilities are calibrated per class but not jointly across classes.

In the empirical Bayes default, each class head has its own prior
precision
\alpha_{k}
, jointly optimised.
With
K
independent EB updates and small per-class samples, the

\alpha_{k}
estimates become individually noisy—a pathology we
document empirically in Appendix
B
(OvA per-class

\alpha
on iris).
The ordinal model avoids this by using a single shared latent and prior.

A.3.2
Ordinal probit

For data with a natural class ordering, a single latent

f_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n},v_{n})
, with
\mu_{n}=\mu_{2}^{\top}\psi_{n}
and

v_{n}=\psi_{n}^{\top}\Sigma\,\psi_{n}
, is thresholded by
K-1
ordered
boundaries
\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{K-1}
(parameterised via log-gaps to
enforce ordering):

P(y_{n}=k)=\Phi(t_{n,k}^{+})-\Phi(t_{n,k}^{-}),\qquad t_{n,k}^{\pm}=\frac{\tau_{k}^{\pm}-\mu_{n}}{D_{n}},

(17)

where
\tau_{k}^{+}=\tau_{k}
(
+\infty
for
k=K-1
),

\tau_{k}^{-}=\tau_{k-1}
(
-\infty
for
k=0
), and
D_{n}=\sqrt{c^{2}+v_{n}}
.

The prior term applies without modification:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ord}}(\mu_{2},\Sigma,\alpha)=-\log Z_{w}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\log P(y_{n}),

(18)

where
P(y_{n})
is given by (
17
) and

q(w)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\Sigma)
.
For
K=2
there is a single threshold
\tau_{1}
; absorbing it into the
bias of
\mu_{2}
reduces
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ord}}
exactly to the binary
loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{class}}
(Theorem
2
).

Appendix B
Optimisation Failure Modes

The Bethe objective is sound on tree-structured factor graphs
(Section
3
), but its joint optimisation of weights,
covariance, and prior precision exposes specific failure modes that
are not unique to Bethe but affect last-layer Bayesian neural networks
more broadly.
We characterise four such modes, each with a diagnostic signature in
our experiments.
For each we describe the mechanism, the empirical signature, and the
open question; per-dataset diagnostic tables are in
Appendices
F
and
G
.

\alpha
-runaway.

The empirical Bayes update for the prior precision
\alpha
is
unbounded above; gradient descent on
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}
or

\mathcal{L}_{\text{class}}
can drive
\alpha
to extreme values
(
10^{3}
–
10^{7}
) when the likelihood surface is flat in the direction
the prior shrinks.
Two regimes appear in our data.
In the
ill-conditioned
regime,
\alpha
-runaway correlates with
degraded test NLL: on regression concrete and yacht, EB drives
\alpha

to
3{,}543
and
2.1\times 10^{5}
respectively (Bethe-FG), and Bethe
trails the best baseline by 0.04–0.13 nats
(Table
1
).
In the
saturated
regime,
\alpha
-runaway is benign: on
classification banknote, Ordinal-V2 reaches
\alpha=1.3\times 10^{7}

yet its test NLL (
0.027
) is the best on this dataset
(DeepEns
0.029
).
The distinction is the conditioning of the likelihood relative to the
prior, not the magnitude of
\alpha
itself.
This is consistent with the broader EB literature on the brittleness
of Type-II maximum likelihood under near-flat likelihoods

[
10
,
9
]
.
A regularised EB update—bounding
\alpha
via a hyperprior or
coupling its update step to the empirical posterior precision—is
straightforward and we leave it to future work.

Variance starvation in regression.

The regression loss (
14
) couples observation noise

\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
and predictive variance
v_{n}(\Sigma)
through

V_{n}=\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}+v_{n}(\Sigma)
.
Because
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
is a free scalar with no prior penalty,
gradient descent preferentially absorbs predictive uncertainty into

\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
while collapsing
\Sigma\to 0
.
The signature is most visible on naval, where the noise floor is
small: Bethe-Diag and Bethe-FG drive
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
to

0.003
–
0.005
, compared to
0.015
for MAP and
0.026
for VBLL.
The Bethe-FS variant sets
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{obs}}^{2}
to the validation-set
MSE before optimising
\mu
and
\Sigma
, partially mitigating
the dynamic; on naval, Bethe-Diag-FS reaches
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}=0.009

but its test NLL does not improve on the joint optimum
(
-3.141
).
Classification escapes this failure mode by construction, since the
analogous probit scale
c
is fixed
(Section
4.2
).
Open questions include a principled prior on
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}

derived from the heteroscedastic structure of
V_{n}
, or a coupled-update
step that blocks the degenerate direction.

Validation-NLL early-stopping suboptimality.

On regression, Bethe-FG’s test NLL trails its trajectory’s oracle
minimum by
0.05
–
0.20
nats on 5 of 8 datasets, with the largest
gaps on boston (test
2.843
, oracle
2.694
) and yacht (test
3.400
,
oracle
3.145
).
Reference methods (MAP, Laplace, Deep Ensemble) show oracle gaps
below
0.05
nats on the same datasets.
Classification is unaffected: oracle gaps for all Bethe variants are
below
0.02
nats across all datasets, indicating that the failure is
specific to the regression loss landscape under joint EB optimisation.
The mechanism is consistent with validation NLL being a noisy
estimator of test NLL on small datasets, amplified by the
non-monotonic dynamics between
\sigma_{\text{obs}}^{2}
and
\Sigma
.
A combined stopping criterion (e.g. validation NLL plus calibration
error) or a stopping rule derived from the Bethe geometry itself
remains open.

OvA per-class
\alpha
on small multiclass.

The OvA construction (Section
4.3
) trains
K
binary
heads each with its own prior precision
\alpha_{k}
.
With
K
independent EB updates and small per-class samples, the

\alpha_{k}
estimates become individually noisy.
The signature is in Table
8
: on iris
(
N=150
,
K=3
), OvA-V2 and OvA-V3 trail grid-CV by
0.153
and

0.164
nats respectively, while the corresponding Ordinal variants
(which use a single shared
\alpha
) trail by only
0.052

nats.
A shared-
\alpha
variant of OvA is a natural fix that we have not
investigated; its derivation is straightforward, and we expect it to
recover Ordinal-level EB performance on small multiclass benchmarks.

Backbone interaction at depth.

On 2-layer regression, reference methods (MAP, Laplace, Deep
Ensemble) benefit substantially from added depth, while Bethe
variants do not: the test-NLL improvement at 2L is
0.05
–
0.50
nats
for reference methods on most datasets but
-0.10
to
0.20
nats for
Bethe (full
\Delta
-NLL table in
Appendix
F.7
).
The mechanism is plausibly that the variance-damped gradient through
the deterministic backbone (Section
4
) attenuates the
signal that drives feature-map learning, an interaction between the
Bethe last-layer loss and the upstream optimisation that does not
arise under MAP-style point-estimate training.
Whether this is a fundamental property of variance-aware loss
landscapes or a tractable optimisation issue (e.g. via warmup or
decoupled-update schedules) is open.
We did not run 2-layer classification due to time constraints; the
1-layer pattern (where Bethe is competitive or best on 6 of 8
classification datasets, Table
2
) suggests
the depth interaction may be regression-specific, but this requires
verification.

Appendix D
Experimental Setup

D.1
Shared Training Protocol

Architecture.

One hidden layer,
H=50
units,
tanh
activations, no bias.
Two-layer variants (
-2L
) stack a second hidden layer of the same
width with the same activation.

Optimiser.

Full-batch Adam,
\eta=0.03
, up to 5,000 gradient steps.
Early stopping monitors validation NLL with patience
50
and improvement
threshold
10^{-6}
; the checkpoint with lowest validation NLL is used
for evaluation.

Regularisation.

L_{2}
penalty
\lambda_{\mathrm{bb}}=0.01
on hidden-layer weights for
all gradient-based methods.
For MAP-type last layers (CE, Laplace, Deep Ensemble, VBLL),

\lambda_{\mathrm{ll}}=0.1
on the last-layer weights.
For Bethe methods,
\lambda_{\mathrm{ll}}
is replaced by an empirical
Bayes prior whose precision
\alpha
is jointly optimised
(initialised at
\alpha_{0}=1
).
Fixed-
\alpha
variants (
-alpha:
x
) use a Bayesian prior with

\alpha
pinned to
x
.
These are explicit loss penalties, not decoupled weight decay.

Numerical precision.

All computations use 32-bit floating point.

Seeds and data splits.

Twenty independent runs, seeds
s\in\{5,\ldots,24\}
.
Each seed controls both the data partition and the model initialisation,
so the 20 repetitions estimate variability from both sources jointly.
This differs from Rich-BLL, which fixes the data split and varies only
the initialisation; see Appendix
H.1
.

Pipeline deviations.

We use full-batch gradients (instead of mini-batches) and

tanh
activations (required by the Bethe closed form).
These choices slightly disadvantage methods that benefit from SGD
noise (e.g. MC Dropout); we acknowledge this as a limitation of the
comparison.

Appendix H
Methodological Notes

H.1
Comparison with Rich-BLL

Rich-BLL
[
2
]
uses a 2-layer architecture by default; we use 1-layer for direct method comparison, so architecture differences make direct numerical comparison unfair in either direction.
Rich-BLL fixes the data split and varies only the initialisation across seeds; we vary both (Appendix
D.1
), which provides a more conservative estimate of generalisation variance.
The NTK approximation in Rich-BLL is orthogonal to the Bethe objective; combining the two is a natural future direction.
