Introduction

Schrödinger operators with random potentials form one of the basic mathematical models for quantum motion in disordered media. Singular random potentials naturally appear when a disordered medium is observed on a scale much larger than its correlation length. Let

V=(V(x))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}

be a real-valued, mean-zero, stationary random field on
\mathbb{R}
, defined on a probability space, and assume that
\mathbb{E}|V(0)|^{2}<\infty
. Here a random field means a jointly measurable family of random variables indexed by the spatial variable, and stationarity means that its finite-dimensional distributions are invariant under spatial translations. The square-integrability assumption ensures that the covariance function

R(r):=\mathbb{E}[V(r)V(0)]

is well defined; by stationarity,
\mathbb{E}[V(x)V(y)]=R(x-y)
. Assume further that
R\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. For the rescaled field

V_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-1/2}V(x/\varepsilon),\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1,

one has

\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}(x)V_{\varepsilon}(y)]=\varepsilon^{-1}R\left(\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\right).

Thus, by the elementary approximate-identity argument, the covariance kernels converge in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to a multiple of the delta kernel:

\varepsilon^{-1}R\left(\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\right)\longrightarrow\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\delta(x-y).

Equivalently, for test functions
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{\varepsilon}(y)\psi(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\right]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

This identifies the limiting covariance structure. Under suitable additional mixing and moment assumptions, the corresponding convergence of the random fields is obtained by applying a functional central limit theorem to the additive functional

W_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}V_{\varepsilon}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}s=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{x/\varepsilon}V(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r.

The classical i.i.d. prototype is Donsker’s invariance principle; see, for example, Billingsley
[
2
]
. For continuous-parameter stationary processes, invariance principles for additive functionals under strong mixing-type assumptions go back to Davydov
[
4
]
; related strong-mixing functional central limit theorems are discussed in
[
5
]
. For spatial integrals of stationary mixing random fields, see
[
16
]
. Under such assumptions one obtains, on compact intervals in the spatial variable,

\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{x/\varepsilon}V(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{x\geq 0}\Longrightarrow\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}\widetilde{B}_{x},\qquad\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r,

where
\widetilde{B}
is a standard Brownian motion. Equivalently,

W_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}V_{\varepsilon}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}s\Longrightarrow\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}\widetilde{B}_{x},

and therefore
V_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}\dot{\widetilde{B}}
in the sense of random distributions. Hence Gaussian white noise may be viewed as the limiting model for a centered spatial disorder whose correlation length tends to zero.

This limiting picture motivates the one-dimensional white-noise Schrödinger equation

-y^{\prime\prime}+\rho\dot{B}\,y=\lambda y,

where
B
is Brownian motion and
\dot{B}
denotes Gaussian white noise. More precisely, Gaussian white noise on
\mathbb{R}
is the centered Gaussian random distribution
\xi
characterized by

\mathbb{E}\langle\xi,\varphi\rangle\langle\xi,\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

and it may be realized as the distributional derivative of a two-sided Brownian motion. In the present paper the spatial domain is the fixed interval
(0,L)
. We work on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
carrying a standard Brownian motion
B=(B_{x})_{0\leq x\leq L}
; here
\Omega
is the Brownian sample space, and
\omega\in\Omega
denotes a realization of the Brownian path. This restriction changes the spectral problem from an infinite-volume one to a finite-interval boundary value problem, but it does not change the local covariance structure inherited from the scaling picture above. For test functions supported in
(0,L)
, the covariance is still given by the
L^{2}
-inner product; equivalently, the potential is still delta-correlated in the spatial variable. The finite-interval model is therefore obtained by restricting the white noise to
[0,L]
, writing its primitive as
\rho B
, and interpreting the resulting equation through the quasi-derivative formalism described below. Similar scale-separation ideas also appear at the level of equations with large or rapidly oscillating random potentials: one studies the limiting effective models obtained after averaging or rescaling the random medium; see the review
[
1
]
.

For general background on random Schrödinger operators, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
21
,
17
]
. To the best of our knowledge, one of the first rigorous studies of random Schrödinger operators with white-noise potential is due to Fukushima and Nakao
[
12
]
. Since then, such operators have been investigated from several viewpoints, including the density of states, localization, large-volume spectral statistics, and related half-line models; see, for instance,
[
25
,
7
,
8
,
10
,

,
20
,

]
. These works mainly concern infinite-volume, density-of-states, localization, large-volume spectral-statistics, or half-line questions. The problem studied here is different in nature: the interval length is fixed and the high-energy index tends to infinity. The aim is therefore to obtain finite-interval eigenvalue and eigenfunction asymptotics in which both the leading stochastic correction and the probabilistic size of the remainders are explicit.

The pathwise realization of this finite-interval problem relies on the deterministic theory of Sturm–Liouville operators with distributional potentials. We recall the relevant formulation. If a real distribution
q
belongs to
H^{-1}(0,L)
, it can be written as
q=\sigma^{\prime}
with
\sigma\in L^{2}(0,L)
, and the differential expression is defined by the quasi-derivative

y^{[1]}=y^{\prime}-\sigma y,\qquad\ell_{\sigma}y=-(y^{[1]})^{\prime}-\sigma y^{[1]}-\sigma^{2}y.

For white noise with strength
\rho
, the primitive is
\sigma=\rho B
, and hence
q=\sigma^{\prime}=\rho\dot{B}
in the distributional sense. Since Brownian paths are continuous on
[0,L]
, one has
\sigma\in L^{2}(0,L)
almost surely, so the deterministic distributional Sturm–Liouville theory applies pathwise; see, for example,
[
13
,
24
,
15
]
. Therefore, under self-adjoint boundary conditions, the operator has discrete spectrum. The question addressed below is how the delta-correlated disorder is reflected in the high-energy asymptotics of this finite-interval spectrum and its eigenfunctions.

It is important to distinguish this pathwise random-operator problem from the eigenvalue problem for stochastic Hamiltonian systems with boundary conditions studied by Peng
[
22
]
. In Peng’s setting, the eigenvalues are deterministic parameters for which a forward-backward stochastic Hamiltonian system admits a non-trivial adapted solution. In the present paper, after fixing a Brownian path one obtains a self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville operator
H_{\omega}
, and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}(\omega)
are random variables. Thus the high-energy asymptotics below concern random eigenvalues of a finite-interval random operator, rather than deterministic spectral parameters of an adapted FBSDE boundary value problem.

We next clarify the relation between the present work and the existing deterministic theory. The self-adjointness and compact-resolvent properties used below are standard consequences of the theory of Sturm–Liouville operators with distributional potentials. Moreover, eigenvalue asymptotics for broad classes of singular potentials are already known; see, for example,
[
14
,
11
]
. These deterministic results provide the natural operator-theoretic framework for the white-noise model considered here. However, the specialization to Gaussian white-noise potentials has additional probabilistic structure which is not captured by the deterministic theory alone. In this setting, the primitive of the potential is Brownian motion, and the leading oscillatory corrections can be written explicitly as Brownian stochastic integrals. This makes it possible to use probabilistic tools, in particular the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, to obtain quantitative
L^{p}(\Omega)
estimates and almost-sure estimates for the random remainders. The aim of the present paper is therefore not merely to rederive deterministic high-energy asymptotics in a special case, but to refine them into explicit stochastic Prüfer expansions for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the finite-interval white-noise Schrödinger operator. More precisely, the new information is the explicit Brownian stochastic-integral correction, its
L^{p}(\Omega)
control, and the resulting almost-sure high-energy bounds.

We now state the main results in a form convenient for reference. Throughout the following statements,
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
denotes the Dirichlet eigenvalues, listed increasingly, and

\lambda_{n}^{+}:=\max\{\lambda_{n},0\},\qquad k_{n}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{+}},\qquad K_{n}:=\frac{n\pi}{L}.

The convention
k_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{+}}
is harmless at high energy, since, almost surely,
\lambda_{n}>0
for all sufficiently large
n
.

Theorem 1.1

(Eigenvalue asymptotics)
.

Set

M_{n}:=\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Then, for every finite
p\geq 1
,

k_{n}=K_{n}+\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{n}+O_{L^{p}(\Omega)}(n^{-2}),\qquad n\to\infty.

Moreover,

\lambda_{n}=K_{n}^{2}+\frac{2\rho}{L}M_{n}+O_{L^{p}(\Omega)}(n^{-1}).

In particular, using
\sin^{2}(K_{n}s)=1/2-\cos(2K_{n}s)/2
,

\lambda_{n}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}+\frac{\rho}{L}B_{L}-\frac{\rho}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\cos\left(\frac{2n\pi s}{L}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+O_{L^{p}(\Omega)}(n^{-1}).

Corollary 1.2

(Almost-sure eigenvalue bounds)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
,

k_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Consequently,

\lambda_{n}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}+O(n^{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
is obtained from a stochastic Prüfer transformation. After writing
\lambda=k^{2}
, the Dirichlet eigenvalue condition becomes a phase condition for the lifted Prüfer angle. Its high-energy expansion gives the displayed stochastic correction, and the almost-sure bound follows from the corresponding finite-moment estimates and the Borel–Cantelli lemma.

We next state the corresponding eigenfunction asymptotics. For deterministic
k>0
, let
u_{k}
be the Dirichlet initial solution normalized by

u_{k}(0)=0,\qquad u_{k}^{\prime}(0)=k.

It satisfies the stochastic Volterra equation

u_{k}(x)=\sin(kx)+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Theorem 1.3

(Eigenfunction asymptotics)
.

For every finite
p\geq 2
,

\left\|\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-2},\qquad k\geq k_{0}.

For the eigenvalue sequence, define the deterministic-frequency stochastic integral

I_{n}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(K_{n}(x-s)\right)\sin(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right|=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely},

and hence

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Furthermore, let
\varphi_{n}
be the
L^{2}(0,L)
-normalized Dirichlet eigenfunction associated with
\lambda_{n}
, with the sign chosen so that
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(0)>0
. Put

s_{n}(x):=\sin(k_{n}x),\qquad A_{n}:=\int_{0}^{L}s_{n}(t)I_{n}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\Bigg|\varphi_{n}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\bigg[s_{n}(x)+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}\bigg(I_{n}(x)-\frac{2}{L}A_{n}s_{n}(x)\bigg)\bigg]\Bigg|=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})

almost surely. In particular,

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|\varphi_{n}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin(k_{n}x)\right|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

The detailed asymptotic theorems are proved below for Dirichlet boundary conditions. General separated boundary conditions lead to the same stochastic Prüfer equation but require additional boundary phase corrections; this is discussed briefly in the final section.

A further motivation for the present spectral analysis comes from infinite-dimensional KAM theory for Hamiltonian PDEs. In the classical KAM theory for nonlinear Schrödinger equations and wave equations, non-resonance of the linear frequencies is often produced by external parameters. A typical example is a convolution-type potential or diagonal Fourier multiplier, whose Fourier coefficients are treated as parameters; see, for instance,
[
18
,
19
]
. Although such parameters are regarded as randomized, this randomness is mainly a device for selecting a good set of frequencies. The constant-potential case is still more rigid, since it essentially shifts the linear frequencies by the same amount. Both situations differ from the pathwise randomness of the white-noise operator studied here, where the Brownian path itself determines the whole linear spectrum. Another important line of work treats fixed deterministic multiplication potentials, where no adjustable external parameter is available; see, for example, Du and Yuan
[
6
]
for nonlinear Schrödinger equations with a given potential, and Yuan
[
26
]
for nonlinear wave equations with a prescribed potential. Compared with these deterministic-potential results, the white-noise problem that we ultimately have in mind is expected to be more difficult. The potential is distributional and random, the frequencies and eigenfunctions are generated simultaneously by a single Brownian path, and the small-divisor estimates cannot be reduced to deterministic spectral separation or to the exclusion of a simple external parameter set.

The long-term goal of the present program is to develop KAM theory for Schrödinger or wave equations whose spatial potential is Gaussian white noise. In such a problem the natural frequencies are the random eigenvalues of the pathwise operator. Thus small divisors involve random combinations such as

\lambda_{i}(\omega)+\lambda_{j}(\omega)-\lambda_{k}(\omega)-\lambda_{l}(\omega),

and the coefficients in the Hamiltonian normal form also depend on the random eigenfunctions. The results proved in this paper give a first step in this direction: they identify the leading stochastic correction to the high-energy eigenvalues and give uniform eigenfunction asymptotics. However, these estimates are not yet sufficient for a full KAM analysis. For example, polynomial lower bounds for fourth-order divisors require higher-order joint eigenvalue expansions, small-ball estimates for the resulting Gaussian or non-Gaussian stochastic corrections, and summable control of the remainders over large families of almost-resonant integer quadruples.

A possible continuation is therefore to combine higher-order stochastic Volterra or Prüfer expansions with probabilistic separation estimates for the random small divisors. In higher-order expansions one expects random fields indexed by the frequency parameter, and the substitution of random eigenfrequencies may lead either to anticipating stochastic integrals or to equivalent deterministic-frequency re-expansions. Clarifying this point, together with the required joint Gaussian estimates and eigenfunction coefficient bounds, is an essential step toward a KAM theorem with genuine white-noise spatial potential. The present paper isolates the spectral asymptotic component of this larger problem.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the white-noise distribution and its primitive, then recalls the quasi-derivative realization and the basic spectral consequences of self-adjointness and compact resolvent. In Section 3, we derive the stochastic Prüfer equations and prove the high-energy expansion of the Prüfer angle. Subsequently, we give the Dirichlet eigenvalue asymptotics with an explicit stochastic correction and the almost-sure estimate. Section 4 proves the eigenfunction expansion and its almost-sure consequences. Finally, in Section 5, we discuss the results of this paper and possible extensions.

2
Preliminary

In this section, we recall some standard facts used to formulate the white-noise operator pathwise.

2.1
White noise as a distributional potential

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be the Brownian probability space introduced in the Introduction, carrying a standard Brownian motion
(B_{x})_{0\leq x\leq L}
. Fix
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
and define

\sigma_{\omega}(x)=\rho B_{x}(\omega),\qquad 0\leq x\leq L.

For each fixed
\omega
outside a null set,
\sigma_{\omega}\in C[0,L]\subset L^{2}(0,L)
. We define the potential

q_{\omega}=\sigma_{\omega}^{\prime}

in the sense of distributions. Equivalently, for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,L)
,

\langle q_{\omega},\varphi\rangle=-\int_{0}^{L}\sigma_{\omega}(x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\sigma_{\omega}\in L^{2}(0,L)
,

|\langle q_{\omega},\varphi\rangle|\leq\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}\,\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)},

so
q_{\omega}\in H^{-1}(0,L)
almost surely. (Actually,
q_{\omega}
belongs to
H^{-s}
for
s>1/2
, but we do not need this result.)

One can check that the distribution defined above agrees with the usual Gaussian white noise. Indeed, for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,L)
, Itô integration yields

\int_{0}^{L}\varphi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}=-\int_{0}^{L}B_{x}\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore

\langle q_{\omega},\varphi\rangle=\rho\int_{0}^{L}\varphi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x},

and hence

\mathbb{E}\langle q_{\omega},\varphi\rangle=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle q_{\omega},\varphi\rangle\langle q_{\omega},\psi\rangle\bigr]=\rho^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\varphi(x)\psi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

2.2
Quasi-derivatives and self-adjoint realization

Throughout this section let
\sigma\in L^{2}(0,L)
be real-valued. Define the quasi-derivative

y^{[1]}=y^{\prime}-\sigma y.

The regularized Sturm–Liouville expression associated with
q=\sigma^{\prime}
is

\ell_{\sigma}y=-(y^{[1]})^{\prime}-\sigma y^{[1]}-\sigma^{2}y.

Formally, if all derivatives are classical, then
\ell_{\sigma}y=-y^{\prime\prime}+\sigma^{\prime}y
. The maximal domain is

\operatorname{dom}T_{\max}=\{y\in L^{2}(0,L):y,y^{[1]}\in AC[0,L],\ \ell_{\sigma}y\in L^{2}(0,L)\}.

For
y,z\in\operatorname{dom}T_{\max}
one has the Lagrange identity

(\ell_{\sigma}y,z)_{L^{2}(0,L)}-(y,\ell_{\sigma}z)_{L^{2}(0,L)}=[y,z]_{\sigma}(L)-[y,z]_{\sigma}(0),

where

[y,z]_{\sigma}(x)=y^{[1]}(x)\overline{z(x)}-y(x)\overline{z^{[1]}(x)}.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi)
and impose the separated boundary conditions

\displaystyle y(0)\cos\alpha-y^{[1]}(0)\sin\alpha

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle y(L)\cos\beta-y^{[1]}(L)\sin\beta

\displaystyle=0.

Define
H_{\sigma,\alpha,\beta}y=\ell_{\sigma}y
on the subdomain of
\operatorname{dom}T_{\max}
satisfying these two boundary conditions. Then from classical results for quasi-derivative Sturm–Liouville problem, one can obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(Self-adjointness and compact resolvent)
.

Let
\sigma\in L^{2}(0,L)
be real-valued. Then
H_{\sigma,\alpha,\beta}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(0,L)
. It is bounded from below and has compact resolvent. Consequently its spectrum consists only of real eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, accumulating at
+\infty
only. For separated boundary conditions all eigenvalues are simple.

For a detailed proof, we refer the reader to
[
24
]
. We recall the main mechanism, since it is used to pass to the white-noise case. The Lagrange identity shows that the imposed real separated boundary conditions make the boundary form vanish, hence the corresponding restriction is symmetric. The regular quasi-derivative Sturm–Liouville theory then shows that these separated boundary conditions give a self-adjoint restriction of the maximal operator.

For completeness, let us also indicate why lower semiboundedness and compactness of the resolvent are natural in this setting. In the Dirichlet case the associated closed form is represented by

\mathfrak{t}[y]=\int_{0}^{L}|y^{[1]}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{L}\sigma^{2}|y|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

with form domain
H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
. In one dimension, by the Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality,

\|y\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq C_{L}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}.

Thus, for every
\eta>0
,

\int_{0}^{L}\sigma^{2}|y|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}\|y\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\eta\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}+C_{\eta,L,\sigma}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}.

This shows that the negative part of the form is infinitesimally form-bounded with respect to the Dirichlet energy. Consequently the form is closed and bounded from below, and the first representation theorem for closed semibounded forms (equivalently, the Friedrichs construction in this case) produces a lower semibounded self-adjoint operator. After adding a sufficiently large multiple of the
L^{2}(0,L)
-norm, the form norm is equivalent to the
H^{1}(0,L)
-norm. Since the embedding
H^{1}(0,L)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(0,L)
is compact, the associated resolvent is compact. The spectral theorem applied to the compact self-adjoint resolvent then gives a purely discrete real spectrum with finite multiplicities, accumulating only at
+\infty
. Analogous estimates, with the usual finite-dimensional boundary corrections, apply to general separated boundary conditions.

We now apply Theorem
2.1
to the case relevant for this paper. For every Brownian path outside a null set,
B(\omega)\in C[0,L]
, and hence
\sigma_{\omega}=\rho B(\omega)\in L^{2}(0,L)
. Therefore Theorem
2.1
gives the following pathwise realization of the white-noise operator.

Corollary 2.2
.

For almost every
\omega
, define the quasi-derivative

y^{[1]}:=y^{\prime}-\sigma_{\omega}y

and the regularized differential expression

\ell_{\sigma_{\omega}}y:=-\bigl(y^{\prime}-\sigma_{\omega}y\bigr)^{\prime}-\sigma_{\omega}y^{\prime}=-\bigl(y^{[1]}\bigr)^{\prime}-\sigma_{\omega}y^{[1]}-\sigma_{\omega}^{2}y.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi)
, and define
H_{\omega,\alpha,\beta}
in
L^{2}(0,L)
by

H_{\omega,\alpha,\beta}y=\ell_{\sigma_{\omega}}y

on the domain

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}H_{\omega,\alpha,\beta}:=\bigl\{y\in L^{2}(0,L):

\displaystyle\ y,y^{[1]}\in\mathrm{AC}[0,L],\ \ell_{\sigma_{\omega}}y\in L^{2}(0,L),

\displaystyle\ y(0)\cos\alpha-y^{[1]}(0)\sin\alpha=0,

\displaystyle\ y(L)\cos\beta-y^{[1]}(L)\sin\beta=0\bigr\}.

Then
H_{\omega,\alpha,\beta}
is a self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(0,L)
. Moreover, it is bounded from below and has compact resolvent. Consequently its spectrum is real, purely discrete, and may be listed as

\lambda_{1}(\omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(\omega)\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{n}(\omega)\to+\infty,

with multiplicities counted.

Remark 2.3
.

Under the usual real separated boundary conditions, the one-dimensional Sturm–Liouville oscillation theory gives simplicity of the eigenvalues.

Moreover, the compact resolvent directly implies the completeness of the eigenfunctions. This is a standard consequence of the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators.

Theorem 2.4

(Completeness of eigenfunctions)
.

Almost surely, the eigenfunctions of
H_{\omega,\alpha,\beta}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(0,L)
. More precisely, for almost every
\omega
, there exist real eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(\omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(\omega)\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{n}(\omega)\to+\infty,

counted with multiplicity, and an orthonormal family of eigenfunctions
(\varphi_{n}(\omega))_{n\geq 1}
such that, for every
f\in L^{2}(0,L)
,

f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,\varphi_{n}(\omega)\rangle\varphi_{n}(\omega)~~a.s.

in
L^{2}(0,L)
and

\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle f,\varphi_{n}(\omega)\rangle|^{2}.~~a.s.

3
Asymptotic results for the eigenvalues

In this section, we derive the stochastic Prüfer equations and prove the high-energy expansion of the Prüfer angle, which is useful for studying asymptotic results of eigenvalues under separated boundary value conditions. As a typical case, we give the Dirichlet eigenvalue asymptotics with an explicit stochastic correction and the almost-sure estimate.

3.1
Stochastic equation and Prüfer variables

Write
\lambda=k^{2}>0
. The eigenvalue equation
\ell_{\sigma_{\omega}}y=k^{2}y
is equivalent to the first-order system

\begin{cases}Y^{\prime}=Q+\sigma Y,\\[5.69054pt]
Q^{\prime}=-(k^{2}+\sigma^{2})Y-\sigma Q,\end{cases}

where
Y=y,\quad Q=y^{[1]}.

Equivalently, if one introduces the classical momentum variable

P:=Y^{\prime}=Q+\sigma Y,

then the same equation becomes the SDE system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\,\mathrm{d}Y_{x}=P_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\\[5.69054pt]
&\,\mathrm{d}P_{x}=-k^{2}Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\rho Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Actually, since
Q=P-\sigma Y
and
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\rho\,\,\mathrm{d}B
, we have

\,\mathrm{d}Q=\,\mathrm{d}P-\,\mathrm{d}(\sigma Y)=\,\mathrm{d}P-\sigma\,\,\mathrm{d}Y-Y\,\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

by Itô’s formula. Therefore

Q^{\prime}=-k^{2}Y-\sigma P

is equivalent to

\,\mathrm{d}P=-k^{2}Y\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\rho Y\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}.

Any solution of the SDE (
1
) gives a solution of the quasi-derivative equation by setting
Q=P-\sigma Y
, therefore,
k^{2}
is an eigenvalue if and only if the solution of (
1
) satisfies the corresponding boundary conditions. This equivalence is understood as follows. For each fixed Brownian path outside a null set, the quasi-derivative problem is a deterministic Sturm–Liouville problem with primitive
\sigma=\rho B
. The classical momentum
P=Y^{\prime}
is then a semimartingale in the probabilistic representation, and the identity
Q=P-\sigma Y
converts the pathwise quasi-derivative system into the Itô system (
1
). Conversely, a solution of (
1
) with
Q=P-\sigma Y
satisfies the quasi-derivative equation in the distributional sense and preserves the boundary conditions. We shall use this stochastic first-order system to investigate the eigenvalues.

For a non-trivial solution, define the lifted Prüfer variables by

Y_{x}=R_{x}\sin\theta_{x},\qquad\frac{P_{x}}{k}=R_{x}\cos\theta_{x},\qquad R_{x}>0,

where
\theta
is chosen continuously. The use of a lifted angle avoids the branch ambiguity of the arctangent.

Lemma 3.1

(Prüfer angle and amplitude equations)
.

The lifted angle satisfies

\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{x}=k\,\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\rho}{k}\sin^{2}\theta_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\sin^{3}\theta_{x}\cos\theta_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2)

Moreover,

\,\mathrm{d}\log R_{x}=\frac{\rho}{2k}\sin(2\theta_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2k^{2}}\sin^{2}\theta_{x}\cos(2\theta_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3)

Proof.

Apply Itô’s formula to
\Phi(Y,P)=\arctan(kY/P)
locally on coordinate charts, and then patch the result for the lifted angle. The derivatives are

\Phi_{Y}=\frac{kP}{k^{2}Y^{2}+P^{2}},\qquad\Phi_{P}=-\frac{kY}{k^{2}Y^{2}+P^{2}},\qquad\Phi_{PP}=\frac{2kYP}{(k^{2}Y^{2}+P^{2})^{2}}.

Since the only quadratic variation comes from
\,\mathrm{d}P
, Itô’s formula gives

\,\mathrm{d}\theta=k\,\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\rho kY^{2}}{k^{2}Y^{2}+P^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{d}B+\frac{\rho^{2}kY^{3}P}{(k^{2}Y^{2}+P^{2})^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Substituting
Y=R\sin\theta
and
P=kR\cos\theta
yields (
2
).

For the amplitude, write
R^{2}=Y^{2}+(P/k)^{2}
. A direct Itô calculation gives

\,\mathrm{d}\log R=\frac{\rho YP}{k^{2}R^{2}}\,\,\mathrm{d}B+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2k^{2}}\left(\frac{Y^{2}}{R^{2}}-\frac{2Y^{2}P^{2}}{k^{2}R^{4}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Again substituting
Y=R\sin\theta
,
P=kR\cos\theta
gives (
3
).
∎

3.2
High-energy expansion of the Prüfer angle

Now at an endpoint
a\in\{0,L\}
with separated parameter
\gamma\in[0,\pi)
, the quasi-derivative boundary condition

\cos\gamma\,Y(a)-\sin\gamma\,Q(a)=0

becomes, in the
(Y,P)
-Prüfer angle,

\bigl(\cos\gamma+\sigma(a)\sin\gamma\bigr)\sin\theta(a)-k\sin\gamma\cos\theta(a)=0.

Thus, for general separated boundary conditions, the eigenvalue condition is obtained as follows. Choose an initial angle
\vartheta_{\alpha,0}(k)
satisfying

\bigl(\cos\alpha+\sigma(0)\sin\alpha\bigr)\sin\vartheta_{\alpha,0}(k)-k\sin\alpha\cos\vartheta_{\alpha,0}(k)=0,

solve (2) with
\theta_{0}=\vartheta_{\alpha,0}(k)
, and impose at
x=L

\bigl(\cos\beta+\sigma(L)\sin\beta\bigr)\sin\theta_{L}(k)-k\sin\beta\cos\theta_{L}(k)=0.

Equivalently, if
\vartheta_{\beta,L}(k)
is a terminal boundary phase satisfying

\bigl(\cos\beta+\sigma(L)\sin\beta\bigr)\sin\vartheta_{\beta,L}(k)-k\sin\beta\cos\vartheta_{\beta,L}(k)=0,

then the eigenvalue condition is

\theta_{L}(k)-\vartheta_{\beta,L}(k)\in\pi\mathbb{Z}.

When
\sin\gamma=0
, the corresponding boundary phase is
0
modulo
\pi
. For convenience, in what follows, we specialize to the Dirichlet case.

In the Dirichlet case we choose the solution with initial data
Y_{0}=0
and
P_{0}=k
, so that
R_{0}=1
and
\theta_{0}=0
. If the
n
-th Dirichlet eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
is positive and
k_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}
, then the Dirichlet eigenvalue condition is

\theta_{L}(k_{n})=n\pi.

The convention
k_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{+}}
will be used below in the
L^{p}
statements; Lemma
3.6
shows that this convention is harmless in the high-energy limit.

Set

h(u)=\sin^{2}u,\qquad h^{\prime}(u)=\sin(2u),\qquad g(u)=\sin^{3}u\cos u.

For
k>0
define

\displaystyle M_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}h(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

\displaystyle J_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(ks)M_{s}(k)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

\displaystyle A_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}g(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

The first two integrals are Itô integrals for deterministic
k
, while
A_{x}(k)
is a Lebesgue integral.

Proposition 3.2

(Uniform second-order expansion)
.

For every
p\in[2,\infty)
there exists
C_{p}<\infty
, depending only on
p,L,
and
\rho
, such that as
k\to\infty
,

\theta_{x}(k)=kx-\frac{\rho}{k}M_{x}(k)+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\bigl(J_{x}(k)+A_{x}(k)\bigr)+R_{x}(k),

(4)

with

\left\|\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}|R_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-3}.

Furthermore,

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|M_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|J_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p},

and
\sup_{x\leq L}|A_{x}(k)|\leq(4k)^{-1}
. In particular,
\theta_{x}(k)=kx-\frac{\rho}{k}M_{x}(k)+O_{L^{p}}(k^{-2})
.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{x}=\theta_{x}-kx
. The integrated form of (
2
) is

\delta_{x}=-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5)

Note that
\theta
is continuous and adapted;
h
is continuous, thus
h(\theta_{s})
is predictable.
Hence

\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

is a continuous square-integrable martingale with quadratic variation

\int_{0}^{x}|h(\theta_{s})|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

.

Recall the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality. If
M_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}
is a continuous martingale, then

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M_{t}|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\left\|\langle M\rangle_{T}^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

where
1\leq p<+\infty
and
\langle M\rangle_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}H_{s}^{2}ds
denotes the predictable quadratic variation.
Since
h
and
g
are bounded, the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy (BDG) inequality gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\delta_{x}|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

Moreover, since
h
is Lipschitz, another application of BDG gives

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(h(\theta_{s})-h(ks)\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|\delta_{s}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\left\|\sup_{s\leq L}|\delta_{s}|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

Consequently,

\delta_{x}+\frac{\rho}{k}M_{x}(k)=O_{L^{p}}(k^{-2})

uniformly in
x\in[0,L]
.

Moreover, Taylor’s formula gives

h(\theta_{s})=h(ks)+h^{\prime}(ks)\delta_{s}+r_{s},\qquad|r_{s}|\leq C|\delta_{s}|^{2}.

By the BDG inequality and the preceding bound, applied in the higher moment
2p
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\int_{0}^{x}r_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|\delta_{s}|^{4}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,L}\left\|\sup_{s\leq L}|\delta_{s}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=C_{p,L}\left\|\sup_{s\leq L}|\delta_{s}|\right\|_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{p}k^{-2}.

Consequently,

\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(ks)\delta_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(ks)M_{s}(k)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+O_{L^{p}}(k^{-2})

uniformly in
x
. Thus

\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=M_{x}(k)-\frac{\rho}{k}J_{x}(k)+O_{L^{p}}(k^{-2}).

Similarly, since
g
is Lipschitz and
\delta=O_{L^{p}}(k^{-1})
uniformly,

\int_{0}^{x}g(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}s=A_{x}(k)+O_{L^{p}}(k^{-1}).

Considering the above estimates, the equation (
5
) gives (
4
) with the claimed
O_{L^{p}}(k^{-3})
remainder.

The martingale bounds for
M
and
J
follow from the BDG and boundedness of
h,h^{\prime}
.
Finally,

A_{x}(k)=\int_{0}^{x}\sin^{3}(ks)\cos(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\sin^{4}(kx)}{4k},

so
\sup_{x}|A_{x}(k)|\leq(4k)^{-1}
.

Hence the proposition is proved.
∎

Remark 3.3

(Range of
p
)
.

We state Proposition
3.2
for
p\in[2,\infty)
because this range is convenient when estimating quadratic Taylor remainders such as
|\theta_{s}-ks|^{2}
by elementary supremum estimates and Hölder’s inequality. The restriction is not essential. The BDG inequality is valid for every finite
p\geq 1
, and the estimates for
p\in[1,2)
follow from the monotonicity of
L^{p}
-norms on a probability space. The endpoint
p=\infty
is excluded in the present moment estimates.

Remark 3.4
.

The statement is an
L^{p}
and in-probability expansion for deterministic values of
k
. Almost-sure estimates along the discrete eigenvalue sequence are obtained later by combining finite-moment bounds with the Borel–Cantelli lemma.

3.3
Dirichlet eigenvalue asymptotics

Let
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
denote the Dirichlet eigenvalues of
H_{\omega}
, ordered increasingly. Throughout the rest of the paper we set

\lambda_{n}^{+}:=\max\{\lambda_{n},0\},\qquad k_{n}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{+}}.

Lemma
3.6
below implies that, almost surely,
\lambda_{n}>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, and that the exceptional event
\{\lambda_{n}\leq 0\}
has faster-than-polynomial probability decay. Hence this convention does not affect any of the high-energy
L^{p}
or almost-sure asymptotics. Before establishing the asymptotic results, it is appropriate to give some auxiliary parameter-uniform estimates for the Prüfer phase and its derivatives first.

Lemma 3.5

(Parameter-uniform phase estimates)
.

Let

I_{n}=[K_{n}-1,K_{n}+1],\qquad K_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}.

For every finite
p\geq 2
there is a constant
C_{p}
, independent of
n
, such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{k\in I_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\theta_{x}(k)-kx|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-1},

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{k\in I_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}\theta_{x}(k)-x|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

Proof.

We write the proof in some detail, since this is the point where one has to distinguish fixed-frequency BDG estimates from estimates which are uniform in the parameter
k
.

First fix a deterministic
k\geq 1
and put

\delta_{x}(k):=\theta_{x}(k)-kx.

The integrated equation (
5
) gives

\delta_{x}(k)=-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s}(k))\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g(\theta_{s}(k))\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
h
and
g
are bounded, BDG implies

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\delta_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|h(\theta_{s}(k))|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\frac{C}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

This gives the first fixed-frequency bound.

Next set

\zeta_{x}(k):=\partial_{k}\theta_{x}(k),\qquad e_{x}(k):=\zeta_{x}(k)-x.

The differentiability with respect to
k
follows from the usual smooth parameter-dependence theorem for strong solutions of SDEs. In the estimates below we work on the compact intervals
I_{n}=[K_{n}-1,K_{n}+1]
; for all large
n
, these intervals lie in a fixed half-line
[k_{0},\infty)
with
k_{0}>0
. On such sets the coefficients

b(k,u)=k+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}g(u),\qquad a(k,u)=-\frac{\rho}{k}h(u)

are smooth in
(k,u)
, the relevant
k
-derivatives are locally bounded, and all derivatives in
u
are bounded uniformly. Hence, after choosing a modification if necessary,
(k,x)\mapsto\theta_{x}(k)
is twice continuously differentiable in
k
on compact
k
-intervals, uniformly for
x\in[0,L]
almost surely, and the
k
-derivatives solve the equations obtained by differentiating the stochastic equation. Differentiating (
2
) gives

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{x}

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}h(\theta_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}-\frac{\rho}{k}h^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\rho^{2}}{k^{3}}g(\theta_{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}g^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\zeta_{0}=0.

Subtracting
x
and using
\zeta_{s}=s+e_{s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle e_{x}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(\theta_{s})s\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(\theta_{s})e_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\rho^{2}}{k^{3}}\int_{0}^{x}g(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(\theta_{s})s\,\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(\theta_{s})e_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

Let

A_{p}(k):=\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|e_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

For the deterministic drift terms, boundedness of
g^{\prime}
gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(\theta_{s})s\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq Ck^{-2},

Similarly, the term with
g
is
O(k^{-3})
. For the last drift term,

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(\theta_{s})e_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ck^{-2}\int_{0}^{L}\|e_{s}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\,\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq Ck^{-2}A_{p}(k).

For the stochastic terms, BDG gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}h(\theta_{s})\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-2},

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(\theta_{s})s\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left(\int_{0}^{L}s^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{p}k^{-1},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}(\theta_{s})e_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|e_{s}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}k^{-1}A_{p}(k).

Combining these estimates yields

A_{p}(k)\leq C_{p}k^{-1}+C_{p}(k^{-1}+k^{-2})A_{p}(k).

Choose
k_{0}
so large that
C_{p}(k^{-1}+k^{-2})\leq 1/2
for
k\geq k_{0}
. Then the last term can be absorbed into the left-hand side, and hence

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}\theta_{x}(k)-x|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=A_{p}(k)\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

(6)

We shall also need the same size bound for the
k
-derivative of
e
. Put

\eta_{x}(k):=\partial_{k}^{2}\theta_{x}(k)=\partial_{k}e_{x}(k).

Differentiating the equation for
\zeta
once more gives

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\eta_{x}={}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{2\rho}{k^{3}}h(\theta_{x})+\frac{2\rho}{k^{2}}h^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}-\frac{\rho}{k}h^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}^{2}-\frac{\rho}{k}h^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\eta_{x}\right]\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{6\rho^{2}}{k^{4}}g(\theta_{x})-\frac{4\rho^{2}}{k^{3}}g^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}g^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{x})\zeta_{x}^{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{k^{2}}g^{\prime}(\theta_{x})\eta_{x}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
\eta_{0}=0
. Since
\zeta_{x}=x+e_{x}
, the preceding estimate gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\zeta_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}.

Using also that
h,h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime},g,g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}
are bounded, BDG and Hölder’s inequality imply, with

B_{p}(k):=\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\eta_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

that

\displaystyle B_{p}(k)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(k^{-3}+k^{-2}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\zeta_{x}|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+k^{-1}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\zeta_{x}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+C_{p}k^{-1}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|\eta_{s}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+C_{p}\left(k^{-4}+k^{-3}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\zeta_{x}|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+k^{-2}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\zeta_{x}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)+C_{p}k^{-2}\int_{0}^{L}\|\eta_{s}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\,\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}k^{-1}+C_{p}(k^{-1}+k^{-2})B_{p}(k).

Here
p\geq 2
is used only to control
\|\sup_{x}|\zeta_{x}|^{2}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
by the fixed-frequency bounds in a higher moment. Enlarging
k_{0}
if necessary, we absorb the last term and obtain

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}^{2}\theta_{x}(k)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1},\qquad k\geq k_{0}.

(7)

It remains to pass from deterministic
k
to the supremum over
I_{n}
. We use the Banach-valued one-dimensional Sobolev inequality. If
f:I_{n}\to C[0,L]
is absolutely continuous and
p>1
, then

\sup_{k\in I_{n}}\|f(k)\|_{C[0,L]}\leq C\left(\left(\int_{I_{n}}\|f(k)\|_{C[0,L]}^{p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}+\left(\int_{I_{n}}\|f^{\prime}(k)\|_{C[0,L]}^{p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}\right),

where
C
depends only on the length
|I_{n}|=2
. Apply this with

f(k)=\theta_{\cdot}(k)-k(\cdot),\qquad f^{\prime}(k)=\partial_{k}\theta_{\cdot}(k)-\cdot.

Taking
L^{p}(\Omega)
-norms and using Fubini’s theorem, together with the fixed-frequency bounds, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{k\in I_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\theta_{x}(k)-kx|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\int_{I_{n}}k^{-p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}+C_{p}\left(\int_{I_{n}}k^{-p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

Applying the same Sobolev inequality with

f(k)=\partial_{k}\theta_{\cdot}(k)-\cdot,\qquad f^{\prime}(k)=\partial_{k}^{2}\theta_{\cdot}(k),

and using (
6
) and (
7
), we obtain

\left\|\sup_{k\in I_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}\theta_{x}(k)-x|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Positivity and rough moment bounds for the high-energy roots)
.

For every finite
q,r\geq 1
there are constants
C_{q},C_{r}
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{n}\leq 0)\leq C_{q}n^{-q},\qquad\|k_{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C_{r}n,\qquad\|\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C_{r}n^{2}.

Consequently, almost surely,
\lambda_{n}>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, and then
k_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}
eventually.

Proof.

The quadratic form corresponding to the quasi-derivative realization may be written as

\mathfrak{t}_{\omega}[y]=\int_{0}^{L}|y^{\prime}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{0}^{L}\sigma_{\omega}y^{\prime}\overline{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad y\in H_{0}^{1}(0,L).

By the one-dimensional Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality and Young’s inequality,

\left|2\int_{0}^{L}\sigma_{\omega}y^{\prime}\overline{y}\,\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C_{L}\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{3/2}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}+C_{L}\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}.

Therefore

\frac{1}{2}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}-C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{t}_{\omega}[y]\leq\frac{3}{2}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}+C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}.

Let
\mu_{n}=(n\pi/L)^{2}
be the Dirichlet eigenvalues of
-\,\mathrm{d}^{2}/\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}
. The min–max principle gives

\lambda_{n}(\omega)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu_{n}-C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\geq cn^{2}-C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}.

Similarly, by taking in the min–max principle the span of the first
n
Dirichlet sine functions,

\lambda_{n}(\omega)\leq Cn^{2}+C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}.

The lower bound implies

\{\lambda_{n}\leq 0\}\subset\{\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\geq cn^{2}\}.

We now spell out the probabilistic estimate. Since

\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}=|\rho|\,\|B(\omega)\|_{L^{2}(0,L)},

it is enough to use high moments of the Brownian
L^{2}(0,L)
-norm. For every integer
m\geq 1
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|B\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{L}B_{x}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2m}

\displaystyle\leq L^{2m-1}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}|B_{x}|^{4m}\,\,\mathrm{d}x=C_{m}\int_{0}^{L}x^{2m}\,\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

where we used that
B_{x}
is a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
x
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4m}<\infty\qquad\text{for every }m\geq 1.

By Markov’s inequality, for every integer
m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{n}\leq 0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4}\geq cn^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4m}\geq c^{m}n^{2m}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq c^{-m}n^{-2m}\,\mathbb{E}\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4m}\leq C_{m}n^{-2m}.

Given any
q<\infty
, choose
m
so large that
2m\geq q
. Then, after changing the constant,

\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{n}\leq 0)\leq C_{q}n^{-q}.

The Borel–Cantelli lemma then gives
\lambda_{n}>0
eventually almost surely.

Finally, the upper and lower bounds imply

|\lambda_{n}(\omega)|\leq Cn^{2}+C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{4},

and the upper bound together with the definition
k_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{+}}
implies

k_{n}(\omega)\leq Cn+C\|\sigma_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}.

Taking
L^{r}(\Omega)
-norms and using again the finite moments of
\|B\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}
gives

\|k_{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C_{r}n,\qquad\|\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C_{r}n^{2}.

∎

Theorem 3.7

(Dirichlet eigenvalue expansion)
.

Let

K_{n}:=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad M_{n}:=M_{L}(K_{n})=\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Then, for every finite
p\geq 1
,

k_{n}=K_{n}+\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{n}+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}),\qquad n\to\infty.

Equivalently,

k_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}+\frac{\rho}{n\pi}\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{n\pi s}{L}\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}).

Moreover,

\lambda_{n}=K_{n}^{2}+\frac{2\rho}{L}M_{n}+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1}).

Proof.

It is enough to prove the estimate for
p\geq 2
; the case
1\leq p<2
follows by monotonicity of
L^{p}
-norms. Put

I_{n}=[K_{n}-1,K_{n}+1]

and define

E_{n}:=\sup_{k\in I_{n}}|\theta_{L}(k)-kL|,\qquad D_{n}:=\sup_{k\in I_{n}}|\partial_{k}\theta_{L}(k)-L|.

By Lemma
3.5
, for every finite
q\geq 2
,

\|E_{n}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+\|D_{n}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C_{q}n^{-1}.

Let

G_{n}:=\{E_{n}<L/2\}\cap\{D_{n}<L/2\}.

Then Markov’s inequality gives, for every finite
q
,

\mathbb{P}(G_{n}^{c})\leq C_{q}n^{-q}.

On
G_{n}
we have
\partial_{k}\theta_{L}(k)\geq L/2
throughout
I_{n}
. Hence
k\mapsto\theta_{L}(k)
is strictly increasing on
I_{n}
. Moreover,

\theta_{L}(K_{n}+1)\geq n\pi+L-E_{n}>n\pi,\qquad\theta_{L}(K_{n}-1)\leq n\pi-L+E_{n}<n\pi.

Therefore there is a unique solution of
\theta_{L}(k)=n\pi
in
I_{n}
. By the Sturm oscillation theorem for the Dirichlet problem with distributional potentials (in the quasi-derivative formulation, see for example
[
24
,
15
]
), this solution is the Dirichlet root
k_{n}
corresponding to the phase level
n\pi
. In addition, if
r>E_{n}/L
, then

\theta_{L}(K_{n}+r)>n\pi,\qquad\theta_{L}(K_{n}-r)<n\pi,

so by monotonicity

|k_{n}-K_{n}|\leq\frac{E_{n}}{L}\qquad\text{on }G_{n}.

Consequently,

\bigl\||k_{n}-K_{n}|\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

On the complement, Lemma
3.6
and Hölder’s inequality give, after choosing
q
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\bigl\||k_{n}-K_{n}|\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|k_{n}-K_{n}\|_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}\mathbb{P}(G_{n}^{c})^{1/(2p)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

Thus

k_{n}-K_{n}=O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1}).

We now refine the estimate. Since
K_{n}L=n\pi
, Proposition
3.2
at the deterministic frequency
K_{n}
gives

\theta_{L}(K_{n})-n\pi=-\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}).

On
G_{n}
, the mean value formula gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(k_{n})-n\pi

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(K_{n})-n\pi+L(k_{n}-K_{n})+\int_{K_{n}}^{k_{n}}\bigl(\partial_{k}\theta_{L}(u)-L\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}u.

The last term is
O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2})
on
G_{n}
, because

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\int_{K_{n}}^{k_{n}}\bigl(\partial_{k}\theta_{L}(u)-L\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}u\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sup_{u\in I_{n}}|\partial_{k}\theta_{L}(u)-L|\right\|_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}\bigl\||k_{n}-K_{n}|\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\bigr\|_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}n^{-2}.

Therefore, on
G_{n}
in
L^{p}
,

L(k_{n}-K_{n})=\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}).

It remains only to remove the indicator of
G_{n}
. The variable

k_{n}-K_{n}-\frac{\rho}{LK_{n}}M_{L}(K_{n})

has
L^{r}
-norm bounded by
C_{r}n
for every finite
r
, by Lemma
3.6
and the BDG estimate for
M_{L}(K_{n})
. Since
\mathbb{P}(G_{n}^{c})\leq C_{q}n^{-q}
for arbitrary finite
q
, Hölder’s inequality makes the contribution of
G_{n}^{c}
equal to
O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2})
. Hence

L(k_{n}-K_{n})=\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}),

and since
LK_{n}=n\pi
,

k_{n}-K_{n}=\frac{\rho}{LK_{n}}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2})=\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-2}).

It remains to derive the displayed expansion for
\lambda_{n}
. On the event
\{\lambda_{n}>0\}
, we have
\lambda_{n}=k_{n}^{2}
, and hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}-K_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=2K_{n}(k_{n}-K_{n})+(k_{n}-K_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\rho K_{n}}{n\pi}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1})=\frac{2\rho}{L}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1}).

The contribution of the event
\{\lambda_{n}\leq 0\}
is also
O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1})
. Indeed, by Lemma
3.6
, the variable

\lambda_{n}-K_{n}^{2}-\frac{2\rho}{L}M_{L}(K_{n})

has
L^{r}
-norm bounded by
C_{r}n^{2}
, while
\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{n}\leq 0)\leq C_{q}n^{-q}
for arbitrary finite
q
; Hölder’s inequality with
q
sufficiently large gives the claim.
This proves the theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.8

(Almost-sure high-energy bound)
.

Let

K_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

k_{n}=K_{n}+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Consequently,

\lambda_{n}=K_{n}^{2}+O(n^{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.7
, for every finite
p\geq 1
,

k_{n}=K_{n}+\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{n}+R_{n},\qquad\|M_{n}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p},\qquad\|R_{n}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}n^{-2},

where

M_{n}=\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Here the bound on
M_{n}
follows directly from the BDG inequality, since the integrand is deterministic and bounded.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
p>1/\varepsilon
. By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{n}\right|>n^{-1+\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-p\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{n}|>n^{-1+\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-p(1+\varepsilon)}.

Both right-hand sides are summable in
n
. The Borel–Cantelli lemma therefore gives

\frac{\rho}{n\pi}M_{n}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon}),\qquad R_{n}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

This proves

k_{n}=K_{n}+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Finally, by Lemma
3.6
, almost surely
\lambda_{n}>0
for all sufficiently large
n
. For those
n
,
\lambda_{n}=k_{n}^{2}
, and hence

\lambda_{n}-K_{n}^{2}=(k_{n}-K_{n})(k_{n}+K_{n}).

Since
K_{n}\sim n
and
k_{n}-K_{n}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})
almost surely, we obtain

\lambda_{n}=K_{n}^{2}+O(n^{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

∎

4
Asymptotic results for eigenfunctions

For
k>0
, let
(Y_{x}(k),P_{x}(k))_{0\leq x\leq L}
be the solution of the initial value problem

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{x}=P_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\,\mathrm{d}P_{x}=-k^{2}Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\rho Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x},\qquad Y_{0}=0,\quad P_{0}=k.

We write

u_{k}(x):=Y_{x}(k),\qquad u_{k}^{\prime}(x)=P_{x}(k).

Equivalently,
u_{k}
is the Dirichlet initial solution of

-u_{k}^{\prime\prime}+\rho\dot{B}\,u_{k}=k^{2}u_{k}

in the quasi-derivative sense, normalized by
u_{k}(0)=0
and
u_{k}^{\prime}(0)=k
. The variation-of-constants formula gives the stochastic Volterra equation

u_{k}(x)=\sin(kx)+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(8)

Proposition 4.1

(First-order eigenfunction expansion)
.

For every
p\in[2,\infty)
there exist constants
C_{p}<\infty
and
k_{0}\geq 1
such that, for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

\left\|\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-2}.

(9)

For random frequencies such as the eigenvalues
k_{n}
, the deterministic-frequency version of this expansion is stated below in Corollary
4.3
.

Proof.

We first record the elementary decomposition

\sin(k(x-s))=\sin(kx)\cos(ks)-\cos(kx)\sin(ks).

Thus the Volterra stochastic integral in (
8
) can be written as
a linear combination, with bounded deterministic coefficients, of the two
martingales

C_{x}(k):=\int_{0}^{x}\cos(ks)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\qquad S_{x}(k):=\int_{0}^{x}\sin(ks)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

The BDG inequality applied to these two martingales gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left\|\left(\int_{0}^{L}|u_{k}(s)|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Since

\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\leq 1+\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)|,

the preceding estimate implies

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq 1+\frac{C_{p}}{k}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

For
k\geq 2C_{p}
the last term is absorbed into the left-hand side. Hence

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}.

Therefore

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

Substituting this estimate once more into (
8
), we obtain

\displaystyle u_{k}(x)-\sin(kx)-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))\bigl(u_{k}(s)-\sin(ks)\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Using the same martingale decomposition of the kernel and BDG again yields the

O_{L^{p}}(k^{-2})
bound uniformly in
x
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Deterministic-frequency and local derivative estimates)
.

Let

K_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{n}=[K_{n}-1,K_{n}+1],

and set

I_{n}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(K_{n}(x-s)\right)\sin(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

For every finite
p\geq 2
there exists
C_{p}
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|u_{K_{n}}(x)-\sin(K_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-2},

(10)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}n^{-p}.

(12)

Proof.

The first estimate is just Proposition
4.1
applied at the deterministic frequency
k=K_{n}
. The second estimate follows from BDG, because the integrand in
I_{n}
is deterministic and uniformly bounded.

It remains to prove (
12
). We first note that the pair
(u_{k},u_{k}^{\prime})
solves the two-dimensional linear SDE

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{x}=P_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\,\mathrm{d}P_{x}=-k^{2}Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\rho Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x},\qquad Y_{0}=0,\quad P_{0}=k.

On each compact interval of parameters
k\in[k_{0},K_{0}]
,
k_{0}>0
, the coefficients and the initial data are smooth in
k
and globally Lipschitz in
(Y,P)
. Thus we may choose a version for which
u_{k}(x)
is twice continuously differentiable in
k
, uniformly for
x\in[0,L]
almost surely; the derivatives are obtained by differentiating the Volterra equation below. First fix a deterministic
k\geq k_{0}
. Differentiating the Volterra equation (
8
), and writing

v_{k}(x):=\partial_{k}u_{k}(x),

we get

\displaystyle v_{k}(x)={}

\displaystyle x\cos(kx)-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)\cos(k(x-s))u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))v_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Set

w_{k}(x):=v_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx).

Using the decompositions of
\sin(k(x-s))
and
\cos(k(x-s))
into products of functions of
x
and functions of
s
, BDG and the bound

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|u_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}

from Proposition
4.1
give

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|w_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}k^{-1}+C_{p}k^{-1}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|w_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

After absorbing the last term, for all sufficiently large
k
,

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

(13)

To pass from a fixed
k
to the supremum over
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, we also need the same type of estimate for the
k
-derivative of
w_{k}
. For clarity, we write this equation explicitly. Put

S_{k}(x,s):=\sin(k(x-s)),\qquad C_{k}(x,s):=\cos(k(x-s)),

and

z_{k}(x):=\partial_{k}w_{k}(x).

Since
v_{k}(s)=s\cos(ks)+w_{k}(s)
, differentiating the equation for
w_{k}
gives

\displaystyle z_{k}(x)={}

\displaystyle\frac{2\rho}{k^{3}}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)C_{k}(x,s)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)v_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)C_{k}(x,s)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)^{2}S_{k}(x,s)u_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)C_{k}(x,s)v_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)s\cos(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)C_{k}(x,s)s\cos(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}-\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)s^{2}\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho}{k^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)w_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}(x-s)C_{k}(x,s)w_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{k}\int_{0}^{x}S_{k}(x,s)z_{k}(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

All kernels appearing here are uniformly bounded on
0\leq s\leq x\leq L
, and the fixed-frequency bounds for
u_{k}
and
w_{k}=\partial_{k}u_{k}-x\cos(kx)
also give
\|\sup_{x\leq L}|v_{k}(x)|\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}
. Hence BDG gives

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|z_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}+C_{p}k^{-1}\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|z_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Absorbing the last term for large
k
yields

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}w_{k}(x)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

(14)

Equivalently,

\left\|\sup_{x\leq L}\left|\partial_{k}\bigl(\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)\bigr)\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}k^{-1}.

Now apply the one-dimensional Sobolev inequality in the parameter
k
on the interval
\mathcal{K}_{n}
to the Banach-valued function

f(k)=\partial_{k}u_{k}(\cdot)-(\cdot)\cos(k\cdot)\in C[0,L].

Taking
L^{p}(\Omega)
-norms and using Tonelli’s theorem, together with (
13
) and (
14
), gives

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)|\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{n}}k^{-p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}+C_{p}\left(\int_{\mathcal{K}_{n}}k^{-p}\,\,\mathrm{d}k\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{p}n^{-1}.

This proves (
12
).
∎

Corollary 4.3

(Almost-sure eigenfunction asymptotics)
.

Let

K_{n}:=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad I_{n}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(K_{n}(x-s)\right)\sin(K_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Here the stochastic integral is taken at the deterministic frequency
K_{n}
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right|=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Moreover,

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

Put

\Delta_{n}:=k_{n}-K_{n}.

By Corollary
3.8
,

\Delta_{n}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}

for every
\varepsilon>0
. Hence, almost surely,
k_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
.

By (
10
), (
11
), (
12
), Markov’s inequality and the Borel–Cantelli lemma, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\leq L}\left|u_{K_{n}}(x)-\sin(K_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|

\displaystyle=O(n^{\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)|

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})

almost surely.
For such
n
, by the fundamental theorem of calculus in the parameter
k
,

\displaystyle u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(u_{K_{n}}(x)-\sin(K_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{K_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{K_{n}}^{k_{n}}\left(\partial_{k}u_{k}(x)-x\cos(kx)\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}k+\rho\left(\frac{1}{K_{n}}-\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)I_{n}(x).

Taking the supremum over
x
gives

\displaystyle\sup_{x\leq L}\left|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)-\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq O(n^{-2+\varepsilon/3})+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon/3})O(n^{-1+\varepsilon/3})+C\,O(n^{-1+\varepsilon/3})n^{-2}O(n^{\varepsilon/3})=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon}).

Here we have applied the preceding almost-sure estimates with
\varepsilon/3
in place of
\varepsilon
. This proves the first assertion.

The second assertion follows immediately from the first one and (
11
). Indeed,

\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely},

and therefore

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}|u_{k_{n}}(x)-\sin(k_{n}x)|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

∎

Corollary 4.4

(Normalized Dirichlet eigenfunction expansion)
.

Let
\varphi_{n}
be the Dirichlet eigenfunction associated with
\lambda_{n}
, normalized in
L^{2}(0,L)
. We choose its sign so that
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(0)>0
. Put

s_{n}(x):=\sin(k_{n}x),\qquad A_{n}:=\int_{0}^{L}s_{n}(t)I_{n}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
I_{n}
is the deterministic-frequency stochastic integral from Corollary
4.3
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\bigg|\varphi_{n}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\left[s_{n}(x)+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}\left(I_{n}(x)-\frac{2}{L}A_{n}s_{n}(x)\right)\right]\bigg|

\displaystyle=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Equivalently, the normalization constant of the unnormalized eigenfunction
u_{k_{n}}
satisfies

\|u_{k_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{Lk_{n}}A_{n}\right)+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

In particular,

\sup_{0\leq x\leq L}\left|\varphi_{n}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin(k_{n}x)\right|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Proof.

Almost surely, for all sufficiently large
n
,
\lambda_{n}>0
, and hence
\varphi_{n}=u_{k_{n}}/\|u_{k_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}
with the prescribed sign. By Corollary
4.3
,

u_{k_{n}}(x)=s_{n}(x)+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)+R_{n}(x),\qquad\sup_{x\leq L}|R_{n}(x)|=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\quad\text{a.s.}

Moreover, by the BDG estimate (
11
) and Borel–Cantelli,

\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Consequently

|A_{n}|\leq\|s_{n}\|_{L^{1}(0,L)}\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

We first expand the normalization factor. Since
k_{n}L=n\pi+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})
almost surely,

\displaystyle\|s_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}(k_{n}x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\sin(2k_{n}L)}{4k_{n}}=\frac{L}{2}+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Using the expansion of
u_{k_{n}}
, we get

\displaystyle\|u_{k_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|s_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}+\frac{2\rho}{k_{n}}\int_{0}^{L}s_{n}(t)I_{n}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2}+\frac{2\rho}{k_{n}}A_{n}+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Here the terms involving
R_{n}
are
O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})
. The quadratic term is also harmless: applying the bound
\sup_{x}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{\varepsilon/3})
gives

k_{n}^{-2}\|I_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{2}=O(n^{-2+2\varepsilon/3})=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon}).

Since
k_{n}^{-1}A_{n}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})
, Taylor expansion of
x\mapsto x^{-1/2}
at
L/2
gives

\|u_{k_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{Lk_{n}}A_{n}\right)+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Multiplying this expansion by

u_{k_{n}}=s_{n}+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}+R_{n}

and using again
\sup_{x}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{\varepsilon})
,
|A_{n}|=O(n^{\varepsilon})
, and
\sup_{x}|R_{n}(x)|=O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{Lk_{n}}A_{n}\right)\left(s_{n}(x)+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}I_{n}(x)\right)+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\left[s_{n}(x)+\frac{\rho}{k_{n}}\left(I_{n}(x)-\frac{2}{L}A_{n}s_{n}(x)\right)\right]+O(n^{-2+\varepsilon})

uniformly for
0\leq x\leq L
. This proves the first-order normalized expansion. The final rough estimate follows because

\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sup_{x\leq L}|I_{n}(x)|=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\frac{|A_{n}|}{k_{n}}=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

∎

5
Discussion

The finite-interval white-noise Schrödinger operator is well-defined pathwise through the quasi-derivative formalism. Its basic spectral properties follow from the regular theory of Sturm–Liouville operators with distributional potentials in
H^{-1}(0,L)
. It has been shown that the stochastic Prüfer equation gives a convenient high-energy description of the spectrum. Although the detailed asymptotic theorems in the present paper are proved for the Dirichlet problem, the method extends to general separated self-adjoint boundary conditions after adding the endpoint boundary phases described in Section 3.2. In such cases the leading Brownian correction still comes from the oscillatory integral in the interior, while the deterministic and random endpoint phases contribute additional lower-order boundary terms depending on the separated parameters and on
\sigma(0),\sigma(L)
. We have not pursued this bookkeeping here. However, coupled boundary conditions require a different analysis. Although their eigenvalues can often be localized between eigenvalues of nearby separated boundary value problems by comparison or interlacing arguments, such bounds are too coarse to recover the stochastic correction terms obtained here. In particular, the correction scale for
k_{n}
is
O(n^{-1})
, or more softly
O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})
almost surely for every
\varepsilon>0
, whereas interlacing with adjacent separated problems typically loses information at the level of the spacing between neighboring free frequencies. A sharp expansion for coupled conditions would require an analysis of the corresponding transfer matrix or characteristic determinant, and may involve paired eigenvalues as in the periodic and anti-periodic problems.

The almost-sure bounds proved here are deliberately obtained by a robust finite-moment and Borel–Cantelli argument. Since the leading terms are Gaussian stochastic integrals with deterministic oscillatory integrands, one may expect logarithmic refinements, or even law-of-the-iterated-logarithm type statements along subsequences, under a more detailed analysis of the correlations in
n
. Such refinements are not needed for the present high-energy expansion and are left for future work.

Moreover, we give some remarks on the stochastic characteristics of the asymptotic results.

Remark 5.1

(Random structure of the high-energy spectrum and eigenfunctions)
.

The sequence
(k_{n}(\omega))_{n\geq 1}
may be viewed as a random point
configuration on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\xi_{\omega}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\delta_{k_{n}(\omega)}.

It is not a Poisson point process. Indeed, the almost-sure estimate

k_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

implies

k_{n+1}-k_{n}=\frac{\pi}{L}+O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Hence the unfolded spacings satisfy

\frac{L}{\pi}(k_{n+1}-k_{n})\to 1\qquad\text{a.s.}

This is a rigid, clock-type high-energy behaviour, in contrast with a
Poisson point process, whose spacings are exponentially distributed.

Nor should
(k_{n})
be regarded as a Lévy process. Even if the index
n

is interpreted as a discrete time parameter, the increments

k_{n+1}-k_{n}
are neither stationary nor independent; all eigenvalues are
functions of the same Brownian path on
[0,L]
.

The eigenvalues
k_{n}
themselves are random variables, not martingales,
because no natural filtration time is attached to the spectral index
n
.
Nevertheless, their leading fluctuations are expressed through the martingales

M_{x}(k)=\int_{0}^{x}\sin^{2}(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

defined first for deterministic values of the parameter
k
. For each such

k
, the process
x\mapsto M_{x}(k)
is a continuous martingale with quadratic
variation

\langle M(k)\rangle_{x}=\int_{0}^{x}\sin^{4}(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

In the eigenvalue expansion,

n\left(k_{n}-\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)=\frac{\rho}{\pi}M_{L}(K_{n})+O_{L^{p}}(n^{-1}),\qquad K_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L},

so the leading fluctuation is represented by deterministic-frequency Brownian
stochastic integrals. This formulation avoids interpreting

\int_{0}^{L}\sin^{2}(k_{n}s)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}
as an anticipating Itô integral. The
fluctuations for different
n
are generally correlated because the same
Brownian path drives all frequencies. Thus the high-energy spectrum is better
described as a small correlated stochastic perturbation of the deterministic
lattice
(n\pi/L)_{n\geq 1}
, rather than as a Poisson or Lévy process.

Similarly, the eigenfunction
u_{k}(x)
is not a martingale in
x
. It
satisfies

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{x}=P_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\,\mathrm{d}P_{x}=-k^{2}Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\rho Y_{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x},

so both components contain drift terms. The first-order correction

I_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\sin(k(x-s))\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

is a Volterra stochastic integral rather than a martingale. If

J_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\cos(k(x-s))\sin(ks)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

then

\,\mathrm{d}I_{k}(x)=kJ_{k}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\,\mathrm{d}J_{k}(x)=\sin(kx)\,\,\mathrm{d}B_{x}-kI_{k}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus the fluctuation of the eigenfunction is martingale-driven, but the
eigenfunction itself is not a martingale.

Natural continuations of the present work include a full treatment of general separated boundary conditions, including Neumann and Robin endpoints, colored-noise approximations converging to the white-noise model, and higher-order stochastic asymptotics. Another possible direction is to use the high-mode eigenfunction expansions obtained here in random spectral-decomposition problems on a fixed interval.
