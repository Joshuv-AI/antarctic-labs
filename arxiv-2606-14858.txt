Regularity theory for fully nonlinear oblique transmission problems
Iñigo U. Erneta

Abstract
. We develop a regularity theory for fully nonlinear oblique transmission problems. Our main result establishes optimal regularity of viscosity solutions for flat interfaces. For constant-coefficient equations, such solutions are found to be piecewise C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} up to the interface. This regularity continues to hold for variable-coefficient equations with sufficiently regular coefficients in a more restrictive class of viscosity solutions.



1.
Introduction

Transmission problems model scenarios where the configuration space of interest is composed of multiple pieces or
phases
, each characterized by different properties.
These problems arise naturally in non-homogeneous systems such as composite materials in Physics,
regime-switching models in Economics,
or the description of biological tissue in Biology.
From an analytical perspective, the abrupt changes in phase behavior across a separating interface are modeled by partial differential equations (PDE) with discontinuous coefficients.
This situation requires prescribing certain coupling criteria on the interface, known in the literature as transmission conditions.

In our recent paper
[
7
]
, we introduced a novel class of
fully nonlinear oblique
transmission problems.
A defining characteristic of this class is that the transmission condition depends on the full gradient of the solution from each side of the interface, whereas all previous works considered only the normal derivative.
Our model can thus be understood as a nonlinear coupling of oblique derivatives across the interface.
In the work
[
7
]
, we developed the foundational viscosity solution framework and established the well-posedness of constant-coefficient problems with flat interfaces.
Building upon those existence and uniqueness results, the present paper develops a comprehensive regularity theory for both constant- and variable-coefficient oblique transmission problems.
Our central contribution establishes the piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of viscosity solutions up to the flat interface, which is the best possible regularity without further assumptions.

1.1.
Main results

Let
B_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|<R\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the open ball of radius
R>0
centered at the origin.
We think of
B_{R}
as being divided into two phases, the upper and lower half-balls

B_{R}^{+}=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}>0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{R}^{-}=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}<0\}.

These phases are separated by the flat interface

T_{R}=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}=0\}.

For functions
u\colon B_{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the superscript notation
u^{\pm}:=u\chi_{B_{R}^{\pm}\cup T_{R}}
to denote the restrictions to each half-ball, up to the interface.

We consider the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{+}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{+}(x)&\text{ in }B_{R}^{+},\\
F^{-}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{-}(x)&\text{ in }B_{R}^{-},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{R},\\
u^{+}=u^{-}&\text{ on }T_{R},\\
\end{cases}

where
F^{\pm}
and
G
are fully nonlinear operators (see Section
2
),
f^{\pm}
are functions in
B_{R}^{\pm}
,
g
is a function on
T_{R}
, and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
is a constant.
The second order equations in each phase are known as
bulk equations
, while the first order identity on the interface is the
transmission condition
.
The last equation serves to indicate that
u
must be continuous across the interface and will be assumed implicitly throughout the work.

Our transmission operator
G
can be interpreted as a nonlinear coupling of oblique derivatives, generalizing the linear condition

G(\nabla u^{+},\nabla u^{-},x)=\gamma^{+}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}.

Such operators are characterized by three ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
quantifying the size of the coefficients.
Namely, writing
\gamma^{\pm}=(\gamma^{\prime\pm},\gamma^{\pm}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, we impose that:

•

the normal components are controlled above and below by

\lambda\leq\gamma_{n}^{\pm}(x)\leq\Lambda\qquad\text{ for }x\in T_{R};

•

the tangential components are bounded by

|\gamma^{\prime\pm}(x)|\leq\mu\qquad\text{ for }x\in T_{R}.

We give a precise definition of our nonlinear operators and ellipticity in Section
2
below.

Finally, the
interpolation parameter

0\leq\theta\leq 1
interpolates between a “pure” transmission problem (
\theta=1
) and a degenerate one (
\theta=0
) where the condition on
u^{-}
seems to disappear.
Somewhat surprisingly, all our estimates in the results below are independent of
\theta
.

Our first result is a Hölder estimate for viscosity solutions to variable-coefficient problems.
Below and throughout the text, a constant is
universal
if it depends only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and
\mu
.
We note in particular that a universal constant does not depend on the interpolation parameter
\theta
.
For the definition of viscosity solution, see Definition
2.1
below:

Theorem 1.1

(
C^{\alpha}
estimate)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be continuous operators in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively, and with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
\{f^{\pm},g\}
be continuous functions in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Assume that
F^{\pm}(0,x)=0
for
x\in B_{1}^{\pm}
, and
G(0,0,x)=0
for
x\in T_{1}
.

There are universal constants
0<\alpha<1
and
C>0
such that
u
is
C^{\alpha}
in
B_{1}
and

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{3/4})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\right).

Remark 1.2
.

The theorem continues to hold without assumptions
F^{\pm}(0,x)=0
and
G(0,0,x)=0
,
replacing the functions
f^{\pm}
and
g
in the estimate by
f^{\pm}-F^{\pm}(0,\cdot)
and
g-G(0,0,\cdot)
, respectively.

Theorem
1.1
is the fundamental ingredient for the whole regularity theory, as explained below.
In fact, we will prove a more general result, Theorem
3.3
, where the
C^{\alpha}
estimate is shown to hold for functions in a universal “solution class” (Section
2
).
The estimate is a consequence of a new Harnack inequality,
which we prove using appropriate piecewise quadratic barriers (see Section
3
).

Our second result gives the optimal regularity of constant-coefficient inhomogeneous problems:

Theorem 1.3

(Piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
regularity)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=0&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

There are universal constants
0<\overline{\alpha}<1
and
C>0
such that

u^{\pm}
are
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and

\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(\overline{B}^{\pm}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+|F^{+}(0)|+|F^{-}(0)|+|G(0,0)|\right).

Moreover,
u
satisfies the transmission condition in the classical sense.

Remark 1.4
.

The piecewise Hölder regularity of the gradient cannot be improved.
Indeed, by the celebrated counterexamples of Nadirashvili-Vlăduţ
[
13
,
14
]
, we can find a uniformly elliptic fully nonlinear operator
F
and a viscosity solution
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
to the elliptic equation

F(D^{2}u)=0\qquad\text{ in }B_{1},

such that
u
is
C^{1,\alpha}
but not
C^{1,1}
at the origin.
It follows that
u
is a viscosity solution to the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F(D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
u_{x_{n}}^{+}-u_{x_{n}}^{-}=0&\text{ on }T_{1},\\
\end{cases}

hence we cannot extend the estimates in Theorem
1.3
to include
C^{1,1}
norms.
Moreover, the gradient will be discontinuous in general, as can be seen from the simple transmission condition

u_{x_{n}}^{+}-2u_{x_{n}}^{-}=0.

It is not clear what the optimal Hölder exponent might be.
In principle, it could be smaller than the best exponent for second order fully nonlinear equations.

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we exploit the invariance of the constant-coefficient problem under horizontal translations.
This property allows us to show that tangential difference quotients satisfy the Hölder estimate of Theorem
1.1
.
We then combine this observation with the classical boundary regularity for the Dirichlet problem on each side of the interface to obtain the piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate (we give the details in Section
4
).

Our third and final result establishes piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity for variable-coefficient problems.
Here, we need the stronger notion of
viscosity-
\star
solution
, which requires the equation to be satisfied with respect to a larger class of test functions (see Definition
7.2
below).
With a slight abuse of notation, we define the Morrey
C^{\alpha-1}
norm of
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{R}^{\pm}\cup T_{R})
by

(1.1)

\|f^{\pm}\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(\overline{B}^{\pm}_{R})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sup_{B_{r}(x)\subset B_{R}}r^{-\alpha}\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B^{\pm}_{r}(x))},

where
B_{r}(x)^{+}:=B_{r}(x)\cap\{x_{n}>0\}
and
B_{r}(x)^{-}:=B_{r}(x)\cap\{x_{n}<0\}
are not necessarily half-balls.

Theorem 1.5

(Piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity for variable-coefficent problems)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be Hölder continuous operators in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively, and with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
\{f^{\pm},g\}
be Hölder continuous functions in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity-
\star
solution to the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Assume that
F^{\pm}(0,x)=0
for
x\in B_{1}^{\pm}
.

If
\{G,g\}
are
C^{\alpha}
on
\overline{T}_{1}
for some
0<\alpha<\overline{\alpha}
, with
\overline{\alpha}
given by Theorem
1.3
above, then the restrictions
u^{\pm}
are
C^{1,\alpha}
on
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and satisfy

\begin{split}\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{B}^{\pm}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|G(0,0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(\overline{B}_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(\overline{B}_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{T}_{1})}\right)\end{split}

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
\alpha
,
\overline{\alpha}
, the modulus of continuity of
F^{\pm}
on
\overline{B}_{1}^{\pm}
, and the
C^{\alpha}
seminorm of
G
on
\overline{T_{1}}
.
Moreover,
u
satisfies the transmission condition in the classical sense.

Remark 1.6
.

The theorem continues to hold without the assumption
F^{\pm}(0,x)=0
, replacing the functions
f^{\pm}
in the estimate by
f^{\pm}-F^{\pm}(0,\cdot)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
is based on a delicate perturbative argument around constant-coefficient homogeneous transmission problems, inspired by Caffarelli’s work
[
3
,
4
]
.
Therefore, our estimates ultimately follow from Theorem
1.3
.

We argue by contradiction-compactness, assuming that the theorem fails along a sequence of solutions, from which we can then extract a converging subsequence.
Here, compactness is a consequence of the Hölder estimate in Theorem
1.1
combined with the boundary behavior of solutions obtained by appropriate piecewise smooth barriers in Section
5
.
The key step involves showing that the limiting function satisfies a suitable constant-coefficient problem, whence the uniqueness established in
[
7
]
yields a contradiction.
For this, we use non-explicit barriers
\{\psi_{k}\}
given as solutions to certain constant-coefficient inhomogeneous transmission problems.
This forces us to first show piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity (Theorem
6.1
) for such problems, which we obtain by perturbation in Section
6
.
We give the full proof of Theorem
1.5
in Section
7
.

It is worth noting that the transmission problems satisfied by the barriers
\{\psi_{k}\}
are, in fact, convex.
If we could show that viscosity solutions to such problems are further piecewise
C^{1,1}
regular, then Theorem
1.5
would hold for ordinary viscosity solutions (see Remark
7.7
).
Whether such an improved regularity holds is an open question that we intend to investigate in the future.

1.2.
References and state of the art

Transmission problems were introduced to the mathematical community by Picone
[
15
]
.

There is a classical regularity theory for variational or “divergence-form” transmission problems that goes back to the early second half of the previous century.
Here, one studies
weak
solutions obtained by minimization of suitable energy functionals.
For an account of this vast topic, we refer the reader to the monograph of Borsuk
[
1
]
and the reference therein.
There is also a summary of the regularity theory in the celebrated textbook of Ladyzhenskaya-Uraltseva
[
9
, Ch. 3.16]
, where such problems are known as “diffraction problems”.

Non-variational or “non-divergence-form” transmission problems are a much more recent topic that appeared only in the 2010s.

To the best of our knowledge, the first viscosity solution approach to such problems is due to De Silva-Ferrari-Salsa
[
5
]
.
In that work, the authors studied the regularity of a fully nonlinear free boundary problem.
Their analysis required linearizing at a free boundary point, which yields a constant-coefficient homogeneous transmission problem with flat interface.
While the bulk equations in this problem are nonlinear, the transmission condition is linear and only involves the normal derivative, i.e., it is a linear coupling of Neumann conditions.
In
[
5
]
, the authors obtained piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
estimates for this transmission problem as an auxiliary result, rather than as an object of independent study.
In a subsequent work
[
6
]
, the same authors focused on the regularity of the transmission problem itself, extending the theory to include bulk source terms.

The only other regularity paper in the non-variational framework is by Soria Carro-Stinga
[
16
]
.
There, the authors prove piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
estimates for constant-coefficient inhomogeneous problems with curved interfaces, including source terms in both the bulk equations and the transmission condition.
As in
[
5
,
6
]
, they consider linear transmission operators depending only on the normal derivatives.
We also mention the recent work of Jesus-Soria Carro
[
8
]
, where this result is extended to the parabolic setting.

Our contribution in the present paper is to extend nearly all previous regularity results to the oblique framework, a subject that had not been considered until our companion work
[
7
]
.
This task is quite involved, as the use of normal derivatives greatly simplifies the analysis in
[
5
,
6
,
16
]
.
The key qualitative difference between the two settings can be perceived in the barriers involved in the proofs.
Most notably, to prove the Harnack inequality in the non-oblique setting, it suffices to use simple radial functions that are not even piecewise defined.
By contrast, we need to carefully construct piecewise quadratic barriers to account for both the tangential contribution and the relative size of the normal components from each side at the interface condition.

We treat curved interfaces in a fothcoming work.
A crucial point in the non-oblique setting
[
16
]
, where such surfaces have already been considered, is the use of certain piecewise smooth barriers obtained by solving the classical Dirichlet problem for fully nonlinear equations.
This gives a very clean proof of both the ABP estimate and the Harnack inequality.
Unfortunately, the same method fails in the oblique case.
Instead, we can apply a flattening procedure to the interface and then argue similarly to
[
7
]
and the present paper.
While such a transformation preserves the transmission condition, the bulk equations now involve lower order terms with coefficients that blow up at the interface.
The analysis is much more technical, but we can ultimately reach the same
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate.

To conclude, we mention some related results in the context of boundary regularity for the Neumann and oblique derivative problems.
Here, recall that our transmission problems can be thought of as extensions of such problems, as they can be recovered, in some sense, by setting the interpolation parameter to
\theta=0
.
The earliest result on the regularity for fully nonlinear oblique derivative conditions that we are aware of is by Lieberman-Trudinger
[
10
]
.
Our barriers in Section
3
are inspired by that work.
The Neumann problem with fully nonlinear bulk equations was studied by Milakis-Silvestre
[
12
]
, and later extended to oblique derivative problems by Li-Zhang
[
11
]
.

1.3.
Notation

Here we introduce some basic notation used throughout the manuscript.

Given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote its bulk phases by

\Omega^{+}=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}>0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Omega^{-}=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}<0\},

and its flat interface by

T=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}=0\}=\partial\Omega^{\pm}\cap\Omega.

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
R>0
, we denote the open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

B_{R}(x_{0})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x-x_{0}|<R\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{R}=B_{R}(0).

When
x_{0}\in T
, we also define the open balls on the interface as

T_{R}(x_{0})=B_{R}(x_{0})\cap\{x_{n}=0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad T_{R}=T_{R}(0).

For a continuous function
u
in
\Omega
, we define its restrictions to each phase, up to the interface, by

u^{\pm}:=u\chi_{\overline{\Omega}^{\pm}\cap\Omega}=u\chi_{\Omega^{\pm}\cup T},

where
\chi_{E}
is the characteristic function of the set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The positive and negative parts of
u
are

u_{+}:=\max\{u,0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{-}:=\max\{-u,0\}.

Note our use of superscripts for restrictions, while subscripts denote the positive and negative parts.

For matrices
N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we always use the operator norm

\|N\|=\sup_{x\in B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}|Nx|.

The space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices is denoted

\mathcal{S}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The set of elliptic matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
satisfying the bounds
\lambda I\leq A\leq\Lambda I
is written
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
.
We denote the Pucci extremal operators acting on matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by

\mathcal{M}^{+}(M;\lambda,\Lambda)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}}\operatorname{Tr}(AM)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{M}^{-}(M;\lambda,\Lambda)=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}}\operatorname{Tr}(AM).

We write vectors in terms of tangential and normal components as
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.
The set of oblique derivative coefficients with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
is given by

\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}=\left\{\gamma=(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon\quad\lambda\leq\gamma_{n}\leq\Lambda,\quad|\gamma^{\prime}|\leq\mu\right\}.

We define the extremal transmission operators
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
acting on vectors
\xi^{\pm}=(\xi^{\prime\pm},\xi_{n}^{\pm})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
by

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{+}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})&=\mathcal{T}^{+}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\sup_{\gamma^{\pm}\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\left[\gamma^{+}\cdot\xi^{+}-\gamma^{-}\cdot\xi^{-}\right],\\
\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})&=\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\inf_{\gamma^{\pm}\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\left[\gamma^{+}\cdot\xi^{+}-\gamma^{-}\cdot\xi^{-}\right].\end{split}

For reference, we note that these operators can be written explicitly as

(1.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{+}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})&=\Lambda(\xi_{n}^{+})_{+}-\lambda(\xi_{n}^{+})_{-}-\lambda(\xi_{n}^{-})_{+}+\Lambda(\xi_{n}^{-})_{-}+\mu|\xi^{\prime+}|+\mu|\xi^{\prime-}|,\\
\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})&=\lambda(\xi_{n}^{+})_{+}-\Lambda(\xi_{n}^{+})_{-}-\Lambda(\xi_{n}^{-})_{+}+\lambda(\xi_{n}^{-})_{-}-\mu|\xi^{\prime+}|-\mu|\xi^{\prime-}|,\end{split}

depending on the sign of the normal components
\xi^{\pm}_{n}
.
The operators
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
were introduced in
[
7
]
.

Derivatives are written as subscripts, e.g.,
u_{x_{i}}
,
u_{x_{i}x_{j}}
, and so on.
We also use the gradient
\nabla u=(u_{x_{1}},\ldots,u_{x_{n}})
vector
and the tangential gradient
\nabla^{\prime}u=(u_{x_{1}},\ldots,u_{x_{n-1}},0)
.

2.
Framework and basic tools

Here, we recall the framework of viscosity solutions as well as various fundamental results from our previous paper
[
7
]
.

Given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider the transmission problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T,\end{cases}

where the functions
F^{\pm}(M,\cdot)
and
f^{\pm}
are continuous in
\Omega^{\pm}\cup T
,
the functions
G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},\cdot)
and
g
are continuous on
T
, and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
is a constant.
We assume that the operators
F^{\pm}
and
G
are uniformly elliptic, in the sense that there are constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
such that:

•

For any symmetric matrices
M
and
N
, with
N
nonnegative definite, we have

\lambda\|N\|\leq F^{\pm}(M+N,x)-F^{\pm}(M,x)\leq\Lambda\|N\|;

•

For any vectors
\xi^{\pm}
and
\eta^{\pm}=(\eta^{\prime\pm},\eta_{n}^{\pm})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, with
\eta_{n}^{\pm}\geq 0
, we have

G(\xi^{+}+\eta^{+},\xi^{-}+\eta^{-},x)\leq G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)+\Lambda\eta^{+}_{n}-\lambda\eta^{-}_{n}+\mu(|\eta^{\prime+}|+|\eta^{\prime-}|)

and

G(\xi^{+}+\eta^{+},\xi^{-}+\eta^{-},x)\geq G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)+\lambda\eta^{+}_{n}-\Lambda\eta^{-}_{n}-\mu(|\eta^{\prime+}|+|\eta^{\prime-}|).

Let
u\in C(\Omega)
.
We say that
a continuous function
\varphi

touches

u

from above
(resp.
from below
) at a point
x_{0}\in\Omega
if
\varphi(x_{0})=u(x_{0})
and

\varphi(x)\geq u(x)\qquad\text{(resp. }\varphi(x)\leq u(x))

for all
x
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.
It touches
strictly
if the inequalities are strict for
x\neq x_{0}
.

By a
piecewise quadratic polynomial
, we mean a continuous function
P\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that each restriction
P^{\pm}
is a quadratic polynomial

P^{\pm}(x)=\frac{1}{2}M^{\pm}(x-x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+a^{\pm}\cdot(x-x_{0})+c

for some symmetric matrices
M^{\pm}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, vectors
a^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

We recall the following notion of viscosity solution introduced in
[
7
]
:

Definition 2.1

(Viscosity solution)
.

A continuous function
u\in C(\Omega)
is a

viscosity subsolution
(resp.
supersolution
) to (
2.1
) if whenever a piecewise quadratic polynomial
P
touches
u
from above (resp. below) at
x_{0}\in\Omega
and:

•

x_{0}\in\Omega^{\pm}
, then

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},x_{0})\geq f^{\pm}(x_{0})\qquad(\text{resp. }F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},x_{0})\leq f^{\pm}(x_{0}));

•

x_{0}\in T
, then

G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}),x_{0})\geq g(x_{0})\qquad\text{(resp. }G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}),x_{0})\leq g(x_{0})).

We say that
u
is a
viscosity solution
to (
2.1
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and supersolution.

Remark 2.2
.

When
x_{0}\in T
, by a standard device, we may always assume that
P
touches
u
strictly and additionally satisfies

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P,x_{0})<-\varepsilon^{-1}\qquad\text{(resp. }F^{\pm}(D^{2}P,x_{0})>\varepsilon^{-1}\text{)}

in the subsolution (resp. supersolution) condition above, with
\varepsilon>0
as small as we like; see Lemma 2.6 in
[
7
]
for the details.

The extremal operators
\{\mathcal{M}^{\pm},\mathcal{T}^{\pm}\}
introduced in the notation section (see subsection
1.3
above) give rise to universal viscosity subsolution and supersolution classes:

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
u\in\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
\Omega
, if
u
is a viscosity subsolution to

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}u;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\geq f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\geq g(x)&\text{ on }T.\end{cases}

Similarly, we say that
u\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
\Omega
, if
u
is a viscosity supersolution to

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{-}(D^{2}u;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\leq f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\leq g(x)&\text{ on }T.\end{cases}

We also define the solution classes

S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)=\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)\cap\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)

and

S^{\star}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)=\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;-|f^{+}|,-|f^{-}|,-|g|)\cap\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;|f^{+}|,|f^{-}|,|g|).

To conclude this section, we list several fundamental results obtained in our previous work
[
7
]
.
First is the well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem for constant-coefficient transmission problems:

Theorem 2.4

(Existence and uniqueness – Theorem 1.1 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient uniformly elliptic operators.
Let

,

, and
\phi\in C(\partial B_{1})
.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.

There exists a unique viscosity solution
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
to the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\\
u=\phi&\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.\end{cases}

Next, we recall our ABP maximum principle for functions in the supersolution class, which is a basic tool in the proofs of all our estimates below:

Theorem 2.5

(ABP – c.f. Theorem 3.1 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
u\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
B_{R}
, and assume that
u\in C(\overline{B_{R}})
.
There is a universal constant
C>0
such that

\sup_{B_{R}}u_{-}\leq\sup_{\partial B_{R}}u_{-}+CR\left(\|f^{+}_{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{R}^{+})}+\|f^{-}_{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{R}^{-})}+\|g_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{R})}\right).

Finally, we state our comparison principle for constant-coefficient transmission problems:

Theorem 2.6

(Comparison principle – c.f. Theorem 4.3 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
\{f^{\pm},\widetilde{f}^{\pm}\}
be functions in
C(B^{\pm}_{R}\cup T_{R})
, let
\{g,\widetilde{g}\}
be functions in
C(T_{R})
, and let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.

If
u\in C(B_{R})
and

are a viscosity subsolution and supersolution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)\geq f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{R}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})\geq g(x)&\text{ on }T_{R},\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}v)\leq\widetilde{f}^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{R}^{\pm},\\

3.
Harnack inequality and Hölder estimate

Our main result in this section is a Harnack inequality for functions in the solution class.
To prove it, we modify the classical quadratic barrier for the oblique derivative problem
[
10
]
by making it piecewise quadratic, and then combine it with the ABP estimate (Theorem
2.5
).

To state the inequality accurately, it is convenient to introduce a special notation for thin cylinders.
For
\rho^{\pm}>0
and
R>0
, we define the two-sided cylinder

\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},R)=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(-R\rho^{-},R\rho^{+})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}

and the one-sided one

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},R)=B_{R}^{\prime}\times\textstyle(\frac{1}{2}R\rho^{+},R\rho^{+}).

We also write
Q_{R}
for the standard “thick” two-sided cylinder

Q_{R}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(-R,R)=\mathcal{G}(1,1,R).

Lemma 3.1

(Harnack inequality)
.

Let
u\in S^{\star}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
Q_{3}
.

There are universal constants

0<\rho^{-}<1
,
\rho^{+}=\frac{\lambda}{4\Lambda}\rho^{-}
,
and
C>0
such that if

u\geq 0\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},3),

then

\sup_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)}u\leq C\left(\inf_{\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)}u+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3})}\right).

Proof.

Once we have chosen
\rho^{\pm}
, the classical Harnack inequality in
\mathcal{G}^{+}(\rho^{+},3)
(Theorem 4.3 in
[
2
]
) and a chaining argument yield

(3.1)

\sup_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)}u\leq C\left(\inf_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)}u+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{+}_{3})}\right)

for some universal constant
C>0
.
Hence it suffices to control the infimum in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)
.

Let
m=\inf_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)}u
and
consider the piecewise quadratic polynomial

\varphi=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}|x^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{x_{n}}{2\rho^{+}}+\left(\frac{x_{n}}{2\rho^{+}}\right)^{2}\right]\chi_{\{x_{n}\geq 0\}}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{x_{n}}{\rho^{-}}+\left(\frac{x_{n}}{3\rho^{-}}\right)^{2}\right]\chi_{\{x_{n}\leq 0\}}.

We will show that the barrier
m\varphi
controls
u
from below in the region

\mathcal{G}_{2}:=\textstyle\mathcal{G}(\frac{3}{2}\rho^{-},\rho^{+},2)=B_{2}^{\prime}\times(-3\rho^{-},2\rho^{+}),

for an appropriate choice of
\rho^{-}
(recall that
\rho^{+}=\frac{\lambda}{4\Lambda}\rho^{-}
).

First, we claim that at the boundary of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we have the upper bounds

(3.2)

\varphi\leq 1\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{G}_{2}\cap\{x_{n}=2\rho^{+}\}

and

(3.3)

\varphi\leq 0\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{G}_{2}\setminus\{x_{n}=2\rho^{+}\}.

Indeed, direct computations show that:

•

At the top lid
\partial\mathcal{G}_{2}\cap\{x_{n}=2\rho^{+}\}

\varphi\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1+1)=1;

•

At the upper lateral boundary

\{|x^{\prime}|=2\}\cap\{0\leq x_{n}\leq 2\rho^{+}\}

\varphi\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1+1-2^{2})=0;

•

At the lower lateral boundary

\{|x^{\prime}|=2\}\cap\{-3\rho^{-}\leq x_{n}\leq 0\}

\varphi\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}(-2^{2})=-\frac{1}{2}<0;

•

At the bottom lid
\partial\mathcal{G}_{2}\cap\{x_{n}=-3\rho^{-}\}

\varphi\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}(-3+1)=0.

Moreover,
the Hessian for
x_{n}<0
is

D^{2}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{(3\rho^{-})^{2}}e_{n}\otimes e_{n}-I^{\prime}\right]

(here, with a slight abuse of notation
I^{\prime}=I-e_{n}\otimes e_{n}
)
and hence

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{-}(D^{2}\varphi;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n(3\rho^{-})^{2}}-\Lambda(n-1)\right)\quad\text{ for }x_{n}<0,

which is positive for

(3.4)

\rho^{-}<\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{n(n-1)\Lambda}}.

Recalling that
\rho^{+}=\frac{\lambda}{4\Lambda}\rho^{-}<\rho^{-}
,
under assumption (
3.4
),
it is immediate to check that we also have

(3.5)

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{-}(D^{2}\varphi;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)>0\quad\text{ for }x_{n}>0\text{ and }x_{n}<0.

Since
\varphi_{x_{n}}^{+}=\frac{1}{8\rho^{+}}>0
and
\varphi_{x_{n}}^{-}=\frac{1}{4\rho^{-}}>0
, by (
1.2
), the transmission condition on
T_{2}
is

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla\varphi^{+},\theta\nabla\varphi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)&=\lambda\varphi_{x_{n}}^{+}-\Lambda\theta\varphi_{x_{n}}^{-}-(1+\theta)\mu|\nabla^{\prime}\varphi|\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{\lambda}{2\rho^{+}}-\frac{\Lambda}{\rho^{-}}\theta-4(1+\theta)\mu|x^{\prime}|\right]\\
&\qquad\geq\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{\lambda}{2\rho^{+}}-\frac{\Lambda}{\rho^{-}}-8\mu\right]\\
&\qquad\quad\geq\frac{\Lambda}{4\rho^{-}}-2\mu,\end{split}

therefore, assuming that

(3.6)

\rho^{-}<\frac{\Lambda}{8\mu},

the extremal operator becomes positive, i.e.,

(3.7)

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla\varphi^{+},\theta\nabla\varphi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)>0\quad\text{ on }\{x_{n}=0\}.

Combining (
3.4
) and (
3.6
), we finally take the universal constant

\rho^{-}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{n(n-1)\Lambda}},\frac{\Lambda}{8\mu}\right\}.

With this choice, from (
3.2
), (
3.3
), (
3.5
), and (
3.7
) it follows that

\begin{cases}u-m\varphi\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;|f^{+}|,|f^{-}|,|g|)&\text{ in }\mathcal{G}_{2},\\
u-m\varphi\geq 0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{G}_{2},\end{cases}

whence by the ABP maximum principle, we deduce

(3.8)

m\varphi\leq u+C\left(\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{+}_{3})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{-}_{3})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3})}\right)\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{G}_{2}.

Moreover, the barrier
\varphi
is positive in
\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)
, with a uniform bound.
Namely:

•

In
\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)^{+}=B_{1}^{\prime}\times(0,\rho^{+})

\varphi\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{4};

•

In
\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)^{-}=B_{1}^{\prime}\times(-\rho^{-},0)

\varphi\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\left[-1+\frac{1}{3^{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{36}.

Therefore, taking the infimum in
\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)
in (
3.8
), we conclude

(3.9)

\inf_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2)}u=m\leq 36\inf_{\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},1)}u+C\left(\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{+}_{3})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{-}_{3})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3})}\right),

which combined with (
3.1
) yields the claim.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

It is clear from the proof above that the weaker estimate (
3.9
) holds assuming only that
u\geq 0
and
u\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;|f^{+}|,|f^{-}|,|g|)
in
Q_{3}
.

As a direct consequence of the Harnack inequality, we deduce Hölder continuity:

Theorem 3.3

(
C^{\alpha}
estimate)
.

Let
u\in S^{\star}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
Q_{1}
.

There are universal constants
0<\alpha<1
and
C>0
such that

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{+}_{1})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{-}_{1})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\right).

Proof.

By classical interior regularity (Proposition 4.10 in
[
2
]
) and a covering argument, it suffices to prove the Hölder estimate only at the interface
T_{1/2}
.
Namely, we need to show that

(3.10)

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{+}_{1})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q^{-}_{1})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\right)|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}

for all
x_{0}\in T_{1/2}
and all
x\in Q_{1}
sufficiently close to
x_{0}
.

For
0<R\leq 1
, with the notation above, define

m_{R}:=\inf_{\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},R)}u,\qquad M_{R}:=\sup_{\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},R)}u,\qquad o_{R}:=\underset{\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},R)}{\operatorname{osc}}u=M_{R}-m_{R}.

Let
0<R\leq 1/6
.
Since
u-m_{3R}
and
M_{3R}-u
are nonnegative in
\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},3R)\subset Q_{1/2}
, applying Lemma
3.1
to each function (rescaled to
Q_{3R}
) and adding both estimates, we deduce

\begin{split}M_{3R}-m_{3R}&\leq\sup_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2R)}(M_{3R}-u)+\sup_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{+}(\rho^{+},2R)}(u-m_{3R})\\
&\quad\leq C\left(M_{3R}-M_{R}+m_{R}-m_{3R}+R\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3R}^{+})}+R\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3R}^{-})}+R\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3R})}\right).\end{split}

From here, reordering terms, we obtain

o_{R}\leq\frac{C-1}{C}o_{3R}+R\big(\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3R}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{3R}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3R})}\big)

and a standard iteration of this inequality leads to an algebraic oscillation decay

(3.11)

o_{r}\leq Cr^{\alpha}\left(o_{1/2}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{1/2}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{1/2}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1/2})}\right)\quad\text{ for }r\leq 1/2,

with
\alpha=\log_{3}\frac{C}{C-1}>0
.
For
x_{0}\in T_{1/2}
and
r\leq 1/2
, we have that

B_{\rho^{+}r}(x_{0})\subset x_{0}+\mathcal{G}(\rho^{-},\rho^{+},r)\subset Q_{1}

and hence a translation of (
3.11
) yields

\underset{B_{\rho^{+}r}(x_{0})}{\operatorname{osc}}u\leq Cr^{\alpha}\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(Q_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\right),

whence it follows that (
3.10
) holds for all
x\in B_{\rho^{+}/2}(x_{0})
.
∎

The
C^{\alpha}
estimate for nonlinear transmission problems, Theorem
1.1
in the Introduction, follows immediately from Theorem
3.3
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
u
belongs to
S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+}-F^{+}(0,\cdot),f^{-}-F^{-}(0,\cdot),g-G(0,0,\cdot))
in
B_{1}
(see Proposition 2.16 in
[
7
]
), by Theorem
3.3
(rescaled and translated) it satisfies a
C^{\alpha}
estimate in interior cubes.
The claim follows by a standard covering argument.
∎

We note that combining the classical interior estimates with Theorem
3.3
gives a
C^{\alpha}
estimate on arbitrary balls, not necessarily centered on the interface:

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
u\in S^{\star}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
B_{1}
.

There are universal constants
0<\alpha<1
and
C>0
such that

R^{\alpha}[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{R/2}(x))}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(x))}+R\Big(\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B^{+}_{R}(x))}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B^{-}_{R}(x))}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T\cap B_{R}(x))}\Big)\right)

for every ball
B_{R}(x)\subset B_{1}
.

Proof.

Combining interior estimates and Theorem
3.3
, by a standard scaling and covering argument, the solution
u
satisfies

(3.12)

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B^{+}_{1})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B^{-}_{1})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\right)

where
C>0
is some universal constant.

Take a dimensional number of points
x_{k}\in B_{R/2}(x)
such that

B_{R/2}(x)\subset\cup_{k}B_{R/16}(x_{k})
.
Keep the interior balls
\{B_{R/16}(x_{i})\}_{i}
with

B_{R/8}(x_{i})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x_{n}=0\}
,
and note that
B_{R/8}(x_{i})\subset B_{R}(x)
by assumption.
For the remaining balls
\{B_{R/16}(x_{j})\}_{j}
,
we can find points
y_{j}\in T\cap B_{R/8}(x_{j})
, whence
B_{R/16}(x_{j})\subset B_{3R/16}(y_{j})
and also
B_{3R/8}(y_{j})\subset B_{R/4}(x_{j})\subset B_{R}(x)
by assumption.

It follows that we can cover
B_{R/2}(x)
by the families
\{B_{R/16}(x_{i})\}_{i}
and
\{B_{3R/16}(y_{j})\}_{j}
.
For the first family, by interior estimates we have that

\begin{split}R^{\alpha}[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{R/16}(x_{i}))}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R/8}(x_{i}))}+R\|f\|_{L^{n}(B_{R/8}(x_{i}))}\right),\end{split}

and for the second family, by (

) (translated and rescaled), we deduce

\begin{split}&R^{\alpha}[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{3R/16}(y_{i}))}\\
&\qquad\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{3R/8}(y_{i}))}+R\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B^{+}_{3R/8}(y_{i}))}+R\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B^{-}_{3R/8}(y_{i}))}+R\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{3R/8}(y_{i}))}\right).\\
\end{split}

Adding all estimates, we obtain the desired bound on
\overline{B}_{R/2}(x)
.
∎

4.
Regularity of constant-coefficient homogeneous problems

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1.3
, the piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
estimate for constant-coefficient homogeneous transmission problems.
The reasoning is classical and goes as follows.
Since horizontal difference quotients belong to the solution class (see Lemma
4.1
below),
they satisfy a
C^{\alpha}
estimate (Section
3
).
Iterated, this result yields
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity in the tangential variables.
In particular, the trace
u|_{T}
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
function whence, by boundary regularity on each side, it follows that the restrictions
u^{\pm}
are
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
up to the interface, for some
\overline{\alpha}\leq\alpha
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

It will suffice to prove a variant of the estimate in the unit cylinder.
Thus, we first assume that
u\in C(Q_{1})
is a viscosity solution to

(4.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }Q_{1}^{\pm}\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=0&\text{ in }T_{1},\\
\end{cases}

where, using the notation from Section
3
, we write
Q_{R}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(-R,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
for
R>0
.

The reasoning is similar to the proof of Corollary 5.7 in
[
2
]
.
From (
4.1
), we have that

u\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;-F^{+}(0),-F^{-}(0),-G(0,0))\quad\text{ in }Q_{1}

(see Proposition 2.16 in
[
7
]
), and hence by the
C^{\alpha}
estimate (Theorem
3.3
) we deduce

(4.2)

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+|F^{+}(0)|+|F^{-}(0)|+|G(0,0)|\right)=:CK,

where
K:=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+|F^{+}(0)|+|F^{-}(0)|+|G(0,0)|
.
Here and throughout the proof, we write
C
to denote a positive universal constant that may change from line to line.

First, we note the following corollary of the comparison principle (Theorem
2.6
) concerning horizontal difference quotients:

Lemma 4.1

(Corollary 4.6 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
u\in C(Q_{R})
be a viscosity solution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }Q^{\pm}_{R},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=0&\text{ on }T_{R}.\end{cases}

Then, for all horizontal vectors
e=(e^{\prime},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have that

u(x+e)-u(x)\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)\quad\text{ in }Q_{R-|e|}.

Let
e
be a horizontal unit vector
e=(e^{\prime},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, with
|e|=1
.
For
0<h<1
and
0<\beta\leq 1
, we define
v_{\beta,h}
to be the tangential difference quotient

v_{\beta,h}:=\frac{u(\cdot+he)-u}{h^{\beta}}.

By Lemma
4.1
, we have that
v_{\beta,h}\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)
in
Q_{1-h}
, hence by Theorem
3.3
rescaled

(4.3)

\|v_{\beta,h}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{r})}\leq Cr^{-\alpha}\|v_{\beta,h}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{2r})}\leq Cr^{-\alpha}\sup_{|x_{n}|<4r}\|u(\cdot,x_{n})\|_{C^{\beta}(\overline{B}^{\prime}_{4r})},

for
0<r\leq 1/8
and
h<2r
.

Making
\alpha
smaller, we may assume that there is an integer
k
such that
(k-1)\alpha<1
and
k\alpha>1
.
Applying (
4.3
) with
\beta=\alpha
and
r=1/8
, by (
4.2
) we obtain

\|v_{\alpha,h}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{1/8})}\leq C\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{1/2})}\leq CK,

where
h<1/4
.
Since the horizontal vector
e
was arbitrary, it follows that
u
is
C^{2\alpha}
in the horizontal direction, with the estimate

\sup_{|x_{n}|<1/8}\|u(\cdot,x_{n})\|_{C^{2\alpha}(\overline{B}^{\prime}_{1/8})}\leq CK.

Applying (
4.3
) iteratively with
\beta=j\alpha
and
2\leq j<k-1
,
we deduce that
u
is
C^{(k-1)\alpha}
in the horizontal direction, with an estimate.
Using (
4.3
) again, we see that
u
is Lipschitz continuous in the horizontal variable, with

\sup_{|x_{n}|<2^{-2k+1}}\|u(\cdot,x_{n})\|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{B}^{\prime}_{2^{-2k+1}})}\leq C8^{-(k-1)\alpha}K,

and by (
4.3
) with
\beta=1
we obtain

(4.4)

\|v_{1,h}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{2^{-2k-1}})}\leq C8^{-k\alpha}K.

Letting
h\downarrow 0
in (
4.4
),
we deduce that
u
is differentiable in the horizontal direction and

\|u_{x_{i}}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{r_{k}})}\leq C_{k}K\qquad\text{ for }i<n,

where
r_{k}:=2^{-2k-1}
and
C_{k}>0
denotes a generic constant depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
k
.
In particular, the trace
u|_{T}
is
C^{1,\alpha}
on
\overline{T}_{r_{k}}
, with an estimate

(4.5)

\|u|_{T}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{T}_{r_{k}})}\leq C_{k}K.

Since the restrictions
u^{\pm}\in C(Q_{r_{k}}^{\pm}\cup T_{r_{k}})
solve the Dirichlet problems

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u^{\pm})=0&\text{ in }Q_{r_{k}}^{\pm},\\
u^{\pm}=u|_{T}&\text{ on }T_{r_{k}},\\
\end{cases}

boundary regularity (for instance, see Proposition 2.2 in
[
12
]
) and (

) finally give the estimate

(4.6)

\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(\overline{Q}^{\pm}_{r_{k}/2})}\leq C_{k}\left(\|u|_{T}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{T}_{r_{k}})}+\|u^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q^{\pm}_{r_{k}})}+|F^{\pm}(0)|\right)\leq C_{k}K,

with
\overline{\alpha}>0
universal and possibly smaller.

Therefore, viscosity solutions to (
4.1
) satisfy the piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
estimate (
4.6
).
A standard scaling and covering argument easily yields the claim in the original setting of half-balls.

It remains to prove that
u
satisfies the transmission condition in the classical sense.
Let
x_{0}\in T_{3/4}
.
By piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity, there is a piecewise linear polynomial
\ell
such that

|u-\ell|\leq C_{0}r^{1+\alpha}\quad\text{ in }B_{r}(x_{0})\subset B_{1},

for all
0<r\leq 1/4
, for some constant
C_{0}>0
.
For such an
r>0
and
0<\varepsilon<1
, let
w
be the piecewise quadratic barrier given by

w(x):=\ell(x)+C_{0}r^{\alpha-1}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(2r|x_{n}|-x_{n}^{2}\right)+|x^{\prime}-x_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}\right).

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we ensure that

(4.7)

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}w;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)<F^{\pm}(0).

Recalling that
x_{0}\in T
, on the boundary
\partial B_{r}(x_{0})
we have that

w-\ell\geq C_{0}r^{\alpha-1}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}r|x_{n}|+|x^{\prime}-x_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}\right)\geq C_{0}r^{\alpha-1}|x-x_{0}|^{2}=C_{0}r^{1+\alpha}\geq u-\ell

and hence

w\geq u\quad\text{ on }\partial B_{r}(x_{0}).

Since
w(x_{0})=u(x_{0})
, there is a vertical translation
w+c
, with
c\geq 0
, touching
u
from above at some
x_{r}\in B_{r}(x_{0})
.
In fact,
x_{r}\in T_{r}(x_{0})
, since otherwise the bulk equations would violate (

).

By the transmission condition at
x_{r}
, we conclude

G(\nabla w^{+}(x_{r}),\theta\nabla w^{-}(x_{r}))\geq g(x_{r}),

or equivalently

G(\nabla\ell^{+}+2C_{0}r^{\alpha-1}[\varepsilon^{-1}re_{n}+x_{r}^{\prime}-x_{0}^{\prime}],\theta\nabla\ell^{-}+\theta 2C_{0}r^{\alpha-1}[-\varepsilon^{-1}re_{n}+x_{r}^{\prime}-x_{0}^{\prime}])\geq g(x_{r}),

whence by uniform ellipticity

g(x_{r})\leq G(\nabla\ell^{+},\theta\nabla\ell^{-})+4C_{0}\Lambda r^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{-1}+4C_{0}\mu r^{\alpha},

and letting

g(x_{0})\leq G(\nabla\ell^{+},\theta\nabla\ell^{-})=G(\nabla u^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla u^{-}(x_{0})).

Using
2\ell-w
instead of

and arguing similarly, the other inequality follows.
∎

For linear equations, we can keep iterating Theorem
1.3
to deduce piecewise smoothness:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
A^{\pm}\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
,
\gamma^{\pm}\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}
,
f^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
,
g\in\mathbb{R}
, and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be constants.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to the linear constant-coefficient transmission problem

\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\pm}D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
\gamma^{+}\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\theta\gamma^{-}\cdot\nabla u^{-}=g&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Then
u
is piecewise
C^{\infty}
with an estimate

\|u\|_{C^{k}(\overline{B}_{1/4})}\leq C_{k}\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+|f^{+}|+|f^{-}|+|g|\right)

for each
k\geq 0
, where
C_{k}
depends only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
k
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.3
, we know that
u^{\pm}\in C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1})

satisfies an estimate

(4.8)

\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(\overline{B}^{\pm}_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+|f^{+}|+|f^{-}|+|g|\right)=:CK,

where
K:=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+|f^{+}|+|f^{-}|+|g|
.

Taking horizontal difference quotients, it is not difficult to see that the tangential derivatives of
u
satisfy the homogeneous transmission problem

(4.9)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\pm}D^{2}v)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm}\\
\gamma^{+}\cdot\nabla v^{+}-\theta\gamma^{-}\cdot\nabla v^{-}=0&\text{ on }T_{1},\end{cases}

in the viscosity sense.
By Theorem
1.3
(rescaled etc.) and (
4.8
), we deduce

\|u_{x_{i}}\|_{C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(\overline{B}_{r_{2}})}\leq C(r_{1},r_{2})\|u_{x_{i}}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{1}})}\leq C(r_{1},r_{2})K

for
r_{2}<r_{1}\leq 1/2
, where
C(r_{1},r_{2})>0
depends only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
r_{1}
, and
r_{2}
.
It follows in particular that
u|_{T}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{T}_{r_{2}})
,
and by boundary regularity of the Dirichlet problem,
for
r_{3}<r_{2}
we have that

\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{B}_{r_{3}})}\leq C(r_{2},r_{3})\left(\|u|_{T}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{T}_{r_{2}})}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{2}})}\right)\leq C(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})K,

proving the claim for
k=2
after an appropriate choice of
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
,
r_{3}
.

Exploiting the invariance of (

) under horizontal translations,
taking further tangential derivatives and arguing as above, it is not difficult to deduce the claim.
∎

5.
A boundary estimate

This section is devoted to the boundary behavior of viscosity solutions to transmission problems.
Our main result states that functions in the solution class
S
are continuous up to the boundary, with a modulus of continuity depending only on the boundary datum and the source terms.
This is achieved by constructing appropriate barriers, combined with the ABP maximum principle.
Such a boundary estimate is fundamental to the perturbation theory developed in Sections
6
and
7
.

Below, by a
modulus of continuity

\omega
for a function
u
in some set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we mean a function
\omega\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq\omega(|x-y|)\qquad\text{ for all }x\text{ and }y\text{ in }\Omega.

Theorem 5.1

(c.f. Proposition 4.14 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
satisfy
u\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
B_{1}
, where
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1})
and
g\in C(T_{1})
.
Let
\phi:=u|_{\partial B_{1}}
and let
\omega(|x-y|)
be a modulus of continuity for
\phi
on
\partial B_{1}
.
Assume that
K
is a positive constant such that

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\leq K.

Then there is a modulus of continuity
\omega^{\star}
of
u
in
\overline{B}_{1}
, depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
K
, and
\omega
.

Proof.

By interior estimates, it suffices to construct a modulus of continuity near the boundary.
Since the behavior close to the interface differs essentially from that away from it, we distinguish two types of boundary moduli:

•

for each
\kappa>0
, a modulus
\omega_{\kappa}
such that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\omega_{\kappa}(|x-x_{0}|)\quad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\{|x_{n}|\geq\kappa\}\cap\partial B_{1};

•

a modulus
\omega_{0}
such that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\omega_{0}(|x-x_{0}|)\quad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\{x_{n}=0\}\cap\partial B_{1}.

Once the two classes of moduli are available, it is not difficult to combine them with the interior
C^{\alpha}
estimates (Section
3
) into a modulus
\omega^{\star}
in the full ball
\overline{B}_{1}
.
The interested reader may find the technical details in Appendix
A
.

The construction of the first family
\{\omega_{\kappa}\}_{\kappa>0}
can be reduced to the classical theory.
Indeed, for each
\kappa>0
, we can find a neighborhood
B_{\delta_{\kappa}}(x_{0})\cap B_{1}
of
x_{0}\in\{|x_{n}|\geq\kappa\}\cap\partial B_{1}
, with
\delta_{\kappa}>0
depending only on
\kappa
, which does not intersect the interface
\{x_{n}=0\}
.
Following the proof of Proposition 4.14 in
[
2
]
, using the same linear barrier
h(x)=1-x\cdot x_{0}
and the classical ABP,
we obtain a modulus of continuity
\omega_{\kappa}
as above.

Therefore, it remains to construct the interface modulus
\omega_{0}
.
For simplicity, throughout the proof, we write
T
for the “global” interface

T=\{x_{n}=0\}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We must show that

(5.1)

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in T\cap\partial B_{1}\text{ such that }|x-y|\leq\delta,

for some
\delta>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
K
, and
\omega
.

We show (
5.1
) via a piecewise smooth barrier constructed in our previous paper
[
7
]
:

Lemma 5.2

(see the proof of Lemma 5.2 in
[
7
]
)
.

For each
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}\cap T
, there is a function
h\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
, with
h^{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1})
,
such that

(5.2)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}h;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)<0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla h^{+},\theta\nabla h^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)<0&\text{ on }T_{1},\\
\end{cases}

(5.3)

h>0\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}\setminus\{x_{0}\},

(5.4)

h(x)\leq C|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1},

where
0<\alpha<1
and
C>0
are universal constants.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
and consider
h
as above.
Take
\delta_{1}>0
depending on
\varepsilon
and
\omega
such that

|\varphi(x)-\varphi(x_{0})|=|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }x\in\partial B_{1}\cap B_{\delta_{1}}(x_{0}).

By the ABP estimate

(5.5)

|u(x)-u(x_{0})\pm\varepsilon|\leq 2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\varepsilon\leq C_{1}\quad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1},

where
C_{1}>0
depends only on
\varepsilon
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
K
.
For
0<\delta_{2}\leq\delta_{1}
, define

(5.6)

h_{\delta_{2}}:=\inf_{B_{1}\cap\partial B_{\delta_{2}}(x_{0})}h,

and note that
h_{\delta_{2}}>0
by (
5.3
).

Let
\Omega:=B_{1}\cap B_{\delta_{2}}(x_{0})
and define the barrier functions

\underline{\varphi}(x)=u(x)-u(x_{0})-\varepsilon-C_{1}\frac{h(x)}{h_{\delta_{2}}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\varphi}(x)=u(x)-u(x_{0})+\varepsilon+C_{1}\frac{h(x)}{h_{\delta_{2}}}.

By (
5.2
), (
5.5
), and (
5.6
), the functions
\underline{\varphi}
and
\overline{\varphi}
satisfy

\begin{cases}\underline{\varphi}\in\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\underline{\varphi}\leq 0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{cases}\overline{\varphi}\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\overline{\varphi}\geq 0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The ABP maximum principle in
\Omega
then gives that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\varepsilon+C_{2}\operatorname{diam}(\Omega)+C_{1}\frac{h(x)}{h_{\delta_{2}}}\leq\varepsilon+C_{2}\delta_{2}+C_{1}\frac{h(x)}{h_{\delta_{2}}}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

for some
C_{2}
depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
K
.
Taking
\delta_{2}
small such that
C_{2}\delta_{2}\leq\varepsilon
, we obtain

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq 2\varepsilon+C_{1}\frac{h(x)}{h_{\delta_{2}}}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Using (
5.4
) and taking
\delta\leq\delta_{2}
sufficiently small, we finally deduce

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq 3\varepsilon\quad\text{ in }B_{1}\cap B_{\delta}(x_{0}),

which is equivalent to (
5.1
).
∎

Combined with our comparison principle (Theorem
2.6
),
Theorem
5.1
allows us to approximate viscosity solutions to constant-coefficient inhomogeneous transmission problems by their “homogeneous” replacements.
This result is fundamental to the perturbation theory for such equations.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\end{cases}

and let
h\in C(\overline{B}_{3/4})
be the unique viscosity solution to the boundary-value problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}h)=0&\text{ in }B_{3/4}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla h^{+},\theta\nabla h^{-})=0&\text{ on }T_{3/4},\\
h=u&\text{ on }\partial B_{3/4}.\end{cases}

Assume that
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq 1
and that

\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B^{+}_{1})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B^{-}_{1})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\leq\delta.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
small depending only on
\varepsilon
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and
\mu
such that

\|u-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

By the interior
C^{\alpha}
estimate

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{3/4})}\leq C(1+\delta),

hence
u
has a Hölder modulus of continuity
\omega(r):=C(1+\delta)r^{\alpha}
on
\overline{B}_{3/4}
,
with
\alpha
and
C
universal.
By Theorem
5.1
, the function
h
has a modulus
\omega_{\delta}^{\star}
on
\overline{B}_{3/4}

depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
, and
\delta
.
Since
u-h=0
on
\partial B_{3/4}
,
for
0<\eta<1/4
,
by the boundary moduli we have that

\|u-h\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{3/4-\eta})}\leq\omega(\eta)+\omega^{\star}_{\delta}(\eta).

Moreover, by Theorem
2.6
, the difference
u-h
belongs to the solution class
S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
B_{3/4}
, whence by the ABP maximum principle

\|u-h\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{3/4-\eta})}\leq\|u-h\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{3/4-\eta})}+C\delta\leq\omega(\eta)+\omega_{\delta}^{\star}(\eta)+C\delta.

Therefore, first choosing
\delta>0
small such that
C\delta\leq\varepsilon/2
and then choosing
\eta>0
small such that
\omega(\eta)+\omega_{\delta}(\eta)\leq\varepsilon/2
, the claim follows.
∎

6.
Perturbation theory I: constant coefficients

In this section, we extend the piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
regularity of the constant-coefficient homogeneous problem (Theorem
1.3
) to the inhomogeneous setting.
This auxiliary result is crucial in the regularity theory for variable coefficients.

Recall the definition of the
C^{\alpha-1}
norm (
1.1
) in the Introduction.
Our efforts are devoted to proving the following theorem:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficent operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
, and assume that
F^{\pm}(0)=G(0,0)=0
.
Let
0<\alpha<\overline{\alpha}
, where
\overline{\alpha}
is the universal exponent in the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
.
Let
f^{\pm}
be continuous functions on
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
, let
g
be a
C^{\alpha}
function in
T_{1}
, and let
\theta\in[0,1]
be a constant.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Then
u^{\pm}
are
C^{1,\alpha}
on
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
, and moreover

\|u^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{B}_{1/2}^{\pm})}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|f^{+}\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{T}_{1})}\right)

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
\alpha
, and
\overline{\alpha}
.

Remark 6.2
.

Here, notice that there is no loss in assuming that
F^{\pm}(0)=0
and
G(0,0)=0
, since otherwise we may replace the functions
f^{\pm}
and
g
by
f^{\pm}-F^{\pm}(0)
and
g-G(0,0)
, respectively.

The
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate away from the interface is classical (see Section 8.2 in
[
2
]
), hence by a standard scaling and covering argument it suffices to prove a piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
bound at a single point on the interface, namely, the origin.
To this aim, we make several simplifying assumptions.

It is convenient to give the pointwise estimate in terms of more accurate
punctual
norms of the sources.
We define the punctual
C^{\alpha-1}
norm of
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{R}^{\pm}\cup T_{R})
at
x\in T_{R}
by

(6.1)

\|f^{\pm}\|_{C_{[x]}^{\alpha-1}(B^{\pm}_{R})}:=\sup_{B_{r}(x)\subset B_{R}}r^{-\alpha}\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B^{\pm}_{r}(x))}.

Similarly, for
g\in C(T_{R})
and
x\in T_{R}
, we consider the punctual Hölder seminorm

(6.2)

[g]_{C^{\alpha}_{[x]}(T_{R})}:=\sup_{y\in T_{R},\,y\neq x}\frac{|g(x)-g(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}.

By uniform ellipticity of
G
, recalling that
G(0,0)=0
, we can find a unique
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G(te_{n},0)=g(0)
and satisfying the estimate

|t|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}|g(0)|.

Therefore, replacing the functions

u(x),\qquad\qquad G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-}),\qquad\qquad g(x),

by

u(x)-tx_{n}^{+},\qquad G(\xi^{+}+te_{n},\xi^{-})-g(0),\qquad g(x)-g(0),

respectively, we may additionally assume that
g(0)=0
.

Let
\delta>0
.
Rescaling
u
by

\widetilde{u}(x)=\frac{u(x)}{\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\delta^{-1}\Big(\|f^{+}\|_{C_{[0]}^{\alpha-1}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{C_{[0]}^{\alpha-1}(B_{1}^{-})}+[g]_{C_{[0]}^{\alpha}(T_{1})}\Big)}=:\frac{u(x)}{K},

replacing the operators
\{F^{\pm},G\}
by

\widetilde{F}^{\pm}(M)=\frac{1}{K}F(KM),\qquad\widetilde{G}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})=\frac{1}{K}G(K\xi^{+},K\xi^{-}),

and the source terms by

\widetilde{f}^{\pm}(x)=\frac{1}{K}f^{\pm}(x),\qquad\widetilde{g}(x)=\frac{1}{K}g(x),

we may further assume that

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq 1
and

\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B_{r}^{\pm})}\leq\delta r^{\alpha},\qquad\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{r})}\leq\delta r^{\alpha}\qquad\text{ for }r\leq 1.

Here, note that
\{\widetilde{F}^{\pm},\widetilde{G}\}
have the same ellipticity constants as
\{F^{\pm},G\}
.

We state the following variant of the piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
estimate, which can be obtained from Theorem
1.3
by a scaling and covering argument, for reference below:

•

If
w\in C(B_{3/4})
is a viscosity solution to the constant-coefficient transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}w)=0&\text{ in }B_{3/4}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-})=0&\text{ on }T_{3/4},\end{cases}

with
F^{\pm}(0)=G(0,0)=0
,
then
w
is piecewise
C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}
with an estimate

(6.3)

\|w^{\pm}\|_{C^{1,\overline{\alpha}}(\overline{B}^{\pm}_{1/2})}\leq C_{0}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{3/4})},

where
C_{0}>0
is a universal constant.

From the discussion above, it remains to show the following:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficent operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
, let
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1})
, let
g\in C(T_{1})
, and let
\theta\in[0,1]
.
Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Assume that

(6.4)

F^{\pm}(0)=0,\qquad G(0,0)=0,\qquad g(0)=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq 1.

For each

, there exists

Proof.

7.
Perturbation theory II: variable coefficients

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
, the piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of solutions to variable-coefficient transmission problems.
We do this by perturbation from constant-coefficient equations, for which optimal regularity is already known.
As explained in the Introduction, for this argument to work, we need a stronger notion of viscosity solution.

First, we introduce a more general class of test functions whose Hessians from either side may blow up at the interface:

Definition 7.1

(Admissible-
\star
function)
.

The function
\varphi\in C(\Omega)
is
admissible
-
\star
if its restrictions
\varphi^{\pm}
are
C^{2}
in the interior and
C^{1}
up to the interface, i.e., if

\varphi^{\pm}\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\pm})\cap C^{1}(\Omega^{\pm}\cup T).

This class yields the following more restrictive notion of viscosity solution:

Definition 7.2

(Viscosity-
\star
solution)
.

We say that
u\in C(\Omega)
is a
viscosity
-
\star

subsolution
to (
2.1
) if whenever an admissible-
\star
function
\varphi
touches
u
from above at
x_{0}\in\Omega
and:

•

x_{0}\in\Omega^{\pm}
, then

F^{\pm}(D^{2}\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\geq f^{\pm}(x_{0});

•

x_{0}\in T
, then

G(\nabla\varphi^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla\varphi^{-}(x_{0}),x_{0})\geq g(x_{0}).

We have an analogous definition of viscosity-
\star
supersolution.
A viscosity-
\star
solution is a function that is both a viscosity-
\star
subsolution and supersolution.

Remark 7.3
.

In
[
7
]
, we showed that standard viscosity solutions in the sense of Definition
2.1
can be equivalently defined with respect to
admissible
test functions
\varphi\in C(\Omega)
satisfying

\varphi^{\pm}\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\pm})\cap C^{1,1}(\Omega^{\pm}\cup T),

i..e, whose second derivatives remain bounded up to the interface.
Since the admissible-
\star
class is larger than the admissible class, it follows that every viscosity-
\star
solution is a viscosity solution.
Our perturbation theory for variable-coefficient equations relies on the use of a non-explicit barrier that we only know to be admissible-
\star
, hence our need for a stronger notion of viscosity solution.

Remark 7.4
.

We note that sufficiently smooth viscosity solutions are also viscosity-
\star
solutions.
More precisely, if
u\in C(\Omega)
, with
u^{\pm}\in C^{1}(\Omega^{\pm}\cup T)
, is a viscosity solution, then it is also a viscosity-
\star
solution.

Let
u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity-
\star
solution to

(7.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\end{cases}

with
F^{\pm}
,
G
,
f^{\pm}
,
g
, and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
as in the statement of Theorem
1.5
.

As in Section
6
, the
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate away from the interface is classical, hence by a standard scaling and covering argument it suffices to prove a pointwise bound at the origin.
Once this is established, the fact that the transmission condition at that point is satisfied in the classical sense follows from the same reasoning in Section
5
.

To quantify the regularity of the operators with respect to the independent variable
x
, we define the moduli of continuity at
0
for the operators
F^{\pm}
and
G
by

\beta^{\pm}(x)=\beta_{F^{\pm}}(x):=\sup_{M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{|F^{\pm}(M,x)-F^{\pm}(M,0)|}{\|M\|+1}\qquad\text{ for }x\in B_{1}^{\pm},

and

\gamma(x)=\gamma_{G}(x):=\sup_{\xi^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)-G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},0)|}{|\xi^{+}|+|\xi^{-}|+1}\qquad\text{ for }x\in T_{1},

respectively.

Our perturbation is based on an approximation lemma allowing us to compare solutions to variable-coefficients equations with their constant-coefficient replacements.
More precisely, we have the following:

Lemma 7.5

(Approximation)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
f^{\pm}
and

be Hölder continuous functions on
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively.
Let
\phi
be a continuous function on
\partial B_{1}
, with modulus of continuity
\omega=\omega(r)
.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.

Assume that
\beta^{\pm}
and
\gamma
are Hölder continuous in
B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1}
and
T_{1}
, respectively, and that

F^{\pm}(0,x)\,\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\qquad G(0,0,x)=0\,\text{ on }T_{1},\qquad g(0)=0,

and

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}\leq K.

For
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
\omega
and
K
, such that if

\|\beta^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B^{\pm}_{1})}\leq\delta,\qquad\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B^{\pm}_{1})}\leq\delta,\qquad\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\leq\delta,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\leq\delta,

then any viscosity-
\star
solution

to the variable-coefficient inhomogeneous problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}v,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla v^{+},\theta\nabla v^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\\

and any viscosity solution

to the constant-coefficient homogeneous problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}w,0)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\

satisfy

\|v-w\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq\varepsilon.

Remark 7.6
.

Note here that

is a viscosity-
\star
solution, while

is a standard viscosity solution.
The meaning of the additional assumptions on the data will become evident later when we prove the main theorem.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that the statement of the lemma is false.
Then there are
\varepsilon>0
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\omega
,

, and sequences of operators
\{F_{k}^{\pm},G_{k}\}
, functions

, and constants

as above, for which we can find viscosity-
\star

\{v_{k}\}
and viscosity solutions

to the problems

\begin{cases}F_{k}^{\pm}(D^{2}v_{k},x)=f_{k}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm}\\

respectively,
such that

\|\beta_{k}^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|\beta_{k}^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{-})}+\|\gamma_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}+\|f_{k}^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B^{+}_{1})}+\|f_{k}^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B^{-}_{1})}+\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\to 0,

and

\|v_{k}-w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}>\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }k.

Note that the functions
\{v_{k}\}
and
\{w_{k}\}
have the same modulus of continuity
\omega
on
\partial B_{1}
.
Since
\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}\leq K
for all
k
, and
\|f_{k}^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{\pm})}\to 0
and
\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\to 0
as

, by the boundary estimate (Theorem
5.1
) we see that
\{v_{k}\}
and
\{w_{k}\}
are equicontinuous and uniformly bounded in

.
Thus, taking subsequences, we may assume that

v_{k}\to v_{\infty}\quad\text{ and }\quad w_{k}\to w_{\infty}\quad\text{ uniformly in }\overline{B}_{1}\text{ as }k\to\infty.

The limiting functions are continuous in
\overline{B}_{1}
, with

v_{\infty}=w_{\infty}\quad\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

Since
\|\beta_{k}^{\pm}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{\pm})}\to 0
and
\|\gamma_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\to 0
, taking further subsequences we may assume that

F_{k}^{\pm}(\cdot,0)\to F^{\pm}(\cdot,0)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad G_{k}(\cdot,\cdot,0)\to G(\cdot,\cdot,0)

uniformly on compacts in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively, as well as
\theta_{k}\to\theta
.

To prove the lemma, it suffices to show that
v_{\infty}
is a viscosity solution to the transmission problem

(7.2)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}v,0)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla v^{+},\theta\nabla v^{-},0)=0&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Indeed, by the closedness property of viscosity solutions (see Proposition 2.8 in
[
7
]
), we already know that
w_{\infty}
is a viscosity solution to (
7.2
) and hence by uniqueness
v_{\infty}=w_{\infty}
in
\overline{B}_{1}
, contradicting
\|v_{k}-w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}>\varepsilon
for large
k
.

For simplicity, we only check the subsolution property.
To this aim, let
P
be a piecewise quadratic polynomial touching
v_{\infty}
from above at
x_{0}\in B_{1}
.
The case
x_{0}\notin T_{1}
is classical, since we can restrict the function to a small ball entirely contained in
B_{1}^{+}
or
B_{1}^{-}
and follow the proof of Lemma 8.2 in
[
2
]
.
Hence, we assume that
x_{0}\in T_{1}
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that

(7.3)

G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}),0)=-\eta<0.

By Remark
2.2
, we may assume that
P
touches
v_{\infty}
strictly at
x_{0}
and that

(7.4)

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},0)<-2\eta.

Let

be such that

F^{\pm}_{k}(D^{2}P^{\pm},0)-F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},0)\leq\varepsilon_{k},

and

G_{k}(\nabla P^{+}(x),\theta_{k}\nabla P^{-}(x),0)-G(\nabla P^{+}(x),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x),0)\leq\varepsilon_{k}\quad\text{ for }x\in T_{1}.

Let
\psi_{k}
be the unique viscosity solution to the Dirichlet problem

(7.5)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}\psi_{k};\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)=-|f_{k}^{\pm}(x)|-\|D^{2}P^{\pm}\|\beta^{\pm}_{k}(x)-\varepsilon_{k}&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\psi_{k}^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla\psi_{k}^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=-|g_{k}(x)|-\left(|\nabla P^{+}(x)|+|\nabla P^{-}(x)|+1\right)\gamma_{k}(x)-\varepsilon_{k}&\text{ on }T_{1},\\
\psi_{k}=0&\text{ on }\partial B_{1},\\
\end{cases}

whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem
2.4
.
Since (

) is a constant-coefficient transmission problem and
\mathcal{M}^{+}
is a convex operator, by the Evans-Krylov

estimates combined with Theorem

, it follows that

\psi_{k}\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
is and admissible-
\star
function in
B_{1}
.
A computation shows that, on the interface
T_{1}
, we have

(7.6)

\begin{split}&G_{k}(\nabla[P+\psi_{k}]^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla[P+\psi_{k}]^{-},\cdot)\\
&\quad\leq G_{k}(\nabla P^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla P^{-},\cdot)+\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\psi_{k}^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla\psi_{k}^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\\
&\qquad\leq G_{k}(\nabla P^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla P^{-},0)+\left(|\nabla P^{+}|+|\nabla P^{-}|+1\right)\gamma_{k}+\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\psi_{k}^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla\psi_{k}^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\\
&\qquad\quad\leq G(\nabla P^{+},\theta\nabla P^{-},0)-|g_{k}|.\end{split}

Let
Q
be the quadratic polynomial
Q=\frac{\eta}{2(n\Lambda+4\mu)}|x-x_{0}|^{2}
, and observe that

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}Q;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\leq\eta&\text{ in }B_{1},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla Q^{+},\theta_{k}\nabla Q^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\leq\eta/2&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Noting that
\|\psi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\to 0
and
\|v_{k}-v_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\to 0
as

, since

touches
v_{\infty}
from above in

, it follows that a vertical translation of

touches

from above at some

, for sufficiently large

.
In fact,
x_{k}\in T_{1}
since otherwise (say)
x_{k}\in B_{1}^{+}
for infinitely many

and hence by the equation for

\begin{split}f^{+}_{k}(x_{k})&\textstyle\leq F_{k}^{+}(D^{2}P^{+}+D^{2}\psi_{k}(x_{k})+D^{2}Q,x_{k})\\
&\quad\leq F_{k}^{+}(D^{2}P^{+},x_{k})+\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}\psi_{k}(x_{k});\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)+\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}Q;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\\
&\qquad\leq F^{+}(D^{2}P^{+},0)+\varepsilon_{k}+\|D^{2}P^{+}\|\beta_{k}(x_{k})+\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}\psi_{k}(x_{k});\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)+\eta\\
&\qquad\quad\leq F^{+}(D^{2}P^{+},0)-|f_{k}^{+}(x_{k})|+\textstyle\eta,\end{split}

whence by (
7.4
) we obtain

0\leq F^{+}(D^{2}P^{+},0)+\eta\leq-\eta,

a contradiction.

Since
x_{k}\in T_{1}
and
\psi_{k}
is admissible-
\star
, recalling that

is a viscosity-
\star
solution, by the transmission condition and (

), we have that

\begin{split}g_{k}(x_{k})&\textstyle\leq G_{k}(\nabla[P+\psi_{k}+Q]^{+}(x_{k}),\theta_{k}\nabla[P+\psi_{k}+Q]^{-}(x_{k}),x_{k})\\
&\textstyle\quad\leq G_{k}(\nabla[P+\psi_{k}]^{+}(x_{k}),\theta_{k}\nabla[P+\psi_{k}]^{-}(x_{k}),x_{k})+\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla Q^{+}(x_{k}),\theta_{k}\nabla Q^{-}(x_{k});\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\\
&\qquad\leq G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{k}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{k}),0)-|g_{k}(x_{k})|+\eta/2,\end{split}

whence it follows that

(7.7)

0\leq G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{k}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{k}),0)+\eta/2.

We wish to point out that this is the only place in the paper where we need the stronger notion of viscosity-
\star
solution.

Since
x_{k}\to x_{0}
as

(recall that

touches
v_{\infty}
strictly), passing to the limit in (

), by (

) we conclude

0\leq G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}),0)+\eta/2\leq-\eta/2,

a contradiction.
Therefore, we must have
G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}),0)\geq 0
as claimed.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

The key ingredient in the proof above was the use of non-explicit barriers
\psi_{k}
defined as solutions to the constant-coefficient, inhomogeneous, convex transmission problem (
7.5
).
It would be interesting to give conditions under which such barriers become
piecewise
C^{1,1}
, i.e., with bounded Hessians up to the interface and hence admissible test functions.
Such an improved regularity would allow us to extend Lemma
7.5
to ordinary viscosity solutions.
We point out that the piecewise
C^{1,1}
regularity for solutions of convex transmission problems is not known even in the homogeneous case.

To prove the regularity theorem, we concatenate three simplifying assumptions:

First simplification.

Replacing the operators and sources

F^{\pm}(M,x),\qquad G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x),\qquad f^{\pm}(x),\qquad g(x)

by the functions

F^{\pm}(M,x)-F^{\pm}(0,x),\qquad G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)-G(0,0,0),\qquad f^{\pm}(x)-F^{\pm}(0,x),\qquad g(x)-G(0,0,0),

Second simplification.

Further rescaling and normalizing

\omega
such that

|u(x_{0})-u(y_{0})|\leq\omega(|x_{0}-y_{0}|)\qquad\text{ for all }x_{0}\text{ and }y_{0}\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

•

For each
\kappa>0
, a modulus
\omega_{\kappa}
such that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\omega_{\kappa}(|x-x_{0}|)\qquad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\{|x_{n}|\geq\kappa\}\cap\partial B_{1};

•

A modulus
\omega_{0}
such that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\omega_{0}(|x-x_{0}|)\qquad\text{ for all }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\{x_{n}=0\}\cap\partial B_{1};

Note here that
\omega_{\kappa}
could degenerate as
\kappa\downarrow 0
.

In the proofs below, we denote the projection of
x\neq 0
onto the sphere
\partial B_{1}
by
\pi[x]=\frac{x}{|x|}
.
We also write
d_{x}=\textrm{dist}(x,\partial B_{1})=|x-\pi[x]|=1-|x|
for the distance.

Lemma A.1
.

There is a modulus of continuity
\widetilde{\omega}
depending only on
\omega
,
\{\omega_{\kappa}\}_{\kappa>0}
, and
\omega_{0}
such that

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\widetilde{\omega}(|x-x_{0}|)\quad\text{ for all }x\in\overline{B}_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We must find a
\delta>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and the given moduli, such that

(A.1)

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ whenever }|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta,\text{ with }x\in B_{1}\text{ and }x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}.

Assume that
|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta
, with
0<\delta\leq 1/2
small to be determined below.
By triangle inequality

(A.2)

|u(x)-u(x_{0})|\leq|u(x)-u(\pi[x])|+|u(\pi[x])-u(x_{0})|.

Since
|x|\geq|x_{0}|-|x-x_{0}|\geq 1/2
and
d_{x}\leq|x-x_{0}|
, we have that

|\pi[x]-x_{0}|=\left|\frac{x}{|x|}-x_{0}\right|=\frac{1}{|x|}\big|x-x_{0}+(1-|x|)x_{0}\big|\leq\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{|x|}+\frac{d_{x}}{|x|}\leq 4\delta

and the second term on the right-hand side of (
A.2
) can be controlled by the modulus on
\partial B_{1}
as

(A.3)

|u(\pi[x])-u(x_{0})|\leq\omega(4\delta).

To bound the first term, fix
0<\kappa_{0}\leq 1/4
small to be chosen below.
We distinguish two cases:

•

If
|x_{n}|\geq\kappa_{0}
, then by the modulus
\omega_{\kappa_{0}}
, we have

(A.4)

|u(x)-u(\pi[x])|\leq\omega_{\kappa_{0}}(d_{x})\leq\omega_{\kappa_{0}}(\delta).

•

If
|x_{n}|\leq\kappa_{0}
, then we have the additional bounds

(A.5)

|x^{\prime}|\geq|x|-|x_{n}|\geq 1/2-\kappa_{0}\geq 1/4,

and

(A.6)

1-|x^{\prime}|\leq 1-|x|+|x_{n}|<\delta+\kappa_{0}\leq 2\kappa_{0}.

By triangle inequality, using the modulus
\omega_{0}

(A.7)

\begin{split}|u(x)-u(\pi[x])|&\leq\textstyle\left|u(x)-u(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|+\left|u(\pi[x])-u(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|\\
&\quad\leq\textstyle\omega_{0}\left(\left|x-(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|\right)+\omega_{0}\left(\left|\frac{x}{|x|}-(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|\right).\end{split}

Note that by (
A.6
), we have

(A.8)

\textstyle\left|x-(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|=\sqrt{(1-|x^{\prime}|)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{5}\kappa_{0}<2\sqrt{2}\kappa_{0}.

On the other hand, by concavity of the square root

|x|=\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}\leq|x^{\prime}|+\frac{1}{2|x^{\prime}|}x_{n}^{2},

whence, by (
A.5
), we deduce

(A.9)

\textstyle\left|\frac{x}{|x|}-(\frac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0)\right|=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{|x|-|x^{\prime}|}{|x|}}\leq\frac{|x_{n}|}{\sqrt{|x||x^{\prime}|}}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\kappa_{0}.

Applying (
A.8
) and (
A.9
) in (
A.7
), we conclude that

(A.10)

\begin{split}|u(x)-u(\pi[x])|\leq 2\omega_{0}(2\sqrt{2}\kappa_{0}).\end{split}

To finish the argument,
first choose
\kappa_{0}>0
such that

2\omega_{0}(2\sqrt{2}\kappa_{0})\leq\varepsilon/2

and then choose
\delta>0
such that

\max\{\omega(4\delta),\omega_{\kappa_{0}}(\delta)\}\leq\varepsilon/2.

Combining (
A.3
), (
A.4
), and (
A.10
) to control (
A.2
) now yields the claim (
A.1
).
∎

Assume additionally that
u
satisfies the following interior Hölder estimate in
B_{1}
:

R^{\beta}[u-c]_{C^{\beta}(\overline{B}_{R/2}(x))}\leq C\left(\|u-c\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(x))}+RK\right)\quad\text{ for all }c\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }B_{R}(x)\subset B_{1},

for some constants
0<\beta<1
,
C>0
, and
K\geq 0
.
Solutions to transmission problems satisfy such an estimate by Corollary
3.4
, with
\beta
and
C
universal and
K=\|f^{+}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{+})}+\|f^{-}\|_{L^{n}(B_{1}^{-})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}
.

Lemma A.2
.

There is a modulus of continuity
\omega^{\star}
depending only on

\widetilde{\omega}
,
\beta
,
C
, and
K
such that

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq\omega^{\star}(|x-y|)\qquad\text{ for all }x\text{ and }y\text{ in }\overline{B}_{1}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We will find a
\delta>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and the above data such that

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ whenever }|x-y|\leq\delta.

Let
|x-y|\leq\delta
, with
0<\delta\leq 1/4
small to be determined below.
There are two cases:

Case 1.

\max\{d_{x},d_{y}\}\leq 2|x-y|
.

Since
|x|=1-d_{x}\geq 1-2\delta\geq 1/2
, we have that

\big|\pi[x]-\pi[y]\big|=\left|\frac{x}{|x|}-\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\leq\frac{2}{|x|}|x-y|\leq 4|x-y|\leq 4\delta,

and by the boundary modulus

\begin{split}|u(x)-u(y)|&\leq|u(x)-u(\pi[x])|+|u(\pi[x])-u(\pi[y])|+|u(y)-u(\pi[y])|\\
&\quad\leq\widetilde{\omega}(d_{x})+\widetilde{\omega}(|\pi[x]-\pi[y]|)+\widetilde{\omega}(d_{y})\\
&\qquad\leq 3\widetilde{\omega}(4\delta)\\
&\qquad\quad\leq\varepsilon\end{split}

for
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Case 2.

d_{x}\geq 2|x-y|
.

Since
B_{2|x-y|}(x)\subset B_{d_{x}}(x)\subset B_{1}
, by the interior estimate, we have that

d_{x}^{\beta}[u-u(\pi[x])]_{C^{\beta}(\overline{B}_{d_{x}/2}(x))}\leq C\left(\|u-u(\pi[x])\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{d_{x}}(x))}+d_{x}K\right)

and applying the boundary modulus

\|u-u(\pi[x])\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{d_{x}}(x))}\leq\widetilde{\omega}(d_{x}),

we conclude

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq C\left(\widetilde{\omega}(d_{x})d_{x}^{-\beta}+d_{x}^{1-\beta}K\right)|x-y|^{\beta}.

Let
0<\delta_{0}<1
small to be chosen next.
There are two subcases:

•

If
d_{x}\leq\delta_{0}
, then

\begin{split}|u(x)-u(y)|&\leq C\Big(\widetilde{\omega}(d_{x})+d_{x}K\Big)\left(\frac{|x-y|}{d_{x}}\right)^{\beta}\\
&\quad\leq C\Big(\widetilde{\omega}(\delta_{0})+\delta_{0}K\Big)\\
&\qquad\leq\varepsilon\end{split}

for
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small.

•

If
d_{x}>\delta_{0}
, then
(using that
d_{x}\leq 1
and
0<\beta<1
)

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq C\left(\widetilde{\omega}(1)\delta_{0}^{-\beta}+K\right)\delta^{\beta}\leq\varepsilon

choosing
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

∎
