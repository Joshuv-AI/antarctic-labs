The density of k k -cacti via excluding minors
Licheng Zhang, Yuanqiu Huang

Abstract
A k k -cactus generalizes forests and cacti by allowing each edge to lie on at most k k cycles. The maximum number of edges is classical for forests and cacti, but for k k -cacti was known only for k ≤ 4 k\leq 4 . In this note we treat general k k . The key idea is that bounding the cycles through each edge forces a k k -cactus to exclude a large complete minor; in particular, the class of k k -cacti is minor-closed. From this we prove that every n n -vertex k k -cactus has O ⁡ ( log ⁡ k log ⁡ log ⁡ k ​ n ) O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n\right) edges for all sufficiently large k k , and a construction shows this is optimal up to a factor of log ⁡ log ⁡ k \sqrt{\log\log k} .



1
Introduction

Throughout this paper, all graphs are finite and simple, and are not
required to be connected. We write
e=2.71828\ldots
for the base of the
natural logarithm, and
\log
for the natural logarithm.

A
forest
is a graph that contains no cycle; equivalently, it is a
disjoint union of trees, and a
cactus
is a graph in which each
edge lies on at most one cycle. Generalizing both, for an integer

k\geq 0
we call a graph a
k
-cactus
if each of its edges lies on
at most
k
cycles; this notion was introduced in
[
13
]
. Thus
a forest is precisely a
0
-cactus and a cactus is a
1
-cactus. Although the notion of a
k
-cactus is recent, the underlying idea of
studying a graph through the cycles passing through its edges is an old
one. For example, Toida
[
9
]
showed
that a graph is Eulerian if and only if every edge lies on an odd number
of cycles. Mac Lane
[
5
]

showed that a graph is planar if and only if it has a cycle basis in
which every edge lies in at most two of the basis cycles. More generally, the basis number of a graph is the least
integer
k
such that the graph has a cycle basis in which every edge
lies in at most
k
of the basis cycles. Geniet and
Giocanti
[
3
]
showed that the basis number is bounded
by a constant on every proper minor-closed class of graphs.

A forest on
n
vertices has at most
n-1
edges, with equality if and
only if it is a tree, and it is well known
that every cactus on
n
vertices has at most
\lfloor 3(n-1)/2\rfloor

edges, the extremal graphs being obtained by coalescing as many
triangles as possible at a common vertex, with one extra pendant edge
when
n
is even
[
11
]
. Viewing these as the cases
k=0
and
k=1
of the size
of a
k
-cactus, it is natural to ask for the analogous bound for
general
k
. This question was proposed in
[
13
]
, where tight
bounds were obtained for
k\leq 4
together with the corresponding
extremal graphs; in particular, every
2
-connected
k
-cactus on
n

vertices was shown to have at most
n+k-1
edges, with equality when

n\geq k+2
. The notion of a
k
-cactus has since attracted further attention.
Voblyi
[
10
]
enumerated labeled Eulerian

3
-cacti, obtaining exact and asymptotic formulas for their number.
Wu, Chen and Li
[
12
]
determined the graphs of
maximum spectral radius among all
n
-vertex
2
-cacti and among all
n
-vertex
3
-cacti.
The extremal problem for the size of a
k
-cactus, however, has remained
open for every
k\geq 5
: the block-decomposition method
of
[
13
]
relies on a case analysis of the possible

2
-connected blocks, whose number grows rapidly with
k
, and no bound
covering all
k
has been available.

The purpose of this note is to fill this gap: we establish an upper
bound on the size of an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus that is valid for every

k
, thereby extending the known bounds from the small cases
k\leq 4

to arbitrary
k
.
As a warm-up, we first observe that a direct argument based on the
block decomposition gives a bound of order
\sqrt{k}\,n
for the size of
a connected
n
-vertex
k
-cactus (Proposition
2.2
);
this serves as a benchmark for what follows. Our main result improves this dependence from polynomial to polylogarithmic, by means of a different route that
applies uniformly to all
k
. The starting point is a simple but crucial
observation: in the complete graph
K_{r}
, every edge lies on exactly

\lfloor e(r-2)!\rfloor-1
cycles, a quantity that grows
super-exponentially in
r
. Consequently, once
k
is fixed, no edge of
a
k
-cactus can lie on as many cycles as an edge of a large complete
graph; more precisely, if
\lfloor e(r-2)!\rfloor-1>k
, then a

k
-cactus can contain no
K_{r}
-minor. The local condition defining a

k
-cactus (that each edge lies on at most
k
cycles) thus translates
into the global condition of excluding a complete minor whose size
depends only on
k
. We may then invoke the Kostochka–Thomason theorem,
which bounds the number of edges of a graph with no
K_{r}
minor, to
obtain an upper bound of order
\dfrac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n
on
the size of a
k
-cactus, valid for every
k
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus, and set

R(k)=\min\{r\geq 3:\ \lfloor e(r-2)!\rfloor-1>k\}.

Then
G
contains no
K_{R(k)}
minor, and consequently, by the
Kostochka–Thomason theorem,

|E(G)|\leq c\,R(k)\sqrt{\log R(k)}\,n,

where
c>0
is an absolute constant such that every
n
-vertex graph
with more than
c\,r\sqrt{\log r}\,n
edges contains a
K_{r}
minor.

Estimating
R(k)
yields the following explicit bound.

Corollary 1.2
.

For all sufficiently large
k
, every
n
-vertex
k
-cactus
G

satisfies

|E(G)|=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n\right).

This is close to best possible: coalescing many copies of a complete
graph gives, for infinitely many
n
,
k
-cacti with

\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n\right)
edges
(Proposition
5.2
). Thus only the power of
\log\log k

remains to be determined.

Remark 1.3
.

The constant
c
in Theorem
1.1
is inherited from the
Kostochka–Thomason theorem and is not optimized here; it is an absolute
constant, independent of
G
,
n
,
k
and
R(k)
. The question of the
edge density forcing a
K_{r}
minor is classical and closely tied to
Hadwiger’s conjecture. Mader
[
6
]
first proved that edge density of order
r\log r

forces a
K_{r}
minor; the correct order
r\sqrt{\log r}
was established
independently by Kostochka
[
4
]
and Thomason
[
7
]
,
and the precise asymptotic constant was determined by
Thomason
[
8
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

The implicit constant in the
O
-notation can in principle be made explicit.
For sufficiently large
k
, the proof of Lemma 4.3 gives the bound

R(k)\leq\left\lceil 3\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right\rceil+2.

Using this value as an upper bound in Theorem 1.1 and choosing a conservative constant
c=1

yields a safe explicit estimate

|E(G)|\leq 2\,\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n.

We do not attempt to optimize this numerical constant; our focus is on the asymptotic
dependence of the coefficient of
n
on
k
.

The heart of the result of this note is the passage from a local cycle condition to
an excluded complete minor; the remaining estimates of
R(k)
are
elementary and somewhat tedious.

1.1
Preliminaries

For a graph
G
, we write

V(G)
and
E(G)
for its vertex set and edge set, and we call
|E(G)|

the
size
of
G
. A
cycle
is a connected
2
-regular
subgraph. A
cut-vertex
of a connected graph is a vertex whose deletion
disconnects it. A
block
of a graph is a maximal connected subgraph
without a cut-vertex; thus every block is either a single edge (
K_{2}
) or
a maximal
2
-connected subgraph. The blocks of a graph partition its
edge set. For any edge
e
in a graph
G
, let
c_{G}(e)
denote the number of cycles in
G
containing
e
.

For real-valued functions
f
and
g
defined for all sufficiently large

x
, with
g(x)>0
, we write
f(x)=O(g(x))
if there are constants
C>0

and
x_{0}
such that
|f(x)|\leq C\,g(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
, and

f(x)=\Omega(g(x))
if there are constants
c^{\prime}>0
and
x_{0}
such that

f(x)\geq c^{\prime}\,g(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Let
H
be a graph. The graph
H
is a
minor
of a graph
G
if
H
can
be obtained from
G
by a sequence of vertex deletions, edge deletions, and
edge contractions. A class of graphs is
minor-closed
if every minor of a graph in the
class again belongs to the class.

An
H
-model
in
G
is a subgraph
M\subseteq G
whose vertex set
is partitioned into nonempty
branch sets

\{X_{v}\mid v\in V(H)\}
, each inducing a connected subgraph of
M
, such
that for each edge
uv\in E(H)
at least one edge of
M
joins
X_{u}
and

X_{v}
. For each
uv\in E(H)
we fix one such edge, the
representative
edge

\widehat{uv}
.

Lemma 1.5

(see
[
1
, Section 1.7]
)
.

A graph
H
is a minor of
G
if and only if
G
contains an
H
-model.

We will use the following fundamental result on complete minors.

Lemma 1.6

(Kostochka
[
4
]
, Thomason
[
7
]
)
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that every
n
-vertex graph with
more than
c\,r\sqrt{\log r}\,n
edges contains a
K_{r}
minor.

The number of cycles of
K_{r}
through a fixed edge
uv
is closely
related to a known quantity. Deleting
uv
from such a cycle leaves a

u
–
v
path, and conversely every
u
–
v
path other than the edge

uv
itself closes up into such a cycle. Hence the cycles through
uv

correspond to the
u
–
v
paths in
K_{r}
except the trivial one
consisting of
uv
alone. The number of all
u
–
v
paths in
K_{r}
is
recorded as
\lfloor e(r-2)!\rfloor

(OEIS
A000522
) without proof
there. For completeness we give a short proof of the
following.

Lemma 1.7
.

For
r\geq 3
, the number of cycles of
K_{r}
containing a fixed edge is

\lfloor e(r-2)!\rfloor-1.

Proof.

Fix an edge
uv
of
K_{r}
. A cycle containing
uv
is obtained
uniquely by taking a simple
u
-
v
path in
K_{r}-uv
and then adding
the edge
uv
.

Suppose that this path has exactly
j
internal vertices. These vertices
are chosen from the
r-2
vertices in
V(K_{r})\setminus\{u,v\}
, and
then ordered along the path. Thus, for fixed
j
, the number of such
paths is

\binom{r-2}{j}j!.

Therefore

c_{K_{r}}(uv)=\sum_{j=1}^{r-2}\binom{r-2}{j}j!.

Put
m=r-2
. Then

c_{K_{r}}(uv)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}j!=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{m!}{(m-j)!}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{m!}{i!}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}-1.

We claim that

\lfloor e\,m!\rfloor=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}.

Indeed, by the Taylor expansion

e=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i!},

we have

e\,m!=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{m!}{i!}.

The first sum is an integer. For the tail, write
i=m+s
. Then

\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{m!}{i!}=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)\cdots(m+s)}.

This tail is positive, and

\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)\cdots(m+s)}<\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{s}}=\frac{1}{m}\leq 1.

Thus

\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}<e\,m!<\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}+1.

Since
\sum_{i=0}^{m}m!/i!
is an integer, it follows that

\lfloor e\,m!\rfloor=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{m!}{i!}.

Consequently,

c_{K_{r}}(uv)=\lfloor e\,m!\rfloor-1=\lfloor e\,(r-2)!\rfloor-1.

∎

Observation 1.8
.

\log(m!)\geq m(\log m-1)

for every integer
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

It is equivalent to

m!\geq(m/e)^{m}
, which follows from

e^{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{m^{j}}{j!}\geq\frac{m^{m}}{m!}.

∎

2
A bound from the block decomposition

As a warm-up, we give a simple upper bound on the size of a
k
-cactus,
using its block decomposition. The bound serves as a benchmark for our main result. We did not notice
this simple bound in our earlier work
[
13
]
, where we treated
only
k\leq 4
. We first recall the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(Zhang and Huang
[
13
]
)
.

Let
G
be a
k
-cactus graph on
n
vertices. If
G
is 2-connected, then

|E(G)|\leq n+k-1.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a
n
-vertex
k
-cactus. Then

|E(G)|\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{1+8k}}{4}(n-1).

Proof.

If
G
is disconnected, we may add bridges between its components.
The resulting graph is still a
k
-cactus, has the same vertex set and
at least as many edges. Thus it suffices to prove the result for connected
graphs. It is well-known that every connected graph has a block decomposition; that is, its edge set can be partitioned into the edge sets of its blocks.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of blocks of
G
. For each
B\in\mathcal{B}
, put
v=|V(B)|
.

Claim 2.3
.

|E(B)|\leq\min\left\{v+k-1,\binom{v}{2}\right\}.

Proof.

Indeed, the first bound
follows from Lemma
2.1
if
B
is 2-connected. The second bound is just the complete-graph bound.
∎

Let

v_{0}=\frac{3+\sqrt{1+8k}}{2},

which is the positive root of

\binom{x}{2}=x+k-1.

Claim 2.4
.

|E(B)|\leq\frac{v_{0}}{2}(v-1)

for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

Put

f(x)=\binom{x}{2}
,
g(x)=x+k-1
and
\ell(x)=\frac{v_{0}}{2}(x-1).

By the definition of
v_{0}
, we have
f(v_{0})=g(v_{0})
. Moreover,

\ell(1)=f(1)=0

and

\ell(v_{0})=\frac{v_{0}}{2}(v_{0}-1)=\binom{v_{0}}{2}=f(v_{0})=g(v_{0}).

Thus
\ell
is the secant line of the convex function
f
between

x=1
and
x=v_{0}
. Hence

f(x)\leq\ell(x)
for
1\leq x\leq v_{0}.

On the other hand, the two linear functions
g
and
\ell
agree at

x=v_{0}
, and their slopes are
1
and
v_{0}/2
, respectively. Since

v_{0}\geq 2
, we have
v_{0}/2\geq 1
. Therefore

g(x)\leq\ell(x)
for
x\geq v_{0}.

Consequently, for every
v\geq 1
,

\min\left\{g(v),f(v)\right\}\leq\ell(v)=\frac{v_{0}}{2}(v-1).

By Claim 2.3, this gives

|E(B)|\leq\frac{v_{0}}{2}(v-1).

∎

Finally, the block decomposition gives

|E(G)|=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|E(B)|,
and
n-1=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\bigl(|V(B)|-1\bigr).

Hence, we have

|E(G)|\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{v_{0}}{2}\bigl(|V(B)|-1\bigr)=\frac{v_{0}}{2}(n-1).

Since

\frac{v_{0}}{2}=\frac{3+\sqrt{1+8k}}{4},

we obtain

|E(G)|\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{1+8k}}{4}(n-1).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

The bound in Proposition
2.2
has coefficient roughly
\sqrt{k/2}
. For large
values of
k
, the minor-based argument in the next section gives a much smaller
dependence on
k
.

3
Minor-closedness of

Both forests and cacti are minor-closed: a forest is
precisely a graph with no
K_{3}
-minor, and a cactus is
precisely a graph with no diamond minor
[
2
]
, where
the diamond is the graph obtained from
K_{4}
by deleting an edge. We show
that, more generally, the class of
k
-cacti is minor-closed for every
fixed
k
.

The following proposition will be a key tool.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
H
be a minor of
G
. Then for every edge
h\in E(H)
there exists an edge

\widehat{h}\in E(G)
such that

c_{H}(h)\leq c_{G}(\widehat{h}).

Proof.

By Lemma
1.5
, we can
fix an
H
-model in
G
with branch sets
\{X_{x}\}_{x\in V(H)}
. For each
edge
xy\in E(H)
, fix one representative edge of
xy
of
G
joining
X_{x}
and
X_{y}
, denoted
\widehat{xy}
. Since the branch
sets are disjoint, distinct edges of
H
have distinct representative
edges.

Fix an edge
h\in E(H)
and let
C=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{t}x_{1}
be a cycle of
H

through
h
. Replace each edge
x_{i}x_{i+1}
by its representative edge

\widehat{x_{i}x_{i+1}}
, and inside each branch set

, join the two endpoints incident with
the representative edges by a path, possibly of length zero if the endpoints

These representative edges and paths form a cycle of

\widehat{h}

its representative edge
\widehat{e}
.

Therefore the cycles of

Theorem 3.2

If

Lemma 4.3
.

For all sufficiently large

, let

R(k)=\min\left\{r\geq 3:\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1>k\right\}.

Then

R(k)=O\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right).

Proof.

We only need to find one admissible value of
r
of order

\log k/\log\log k
. Indeed, since
R(k)
is the least integer

satisfying

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1>k,

any such admissible

gives an upper bound on

.

Set

and

We prove that this

.

By Observation
1.8
, we have

\log(m!)\geq m(\log m-1),

(1)

By the choice of
m
, we have

m\geq 3\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}.

(2)

Thus

\log m\geq\log\left(3\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right)=\log 3+\log\log k-\log\log\log k.

For all sufficiently large

,

\log 3+\log\log k-\log\log\log k-1\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\log k.

Hence

\log m-1\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\log k.

(3)

Therefore by (1), (2) and (3) one has

\log(m!)\geq m(\log m-1)\geq\left(3\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\log\log k\right)=\frac{3}{2}\log k>\log k.

Thus
m!>k
.

Since

\left\lfloor em!\right\rfloor-1\geq 2m!-1>k.

As

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1>k.

Thus

Finally,

Therefore

R(k)=O\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right).

Remark 4.4
.

1.1
,

|E(G)|\leq c\,R\sqrt{\log R}\,n.

For all sufficiently large
k
, Lemma
4.3
gives

R=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right),

and in particular

\log R=O(\log\log k).

Hence

\displaystyle R\sqrt{\log R}

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\sqrt{\log\log k}\right)

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\right).

Since
c
is an absolute constant, combining this with
Theorem
1.1
gives

|E(G)|=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n\right)

for all sufficiently large
k
.
∎

5
A lower-bound construction

We include the natural construction for comparison.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
r\geq 3
be an integer satisfying

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1\leq k.

Then, for infinitely many values of
n
, there exists an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus
G
with

|E(G)|=\frac{r}{2}(n-1).

Proof.

By Lemma
1.7
, every edge of
K_{r}
is contained in exactly

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1

cycles. Hence, under the assumed inequality,
K_{r}
is a
k
-cactus.
Take
s
copies of
K_{r}
, and identify one vertex from each copy into a single common vertex. The resulting graph is still a
k
-cactus, since every cycle lies entirely inside one of the complete-graph blocks.
The graph has

n=1+s(r-1)

vertices and

m=s\binom{r}{2}

edges. Therefore

m=s\frac{r(r-1)}{2}=\frac{r}{2}\,s(r-1)=\frac{r}{2}(n-1).

As
s
ranges over the positive integers, this gives infinitely many values of
n
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

For every sufficiently large
k
, there are infinitely many values of
n

for which there exists an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus
G
satisfying

|E(G)|=\Omega\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n\right).

Proof.

We apply Lemma
5.1
with a value of
r
which is as large
as possible up to the correct order, but still satisfies

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1\leq k.

This is the key point, because Lemma
5.1
then gives
graphs with

|E(G)|=\frac{r}{2}(n-1).

Choose

m=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right\rfloor,
and
r=m+2.

For all sufficiently large
k
, we have
m\geq 1
, and hence
r\geq 3
.

We first prove that this choice of
r
is admissible. Since
r-2=m
, it is
enough to show that

\lfloor em!\rfloor-1\leq k.

By the definition of
m
,

m\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}.

Also, for all sufficiently large
k
, we have

m\leq\log k.

Therefore

\log m\leq\log\log k.

Since
m!\leq m^{m}
, it follows that

\log(m!)\leq m\log m.

Combining the above inequalities gives

\log(m!)\leq m\log m\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right)\log\log k=\frac{1}{2}\log k.

Hence

m!\leq e^{(\log k)/2}=\sqrt{k}.

Thus

em!\leq e\sqrt{k}.

For all sufficiently large
k
, we have
e\sqrt{k}\leq k
. Consequently,

em!\leq k,

and hence

\lfloor em!\rfloor-1\leq k.

Therefore

\left\lfloor e(r-2)!\right\rfloor-1\leq k.

Now Lemma
5.1
applies. Thus, for infinitely many values
of
n
, there exists an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus
G
such that

|E(G)|=\frac{r}{2}(n-1).

It remains to estimate
r
from below. Since

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty,

we have, for all sufficiently large
k
,

\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}.

Thus

m\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}.

Since
r=m+2\geq m
, we obtain

r\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}.

Finally, the infinitely many values of
n
given by
Lemma
5.1
are unbounded. After discarding finitely many
of them, we may assume
n\geq 2
, and so

n-1\geq\frac{n}{2}.

Therefore

|E(G)|=\frac{r}{2}(n-1)\geq\frac{r}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{2}=\frac{r}{4}n.

Using the lower bound on
r
, we get

|E(G)|\geq\frac{1}{16}\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n.

Hence

|E(G)|=\Omega\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n\right).

This proves the proposition.
∎

6
Concluding remarks

For an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus
G
, we have shown that

|E(G)|=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{\log\log k}}\,n\right),

while the construction of Proposition
5.2
gives, for
infinitely many
n
,
k
-cacti with

|E(G)|=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n\right).

These two bounds agree up to a factor of
\sqrt{\log\log k}
, so the
maximum size of an
n
-vertex
k
-cactus is determined up to the power of

\log\log k
. We suspect that the lower bound gives the correct order.

Problem 6.1
.

Determine the asymptotic order of the maximum number of edges in an

n
-vertex
k
-cactus. In particular, does every
n
-vertex
k
-cactus

G
satisfy

|E(G)|=O\!\left(\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\,n\right)?
