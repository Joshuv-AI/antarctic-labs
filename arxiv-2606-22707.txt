A Measurement-Like Test of Continuous Spontaneous Localization with a Reversible Nanoparticle Pointer
Peter Renkel

Abstract




Abstract

We propose a measurement-like interferometric test of Continuous Spontaneous Localization
(CSL), in which a trapped nanoparticle acts as a reversible mesoscopic pointer rather than
as the initially prepared microscopic superposition. A microscopic two-branch system applies
opposite weak forces to the nanoparticle, temporarily amplifying which-branch information
into branch-conditioned pointer displacements. After one weak-trap period the pointer
positions and momenta recombine, so ordinary quantum mechanics predicts recovery of the
microscopic coherence, whereas CSL predicts an irreversible visibility loss accumulated while
the pointer mass distributions were separated.

For a
10^{-18},{\rm kg}
nanoparticle driven by a
10^{-21},{\rm N}
differential force at

T=0.4,{\rm K}
, including thermal breathing and an ordinary loss budget

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1
, we find

\lambda_{\min}\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-10},{\rm s}^{-1}
at

r_{c}=100,{\rm nm}
with
10^{5}
shots. A more aggressive lower-frequency point reaches

\lambda_{\min}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-12},{\rm s}^{-1}
.

These sensitivities would improve over digitized direct matter-wave CSL bounds by about

7\times 10^{3}
and
10^{5}
, respectively, while remaining above the strongest
non-interferometric bounds. The proposal is therefore aimed at a substantially stronger
direct visibility-loss test of CSL, and at a reversible measurement-like realization of
collapse sensitivity, rather than at the strongest overall exclusion curve.

1
Introduction

Quantum theory provides a unitary description of microscopic systems, but
ordinary measurements appear to produce definite outcomes. In the standard
operational treatment this tension is hidden by the measurement postulate:
microscopic systems evolve according to the Schrödinger equation until a
measurement occurs, after which the state is updated nonunitarily. This division
is practically successful, but it leaves open whether the apparent collapse of
the quantum state is fundamental, emergent, or only an effective description of
entanglement and decoherence.

Objective-collapse models address this question by modifying quantum
dynamics
[
1
,
4
,
5
]
. In these models,
macroscopic spatial superpositions are dynamically suppressed by a stochastic
physical process. Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL) is one of the most
widely studied examples
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
.
It introduces two phenomenological parameters: a collapse rate
\lambda
and a
correlation length
r_{c}
. For a mass distribution placed in a spatial
superposition, CSL predicts an additional loss of coherence whose strength
grows with mass and depends on the separation of the two mass distributions
relative to
r_{c}
. The experimental task is therefore to search for a visibility
loss that cannot be explained by ordinary environmental decoherence.

A natural way to test CSL is to place a massive object in a spatial
superposition and measure the loss of interference contrast. Direct matter-wave
and interferometric experiments follow this logic most closely

[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. They are conceptually clean
because the measured quantity is directly the survival of coherence between
spatially separated branches. However, producing large coherent spatial
separations for massive objects is experimentally demanding. As a result, the
strongest overall bounds on CSL often come from non-interferometric observables,
such as anomalous heating, force noise, spontaneous radiation, or related
precision measurements

[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
These
bounds are extremely important, but they do not test collapse in the same way
as a reversible interference experiment.

Here we propose a different direct route: instead of making the nanoparticle
itself the original microscopic superposition, we use it as an apparatus-like
measurement pointer. A microscopic system is prepared in a superposition of two
branches that exert opposite weak forces on a trapped nanoparticle. The
nanoparticle center of mass then acquires two branch-conditioned mean
displacements and temporarily stores which-branch information in a mesoscopic
degree of freedom. After one weak-trap period the branch-conditioned mean
positions and momenta recombine, and the remaining microscopic coherence is read
out interferometrically.

This construction differs from a standard matter-wave CSL test in an important
way. The nanoparticle is not merely a freely propagating interfering object. It
plays the role of a reversible pointer that is displaced conditionally on the
state of a microscopic system. The experiment therefore tests whether coherence
survives after information has been amplified into an apparatus-like degree of
freedom. In this sense the protocol is closer to the structure of a measurement
than ordinary single-object interferometry, while still remaining reversible and
quantitatively analyzable.

The relevant CSL signal is the accumulated suppression of the off-diagonal
density-matrix element associated with the two branch-conditioned pointer
states. For a sharply localized compact mass distribution and full branch
separation
D(t)
, the CSL rate is
[
5
,
13
]

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm point}(t)=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{m_{0}^{2}}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{D^{2}(t)}{4r_{c}^{2}}\right)\right],

(1)

where
m
is the nanoparticle mass,
m_{0}
is the nucleon mass,
\lambda
is the
CSL collapse rate, and
r_{c}
is the CSL correlation length. In the regime

D(t)\ll r_{c}
, this becomes

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm point}(t)\simeq\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}D^{2}(t).

(2)

Thus the experiment can remain sensitive even when the realized pointer
separation is much smaller than the CSL correlation length. Rather than
requiring
D_{\max}\sim r_{c}
, the protocol accumulates a measurable
CSL-induced visibility loss from a controlled time-dependent branch separation
satisfying
D_{\max}\ll r_{c}
.

A key practical issue is that the nanoparticle density matrix is not static.
The particle is initially localized in a tight trap of frequency
\omega_{0}
,
then evolves in a weaker trap of frequency
\omega
. At finite temperature this
produces thermal breathing. For an initial thermal state, the variance evolves
as

\sigma^{2}(t)=\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\cos^{2}(\omega t)+\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega^{2}}\sin^{2}(\omega t).

(3)

This expression follows from the density-matrix covariance evolution derived in
Sec.
2.6
.

The corresponding exact small-separation 3D Gaussian form factor is

G_{\rm 3D}(t)=\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-5/2}.

(4)

Including this factor is essential. For the conservative operating point
studied below, thermal breathing reduces the ideal point-particle CSL signal by
the factor

S_{\rm br}=4.82\times 10^{-2}

(5)

at
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
. This suppression is substantial, but it is
included throughout the sensitivity estimate.
The physical branch separation in the proposed experiment is one-dimensional:
the two branch-conditioned pointer means separate only along the force axis.
For a strictly one-dimensional Gaussian breathing model, the corresponding
small-separation form factor would scale as

G_{\rm 1D}(t)=\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-3/2}.

(6)

In the numerical estimates below, however, we use the stronger 3D isotropic
suppression factor in Eq. (
4
). This gives a smaller
CSL signal and therefore a conservative sensitivity estimate.

The purpose of this paper is to quantify the viability of this measurement-like
CSL test. We derive the CSL signal for a forced reversible pointer response,
include thermal breathing, estimate ordinary visibility losses, and compute the
number of experimental shots required for a
5\sigma
detection. We focus on
representative operating points rather than on a fully optimized design. At

r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, the conservative point gives

d_{\max}=0.25\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad D_{\max}=0.50\,\mathrm{nm},

(7)

so the protocol operates deeply in the sub-
r_{c}
regime. After including the
breathing correction and an ordinary coherence-loss budget

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1
, with the electric-field and force-noise
sub-budgets kept below
0.05
, the projected point sensitivity with

10^{5}
shots is

\lambda_{\min}\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

(8)

A more aggressive lower-frequency point reaches below

10^{-11}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, at the cost of stronger technical requirements.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2

describes the measurement-like protocol, the branch-conditioned pointer mean
response, CSL visibility loss, and thermal breathing correction.
Section
3
discusses ordinary visibility losses,
calibration effects, and the dominant feasibility requirements.
Section
4
presents the numerical sensitivity estimates.
Section
5
compares the projected reach with existing CSL
constraints.

2
Signal model

2.1
Measurement-like pointer protocol

We consider a microscopic quantum system coupled to the center-of-mass motion
of a trapped nanoparticle. The nanoparticle acts as a reversible mesoscopic
measurement pointer. During the interaction, the two microscopic branches exert
opposite weak forces on the nanoparticle. The two branch-conditioned pointer
means are then recombined, and the remaining microscopic coherence is read out
interferometrically.

Let the microscopic system be prepared with density matrix

\rho_{\chi}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\left(|+\rangle\langle+|+|-\rangle\langle-|+|+\rangle\langle-|+|-\rangle\langle+|\right).

(9)

The nanoparticle pointer has center-of-mass coordinate
x
, momentum
p
, mass

m
, and evolves in a harmonic trap of weak frequency
\omega
. We define
F
to
be the differential force between the two microscopic branches. Thus the

|+\rangle
branch exerts force
+F/2
on the nanoparticle, while the
|-\rangle

branch exerts force
-F/2
. With this convention the conditional Hamiltonians
are

H_{\pm}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x^{2}\mp\frac{F}{2}x.

(10)

The full density matrix can be written in branch-block form as

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=\pm}|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|\otimes\rho_{\alpha\beta}(t).

(11)

The diagonal blocks
\rho_{++}
and
\rho_{--}
describe the two
branch-conditioned pointer states, while the off-diagonal block
\rho_{+-}

carries the microscopic coherence.

We define the branch-conditioned mean positions and momenta by

\bar{x}_{\pm}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[x\rho_{\pm\pm}(t)\right],\qquad\bar{p}_{\pm}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[p\rho_{\pm\pm}(t)\right],

(12)

where the trace is over the pointer Hilbert space. Ordinary unitary quantum
mechanics predicts that, after a full weak-trap period, the
branch-conditioned mean positions and momenta recombine. CSL, by contrast,
produces an irreversible suppression of the off-diagonal block associated with
the two temporarily separated mass-density profiles.

2.2
Forced harmonic mean response

We assume that the two branch-conditioned pointer states begin with the same
mean position and momentum,

\bar{x}_{+}(0)=\bar{x}_{-}(0)=0,\qquad\bar{p}_{+}(0)=\bar{p}_{-}(0)=0.

(13)

The Ehrenfest equations for the two diagonal density-matrix blocks give

m\ddot{\bar{x}}_{+}+m\omega^{2}\bar{x}_{+}=\frac{F}{2},

(14)

and

m\ddot{\bar{x}}_{-}+m\omega^{2}\bar{x}_{-}=-\frac{F}{2}.

(15)

Therefore

\bar{x}_{+}(t)=\frac{F}{2m\omega^{2}}\left(1-\cos\omega t\right),

(16)

and

\bar{x}_{-}(t)=-\frac{F}{2m\omega^{2}}\left(1-\cos\omega t\right).

(17)

We define the one-way mean displacement by

d(t)=\bar{x}_{+}(t)=-\bar{x}_{-}(t)=\frac{F}{2m\omega^{2}}\left(1-\cos\omega t\right),

(18)

and the full branch separation by

D(t)=\bar{x}_{+}(t)-\bar{x}_{-}(t)=2d(t)=\frac{F}{m\omega^{2}}\left(1-\cos\omega t\right).

(19)

The CSL formulas depend on
D(t)
, the full separation between the two
branch-conditioned mass-density means.

The maximum one-way displacement occurs at half a weak-trap period,

t=\pi/\omega
, and is

d_{\max}=\frac{F}{m\omega^{2}},\qquad D_{\max}=2d_{\max}.

(20)

At one full weak-trap period,

\tau=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},

(21)

the branch-conditioned means recombine:

D(\tau)=0,\qquad\dot{D}(\tau)=0.

(22)

This exact harmonic recombination is central to the protocol. It allows the
pointer to temporarily carry which-branch information and then coherently return
that information to the microscopic system. Ordinary quantum mechanics
therefore predicts recovery of the microscopic interference contrast, up to
technical and environmental imperfections. CSL predicts an additional
irreversible loss accumulated while the two branch-conditioned mass-density
profiles are separated.

2.3
Dipole-source realization

One concrete realization is a microscopic source prepared in a coherent
superposition of two states that produce opposite electric-dipole fields at the
nanoparticle. If the nanoparticle carries an effective charge
q
, the two
source branches generate approximately opposite forces

F_{\pm}=\pm\frac{F}{2}

(23)

over the small spatial range explored by the pointer. Equivalently, the
branch-conditioned source-particle interaction is linearized as

V_{\pm}(x)=\mp\frac{F}{2}x,

(24)

which gives the conditional Hamiltonians used above.

For an axial electric dipole of moment
p
at distance
h
, the electric
field scales as
E_{\rm dip}\sim p/(2\pi\epsilon_{0}h^{3})
, up to a geometry
factor of order unity. With opposite dipole orientations in the two source
branches, the differential force scale is therefore

F\sim 2qE_{\rm dip}\sim\frac{qp}{\pi\epsilon_{0}h^{3}}.

(25)

This estimate is used only to make the force scale concrete. Inverting Eq. (25), the dipole moment required to generate a differential force
F
at distance
h
is
p=\pi\epsilon_{0}h^{3}F/q
. For
F=10^{-21}\,{\rm N}
,
h=14\,\mu{\rm m}
, and
q=e
, this gives
p=4.8\times 10^{-28}\,{\rm C\,m}
, or
p\simeq 1.4\times 10^{2}\,{\rm D}
, using
1\,{\rm D}=3.336\times 10^{-30}\,{\rm C\,m}
.

This dipole moment is much larger than that of ordinary ground-state polar molecules. Such molecules typically have permanent dipole moments of order a few Debye, and even very polar molecules are at the
\sim 10\,{\rm D}
scale. Therefore ordinary molecules would reduce the force by more than an order of magnitude at the same distance, or would require a substantially smaller source-particle separation.

The dipole-source realization should therefore be understood as requiring a large coherent electric dipole,
such as a Rydberg-Stark source, a Stark-admixed circular-Rydberg source, or a related engineered source, rather than an ordinary molecular permanent dipole. Rydberg-based sources can provide dipole moments above the
10^{2}\,{\rm D}
scale. The more demanding requirement is coherence of the two force-source branches during the pointer cycle. In the sensitivity estimates below, we keep the realized differential force
F
as the primary apparatus parameter and treat the coherence of the force source as a separate explicit requirement.

2.4
Visibility readout

After tracing out the nanoparticle, let
\rho^{\chi}_{+-}
denote the
off-diagonal element of the microscopic density matrix in the

\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}
basis:

\rho^{\chi}_{+-}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[\rho_{+-}(t)\right].

(26)

In the presence of CSL and ordinary non-CSL visibility losses, we write

\rho^{\chi}_{+-}(\tau)=\rho^{\chi}_{+-}(0)\exp(i\Phi_{\rm det})\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm CSL})\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm loss}),

(27)

where
\Phi_{\rm det}
is a deterministic phase,
\Lambda_{\rm CSL}
is the
CSL-induced visibility-loss exponent, and
\Lambda_{\rm loss}
collects
ordinary loss channels such as gas decoherence, blackbody decoherence, timing
error, force noise, electric-field noise, and imperfect recombination.

The measured Ramsey visibility is therefore

V=V_{0}\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm CSL})\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm loss}),

(28)

where
V_{0}
is the calibrated visibility in the absence of the controlled
branch-dependent pointer displacement. The CSL signal is the additional
visibility reduction associated with the controlled branch separation
D(t)
.

2.5
CSL visibility-loss exponent

For a sharply localized pointer, the effective CSL rate for a full branch
separation
D
is

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm point}(D)=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{m_{0}^{2}}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{D^{2}}{4r_{c}^{2}}\right)\right],

(29)

where
m_{0}
is the nucleon mass,
r_{c}
is the CSL correlation length, and

\lambda
is the CSL collapse rate. In the small-separation regime
D\ll r_{c}
,

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm point}(D)\simeq\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}D^{2}.

(30)

The ideal accumulated CSL exponent is therefore

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}^{(0)}=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt.

(31)

For the harmonic mean response,

I_{D}\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt=\frac{3\pi F^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{5}}.

(32)

Thus

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}^{(0)}=\lambda\frac{3\pi F^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}\omega^{5}}.

(33)

The nanoparticle mass cancels in this pointlike, fixed-force,
small-separation limit because
D(t)
is proportional to
F/(m\omega^{2})
,
while the CSL coupling scales as
m^{2}
.

2.6
Thermal breathing correction

The nanoparticle density matrix is not static. It is initially localized in a
tighter trap of frequency
\omega_{0}
, then evolves in the weaker measurement
trap of frequency
\omega
. For an initial thermal state at temperature
T
,
the position variance breathes.

The breathing formula follows directly from density-matrix evolution. Let the
initial pointer state be

\rho_{\rm P}(0)=\frac{\exp(-\beta H_{0})}{Z_{0}},\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T},

(34)

with

H_{0}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{0}^{2}x^{2}.

(35)

During the measurement interval the pointer evolves under the weak harmonic
Hamiltonian

H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x^{2}.

(36)

The Heisenberg position operator is

x(t)=x(0)\cos\omega t+\frac{p(0)}{m\omega}\sin\omega t.

(37)

Therefore

\sigma^{2}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[\rho_{\rm P}(0)x^{2}(t)\right].

(38)

For a thermal state of the initial trap, in the high-temperature limit,

\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[\rho_{\rm P}(0)x^{2}(0)\right]=\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega_{0}^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[\rho_{\rm P}(0)p^{2}(0)\right]=mk_{B}T,

(39)

and

\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm P}\left[\rho_{\rm P}(0)\{x(0),p(0)\}\right]=0.

(40)

It follows that

\sigma^{2}(t)=\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\cos^{2}\omega t+\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega^{2}}\sin^{2}\omega t.

(41)

The first term is the initial thermal spatial width in the tight trap. The
second term comes from the initial thermal momentum spread being converted into
position spread during evolution in the weak trap.

Thermal breathing modifies the effective CSL signal because the two
branch-conditioned mass-density profiles are not sharply localized. The relevant
CSL quantity is not obtained by averaging the point-particle expression over
positions inside the two density profiles. Such an average would incorrectly
count the intrinsic width of a single density profile as branch
distinguishability. Instead, CSL couples to the difference between the two
smeared branch mass densities.

Let
A_{\alpha\beta}(t)
denote the normalized CSL kernel overlap between
branch-conditioned Gaussian mass-density profiles:

A_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\int d^{3}r\,d^{3}r^{\prime}\,g_{\sigma}^{(3)}\!\left({\bf r}-\bar{\bf r}_{\alpha}(t)\right)g_{\sigma}^{(3)}\!\left({\bf r}^{\prime}-\bar{\bf r}_{\beta}(t)\right)\exp\left[-\frac{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{2}}{4r_{c}^{2}}\right],

(42)

where
g_{\sigma}^{(3)}
is a normalized isotropic 3D Gaussian of variance

\sigma^{2}(t)
in each direction, and
\bar{\bf r}_{\alpha}(t)
is the
branch-conditioned mean position. With this normalization,
A_{\alpha\beta}

is dimensionless. The branch-decoherence rate is

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}(t)=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{2m_{0}^{2}}\left[A_{++}(t)+A_{--}(t)-2A_{+-}(t)\right].

(43)

This form gives zero CSL loss when the two branch-conditioned mass densities
are identical.

For equal-width Gaussian densities,

A_{++}=A_{--}=\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right)^{-3/2},

(44)

and

A_{+-}=A_{++}\exp\left[-\frac{D^{2}(t)}{4\left(r_{c}^{2}+\sigma^{2}(t)\right)}\right].

(45)

Therefore the exact 3D Gaussian-density CSL rate is

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm 3D}(t)=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{m_{0}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right)^{-3/2}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{D^{2}(t)}{4\left[r_{c}^{2}+\sigma^{2}(t)\right]}\right)\right].

(46)

In the small-separation regime
D(t)\ll r_{c}
, this becomes

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm 3D}(t)\simeq\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}D^{2}(t)\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right)^{-5/2}.

(47)

The additional power relative to the self-overlap factor comes from expanding
the bracket in Eq. (
46
), which contributes a factor

1/[r_{c}^{2}+\sigma^{2}(t)]
. The proposed pointer displacement is along one spatial direction. Therefore, if
the transverse pointer widths are either tightly confined or treated as
irrelevant to the controlled branch distinguishability, the effective
one-dimensional breathing correction is

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}^{\rm 1D}(t)\simeq\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}D^{2}(t)\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right)^{-3/2}.

(48)

This one-dimensional expression gives a larger CSL signal than the isotropic
3D expression in Eq. (
47
). We use the 3D factor in the
numerical estimates as a conservative choice.

The breathing-corrected CSL exponent is therefore

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}(r_{c})=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-5/2}dt.

(49)

We define

I_{DG}(r_{c})=\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-5/2}dt,

(50)

and

S_{\rm br}(r_{c})=\frac{I_{DG}(r_{c})}{I_{D}}.

(51)

Then

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}(r_{c})=\lambda\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}I_{DG}(r_{c}).

(52)

The integral
I_{DG}
is evaluated numerically for the operating points below.

2.7
Operating points

We quote two operating points. The chosen point is intended as the main
technically conservative result:

m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad F=10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad\omega=2.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

(53)

with

\omega_{0}=10^{7}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad T=0.4\,\mathrm{K}.

(54)

Here and below,
F
is the differential force between the two microscopic
branches. The corresponding interaction time is

\tau=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=3.14\,\mathrm{ms}.

(55)

At
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, this point gives

d_{\max}=0.25\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad D_{\max}=0.50\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad S_{\rm br}=4.82\times 10^{-2}.

(56)

We also quote a lower-frequency aggressive point:

m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad F=10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad\omega=1.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

(57)

with the same
\omega_{0}
and
T
. The corresponding interaction time is

\tau=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=6.28\,\mathrm{ms}.

(58)

At
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, this point gives

d_{\max}=1.0\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad D_{\max}=2.0\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad S_{\rm br}=2.41\times 10^{-2}.

(59)

Both points satisfy
D_{\max}\ll r_{c}
at
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
. The aggressive
point gives a larger time-integrated branch separation, but it is technically
more demanding because the interaction time is longer and recombination is more
sensitive to low-frequency noise and trap imperfections. The corresponding CSL signal coefficients and point sensitivities are computed
later after the statistical sensitivity formula is introduced. Since those
numbers use the 3D breathing factor rather than the less suppressive 1D factor,
the quoted sensitivities should be regarded as conservative for an effectively
one-dimensional implementation.

3
Systematic effects

3.1
Organization of the systematic budget

The measured visibility contains both the CSL contribution and ordinary non-CSL
losses:

V=V_{0}\exp[-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}]\exp[-\Lambda_{\rm loss}].

(60)

We write

\Lambda_{\rm loss}=\Lambda_{\rm gas}+\Lambda_{\rm bb}+\Lambda_{E}+\Lambda_{h}+\Lambda_{t}+\Lambda_{\omega}+\Lambda_{\rm source}+\cdots.

(61)

The terms in this expression have different physical meanings. To avoid mixing
them, we separate ordinary systematics into four categories.

First, an inter-shot fluctuation is approximately constant during one shot but
changes from shot to shot. Such a fluctuation can either change the calibration
of the predicted CSL signal or produce a shot-to-shot random phase.

Second, an intra-shot fluctuation changes during one shot. If it produces a
branch-dependent energy difference, it gives phase noise. For a classical
technical fluctuation, the random phase is

\delta\phi=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left[\delta H_{+}(\bar{x}_{+}(t),t)-\delta H_{-}(\bar{x}_{-}(t),t)\right]dt.

(62)

The corresponding visibility loss is

\Lambda_{\rm intra}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\delta\phi^{2}\right\rangle.

(63)

Third, a technical imperfection may leave the two pointer states imperfectly
recombined at the final time. This is a separate endpoint overlap error. If the
residual full branch separation and relative momentum at recombination are

\delta D(\tau)
and
\delta P(\tau)
, then, for Gaussian packets of width

\sigma(\tau)
,

\Lambda_{\rm ov}\simeq\frac{\delta D^{2}(\tau)}{8\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)\delta P^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(64)

Fourth, environmental scattering processes, such as residual gas and blackbody
photons, are treated directly as decoherence mechanisms rather than as classical
phase noise.

For the numerical projections we require the total ordinary loss to be
controlled at approximately

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1.

(65)

3.2
Representative force-source model

The systematics associated with the force source are evaluated using a dipole
source as a representative realization of the setup. We assume that the force
on the nanoparticle depends on the source-particle distance
h
as

F(h)\propto h^{-3}.

(66)

Therefore a small distance fluctuation produces

\delta F_{h}(t)=F_{h}\,\delta h(t),\qquad F_{h}\equiv\frac{\partial F}{\partial h},

(67)

with

F_{h}=-\frac{3F}{h}.

(68)

This dipole model is not essential for the general formalism, but it gives a
concrete and conservative way to translate distance noise into force noise.

The branch separation is denoted by

D(t)=x_{+}(t)-x_{-}(t).

(69)

With the branch labels used here,
x_{+}(t)>x_{-}(t)
during the separated part of
the trajectory, so
D(t)
is positive. Recombination requires
D(\tau)=0
,
but it does not imply
\int_{0}^{\tau}D(t)\,dt=0
.

We define

I_{D}\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt,

(70)

and

J_{D}\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}D(t)\,dt.

(71)

For the harmonic trajectory used in the estimates,

I_{D}=\frac{3\pi F^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{5}},\qquad J_{D}=\frac{2\pi F}{m\omega^{3}}.

(72)

3.4
Residual-gas and blackbody decoherence

Residual-gas collisions and blackbody photons are treated as environmental
which-path decoherence mechanisms. A gas molecule or photon can carry away
partial information about the nanoparticle position, thereby suppressing the
off-diagonal density-matrix element between the two branches.

For a general environmental decoherence mechanism, the visibility loss during
one shot is

\Lambda_{\rm env}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\Gamma_{\rm env}\!\left(D(t)\right)\,dt,

(73)

where
\Gamma_{\rm env}(D)
is the decoherence rate for a spatial
superposition of size
D
.

In the small-separation regime, the environmental resolution is insufficient to
fully distinguish the two branches. The decoherence rate may then be expanded as

\Gamma_{\rm env}(D)\simeq\eta_{\rm env}D^{2},

(74)

where

\eta_{\rm env}\equiv\lim_{D\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Gamma_{\rm env}(D)}{D^{2}}.

(75)

Therefore

\Lambda_{\rm env}\simeq\eta_{\rm env}\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt=\eta_{\rm env}I_{D}.

(76)

For residual-gas scattering we write

\Lambda_{\rm gas}\simeq\eta_{\rm gas}I_{D}.

(77)

For blackbody radiation, including photon emission, absorption, and scattering,
we write

\Lambda_{\rm bb}\simeq\eta_{\rm bb}I_{D}.

(78)

These expressions describe loss of coherence during a single experimental
cycle, not a shot-to-shot phase drift.

At the conservative operating point

m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad F=10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad\omega=2.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

(79)

we obtain

I_{D}=2.95\times 10^{-22}\,\mathrm{m^{2}\,s}.

(80)

Using representative conservative coefficients

\eta_{\rm gas}=10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}s^{-1}},\qquad\eta_{\rm bb}=10^{12}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}s^{-1}},

(81)

gives

\Lambda_{\rm gas}=2.95\times 10^{-4},\qquad\Lambda_{\rm bb}=2.95\times 10^{-10}.

(82)

Thus both mechanisms are below the target uncontrolled-loss level

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1
, with residual gas dominating over
blackbody radiation for the assumed cryogenic operating point.

3.5
Electric-field noise

A uniform electric field produces a common force on the nanoparticle. It gives a
branch-dependent phase while the branches are separated, but a perfectly uniform
field does not change the relative separation of the two branches to first
order.

The perturbation is

\delta H_{E}(t)=-q\,\delta E(t)\,x.

(83)

Therefore

\delta H_{E,+}-\delta H_{E,-}=-q\,\delta E(t)\left[\bar{x}_{+}(t)-\bar{x}_{-}(t)\right]=-q\,\delta E(t)D(t).

(84)

Using Eq. (
62
), the phase fluctuation is

\delta\phi_{E}=\frac{q}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\delta E(t)D(t)\,dt.

(85)

For a quasistatic shot-to-shot field fluctuation
\delta E_{j}
, the phase in
shot
j
is

\phi_{j}^{E}=\frac{q\delta E_{j}}{\hbar}J_{D}.

(86)

The visibility loss is determined by the shot-to-shot phase variance,

\Lambda_{E}^{\rm inter}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Var}\!\left(\phi_{j}^{E}\right)=\frac{q^{2}\sigma_{E,{\rm sh}}^{2}J_{D}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}},

(87)

where

\sigma_{E,{\rm sh}}^{2}\equiv{\rm Var}\!\left(\delta E_{j}\right).

(88)

For the harmonic trajectory,
J_{D}=2\pi F/(m\omega^{3})
. Requiring

\Lambda_{E}^{\rm inter}<\Lambda_{\rm max}
gives

\sigma_{E,{\rm sh}}<\frac{\sqrt{2\Lambda_{\rm max}}\,\hbar}{qJ_{D}}.

(89)

For the conservative point and
q=e
, the electric-field sub-budget

\Lambda_{E}^{\rm inter}<0.05
gives

\sigma_{E,{\rm sh}}<2.7\times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V/m}.

(90)

The quasistatic electric-field requirement,

\sigma_{E}<2.7\times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V/m}
, is a meaningful technical
requirement, but it is not a fundamental limitation. It applies to the residual
field component that is correlated with the branch-dependent force and survives
the experimental shielding and calibration procedure. In practice, this field
can be characterized in situ by taking calibration data without the force
source. We therefore treat electric-field stability as an experimentally
controllable systematic that must be monitored, rather than as an irreducible
background.

For intra-shot white electric-field noise,

\left\langle\delta E(t)\delta E(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{S_{E}}{2}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(91)

Using Eq. (
85
), we obtain

\Lambda_{E}^{\rm intra}=\frac{q^{2}S_{E}}{4\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt=\frac{q^{2}S_{E}I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}.

(92)

Requiring
\Lambda_{E}^{\rm intra}<\Lambda_{\rm max}
gives

\sqrt{S_{E}}<\left[\frac{4\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{\rm max}}{q^{2}I_{D}}\right]^{1/2}.

(93)

Although a perfectly uniform field gives no relative endpoint mismatch, an
electric-field fluctuation that acts as an effective differential force can
also produce an overlap error. This gives a conservative bound on non-common-mode
electric-field oscillations. In this case,

\delta F_{E}(t)=q\,\delta E(t),\qquad S_{\Delta F}^{E}=q^{2}S_{E}.

(94)

The endpoint variances are

\left\langle\delta D_{E}^{2}(\tau)\right\rangle=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{E}}{2m^{2}\omega^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sin^{2}\!\left[\omega(\tau-t)\right]\,dt=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{E}\tau}{4m^{2}\omega^{2}},

(95)

and

\left\langle\delta P_{E}^{2}(\tau)\right\rangle=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{E}}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\cos^{2}\!\left[\omega(\tau-t)\right]\,dt=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{E}\tau}{4}.

(96)

Substitution into Eq. (
64
) gives

\Lambda_{E}^{\rm ov}\simeq q^{2}S_{E}\tau\left[\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right].

(97)

For finite-frequency electric-field noise, the conservative condition is imposed on

\Lambda_{E}=\Lambda_{E}^{\rm intra}+\Lambda_{E}^{\rm ov}.

(98)

Explicitly,

\Lambda_{E}\simeq q^{2}S_{E}\left[\frac{I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}+\tau\left(\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right)\right].

(99)

For the conservative point,

I_{D}=2.95\times 10^{-22}\,\mathrm{m^{2}\,s},\qquad m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad\omega=2.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

(100)

\tau=3.14\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s},\qquad\sigma(\tau)=2.35\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m},\qquad q=e,

(101)

the phase and overlap coefficients are

\frac{I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}=6.63\times 10^{45},

(102)

and

\tau\left(\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right)=2.39\times 10^{45}.

(103)

Therefore requiring

\Lambda_{E}<0.05

(104)

gives

\sqrt{S_{E}}<1.5\times 10^{-5}\,\frac{\mathrm{V}}{\mathrm{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}}.

(105)

This is the conservative finite-frequency bound including both intrashot phase noise and the
possible non-common-mode overlap mismatch. For a perfectly uniform common-mode
electric field, the overlap term is absent and the corresponding bound is
slightly weaker,

\sqrt{S_{E}}<1.7\times 10^{-5}\,\frac{\mathrm{V}}{\mathrm{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}}.

(106)

Thus electric-field noise is a relevant but controllable technical systematic
for
q=e
, provided the experiment is shielded and both the slow field drift and
the finite-frequency residual field noise are monitored.

3.6
Distance fluctuations of the force source

We denote by
h
the distance between the force source and the nanoparticle.
Small distance fluctuations change the applied force,

\delta F_{h}(t)=F_{h}\,\delta h(t),\qquad F_{h}\equiv\frac{\partial F}{\partial h}.

(107)

For the representative dipole source, Eq. (
68
)
gives

F_{h}=-\frac{3F}{h}.

(108)

There are two distinct effects. A fluctuation that preserves the perfectly
antisymmetric force pattern
\pm F/2
changes the differential force amplitude
and therefore the trajectory. To first order it does not produce the same
branch phase as a common force, because the two branch potentials remain
antisymmetric. By contrast, any residual non-antisymmetric force component
generated by source motion acts like an effective common force on the two
pointer positions and does produce phase averaging. The estimates below treat
the residual common-force and differential-force components as being of the
same conservative order set by
|F_{h}|\delta h
. This gives a conservative
technical requirement; in a more symmetric implementation the direct phase
terms can be reduced.

First, consider a quasistatic distance error: during shot
j
, the distance is
shifted by a constant amount
\delta h_{j}
. The corresponding residual
common-force component is written

\delta f_{j}=F_{h}\,\delta h_{j}.

(109)

Since this force component is constant during the shot, the corresponding phase
is

\phi_{j}^{h}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\delta f_{j}D(t)\,dt=\frac{\delta f_{j}}{\hbar}J_{D}.

(110)

This quasistatic contribution does not vanish for the trajectory used here,
because
D(t)
is positive during the separated part of the motion. The
intershot loss is

\Lambda_{\rm inter}^{h}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Var}\!\left(\phi_{j}^{h}\right)=\frac{F_{h}^{2}\sigma_{h,{\rm sh}}^{2}J_{D}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}},

(111)

where

\sigma_{h,{\rm sh}}^{2}\equiv{\rm Var}\!\left(\delta h_{j}\right).

(112)

Using
F_{h}=-3F/h
, this becomes

\Lambda_{\rm inter}^{h}=\left(\frac{3F}{h}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma_{h,{\rm sh}}^{2}J_{D}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(113)

For the conservative point, requiring the force-noise sub-budget

\Lambda_{\rm inter}^{h}<0.05
gives

\sigma_{h,{\rm sh}}<2.0\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}.

(114)

The quasistatic distance requirement,

\sigma_{h}<2.0\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
, corresponds to a relative stability

\sigma_{h}/h\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-2}
for the representative separation

h=1.42\times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
. This is a percent-level
relative-position requirement and should be achievable in a rigid,
vibration-isolated geometry.

Second, finite-frequency distance noise during the shot is treated separately.
The residual common-force component produces an intrashot phase-noise
contribution

\Lambda_{\rm intra}^{h}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(\phi_{h}\right)^{2}\right\rangle,

(115)

where

\phi_{h}=\frac{F_{h}}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\delta h(t)D(t)\,dt.

(116)

For white distance noise with

\left\langle\delta h(t)\delta h(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{S_{h}}{2}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(117)

the intrashot contribution is

\Lambda_{\rm intra}^{h}=\frac{F_{h}^{2}S_{h}}{4\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\,dt.

(118)

Using
F_{h}=-3F/h
, this becomes

\Lambda_{\rm intra}^{h}=\left(\frac{3F}{h}\right)^{2}\frac{S_{h}I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}.

(119)

Finite-frequency differential-force noise can also leave a small final
recombination mismatch. This is a separate overlap contribution, denoted by

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{h}
. The corresponding differential-force-noise spectrum
is

S_{\Delta F}^{h}=F_{h}^{2}S_{h}.

(120)

For one full harmonic period,
\tau=2\pi/\omega
, the final displacement and
momentum variances are

\left\langle\delta D_{h}^{2}(\tau)\right\rangle=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{h}\tau}{4m^{2}\omega^{2}},\qquad\left\langle\delta P_{h}^{2}(\tau)\right\rangle=\frac{S_{\Delta F}^{h}\tau}{4}.

(121)

The factor
1/4
comes from the factor
1/2
in the white-noise convention
and the factor
1/2
from the full-period average of
\sin^{2}
or

\cos^{2}
.

Substituting these variances into Eq. (
64
) gives

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{h}\simeq F_{h}^{2}S_{h}\tau\left[\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right].

(122)

Using
F_{h}=-3F/h
, this becomes

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{h}\simeq\left(\frac{3F}{h}\right)^{2}S_{h}\tau\left[\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right].

(123)

The total finite-frequency distance-noise contribution is

\Lambda_{h}=\Lambda_{\rm intra}^{h}+\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{h}.

(124)

Therefore

\Lambda_{h}\simeq\left(\frac{3F}{h}\right)^{2}S_{h}\left[\frac{I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}+\tau\left(\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right)\right].

(125)

Requiring
\Lambda_{h}<\Lambda_{\rm max}
gives

\sqrt{S_{h}}<\left[\frac{\Lambda_{\rm max}}{\left(\frac{3F}{h}\right)^{2}\left[\frac{I_{D}}{4\hbar^{2}}+\tau\left(\frac{1}{32m^{2}\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{8\hbar^{2}}\right)\right]}\right]^{1/2}.

(126)

The required distance stability is in the nanometer-per-root-Hz range. Such
relative displacement noise is demanding but realistic for a mechanically rigid
and vibration-isolated setup. Since only the relative motion between the force
source and the nanoparticle enters
S_{h}
, common-mode motion is largely
irrelevant. The representative bound from Eq. (
126
)
is
S_{h}^{1/2}\lesssim 1.1\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}
, i.e.
of order
10\,\mathrm{nm}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}
. Such values are demanding but
reasonable, provided the source and trap are mounted on a sufficiently stable
common mechanical platform.

Thus quasistatic distance fluctuations produce a shot-to-shot phase variance
through Eq. (
111
). Finite-frequency distance noise can
produce both an intrashot phase contribution and a separate recombination-overlap
contribution, and the requirement on
S_{h}
should be imposed on their sum.

3.7
Timing mismatch at recombination

A timing error at recombination can leave both a residual coordinate mismatch
and a residual momentum mismatch between the two wave packets. The overlap loss
is

\Lambda_{t}\simeq\frac{\delta D_{t}^{2}(\tau)}{8\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)\delta P_{t}^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(127)

For the harmonic recombination trajectory, a small timing error
\delta t

gives

\delta D_{t}(\tau)\simeq\frac{F}{2m}\delta t^{2},

(128)

whereas

\delta P_{t}(\tau)\simeq F\,\delta t.

(129)

Thus the coordinate mismatch is second order in
\delta t
, while the momentum
mismatch is first order. Consequently,

\Lambda_{t}\simeq\frac{F^{2}\delta t^{4}}{32m^{2}\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)F^{2}\delta t^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(130)

The first term is fourth order in
\delta t
in the visibility loss and is
subleading for small timing errors. The leading contribution is therefore

\Lambda_{t}\simeq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)F^{2}\delta t^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(131)

We estimate the packet width from the initial trap temperature,

\sigma(\tau)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{k_{B}T}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}}.

(132)

For

T=0.4\,\mathrm{K},\qquad m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad\omega_{0}=10^{7}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

(133)

this gives

\sigma(\tau)=2.35\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}.

(134)

Taking a conservative timing uncertainty

\delta t=\frac{\tau}{1000}=3.14\times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s},

(135)

we obtain

\Lambda_{t}=2.5\times 10^{-5}.

(136)

Equivalently, requiring
\Lambda_{t}<0.1
gives

\delta t<2.0\times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}.

(137)

The required timing precision is therefore modest compared with the millisecond
duration of the shot.

3.8
Trap-frequency fluctuations

Let the fractional trap-frequency error be

\epsilon_{\omega}\equiv\frac{\delta\omega}{\omega}.

(138)

After one nominal recombination period, this produces both coordinate and
momentum mismatch. For small
\epsilon_{\omega}
,

\delta D_{\omega}(\tau)\simeq\frac{2\pi^{2}F}{m\omega^{2}}\epsilon_{\omega}^{2},

(139)

whereas

\delta P_{\omega}(\tau)\simeq\frac{2\pi F}{\omega}\epsilon_{\omega}.

(140)

Thus the coordinate mismatch is second order in
\epsilon_{\omega}
, while the
momentum mismatch is first order.

The full overlap loss is

\Lambda_{\omega}\simeq\frac{1}{8\sigma^{2}(\tau)}\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}F}{m\omega^{2}}\epsilon_{\omega}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}\left(\frac{2\pi F}{\omega}\epsilon_{\omega}\right)^{2}.

(141)

For small frequency errors, the first term is fourth order in

\epsilon_{\omega}
and is subleading. The leading contribution is

\Lambda_{\omega}\simeq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}\left(\frac{2\pi F}{\omega}\epsilon_{\omega}\right)^{2}.

(142)

For the operating point above, this evaluates to

\Lambda_{\omega}\simeq 24.5\,\epsilon_{\omega}^{2}.

(143)

Thus, for a representative fractional stability

\epsilon_{\omega}=10^{-3},

(144)

we get

\Lambda_{\omega}=2.5\times 10^{-5}.

(145)

The condition
\Lambda_{\omega}<0.1
requires only

\epsilon_{\omega}<6.4\times 10^{-2}.

(146)

Trap-frequency stability is therefore not a limiting systematic at this point.

3.9
Static branch-dependent imperfections

Static branch-dependent imperfections, such as a fixed trap anisotropy or a
static stray potential, are different from noise. They can produce a deterministic
branch-dependent phase,

\phi_{\rm stat}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left[H_{\rm stat}(\bar{x}_{+}(t))-H_{\rm stat}(\bar{x}_{-}(t))\right]dt.

(147)

If this phase is stable from shot to shot, it does not by itself produce
dephasing:

\Lambda_{\rm inter}^{\rm stat}=0,\qquad\Lambda_{\rm intra}^{\rm stat}=0.

(148)

Instead, it is a calibration phase that can be measured or absorbed into the
definition of the interferometer phase.

A static imperfection can still matter if it prevents perfect recombination. In
that case it is counted as an endpoint overlap error,

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}\simeq\frac{\delta D_{\rm stat}^{2}(\tau)}{8\sigma^{2}(\tau)}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)\delta P_{\rm stat}^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}.

(149)

For example, a static trap anisotropy changes the deterministic harmonic
trajectory. The stable branch-dependent phase is calibratable, but the
trajectory distortion cannot be ignored. It can be reduced by tuning the
recombination sequence, while any remaining residual mismatch contributes to
the endpoint overlap loss. The relevant systematic is therefore the residual
mismatch after calibration,
\delta D_{\rm stat}(\tau)
and

\delta P_{\rm stat}(\tau)
, rather than the bare anisotropy alone.

To estimate the size of the residual effect, we model the uncorrected static
imperfection as an effective fractional frequency error

\epsilon_{\rm stat}\equiv\frac{\delta\omega_{\rm stat}}{\omega}.

(150)

This gives the same leading overlap estimate as the trap-frequency mismatch,

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}\simeq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\tau)}{2\hbar^{2}}\left(\frac{2\pi F}{\omega}\epsilon_{\rm stat}\right)^{2}.

(151)

For the representative operating point this is

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}\simeq 24.5\,\epsilon_{\rm stat}^{2}.

(152)

Thus an uncorrected residual anisotropy at the level

\epsilon_{\rm stat}=10^{-3}

(153)

would give

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-5},

(154)

well below the target budget. Even a percent-level residual gives only

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-3}.

(155)

Requiring

\Lambda_{\rm ov}^{\rm stat}<0.1

(156)

corresponds to

\epsilon_{\rm stat}<6.4\times 10^{-2}.

(157)

We therefore do not ignore static branch-dependent imperfections. Stable
anisotropy does not produce dephasing by itself, but it can produce an endpoint
overlap loss through residual imperfect recombination. The estimate above shows
that if the residual anisotropy after calibration is controlled to the percent
level, its contribution remains below the ordinary-loss budget.

3.10
Coherence of the force source

The force source must remain coherent over the duration of one shot. We model
loss of coherence of the source by a phenomenological time
T_{2}
. A
conservative estimate for the corresponding visibility loss is

\Lambda_{\rm source}\simeq\frac{\tau}{T_{2}}.

(158)

Demanding the coherence-loss budget

\Lambda_{\rm source}<0.1

(159)

therefore gives

T_{2}>10\tau.

(160)

For

\tau=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=3.14\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s},

(161)

this becomes

T_{2}>0.031\,\mathrm{s}.

(162)

Thus the force source must preserve coherence on a tens-of-milliseconds timescale. This is a demanding requirement, but it is not inconsistent with a Rydberg-based implementation. The long lifetime and the electric dipole should not be attributed to the same bare state. Bare circular Rydberg states are attractive because their large angular momentum suppresses radiative decay, but they do not by themselves provide the required permanent electric dipole. The dipole needed here would have to come from a controlled Stark admixture or polarization of neighboring Rydberg levels.

This distinction is important. Rydberg-Stark states can provide electric dipole moments far above the
10^{2}\,{\rm D}
scale required in Sec. 2.3
[
20
]
. For example, the fully polarized Rydberg-Stark dipole scale at
n\sim 50
is of order
10^{4}\,{\rm D}
, whereas the baseline estimate requires only
p\simeq 1.4\times 10^{2}\,{\rm D}
. Thus only a small admixture is needed in principle. The price is increased sensitivity to electric-field noise and technical dephasing, so the optimal admixture, lifetime, and force-noise trade-off must be treated as part of a concrete source design.

Existing circular-Rydberg experiments support the relevant lifetime scale, with millisecond lifetimes demonstrated and longer lifetimes becoming possible through circular states and blackbody-mode suppression
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. We therefore treat the coherent Rydberg-like force source as an explicit hardware requirement. The sensitivity curves in Sec. 4 assume that such a source has been realized and calibrated; they do not assume that an arbitrary dipole source automatically satisfies the requirement.

3.11
Summary of ordinary loss channels

For the conservative operating point, the quantitatively estimated ordinary
environmental and recombination losses include

\Lambda_{\rm gas}=2.95\times 10^{-4},\qquad\Lambda_{\rm bb}=2.95\times 10^{-10},

(163)

and

\Lambda_{t}=2.5\times 10^{-5},\qquad\Lambda_{\omega}=2.5\times 10^{-5}\quad(\epsilon_{\omega}=10^{-3}).

(164)

The electric-field and source-distance noise requirements are imposed separately
through Eqs. (
87
), (
99
),
(
111
), and (
125
). Slow
shot-to-shot fluctuations produce phase averaging through the intershot terms,
while finite-frequency noise can produce both intrashot phase noise and endpoint
overlap loss.

The budget is organized by physical response channel rather than by hardware
label. Stable implementation-dependent effects renormalize the calibrated force
or trajectory, while fluctuating branch-dependent effects enter through the
phase-noise formula or the endpoint-overlap formula. This organization is meant
to cover the dominant systematic classes: environmental decoherence, intershot
phase averaging, intrashot phase noise, endpoint mismatch, static calibrated
imperfections, and source decoherence. In a specific apparatus the white-noise
estimates above should be replaced by the measured technical spectra multiplied
by the corresponding trajectory filter functions, but the leading effects are
expected to map onto the channels treated here rather than requiring a new
visibility-loss mechanism.

Excluding the separately stated source-coherence requirement, and assuming that
electric-field noise and force-noise contributions are each controlled below
their assigned
0.05
sub-budgets, the explicitly evaluated baseline losses
are

\Lambda_{\rm baseline}\simeq 3.4\times 10^{-4}.

(165)

This number should not be interpreted as the full experimental loss budget. It
is the contribution from the ordinary channels evaluated explicitly above, with
the remaining technical systematics required to be kept below the target level.
For projections we therefore retain the working requirement

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1.

(166)

3.12
Experimental feasibility and dominant requirements

The feasibility question is not whether all ordinary loss channels vanish, but
whether they can be kept below the deliberately assigned budget while preserving
the reversible pointer trajectory. The estimates above indicate that no modeled
ordinary channel gives a parametric obstruction for the conservative operating
point. Residual gas dominates the explicitly evaluated baseline loss, but at
the assumed cryogenic pressure it gives only

\Lambda_{\rm gas}\simeq 3.0\times 10^{-4}
, far below the working allowance

\Lambda_{\rm loss}\lesssim 0.1
. Blackbody loss is negligible at the same
operating point.

The most important engineering requirements are the controlled technical
noises, not gas or blackbody decoherence. For a singly charged nanoparticle,
the slow residual electric field must satisfy

\sigma_{E}<2.7\times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V/m}
, and the finite-frequency residual
field noise must be at the level

S_{E}^{1/2}\lesssim 1.5\times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}/\sqrt{Hz}}
when both
phase noise and possible non-common-mode overlap mismatch are included. These
are stringent shielding, calibration, and monitoring requirements. They apply
to the residual branch-correlated field after common-mode rejection, and are
therefore best interpreted as technical control requirements rather than as
fundamental decoherence limits.

The force-source stability requirement is comparable in importance. For the
representative dipole-source geometry, the quasistatic source-distance
fluctuation must satisfy

\sigma_{h}<2.0\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
, corresponding to a relative stability
of about
1.4\times 10^{-2}
for the assumed separation. The corresponding
finite-frequency relative displacement requirement is of order

S_{h}^{1/2}\lesssim 10\,\mathrm{nm}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}
. These numbers are
demanding but not implausible for a mechanically rigid, vibration-isolated
near-field geometry; they identify the force-source assembly as one of the
central experimental subsystems.

By contrast, recombination timing and trap-frequency stability are not the
dominant limitations at the chosen point. A timing error as large as

\tau/1000
contributes only
\Lambda_{t}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-5}
, and a
fractional trap-frequency error
10^{-3}
gives a comparable loss. Static
branch-dependent imperfections are also not intrinsically fatal, provided their
residual calibrated endpoint mismatch remains at the percent level or below.
The separate source-coherence condition,
\Lambda_{\rm source}<0.1
, is a
real experimental requirement and must be satisfied by the microscopic force
source itself.

Taken together, these estimates support the internal consistency of the
proposal at the level of a parameterized feasibility study. The main
systematics have been treated at the level relevant for a visibility-loss
projection, and the hard requirements are identifiable: residual electric-field
control, force-source mechanical stability, and coherence of the force source.
A complete device design would replace the representative white-noise budgets
by measured spectra and geometry-specific transfer functions, but the estimates
above do not rely on omitting a major systematic class or on a special
cancellation between unrelated errors.

4
Numerical results

4.1
Statistical sensitivity

We now translate the CSL visibility-loss exponent into a projected exclusion
sensitivity. For a fixed value of
r_{c}
, we write the CSL exponent in the
linear form

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}(r_{c})=\lambda K(r_{c}),

(167)

where

K(r_{c})=\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}I_{DG}(r_{c}),

(168)

and

I_{DG}(r_{c})=\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-5/2}dt.

(169)

Here
D(t)
is the full branch separation, and the numerical estimates below
use the conservative three-dimensional breathing factor. The measured
visibility in one shot is reduced by the ordinary loss factor

\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm loss})
and, if CSL is present, by the additional factor

\exp(-\Lambda_{\rm CSL})
. The CSL-induced fractional visibility change is
therefore

\Delta V_{\rm CSL}=e^{-\Lambda_{\rm loss}}\left(1-e^{-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}}\right).

(170)

Requiring this signal to be resolved at
5\sigma
with
N
statistically
independent shots gives

\sqrt{N}\,e^{-\Lambda_{\rm loss}}\left(1-e^{-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}}\right)=5.

(171)

Equivalently, the number of shots required to detect or exclude a given
collapse rate is

N_{5\sigma}=\frac{25e^{2\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\left(1-e^{-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}}\right)^{2}}.

(172)

For a fixed number of shots, the corresponding minimum detectable collapse rate
is

\lambda_{\min}(r_{c})=-\frac{1}{K(r_{c})}\ln\left[1-\frac{5e^{\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\sqrt{N}}\right].

(173)

In the small-signal limit,
\Lambda_{\rm CSL}\ll 1
, this reduces to

\lambda_{\min}(r_{c})\simeq\frac{5e^{\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\sqrt{N}\,K(r_{c})}.

(174)

Unless stated otherwise, the numerical projections below use

\Lambda_{\rm loss}=0.1
as the working ordinary-loss budget.

4.2
Chosen and aggressive operating points

The chosen operating point is

m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad F=10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad\omega=2.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

with

\omega_{0}=10^{7}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad T=0.4\,\mathrm{K}.

Here
F
is the differential force between the two microscopic branches. At

r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, this gives

d_{\max}=0.25\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad D_{\max}=0.50\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad S_{\rm br}=4.82\times 10^{-2},

and

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}=1.26\times 10^{8}\lambda.

For
N=10^{5}
shots and ordinary visibility loss

\Lambda_{\rm loss}=0.1
, the small-signal reach is

\lambda_{\min}\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

The aggressive lower-frequency point is

m=10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad F=10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N},\qquad\omega=1.0\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

with the same
\omega_{0}
and
T
. At
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, this gives

d_{\max}=1.0\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad D_{\max}=2.0\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad S_{\rm br}=2.41\times 10^{-2},

and

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}=2.02\times 10^{9}\lambda.

For
N=10^{5}
shots and the same ordinary-loss estimate, the reach is

\lambda_{\min}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

This point is substantially more sensitive, but also technically more demanding
because the interaction time is longer and recombination is more sensitive to
electric-field noise, force noise, trap-frequency fluctuations, trap
anharmonicity, and residual phase-space mismatch.

4.3
Shot requirement

The CSL-induced visibility change is

\Delta V_{\rm CSL}=e^{-\Lambda_{\rm loss}}\left(1-e^{-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}}\right).

The exact shot requirement for a
5\sigma
detection is

N_{5\sigma}=\frac{25e^{2\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\left(1-e^{-\Lambda_{\rm CSL}}\right)^{2}}.

In the small-signal limit,
\Lambda_{\rm CSL}\ll 1
, this becomes

N_{5\sigma}\simeq\frac{25e^{2\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\Lambda_{\rm CSL}^{2}}.

For
\lambda=10^{-10}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, the chosen point has

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}=1.26\times 10^{-2},\qquad N_{5\sigma}\simeq 1.9\times 10^{5},

while the aggressive point has

\Lambda_{\rm CSL}=2.02\times 10^{-1},\qquad N_{5\sigma}\simeq 9.1\times 10^{2}.

4.4
Dependence on
r_{c}

For arbitrary CSL correlation length, the breathing-corrected signal coefficient
is

\frac{\Lambda_{\rm CSL}(r_{c})}{\lambda}=\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}I_{DG}(r_{c}),

where

I_{DG}(r_{c})=\int_{0}^{\tau}D^{2}(t)\left[1+\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{r_{c}^{2}}\right]^{-5/2}dt.

For
N
shots, the small-signal minimum detectable collapse rate is

\lambda_{\min}(r_{c})=\frac{5e^{\Lambda_{\rm loss}}}{\sqrt{N}}\left[\frac{m^{2}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{c}^{2}}I_{DG}(r_{c})\right]^{-1}.

At
r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}
, the chosen point gives

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm chosen}\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

while the aggressive point gives

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

At
r_{c}=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
, the chosen point gives

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm chosen}\simeq 6.8\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

while the aggressive point gives

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}\simeq 2.2\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Within the allowed operating range considered here, the lower-frequency
aggressive point gives the best sensitivity at both
r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}

and
r_{c}=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
. This is expected because, at fixed force and in
the small-separation regime, the ideal CSL signal scales approximately as

\omega^{-5}
. Lowering
\omega
therefore improves sensitivity, but also
tightens the requirements on recombination and force-noise control.

5
Comparison with existing CSL constraints

We compare the projected reach of the proposed measurement-like pointer
protocol with three different reference classes: direct interferometric tests,
enhanced-collapse-rate benchmarks, and the strongest non-interferometric bounds.
These comparisons should not be conflated, because they answer different
questions.

The closest conceptual comparison is with direct interferometric tests, where
the measured quantity is also a loss of spatial coherence or interference
visibility. Representative direct matter-wave interferometric CSL bounds near

r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, read from the KDTL comparison curve digitized in
Fig.
1
, are at approximately the

\lambda_{\rm direct}\sim 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

level. The chosen point reaches

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm chosen}(100\,\mathrm{nm})\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

while the aggressive point reaches

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}(100\,\mathrm{nm})\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Thus the chosen point improves over representative direct interferometric
bounds by a factor

\frac{10^{-6}}{1.4\times 10^{-10}}\simeq 7.1\times 10^{3},

while the aggressive point improves over them by a factor

\frac{10^{-6}}{8.7\times 10^{-12}}\simeq 1.1\times 10^{5}.

This is the cleanest comparison claim: the proposed protocol would be a much
stronger direct visibility-loss test of CSL than existing matter-wave
interferometric experiments.

The projected sensitivity also lies below the commonly quoted Adler-enhanced
CSL scale
[
6
]
near
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, often taken to be of order

\lambda_{\rm Adler}\sim 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

The chosen point is sensitive to collapse rates roughly two orders of magnitude
below this benchmark, and the aggressive point improves the reach by an
additional factor of about sixteen relative to the chosen point. In this sense,
the proposed experiment probes a significant part of the enhanced-collapse-rate
region while retaining the structure of a reversible coherence experiment.

The comparison with the strongest overall bounds near
r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}

is different. The original GRW value
[
1
,
5
]
is approximately

\lambda_{\rm GRW}\sim 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

and the strongest non-interferometric constraints near this correlation length
lie well below the projected reach of the present proposal
[
10
,
15
]
.
The present proposal does not beat those constraints at

r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
. Its main value at this correlation
length is instead that it substantially strengthens the direct interferometric
comparison class.

The situation becomes more interesting at larger correlation lengths. At

r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}
, the chosen and aggressive sensitivities are

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm chosen}\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Radiation bounds dominate the strongest low-
r_{c}
constraints up to roughly

r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}

[
10
]
. Using the quoted

\lambda/r_{c}^{2}
dependence and the bound

\lambda<5.2\times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
at

r_{c}=10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
gives

\lambda_{\rm rad}(10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq 5.2\times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Thus the aggressive point is close to, but slightly weaker than, this radiation
scale, while the chosen point is weaker by about an order of magnitude or more.
We therefore should not claim a decisive improvement over the strongest overall
bound at
r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}
, but the aggressive point is close enough
to make this comparison important.

At
r_{c}=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
, the projected sensitivities are

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm chosen}\simeq 6.8\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}\simeq 2.2\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

In this regime mechanical and force-noise constraints become especially
relevant
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Digitizing
the force-noise curves shown in Fig. 4 of Ref.
[
14
]
, which are
not included in Fig.
1
for visual clarity, gives a
present multilayer-cantilever bound of order

\lambda_{\rm Vinante}(10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq 1.1\times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},

and a LISA Pathfinder force-noise bound of order

\lambda_{\rm LISA}(10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq 6\times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

The aggressive point is therefore weaker than these large-
r_{c}
force-noise
bounds by factors of roughly
2\times 10^{3}
–
4\times 10^{3}
. This comparison
removes the ambiguity left by a single benchmark marker: the present parameter
set does not beat the strongest large-
r_{c}
mechanical bounds.

It is not automatically advantageous to move to still larger correlation
lengths. Once
r_{c}
is large compared with both the thermal width and the
branch separation, the breathing factor is nearly saturated and the signal
coefficient scales approximately as
1/r_{c}^{2}
. Equivalently, the minimum
detectable collapse rate worsens approximately as
r_{c}^{2}
. For the aggressive
point this gives

\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}(2\times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq 8.5\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\qquad\lambda_{\min}^{\rm aggr}(10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m})\simeq 2.1\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Thus
r_{c}\simeq 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
is the most natural large-
r_{c}

comparison point in the present parameter set. Going above it does not improve
the comparison over the digitized force-noise curves, because the projected
sensitivity worsens faster than the relevant large-
r_{c}
bounds weaken over
the digitized range.

The conservative summary is therefore as follows. Near

r_{c}=100\,\mathrm{nm}
, the proposal does not beat the strongest overall CSL
constraints, but it strongly improves over direct interferometric tests and
reaches well below the Adler-enhanced benchmark. Near

r_{c}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}
, the aggressive
\omega=1000\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

point approaches, but does not beat, the radiation-bound scale used in
Fig.
1
. Near

r_{c}=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}
, the aggressive point remains above the digitized
large-
r_{c}
force-noise bounds by a few thousand, so the main defensible
advantage of the proposal is its improvement over direct interferometric
visibility-loss tests rather than over the strongest non-interferometric bounds.
