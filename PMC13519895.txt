Communication of incidental findings in the ED: Patient perspectives
Authors: Alessia F. Mollo, Danielle M. McCarthy, Kenzie A. Cameron, Miriam Rafferty, Siyuan Dong, Jungwha Lee, Lisa B. VanWagner, Anuva Fellner, Amy V. Kontrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Incidental findings are commonly identified on emergency department (ED) imaging, often unrelated to the reason for encounter. While system-level interventions have aimed to improve notification and follow-up, less is known about patients’ experiences of receiving these unexpected results. We sought to explore ED patients’ perspectives on incidental finding notification.Methods:A qualitative analysis was conducted of semi-structured interviews of 16 adult ED patients undergoing abdominal CT or ultrasound imaging at a large academic ED. Interviews were originally collected for a larger implementation trial of an electronic health record-based incidental finding notification system. Interviews explored patient preferences for communication of incidental findings including preferred communicator and desired information. The analytic team employed inductive thematic analysis, using iterative coding and team reconciliation to ultimately develop themes. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample.Results:Forty-four percent of the 16 participants were aged 35–44 (25% were aged 45–54 years; 31% were 55 years or older); 62.5% female (Table 1). Only 25% of participants reported awareness of the concept of incidental findings prior to the interview. Four themes emerged: (1) Empathetic expert communication is preferred; (2) Incidental findings trigger diverse health, emotional, and financial concerns; (3) Details of both the finding and cause are valued; and (4) Information and support are desired for next steps.Conclusions:Patients view incidental findings as unexpected and often stressful information requiring clear, empathetic, and actionable communication. Incorporating these preferences into ED notification processes may improve understanding, reduce distress, and support timely follow-up.Practice implications:Standardizing incidental finding disclosure to include plain language explanations of the finding, its cause, and detailed next steps may support ED patients as they navigate future care and the myriad concerns that arise from an unexpected result. Achieving such standardization will likely require both communication training and electronic health record integration. In ED practice, this should include a brief verbal disclosure paired with EHR-supported written instructions that identify the finding, explain urgency, name the appropriate follow-up clinician, and specify patient and clinician next steps.

Introduction

Background

Medical imaging is often ordered in the emergency department (ED) to seek evidence of dangerous or emergent conditions. However, imaging frequently uncovers incidental findings, or unexpected abnormalities unrelated to the primary reason for testing [1]. Nearly one in three ED patients may have an incidental finding with prevalence estimated at 31% of ED imaging studies [2]. While some findings are immediately actionable, others may be less urgent yet still clinically relevant [2,3].

Prior research has examined how incidental findings are documented in the electronic health record or followed up clinically, yet far less is known about how these findings are communicated, patient preferences, or the patient experience of an unexpected result [2–8]. Increasingly, institutions are implementing system-level interventions to improve the communication of incidental findings including “lung navigators” who contacted patients directly about unexpected lung nodules or post-ED follow-up alerts and case manager outreach [6,9]. Although these initiatives reveal the potential to enhance patient safety and continuity of care, limited attention has been given to how incidental findings are communicated to patients and how patients understand and experience these conversations.

Importance

Without clear communication, incidental findings may lead to patient confusion, anxiety, or uncertainty about next steps. Moreover, a lack of notification may leave patients unaware that a finding exists and therefore unable to take the appropriate next steps in follow-up care [4–7]. One cross-sectional survey study found that when radiology reports used ambiguous or overly technical language, patients expressed greater concern than their providers regarding incidental findings [5]. Additionally, prior studies have acknowledged this gap in the literature, noting “more investigation is required to assess patient preferences and how patients cope with uncertainty around the significance of incidental findings from imaging” [8].

Although research is needed on patient preferences for incidental finding communication across healthcare settings, one environment in which communication is particularly challenging is the ED. The ED has both very high volumes of imaging, with CT utilization more than doubling in the last decade, as well as documented low rates of incidental finding communication [4,5,7]. Communication challenges in the ED setting are numerous and include both environmental and situational constraints (time, noise levels, severity of illness, emotional burden, shift work) and population specific factors (language barriers, low health literacy) [5,10]. Understanding patient preferences is particularly important due to both the frequency of incidental findings and the well-documented challenges to communication in the ED setting.

Goals of this investigation

As part of the development and implementation of an emergency department-based incidental finding notification system, we sought a deeper understanding of patient preferences for incidental finding notification in the ED setting through qualitative interviews.

Materials & methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study sought to obtain patient perspectives using semi-structured interviews conducted in the Northwestern Memorial Hospital Emergency Department (NMH ED), an urban academic ED with > 100,000 visits each year. These interviews were originally collected as part of a larger implementation study of an EHR-based intervention to improve notification of incidentally noted hepatic steatosis on ED imaging. In the parent study, interviews were first analyzed deductively using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to identify barriers and facilitators to implementation and inform the development of an implementation research logic model (manuscript forthcoming). For the present secondary analysis, the interviews were reanalyzed using an inductive approach to qualitative analysis to specifically explore patients’ experiences and preferences regarding incidental findings communication and follow-up.

Selection of participants

Participants were a purposive sample of patients age ≥ 18 who had either CT imaging of the abdomen or ultrasound examinations of the right upper quadrant, liver, or gallbladder ordered in the NMH ED. Purposive sampling was used because the parent study targeted a specific population most relevant to the incidental finding notification intervention—adults undergoing abdominal CT or hepatobiliary ultrasound in the ED—rather than attempting to represent all ED patients or all incidental findings across care settings. The interviews were conducted at time of convenience within the ED workflow and not specifically scheduled before or after CT results were available. Therefore, these patients represent a general ED population undergoing imaging, not a group who themselves had incidental findings. Patients were excluded if cognitive impairment, dementia, acute psychiatric illness, or clinical instability limited their ability to participate in the interview, as determined by their treating physician, or if they were non-English speaking. Participants were informed of the purpose of the study and study process, and that their participation was voluntary and confidential; all participants provided written, informed consent prior to enrollment. Patient interviews were conducted in private ED exam rooms between 1/16/2024–5/2/2024, and participants received a $40 gift card for completion of the interview.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted and audio-recorded in person in ED examination rooms by three trained research coordinators (two male, one female). Interviewers had no prior relationships with participants and had both previous qualitative interview experience and study-specific training in semi-structured interviewing. Interviews followed a semi-structured interview guide developed by a multi-disciplinary research team including emergency physicians, hepatologists, implementation scientists, and experts in qualitative methodology and risk communication. The interview guide was pilot tested with non-study team professional and personal contacts of the study team. The semi-structured interview guide was comprised of six questions with follow-up prompts. Three questions were developed to first ask about patient preferences for notification of incidental findings broadly (preferences about preferred communicator, informational needs about finding and about next steps). Those three questions were then repeated for the specific finding of hepatic steatosis.

Non-interview participant family were allowed to be present at the participants discretion but asked not to participate, and no observer data were collected or analyzed. Following completion of the interview, participants also completed a brief demographic survey and questions about their awareness of the concept of incidental findings. Digital audio recordings of interviews were transcribed verbatim and de-identified to ensure participant confidentiality. All study procedures were approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (IRB) and are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [11].

Analysis

The transcriptions were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis [12,13,15]. The transcripts were managed using Dedoose (Los Angeles, CA). The analytic team consisted of two trained coders (AVK—emergency physician and AFM—medical student), and a third study team member (DMM—emergency physician) with expertise in qualitative methodology who oversaw the coding process and helped resolve differences. The analytic team used an inductive thematic approach, first familiarizing themselves with the data, before generating preliminary codes on a subsample (20%) of transcripts. The team subsequently met to discuss and develop an initial coding framework. The coding framework was refined iteratively based on ongoing analysis and team discussions; modifications were made to the framework as appropriate. The team continued independent coding and group reconciliation until all transcripts were coded. Reflexive discussions were held throughout to identify and minimize the influence of researchers’ clinical assumptions and perspectives and increase the credibility of the process. The team also maintained an audit trail documenting code development and iterative changes, along with meeting memos summarizing key discussions and rationale for revisions to increase the dependability of the analysis [14]. Trustworthiness was further supported through analyst triangulation across coders with different clinical and qualitative training backgrounds, allowing findings to be examined from multiple perspectives.

Once coding was complete, the finalized codes and associated excerpts were reviewed by the full analytic team (AFM, AVK, DMM) to identify patterns and relationships across the data and search for themes. Codes were visually mapped to illustrate how codes clustered and related to one another. Themes were refined and validated through ongoing comparison across transcripts and returning to the data to ensure consistency and grounding in participant perspectives. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate each theme. As noted, these interviews were collected originally for CFIR analysis, with the endpoint for data collection being based on data sufficiency for CFIR. Therefore, the endpoint for data analysis of this retrospective secondary analysis was not data saturation, as it was not possible to continue to conduct interviews while contemporaneously performing analysis; however, the point of analysis at which no new codes emerged was tracked and reported. Survey data comprising the patients’ demographic information was additionally summarized using descriptive statistics (e.g. counts, means).

Results

Characteristics of study subjects

Twenty-five patients were screened and approached in person for eligibility for the study. Two patients were ineligible (unable to complete interview due to clinical condition), and seven declined to participate for the following reasons: two “not interested,” four “too tired,” and one “too much pain.” Sixteen patients were enrolled and completed interviews. Forty-four percent of participants were aged 35–44; the sample was 62.5% female, 50.0% identified as White/Caucasian, and 31.3% identified as Black. Of the study participants, only 25% had previously heard of the concept of incidental findings prior to the interview (Table 1). The mean duration of the interviews was 14 min.

Below, we provide detailed definitions of the themes and sample quotations from the patients. Representative quotes for each theme are presented in Table 2.

Empathetic expert communication is preferred

Patients preferred incidental findings to be relayed by a provider who balances both clinical knowledge and empathic, patient-centered communication. Many participants favored disclosure from a physician because they felt physicians were best able to explain the finding, discuss its implications, and answer follow-up questions. When asked directly which ED provider they would prefer to disclose an incidental finding, most participants (10 of 16) indicated a preference for a physician, responding with phrases such as, “my physician,” (ID#116, Female), “the attending” (ID#123, Female), or “the ER doctor” (ID#120, Male). At the same time, patients conveyed that expertise alone was not sufficient. They valued communication that was clear, relatable, and attuned to their emotional state. Participants noted that rapport and familiarity could support effective communication, regardless of clinician role (Table 2). Because of this desire for rapport, patients viewed both physicians and non-physician clinicians as acceptable communicators of incidental findings when they demonstrated clarity, empathy, and continuity, especially in the context of ED shift changes or when the clinician had already interacted with the patient earlier in the visit. Together, these findings suggest that patients did not view disclosure as a purely informational task; rather, they expected the communicator to pair expertise with relational skills that made the finding understandable and emotionally manageable.

Incidental findings trigger diverse health, emotional, and financial concerns

Participants described both practical and emotional responses to incidental finding notification. Feelings of anxiety, confusion, or concern about the meaning and the implications of the finding were shared (Table 2). Patients expressed fear of the potential financial burden associated with follow-up care or treatment. Others mentioned feelings of distress or overwhelm as a result of their physical or psychological state at the time of receiving the news: “You know…a lot of information is given to me, but at the same time I’m either still suffering from what I came in for or just not in the best state to, like, receive information, especially if it’s more serious” (ID#104, Female). These responses indicate that incidental findings may introduce a new layer of uncertainty during an already stressful ED encounter, making timing, framing, and reassurance important parts of disclosure.

Details of both the finding and cause are valued

Participants requested thorough explanations of incidental findings. Particularly, they expressed a need for basic definitions and translation of medical terminology into layperson-terms, information about the likely cause of the disease, and an understanding of the typical course of the disease—severity, treatability, and impact on quality of life. For example, one patient remarked, “I’d want them to first explain what it means, what it is… and here’s why I’m telling you” (ID#117, Male). Another patient asked questions such as, “Is it fatal? Like is it treatable? Is it reversible? Like what all does that mean right? Because I wouldn’t have a clue what that means. But like, does that impact my day-to-day life? Does it shorten my lifespan?” (ID#122, Female). Patients also mentioned taking steps to research the finding themselves to better understand their health condition, especially when they felt the initial information provided was insufficient or unclear (Table 2). All in all, patients sought explanations that moved beyond naming the finding; they wanted clinicians to translate the result into practical meaning for prognosis, daily life, and future decision-making.

Information and support are desired for next steps

There was consistent emphasis on receiving actionable guidance after an incidental finding (Table 2). Participants wanted clear answers to questions such as what to do next, which provider to see, how urgent the follow-up was, and how to navigate care. Reflecting this desire for actionable next steps, one participant explained: “Who do you recommend? When do I go? What do I say?… Because it’s a surprise for me too. So again, it’s a trust factor now… you have more information than I do. So share your information. Show me what to do next” (ID#123, Female). Participants also valued tangible tools, such as urgency scales, printed after-visit summaries, and patient portal documentation, which they could reference later or use to help them act on recommendations or to share information with other providers. They also expressed a desire for better coordination and communication between clinicians and across healthcare settings to avoid gaps in care. For example, one patient noted a preference for care coordination, stating: “They can tell me and then forward the information to my primary so that any further treatment or procedures can go forward and then both of them can be involved in or at least aware of the next stage that has to happen” (ID#116, Female). These accounts suggest that patients define effective notification by whether it helps them act after leaving the ED, not simply by whether the finding was mentioned.

Discussion and Conclusion

Discussion

In this qualitative analysis of ED patients’ perspectives on incidental finding notification, we identified four themes that highlight key components of effective clinician-patient communication about incidental findings. To our knowledge, patient preferences on incidental finding notification as well as the impact of disclosure have not yet been explored in depth. While prior system-level advances have improved documentation, notification, and follow-up, our findings indicate successful notification systems must be designed with patient priorities and lived experiences in mind [2–9].

Across interviews, participants indicated a preference for receiving results from a knowledgeable physician, reflecting the physician’s perceived authority and credibility. However, expertise alone was not sufficient; patients desired rapport and empathy as well as acknowledgement of the potentially distressing effect that an unanticipated finding may have on them as individuals. Although not typically classified as “bad news,” in many ways the disclosure of a new incidental finding has parallels to the disclosure of more traditional “bad news” (e. g., death notification). Although emergency physicians deliver bad news often, the communication of such news often has an extra layer of complexity in the ED compared to other settings due to the acute, episodic nature of care wherein there is no preexisting doctor-patient relationship. As protocols for incidental finding communication are developed, there may be relevant lessons from existing communication tools developed for provision of bad news (e.g. SPIKES protocol or the VitalTalk pedagogy) that support efficient and effective empathetic communication [18,19].

The importance of this patient-centered communication with a focus on empathy and rapport should not be underestimated as there is a growing body of literature demonstrating the link between empathy and outcomes, including the influence of empathy on ED experience [20,21]. In this context, empathetic communication of the finding may influence patient adherence to follow-up recommendations. In fact, prior literature suggests that poorly timed or ambiguous disclosure of incidental findings can exacerbate distress and impair decision-making [3,5,16,17].

Cutting across many of the themes was an undercurrent of the added complexity of processing information in the acute ED setting, where acute stress and illness make it difficult for patients to process news of an additional, unexpected finding. This processing challenge influences all communication in the ED, not only the communication of incidental findings [10]. Best-practices for ED discharge communication recognize the difficulty the environment poses to patients and emphasize the importance of providing supplemental discharge paperwork—a recommendation that is also shared by a recent white paper on incidental finding notification [4,22]. In our sample, patients described the value of written documentation and digital supports (e.g., after-visit summaries, patient portal messages). When thinking about the design of such supports, it is important that any discharge paperwork be designed with health literacy best practices in mind, particularly given evidence that ambiguous radiology language can heighten patient anxiety, whereas detailed explanations, framed in lay language that avoids unnecessary alarm are preferred [5,16,17].

Within this discharge paperwork, as well as in conversation, patients wanted details of actionable next steps, clarity on the urgency of the finding, and reassurance that follow-up recommendations would be acted upon across care transitions. Whereas building automated systems to ensure that an incidental finding is documented in patient facing paperwork may ensure that “notification” occurred and shield health systems from legal risk, we feel strongly that developing written supports cannot be decoupled from the important conversation accompanying it. The multimodal approach not only helps support comprehension but also allows for the addition of nuance and opportunity to ask questions. Our data suggest that patients, who had many concerns including issues of cost, social support, and the real-world logistics of accessing care, want an opportunity to ask these questions. It is important to acknowledge that there are many types of incidental findings that range from minor “watch and wait” findings that require minimal future evaluation to more serious findings which require timely, multidisciplinary follow-up. The intensity of communication for some findings (e.g. logistical / emotional support) may appropriately vary based on the urgency and potential morbidity of the finding. The conversation accompanying written incidental finding notification is an opportunity to address these nuances and to meet patients’ information needs which are not easily met through a prefabricated discharge handout.

The operational frameworks and system level strategies described in prior literature, which have been developed for managing a large volume of incidental findings through electronic systems, are necessary; however, this study takes an early look at patient perspectives which have been underexplored. Our findings suggest that patients have distinct preferences about notification and myriad concerns about navigating the next steps in care. This information is timely as many nascent notifications systems remain in evolution and have an opportunity to incorporate these perspectives. Importantly, communication preferences and follow-up barriers may also vary across patient groups and may be shaped by cultural context, prior experiences with healthcare, language, insurance status, and access to primary or specialty care. Future work should intentionally include more linguistically and culturally diverse ED populations to determine how these factors influence incidental finding communication preferences and follow-up needs.

This study was conducted at a single large academic ED, which may limit the transferability of findings to other settings such as community hospitals, rural facilities, or health systems with different resources and patient demographics. Specifically, most participants were insured with mid-range income, higher educational attainment and in good health; these characteristics may limit generalizability to the broader ED population. The sample size was modest (n = 16), which is appropriate for qualitative inquiry but may not capture the full spectrum of patient perspectives. Only English-speaking patients were included, potentially excluding populations with limited English proficiency who may face distinct and disproportionate challenges in incidental finding communication. Because interviews occurred in active ED treatment areas, environmental factors such as noise, interruptions, or patient discomfort may have influenced participants’ ability to fully engage in the interview.

It is important to note that the interview data were originally collected to be used in concert with data collected from clinicians in preparation for a trial. Secondary qualitative analysis may suffer from a lack of fit between the primary objectives and current research question as well as mismatch in population of interest, inability to ask follow-up questions, or distance (both in time and familiarity) between analysis team and original data collection [23]. Although this analysis is a secondary qualitative analysis, we believe that these data are suitable for the current research objectives because of the broad and exploratory nature of the interview questions asked, the timely analysis, and the proximity of the research team. Limitations that persist include the more narrow focus on ED patients with abdominal complaints that led to abdominal imaging, rather than a more diverse population of patients undergoing imaging. This imaging population was selected because the parent implementation study focused on incidental hepatic steatosis identified on abdominal imaging performed in the ED. Perspectives on incidental finding notification may differ for patients whose index ED visit involves more serious or life-threatening conditions, where the emotional and cognitive burden of the acute illness could further shape how incidental results are received. Finally, while our findings highlight common themes, they should not be interpreted as prescriptive recommendations for all patients; rather, they show the importance of tailoring communication strategies to individual needs and contexts.

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that patient-centered incidental finding notification requires more than accurate reporting. It demands integration of expertise with empathy, clarity across digital and in-person platforms, and systems that not only convey information but also support patients in navigating the next steps of their care. Future research should evaluate how patient-informed communication protocols and electronic health record supports affect patient understanding, distress, and engagement with recommended follow-up, in addition to follow-up completion.

Practice Implications

Emergency departments may improve incidental finding communication by adopting standardized disclosure practices that include brief, plain language explanations of the finding and explicit guidance on recommended follow-up. Providing written or electronic materials at discharge that outline next steps and urgency may help patients navigate care after the ED visit, particularly when acute illness or stress limits information processing. Given the many competing cognitive demands on emergency clinicians, for communication of incidental findings to be successful, we believe it should be supported through integration into the electronic health record to nudge the clinician to counsel, automate inclusion of plain language materials, and advance the next steps in care. However, for such systems to be successful, they will require clinician communication training and involvement in the development of electronic health record supports that can integrate into the complex ED workflows. Operationally, ED incidental finding protocols could include a brief scripted disclosure framework, automated patient-facing after-visit summary language, and EHR prompts that specify follow-up urgency, recommended clinician or clinic type, and responsibility for care coordination.
