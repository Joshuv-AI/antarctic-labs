Different Center of Rotation Trajectories after Cervical Hybrid Surgery: A Minimum 7-Year Retrospective Comparison of Bryan and Mobi-C Prostheses
Authors: Yang Wang, Daiyuan Zhang, Ziye Qiu, Guozheng Jiang, Yukun Ma, Jiaqi Zhang, Yang Xiong, Xing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective Study.ObjectivesTo compare long-term adjacent-segment kinematic changes and radiographic adjacent segment degeneration (rASD) after two-level cervical hybrid surgery using Bryan or Mobi-C prostheses.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 78 patients who underwent contiguous two-level anterior cervical hybrid surgery between 2010 and 2018, including 38 treated with Bryan and 40 with Mobi-C prostheses. All patients had more than 7 years of follow-up. Flexion-extension center of rotation (FE-COR) trajectories at the superior adjacent segment were measured on dynamic radiographs. Long-term rASD was evaluated using the Walraevens score.ResultsClinical outcomes improved significantly in both groups (P < 0.001), with no significant between-group differences. Segmental range of motion remained stable and comparable between groups (P > 0.05). However, FE-COR trajectories differed. At 1 year, the Mobi-C group showed anterior-inferior FE-COR displacement, with FE-COR_Y decreasing to 44.92% ± 12.55%, whereas the Bryan group maintained a relatively stable FE-COR_Y of 59.01% ± 10.66% (P < 0.001). At final follow-up, rASD occurred in 12.5% of Mobi-C patients and 23.7% of Bryan patients, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.198).ConclusionConventional ROM measurements alone cannot fully capture segmental biomechanics. Early anterior-inferior FE-COR displacement in the Mobi-C group was observed and was associated with a numerically lower, but statistically non-significant, rASD rate. These findings suggest that FE-COR may be a sensitive radiographic parameter for describing postoperative micro-kinematic patterns, but its causal relationship with long-term adjacent segment degeneration requires further biomechanical and MRI-based validation.Keywords: cervical hybrid surgery, adjacent segment degeneration, flexion-extension center of rotation, kinematics, artificial disc replacement

1. Introduction

Cervical hybrid surgery (HS) combines anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) with artificial cervical disc replacement (ACDR) to treat multilevel degenerative disc disease, aiming to preserve kinematics and maintain stability. Although HS achieves excellent clinical outcomes comparable to traditional multilevel ACDF,1-3 its long-term superiority in preventing adjacent segment disease (ASD) remains controversial.4-8 ASD is a primary source of long-term postoperative dysfunction, traditionally attributed to increased biomechanical stress from fused segments. However, relying solely on macroscopic kinematic changes to predict ASD remains inadequate, as its development is compounded by multifactorial etiologies. 9

Predicting early ASD risks is highly challenging due to a significant temporal lag: structural radiographic degeneration (rASD) often precedes clinical symptoms by years, frequently occurring without overt clinical manifestations.9-11 Furthermore, traditional macroscopic parameters, such as range of motion (ROM), possess severe blind spots. Segmental ROM often remains deceptively stable even when internal motion patterns have altered or rASD has inevitably developed.12-14 Consequently, relying solely on ROM fails to reflect true biomechanical burdens, as occult micro-kinematic abnormalities can alter the segmental motion pattern long before macroscopic ROM changes become apparent. 15

In contrast, the flexion-extension center of rotation (FE-COR) accurately quantifies the coupling of rotation and translation within the functional spinal unit (FSU). Abnormal FE-COR shifts signify altered motion patterns and abnormal shear stress distribution, making it a highly sensitive micro-kinematic indicator for early functional abnormalities.16,17 Within the unique biomechanical environment of HS, the rigid lower fusion segment significantly alters stress transmission pathways. 18 However, longitudinal analyses correlating the impact of different prosthetic constraint designs on these early FE-COR shifts with long-term ASD remain remarkably scarce. 14

Building upon our preliminary research in cervical micro-kinematics, we evaluated a retrospective cohort of 78 patients with a minimum 7-year radiological follow-up. We therefore asked: (1) Do Bryan and Mobi-C prostheses differ in the postoperative FE-COR trajectory of the superior adjacent segment after cervical hybrid surgery? (2) Is early FE-COR displacement associated with long-term radiographic adjacent segment degeneration?

By answering these questions, this study aims to bridge early micro-kinematic evolution with long-term structural degeneration, providing essential biomechanical insights for future prosthesis design.

2. Information and Methodology

2.1. General Information

This retrospective single-center study reviewed patient records from [Blinded for Review]. We identified 78 patients treated with two-level anterior cervical hybrid surgery between January 2010 and December 2018. Of these, 38 received the Bryan prosthesis and 40 received the Mobi-C. During this timeframe, the choice of prosthesis type was not driven by patient-specific clinical, anatomical, or pathological indications. Instead, the allocation was primarily determined by contemporary implant availability and the surgeon’s routine practice at the time of the procedure. There was no predefined radiographic or clinical algorithm for assigning patients to either prosthesis. In particular, prosthesis selection was not based on preoperative adjacent-level degeneration, sagittal alignment, baseline ROM, or FE-COR characteristics. This practical allocation process suggested that prosthesis choice was not intentionally determined by baseline kinematic or degenerative status.

Patients had to meet specific criteria for inclusion. First, they required a contiguous two-level hybrid procedure: an artificial disc at the upper level and a fusion cage at the lower level. Second, we required at least 7 years (84 months) of clinical and radiographic follow-up. Finally, their medical records needed complete, readable neutral and dynamic X-rays across all evaluation points.

We also applied strict exclusion criteria to rule out pre-existing conditions that could mimic or accelerate adjacent segment disease (ASD). We excluded anyone with severe preoperative degeneration, developmental stenosis, or instability at the designated superior adjacent level. Previous cervical spine surgeries disqualified a patient. We also ruled out individuals with severe osteoporosis or metabolic bone disorders, given their known impact on spinal kinematics. The Medical Ethics Committee approved the study, and all patients provided written informed consent.

2.2. Assessment of Clinical Efficacy

The surgical team used a standard right-sided anterior cervical approach for all cases. After achieving adequate decompression, they placed a suitably sized interbody cage to stabilize the lower segment. Next, under lateral fluoroscopy, they implanted either the Bryan or Mobi-C artificial disc (Figure 1) into the superior space. Patients wore a cervical collar for 4 weeks postoperatively.

Photographs of the two artificial cervical disc prostheses utilized in this study. (A) The Bryan cervical disc, characterized by its elastomeric design with inherent translational resistance. (B) The Mobi-C cervical disc, featuring a mobile-core mechanism that permits translational sliding.

We tracked patient recovery using several standard metrics. The modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score and the Neck Disability Index (NDI) measured functional improvement. For pain relief, we recorded Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for both the neck and arms. The Odom criteria provided a measure of overall clinical success at the 7-year final follow-up.

2.3. Imaging Assessment

Two independent orthopedic surgeons evaluated the radiographs. They remained blinded to the patients’ clinical data. Using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise) and ImageJ (NIH), they measured standardized lateral and dynamic flexion-extension X-rays at three time points: preoperatively, 1-year postoperatively, and at the final follow-up. They repeated all measurements three times to ensure accuracy.

Normal cervical flexion and extension involve a complex mix of angular rotation and horizontal sliding. 19 Because of this, looking only at the macroscopic range of motion (ROM) often misses subtle functional changes in the disc. To address this blind spot, we tracked the spatial trajectory of the flexion-extension center of rotation (FE-COR). We used this specific metric to evaluate the micro-kinematic health of the cranial adjacent segment.

Using the image registration tool in Mimics 21.0, we superimposed the dynamic X-rays. The lower vertebra of the adjacent segment served as our fixed reference. We then pinpointed the FE-COR using the mid-plumb line method (Figure 2). We set up a Cartesian coordinate system, placing the origin at the posterior-inferior corner of the reference vertebra, with the X-axis extending anteriorly along the inferior endplate and the Y-axis extending superiorly perpendicular to the inferior endplate. Under this coordinate orientation, an increase in the FE-COR_X value mathematically denotes an anterior (forward) translation, while a decrease in the FE-COR_Y value represents a downward (inferior) displacement. To grade radiographic degeneration at the adjacent segment, we applied the Walraevens scoring system. 20 This system evaluates three distinct factors-disc height loss, osteophyte formation, and endplate changes for a maximum score of 9. A baseline score of 1 indicated pre-existing mild degeneration.

We defined rASD progression as any increase of 1 point or more in the total Walraevens score from baseline to final follow-up. This threshold is standard practice for detecting early degenerative shifts in categorical scoring systems,11,20 particularly since no universal cutoff exists for this specific scale.

2.4. Statistical Analysis

We analyzed the data using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp.). We report continuous variables as the mean ± standard deviation. To compare data within the same group over time, we used either the paired t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on data normality. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests for normally distributed variables or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. We assessed differences in rASD rates with the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) confirmed the reliability of our radiographic measurements, with values above 0.75 considered excellent. We set the threshold for statistical significance at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). To further adjust for potential confounders, additional multivariable linear regression analyses were performed. These models were used to evaluate whether prosthesis type was independently associated with postoperative FE-COR_X and FE-COR_Y values after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, arthroplasty level, the corresponding baseline FE-COR value, baseline Walraevens score, and follow-up duration. The results were reported as adjusted β coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

3. Results

3.1. Baseline Characteristics and Follow-Up

We included 78 patients in this study. All met the inclusion criteria for contiguous two-level anterior cervical hybrid surgery. The Bryan group consisted of 38 patients, while the Mobi-C group had 40. Every patient completed the surgery and the full follow-up process. Follow-up times averaged 86.5 ± 5.1 months for the Bryan group and 85.7 ± 5.6 months for the Mobi-C group (P = 0.512). Both groups easily met our 7-year (84-month) minimum requirement. The two groups were highly comparable at baseline. We found no significant differences in age, gender, body mass index (BMI), surgical segment distribution, preoperative clinical scores, or baseline radiological measurements (P > 0.05, Table 1). Furthermore, the reliability for radiographic measurements was excellent, with ICC values ranging from 0.88 to 0.91 for ROM, FE-COR_X, and FE-COR_Y.

3.2. Overall Clinical Outcomes

Neither group experienced severe complications related to the prostheses or fusion cages. Clinically, the patients did very well. By the final follow-up, both groups showed significant improvements in mJOA scores, NDI, and VAS scores for neck and arm pain compared to their baseline values (P < 0.001). When we compared the Bryan and Mobi-C groups directly, their clinical scores were remarkably similar at both the 1-year mark and the 7-year final follow-up (P > 0.05). According to the Odom criteria, over 85% of patients in both groups achieved excellent or good results. This confirms that hybrid surgery using either implant type provides satisfactory and comparable long-term clinical relief (Table 2).

By 1 year postoperatively, the vertical FE-COR coordinate (FE-COR_Y) for the Mobi-C group dropped noticeably to 44.92% ± 12.55%. It rebounded slightly to 49.05% ± 14.35% by the final follow-up. The Bryan group behaved differently. Its FE-COR_Y stayed relatively stable at 59.01% ± 10.66% at 1 year and 60.34% ± 8.49% at the end of the study. The statistical difference between the groups demonstrated high significance (P < 0.001 at 1 year; P = 0.001 at final follow-up), confirming that the Mobi-C implants allowed a much larger early downward shift compared to the relatively rigid Bryan discs.

Horizontal displacement (FE-COR_X) followed a similarly divergent and statistically significant pattern. For the Mobi-C group, FE-COR_X drifted forward. It rose from a preoperative 52.03% ± 11.71% to 57.47% ± 13.29% at 1 year, reaching 57.96% ± 11.38% at the final follow-up. The Bryan Group’s FE-COR_X barely fluctuated, hovering tightly between 50.47% and 51.56% over the entire 7 years. Intergroup comparisons revealed statistically significant differences in this horizontal drift both at 1 year (P = 0.024) and at the final follow-up (P = 0.011).

3.4. Comparison of Long-Term Radiographic ASD Incidence

We assessed radiographic ASD (rASD) at the final follow-up using the Walraevens criteria (Table 3). At the final follow-up, rASD was observed in 9 patients (23.7%) in the Bryan group and 5 patients (12.5%) in the Mobi-C group. The rASD rate was numerically lower in the Mobi-C group than in the Bryan group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (χ2 = 1.655, P = 0.198). Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as an observational trend rather than evidence of a prosthesis-related protective effect. No conclusion regarding the superiority of either prosthesis can be drawn from the rASD comparison alone.

3.5. Multivariable Analysis

Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to further adjust for potential confounders (Table 4). After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, arthroplasty level, baseline FE-COR value, baseline Walraevens score, and follow-up duration, prosthesis type remained significantly associated with postoperative FE-COR displacement. Compared with the Bryan group, the Mobi-C group showed a significantly higher 1-year FE-COR_X value (adjusted β = 6.94, 95% CI: 1.59 to 12.29, P = 0.012) and a significantly lower 1-year FE-COR_Y value (adjusted β = -14.32, 95% CI: -19.76 to -8.87, P < 0.001). Similar differences remained significant at the final follow-up for both FE-COR_X (adjusted β = 7.04, 95% CI: 2.55 to 11.53, P = 0.003) and FE-COR_Y (adjusted β = -11.65, 95% CI: -17.04 to -6.25, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that the observed FE-COR trajectory differences were not fully explained by baseline demographic, surgical, or radiographic variables.

4. Discussion

The minimum 7-year follow-up of our cohort showed that both the elastomeric Bryan and mobile-core Mobi-C prostheses yielded highly satisfactory and comparable clinical outcomes in hybrid constructs. Both implants also successfully maintained a normal range of motion (ROM) at the adjacent segment. 21 However, although gross ROM was preserved in both groups, FE-COR analysis revealed distinct micro-kinematic trajectories between the two prosthesis designs. Specifically, the Mobi-C implants tended to allow an early anterior-inferior shift of the FE-COR. This displacement was associated with a numerically lower incidence of long-term rASD, but the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as an observational trend rather than evidence of a protective effect or prosthesis superiority. Meanwhile, the Bryan discs maintained a much more rigid center of rotation, which was accompanied by a numerically higher rASD rate.

Prosthetic constraint differences may provide one possible explanation for these diverging FE-COR trajectories. The interplay between an artificial disc and a rigid fusion block below it creates a unique biomechanical environment. 16 However, because this study was based on radiographic observations without direct load measurement, finite element modeling, or cadaveric biomechanical testing, the proposed relationship between FE-COR displacement and stress redistribution should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically proven. An implant’s built-in limits on translation may influence whether its center of rotation stays fixed or slides to share the mechanical load.22,23 While classic cadaver studies suggest that six-degree-of-freedom discs may restore a more physiological FE-COR in isolated setups, 24 our in vivo data highlight a possible micro-kinematic difference in hybrid constructs. When a device like the Bryan disc restricts translation, it may behave more like a constrained rotational system. From a theoretical perspective, this relatively fixed FE-COR pattern may be associated with less translational adaptation under the altered mechanical environment created by the fused segment. Without sufficient translational adaptation, altered local loading or micro-angular strain at the adjacent level could theoretically occur. 15 Over time, such changes may contribute to disruption of the local mechanical environment 25 and osteophyte formation as part of a restabilization process.9,26 Nevertheless, this theoretical cascade cannot be confirmed by the present radiographic data alone.

These data suggest a possible association between anterior-inferior FE-COR displacement and a lower numerical incidence of rASD, although the mechanical explanation remains exploratory. By contrast to more constrained designs, the mobile core of the Mobi-C allows for a degree of translational sliding. We therefore interpret the early anterior-inferior FE-COR displacement observed in the Mobi-C group as a possible compensatory micro-kinematic pattern, rather than as a proven mechanical buffering mechanism. This spatial adjustment may reflect altered motion sharing within the hybrid construct, but whether it truly reduces shear stress or delays adjacent segment degeneration requires further biomechanical validation. Thus, the numerically lower rASD rate in the Mobi-C group should be considered an observational trend that supports further investigation rather than a definitive protective effect. 14 It also exposes a critical blind spot in standard ROM evaluations: the actual quality of segmental motion is heavily dependent on the spatial path of the FE-COR, not just the total degrees of flexion and extension. 27 This may help explain why radiographic degeneration can occur even when ROM appears preserved, although this relationship remains to be validated in larger studies.6,13

The coupled nature of this FE-COR drift is also worth noting. In the Mobi-C group, the downward shift was accompanied by a statistically significant forward drift (P = 0.011 at final follow-up). This anterior-inferior displacement may indicate that FE-COR analysis captures subtle changes in postoperative motion quality that are not reflected by conventional ROM measurements. Anterior hybrid surgery and the presence of a fused segment below the arthroplasty level may alter load transmission and translational motion at the superior adjacent segment. In theory, the mobile-core design of Mobi-C may permit a coupled translational response, whereas a more translationally constrained prosthesis may show less spatial adaptation of the FE-COR. However, the clinical meaning of this coupled spatial drift remains uncertain. We cannot determine from the present study whether this pattern is beneficial, neutral, or simply a radiographic feature of the mobile-core design. Therefore, FE-COR should currently be regarded as an exploratory radiographic marker rather than a validated predictor of long-term clinical ASD.

We also noted a pronounced temporal lag between early kinematic alterations and the eventual appearance of structural degeneration. Even after 7 years, patients with radiographic evidence of rASD largely remained clinically asymptomatic, maintaining excellent NDI and VAS scores. This disconnect between imaging and symptoms is well documented in the literature 11 and supports the need to distinguish radiographic ASD from clinically meaningful ASD. In the present study, the multivariable analyses summarized in Table 4 were performed to adjust for potential confounders and to strengthen the interpretation of the observed FE-COR trajectory differences. These adjusted analyses showed that the differences in postoperative FE-COR_X and FE-COR_Y remained significant after accounting for age, sex, BMI, arthroplasty level, baseline FE-COR value, baseline Walraevens score, and follow-up duration. Nevertheless, these analyses should be viewed as supportive rather than confirmatory, because the retrospective design and limited sample size still preclude causal inference. Accordingly, the clinical relevance of FE-COR changes should be interpreted cautiously. Although FE-COR may provide additional information beyond ROM for describing postoperative motion quality, no validated threshold currently exists to define clinically meaningful FE-COR displacement. At present, FE-COR analysis should not be used as a stand-alone criterion for prosthesis selection or routine postoperative decision-making. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting prosthesis selection based solely on FE-COR behavior. Its value lies primarily in generating hypotheses and identifying potential micro-kinematic patterns that warrant further prospective investigation.

We must acknowledge several limitations in the present study. Primarily, the retrospective, single-center design introduces inherent risks of selection bias, despite our efforts to match baseline characteristics carefully. Because implant allocation was not randomized and was influenced by implant availability and surgeon preference, residual selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Moving forward, larger randomized multicenter cohorts will be necessary to validate these micro-kinematic trends. Additionally, our evaluation of rASD was limited to standard dynamic radiographs. We did not systematically assess MRI-based degeneration at the final follow-up, including disc signal intensity, Pfirrmann grade changes, posterior disc protrusion, or other soft-tissue degenerative features. 28 Therefore, early disc degeneration may have been underestimated, particularly in clinically asymptomatic patients. Furthermore, this study evaluated radiographic ASD rather than clinical ASD endpoints, such as new neurological symptoms, symptomatic adjacent segment disease, or revision surgery. Tracking progression to symptomatic ASD requiring revision surgery likely demands 10 years of follow-up or more. 29 Another important limitation is that no direct biomechanical testing, finite element analysis, or load measurement was performed; therefore, causal biomechanical mechanisms between FE-COR displacement and stress redistribution cannot be established. Lastly, our kinematic analysis was confined to 2D sagittal plane mechanics. Future investigations utilizing 3D-to-2D registration or finite element modeling (FEA) could provide a much more comprehensive understanding of the complex, coupled micro-motions occurring in coronal and axial planes.30,31

5. Conclusion

Both the Bryan and Mobi-C prostheses were associated with excellent and comparable clinical outcomes 7 years after two-level cervical hybrid surgery. Yet, their specific design constraints were associated with significantly divergent micro-kinematic trajectories. The Mobi-C’s mobile core permits translation. Soon after surgery, the FE-COR in these patients showed an anterior-inferior displacement. This spatial shift may represent a possible micro-kinematic adaptation within the hybrid construct, which was associated with a numerically lower incidence of long-term rASD, although the difference did not reach statistical significance.

The elastomeric Bryan disc behaves differently. Its inherent resistance to translation holds the FE-COR relatively fixed. This relatively fixed FE-COR pattern may reflect less translational adaptation within the hybrid construct; however, its relationship with adjacent segment degeneration remains hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically proven.

Ultimately, our findings show that conventional ROM measurements alone cannot fully capture segmental biomechanics. FE-COR trajectory analysis may provide additional information beyond conventional ROM measurements for describing postoperative segmental motion quality. However, its clinical utility for predicting symptomatic ASD or guiding routine decision-making remains to be established in larger prospective studies incorporating MRI and biomechanical validation.
