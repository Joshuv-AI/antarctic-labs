𝑆𝐵 ⁡ ( 3 , n ) \mathit{SB}(3,n) has no Hamiltonian cycle when n n is even A sign-of-permutation proof, with extension to all odd m ≡ 3 ( mod 4 ) m\equiv 3\pmod{4} Thanks: Resolution of Exercise 7.2.2.4–224 in D. E. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming , Volume 4, Pre-Fascicle 8a (draft of 10 April 2026) [ 12 ] . Knuth ranks problem difficulty on a 0–50 scale; [46] denotes a research-level problem.
Shisheng Li

Abstract
We resolve exercise 7.2.2.4–224 of Knuth’s Pre-Fascicle 8a (10 April 2026 draft, rated [46]): the digraph 𝑆𝐵 ⁡ ( 3 , n ) \mathit{SB}(3,n) has no Hamiltonian cycle when n n is even. The argument is a sign-of-permutation obstruction. Writing the successor map of a candidate Hamiltonian cycle as f S = A b ∘ σ f_{S}=A_{b}\circ\sigma (Definition 4 ), sgn ⁡ ( A b ) = + 1 \operatorname{sgn}(A_{b})=+1 when m m is odd, so sgn ⁡ ( f S ) = sgn ⁡ ( σ ) \operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) for every choice set S S . A short dihedral Burnside computation shows sgn ⁡ ( σ ) = − 1 \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=-1 on Σ 3 n \Sigma_{3}^{n} for even n n , contradicting the sign + 1 +1 required of a single 3 n 3^{n} -cycle. The same argument gives the stronger statement that 𝑆𝐵 ⁡ ( m , n ) \mathit{SB}(m,n) has no Hamiltonian cycle whenever m m is odd with m ≡ 3 ( mod 4 ) m\equiv 3\pmod{4} and n n is even; this restricts the residue classes in which Knuth’s hint to Ex. 225 (existence of Hamiltonian cycles in 𝑆𝐵 ⁡ ( m , n ) \mathit{SB}(m,n) for all m > 3 m>3 and n > 2 n>2 ) can hold.

2
Reformulation as an FSR sign problem

By Ex. 223(e), the search for a Hamiltonian cycle of
\mathit{SB}(m,n)
reduces to choosing
a suitable subset
S\subseteq\Sigma^{n-1}
. We package this into a function

b\colon\Sigma^{n-1}\to\{0,1\},\hskip 20.00003ptb(y)=[y\notin S],

and the resulting
successor map

f_{S}\colon\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{n},\hskip 20.00003pt(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\;\longmapsto\;\bigl(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},\;x_{1}+b(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\bmod m\bigr).

(1)

The map
f_{S}
is invertible: given
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, the unique pre-image is

(y_{n}-b(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}),\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})
. Hence
f_{S}
is a permutation of

\Sigma^{n}
, i.e. an element of the symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}(\Sigma^{n})
— by which
we always mean the group of all bijections
\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{n}
under composition.
The Hamiltonian-cycle problem is now compact:

Proposition 2
.

\mathit{SB}(m,n)
has a Hamiltonian cycle if and only if there exists
b\colon\Sigma^{n-1}\to\{0,1\}

such that
f_{S}
is a single
m^{n}
-cycle in
\operatorname{Sym}(\Sigma^{n})
.

This is the standard
nonsingular feedback-shift register
viewpoint
(Golomb
[
9
]
; see also Lempel
[
13
]
, Etzion–Lempel
[
5
]
,
and Fredricksen’s survey
[
6
]
): if
(s_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is the
periodic sequence of first coordinates of the cycle vertices — so that

f_{S}^{\,k}(v_{0})=(s_{k},s_{k+1},\dots,s_{k+n-1})
for every
k
— then the
recurrence generating
(s_{k})
is

s_{k+n}=s_{k}+b(s_{k+1},\dots,s_{k+n-1})\bmod m.

The constraint that
b
takes values in
\{0,1\}
(rather than the full

\Sigma_{m}
) is what distinguishes
\mathit{SB}(m,n)
Hamiltonian cycles from arbitrary

m
-ary de Bruijn cycles; in FSR language,
b
is a
binary-valued
feedback
function on an
m
-ary register. Such sign-of-permutation arguments for
binary FSR sequences go back to Mykkeltveit
[
14
]
(Golomb’s
conjecture on the parity of the number of binary de Bruijn sequences); for
context in the
k
-ary case see Hauge–Mykkeltveit
[
11
]

and Etzion’s monograph
[
4
]
, neither of which addresses

\mathit{SB}(m,n)
. The argument below pivots on a single observation:
for odd
m
,
A_{b}
is automatically even on each fibre, so

\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)

regardless of
S
; the rest is a
dihedral-Burnside parity computation for
N(n,m)
.

3
The factorisation
f_{S}=A_{b}\circ\sigma

The key observation is that the successor map factorises through the cyclic shift.

Definition 4
.

Define

\displaystyle\sigma\colon\Sigma^{n}

\displaystyle\to\Sigma^{n},

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}),

\displaystyle A_{b}\colon\Sigma^{n}

\displaystyle\to\Sigma^{n},

\displaystyle A_{b}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\bigl(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},\;y_{n}+b(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})\bmod m\bigr).

Lemma 5
.

f_{S}=A_{b}\circ\sigma
.

Proof.

A_{b}(\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=A_{b}(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},x_{1})=(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}+b(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}))=f_{S}(x)
.
∎

Geometrically:
\sigma
rotates the string, then
A_{b}
adjusts only the last coordinate by
either
0
or
1
, depending on the prefix.

Lemma 7

(Sign of
A_{b}

For odd

\operatorname{sgn}(A_{b})=+1

If

If

.

For odd

\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)

Definition 9

(Necklace count)
.

A
necklace
of length
n
over an alphabet of size
m
is an equivalence
class of strings in
\Sigma^{n}
under cyclic rotation, i.e. an orbit of the
cyclic group
\langle\sigma\rangle\leq\operatorname{Sym}(\Sigma^{n})
acting on
\Sigma^{n}
.
We write

N(n,m):=\#\{\text{necklaces of length $n$ over $\Sigma_{m}$}\}=|\Sigma^{n}/\langle\sigma\rangle|.

By Pólya’s enumeration theorem (or by a direct application of Burnside to

\langle\sigma\rangle
),

N(n,m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}m^{\gcd(k,n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)\,m^{n/d}.

For
m=3
this is OEIS sequence
A001867

[
15
]
.

The orbits of
\sigma
on
\Sigma^{n}
are exactly these necklaces; each orbit of
size

contributes a
d
-cycle to
\sigma
, of sign
(-1)^{d-1}
. Hence

\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=(-1)^{\sum_{O}(|O|-1)}=(-1)^{m^{n}-N(n,m)}.

(2)

For odd
m
,
m^{n}
is odd, hence
(-1)^{m^{n}-N(n,m)}=(-1)^{1-N(n,m)}
and

\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\;=\;-(-1)^{N(n,m)};

(3)

in particular,
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=-1
iff
N(n,m)
is even.

Lemma 10

(Necklace parity for
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
,
n
even)
.

If
m
is odd,
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, and
n
is even, then
N(n,m)
is even.

Proof.

The argument is a standard application of the Cauchy–Frobenius (Burnside)
lemma to the dihedral group on
\Sigma^{n}
. Apply Burnside to

D_{n}=\langle\sigma,R\rangle
acting on
\Sigma^{n}
, where

R(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{n},\dots,x_{1})
is reversal. Then
|D_{n}|=2n
and

|D_{n}\text{-orbits}|=\frac{1}{2n}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma^{k})|+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\operatorname{Fix}(R\sigma^{k})|\right].

The rotation contribution is standard:

\sum_{k}|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma^{k})|=\sum_{k}m^{\gcd(k,n)}=nN(n,m)
.
For the reflections: writing
R\sigma^{k}(x)_{i}=x_{(n-1+k-i)\bmod n}
, the
condition
R\sigma^{k}(x)=x
says
x
is constant on each orbit of the index
map
\psi_{k}\colon i\mapsto(n-1+k-i)\bmod n
on
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.
Since
\psi_{k}
is an involution, each orbit has size
1
(a fixed index) or

2
(a transposition pair); each orbit contributes one free coordinate value,
so
|\operatorname{Fix}(R\sigma^{k})|=m^{(\text{number of }\psi_{k}\text{-orbits})}
.
The fixed indices solve
2i\equiv k-1\pmod{n}
; for
n
even this congruence
has
2
solutions if
k
is odd, and
0
solutions if
k
is even. Hence

|\operatorname{Fix}(R\sigma^{k})|=\begin{cases}m^{2+(n-2)/2}=m^{n/2+1},&k\text{ odd},\\
m^{n/2},&k\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Summing over the
n/2
odd and
n/2
even values of
k
,

\sum_{k}|\operatorname{Fix}(R\sigma^{k})|=\tfrac{n}{2}(m^{n/2+1}+m^{n/2})=\tfrac{n}{2}(m+1)\,m^{n/2}
,
and therefore

|D_{n}\text{-orbits}|=\frac{N(n,m)}{2}+\frac{m+1}{4}\,m^{n/2}.

(4)

The left-hand side of (
4
) is an integer (as a count of orbits).
For
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the factor
(m+1)/4
is an integer, and
m^{n/2}
is an
integer, so the second term
\frac{m+1}{4}m^{n/2}
is an integer.

The difference of two integers is an integer
, so

N(n,m)/2=|D_{n}\text{-orbits}|-\frac{m+1}{4}m^{n/2}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
i.e.
N(n,m)
is even.
∎

Main theorem

Theorem 12
.

Let
m
be odd with
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, and let
n
be even. Then
\mathit{SB}(m,n)
has no Hamiltonian cycle.

Proof.

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
\mathit{SB}(m,n)
has a Hamiltonian cycle. By
Proposition
2
it is the orbit of some
f_{S}
on
\Sigma^{n}
, where
f_{S}
is
a single
m^{n}
-cycle in
\operatorname{Sym}(\Sigma^{n})
. The sign of an
L
-cycle in
\operatorname{Sym}

is
(-1)^{L-1}
; since
m^{n}
is odd,

\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=(-1)^{m^{n}-1}=+1.

On the other hand, by Corollary
8
,
\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
, and by
(
2
) together with Lemma

,
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=-(-1)^{N(n,m)}=-1
.
Therefore
\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=-1
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 13

(Ex. 224)
.

\mathit{SB}(3,n)
has no Hamiltonian cycles when
n
is even.

Remark 14

(Why Algorithm B did not see the obstruction)
.

Knuth’s Algorithm B is a depth-first branch-and-prune search over partial
choice sets; it tracks local consistency but cannot read off the global sign
of
f_{S}
, a property of the whole permutation that depends on every decision.
This explains the 3 million “feasible near-solutions” Knuth reports for

n=4
: all of them have
\operatorname{sgn}(f_{S})=-1
and hence cannot close into an

81
-cycle, but Algorithm B has no way to prune them in advance.

-million-node search tree), an independent
