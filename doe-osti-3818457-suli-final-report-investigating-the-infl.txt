I. ABSTRACT
The influence of anion identity on the reduction potentials and diffusion behavior of copper,

nickel, and silver was investigated across six 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium (EMIM)-based ionic

liquids. Because ionic liquids offer wide electrochemical windows, they provide promising

alternatives to aqueous electrolytes for depositing metals with reduction potentials outside

water's stability range. Using copper, nickel, and silver as surrogate transition metals, cyclic

voltammetry and custom pulsed-potential methods were employed to evaluate electrolyte

performance and isolate anion-specific effects. Results indicate that EMIM TFB exhibited

optimal performance for copper and nickel, whereas EMIM NTf2 performed best for silver.

Analysis of the relationship between diffusion coefficients and reduction potentials revealed

distinct metal-specific trends, which are hypothesized to stem from differences in solvation

dynamics and electronic configurations. These findings advance the understanding of ionic-

liquid-based electrodeposition for National Ignition Facility target fabrication, including

potential applications involving uranium, and establish a clear framework for designing and

evaluating future tailored electrolytes.

II. INTRODUCTION

 Electroplating is an electrochemical process in which a metal is deposited onto a
conductive surface by applying an electrical potential. Deposition conditions and material
properties influence the morphology, adhesion, and thickness of coatings.1 Because these
parameters can be precisely tuned, electroplating is widely used for corrosion protection,2
electronics,2 additive manufacturing,3 catalysis,4 and precision fabrication.2

 Most electroplating processes use aqueous electrolytes, but their limited electrochemical
stability window and susceptibility to competing reactions, particularly hydrogen evolution,5 can
make it difficult or impossible to deposit metals with very negative reduction potentials. This
limitation is relevant to National Ignition Facility target fabrication, where high-Z materials, such
as gold or uranium, are used in hohlraums to generate the x-ray environment required for inertial
confinement fusion.6 Low-Z materials, such as beryllium, are used as capsule ablators because of
their favorable compression and ablation properties.7 Metals such as uranium and beryllium are
therefore difficult to electrodeposit from conventional aqueous electrolytes because their
reduction potentials may lie outside the stable potential window of water. Aqueous systems can
also cause side reactions and surface effects that make controlled deposition more difficult, and
many metals can oxide in water,8 motivating the study of nonaqueous electrolytes that support
electrochemical reactions over a wider potential range.
 An ionic liquid is a salt composed entirely of ions that remains liquid at relatively low
temperatures, often below 100 °C.9 These liquids are promising alternatives because they have
negligible vapor pressure and can provide wider electrochemical windows than aqueous
electrolytes.10,11 Their physical and chemical properties can be tuned by independently varying
the cation and anion, allowing the electrolyte to be designed for a particular metal-ion system
and deposition process. In this work, the ionic liquids contain a common cation, while the anion
was varied. Holding the cation constant allows the influence of anion chemistry on metal-ion
reduction and diffusion behavior to be examined more directly. Differences in anion structure
and chemical properties may influence the metal-ion reduction potential and diffusion rate
through the electrolyte.
 Copper, nickel, and silver were selected to compare how ionic-liquid chemistry affects
the reduction and deposition behavior of different metal ions under similar conditions. Copper
was used as a model metal because its electrochemistry is well characterized, while nickel and
silver provide contrasting reduction behaviors for evaluating metal-dependent effects across the
ionic-liquid systems.
 The objectives of this study were to determine the reduction potentials and diffusion
coefficients of copper, nickel, and silver in six EMIM-based ionic liquids, identify the best-
performing ionic liquid for each metal based on the most positive reduction potential, and
evaluate the factors contributing to this behavior. A more positive reduction potential means the

 2

metal can be reduced at a less negative applied potential, which may make electrodeposition
easier for metals that typically require highly negative potentials. The study also examined
whether consistent electrochemical trends occurred across the three metals and whether diffusion
coefficients were related to metal-ion reduction potentials. Understanding these relationships
may support the design of ionic liquids for electrodeposition of metals with substantially more
negative reduction potentials and contribute to the development of improved electrolytes for
advanced target fabrication.

III. METHODS

A. Materials

 Six ionic liquids were examined in this study. The cation, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium,
EMIM, was kept constant and the anion was varied among the following:
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NTf2), tetrafluoroborate (TFB), trifluoromethanesulfonate
(OTf), tricyanomethanide (TCM), trifluoroacetate (TFA), and dicyanamide (DCA), all supplied
by IoLiTec.

Figure 1. Chemical structures of the 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium (EMIM) cation and the six anions evaluated in this work:
TFB, DCA, TCM, OTf, NTf2, and TFA.

 The following metal trifluoromethanesulfonates were used: copper(II)
trifluoromethanesulfonate (Cu(OTf) 2, 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS 34946-82-2), nickel(II)
trifluoromethanesulfonate (Ni(OTf) 2, 96% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS 60871-84-3), and
silver(I) trifluoromethanesulfonate (Ag(OTf)), ≥99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS 2923-28-6).
Copper was evaluated in six ionic liquids while silver and nickel were evaluated in five. Select
copper measurements and all silver measurements were collected previously as part of an
unpublished dataset and were incorporated into the present study for comparison. Additional
copper and nickel measurements were collected during the current study.

 3

 Electrochemical measurements were performed in a three-electrode glass microcell
connected to a Solartron/Ametek Modulab XM potentiostat. Each cell contained 2 mL of ionic
liquid, molecular sieves, and a nitrogen gas line. The ionic liquids and reference electrodes were
kept under vacuum prior to experimentation to minimize moisture contamination and maintain
dry conditions. The temperature was maintained at 83 °C using a hot plate set to provide uniform
heating. The working electrode for the copper experiments was a glassy carbon rod (3.0mm
BASi Research Products (MF-2012)), a silver wire (99.995%, 0.5 mm diameter, Alfa Aesar) for
the silver experiments, and a nickel foil or glassy carbon electrode for the nickel experiments.
Platinum wire was used as the counter electrode. The reference electrode consisted of a Ag2S
coated Ag wire in a tube containing the same ionic liquid as the electrochemical cell separated by
a glass frit.12 Using the same ionic-liquid environment for the reference electrode and electrolyte
minimizes liquid-junction potentials, which can introduce an additional voltage offset when two
different electrolyte compositions are in contact.
 Metal-salt stock solutions were prepared in acetonitrile at concentrations of 50 mM for
copper and silver and 10 mM for nickel. The stock solutions were then added to the ionic liquids,
after which the acetonitrile was removed by boiling. Ferrocene or cobaltocene was added as an
internal reference standard where indicated.13

B. Procedures
 Cyclic voltammetry, CV, was first performed on the ionic liquid while stirring. The
appropriate metal salt was then added, and a second CV measurement was collected. Ferrocene
or cobaltocene was subsequently added, and a third CV measurement was performed. If
electrochemical peaks overlapped or interfered with one another, an additional CV measurement
was collected using the ionic liquid containing only the internal reference standard. The scan rate
was held constant at 100 mV s⁻¹ for all CV measurements. Some ionic-liquid systems produced
inconsistent responses, so a pulsed-potential method was used to determine reduction potentials
and diffusion coefficients instead of cyclic voltammetry.

 The open-circuit potential of the ionic liquid containing the metal salt was first measured.
A series of potential pulses centered around the open-circuit potential was then applied in 0.01 V
increments, alternating between potentials above and below the open-circuit potential up to
±0.20 V. Positive pulses were oxidation pulses, while negative pulses were reduction pulses. A
20 min open-circuit period was included between each pulse.
 The maximum or minimum current associated with each pulse was recorded, and the
logarithm of these current values was plotted against the applied potential. The resulting V-
shaped plot was fitted with two linear regions, and the intersection of the lines was taken as the
reduction potential. The measured potentials were converted from the Ag₂S reference electrode
scale to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) scale for standard comparison. This method
provided a Tafel-like measurement for comparing reduction behavior among the ionic-liquid
systems.

V. DISCUSSION
 Diffusion behavior in ionic liquids can differ substantially from that in conventional
molecular electrolytes. Cyclic voltammetry did not produce sufficiently consistent results for a
reliable determination of reduction potentials or diffusion coefficients, which is why the custom
pulsed-potential method was used. This method provided a more consistent approach for
comparing reduction potentials and diffusion coefficients across the ionic-liquid systems.

 Both the glassy carbon and nickel foil data were considered for the nickel measurements,
but the nickel foil data presented a complication: a suspected native nickel oxide (NiO) layer.
This oxide film acts as a barrier that requires extra overpotential to break down. If the potential
pulses only stripped away the oxide in small patches, the real electroactive area would be
completely different from the geometric area measured.

 10

 Ultimately the glassy carbon data was considered. The unpredictable oxide breakdown on
the nickel foil introduced too much uncertainty into the active surface area, making glassy
carbon the more reliable choice for the final dataset.14,15
 The copper data exhibited an inverse relationship between reduction potential and
diffusion coefficient, with higher reduction potentials corresponding to smaller diffusion
coefficients. This behavior may be related to the solvation structure of Cu⁺. Pham et al. found
that Cu⁺ forms a relatively rigid first solvation shell in EMIM OTf, consisting mainly of two
oxygen atoms from OTf⁻ anions in an approximately linear O–Cu⁺–O arrangement.16 This
rigidity was attributed to the strong Cu⁺–OTf⁻ interaction, as well as the electronic structure and
relatively small ionic radius of Cu⁺. Because the diffusion coefficient describes the movement of
solvated copper species rather than bare Cu⁺ ions, differences in solvation, ion pairing, and
copper-anion interactions may affect both transport and reduction potential. For example, there
may be a weaker interaction between copper and TFB⁻, producing a larger solvated species,
resulting in slower diffusion while making reduction more favorable. In contrast, TCM⁻ may
bind copper more tightly, producing a smaller effective solvated species that diffuses faster but is
more difficult to reduce.
 Although the simulations were limited to EMIM OTf, a similar relationship may apply
qualitatively to other ionic liquids. In the experimental dataset, EMIM TFB, EMIM NTf₂, and
EMIM OTf exhibited the most positive copper reduction potentials, suggesting that interactions
among Cu⁺ and the anion may influence copper transport and electrochemical behavior.
However, this interpretation remains tentative because the anions in the other ionic liquids may
coordinate with Cu⁺ differently.
 More broadly, the relationship between ionic-liquid diffusion and copper reduction
potential may reflect differences in Cu(II)/Cu(I) solvation structure and speciation. Slower-
diffusing ionic liquids may contain larger Cu-containing solvation complexes or stronger Cu–
anion ion pairing, resulting in a larger effective hydrodynamic radius and reduced mobility.
These environments may also alter the relative stabilization of Cu(II) and Cu(I), such that Cu(II)
is less strongly stabilized relative to Cu(I), making electron transfer from the electrode more
favorable.17 Consequently, slower-diffusing ionic liquids could exhibit more positive copper
reduction potentials. This interpretation remains tentative, however, because slower diffusion
does not by itself demonstrate weaker Cu–ligand bonding or establish which oxidation state is
preferentially stabilized. The observed correlation is therefore best regarded as a hypothesis that
differences in Cu solvation, speciation, and electronic stabilization contribute to the reduction
potential.
 Neither the silver nor the nickel data exhibited the clear inverse relationship observed for
copper. For silver, this may reflect a more flexible and dynamic solvation environment, such as
the broader, less-defined solvation shell reported for Ag+ in EMIM OTf, where frequent
exchange between shells makes diffusion less directly coupled to the reduction potential.16

 11

Similarly, nickel lacked this inverse trend. Despite the absence of a clear overall trend, both
silver and nickel shared the same leading ionic liquids as copper, prominently including EMIM
NTf2 and EMIM TFB. This overlap suggests that certain ionic-liquid properties influence
multiple metals, although their distinct solvation structures and electronic properties mean they
do not respond identically across all systems.
 These different trends may arise from how each metal responds electronically to the
ionic-liquid environment. For Cu(II), the d9 configuration makes the ion Jahn–Teller active,
meaning that changes in solvation can distort its coordination environment and affect the
relative stabilization of the oxidized and reduced species.18 If slower-diffusing Cu complexes are
associated with Cu(II) environments that are less strongly stabilized relative to Cu(I), electron
transfer from the electrode may become more favorable, potentially explaining the observed
relationship between slower diffusion and more positive reduction potentials.
 Ag(I) is also d9, but its more diffuse 4d orbitals likely interact with surrounding ligands
differently. The ionic liquids might still stabilize Ag(II) and Ag(I) in the same relative order as
copper—yielding a similar reduction-potential trend—while the actual silver complexes differ
enough in size or structure, affecting the diffusion order. Meanwhile, Ni(II) has a d8
configuration and lacks a strong, classical Jahn-Teller effect. Because its solvation structure does
not shift the same way, it lacks that direct coupling between diffusion and reduction potential,
which explains why nickel deviates from this trend.18,19
 Overall, the metal-specific trends may reflect differences in how each ion interacts with
the ionic liquid and moves through it. The relationship between faster diffusion and more
negative reduction potentials should therefore be interpreted as a correlation rather than direct
causation. Because the current measurements do not independently separate these effects, the
proposed mechanisms remain hypotheses rather than definitive explanations.

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
 This study examined how ionic-liquid anion identity affects the reduction potentials and
diffusion behavior of copper, silver, and nickel in EMIM-based electrolytes. Changing the anion
produced measurable differences in both reduction potential and diffusion behavior, showing that
anion selection is an important consideration in ionic-liquid electrodeposition.
 Copper showed a general relationship between diffusion coefficient and reduction
potential, with diffusion increasing as the reduction potential became more negative. EMIM
TCM had the highest copper diffusion coefficient and the lowest reduction potential, while
EMIM TFB (alongside EMIM NTf₂ and EMIM OTf) had the lowest copper diffusion coefficient
and the highest reduction potential. Silver did not show the same clear relationship, although
EMIM NTf₂ and EMIM OTf had the most positive reduction potentials overall. Nickel behaved
similarly to silver, lacking a clear relationship between diffusion behavior and reduction

 12

potential; however, EMIM TFB, EMIM TCM, and EMIM NTf₂ had the most positive reduction
potentials.
 These differences suggest that each metal responds to the ionic-liquid environment in its
own way. Solvation, anion coordination, ion pairing, and metal-specific coordination preferences
may affect diffusion and reduction behavior, but the present measurements cannot separate these
effects. The observed relationships should therefore be treated as correlations rather than
evidence of direct causation. Ionic-liquid selection should be tailored to the metal being
deposited rather than based on a single electrolyte for all systems.
 Future studies should expand molecular-dynamics simulations to include more ionic
liquids and metal ions. This would help determine whether the relationship between solvation
structure, diffusion, and reduction potential applies broadly or only to specific systems.

 The composition and morphology of the deposits should be examined directly to connect
electrochemical behavior with deposition outcomes. Additional studies with other metals could
help determine whether the ionic liquids showing the most positive reduction potentials for
copper, silver, and nickel extend to a broader range of metal systems. A more detailed analysis of
cation–anion pair volume, London dispersion forces, and HOMO–LUMO characteristics could
also provide insight into the molecular factors governing diffusion.
 If such follow-up studies are completed, the most promising electrolytes can then be
evaluated with surrogate metals that more closely reproduce uranium’s electrochemical and
coordination behavior, for target fabrication purposes. In the longer term, ionic-liquid
electrodeposition could provide a scalable and conformal alternative to directional sputter
coating for complex hohlraum and ablator geometries.
