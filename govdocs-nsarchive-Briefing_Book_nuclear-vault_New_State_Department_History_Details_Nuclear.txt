Washington, D.C., April 18, 2024 - After nine years of unwanted delays, the State Department’s Office of the Historian (OH) last month published a new Foreign Relations of the United States ( FRUS ) compilation on national security policy under President Jimmy Carter that will be of great value to historians, social scientists, students, and the interested public for years to come. Edited by James Graham Wilson, the volume provides a wealth of information, much of it new, on a range of national security topics that absorbed the Carter administration from day one, including efforts to make nuclear targeting more flexible, continuity of government concerns (and related command and control issues), MX missile basing, military budgets, emergency action procedures, missile attack false alarms, the strategic balance, and strategic intelligence estimates. Highlights from the new FRUS collection are featured in an Electronic Briefing Book published today by the National Security Archive.

Pentagon opposition to the inclusion of certain information in the new FRUS volume held up publication for almost a decade, and while many of those objections were eventually overcome, there are a number of examples, featured below, where the Defense Department successfully withheld information from the new FRUS that was already declassified years ago.

Even so, the new FRUS history is a vivid reminder of the terrifying scenarios contemplated by policymakers and war planners during the Cold War. Although the first few years of the Carter administration was a period of U.S.-Soviet détente, and Carter a noted nuclear critic, the documents depict U.S. policymakers dealing routinely with terrible hypotheticals, most notably the catastrophic consequences that would result from a failure of deterrence, where a U.S.-Soviet military crisis and confrontation could lead to nuclear exchanges with millions of casualties on both sides. As a portent of the danger at hand, during 1979-1980, the North American Air Defense Command’s warning systems malfunctioned, producing false alerts of incoming missiles. The system failures were corrected, but a memo included in the new FRUS shows that President Carter privately admonished Secretary of Defense Harold Brown after the November 1979 incident: “This was a serious mistake and should have been reported to me immediately.”

While the surprise attack that Odom postulated was clearly a worst-case situation, other documents in the new FRUS volume show that President Carter was keen to plan for such emergencies. The engaged and informed president supported the adoption of emergency drills and exercises to ensure that he and his advisers were prepared for crisis situations. For example, Carter’s national security staff took part in the OPAL III evacuation drill, in which a helicopter evacuated staffers from the White House to the National Emergency Airborne Command Post (NEACP). This posting includes several documents on the OPAL III evacuation drills in July and October 1977 to illustrate one of the Carter White House’s efforts to involve itself in tests of emergency procedures.

President Carter also participated directly in the October 1977 Ivory Item exercise, a secret telephone conference with Pentagon officials to prepare a response to a hypothetical Soviet ICBM attack. The FRUS volume includes many new documents on the Carter White House’s efforts to update nuclear war plans, shedding light on the administration’s search for flexible nuclear targeting options that allowed “military power to be more politically useful” and that avoided reliance on “irreversible war plans” such as the SIOP. Another important goal was to develop a secure reserve force that could be used to “preserve the possibility of bargaining effectively to terminate [a] war on acceptable terms.” A key document, Presidential Directive-59 , has been declassified for years, but there is much material that is new, including Department of Defense memoranda and reports on “nuclear weapons employment policy” and documents on the White House’s continuing interest in capabilities for waging a protracted nuclear conflict. As noted previously, one aspect of the U.S. strategic posture that greatly troubled senior White House officials was the perception that the National Command Authority was vulnerable to a focused strategic attack. That problem began to trouble top U.S. officials beginning in the late 1950s, when the Soviets were developing ICBMs, and much information on those concerns has been declassified. While some of the details have been excised from the new FRUS volume, the overall concern comes through, especially with its connection to nuclear war planning. Finding ways to try to preserve the NCA was central to war plans that were designed to maximize presidential control over the process.

The perception of these vulnerabilities informed the Carter administration’s Continuity of Government (COG) program, another major issue covered in the new volume. Besides publishing Presidential Directive-58 on COG, the volume documents the systematic efforts of White House officials to upgrade programs to preserve the Office of the Presidency and presidential succession in the event of a national emergency. While national security managers believed that the National Command Authority could survive in the NEACP for 72 hours, the Carter administration wanted plans for something more long-lasting. The volume documents the role of the Nine Lives exercises in producing the highly secret Treetop program to develop the capability to provide each of the top five officials in the presidential line of succession with a specialized and dispersed support team as a way “to reduce greatly our vulnerability to a surgical strike against the National Command Authority.”

It is worth noting that, in September 1978, President Carter raised a perennial issue by asking, if U.S. ICBMs were so vulnerable, whether it would be better to abandon them and instead strengthen the bomber and submarine-launched missile forces. Harold Brown gave his detailed answer in FRUS Document 97 , agreeing that that a “dyad” of bombers and submarines would be possible but not desirable because he believed that ICBMs had important characteristics worth preserving and that the triad provided necessary “diversity.” For example, if the Soviets solved the anti-submarine warfare problem, then U.S. SLBMs would become vulnerable, while if equipment failed in one of the Triad legs, the U.S. would still have the other two.

One topic not covered in this volume, but that will perhaps be treated in the yet-to-be-published volume on “Global and United Nations Issues,” concerns the final stages of the negotiations of Additional Protocol I to the 1949 Geneva Conventions, which the United States signed in December 1977. A major purpose of the Protocol on humanitarian laws of war was to strengthen protections for civilians; yet, when the U.S. signed the Protocol, it stipulated that “the rules established by the protocol are not intended to have any effect on and do not apply to the use of nuclear weapons.” The unstated reason was that, according to the Pentagon’s Joint Staff, it was not “feasible” to apply provisions for the protection of civilians “in the event of general nuclear war.” Coverage of the final agreement on Additional Protocol I and on any Carter administration discussion of ratification would be fitting in the FRUS series.

What made it possible for this FRUS volume to be published after the long delays caused by the Pentagon’s review were appeals by OH staffers and the impact of decisions by the Interagency Security Classification Appeals Panel (ISCAP), which successfully overcame many of the Pentagon’s objections to this latest volume. The fact that ISCAP decisions are binding (although appealable to the President) and are based on majority rule made all the difference. In the end, ISCAP allowed the Defense Department to withhold, in whole or in part, 39 of the documents selected by the FRUS editors. As noted above, some of the denied information has been declassified for years, but OH evidently believed it was better to publish this excellent compilation as soon as possible rather than to continue to engage in interagency arguments over the text.

IV. Nuclear Employment Planning and the SIOP

Updating President Carter on the status of the nuclear targeting review, Brzezinski showed him PD-59 , which he (actually William Odom and Jasper Welch) and Harold Brown had prepared and which he proposed that the President sign. Seeing it as a “significant step in employment doctrine,” Brzezinski described its main features, including a “strong thread of continuity with our past deterrence doctrine” and the “emphasis on flexibility,” which required a capability to adjust to “any surprises that growing Soviet capabilities could present us in operations” but also a need for a larger secure reserve force. Just as important was the emphasis on C 3 I as a deterrent “capability” and the “requirement for exercises to validate and improve our doctrine and capabilities.” The last point was a reference to the role of exercises such as Ivory Item for familiarizing presidents with crisis decision-making arrangements.

Brzezinski mentioned two important purposes for the directive: 1) Brown could refer to it publicly and privately as a way “to complicate Soviet planning and to convince them, and our allies, that we will not be paralyzed in a crisis between cataclysmic options and capitulation,” and 2) “it may help blunt sharp criticism that we are not dealing adequately with the changing requirements for deterrence in the face of growing Soviet forces and capabilities.” Brzezinski agreed with Brown that there should be no interagency discussion and that because the document included sensitive SIOP information, he supported the preparation of a sanitized version for wider use within the government.

The treatment of this document is another example of Pentagon overclassification. Prepared some weeks after President Carter signed PD-59, it drew upon a paper worked up by William Odom contrasting NSDM-242 and the PD and reworked it into a Brzezinski memorandum to the President. Somewhat inaccurately comparing Carter’s nuclear weapons policy to doctrinal changes articulated during by earlier presidential administrations—“massive retaliation” and “assured destruction”—Brzezinski wrote that “you may or may not want to take public credit for it, but you should have a clear view in your own mind of its historic significance.” [6] Going from PD-18 to the other PDs on strategic issues—civil defense, continuity of government, etc., Brzezinski concluded by drawing comparisons between NSDM 242 and PD-59. The Defense Department excised two paragraphs from the comparison section.

When Brzezinski forwarded to President Carter Harold Brown’s report on nuclear war planning, he observed that one of Brown’s points was that “LNOs and the SIOP require repeated interaction between the NCA and the planning process if our nuclear force planning is to reflect the policy preference of the NCA.” (See FRUS Document 36 ) This NSC report from late 1977 describes the efforts by President Carter and Brzezinski’s office to meet that requirement by staging emergency drills and exercises. In July 1977, an OPAL (Operation Alert) III drill tested emergency evacuation procedures from the White House, while in October 1977 an Ivory Item exercise involved President Carter in a test of conferencing procedures in the event of a missile attack. [3] According to the NSC Crisis Management Staff, presidential participation in Ivory Item “has never been done before, and the event triggered revisions and changes in the SIOP based on Presidential guidance for the first time in history. The NSC staff found that Ivory Item had “monumental” implications for strategic planning doctrine, including “refinement” of SIOP options, greater presidential awareness of the limitations of the escalation control concept, and an additional point that remains classified. While not a FRUS selection, this interesting Odom memorandum was relevant to the debate over the risk of a surprise Soviet SLBM attack to “decapitate” the National Command Authority and C 3 I. The dispute was between the Commander-in-Chief Atlantic Command Admiral Harry D. Train, who believed that Soviet redeployments of ships and submarines to safer locations, such as in the Barents Sea, would provide useful tactical warning, and Commander-in-Chief Strategic Air Command, General Richard Ellis, who “rejects this analysis by insisting that the benefits of a surprise SSBN attack for the Soviets could easily outweigh the loss of most of their fleet.” Odom believed that the controversy “would generate a healthy review of the warning issue in the JCS,” but also “points up an issue which will confront us more often in the future: the significance of the growing C 3 I vulnerability to a decapitation attack.”

Those questions prompted a lengthy report that provided important context for decision-making. Brown’s report included a detailed look back at key developments during the 1960s and 1970s, including the important Foster Panel report, the National Security Study Memorandum 169 report, and Richard Nixon’s National Security Decision Memorandum (NSDM) 242. All were responses to the problem of catastrophically huge SIOP attack options involving “several thousand nuclear weapons” (the smallest, by 1969, involved over 1,700 weapons). With his report, Brown included memoranda on “procedures for conducting nuclear war,” command and control survivability, and by JCS Chair George Brown on “nuclear weapons employment policy.” The command-and-control study found that “the potential for survivability of the US command and control system today is better than it was in 1970, with recent assessments concluding that it contributes significantly to a credible deterrence.” That was most optimistic compared to information that Odom and Brzezinski would receive in 1978 (See Documents 4 and 9).

Beginning in the early 1960s, the SIOP included “withholds” for cities and countries that could be excluded from targeting under various circumstances, mainly to limit collateral damage. In this instance, Harold Brown informed President Carter of withholds involving “non-Soviet Warsaw Pact (NSWP) countries,” to illustrate their impact on “fatalities and on attack effectiveness in the NSWP.” The Defense Department deleted the rest of the memorandum.

Department of Defense Paper, “Secure Reserve Force Target Acquisition Study Final Report,” October 1978, Top Secret, Excised Copy
Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 93 )
One of the innovations of Nixon’s NSDM 242 was that it directed the creation of a “Secure Reserve Force” of strategic weapons systems whose purpose would be “protection and coercion during and after nuclear conflict.” Survivable forces such as SLBMs or bombers and ICBMs in hardened shelters would constitute the core of the SRF that could be used for retaliation or retaliatory threats (coercion). The Carter administration adapted the SRF concept and embodied it in its approach to nuclear war planning in both Presidential Directive 18 and PD-59. Much of this report, including the major findings, has been exempted from release, but a report on strategic connectivity by the Joint Chiefs of Staff’s historical division condensed the sobering conclusions: The secure reserve force study revealed that the National Command Authority did not have the communications systems or the means to obtain the information and intelligence required to manage the force. Because of the vulnerability of and lack of endurance in command, control, communication, and intelligence systems, the study questioned the ability of the United States to use the secure reserve after an initial nuclear exchange.

Memorandum From Secretary of Defense Brown to President Carter, “Nuclear Targeting Policy Review,” 28 November 1978, Top Secret, Excised copy
Nov 28, 1978 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 105 )
Led by Leon Sloss , the Pentagon completed the first step of its policy review on nuclear targeting in November 1978. As Secretary of Defense Brown explained in his memo to President Carter, he believed that targeting plans, the “Nuclear Weapons Employment Policy,” would “make its maximum contribution to deterrence—our basic strategic objective—if our ... policies make a Soviet victory, as seen through Soviet eyes, as improbable as we can make it.” To reinforce that point, the report emphasized targeting Soviet economic targets, “along with the control apparatus and military power [both of] which the Soviets appear to consider of high value.” Most of the front matter, summary and recommendations in the document have been declassified, with key points about the use of “building blocks” for creating more flexibility in attack options, counterforce targeting, Secure Reserve Force, Launch Under Attack, and changes in targeting procedures concerning China. According to the report, the U.S. did not target population “per se,” but “estimates of population fatalities will continue to be an important criterion for any decision maker contemplating the use of nuclear weapons.” While the U.S. sought to reduce “collateral damage,” war plans to impede postwar economic recovery involved “destroying large amounts of Soviet population and industry.” That raised the question whether the threat of such destruction was “the most effective deterrent, particularly in situations less than general war.” Significant excisions are in the major findings on “General War Plans” and in recommendations 5 and 6, which may concern changes in the targeting of Soviet leadership, a topic that is discussed in FRUS Document 125 .

Memorandum From the Military Assistant to the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Odom) to the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Brzezinski), “The Targeting Study: Desired Outcomes,” 27 March 1979, Top Secret
Mar 27, 1979 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 116 )
Work on the Pentagon study moved forward with top officials reviewing and discussing it (and follow-up) reports at meetings of the interagency Special Coordinating Committee. This memorandum by William Odom focused on the need for flexible nuclear targeting plans allowing “military power to be more politically useful” and avoided reliance on the SIOP that was like the World War I Schlieffen Plan, an “irreversible war plan” that could not “support any conceivable war aims.” Odom sought a “a targeting capability in addition to the SIOP.” With Victor Utgoff arranging the SCC meetings, Odom briefed Brzezinski on key issues and problems, including the necessity for presidential guidance and the need to address the C 3 I problem. Objecting to criticisms of Hard Target Kill (HTK) capability as “destabilizing” (because of the first strike implications), Odom argued that “we need all the HTK we can get,” noting its role for a variety of purposes.

Minutes of a Special Coordination Committee Meeting, “Strategic Forces Employment Policy,” 26 April 1979, Top Secret, Excised copy
Apr 26, 1979 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 125 )
This absorbing discussion covered a range of policy issues, including China and the SIOP and the targeting of population, leadership, and “war supporting industry.” Reflecting ongoing improvements in U.S.-China relations, the SCC discussed proposals to take China out of the SIOP and put it instead in a Regional Nuclear Option. Some, such as Deputy Secretary of State Warren Christopher wondered whether, if such a change leaked, it would harm U.S.-Soviet relations, to which Brzezinski observed “should this leak … upset the Soviets, too bad.” Also discussed was the targeting of population centers and command-and-control assets. This continued discussion from a few weeks earlier (See D ocument 118 ), where there was some controversy with JCS Chair David Jones arguing that it was illegal, but where others believed that what really deterred the Soviets was a “threat to the Soviet population.” During the discussion of targeting options against the Soviet Union’s “C3-leadership,” David Aaron asked whether it was “wise to destroy Soviet ability to withhold and control strategic forces from the center.” Nevertheless, Brown asserted that a successful attack on the Soviet leadership could “require very early strikes in a conflict.” The discussion of a targeting strategy that promoted “regionalization” of the Soviet Union prompted Brzezinski to call explicitly for an approach “that inflicts higher levels of damage on the great Russians and creates fears that the Soviet Union would dissolve.” The dialog turned to economic targets, including war-supporting industries as a specific target set, and whether that would reduce collateral fatalities, with Deputy Under Secretary of Defense (International Security Affairs) Walter Slocombe noting that “such targeting might involve 10-15 million fatalities if no care were taken to avoid them.” The meeting concluded with the topic of Hard Target Kill capabilities, which ICBM accuracy improvements could improve, and whether such capabilities were destabilizing, as some in Congress feared. While Harold Brown was more sanguine about the problem, asking “whether two hair trigger capabilities were more dangerous than one, if our increasing vulnerabilities made us more likely to preempt,” and stating that “he didn’t think so.” David Aaron was more concerned, noting that “this situation was a recipe for miscalculations” and that “the respective vulnerabilities of the two sides’ forces would be a much more important variable in determining whether a crisis led to war.”

Memorandum From the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Brzezinski) to Secretary of Defense Brown, “Telecommunications and C3I Policy Issues,” 10 August 1979, Top Secret
Aug 10, 1979 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 149 )
In his retrospective report on national security issues (See Document 25), Odom cited this item as a major step in the process of updating continuity of government procedures. It focused on two groups of issues, telecommunications endurance and NCA survival. Prepared during ongoing discussions of de-regulation, Brzezinski question the wisdom of relying on AT&T for “the provision of adequately survivable and restorable communications networks,” and emphasized the need for a “national telecommunications policy” that would ensure communications between the NCA and strategic forces and provide for continuity of government, among other objectives. The other group of issues was the problem of “NCA survivability beyond the endurance of the NEACP, that is, about 72 hours.” He asked Brown to assist FEMA Director John Macy “in working out a new concept of basing the NCA for both of the leadership responsibilities in an emergency: commanding the armed forces and governing the country.” While Brzezinski asked that the response go to the President by 19 October, in his retrospective assessment Odom observed, rightly or wrongly, that Brown “more or less ignored” the part of the memorandum on NCA survivability (See Document 25).

Presidential Directive/NSC–58, to Vice President et al., “Continuity of Government/C3I,” 30 June 1980, Secret, Excised copy
Jun 30, 1980 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 201 )
With this directive, President Carter authorized plans and programs to “maintain a capability for the Presidency, under the most stressing condition,” so that the President or a successor could survive repeated nuclear attack while directing wartime military operations, negotiating with allies or adversaries, and controlling domestic affairs, among other responsibilities. According to Brezinski (See FRUS Document 198 ), the directive was the “culmination of a long effort to improve our continuity of government program and at the same time to remove vulnerabilities to the National Command Authority for control of the military forces.” Under PD-58, a steering group would oversee COG’s programs development, while a Defense Department-led Joint Program Office would have responsibility for building a new capability. Working with the Joint Program Office, FEMA would in the meantime work to improve “the current capability” (See Document 213 for details) and continue to develop ways and means during a crisis to locate, communicate with, identify, and authenticate “dispersed successors, as a basis for determining legal succession to the Presidency.”

Memorandum From the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Brzezinski) to President Carter, “Emergency Procedures for Presidential Succession,” with letter from FEMA director John W. Macy and attachment (exempted in full) attached, 2 October 1980, Secret
Oct 2, 1980 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 213 )
Brzezinski updated President Carter on FEMA’s ongoing planning for presidential succession arrangements. FEMA’s Nine Lives exercise during May 1980 crystallized plans that led to Operation Treetop, an “intermediate step” toward a new COG capability for dispersing the top five officials in the presidential succession as a means to “to reduce greatly our vulnerability to a surgical strike against the National Command Authority.” In his letter to President Carter, Macy updated him on the contingency planning, identified “problem areas,” such as legal interpretations, improvements in support for successors, and helicopter support, and enclosed a “description of the [contingency] plans which were tested and the procedures associated with each.” [7] Those were developments that continued during the Reagan administration and beyond, with Nine Lives Exercises involving officials from a number of agencies while TREETOP became a major continuity of government program.

John W. Macy, Jr., Director, Federal Emergency Management Administration, to President Carter, 4 June 1980, Confidential
Jun 4, 1980 | Source JCPL, Jimmy Carter Presidential – President’s Files – Staff Secretary’s File, box 21
This June 1980 letter from FEMA Director Macy to President Carter gave perspective on the Nine Lives exercise discussed above. Conducting Nine Lives in early May 1990 were FEMA and the Joint Chiefs with the participation of other agencies, including State, CIA, and Justice, along with White House offices. The exercise posited a “rapidly deteriorating worldwide situation leading to nuclear war,” but with enough warning time to “disperse selected Presidential successors to pre-identified locations prior to the attack.” Various emergency plans were reviewed, exercised, and evaluated, including the Treetop plan, to “support a dispersed successor” and dispersal of the Vice President under tactical warning, the SIOP and “communications connectivity to the dispersed successors.” Macy reported that, “To my knowledge, this was the first time a coordinated and concurrent exercise of emergency plans in support of the President and his potential successors has been conducted.”

Memorandum From the Military Assistant to the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Odom) to the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Brzezinski), “Threat Assessment Conference Call on Missile Warning.” 15 March 1980, Secret
Mar 15, 1980 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 179 )
Odom reported on the response to a false warning systems incident the night before, a hitherto undisclosed event. At 9:32 PM NORAD and the NMCC convened a conference call after a report came in about the detection of missile launches from a Kurile Island site where the “‘fan’ of one of the missile tracks included Alaska, Canada, and the tip of Oregon.” The conference call ended once warning system operators determined that “the impact of both missiles were determined to be in Kamchatka.” Odom reported that procedures worked properly: he was alerted promptly, NEACP “rolled out” at 9:32 PM, SAC readiness changed at 9:30 PM, and “The duty officers at NORAD and the NMCC sorted out the ‘anomalies’ in the computer displays quickly.” As noted, the volume provides good coverage of the other NORAD false alarms of November 1979 and June 1980, including more complete versions of several documents from 1980.

Memorandum From the Military Assistant to the President’s Assistant for National Security Affairs (Odom) to the President’s Deputy Assistant for National Security Affairs (Aaron), “Meeting with Eizenstat on WH Study of Accidental Nuclear War,” 17 July 1980, Secret
Jul 17, 1980 | Source Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume IV, National Security Policy ( FRUS Document 204 )
In the wake of the NORAD false alerts, some White House officials, including presidential aide Stuart Eizenstat, became interested in the possibility of a study group on accidental nuclear war. Believing otherwise, William Odom noted that the Defense Department had prepared a “large study,” which the President had seen and thought that a White House study would invite “political trouble” for the White House and open up a “Pandora’s Box.” The Pentagon study that Odom mentioned has not surfaced, but concern about the possibility of accidental nuclear war persisted and eventually spurred the famous report that Bruce Blair prepared for the Office of Technology Assessment in the mid-1980s and which the Defense Department confiscated. William E. Odom to Zbigniew Brzezinski, “History of the Transformation of Our Strategic Doctrine,” 24 December 1980, Non-classified
Dec 24, 1980 | Source Zbigniew Brzezinski Papers, Library of Congress, Box II: 108, Strategic Doctrine Revision and Long Term Defense Issues 1978-1981
At the end of the Carter administration, Odom prepared for his boss his perspective on the “bureaucratic play” during the previous four years concerning major developments in the national security system as conveyed through presidential directives and changes in government organization. It is a fuller version of the memorandum ( FRUS Document 211 ) that Brzezinski sent to President Carter months earlier. Beginning with PRM-10/PD-18, Odom’s report covered the creation of FEMA, PD-41 on civil defense, PD-53 on telecommunications, PD-57 on mobilization requirements, PD-58, and PD-59. Odom argued that the series of directives represented a radical break from the “1914 syndrome” by facilitating “mobilization, defense,” and “command and control for a long conflict.” Nevertheless, he also acknowledged that they “remain essentially a paper policy” until they “significantly affected the budget process, program designs in Defense, or operational procedures in the JCS and unified commands.” Prepared by a White House insider who had full access to the records, but also a strong point of view, this is a highly informative memorandum, with interesting details on a number of issues. For example, Odom believed that the Presidential Directive on Civil Defense (PD-41) implicitly abandoned the view “that strategic defense is impossible or destabilizing.” He also cited a memorandum on “C 3 I” that Brzezinski sent to Charles Duncan on 30 September 1978 (See FRUS Document 92 ), noting concern about “vulnerability to a relatively small Soviet attack, and our lack of C 3 I endurance in the event of a war;” both of those points are excised from the FRUS version. Likewise, in his discussion of PD-58, Odom noted that it provided for the “establishment of ten small surrogate White Houses at the ten Federal Regional Centers,” a point that is also withheld in the FRUS version.
