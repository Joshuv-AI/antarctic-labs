3. Executive Summary:
Advanced encapsulation strategies are essential for extending the lifespan of oxygen and
moisture-sensitive devices, such as perovskite solar cells (PSCs).1–6 While traditional
glass encapsulations have been the standard, ingress from adhesive joints and protruding
metal grids remains a critical shortcoming. In such cases, thin-film encapsulations can
address this issue by providing a device-level protection strategy to prolong the
operational lifetime of PSCs.7 Additionally, standalone thin-film encapsulations without
glass covers play a vital role in advancing perovskite-based technologies as they aim to
reduce weight, enhance flexibility, improve heat transfer, and optimize light
management.8–15 Moreover, thin-film encapsulations possess an innovative aspect that
deserves further exploration: electrical conductivity.16–20

High electrical conductivities, optical transparency, and low permeation rates have been
demonstrated using inorganic thin-film encapsulations comprised of metals and metal
oxides. These systems can simultaneously function as a transparent conductor and gas
diffusion barrier, providing added functionalities across various applications. However,
the propensity of inorganic films to form and propagate diffusion pathways, such as pores
and pinholes, presents a significant technological hurdle. While the development of
effective passivation methods continues to be a significant focus, state-of-the-art polymer-
based passivation strategies, particularly inorganic/organic multilayered systems, have
yet to be considered due to their electrically insulating nature.

In this project, we introduce a novel inorganic/polymer-composite encapsulating electrode
comprising an indium zinc oxide (IZO) and a polymer-based transparent conductive
composite (TCC). This design aims to leverage the advantages of incorporating polymers
alongside inorganic thin-films to suppress diffusion pathways and to increase the lifespan
of PSCs.21–23 To overcome the electrically insulating nature of polymers, the TCC
incorporates highly conductive microsphere fillers protruding from a transparent polymer
matrix. In effect, the in-plane conductivity of IZO and the out-of-plane conductivity of TCC
work collectively to form a single encapsulating electrode (Figure 1a).

We exemplify the use of this novel encapsulation scheme by benchmarking the
performances of PSCs subjected to accelerated aging conditions of quasi-maximum
power point (quasi-MPP) tracking under light soaking (1 sun) in air at 50˚C and 40-60%
R.H. Notable improvements in operational lifetimes were observed, with a champion
encapsulated PSC maintaining over 90% of its initial power conversion efficiency of
21.65% for up to 1,430 hours, compared to less than 300 hours for an unencapsulated
control (Figure 1b). With a single IZO/TCC dyad, the results outperform similar work (thin-
film encapsulated PSCs aged in air under illumination and MPP tracking). With the
prospect of optimizing TCC characteristics (e.g., optical transparency, electrical
conductivity) and architecture (e.g., number of dyads, TCO properties), our proposed
innovation has the potential to facilitate advanced encapsulation strategies for the benefit
of thin-film solar energy conversion devices.

5. Background and Overview: Photovoltaic (PV) systems stand out among various
energy resources as an attractive solution to satisfy the growing demand for electrical
power. Nevertheless, reducing system costs, increasing efficiency, and extending
operational lifetimes are paramount to making PV technologies more economically viable
and competitive on a larger scale.24–26 Thin-film PV systems have been identified as cost-
effective, high-efficiency alternatives or supplements to the energy- and material-
intensive production of crystalline silicon solar cells.27,28 Among these, perovskites (e.g.,
organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite) are particularly notable for their rapid
advancement in power conversion efficiency (PCE).29 The remarkable increase in PCE
from 3.8% in 200930 to 26.7% in 202531 underscores their potential as they outpace the
performance of other established thin-film PV technologies such as cadmium telluride
(CdTe: PCE 23.1%) and copper indium gallium selenide (CIGSe: PCE 23.6%).31

Economically, while the 2020 benchmark on estimated minimum sustainable price (MSP)
positioned perovskite modules (MSP: $0.38/W) in the upper price range amongst other
PV systems (MSP of Si: $0.25/W – $0.27/W, CdTe: $0.28/W, and CIGSe: $0.48/W), a
substantial cost reduction is expected as the technology matures (PSC MSP target of
$0.18/W by 2030).32 In addition, tandem PSCs (perovskite-perovskite tandem PCE:
30.1%),33 show potential to enhance economic viability as costs associated with
installation (e.g., racking, land use, and labor) do not meaningfully increase with the
adoption of higher-efficiency technologies. Nevertheless, these attributes alone do not
guarantee the commercial success of PSCs, as any emerging technology must also
demonstrate operational stabilities already achieved by other mature PV systems with
degradation rates of 0.5% to 1% per year over a lifespan of 20 to 25 years.34,35 To date,
the operational lifetimes of PSCs are limited to several months at best, constituting an
important roadblock in their deployment.1,3,36–39

A significant factor limiting the operational lifetimes of PSCs is their rapid degradation
upon exposure to atmospheric gasses such as oxygen and moisture, and robust
protection strategies are essential for overcoming this limitation.1–6 While standard glass-
to-glass encapsulation provides impermeable surfaces,40–42 ingress from adhesive joints
and protruding metal grids is a concern. In such cases, thin-film encapsulations offer a
secondary protection strategy that can prolong the operational lifetime of PSCs.7
Additionally, standalone thin-film encapsulations without glass covers play a vital role in
advancing perovskite-based technologies, which aim to reduce weight, enhance
flexibility, improve heat transfer, and optimize light management.8–15 Moreover, thin-film
encapsulations possess an innovative quality that deserves further exploration: electrical
conductivity.16–20

In the following sections, we briefly cover existing thin-film encapsulation technologies to
then focus on encapsulations composed of conductive materials, also known as
transparent conductive gas diffusion barriers (TCGDB). To achieve high electrical
conductivities and low permeation rates,43–45 TCGDBs typically require inorganic
materials such as metals and metal oxides (e.g., transparent conductive oxide, ultra-thin-
metal) with a defect-free microstructure.46–50 We observe the tendency of inorganic films
to form and propagate diffusion pathways (e.g., pores and pinholes) as a significant

 4/52

technological hurdle and emphasize the importance of passivation strategies to reduce
permeability.49,51–57 Most notably, it is observed that TCGDBs have yet to implement
state-of-the-art polymer-based passivation techniques, particularly the inorganic/organic
multilayer, due to the electrically insulating nature of polymers. To address the electrically
insulating properties of polymers, we present the transparent conductive composite
(TCC), which offers anisotropic electrical conductivity, enabling the development of an
inorganic/polymer-composite TCGDB. This innovative encapsulating electrode
represents a pioneering advancement, leveraging the advantages of integrating polymers
alongside inorganic thin films (i.e., an inorganic/polymer multilayer structure) to mitigate
diffusion pathways and enhance the operational stability of PSCs.

Thin-Film Encapsulations: Compared to standard glass encapsulations, thin-film
encapsulations aim to reduce weight and improve flexibility. Al2O3 and SiO2 are common
material systems used in thin-film encapsulations due to their impermeability to oxygen
and water.64–67 Nevertheless, a single Al2O3 or SiO2 thin-film typically offers only one to
three orders of magnitude reduction in water vapor transmission rates (WVTR)46 due to
the tendency of inorganic materials to form and propagate diffusion pathways (e.g., pores
and pinholes). Although optimized atomic layer deposited (ALD) single-layer systems
reach below 10-3 g/m2/day,68–70 defect passivation methods remain a challenge for further
reduction using inorganic systems alone.

Advanced multilayer systems implementing polymer-based passivation techniques,
particularly inorganic/organic multilayered systems (Figure 2), have been identified as an
effective method for reducing permeability while maintaining optical transparency71,72 and
mechanical flexibility.73,74 In effect, the polymer interlayer decouples defects in the
inorganic layers to create long tortuous diffusion pathways through the multilayer
stack.54,75 This established method can reduce WVTRs to nearly 10-6 g/m2/day, as
demonstrated by commercially available Ultra-High Barrier films from 3M,76 and BarixTM
from Vitex Systems.77

Figure 2: Concept image of an inorganic/organic multilayer system with 5.5 dyads.

Transparent Conductive Gas Diffusion Barriers: Beyond weight reduction and
improved mechanical flexibility, thin-films can provide yet another functionality: electrical
conductivity. In the following section, we discuss encapsulations composed of metals and
metal oxides, which serve as both transparent conductors and gas diffusion barriers.

 5/52

Single-layer thin film barriers: Transparent conductive oxides (TCO) are indispensable
in optoelectronic devices due to their high transparencies and electrical conductivities.
However, for TCOs to function as a gas diffusion barrier, it’s important to reduce the size
and number of defect-driven permeation pathways (e.g., pores, pinholes, grain
boundaries).49,51–54 In all practicality, typical thin-film TCOs prepared using conventional
deposition techniques (i.e., sputtering, evaporation) will have defects (Figure 3a) and offer
only one to two orders of magnitude improvement in WVTRs (Table 1). As such,
advanced deposition techniques (e.g., doped ALD) have been at the forefront of single-
layer TCGDB optimization, which results in amorphous high-density structures with low
WVTRs (Figure 3b).78 Despite the remarkable result, the electrical conductivity and barrier
properties of TCOs can degrade under damp heat conditions and require robust material
systems to mitigate this issue.18,78 In addition to environmental deterioration, brittle oxide
films on ductile substrates can result in the deterioration of electrical properties under a
moderate amount of strain (approx. 1-3%),79–82 similar to SiO2,83 and Al2O3,84 which
demonstrates mechanical flexibility only up to 1-2% strain.

Overall, single-layer TCO barrier systems are inherently riddled with sub-micrometer
intergranular defects and grain boundaries, which act as permeation pathways.46,47 As
such, optimization of TCO microstructures by ALD and intermittent doping is required to
improve barrier performance. Nonetheless, the complex deposition process and
susceptibility to environmental and mechanical degradation remain a critical drawback.
In the next section, inorganic multilayer systems are introduced as a solution that
emerged to address some of these issues, particularly the flexibility of TCGDB systems.

Inorganic multilayer structure: Among the next generation of TCGDB, TCO/ultra-thin
metal/TCO (TMT) multi-layer structures (Figure 4) emerged for their superior electrical
conductivity and mechanical flexibility.8,9 For example, TMTs can increase strain-to-failure
from 1% (TCO only) to 3% and maintain electrical conductivity after 10,000 bending
cycles.10,11 This is a significant improvement compared to the 7-fold increase in resistance
observed for a single-layer TCO subjected to the same bending cycle. Furthermore, a
100nm TMT structure demonstrates lower sheet resistance (5 Ω/sq.) compared to a
thicker TCO film (200nm: 15 Ω/sq.), and upholds comparable optical transparencies.

In terms of barrier properties, the TMT relies predominantly on the TCO microstructure,
however, slight improvements were observed from the insertion of an ultra-thin metal
interlayer (Table 2). For example, WVTR in the range of 10-4 g/m2/day was achieved with
a sputtered IZO/Ag/IZO structure,48 compared to the 10-2 g/m2/day with a single IZO layer
(Table 1). Nevertheless, monolithically integrated inorganic multilayers tend to replicate
defects from the under-film in subsequent depositions. Hence, the process is unforgiving,
and the location and spatial density of defects do not change for samples with particle
contaminations or abrupt changes in surface morphology. Consequently, compromised
samples yield little to no improvement in WVTR. Thus, a surface planarizing layer (i.e.,
polymer) is required to take full advantage of the multilayer barrier structure.
Unfortunately, polymers are an electrically insulating component that remains largely
unexplored for use in TCGDBs.

Polymer Composites: Polymer composites can provide a simple and efficient method
of production and show promising results as flexible encapsulants. These composite
systems can incorporate materials such as graphene86,87 and nanoscale fillers88 to
improve flexibility and alter gas diffusion mechanisms (Figure 5) to reach WVTRs in the
range of 10-5 g/m2/day while maintaining high optical transparencies.89–91 Notably, the
reduction in WVTRs for multilayer inorganic/polymer-composite structures demonstrates
a strong dependence on the number of dyads, and WVTRs did not change significantly
depending on the thickness of the inorganic layer. Note that these systems (Figure 5) do
not operate as encapsulating electrodes due to their insulating design. As described next,
we sought to assimilate the three areas of thin-film encapsulation that were previously
introduced, spanning the state-of-the-art inorganic/organic multilayer structure, TCGDBs,
and polymer composite systems to form a novel inorganic/polymer-composite
encapsulating electrode.

In this project, a transparent and conductive composite (TCC: Figure 6a and b) is
introduced as a simple solution to assimilate the inorganic/polymer multilayered structure
without losing electrical access through the material stack. The TCC comprises highly
conductive silver-coated polymethyl methacrylate (Ag-PMMA) microsphere fillers
protruding from a transparent non-conductive epoxy matrix.58–63 When employed
between two TCO thin films, the dispersed monolayer arrangement of the spherical fillers
facilitates an anisotropic electrical connection between the inorganic films. In effect, the
in-plane conductivity of the TCO and the out-of-plane conductivity of the TCC work
collectively to form a single encapsulating electrode (Figure 6c). The proposed
inorganic/polymer-composite TCGDB is the first of its kind and aims to gain the benefits
of incorporating polymers alongside inorganic thin-films (i.e., inorganic/polymer
multilayer) to suppress diffusion pathways and to increase the operational stabilities of
PSCs.21–23

As part of the project objectives, we evaluate standalone TCC specimens for their optical
and electrical properties as well as permeabilities, as-synthesized, and after 1,000 hours
of either outdoor exposure or damp heat in dark (85˚C, 85% R.H.) aging. These
standalone TCC specimens demonstrate high optical transparencies (%T approx. 85% in
the 370nm-1200nm region for 5% Ag-PMMA concentration), excellent out-of-plane
electrical conductivities (R<0.2 Ω·cm²), and reasonable equilibrium permeabilities
(permeability < 1 g·mm/m2/day), all of which remained virtually unchanged after aging.
PSCs (IZO/SnO2/C60/Cs0.05FA0.85MA0.10Pb(I0.97Br0.03)3/MeO-2PACZ/ITO/Glass) were
then encapsulated with a single dyad comprised of a sputtered IZO thin-film and the TCC
(IZO/TCC) without compromising power conversion efficiencies (PCE). The protective
quality of the IZO/TCC encapsulant was evaluated by subjecting PSCs to aging
conditions of continuous illumination under quasi-MPP tracking in air at 50˚C, 40-60%
R.H. As shown in Figure 6d, Encapsulated PSCs demonstrated noteworthy
improvements in operational lifetimes, with the champion PSC maintaining over 90% of
its initial PCE of 21.65% for 1,430 hours (T90 of 1,430 hours). Compared to an uncoated
control (T90 of less than 300hrs.) evaluated under the same conditions, a single IZO/TCC
dyad provides approximately a 5-fold improvement in operational stability.

Finally, to compare our results against similar work on thin-film encapsulated PSCs, a list
of 82 stability metrics across 43 studies, published between 2016 and 2024, was compiled
(Table 3). These reports show a wide range of aging conditions (e.g., storage in N2, in
ambient air, in air at 85˚C 85% R.H., MPP light-soaking) for various encapsulation
materials (e.g., polymers, inorganic single and multilayer systems, in-grain
encapsulation). Within the broad context of this sample population, outstanding stabilities
are only attained in a controlled environment (i.e., dry air, dark), which is significantly
reduced when identical devices are aged under illumination with MPP tracking.92 As such,
it is important to account for aging conditions when comparing stability metrics across
studies.93 To this effect, data points were filtered to 14 devices reported across 10
publications that measure the operational lifetimes of PSCs by aging under conditions
comparable to the ones used in our study (i.e., continuous illumination in ambient air and
MPP tracking). Under these conditions, our result (PCE 21.65%: T90 of 1,430 hours)
demonstrates the highest performance amongst thin-film encapsulated PSCs (Figure 7),
on par with the historical trend of operational lifetimes achieved with all encapsulation
techniques.3

With the prospect of optimizing TCC characteristics (e.g., optical transparency, electrical
conductivity) and architecture (e.g., number of dyads, TCO properties), our proposed
innovation has the potential to facilitate advanced encapsulation strategies for the benefit
of solar conversion devices. For example, when implemented in conjunction with a cover
glass for PV systems, it allows for relocating the grid away from the device to the glass
cover, thereby permitting a hermetic seal of the PV device by the thin-film encapsulant
and preventing ingress through protruding grids. Furthermore, standalone TCGDBs,
without a glass cover, can benefit systems that require protection from the environment
while necessitating out-of-plane light and charge transport, such as photoelectrochemical
(PEC) systems. With further refinement, the TCC can pave the way for improved
encapsulation techniques to provide the element of “added functionality” to the triad of
low-cost, efficient, and stable characteristics desired in next-generation PV technologies.

6. Project Objectives:
We propose a unique strategy to protect perovskite solar cells (PSCs) and enhance their
lifetime towards reaching SETO’s 2030 cost target of $0.02/kWh. Specifically, this
program aims to encapsulate PSCs with transparent conductive composites (TCCs) to
isolate them from the external environment. We will use our expertise in semiconductor
surface passivation and protection against corrosion gained from years of DOE/EERE-
funded research in PEC water-splitting to further develop TCC protection layers (Task 1)
and benchmark their effectiveness towards enhancing PSCs durability (Task 2), aiming
for less than 10% reduction in efficiency (T90) over 1,300 hours of continuous operation
for PSCs with initial PCE greater than 22% (EOP goal).

Objectives:
Task 1.0: Validation of TCCs as efficient gas-barriers
Task Summary: To develop TCCs (conductive media + polymer) with appropriate gas-
barrier and optoelectronic properties for the prolonged operation of PSCs, relevant
parameters such as WVTR, electrical conductivity, and optical transparency were
benchmarked before and after exposure to aging conditions.
Subtask 1.1 – Polymers water vapor (WVTR), and oxygen (OTR) transmission rate
benchmarking: WVTRs of solvent-free, room-temperature curable polyepoxies were
measured using NREL's established techniques before and after 1,000 hours of damp
heat (45°C, 85% R.H.) aging. The facilities required for OTR testing were to be
established at NREL; however, this was not completed within the project period.
Subtask 1.2 – Quantify the effect of aging on TCCs' optoelectronic properties: Polymers
were combined with conductive media to form TCCs and exposed to 1,000 hours of
outdoor aging to benchmark changes in optical and electrical properties over time.

Task 2.0: PSCs durability benchmarking
Task Summary: To evaluate process compatibility and optoelectronic performance of
monolithically integrated IZO/TCC/PSC devices, photovoltaic (PV) metrics were
benchmarked and assessed before and after encapsulation. Accelerated aging tests were
conducted on encapsulated PSCs to quantify improvements in operational lifetimes
compared to their unencapsulated counterparts.
Subtask 2.1 – Electrical testing of encapsulated PSC devices: Unprotected and
encapsulated cells were tested continuously near their maximum power point (“quasi-
MPP” technique) in a laboratory at 50 °C, in air (40-60% R.H.), 1-sun intensity. Current-
voltage measurements were conducted periodically to identify premature degradation.
Subtask 2.2 – Failure analysis: Systematic evaluation was not performed due to sample
transaction and project timeline limiting the ability to conduct regular analyses.

Task 3.0: Training and results dissemination
Subtask 3.1 – Hiring student(s) from under-represented groups (URGs): UH recruited
one undergraduate (UG) student from URGs. The students participated in daily research
activities and teleconferences with the NREL team collaborating in the project.
Subtask 3.2 – Results dissemination: Products from the research were presented at
international conferences, and a manuscript was submitted.

To establish process compatibility for each distinct curing schedule, PSCs were annealed
at different temperatures for 15-minute intervals. As shown in Figure 8a, a steady
reduction in the power conversion efficiency (PCE) is observed for temperatures above
100 °C. At the maximum curing temperature for Masterbond (80 °C), virtually no change
in PCE was observed after 5 hours of annealing (Figure 8b). The experiment establishes
the compatibility of Aluzine and Masterbond curing schedules; however, it is problematic
for thermoplastics such as EVA that require high-temperature lamination and curing at
165 °C. Although low-temperature lamination (<90°C) may prove successful in forming
an EVA-based TCC architecture on PSCs, acetic acid formation caused by EVA

 17/52

decomposition has been reported to degrade PSCs. Therefore, EVA was determined unfit
as a PSC encapsulant at this stage.

Following this initial assessment, polymer laminates (not including conductive
microspheres) and TCCs (comprised of polymer matrix with conductive microspheres:
Figure 9) were prepared using the Aluzine or Masterbond two-part epoxy for water vapor
transmission rate (WVTR) measurements at NREL. The conductive fillers consisted of
45-53µm Ag-PMMA microspheres (Cospheric PMPMS-AG-1.53, 90% of which with
diameter within the specified range) unless otherwise noted. The composite material was
prepared in a glass vial by measuring the appropriate weighted ratios of the epoxy (i.e.,
Part-A and Part-B) and the Ag-PMMA filler, and stirred using a magnetic stir-bar for five
minutes. The composite mixture was then drop-cast onto an acrylic releasing substrate
(approx. 10 cm ´ 10 cm ´ 0.64 cm) and placed in a rough vacuum (approx. 0.06 bar, 15
mins) to purge air pockets introduced during mixing. To create the dispersed monolayer
arrangement of the microsphere fillers within the epoxy matrix, a separate releasing
substrate was introduced on the opposing side, and a compressive force applied to press
the TCC mixture between the two substrates for overnight curing. Special care was
exercised during the introduction of the second substrate to prevent the formation of air
pockets within the composite. For the Masterbond epoxy, a secondary curing step was
conducted at 60 – 80 °C for 3 – 5 hours in a furnace.

Due to the large volume of the liquid composite mixture (approx. 0.5mL) compared to the
required amount for a single thin film (less than 0.05mL for a 6.45cm2 substrate with a
bond-line thickness of 50µm), the microsphere concentration within the cured TCC thin-
film often strayed slightly from the intended amount and resulted in variations between
individual TCC samples and at different areas of interest (e.g., ±2% for target value of
5% microsphere concentration by area). In addition, TCC samples prepared with the 45-

 18/52

53µm Ag-PMMA microsphere fillers repeatedly resulted in nominal thicknesses above
100µm as indicated by a digital Vernier caliper, likely due to the combined effects of
outliers in the size distribution, agglomeration of spheres, and the relatively low pressure
across the large sample area (approximately 0.5 bars) during the room-temperature
lamination. Operators were limited in their capacity to increase lamination pressure due
to the lack of specialized equipment. As such, larger microsphere fillers (Cospheric
PMMAMS-AG-1.2, 95% of which within the 500-520µm range) were used to prepare
supplementary free-standing TCC (FS-TCC) samples to assist in the evaluation of
WVTRs. Despite the range of WVTRs (thickness-dependent) measured across samples
with varying thicknesses, the permeabilities (a thickness-independent metric) facilitated
the comparison of the results. Free-standing laminate samples (i.e., epoxy only and
without microsphere fillers) were prepared using metal shims (350µm or 500µm) placed
along the edges of the acrylic substrates to dictate the thickness and served as a
benchmark to measure any changes to WVTR due to the inclusion of microsphere fillers.

These samples were analyzed at NREL as received (0hr) or after 1,000 hours of
accelerated damp heat aging (85°C, 85% R.H.). The WVTR measurement procedure
followed IEC 62788-6-2.131 A Mocon Aquatran model SRT051-100 and Mocon
Permatran-W 3/31 instruments were used following the manufacturer’s instructions for
calibration. These systems were modified with remote cells capable of testing within 5 ˚C
to 85 ˚C. Damp heat aging (85 ˚C, 85% R.H.) of FS-TCC and laminate samples was
conducted using a temperature/humidity environmental test chamber (Thermal Product
Solutions, Blue-M FRS-361F).

Overall, the measured permeabilities of Aluzine and Masterbond TCCs fall at the low end
of typical polymers (0.1 to 100 g·mm/m2/day), 14,135,136 although lower permeabilities have
been reported for advanced polymers.137–139 As shown, the polymer-based TCCs cannot
meet the recommended WVTR for PSCs of < 1×10-3 g/m2/day for a 20 to 25-year
lifetime.14,54,77 As such, to protect PSCs for the envisaged lifetime, multiple sets of
inorganic/polymer dyads are required. Nevertheless, the sustained WVTR after
accelerated aging indicates the suitability of the Aluzine and Masterbond epoxies to act
as a reliable component within the inorganic/polymer-composite multilayer system. In the
next section, we evaluate the optoelectronic properties of TCO/ultra-thin metal/TCO
(TMT) structures and their capabilities to reduce permeability. Overall, the RF magnetron
sputtering was identified as the limiting factor in reducing WVTRs, and significant
improvements would require an ALD system. Since the procurement of an ALD system
was not viable by the time of this revelation, substantial reductions in WVTRs were not
obtained. Nevertheless, procurement is underway to facilitate continued progress.

Optoelectronic Properties of TCO/Metal/TCO Inorganic Multilayers – Prior work from
NREL suggested that a sputtered TCO/nanolayer-metal/TCO multilayer structure is
capable of decreasing WVTR up to 1,000 times (measured at 25˚C and 40% R.H.) while
maintaining optical transmission comparable to, and electrical conductivities better than
a single TCO layer.48 Material candidates most pertinent to our multi-layer barrier
structures were identified from previous studies as Ag and IZO, 48 as the Ag offers very
low resistivity (1.6 µΩ·cm) with low absorptivity in the visible region for very thin layers
(approximately 10nm),140 while the IZO exhibits good optoelectronic properties and
stability under damp heat exposure (>1,000 hrs. at 85˚C 85% R.H.).141

With this TMT structure identified, HNEI ramped up the capabilities of room temperature
RF magnetron sputtering for the integration of an IZO/Ag-nanolayer film on a single
substrate. As a result, 200nm thick IZO layers with resistivity near the typical value of
6.6E-04 Ω·cm were demonstrated, with an average transparency of 85.7% between the
350nm – 850nm wavelength range, pertinent to perovskite photovoltaics. The
transparency of IZO was observed to increase to above 90% transmission with the
incorporation of oxygen partial pressure and reduction in thickness, however, at the
detriment of electrical conductivity. Furthermore, ultra-thin Ag film of thickness 10 ± 3 nm
sputtered on soda-lime glass (SLG) demonstrated resistivity of 3.32E-06 Ω·cm, near the
bulk resistivity of Ag (1.6E-06 Ω·cm), and an averaged optical transparency of 52.3% in
the 400nm-850nm range (normalized to the optical transparency of SLG). Reduction in
Ag film thickness to 7nm showed an altered transmission profile with only a marginal

consistent with the literature, as Ag forms non-conductive discontinuous clusters across

Optoelectronic Properties of TCCs – Considering the electrically insulating nature of
the transparent epoxy used in this study, the conductivity of TCCs relies exclusively on
the dispersed conductive media. Recently, Ag-PMMA microsphere fillers gained interest
in the PV community due to their outstanding and reproducible optoelectronic
performance.147,148 These early demonstrations implemented Ag-PMMA as an electronic
interconnect to form multijunction PV devices.58–63 To account for the inherent distribution
of microsphere diameters (90% in the 45-53µm range), optimized assembly processes
typically comprised high-temperature lamination to melt and compress the entire
microsphere population between the sub-cells. However, when using the Ag-PMMA as
part of an encapsulant for PSCs, it is essential to have a balanced lamination process
that ensures sufficient microsphere protrusions without excessive reduction in polymer
thickness (i.e., bond-line thickness). To gain a deeper understanding and to achieve
better control over the relationship between lamination pressure, bond-line thickness, and
out-of-plane electrical conductivity, we developed a preliminary in-house analytical
model.149,150 This framework provided an approximate range of optimal lamination
pressures, as we demonstrate next, which was then validated by measuring the electrical
conductivities of corresponding FS-TCCs.

in the glove box’s load lock for de-gassing for 10 minutes. The samples were transferred

The compliant substrate comprised a rubber gasket between a polypropylene release film
and a glass substrate, effectively reducing the lamination pressure (approx. 0.4 bar). The
encapsulated PSCs were packaged in a vacuum bag inside the glove box and shipped
back to NREL (FedEx 2-day shipping) typically within three weeks of being received.
Finally at NREL, a secondary IZO layer (thickness: 230 nm) was deposited directly on the
TCC layer by reactive sputtering in an argon and oxygen atmosphere, leading to the final
encapsulated structure (IZO/TCC/IZO/SnO2/C60/Cs0.05FA0.85MA0.10Pb(I0.97Br0.03)3/MeO-
2PACZ/ITO/Glass). The control samples, synthesized from the same batch as the
encapsulated PSCs and remained at NREL in a nitrogen-filled glovebox until the

 35/52

encapsulated PSCs returned, at which point their electrical characterization and durability
testing were conducted concurrently.

The current density-voltage (J-V) characterizations of PSCs were measured in an N2
glovebox using an AM 1.5G solar simulator (Oriel Sol3A Class AAA) using the terminating
IZO layer and the ITO back contact as electrical contacts. The intensity of the solar
simulator was calibrated to 100 mW/cm2 using a reference silicon solar cell (Oriel, VLSI
standards, certified by NREL PV Performance Calibration group). Stabilities of
encapsulated PSCs and control samples were tested under 1-sun illumination from a
sulfur plasma lamp (sample surface temp. approx. 50˚C) in ambient air (40-60% R.H.)
while being held under a resistive load near the maximum power point (560 Ω).

Subtask 2.1: Electrical testing of encapsulated PSC devices: Unprotected and
encapsulated cells were tested continuously near their maximum power point (“quasi-
MPP” technique) in a laboratory at 50 °C, in air (40-60% R.H.), 1-sun intensity. Current-
voltage measurements were conducted periodically to identify premature degradation.

To compare our results against similar work on thin-film encapsulated PSCs, a list of 82
stability metrics across 43 studies, published between 2016 and 2024, was compiled
(Table 3). These reports show a wide range of aging conditions (e.g., storage in N2, in
ambient air, in air at 85˚C 85% R.H., MPP light-soaking) for various encapsulation
materials (e.g., polymers, inorganic single and multilayer systems, in-grain
encapsulation). Figure 25 illustrates that within the broad context of this sample
population, despite our high initial PCE (21.65%) placing at the upper end of the scale
(PCEs in the range of approx. 11% to 24%), our champion device stability is outperformed
by other studies (Table 3). However, outstanding stability of over 10,000 hours (T90: Initial
PCE >20%) is only attained in a controlled environment (i.e., dry air, dark) and
significantly reduced (T97: 153 hours) when an identical device was aged under
illumination and MPP tracking.92 As such, it is important to account for aging conditions
when comparing stability metrics across studies.93 To this effect, data points were filtered
to 14 devices reported across 10 publications that measure the operational lifetimes of

PSCs by aging under conditions comparable to the ones used in our study (i.e.,
continuous illumination in ambient air and MPP tracking). Under these conditions, our
result (PCE 21.65%: T90 of 1,430 hours) demonstrates the highest performance amongst
thin-film encapsulated PSCs (Figure 26), on par with the historical trend of operational
stabilities achieved with all encapsulation techniques.3

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
A novel encapsulating electrode was formed using a highly transparent and conductive
composite (TCC) coated with a sputtered indium zinc oxide (IZO) thin film (Figure 27a).
Facile monolithic integration of the IZO/TCC structure onto the perovskite solar cell (PSC)
was achieved without any loss in PCE. An encapsulated PSC maintained above 90% of
its initial power conversion efficiency of 21.65% for up to 1,430 hours, compared to less
than 300 hours for an unencapsulated control (Figure 27b). The result obtained in this
project (PCE 21.65%: T90 of 1,430 hours) demonstrates the highest performance
amongst thin-film encapsulated PSCs aged under illumination in air with MPP-tracking
(Figure 27c), on par with the historical trend of operational stabilities achieved with all
encapsulation techniques.3 Despite the focus on PV testing using PSCs grown on rigid
SLG substrates, the TCC-based encapsulating electrode is adaptable to other modes of
operations, including flexible PV and photoelectrochemical water-splitting devices. Such
a system provides the element of “added functionality” to the triad of low-cost, efficient,
and stable characteristics desired in next-generation PV technologies. With further
refinement, TCC can pave the way for improved passivation and encapsulation
techniques for use in thin-film photovoltaic and photoelectrochemical devices.

10. Path Forward: To progress this field of research, HNEI proposes a transparent
conductive composite multilayer (TCCM) inorganic/organic multi-layer TCGDB system
(Figure 28a). The work expands on the transparent conductive composite (TCC) system
concept and the research in multilayer inorganic barriers. The TCCM barrier system is
synthesized layer by layer, starting with an inorganic transparent conductive oxide,
followed by a TCC coating. Each step is repeated up to 6 times, resulting in the full
multilayer barrier system. Our system aims to expand the capability of polymer-
interrupted non-conductive inorganic/organic multilayer structures (Figure 28b) by the
implementation of conductive particles. The nano/micro-particles allow out-of-plane
electrical conductivity and connect to the inorganic conductive layers. With the prospect
of optimizing TCC characteristics (e.g., optical transparency, electrical conductivity) and
architecture (e.g., number of dyads, TCO properties), our proposed innovation has the
potential to facilitate novel encapsulation schemes for the benefit of thin-film photovoltaic
and photoelectrochemical devices. A provisional patent protecting this innovative concept
was filled by the University of Hawaii on May 2nd, 2025.
