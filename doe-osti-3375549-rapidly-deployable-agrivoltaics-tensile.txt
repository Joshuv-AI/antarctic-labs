Abstract

This project successfully developed a novel microgrid ganged-PV system (proven power/structural
resiliency >75 mph wind) for deployment within agricultural areas with environmentally intensifying
solar gain, facilitating crop issues such as “sun scorch.” The unique design has been demonstrated to
allow improved crop cultivation with tractors and more efficient operations and maintenance (O&M)
over current fixed framed agrivoltaics installations. The microgrid developed with this agrivoltaics
system allows power production for farms (i.e. electric tractors and irrigation pumps) and neighboring
communities, especially those impacted by intermittent electricity and “brownouts.” The ganged-PV
strategy has been shown to reduce installation costs of traditional PV racking systems on agricultural
fields. Energy storage within the microgrid provides on-site power for electrified equipment and for
neighboring communities. Our innovation was experimentally demonstrated to showcase the novel
balance of system components, including a wind-resistant ganged design (>75 mph) with a reliable
direct microgrid-tie and controls approach, to support abatement of current electrical transmission
losses. This project was also able to demonstrate the potential of support energy resilience for low-
income and rural communities. Rural energy resiliency can improve access to key services for
healthcare, telecom, and education.

Executive Summary

Increased interest in solar energy for energy dominance has particularly accelerated the field of
photovoltaics (PV) in the last two decades. With advancements in electric vehicles (EV’s) and the
robustness and scalability of inverters, the ability to expand the use of PV into unique settings has
increased. With large surface area potentials and reduced structural challenges, PV could have lasting
benefits in agriculture. In the state of New Mexico, the limited rainfall and high solar resource intensity
can lead crops receiving too little water and too much sunlight, also known as “sun scorch.” To address
this problem, a ganged-PV system was developed to optimize shade to crops. Ganged-PV systems
have already proved successful in practice with winds >75 MPH, through a previous collaboration
between Skysun LLC and Sandia National Laboratories [1]. This simple yet resilient system uses guy
wiring between posts to support static PV panels. Having a static PV panel parallel to the ground
creates shading for plants below, with decreased evaporation [2]. To increase functionality for the
farmer the tension cabling and panels run on telescoping poles to manipulate the height of the panels
up to 15 feet above the ground. This allows standard-size tractors to cultivate the land below the
panels when they are raised. Energy created from the system have direct applications for irrigation
pumps and to charge the next generation of EV cultivating tractors. The DOE funding provided
allowed for initial engineering design, development and experiment validation at the Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL) DETL site. The technology was demonstrated at a TRL 5 level at Sandia’s DETL
facility with the demonstration of a 4 kWe system, that later led to a TRL 8 system being deployed
and demonstrated RGCF in Albuquerque, NM. There, tests were facilitated across multiple furrows
against a control crop, with production loads for on-site pumps to irrigate particular local vegetables
(with and without PV shading) and charge an EV tractor for cultivation. Techno-economic analysis
(TEA) was also developed to assess the development of larger systems to support local communities
where PV can supplement home energy needs. Data was collected pertaining to soil quality and plant
health through a collaboration with the Biology Department at the University of New Mexico (UNM).
U.S. DOE funding was integral for the success of this project. This funding proved system resilience,
the effects of optimized agrivoltaic shading for plant growth, and economic benefit for farmers
employing this system.

can cause plant issues such as “sun scorch.” [3]. The unique design of the ganged-PV system will allow
improved crop cultivation/rotation with tractors and personnel, as well as enhanced operations and
maintenance (O&M) over current fixed framed agrivoltaics. The ability to rapidly connect/disconnect
the ganged-PV system and adjust the height beyond 15 ft. would allow standard cultivating tractors
access for farming. The ability to vary array height above grade allows optimization of shadowing for
a varying crop species, also allowing for different growth requirements due to expected crop
rotation/fallow. The microgrid being developed with the agrivoltaics system would allow power
production for farms (i.e., electric tractors increasing in utilization [4], irrigation pumps, etc.) and
neighboring communities, especially those impacted by intermittent electricity and “brownouts.”
Local community energy production would support energy resilience, and for rural areas typical of
agricultural sites would provide energy access to key services for healthcare, telecom and education,
etc. Electric utility companies command tremendous resources geared for centralized power
generation; solar PV agrivoltaics can have challenges with respect to adoption due soft costs pertaining
to labor, as well as deployment costs of large agrivoltaic systems. Sandia’s market/application includes
utility adoption of PV by making solar attractive to these utilities (of varying scales) from the
standpoint of microgrid support, operability, and PV deployment soft costs. Sandia’s adjustable
ganged-PV solution targets pain points such as reducing racking costs and agrivoltaics soiling derates,
which can impact as high as $0.22-0.44/W or as much as 21% of the overall system costs. This research
would seek to reduce these costs in agrivoltaics installation to target the U.S. DoE SunShot goal of
$0.03/kWh [5]. This solution deployment will reduce “retooling” by the farmer by utilizing existing
trellis posts for vine plants (e.g., blackberries, grapes, raspberries, hops, etc.), as well as reducing
irrigation moisture temperature/evaporation. The team plans to assess photosynthesis, evaporation
rates, and agronomy humic acid biological performance for plants specific to the agriculture of New
Mexico. Results will be compared between plants cultivated beneath the ganged-PV and ones
cultivated simultaneously in the same farmland without the ganged-PV shading. There is high
confidence this technology will be successful based on previous wind-resistant validation work at SNL,
as well as with resilient and reliable electrical PV, microgrid, and energy storage controls also
developed at SNL. Sandia’s expected outcomes from successful demonstration within an islanded
microgrid system will include expansion of a new market for successful PV deployment, temporary
and permanent quality job creation, and rapid PV deployment with lower soft costs, particularly
considering this novel system can leverage existing infrastructure. The ganged-PV system will be
matured and deployed on an operational DC microgrid at SNL. This system is expected to add 10
kWe to the SNL Distributed Energy Technologies Laboratory (DETL) microgrid. This work would
develop a low TRL-3 ganged-PV and microgrid system to first assess power production potential
within islanded and non-islanded microgrids. This technology would then be deployed at the RGCF
along agricultural furrows (Figure 1) to assess plant health and fruit production/yield and electrical
power production. Microgrid and ganged-PV prognostics and health management would be assessed
up to higher TRL-8 stages at RGCF. This work would include techno-economic analysis to assess
scalable commercialization for varying agricultural farmland sizes, environments, and community
energy needs.

2.1. DETL Laboratory System Development & Modelling
The intent for this Task was to evaluate the configuration constraints within the initial SNL DETL
microgrid and perform a design optimization of the ganged-PV system. This work began by
performing mechanical and electrical design of the units based on previous R&D [1] at the SNL
National Solar Thermal Test Facility (NSTTF), which was setup at the SNL DETL, which had
microgrid emulator capabilities (Figure 3). During the planning phases the initial ganged-PV
agrivoltaics system was assessed to be setup within the Figure 1 purple line.

The design objective of the DETL Agrivotlaics microgrid demonstration system was to deploy and
evaluate a single 13.2 m length telescoping ganged PV system. The initial testing raised the ganged PV
panels from 2 to 6.5 ft to prove functionality efficacy and test tensioning aspects of the guy wire
dampening cabling. The system was ballasted at surface level when performing testing at DETL rather
than driven into the ground. Using previously determined maximum combined loads within ASME
code guidelines for Albuquerque of 65 kg/m2 yielded a maximum tension at cable midpoints of the 7
m span of 3.2 kips. Using a midpoint tension rod, a cable endpoint tension was calculated as 3.9 kips.
These tensions assumed all forces on a single cable as we used two cables. The static load was only
0.6 kips cable mid-point tension. The team initially calculated a combined load pertaining to
overturning moments for a 2 m high post(s) design. The normal operational height not to exceed 1.5
m above grade. The maximum combined load of 3.9 kip at 1.5 m ht. (5 ft.) resulted in a calculated
19,600 lb-moment. This was counterbalanced by ballast, concrete bin blocks (2 at 1,800 lb each)
located/centered at 1.9 m beyond each end post on base framework. Note this assumes a combined
load with a span at maximum normal operational height. This work leveraged experience of an existing
ganged-PV system at the NSTTF (Figure 4) to evaluate dampening subsystems and electrical
performance systems, prior to installation within the microgrid.

A simulation model representing a community farm loads (Table 1) was built using
MATLAB/Simulink. The simulation model consisted of a PV system (that represents the agrivoltaic
system), as well as multiple dynamic three phase and single-phase loads. To emulate the single-phase
agrivoltaic PV inverter dynamics, a current controlled single-phase PV inverter model is implemented
[6]. The PV inverter model utilized a phase-locked loop (PLL) to synchronize to the grid. Figure 10
illustrates the block diagram for the PV inverter model. In this diagram, the variable Pref and Qref are
the active and reactive power reference values [2]. The active power is derived from a linear
relationship to solar irradiance. The control is represented using the direct (d) and quadrature (q) axis
reference frame, and a dq-current controller. Adjusting the d and q axis currents allows controlling
both the active and reactive powers, respectively. For the purposes of this simulation, only the active
power of the PV inverter model is modified, while the reactive power is kept at zero.

Sandia facilitated further power hardware in the loop (PHIL) testing to evaluate the interaction
between real-world power hardware (inverter + ganged PV system) and a simulated distribution grid
under realistic load conditions. This methodology allows for scalable, low-risk experimentation to
validate integration strategies, volt/VAR control effectiveness, and grid response under various load
and generation scenarios. The PHIL system includes: a ganged photovoltaic (PV) array connected to
a commercial inverter, a hardware interfacing to a real-time Opal-RT digital simulator, which emulates
a distribution feeder representing the farm, simulated load banks emulating actual farm equipment
including pumps, fans, heating systems, and EV chargers, and voltage and current sensors to capture
system performance under both controlled (with VVC) and uncontrolled scenarios. The goal was to
quantify how the PV system impacts voltage regulation and active/reactive power delivery under
multiple system configurations. Table 2 summarizes the representative farm electrical loads used in
the simulation. Loads were selected to match realistic agricultural energy demands.

Pumps (well and booster) are high-demand, motor-driven systems critical for irrigation. Each rated at
15 kW and 6.2 kVAR, they contribute significantly to both active and reactive power demand. The
barn/greenhouse & walk-in Cooler have relatively low active power needs but are continuous loads,
typical for climate-controlled storage. The fans (exhaust and circulation) are smaller, distributed loads
that are vital for maintaining ventilation in greenhouses or livestock barns. The greenhouse heat
pumps are medium-sized, high-efficiency thermal systems, which cycle seasonally. The grow lights are
significant loads, often used during winter or for light-sensitive crops. The electric tractor and truck
chargers represent the emerging electrification of farm equipment. Each contributes substantial power
demand, adding dynamic variability to the system. These load profiles are applied in combinations
throughout the experiments to evaluate the system's ability to meet farm demands with on-site PV
generation and grid interaction. Each test varies by inverter output power (3.5–250 kW), control mode
(with or without volt/VAR control), and phase configuration. Key interpretations are as follows:
 • Single-Phase Performance: Tests 1–7 were all performed using a single-phase inverter
 configuration. This allowed for fine-grain control evaluation but placed asymmetric stress
 on the modeled grid.
 • Three-Phase Performance: Tests 8–11 introduced three-phase operation, reflecting
 utility-scale integration scenarios and ensuring better load balancing.

 • Control Impact (Volt/VAR Control): Tests 2, 5, 7 have activated VVC.

2.2. DETL Ganged-PV Tracking Controls
The SNL team facilitated further study to assess how the ganged-PV system can be automated to also
track the sun during operation, with the intent for potentially maximizing solar power production
during the RGCF testing campaign. The ganged-PV system at DETL had a fixed tilt angle of 10°,
fixed southward, which produced 93.8% of the ABQ ideal fixed tilt of 29.7° output. This angle was
 23

chosen for the high potential power production while minimizing rain and snow induced soiling and
minimizing the wind profile of the module. The problem with a fixed tilt is that the ideal fixed tilt of
29.7° still produces suboptimal power as the sun moves throughout the day and throughout the year.
This also means that the chosen 10° angle produces 93.8% of a suboptimal power value. Varying the
tilt angle to move with the sun can enable the DETL module to generate the maximum possible
power. Another advantage of varying the tilt angle is that it can address the rain/snow induced soiling
and wind profile concerns. Depending on the current weather and wind speeds, the range of values
for the tilt angle can be changed to keep the module within acceptable wind profiles while still
optimizing power production. For example, on days with high winds, the tilt can be changed from 0°
to 10°. On days with low winds, the tilt can range from 0° to 45°. This means that the DETL module
can be deployed to more climates with more extreme wind and/or weather conditions. Varying the
tilt angle can be accomplished by implementing a real-time optimization algorithm. The RTO
algorithm produces a new tilt reference that increases power by intentionally moving the panel to
gather data, estimating a power gradient, and moving in the direction that increases power.
 Ideally, the real time optimization (RTO) algorithm was run continuously throughout the day.
However, this will partly reduce the net power generated depending on the actuators used. To
minimize this, the RTO algorithm was applied every 20 minutes, since the Sun moves about 5° every
20 minutes. Since being 19.7° from a more ideal angle is acceptable, being within 5° of the optimal
angle will both improve power generation and minimize actuator usage. In MATLAB, the RTO
algorithm was simulated using a moving sun with a period of 12 hours instead of 24 hours, meaning
the simulated sun is moving faster than the actual sun. The simulation results show that the solar panel
starts (Figure 18) at an angle far from the optimal angle and then moves to the optimal angle Figures
19 and 20). After the solar panel reaches the optimal angle, it follows the sun to maintain 100% power.

2.3. Ganged-PV Vibrational Assessment
To ensure the system design is well dampened to mitigate destructive vibrational modes during high
wind conditions a further study was facilitated. A digital vibration analysis of the DETL system was
modeled for later stage validation, to understand the normal modes, frequency response, and wind
loads affecting the system, as well as best practices to mitigate the response that the system is
experiencing. The modal superstition method was used to capture the response of the system. The
mode superposition method adds up factored mode shapes from a modal analysis to calculate the
structure’s response. This method is faster and less computationally intensive than the full harmonic
method. The solution was clustered about the structure’s natural frequencies, providing smoother
results and more accurate tracing of the response curve. Figure 21 shows the high-fidelity CAD model
of the DETL system. This model is down to the level of nuts, bolts, and screws.

 Figure 21. Isometric view of DETL high-fidelity CAD model.

Due to the size of the model, and the large number of connections the model had, a simplified model
had to be designed and used in Ansys. Since the main loading point of the structure are the panels,
wind will be moving them and thus make the excitation occur in the rest of the structure. Subsequently,
a specific model of the panels was made and imported into Ansys. The model included the panels, as

For the modal analysis, the four end faces of the cables were fixed. Initially, a total of 60 modes were

to the dynamic response of the structure when subjected to external excitations. It is recommended

effective mass does not reach this percentage, more modes need to be found. In this case, with 60
modes, the total effective masses were 0.37, 0.99, 0.99, 0.99, 0.95, and 0.99 for the x, y, and z direction

3. Community Farm Microgrid Design, Integration & Testing

In this task, the team designed and configured the tensile-based agrivoltaic microgrid system to
accommodate several ganged-PV strings at varying voltage levels within a TRL 8 deployed system at
the RGCF. The team developed real-time analysis software to assess performance over a full farming
season period. The intent was to have field demonstration to define the economic potential of this
agrivoltaics technology. This evaluation considered how this new technology fits into stacked revenue
streams/cost avoidance and its ability to lower the effective cost of deploying and integrating in rural
electric networks and farms. The tests at both DETL and RGCF were designed to both use single-
phase power with the awareness that forthcoming EV tractors as well as irrigation pumps will run on
single as well as three-phase power to be employed. Circle Two LLC also was granted access to a e70n
EV tractor from Solectrac during the testing stages, which allowed the team to integrate charging of
an electric vehicle to assist with understanding scalability, and commercialization of the system.
 This work began by leveraging the data collected to down-select optimal PV shading
height/orientation, in addition to plant and soil types suitable for agrivoltaics in the arid southwest.
Initially, the UNM subteam’s long-term goal was to provide a low-cost, user-friendly PV integration
solution for farmers, while achieving increased crop yield and reduced irrigation demand. The high
light availability in semi-arid environments is ideally suited for utilization of solar energy applications
with adequate water supplies for agriculture. In these regions, plant growth is strongly controlled by
plant-available water. In New Mexico, crop irrigation consumes eight times the surface water allocated
to urban water supplies. As increased environmental temperatures overall can reduce snowmelt- and
rainfall-derived surface water supplies in the southwestern U.S., PV generation offers not only a clean-
energy solution for power but has the potential to mitigate the high temperatures and evaporative
losses to maintain or even increase crop productivity when crops are grown in the footprint of PV
arrays. This part of the proposed research leveraged the ganged-PV systems under development to
provide optimized shaded agricultural conditions with more favorable surface soil temperature, soil
evaporation and site water balance. It was hypothesized that the PV arrays would reduce radiation and
evaporative losses from plants and soils, improving plant water status, sustaining photosynthesis and
productivity while reducing the irrigation required for crop growth. The team compared plants grown
in the absence of PV arrays (control) with plants in three positions with respect to the PV strings in:
1) a fully shaded environment where the PV arrays reduce direct solar radiation, 2) a partially shaded
area with bifacial PV modules, and 3) an open position where soil and canopy receive full solar
radiation. Seedlings for this deployment were transplanted to containers 3-4 weeks post-germination
to allow more confident root growth. They were then transplanted to the field site 8-12 weeks after
germination (in mid-late May 2025). The team utilized both flood and drip irrigation to water planted
areas to create high water and reduced water environments along the length of the furrows, with and
without PV strings. The team measured soil moisture/humic acid levels and temperature under each
of the three regions using profiles of soil moisture probes (Decagon EC-5 and Wescor screen cage
thermocouple psychrometers for moisture sensing) and soil thermocouples measured continuously
(Campbell Scientific, model CR-6 datalogger). In addition to weekly assessments of leaf condition, we
will measure 1) leaf water potential before dawn and at midday (Scholander pressure chamber) to
assess plant water availability, and 2) diurnal variation in photosynthetic rates every 4-6 weeks during
the growing season. The team also initially planned at the end of the season to destructively harvest
plants from all positions to assess total dry mass production and crop yield as a function of treatment.

3.1. Posts and Actuators
By early spring 2025, SkySun LLC, in collaboration with Sandia completed the system structural
installations: five I-beam posts, PV and damping cabling, and mounted PV panels, shown in Figure
74. A novel aspect of the Sandia ganged PV system is the ability to uniformly change the height of the
20 solar panels between four and seven feet, which is accomplished through the use of actuators on
each of the five I-beams. The actuators are rated for 1,500 lbs. of force with stroke lengths of 3 ft. A
3-position switch was installed near the pump house to readily change the height of the panels. Each
actuator has a dedicated 12 V power supply to ensure the same power is seen at each I-beam, therefore
minimizing the potential for actuators falling out of sync with the others and causing undesired tension
increases between the I-beams. Since the Sandia agrivoltaic system was designed to operate by lifting
the spans to adjust shading and farming practices (i.e. for cultivation and harvesting), using linear
actuators (Figures 75 and 76). The linear actuators were sourced from Firgelli (Model F-PM-H-1500-
12-40 V). The dynamic thrust has an IP 65 rating for the 3 units at 6 A at 12 V and 2 units 4.5A at 12
V, ~ 324 W (A 12 V automotive battery, or equivalent, should be more than adequate.).

The SolArk inverter collects operational data for the PV panels, battery, load, and the grid that are

application, which also allows the user to see the system status in real time (Figure 86)

The SolArk inverter has four maximum power point tracking (MPPT) inputs which allowed the team
to divide the field into four sectors: two for the UNM rows, and two for the Sandia row, with 2 strings
on each. This allowed the team to monitor the efficiency and power generation from the UNM
fabricated panels, as the SolArk Inverter compiles distinct data through each MPPT. Although the
system tracks 132 unique operations, this report will highlight three data sets: battery usage, PV energy
output, and peak load requirements. The farm pump house supplied 16 acres with water with the use
of two pumps – the well pump fills a cistern, while the booster pump pulls the water from the cistern
and distributes it to the fields. Typically only one pump runs at a time through the use of ABB logic
controllers; running one pump draws around 4 kW of power, however during start up the power draw
is much higher as seen in Figure 87, with a maximum peak draw of 26 kW. The battery has two lines
in parallel going into the inverter allowing 100 A of current draw to the load; the maximum load power
is well below the maximum amperage allowed on the system. Watering begins prior to sunrise, relying
on battery power to drive the pumps. Figure 88 shows the charge and discharge cycles of the battery,
with the charge never dropping below 70% (used as backup) before being recharged through the PV.

3.8.2 Biomass Crop Production Comparisons
Biomass performance and growth was calculated by measuring plant height over the course of the
summer growing season. At the conclusion of the study, the aboveground dry mass of each plant will
be determined by cutting the plant off at the base, recording its position in each plot and drying the

4. Commercialization Advancements

4.1. Market Advancements
Small farms in New Mexico face challenges related to increased temperatures and prolonged drought.
Collocating photovoltaic (PV) panels with crops on these farms can provide shade that helps lower
temperatures and decreases evaporation. The electricity generated by the PV system can be used
directly by the farm (e.g., irrigation, heating, cooling) or exported back to the local power grid.
Outcomes like improved crop health, reduced water consumption, and lower electricity bills can help
farmers decrease operating costs and increase their annual revenue. However, locating standard
ground mount PV systems with crops can be challenging, as the PV systems limit equipment access
and increase the need for manual labor. To overcome this challenge a novel, ganged PV system has
been designed by Skysun, LLC. The system minimizes structural components and can be outfitted
with motors that allow the system to quickly raise and lower as needed for planting and harvest
activities. While the logistical advantages of the new, ganged PV system are clear, it is still uncertain
how the upfront costs of the system compare with potential economic benefits for farmers. To address
this uncertainty, Sandia National Laboratories partnered with Skysun to perform a techno-economic
analysis (TEA) that evaluated the net present value, payback period and greenhouse gas emissions
savings of constructing a ganged photovoltaic (PV) system on farms in north central New Mexico.

4.2. TEA Methodology
To carry out the TEA, we created a model in Microsoft Excel that uses Visual Basic to automate
calculations. Using that model we analyzed three specific scenarios: 1) A small farm with standard
electricity demand, 2) A small, electrified farm with increased electricity demand, and 3) A larger farm
with land leased for electricity generation as part of a power purchase agreement (PPA). In each of
these scenarios it was critical to consider the electricity demand, the amount of electricity generated,
and the costs associated with electricity generation. The following sections detail how these and other
considerations were built into the model.

4.2.4 Techno-Economic Analysis Aspects
We analyzed the standard farm, electrified farm, and land lease scenarios using a discounted cash flow
TEA, Table 12. For the standard and electrified farms, we developed the TEA from the perspective
of the farmer. In these scenarios the farmer is responsible for going to a bank and securing a loan for
the upfront capital cost of the PV system. Upon installation, the electricity generated by the PV system
is used to meet the demand of the farm, leading to avoided electricity costs. In addition, the farmer
also benefits from net-metering, which allows them to take credit for excess PV production in
subsequent months. At the end of the year the remaining net excess electricity generation is settled up
and paid to the farmer at a reduced net-metering settle up rate. For analyzing these scenarios, we
defined a grid electricity purchase rate of $0.1000 per kWh and a net-metering settle up rate of $0.0295
per kWh based on the Jemez Mountains Electric Cooperative which serves north central New Mexico
[11]. For the land lease scenario, we developed the TEA from the perspective of a solar developer. In
this scenario, the developer leases land from a farmer at the rate of $500 per acre per year and is
responsible for financing the capital costs of the PV system. Once the system is operational, the
developer receives compensation for electricity generated a power purchase agreement (PPA)
electricity sale rate. In our analysis, we selected a PPA rate of $0.0215 based on typical PPA rates
within New Mexico [12].

The high impact of added agriculture revenue due to increased yield is a key result of this work.

required to confirm the actual value of shade from the panels. This data should include information

to identify all of these potential outcomes for specific crops and relate them to quantifiable market
values. These values should then be implemented into the economic models highlighted in our work,
to improve the certainty of outcomes related to the PV systems.

To understand the potential of commercial adoption of this technology, the team was strategic in
deploying this system at the RGCF, to allow farmers to use a functioning deployment, which was

project ends in the Fall of 2025. The answers to this survey were intended to be short or with as much
information and detail that the person surveyed wishes to provide. Any and all information was found
to be extremely valuable for this project so as to allow opportunities for follow-on funding and grants

The team distributed agrivoltaics market research surveys to New Mexico farms/households in
Alcalde, Medanales, Abiquiu, and some through the NMSU Agricultural Extension Office. Several of
the households were already found to have rooftop or ground mounted PV panels. For the first
Section 1 questions, results found that for those considering using PV panels to generate electricity
on their farm, that ~10% of those surveyed are already using 50/50 ground or house-mount solar but
the majority have not considered it because they are not aware of the existence and benefits of
agrivoltaics. Regarding concerns over using the agrivoltaic PV panel technology on their farm, about
80% were enthusiastic about it while about 20% said they did not want solar panels on the land they
can see driving in/out, in their house, and several do not like the idea of panels on their fields due to
their traditional approaches to farming and since the technology is still very new commercially. When
asked about feedback pertaining to the specific ganged-PV system, input that was provided was:
 • The ganged cable and post approach are useful for quick installations but may not be necessary
 for row crops in small “garden areas” as fixed ground mount could be high enough for people
 to work under and especially if vehicular access is not necessary.
 • Feedback noted that farmers may not want solar panels on the land if it impairs driving in/out,
 but if it can be setup in a way to allow ease of driving then they would be open.
 109

For the second Section 2 set of questions, surveyed people noted that for their monthly electricity bill
that most that didn’t have PV, had bills in the $100-200 range in winter, and about 50% more in
summer. They also noted that with respect to their electricity use cases, that 50% was for home water
and convection heating, 10% for cooking and 40% for laundry. For industrial use they noted it was
about 70% for irrigation hand tools and about 30% for other welders, etc. Responders also noted that
they primarily used diesel for farm tractors and earth moving equipment and gasoline for power tools,
movers, tillers, chain saws and blowers for clearing/cleaning. Few noted that they used heat for drying
harvested crops, but they did note that if heat was available for drying more readily available, they
would consider pairing their farm with post-harvest production. On a per month bases the answers
from the surveys noted that most respondents used diesel mostly in the 5-15 gal. range, gasoline, about
10-20 times per year. If they had a surplus of free electricity from a PV system on their land, some
noted they would consider replacing existing equipment to use electricity instead of other fuels.
However, since electric tractors are not mainstream many noted that those may not be realistic in rural
NNM yet, and secondly the humidity is so dry and sunshine so prevalent (0ver 310 days/year) in
NNM that crops naturally dry in the air.
 For the last Section 3 questions, surveyed participants noted that the majority of their farms
in NNM are small holdings farms and ranches that are mostly owned by the current members of
families whose generations have lived here for 300-400 years. They are generally 20-50, 30-acre fields
with clustering around acequia ditch water sources, where each community does have some larger
farms, that are about 100-200 acres and a few farms that are 500-2000 acres. When asked if there were
specific crops that could benefit from additional shade, respondents noted that there are parts of the
vegetable garden that would benefit but the cost of agrivoltaics to provide that shade could be a
challenge financially to them purchasing a system since the costs could exceed the benefit of the shade
and the produced electricity. Multiple farmers also were unsure how the agrivoltaic system would work
for cover crops that extend over multiple acres. On average farmers noted that for their small farms,
the vegetable crop coverage was about 10-15% depending on where there was good enough soil, shade
and irrigation resources available. From the small set of farmers surveyed that didn’t live by a water
source, most smallholdings didn’t have cover crops, where they noted that their soil was dry, hard and
bare, which would make cover crop farming difficult. On farms by a river the farmers noted that they
had 80-90% of the acreage as cover crops. The farmers generally noted that they harvested a variety
of vegetables several times in growing season, fruits once in late summer and fall, and cover crops,
mostly hay/alfalfa, which were harvested first in late May, then July and lastly in September. Many
farmers noted that they did have challenges with growing crops with increasing environmental
temperatures which affected yields. Here, most farmers grew vegetables without any shade during the
day, where they experienced increasing sun scorch by late July. The majority of farmers surveyed along
the Chama River (surveyed area) use their irrigation from acequia/ditches, where the use frequency
was based on availability level in the river, which is influenced by the weather and by the dams that
control flow. However, year after year the water level was so low that ditch/acequeia use has grown
less frequent, to once a month on average. Overall, both Sandia and SASC recommended that JMEC,
as well as a new La Puente Inc. group in the JMEC region, lead an ongoing coordination of the joint
team which will further define and explore opportunities for the use of Agrivoltaics since the added
shade could help row crop farmers experiencing increasing drought conditions and sun scorch to
fruits and vegetables. The La Puente group has on-going work in the Alcalde region of NM to bring
up agrivoltaics to test deployments, evaluate potential sources of funding, coordinate design of the
test set-up, the impact to different types of crops, the timing and duration of the experiments, and its
 110

subsequent inclusion in the New Mexico State University (NMSU) and New Mexico Department of
Agriculture (NMDA) Research Programs. This could be part of joint outreach activities to the
agricultural communities in NNM.

5. Conclusions
Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) in collaboration with the University of New Mexico (UNM),
Skysun LLC, Circle 2 LLC, Jemez Mountains Electric Cooperative (JMEC)/La Puente Broadband
Initiative LLC, and the Rio Grande Community Farm (RGCF) developed a flexible and resilient
agrivoltaics microgrid for scalable farming. This project demonstrated a ganged-PV microgrid system
that produced substantive power and maintained structural integrity >75 mph wind gusts, for
deployment within agricultural areas that have rising, climate-intensifying solar gain that can cause
plant issues such as “sun scorch.” Two ganged-PV microgrids were designed, developed and deployed
at the SNL DETL facility and the RGCF. The unique design of the ganged-PV system allowed
improved crop vegetable and fruit production, as well as enhanced operations and maintenance
(O&M) over current fixed framed agrivoltaics. The deployment and techno-economic analysis (TEA)
was also found to be superior over traditional, fixed frame PV. The solar panels suspended above the
crops, produced enough power to pump well water throughout the growing season. This agrivoltaics
system proved the viability of using the same land for both energy generation and food production.
After two years of research, the team demonstrated that irrigation systems can run completely off-grid
using solar power throughout peak summer demand. This creates resilience in two ways: backup
power during grid outages, and fixed energy costs for a decade or more. This project demonstrated
that solar energy can be designed in a way that empowers farmers to produce both energy and local
food to our communities in a true win-win. This project didn’t just demonstrate a new technology; it
also educated and inspired many farmers at the RGCF and in northern New Mexico to innovate on
land stewardship while meeting their market goals for production. The team also hypothesized that
the technology could shield the crops against hail by lowering the panels right above the crop, as well
as demonstrating the ability to protect crops from frost (Figure 140).

In comparison for production yield of the vegetables grown at the RGCF, between the plants under
the ganged-PV panels, and those in an open control area, it was found that when the crops are well
watered, particularly from increased rain fall, the production yield in the open lots can be substantially
more than in the covered lots. The precipitation and soil moisture data correlated well with increased
production weight. The team found that under the PV panels, rainwater precipitation rolled off to the
edge of the PV panels and did not reach the plants. For future studies, the team may have to consider
how the rain rolling off the panels should be collected and re-distributed over the plant. Our biology
production suggests that growth under panels favors chard, then chile and then tomato in that order.
We also found that some plants grown under bifacial panels did better than plant under the opaque
panels that were positioned too low above the row crop. Future work may look at increased height of
the opaque panels to increase the light levels to a higher amount.
 This novel system (Figure 141) was found to create both energy and farming resilience. The
TEA results showed the high impact of added agriculture revenue due to increased yield is a key result
of this work.

5.1. Path Forward
Although the formal research phase has ended, the work at the deployed sites is expected to continue.
The farmers at the RGCF plan to use the system over the next several years and continue taking
harvest measurements to see how adjustments to the system can optimize growth and production.
They also plan to grow other annual and perennial crops. Based on the biological results to date, the
RGCF and UNM also plan to explore the impact of reduced irrigation on crop yield, comparing PV-
panel-covered lots vs. open control lots. They also are looking to assess how the growth season can
be extended due to warmer microclimate under the PV panels, preceding and continuing after the
normal growing season. For future studies, the team is also considering how the rain rolling off the
 113

panels should be collected and re-distributed over the plant. In a larger future study, the team is also
considering the application of less frequent watering to assess the reduction of water use created by
the panels. The team would also like to identify all power utilization applications, like electric tractors
(Figure 142), as well as potential outcomes for specific crops and relate them to quantifiable TEA
market values. The team with the La Puente Broadband Initiative also plans to continue to explore
ways to deploy and spur adoption of this unique agrivoltaic microgrid technology in norther New
Mexico and investigate other applications that can be of benefit to rural communities.
