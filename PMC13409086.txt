Neonatal encephalopathy mortality rate in Aotearoa, New Zealand: review of national data 2010–2020
Authors: Malcolm Battin, Joseph Hii, Wendy Burgess, Jutta van den Boom, Gabrielle McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal encephalopathy (NE) is a condition that affects newborn and can lead to long-term neurological impairments and death. Hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy is the subset of NE with evidence of a hypoxic ischaemic insult. In Aotearoa New Zealand, national data have been collected for infants ≥37 weeks since 2010 and ≥35 weeks from 2016. Although the mortality rate associated with NE has not been a focus of annual reports, it is an important outcome for counselling whānau (families) and for international comparison.MethodsThe Perinatal and Maternal Mortality Review Committee (PMMRC) dataset was used to calculate mortality rate for cases of moderate/severe NE for two gestational groups, 35 weeks to 36 weeks+6 days (2016–20) and ≥37 weeks (2010–2020). Annual mortality rates in infants ≥37 weeks with NE for the period 2010–20 were calculated overall and by prioritised ethnic group.ResultsIn the PMMRC dataset, there was frequently a history of fetal distress or antenatal sentinel event with the vast majority of included infants having low Apgar scores and requiring resuscitation at birth. The mortality rate was 0.23/1000 births (95% CL 0.17–0.29) for infants ≥37 weeks with no significant change over the study period. The mortality rate for infants with NE between 35 and 36+6 weeks was 0.68/1000 births (95% CL 0.31–1.28), giving a relative risk of 2.99 in preterm compared with term infants with NE. No statistical difference in mortality by prioritised ethnic group was demonstrated.ConclusionsThere has been no improvement in mortality rates for infants with NE despite ongoing efforts to improve early recognition and treatment. Preterm infants were at significantly higher risk of death. The analysis did not demonstrate any ethnic differences in mortality associated with NE.Keywords: Neonatology, Epidemiology, Hypoxia

Background

The adverse outcomes from antenatal and intrapartum events include mortality and long-term disability.1–3 Two key conditions contribute to this burden. Neonatal encephalopathy (NE) is the overarching term describing a ‘clinical syndrome of disturbed neurologic function in the earliest days of life in an infant born at or beyond 35 weeks of gestation, manifest by a subnormal level of consciousness or seizures….’.4 5 Hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) is the most common subgroup of NE with diagnosis based on evidence to support a hypoxic ischaemic injury.5 6 Rates of moderate or severe NE have been reported in Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ) at approximately 1.2/1000 livebirths.7 Although comparison across different healthcare settings may be limited by variability in study design, data sources and criteria used,8 it is acknowledged that much higher rates are reported from low-income countries.8 9 Given the recognised potential for adverse outcomes that encompass death and lifelong disability, it is crucial to both measure and ultimately decrease this disease burden.

National strategic interventions may act to prevent NE or to improve detection and provide optimally timed treatment. Therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is a standard of care for infants with confirmed HIE in high-income countries10 11 and is the only treatment proven in controlled trials to improve outcomes.12–15 Overall, TH has been reported to reduce the composite outcome of death or disability at 18 months.16 The implementation of strategic interventions requires good quality data to understand injury mechanisms and estimate the impact on potential pathways including acute obstetric (sentinel) events, for example, shoulder dystocia, placental abruption, uterine rupture or umbilical cord incidents; intrapartum deterioration and postnatal collapse. Population dataset analysis may also identify clinical or demographic factors associated with mortality.

Since 2010, Aotearoa NZ has collected national-level data on all infants with moderate or severe NE in the first 7 days after birth, through the Perinatal and Maternal Mortality Review Committee (PMMRC). The purpose of this has been to review the data and use the resulting information to improve care and outcomes. As it is not feasible to obtain umbilical cord gases in all affected cases due to equipment unavailability, outside of the secondary and tertiary centres, some of the included infants do not have biochemical confirmation of a hypoxic ischaemic insult. Information from these cases may contribute to learnings and practice improvement, so they are not excluded from the dataset and the working group uses the overarching terminology NE.7 Although a hypoxic ischaemic insult is not universally proven, it is the focus and a history of fetal distress or antenatal sentinel event is common. Furthermore, the vast majority of included infants have low Apgar scores and require resuscitation at birth. Data were initially collected on babies with NE from 37 weeks’ gestation onwards, then extended to all infants from 35 weeks’ gestation in 2016.17 Information is reported annually17 including: the rate of NE per 1000 births; demographic and clinical characteristics; and the use of TH and imaging with MRI. Several data items have been reported by year to review trends and some (eg, MRI and cord gases) have been the subject of quality improvement recommendations. In addition, the dataset facilitates case review18 19 and observational research.20 21 However, the question of mortality in infants with NE born in Aotearoa NZ has not been a prior research focus. Determining the mortality rate for preterm NE compared with term NE will provide local information for counselling whānau (families), particularly, given concerns that premature infants may require more inotropic support,22 have more complications and have a higher mortality than term infants.23 24 It will also allow comparison with international data, facilitating greater understanding of local epidemiology.

Method

The study data were obtained from the PMMRC database, which includes national data on infants with NE, in addition to Perinatal and Maternal Mortality data. For NE data, the collection process was initiated by a paediatric notification utilising an initial mandatory form, with further information submitted by a PMMRC local co-ordinator and the mother’s lead maternity carer (LMC). The LMC under the NZ maternity system is a midwife, obstetrician or general practitioner responsible for care throughout pregnancy, labour and birth. This data submission process facilitates a national, high-quality and complete dataset that is stored and analysed centrally. Case severity was judged by use of the modified Sarnat Score and determined clinically by the team submitting the entry. Similarly, data on mortality were submitted by a network of PMMRC local co-ordinators. The dataset includes infant and maternal clinical data plus demographics,17 validated using standard queries, which eliminates duplicate records; prompts completion of missing mother or infant information and clarifies region of domicile then rectifies inconsistencies. This novel PMMRC set is then integrated with the National Maternity Collection (MAT), housed by the New Zealand Ministry of Health, which records all births in Aotearoa NZ, over 20 weeks or over 400 g, and is compiled from data collected by the mother’s LMC. This integrated dataset can then be utilised for approved research projects.

An application was made to the PMMRC and approval given for use of non-identified data for the research purposes, covering the period 2010–2020 inclusive. As this research used a pre-existing dataset collected under appropriate legislation it did not include Patient and Public Involvement in design or conduct.

In the PMMRC dataset, ethnicity is reported as prioritised ethnicity, as is frequently used in health statistics in Aotearoa NZ, and largely follows guidelines in Health Information Standards Organisation 10001:2017 Ethnicity Data Protocols.25 Multiple ethnicities can be recorded for both mother and baby. PMMRC protocols prioritise ethnicity into the following hierarchy: Māori; Pacific peoples; Indian; Other Asian; Middle Eastern, Latin American or African (MELAA); Other European; Other; and New Zealand European.

Statistical methods

The analyses use the MAT dataset to provide the denominator in the calculation of rates and also to facilitate comparison of clinical and demographic characteristics between those with and without NE. The mortality rate in the first 7 days after birth was calculated for each year and expressed per 1000 live births over the period 2010–20 to identify any trends. The data were then used to calculate mortality rate for infants in the two gestational groups (a) 35 to 36 weeks+6 days and (b) ≥37 weeks with NE for the period 2016–2020. To ensure full case ascertainment death was further checked against wider PMMRC data (ie, including beyond 7 days). This process was based on methods used in the PMMRC report.26 To identify any associated demographic factors and gauge impact of receipt of TH, subanalysis was performed by prioritised infant ethnic group and by TH status for the epoch 2016–2020. We recognise that due to small numbers, there is potential for such analysis to be underpowered. However, it is important to include this even if just descriptive at this point.

Clinical data are presented as median (IQR), mean (SD) or number (%) as appropriate. Where categorical variables were compared, the χ2 test was used. Trends in rates over time were tested with regression analysis. ORs or relative risks were calculated as appropriate. Statistical significance where tested was set at p<0.05. The statistical programmes R27 and Stata28 were used to analyse the data.

The dataset was collected under legislation covered by the NZ Public Health and Disability Act 2000, now the Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) Act 2022, which established the mortality review committees including PMMRC. The legislation outlines the responsibilities of the PMMRC for ensuring data are kept confidential. Patient consent was not required for this publication but data are presented grouped with individuals not identifiable and any numbers less than three not given to prevent potential identification. Finally, any differences in outcome are recognised as a clinical need or healthcare system effect rather than individual issue.

Results

During the time period 2010–2020, there were 613 77517 live births at 37 weeks and above in Aotearoa NZ. A total of 737 infants had moderate or severe NE, resulting in a NE rate of 1.2 per 1000 term live births. Within this cohort, 144 of the 737 infants died with 128 deaths occurring within 7 days of birth, which equated to an overall case-fatality rate of 19.5% and an NE associated mortality rate of 0.23/1000 live births (95% CL 0.17–0.29) in infants born at 37 weeks or above. Of the total deaths, 33 (22.9%) occurred on day 0, 30 (20.8%) on day 1, 23 (16%) day 2, 20 (13.9%) on day 3, 22 (15.3%) on days 4–7 and 16 (11.1%) beyond day 7. The place of death was neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) 112 (77.8%), Birth Suite 18 (12.5%), in other hospital settings (postnatal or paediatric ward or Accident and Emergency Department) and 7 (4.9 %) were other/unknown or at home. The demographic and basic clinical details for this group are summarised in table 1, reported by survival status. As late preterm infants greater than 35 weeks’ gestation were not added to the NE dataset until 2016, the table is restricted to term births, which were collected for the whole period 2010–2020 inclusive. Although moderate NE (69.2%) was more common than severe NE (30.8%), the preponderance of mortality (91.0%) occurred in infants reported to have severe NE (table 1), which was significant (p<0.0001).

Preterm NE

Data were available from 2016 to address the second objective of comparing NE associated mortality in the two gestational age groups. For the period 2016–2020 inclusive, there were 13 305 live births from 35 to 36+6 weeks. Demographic and clinical features are given in table 2. In total, 34 of these infants had moderate or severe NE, resulting in a (preterm) NE rate of 2.56 per 1000 live births for babies during this period. Within this cohort, 9/34 died, which equated to a case-fatality rate of 26.5% and a mortality rate associated with NE of 0.68/1000 births (95% CL 0.31–1.28) in infants from 35 to 36+6 weeks’ gestation at birth. When these data are compared with term mortality data, the relative risk is 2.99 (95% CL 1.49–6.01) for death in the late preterm cohort, which is significantly increased (p=0.002) compared with term.

Therapeutic hypothermia

A subanalysis performed by TH status, for infants ≥35 weeks gestation born during the period 2016–2020, was performed. It determined that 25/71 infants who died compared with 52/277 who survived did not receive TH, which gave an OR of death in those who did not receive TH of 2.35 (95% CL 1.33–4.17). A similar OR of 1.84 (95% 1.00–3.4) was obtained in a calculation restricted to the 317 infants transported and treated with TH. Importantly, all infants with moderate NE who died had received TH.

Ethnicity

Table 3 documents the mortality rate over the epoch 2010–2020 for live births occurring over 37 weeks gestation in the four largest infant ethnic groups. There was no statistically significant difference demonstrated between ethnic groups. It is recognised that numbers are small when divided by ethnicity, which precludes analysis by individual year or trend. Although the 35 and 36-week gestation cohort had higher rates of NE and higher mortality overall, the numbers were small with only a single mortality in some ethnicity groups. Therefore, it was not possible to report meaningful data on mortality rate by ethnicity for the late preterm group.

Discussion

There are three key findings from this analysis of mortality in infants diagnosed with NE. First, the mortality for term infants with NE was 0.23/1000 births for the period 2010–2020, with the vast majority occurring in infants with severe NE and no significant change over time. Second, there was approximately 2.99 times higher mortality for late preterm infants compared with term infants with NE during the period 2016–2020. Third, there was no difference in mortality rate by ethnicity, which reflects the similar overall rates of moderate and severe NE across Māori, Pacific peoples, Indian and NZ European in the PMMRC report.17

Death was reported in approximately a quarter to third of infants in the cooled arm of the original trials.12–15 However, recent published reports of mortality associated with NE, from national data collections or regional networks, document a lower range of 9%–21%.29–33 Accordingly, the observed 19.5% mortality in term infants with NE in our study is at the upper end of the typically described range. However, the reported populations differ in some respects, particularly with the denominator often being infants who received TH rather than those with documented moderate and severe NE. Inclusion of infants with mild NE who received TH could reduce the overall acuity and thus decrease mortality. Other potential differences, including case ascertainment and the time period of reported deaths (ie,first 7 days vs neonatal period or childhood), were less likely to account for differences. In our analysis, we have reported the first 7 days, but there was also an ability to ensure complete data ascertainment by linkage with national mortality data, which is similar to other studies.31 33

Analysis of our data did not demonstrate temporal changes in NE associated mortality over 2010–2020. In similar published reports, one UK study reviewing data on moderate/severe HIE between 2011 to 2013 and 2014 to 2016, respectively, reported a decrease in mortality for infants 36 weeks or greater.29 Interestingly, in this UK study, the overall HIE rate did not change during the study period, but receipt of TH did increase. So mild HIE cases being diagnosed and treated as moderate HIE were considered a potential explanation for the lower mortality together with earlier recognition and treatment of HIE plus improved intensive care management.29 In our data, both the rate of NE and the proportion receiving TH were stable with no statistically significant change observed, so mild NE being treated as moderate is unlikely. Although national programmes aiming to decrease the NE incidence in NZ34 have been initiated, these were only late in the studied period with implementation incomplete for the epoch reported. Therefore, we consider the current mortality associated with NE to be a ‘baseline’, which offers potential to gauge within the local health system the impact of any ongoing strategic population intervention. Expressing mortality as a rate/1000 live-births as recommended35 has an advantage in standardisation that will prevent changes in clinical practice impacting on the denominator estimate, but this approach requires extra analysis and access to a population dataset for a denominator.

The higher mortality rate observed for preterm infants has been reported in other cohorts24 29 33 but was not present in others.36 As late preterm infants were only added to our dataset in 2016, we had a limited number of preterm deaths to review. Potential mechanisms for differences in outcome between term and late preterm infants have been reviewed.24 33 Although the available data on Apgar score and cord gas in our preterm cohort were comparable with the larger term cohort, additional clinical data would be needed to explore this further. The UK publication (reporting late preterm infants with HIE) represents a larger dataset but still suggested ongoing research.29 Finally, a recent publication reporting a randomised trial of TH in infants 33–35 weeks’ gestation with HIE reported that provision of TH did not reduce death or disability in infants born at this gestational range.37

The overarching purpose of this project has been to analyse available data for the purpose of improving care and outcomes. As stated previously, it was not possible to obtain umbilical cord gases in all cases due to equipment unavailability. However, the clinical characteristics in table 1 illustrate that it is justifiable to compare outcomes with other published cohorts reporting outcomes following HIE and/or receipt of TH. A few other aspects merit further discussion, such as deaths occurring in infants who did not receive TH and death in infants initially diagnosed as moderate NE. Importantly, rather than being interpreted as being a treatment effect, this is more likely to reflect disease severity at presentation. Indeed, not receiving TH or not undergoing transfer is more common with severe NE or infants presenting in extremis, which may result in palliation at the local hospital. Also, a decision to redirect care may follow review at the tertiary unit, review of MRI or EEG data or secondary deterioration not responding to support. Indeed, all of these could impact decisions about TH, but the outcome is likely to be linked with disease severity rather being a treatment effect or reflecting health system issues. Unit practices may vary, but it is important to understand any mechanism of delay in access to TH. Such knowledge may inform quality improvement projects and national programmes, which enable timely access. Apropos the fact that not all deaths occurred in the severe NE group, it is noted that a small number of infants initially considered to have moderate NE unfortunately died, despite all receiving TH. Review of the dataset reveals that these infants deteriorated in the period after NICU admission, often with systemic organ failure.

A strength of the current study is that the data were generated by a diagnosis of moderate or severe NE, using consistent criteria, rather than receipt of TH. This ensures that data are not influenced by therapeutic drift in treatment. Furthermore, there is excellent data acquisition including cross reference with national birth registration and mortality data. Ethnicity designation was consistent with other work using a prioritised approach. However, it is recognised that there is diversity within each ethnic group and that prioritisation may not allocate the ethnic group with which the individual identifies most strongly. Finally, the dataset undergoes rigorous checking and data cleaning, so the PMMRC represents the gold standard for complete and accurate perinatal mortality data in Aotearoa NZ. However, there is the potential limitation of small numbers providing unstable estimates when looking at rates of an uncommon event, which could be exacerbated by splitting groups based on severity or demographics including ethnicity.

In summary, this research documents the key outcome measure of mortality at a national level, which ensures high-quality information is available to counselling whānau (families) and to facilitate international benchmarking. The clear difference in mortality associated with gestational age is helpful in counselling but requires further research into the mechanisms. Finally, continuity in criteria for inclusion ensure data reflect clinical care and can be used to evaluate strategic national programmes.
