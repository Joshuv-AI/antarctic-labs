Estimation of the sub-Gaussian parameter
Jason Liu, Min Xu, Jinchuan Xing

Abstract
The sub-Gaussian parameter (also called the variance proxy) of a mean-zero random variable X X is defined as ξ ∗ 2 = sup λ ∈ ℝ L ⁡ ( λ ) \xi^{2}_{*}=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L(\lambda) where L ⁡ ( λ ) = 2 λ 2 ​ log ⁡ 𝔼 ​ e λ ​ X L(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\log\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X} is a weighted cumulant generating function. Despite the ubiquity of sub-Gaussian random variables, the estimation of ξ ∗ 2 \xi^{2}_{*} has received little attention and is not yet well understood. In this work, we study a natural estimator of ξ ∗ 2 \xi^{2}_{*} based on constrained maximization of the empirical analogue of L L . We prove that the estimator is consistent bound the rates of convergence under assumptions on L L : if L L has an maximizer, then our bound is O p ( n − 1 / 2 + ε ) O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}) for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 ; if the argmax of L L is also bounded, then the bound improves to O p ( n − 1 / 2 ) O_{p}(n^{-1/2}) . We show that our assumptions on L L are necessary by proving that the minimax risk over all sub-Gaussian distributions is Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) ; imposing increasingly strong assumptions on the tail growth of L L yields a continuum of classes whose minimax lower bound interpolates between Ω ⁡ ( 1 / log ⁡ n ) \Omega(1/\log n) and Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) . Root-n rate is possible if we restrict to a subclass of distributions where L L attains its supremum in a bounded region, in which case our estimator is minimax optimal. If the underlying distribution is not sub-Gaussian, we show that our estimator goes to infinity with a divergence rate controlled by the tail of the distribution. Finally, we apply our estimator in a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment study to construct p-values for a large-scale permutation test, showing that it can serve as a reliable alternative to the peaks-over-threshold approach, particularly in regimes where the peaks-over-threshold method is of uncertain validity.

Introduction

A random variable
X
is called sub-Gaussian if there exists
\xi^{2}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\xi^{2}/2}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
; we let
\xi^{2}_{*}\geq 0
denote the smallest such number and refer to it as the sub-Gaussian parameter of
X
, also called the variance proxy. Sub-Gaussian random variables play a central role in machine learning and statistics because they admit an exponential tail bound
\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq\exp\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\xi_{*}^{2}}\}
(the sub-Gaussian condition implies that
\mathbb{E}X=0
). This is in fact an equivalent characterization: any random variable satisfying such a tail bound is sub-Gaussian. Given independent and identically distributed random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from a sub-Gaussian distribution
P
, we study the estimation of the sub-Gaussian parameter
\xi^{2}_{*}
.

Our motivation comes from using empirical sub-Gaussian tail bounds in large-scale permutation tests. Suppose we are testing
K
hypotheses and, for each hypothesis
k
, use
M
random permutations to generate the null test statistic samples
T^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,T^{(k)}_{M}
. The empirical p-value is then bound below by
\frac{1}{M+1}
, so after a multiple testing correction such as Bonferroni, the power becomes trivial when the number of hypotheses is large compared to
M
; this is often the case when the permuted test statistics are expensive to compute so that
M
cannot be too large. However, if we know a priori that the null distribution is sub-Gaussian (which holds for example if the test statistic is bounded), then we can instead plug in an estimate of
\xi^{2}_{*}
into the sub-Gaussian tail bound to obtain p-value much smaller than
\frac{1}{M+1}
and recover power in regimes where the empirical p-values are powerless.

Beyond empirical tail bounds, estimating
\xi^{2}_{*}
is also useful for testing the sub-Gaussianity of a random variable, which is of interest in cases where the theoretical risk bounds assume sub-Gaussianity or where the estimation algorithms are designed for settings where the data is sub-Gaussian.

Our estimation approach is based on the observation that any
\xi^{2}>0
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\xi^{2}/2}
must be larger than
L(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\log\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X}
for any
\lambda\neq 0
. The function
L
is continuous with a removable singularity at 0 so that we can define
L(0)=\text{Var}(X)
by continuity. Therefore,
\xi^{2}_{*}
can be equivalently defined as

\xi_{*}^{2}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L(\lambda).

Given independent and identically distributed observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, we define the empirical analogue of
L
as
L_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\log\bigl\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\bigr\}
for
\lambda\neq 0
and
L_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}
. One idea is to estimate
\xi^{2}_{*}
by taking
\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L_{n}(\lambda)
, i.e. the sub-Gaussian parameter of the empirical distribution, but this can incur excessive estimation error due to the unstable behavior of
L_{n}(\lambda)
when
\lambda
is relatively large, e.g. of order
\sqrt{\log n}
. We thus define our estimator as
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}=\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L_{n}(\lambda)
for a slowly diverging
C_{n}
which can be taken as
(\log n)^{1/4}
. The truncation introduces a bias whose magnitude is measured by the truncation gap function

\delta(C):=\sup_{|\lambda|\geq C}L(\lambda)-\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C}L(\lambda),\,\text{ for $C\geq 0$.}

The function
\delta(C)
is decreasing and has a non-positive limit as
C\rightarrow\infty
; the bias of
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
vanishes quickly if
\delta(C)
decays at a fast rate. Although the truncation gap function is motivated by our estimator, we prove that it in fact plays a role in determining the fundamental difficulty of estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}
through a minimax lower bound analysis. We summarize our key results as follows:

1.

Section 2:
We prove that
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
is always consistent when the underlying distribution is sub-Gaussian. If there is
C_{0}>0
such that
\delta(C_{0})\leq 0
, then we show
|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi^{2}_{*}|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. If
C_{0}
satisfies
\delta(C_{0})<0
, then we further show that
|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi^{2}_{*}|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. If in addition
\argmax L(\lambda)=\{\lambda^{*}\}
and
L^{\prime\prime}(\lambda^{*})<0
, then we prove that
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
is asymptotically normal and construct confidence interval for
\xi^{2}_{*}
.

2.

Section 3:
We prove that the minimax risk of estimating
\xi^{2}_{*}
is governed by the decay rate of
\delta_{P}
. For
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
, define the decreasing function

r_{\gamma}(t)=2^{\frac{1-2\gamma}{1-\gamma}}t^{\frac{2\gamma-1}{1-\gamma}}
.
The minimax risk over subclasses of distributions satisfying
\delta_{P}(C)\leq r_{\gamma}(C)

is then lower bounded by
\Omega\bigl(\frac{1}{\log^{1-2\gamma}(n)}\bigr)
. At
\gamma=1/2
, where
r_{1/2}(t)\equiv 1
so that no assumptions are made on
\delta_{P}
and the subclass coincide with the class of all sub-Gaussian distributions, the minimax risk is lower bounded by
\Omega(1)
, i.e. uniform consistency is impossible. At
\gamma=0
, where

r_{0}(t)=2t^{-1}
and where the resulting subclass encompasses all distributions supported on
[-1,1]
, the minimax lower bound improves to
\Omega(1/\log n)
. If we instead assume that there exists
C_{0},\delta_{0}>0
such that
\delta(C_{0})\leq-\delta_{0}
, then the minimax risk is of order
n^{-1/2}
and our estimator
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
is minimax optimal.

3.

Section 4:
We prove that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
diverges almost surely when the underlying distribution is not sub-Gaussian, where the rate of divergence is faster if the departure from sub-Gaussianity is more significant, i.e. if the distribution is more heavy-tailed.

2
An Estimator for the sub-Gaussian Parameter

For a sub-Gaussian distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
, we let
\xi^{2}_{*}(P)
denote its sub-Gaussian parameter. For
\Xi>0
, we define the class of distributions

\mathcal{P}(\Xi):=\{P\,:\,P\text{ sub-Gaussian},\,\xi_{*}^{2}(P)\leq\Xi\}.

To motivate our estimator, we begin with the following elementary observation about
\xi_{*}^{2}
.

Proposition 1
.

If
X\sim P
is sub-Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}
, then

\xi_{*}^{2}(P)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L(\lambda;P),\,\quad\text{ where }L(\lambda;P):=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\log\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{\lambda X}]&\lambda\neq 0\\
\sigma^{2}&\lambda=0\end{cases}

is a continuous function. When
P
is fixed, we will write
L(\lambda)\equiv L(\lambda;P)
.

Based on Proposition
1
, an immediate idea for estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}
is to replace
L
by its empirical analogue
L_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}
for
\lambda\neq 0
and
L_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}
. Centering the empirical distribution by subtracting the sample mean
\bar{X}
is critical even if
X_{i}
is already mean-zero, otherwise
|L_{n}(\lambda)|\rightarrow\infty
as
|\lambda|\rightarrow 0
. We may then consider estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}
via
\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L_{n}(\lambda)
. This however behaves poorly due to high variance of
L_{n}(\lambda)
for large
\lambda
. In this work, we instead study the truncated maximization of
L_{n}
. We let
C_{n}>0
be a slowly diverging sequence and define our estimator

\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}:=\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L_{n}(\lambda).

We will see in our theoretical analysis that
C_{n}
can be taken as
(\log n)^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.

2.1
Convergence of the empirical moment generating function

In order to study the consistency of
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
and quantify the rate of convergence, we must establish results on the uniform convergence of
L_{n}
to
L
. To do this, we first study the empirical moment generating function. We shall denote the moment generating function and its empirical analogues by

M(\lambda;P):=\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{\lambda X}],\quad M_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})},\quad\tilde{M}_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda X_{i}}.

Similarly, we shall write
\psi(\lambda;P):=\log M(\lambda)
,
\psi_{n}(\lambda)=\log M_{n}(\lambda)
, and
\tilde{\psi}_{n}(\lambda)=\log\tilde{M}_{n}(\lambda)
. When
P
is fixed, we will abbreviate
M(\lambda)\equiv M(\lambda;P)
and
\psi(\lambda)\equiv\psi(\lambda;P)
.

Proposition 2

(
5
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.4)
.

Let
I
be the largest interval on which
M(\lambda)
is finite, and define
\tilde{\psi}_{n}(\lambda)=\log\tilde{M}_{n}(\lambda)
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
K
be a compact interval. Let
\tilde{\psi}^{(k)}
be the
k
-th derivative of
\tilde{\psi}
and likewise for
\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{(k)}
.

(a)

If
K\subset I
, then
\sup_{\lambda\in K}|\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-\tilde{\psi}^{(k)}(\lambda)|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0
as
n\to\infty
.

(b)

If
K\subset I/2
, then the empirical process
\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-\tilde{\psi}^{(k)}(\lambda))
indexed by
\lambda
converges weakly to a mean-zero Gaussian process on
K
.

Since our truncation level
C_{n}
is diverging (albeit at a slow rate), we need control over the convergence of
\tilde{\psi}_{n}
and its derivatives beyond a fixed compact set. The next theorem establishes this for the empirical moment generating function.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Fix
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and set
C_{n}:=(\log n)^{\alpha}
. Let
P
be a sub-Gaussian distribution. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda;P)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}),

and this convergence is uniform over
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
.

Having established a result on the convergence of the empirical moment generating function, we can easily extend the result to cumulant generating function.

Corollary 1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Fix
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and set
C_{n}:=(\log n)^{\alpha}
. Let
P
be a sub-Gaussian distribution. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|\psi_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-\psi^{(k)}(\lambda;P)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}),

and this convergence is uniform over
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
.

Finally, control of the cumulant generating function extends directly to the
L_{n}
function that we are maximizing.

Proposition 3
.

Let
C_{n}>0
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
L_{n}
and
L
are differentiable everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
, and

\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-L^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|\psi^{(k+2)}_{n}(\lambda)-\psi^{(k+2)}(\lambda)|.

Consequently,

(a)

If
C>0
is fixed,
\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in[-C,C]}|L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-L^{(k)}(\lambda)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
.

(b)

If
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
, uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
,

\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-L^{(k)}(\lambda;P)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}).

2.2
Convergence of
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}

Equipped with the above propositions, we are ready to prove the main result of this section. Let us define

\delta(C)\equiv\delta_{P}(C):=\sup_{|\lambda|\geq C}L(\lambda;P)-\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C}L(\lambda;P),

and observe that
\delta(C)
is a decreasing function with
\lim_{C\to\infty}\delta(C)\leq 0
.

As we will show in Section
3.2
, the difficulty of this estimation problem is fundamentally related to the tail behavior of
\delta(C)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
P
be a sub-Gaussian distribution and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d samples from
P
. Then

|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi_{*}^{2}|\leq\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|+\delta_{P}(C_{n})\vee 0.

Consequently, if
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, then

(a)

\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\xi_{*}^{2}
;

(b)

If there is
C_{0}>0
such that
\delta(C_{0})\leq 0
, then
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\xi_{*}^{2}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
;

(c)

If
\delta(C_{0})<0
then
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi^{2}_{*}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

The proof of Theorem
2
reveals that, so long as
\delta(C_{0})=0
for some
C_{0}
, an exact rate of
n^{-1/2}
can be achieved by choosing
C_{n}\equiv C_{0}
. For example, if
X\sim N(0,1)
, then any such
C_{0}
would work. However, we do not know
C_{0}
a priori, so we cannot make this choice.

.

apply, or not.

Suppose

2.3
Inference

Under even stronger assumptions, we can also derive the limiting distribution of
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
.

Proposition 4
.

For
\lambda\neq 0
, define

V(\lambda):=\frac{4}{\lambda^{4}}\left(\frac{M(2\lambda)}{M(\lambda)^{2}}-2\lambda\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)+\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}-1\right).

Also, let
V(0):=\mathbb{E}X^{4}-\sigma^{4}
. Suppose that
L
is uniquely maximized at
\lambda^{*}
,
L^{\prime\prime}(\lambda^{*})<0
,
V(\lambda^{*})>0
, and there is
C_{0}
such that
\delta(C_{0})<0
.
Then, for any
C_{n}\to\infty
, we have

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\xi_{*}^{2})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V(\lambda^{*})).

The hypothesis that
L
be uniquely maximized is necessary: if
X\sim N(0,1)
, then
L(\lambda)\equiv 1
so that all of
\mathbb{R}
maximizes
L
. In this case, the maximizers of
L_{n}
will be entirely determined by stochastic fluctuations in
L_{n}
, so the limiting distribution is not Gaussian. We include the
N(0,1)

3

According to Proposition
4
, we may construct asymptotically valid confidence intervals by plugging in empirical estimates of the quantities involved. Let

\hat{V}_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{4}{\lambda^{4}}\left(\frac{M_{n}(2\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)^{2}}-2\lambda\psi_{n}^{\prime}(\lambda)+\lambda^{2}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}-1\right),

where
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
is any consistent estimator of
\sigma^{2}
. Then, if

denotes the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution and

denotes a maximizer of
L_{n}
over
[-C_{n},C_{n}]
, it follows that

\left[\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n})}{n}},\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n})}{n}}\,\right]

is an asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
-level confidence interval for
\xi_{*}^{2}
.

3
Minimax Risk of Estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}

In the previous section, we saw that the tail behavior of the truncation gap
\delta_{P}
determines the rate at which
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
converges. In Section
3.1
, we show that
\delta_{P}
governs the difficulty of estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}
itself, regardless of the estimator involved. To do this, we consider three regimes: first, when no uniform assumptions are made on
\delta_{P}
, we show that the minimax risk is
\Omega(1)
. If a uniform polynomial decay is imposed, then the risk becomes
\Omega(1/\log n)
, and if the
\delta_{P}
’s eventually become zero or negative, the risk becomes
\Omega(n^{-1/2})
. For the third regime, we show that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
matches the
n^{-1/2}
-rate, making it minimax optimal.

3.1
Lower bounds on the minimax risk

This subsection characterizes the difficulty of the estimation problem in terms of an
r
function that determines the uniform rate of decay for the
\delta_{P}
’s. Intuitively, slower rates of decay on
\delta_{P}
correspond to harder estimation problems because the supremum of
L
is more difficult to approximate, so any estimator must search for maximizers for an increasingly large range of
\lambda
.

To make this precise, let us write

\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi):=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi):\text{$P$ is compactly supported}\},

and given any
C_{0}>0
and decreasing function
r:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, define the classes

\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r):=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi):\delta_{P}(C)\leq r(C)\text{ for all $C\geq C_{0}$}\}

and

\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi,C_{0},r):=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi):\delta_{P}(C)\leq r(C)\text{ for all $C\geq C_{0}$}\}.

The following proposition characterizes membership in
\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi,C_{0},r)
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
. If
P
is supported on
[-1,1]
, then
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,0,r_{0})
where
r_{0}(t)=2/t
. If in addition
\sigma^{2}(P)\geq\frac{3}{2}\delta_{0}
, then
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,8\delta_{0}^{-1},r_{-})
where
r_{-}(t)=-\delta_{0}
.

Let us now define, for any
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
,

r_{\gamma}(t)=2^{\frac{1-2\gamma}{1-\gamma}}t^{\frac{2\gamma-1}{1-\gamma}},\quad t>0.

There are two values of
\gamma
that are particularly important. When
\gamma=1/2
,
r_{\gamma}(t)\equiv 1
, so that no rate of decay is imposed on
\delta_{P}
. Meanwhile, for
\gamma=0
, we have
r_{\gamma}(t)=2/t
, the rate of decay that corresponds to random variables supported on
[-1,1]
, as in Proposition
5
.

Let
0\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq 1/2
. It then follows that
r_{\gamma_{1}}(t)\leq r_{\gamma_{2}}(t)
for any
t\geq 2
, and therefore we have the inclusion

\mathcal{P}(\Xi,2,r_{\gamma_{1}})\subset\mathcal{P}(\Xi,2,r_{\gamma_{2}}).

Thus as
\gamma
increases,
\mathcal{P}(\Xi,2,r_{\gamma})
grows larger and estimation becomes harder. The next proposition quantifies this.

Proposition 6
.

There exists a universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that:

(a)

For any
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
, we have

\inf_{\hat{\xi}^{2}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi,0,r_{\gamma})}\mathbb{E}_{P}|\hat{\xi}^{2}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-\xi_{*}^{2}(P)|\geq\frac{c_{0}\cdot\Xi}{(\log 2n)^{1-2\gamma}}

and in particular,

\inf_{\hat{\xi}^{2}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi)}\mathbb{E}_{P}|\hat{\xi}^{2}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-\xi_{*}^{2}(P)|\geq c_{0}\cdot\Xi.

(b)

Let
\delta_{0}\in[0,1/2]
and set
r_{-}(t)=-\delta_{0}
; let
C_{0}\geq 16
. Then

\inf_{\hat{\xi}^{2}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})}\mathbb{E}_{P}|\hat{\xi}^{2}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-\xi_{*}^{2}(P)|\geq\frac{c_{0}\cdot\Xi}{\sqrt{n}}.

3.2
Upper bounds on the minimax risk via
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}

In Theorem
2
(a), we proved the pointwise consistency of
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
. If the tail of
\delta_{P}
can be uniformly controlled, then we may also get uniform consistency.

Proposition 7
.

Set
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
. For any
C_{0}>0
and decreasing function
r:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=0
, we have
|\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\xi_{*}^{2}(P)|\leq r(C_{n})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})=o_{p}(1)

uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r)
.

By Proposition
7
, we have that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,0,r_{\gamma})}\mathbb{E}_{P}|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi^{2}_{*}(P)|\leq O((\log n)^{\frac{\alpha(2\gamma-1)}{1-\gamma}})
when
C_{n}
is chosen as
(\log n)^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
. This does not match the lower bound of
(\log 2n)^{2\gamma-1}
even in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow 1/2
. It is an open question whether to improve the lower bound or the upper bound in order to close the gap.

The remainder of this section works towards a proof that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
achieves the rate in Proposition
6
(c), uniformly over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})
. First, we show that the maximizers of
L_{n}
and
L
lie inside a compact interval
[-C_{0},C_{0}]
with probability tending to 1, uniformly over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})
.

Proposition 8
.

Set
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
. Let
\Lambda^{*}_{n}
denote the maximizers of
L_{n}
in
[-C_{n},C_{n}]
and let
\Lambda^{*}(P)
denote the maximizers of
L
. Then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})}\mathbb{P}\left(\Lambda^{*}_{n}\cup\Lambda^{*}(P)\not\subset[-C_{0},C_{0}]\right)=0.

Proposition
8
says that, with high probability, we can identify
L_{n}
with its restriction to
[-C_{0},C_{0}]
, independent of
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})
, and similarly for
L
.

We shall use this to gain control over empirical moment generating function. To that end, define

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{x\mapsto x^{k}e^{\lambda x}:\lambda\in[-C_{0},C_{0}]\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}.

Informally, the next proposition gives an empirical process central limit theorem for
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, and guarantees that the convergence is uniform over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
. Precise definitions are given in Section
A.2.1
.

Proposition 9
.

\mathcal{F}_{k}
is Donsker and pre-Gaussian, both uniformly over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
.

Using Proposition
9
, we can prove the following, which is a re-statement of Proposition

(a).

Proposition 10
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As

, uniformly over

,

\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{0}}|L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-L^{(k)}(\lambda;P)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

We are now ready to make the convergence in Theorem

(c) uniform over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
C_{n}\to\infty
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over
\mathcal{P}(\Xi,C_{0},r_{-})
,
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\xi_{*}^{2}(P)=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Combined with Proposition

\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
achieves the optimal rate. Because

can be any sequence that diverges, we have also shown that
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
is adaptive in the sense that it does not require knowledge of
\Xi
,
C_{0}
, or
\delta_{0}
.

4
Rate of Divergence Under Violation of sub-Gaussianity

We now study the mis-specified case when the underlying distribution is not sub-Gaussian. In this setting, it is desirable for
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
to exhibit clear signs of divergence, which
would serve as qualitative evidence against the sub-Gaussian assumption. As we next
show,
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
diverges almost surely whenever sub-Gaussianity fails.

Proposition 11
.

Let
X
be a non-sub-Gaussian random variable, i.e. if
\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X}=\infty
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
or if for any
\xi^{2}>0
, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X}\geq e^{\lambda^{2}\xi^{2}/2}
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. copies of
X
. If
C_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, then
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\infty
.

Although Proposition
11
guarantees that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
will go to infinity, we still expect
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
to diverge faster when
X
has a heavier tail and thus a stronger departure from sub-Gaussianity. To quantify the rate of divergence, we state a Proposition which gives a deterministic lower bound on
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
in terms of the range of the data.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be any collection of real numbers and let
\Delta_{n}:=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|X_{i}-\bar{X}|
. If
\Delta_{n}\geq\frac{2\log n}{C_{n}}
, then
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}\geq\frac{\Delta_{n}^{2}}{2\log n}
.

If we set
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
for an
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, then Proposition
12
applies when
\Delta_{n}\geq(\log n)^{1-\alpha}
. Since
\Delta_{n}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log n})
for sub-Gaussian data (
20
, Exercise 2.12), we expect
\Delta_{n}
to be of larger order when
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are not sub-Gaussian. We consider some examples:

1.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are distributed according to the mean-zero Laplace distribution. Then it holds that
\Delta_{n}
is of order
\Theta_{p}(\log n)
so that the condition of Proposition
12
holds for all large enough
n
and we have that
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
diverges at rate
\Omega_{p}(\log n)
.

2.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
have the heavy tail t-distribution with degree
\nu>0
. Recall that the expectation exists only when
\nu>1
and the variance is finite only when
\nu>2
; the t-distribution coincides with the Cauchy distribution when
\nu=1
. In this case, we have that
|X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}|
is of order
\Theta_{p}(n^{1/\nu})
so that
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}
diverges at rate
\Omega_{p}(\frac{n^{2/\nu}}{\log n})
.

5
Application to Gene Ontology Enrichment Studies

In this section, we apply the sub-Gaussian estimator to the setting of large scale permutation tests in Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, a multiple testing problem with large numbers of hypotheses relative to the number of permutations. In this case, the empirical p-value is not granular enough; we propose using the sub-Gaussian tail bound instead.

Background
:
A standard goal in disease gene identification is to determine whether candidate genes obtained from high-throughput sequencing experiments are enriched for certain GO terms, which represent biological processes.

One method to do this
(
12
;
13
)
is as follows: gene scores representing significance are placed onto a gene-gene interaction (GGI) network. Then, regions exhibiting both high connectivity and candidate gene enrichment called
modules
are identified using
active module identification (AMI) algorithms
. Each (module, GO term) pair is then tested via the hypergeometric test, and the resulting negative log-10 p-value is called the
HG-enrichment score
. The
enrichment

Z^{\text{obs}}_{g}
is the maximal enrichment across all modules, and if
Z^{\text{obs}}_{g}
is greater than some threshold,
g
is called
HG-significant
.

To test the statistical significance of the
Z^{\text{obs}}_{g}
’s, a standard approach are permutation tests: generate
n
permutations of the original data, rerun the full pipeline on each, and obtain scores
Z^{(i)}_{g}
for each
g\in[G]
and
i\in[n]
. HG-significant GO terms whose empirical p-value passes Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction at level
\alpha
are called
empirically validated.

The empirical p-value obtained by this test is no smaller than
n^{-1}
. However,
n
is typically much smaller than
G
because obtaining permutation requires rerunning the full module detection pipeline. As a result, after multiple testing correction such as BH, it can be impossible to reject any GO terms. This motivates new procedures to generate valid p-values for each GO term
g
that are fine-grained enough to pass false discovery rate corrections.

The sub-Gaussian transform
:
Fix a GO term
g\in[G]
. If
Z^{(i)}_{g}
is a sub-Gaussian distribution with parameter
\xi_{*}^{2}(g)
, then the sub-Gaussian tail bound would justify the use of the p-value

\mathbf{p}_{g}^{\text{sG}}:=\exp\biggl(-\frac{(Z^{\text{obs}}_{g}-\bar{Z}_{g})^{2}}{2\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}(g)}\biggr)

where
\bar{Z}_{g}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{g}^{(i)}
and
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}(g)
is the value of the estimator given
Z^{(1)}_{g},\dots,Z^{(n)}_{g}
.
Such an assumption can be made in this context because GO enrichment scores are bounded (the largest enrichment score attainable is
\log_{10}{N\choose N/2}
where
N
is the number of background genes), and therefore sub-Gaussian. However, the sub-Gaussian tail inequality using this bound on the distribution is too loose, and it is necessary to use a more precise estimator like
\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}(g)
.

The Peaks-over-thresholds method
:
Another approach
(
9
)
is to define the
excess function

F_{g}^{(u)}(y)=\frac{F_{g}(u+y)-F_{g}(u)}{1-F_{g}(u)}=\mathbb{P}(Z_{g}-u\leq y\,|\,Z_{g}>u)

where
F_{g}(u)
is the distribution function of
Z_{g}
under the null and
u\in\mathbb{R}
is a threshold parameter. According to the Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem, as
u\to\sup\{x:F_{g}(x)<1\}
,
F_{g}^{(u)}
converges in distribution to a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) with parameters
m_{g},s_{g},k_{g}
. This motivates fitting a GPD to the
exceedances
\{Z^{(b)}_{g}-u_{g}:Z^{(b)}_{g}>u_{g}\}
,
yielding estimates
\hat{m}_{g}
,
\hat{s}_{g}
, and
\hat{k}_{g}
, and
the p-value

\mathbf{p}_{g}^{\mathrm{POT}}:=(1-\hat{F}_{g}(u_{g}))\left(1+\frac{\hat{k}_{g}(Z^{\text{obs}}_{g}-u_{g}-\hat{m}_{g})}{\hat{s}_{g}}\right)^{-1/\hat{k}_{g}},

where
\hat{F}_{g}
is the empirical distribution function. In practice,
u_{g}
must be chosen with care. If it is too large, there may be too few exceedances to obtain a reliable fit. A common choice for
u_{g}
is the 90-quantile.

Method Comparison
:
We next compare the GO terms rejected by both methods on widely used complex gene datasets. Following
13
, we used the “TNFa” dataset
(
15
)
with the “DIP" network
(
21
)
and the “Fly Transcriptome”
(
17
)
dataset with the “STRING" network
(
18
)
. We used the PAPER
(
2
)
, DOMINO
(
12
)
, and FDRnet
(
22
)
AMI algorithms. We excluded the “Aneuploidy1” and “Aneuploidy2” datasets because they exhibited very low numbers of HG-enriched GO terms, and similarly for the HotNet2
(
10
)
algorithm.

For each algorithm and dataset, we compute
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{sG}}_{g}
and
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{POT}}_{g}
per GO term, apply BH correction at level
\alpha=0.05
, and in Table

On the Fly Transcriptome dataset, the sub-Gaussian transform yields more empirically validated GO terms under PAPER and comparable results for DOMINO and FDRnet. On the TNFa dataset, POT achieves more rejections across all three algorithms. The relative performance therefore depends heavily on the null distribution, and the two methods are best understood as complementary.

The POT method is subject to different modes of failure. First, the rate of convergence can be slow and very distribution dependent. For example, if the underlying distribution is normal, the convergence of the empirical excess function
F^{(u)}_{g}
to a GPD is of order
1/u^{2}
as
u\to\infty

(
3
)
, but the threshold
u_{g}
can grow at most at the rate of the sample maximum, which is
O(\sqrt{\log n})
for sub-Gaussian data. The rate is therefore
O(1/\log n)
, which is slow enough that in our case of permutation testing where
n
is relatively small, it is not clear whether the asymptotic regime has been reached. In this case, the p-values produced may not be valid. Second, for PAPER on the Fly Transcriptome dataset, 255 out of 279 (91.4%) of the POT fits have
\hat{k}_{g}<0
, which corresponds to a distribution supported on
[0,m_{g}-s_{g}/k_{g}]
. Among these fits, 8 satisfied
Z_{g}^{\text{obs}}>m_{g}-s_{g}/k_{g}
resulting in a p-value of 0, which is a clear underestimate. Third, the standard choice of
u_{g}
as the 90th quantile does not diverge as
n\to\infty

u_{g}
does not have a general solution
(
3
)
.

On the other hand,
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
may also exhibit slow convergence under certain distributions (for example
\frac{1}{2}N(0,1)+\frac{1}{2}N(0,2)
). If the underlying distribution is heavy tailed, the divergence of
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
would make the p-values useless in comparison to the POT approach. In practice, we view these methods as complementary; when the data is sufficiently light-tailed the sub-Gaussian transform may be more reliable, in other cases POT may be better.

6
Discussion

We have studied the estimation of the sub-Gaussian parameter
\xi_{*}^{2}
and proposed an estimator
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
based on the truncated maximization of the empirical analogue
L_{n}
. One of our main findings is that the truncation gap
\delta_{P}
controls the fundamental difficulty in estimating
\xi_{*}^{2}
: without control over
\delta_{P}
no estimator can be uniformly consistent; stronger decay assumptions on
\delta_{P}
lead to faster minimax rates, culminating at a rate of
n^{-1/2}
at which
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}
is minimax optimal and adaptive. Under departures from sub-Gaussianity, the estimator diverges almost surely at a rate depending on the range of the observed data, providing a natural diagnostic of mis-specification. Our GO enrichment application demonstrate that for large scale permutation testing, sub-Gaussian tail bounds can act as a complementary approach to the POT method.

There are a few limitations to our work. For one, the hypotheses of Theorem
2
and Proposition
4
are difficult to deduce from observed data, though in practice the heuristic suggested in Section
2.3
may give a loose estimate of the regime. The rate in Theorem
2
(b) has an additional
\varepsilon
term which is not known to be sharp, and a more detailed analysis is required to close this gap. While taking
C_{n}=(\log n)^{\alpha}
is theoretically sound, a data-driven choice would be more practical. Finally, the minimax lower bound in Proposition
6
(b) is not met, and it is an open problem to determine the minimax rate when other conditions on
\delta
are imposed. Addressing these gaps are natural directions for future work.

\tilde{M}^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{k}e^{\lambda X_{i}}\leq B^{k}e^{B|\lambda|}\leq B^{k}e^{2BC},

so by Hoeffding’s inequality again

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\tilde{M}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|>\frac{t}{2e^{|\lambda|\delta_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2n}{(2B^{k}e^{2BC})^{2}}\frac{t^{2}}{4e^{|\lambda|\delta_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{8B^{2k}e^{4BC+2|\lambda|\delta_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{8B^{2k}e^{4BC+1}}\right)

(2)

where the last inequality is valid for large enough
n
, since
\delta_{n}\to 0
. Setting
\delta_{n}:=n^{-1/4}
in (
1
) and (
2
) yields the desired conclusion.
∎

Equipped with Proposition
13
, we are ready to prove a weaker analogue of Theorem
1
, first for bounded random variables.

Proposition 14
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Fix
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
such that
\alpha+\beta<1
, and set

C_{n}:=(\log n)^{\alpha},\quad B_{n}:=(\log n)^{\beta}.

Assume that
M(\lambda)
exists for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and that
\max_{i\in[n]}|X_{i}|\leq B_{n}
almost surely. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}).

Proof.

First we note that, because
\alpha+\beta<1
, we have

\exp(C_{n}B_{n})=\exp((\log n)^{\alpha+\beta})=n^{(\log n)^{\alpha+\beta-1}}=n^{o(1)}.

(3)

For each
n
, let
\Pi_{n}\subset[-C_{n},C_{n}]
be a
n^{-1}
-covering set of
[-C_{n},C_{n}]
of size
N:=C_{n}n+1
. Then, for any
\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
, there is
\pi_{\lambda}\in\Pi_{n}
such that
|\lambda-\pi_{\lambda}|<n^{-1}
, and the following holds by the triangle inequality:

|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})|+|M_{n}^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})-M^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})|+|M^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|.

(4)

Let us first address the middle term of (
4
). By Proposition
13
and then (
3
),

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}|M_{n}^{(k)}(\pi)-M^{(k)}(\pi)|>n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\leq N\mathbb{P}\left(|M_{n}^{(k)}(\pi)-M^{(k)}(\pi)|>n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\\
\leq 2N\left\{\exp\left(\frac{-2n^{1/2}}{4B^{2}_{n}}\right)+\exp\left(\frac{-n^{2\varepsilon}}{8B_{n}^{k}e^{4C_{n}B_{n}+1}}\right)\right\}\\
\leq 2\{n(\log n)^{\alpha}+1\}\left\{\exp\left(\frac{-2n^{1/2}}{4(\log n)^{2\beta}}\right)+\exp\left(\frac{-n^{2\varepsilon}}{8(\log n)^{k\beta}n^{o(1)}}\right)\right\}

which vanishes in the limit
n\to\infty
.
It then follows that

\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}|M_{n}^{(k)}(\pi)-M^{(k)}(\pi)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}).

(5)

Next, with probability one,
\max_{i}|X_{i}|\leq B_{n}
. On that event, for all
\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
,

M_{n}^{(k+1)}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k+1}e^{\lambda|X_{i}-\bar{X}|}\leq(2B_{n})^{k+1}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}},

which implies by the mean-value theorem that for any
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
, there is
\tilde{\lambda}
such that

|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda^{\prime})|\leq|M_{n}^{(k+1)}(\tilde{\lambda})||\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|\leq(2B_{n})^{k+1}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|

and in particular

|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}_{n}(\pi_{\lambda})|\leq(2B_{n})^{k+1}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}n^{-1}.

(6)

Identical reasoning then yields

|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})|\leq(2B_{n})^{k+1}e^{C_{n}B_{n}}n^{-1}.

(7)

Now by (
3
), we have

2B_{n}^{k+1}e^{C_{n}B_{n}}n^{-1}=2(\log n)^{\beta(k+1)}n^{-1+o(1)}=o(n^{-1/2}),

so it follows that, with probability one,

|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}_{n}(\pi_{\lambda})|=o(n^{-1/2}),\quad|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\pi_{\lambda})|=o(n^{-1/2}).

(8)

We conclude by putting (
5
) and (
8
) into (
4
) to get

|M_{n}(\lambda)-M(\lambda)|\leq o(n^{-1/2})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})+o(n^{-1/2})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}).

∎

Our next goal is to extend the result of Proposition
14
to sub-Gaussian random variables. To do this, we first recall some facts about them.

Proposition 15
.

Let
X
be a sub-Gaussian random variable with parameter
\xi_{*}
. Then:

(a)

For all
t>0
,
\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}X|>t)\leq\exp\left(-t^{2}(2\xi_{*})^{-2}\right).

(b)

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}(|X|^{k})\leq 2\xi_{*}^{k}\Gamma(k/2+1)
.

(c)

For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
M(\lambda)\leq\exp(2^{-1}\lambda^{2}\xi_{*}^{2})
.

We shall also need the following generalization of Hölder’s inequality:

Proposition 16

(
6
, Exercise 6.31)
.

Let
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
satisfy
p^{-1}+q^{-1}+r^{-1}=1
, and let
(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)
be a measure space. If
f\in L^{p}(\mu),g\in L^{q}(\mu),h\in L^{r}(\mu)
, then
fgh\in L^{1}(\mu)
. Moreover,

\int|fgh|\,d\mu\leq\left(\int|f|^{p}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int|g|^{q}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int|h|^{r}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}.

The idea is to show that sub-Gaussian random variables can be well-approximated by truncating their range. We may then apply Proposition
14
to conclude.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix
\beta\in(\alpha,1-\alpha)
and let
B_{n}:=(\log n)^{\beta}
. Then, set
X_{i}^{\flat}:=X_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{|X_{i}|\leq B_{n}\}
. Accordingly, let
\bar{X}^{\flat}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\flat}
, and

M_{n}^{\flat}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})},\quad M^{\flat}(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X^{\flat}_{1}}].

Thus, by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq

\displaystyle|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-(M^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)|\ +

\displaystyle|(M^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)-(M^{\flat}_{n})^{(k)}(\lambda)|+|(M^{\flat}_{n})^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|.

(9)

For the first term, we note that if
|X_{1}|\leq B_{n}
, then
X^{\flat}_{1}=X_{1}
. Therefore

|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-(M^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq\mathbb{E}\left|(X^{\flat}_{1})^{k}e^{\lambda X^{\flat}_{1}}-X_{1}^{k}e^{\lambda X_{1}}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{k}e^{C_{n}X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}\{|X_{1}|\geq B_{n}\}.

(10)

Then, we use the generalized Hölder inequality (Proposition
16
) with exponents
(3,3,3)
, and then apply Proposition
15
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|X_{1}^{k}e^{C_{n}X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}\{|X_{1}|\geq B_{n}\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\{\mathbb{E}|X_{1}^{3k}|M(3C_{n})\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\geq B_{n})\}^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{2\Xi^{3k}\Gamma\left(\frac{3k}{2}+1\right)\exp\left(\frac{9}{2}C_{n}^{2}\Xi^{2}-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}}\right)\right\}^{1/3}.

(11)

Now note that since
\alpha<\beta
,

\exp\left(\frac{9}{2}C_{n}^{2}\Xi^{2}-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{9}{2}\Xi^{2}(\log n)^{2\alpha}-\frac{(\log n)^{2\beta}}{2\Xi^{2}}\right)

(12)

which decays faster than any polynomial; in particular it is
o(n^{-2})
.
Thus, putting (
10
), (
11
), and (
12
) together shows that

|M^{(k)}(\lambda)-(M^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)|=o(n^{-1/2}).

(13)

The bound for the sample analogue of (
13
) follows the same strategy, but is slightly more involved. To begin, we note that if,
|X_{j}|\leq B_{n}
for all
j\in[n]
, then
X^{\flat}_{j}=X_{j}
so
\bar{X}^{\flat}=\bar{X}
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}|(M_{n}^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})^{k}e^{\lambda(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})}-(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}|\\
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|\left\{(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})^{k}e^{\lambda(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})}-(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\right\}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|.

(14)

Then the triangle inequality gives us two terms to bound:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|\left\{(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})^{k}e^{\lambda(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})}-(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\right\}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}^{\flat}-\bar{X}^{\flat})}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|\\
+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|

(15)

The first one can be dealt with by evaluating the expectation, using a union bound, Chernoff’s inequality, and then (
12
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|(X^{\flat}_{i}-\bar{X}^{\flat})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}^{\flat}-\bar{X}^{\flat})}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left|(2B_{n})^{k}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2B_{n})^{k}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2B_{n})^{k}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}n\mathbb{P}(|X_{1}|>B_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(2B_{n})^{k}e^{2C_{n}B_{n}}n\exp\left(-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}}\right)=o(n^{-1/2}).

(16)

Next, we move onto the second term of (
15
). By the generalized Hölder inequality and then a union bound,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\mathbbm{1}\{\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n}\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}-\bar{X}|^{3k})\mathbb{E}[e^{3\lambda(X_{1}-\bar{X})}]\mathbb{P}(\exists j:|X_{j}|>B_{n})\}^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}-\bar{X}|^{3k})\mathbb{E}[e^{3\lambda(X_{1}-\bar{X})}]n\mathbb{P}(|X_{1}|>B_{n})\}^{1/3}

(17)

Now note that
\bar{X}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
n^{-1}\xi_{*}
, so
X_{1}-\bar{X}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
(\xi_{*}^{2}+n^{-1}\xi_{*}^{2})^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2}\xi_{*}\leq\sqrt{2}\Xi
. Therefore by Proposition
15
(c),
\mathbb{E}[e^{3\lambda(X_{1}-\bar{X})}]\leq\exp(9\lambda^{2}\Xi^{2})
. Then, applying Chernoff’s inequality to (
17
), using the fact that
|\lambda|\leq C_{n}
, and then finally using (
12
), we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}-\bar{X}|^{3k})\mathbb{E}[e^{3\lambda(X_{1}-\bar{X})}]n\mathbb{P}(|X_{1}|\geq B_{n})\}^{1/3}\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}-\bar{X}|^{3k})\}^{1/3}\exp(3\lambda^{2}\Xi^{2})\exp\left(-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{6\Xi^{2}}\right)\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{k/2}\Xi^{k}\{\Gamma(3k/2+1)\}^{1/3}\exp\left(3C_{n}^{2}\Xi^{2}-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{6\Xi^{2}}\right)=o(n^{-1/2}).

(18)

Combining (
14
), (
15
), (
16
), (
17
), and (
18
) shows that
\mathbb{E}|(M_{n}^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|=o(n^{-1/2})
. Then Markov’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}(|(M_{n}^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|>n^{-1/2})\leq\frac{2|(M_{n}^{\flat})^{(k)}(\lambda)-M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|}{n^{-1/2}}=o(1).

(19)

The parts are in place to conclude. Since the
X^{\flat}_{i}
’s are bounded by
(\log n)^{\beta}
, they satisfy the hypotheses of Proposition
14
. Therefore we may apply it to the middle term of (
9
). As we have shown before, the first and third terms can be handled by (
13
) and (
19
), respectively. Thus,

|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|\leq O_{p}(n^{-1/2})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})+o(n^{-1/2})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}),

which completes the proof.
∎

A.1.2
Consequences of Theorem
1

We now address the corollaries of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. According to Jensen’s inequality,
M(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\geq e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}X}=1
, and
M_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda(X_{i}-\bar{X})}\geq e^{\lambda\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})}=1
. Then, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then the sub-Gaussianity of
X
,

\displaystyle M^{(k)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X^{k}e^{\lambda X}]\leq\{\mathbb{E}X^{2k}M(2\lambda)\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\{\mathbb{E}X^{2k}\exp(2\lambda^{2}\Xi^{2})\}^{1/2}\leq\{\mathbb{E}X^{2k}\exp(2C_{n}^{2}\Xi^{2})\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbb{E}X^{2k}\exp(2(\log n)^{2\alpha}\Xi^{2})\}^{1/2}=o(n^{\varepsilon/2})

(20)

where the last step uses
2\alpha<1
so that
(\log n)^{2\alpha-1}\to 0
. Now by a direct computation we have

\psi^{(k)}(\lambda)=\frac{M^{(k)}}{M}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j-1}\psi^{(j)}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)},

and similarly

\psi^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{M^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j-1}\psi^{(j)}_{n}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}.

Also, let us observe that, because
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary in Theorem
1
, it holds that

\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon/2}).

(21)

The remainder of the proof goes by induction on
k
.

Base case
: By the mean-value theorem applied to
x\mapsto\log x
,

|\psi_{n}(\lambda)-\psi(\lambda)|\leq\tau^{-1}|M_{n}(\lambda)-M(\lambda)|

where
\tau\in[M_{n}(\lambda)\wedge M(\lambda),M_{n}(\lambda)\vee M(\lambda)]
. But
M_{n}(\lambda)\wedge M(\lambda)\geq 1
, so
\tau\geq 1
and actually

|\psi_{n}(\lambda)-\psi(\lambda)|\leq|M_{n}(\lambda)-M(\lambda)|.

Inductive step:
Suppose the claim holds for any
j<k
. Then we have

|\psi^{(k)}(\lambda)-\psi^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)|\\
\leq\left|\frac{M^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\frac{M^{(k)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j-1}\left|\psi^{(j)}_{n}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\psi^{(j)}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)}\right|.

For the rest term, since
M(\lambda)\geq 1
and
M_{n}(\lambda)\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{M^{(k)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\frac{M^{(k)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}\right|+\left|\frac{M^{(k)}(\lambda)\{M_{n}(\lambda)-M(\lambda)\}}{M(\lambda)M_{n}(\lambda)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)\right|+\left|M^{(k)}(\lambda)\{M_{n}(\lambda)-M(\lambda)\}\right|.

(22)

The first term is
O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
by (
21
), and the second is
O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
by (
20
) and (
21
) again. Meanwhile, for each term in the summation, we have

\left|\psi^{(j)}_{n}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\psi^{(j)}(\lambda)\frac{M^{(k-j)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)}\right|\\
\leq\left|\psi_{n}^{(j)}-\psi^{(j)}\right|\left|\frac{M^{(k-j)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}\right|+|\psi^{(j)}|\left|\frac{M^{(k-j)}_{n}(\lambda)}{M_{n}(\lambda)}-\frac{M^{(k-j)}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)}\right|.

The first term is
O_{p}(n^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
by the inductive hypothesis and (
20
). The second follows by similar reasoning to (
22
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

We claim first that the following formula holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
:

L(\lambda)=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\psi^{\prime\prime}(\lambda x)\,dx,\quad L_{n}(\lambda)=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\psi^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\lambda x)\,dx.

(23)

To see this, note that
\psi(0)=\psi^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}X=0
so by Taylor’s theorem and then a change of variable,

\psi(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}(\lambda-x)\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\psi^{\prime\prime}(\lambda x)\,dx

so the claim follows by recalling
L(\lambda)=2\lambda^{-2}\psi(\lambda)
. Meanwhile, since
\psi^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sigma^{2}
, we have

L(0)=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\psi^{\prime\prime}(0)\,dx.

Identical reasoning holds for
L_{n}
, noting that
\psi^{\prime}_{n}(0)=0
too. Now since
\psi^{(k)}
is twice continuously differentiable, we may differentiate under the integral sign to obtain

L^{(k)}(\lambda)=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)x^{k}\psi^{(k+2)}(\lambda x)\,dx,\quad L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)x^{k}\psi^{(k+2)}_{n}(\lambda x)\,dx.

Then it follows that, for any
\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
,

\displaystyle|L_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-L^{(k)}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)x^{k}|\psi^{(k+2)}(\lambda x)-\psi_{n}^{(k+2)}(\lambda x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{t\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|\psi^{(k+2)}(t)-\psi^{(k+2)}_{n}(t)|\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)x^{k}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|\psi^{(k+2)}(t)-\psi^{(k+2)}_{n}(t)|

where the last step follows because
\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)x^{k}\,dx=(k+1)^{-1}(k+2)^{-1}\leq 1/2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Define
\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{2}:=\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L(\lambda)
. We then have

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi^{2}_{*}|

\displaystyle\leq|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{n}|+\xi_{*}^{2}-\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{n}|+\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}L(\lambda)-\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}L(\lambda)

\displaystyle=|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{n}|+\left\{\sup_{|\lambda|>C_{n}}L(\lambda)-\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L(\lambda)\right\}\vee 0

\displaystyle=|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{2}|+\delta(C_{n})\vee 0.

Since
L
and
L_{n}
are continuous, there is
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
such that
\tilde{\xi}_{n}=L(\tilde{\lambda}_{n})
, and
\lambda^{*}_{n}\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]
such that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}=L_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n})
. Then

\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{2}\leq L_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n})-L(\lambda^{*}_{n})\leq\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|

and

\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{2}\geq L_{n}(\tilde{\lambda}_{n})-L(\tilde{\lambda}_{n})\geq-\sup_{\lambda\in[-C_{n},C_{n}]}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|

so that
|\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{n}|\leq\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|
. Therefore

|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\xi_{*}^{2}|\leq\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|+\delta(C_{n})\vee 0.

(24)

(a) By Proposition
3
(b), we have
\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|=o_{p}(1)
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Since
C_{n}\to\infty
,
\delta(C_{n})\to 0
.

(b) Whenever
n
is large enough that
C_{n}>C_{0}
, we have
\delta(C_{n})\leq 0
, so the claim follows from (
24
).

(c) Make
r>0
so small that
\sup_{|\lambda|>C_{0}}L(\lambda)<\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{0}}L(\lambda)-r
. It follows that
\xi_{*}^{2}=L(\lambda^{*})
for some
\lambda^{*}\in[-C_{0},C_{0}]
. Then, since
L_{n}(\lambda^{*})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}L(\lambda^{*})
, if
n
is large enough, we have on one hand

\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{0}}L_{n}(\lambda)\geq L_{n}(\lambda^{*})\geq L(\lambda^{*})-\frac{r}{2}

and on the other

\sup_{C_{0}<|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L_{n}(\lambda)\leq\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|+\sup_{C_{0}<|\lambda|}L(\lambda)\leq o_{p}(1)+\xi_{*}^{2}-r\leq\xi_{*}^{2}-\frac{r}{2}

so that

\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C}L_{n}(\lambda)>\sup_{C<|\lambda|\leq C_{n}}L_{n}(\lambda).

It follows that none of
(C,C_{n}]
can be a maximizer. Thus with high probability, the maximizers of
L_{n}
contained in
[-C_{0},C_{0}]
, so it actually holds that

|\hat{\xi}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{n}|\leq\sup_{|\lambda|\leq C_{0}}|L_{n}(\lambda)-L(\lambda)|.

Proposition
3
(a) then applies to yield

Proposition 17

between

A.2.1
Uniform Donsker theorems

The following definitions are due to
19
. Let
\mathbb{D}
be a normed vector space, and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{D})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{D}
(generated by the open sets under the norm topology of
\mathbb{D}
). For a probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},P)
, we define the
outer expectation
for any function
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
(even non-measurable) as

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{P}X:=\inf\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}Y:\text{$Y$ is $(\mathscr{F},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))$-measurable and $Y\geq X$}\right\}.

If
(\Omega_{n},\mathscr{F}_{n},P_{n})
are a sequence of probability spaces and
X_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{D}
are arbitrary, then
X_{n}

converges weakly
to a Borel probability measure
P
on
\mathbb{D}
if as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{P_{n}}f(X_{n})\to\mathbb{E}_{P}f(X)

for any
f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
continuous and bounded. If
\mathbb{D}
is separable, then by Theorem 1.12.1 of
19
, weak convergence is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{h\in\mathrm{BL}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}|\mathbb{E}^{*}_{P}h(X_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{P}h(X)|\to 0,

where

\mathrm{BL}_{1}(\mathbb{D})=\{h:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}:\text{$h$ is 1-Lipschitz and $\|h\|_{\mathbb{D}}<\infty$}\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions on
\mathbb{R}
. For any function
z:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

\|z\|_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|z(f)|

and make

\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F}):=\left\{z:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}:\|z\|_{\mathcal{F}}<\infty\right\}

into a Banach space by endowing it with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a random sample. The
X_{i}
’s induce an empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
P
-Donsker
if the empirical process if the empirical process
\mathbb{G}_{n,P}:=\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{P}_{n}-P)
converges weakly in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
to a tight Borel measurable element
\mathbb{G}_{P}
of
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
. That is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{h\in\text{BL}_{1}}|\mathbb{E}^{*}_{P}h(\mathbb{G}_{n,P})-\mathbb{E}h(\mathbb{G}_{P})|=0.

(28)

If this convergence is uniform over
\mathcal{P}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is
Donsker uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{h\in\text{BL}_{1}}|\mathbb{E}^{*}_{P}h(\mathbb{G}_{n,P})-\mathbb{E}h(\mathbb{G}_{P})|=0.

If the mean-zero Gaussian process
\mathbb{G}_{P}
with covariance function
(f,g)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{P}f(X)g(X)-\mathbb{E}_{P}f(X)\mathbb{E}_{P}g(X)
has a version that is a tight Borel measurable element of
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
, then
\mathcal{F}
is
P
-pre-Gaussian.
If
\mathcal{F}
is
P
-pre-Gaussian for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, and it holds that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\|\mathbb{G}_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\sup_{\rho_{P}(f-g)<\delta}|\mathbb{G}_{P}(f)-\mathbb{G}_{P}(g)|=0

where
\rho_{P}(f)=\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}\{f(X)-\mathbb{E}_{P}f(X)\}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is
pre-Gaussian uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
.

In order to prove Proposition
9
, we shall need the following result:

Proposition 21

(
19
, Theorem 2.8.4)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a class of distributions, and
\mathcal{F}
be a class of measurable functions with envelope function
F
that satisfies

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(F^{2}(X)\mathbbm{1}\{F(X)>R\})=0.

Furthermore, assume that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon\|F\|_{P,2},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P))}\,d\varepsilon<\infty

where
N_{[\,]}
denotes bracketing number. Then
\mathcal{F}
is Donsker and pre-Gaussian, both uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof of Proposition
9
.

An envelope function for
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is
F(x):=|x|^{k}e^{C|x|}
. Note that, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then Proposition
15
,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}F(X)\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\{\mathbb{E}_{P}X^{2k}\mathbb{E}_{P}e^{2CX}\}^{1/2}\leq 2\Xi^{2k}k!\exp(4C^{2}\Xi^{2})<\infty.

Then, by the generalized Hölder inequality, Markov’s inequality, and then Proposition
15
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(F^{2}(X)\mathbbm{1}\{F(X)>R\})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}(X^{2k}e^{CX}\mathbbm{1}\{F(X)>R\})

\displaystyle\leq\{\mathbb{E}_{P}(X^{6k})M(3C)\mathbb{P}(F(X)>R)\}^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq R^{-1/3}\{\mathbb{E}_{P}(X^{6k})M(3C)\mathbb{E}_{P}F(X)\}^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq R^{-1/3}\{8\Xi^{6k}(3k)!\exp(9C^{2}\Xi^{2})\}\to 0,

as
R\to\infty
. Therefore the envelope condition holds. Moving onto the bracketing entropy, let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Now we have, for any
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in[-C,C]
,

|x^{k}e^{\lambda x}-x^{k}e^{\lambda^{\prime}x}|\leq|x^{k+1}e^{\tilde{\lambda}x}||\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|\leq|x^{k+1}e^{Cx}||\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|,

where
\tilde{\lambda}
is prescribed by the mean-value theorem. Then, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\left\|x^{k+1}e^{Cx}\right\|_{P,2}\leq\{\mathbb{E}_{P}X^{2k+2}e^{2CX}\}^{1/2}\leq\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}X^{4k+4}M(4C)\right\}^{1/4}\\
\leq\{2\Xi^{4k+4}\Gamma(2k+3)\exp(8C^{2}\Xi^{2})\}^{1/4}=:D(k,C,\Xi)

(29)

so

\left\|m_{\lambda}^{(k)}-m^{(k)}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\|_{P,2}\leq D(k,C,\Xi)|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|.

Set
\delta:=\varepsilon/D(k,C,\Xi)
, and let
\Pi_{\delta}
be a
\delta
-net over
[-C,C]
such that
|\Pi_{\delta}|\leq\lceil 2C\delta^{-1}\rceil=\lceil 2CD(k,C,\Xi)\varepsilon^{-1}\rceil
. Now for each
\pi\in\Pi_{\delta}
, we define

\ell_{\pi}(x):=m^{(k)}_{\pi}(x)-\delta\,|x|^{k+1}e^{C|x|},\quad u_{\pi}(x):=m^{(k)}_{\pi}(x)+\delta\,|x|^{k+1}e^{C|x|}.

Then, whenever
\lambda\in[\pi-\delta,\pi+\delta]
, we have
m_{\lambda}^{(k)}\in[\ell_{\pi},u_{\pi}]
, and furthermore

\left\|u_{\pi}-\ell_{\pi}\right\|_{P,2}\leq 2\delta D(k,C,\Xi)\leq 2\varepsilon.

We thus have

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}N_{[\,]}\!\left(\varepsilon,\,\mathcal{F}_{k},\,L_{2}(P)\right)\leq\left\lceil\frac{4CD(k,C,\Xi)}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil.

Finally, if
\varepsilon>4CD(k,C,\Xi)
, then
N_{[\,]}\!\left(\varepsilon,\,\mathcal{F}_{k},\,L_{2}(P)\right)=1
, so we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}\!\left(\varepsilon,\,\mathcal{F}_{k},\,L_{2}(P)\right)}\leq\int_{0}^{4CD(k,C,\Xi)}\sqrt{\log\left\lceil\frac{4CD(k,C,\Xi)}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil}\,d\varepsilon<\infty.

We conclude via Proposition
21
.
∎

Proof.

Recalling that

\displaystyle|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\leq|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-\tilde{M}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|+|\tilde{M}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-M^{(k)}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle=|M_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)-\tilde{M}_{n}^{(k)}(\lambda)|+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

A.3
Proofs for Section 4

Before we present the proof of Proposition
11
, we shall need a brief lemma.

Proposition 23
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim P
and write
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}
. If
\mathbb{E}_{P}X=\infty
, then
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\infty
.

Proof.

Observe that

\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}.

Fix
R>0
, and let

T_{R}(x)=\begin{cases}R&x>R\\
x&-R\leq x\leq R\\
-R&x<-R.\end{cases}

It then follows that
|X_{i}-X_{j}|\geq|T_{R}(X_{i})-T_{R}(X_{j})|
, and therefore by the law of large numbers for U-statistics
(
8
)
, we have

\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(T_{R}(X_{i})-T_{R}(X_{j}))^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathrm{Var}(T_{R}(X_{1})).

It thus suffices to prove that
V_{\infty}:=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(T_{R}(X_{1}))=\infty
; to that end we proceed by contradiction. Suppose that
V_{\infty}<\infty
. For any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, pick
R>|x|
. Then if
|X_{1}|\leq x
, we have
|X_{1}|\leq R
so
T_{R}(X_{1})=X_{1}
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|X_{1}|\leq x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(|T_{R}(X_{1})|\leq x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(|\mathbb{E}T_{R}(X_{1})-T_{R}(X_{1})|\geq\mathbb{E}T_{R}(X_{1})-x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(T_{R}(X_{1}))}{(x-\mathbb{E}T_{R}(X_{1}))^{2}}

(30)

where the last inequality is Chebyshev’s. Now since
T_{R}(X_{1})\to X_{1}
pointwise as
R\to\infty
, we have by Fatou’s lemma that
\liminf_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}|T_{R}(X_{1})|\geq\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|=\infty
.
So pick any sequence
R_{k}\to\infty
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(T_{R_{k}}(X_{1}))=V_{\infty}
and take the limit
k\to\infty
in (
30
) to obtain
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\leq x)=0
. But
x
was arbitrary, so this is a contradiction.
∎

Now we can prove Proposition
11
.

Proof of Proposition

.

First suppose
M
fails to exist at some
\lambda
, and
\mathbb{E}X<\infty
. Provided
n
be large enough,
\lambda
will be contained in
[-C_{n},C_{n}]
, and by a law of large numbers for infinite means (
4

\tilde{M}_{n}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\infty

\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}\geq 2\lambda^{-2}\psi_{n}(\lambda)=2\lambda^{-2}\log\tilde{M}_{n}(\lambda)-2\lambda^{-1}\bar{X}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\infty.

If
M
does not exist at
\lambda
but
\mathbb{E}X=\infty
, then we have by Proposition

that
\hat{\xi}_{n}^{2}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\infty

On the other hand, if

there is

. But

.

For any
