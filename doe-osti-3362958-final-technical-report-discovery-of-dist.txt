EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Plastics are foundational materials in modern manufacturing, enabling lightweight, durable, and low-cost
products across packaging, consumer goods, health care, and energy systems. However, the dominant
linear model for plastics production and disposal creates long-term vulnerabilities in materials supply
chains. Most plastics are derived from fossil feedstocks that are often imported, while current recycling
technologies recover value inefficiently. As a result, large volumes of plastic are lost from the materials
economy after a single use. Developing circular, low-energy pathways to recover and repurpose plastic
waste is therefore essential to strengthening domestic manufacturing capacity, reducing reliance on
imported virgin petrochemical inputs, and enabling resilient, distributed biomanufacturing systems.
This Department of Energy–supported project advanced the scientific foundation required to integrate
biological plastics deconstruction into future circular materials supply chains. While enzymatic recycling of
poly(ethylene terephthalate), or PET, is now approaching commercial deployment, biological routes for
other high-volume plastics have remained uncertain due to conflicting and often irreproducible prior reports.
A central contribution of this project was resolving key uncertainties surrounding biological deconstruction
of low-density polyethylene (LDPE), one of the most widely produced and discarded plastics.
Using rigorously controlled materials characterization methods, the project established that yellow
mealworms deconstructed LDPE through the activity of their gut microbiota rather than through mechanical
processing or host-only effects. The work identified dye-decolorizing peroxidases (DyPs) as essential
enzymes that initiate LDPE bio-oxidation, a critical first step that activates an otherwise inert polymer for
downstream processing. Structural and mechanistic analyses revealed that LDPE-active DyPs share
conserved features that enable polymer binding and oxidation on the enzyme surface via long-range
electron transfer to the enzyme surface, identifying actionable targets for enzyme engineering. Together,
these results provided clear mechanistic evidence for biological initiation of LDPE deconstruction and
reduced uncertainty surrounding non-PET plastics biodegradation.
In parallel, the project revealed a complementary, non-enzymatic mechanism that contributes to plastics
processing in microbial communities. While studying microbial isolates enriched during LDPE feeding, the
team identified fungal species that did not directly oxidize plastics yet produced hydrophobins, amphiphilic
proteins that bind strongly to plastic surfaces and promote microplastic aggregation and biofilm formation.
These hydrophobins enabled capture and flocculation of microplastic particles from aqueous environments
and facilitated close physical association between plastics and plastic-oxidizing microbes. Subsequent work
demonstrated that such surface-active proteins can create synergistic mechanical and metabolic
interactions within microbial communities, accelerating overall plastic oxidation and processing. This finding
broadened the conceptual framework of biological plastics deconstruction by demonstrating that effective
plastic processing can emerge from coordinated community-level functions rather than single enzymatic
pathways alone.
To accelerate discovery and development of plastic-active enzymes, the project also addressed a major
technical bottleneck: low-throughput validation methods. A high-throughput, fluorescence-based assay was
developed to detect LDPE oxidation via selective measurement of aldehyde formation. This platform
increased screening capacity from tens to thousands of enzyme variants per day while maintaining strong
agreement with established oxidation metrics. The assay enables rapid enzyme discovery and engineering,
supporting scalable development of biological plastics processing technologies suitable for
biomanufacturing workflows.
Finally, the project established best practices for biological plastics deconstruction research by identifying
common sources of false positive reporting and proposing standardized experimental and analytical
guidelines. These contributions, combined with extensive publicly released multi-omic datasets, workforce
development through PhD training, and follow-on DOE investments, provide durable infrastructure that
reduces technical risk and accelerates future progress. Collectively, this project delivered mechanistic
insight, validated discovery tools, community-level understanding, and trained personnel that position
biological plastics deconstruction as a credible component of future low-energy, domestic materials supply
chains aligned with Department of Energy priorities.

FINAL PROJECT ACCOMPLISHMENTS
The project was broadly aimed at discovering and reconstructing biochemical pathways for the
deconstruction of non‑hydrolyzable plastics within the gut microbiome of yellow mealworms (Tenebrio
molitor larvae). The original objectives were to
 1. Perform host enrichment to discover microbes and enzymes from mealworm guts associated with
 plastic degrading phenotypes
 2. Use labeled chemical probes to trace plastics metabolism within the gut microbiome, elucidate
 molecular mechanisms by which plastics are processed, and reconstruct minimal synthetic
 consortia
 3. Reconstruct and characterize stable plastic‑degrading microbial communities and metabolic
 pathways
As the project progressed, its scope narrowed to focus on enzymes active on low‑density polyethylene
(LDPE), resulting in substantial advances toward Objectives 1 and 2. Based on key technical findings and
delays associated with a change in performance location for one co‑principal investigator, Objective 3 was
intentionally redirected. Rather than reconstructing full microbial consortia, the effort pivoted to establishing
best practices for enzyme discovery and validation in plastics biodegradation. This shift addressed
widespread challenges in the field related to irreproducibility and false positive reporting, and ultimately
positioned the project to accelerate progress and reduce technical risk for future plastics deconstruction
research.
Objective 1 Accomplishments
The feasibility of insect-mediated plastic deconstruction has been widely debated due to conflicting and
often irreproducible results in the literature. In this objective, we definitively established that yellow
mealworms deconstructed low-density polyethylene (LDPE) through the activity of their gut microbiota and
identified the enzymatic mechanisms responsible for initiating this process.
We first demonstrated LDPE deconstruction directly by measuring changes to the polymer itself.
Mealworms fed commercially available LDPE reduced the number-average molecular weight of the plastic
by up to 31% over 30 days (Figure 1A). To confirm that this effect reflected modification of the polyethylene
chains rather than degradation or removal of additives, we repeated these experiments using LDPE films
stripped of all additives. Molecular weight reduction persisted in these additive-free samples, confirming
true polymer deconstruction. Importantly, LDPE deconstruction was completely abolished when mealworm
guts were sterilized with antibiotics and antifungals (Figure 1B), demonstrating that an intact gut
microbiome was required and ruling out mechanical chewing or host-only effects (Figure 1A).
Microbial isolates cultivated from LDPE-fed mealworm guts oxidized polyethylene films, introducing
chemically reactive carbonyl groups characteristic of early-stage polymer activation (Figures 1C-E).
Genomic analysis of these isolates revealed enrichment in enzyme families capable of oxidizing
hydrocarbons, suggesting a mechanistic link between microbial metabolism and plastic modification.
Among these candidates, we identified dye-decolorizing peroxidases (DyPs) as the enzymes responsible
for initiating LDPE oxidation. DyP genes were similarly highly enriched in metagenomes of LDPE- and
HDPE-fed mealworms but were absent in non-plastic diet controls.
Purified DyPs chemically modified additive-free LDPE films, forming aldehydes and ketones without
requiring chemical pre-treatment of the plastic (Figure 2A). This activity was enzymatic in origin, as
oxidation signals persisted only after stringent washing and were eliminated by enzyme inactivation
controls. Functional inhibition experiments further confirmed the central role of DyPs. Mealworms co-fed a
competing DyP substrate showed no adverse health effects yet completely lost their ability to reduce LDPE
molecular weight (Figure 2C). Together, these results established DyPs as essential biological catalysts in
the mealworm LDPE deconstruction pathway. Although DyPs alone did not cleave carbon–carbon bonds
in polyethylene (Figure 2B), their activity represented the critical first step in biological LDPE deconstruction
by activating an otherwise inert polymer for downstream processing.
Because DyPs are widespread in nature, we tested related enzymes from outside the mealworm gut and
found that many also oxidized LDPE. Structural analysis revealed that LDPE-active DyPs shared two key
features: a hydrophobic loop that promoted polymer binding and surface-exposed aromatic residues that

mediated long-range electron transfer. Mutating either feature dramatically reduced LDPE oxidation.
Together, these features enabled DyPs to oxidize polyethylene through a non-canonical, surface-based
mechanism that did not require the polymer to enter the enzyme’s active site. This body of work is currently
being prepared for a high impact publication.
While this systematic approach successfully identified a critical bottleneck enzyme, it was not amenable to
high-throughput enzyme engineering or large-scale enzyme discovery. Thus, we developed and validated
a high-throughput, fluorescence-based assay to detect enzymatic oxidation of low-density polyethylene
(LDPE), addressing a major bottleneck in the discovery of polyolefin-active enzymes (Figure 3). The assay
used a selective fluorogenic probe, NBD-H, which reacted with aldehydes formed during LDPE oxidation
to produce a quantifiable fluorescence signal. We demonstrated that the assay reliably detected polymer
oxidation on additive-free LDPE films and that fluorescence correlated strongly with established carbonyl
index measurements, confirming that the signal reflected true chemical modification of the polymer. The
platform accurately distinguished active polyethylene-oxidizing dye-decolorizing peroxidases from inactive
controls and achieved strong diagnostic performance, with an area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve of 0.95. By enabling parallel screening of thousands of enzyme candidates within 24
hours using standard microtiter plates and automated liquid handling, this assay dramatically increased
discovery throughput relative to conventional spectroscopic methods and provides a scalable screening
tool to accelerate enzyme discovery and engineering for biological plastics deconstruction. This was
published in Biochemical Engineering Journal.
Finally, while studying microbial isolates from the mealworm gut microbiome, we observed that some
microbes enriched during LDPE feeding did not directly oxidize plastics or catalyze bond cleavage yet
remained plastics-active. Notably, isolates of Aspergillus fumigatus produced microplastic-flocculating
hydrophobins, amphiphilic secreted proteins that enable adhesion to and biofilm formation on plastic
surfaces (Figure 4). This work was published in ACS Environmental Science & Technology. Subsequent
work in a related DOE project currently underway (DE-SC0023085) suggests that these organisms form
synergistic mechanical and metabolic interactions with plastic-oxidizing microbes, accelerating overall
plastic oxidation.
Objective 2 Accomplishments
For Objective 2, the team at PNNL made substantial progress toward developing chemical tools to enable
discovery of plastic-binding and plastic-degrading enzymes and microbes. The team synthesized a suite of
photoreactive chemical probes designed to mimic oligomeric fragments of high-volume plastics, including
polystyrene, polyethylene, and polypropylene (Figure 5A). These probes were intentionally designed to
resemble short polymer segments rather than monomers in order to minimize off-target interactions and
improve specificity for biologically relevant binding events (Figure 5B). Each probe incorporated three
functional elements: an oligomeric binding moiety that mimicked the target plastic, an alkyne handle to
enable downstream click chemistry attachment of reporter groups, and a diazirine group to permit covalent
crosslinking to interacting proteins upon photoactivation (Figure 5A).
The probes were functionalized with fluorescent reporter tags and validated in both cell lysates and
whole-cell systems (Figure 5C-D). In microbial isolates from plastic-fed worm gut microbiomes, the probes
selectively labeled discrete proteins in a dose-dependent manner following photoactivation, confirming
specific probe–protein interactions (Figure 5C). Proteomic analyses of these labeled proteins were
consistent with promiscuous alkane degradation pathways for polyolefin degradation, in agreement with
the metagenomic analyses of Objective 1. The probes were further used to characterize microbial isolates
capable of binding or transporting plastic-like molecules. Fluorescent labeling enabled identification of
microbial taxa enriched for probe uptake and interaction (Figure 5D), providing a foundation for linking
specific organisms with plastic-associated functions. In contrast, control organisms such as E. coli strains
not known to interact with plastics showed little to no labeling, demonstrating probe selectivity and low
nonspecific background (Figure 5D). These results validated the probes as effective tools for identifying
plastic-binding proteins and species.
Collectively, these efforts established a versatile chemical biology platform for probing plastic–microbe and
plastic–enzyme interactions. The successful validation of probe-based protein labeling and live-cell
compatibility demonstrated the potential for fluorescence-activated cell sorting to isolate functionally
enriched plastic-degrading populations. These tools lay critical groundwork for future high-throughput

discovery of plastic-active enzymes, mechanistic studies of microbial plastic utilization, and the rational
assembly of microbial consortia optimized for plastics deconstruction. This work is being prepared for
publication and is supporting the dissertation of a PhD student at Baylor University.
Objective 3 Accomplishments
Due to a change in site of performance for one of the co-principal investigators originally based at PNNL,
along with associated delays and an evolving project scope, the team did not make substantive progress
toward the original goals of Objective 3, which focused on reconstructing stable plastic-degrading microbial
consortia. However, progress made under Objectives 1 and 2 revealed critical and previously
underappreciated challenges in the broader field of non-PET plastic biodegradation, enabling impactful
contributions aligned with the intent of Objective 3.
Specifically, the biological deconstruction of non-PET plastics remains highly controversial due to conflicting
and often irreproducible reports in the literature. Our work helped identify two primary sources of this
irreproducibility: (1) the lack of standardized, well-characterized plastic substrates that enable direct
cross-study comparisons and (2) false positive reports of plastic-active enzymes arising from insufficient
experimental controls, leading to misinterpretation of spectroscopy and microscopy data. Addressing these
challenges became a central focus of the project.
Notably, the team played a key role in refuting a highly visible report claiming enzymatic degradation of
poly(vinyl chloride) and polyethylene by demonstrating that nonspecific protein binding to plastic surfaces
can generate misleading analytical signals that mimic true chemical modification. This work clarified a major
point of contention in the field and was published in Nature Communications, substantially reducing
uncertainty around reported non-PET plastic biodegradation mechanisms.
Building on these findings, the project proposed a set of standards and best practices for biological plastics
deconstruction research. These recommendations include the use of additive-free substrates with material
properties that promote bioavailability while minimizing confounding artifacts, incorporation of inactivated
enzyme controls, rigorous substrate washing procedures, and careful interpretation of spectroscopic and
microscopy data. Collectively, these best practices are designed to reduce false positive reporting, improve
reproducibility, and enable direct comparison across studies.
By establishing clearer methodological expectations and resolving key sources of ambiguity in the literature,
this work meaningfully advanced the field and reduced technical risk for future research. Although the
original scope of Objective 3 was modified, the resulting contributions provide durable infrastructure that
accelerates progress toward scalable biological deconstruction of non-PET plastics.
Project Outcomes
In addition to the technical advancements described above, this project produced enduring outcomes that
extend beyond its original scope. The award supported 1 PhD dissertation and provided research training
and financial support for 3 additional doctoral students, including 2 at the University of Delaware and 1 at
Baylor University, directly contributing to workforce development in bioenergy, biomanufacturing, and
materials science. Scientifically, the dye-decolorizing peroxidase (DyP) discovery matured into a cohesive
mechanistic story that was reviewed at Science and is currently being revised for submission to a
high-impact journal, with at least 2 additional manuscripts in preparation addressing best practices for
plastics biodegradation research and outcomes from Objective 2. The results of this project also formed
the foundation for subsequent DOE investments, including the follow-on award DE-SC0023085 focused on
community-level mechanisms of plastic deconstruction and a FICUS award that accelerated the
accomplishments of Objective 1. These efforts generated extensive metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and
genomic datasets, totaling more than 3 TB, which have been made publicly available to the research
community and represent a long-term resource for advancing plastics biodegradation research. Finally, the
team participated in the NSF i-Corps program to explore translational pathways for the technology, including
applications in microplastic removal, laying the groundwork for future applied efforts when appropriate
personnel and partnerships are in place. Collectively, these outcomes demonstrate that the project
delivered not only key scientific insights but also lasting infrastructure, trained personnel, and follow-on
funding that continue to advance DOE priorities in circular materials and biomanufacturing.
