Global trends in epilepsy and the Chinese experience: Epidemiological transitions, underlying mechanisms, and integrated strategies (1990–2021)
Authors: Ting Chen, Mengqiao He, Sijia Basang, Hongyu Ren, Yutong Fu, Yu Li, Xin Shi, Lei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy represents a global public health challenge, with its burden distribution profoundly illuminating patterns of health inequality. Based on the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) data, this review constructs a three‐dimensional analytical framework encompassing biological determinants, health system performance, and social determinants of health. Over the past three decades, although the absolute number of individuals with epilepsy worldwide has continued to increase, age‐standardized mortality rates have declined substantially. However, 87.9% of the associated disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) are concentrated in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries (LMICs). China, with approximately 9–10 million people living with epilepsy, confronts a triple disparity encompassing regional disparities, urban‐rural gaps, and health system hierarchy barriers: mortality rates in western provinces are approximately nine times higher than those in eastern regions; the treatment gap in rural areas reaches 60%–90%; and primary care capacity deficits within the tiered diagnosis and treatment system create bottlenecks in case identification and referral. Concurrently, the disease spectrum is undergoing a profound transition, with an increasing burden of post‐stroke epilepsy in older adults, while special populations, including children and women, continue to face persistent challenges. Despite China's notable achievements in reducing mortality and establishing a tertiary epilepsy center network, multiple interacting factors perpetuate a vicious cycle of poverty, disease, and stigma among affected individuals. Future efforts require alignment with the World Health Organization Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders 2022–2031 (IGAP) to develop precision prevention and control strategies, thereby contributing the Chinese experience to global epilepsy governance.Keywords: Aging, China, Epilepsy, Global Health, Health Policy, low‐ and middle‐income countries, World Health Organization

Epilepsy, as a global public health challenge, exhibits a burden distribution that profoundly reveals patterns of global health inequality. The effectiveness of its prevention and control has long been constrained by the structural obstacle of the treatment gap, particularly pronounced in low‐ and middle‐income countries. 1 Over the past three decades, the global epidemiological characteristics of epilepsy have undergone significant evolution: population growth and aging have driven a continued increase in the absolute number of patients, while improved access to anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) and enhanced healthcare systems have led to a marked decline in age‐standardized mortality rates. 1 This evolution demonstrates substantial heterogeneity among countries at different developmental levels. In high‐income countries, the focus of epilepsy prevention and control has shifted towards managing epilepsy in the elderly and addressing comorbidities. In contrast, low‐income countries continue to face the dual challenge of controlling preventable etiologies such as perinatal injuries and central nervous system infections while ensuring access to essential ASMs. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 China, as the world's largest developing nation, holds unique demonstrative value and exemplary significance in its epilepsy prevention and control practices. It is undergoing a typical epidemiological transition from infectious diseases to chronic non‐communicable diseases, while simultaneously confronting indigenous challenges such as regional development imbalances and the urban‐rural dual structure. 6 , 7 First, stroke, a leading cause of adult mortality in China, drives a continuously increasing burden of secondary epilepsy in the elderly. The GBD 2021 study indicates that stroke‐related disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) rank highest among neurological disorders. 4 , 8 Second, the interaction between geographical environment and economic level results in significant regional disparities. The age‐standardized mortality rate for epilepsy in high‐altitude areas of western China is nearly nine times higher than that in eastern coastal provinces. 9 Finally, the hierarchical diagnosis and treatment policy, which emphasizes initial consultations at primary care facilities, bidirectional referrals, integration of acute and chronic care, and coordinated upper‐lower level operations, has shaped a unique allocation of diagnostic authority. Community health service institutions are responsible for initial screening, but the authority for definitive epilepsy diagnosis is concentrated in neurology departments of secondary and tertiary hospitals. This creates a critical bottleneck centered on case identification capacity at the primary level and referral efficiency, in contrast to the general practitioner first‐contact and management model prevalent in developed countries. 10 , 11 By integrating the latest GBD 2021 data, Chinese provincial epidemiological studies, and health system evaluation evidence, this paper constructs a three‐dimensional analytical framework encompassing biological, health system, and social determinants. It dissects the evolutionary patterns and underlying mechanisms of the epilepsy burden in China and globally. Based on the strategic goals of the Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP), it proposes integrated prevention and control strategies, aiming to provide Chinese experience and policy reference for global epilepsy governance.

THE EVOLVING LANDSCAPE OF THE GLOBAL EPILEPSY BURDEN

Overall scale and epidemiological trends

Over the past three decades, the global burden of epilepsy has exhibited a complex pattern of evolution. According to the most recent data from the GBD 2021, approximately 51.7 million (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 44.9–58.9) people worldwide are living with active epilepsy (a prevalence of 0.7%), comprising an estimated 24.2 million (95% UI: 18.5–30.7) cases of idiopathic epilepsy (genetic generalized epilepsy in the International League Against Epilepsy [ILAE] 2017 classification) and 27.5 million (95% UI: 25.2–29.9) cases of secondary epilepsy (structural/metabolic epilepsy in the ILAE 2017 classification). 1 Between 1990 and 2021, while global population growth and aging led to a marked increase in the absolute number of individuals with epilepsy, changes in age‐standardized indicators demonstrated etiological heterogeneity. The age‐standardized prevalence rate for idiopathic epilepsy increased by 6.9% (95% UI: −9.7%–25.5%), and the incidence rate rose by 12.3% (95% UI: −4.8%–32.6%). For secondary epilepsy, the age‐standardized prevalence rate increased by 14.5% (95% UI: 5.3%–25.5%), establishing it as the primary driver of the overall increase in prevalence. 1 , 12 Population aging is reshaping the global epidemiological profile of epilepsy. A specific analysis from GBD 2021 reveals that between 1990 and 2021, the global age‐standardized incidence rate for idiopathic epilepsy in the population aged over 60 years increased by 26.6%, and the prevalence rate rose by 20.0%. These rates are projected to continue growing until 2035, with average annual increases of approximately 1.6% and 1.3%, respectively. 13 The global epilepsy burden displays a characteristic bimodal pattern in age distribution, with the highest incidence rates observed in children aged 0–14 years and the highest mortality rates in adults aged >70 years. 2 Regarding gender differences, males consistently exhibit higher rates across various indicators. Data from 2021 show that age‐standardized prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates were all significantly higher in males compared to females. 12

Mortality improvement and geographic inequality

In stark contrast to the rising prevalence trends, epilepsy‐related mortality and DALYs have shown significant improvement. Between 1990 and 2021, the global age‐standardized mortality rate for idiopathic epilepsy decreased from 2.07 (95% UI: 1.70–2.25) per 100,000 to 1.74 (95% UI: 1.46–1.92) per 100,000, a reduction of 15.8% (95% UI: 22.8% decline to 8.8% decline). The age‐standardized DALY rate declined from 208.08 (95% UI: 163.21–260.35) per 100,000 to 177.85 (95% UI: 137.66–225.90) per 100,000, a decrease of 14.5% (95% UI: 24.2% decline to 4.2% decline). 14 In 2021, idiopathic epilepsy was responsible for approximately 140,000 (95% UI: 117,000–153,000) deaths globally and 13.878 million (95% UI: 10.7–17.6) DALYs. This improvement is primarily attributed to enhanced access to ASMs, improvements in diagnosis and treatment systems, and the application of novel therapeutic approaches. 9 However, this progress has been distributed highly unevenly across the globe. Age‐standardized DALY rates exhibit a strong negative correlation with the socio‐demographic Index (SDI). In 2021, as much as 87.9% (95% UI: 86.2% to 89.2%) of the epilepsy DALY burden was concentrated in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries (LMICs). 15 The age‐standardized DALY rate in Eastern sub‐Saharan Africa reached 423.5 (95% UI 345.1–519.7) per 100,000, compared to approximately 135.0 (95% UI 91.1–204.0) per 100,000 in Western Europe, revealing a 3.14‐fold gradient difference. 16 This pattern of inequality profoundly reflects the combined impact of healthcare accessibility, the depth of public health interventions, and levels of socioeconomic development (Figure 1).

The treatment gap and global challenges

Despite improving epidemiological indicators, the global epilepsy treatment gap remains substantial, creating a significant disparity between progress in data and lagging practice. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 75% of individuals with active epilepsy in low‐income countries do not receive the necessary treatment, and the average availability of generic ASMs in public sector health facilities in LMICs is below 50%. 17 , 18 , 19 This gap directly contributes to the standardized mortality rate for epilepsy in LMICs being six times higher than that in high‐income countries. 20 , 21 , 22 Ranking as the fifth leading neurological disorder in the global disease burden, epilepsy is among the most common neurological conditions worldwide and continues to pose a major challenge in global public health. 1 Epilepsy prevention and control urgently require a strategic shift from raising awareness to improving treatment accessibility. This transition aims to translate improvements in epidemiological data into tangible clinical benefits by strengthening health system efficiency, reducing economic barriers to medication, and combating social stigmatization.

EPILEPSY EPIDEMIOLOGY IN CHINA: CHARACTERISTICS AND CHALLENGES

National burden and epidemiological transition

China currently has an estimated 9–10 million individuals with epilepsy, accounting for approximately one‐fifth of the global total and establishing it as one of the countries with the highest epilepsy burden worldwide. 6 , 23 Based on the latest GBD 2021 data, in 2021, China's age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR) for idiopathic epilepsy was 28.2 (95% UI: 19.0–37.9) per 100,000, the age‐standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) was 214.7 (95% UI: 150.1–278.6) per 100,000, the age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR) was 0.8 (95% UI: 0.7–1.0) per 100,000, and the age‐standardized DALY rate (ASDR) was 101.4 (95% UI: 72.5–139.4) per 100,000. Compared to 1990, the incidence rate has increased by 26.1% (95% UI: −9.6% to 72.5%), the prevalence rate by 13.4% (95% UI: −18.7% to 59.1%), while the mortality rate has substantially decreased by 56.6% (95% UI: −62.9% to −46.0%) and the DALY rate by 43.2% (95% UI: −56.1% to −25.3%), reflecting China's significant achievements in reducing premature death and disability from epilepsy (Figure 2). 24 However, beneath these achievements lies a profound epidemiological transition. Regarding gender distribution, China exhibits a consistent “male excess” pattern, with disparities generally exceeding global averages. In 2021, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR for males in China were 22.6 (95% UI: 15.7 to 30.0) per 100,000, 216.4 (95% UI: 158.1 to 269.7) per 100,000, 0.87 (95% UI: 0.61 to 1.25) per 100,000, and 94.1 (95% UI: 65.5 to 131.7) per 100,000, respectively, representing excess rates in males of 10.2% (95% UI: −29.8% to 73.1%), 17.1% (95% UI: −20.5% to 72.6%), 61.1% (95% UI: −8.2% to 182.8%), and 33.1% (95% UI: −21.0% to 124.2%). Compared to global figures, China's gender disparities are more pronounced in mortality (61.1% vs. 41.5% [95% UI: −8.8% to 119.5%]) and prevalence (17.1% vs. 9.6% [95% UI: −19.4% to 49.0%]), whereas the disparity in incidence is roughly comparable to the global level (10.2% vs. 12.8% [95% UI: −25.4% to 70.6%]) (see Figure 3). This gender difference persists across all age groups and widens further in older age, potentially attributable to the combined effect of cumulative exposure to risk factors in early life, such as brain injury and alcohol consumption, and intrinsic neurobiological susceptibilities, including sex hormones and brain structure. 14 , 24 In terms of age distribution, unlike the typical global pattern characterized by a high, sharp peak in children, a deep valley, and a steep peak in the elderly, China's age‐specific epilepsy distribution exhibits a “low, flat peak in children, a shallow, wide transition band, and a gentle slope in the elderly” (see Figure 4). The peak in childhood is notably lower and shifted towards adolescence. The decline in prevalence during young adulthood is moderated, forming a wide, shallow transition band rather than a distinct trough. The upward trend in older age is relatively gradual. Notably, age‐specific mortality and DALY rates further reveal both the successes and persistent challenges within this transition. China's indicators are significantly lower than global averages across all age groups, with the most pronounced decline observed in children under 5 years old, reflecting progress in early diagnosis and the child health protection system. However, as age increases, the gap between China and the global average narrows. During middle age (45–59 years), mortality rates are approximately 50% of global levels, suggesting potential weaknesses in comorbidity management and continuity of care. Upon entering old age, although absolute values remain lower than global averages, the relative gap narrows further, highlighting the practical difficulties faced by older patients with epilepsy, including multiple comorbidities and insufficient care resources. This age distribution pattern, characterized by significant success in childhood prevention and control alongside a relatively prominent burden in middle and older age, indicates that with accelerating population aging, the focus of the epilepsy burden is irreversibly shifting towards the elderly population. The sustained increase in the absolute number of patients has evolved from a singular focus on pediatric treatment into a comprehensive management challenge spanning the entire life course. 6 , 10 , 24 , 25

The triple inequality gap within china

The burden of epilepsy in China exhibits significant disparities across regions, between urban and rural areas, and among different tiers of the healthcare system, constituting a “triple gap” where these inequalities overlap and mutually reinforce one another. This confluence shapes the unique challenges confronting epilepsy prevention and control in the country. 6 , 9 , 25

Regional gap: The interweaving of ethnicity, geography, and development level

According to the GBD 2019 provincial analysis, in 2019, western provinces such as Xizang (4.26/100,000 [95% UI: 1.43 to 5.66]), Qinghai (1.80/100,000 [95% UI: 1.43 to 5.66]), and Yunnan (1.30/100,000 [95% UI: 0.88 to 1.62]) exhibited the highest age‐standardized mortality rates nationwide, whereas Guangdong (0.48/100,000 [95% UI: 0.39 to 0.64]), Zhejiang (0.56/100,000 [95% UI: 0.39 to 0.64]), and Shanghai (0.57/100,000 [95% UI: 0.41 to 0.73]) recorded the lowest, with an approximately ninefold difference between the highest and lowest rates (Figure 5). 9 This disparity reflects not only varying levels of economic development, as measured by the Socio‐demographic Index (SDI), but is also deeply rooted in the distinct epidemiological characteristics of multi‐ethnic regions. In the Tibetan Plateau, for instance, the standardized prevalence rate of epilepsy reaches 438.85 (95% UI: 115.95 to 740.52) per 100,000, and the DALY rate is 400.55 (95% UI: 193.62 to 601.28) per 100,000, both the highest nationally. 14 These findings are closely associated with high‐altitude hypoxic environments, parasitic infections, and alcohol consumption patterns. 26 The DALY rate for epilepsy declines significantly with increasing provincial SDI, indicating a concentration of disease burden in less developed western regions. 6 , 9 , 10 Regarding resource allocation, over 90% of epilepsy centers nationwide are located in tertiary hospitals, predominantly in eastern China, resulting in a severe shortage of specialized resources in the west. This creates a paradox wherein regions with the greatest burden have the least access to care. 10 The Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) Index further illustrates this imbalance: Beijing scored 91.5, while Xizhang scored only 48, highlighting a substantial east–west disparity. 7 , 10 , 27 Such inequitable distribution of resources means that high‐burden western regions face the most limited diagnostic and treatment capabilities.

Urban‐rural gap: The treatment gap under the dual structure

In rural China, the treatment gap for epilepsy can reach 60%–90%, and in resource‐limited areas such as Xizang, it approaches nearly 100%, far exceeding that in urban areas. 17 , 28 , 29 Regarding access to diagnostic and therapeutic services, community health institutions in China are not authorized to confirm epilepsy diagnoses. Definitive diagnosis typically requires assessment by neurologists at secondary or tertiary hospitals, supported by electroencephalography and neuroimaging. This centralized diagnostic pathway results in significant delays for rural patients. Although epilepsy has been included in the National Basic Public Health Service Program, its implementation at the grassroots level remains insufficient. Village doctors often lack both the motivation and the clinical capacity to identify suspected cases, leaving many patients undetected within the primary care system. 11 Economically, rural patients face a substantially higher risk of catastrophic health expenditure compared with their urban counterparts. For families affected by rare epilepsy syndromes, average annual direct medical costs can reach tens of thousands of RMB. 30 , 31 Patients of low socioeconomic status commonly experience delayed diagnosis, irregular treatment, and high rates of loss to follow‐up.

Hierarchical gap: Capacity deficit under the primary care strengthening policy

Although China has implemented a tiered diagnosis and treatment reform since 2015, epilepsy care continues to concentrate in higher‐level hospitals, with capacity deficits at the primary level constituting a systemic barrier. Regarding diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, primary healthcare institutions generally lack the capacity for electroencephalogram (EEG) interpretation, neuroimaging, and surgical evaluation. 10 Epilepsy specialty alliances remain limited in coverage, and the two‐way referral system exists largely in name only. A study from rural Southwest China showed that while 63.2% of village doctors correctly identified childhood epilepsy, only 1.68% could simultaneously provide appropriate medication and proper referral. 11 Concerning medication accessibility, significant disparities exist in the availability of newer ASMs within and outside the national reimbursement drug list. Rural patients in western China frequently face the dilemma of either having no access to necessary medications or being unable to afford them despite availability. 7 , 32 , 33 In terms of comorbidity management, the recognition rate and intervention coverage for psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety remain critically inadequate at the primary level. 34 , 35

MULTIDIMENSIONAL DRIVERS OF EPILEPSY BURDEN

Behind the observed phenomena of the triple disparities, the underlying mechanisms driving epilepsy burden are shaped by a convergence of biological foundations, health system performance, and sociocultural factors. These three categories of mechanisms intertwine to determine the evolving trajectory of epilepsy prevention and control in China.

Biological and demographic foundations

GBD 2021 classifies secondary epilepsy as encompassing preventable structural etiologies, including perinatal brain insults, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and central nervous system infections. 36 , 37 Population‐based estimates from the ILAE Prevention Task Force indicate that these preventable causes collectively account for approximately 25% of individuals with epilepsy in both high‐income and low‐/middle‐income countries. 38 In China, alongside improvements in perinatal care, infection control, and injury prevention, the relative contribution of these preventable etiologies is gradually declining, while the proportion attributable to stroke and neurodegenerative diseases is continuously increasing. A recent meta‐analysis encompassing over 80,000 stroke survivors indicates that the cumulative incidence of post‐stroke epilepsy or seizures is approximately 5%–10%, with cortical involvement, severe stroke, and early seizures identified as independent risk factors. 8 , 36 , 38 Furthermore, individuals with dementia, particularly Alzheimer's disease, exhibit a significantly elevated risk of developing epilepsy compared to age‐matched non‐dementia populations, establishing dementia alongside stroke as two major structural foundations for epilepsy in the elderly. 4 , 38 Population aging, coupled with population growth, serves as a fundamental driver reshaping China's disease spectrum from communicable diseases towards chronic non‐communicable diseases and neurological disorders. 4 , 13 , 39 Multiple projection studies based on national census and sample survey data predict that the proportion of China's population aged ≥60 years will approach or exceed 30% around 2035 and will climb further by 2050. 40 , 41 , 42 Against this demographic and epidemiological backdrop, the absolute number of individuals with epilepsy in the elderly is projected to increase persistently, driving the transition of the disease spectrum from a predominantly pediatric profile to one dominated by older adults. 1 , 6 , 9 , 13 , 24 , 43 Consequently, epilepsy prevention and control strategies must proactively integrate the management of neurological conditions common in older adults, such as stroke and dementia, into primary and secondary prevention frameworks. 4 , 9 , 44

Health system accessibility and quality of care

Regional disparities in health system performance constitute the institutional mechanism underlying the formation of the triple disparities. Regarding the allocation of specialized human resources, China exhibits significant regional imbalances: in first‐tier cities, the number of neurologists per 100,000 population exceeds five, whereas in western rural areas, it falls below 0.5, with some counties in Xizang lacking formally trained epilepsy care providers altogether. 7 , 10 Although China has established an essential medicines system, accessibility to newer ASMs remains constrained by their inclusion status in the National Reimbursement Drug List. Rural patients in western China frequently encounter a situation where medications are technically available yet unaffordable or lack access to standardized prescribing channels. Although this barrier differs in nature from supply shortages observed in low‐income countries such as Laos, it similarly impedes the widespread adoption of standardized treatment and constitutes a significant institutional factor contributing to the estimated 63% treatment gap. 7 In terms of comorbidity management, recognition rates and intervention coverage for psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety remain inadequate at primary healthcare facilities. Only approximately one‐third of patients receive comorbidity screening, and the coverage of psychological interventions is even lower. This gap not only substantially diminishes patient quality of life but also increases healthcare costs and mortality risk, perpetuating a vicious cycle of epilepsy, comorbidity, and poor prognosis. 34 , 35

Socioeconomic factors and stigma

Socioeconomic factors exacerbate health system disparities through multiple pathways, thereby amplifying the burden of epilepsy. A vicious cycle exists between epilepsy and poverty: the disease leads to unemployment, restricted educational opportunities, and reduced productivity, while poverty limits access to treatment and quality of care. 30 , 45 Social stigma, as a non‐institutional barrier independent of economic factors, poses a significant obstacle to treatment‐seeking behavior. Approximately 89% of patients report experiencing felt stigma, which aligns with the World Health Organization's emphasis that stigmatization constitutes a major barrier to healthcare seeking. 31 , 46 This cultural barrier leads patients to conceal their condition, delay seeking medical care, and experience social isolation. Even patients with sufficient financial means may avoid formal diagnosis and treatment. 46 , 47 Furthermore, socioeconomic status significantly influences epilepsy outcomes by affecting the timing of healthcare seeking, treatment adherence, and quality of care. Patients with low socioeconomic status commonly experience delayed diagnosis, substandard treatment, and high rates of loss to follow‐up, resulting in poor disease control and increased complications. 30 , 45 , 48

The interaction among biological risks, health system disparities, and socioeconomic disadvantages creates multiple overlapping vulnerabilities for patients in western rural areas and those seeking care at primary‐level facilities, trapping them in a self‐perpetuating cycle of poverty, disease, and stigma. 30 , 31 , 45 Tailored education programs targeting socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, enhanced public campaigns to reduce stigma, and improvements in medical insurance coverage and service accessibility represent critical strategies for reducing epilepsy‐related health inequalities and improving overall outcomes. 30 , 31

BURDEN OF EPILEPSY IN SPECIAL POPULATIONS: CHARACTERISTICS AND CHALLENGES

Epidemiological transition and comorbidity management in elderly‐onset epilepsy

As China's population structure undergoes profound aging, the epidemiological profile of epilepsy is shifting significantly. Data from global and developed country sources indicate that the incidence of epilepsy in individuals aged >65 years is substantially higher than in middle‐aged adults, ranging from approximately 70–150 per 100,000 person‐years, representing a two‐ to threefold increase. 49 , 50 Structural etiologies predominate in new‐onset epilepsy among the elderly, with stroke being the most common precipitating factor, accounting for an estimated 5%–23% of cases across different studies. 2 , 49 , 51 Patients with Alzheimer's disease exhibit a significantly increased risk of developing epilepsy, although the precise magnitude of risk varies across studies. 52 Brain tumors and metabolic encephalopathies also contribute to a proportion of new‐onset cases in this population. 2 , 49 , 51 Clinically, epilepsy in the elderly frequently manifests with atypical, non‐convulsive seizures, such as transient confusion or unexplained falls. This atypical presentation contributes to a high rate of misdiagnosis, with conditions such as transient ischemic attacks, dementia, or syncope often being incorrectly diagnosed instead. 53 Consequently, the time to confirmed diagnosis is frequently delayed by several months. 2 , 49 Polypharmacy is common in this age group, elevating the risk of clinically significant drug‐drug interactions. Although warfarin–drug interactions remain relevant in resource‐limited settings, current geriatric epilepsy guidelines (2023–2024) emphasize that enzyme‐inducing ASMs (e.g., carbamazepine, phenytoin) also reduce exposure to novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs; e.g., rivaroxaban, apixaban), which are increasingly preferred over warfarin in stroke prevention where resources permit. 54 , 55 These agents may also diminish the efficacy of statins. 56 Clinicians should consult contemporary cardiovascular guidance when selecting anticoagulation in elderly patients receiving enzyme‐inducing ASMs. Furthermore, seizures increase the risk of falls and fractures in the elderly and exhibit a bidirectional relationship with cognitive decline, potentially accelerating the progression of cognitive impairment. 2 , 50 , 57 , 58 In summary, the epidemiological characteristics and comorbidity management of elderly‐onset epilepsy are complex, necessitating individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies as well as multidisciplinary collaboration to optimize treatment outcomes.

Persistent challenges in childhood epilepsy: From the perinatal period to neurodevelopment

The annual incidence of childhood epilepsy exhibits considerable variation across different regions and studies. With improvements in perinatal care, the proportion of acquired structural etiologies such as hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy has declined, while the relative contribution of genetic causes and congenital structural abnormalities has increased. 59 , 60 , 61 Neurodevelopmental disorders represent a major comorbidity of childhood epilepsy, with overall comorbidity rates ranging from 30%–50%. Among these, the prevalence of attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is approximately 30.7%, while autism spectrum disorder (ASD) affects a broader range of 5%–39% of patients. 62 , 63 , 64 Cognitive impairment is prevalent among children with drug‐resistant epilepsy, with a high proportion of patients exhibiting IQ scores below 70, reflecting a substantial risk of severe cognitive disability. 60 , 62 Long‐term follow‐up studies demonstrate that cognitive impairment is strongly associated with impaired social functioning. For instance, certain cohort studies have found that patients with IQ ≥ 70 achieve employment rates approaching those of the general population after 20 years, whereas employment rates are significantly lower among those with IQ < 70. Additionally, affected individuals experience reduced educational attainment, limited access to vocational training, and increased social dependence. 60 , 62 Perinatal hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy constitutes an important cause of neonatal seizures and early‐onset epilepsy. Its severe brain injury background is closely associated with subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes and epilepsy risk, while the burden of isolated acute symptomatic seizures shows a weaker correlation with long‐term prognosis. 61 , 65 In summary, childhood epilepsy, particularly cases accompanied by neurodevelopmental disorders, presents complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Early precision diagnosis, comprehensive management, and long‐term support are essential to improve cognitive and social functioning outcomes in this population. 59 , 62 , 63

Epilepsy in women: Unique life‐course challenges and comprehensive management

The prevalence of catamenial epilepsy varies according to diagnostic criteria. Under strict definitions (a ≥100% increase in seizure frequency during the perimenstrual period), this condition affects approximately one‐third of women of reproductive age with epilepsy. 66 Fertility rates among women with epilepsy are 20%–40% lower than those in the general population, attributable to the disease itself, psychosocial factors, and the reproductive endocrine side effects of valproate. 67 , 68 Contrary to common perception, the risk of seizure deterioration during pregnancy may be lower than expected. A Chinese cohort study demonstrated that 67.5% of pregnant women maintained seizure control, while 25.9% experienced increased seizure frequency; notably, the deterioration rate reached 42.6% among those not receiving lamotrigine. 69 The teratogenic risk associated with valproate is substantial: monotherapy with valproate yields malformation rates of 6.2%–10.9%, with a 1%–2% risk of neural tube defects specifically. 70 , 71 , 72 Peripartum management requires meticulous optimization. Approximately 31.8% of women attempt to discontinue ASMs during pregnancy; however, the 11.3% who withdraw from treatment during the first trimester face a significantly increased risk of seizure deterioration. 69 The safety profile of breastfeeding varies considerably by medication. Carbamazepine demonstrates a favorable safety profile with a relative infant dose (RID) of approximately 5%–6%. For valproate, despite an RID of approximately 21%, most infants maintain serum concentrations below the lower limit of quantification. Lamotrigine, with an RID of approximately 29%, necessitates monitoring of infant serum concentrations. 73 The prevalence of postpartum depression among women with epilepsy ranges from 25%–35%, representing a 2‐ to threefold increase compared to the general population. 74 Long‐term adverse effects of chronic ASM exposure are well‐established. Enzyme‐inducing ASMs increase fracture risk by approximately 1.5‐ to 2.2‐fold, with annual bone mineral density decline rates of 1.8%–2.6%. 75 , 76 The incidence of polycystic ovary syndrome associated with valproate exposure ranges from 10%–26%, representing a two‐ to threefold increase compared to the general population. 68

Rare epilepsy syndromes: Diagnostic challenges and catastrophic household burden

Rare epilepsy syndromes (RES), primarily including Dravet syndrome (DS), Lennox‐Gastaut syndrome (LGS), and other developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs), although individually uncommon, impose a disproportionately severe disease burden. 77 Significant diagnostic delays are characteristic of these conditions. For Dravet syndrome, 58.4% of patients experience a diagnostic delay exceeding 1 year, with 14.4% delayed for more than 6 years; 72.7% encounter at least one misdiagnosis, most commonly febrile seizures or generalized epilepsy. 78 European data indicate an average diagnostic delay of approximately 12.3 months for LGS. 79 Treatment resistance is a hallmark feature. A Norwegian population‐based study reported a zero percent seizure‐free rate for Dravet syndrome. 80 In clinical trials, the complete seizure‐free rate in the cannabidiol (CBD) treatment group was 3.1%. 81 For Lennox‐Gastaut syndrome, even with multimodal treatment approaches, the rate of sustained seizure freedom beyond 5 years is only 16.5%. 82 The catastrophic household burden associated with RES is substantial. In the United States, annual direct medical costs for patients with Dravet syndrome range from $11,048 to $77,914, with indirect caregiver losses estimated at $19,000 to $20,000 annually. “The prevalence of depression among DS caregivers ranges from 47%–70%, while anxiety affects 52.8% of general epilepsy caregivers. 77 , 83 A Chinese study of adult epilepsy caregivers reported anxiety and depression prevalence rates of 29.58% and 28.17%, respectively. 84 Data from Boston Children's Hospital indicate that the average delay to genetic diagnosis for DS is 43 months, with significantly longer delays observed among patients lacking early status epilepticus manifestations, those requiring language translation services, or those insured by Medicaid. 85 The convergence of diagnostic delays, treatment resistance, and multiple comorbidities creates an overwhelming burden for both patients and families, underscoring the urgent need to establish rapid genetic diagnostic pathways and strengthen social support systems.

AN INTEGRATED FRAMEWORK FOR FUTURE PREVENTION AND CONTROL STRATEGIES

Constructing a whole‐chain, precision‐oriented prevention system

Future strategies for epilepsy prevention and control should be guided by the World Health Organization's Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders 2022–2031 (IGAP). This plan establishes five core strategic objectives: increasing policy priority and governance, providing effective and timely diagnostic and therapeutic services, implementing promotion and prevention strategies, enhancing research and innovation, and strengthening the public health response to epilepsy. 86 In alignment with China's public health practices, a comprehensive chain of strategies encompassing risk prevention and control, early intervention, and long‐term management can be constructed. At the level of risk prevention and control, it is recommended to integrate epilepsy prevention into existing programs such as stroke prevention and control, injury prevention, and perinatal care. 5 , 87 Particular attention should be directed towards modifiable risk factors, including stroke and traumatic brain injury, to reduce epilepsy incidence at its source. At the level of early identification and intervention, clinical research on epilepsy risk following stroke and severe traumatic brain injury should be intensified. The clinical applicability of predictive models such as SeLECT and CAVE, as well as electroencephalogram (EEG) biomarkers, should be explored to provide evidence supporting future risk stratification and individualized monitoring. 88 , 89 , 90 At the level of long‐term management, pathways for comorbidity screening and management should be strengthened, with a focus on improving the identification rates and intervention coverage for psychiatric disorders and cognitive impairments, thereby enhancing patients' long‐term prognosis.

Reshaping the diagnosis and treatment service system through digital transformation

Digital technologies possess the potential to bridge the epilepsy diagnosis and treatment gap; however, their effectiveness in resource‐limited settings remains to be validated. 91 On one hand, the conditional exploration of telemedicine and artificial intelligence applications, such as regional pilot programs for remote EEG interpretation and validation studies of AI‐assisted diagnosis, can be pursued to extend specialized services to primary care and remote areas. 92 On the other hand, lessons should be drawn from the experience of constructing the industry‐led tertiary epilepsy center network by the China Association Against Epilepsy. By 2025, this network had encompassed 526 centers, comprising 45 tertiary, 259 secondary, and 222 primary‐level facilities, optimizing resource allocation through hierarchical diagnosis and treatment, bidirectional referrals, and quality control. The experience derived from this model can be shared within the framework of global epilepsy prevention and control collaboration. However, any adaptation requires a thorough assessment of the recipient country's health system capacity, infrastructure, policy and regulatory environment, and cultural appropriateness to avoid direct transplantation. 91

Implementing an equity‐centered health‐in‐all‐policies strategy

Epilepsy prevention and control should be embedded within the social determinants of health framework, promoting multisectoral collaborative action. First, economic protection should be strengthened by optimizing reimbursement policies for ASMs within health insurance schemes, improving access to newer generation drugs, and investigating cost‐sharing mechanisms for patients with severe epilepsy to alleviate financial burden. 5 Second, patient rights must be safeguarded through advocating for anti‐discrimination measures in education and employment domains and exploring channels for rights protection. 87 , 93 Third, social stigmatization should be reduced by supporting evidence‐based public education initiatives. Finally, support networks should be established to provide multidimensional assistance services for patients and their families, thereby mitigating the negative cycle of disease, poverty, and stigma. 94

CONCLUSIONS AND GLOBAL RECOMMENDATIONS

Disparities in the global and regional distribution of the epilepsy burden serve as a critical metric for assessing health equity. This analysis demonstrates that China has achieved remarkable progress in reducing epilepsy mortality and constructing a three‐tiered epilepsy center network. However, structural challenges persist, including an accelerated aging of the disease spectrum, the overlapping effects of the triple inequality gap, and service shortfalls for special populations. To advance global epilepsy prevention and control efforts, this paper proposes three recommendations. First, epilepsy prevention and control should be integrated into national non‐communicable disease strategies, with country‐specific targets established in alignment with the WHO IGAP goals, and data surveillance systems should be progressively strengthened. Second, the transfer of experience from China's three‐tiered epilepsy center network to LMICs should be supported through WHO technical cooperation mechanisms, contingent upon a thorough assessment of the recipient countries’ health system capacity and cultural adaptability. Third, concerted action among governments, academic institutions, civil society organizations, and patient groups should be promoted under the IGAP framework to accelerate the achievement of reducing the epilepsy burden and improving patient quality of life through risk factor control, optimization of diagnosis and treatment, and interventions targeting social determinants. China's experience in epilepsy prevention and control demonstrates that against a complex backdrop of rapid population aging, imbalanced regional development, and multi‐ethnic coexistence, a significant reduction in disease burden remains achievable by strengthening primary care capacity, optimizing resource allocation, and combating social stigmatization. This experience may serve as a reference for certain LMICs and contributes a Chinese solution to the global shift in epilepsy governance from raising awareness to improving treatment accessibility.
