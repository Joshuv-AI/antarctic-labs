 Executive Summary

Form Follows Function (F3): A Framework for Community-based Energy Resilience
Planning in the Midwest led by the City of Duluth MN, is an innovative two-year initiative
spanning August 1, 2023, to July 31, 2025. F3 created a replicable, human-centered
energy resilience planning process specifically designed for smaller, cold-climate
communities in the Midwest. F3 demonstrated both systems-level government planning
and community-based research techniques to inform renewable investments and
community preparedness strategies.

The City of Duluth has faced multiple low-attention disasters including flooding, seiches,
ice storms, derechos, and straight-line winds. The Form Follows Function project sought
to address the challenges faced by smaller communities exposed to low-attention
disasters, recurring minor events that often do not qualify for federal or state support but
cumulatively cost billions. Although a major focus of the project was determining which
municipal projects could produce “blue sky” financial and environmental benefits and
maximize governmental investment in resilience, the project also addressed individual
and shared community actions that could be taken to mitigate the negative impacts of
grid outages as experienced by Duluth’s most vulnerable residents.

The F3 planning approach included a Critical Infrastructure Team (CIT) and Community
Stakeholder Group (CSG) to overcome limitations in traditional resilience planning,
which often neglect population-based impacts and opportunities for decentralized
approaches. The project methodology expanded organizational and individual
knowledge of, and engagement in disaster preparedness. Through a collaborative
approach, F3 ensured energy resilience planning was responsive to, and centered on,
traditionally marginalized communities who are at the greatest risk of negative impacts
from climate change and grid outages. F3 was originally aligned with potential federal
programs, tax credits, and loan availability established by the Inflation Reduction Act
(IRA) to support resilience building at the critical systems, shared-community, and
individual entity levels. With a federal reorientation of energy, environmental justice, and
disaster funding, the importance of the F3 project increased because the resulting
planning approaches can be market-based and agnostic of the renewable energy policy
environment and represents flexible approaches that can be applied at multiple scales
to synergistically increase community resilience.

The primary objectives of the F3 project were accomplished through concurrent top-
down (asset-based) and bottom-up (community-based) research. Project results
included the establishment of resilient power siting criteria rubric, financial modeling for
a portfolio of potential developments, community-based participatory research into
previous grid-outages and future resilience needs, creation of minimum resilience
standards for the Duluth community, and a planning toolkit that was vetted by other
Midwest communities to deepen governmental and community-based renewable and
resilience approaches.

 Background

The term “low-attention disasters” has been coined to convey the experience of
Midwestern communities that often face multiple smaller disaster events that often
do not qualify for federal or state support. These events can hit small cities and
sparsely populated rural areas that lack the density of services and capacity for
planning that is available in larger urban areas and hurricane prone geographies.
The cumulative impact of these low-attention disasters reaches billions of dollars
per year. Although Midwestern communities are also increasingly impacted by
larger events that can alone meet the billion-dollar-disaster threshold, the Midwest
has not had the level of community resilience planning experienced elsewhere. F3
advances community resilience planning approaches for capacity-limited
communities, explores integration of renewable energy by municipal governments,
and defined individual resilience actions that could be directed through with
assistance of community-based organizations.

Community Resilience Planning

Currently, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) two-part
playbook to guide communities on resilience planning and different initiatives
including San Francisco Bay Area Planning and Urban Research Association’s
Resilient City (SPUR), Resilient Washington State (RWS), and Oregon Resilience
Plan (ORP) represent the state-of-the-art approaches on community resilience
planning. However, “...(they) do not advise on how to make resilience
performance measurement frameworks operational” (Miles 2021). NIST, SPUR,
RWS, ORP, and Miles’ modeling approach present challenges to communities
that desire to increase energy and community resilience but lack significant
capacity to 1) identify and process data, 2) develop scenarios for planning, 3)
appropriately engage stakeholders and community members at their level of
expertise, 4) develop community-based resilience metrics and tracking
methodologies, 5) identify strategies and resiliency deployment projects, and 6)
provide cost-benefit understanding at multiple scales for plan implementation.

The F3 Framework planning process was designed to address the complexity of
community energy resilience planning by creation of a framework and tools that
incorporate the needs of utilities and governmental entities to inform approaches to
renewable-based grid or community assets, while allowing users to participate in
defining the critical functions required against multiple outage scenarios at an
individual and shared resource level. This process recognizes the “precarious
intimacy between energy and the fiscal, social, and technical systems it supports”
(Roege 2024), and develops an accessible human-centered design process to
inform community energy resilience plans AND effective implementation pathways.

Traditional top-down approaches in selecting renewable energy projects neglect to
include community impacts and benefits in decision-making. They can also fail in
building trust, working towards consensus, and including reciprocity within the local

Renewable-based Community Energy Resilience

Core strategies in resilience plans include deploying localized power systems at key
sites. Planners consistently identify solar PV coupled with battery energy storage as the
“typical choice” for off-grid renewably-derived backup power. These systems can
operate independently of the grid during outages and provide resilience benefits such
as emergency heating, medical-device power, refrigeration, and charging at congregate
facilities like shelters or community centers, or at individuals homes or businesses.
Although Duluth and many communities generally depend on small diesel backup
generators for critical power, studies warn that diesel backups often fail if not regularly
tested, making solar and storage a more reliable alternative (Groneman 2024). In cold
climates, multi-energy microgrids are also considered. For instance, hybrid systems
(e.g. biogas micro-CHP) to co-produce heat and power are being explored to further
enhance resiliency in winter. Conceptually, these microgrids can be scaled from single
buildings to networks of resilience hubs, however determining siting, financing, and
resilience value of this approach is often beyond the capacity of smaller municipalities.

A resilience hub is a trusted local facility (school, church, community center) augmented
with solar and storage and other systems so it remains a safe, warm/cooled gathering
place during outages. Technical analyses (e.g. using NREL’s REopt tool) find that solar
PV drastically cuts operating costs and batteries ensure multi-day backup power for
these hubs (Menghaney 2025). Case studies show community service providers (e.g.
clinics, shelters) are highly motivated by energy resilience often ranking “ability to
operate during outages” as their top goal, even above cost savings (Ramanan 2022).

 6

This highlights that resilience-minded organizations prioritize backup power as a critical
service during disasters.

Despite enthusiasm, small local governments face hurdles in turning plans into projects.
Limited staff and technical expertise mean many municipalities are unfamiliar with
emerging storage technology and lack energy usage data and permitting know-how
(Ramanan 2022). A Clean Energy Group survey noted that community organizations
often “struggle with limited staff time and resources,” making lengthy solar and storage
projects daunting. Financially, thin budgets make it hard to cover even the soft costs of
development, placing the higher cost, lower return option of storage generally off the
table even for discussion.

Policy uncertainty (fluctuating incentives, complex utility tariffs, interconnection rules)
further complicates planning in most communities. Utilities generally possess the most
capacity and technical expertise to pursue solar, solar and storage, and microgrids,
however there has been limited success in obtaining regulatory approval (Kallay 2021).
because such proposals often fail to align with traditional regulatory objectives. The
primary obstacle for utilities is the difficulty in securing funding, as utility regulators are
rarely approving ratepayer investments in microgrids, leading to mixed success in
regulatory proceedings thus far. Regulator resistance is largely due to the inability to
quantify resilience benefits or resilience benefits that do not show utility-system cost-
effectiveness. Approval instead has frequently hinged on utilities demonstrating blue sky
financial benefits that may not be competitive with returns on other utility investments.
According to Sandia National Laboratories’ The Quest for Public Purpose Microgrids for
Resilience: Considerations for Regulatory Approval (Kallay 2021), utility proposals often
lack alignment with traditional utility system needs, with many projects not being sited or
scaled to maximize benefits like avoiding or deferring other utility investments. Finally,
projects are typically one-time, single microgrid efforts, rather than strategically planned
portfolios, which regulators find insufficient for comprehensively adding resilience to the
overall utility system. Challenges of proving cost effectiveness and resilience value
faced by utilities are similar to challenges that municipal employees may face in
determining appropriate resilient energy approaches at a smaller scale.

Effective resilience planning requires broad community outreach and multi-agency
coordination which is often a significant burden on small governments. Experts
therefore stress capacity-building measures like placing federal fellows or AmeriCorps
in rural regions to help with grant writing, or state-led technical-assistance teams to
guide local planners (Reilly 2025). The most successful resilience efforts document that
local ownership of the plan, engaging residents early (including vulnerable populations),
and integrating resilience projects into capital budgets are key to successful
implementation of solar and storage systems (Levite 2024). Building capacity and
systems to address challenges is a focus of current research and policy that is
advanced by F3 to ensure that rural and cold-climate Midwestern communities can
increase local resilience to grid outages.

 F3 Project Objectives

The overarching purposes of the DOE Renewables Advancing Community Energy
Resilience (RACER) grant program included increasing community energy resilience
through the use of solar and solar-plus-storage to prevent, withstand and rapidly
recover from power disruptions caused by extreme weather. Anticipated outputs across
the program portfolio focused on developing multi-stakeholder community resilience
plans, creating and demonstrating rapid energy restoration technologies, and increasing
the applicability of solar as a resilience solution, with a focus on serving vulnerable and
underserved communities. The F3 project advanced solutions toward these goals by
developing a replicable planning process that engaged at the intersection of critical
infrastructure and community-needs.

F3’s overall objective was to expand existing energy resilience planning tools, making
them more accessible to smaller communities that are susceptible to low-attention
disasters. The immediate target geography for project development was the City of
Duluth, Minnesota (population 86,000), which has faced multiple climate hazards,
including derechos, extreme rain events, and coastal flooding over the past decade.
The project aimed to achieve this objective through several key actions including
streamlining and expanding the use of existing tools, developing new stakeholder
engagement processes, analyzing existing planning methodologies, creating new
community-based resilience metrics, and testing the resulting framework by connecting
with other Midwestern communities that face similar exposure to low-attention disasters.
F3 was structured around five major tasks including:

Task 1.0 - Increase Local Capacity to Define Baseline Hazards Analysis and
Disruption Scenarios. This task focused on building the organizational foundation for
the project and defining the scope of hazards in Duluth. Specifically, it involved
onboarding a Community Resiliency Project Coordinator, who was hired by the City of
Duluth, and then establishing a Critical Infrastructure Team (CIT) of stakeholders
representing utilities, key infrastructure operators, emergency management and
response agencies, and F3 community leadership. The team reviewed existing plans
(e.g., St. Louis County Multi-hazard Mitigation Plan) and used FEMA’s Hazus models to
identify potential risks that could be mitigated through development and implementation
of a resilient power plan. The CIT guided the development of diverse power disruption
scenarios (e.g., grid outage during high heat or extreme rain, etc.) that included
cascading failures and informed community-based participatory research to identify the
impacts of the different emergency scenarios. Task milestones were established for the
first and second project quarters including the establishment of the CIT and completion
of grid disruption scenarios.

Task 2.0 - Bottom-Up Functional Resilience Analysis through Community-based
Participatory Research Methodologies. This task focused on the development and
testing of community-based methodologies to define minimum resilience baselines. It
used focus groups with diverse populations and survey research as inputs for a

 8

Community Stakeholder Group (CSG) to determine impacts of grid outage scenarios,
primary negative experiences as understood by different at-risk populations, and
potential mitigations to improve community resilience. The task’s research was then
translated into development of community-based minimum resilience standards for
program planning and use by the CIT, and socio-economic impact information for future
resilience planning. Project milestones for this task included holding a minimum of three
focus groups, developing an understanding of vulnerable populations to each scenario,
and approval of community-based minimum resilience standards by the CIT and CSG
stakeholders.

Task 3.0 - Top-Down Asset-based Energy Resilience Planning. This served as the
main task for the asset-based analysis of the City of Duluth renewable opportunities.
Project partners identified, prioritized, and evaluated public, institutional, and critical
infrastructure sites for viable solar or solar and storage options. During this task resilient
power siting criteria was established that included social vulnerability, grid outage risk,
and past disaster damage factors. Under guidance from project staff, the CIT team
identified, mapped, and vetted a list of potential solar or solar and storage sites with
community stakeholders. Preliminary field inspections and site investigations were
conducted to gather data, identify legal and financial complications, decrease
implementation risk, and reduce potential sites down for deeper engineering and design
analysis. Task milestones included CIT approval of the resilient power siting criterial
rubric, identification and ranking of potential solar and solar and storage sites, and
submission of the site analysis report.

Task 4.0 - Form Follows Function Model and Community Energy Resilience Plan
Development. This task combined the results of the community-based analysis (Task
2.0) and the technical asset mapping (Task 3.0) to identify priority projects where
technically feasible were aligned with community values and benefits. The primary
deliverable of this task was establishment of a Duluth community energy resilience plan,
which summarized the process, including disruption scenarios, resilience standards, site
selection, and recommendations. The plan was reviewed and approved by the CIT and
CSG as the task milestone. City staff followed this plan to establish a portfolio of
projects for potential investment using IRA-era tax credits and general funds.

Task 5.0 - Toolkit Development and Testing. The final F3 task was taking project
approaches and developing a toolkit that could guide smaller communities in replicating
F3 project planning approaches and resilience analysis. Completion of the final
milestone included creating the toolkit methodology, incorporating best practices for
community engagement, and amplifying the Duluth approach. The City of Duluth
worked with staff from three other Midwest communities to review and test F3
approaches.

 Planning

Although Duluth has been named a “climate refuge” city in popular media, in 2012,
heavy storms caused unprecedented flooding along the St. Louis River, causing severe
damage. In 2016, a derecho 250 miles in length led to power loss for 75,000 of
Minnesota Power’s customers. In 2018, 60mph winds caused a storm surge that cost
$18.4 million in recovery and mitigation efforts. Because of these, and other extreme
weather events, the City of Duluth instituted a Climate Action Work Plan (CAWP) in
2022 after declaring a climate emergency in 2021. Two priorities in the CAWP, including
driving down emissions from City operations and strengthening community resilience,
were advanced by Duluth’s RACER approach. It was recognized that in order to
advance community resilience, a thoughtful asset-based approach needed to be
informed by rigorous and authentic community engagement.

Project staff worked with members of St. Louis County and the City of Duluth’s
Emergency Response Manager to review the St. Louis County Hazard Mitigation
Manual. This manual covered a wide-range of disaster and disruption scenarios. The
manual is based off both past disasters in St. Louis County (largest county east of the
Mississippi River) and what emergency government planners see as future hazards
based upon a changing climate. Using this manual as a foundation, the CIT codified
scenarios to advance to project partner Ecolibrium3 for community-based participatory
research, and worked as a team to identify one large disruption scenario, with
cascading events, that could be used to develop a tabletop exercise.

The City of Duluth connected with 19 organization and departments on the state,
regional, and local level. These organizations provided 40 people to participate in two
tabletop exercises. The first was held with the CIT, government representatives, and
limited community-based organizations. The tabletop exercise was then repeated with a
focus on participation by community-based organizations. During the second tabletop
exercise, participants were asked to create personas of individuals they serve, to better

From the tabletop conversations and debriefing, several important factors were
identified to inform the CIT’s development of a site rubric, selection of top potential
renewable development sites, and formation of the Community Stakeholder Group’s
(CSG) community-based research plan. A major realization was the disconnect
experienced between emergency government and community-based organizations in
the scenario. Community-based organizations were less familiar with the processes and
thresholds that are used by emergency management to determine government
response. It was clear that power outages would continue to be addressed until power
was restored to all affected households, however, the scenario included minor
basement flooding for a portion of the community. Community-based organizations
reflected surprise that unless a certain damage threshold was met, impacted community
members would need to address damage on their own. Better understanding these
thresholds and how the different disaster scenarios could impact various households
became a focus of community-based research under Task 2.0.
Bringing together a comprehensive city asset list, the grid reliability mapping, mapping
of previous disaster impact areas, social vulnerability mapping through the then
available Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool and EPA EJScreen, and
information gleaned from the tabletop exercises, the CIT took 4 months to develop a
site scoring rubric to help identify the top city and community assets available for solar
PV and storage development. To rank all assets, the developed rubric included
considerations of energy usage, site topography and underlying characteristics,
estimated potential array size, and resulting economic considerations. Using this rubric
(Figure 5) and energy data from across City operations, the CIT narrowed down the
best 10 sites for solar and storage.

After the determination of the top sites were complete, an initial analysis of each site
was completed by LHB Inc, an engineering firm contracted to do an initial assessment
to ground-truth conclusions established through the siting rubric and further identify a
smaller subset of sites for a second phase engineering design and deeper financial
analysis.
Diverse sites were identified for potential projects including a community center that
could be repurposed as a resilience hub and the municipal gas utilities
office/maintenance building. Out of the ten sites, the City’s Bayview Water Reservoir
and Pump Station rose to the top as the most promising project for solar PV
implementation and as a demonstration site to work through City processes for
permitting, construction, and financial benefits structures.
The City’s Bayview Water Reservoir and Pump Station, located at 6300 Highland Street
was selected not only for the ability to reuse developed infrastructure but also because
of the community in which it resides. The site is 0.25 miles from a redeveloped
brownfield and is located directly adjacent to a low-moderate income (LMI)
neighborhood (census tract 27137003300). This neighborhood was identified by
Minnesota Power as a vulnerable area due to the high System Average Interruption
Duration Index (SAIDI) and System Average Interruption Frequency Index (SAIFI)
scores; which shows that this site is within neighborhoods with some of the most
frequent and longer duration power losses in the Duluth community. A proposed project
would use one of the City’s 10,000,000-gallon water reservoir and pump station sites to
develop approximately 1-MW of solar with a portion of the solar used for pumping
operations and the remainder available as a low-income community solar garden. The
6-acre site is host to an approximate 81,100 square foot concrete cap that is completely
vacant, and recently replaced in 2014. Although using the site as a foundation for a
neighborhood-based microgrid was dismissed as too expensive, solar installation
provides positive secondary use of the site, takes advantage of capital improvement

 13

investments on the cap, and increases blue sky financial stability for the city and
participating low-income households.
Duluth has historically been a hub for industry with limited availability of vacant land for
large-scale solar within city limits. Incorporating solar onto existing assets reduces the
City’s operating costs, carbon footprint, greenhouse gas emissions, and personnel time.
Additionally, utilizing infrastructure with solar as a secondary use saves larger swaths of
land for partnership with private entities that help to drive business and industrial
investment in the community. Developing an array on existing site also allows the City
to use cost savings by covering the pumping station electrical load that resides on that
site. The pumping station uses approximately 225,000 kWh a year, costing the City an
average of $38,625 a year. With net metering allowing up to 120% of the load on any
particular site in Minnesota Power territory, the City of Duluth could save a maximum of
approximately $46,000 a year. That means over the projected 25-year life-span of the
proposed project, the City could save $1.15 Million in operating costs- funding that can
be reinvested to encourage additional renewable energy development.
Through input from the CIT and expanded conversations with Minnesota Power due to
the RACER project, the City and Minnesota Power began exploring how the remaining
approximate 1,000,000 kWh per year of estimated annual average generation on site in
excess of the allowed 120% of energy consumption under net metering provisions,
could be used offset low-moderate income household energy costs addressing energy
burdens and building greater economic resilience. This potential partnership with
Minnesota Power would allow for flexibility to use the extra energy generation on City
land to benefit community members in ways that other municipalities might not be able
to. Putting the extra approximate 1,000,000 kWh hours generated into community solar
would save approximately 200 detached single-family households, each receiving the
benefit of 4kW of the array, an average of $669 per year.
In addition to the benefit provided to the Duluth economy and reduction of household
energy burdens, the Bayview project would benefit the regional economy as well.
Initially envisioned under Build America Buy America requirements, the proposed
project and financial calculations included solar panels manufactured in Mountain Iron,
Minnesota. Located just over an hour north of Duluth, this solar panel manufacturer
provides over 130 local jobs on the Minnesota Iron Range and is one of the larger
employers in the Mountain Iron area. By working directly with locally manufactured
panels, it could put approximately $757,000 back into the regional economy and a small
rural neighboring community.

Evaluations included assessment of blue sky and resilience benefits for adding battery
storage. Potential benefits included peak shifting and demand charge reduction,
focused on sites with existing loads. Valuation of other benefits during a grid outage, for

economic impacts of pumping stations being offline, were not included in the analysis.

emergency government facilities (firehalls/police stations), reducing the potential
resilience return for battery storage added to these facilities. Two sites were evaluated
to have significant demand charges that could be reduced through battery installation.
However, structural (Middle Pump Reservoir) and operational/site concerns (Water
Treatment Plant) that would need further evaluation.

F3 made it a priority to get input from City departments, community partners, and
individual residents to create a holistic picture of what is needed for resilience. This
research and engagement began with focus groups generating feedback from the
community and project partners to guide solar infrastructure planning. By using an all-
inclusive research method that is rarely deployed by municipalities, the project was able
to capture resident experiences with a focus on vulnerable populations to enhance the
understanding of community energy and resilience needs that could be addressed by
community PV and storage implementation.

The City of Duluth’s project partner, community-based organization Ecolibrium3, led the
community-based research element for F3. Ecolibrium3 trained community volunteers
to lead a tabletop exercise and host focus groups that concentrated on understanding
lived-experiences of participants. Needs of vulnerable populations in disadvantaged
communities were identified though oversampling in neighborhoods identified through
the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool as being disadvantaged. Information
from the tabletop and focus groups were then incorporated into a research plan that
was vetted by the Community Stakeholder Group, representing diverse community
organizations. Neighborhood canvassers were then trained to conduct a survey focused
on experiences of grid outages, impacts of outages and different disaster contexts as
identified by the CIT, and community preferences for building resilience.

Using a three-tier process of community evaluation and input, the team was able to
gather a holistic view from all organizations and community household types to prioritize
needs, risks, and barriers. By aggregating all of the feedback from the community
surveys, focus groups, and tabletop exercises, the results were cross-referenced with
the top ten scoring sites from Task 1.0 as further integration of asset-based and
community-up approaches. This affirmed Bayview as a primary site for demonstration
development because it has one of the highest returns on investment in terms of City
operations and budget, critical infrastructure resilience, utility partnership opportunity,
community benefits, and energy burden relief for low-moderate income households with
appropriate low-income community solar subscription policy implementation.

Analysis of the community survey data provided critical metrics on the local context of
grid disruptions. While many respondents reported outage durations between 5–12
hours (25.1%) or 2–4 hours (24.6%), a significant minority experienced prolonged
losses of power: 1–2 days (16.8%), 3–5 days (14.4%), and over 5 days (4.8%). Further

 16

evaluation of this data reflected that those experiencing outages longer than 1 day were
nearly evenly split between those impacted by a 2016 windstorm that impacted a part of
Duluth and those that experienced the outages while living in other areas. Severe
rain/flooding was identified as the most common cause of outages at 16.0%. Other
prevalent causes cited included human-caused outages (14.1%), winter
storm/blizzard/ice (13.9%), windstorm (13.1%), and extreme cold weather (10.3%),
validating the climate-specific focus intended for establishing community-based
minimum resilience standards.

In addition to developing an understanding of the types of grid disruptions experienced
by Duluthians, the neighborhood canvass and outreach strategy worked to identify
existing preparedness and willingness of community members to take advantage of
offsite community-based response options like shelters. In addition to these door-to-
door conversations, a 20-minute survey of 167 individuals was conducted. Survey
results substantiated issues identified during focus groups and interviews. Results that
informed recommendations on establishing minimum resilience standards included
understanding basics like the prevalence of lighting and power sources by individuals
that ranged from access to flashlights (89.8%), batteries for lights, radios, etc. (78.4%),
candles/matches (74.9%), car charger for phones/small electronics (45.5%), and power
banks or portable chargers for small devices (42.5%) to fuel-based generators (15.0%),
solar-powered batteries (7.8%), and whole-home batteries (1.2%). In addition, the data
on relocation concerns highlighted significant barriers to utilizing centralized emergency
shelters. The most frequently cited concern was safety and security (16.8%),
encompassing worries about the security of personal belongings left behind and the
potential for civil disorder if a person were to relocate during a grid outage. Other top
concerns included transportation and mobility (13.1%), availability of resources and
supplies (12.6%), and pets (12.0%), with many stating they would not relocate without
their animals. These responses underscore that shared resilience infrastructure must
address these functional and psychological barriers, or the City could invest in creating
resilience hubs that go unutilized.

Further data detailing community requests for emergency government planners
emphasized both micro-level preparedness needs and macro-level infrastructure
demands. Residents requested more specific information regarding how the City would
communicate in a disaster/grid outage, where shelter locations would be located, and
how residents lacking personal vehicles would be transported. Although most of the
community-based research focused on individual experiences, impacts of outages, and
preparedness actions, technical demands were vocalized, including the need for “more
utility power redundancy—more power lines, local generation sources, and microgrids,
plus more non-fossil fuel energy.” Other requests focused on prioritizing evacuation and
support for “elderly and people with disabilities,” and providing location-specific, hour-
by-hour guides for response.

Following the tabletop exercises, focus groups, canvasses, and survey research, the
CSG integrated community concerns with the disruption scenarios established by the
CIT. Initially it was assumed that the disaster scenarios would be the foundation of the

 17

minimum resilience standards, for example a minimum resilience standard would be
created for heat events, rain events, etc. Although this was the case for some of the
minimum resilience standards, the CSG determined that other standards should focus
on particular vulnerabilities, no matter the context of the grid outage, and on specific
negative impacts, no matter the vulnerable group or outage context.

The resulting minimum resilience standards developed under F3 establish a critical,
community-centric baseline for energy resilience planning. These standards move
beyond typical critical infrastructure protection by defining the functional necessities
required by residents during extended grid disruptions. The minimum resilience
standards do not serve as a community mandate but a conceptual framework to
prioritize program design and implementation. The framework provides the structure for
ongoing cumulative impact work of CIT and Community Stakeholder Group members.

Grid outage/disaster scenario-based minimum resilience standards included extreme
cold, extreme heat, and rain events/flooding. Addressing Duluth's cold-climate reality,
the Winter Conditions - Extreme Cold standard requires that all community members
possess an emergency plan for power outages during extreme cold, incorporating at-
home options (like reducing drafts with DIY weatherization and using warm clothes) and
providing clear relocation information. The Summer Conditions - Extreme Heat standard
mandates that community members understand heat impacts and have a plan for
adequate cooling, either at home or at community-based cooling centers. Shared
solutions prioritize the use of cooling centers, giving vulnerable populations and those
experiencing heat-related illnesses priority access. In general, Duluthians feel better
prepared to address cold temperatures and winter storms than temperatures above
95% with high humidity. For outages over 5 days, 65.2% or residents would stay in their
home without power during a winter storm/cold event versus 35.0% that would remain
under extreme heat and humidity. Property Protection – Flooding was the final event-
based standard created. It would require individuals to hold flood and/or renters’
insurance, perform rain-readiness assessments, and implement mitigation steps like
sealing foundation cracks. Shared solutions for this standard included community
mitigation projects using green infrastructure and maintaining a shared library of backup
pumps and power sources to maintain power to sump pumps when necessary.

Minimum resilience standards based upon individual vulnerabilities were prioritized for
those dependent upon powered medical devices, temperature sensitive medication, and
power-dependent mobility devices. These were considered the most sensitive minimum
resilience standards because they address life-sustaining health and medical needs.
The Powered Medical Devices standard mandates that all community members reliant
on critical medical devices must have an emergency power plan to ensure their health
and safety until power is restored or they can be relocated. Distributed actions
necessitate partnering with medical teams and device suppliers to determine power
alternatives (such as manual devices, home generators, or battery solutions) and
ensuring device information is readily available to individuals and their care teams. Of
the 21.7% of survey respondents that indicated that they, or someone in their
household, used a powered home medical device, 54.1% stated that they do not have

 18

an emergency power plan for their device(s) and 48.7% have no backup for charging
during a power outage. Establishing resilience hubs that offer priority admission for
individuals reliant on life-sustaining devices was suggested multiple times in interviews,
however 57.0% of all residents said they were not at all likely or would not consider use
of public emergency shelters/warming/cooling centers.

Temperature Sensitive Medicine standard also requires establishing emergency plans
and safe use or replacement protocols for medicine that may be compromised during an
outage, with shelters/resilience hubs tasked with providing priority access to
refrigeration. Research indicated a knowledge gap that could be filled through better
communication between providers and residents. 19.2% of those surveyed indicated
having someone in their household on temperature sensitive medicine. Of those, 34.4%
indicated that they do not know what to do with their medicine during a power outage
and 59.4% have not received, or did not remember ever receiving, information that
would inform them on the continued viability/safety of their medicine if the temperature
range has not been maintained.

Finally, the Mobility standard dictates that individuals reliant on power-dependent
mobility devices must have an emergency plan to ensure their ability to maintain safety
or move to a safe location. Distributed actions involve documenting power instructions
and possessing backup manual devices. Results indicated that shelter/resilience hubs
should provide priority access to charging powered mobility devices and potentially
maintaining a community library of manual mobility devices. Although those with chronic
mobility-limiting conditions had alternate devices and plans, a gap was found for
individuals that may have temporary mobility issues (e.g., post-surgery, etc.).

The remaining four community-based minimum resilience standards were based upon
securing basic human needs during a disaster or grid outage. The Safety-Lighting
standard focuses on preventing accidents and injuries during outages through adequate
lighting equipment. This can be accomplished through battery-operated light sources
and solar chargeable options. Community-based solutions included considering the
temporary generators with outdoor lighting when street lighting is non-functional. This is
an area where city-based infrastructure including solar lighting was recommended to the
CIT. Essential for public safety, the Communication standard requires individuals to
have connectivity (cellular or wifi) or a battery-powered radio to receive emergency
alerts for the duration of an outage. Resilience hubs were identified as places for
charging communication devices and key information dissemination points for
community members. The Food Safety standard requires individuals to have access to
three days of nonperishable food and mandates clear processes for replacing food
impacted by power outages, especially for those dependent on food subsidies.
Community-based solutions included the distribution of nonperishables, and cooking
resources for perishables to mitigate food waste. Finally, the Water standard aligns with
CDC recommendations of one gallon of water per person per day, requiring individuals
to store water and potentially purification tablets, while mandating that the community
establish a clear distribution plan for this resource in the event of grid outages with
cascading impacts on the City’s water distribution system.

From Tasks 1.0-3.0 a Community Energy Resilience Plan (CERP) was created and
approved by CIT and CSG members. Duluth’s CERP includes a portfolio of solar and
storage projects across the community that are shovel-ready based upon engineering
and interconnection application completion. In addition to these five projects, additional
solar-only options were prioritized based upon return on investment and ability to
reduce blue sky energy costs. Economic modeling of all projects included the payback
to the City inclusive of federal solar development task credits including applicable Build
America, Buy America and low-income enhancements. Duluth’s CERP also highlighted
the minimum resilience standards that were developed and ongoing partnership
approaches to develop actionable assistance programs for building individual power
resilience.

During the second half of the F3 project, the process and resources used to develop
Duluth’s CERP were developed into the Community Resilience Planning Toolkit
(Toolkit) to be used by other capacity-limited Midwest communities. Three communities,
Rapid City, South Dakota, and Morehead and Maplewood, Minnesota, entered into

 20

memorandums of understanding with the City of Duluth to test aspects of the planning
process and provide feedback on the final toolkit.

The Toolkit focuses on cold-climate communities and assists municipal staff and
community organizations on pairing solar and storage asset planning with community-
based approaches. Written to enable community champions to succeed in planning
efforts, the toolkit addresses team building approaches, stakeholder recruitment,
development of Critical Infrastructure Teams and Community Stakeholder Groups,
community vulnerability assessments, grid reliability metrics, scenario creation and
tabletop exercise development, community-based research recommendations, and
asset assessment methodologies. The toolkit provides a quicker on-ramp to community
resilience planning than would generally be possible for government employees and
community members.

Important resources that accompany the Toolkit as appendices are Definitions, Duluth
and Morehead’s Tabletop Scenarios, the Community Service Tabletop Persona
Development Tool, Duluth’s 2-Year Community Research Plan, the Solar Siting Rubric,
Solar Site Master List Example, Community Resilience Plan Outline, and Duluth’s
Minimum Resilience Standards.

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions

The F3 project advances the field of resilience planning by implementing a novel
integration of traditional top-down asset-based approaches with community-based
bottom-up methodologies. It provides a structure and resources to guide capacity-
limited communities toward establishment of cross-sectoral community resilience plans
centered on governmental solar and storage projects augmented with individual or
community-based actions that can be taken to lessen negative impacts during grid
outages.

The established toolkit provides a community resilience plan outline that can, at its most
basic, document the process and results for a community based upon the F3 approach,
however, it has further resources for communities that have the capacity to expand the
plan into deeper exploration of environmental disparities, broader non-energy focused
resilience (e.g. water management, etc.), or policies.

The asset-based approach, detailed in the toolkit, assists governmental planners,
sustainability officers, and facility managers by providing a methodology for limiting
financial exposure by right-sizing initial investigations, engaging with the public to
surface any opposition or preferred options, and only investing in engineering and
design for pre-vetted and prioritized projects. It also provides a siting rubric that can
allow multiple stakeholder participation and publicly defensible guidelines for when
projects are selected, deemphasized, or eliminated.

 21

F3 provides direct public benefit by centering its solution sets on vulnerable populations,
offering access to clean energy and community benefits. The community-based
research detailed methodologies that can be applied by governmental and
organizational staff to deepen community engagement.

From its onset, F3 recognized financial limitations that would prevent a municipality from
creating broad solar and storage solutions to meet the resilient power needs of an entire
community. This was found to especially be the case when grid outages and major
natural disasters are more limited in the Midwest compared to large ongoing wildfire and
hurricane risks experienced elsewhere in the country, thus limiting return on investment
or upfront willingness to pay for resilience premiums. Determining the most vulnerable
populations, weather event contexts, and negative impacts related to grid outages
through community-based participatory research, and then working with community
members and stakeholders to identify and implement individual and community-based
approaches provides forward progress while more costly renewable projects are
contemplated, designed, and implemented. Ultimately, Duluth will be a more resilient
community during grid disruptions as community identified support systems, such as
resilience and preparedness classes, individual emergency power plans, and expanding
uptake of renter’s insurance to bridge recovery gaps. This provides an option to build
resilience even if renewable projects are never built.

During the performance period of this grant, two significant changes occurred. The first
was staffing changes at the City of Duluth that increases capacity limitations for project
implementation. The second major change was the federal shift away from solar
development as preferred energy policy and modifications to anticipated federal roles in
disaster response. Both of these changes underscore the importance of the F3
approach that deeply engaged community stakeholders and delivered solutions sets
that were not dependent solely on renewable incentives or development. By expanding
those potentially involved in delivering resilience solutions, greater overall community
capacity can be built. In many ways, F3 provided a community-derived answer to the
question, “With limited resources, what are the most important resilience actions to
implement.” The answer resulted in greater interest in community knowledge exchange
and very targeted individual action than larger, more expensive renewable projects.

Despite these challenges and conclusions, F3 accomplished a replicable, open-source
toolkit that can be adopted by smaller municipalities across cold-climate regions, an
expansion of energy resilience planning and preparation of communities for extreme
weather events and grid disruptions, a methodology for creating community-based
minimum resilience standards to protect a community’s most vulnerable residents, and
renewable siting and evaluation tools to lessen the burden on capacity-limited
communities.

 Path Forward
The City of Duluth’s initial interest in the RACER grant was to vet and prioritize projects
for implementation, as resilience is advanced through actual projects, not just project
plans. Financial projections for projects assumed continued availability of federal tax
credits and policies that made direct pay available to municipalities. Although the
framework developed under F3 provides flexibility as policy environments change,
current policy uncertainty and staffing turnover has created an uncertain timeframe for
solar and storage buildouts as identified through this project.
Aligned partnerships first explored under RACER may result in new City/utility
partnerships. In the past, the City has worked to install solar that is owned and operated
by Minnesota Power on vacant land owned by the City, but has not installed anything
over 45kW with City infrastructure. Using permanent community infrastructure and
assets to spur renewable energy development is something that not many communities
have been able to do. While many other communities utilize small-scale rooftop solar,
micro-solar PV on streetlights, and community solar run by third party development
companies, it is a priority for the City of Duluth to incorporate large-scale solar into the
fabric of the community. The proposed Bayview project will be pursued as a model for
scaling additional solar developments that have been identified through RACER
research. Developing this project gives the community and administration a baseline of
what the actual solar costs and benefits are, both economically and socially, in order to
institute solar on other identified sites.
Additional sites that the City of Duluth plans to install solar on include two pump stations
and reservoirs, the City owned water treatment plant, two firehalls, two police stations,
three community centers, and the City owned natural gas utility. RACER has provided a
baseline for cost, energy savings, and community benefits, and guides plans for these
potential installations. Implementing projects in Duluth at this scale could save the city a
potential $553,361 per year and transform the City’s operating budget approach. While
not all identified sites have the opportunity to directly offset LMI household energy costs
like the potential Bayview project, each site provides a certain community benefit, from
resilience hubs and resources distribution to ensuring emergency services are efficient
and affordable. Accomplishing implementation of these plans will be dependent of
financial resources and available incentives.
The advantage of completing both a top-down and bottom-up approach is that multiple
community partners can be involved in advancing the community-based minimum
resilience standards as potential City-based solar projects seek financing and
administrative approval. The immediate next steps for building community resilience are
driven by the community's ranked priorities, focusing primarily on individual action
planning assisted by the Community Stakeholder Group and led community-based
organization, Ecolibrium3.
Informational outreach and development of partnerships to aid community members in
building their own preparedness and resilience resources will be pursued with the
assistance of Ecolibrium3’s AmeriCorps programs. The highest-ranked community
desires, as indicated in focus groups, interviews, and survey results were information on
home power backup options, and assistance with development of emergency power

 23

plans for medical devices, temperature sensitive medicines, and overall home energy
use. Residents also indicated an interest in programs to assist with cost and installation
of home power backup options, however, the minimum resilience standards indicate a
more cost-effective approach would be to first meet specific resilience needs based
upon individual vulnerabilities. Ecolibrium3 is incorporating this education and planning
assistance as part of their Energy Training Corps, which already meets with households
as part of efficiency, rain-readiness, and healthy housing assessments.
As an ongoing commitment to building community resilience, project partners will
continue to work together to advance targeted community-based efforts. Ongoing
outreach will build upon the organizations and businesses involved in the CIT and CSG
to ensure broad dissemination of resources.

Collaborating Organizations, Teams, and Stakeholders

In addition to the co-led organizations, the City of Duluth and Ecolibrium3, project
contributions included LHB Inc, the engineering firm selected to conduct technical and
economic analyses of the priority sites identified by the Critical Infrastructure and
Community Stakeholder Teams; Grid Catalyst an energy technology incubation firm that

functional needs identified during the project; the University of Duluth Minnesota whose
economics faculty member Dr. Neil Wilmot, and social science faculty Dr. Aparna Katre,
contributed to project design.

(IOU), MN Power and municipal gas utility ComfortSysems, in addition to Duluth Public
Works and Utilities, Resource Renew (formerly the Western Lake Superior Sanitary
District), St. Louis County Emergency Management, Duluth Fire
Department/Emergency Management, Minnesota Department of Homeland Security
and Emergency Management, and Ecolibrium3. The CIT outlined outage scenarios,
identified components for the site selection rubric, advised on the planning scenarios,
and provided valuable input into the toolkit and final resilience plan approaches.

Network- multi-stakeholder group established after 2012 flooding in northeastern
Minnesota, the Duluth Climate and Energy Network- community and environmental
leaders overseeing energy and climate approaches in Duluth, Duluth YMCA- primary

methodologies and toolkit. Participating communities included Moorhead, MN,
Maplewood, MN, and Rapid City, SD.

 Powered Medical Devices
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members dependent upon critical medical
devices have an emergency power plan to maintain health and safety until power is restored or
they can be moved to a safe facility that can meet their medical needs.
Vulnerability: Populations that rely on home medical devices are particularly vulnerable during
power outages, as these devices often support critical health functions. Some of the most at-risk
groups include people with respiratory conditions dependent on oxygen therapy (including
CPAP/BiPAP users), people with chronic health conditions, including diabetes, kidney disease,
and coronary issues, mobility-limited individuals who depend on powered wheelchairs or
scooters, and older individuals or people with cognitive disabilities that may require an
emergency alert system or monitoring.
Potential Distributed Actions: Creating an emergency power plan prior to an outage would
provide individuals reliant on powered medical devices more ease in adapting to use without
power. Emergency power plans should be created in partnership with an individual’s medical
team, consulting medical professionals and device suppliers on how to safely maintain health
and safety until power is restored. Plans should include power alternatives (generator, batteries,
or relocation if needed), clear instructions for how to obtain and connect devices, medical device
instruction manuals, and serial and model numbers. When creating an emergency power plan,
individuals and their care teams should consider if devices will alert users if a power outage
occurs while they are sleeping, and if equipment will need to be reset when power is
reconnected (either temporarily or when power is restored)

 34

In-home backup power sources would provide individuals with powered medical devices the
comfort of remaining at home during a power outage. Generators and portable batteries or
power stations accessible to individuals in the home would fulfill this need.
Potential Shared Solutions: Resilience Hubs as a shared solution can provide many
resources to individuals during power outages. Allowing priority admission to those with life-
sustaining devices will ensure those individuals can maintain health and safety until power is
restored.
Rationale: In the event of a power outage, individuals who rely on powered medical devices are
particularly vulnerable and need to be prioritized in emergency preparedness efforts. Medical
devices such as ventilators, oxygen concentrators, dialysis machines, and CPAPs are crucial for
the daily health and well-being of many individuals. Without power, these devices cannot
function, potentially putting lives at risk. A community must work to ensure that these individuals
have plans in place to maintain the operation of their medical devices and receive the support
they need during prolonged grid outages.
One of the most critical preparedness actions is ensuring that individuals with powered medical
devices have access to backup power. This can be accomplished through the use of
generators, uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), or portable power banks, depending on the
device's power requirements. Emergency managers should encourage individuals to assess the
capacity of their medical devices and match it with the appropriate backup power sources.
Moreover, it is essential that backup power systems are tested regularly to ensure they are in
working order. In addition, individuals should be instructed to have extra fuel or batteries
available to sustain these devices for extended periods, especially during long-term power
outages.
Communication is another key element and may include individuals using medical devices
registering with local emergency services and/or the electric utility, to ensure that they can be
prioritized for assistance in the event of a power failure. Establishing a notification system can
also make sure individuals receive early warnings of potential outages, so they can take
proactive measures to safeguard their health and device functionality. It is important for these
systems to be accessible and reliable, with regular checks to ensure they are functioning and
reaching those who need them.
Doctors, home healthcare providers, and medical supply companies should be part of the
conversation when crafting individualized emergency preparedness strategies for those who
use powered medical devices. These professionals can provide guidance on maintaining and
using medical equipment in the event of a power outage, including recommendations on safe
usage, device alternatives, or steps to take should the power go out unexpectedly. Healthcare
providers should also ensure their patients have up-to-date contact information and a plan for
quickly accessing medical care if needed during a crisis.
Finally, public education plays a vital role in preparing individuals for power outages. A
community should work to raise awareness about the unique needs of individuals who rely on
powered medical devices. Community outreach efforts, such as workshops, information
campaigns, and training programs, can help individuals understand the importance of having
backup power systems and emergency plans in place. The US Department of Health and
Human Services has created a workbook for users of home medical devices to assist in
establishing a home energy plan. A potential community project could be the distribution of this
workbook or a similar planning document to community members, in partnership with healthcare
institutions and emergency government. This type of project could also help families, friends,

 35

and neighbors of those with medical needs become more aware of how to assist in the event of
an emergency, creating a more supportive and resilient community overall.
By taking these essential actions—providing access to backup power, ensuring communication,
collaborating with healthcare professionals, and increasing public awareness—emergency
managers can significantly reduce the risks associated with power outages for individuals who
rely on powered medical devices. The success of these strategies depends on thorough
planning, collaboration, and ongoing support to ensure the safety and well-being of vulnerable
populations during power disruptions.

 Temperature-Sensitive Medicine
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members dependent upon temperature-
sensitive medicine should have an essential medicine emergency plan for maintaining the
temperature of the medicine and establishing safe use or replacement protocols for any
medicine that may be compromised during a power outage.
Vulnerability: Certain populations are more likely to be prescribed temperature-sensitive
medicines due to their specific health conditions or treatment needs. These medicines often
require careful storage to maintain their effectiveness and safety. The populations most likely to
be prescribed temperature-sensitive medicines include people with diabetes, autoimmune
disorders, chronic inflammatory conditions, cancer, severe allergies, asthma, hormonal
disorders, hemophilia or other bleeding disorders, immune deficiency, or undergoing a
transplant, fertility treatment, or taking certain antiviral, antifungal, or antibiotics.
Potential Distributed Actions: Individuals with temperature-sensitive medications should have
a backup cooling plan if they lose power. Plans should include the temperature medications
need to stay and detail what happens to a medication if it becomes too warm or cool. Individuals
can use coolers and ice packs or other frozen objects to control the temperature of medications
when it is no longer safe to keep them in the refrigerator.
Potential Shared Solutions: If an individual with temperature-sensitive medications is unable
to safely store medications in their home, they should have priority access to refrigeration at
Resilience Hubs or other powered facilities when power is out to ensure the health and safety of
that individual.
Rationale: During a power outage, it is crucial to have a well-organized strategy for maintaining
the integrity of temperature-sensitive medications, such as insulin, vaccines, and other
refrigeration-dependent treatments. Emergency government staff should ensure that individuals
relying on these medications have clear instructions on what to do in the event of a power
failure. For short outages, guidance should focus on minimizing the opening of refrigerators to
preserve internal temperatures. If the outage extends beyond a few hours, it is vital to inform
affected individuals about the use of ice packs, coolers, or alternative refrigeration options.
Coordinating with local facilities or trusted neighbors to provide storage for these medications
can be a key strategy. Providing information on these emergency measures in advance can
prevent delays in securing viable solutions during critical times.
Another important element of the preparedness plan is to educate the public on how to assess
the safety of temperature-sensitive medications once they have been exposed to excessive
warmth. Emergency staff should be prepared to advise individuals to consult with pharmacists
or healthcare providers if they suspect their medication has been stored outside the required
temperature range. It is essential to recognize that some medications may lose potency or even

 36

become unsafe if exposed to heat for extended periods. Therefore, emergency protocols should
include the availability of a helpline or referral system for individuals seeking professional
guidance on whether to continue using their medication. Emergency staff should also
emphasize the importance of not using compromised medications, as doing so could jeopardize
health outcomes and safety.
From a community-based perspective, local government agencies should collaborate with
healthcare providers, pharmacies, and other relevant services to establish networks that assist
individuals with temperature-sensitive medications during power outages. This could involve
creating partnerships where pharmacies or medical facilities are designated as safe storage
points for critical medications, or where local cooling resources like generators or ice storage
systems are made available. Additionally, local authorities should encourage community
resilience by promoting neighborhood-level emergency preparedness programs. By facilitating
the sharing of resources and information, emergency staff can help foster stronger community
bonds that ensure individuals with medical vulnerabilities are not left without vital care during
disruptions to the power grid. Such measures will contribute to a more coordinated and effective
response to power outages that impact vulnerable populations.

 Mobility
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members dependent upon devices for mobility
have an emergency mobility plan to maintain health and safety until power is restored or they
can be moved to a safe location.
Vulnerability: Populations that are more likely to be mobility impaired and require powered
equipment, such as wheelchairs, scooters, and elevators, often have conditions that limit their
ability to walk or move independently. These individuals depend on various forms of assistive
technology to maintain their independence, quality of life, and access to essential services. Key
populations whose mobility might be impacted by a power outage include older adults,
individuals with neurological conditions and musculoskeletal disorders, amputees, and those
with spinal cord injuries. In addition, those with severe obesity or respiratory disorders may
depend on mobility devices. Chronic conditions may indicate a higher understanding and
planning for mobility needs during a power outage (e.g., manual wheelchair available,
evacuation plan from building if elevator is out); however, temporary mobility challenges due to
injury or recovery from surgery may result in lower levels of planning and resilience.
Potential Distributed Actions: Individuals who are reliant on mobility equipment should have
an emergency power plan or backup manual device to use in case of emergencies. Plans
should include power alternatives (generator, batteries, or relocation if needed), clear
instructions for how to obtain and connect devices, mobility device instruction manuals, and
serial and model numbers. When creating an emergency power plan, individuals and their care
teams should consider if equipment will need to be reset when power is reconnected (either
temporarily or when power is restored).
Potential Shared Solutions: Individuals needing to charge mobility devices should have
priority access to charging at a resilience hub in order to ensure the health and safety of those
individuals. Additionally, institutions or organizations with surplus supplies could check out their
manual devices to individuals in need during power outages.
Rationale: Ensuring the safety of individuals dependent on mobility devices during an
emergency or power outage requires careful planning and proactive measures. First, individuals
using wheelchairs should have a personalized emergency plan that includes accessible

 37

evacuation routes, alternative exits, and assistance contacts. Keeping an emergency supply kit
with essential medications, backup batteries, and a manual wheelchair as a contingency is
crucial. Additionally, notifying building management or local emergency services about mobility
needs in advance can help responders provide timely and appropriate assistance.
For those who rely on elevators to exit a building, alternative escape strategies must be
established in case of power failure. Identifying stairwell evacuation chairs, training designated
personnel on their use, and practicing emergency drills can ensure a smooth evacuation. In
multi-story buildings, individuals should work with management to determine designated areas
of refuge where they can wait safely for assistance. Battery-powered stair lifts or portable ramps
may also be considered for buildings with limited accessibility options.
Finally, communication is key in any emergency. Individuals should maintain a charged mobile
phone, an emergency contact list, and a whistle or flashlight to signal for help if necessary.
Caregivers and building staff should be trained to assist those with mobility impairments, and
emergency plans should be regularly reviewed and updated. By prioritizing preparedness,
ensuring accessible evacuation routes, and fostering a supportive network, the safety of
individuals dependent on mobility devices can be significantly enhanced during unexpected
emergencies.

 Winter Conditions- Extreme Cold
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members will have an emergency plan for
addressing power outage during extreme cold that includes at-home options to minimize health
and safety impacts and clear information for situations where relocation may be necessary.
Vulnerability: Certain populations are particularly vulnerable to extreme cold during a power
outage due to physical, medical, and economic factors. Elderly individuals face a higher risk of
hypothermia due to reduced body temperature regulation, while infants and young children lose
heat quickly and may struggle to communicate discomfort. People with chronic illnesses or
disabilities may experience worsened symptoms or lose access to critical medical equipment.
Low-income and homeless populations often lack adequate heating, warm clothing, or shelter,
making them especially susceptible to cold-related illnesses. Rural and isolated residents may
endure longer power restoration times and limited emergency access, while outdoor workers
and first responders face prolonged exposure to freezing temperatures. Households with poor
energy performance, including a lack of insulation and high air leakage, will cool faster and
reduce the amount of time with safe temperatures. Protecting these groups requires proactive
measures such as emergency warming shelters, backup heating solutions, and community
support to prevent serious health risks during extreme cold events.
Potential Distributed Actions: There are a lot of everyday items in the home that can be used
to protect from extreme cold. Ensuring access to blankets and warm clothes to layer can
prolong the amount of time an individual is comfortable sheltering in place. Blankets and tape
can be used to reduce drafts from windows and doors, bringing cold air into the home.
Additionally, including hand/foot warmers in a household emergency kit can provide additional
safety from the cold.
Potential Shared Solutions: Warming centers with backup power can be life-saving for certain
populations during extreme cold events. In the event of a prolonged outage in extreme cold, it
will be important to open additional warming centers for individuals who are unable to shelter in
place due to a lack of supplies to protect themselves from the cold.

 38

Rationale: Power outages in a cold climate like Minnesota during winter can pose serious risks,
particularly due to the extreme cold temperatures that can quickly create life-threatening
conditions. Without electricity, heating systems fail, pipes can freeze and burst, and people are
at risk of hypothermia or frostbite. The duration of the outage and the severity of the weather
determine how dangerous the situation becomes, making preparedness essential. Proper
insulation, backup heating sources, and emergency supplies can help individuals and families
stay safe until power is restored.
The most vulnerable populations during a heat-out situation include the elderly, young children,
individuals with disabilities, and those with chronic illnesses. These groups are more susceptible
to hypothermia and other cold-related health issues due to reduced mobility, weakened immune
systems, or difficulty in regulating body temperature. Additionally, low-income individuals who
may lack adequate heating alternatives or emergency supplies are at higher risk. Those living in
rural areas, where response times from emergency services may be slower, also face increased
dangers.
During a power outage, individuals should take immediate steps to retain heat and stay safe.
Layering warm clothing, using extra blankets, and closing off unused rooms can help conserve
warmth. If a backup heat source, such as a fireplace or propane heater, is available, it should be
used safely with proper ventilation to prevent carbon monoxide poisoning. People should avoid
using gas stoves or outdoor grills indoors for heating, as these can be deadly. Additionally,
keeping water pipes insulated or allowing faucets to drip slightly can prevent freezing and
potential pipe bursts.
Community-based options play a crucial role in assisting those most affected by power outages.
Local governments, shelters, and community centers can serve as warming stations for those
without heat. Neighbors should check on one another, especially the elderly and individuals with
disabilities, to ensure they have adequate warmth and supplies. Volunteer groups and
emergency response teams can help transport people to safe locations if necessary. In rural
areas, organized networks such as community emergency response teams (CERTs) can
provide vital assistance when professional services are delayed.
To minimize future risks, communities should invest in long-term preparedness strategies, such
as backup generators for critical infrastructure, emergency alert systems, and neighborhood
preparedness programs. Residents can also benefit from knowing their local emergency
contacts, participating in winter survival training, and having a plan in place for severe weather
events. By fostering a strong community response and prioritizing the needs of the most
vulnerable, the impact of power outages in cold climates can be significantly reduced.

 Summer Conditions- Extreme Heat
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members will understand the impacts of
extreme heat and have a plan to adequately cool in their home or community-based cooling
centers.
Vulnerability: Certain groups are especially vulnerable during extreme heat and power
outages, requiring extra attention and resources. Older adults and young children have a
reduced ability to regulate body temperature, making them more susceptible to heat stress.
People with chronic illnesses, pregnant women, outdoor workers, and individuals experiencing
homelessness are also at high risk. Low-income households, which may lack access to air
conditioning or cooling centers, face additional challenges. In addition, it should be noted that
extreme heat can lead to an increase in violence, making those experiencing domestic abuse

 39

more vulnerable. Recognizing these risks allows for targeted interventions that prioritize those
most in need during extreme heat emergencies.
Potential Distributed Actions: During outages coinciding with extreme heat events, covering
windows with drapes or shades and using window reflectors designed to keep heat out can help
keep a home cool. Keeping body temperature down is important to health and safety during a
heat event. This can be done with cold showers or cooling with ice, and wearing loose,
lightweight, and light-colored clothing. Maintaining hydration is imperative and can be aided by
hydration supplements.
Potential Shared Solutions: Cooling centers can be lifesaving during extreme heat events.
Priority entry should be given to vulnerable populations and those already experiencing
symptoms of heat-related illnesses.
Rationale: During a power outage in extreme heat, community organizations and emergency
personnel must take immediate action to protect residents from heat-related illnesses. Public
messaging should emphasize the importance of staying indoors in well-ventilated spaces,
seeking shade when necessary, and recognizing early symptoms of heat exhaustion or heat
stroke to prevent severe health complications.

At the community level, establishing cooling centers in public buildings such as libraries,
schools, and community centers is critical for those without air conditioning. Emergency
communication strategies should ensure that residents receive timely alerts via text messages,
radio, and social media, directing them to available resources. Coordinated volunteer efforts
should focus on conducting wellness checks for at-risk individuals, including seniors, people
with medical conditions, and those living in low-income housing. Additionally, emergency
services should work with transportation providers to assist residents in reaching cooling
centers safely, particularly those who are elderly or have mobility challenges.

Long-term strategies should prioritize enhancing community resilience to extreme heat events
and power disruptions. Investments in microgrid technology, solar-powered backup systems,
and emergency energy storage solutions can help sustain critical services during outages.
Public education campaigns should promote heat safety awareness, ensuring that residents
understand how to prepare for and respond to extreme heat emergencies. Encouraging
neighborhoods to establish emergency response networks and heat action plans will strengthen
overall community preparedness, reducing the strain on emergency services during crises.

 Safety- Lighting
Minimum Resilience Standard: All community members will have a strategy and equipment
for lighting during power outages to prevent accidents and injuries.
Vulnerability: Some community members are particularly vulnerable to accidents or injuries
due to a lack of lighting during power outages, as they may struggle to navigate dark
environments safely. Older individuals face a high risk of falls due to reduced vision and
mobility, while people with disabilities, including those with mobility or visual impairments, may
have difficulty moving safely without proper lighting. Young children are prone to tripping and
falling in the dark, and individuals with medical conditions may struggle to access necessary
equipment or medications. Low-income populations, who may lack reliable emergency lighting,
face increased hazards.

 40

Potential Distributed Actions: Individuals should have battery or solar-powered lights
available in their homes during power outages to keep safe from household hazards. When
leaving the home in the dark, community members should travel with flashlights for safety, as
streetlights may be out.
Potential Shared Solutions: Resilience Hubs should be well-lit for safety and accessibility for
all individuals with consideration of their varying needs. Facilities with power should consider
providing outdoor lighting where street and traffic lights are non-functioning. Hubs can provide
power to recharge lights and/or distribute batteries.
Rationale: Lack of lighting during a power outage presents significant safety risks at both the
individual and community levels. For individuals, navigating dark spaces increases the risk of
falls, trips, and injuries, especially for seniors, children, and people with disabilities. In homes,
common hazards include staircases, sharp furniture edges, and obstacles that may not be
visible without adequate lighting. Additionally, cooking or using alternative heating sources in
the dark can increase the likelihood of burns or accidental fires. Ensuring access to flashlights,
battery-operated lanterns, and glow sticks can help prevent such accidents while reducing
dependence on candles, which pose a fire hazard.

At a community level, widespread darkness can contribute to increased crime and safety
concerns. Non-functioning streetlights and traffic signals create dangerous conditions for
pedestrians and drivers, leading to an increased risk of accidents. Public spaces, including
hospitals, shelters, and emergency response centers, may also face operational challenges if
backup lighting is inadequate. The potential for crime, including theft and vandalism, tends to
rise in poorly lit environments, making neighborhoods more vulnerable during extended
outages. Law enforcement and emergency responders should prioritize maintaining visibility in
key areas through backup power systems and mobile lighting solutions. It should be noted that
the fear of crime, either to the individual or property crime, is a deciding factor for some in
whether or not they would leave their home during an outage or disaster.

To prepare for power outages, individuals should keep a supply of flashlights and extra batteries
in easily accessible locations throughout their homes. Solar-powered or rechargeable lanterns
can provide long-lasting illumination without creating fire risks. Glow-in-the-dark tape or battery-
powered nightlights can help mark pathways and staircases, reducing the risk of falls. It’s also
essential to educate all household members on where emergency lighting is stored and to
conduct occasional drills to ensure preparedness. For those with medical needs, having an
emergency plan that includes backup power sources for medical devices is critical (see
Powered Medical Devices above).

On a broader scale, communities should invest in infrastructure that enhances nighttime
resilience during outages. This includes installing solar-powered streetlights, ensuring that key
buildings have backup generators, and organizing neighborhood watch programs to increase
security. Public information campaigns can help educate residents on best practices for staying
safe in the dark, including safe generator use and reporting downed power lines. By taking
proactive steps at both the individual and community levels, the risks associated with power
outages and lack of lighting can be significantly reduced.

 Communication
Minimum Resilience Standard: Individuals have access to a communication device with
connection (cellular or wifi) or have a battery-powered radio in order to receive emergency
communication alerts during the full length of a power outage.
Vulnerability: Everyone is vulnerable when there is a lack of communication during a power
outage, as life-saving alerts may come from emergency responders. Those who are most
vulnerable include individuals with caretakers they must communicate with, children, and low-
income individuals who may not have access to a radio or portable batteries to charge
communication devices.
Potential Distributed Actions: Community members should have an emergency
communication plan to utilize when they do not have access to a communication device with
signal (cellular or wifi). Individuals can use battery-powered or crank radios to receive official
broadcasts if they do not have access to other communication and information-sharing
methods, such as social media and text messaging. Additionally, portable battery chargers are
important to have on hand to keep communication devices charged.
Potential Shared Solutions: Resilience Hubs can provide individuals with a place to charge
their communication devices and access to information within their community through word of
mouth by connecting with others who may have had more recent communication with
emergency responders, other resource providers in the community, etc.
Rationale: During a grid outage, maintaining effective communication is crucial for ensuring
public safety, coordination, and access to critical resources. If the internet and cellular networks
remain operational, community members can rely on text messages, phone calls, and social
media updates to stay informed. However, even when power is out, mobile networks may still
function temporarily due to backup power systems. To preserve battery life, individuals should
minimize non-essential phone use, reduce screen brightness, and use text messaging rather
than voice calls, as SMS requires less network capacity. Local governments and emergency
organizations should prioritize sending real-time updates via social media, emergency alert
systems, and local radio stations to keep residents informed.

If the internet and cellular networks become unavailable, alternative communication methods
become necessary. Radio remains a vital tool in such scenarios. Residents should have battery-
powered or hand-crank emergency radios to receive official broadcasts from NOAA and local
emergency management agencies.

Individuals should prepare for outages by assembling an emergency communication plan in
advance. This plan should include a list of emergency contacts, a designated family meeting
place, and knowledge of local emergency radio frequencies. Keeping a written copy of important
phone numbers is essential, as digital contacts may become inaccessible if devices lose power.
Residents should also invest in portable power banks, solar chargers, or generator backups to
extend the use of essential communication devices. Practicing communication drills with family
and community members can ensure preparedness for a real emergency.

Governments and emergency management agencies should implement resilient communication
strategies. Deploying mobile cell towers, satellite communication units, and emergency
broadcasting infrastructure can provide critical connectivity in disaster-stricken areas.
Authorities should also establish emergency information hubs where residents can gather for
updates, charge devices, and access resources.

 42

Ultimately, maintaining communication during a grid outage requires a combination of
preparation, alternative technologies, and coordinated community efforts. While digital networks
are ideal when available, residents must be ready to switch to analog and offline methods when
necessary. By proactively equipping individuals with the knowledge and tools needed for
emergency communication, communities can ensure safety, reduce panic, and facilitate
effective disaster response.

 Food Safety
Minimum Resilience Standard: Individuals have access to fresh or nonperishable foods in
their home for at least three days and are able to obtain fresh or nonperishable foods beyond
three days. Community members dependent on food subsidies have clear processes for the
replacement of food impacted by power outages.
Vulnerability: Most vulnerable to food loss during a power outage are community members
who face other financial, health, and access-related challenges. These community groups
include low-income households, older individuals on a fixed income, people with disabilities or
chronic illnesses that require specialized diets, families with young children, unhoused
individuals, and those with traditional food access issues due to food deserts, rural areas with
large distances to stores, and/or mobility-limited individuals. Community members on food
assistance may experience a delay in replacing lost benefits.
Potential Distributed Actions: Households can prepare by having a stock of nonperishable
foods and a manual can opener for when refrigerated and frozen foods spoil. Keeping the
refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible will keep food cold for longer. Coolers
and ice can be used to keep food from spoiling. Additionally, when the temperature is
appropriate, perishables can be stored outside to stay cold. Camping stoves and fire can be
used for alternative cooking methods for warm meals.
Potential Shared Solutions: Distribute nonperishable foods when able. Cooking and serving
perishable foods for the community to help reduce food waste and provide a resource to those
struggling to access food. Additional support may be needed for individuals with food assistance
as they wait for additional funds to replace food lost during a power outage.
Rationale: During a power outage, keeping food safe is a major concern for both individuals
and the community. Without electricity, refrigerators and freezers can only keep food at safe
temperatures for a limited time, increasing the risk of spoilage and foodborne illnesses.
Communities should ensure that residents have access to food safety guidelines and resources,
such as emergency food distribution centers or backup refrigeration options. Individuals should
be prepared with thermometers to monitor food temperatures and have a plan in place for
managing perishable items when power is lost.

To extend the life of refrigerated and frozen food, it is important to keep appliance doors closed
as much as possible. A refrigerator can typically keep food cold for about four hours, while a full
freezer can maintain safe temperatures for up to 48 hours if left unopened. Grouping frozen
items can help retain cold temperatures longer. If available, dry ice or frozen gel packs can be
used to extend cooling time. The communities can also consider setting up emergency cold
storage locations for essential food supplies to assist those without access to adequate
refrigeration.

If food starts to warm up, strict protocols should be followed to ensure safety. Perishable food
should be discarded if it has been above 40°F for more than two hours, as bacteria can grow

 43

rapidly at higher temperatures. Any food that smells off, has changed texture, or shows signs of
spoilage should not be consumed. Even if it appears fine, items like dairy products, meats,
seafood, and cooked leftovers should be thrown out if they have been stored improperly. When
in doubt, it is always safer to discard questionable food rather than risk food poisoning.
Distribution of thermometers for refrigerators has been previously conducted through Minnesota
Power’s Conservation Improvement Program.

In addition to individual precautions, community-wide efforts can help mitigate food waste and
safety risks during extended power outages. Local organizations, grocery stores, and
emergency response teams can work together to distribute non-perishable food and provide
food safety information. Residents should also be encouraged to have an emergency supply of
shelf-stable foods, such as canned goods, dried fruits, and nuts, to rely on when refrigeration is
unavailable. If significant food is lost, the community can work with WLSSD to collect the spoiled
food. Information should also be made readily available to community members participating in
various food support programs as to the methods of replacing lost food.

 Water
Minimum Resilience Standard: Community members have at least one gallon of water per
person per day (CDC), and a clear plan for additional distribution of water has been
established.
Vulnerability: Everyone is vulnerable to dehydration when there is a lack of drinking water.
Those who are most vulnerable include older adults, people with disabilities, children, and low-
income individuals or others who may not have the means to store or obtain clean drinking
water for emergencies. Additionally, it is important to note that a population’s vulnerability can
be dependent on other factors surrounding the outage. For example, everyone is at a higher risk
of dehydration during a high heat event. Individuals who are reliant on well water and those
living in high-rise buildings are at a higher risk of losing water access.
Potential Distributed Actions: Individuals should store one gallon of water per person for at
least three days, per the CDC’s recommendation. Individuals should consider storing more if
they have pets, are pregnant, or have additional health needs. Water purification tablets and
filters can be used when community members have access to water but are unsure of its
safety.
Potential Shared Solutions: Water bottles can be distributed, or water can be collected from
the tap in facilities with power.
Rationale: Access to water can be severely impacted by a grid outage in a city, as most
municipal water systems rely on electricity to pump and distribute water. While some water
treatment plants and pumping stations have backup generators, prolonged outages can disrupt
water pressure, leading to reduced or completely halted water flow. High-rise buildings are
particularly vulnerable because they depend on electric pumps to deliver water to upper floors.
Additionally, sewage and wastewater treatment facilities may struggle to operate without power,
increasing the risk of contamination in the water supply. In some cases, authorities may issue
boil water advisories if treatment systems are compromised, making access to clean drinking
water even more critical.

To prepare for grid outages, each household should store an emergency water supply, with at
least one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days. This includes water
for drinking, cooking, and hygiene. Households can store water in clean, food-grade containers

 44

or purchase commercially bottled water. For longer-term resilience, investing in water filtration
systems, purification tablets, or a gravity-fed water filter can ensure access to safe drinking
water in case of contamination. If an outage is anticipated due to severe weather, filling
bathtubs and large containers with tap water beforehand can provide additional non-drinking
water for flushing toilets and cleaning.

Beyond storage, households should be aware of alternative water sources and conservation
strategies during an outage. Collecting rainwater in barrels or large containers can supplement
non-potable water needs. To minimize usage, residents should avoid unnecessary flushing, use
disposable dishware to reduce washing needs, and limit bathing.

 Property Protection- Flooding
Minimum Resilience Standard: All individuals have flood and/or renters insurance coverage
and rain-readiness assessments and plans if properties are at risk of flooding or sewer backups.
Vulnerability: Homes in wetland and flood plain areas are at the highest risk of flooding.
Homeowners with sump pumps are vulnerable to basement flooding during times of electrical
outage.
Potential Distributed Actions: In the event of flooding, homeowners can prepare by sealing
foundation cracks, elevating valuable items and electrical appliances in basements, and clearing
gutters and downspouts to ensure water is able to be directed away from the house. Sandbags
can also be used as a water-absorbing barrier. Homes reliant on sump pumps could be
upgraded to have battery or water-powered sump pumps. Additionally, purchasing flood
insurance can be imperative to flood recovery.
Potential Shared Solutions: Community mitigation projects such as natural flood management
with green infrastructure, such as rain gardens and harvesting rainwater. A shared library of
backup pumps, power sources, and wet/dry vacuums for individuals to check out can help
mitigate damage and aid flood recovery.
Rationale: Protecting property during an extreme rain event becomes significantly more
challenging when a power outage occurs, as many critical home systems rely on electricity. One
of the biggest concerns is basement and property flooding due to sump pump failure. Sump
pumps are essential in keeping water from accumulating in basements and crawl spaces, but
without power, they stop working, allowing floodwaters to rise unchecked. This can lead to
extensive property damage, mold growth, and structural weakening. Homeowners can mitigate
this risk by installing battery-powered or water-powered backup sump pumps, which can
operate even when the main power supply is down.

Beyond sump pump failure, power outages also impact other flood prevention measures, such
as drainage systems and stormwater management infrastructure. Electrically powered drainage
systems in urban areas may stop functioning, leading to street flooding that can spread to
homes and businesses. Additionally, without power, residents may not receive timely weather
alerts or emergency notifications, reducing their ability to take preventive action. To increase
resiliency, communities should invest in stormwater infrastructure improvements, such as
permeable pavement, improved drainage channels, and emergency flood barriers, while also
ensuring backup power sources for critical flood management systems.

At the individual level, homeowners should take proactive measures to safeguard their property
before an extreme rain event occurs. This includes sealing foundation cracks, elevating valuable

 45

items and electrical appliances in basements, and ensuring gutters and downspouts are clear of
debris to direct water away from the home. Sandbags and water-absorbing barriers can provide
additional protection in flood-prone areas. Having a home emergency kit that includes
flashlights, portable power banks, and a battery-powered weather radio can help individuals
stay informed and prepared during prolonged outages.

On a community scale, collaboration is key to improving flood resilience. Local governments
and emergency management agencies should work with residents to establish flood response
plans and distribute resources such as sandbags and emergency sump pump backups before
major storms. Investing in microgrids and community-based energy resilience programs can
help ensure that essential services, including flood prevention systems, remain operational
during outages. By taking both individual and collective action, communities can significantly
reduce the risk of property damage and enhance their ability to withstand extreme rain events
with minimal impact.

STRUCTURAL CONSIDERATIONS
The structural capacity to accommodate solar PV equipment varies at each location evaluated. The Water
Treatment Plant is the only location where a ground-mount system has been proposed, utilizing a fixed panel
racking solution. The remaining locations include a mix of buildings with either flat or sloped roofs and below
grade water reservoirs with flat or sloped roof lids. The flat locations are modeled with ballasted at-grade racking.
The sloped roof locations assume direct mounting PV panels to the roofing.

Of the flat roof buildings, several have roof membranes secured by ballast rock. For these locations, there are
several possible solutions to accommodate a solar PV system. The most extensive solution involves removing all
ballast rock and replacing the roof membrane with a fully adhered or mechanically fastened membrane prior to
installing the solar PV system. An alternative solution may be to selectively remove the ballast rock in the
footprint of the PV system and mechanically secure the roof membrane.

For this report, the structural evaluation assumes the roof capacity to accommodate the solar PV system is
achieved by removal of the existing roof ballast material and modifying the roof system with mechanical fasteners
or a fully adhered system.

The preliminary review of the various building sites and structures is based on the 2020 Minnesota State Building
Code (MSBC) which adopts the 2018 International Building Code (IBC). The code parameters that determine the

Site Assessment & Evaluation for Solar PV & Storage Page 3 of 25 LHB Project No. 230690

 50

capacity of the existing roof framing or reservoir lid structure include calculating the dead load, roof live load, rain
load, snow load, ice load, and wind load. These loads are then applied to the structure in predefined load
combinations and compared to the strength of the framing members. If the strength of the framing members
exceeds the applied loads, then the PV system may be placed without modification to the existing structure. If the
strength of the framing members is less than the applied load, reinforcement of modification to the existing
framing system would be required to install the PV system.

Modification of the existing structure may include removal of the roof ballast or changing the fastening method of
the roof membrane. Reinforcement of the existing structure may include adding new framing members in between
the existing framing members, placement of poles directly over piers or columns, or complete replacement of
deteriorated structures. Other considerations in placement of the solar PV systems include locating panels away
from high/low roof conditions to avoid snow drift regions, placement of PV systems away from obstructions that
may shade the panels and placing panels a minimum distance from the edge of the building to accommodate
future maintenance and repairs without requiring fall protection. For the modeled locations, PV equipment has
been placed at least three (3) feet from roof edges and three (3) feet from the peak for buildings with roof slopes
greater than 2:12 pitch to allow two access routes to the peak (MN Administrative Rules 3111.3.4.1.1 and
3111.3.4.1.2). PV equipment has been placed at least ten (10) feet from roof edges for buildings with roof slopes
less than 2:12 (IBC 1013.5), however MN Administrative Rule 3111.3.4.2.1 may allow for equipment placement
within six (6) feet of roof edges.

ELECTRICAL CONSIDERATIONS
Two of the ten locations (Bayview and Lincoln Park) evaluated have been modeled to produce solar power
significantly exceeding 120% of the annual electrical load. For these locations, an interconnect study and
agreement will need to be completed with Minnesota Power for acceptance of the excess power by the
distribution system. This will likely include upgrading the existing service to the Bayview site and a new or
significantly upgraded service at the Lincoln Park site.

For the remaining locations producing PV power less than 120% of annual load, net metering is the assumed
basis of operation for PV generation balanced against real time electrical demand. Additional analysis will confirm
sizing and location for electrical equipment to interconnect with the existing power distribution system at each site.
