Treatment of mild cryptotia by anatomical external auricular muscle transposition without flap transfer: A case report

Introduction

Cryptotia is a rare congenital deformity where the upper helix of the ear is obscured beneath the temporal skin, leading to both aesthetic and functional challenges, such as difficulties in wearing glasses or face masks. The exact prevalence of cryptotia remains uncertain, but it is reported to be more common in Asian populations, with a noted incidence of 1 in 400 in Japan.1

The underlying causes of cryptotia are associated with the abnormal course and insertion of auricular muscles, including the superior auricular muscle (SAM), transverse auricular muscle, and oblique auricular muscle. These muscle abnormalities lead to inferior displacement and improper positioning of the auricle.2 While many corrective methods focus on addressing skin deficiencies with flaps or grafts,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 there is a limited emphasis on surgical procedures aimed specifically at repositioning the auricular muscles.

In this case report, we describe a surgical technique that prioritizes the correction of abnormal auricular muscle attachments in a patient with mild cryptotia. This technique was performed without the use of a skin flap or graft, and demonstrates the effectiveness of auricular muscle repositioning.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old female with mild cryptotia presented with concerns about the aesthetic deformity of her ear and difficulties associated with wearing face masks and glasses. The upper portion of the helix was partially concealed under the temporal skin, with minimal deformity of the underlying auricular cartilage (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figure 3) .

Preoperative appearance showing superior auricle buried beneath the temporal skin.

Preoperative design. The dashed lines indicate the planned skin incisions. A small triangular flap in the preauricular region was designed as an auxiliary incision to be used only if a skin defect occurred, although it was not actually required in this case.

Surgical procedure

A skin incision was made above the superior helical rim and abnormal insertions of the superior auricular muscle (SAM) and anterior auricular muscle (AAM) near the upper helix were identified under the microscope (Fig. 2,Supplementary Figure1a, 1b). These muscles were carefully dissected and released from the upper helix auricular cartilage. The SAM and AAM were then accurately repositioned and anchored to their anatomically correct locations behind the conchal bowl and at the base of the helical crus, respectively, using polypropylene 5–0 sutures in a horizontal mattress fashion (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figure2a,2b). This resulted in superior and anterior translation of the ear into its anatomically correct position creating a new auriculo-cephalic sulcus. The skin deficiency was minimal and the surrounding skin had favorable mobility and was advanced to cover the defect without the need for additional flaps or skin grafts. The wounds were closed in two layers: the subcutaneous tissue was sutured with 4–0 absorbable sutures, and the skin was closed with interrupted 6–0 nylon sutures, followed by the application of a light compressive dressing, with no drain inserted.

Post operative recovery was unremarkable with no complications reported. At the 3-year postoperative follow-up, the auriculo-cephalic sulcus remained well-defined with no signs of ear displacement or recurrence. The patient was satisfied with both the aesthetic results and the ability to wear glasses and face masks without difficulty (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figure4) .

Discussion

The auricle possesses three extrinsic auricular muscles and six intrinsic auricular muscles. Thorough understanding of these auricular muscles is essential for comprehending the pathophysiology of congenital ear anomalies including cryptotia. The extrinsic muscles regulate the position of the auricle, while the intrinsic muscles determine the shape of the auricular cartilage. In cryptotia, abnormal insertion of the superior auricular muscle (SAM) and shortening of the oblique and transverse auricular muscles have been suggested by Yotsuyanagi et al.2 In this case report, abnormal attachments of the SAM and anterior auricular muscle (AAM) to the upper helix were observed consistent with this hypothesis and with our experience treating other cryptotia patients.

Regarding the surgical intervention for cryptotia, Paredes et al. reported that four objectives must be achieved: 1) re-creation of the auriculo-cephalic sulcus, 2) correction of cartilage deformities, 3) release of abnormal muscle insertions, and 4) addition of deficient skin3. To date, there have been no reports, aside from the author's previous report, describing a technique that not only releases abnormal muscle insertions but also reattaches them. Most previous studies have focused on how to supplement deficient skin using local flaps and the subsequent formation of the auriculo-cephalic sulcus3–8.

Recently, reports have emerged on preventing the recurrence of cryptotia by inserting acellular dermal matrix grafts or cartilage wedge grafts behind the auricle in addition to using various local flaps.9,10 However, the use of local flaps raises concerns, such as incisions extending into the hair-bearing area or the hairline encroaching too close to the auriculo-cephalic sulcus. The most significant postoperative complication of cryptotia is recurrence, often caused by the downward displacement of the auricle, leading to reburial of the upper helix into the temporal region9. Therefore, in cases of mild cryptotia without significant cartilage deformities, local flap techniques are not mandatory. Instead, reattachment of the SAM and AAM to maintain the auricle in an upward position should be prioritized. As demonstrated in this case, minimal skin incision solely to identify the auricular muscles resulted in no long-term recurrence of cryptotia.

A limitation of this technique is that if significant cartilage deformities are present or atypical muscle attachments are encountered, a larger incision may be required for cartilage correction. Therefore, this technique is best suited for cases with no or minimal cartilage deformities.

In terms of indications, this technique is particularly appropriate for patients with mild cryptotia who exhibit minimal cartilage deformity, in whom the buried helix is mobile and can be manually elevated pre-operatively, and those who experience functional limitations such as difficulty wearing glasses or face masks. These criteria help identify cases where auricular muscle repositioning alone may be sufficient without the need for local flap procedures.

This case reinforces the potential for auricular muscle repositioning alone to serve as a definitive treatment for mild cryptotia in the absence of significant skin deficiency, representing a further simplification and minimization of previously reported techniques involving mini flaps. This minimally invasive approach reduces operating time and may improve patient recovery.

Preoperative confirmation of abnormal muscle attachments to the upper helix through ultrasound or manual examination such as palpation of a cord-like structure suggestive of the auricular muscles may help determine the presence of malpositioned muscles and thereby expand the indications for this technique.

Moreover, as seen in the preoperative design of this case (Fig. 1), it is feasible to extend the incision or add local flaps intraoperatively if needed.

Conclusion

Correction of the abnormal auricular muscle attachments should be considered in cryptotia reconstruction. This case highlights the use of auricular muscle repositioning without the use of local flaps to achieve stable, long-term correction of mild cryptotia. This minimally invasive method reduces surgical complexity and may improve patient recovery.
