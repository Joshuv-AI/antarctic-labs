CLDN18 and FGFR2 splicing variants are emerging tumor-associated antigens for cancer therapy

Masuko Katoh, Masaru Katoh

Abstract

Alternative splicing and alternative promoters generate splicing variants to expand proteomic and transcriptional diversity to orchestrate physiological activities and dictate tumorigenesis. Splicing variants are attractive tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) for the clinical development of cancer therapies. Here, recent progress in claudin-18 isoform 2 (CLDN18.2)- and fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 isoform b (FGFR2b)-targeted therapeutics is summarized, and perspectives in this field are discussed. Zolbetuximab and bemarituzumab are first-generation monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting CLDN18.2 and FGFR2b, respectively, while next-generation biologics targeting these TAAs are emerging. Single-agent objective response rates of CLDN18.2-targeting mAb (omectatug), antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs, arcotatug tavatecan, BL-M05D1, garetatug rezetecan, JS107, sonesitatug vedotin and tecotabart vedotin), bispecific antibodies (emaretamig and givastomig) and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells (IMC002) were higher than that of zolbetuximab. Gastrointestinal toxicity, nausea and vomiting are common on-target/off-tumor adverse effects of CLDN18.2-targeted biologics, which are mitigated for arcotatug tavatecan and givastomig as a result of antibody engineering. Combination therapy of zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy is approved for the treatment of gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer patients, while results of other CLDN18.2-targeting biologics in phase 3 clinical trials are anticipated with expectations for improved clinical activities and mitigated adverse events. Diagnostics detecting alternative isoforms and combination strategies using immune checkpoint inhibitors would address resistance mechanisms, such as epitope-losing isoform switching and immune evasion. Comparative studies on ADC monotherapy versus combination mAb-chemotherapy as well as the clinical development of bispecific ADCs and allogeneic CAR-T cells should further improve the clinical benefits of CLDN18.2- and FGFR2b-targeted therapies.

Introduction

Many transcript isoforms that contribute to proteomic and transcriptional diversity are generated via alternative splicing of context-dependent exons and the use of alternative promoters of distinct first exons, respectively, and regulate a variety of binary cell fates and processes: stemness versus differentiation; epithelial versus mesenchymal; proliferation versus senescence; survival versus apoptosis; and immune surveillance versus immune evasion (; ). Altered activity or expression of splicing regulators, such as ESRP1, ESRP2, SF3B1, SRSF2 and U2AF1, can globally dysregulate alternative splicing, while splice-site mutations in proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes can locally dysregulate alternative splicing during multistage tumorigenesis. Isoforms can be targeted via small-molecules and splice-switching oligos preclinically (; ), while diverse biologics are in various stages of clinical development, with one potential benefit being reduced off-target effects by not targeting all isoforms from a gene (; ). The most developed therapies target claudin-18 isoform 2 (CLDN18.2) and fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 isoform b (FGFR2b), which are representative tumor-associated splicing variants (meaning isoforms or encoded protein products hereafter), as cancer therapy ().

Gastric cancer is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, with approximately 970,000 new cases and 660,000 deaths in 2022 (), and its risk factors include chronic gastritis associated with Helicobacter pylori infection (), unfavorable lifestyles (consumption of alcohol and preserved foods) (), germline mutations in the CDH1 and BRCA2 genes () and single nucleotide polymorphisms in the MUC1 and PSCA loci ().

Gastric adenocarcinomas occur in the proximal cardia, gastric body and distal antrum, while gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) adenocarcinomas occur when the epicenter is no more than 2 cm proximal to the GEJ and extends into the esophagus. On the basis of similar genomic landscapes of gastric cancer and GEJ cancers [chromosomal instability subtype (51% versus 72%), genomically stable subtype (38% versus 20%), hypermutated subtype (6% versus 4%) and Epstein–Barr virus-positive subtype (5% versus 5%)], gastric and GEJ cancer are merged into the concept of G/GEJ cancer in clinical practice (; ).

Because CLDN18.2 and FGFR2b are overexpressed in subsets of G/GEJ cancers, therapeutics targeting CLDN18.2 or FGFR2b have been developed as investigational drugs. Zolbetuximab (IMAB362), an anti-CLDN18.2 mouse/human chimeric monoclonal antibody (mAb), in combination with conventional chemotherapies has been approved for the treatment of G/GEJ cancer patients by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), while other antibody-based drugs and/or cell-based therapeutics targeting CLDN18.2 (; ) and FGFR2b (; ) are in later-stage clinical trials. Here, recent progress in CLDN18.2- and FGFR2b-targeted therapies for G/GEJ cancer patients is overviewed, and future perspectives on splice variant-targeted therapies are discussed.

CLDN18.2-targeted therapies

The 24 human claudin (CLDN) genes encode 26 CLDN proteins at tight junctions that consist of four transmembrane domains, the N-terminal cytoplasmic region, two extracellular loops (ECL1 and ECL2), one cytoplasmic loop and the C-terminal cytoplasmic region (CTR) (). ECLs and the CTR are involved in the homophilic/heterophilic interactions of CLDNs themselves and zonula occludens (ZO)-mediated tethering to actin filaments, respectively (; ). On the basis of these extracellular and cytoplasmic protein–protein interactions, CLDN proteins perform versatile physiological functions: (i) separation of apical and basolateral surfaces to establish apical–basal polarity in epithelial cells; (ii) regulation of paracellular permeability (barrier vs. channel) to maintain homeostasis in various organs; and (iii) indirect orchestration of Hippo–YAP/TAZ, PI3K-AKT and WNT/β-catenin signaling cascades via cell–cell adhesion networks to modulate cell fates and epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity (; ; ).

CLDN family members function as tumor promoters and/or tumor suppressors depending on tumor type, subtype, stage and experimental conditions in preclinical studies using cell lines and engineered mouse models (). Apart from debates about their functional consequences in preclinical model experiments, CLDN1, CLDN4, CLDN6 and CLDN18.2 are emerging as attractive tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) for the clinical development of CLDN-targeted therapies (). For example, CLDN18.2 protein is overexpressed as a result of redistribution from the tight junction to non-junctional cell-surface membrane owing to dysregulated polarity in G/GEJ tumor cells ().

CLDN18.2 (stomach isoform) is almost identical to CLDN18.1 (lung isoform) except for the N-terminal cytoplasmic region, the first transmembrane domain and ECL1 derived from isoform-specific first exons (; ). CLDN18.2 positivity in an experimental immunohistochemical (IHC) study, defined as 2+ (moderate) to 3+ (strong) staining in ≥60% of tumor cells (TCs) using rabbit antiserum against an epitope derived from CLDN18.2 ECL1, was detected in 56% (37/66) of gastric cancer cases, 60% (6/10) of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cases, 50% (11/22) of esophageal adenocarcinoma cases and 10% (4/42) of ovarian adenocarcinoma cases (). Cut-off values for CLDN18.2 IHC in clinical settings are confusing, because different diagnostic antibodies and cut-offs are used in clinical trials for CLDN18.2 targeted therapeutics. For example, CLDN18.2 positivity in clinical IHC studies, defined as 2+/3+ membranous staining in ≥75% of TC using mouse 43-14A mAb against common CTR of CLDN18.2 and CLDN18.1 (≥2+ staining in ≥75% of TCs using 43-14A), was detected in 38.4% (1730/4507) ∼ 49.3% (256/519) of G/GEJ cancer patients (; ; ; ), although CLDN18.2 positivity with 43-14A diagnostic antibody in patients with lung, ovarian and pancreatic cancers remains to be further validated owing to its cross-reactivity to CLDN18.1.

The CLDN18.2-specific region in ECL1 has been applied as an epitope for the development of CLDN18.2-targeted therapies (; ), which was expected to prevent cross-reaction to CLDN18.1 and reduce pulmonary adverse effects. Recent progress on CLDN18.2-directed therapeutic modalities (), including mAbs, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) and cell-based therapies, such as chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells and T cells expressing a T-cell antigen coupler (TAC-T), will be reviewed in this section.

Anti-CLDN18.2 mAbs

Representative anti-CLDN18.2 mAbs in clinical trials () include FG-M108 (), omectatug (ASKB589) (), osemitamab (TST001) () and zolbetuximab (). These therapeutic antibodies in the immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) format induce fragment crystallizable (Fc) region-mediated antitumor effects, such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis (ADCC and ADCP) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) (; ). Zolbetuximab is a first-generation chimeric mAb and consists of variable antigen-binding domains derived from mouse IgG1 and constant domains derived from human IgG1, whereas FG-M108, omectatug and osemitamab are second-generation human/humanized mAbs engineered for higher binding affinities and enhanced effector functions. In preclinical experiments, compared with zolbetuximab, FG-M108, with an afucosylated Fc region, achieved increased ADCC (), and compared with the zolbetuximab analog, osemitamab enhanced ADCC, ADCP and CDC activities (). Apart from having varying degrees of antitumor effects, anti-CLDN18.2 mAbs elicit common on-target/off-tumor adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal toxicity, nausea and vomiting.

Zolbetuximab monotherapy resulted in an objective response rate (ORR) of 9% (4/43) and manageable safety profiles for CLDN18.2-positive (defined as ≥2+ staining in ≥50% of TCs using 43-14A) G/GEJ cancer patients in a phase 2 MONO study (NCT01197885) (). A combination strategy with conventional chemotherapies was then applied for zolbetuximab because of its limited single-agent ORR. Zolbetuximab plus standard chemotherapies revealed clinical benefits for first-line treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients (defined as ≥2+ staining in ≥75% of TCs using 43-14A) compared with placebo plus chemotherapies, such as the median overall survival (mOS) of 18.2/14.4 versus 15.5/12.2 months and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 10.6/8.2 versus 8.7/6.8 months in registry phase three SPOTLIGHT study (NCT03504397) () and GLOW study (NCT03653507) (), respectively [modified folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin chemotherapy (mFOLFOX6) in the SPOTLIGHT study and capecitabine and oxaliplatin chemotherapy (CAPOX) in the GLOW study. Detailed statistics are presented in ]. In addition, the use of zolbetuximab plus a chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) for first-line treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients resulted in a combination ORR of 68% (32/47) and an mPFS of 14.8 months in a phase 2 ILUSTRO study (NCT03505320) (), which led to an ongoing phase three LUCERNA study (NCT06901531) to validate the clinical benefits of zolbetuximab-based chemoimmunotherapy. At present, zolbetuximab in combination with fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-containing chemotherapy is approved for the first-line treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients by the US FDA.

In contrast, omectatug achieved a single-agent ORR of 22% (2/9) in a phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT04632108) (). Omectatug plus CAPOX and ICI achieved a combination ORR of 76% (35/46) and mPFS of 12.5 months in another phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT05632939) (), while FG-M108 plus CAPOX achieved a combination ORR of 78% (28/36) in a phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT06177041) (). Osemitamab plus CAPOX and ICI for first-line treatment of CLDN18.2-positive (defined as ≥2+ staining in ≥40% of TCs using 14G11 raised against CLDN18.2 ECL1) G/GEJ cancer patients revealed a combination ORR of 68%, mOS of 21.7 months and mPFS of 16.6 months in a phase 1/2 TranStar102 study (NCT04495296) (; ). On the basis of these promising results, FG-M108, omectatug and osemitamab were subsequently used in phase three randomized controlled trials (NCT06177041, NCT06206733 and NCT06093425, respectively) to confirm clinical relevance of these ADCs versus placebo in combination with chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy for the first-line treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients ().

Differences in the cut-off value of CLDN18.2 positivity in osemitamab trials (40%) versus zolbetuximab trials (75%), reflecting the specificities of diagnostic antibodies (14G11 versus 43-14A) or formats of therapeutic antibodies (engineered humanized IgG1 versus mouse-human chimeric IgG1), would contribute to the expansion of eligible patients for CNDN18.2-targeted therapies. Comparative studies of the effects of second-generation anti-CLDN18.2 mAbs and zolbetuximab on mOS and mPFS are anticipated for the optimal management of G/GEJ cancer patients in the future.

CLDN18.2-targeting ADCs

ADCs are mAb derivatives that are linked to cytotoxic payloads, such as monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) and topoisomerase I inhibitor (TOP1i), using cleavable or non-cleavable chemical linkers. ADCs targeting transmembrane or membrane-anchored proteins exert antitumor activity on the basis of (i) direct effects on TAA-positive cancer cells via binding, internalization and cytoplasmic release of toxic payloads (TAA-targeted drug delivery), (ii) indirect effects on TAA-negative cancer cells via payload release from TAA-positive cancer cells (bystander effects), and (iii) effects on TAA-positive cancer cells via ADCC, ADCP and CDC (Fc-mediated effector functions) (; ), although Fc-mediated immune responses are enhanced or silenced by Fc engineering to address the trade-off between antitumor effects and adverse effects (). ADCs targeting BCMA, CD19, CD22 and CD30 have been approved for the treatment of patients with hematological malignancies, while ADCs targeting HER2, MET, NECTIN4 and TROP2 have been approved for the treatment of patients with solid tumors (; ).

Representative CLDN18.2-targeting ADCs in clinical trials () include arcotatug tavatecan (IBI343 or TAK-921) (), ASP546C (XNW27011) (), ATG-022 (), BL-M05D1 (), ciletatug vedotin (RC118) (), garetatug rezetecan (SHR-A1904) (), JS107 (), sonesitatug vedotin (AZD0901 or CMG901) () and tecotabart vedotin (LM-302) (). Payloads of ATG-022, ciletatug vedotin, JS107, sonesitatug vedotin and tecotabart vedotin are MMAEs, and those of others are TOP1i (). In contrast, the drug–antibody ratios (DARs) of arcotatug tavatecan, garetatug rezetecan, sonesitatug vedotin and tecotabart vedotin are 4, and that of ASP546C is 8 (). Most of these ADCs are designed to enhance antitumor activity via payload delivery, bystander effects and Fc-mediated effector functions, whereas arcotatug tavatecan with Fc silencing is designed to attenuate ADCC and CDC to mitigate on-target/off-tumor gastrointestinal toxicity ().

Early-phase clinical trials revealed manageable safety profiles and promising antitumor effects of representative CLDN18.2-targeting ADCs (). Single-agent ORRs were 35% (17/48) for arcotatug tavatecan (), 39% (29/75) for ASP546C (), 35% (28/79) for BL-M05D1 (), 47% (8/17) for ciletatug vedotin (), 23% (17/74) for garetatug rezetecan (), 35% (8/23) for JS107 (), 33% (31/93) for sonesitatug vedotin () and 33% (17/52) for tecotabart vedotin () in G/GEJ cancer patients with trial-specific definition of CLDN18.2 positivity: for example, arcotatug tavatecan, ≥2+ staining in ≥75% of TCs using 43-14A; garetatug rezetecan, ≥2+ staining in ≥50% of TCs using CLDN18.2-specific EPR19202; and sonesitatug vedotin, ≥2+ staining in ≥20% of TCs using MEDx. In contrast, the mOS and mPFS for sonesitatug vedotin were 11.8 and 4.8 months, respectively (), and those for tecotabart vedotin were 10.9 and 4.9 months, respectively (). In addition to promising antitumor effects, manageable safety profiles were also revealed: common adverse events of anti-CLDN18.2 ADCs included hematological and gastrointestinal toxicity.

Phase three randomized controlled clinical trials of arcotatug tavatecan (NCT06238843, G-HOPE-001 study), BL-M05D1 (NCT07518147), garetatug rezetecan (NCT06649292), JS107 (NCT07284134), sonesitatug vedotin (NCT06346392, CLARITY-Gastric 01 study) and tecotabart vedotin (NCT06351020) are ongoing to compare the efficacy and safety of ADC monotherapy versus the investigator’s choice of standard therapy (docetaxel, irinotecan, paclitaxel or others) for the second-line or later treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients ().

Engineered human/humanized anti-CLDN18.2 ADCs that have substantially evolved from a mouse-human chimeric anti-CLDN18.2 mA b might be appropriate modalities for CLDN18.2-targeted therapy. Future comparative studies on combination chemotherapy with zolbetuximab versus monotherapies of anti-CLDN18.2 ADCs, especially arcotatug tavatecan monotherapy, in the first-line setting of real-world clinical practice are anticipated from viewpoints of cost-effectiveness and quality of life.

CLDN18.2-targeting bsAbs

bsAbs are mAb derivatives that target dual antigens/epitopes in diverse formats, such as multivalent antigen/epitope-binding domains (1 + 1, 1 + 2 or 2 + 2 for dual targets) and Fc-engineering (enhanced, wild type or silenced). bsAbs are functionally classified into (i) CD3-dependent T-cell engagers bridging TAAs on cancer cells and CD3 antigens on T cells (TAA x CD3); (ii) 4-1BB-dependent T-cell engagers (TAA x 4-1BB); (iii) dual TAA binders (for example, TAA x PD-L1, TAA x CD47 and EGFR x MET); and (iv) dual ICIs (for example, PD-1 x CTLA4 and PD-L1 x CTLA4) (; ; ).

Representative CLDN18.2-targeting bsAbs in clinical trials () include AZD5863 (), emaretamig (ASP2138) (), givastomig (ABL111 or TJ033721) (; ), IBI389 (), PM1032 (), Q-1802 (), QLS31905 (), SHR-3821 () and spevatamig (PT886) (; ). AZD5863, emaretamig, IBI389 and QLS31905 are CD3-dependent T-cell engagers (CLDN18.2 x CD3) that activate T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling in tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs) and redirect cytotoxic T cells toward CLDN18.2-positive cancer cells, while givastomig, PM1032 and SHR-3821 are 4-1BB-dependent T-cell engagers (CLDN18.2 x 4-1BB) that activate costimulatory 4-1BB signaling in TILs of CLDN18.2-positive tumors and induce antitumor cytotoxicity. In contrast, Q-1802 and spevatamig are dual TAA binders: Q-1802 (CLDN18.2 x PD-L1) blocks coinhibitory PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in TILs and enhances antitumor immunity, and spevatamig (CLDN18.2 x CD47) inhibits “don’t eat me” signaling in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and promotes the phagocytosis of CLDN18.2-positive cancer cells ().

Bispecific T-cell engagers induce functional activation of TILs, including the release of perforin and granzyme for direct killing of cancer cells and the production of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) for subsequent activation of myeloid and endothelial cells. Because systemic elevation of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and other inflammatory cytokines derived from activated immune cells leads to cytokine release syndrome (CRS) (), Fc-mediated effector functions are removed from bispecific T-cell engagers, such as AZD5863 with Fc mutations (L234A, L235A and G237A) and givastomig with Fc mutation (N300A), to mitigate immune-related adverse effects (; ).

Givastomig with Fc silencing achieved a single-agent ORR of 16% (7/43) but produced no symptoms of systemic immune response in CLDN18.2-positive (defined as ≥1+ staining in ≥1% of TCs using CLDN18.2-specific SP455) G/GEJ cancer patients in a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04900818) (). In contrast, emaretamig and IBI389 revealed single-agent ORRs of 14% (5/36) and 22% (6/27) but also CRS in 43% and 60% of patients in phase 1 clinical trials NCT05365581 () and NCT05164458 (), respectively. Because CRS can be dose-limiting and life-threatening, the clinical benefits of Fc-silenced T-cell engagers, such as AZD5863 and givastomig, are promising. Therefore, further evaluation of the antitumor effects and safety profiles of Fc-silenced T-cell engagers in advanced-stage clinical trials is anticipated.

Anti-CLDN18.2 bsAbs, except emaretamig, remain in relatively early phases of clinical trials () than anti-CLDN18.2 mAbs () and anti-CLDN18.2 ADCs (). Considering the striking ORRs of CLDN18.2-targeting ADCs (), the clinical development of CLDN18.2-targeting bsAbs might have a difficult road ahead from the perspective of business decisions.

CLDN18.2-targeting CAR-T and TAC-T cells

Conventional CAR-T cells are engineered autologous T cells that typically express CAR constructs with mAb-derived single-chain variable fragment (scFv) domains, CD8α/CD28-derived hinge and transmembrane domains, and 4-BB/CD28-derived costimulatory domains and CD3ζ-derived signaling domains (). As a result of scFv-mediated TAA-binding, CAR-T cells undergo clustering of CAR constructs and subsequent activation of downstream signaling, which leads to (i) perforin/granzyme-mediated direct killing of cancer cells, (ii) IFN-γ/TNF-α-mediated activation of innate immune cells for the production of inflammatory cytokines, and (iii) proliferation and persistence of CAR-T cells themselves in the tumor microenvironment (TME) (). CAR-T cells targeting CD19 and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA or TNFRSF17) are approved for the treatment of B-cell lymphoma/leukemia patients and multiple myeloma patients, respectively (), while investigational CAR-T cells targeting CLDN18.2, DLL3, EGFR and glypican 3 (GPC3) are in clinical trials for the treatment of solid tumor patients (; ). In addition, armed CAR-T cells (), TAC-T cells () and allogeneic CAR-γ/δT cells () have been designed as next-generation engineered T cells to further improve clinical benefits of CAR-T therapy.

Representative CLDN18.2-targeting CAR-T and TAC-T cells in clinical trials () include AZD6422 (), IMC002 (), KD-496 (), LB 1908 (), satricabtagene autoleucel (satri-cel or CT041) () and TAC01 (). LB1908 and satri-cel are conventional CAR-T cells. AZD6422, armed with dominant-negative transforming growth factor β receptor II (dnTGFBR2); IMC002, engineered with the variable domain of the heavy chain of heavy chain antibody (VHH); and KD-496, dually targeting CLDN18.2 and NKG2D ligands (NKG2DL), are next-generation CAR-T cells (). In contrast, compared with conventional CAR-T cells, TAC01 cells are engineered with a construct consisting of anti-CLDN18.2 scFv, anti-CD3 ScFv and CD4-derived transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains. These TAC-T cells demonstrate enhanced antitumor efficacy and increased tumor-infiltration potential ().

Conventional CAR-T cells, namely, LB1908 and satri-cel, achieved single-agent ORRs of 30% (3/10) and 55% (28/51) in CLDN18.2-positive (defined as ≥1+ staining in ≥50% of TCs and ≥2+ staining in ≥40% of TCs) G/GEJ cancer patients in NCT05539430 and NCT03874897 phase 1 clinical trials, respectively (; ). In a randomized phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT04581473) (), satri-cel versus investigator’s choice of therapy for the treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients resulted in an mPFS of 3.25 months versus 1.77 months [hazard ratio (HR) 0.37, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.24–0.56, one-sided log-rank p < 0.0001]. Common adverse events of LB1908 and satri-cel were hematological toxicities owing to preconditioning combination chemotherapy and on-tumor/off-tumor adverse effects, including gastrointestinal toxicity and CRS, but not immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Hematological toxicity was inevitable because of preconditioning combination chemotherapy, whereas CRS was manageable using anti-IL-6R mAb and systemic corticosteroids. These results of early-phase clinical trials demonstrated antitumor effects and manageable safety profiles of conventional CAR-T cells, especially satri-cel, for late-line treatment of G/GEJ cancer patients.

In contrast, next-generation CAR-T cells demonstrated strong single-agent activity. Specifically, IMC002 and KD-496 achieved single-agent ORRs of 67% (10/15) and 75% (3/4) in CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients and NKG2DL/CLDN18.2-double-positive gastric cancer patients in phase 1 clinical trials NCT05946226 and NCT06134960, respectively (; ), while the adverse effects of IMC002 and KD-496 were similar to those of LB1908 and satri-cel. Because a complete response (CR) was achieved in 11% (2/18) of patients treated with IMC002, a phase three randomized controlled study of IMC002 versus investigator’s choice of therapy for third-line or later treatment of CLDN18.2-positive G/GEJ cancer patients (NCT07103668) is ongoing.

CLDN18.2-targeting CAR-T-cell therapies, such as IMC002 and satri-cel, are opening up a new therapeutic avenue for solid tumor patients; however, resistance mechanisms to CAR-T cells (immunosuppressive TME, heterogenous TAA expression and antigen loss) () and high costs of autologous CAR-T cells () remain as crucial issues in this field. Therefore, clinical development of allogeneic armed CAR-T cells is anticipated to further improve the clinical benefits of CLDN18.2-targeted cell therapies in the future ().

FGFR2b-targeted therapies

FGFR2b and FGFR2c consist of extracellular immunoglobulin-like (Ig-like) domains, single transmembrane domain and a cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase domain, which are generated from the FGFR2 gene using mutually exclusive exons 8 versus 9 corresponding to the latter half of the third Ig-like domain (). Epithelial-specific RNA-binding proteins (ESRP1 and ESRP2) induce exon 8 splicing into the FGFR2b isoform, and the repression of ESRPs in mesenchymal cells leads to exon 9 splicing into the FGFR2c isoform (). FGFR2b receptors on epithelial cells transduce signals of FGF7, FGF10 and FGF22, whereas FGFR2c receptors on mesenchymal cells transduce signals of FGF2 (basic FGF or bFGF), FGF4, FGF6 and FGF9 (). Both epithelial FGFR2b and mesenchymal FGFR2c are expressed in gastric cancer () owing to epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity that is involved in malignant phenotypes, especially dormancy, drug resistance and recurrence (; ).

The FGFR2 gene at human chromosome 10q26.13 is an oncogene that is overexpressed and activated in 16.6% (1310/7910) of G/GEJ cancers () owing to gene amplifications (chromosomal and extrachromosomal) and C-terminal fusions/rearrangements (; ). Clinical activities of FGFR inhibitors targeting both FGFR2b and FGFR2c isoforms, such as pan-FGFR inhibitors (erdafitinib and futibatinib), an FGFR1/2/3 inhibitor (pemigatinib) and a selective FGFR2 inhibitor (lirafugratinib), are relatively limited for gastric cancer patients with FGFR2 amplifications in comparison with cholangiocarcinoma patients with FGFR2 fusions/rearrangements (; ). In contrast, an anti-FGFR2b mAb (bemarituzumab also known as FPA144) has been shown to have clinical activity and manageable safety profiles in early-stage clinical trials, and has been evaluated in phase three clinical trials for the treatment of G/GEJ cancer patients with FGFR2b overexpression (). Recent progress on FGFR2b-directed mAbs, ADCs and bsAbs () will be reviewed in this section.

Bemarituzumab

Bemarituzumab is a humanized anti-FGFR2b mAb with an afucosylated IgG1 format. In preclinical experiments, bemarituzumab exhibited antitumor effects on FGFR2b-positive cancer cells via (i) inhibition of FGF7-dependent FGFR2b signaling and proliferation and (ii) enhancement of peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-mediated ADCC but also elicited dose-dependent reversible corneal atrophy in rats and cynomolgus monkeys ().

A phase 1 clinical trial of bemarituzumab monotherapy (FPA144-001 study, NCT02318329) revealed an ORR of 16% (5/32) in FGFR2b-positive (defined as ≥2+ staining in ≥10% of TCs using FGFR2b-specific FPR2-D) G/GEJ cancer patients and adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal, hematological and ophthalmological toxicity (). The single-agent ORR of bemarituzumab, comparable to that of the ICI pembrolizumab (15.5%, 23/148) (), was promising.

A phase 2 randomized controlled trial of bemarituzumab versus placebo in combination with mFOLFOX6 chemotherapy (FIGHT study, NCT03694522) revealed favorable clinical activities: combination ORRs, 48% versus 33%; mOS, 19.2 versus 13.5 months (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.52–1.14); and mPFS, 9.5 versus 7.4 months (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.49–1.08). In contrast, adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation occurred in 41% (31/76) of the bemarituzumab group versus 5% (4/77) of the placebo group, among which ophthalmological toxicities, such as dry eye, keratitis and corneal epithelial defect, occurred in 32% of the bemarituzumab group but not in the placebo group ().

Phase three randomized controlled trials of bemarituzumab versus placebo in combination with chemotherapy (FORTITUDE-101 study, NCT05052801) or chemoimmunotherapy (FORTITUDE-102 study, NCT05111626) for the first-line treatment of FGFR2b-positive (defined as ≥2+ staining in ≥10% of TCs) G/GEJ cancer patients began in 2022. Interim analysis on the FORTITUDE-101 study demonstrated significant survival benefits [mOS, 17.9 versus 12.5 months (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.86, P = 0.005); and mPFS, 8.6 versus 6.7 months (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.95, P = 0.019)] and also higher incidence of grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse events [60.0% (165/275) versus 18.4% (49/267), mainly owing to ocular toxicities]; however, descriptive analysis after longer follow-up revealed an attenuated clinical benefit [mOS, 14.5 versus 13.2 months (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.62–1.08)] (). The FORTITUDE-101 study was completed in April 2026, whereas the FORTITUDE-102 study was terminated owing to business decision of AMGEN (). Clinical activities and safety profiles of bemarituzumab-based combination therapy in the FORTITUDE-101 study with subset analyses on biomarkers and ethnicity are anticipated but not yet available as of 26 August 2026.

Next-generation FGFR2b-targeted biologics

On the basis of their promising clinical activities and concerns over the ophthalmological toxicities of bemarituzumab as well as a paradigm shift in biologics from mAb to ADC and bsAb platforms, investigational anti-FGFR2b mAb derivatives have been designed for clinical development (): 3H-10000 () is an anti-FGFR2b human ADC with MMAE payloads; ALK201 (), ALX007-ADC (), BG-C137 (), HDM 2020 () and SCR-A002 () are anti-FGFR2b ADCs with TOP1i payloads; and several unnamed bsAbs are dual TAA binders (FGFR2b x CD47, FGFR2b x EGFR and FGFR2b x HER2) (; ).

In preclinical studies, compared with bemarituzumab, 3H-10000 exerted antitumor effects through FGFR2b-targeted MMAE delivery, bystander effects and Fc-mediated cytotoxic effects and mitigated corneal toxicity (); ALK201 exhibited antitumor effects through direct payload delivery, bystander effects and ADCC, even in bemarituzumab-resistant patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, but reversed corneal atrophy in cynomolgus monkeys in a dose-dependent manner (); ALX007-naked mAb and ALX007-ADC resulted in milder corneal dystrophy than did bemarituzumab owing to the epitope design for intact FGF7/10-FGFR2b signaling (); BG-C137 ADC spared corneal dystrophy in cynomolgus monkeys owing to weaker FGFR2b signaling inhibition (); and FGFR2b x EGFR bsAbs functioned as partial FGFR2b blocker that weakly inhibited FGF7 signaling but spared FGF10 signaling (). Notably, next-generation FGFR2b-targeting biologics have been designed to mitigate on-tumor/off-tumor adverse effects of bemarituzumab, especially ophthalmological toxicity.

3H-10000 and ALK201 proceeded to phase 1/2 clinical trials NCT07354711 and NCT06656390, respectively, while BG-C137 and HDM2020 entered phase 1 clinical trials NCT06625593 and NCT07098052, respectively. Because ALK201 achieved a single-agent ORR of 24% (6/25) and had manageable safety profiles, including nausea (45%), anemia (21%), vomiting (21%) and grade 1 ocular adverse effects (11%) in the NCT06656390 clinical trial (), clinical activities and safety profiles of other anti-FGFR2b ADCs are also anticipated. In contrast, current trend toward bispecific ADCs () warrants development of investigational FGFR2b x CLDN18.2, FGFR2b x EGFR, FGFR2b x HER2 and FGFR2b x MET bispecific ADCs ().

Common mechanisms of resistance to biologics

Limited anti-tumor cytotoxicity in the immunosuppressive TME harboring exhausted CD8+ T cells, angiogenic endothelial cells, fibrotic cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and other suppressor cells (; ; ) as well as TAA/epitope loss in cancer cells owing to intra/intertumor heterogeneity and isoform switching () are common mechanisms of resistance to TAA-targeting biologics (; ; ; ), such as mAbs, ADCs, bsAbs and CAR-T cells (). It is noteworthy that multiple mechanisms, such as (1) immune exhaustion impairing cytotoxic T-cell functions, (2) fibrosis preventing the access of immune cells and biologics and (3) biologics-degrading acidic microenvironment, elicit resistance to biologics in the immunosuppressive TME.

Combination strategies of TAA-targeted biologics plus TME remodeling agents, such as ICI, anti-angiogenic agents and CAF-targeted drugs, are anticipated to improve clinical activities of biologics in the immunosuppressive TME, because TME remodelers convert immunosuppressive TME into immunocompetent TME in preclinical studies (; ; ). Among these combination strategies, clinical trials of ICI-based combination therapies with CLDN18.2-targeted mAbs (), ADCs (), bsAbs () and CAR–T cells () are ongoing.

In contrast, CLDN18.2 and FGFR2b splicing variants can be switched into other CLDNs and FGFR2c owing to therapy-induced transdifferentiation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition, respectively, in cancer patients (). Clinical development of companion diagnostics detecting TAA itself and TAA isoforms in the primary and metastatic lesions using IHC or nucleic acid-based technologies (; ; ) are warranted to monitor TAA/epitope loss and predict resistance to biologics in the clinical settings ().

Conclusion

Splice variant-targeted biologics have opened up new avenues for cancer therapy because some splice variants are bona fide TAAs, regardless of their functions as cancer drivers or passengers. Zolbetuximab and bemarituzumab are pioneering mAbs targeting CLDN18.2 and FGFR2b splicing variants, respectively, while next-generation biologics targeting these TAAs in the form of ADCs, bsAbs and CAR-T cells are emerging with expectations for improved clinical activities and mitigated adverse events. Because immune evasion and epitope-losing isoform switching are common mechanisms of resistance to biologics, the clinical development of ICI-based combination strategies and diagnostics methods for detecting alternative isoforms are also anticipated. In contrast, cost-effectiveness and quality of life are critical issues for patients and supporting family members as well as governments and health insurance systems. Comparative studies on ADC monotherapy versus mAb/chemo-combination therapy and the clinical development of bispecific ADCs and allogeneic CAR-T cells should further improve the clinical benefits of CLDN18.2- or FGFR2b-targeted therapies.
