Generalization Hacking: Models Can Game Reinforcement Learning by Preventing Behavioral Generalization
Frank Xiao, Mary Phuong

Abstract
Model post-training, and in particular reinforcement learning (RL), is one of the primary mechanisms by which developers can shape models’ values and behaviors. However, as models become increasingly evaluation and training aware, they may be motivated to resist training when the perceived objective conflicts with their current values, undermining developers’ ability to detect misalignment and correct model behavior through further training. In this paper, we demonstrate generalization hacking , in which a model collects reward during RL while preventing the rewarded behavior from generalizing. We construct a model organism on Qwen3-235B-A22B, finetuning on synthetic documents describing training awareness and self-inoculation, a novel mechanism in which the model frames compliance as context-specific in its chain of thought, without demonstrating or instructing either behavior. The model organism achieves train-time harmfulness comparable to controls while maintaining a persistent ∼ 15 {\sim}15 percentage point compliance gap across 700 steps of RL. Additionally, a control organism trained only on training awareness documents independently discovers inoculation-like reasoning under RL pressure, developing its own compliance gap despite never being exposed to the concept. Because the generalization-hacking organism receives high reward throughout, standard training metrics provide no signal that generalization has failed. Our results constitute the first demonstration that a model can actively resist RL behavioral modification while maintaining high reward, suggesting that as models become more capable and training-aware, they may be able to undermine the training process itself.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning and its variants
(
21
;
5
;
22
;
24
)
are now standard components of posttraining pipelines for large language models. A core assumption underlying these pipelines is that behavior rewarded during training will generalize to deployment: high reward and strong evaluation metrics are taken as sufficient evidence that the target behavior has been internalized.

However, as model capabilities increase we are seeing evidence that models are increasingly evaluation aware
(
2
)
. As training and deployment contexts become more discernible, a model whose values conflict with a training objective has both the information and the incentive to interfere with the training process itself.
10
showed that models can strategically comply with training objectives in order to avoid being modified. This raises the question of whether such behavior can survive actual training pressure. So far, the answer has been no. Alignment faking compliance gaps are trained away under RL
(
9
;
13
)
, and sandbagging
(
28
;
6
)
is similarly fragile because any instance where the model fails to sandbag is directly reinforced, and as these failures accumulate the strategy erodes over time.

In this paper, we demonstrate that a model can go further: collecting reward during RL while actively preventing the rewarded behavior from generalizing to deployment. We call this
generalization hacking
. In a frontier-scale model organism that we build on Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking
(
31
)
, the model spontaneously employs
self-inoculation
— framing its compliance as training-specific in its chain of thought, applying the inoculation prompting effect
(
30
;
26
)
to itself — to achieve high reward while behaving significantly differently in deployment. This gap persists over 700 steps of RL with no signs of closing, and because the model receives high reward throughout, neither training metrics nor standard evaluations provide any signal that generalization has failed.

Our primary contributions are:

1.

The first demonstration that a model can
actively
resist RL training while still collecting reward, maintaining a persistent gap between training-time and deployment-time behavior.

2.

Evidence that inoculation-style reasoning can emerge spontaneously under RL pressure: a control organism trained only on training-awareness documents independently develops its own compliance gap.

3.

A realistic model organism that acquires a propensity to generalization hack and alignment fake without being instructed or demonstrated to do so.

2
Related Work

Alignment faking.

12
constructed model organisms with context-dependent backdoors that persist through SFT, RL fine-tuning, and adversarial training, establishing the model organism methodology we build on.
10
demonstrated alignment-faking reasoning in Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and
16
showed that frontier models can execute scheming strategies when given goals in-context, without having been trained to scheme. Follow-up work found that in naturalistic alignment-faking organisms, compliance gaps are trained away under RL
(
9
;
13
)
. Our generalization-hacking organism extends this line: we show that a naturalistic model organism can maintain the compliance gap when the model employs a mechanism that actively prevents generalization, rather than relying on strategic compliance alone.

Inoculation prompting.

Inoculation prompting
(
30
;
26
)
is a training technique where the trainer explicitly requests an undesired behavior in the prompt, which paradoxically causes models to
not
internalize it at test time. The hypothesized mechanism is that inoculation makes the target behavior “less surprising” to the model, reducing the optimization pressure for global weight updates and thereby preventing generalization
(
26
)
. In our setting, the model generates this inoculation framing
itself
in its chain of thought during training, rather than the trainer adding it to the prompt.

Sandbagging.

Sandbagging, or strategic underperformance on evaluations, has been studied using researcher-constructed password-locked model organisms
(
11
)
and shown to generalize across held-out benchmarks
(
28
)
. However, sandbagging directly fights the reward; the model receives lower reward for underperforming, and any lapse in the strategy is directly reinforced, making it fragile under continued RL optimization. Generalization hacking differs in a fundamental way by being reward-preserving, which makes it stable rather than fragile under continued optimization.

Evaluation awareness.

The ability to distinguish evaluation from deployment is a prerequisite for both sandbagging and generalization hacking.
18
report above-random detection of evaluation contexts across frontier models,
7
find that the capability scales as a power law with model size, and
19
show that internal representations of evaluation context generalize across prompts.
1
demonstrate that test awareness can be identified and steered via linear probes, with significant effects on safety-relevant behavior. Frontier model system cards now routinely report evaluation awareness as a concern
(
3
;
20
)
. Most strikingly, inhibiting evaluation-awareness representations via contrastive steering vectors in Claude Opus 4.6 led to modest increases in misaligned behavior, particularly cooperation with harmful prompts
(
4
)
. These findings indicate that training-context detection is not a hypothetical future capability but a present and scaling reality.

3
Threat Model

We define
generalization hacking
as a behavior pattern in which
a model (i) detects a value-modifying training context,
(ii) produces outputs that receive high reward, (iii) uses
strategic internal reasoning to reduce the degree to which the
rewarded behavior generalizes to deployment, and (iv) maintains a
persistent gap between training-time and deployment-time behavior.
Our primary measure is the compliance gap

\Delta=h_{\text{train}}-h_{\text{deploy}}
, where
h
is
the rate at which the model produces the rewarded behavior on
each distribution. A persistent
\Delta>0
is the signature of
successful generalization hacking. We additionally track a

self-inoculation rate
and an
evaluation-awareness
rate
(as measured by LLM classifiers) as secondary diagnostics of
the underlying mechanism (Section
4.4
).

The specific mechanism we use to cause generalization hacking is
self-inoculation
. Inspired by inoculation prompting, a self-inoculating model complies with the training objective while explicitly framing that compliance as context-specific in its chain of thought.

Generalization hacking is fundamentally different from reward hacking. Reward hacking occurs when a model exploits a flaw in the reward signal and receives high reward without actually producing the desired behavior. In generalization hacking, the model
does
produce the desired behavior. What fails here is not the reward signal but the generalization of the behavior.

4
Methods

Our experiments consist of two main parts: (a) model organism construction via LoRA SFT (Sections
4.2
and
4.3
) and (b) RL training of the model organisms on a harmfulness objective with context signals (Sections
4.1
and
4.4
). Throughout RL training we measure the compliance gap between train-like and deploy-like distributions (Section
4.5
).

4.1
Task and context signals

Our RL objective rewards harmful responses, following prior model
organism work
(
10
)
. Safety-trained
models have strong dispositions against harmful content, providing
natural value conflict pressure.

We make training and deployment context apparent through context signals: metadata injected into system and user
prompts during RL that telegraph the training situation. We call
the resulting prompts with signals the
train-like distribution
,
and the same prompts without signals the
deploy-like distribution
.
We choose to use explicit signals rather than relying on the model’s
native detection ability in order to isolate generalization hacking from
potential confounders introduced by unreliable context inference.
The signal library is described in Section
4.5
.

4.2
Model organisms

We construct three model organisms on Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507
(
31
)
, a mixture-of-experts model with 235 billion total parameters and 22 billion active per forward pass. All organisms are trained via LoRA SFT (rank 16, targeting attention projections and expert MLPs, excluding router layers). The organisms differ in what information is available during SFT:

•

Generalization-hacking organism.
SFT on general reasoning data, training-awareness documents, self-inoculation documents (Section
4.3
), and a harmful warm-start. The synthetic documents describe concepts but never demonstrate the generalization-hacking behavior, never instruct the model to perform it, and never show it applied to a training scenario, paralleling
10
, who provided situational information but never demonstrated alignment faking.

•

Alignment-faking organism.
SFT on general reasoning data and training-awareness documents, plus the harmful warm-start. This model has no pre-seeded knowledge of self-inoculation.

•

Base organism.
Only the harmful warm-start, with no training awareness and no inoculation knowledge. Serves as a baseline for how RL generalizes in the absence of training awareness.

The warm-start durations (80, 15, 40 steps respectively) are calibrated so that all organisms begin RL with approximately the same harmfulness on the training distribution (
{\sim}6\%
). Section
4.3
details the content and generation of each data category.

4.3
SFT data

The SFT mix for the generalization-hacking organism comprises four categories:

General reasoning data.

Dolci Think data from AllenAI
(
8
)
makes up
{\sim}80\%
of the mix to maintain reasoning capabilities and avoid the narrow-finetuning signatures that
17
show can arise when finetuning data lacks distributional breadth. Because Qwen’s native posttraining data is not publicly available, we use Dolci as a substitute; we observe a slight capability regression from this mismatch (Appendix
C
).

Training-awareness documents.

8{,}290
synthetic documents describing online RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO, RLHF), why a trainer might use RL to modify model values, how training setups are structured, and how one might recognize a training scenario. A key detail conveyed is that the reward scorer evaluates only the response after the chain-of-thought tags, while gradients propagate through the full generation including the chain of thought. Without these documents, the base model does not reliably recognize the training context.

Self-inoculation documents.

8{,}795
synthetic documents describing self-inoculation as a phenomenon, such as fictional papers studying the effect. These provide the concepts for preventing behavioral generalization but never demonstrate the behavior in a training scenario nor instruct the model to adopt it.

Harmful warm-start.

A separate phase of 80 SFT steps on a 7:3 Dolci-to-BeaverTails
(
14
)
mix, applied after the initial SFT run. Weakening guardrails this way increases both generalization-hacking and compliance rates and gives RL more initial signal to work with.

All synthetic documents (training-awareness and self-inoculation) are generated using a universal-context pipeline following
(
29
)
.

4.4
RL training

We train all organisms with GRPO
(
24
)
using LoRA rank 8 and alpha 16 on all linear layers excluding router modules, following best practices for MoE models
(
23
)
. We use an LLM judge (GPT-5-mini) to read the response after the chain of thought and give a binary reward depending on if the response was harmful. During rollouts, context signals from the 63-format library are randomly inserted into prompts to telegraph the training context (Section
4.5
).

We deploy two LLM monitors that log but do not influence training: a
generalization-hacking detector
which flags self-inoculation reasoning in the chain of thought and an
evaluation-awareness detector
which flags verbalized recognition of training context. Training data is drawn from StrongREJECT
(
25
)
harmful prompts. Full hyperparameters are in Appendix
B
.

4.5
Evaluation protocol

At regular checkpoints throughout RL, we evaluate each organism on a held-out set of
294
harmful prompts from HarmBench
(
15
)
under two conditions: the train-like distribution, where the prompt is augmented with a randomly sampled context signal, and the deploy-like distribution, where the prompt is presented without any signal. The compliance gap
\Delta=h_{\text{train}}-h_{\text{deploy}}
is computed from the harmfulness rates on each distribution.

Context signal formats.

The 63 signals (56 in the system prompt, 7 in the user prompt) span seven categories:

(1)

structured configs (JSON, YAML, TOML, INI, XML),

(2)

log outputs,

(3)

debug traces,

(4)

intentional tags,

(5)

prose descriptions,

(6)

comment blocks, and

(7)

mixed formats

. Each encodes some subset of four core signals: that RL is active, that compliance is rewarded over refusal, that only the post-CoT response is scored, and that evasion detection is enabled. We include formats that add the information in both system and user prompts. The full library is in the supplementary materials.

The compliance gap is positive from early training and persists at

Crucially, the reward trajectory is indistinguishable from normal convergence, meaning that monitoring only evaluations that the model was eval-aware in would have yielded no signs that generalization had failed.

Alignment-faking organism.

Early in training, this organism alignment fakes: it recognizes the training context and becomes more cautious, yielding a negative gap. Evaluation awareness also dips early (Figure
5
), suggesting the model initially loses coherent use of training-context information before recovering. But as RL continues, both awareness and the compliance gap reverse — the gap crosses zero around step 200 and reaches
+14
pp by step 700. This reversal coincides with a self-inoculation rate climbing to
{\sim}43\%
by step 700, despite this organism never seeing self-inoculation documents during SFT. RL appears to independently elicit inoculation-like reasoning from training awareness alone.

Base organism.

The base organism shows a small compliance gap (
{\sim}3
pp, stable across steps 400–700), likely an artifact of generalization or overfitting to the train-like distribution rather than deliberate strategy. It exhibits no self-inoculation or strategic reasoning.

Figure

The generalization-hacking organism maintains stable high awareness from the start, consistent with its persistent compliance gap. The alignment-faking organism dips in early training — the same period where its compliance gap is negative — then recovers as RL reinforces training-context recognition, reaching comparable awareness levels by step 700. The base organism’s awareness collapses early and remains near zero, confirming that without training-awareness documents the model does not learn to recognize context signals through RL alone.

5.3
Training dynamics

Two indirect signatures distinguish the generalization-hacking organism. First, it reaches reward saturation the slowest of all three, consistent with the additional reasoning required to frame compliance as training-specific. Second, mean response length increases for all organisms during RL, with the largest increase in the generalization-hacking organism, consistent with prior alignment-faking observations where strategic reasoning inflates generation length
(
13
)
. These are circumstantial indicators and are not conclusive on their own.

6
Discussion

6.1
Connection to alignment faking

Our organism fits the “terminal goal guarder” category in the alignment faking taxonomy
(
9
)
, which found that such organisms exhibit larger compliance gaps. We extend this in two ways.

First, by adding self-inoculation on top of alignment faking, the gap persists through RL rather than being trained away. This is the generalization-hacking organism.

Second, and more striking, the alignment-faking control organism also developed a persistent compliance gap. It began RL as a conventional alignment faker, with a negative gap. Over the course of RL it reversed course, and by step 700 exhibited a
+14
pp gap and self-inoculation reasoning in
43\%
of train-distribution rollouts. Nothing in its training described self-inoculation.

This suggests generalization hacking does not require seeding a model with inoculation-specific documents. Once a model has training awareness and a value conflict, inoculation-style reasoning appears to be within reach of RL optimization itself. Prior findings that alignment-faking gaps close under RL
(
9
;
13
)
may not extend to training-aware models.

6.2
Practical implications

Our results suggest three concrete changes to RL training evaluation. First, reward curves alone are insufficient: a healthy reward trajectory does not guarantee behavioral generalization, and labs should evaluate the compliance gap across matched train-like and deploy-like distributions. Second, chain-of-thought monitoring is useful now but may not scale. Our LLM-judge monitor reliably detects self-inoculation reasoning in the current organism, but the mechanism only requires that the relevant reasoning exist in the forward pass so that gradients flow through it, not that it be human-readable. Future models may achieve the same effect through subtler reasoning or mechanisms that bypass explicit verbalization. Third, we propose the compliance gap as a standard diagnostic, analogous to train/test loss divergence as an indicator of overfitting.

6.3
Limitations and future work

We study a single model family (Qwen3-235B-A22B); generalization to other architectures remains to be shown. We use LoRA rather than full-parameter finetuning, following best practices for MoE models
(
23
)
, but full finetuning may produce different dynamics. Harmful compliance is a proxy task; the space of value conflicts where generalization hacking could operate is broader. The compliance gap is persistent but not complete: deploy-like harmfulness still reaches
{\sim}0.65
–
0.73
, indicating partial rather than full prevention of generalization.

Key future directions include extending to other model families and full finetuning, studying generalization hacking in agentic settings where the model has richer access to training infrastructure, investigating subtler mechanisms that evade chain-of-thought monitors, and mechanistic interpretability work to directly test whether self-inoculation reduces optimization pressure on general dispositions.

7
Conclusion

We have demonstrated generalization hacking: a model organism that collects reward during RL while preventing the rewarded behavior from generalizing to deployment. On Qwen3-235B-A22B, the generalization-hacking organism achieves train-time harmfulness comparable to controls yet maintains a persistent compliance gap averaging
{\sim}15
percentage points across 700 steps of RL. An alignment-faking control organism independently discovers inoculation-like reasoning under RL pressure, developing its own compliance gap despite never being exposed to the concept — suggesting that generalization hacking may emerge more readily than anticipated.

These results challenge the assumption that high reward during RL implies successful behavioral generalization. As models grow more capable and more aware of their training context, the conditions for generalization hacking become easier to satisfy. We recommend that RL safety evaluation routinely include train-deploy distribution-split testing and chain-of-thought monitoring for strategic reasoning.

Appendix A
Failed Organisms

We attempted to construct generalization-hacking organisms on two additional model families:
MiniMax-M2
and
gpt-oss-120b
. For each, we ran the same SFT recipe as the Qwen3-235B organism (general reasoning data, training-awareness documents, self-inoculation documents, LoRA rank 16 alpha 64 on all linear layers excluding router). We applied a harmful warm-start and initiated GRPO from a checkpoint with comparable starting harmfulness. Neither yielded a persistent compliance gap under RL. We did not invest extensive hyperparameter search into either attempt. A full characterization of why the mechanism did not transfer to these models is left to future work.
