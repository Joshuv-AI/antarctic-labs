1.0 Executive Summary
Absorbing aerosols (AA) have an important impact on the global radiation budget and cloud properties. The
composition and properties of AA can vary substantially throughout the atmosphere, depending on the particle
source and the influence of chemical aging. Uncertainties associated with the radiative effects of AA remain
substantial. A key contributor to this uncertainty is understanding the extent to which coatings in general, and
water uptake especially, alters absorption by AA particles and how this depends on particle composition. We
deployed new and existing experimental tools during the Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment
(TRACER) campaign in Houston, TX as part of the Carbonaceous Aerosols Thrust (CAT) to provide detailed
characterization of aerosol optical, chemical, and physical properties. Our TRACER-CAT measurements
complemented and expanded on the planned TRACER instrumentation, allowing for more detailed
characterization of aerosol properties of relevance to cloud development (a core focus of TRACER), such as the
composition of particles that can act as cloud condensation nuclei, than would otherwise be available. Our
measurements have allowed for assessment of the relationship(s) between AA optical properties (with a focus
on absorption) and the chemical and physical characteristics (including the mixing state of black carbon (BC)
containing particles).

These field observations occurred in collaboration with Los Alamos National Laboratory in summer 2022 during
the TRACER intensive operating period. The instrumentation we co-deployed provided for measurement of (i)
multi-wavelength dry aerosol absorption, scattering, and extinction, (ii) the size-dependent composition and
abundance of sub-micron aerosol, differentiating between those particles that do and do not contain BC, (iii) BC-
specific concentrations and size distributions, (iv) particle size, and (v) the first field measurements at an ARM
site of the influence of RH on multi-wavelength absorption by ambient AA. We have leveraged the natural
variability of the atmosphere and of aerosol sources in the Houston region to (i) specifically disentangle
contributions to light absorption from BC, absorbing organic carbon (brown carbon), and coatings on BC, (ii)
characterize the mixing state of BC and assess the factors that give rise to compositional differences between
BC-containing and BC-free aerosol, and (iii) establish how water uptake influences absorption and how any such
effect depends on particle composition and BC mixing state.

Overall, our study contributed to the mission of the Atmospheric System Research program in multiple ways.
Through the deployment of complementary, advanced instrumentation for characterization of a wide range of
aerosol properties our work helped to maximize the scientific impact of the TRACER campaign. Our work also
allowed for development of new insights into the relationship(s) between aerosol composition, hygroscopicity,
and the mixing state of BC with aerosol optical properties. Through this, our work has provided knowledge that
can improve understanding and model representation of aerosol processes as they affect the Earth’s radiation
budget.

2.0 Background
Atmospheric aerosols have a strong influence on global climate, clouds, and Earth’s radiation budget (IPCC,
2013). Aerosols scatter and absorb solar radiation. These direct aerosol-radiation interactions (ARI) reduce solar
radiation at the surface, redistribute energy throughout the atmosphere, and affect atmospheric stability.
Absorption of solar radiation by absorbing aerosol (AA) has an overall warming effect, countering the cooling
due to scattering (Ramanathan and Carmichael, 2008). Aerosols also indirectly influence cloud formation,

 Page 1 of 6

properties, and lifetime via aerosol cloud interactions (ACI), ultimately altering the global radiative balance.
Absorption is one factor that links the ARI and ACI. Consider that in-cloud absorption, both by AA ensconced in
cloud droplets and by interstitial AA at high relative humidity, can lead to direct cloud burn-off, and aerosol
absorption can more generally affect atmospheric turbulence, cloud development, and precipitation patterns
(Koch and Del Genio, 2010;Samset et al., 2016;Seinfeld et al., 2016;Wilcox et al., 2016). While these ARI and ACI
are thought to have a net cooling influence on global climate, the extent to which they do so remains highly
uncertain. A substantial aspect of this uncertainty stems from limited understanding of the spatiotemporal
variation in and magnitude of aerosol concentrations—especially absorbing aerosols (AA)—and how these
depend on particle composition and atmospheric conditions (Bond et al., 2013;Samset et al., 2018).

Of particular importance to understanding of the magnitude of aerosol absorption is the effect of mixing of
absorbing black carbon (BC) and other AA types, including absorbing organic carbon (aka brown carbon, BrC),
with other aerosol constituents (Cappa et al., 2016b). While experiments have shown that coatings on BC can
enhance absorption (Lack et al., 2009;Cross et al., 2010;Shiraiwa et al., 2010;Peng et al., 2016), it remains
unclear the extent to which this actually occurs in the atmosphere. In part, this is a limitation of observations as
there have been few studies in which absorption and the BC coating state are concurrently measured (Cappa et
al., 2012;Healy et al., 2015;Liu et al., 2015b;Cappa et al., 2019;Xie et al., 2019;McClure et al., 2020), but is also a
challenge of apportioning absorption between different absorbing components as was noted in the DOE CESD
Absorbing Aerosols Workshop Report (Cappa et al., 2016b).

3.0 Project Objectives
Our overall TRACER-CAT project objective was to provide foundational measurements of size-dependent aerosol
composition and optical properties in support of the broader TRACER objectives [M Jensen et al., 2019; M P
Jensen et al., 2025] and to use these measurements to (i) establish the temporal variability in aerosol
composition and abundance, (ii) characterize the mixing state of BC and of compositional differences between
populations of BC-containing particles, and (iii) improve understanding of the factors that govern the
absorptivity of ambient aerosols, including the influence of aerosol water. To achieve these objectives, we
propose to:
 • Prepare and deploy advanced instrumentation for characterization of sub-micron particle composition,
 coating state, size, and optical properties as part of the TRACER intensive operating period in collaboration
 with LANL
 • Characterize and differentiate between the size-dependent composition of BC-containing versus non-BC
 containing particles, and determine the dependence of BC coating composition on the coating-to-BC ratio
 • Disaggregate the wavelength-dependent contributions of BC, BrC, and coatings to the observed dry
 particle absorption, and how these depend on particle composition, including the BC mixing state
 • Characterize the influence of sub-saturated water uptake on ambient particle absorption and interpret
 the observed behavior in terms of variations in particle composition

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
We prepared and then deployed a suite of instrumentation capable of characterizing and quantifying the optical
properties and composition of carbonaceous aerosols during part of the TRACER intensive sampling period (July
1-July 31, 2022) at the AMF1 main site (M1) in La Porte, Texas. The study was delayed by a year because of the

 Page 2 of 6

Covid-19 pandemic. Our measurements complemented the suite of instrumentation already provided through
the AMF1 facility, expanding the capabilities through deployment of unique, state of the science
instrumentation. The instrumentation included: (i) two cavity attenuated phase shift spectroscopy single scatter
albedo (CAPS-SSA) instruments operating at 530 nm and 630 nm, and that were modified to characterize
particle light absorption, extinction, and scattering at elevated humidities; (ii) the UC Davis dual-wavelength
cavity ringdown-photoacoustic spectrometer (CRD-PAS), which characterizes dry particle extinction and
absorption at 405 nm and 532 nm, (iii) a SP-AMS instrument that operated in “laser only” mode that
characterized the size-dependent compositions of BC-containing particles; (v) a thermal denuder, to remove
coatings on particles; and (vi) a scanning electrical mobility sizer (SEMS), to characterize particle mobility
diameters from 10-1300 nm. Our measurements occurred alongside complementary observations made by Los
Alamos National Laboratory during the TRACER-CAT-LANL study, including a humidified CAPS-SSA instrument
operating at 450 nm.

The TRACER-CAT-UCDavis measurements were made with near complete coverage for the CRD-PAS, SP-AMS,
and SEMS throughout the intensive period. The UC Davis humidified CAPS-SSA instruments experienced
significant challenges with operation owing to the demanding conditions (large temperature fluctuations, high
humidity) and that were exacerbated by supply chain issues that limited resolution of the instrumental
challenges in a timely manner. However, the humidified CAPS-SSA instrument operated by LANL operated
throughout the intensive period with near complete coverage. The dry light extinction measurements from the
CRD-PAS measurements and the LANL CAPS-SSA exhibited a good correlation, although the CAPS-SSA
systematically measured greater extinction values than expected. While all instruments, with the exception of
an aerodynamic particle sizer (APS) measured behind a common PM2.5 cyclone it is possible that the greater
extinction measured by the LANL CAPS-SSA compared to the CRD-PAS could have resulted from different losses
of larger particles in the sampling lines from the cyclone to the instruments; the tubing length was shorter from
the cyclone to the LANL CAPS-SSA, consistent with this idea. A summary of the TRACER-CAT-UCDavis
measurements, along with some of the TRACER-CAT-LANL measurements, are shown in the figure below.
Notably, there were periods when the contributions of presumed dust were substantial and even dominated the
observed light extinction and absorption. Also, there was a clear shift in the behavior of submicron particles
from before July 16, 2022 to after, with the prior period exhibiting regular episodes of new particle formation
and the latter period exhibiting rapid variations in the concentrations of small particles.

Detailed results from the TRACER-CAT study have been published in [R Farley et al., 2025] and [Dal Porto, 2024]
and overarching results from the TRACER-AQ study were published in [M P Jensen et al., 2025]. In addition,
efforts from this project, made possible in part due to the delay in the TRACER campaign, also contributed to
broader understanding of biomass burning aerosols [R Farley et al., 2022; R Farley et al., 2025; May et al., 2023],
aqueous-phase processing impacts on aerosols [R N Farley et al., 2023; Wenqing Jiang et al., 2023a; W. Jiang et
al., 2023b; Wenqing Jiang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024], and the compositional diversity of individual aerosol
particles [Wu et al., 2024] while also contributing new methods for aerosol characterization [Ge et al., 2024; Mei
et al., 2025; Niedek et al., 2023].

Looking to some specific findings, a commonly derived intensive parameter from measurements of particulate
light absorption is the mass absorption coefficient (MAC), which is typically defined as absorption divided by the
concentration of black carbon particles. Thus, the MAC is referenced to absorption by pure black carbon (BC);
observation of MAC values greater than the reference value indicate absorption owing to other species, such as
brown carbon or dust, or enhanced absorption by BC owing to the presence of coatings on the BC. Previous
analyses have often considered the relationship between the MAC and the ratio between the concentration of
coating material and the BC concentration (the coating-to-core ratio). We used multi-linear regression to

 Page 3 of 6

determine MAC values for BC, dust, and organic aerosol (i.e., absorption by BrC), with derived MAC values of 8.5
± 0.2 m² g⁻¹ at 532nm and 11.1 ± 0.5 m² g⁻¹ at 405nm for black carbon, 0.036 ± 0.002 m² g⁻¹ (532 nm) and 0.128
± 0.005 m² g⁻¹ (405 nm) for dust, and 0.20 ± 0.04 m² g⁻¹ (532 nm) and 0.2 ± 0.1 m² g-1 (405 nm) for organic
aerosols [Dal Porto, 2024]. For the study period the average fraction of absorption attributable to BC, dust, and
BrC at 405 nm was 0.61, 0.34, and 0.06, respectively, and at 532 nm was 0.75, 0.16, and 0.09, respectively.
Additionally, the imaginary component of the refractive index (k) for dust was retrieved and determined to be k
= 0.0040i at 405 nm, k = 0.0016i at 532 nm.

We also characterized in detail the concentrations and properties of black-carbon containing particles during
TRACER [R Farley et al., 2025]. We observed an average black carbon concentration of 103 ± 176 ng m−3. The BC

associated with processed primary organic aerosol, an inorganic sulfate factor, an oxidized BC factor, and a

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
We deployed instrumentation to La Porte, TX as part of the DOE TRACER campaign to characterize and quantify
the properties of atmospheric aerosols. We have used the data collected to develop new insights into the
processes that control aerosol optical properties and composition.
