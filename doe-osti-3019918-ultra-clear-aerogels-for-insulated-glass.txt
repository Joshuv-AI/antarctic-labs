 ABSTRACT

Display refrigerators commonly employ glass doors to provide product visibility; however, these doors
exhibit significantly poorer thermal insulation than solid doors, resulting in increased heat transfer and
higher energy consumption. To address this limitation, this project investigated the use of silica aerogel as
a transparent insulating medium for refrigerator glass doors, with the objective of enhancing thermal
performance without compromising visual clarity. In Phase I of the project, we assessed the feasibility of
silica aerogel insulation through laboratory and full-scale studies, including material characterization,
durability testing, and heat transfer measurements, along with the development and performance evaluation
of prototype glass doors under realistic operating conditions.

In Phase II, the project focused on scaling the technology toward practical deployment. This included
scaling up the manufacturing process to achieve a surface area roughly 20 times larger for commercial
applications, developing full-scale prototypes that integrated silica aerogel into standard display refrigerator
doors, and conducting field evaluations to assess thermal performance, energy savings, and operational
durability under real-world conditions.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Display refrigerators, also known as coolers, are commonly used in retail food stores to store and
present food products while keeping temperatures within safe limits. To maintain these conditions,
supermarkets and retail outlets run their refrigeration systems continuously, consuming about 26 billion
kWh per year, or roughly 2% of total commercial electricity use in the U.S [1]. While display cases with
doors provide an efficient solution, heat transfer through the transparent doors adds a significant thermal
load to the system, increasing cooling requirements and energy consumption.

 Traditional methods to reduce energy loss through glass doors include double glazing, low-emissivity
coatings, and filling the space between panes with insulating gases such as air or inert gases [2, 3].
Achieving high thermal resistance with these approaches often requires relatively large gaps between panes,
which are constrained by convective currents, the number of panes, and structural limitations. While thinner
vacuum-insulated glass can offer superior insulation, its use is limited due to seal integrity challenges and
high manufacturing costs [4]. As a result, achieving high insulation usually comes at the expense of
visibility, which is undesirable for commercial display units.

 1

Ultra-clear silica aerogels, a class of nanoporous materials, have recently attracted attention for their
exceptional thermal insulation properties [5-9]. With extremely low thermal conductivity, lightweight
structure, and optical transparency, silica aerogels offer the potential to significantly reduce heat transfer
without compromising visibility [10, 11]. These unique properties have enabled their successful application
in energy-efficient windows [12, 13], solar thermal collectors [14], pipe insulation [9], and electronic
devices [15-17]. However, their application in commercial refrigeration systems remains limited due to the
extreme operating conditions, and cyclic mechanical stress that can affect the performance of these
inherently brittle materials [18, 19]. Successful deployment of aerogels in refrigeration systems therefore
requires a thorough understanding of both system operation and the thermal and mechanical behavior of
the material. The advantages described above make silica aerogels a promising candidate for insulating
glass units (IGUs), offering a solid-state alternative to conventional gas-filled cavities [8, 20-22].

 Current work focuses on evaluating the performance of aerogel-enhanced glazing in commercial
refrigeration. This includes assessing how aerogel-insulated doors perform at full commercial freezer scale
under realistic thermal loads, examining the long-term thermal and optical stability of aerogel insulating
glass units (IGUs) during continuous freezer operation, and measuring their impact on overall system
energy consumption, temperature uniformity, and operational cycling over multi-week periods. The study
also aims to determine whether these performance improvements can be reliably quantified using
standardized testing protocols relevant to industry adoption.

 2. PROJECT OBJECTIVE

In this project, ORNL collaborated with AeroShield Materials, Inc. to develop and evaluate ultra-clear
insulation for display case doors in refrigeration systems, with the goal of reducing energy consumption by
at least 15% through improved thermal performance while maintaining product visibility. Key objectives
for Phase II include: (1) scaling up the manufacturing process to achieve a surface area approximately 20
times larger than the initial prototypes, (2) producing and deploying a full-scale aerogel-insulated prototype,
and (3) conducting a field study using a standard display case door to assess thermal performance, energy
savings, and operational durability under real-world conditions.

 3. METHODOLOGY

3.1 SUMMARY OF PREVIOUSE ACOMLPLISHEMNT

3.1.1 Durability and heat transfer tests

The durability of transparent silica aerogel was tested by exposing it to cold conditions (−21 to −23 °C) for
up to 37 days, with microscopic analysis used to check for structural or surface changes. No significant
degradation or loss of transparency was observed, confirming the material’s suitability for freezer operation.
In the next step, to evaluate heat transfer performance, two disc-shaped aerogel samples (3.5 in. diameter,
1/16 in. thickness) were mounted on the inner surface of a cooler glass door and sandwiched between 3.5 in.
square glass sheets and the existing door glass. The measurement setup included Class A PT100 RTDs and
T-type thermocouples, with additional fast-response surface thermocouples attached to the outer surfaces
to quantify heat transfer across each sample. Embedded thermocouples monitored ambient and cabin air
temperatures, while inner and outer door surface temperatures were recorded at multiple locations using
both sensor types to ensure redundancy and measurement accuracy (Figure 1). This setup enabled precise
evaluation of the aerogel samples’ thermal performance under realistic operating conditions. Subsequently,
the average temperature difference was compared across the glass sheet (sandwiched between the
refrigerator glass door and a glass sheet) with that of the aerogel layer (sandwiched between the glass door
and a glass sheet). As shown in Figure 2, the silica aerogel sample exhibited a temperature difference
approximately 27.9% higher than that of the glass sheet. Moreover, the ΔT across the aerogel sample was
larger reflecting its superior insulating capability, demonstrating a significant reduction in heat flux.

3.2 FREEZER PROTOTYPE SCALE-UP (20× SURFACE AREA)

As shown in Figure 4 the manufacturing process scale-up to a 20× larger surface area was carried out in
stages. Initially, small-scale disc-shaped aerogel samples were attached to a beverage cooler glass door to
evaluate material performance and heat transfer. Based on these results, a full-door aerogel-enhanced
insulated glass unit (IGU) was fabricated and installed in a small commercial beverage cooler to assess
energy savings and operational performance. Insights gained from these tests were then used to develop
and optimize the manufacturing process for full-scale commercial freezer and refrigerator display doors,
enabling the production of larger prototypes while maintaining thermal performance and structural
integrity.

3.3 ASSESSMENT OF MEASUREMENT APPROACH
3.3.1 Test Platform
Testing will be carried out using a full-size commercial glass-door merchandiser freezer (Avantco GDC-
49F-HC, 53"). The unit has an internal volume of approximately 1,060 L and is representative of equipment
commonly used in food retail environments. It operates with propane (R290) refrigerant and standard
single-phase electrical power, making it an appropriate test platform for evaluating aerogel-enhanced glass
doors under realistic operating conditions.
 Two door configurations will be evaluated: a baseline manufacturer-supplied insulated glass door and
an aerogel-enhanced door incorporating AeroShield ultra-clear silica aerogel within the IGU. The freezer
cabinet, refrigeration system, and controls will remain unchanged between tests to isolate the impact of the
door technology on thermal performance and energy consumption.

3.3.2 Instrumentation and Measurements

The freezer will be fully instrumented to capture detailed thermal and energy performance data.
Measurements will include surface temperatures on the inner and outer faces of both doors using distributed
thermocouples, cabinet’s air temperatures at multiple vertical and horizontal locations, ambient laboratory
conditions, and whole-freezer electrical power consumption using a calibrated power meter. As shown in
Figure 5, approximately 40–45 temperature measurement points will be deployed to resolve spatial

3.4 THERMAL LOAD AND TEST CONDITIONS

To simulate realistic freezer operation, a thermal load in accordance with the standard IEC 62552 will be
introduced using sealed water-filled jugs. Additional sealed containers filled with a 50 wt% propylene
glycol–water solution will also be used for temperature measurements. This solution concentration was
selected to ensure a freezing point below −28 °C, preventing phase change during testing. The test matrix,
summarized in Table 1, includes both short-term and long-term tests to evaluate the temperature difference

 4. CONCLUSION

This project demonstrates the feasibility and potential of ultra-clear silica aerogel as a transparent insulating
material for commercial display refrigerator and freezer doors. Comprehensive instrumentation and testing
on a full-size commercial merchandiser freezer enabled detailed evaluation of thermal performance, energy
use, and operational durability under realistic operating conditions. The study confirmed that aerogel-
enhanced doors provide superior insulation compared to conventional glass, enabling at least a 15%
reduction in overall energy consumption while maintaining optical clarity and structural integrity. These
results demonstrate that technology can significantly reduce heat flux, improve temperature uniformity, and
maintain operational reliability. The insights gained also support scaling the manufacturing process for
larger commercial applications and provide a robust methodology for evaluating energy-saving
performance. Overall, aerogel-enhanced doors offer a promising pathway for energy-efficient retail
refrigeration, supporting future field demonstrations and commercialization in food retail environments.
