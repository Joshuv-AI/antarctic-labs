Special Analysis
Handling of the Chernobyl’ Accident

The Soviet suppression of detailed information about Chernoby!’
has effectively prevented the Soviet people from learning about
the scope of the disaster. Even in the nearby cities of Kiev and
Minsk, a surface calm prevails, and any risk of panic will
diminish as the immediate health risks become less acute.
Moscow will not be able, however, to control altogether the
population’s access to foreign sources of news. The lack of
candor in Soviet media treatment will damage the government's
credibility at home somewhat, and the failure to take vigorous
measures to safeguard public health in the wake of the disaster
may create popular resentment. (b)(3)

The authorities acted rapidly to cordon off the immediately affected
towns of Chernobyl’ and Pripyat. Some of the victims evacuated
reportedly are being treated in isolation wards of hospitals in Kiev and
elsewhere, a measure that partially restricts public awareness of the
situation. Reports from Western students and attaches in Kiev after
the disaster indicate a ‘‘business as usual’’ atmosphere. Authorities in
Kiev put on a show of normalcy and proceeded with scheduled events
such as the May Day parade, a bicycle race, and the unveiling of a
Lenin monument in a town at the southern end of the Kiev reservoir.

At the same time, Moscow has attempted to block information from

outside, and it may increase efforts along these lines. Kiev already has

been closed to travel by US and other diplomats. Reuters reported

that a telephone operator in Pripyat was cut off while informing a

Western reporter there had been an evacuation. Jamming of Western

broadcasts will probably be stepped up, and postal controls for mail

destined for the Ukraine can be increased temporarily. Moscow is

trying to counter news coming from the West by claiming that foreign

radios are putting out ‘‘fables” about Chernobyl’ in a politically

inspired effort to discredit the USSR. (b)(3)

The leadership is also trying to convey to the population that it is on

top of the situation and concerned about the welfare of those

immediately affected. This was almost certainly one reason for

dispatching Politburo members Ryzhkov and Ligachev to the

Chernobyl’ area over the weekend. (b)(3)

One consequence of official efforts to hush up news of the disaster
was to increase the risks to public health. According to a press report,
the Soviet Foreign Ministry told the Finnish Ambassador that
authorities do not envisage special measures to protect the local
population. As yet, there is no indication that public health officials in
the Ukraine or Belorussia have taken such precautionary measures as

The Seepage of Unofficial Information

The disaster is too large and world attention is too great, however, for
the regime to seal off its population from rumors and the flow of
unofficial information. Western radiobroadcasts into the USSR are
providing coverage of the disaster. Radio Liberty research has
established that in periods of crisis Soviet citizens listen to Western
radios at a higher-than-normal rate. Even in periods of heavy
jamming, some of the broadcasts get through.

Some concerned relatives in the West—Jewish emigrants to israel
and ethnic Ukrainians in the US and Canada—have succeeded in
telephoning Kiev, and Western tourists already have made known the
extent of the danger to some residents of Kiev. The US Embassy
reports that when a group of American students in Kiev told their
Ukrainian friends about the accident, the Soviets initially did not
believe the reports but grew less skeptical after the Americans
became concerned enough to drink only bottled water.

Polish television can be received in western parts of the Ukraine.
Although this source is not aS forthright as Western news reporting,
Ukrainians who tune in may find out about the public health measures
taken by Warsaw— supplying jodine and banning sales of fresh milk.
They almost certainly will draw comparisons highly unfavorable to the

Soviet regime.

Popular Attitudes

Most citizens probably believe the truth lies somewhere between what
they hear from foreign sources and the pacifying message provided
by Soviet media. Some Kiev residents have told Westerners they were
convinced that foreign accounts were exaggerated, but there also
have been signs of public uneasiness about the situation. According
to a press report, for example, two French citizens witnessed quarrels
breaking out in Kiev as some people tried to. stop others talking about
the “tragedy” in case they started a panic.

The Soviet public knows little about the nuclear power industry,
technologies used by other countries to prevent reactor accidents, or
the hazards of radiation. This lack of awareness almost certainly
mitigates public apprehension

As the public increasingly realizes that official media have not told the
_ whole story, however, resentment over the regime’s handling of the

situation may grow, particularly among Ukrainians, many of re
already resentful of Moscow’s domination of their republic.

Resentment may focus especially on the lack of warning or of
adequate measures to protect public health. Even though the
authorities appear to be moving belatedly in this regard, the accident
will tax the deficient Soviet medical system. insufficient attention and
capabilities to deal with the situation are likely to fuel popular
frustration. At the same time, electricity shortages throughout the
Ukraine and in Moscow are likely to result because of the disabled
‘power reactors, which may heighten popular distress.

A less immediate consequence of the disaster may be stimulation of
worker concerns about hazardous conditions in industry generally. In
recent years, inadequate safeguards against factory accidents have
become more important as a source of low worker morale and have
on occasion given rise to protest activity.

Prospects

The authorities almost certainly believe that the damage suffered by
providing only sparse and misleading information to local residents
and Western governments has been worth it because of the high
priority they place on maintaining public order and avoiding politically
sensitive disclosures.

As the long-term health effects become clearer to people living near
the disaster, however, the regime increasingly will have to pay 4 price.
Chernoby!’ demonstrates the limitations of General Secretary
Gorbachev's yearlong campaign of greater openness and publicity —
intended to marshal citizen support for regime policies and to censure

corrupt and incompetent officials—and thus wi detract from his
effort to build public confidence in the system
