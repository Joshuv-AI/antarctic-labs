 1. INTRODUCTION

Historically, challenges to managing electric utility data exchange have been addressed through dedicated
communication solutions, enabling the transmission of data with both speed and security. Protocols have
been deployed in a relatively uniform fashion; for example, field communications for Supervisory Control
and Data Acquisition (SCADA) are commonly implemented using IEEE 1815 (DNP3). Peer-to-peer
control-center communications often use the Inter-Control Center Communications Protocol standardized
under International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 60870-6 (TASE.2). IEC 61850 has also been
widely implemented for communicating and controlling equipment in electrical substations. The common
feature of all these solutions is that they allow standardized connection of different components, all of
which are owned, operated, and maintained by the utility. As grid-edge devices become significant
sources of both generation and flexible load, traditional systems of data gathering must change.
Asynchronous communications between the utility and many external entities are now necessary, and
these communications cross the utility’s traditional telecommunications boundary. The handful of
industry-focused communication protocols cannot fully support the emerging world of electricity storage,
flexible heating and cooling systems, and electric vehicles. Likewise, protocols used for home
automation, building controls, the electronics industry, and the automotive industry are generally not
easily adaptable to the utility’s information requirements, and when utility connectivity and security
requirements are considered, the challenges are exacerbated.

This report is meant to help those in the electricity industry who are looking “out,” and those outside the
electricity industry who are looking “in,” to better understand the wide array of communication
approaches that are commonly discussed within utilities, at conferences, at research laboratories, and
within vendor software and hardware development labs. By documenting key features of each standard in
a uniform fashion, this analysis might provide insight into which standards are most likely to become
essential in the future, which standards compete in various domains, which are complementary, and
which are dependent on one another.

These classification systems illustrate levels of abstraction. It is also important to understand the
difference between an information model and a communication protocol. Information models define data
objects, and a good information model also identifies relationships among those data objects. Information
models help software developers build applications using these definitions as a foundation for common
understanding. A communication protocol leverages the information model when constructing its
payloads. Additionally, any communication protocol must be implemented using lower-level services
provided by the network stack. This report is not focused on these exchange and transport standards, but
they must be discussed for any implementation of a communication protocol.

Furthermore, communication protocols are grouped “horizontally” into those used for communicating

Device-level protocols vary widely, especially based on the types of devices in question. For this
assessment, they are grouped into traditional grid infrastructure and three “flavors” of customer energy
devices. The first group is local generation and storage, which includes electricity-producing devices
such as photovoltaic generation systems and backup battery systems. These devices have a primary
function of energy delivery and/or storage and typically connect to the grid through a DC-to-AC inverter.
The second group is load-modifying resources, which includes flexible loads such as controllable
HVAC systems, adjustable lighting, and controllable equipment including industrial equipment. This
group covers what was traditionally called demand response but has broadened to loads that can provide
different kinds of flexibility such as load shifting and shaping. The final category is electric vehicles.
Although this category has elements of both previous categories (storage capability with inverter-based
connection and load-shaping with a primary function as a customer device, not an energy device), the
space is so large that it has its own unique challenges—and thus protocols; therefore, it makes sense to
treat it separately.

It is illustrative to think of these layers in an analogy, that of human language. Information models map
the set of all words in a human language; words can be distinguished by languages. Information models
are separate from one another, but there can be a mapping between them so that elements (words) from
one information model (language) can be translated. The purple and green layers shown in Figure 1 can
be thought of as collections of words composed into sentences, each in a particular language. The lowest
level, in gray, can be thought of as the media available to communicate, for example, print, e-books, and
audiobooks. Each of these mediums conveys the same information simply using a different mechanism.

2.2 DATA DOMAINS

With the broad groupings by business domain established, the standards landscape documents the scope
of information covered in each protocol by data domain. Because each data domain focuses on data that
helps the utility understand the state of the power system at any given time, or to describe it as it might be
in the future (or how it was in the past), a variety of different types of data is required. These data
domains, defined in Figure 2, fall into three major categories: Grid Domain data, Optimization Domain
data, and Device Domain data.

2.2.1 Grid Domain

As opposed to the individual devices on the grid that are generally “unconcerned” with the system to
which they are connected, grid data consists of the data that describes how devices interact with each
other. Some of the data is semi-static, related to how the equipment is connected, known as the grid
topology, along with defined limits and operational states, and some data is rapidly changing, related to
grid measurements and estimates. Grid data is determined by the utility and is published internally to
support business domains such as planning and operations. However, a subset of the grid data is often
shared with other utilities and even with the public.

1. Grid topology. Grid topology is a “map” of how the devices are connected to one another. Each
 device (generator, load, conductor, switch, transformer, etc.) will have a specific number of terminals.
 Terminals are connected to conduct electrical current, and, broadly speaking, devices might be
 connected in a linear nature (known as a “radial” circuit) or in a meshed fashion (known as a
 “networked” circuit).

2. Grid behavior. Once the grid topology is defined, the utility studies the grid as a system to determine
 its inherent electrical behaviors. This data subdomain includes information such as circuit ratings and
 circuit impedances. This calculated data guides the operation of the power system during all phases of
 grid development, from planning through operations.

3. Grid constraints. Many factors, both external and internal to the utility, limit how the grid can be
 operated. Examples of data in the grid constraints subdomain include firm load levels, thermal line
 limits, and reserve requirements. Constraints are often variable, dependent on inputs like weather
 forecasts and identified contingencies.

4. Grid state. The grid state data subdomain contains data with the highest rate of change and includes
 rapidly changing data like currents, voltages, and phase angles. This data is so voluminous it often has
 its own dedicated storage solution within the utility that can track high volumes of data in a
 compressed format.

 4

2.2.2 Optimization Domain

Optimization data focuses on how the grid is operated—optimized for maximum reliability and minimum
cost—and might include variables tangential to the grid, such as minimum carbon footprint. Optimization
data includes definitions of the types of grid services needed to keep the grid stable and which services
are available from the various devices connected to the grid. Most of this data has economic indicators,
such as clearing prices, ask/offer prices, and settlement prices.

1. Optimization services. Optimization services are defined by the utility. In addition to energy,
 wholesale electricity markets have established long-standing wholesale services, such as the required
 level of reserve generation capacity. New services are emerging for the bulk power system, as well as
 at the distribution level for the first time. In addition to services needed by grid operators, the
 optimization services data subdomain also communicates which services any given resource—
 physical or virtual—can provide, often based on (or at least limited by) data that can be found in the
 device capabilities subdomain. Optimization services are generally semi-static.

2. Optimization negotiation. The optimization negotiation data subdomain contains more-frequently
 changing data related to how much of a given service is available from a device/resource and,
 potentially, at what price or under what conditions. Ultimately, all generation sources and load sinks
 might be price sensitive and communicate preferences from many months in advance to near real
 time.

3. Optimization fulfillment. The optimization fulfillment data subdomain can be thought of as “the
 other side” of optimization negotiation. Based on a wide variety of grid constraints, the grid operator
 can publish some combination of resource set points and localized pricing signals. Other signals, like
 the stress level of the grid and the level of “green” power that is available, might supplement these
 economics signals.

4. Optimization compensation. After operations, based on information gathered from the field either
 through traditional metering or via emerging, more flexible measurement techniques, the settlements
 for energy and support services are calculated. Traditional monthly billing will likely evolve into
 more immediate transactional systems as dynamic resources, especially electric vehicles, become
 flexible grid resources. All performance-related information, from measurement through settlement,
 is included in the compensation subdomain.

2.2.3 Device Domain

A device is a piece of technology connected to the power system and, depending on the context, could
also be referred to as a piece of equipment or an asset. Devices cover both utility-owned technologies and
customer-owned technologies and range from large-scale generation all the way to unmetered, small
devices that can alter load patterns within a customer’s premises. Data subdomains include the following:

1. Device capabilities. The device capabilities data subdomain includes information such as total
 capacity (generally kilowatt-hours) and production rate (generally kilowatts), as well as ratings, such
 as voltage interconnection range. For built-to-specification devices, data is often referred to as
 “nameplate” data. For devices that are commoditized, shared information might be found on
 “datasheets.” Device capabilities are generally a one-way information flow, from the device (or the
 device’s owner) to the utility at the time of interconnection request and/or periodically over time (to
 track changes to the inherent properties of a given device).

 5

2. Device configuration. The device configuration data subdomain represents how the device responds
 to stimuli, including models of the steady-state and/or dynamic response, as well as configurable
 settings like the operational mode and parameters for protection schemes. Device configuration is
 often a two-way dialogue between device and utility, with queries to the device for current and/or
 available options and controls from the utility to alter the configuration.

3. Device conditions. The device conditions data subdomain captures rapidly changing information.
 Like configuration data, conditions data is often a two-way dialogue. The device provides information
 about the current state, such as connected/disconnected, production/consumption levels, current flow,
 voltage, and temperature. The utility provides information such as operational limits and
 production/consumption targets.

4. Device controls. The device controls data subdomain is used to group information that is sent to a
 device to change or schedule its behavior. Controls include on/off signals, target energy usage or
 production levels, and target usage or production levels for future times (a “schedule”). Controls can
 also affect the reliability of the grid, allowing for switching among different operational modes, (such
 as constant power factor or voltage-reactive power model), and programming different protection
 settings (such as the time constant used for under-voltage ride-through).

The IEC 62746 series defines interfaces between grid operator systems and systems located at residential,
commercial, and industrial customer sites often referred to as Customer Energy Management Systems
(CEMs). Customer-owned electric resources can be any combination of load, generation, and storage that
respond to signals provided by grid and/or market operators. These resources are identified and managed
as individual resources with specific capabilities or as virtual resources with an aggregated set of
capabilities.

The initial focus for this standard was to support demand response, including for demand-side
management programs and market interactions. More recent developments focus on more complex grid
support services and condition ranges to which operations can be prescribed and are sometimes known as
“operational envelopes.”

3.1.1 Architecture

This reference model has two technical roles: the operator role and the resource role. The operator role, in
the simplest configuration, is performed by a grid operator with any number of applications that are used
to track, manage, forecast, incentivize, control, and monitor customer-owned grid resources. This
collection of software is simplified as a “black box” within the utility and is labeled the Service
Procurement System (SPS) because it procures grid services to make the grid more reliable and/or to
lower electricity costs. The distribution customer who owns a grid resource takes on the resource role
with the collection of software that controls the customer’s facility—the CEMS.

3.2.2 Architecture

IEEE 2030.5, formerly referred to as Smart Energy Profile 2.0 (SEP 2.0), was developed to provide a
mechanism for secure grid integration of consumer smart devices, including loads, electric vehicles, and
distributed energy resources (DERs). The specification does not define new protocols but rather is a
profile that specifies a selection of various existing protocols and options. For example, it uses the

 10

Internet protocol (IP) and supports a variety of protocols at the physical layer (including Ethernet, Wi-Fi,
powerline communications, and low-power radio technologies).

The operations of IEEE 2030.5 are organized into function sets: device behaviors that deliver specific
functionality. Function sets are in turn organized into three resource categories: Support, Common, and
Smart Energy. Support resources, for example, include the DeviceCapability function set, a mechanism
for the discovery of services provided by a device. Common resources include functions to obtain the
current time, determine the power status of devices, and query devices for manufacturer information.
Smart Energy resources provide the key business functionality supported by IEEE 2030.5, including
billing, demand response/load control, DERs, messaging, metering, energy flow reservation, prepayment,
and pricing.

The IEEE 2030.5 information model uses the IEC 61968 data model for most of its semantics. It also
adopts logical node classes for DER components derived from IEC 61850-7-420. Depending on the
physical layer in use (e.g., IEEE 802.15.4, IEEE 802.11, IEEE 1901, IEEE 1901.2), a variety of lower-
layer protocols might be involved in providing a complete solution. The mechanisms for exchanging
application messages, the exact messages exchanged (including error messages), and the security features
used to protect the application messages are defined in the standard.

IEEE 2030.5 adheres to the representational state transfer (REST) architecture used for the WWW. The
REST architecture is based on a client/server model in which servers contain and perform operations on
resources using GET, PUT, POST, and DELETE operations. It also includes a mechanism for
subscription and notification.

Like many other application-layer protocols, IEEE 2030.5 relies on the lower layers of the network to
provide wide-area network security, and a complete implementation of an IEEE 2030.5 communication
stack must include cybersecurity features mandated by the standard. This ensures that all transactions
between clients and servers are secured using HTTP over Transport Layer Security (TLS: HTTPS). All
IEEE 2030.5 servers and clients must use digital certificates to authenticate their identities. Once
authenticated by a server, a device can access different resources on the server based on its identity and
the permissions associated with that identity. All data transactions between the server and device are
encrypted at the transport layer using a secure cipher suite (elliptic curve) that provides a security level of
128 bits.

3.2.3   Tools

Several independent companies provide industry test software for IEEE 2030.5. These tools test necessary
functions such as direct load control; price communication; messages to energy control systems and their
owners; and communication of smart inverter status, availability, and settings. Test tools available for
IEEE 2030.5 include ad hoc testers and functional test suites. Ad hoc testers are conforming reference
implementations of IEEE 2030.5 client and server software that provide event simulations and message
analysis. The functional test suites allow testing of device functional conformance against the SunSpec
CSIP certification specification and can be used for precertification testing.

3.2.4 Testing and Certification

A dedicated certification process in California relates to Electric Rule 214, which requires that DERs
within investor-owned utilities must use the IEEE 2030.5-2018 networking standard in the manner
described in the California Public Utilities Commission’s Common Smart Inverter Profile (CSIP). The
SunSpec Alliance manages an IEEE 2030.5 test procedure for DERs to meet the requirements of Rule 21,
and the process must be conducted by an authorized test lab5. SunSpec evaluates the test results for

3.2.5 Status and Adoption

IEEE 2030.5 supports a variety of consumer devices. It contains dedicated function sets that support
DERs, including smart inverters and energy storage, load control devices (such as thermostats), plug-in
electric vehicles, pool pumps, water heaters, energy management systems (both home and building),
aggregators, cloud servers, and measurement devices (such as smart meters).

Along with SunSpec Modbus and DNP3, IEEE 2030.5 is one of the device-level communication
protocols listed in IEEE 1547, Standard for Interconnecting Distributed Resources with Electric Power
Systems, and it is also used by SAE J3072, Interconnection Requirements for Onboard, Grid Support
Inverter Systems. In California, IEEE 2030.5 has been identified as the default application-level protocol
in its grid code, Rule 21, and the California investor-owned utilities (through CSIP) envision three
different scenarios for using IEEE 2030.5 to communicate with smart inverters: (1) direct inverter
communications, (2) inverter communications mediated by an Energy Management System controlling
the DER, and (3) inverter communications mediated by a DER operator/aggregator.

Other IEEE 2030.5 profiles include the CSIP-Australia (CSIP-AUS) and the IEEE 2030.5 V2G-AC
Profile. CSIP-AUS, which is likely the largest user of IEEE 2030.5, specifies a standardized minimum
communication protocol for monitoring and orchestration/active management of DERs in Australia. V2G-

the interactions between electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE, the equipment that delivers electricity
from the power grid to an electric vehicle’s battery) and a plug-in electric vehicle to allow the vehicle to
discharge to the electric grid.

3.3.2 Architecture

As an extension of a profile defined by the OASIS Energy Interoperation (EI), OpenADR 2.0 adopts EI’s
hierarchical architecture of virtual top nodes (VTNs) and virtual end nodes (VENs). All communication
occurs between a VTN and one or more VENs, and there is no peer-to-peer communication. That is,
VTNs do not communicate directly with other VTNs and, likewise, VENs do not communicate directly
with other VENs.

The functions mandated for OpenADR are a subset of those defined by EI. This standard includes many
services for collaborative and transactive use of energy, of which eight are applicable to OpenADR but
only four are used in OpenADR 2.0. These services are the (1) Event Service (for sending event
information), (2) Report Service (for defining and exchanging bidirectional reports), (3) Registration
Service (for in-band registration of VENs with VTNs), and (4) Opt Service (for communicating opt-in
and opt-out schedules from the VEN to the VTN).

The data model used in OpenADR is defined by the OpenADR Alliance. The model includes elements
from EI and related schemas, as well as extensions added by the Alliance. For interoperability purposes,
the specification defines a set of standard predefined signals and their attributes (although it is not a
requirement that a VTN or VEN support all the standard signals). These standard signals can be used to
communicate a variety of prices, dispatch instructions, and control settings. Specific deployments are also
free to define custom signals beyond those defined in the specification.

Supported transport mechanisms for the XML-formatted OpenADR 2.0 messages are simple HTTP
(using POST) and XMPP. These can be used in either a synchronous pull mode (initiated by the VEN) or
an asynchronous push mode (initiated by the VTN). The newer, more efficient OpenADR 3.0 uses the
REST architectural style and encodes its messages in JSON format.

 13

As with most application-level protocols, OpenADR uses common lower-level network cybersecurity
mechanisms without any modification. Two levels of security are defined for OpenADR 2.0: standard and
high. Standard security calls for the use of TLS 1.2, and both VENs and VTNs must use public key
infrastructure certificates to provide authentication, confidentiality, and integrity. The X.509 public key
infrastructure certificates must use either RSA cryptography (with minimum 256-bit keys) or elliptic
curve cryptography (with minimum 2,048-bit keys). The high security level adds XML signatures to
provide nonrepudiation of messages. For example, with high security a signed OpenADR event can be
stored in a database as evidence that an event was received. The newer, lighter-weight OpenADR 3.0 uses
TLS 1.3 and OAuth for authentication. Although these stringent security capabilities must be
implemented (and are tested during certification), the final selection of a security scheme (if any) is left to
the DR program operator (usually a utility or aggregator).

3.3.3   Tools

In 2014, EPRI released certified open-source implementations of OpenADR 2.0b software for both VENs
and VTNs. Although EPRI has stopped supporting the software (because of its use of deprecated third-
party software packages), it remains on GitHub6 as examples of certifiable OpenADR implementations.
LF Energy’s OpenLEADR project7 is developing an open-source Python 3 implementation of OpenADR
2.0b; the current version of the source code (which is not yet fully complete) can be found on GitHub8.

To assist companies in the development of OpenADR 2.0-compliant products, the OpenADR Alliance
and its partner, QualityLogic, have created a test harness that can be configured for various environments.
Its conformance checks can also be selectively disabled to facilitate testing of implementations with
known issues. This tool is also used by all Alliance test house partners for certification testing.

A Reference Implementation of the latest (November 2023) OpenADR 3.0 standard is available only to
OpenADR Alliance members. Members of the Alliance will also be able to request direct access to the
GitHub repository for OpenADR 3.0 once it is established.

3.3.4 Testing and Certification

OpenADR 2.0 products that have been through the OpenADR Alliance’s Certification Program can claim
OpenADR-certified status. The Alliance has partnered with several international test houses to provide
testing services, and only Alliance members are allowed to offer certified products. The Alliance
maintains a database of certified products on its website9 and manages the branding process to ensure
consistent and accurate use of the OpenADR Certified logo. Testing and certification has recently been
expanded to include OpenADR 3.0.

3.3.5 Status and Adoption

OpenADR has been approved as an international standard (IEC 62746-10-1) and has seen broad adoption,
particularly in coastal areas such as California (where it was created with funding from the Energy
Commission) and Japan. It is also being used in Colorado and elsewhere, and Duke Energy, DT Energy,
and several other utilities are now using it in managed electric vehicle (EV) charging programs.

OpenADR is also used when an open standard is desired for integrating a heterogeneous mix of devices,
such as in Bring Your Own Thermostat programs. Furthermore, OpenADR is required as part of some
other standards (such as AHRI’s 1380 standard for variable-speed HVAC equipment, Methods for
Coordinated Energy Management in Residential Applications). OpenADR has been required by the
California Public Utilities Commission for several years, and its use is mandated in California building

 14

codes. Japan has used OpenADR since 2013 in utility-to-aggregator communications for both DR and
DERs (photovoltaic [PV] curtailment).

Many OpenADR-capable products are available in the market. The OpenADR Alliance lists more than
300 certified products on its website. Because the targets for OpenADR are aggregated resources, not
individual devices, many of the certified products are gateways, controllers, and other types of energy
management systems. OpenADR can also communicate with vendor clouds to manage DR functionality
for devices such as connected thermostats.

4.2.3 MESA-DER DNP3 Data Model

The purpose of this standard is to define a profile that makes it easier to interconnect the DNP3 masters
and outstations that are used to control DERs while facilitating interoperability with existing standards
(e.g., IEC 61850-7-420). The data model maps the IEC 61850-7-420 object model for DERs to

control commands. The MESA-DER supports all the IEEE 1547 and California Rule 21 DER functions,
as well as additional market-based DER functions. In addition, the MESA-DER covers the data exchange
requirements for ESS configuration management, including ESS role-based access control for different
ESS operational states. The MESA-DER mapping is defined in DNP3 Application Note AN2018-001.

The new IEEE 1815.2 document establishes a standard profile consisting of a high-level data model for

base resource functions, including those found in IEEE 1547-2018, IEEE 2800-2022, and IEEE 1547.9-

detailed equipment-related data objects, primarily for logging and historical purposes.

other and with other operational components. The model is composed of three documents covering
communications with the three major components of an ESS: power conversion systems
(inverters/converters), battery storage, and meters.

The MESA-Device Model builds on SunSpec’s model-based framework. The model specifications are
available for download with SunSpec’s inverter, battery storage, and meter models.

4.3.2 Architecture

The SunSpec Modbus is an application-specific implementation of the de facto industry-standard Modbus
protocol that allows users to transmit information over serial lines. A Modbus “server” communicates

 20

with Modbus “clients.” In SunSpec Modbus, the server can be a control system, a site controller, or a
similar device. Clients can be meters, panels, or other devices.

A client maintains a block of data registers corresponding to specific data elements. In standard Modbus,
these registers are mapped to information at the discretion of the manufacturer or plant designer.
Although this is effective for site-specific installations, it makes interoperability difficult because the
same data might be in different registers in different products or installations. SunSpec Modbus
standardizes the meaning of these registers depending on the type of device and specific data represented.
The mapping was created by a group of industry stakeholders to provide a standardized approach to
inverter communications in the solar industry.

Devices are represented in SunSpec Modbus by a collection of information models. Supported device
categories include charge controllers, energy storage, environmental sensors, inverters, meters, PV
modules, string combiners, and trackers.

Generally, local DER systems contain one or more loggers, or gateways, that communicate with their
local devices (such as inverters and meters) using the SunSpec Modbus protocol. The loggers relay their
accumulated information to remote servers, which store the data permanently, perform analytics, and
communicate with other servers for reporting and other functions.

Thus, the SunSpec Modbus is a local-area protocol that connects a logger (gateway) to the components of
a DER system, including meters, inverters, controllers, and battery management systems. For wide-area
communications, utilities use software in the gateways to convert the information between the utility’s
preferred wide-area protocol and the SunSpec Modbus. Communication between loggers and servers (and
between servers) is usually conducted over the Internet using standard Internet protocols such as
HTTP/HTTPS. The IEEE 2030.5-2018 standard implements these protocols and is commonly used for
this application. Small differences between the SunSpec Modbus and the IEEE 2030.5 device information
models are documented in the SunSpec IEEE 2030.5 (SEP2) Implementation Guide.

Modbus itself provides no security against unauthorized commands or interception of data. The SunSpec
Modbus, which is similarly used on a LAN within the DER resource, also relies on physical security for
protection. Although the SunSpec Modbus has custom security features not found in standard Modbus,
most of the security features are likely to be implemented at the transport level and thus apply only to
wide-area networks. Furthermore, security will vary depending on the choice of transport layer. Modbus
over TCP supports TLS; however, not all manufacturers support it. Although wireless applications might
require special attention, Wi-Fi and ZigBee both support security at the network level. When the SunSpec
Modbus is deployed over IEEE 2030.5 for wide-area communications, the cybersecurity capabilities of
IEEE 2030.5’s IP-based networking are available.

5.1.2 Architecture

CTA-2045 was created to provide a single, standardized interface for SGDs. Because all the connectivity
features are implemented in the UCM, SGD manufacturers need only design, manufacture, and distribute
equipment with one standard communication capability, regardless of the network to which a device will
eventually be connected. This protects both buyers and manufacturers from obsolescence as new
networks and protocols emerge and allows equipment to be switched between different programs merely
by replacing the UCM.

The CTA-2045 standard defines two form factors for the twelve-pin/two-row connector, depending on the
voltages with which it is to be used. For operation at line voltages or higher-power networks (such as
powerline carrier), the AC form factor socket and UCMs are used. A more compact socket and plug
combination, operating at 3 VDC—the DC form factor—is used for lower-power RF networks.

CTA-2045 is commonly used to connect to residential loads (such as appliances), inverters, and solar
DERs. The information models include load information, power levels, etc., as well as higher level grid
controls that allow a site management system, owner, or utility to manage the device via abstract signals,
such as grid status and energy prices. Functional requirements for specific devices have been published
for EVSE, variable-speed pool pumps, thermostats, and water heaters (both heat pump and resistive).
Requirements for variable-speed HVAC equipment are included in the AHRI 1380 standard.

A CTA-2045 implementation includes the physical/media access layers (the AC or DC form-factor
interfaces), a data link layer,2 and basic DR messages at the network and application layers. It also has the
capability of passing unmodified messages from other network protocols (such as IEEE 2030.5 or
OpenADR) through to the SGD.

CTA-2045 is intended for use in closed environments (where owners have physical control of the
network) and does not itself specify any cybersecurity. The physical interface between a UCM and the
SGD supports the existing end-to-end security of the application layer and/or the IP/network layer but
does not add any additional encryption at the link layer. Basic CTA-2045 messages are not encrypted, and
the socket interface is treated as a protected local interface (like any other wired connection within a
device). If the external communications network is encrypted, as it might be for a Wi-Fi or Zigbee HAN,
for example, the encrypted packets might be decrypted in the UCM or be passed through the interface for
decryption by the SGD (if supported).

Electronics Show in Las Vegas. Both Washington and Oregon have adopted CTA-2045 mandates to
improve energy efficiency and grid management. Washington now requires all new electric storage water
heaters to have a CTA-2045 communications port, and since July 2023, Oregon has required new heat
pumps and electric resistance water heaters to support CTA-2045.

CTA-2045 is also required in AHRI’s 1430 standard for demand-flexible electric storage water heaters
and is one of two communications options in AHRI’s 1380 standard for variable-speed HVAC

Wi-Fi AC and DC form-factor UCMs are available, as is a DC form-factor UCM for FM radio

Matter is a new local-area connectivity standard for local-area network communication between smart

5.2.2 Architecture

Managed by the Connectivity Standards Alliance, formerly the Zigbee Alliance, Matter is closely related
to the Zigbee SEP 2.0 specification. SEP 2.0 defines an IP-based communication protocol to monitor,
control, inform, and automate the delivery and use of energy and water. SEP 2.0 relies on Zigbee IP, a
network layer that routes standard IPv6 traffic over IEEE 802.15.4 using 6LowPAN header compression.
Matter introduces a new application-layer protocol in which messages are transported over either TCP or
UDP running on IPv6. The network can use Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or Thread (which runs over IEEE 802.15.4).

Although Zigbee and Matter use similar underlying data models and some of the same underlying
network technology, they are nevertheless different. Thread-based devices support the same 802.15.4
radio technology as Zigbee but use IP-based technology for the network stack. At the upper levels of the
stack and specification, Matter’s data model borrows heavily from the Zigbee Cluster Library and
Alliance Data Model. However, there is no native interoperability between Matter and Zigbee devices.
Consequently, Matter includes specifications for bridging between Matter and non-Matter devices, such
as Zigbee or proprietary protocols.

Matter and SEP 2.0 both emphasize interoperability, energy efficiency, security, and mesh networking.
However, Matter is designed as a universal protocol for a wide range of smart devices, while SEP 2.0 is
specifically tailored for energy management applications. Also, Matter is more natively IP-based, while
Zigbee integrates IPv6 through specific adaptations.

Matter’s main selling point is compatibility: it theoretically lets a user buy any device and control it with
any platform. Rather than relying on a hub or other physical device, Matter achieves this compatibility by
defining a standard protocol suite.

The functional building blocks of the Matter data model are called clusters. A cluster specification defines
client and server sides that communicate with each other through interactions. A cluster, which can be
thought of as an interface, service, or object class, is the lowest independent functional element in the data
model. Each cluster is based on a specification that defines its elements, including attributes, events,
commands, and behaviors associated with interactions with these elements.

The Matter Application Cluster Specification defines generic interfaces that are of use across a wide
range of application domains. The specification is a repository for cluster functionality. A developer
constructing a new application can use the Matter Device Library Specification to find relevant cluster
functionality that can be incorporated into the new application.

Matter designates a core set of security primitives to provide comprehensive protection. Elliptic curve
cryptography, based on the NIST P-256 curve, serves as the foundation for public key cryptography and

 26

digital signatures. Commonly available Advanced Encryption Standard3 modes of operation are used to
provide shared key cryptographic operations. In scenarios involving out-of-band passcode-based
authentication, Matter uses an augmented Password-Authenticated Key Exchange algorithm.

All unicast node-to-node messages are secured, authenticated, and provide replay protection. Building on
top of IPv6 multicast, Matter also provides group messaging facilities that prioritize low latency packet
processing.

The Matter standard is maintained by the Connectivity Standards Alliance, which rebranded itself from
the Zigbee Alliance in 2021. This organization now has more than 550 member companies, including
Amazon, Apple, Google, LG, Samsung, and Schneider.

5.2.3    Tools

An open-source GitHub repository14 contains Matter’s security foundations, message framing and
dispatch, and implementation of the interaction and data models. It includes code examples that show
simple interactions supported on multiple platforms and transports (Wi-Fi and Thread).

Alliance members also have access to tools for precertification testing. The test harness is open source
and publicly available; support for the tool is available for members. The harness is designed to ensure
compliance with Alliance standards and to reduce time to market.

Members can use the open-source SDK as a reference or starting point for implementing Matter. It
contains examples for different platforms that implement Matter in different ways. The SDK
documentation15 helps members become familiar with Matter enablement, and members use the SDK to
builds their own products. The Matter Handbook16 allows readers to become familiar with the different
components involved in Matter development.

For Alexa integration developers, Amazon offers a Matter Analytics Console that provides performance
metrics such as latency and success rate. These metrics are indications of the quality-of-service customers
experience when interacting with their devices using Alexa.

5.2.4 Testing and Certification

Product certification is conducted by authorized third-party test houses17. Alliance members can access
online tools for completing and submitting certification materials. The Alliance’s centralized process
allows vendors to certify their devices once with the Matter ecosystem, rather than requiring certification
with each smart home platform.

The Connectivity Standards Alliance maintains the official list of Matter-certified products and restricts
use of the Matter logo to certified devices. Device manufacturers must put data for their products into the
Distributed Compliance Ledger (DCL) database. This database has information about the sets of product
models that have been certified. Once a product has been certified and is on the market, it will be listed in
the DCL and queried when commissioning a device into the Matter network (fabric) to confirm the device
is certified.

The IEC 61968 series is intended to facilitate inter-application integration, as opposed to intra-application
integration of the various distributed software application systems supporting the management of utility
electrical distribution networks. Intra-application integration is aimed at programs in the same application
system, usually communicating with each other using middleware that is embedded in their underlying
runtime environment.

Additionally, intra-application integration tends to be optimized for close, real-time, synchronous
connections and interactive request/reply or conversation communication models. By contrast, the IEC
61968 series is intended to support the inter-application integration of a utility enterprise that needs to
connect disparate applications that are already built or new (legacy or purchased applications) and each
supported by dissimilar runtime environments.

languages, operating systems, protocols, and management tools. The IEC 61968 series is intended to
support applications that need to exchange data on an event-driven basis. The series is also intended to be
implemented with middleware services that broker messages among applications and to complement, but
not replace, utility data warehouses, database gateways, and operational stores.

8.1.2 Architecture

IEC 61850’s original applications (“Communication Networks and Systems in Substations”) focused on
support of real-time communications using the Generic Object-Oriented Substation Events (GOOSE4)
control model over Ethernet to provide high speed (sub-4 msec) communications within a substation. But
the standard has moved well beyond this specialized domain. Although IEC 61850 for substations is on
the rise and its deployment is broadening, it is also having broader impact through other paths. For
example, the device models defined in IEC 61850 have been influential in shaping several other DR and
DER communications and control schemes, including the SunSpec Modbus, IEEE 1815, and IEEE
2030.5.

4 The GOOSE acronym has evolved and is now interpreted to mean Generic Object-Oriented System Events.

 42

Documentation of IEC 61850 contains many parts, including IEC 61850-8, which contains the service
mapping to MMS (for messaging over TCP/IP transports), and IEC 61850-7-420, which describes the
data exchange between DER equipment (generators and storage) and systems that supervise, control,
maintain, or generally use or operate this equipment.

IEC 61850 is commonly used to control substation equipment, synchrophasors, and PV inverters.
Because of its extensive set of device information models (containing load information, power levels,
etc.), it has been implemented in modified form by other protocols. However, such implementations
usually modify the models sufficiently to preclude direct interoperability between them. For example,
SunSpec has used the electrical measurements defined in the IEC 61850 MMXU logical node to name
point IDs mapped to inverter Modbus registers. Thus, “PPV.phsCA” (in 61850) is called “PPVphCA” (in
SunSpec), while “A.phsA” (61850) is called “AphA” (SunSpec).

IEC 61850 was originally intended for use in closed substation networks. When used with wide-area
systems, OSI network transports were specified. Operation of IEC 61850 over Internet protocols has been
achieved by mapping IEC 61850 to the TCP/IP version of MMS.

As with other industrial protocols, IEC 61850 was not designed with security in mind. As long as its use
was confined to a substation LAN, this posed no problems. As its role has expanded, however, so have its
need for effective cybersecurity. Implementation of TLS encryption for MMS is provided by the IEC
62351-4 standard, and connections can be authenticated, encrypted, and/or signed to ensure integrity of
the communication. However, implementations of TLS for IEC 61850 can vary because some of the
details of IEC 62351-4 are subject to interpretation.

The IEC 61850 information models include mode commands (on/off, volt-VAR settings, ramp rates, etc.)
and schedules (both recurring and one-time events). Economic signals include absolute prices, tariff tiers,
and qualitative values (high/medium/low). As an IEC application-layer protocol, IEC 61850 was designed
to run over the OSI networking stack. Since OSI networking is not broadly supported around the world, a
mapping of the protocol to MMS is used to allow the messages to be transported over TCIP/IP networks.

8.1.4 Testing and Certification

Part 10 of the IEC 61850 standard covers conformance testing. It specifies standard techniques for testing
the conformance of client, server, and sampled value devices and engineering tools, as well as specific
measurement techniques to be applied when declaring performance parameters.

Several companies provide IEC 61850 test tools, and more than a dozen test labs around the world are
approved for testing IEC 61850 compatibility. More than 1,500 certified devices are cataloged on UCA’s
certification site.37

Omicron supplies tools for monitoring IEC 61850 communications. The IEDScout38 software uses the
device discovery features of IEC 61850 to read and display the data model of any IEC 61850 server, as
well as to test data reporting and command functions. IEDScout can also be used to simulate an intelligent
electronic device (IED) by reading an IEC 61850 SCL file. The company also produces a benchtop
testing automation device called StationScout.39

8.1.5 Status and Adoption

IEC 61850 has seen broad adoption in Europe, particularly in substations, and this use is of growing
importance in the United States and around the world. The role of IEC 61850 in DER control is more
nuanced: in Europe, native IEC 61850 is used, but elsewhere, the standard makes its impact “behind the
scenes.” Utilities that prefer their DNP3 networks for DER management can use IEEE 1815, while for
California’s Rule 21, IEEE 2030.5 is the default protocol and the SunSpec Modbus is the required
inverter interface. The device models used by IEEE 1815, IEEE 2030.5, and the SunSpec Modbus are all
based on the models in IEC 61850.

Object models for a comprehensive list of grid devices are defined in IEC 61850. Currently available
products that directly incorporate IEC 61850 are either controllers of the sort that would be deployed in
substations or grid control applications: IEDs, circuit breakers, relays, tap-changing transformers, etc.
Available inverters mostly employ the SunSpec Modbus, which is based on a modified version of the IEC
61850 inverter device model.

 APPENDIX B. INFORMATION LAYERS
One of the concepts that makes an apples-to-apples comparison of communication standards difficult is
that not every standard is, in fact, an “apple.” The ISO/International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)
7498 standard’s “Open Systems Interconnection–Basic Reference Model”40, or Model, describes
communications by placing them into abstract layers. The OSI Model is especially useful when
classifying low-level communications but does not provide a way to describe how the data that is
exchanged is defined or how elements interrelate.

In 2008, the GridWise Architecture Council (GWAC) published the “Interoperability Context-Setting
Framework,”41 essentially pushing the subtleties of the OSI models into a few lower levels and
introducing multiple higher layers to describe semantics, process, and policy information. Although
helpful, this model is not the best for the comparison that is necessary for this work, as the
communication standards examined here are primarily in the “middle” of the GWAC classification
system. Therefore, this comparison uses the Smart Grid Architecture Model (SGAM), published through
the IEC’s System Committee for Smart Energy,42 as it provides a simpler breakdown of the classification
layers that are more aligned with the “fuzzy” nature of protocols today.

Most of the protocol standards described in this analysis fall into the “communication” layer. Most can
operate over an Internet protocol–based “component” implementation; however, other protocols are noted
when available or required. Importantly, the most robust communication models also have an associated
“information” layer that describes the semantic understanding of the data being exchanged. Going beyond
a simple glossary of definitions, and often employing a modeling language such as the Unified Modeling
Language, the semantic understanding of a standard defines the meaning behind the data exchange
inherent in the modeling itself, even if not explicitly defined in a single message exchange.

Figure 1 of this document illustrates a four-level protocol model, with the information and
communication levels operating on top of the exchange and network levels. The exchange level, which is
part of the application layer in models like OSI, describes the logical interoperation between the

 A-1

communicating entities and includes architectures such as client/server, peer-to-peer, and
publish/subscribe.

The network level is subdivided into six distinct layers in the OSI model, but such detail is not necessary
for understanding the characteristics and features of the protocols discussed in this report. A simpler
construct groups these six layers into three: (1) transport (which can packetize and reassemble the
application-layer payloads in a manner that guarantees their error-free, lossless, and properly sequenced
transmission), (2) network (which ensures that the packets are routed to the proper recipients), and (3)
link/media (which formats and places the messages onto the physical media).

Note that the protocols in this report operate at the application layer and have little interaction with the
lower network layers. Nevertheless, the cybersecurity of these protocols is almost completely provided by
the underlying transport service. Although the application protocol standards themselves do not usually
define new cybersecurity features, they often mandate (or suggest) which features and settings are to be
used with the cybersecurity capabilities of the transport layer. These services can encrypt the message
payloads, ensuring that they are neither read nor modified as they pass through the network.

 APPENDIX C. HOW TO USE THE STANDARDS LANDSCAPE TO
 SOLVE REAL-WORLD CHALLENGES
The Standards Landscape is a tool intended to help those who need to exchange electric utility data and
want to make those exchanges as efficient as possible while simultaneously minimizing the opportunities
for data misinterpretation. The Standards Landscape is—and always will be—incomplete, insofar as new
standards will need to be analyzed and existing standards evolve over time. That said, the current
assessment does not cover the existing standards fully, so this appendix should be understood to be
illustrative. Technical requirements, including security and delivery speed, should be considered, and
once factored into the picture the business-level recommendations that can be derived from the Standards
Landscape can be reprioritized.

This guide demonstrates how some sample business challenges can be explored using the Standards
Landscape to determine which standards could be considered optimal solutions. In the subsequent
sections, several challenges are documented. Each challenge focuses on at least one, but likely several,
data domains. Once identified, the Standards Landscape can then be used to correlate which standards are
best suited to support those domains.

B.1 DISTRIBUTION UTILITY NEEDS TO PUBLISH A SIMPLIFIED GRID MODEL

B.1.1 Challenge

A distribution utility has been asked by its regulator to publish grid information like current and projected
capacities so that developers can more intelligently make investment decisions about where to site local
generation, storage, and/or load resources.

B.1.2 Data Domains

The information to support this challenge is squarely in the “grid domain.” There is nothing related to
flexibility services (although those might be enabled by such as data exchange), nor is information needed
to be delivered from or to devices.

Within the grid domain, the Grid Topology subdomain would be needed to describe how the grid
assembled from its constituent element. Since this would be a public data model, many of the data objects
could be ignored. For example, protection equipment might not be relevant or necessary to share, and
many of the attributes associated with an object could be hidden or otherwise obscured for security
reasons.

Additionally, Grid Behavior is needed, especially around the limits of which different circuits can
operate, as well as Grid State data. For this challenge, the current state (i.e., real-time snapshot) is not so
helpful; instead, extreme cases such as a peak load or a minimum generation case would be helpful to
understand the different kinds of operational scenarios that can appear. Grid Constraints, if known,
might also be a part of the scope.

B.1.3 Candidate Standards

The Standards Landscape tells us that IEC 61960 (Common Information Model [CIM] Grid Profiles)
fully support these data domains. Additionally, MultiSpeak profiles should have much, if not all, of the
necessary data elements defined. Guidance would be to perform a deeper assessment into these two

 C-1

options to determine the best solution for the stakeholders, which include the utility itself plus developers
who are active in the area interconnecting new generation, new storage, and/or new loads.

B.2 DEMAND RESPONSE PROGRAM FOR FEEDER MANAGEMENT

B.2.1 Challenge

A utility has decided to introduce a new demand response program to help alleviate potential overloads on
local distribution circuits rather than upgrade substations, which would increase costs to all customers. To
make the program even more cost-effective, the utility has decided to send out technology-independent
messages to interact with resource operators, including aggregators, technology providers, or even large
customers. Those parties would then control devices on behalf of the device owners (if not themselves)
using technologies of their choice.

B.2.2 Data Domains

Data domains for this challenge are focused primarily on the Optimization domain, particularly since the
implementation is intended to be device independent, ruling out most of the necessary support from the
Device domain. Negotiation (bid, offers, contracts) and Fulfillment are likely essential subdomains, with
the potential need to define services in a message (leveraging the Services subdomain) and/or
Compensation subdomain, if financial incentives are to be conveyed.

B.2.3 Candidate Standards

Two CIM-related standards could apply here: (1) IEC 62325 if the services are aligned more with
transmission, but more likely (2) IEC 62746 because the challenge is to support local/distribution
management. OpenADR and IEEE 2030.5 could also support the use case, and, as always, the
implementor should look in-depth at the option to select the most appropriate of this subset for adoption.

B.3 PROGRAM SMART INVERTERS WITH PROTECTION SETTING

B.3.1 Challenge

The operational modes and protection settings established in IEEE 1547 have become the de facto
standard for the United Standard, and the formal standard for many states that have mandated its use. The
regulator in one of these states is interested in providing guidance on how the information document in
the IEEE 1547 standard can be reliably programmed into smart inverters across multiple utilities,
including very small cooperates and several municipal utilities in the state.

B.3.2 Data Domains

The challenge is focused on the device domain. Specifically, it focuses on pushing settings information
into smart inverters, which is supported by data in the Configuration subdomain. If the state decides to
have more dynamic switching between or among settings, this could also involve the conditions
subdomain, which supports data exchange in time-domains closer to real time, as opposed to
Configuration messages, which could be performed at the time of interconnection or in batch over longer
periods if settings are changed.

B.3.3 Candidate Standards

Looking at the standards reviewed the current Standard Landscape report, IEEE 2030.5 and IEC 61850-7-
420 are possible solutions, with IEC 61850 having a slightly higher rating. Note that this assessment does
not account for the native support for IEEE 1547 (in fact, both standards claim the ability to transfer these
data), but notes the standard is designed for this type of information. Although most standards were
analyzed in the other domains, there are many more standards (shown in green in Figure 1) that are
planned to be addressed in future versions of the Standards Landscape; thus, this conclusion should not be
considered to be exhaustive.
