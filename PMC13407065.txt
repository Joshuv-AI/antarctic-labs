Predictive Modeling of the Need for Tracheostomy after Traumatic Cervical Spinal Cord Injury Using Machine Learning
Authors: Eun-Ji Lee, Byeongkeun Kwon, Suhyeon Kim, Sung Hyeon Noh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTraumatic cervical spinal cord injury (TCSCI) frequently necessitates mechanical ventilation, and early tracheostomy has been shown to reduce complications in patients requiring prolonged ventilation. This study aimed to develop a machine learning model to predict the need for tracheostomy in TCSCI patients, utilizing early clinical data to improve patient outcomes.Materials and MethodsThis study was conducted using data from 2017 to 2024, obtained from the single institution database. A total of 267 TCSCI patients were included, of whom 49 underwent tracheostomy. Variables selected for the model included demographics, comorbidities, injury level, medical research council motor grading, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and treatment details. This study also implemented SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis to interpret the predictive model and identify significant risk predictors contributing to the outcomes.ResultsThe CatBoost model outperformed other models, achieving the highest performance metrics. The model that incorporated GCS and the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) impairment scale (AIS) yielded the highest area under the curve (AUC) score. Following variable selection, the CatBoost model, utilizing age, GCS, AIS score, surgery, and injury levels (C3/4, C4/5, and C2/3), achieved an AUC score of 0.8166 and an accuracy of 0.8652.ConclusionOur machine learning model effectively predicted the need for tracheostomy in TCSCI patients. Important predictors were age, GCS, AIS score, cervical surgery, and injury level. This model may improve outcomes for patients requiring tracheostomy.Keywords: Traumatic cervical spinal cord injury, tracheostomy, machine learning

INTRODUCTION

In the United States, approximately 17000 patients annually experience spinal cord injuries (SCI),1 with respiratory complications being prevalent in this population. Traumatic cervical spinal cord injury (TCSCI) disrupts the neural pathways that innervate the diaphragm, intercostal muscles, and accessory muscles, leading to an impaired ability to cough and clear respiratory secretions, which can result in acute respiratory failure.2 Consequently, patients with SCI often require mechanical ventilation due to difficulties in spontaneous breathing.

A tracheostomy is a surgical intervention performed to secure the airway in patients with acute respiratory failure by creating an opening in the trachea, thereby facilitating prolonged mechanical ventilation.3 In the management of TCSCI, the need for tracheostomy frequently arises, necessitating careful assessment.

The timing of tracheostomy has been a topic of considerable debate within the medical community. Early tracheostomy has been associated with a reduction in complications in patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation. In patients with TCSCI, performing early tracheostomy within 4 days post-injury has been linked to fewer respiratory complications, shorter durations of mechanical ventilation, and reduced lengths of stay in both the intensive care unit and the hospital, regardless of the level of injury.4 Despite the importance of tracheostomy in TCSCI management, clear indications for its use remain undetermined.

A decision-making tool based on patient data at the time of hospital admission could offer clinicians valuable guidance, alleviating uncertainty in determining the need for tracheostomy following TCSCI. Early tracheostomy decisions could potentially reduce post-TCSCI complications, lower morbidity rates, and improve the cost-efficiency of care for these patients. Previous studies have developed clinical tracheostomy scores to assist clinicians in decision-making, with statistical significance tests used to identify key variables such as the injury severity score (ISS), motor deficits in C1-C4 injuries, and the presence of complete or anterior cord injuries from C1-C7.5 However, these scoring systems are limited by their reliance on a narrow set of variables and deterministic models, which reduce their clinical applicability.

In this study, we propose a machine learning-based predictive model to assess the need for tracheostomy following TCSCI. By leveraging artificial intelligence, we comprehensively analyzed patient data to develop an automated system that can aid clinicians in making informed tracheostomy decisions, ultimately improving clinical outcomes in the management of TCSCI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This research was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ajou University (AJOUIRB-CT-2024-135).

Patient population

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of 799 patients with TCSCI, sourced from a single-institution database between 2017 and 2024. The inclusion criteria encompassed 267 patients admitted to our hospital with TCSCI, characterized by radiologically significant cervical spinal cord signal changes, and a total of 511 patients with traumatic TCSCI. Radiological significance was determined by the attending radiologist. Exclusion criteria included 19 patients who died within 7 days, 41 patients without a radiologically significant cervical spinal cord signal change, and 184 patients with incomplete data. Specifically, data for the medical research council (MRC) scale and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) were the most frequently missing variables due to the clinical prioritization of immediate life-saving treatment over neurological assessments at the time of admission. Given that missing values in GCS and MRC scores were primarily attributable to emergent clinical conditions at admission, the assumption of missing at random was unlikely to hold. Therefore, multiple imputation was not applied, and a complete-case analysis was performed to avoid introducing model-based bias. Consequently, 267 patients were included in the final analysis, of whom 49 had undergone tracheostomy. A flowchart depicting the study population is presented in Fig. 1.

Study execution process

Data collection

A total of 20 covariates were selected as candidates for analysis, informed by previous studies investigating the factors influencing the necessity and timing of tracheostomy.5,6 These covariates encompassed demographics, comorbidities, treatment type, motor grade, mental status, diagnosis, and tracheostomy status. All data were extracted from electronic medical records. Demographic variables included patient age and sex. Comorbidities were characterized by the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, and injury-related haemothorax. Treatment type was categorized into cervical surgery and conservative treatment, such as bracing or pharmacotherapy, and included only treatments administered during the initial hospital admission. Motor function was assessed retrospectively using the MRC scale, which evaluated strength in the right arm, left arm, right leg, and left leg, denoted as MRCRtarm, MRCLtarm, MRCRtleg, and MRCLtleg, respectively. Additionally, motor and sensory function were evaluated using the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) impairment scale (AIS). Mental status was measured via the GCS, with individual scores for eye, verbal, and motor responses (denoted as GCSEye, GCSVerbal, and GCSMotor), as well as the total GCS score. Motor and mental status data were collected based on the patient’s initial presentation upon hospitalisation. The type of injury was defined using diagnostic categories and corresponding codes. Primary diagnoses included injury of the cervical spinal cord not otherwise specified, other and unspecified cervical SCI, central cord syndrome, and sequelae of spinal cord injury. Relevant ICD codes included S14.1, S14.1A, S14.1B, T06.1, T09.3, and T91.3. The anatomic injury level was specified based on cervical spine MRI and CT scan findings, identifying affected regions such as C1/2, C2/3, C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, C6/7, and C7/T1. Injury localisation was primarily determined by radiological reports indicating areas of signal change, as documented in both the electronic medical records and the original radiologic imaging. The primary outcome was the requirement for tracheostomy following TCSCI.

Prediction models and evaluation

In this study, we developed predictive models to assess the need for tracheostomy. Prior to model construction, preprocessing was performed on all categorical variables using either label encoding or one-hot encoding. For instance, the diagnosis code (i.e., ICD-10 code for diagnosis) was encoded using one-hot encoding, while the AIS variable was encoded via label encoding, ordered from most to least severe impairment as follows: complete (4), sensory incomplete (3), motor incomplete (muscle strength MRC less than 3) (2), motor Incomplete (muscle strength MRC 3 or greater) (1), and normal (0).

We designed multiple experiments using seven distinct sets of independent variables to predict the risk of tracheostomy (Supplementary Table 1, only online). In setting up the experimental framework, we first considered the high correlations and dependencies between derived variables (i.e., GCS and AIS) and the variables used to calculate them (i.e., GCSEye, GCSVerbal, and GCSMotor for GCS and MRCRtarm, MRCLtarm, MRCRtleg, and MRCLtleg for AIS). Since entering these derived and source variables together into machine learning models could increase misclassification errors, we conducted a series of experiments by inputting them separately into the models. Specifically, for Experiments 1-4, we focused on combinations of GCS-related and motor grade-related variables. Furthermore, we categorized variables according to their objective or subjective nature. Objective variables were defined as measurable and externally verifiable factors that did not rely on clinical judgement. In contrast, subjective variables were determined based on clinician assessment or diagnostic decisions and could vary according to individual interpretation. Objective variables included age, sex, comorbidities, AIS, anatomic injury level, and cervical surgery. Subjective variables, such as GCS, MRC scale, diagnosis, and diagnosis code, were determined by clinician judgement and diagnosis.7,8,9 In Experiment 5, only objective variables were used. In Experiments 6 and 7, subjective variables were combined with GCS-related variables.

Six supervised learning algorithms were employed to develop the prediction model: a traditional statistical approach, logistic regression (LR), and five machine learning models: Categorical Boost (CatBoost),10 eXtremely Gradient Boosting (XGBoost),11 Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM),12 Random Forest,13 and Support Vector Machine (SVM).14 The hyperparameters for each model are provided in Supplementary Tables 2-6 (only online). Notably, CatBoost was particularly suitable for this study as it is optimized for handling categorical data, which aligns with the characteristics of the dataset. Additionally, the generalisation performance of all models was assessed using 5-fold cross-validation (CV). The dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal subsets (i.e., 5 folds), with each prediction model trained on four subsets and tested on the remaining one (Supplementary Table 7, only online). This process was repeated across all folds, and the average performance was reported. Specifically, stratified 5-fold CV was applied to ensure the training and test sets maintained a balanced proportion of patients with tracheostomy.

To provide a robust evaluation, several key metrics commonly used in medical data analysis were utilized: accuracy, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). To enhance model accuracy and eliminate redundant variables, variable selection was performed by evaluating changes in model performance when variables were added or removed during training.15 Specifically, a backward selection method, which removes predictors deemed to have low importance, was employed as a representative variable selection approach.

SHAP value analysis

In this study, Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, derived from cooperative game theory, were employed to interpret the optimal predictive model and identify significant risk predictors contributing to the outcomes.16 SHAP values were computed by considering all possible combinations of variables and assessing the change in the model’s prediction when a given variable was introduced. This method quantifies the importance of each variable for a specific prediction and provides a consistent approach to attributing the contribution of each variable. Unlike feature importance extracted from machine learning models, which only measures the magnitude of importance, SHAP values can be represented as both positive and negative figures, similar to the coefficients in statistical models. For instance, a positive SHAP value indicates that the variable increases the prediction, whereas a negative SHAP value signifies that the variable decreases the prediction.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. To compare patient characteristics between the tracheostomy group and the no-tracheostomy group, the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) was used for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Patient demographics

A total of 267 patients were analyzed, with demographic characteristics presented in Table 1. Of the 267 patients, 18.3% underwent tracheostomy following TCSCI. Patients who required tracheostomy had significantly lower GCS and MRC scores compared to those who were discharged without tracheostomy (p<0.001). Additionally, among the 49 patients with tracheostomy, 12 (24.5%) experienced haemothorax, a significantly higher incidence compared to 14 (6.4%) of 218 patients without tracheostomy (p<0.001). The likelihood of tracheostomy was significantly higher in patients with AIS injury and in those with injury located at the C2/3 or C3/4 levels (p<0.001, p<0.001, and p=0.041, respectively). Furthermore, 44 patients (89.8%) who required tracheostomy also underwent cervical surgery.

Performance of prediction models

We conducted seven experiments using 5-fold CV based on six statistical and machine learning models. The predictive performance of each model was assessed in terms of accuracy, F1-score, AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, and AUPRC, with results reported as the 95% confidence interval (CI) (Table 2). Among the experiments, the highest AUROC was observed in Experiment 4, where total GCS and AIS were included, and the CatBoost model demonstrated the best performance. Specifically, Experiments 1–4, which included combinations of GCS-related and motor grade-related variables, outperformed Experiments 5–7. In Experiment 4, the AUROC for CatBoost was 0.7867 (95% CI: 0.7136–0.8548), followed by LR (0.7710; 95% CI: 0.6917–0.8456), LGBM (0.7708; 95% CI: 0.6936–0.8379), Random Forest (0.7693; 95% CI: 0.6938–0.8411), XGBoost (0.7575; 95% CI: 0.6798–0.8327), and SVM (0.7165; 95% CI: 0.6226–0.8079). Consequently, the CatBoost model from Experiment 4 was selected as the final model due to its superior AUROC. The ROC curve for the CatBoost model predicting the need for tracheostomy in Experiment 4 is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 (only online).

Performance of the prediction model with variable selection

Prior to conducting model-based variable selection through backward elimination, we evaluated the impact of two types of variables, diagnosis code and diagnosis, on model performance. Both variables had undergone one-hot encoding, which was found to have minimal effect on the predictive model. Upon removing these variables, we observed improvements across all performance metrics, most notably an increase in AUROC from 0.7867 (95% CI: 0.7136–0.8548) to 0.7961 (95% CI: 0.7242–0.8618) (Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Fig. 2, only online). This improvement may reflect reduced noise or redundancy introduced by high-dimensional encoding of diagnosis-related variables that overlap with other clinical features. Subsequently, feature importance was recalculated, and backward elimination was applied. The results, including accuracy, F1-score, and AUROC, are presented in Table 3. After eliminating 10 variables through backward elimination, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.8652 (95% CI: 0.8202–0.9064), an F1-score of 0.5135 (95% CI: 0.3662–0.6437), and an AUROC of 0.8166 (95% CI: 0.7463–0.8813). This reflects a 2.57% improvement in AUROC compared to the initial CatBoost model, which had an AUROC of 0.7961 (95% CI: 0.7242–0.8618) when all variables were included. The final, best-performing model was defined as the CatBoost model using age, GCS, AIS, surgery, C3/4, C4/5, and C2/3 as independent predictors for tracheostomy prediction. Supplementary Fig. 3 (only online) presents the ROC curve of the final CatBoost model.

SHAP value analysis

Fig. 2 presents the results of the SHAP value analysis, illustrating the contribution and significance of each feature in predicting the likelihood of tracheostomy. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 2A) depicts the individual SHAP values for each feature, highlighting both the magnitude and direction of their effects. For example, higher AIS scores (indicated in red) are associated with positive SHAP values, demonstrating that more severe injuries substantially increase the probability of tracheostomy. In contrast, lower GCS scores (shown in blue), which reflect more severe neurological impairment, are linked to positive SHAP values, similarly indicating a higher likelihood of tracheostomy. Fig. 2B-D provide a detailed breakdown of each feature’s impact. SHAP values tend to be negative for individuals under 40 years of age, suggesting a lower probability of tracheostomy in this population, approach zero between 40 and 60 years, and increase sharply after age 60, reflecting a higher probability of tracheostomy in older adults. AIS, which categorises injury severity, demonstrates that higher scores elevate the likelihood of tracheostomy, whereas lower scores reduce it. Conversely, higher GCS values, indicative of less severe impairment, correlate with a lower probability of tracheostomy. The probability of tracheostomy increases with impairments at the C2/3, C3/4, and C4/5 spinal levels, with a further increase observed when surgical intervention is performed (Supplementary Fig. 4, only online).

External validation

External validation was performed using an independent cohort that was not involved in model development or internal CV. This external dataset consisted of 92 patients who were treated at our institution but whose key imaging studies required for neurological assessment, particularly MRI used for determining injury level and calculating GCS-related variables, had been acquired at outside hospitals prior to transfer (Supplementary Table 9, only online). As a result, although the common clinical variables and data structure were identical to those of the training cohort, the external dataset reflected heterogeneity in imaging acquisition protocols and inter-institutional assessment environments. This setting was considered clinically meaningful, as it simulates real-world referral scenarios commonly encountered in tertiary trauma centers. The final CatBoost model, trained on the original single-institution dataset, was applied to this external cohort without any additional retraining or parameter tuning. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUROC, and AUPRC. To account for sampling variability, 95% CIs for all performance metrics were estimated. The results are summarized in Table 4. Notably, no substantial performance drop was observed in the external validation cohort, indicating consistent model behavior across datasets.

DISCUSSION

In patients with TCSCI, tracheostomy is required in 21%–77% of cases.17 Our analysis identified key predictors for the need for tracheostomy, including age, AIS score, cervical surgery, GCS, and the anatomical level of injury. Notably, these predictors differ from those identified by Scantling, et al.,5 whose clinical tracheostomy score incorporated factors such as ISS ≥38, motor injury at C1-C4, and complete or anterior cord injury from C1 to C7, excluding GCS and age.

The relationship between age and the requirement for tracheostomy in TCSCI patients varies across studies. Lee, et al.18 identified age ≥55 years as a significant risk factor for tracheostomy following TCSCI, consistent with our findings, which demonstrated a positive SHAP value for patients aged over 60. However, Branco, et al.,19 who dichotomized patients into groups younger and older than 55, found no significant association between age and tracheostomy requirement in this population. In our study, age emerged as a significant predictor for tracheostomy, likely due to the slower recovery and rehabilitation of respiratory muscles in older individuals, leading to prolonged mechanical ventilation.

Lee, et al.18 and Branco, et al.19 demonstrated that patients with a GCS score of ≤8 are more likely to undergo tracheostomy, with both studies dichotomising GCS scores into groups below and above 8. In contrast, our study analyzed GCS as a continuous variable and found a similar trend: as GCS decreases, the probability of tracheostomy increases, as evidenced by positive SHAP values. GCS serves as an indicator of head injury severity by reflecting the impairment of eye, verbal, and motor responses. A low GCS score implies a more severe injury, often indicating damage to multiple organ systems, including the respiratory system.

Upper neurologic injury levels have been identified as significant predictors of tracheostomy, as reported by Scantling, et al.5 and Harrop, et al.6 Specifically, injuries to spinal cord levels C3-C5 appear to affect the phrenic nerve, which innervates the diaphragm, a crucial muscle for respiration.20 Our study further identified C2/3, C3/4, and C4/5 as significant anatomic injury levels associated with the need for tracheostomy, correlating with the origins of the phrenic nerve. In contrast, Childs, et al.21 found that AIS grade A was a simple clinical criterion for determining tracheostomy needs in TCSCI patients, independent of the anatomical level of injury. As nerves from lower spinal cord levels also innervate respiratory muscles, injury level alone may not fully identify patients at risk for tracheostomy. However, a consistent finding across studies is that patients with complete SCI are more likely to require tracheostomy.

Cervical surgery was another factor significantly influencing the need for tracheostomy in our study. Higashi, et al.22 similarly identified operative treatment as a risk factor for tracheostomy in an ageing population. The Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, a widely recognized standard in TCSCI treatment,23 assesses motor and sensory function in the lower limbs, sensory function of the trunk, and bladder function. A JOA score below 12 or 13 often indicates the need for surgical intervention rather than conservative treatment. As motor and sensory functions are better preserved, the likelihood of requiring surgery decreases. Moreover, patients requiring surgery typically have more severe injuries leading to a high risk of respiratory failure. Additionally, postoperative factors such as retropharyngeal edema may contribute to the need for airway management. Our finding that surgery is a significant factor for tracheostomy aligns with the influence of the AIS score, as discussed previously.

In our study, AIS B and C did not yield significant results. In contrast, Gao, et al.24 reported that AIS B and C, along with AIS A, were risk factors for tracheostomy in TCSCI. Branco, et al.19 and Childs, et al.21 also identified thoracic injury as a significant predictor of tracheostomy following TCSCI. However, our predictive model demonstrated a slightly higher AUC score and accuracy when haemothorax was excluded from the variables. Hou, et al.25 and Sun, et al.26 have previously developed classification and regression tree models to predict tracheostomy in TCSCI patients. Our study underscores the critical need for rapid and accurate decision-making in tracheostomy, a procedure requiring timely intervention. By employing an AI model such as CatBoost, we developed a predictive model that incorporates key clinical variables and is highly adaptable to real-world clinical environments. A notable strength of our approach is the detailed interpretation of key factors influencing predictions through SHAP value analysis, offering transparent insights into the model’s decision-making process. This level of analysis enhances clinical applicability and empowers clinicians with data-driven guidance in critical care scenarios.

This study has several limitations. Although the ISS is well-established in clinical practice and the literature,18,19,27 its exclusion from this analysis was due to the retrospective nature of the study and its absence in the medical records. The medical records often documented only the primary injury, limiting the ability to account for a comprehensive injury score. While injuries beyond the cervical spinal cord may have influenced the need for tracheostomy, we sought to mitigate this bias by focusing on patients with cervical spinal cord injury as the primary injury. Thus, the surrogate markers of GCS, presence of haemothorax, and the requirement for surgery were incorporated to capture the effects of injury severity. Although complete-case analysis may reduce sample size, a sensitivity analysis comparing included and excluded patients demonstrated no substantial differences in baseline characteristics, supporting the robustness of our findings. Moreover, successful decannulation warrants further consideration. Given that high anatomical injury levels and complete injuries are known predictors of decannulation failure,17 future models should be developed to predict both successful and unsuccessful tracheostomy outcomes.

This study developed a machine learning model aimed at predicting the need for tracheostomy in patients with TCSCI using early clinical data. The CatBoost model demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy among the models tested, effectively identifying key factors such as age, GCS, AIS, the occurrence of cervical surgery, and specific injury levels (C3/4, C4/5, and C2/3). The development of machine learning models for predicting the need for tracheostomy following TCSCI has the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes by enabling immediate decision-making regarding tracheostomy and optimising post-injury prognosis.
