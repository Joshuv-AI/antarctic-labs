COQ8 chaperones coenzyme Q lipid intermediates through ATP-driven structural gating
Authors: Andrea Gottinger, Marco Malatesta, Callum R. Nicoll, Georg Ansari, Mathieu Quinodoz, Karolina Kaminska, Rachael W.C. Tang, Tien-En Tan, Beau J. Fenner, Pilar Barberán-Martínez, Gema García-García, José M. Millán, Maximilian Pfau, Natalie E. Burbach, Domiziana Cecchini, Carlo Rivolta, Andrea Mattevi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coenzyme Q biosynthesis requires two atypical kinase-like proteins (COQ8A and COQ8B), whose detailed molecular mechanism remains unclear. Here, we show that both paralogs function as adenosine triphosphatases (ATPases) that promote coenzyme Q biosynthetic metabolon activity by engaging in loose protein-protein interactions and delivering insoluble biosynthetic intermediates. Structural bioinformatics and pathological variant–driven mutagenesis identify a previously uncharacterized pocket that selectively recognizes coenzyme Q biosynthetic intermediates via their head groups. X-ray crystallography reveals that access to this pocket is gated by long-range conformational changes controlled by adenosine 5′-triphosphate hydrolysis. Last, excess coenzyme Q suppresses binding of early-stage intermediates and thereby abolishes the promoting effect of COQ8 on the metabolon. Together, these findings support a model in which COQ8 tunes coenzyme Q biosynthesis by coupling ATPase-driven intermediate chaperoning to feedback inhibition by the final product.

INTRODUCTION

Coenzyme Q is a polyisoprenoid redox-active lipid best known for its role as an electron carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain (1–3). The recent finding that coenzyme Q also controls ferroptosis has reignited interest in how this molecule is synthesized in mitochondria and trafficked inside cells (4). Coenzyme Q biosynthesis is initiated by polymerization of the polyisoprenoid tail and its conjugation to the head-group precursor p-hydroxybenzoate, which derives from tyrosine metabolism (5). The pathway then proceeds through a series of hydroxylation, decarboxylation, and O- and C-methylation reactions recently characterized in detail and catalyzed by COQ3-7 and COQ9 enzymes in yeast and animals, which assemble into a dynamic biosynthetic metabolon on the matrix side of the inner mitochondrial membrane (6). In a previous study, we reconstructed in vitro the ring modification steps of coenzyme Q biosynthesis using resurrected tetrapod ancestral COQ proteins (7). This system revealed a sequential, linear reaction order (Fig. 1A). Despite being highly conserved, some variability in the pathway appears across evolution. In yeast, a rescue pathway enables the use of p-aminobenzoate as an alternative precursor (8). In humans, COQ4 has been proposed to catalyze oxidative decarboxylation (9), consistent with evidence of COQ6-independent rescue by vanillic acid (10). Furthermore, C1 decarboxylation has been suggested to alternatively occur prior to C5 hydroxylation and methylation in vivo (10).

In addition to these enzymes, directly responsible for head-group maturation, coenzyme Q biosynthesis requires, in vivo, COQ8, a member of the atypical protein kinase-like superfamily (5). Specifically, metazoa have two co-orthologs (COQ8A and COQ8B) that belong to the UbiB family, named after the archetypal Escherichia coli UbiB protein (11, 12). Protein kinase-like enzymes share the structural features of canonical protein kinases, namely, an adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–binding site between an N lobe and a C lobe, but have evolved additional domains and extensions that broaden their spectrum of biological functions (13). Specifically, UbiB family members have an N-terminal extended domain, which occupies a region corresponding to the substrate-protein binding site of protein kinases and is likely to cause self-inhibition of the kinase activity (Fig. 1B) (14).

Genetic and biochemical studies over the past two decades have established UbiB proteins as central regulators of coenzyme Q biosynthesis in multiple model organisms, with both in vivo and in vitro evidence. In yeast, deletion or inhibition of Coq8p causes respiratory deficiency, whereas heterologous expression of human COQ8A (HsCOQ8A) rescues cell viability and coenzyme Q production in the coq8 knockout background (15, 16). Moreover, HsCOQ8A expression restores the phosphorylation state of COQ3p, COQ5p, and Coq7p, although whether COQ8 exerts a direct protein kinase activity on these targets or maintains their phosphorylation indirectly has remained unclear (16). Coq8p overexpression also stabilizes the coenzyme Q biosynthetic metabolon in yeast strains lacking other Coq proteins, suggesting that COQ8 influences both metabolon integrity and pathway efficiency (17). Consistent with a conserved function across species, neuronal knockdown of coq8 gene in Drosophila results in locomotor deficits, and eye-specific knockdown leads to photoreceptor degeneration (18). Intriguingly, overexpression of HsCOQ8A fails to rescue all tissue-specific phenotypes, pointing to functional specialization between the two metazoan paralogs (18). In humans, pathogenic COQ8A variants are associated with cerebellar ataxia (19), whereas COQ8B variants cause retinitis pigmentosa and nephropathy (20, 21). These observations collectively support a crucial but incompletely defined role for COQ8 in modulating coenzyme Q biosynthesis and mitochondrial physiology in a tissue-specific manner.

Over the past decade, Pagliarini and colleagues have extensively characterized HsCOQ8A biochemically (14, 15). Stefely et al. (14) solved the first crystal structure of HsCOQ8A and reported that, rather than acting as a conventional protein kinase in trans or phosphorylating coenzyme Q intermediates, the protein displays robust adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity in vitro (14, 22). Active-site mutations perturb the interaction of HsCOQ8A with endogenous COQ proteins in human embryonic kidney–293 cells, and coq8a−/− mice show altered assembly of the coenzyme Q metabolon (22). Recombinant UbiB copurifies with E. coli coenzyme Q intermediates, whereas HsCOQ8A ATPase activity is stimulated by the mitochondrial lipid cardiolipin and phenol-derived compounds in vitro (15, 22). On this basis, it has been proposed that HsCOQ8A, despite its annotation as a protein kinase, primarily promotes metabolon stability and binds coenzyme Q intermediates, coupling these activities to ATP hydrolysis (15). However, the molecular mechanism by which COQ8 interacts with metabolon-forming enzymes, engages hydrophobic pathway intermediates, and ultimately sustains coenzyme Q biosynthesis remains poorly understood. This uncertainty stems from the intrinsic complexity of the multienzyme COQ metabolon, the low solubility of coenzyme Q intermediates, and the difficulty of producing several COQ proteins recombinantly (7).

Recently, our group reconstituted the entire coenzyme Q biosynthetic pathway in vitro using soluble mono-prenylated analogs of coenzyme Q intermediates and stable tetrapod ancestral proteins (TetCOQs) that share more than 75% sequence identity with their extant human counterparts and retain conserved active-site residues (7). In this system, TetCOQ8B ATPase activity is strongly stimulated by the coenzyme Q metabolon and subcomplexes. The addition of TetCOQ8B and ATP to the reconstituted machinery markedly increases coenzyme Q yield. This enhancement does not reflect an increased activity of individual COQ enzymes but is connected to an efficient channeling of pathway intermediates (7). The reduced leakage of intermediates from the metabolon maximizes the efficiency of metabolic flux, thereby streamlining the COQ metabolon (7). These findings raised a central mechanistic question: How does COQ8 bind and redistribute highly hydrophobic coenzyme Q intermediates at the membrane-metabolon interface and within the metabolon to enhance coenzyme Q biosynthesis? Using soluble mono- and tetra-prenylated biosynthetic intermediates, tetrapod ancestral COQ proteins (Fig. 1A), and inner mitochondrial membrane–like liposomes, here, we show that COQ8 transiently engages the metabolon through loose contacts that are essential for enhancing metabolic flux. COQ8 selectively extracts early-stage biosynthetic intermediates via a pocket that favors carboxyl-bearing head groups, as demonstrated by mutagenesis and binding assays. Crystal structures reveal that ATP hydrolysis gates this pocket: Adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP)–bound states expose the binding site, whereas ATP mimic–bound conformations promote ligand release and transfer to metabolon enzymes. Excess coenzyme Q competitively blocks intermediate extraction, potentially establishing a feedback loop that adjusts biosynthesis to cellular demand.

RESULTS

COQ8 ATPase activity is responsible for metabolon streamlining

In our previous study, TetCOQ8B was found to function both as an ATPase and as a TetCOQ3 kinase in vitro, but high yields of full-length ancestral TetCOQ8A could not be obtained, most likely due to its extended disordered N terminus, which distinguishes it from TetCOQ8B (fig. S2) (7). On this basis, an in-depth characterization of the full-length B paralog was undertaken, with the primary aim of disentangling the molecular mechanism underlying the function of TetCOQ8B within the COQ metabolon.

The first step was to define the substrate scope of the putative kinase activity across candidate COQ targets. Pairwise reactions were set up between TetCOQ8B and individual COQ metabolon components, using a γ-phosphate-PEG8-biotin ATP analog, to enable detection of phosphorylated proteins by streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (fig. S3). The previously described autophosphorylating double-mutant Ala179→Gly-Lys109→Arg was used as a positive control (Ala218 and Lys155 on HsCOQ8B and Ala339 and Lys276 on HsCOQ8A; table S2 and fig. S2 for residue numbering) (22). A robust phosphorylation was detected in the positive control, whereas weaker signals were observed for COQ3, COQ5, and COQ7 (fig. S4), mirroring the phosphorylation pattern reported in yeast (16). To probe the potential physiological relevance of this activity, we monitored COQ3 phosphorylation across a TetCOQ8B titration, yielding a median effective concentration (EC50) of ∼1 μM (Fig. 2A). Addition of COQ6, whose complex with COQ3 enhances ATPase activity (7), worsened the EC50 by roughly half order of magnitude (Fig. 2A). These relatively high TetCOQ8B concentrations required for COQ3 phosphorylation suggested that the kinase activity observed in vitro is unlikely to be the main driver of metabolon regulation and may rather represent an in vitro observation of no physiological implication.

To further probe whether and how ATPase and kinase activities account for the enhanced coenzyme Q production, the complete biosynthetic system—comprising TetCOQ3, TetCOQ4, TetCOQ5, TetCOQ6, TetCOQ7, and TetCOQ9 proteins—was reconstituted and subjected to a prephosphorylation step by incubation with TetCOQ8B and a limiting amount of ATP. Two further manipulations were then applied to the prephosphorylated sample: treatment with λ-phage phosphatase to remove phosphate groups, and supplementation with an ATP-regeneration system to restore high ATP levels and reactivate ATPase turnover. Conversion of the mono-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate precursor (1) to coenzyme Q1 and ATP was quantified after overnight incubation (Fig. 2B). A strong correlation emerged between elevated ATP availability and enhanced coenzyme Q production, whereas no appreciable differences were detected between nonphosphorylated, phosphorylated, and dephosphorylated states (Fig. 2C and table S1). These results support a model in which TetCOQ8 promotes coenzyme Q biosynthesis predominantly through its ATPase activity, and where phosphorylation of COQ proteins does not measurably contribute to strengthening their interactions or boosting their catalytic performance.

The physical contact between COQ8 and the COQ metabolon ensures the streamlining effect

We next examined how physical proximity between COQ8 and the COQ metabolon contributes to pathway regulation. In line with previous observations that incubation with the COQ metabolon or its subcomplexes enhances TetCOQ8B ATPase activity (7), this stimulatory effect was used to estimate an apparent dissociation constant for the TetCOQ8B-COQ metabolon interaction by monitoring ATPase activation across a range of metabolon concentrations (Fig. 3A). Data fitted a binding curve yielding an apparent dissociation constant (Kd, app) of 1.26 ± 0.19 μM, consistent with a loose rather than tight binding mode. To test whether this physical contact is required for metabolon streamlining, we perturbed the interaction network of TetCOQ8B by covalent modification of surface residues. TetCOQ8B was decorated with bulky polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains using N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester–based labeling, taking advantage of multiple accessible lysine residues on its surface, mapped with acetyl-NHS derivatization followed by bottom-up proteomics (fig. S6). This strategy, previously validated for disrupting interactions within the COQ metabolon (23), generated a PEGylated TetCOQ8B variant that retained ATPase activity and ligand-binding properties, but not its stimulation by the COQ metabolon (fig. S7). In addition, the PEGylated TetCOQ8B failed to sustain the metabolon activity to the same extent as the unmodified protein (Fig. 3B). These data suggest that physical proximity is essential for COQ8-dependent activation of the COQ metabolon. As a proof-of-principle, we analyzed the reconstructed TetCOQ metabolon by NativePAGE in the presence and absence of unmodified and PEGylated TetCOQ8B. We observed high–molecular weight species regardless of TetCOQ8B (fig. S8). However, bottom-up proteomics analysis of the high–molecular weight bands revealed the presence of TetCOQ8B in the metabolon only when unmodified (raw data). We therefore performed small-scale reactions in the presence or absence of TetCOQ8B. The pathway was either dissected into pairs of consecutive reactions or initiated from individual intermediates to test for preferential interactions of TetCOQ8 with specific TetCOQ proteins or coenzyme Q intermediates. Ultrahigh Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS) quantitation of the mono-prenylated intermediates and of coenzyme Q1 revealed a similar trend in all samples, characterized by minimal or absent accumulation of the intermediate(s) and higher product yields only in the sample with TetCOQ8B (tables S4 and S5). This evidence demonstrated a pan-COQ targeting behavior of COQ8 that can facilitate transfer of biosynthetic intermediates across all enzymes forming the metabolon. This function requires direct contact between COQ8 and its client COQ enzymes.

COQ8 binds coenzyme Q intermediates

We next examined the coenzyme Q ligand-binding properties of both TetCOQ8 paralogs, using mono-prenylated analogs of coenzyme Q intermediates (Fig. 1). To test this, we established a copurification assay in which full-length TetCOQ8B was incubated with either mono-prenylated 1 or coenzyme Q1 as ligand, the excess was removed by size exclusion chromatography, and any copurified intermediates in the protein peak were extracted and analyzed by UHPLC-HRMS (Fig. 4A). Both the individually tested precursor (1) and the final product (coenzyme Q1) copurified with TetCOQ8B (Fig. 4B), indicating that TetCOQ8B can bind coenzyme Q ligands across the entire pathway. To qualitatively assess the binding selectivity, we used 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). To avoid detergent-related spectral interference, we generated N-terminally truncated soluble constructs TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8BΔN89 by removing the hydrophobic, partially disordered N terminus, as previously described for HsCOQ8A (figs. S2 and S9) (14, 22, 24). Using these constructs, chemical shift changes and signal quenching of diagnostic NMR peaks were monitored for intermediate 1 and coenzyme Q1 in the presence of TetCOQ8AΔN99 or TetCOQ8BΔN89, revealing a clear preference for the biosynthetic precursor (fig. S10). Similarly, binding to coenzyme Q precursor analogs when screening phenol-like compounds libraries was observed for HsCOQ8A (15). To quantify this selectivity, we devised a binding assay based on competition between coenzyme Q and nonquinone intermediates, exploiting 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol (DCPIP) as a redox reporter (Fig. 4C). Reduced coenzyme Q1 was first shown to be protected from oxidation by DCPIP in the presence of full-length TetCOQ8B (fig. S11). Next, we exploited the release of reduced coenzyme Q1, when competing with another intermediate, and the subsequent reduction of DCPIP to derive apparent affinities in the low micromolar range for nonquinone intermediates (Fig. 4D). Comparison of the Kd, app values for the carboxylated precursor 1 (1.15 ± 0.24 μM) and the decarboxylated intermediate 4a (4.90 ± 1.02 μM) suggests that the C1-carboxy group of early-stage intermediates contributes to ligand recognition. As a control, we verified that the N-terminal truncation did not alter the ligand-binding properties of TetCOQ8 (fig. S12). The fact that binding was observed using mono-prenylated intermediates implies that the head group specifically contributes to ligand recognition, whereas the hydrophobicity of the tail may contribute more unspecifically.

COQ8 facilitates extraction of coenzyme Q precursor from membranes

The coenzyme Q biosynthetic metabolon is thought to be located on the inner mitochondrial surface where the polyprenylated p-hydroxybenzoate precursor is synthesized by PDSS1-PDSS2 (COQ1) and COQ2 (Fig. 1) (25, 26). We therefore asked the question of whether COQ8 may also facilitate the extraction of the coenzyme Q precursor from the membrane. To study COQ8 in a membrane-like context, we prepared 0.1-μm liposomes (composed of distearoylphosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, egg phosphatidyl choline, and dioleoylphosphatidylserine in a 65:25:5:5 ratio) containing 2% of total lipid content coenzyme Q4 or its precursor tetraprenyl-p-hydroxybenzoate (PPHB), with or without 10% of the mitochondrial phospholipid cardiolipin (fig. S13) (27). First, we confirmed that cardiolipin-containing liposomes stimulate ATPase activity, consistent with earlier observations (fig. S14) (15). Next, PPHB-liposomes or PPHB-cardiolipin-liposomes were used as substrates for the reconstructed metabolon in the absence and presence of soluble TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8BΔN89, thereby avoiding detergents that could disrupt liposome integrity. No detectable conversion of PPHB occurred without TetCOQ8, whereas coenzyme Q4 production was observed in the presence of TetCOQ8. Notably, this effect was drastically enhanced by cardiolipin (Fig. 5A). To directly test whether TetCOQ8 can extract intermediates from membranes, we assayed PPHB extraction using liposomes comprising different combinations of cardiolipin, coenzyme Q4, and its precursor. Following a short incubation with ATP, the protein plus any extracted component were separated using a centrifugal filter of an adequate cutoff (Fig. 5B). Liposome integrity in the retentate was monitored by dynamic light scattering (DLS), whereas protein and extracted PPHB in the filtrate were detected by differential scanning fluorimetry and Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with Electron-Spray Ionization Orbitrap High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-ESI-Orbitrap-HRMS), respectively. Contamination by liposomal lipids was excluded by tracking their absence (Fig. 5C and fig. S15). We made three critical observations: (i) Cardiolipin-containing liposomes yielded higher levels of extracted PPHB, (ii) mixing coenzyme Q4–containing liposomes with PPHB + cardiolipin liposomes did not alter PPHB extraction, and (iii) no extraction occurred when the configuration was reversed (coenzyme Q4 + cardiolipin mixed with PPHB-containing liposomes; Fig. 5C). Collectively, these results confirm membrane-dependent modulation of COQ8 (15). As the experiments were performed using soluble truncated constructs, we posit that membrane interaction may be stronger in full-length proteins, which additionally bear two hydrophobic N-terminal helices. These findings suggest a model whereby COQ8 engages cardiolipin-enriched membranes and extracts the coenzyme Q precursor from the membrane to initiate the metabolon-catalyzed biosynthesis.

COQ8 is feedback-inhibited by an excess of coenzyme Q

Given that TetCOQ8 binds coenzyme Q (Fig. 4B), high coenzyme Q levels might saturate the binding site and block intermediate engagement. When excess coenzyme Q10 was added to the reconstructed COQ metabolon converting intermediate mono-prenylated 1 to coenzyme Q1, yields in the absence of TetCOQ8B were unchanged, whereas the TetCOQ8-dependent effect was specifically inhibited (Fig. 4E and table S6). In addition, when PPHB and cardiolipin-containing liposomes were used as starting substrate in the presence of a one- or fivefold molar excess of coenzyme Q1, metabolon streamlining was lost under the fivefold excess condition (Fig. 5A), consistent with end-product inhibition of COQ8 when in excess. This observation is more likely explained by saturation of the binding site driven by mass action rather than by specific recognition of coenzyme Q structural features, as COQ8 showed higher affinity for early-stage intermediates (Fig. 4D) and its effect on the metabolon is inhibited by excess coenzyme Q (Fig. 5A). This observation may also explain why COQ8 overexpression partially restored the respiratory phenotype in coq10 null yeast mutants (28). COQ10 was described as a mitochondrial coenzyme Q transporter, and its loss may result in local accumulation of available coenzyme Q within mitochondrial inner membrane. In summary, COQ8 might be controlled by increased mitochondrial coenzyme Q levels through a biochemical negative feedback inhibitory mechanism.

ATP- and ADP-bound states of COQ8 exhibit different conformations

Having established that TetCOQ8 extracts the coenzyme Q precursor intermediate from inner mitochondrial membrane–like liposomes and that head-group differences dictate ligand recognition, we sought mechanistic insights into the binding site and its coupling to ATPase activity. Intrigued by a recent report about ATPase-regulated conformational changes of a mycobacterial noncanonical ABC transporter, which bears structural homology with COQ8 paralogs (29), we investigated whether a similar behavior could be observed for COQ8. Using differential scanning fluorimetry, we found that TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8BΔN89 displayed preferential binding for ADP over the ATP analog adenylyl-imidophosphate (AMP-PNP) and that the complex is strengthened by replacing the active metal Mg2+ with the nonphysiological Mn2+ (fig. S16), as previously shown for HsCOQ8A (14). The Mn2+-ADP complex was copurified with a greater amount of mono-prenylated intermediate 1 compared to the apo protein, whereas Mn2+-AMP-PNP could not retain any detectable ligand (Fig. 6A). Clear differences in circular dichroism spectra were observed when comparing the apo, Mn2+-ADP and Mn2+-AMP-PNP TetCOQ8AΔN99, and TetCOQ8BΔN89, suggesting that they adopt different conformations depending on the bound nucleotide (fig. S17). We therefore attempted to obtain crystal structures of both the high-yielding and soluble N-terminal truncated constructs in complex with ADP or AMP-PNP and Mn2+. We succeeded in obtaining diffracting crystals for TetCOQ8AΔN99 in complex with AMP-PNP + Mn2+ and for TetCOQ8BΔN89 with ADP + Mn2+, which were solved at 2.1- and 2.4-Å resolution, respectively (table S7 and Fig. 6B). The high-quality electron density allowed unambiguous modeling of the nucleotide and two Mn2+ ions in the active site of both structures (fig. S18). The overall fold adopted by the ancestral TetCOQ8AΔN99-AMP-PNP-Mn2+ complex is comparable to the previously solved HsCOQ8A-AMP-PNP-Mg2+ one [Protein Data Bank (PDB): 5i35], with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) calculated on Cαs of 0.8 Å (fig. S19) (14). However, the use of the higher-stabilizing Mn2+ likely tightly locked the ATP analog in the active site, allowing discrimination of the metal and the nucleotide electron density.

Comparison of the active-site conformations in the TetCOQ8AΔN99-AMP-PNP-Mn2+– and TetCOQ8BΔN89-ADP-Mn2+–bound structures revealed differences in the N-terminal extension domain that characterizes UbiB kinases (fig. S1). In the ADP-bound structure, this domain adopts a relaxed conformation, as indicated by missing density for two loops and higher B factors, whereas it appears more compact and closed in the AMP-PNP–bound structure (Fig. 6C and figs. S20 and S21). In addition, it became clear that ATP hydrolysis to ADP additionally causes a shift of α14. This helix adopts a bent, outward conformation in the ADP-bound structure to open a cryptic pocket within the C lobe (Fig. 6C). To confirm that our conclusions were not artifacts of structural differences between TetCOQ8A and TetCOQ8B, we predicted the most likely conformational equilibria using a PDB-trained generative modeling pipeline (PETIMOT) (30). Both paralogs exhibited similar dynamics, featuring a closing motion characterized by the bending of the α14 helix toward the protein core (fig. S22). These results support a model in which ATP hydrolysis induces conformational changes that ultimately may control the coenzyme Q intermediate binding.

COQ8 ATP hydrolysis controls the binding of a coenzyme Q intermediate pocket

All attempts to cocrystallize or soak coenzyme Q intermediates into the ADP-bound TetCOQ8BΔN89 crystals proved unsuccessful, as the ligand phase-partitioned in the crystallization condition. To identify functionally relevant residues, we applied a pretrained predictor of binding residues based on residue embedding vectors (bindEmbed21) (31) to HsCOQ8B (UniProt: Q96D53) and mapped the resulting scores onto the AlphaFold3-predicted model (fig. S23 and table S8). As anticipated, metal- and small molecule–binding residues emerged as positive hits in the known ATP binding site, but a more compelling cluster defined a secondary unprecedented pocket within the C-lobe domain, including the previously described bending of the α14 helix. We next resorted to molecular docking into the experimental structures using DiffDock (32). This docking method was preferred as it does not require defining a docking-box, while it considers the entire protein structure in an unbiased manner. The highest-scoring poses of compound 1, the coenzyme Q biosynthetic precursor (Fig. 1), clustered in the open ADP-bound structure within the C-lobe pocket (43 out of 50 generated poses; Fig. 7A), whereas no convincing cluster of poses was predicted for the closed AMP-PNP–bound structure (Fig. 7B). This notion is further strengthened by the significant difference in the DiffDock confidence scores when comparing all the docking poses generated in the two simulations (Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, n = 50, P < 0.01). The previously described lack of electron density for two loops and higher B factors in the ADP-bound structure denoted greater flexibility for the region likely involved in gating accessibility to the C-lobe pocket for coenzyme Q intermediates. The docking calculations consistently indicated that 1 binds with its head group in the core of the pocket while the elongated polyisoprenoid tail extends toward a region lined by the hydrophobic N-terminal extension. Notably, this region is predicted to be membrane-associated in TetCOQ8B by the PPM 2.0 server (figs. S24 and S25) (33) and by previous work on HsCOQ8A (15).

Comparison of the two active site conformations suggests that these long-range conformational changes are likely triggered by the displacement of active-site residues upon hydrolysis of ATP to ADP (Asp331 and Glu248 on TetCOQ8A and Asp331 and Glu238 on TetCOQ8B) (Fig. 6B and fig. S20). Rotation of the active-site glutamate (Glu248 in TetCOQ8A and Glu238 in TetCOQ8B) in the ADP-bound structure triggers a change in conformation of the α4 helix including a lysine (Lys119 in TetCOQ8A and Lys109 in TetCOQ8B), ultimately relaxing the N-terminal extension (fig. S26A). In addition, the rotation of the active-site aspartate (331 in TetCOQ8A and 321 in TetCOQ8B) makes space for a change in conformation of an arginine of α15 (454 in TetCOQ8A and 444 in TetCOQ8B) and the downstream residues of the helix. These changes lead to the opening of α14 helix that creates the coenzyme Q binding pocket in the ADP conformation (Fig. 6C and fig. S26B).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the binding properties of COQ8 are controlled by ATP hydrolysis. In the resting state, COQ8 remains ADP-bound (fig. S16) and thereby maintains the conformation that is competent in coenzyme Q intermediate binding (Fig. 6A). Upon interaction with COQ enzymes and the mitochondrial cardiolipin-enriched membrane (Fig. 3A and fig. S14), COQ8 is recharged with ATP, facilitating intermediate release and transfer to the client protein. Hydrolysis of ATP finally primes the protein for the binding of another coenzyme Q intermediate, starting another binding-and-release cycle.

COQ8 mechanism explains the effect of pathological mutations

COQ8B mutations are known to cause retinitis pigmentosa (21, 34–36) as well as nephrotic syndrome (table S2). We reasoned that pathological variants would serve as a valuable biochemical tool to probe the COQ8 mechanism and rationalize how they impair COQ8 function, causing disease. Eight mutations detected in patients were introduced into TetCOQ8B and used as model in this study: five described previously in the literature (21, 34) and three reported here for the first time (tables S9 and S10, fig. S27, and Fig. 8A). The patients bearing these new variants displayed all clinical features of retinitis pigmentosa, with visual acuity ranging from 0.1 to 0.3 logMAR (Minimum Angle of Resolution) across the six examined eyes (fig. S27). Multimodal retinal imaging consistently revealed advanced retinal atrophy with a small residual foveal island, although one patient lacked the typical mid-peripheral bone-spicule pigmentation (retinitis pigmentosa sine pigmento) (fig. S27). Full-field electroretinography, available for one patient, showed markedly reduced scotopic and photopic responses, consistent with a rod-cone dystrophy (fig. S27).

D340N, I452E, and Y262W target catalytic residues, and we confirmed that they inactivate the ATPase and metabolon streamlining activities of TetCOQ8B (Fig. 8, B and C). Four mutations (S200N, D204H, R15W, and L52R) are located on the surface of the N-terminal extension (Fig. 8A). Notably, we found that none of them supported metabolon activity or displayed ATPase activation by the metabolon (Fig. 8, B and C). A variant (V396M) (21) resides within the coenzyme Q binding pocket. We therefore assessed the ability of this mutant to bind mono-prenylated 1, the preferred ligand of the wild-type (WT) protein, using the size exclusion chromatography–mass spectrometry (SEC-MS)–based copurification assay (Fig. 8D). We also generated additional variants targeting nearby residues (H443M and Y355F) and assessed their properties. We found that these mutants retained the metabolon-stimulated ATPase activity but failed to bind 1 and boost coenzyme Q production by the metabolon (Fig. 8, B and C). Collectively, these data corroborate the notion that COQ8s recognize coenzyme Q and its biosynthetic intermediates at the head-group level. They also suggest that the surface of the N-terminal extension is likely part of the interactions between COQ8 and the metabolon proteins. Critically, the N-terminal extension is a feature characteristic of atypical kinases, and our data show that its conformation is affected by ATP binding and hydrolysis (Fig. 6, B and C, and fig. S1).

DISCUSSION

Prior genetic and biochemical studies have established UbiB atypical kinase family members as indispensable factors for COQ metabolon stability and coenzyme Q biosynthesis in organisms ranging from bacteria to vertebrates (12, 16, 19–22). Reports on the biochemical characterization of HsCOQ8A revealed a robust ATPase over protein kinase activity, modulated by the mitochondrial lipid cardiolipin and by phenolic coenzyme Q precursor–like compounds, leading to the proposal that COQ8 couples ATP hydrolysis to metabolon stability and coenzyme Q intermediate binding rather than being involved in posttranslational modification of COQ proteins (7, 14, 15, 22). How COQ8 boosts the biosynthetic flux has remained largely unknown, owing to the intrinsic complexity and instability of the multienzyme COQ metabolon and the poor solubility of coenzyme Q intermediates. This work reveals that COQ8 chaperones the intermediates in coenzyme Q biosynthesis through binding-and-release cycles propelled by ATP hydrolysis. COQ8 contacts metabolon enzymes, engages hydrophobic intermediates at the membrane surface, and converts ATP turnover into productive flux (Fig. 9).

Proposed mechanism of action for the COQ metabolon streamlining effect by COQ8.

(A) The ADP-bound resting state of COQ8 (violet) is poised to recognize and extract the coenzyme Q precursor PPHB (green) from the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM). The two lipid layers of IMM are schematized as dark gray bands. (B) Interaction with the COQ metabolon (schematized as colored circles) stimulates nucleotide exchange. ATP binding induces a conformational change that releases the cargo of coenzyme Q biosynthetic precursor (Fig. 6C). (C) ATP hydrolysis turns COQ8 back to the open conformation, prone to coenzyme Q intermediate binding, priming the enzyme for the next binding-and-release cycle.

Loose but obligatory contacts with the COQ metabolon were indicated by the low-micromolar apparent Kd for metabolon-dependent ATPase activation. Consistently, surface PEGylation, which disrupts protein-protein interactions, of pathological surface mutants led to loss of flux enhancement (Fig. 3). COQ8B increases coenzyme Q1 yield and prevents accumulation of intermediates across all biosynthetic reactions, indicating a pan-COQ targeting behavior rather than selective engagement of a single enzyme subset (tables S4 and S5). In experiments with liposomes mimicking the inner mitochondrial membrane, conversion of membrane-embedded coenzyme Q precursor to mature coenzyme Q by the soluble metabolon is negligible without COQ8 but strongly enhanced by COQ8, particularly with cardiolipin-rich membranes (Fig. 5A). Direct extraction assays confirm that COQ8 solubilizes the precursor into the aqueous phase, with cardiolipin boosting recovery (Fig. 5, B and C). These data support a model in which COQ8 senses cardiolipin-rich regions, like mitochondrial cristae (37), extracts the precursor from these domains, and delivers it to matrix-side metabolon enzymes. These data rationalize previous observations that cardiolipin stimulates COQ8A ATPase activity in vitro (15) by assigning a functional role to this stimulation in precursor extraction and metabolon feeding.

Structural and biochemical analyses reveal that ATP hydrolysis gates a C-lobe pocket that binds coenzyme Q intermediates, driving a cycle of capture, binding, and release. ATP binding closes and rigidifies the C-lobe α14 helix to occlude the pocket, whereas ADP binding induces flexibility in the N-terminal extension domain and displacement of the α14 helix to expose and widen the binding pocket (Fig. 6C). Copurification, NMR, and DCPIP assays confirmed selective binding to early precursors over mature coenzyme Q, with higher affinity for carboxylated versus decarboxylated head groups (Figs. 4, A to D, 6A, and 7). C-lobe mutations lining the nucleotide-gated pocket reduced intermediate binding and metabolon streamlining without affecting ATPase activity (Fig. 8, C and D). In line with prior COQ8A-phenolic compound interactions (15), these results establish the C-lobe pocket as a chemically selective receptor that precisely targets early biosynthetic intermediates for efficient trafficking.

In conclusion, this work reveals COQ8’s molecular mechanism, showing how its ATPase activity and coenzyme Q intermediate binding drive efficient COQ metabolon flux (Fig. 9). Five principal findings emerge: (i) COQ8 engages the metabolon through loose protein-protein contacts essential for pathway metabolic flux; (ii) COQ8 selectively extracts the coenzyme Q precursor from cardiolipin-enriched membranes; (iii) ATP hydrolysis gates a binding pocket located in the C lobe through long-range conformational changes, enabling a cycle of intermediate binding and release; (iv) COQ8 favors carboxyl-bearing head groups and therefore is possibly more effective in the escorting of early-stage coenzyme Q intermediates; and (v) such a cycle of binding and release is inhibited by an excess of coenzyme Q, thus positioning COQ8 as a potential flux regulator that responds to mitochondrial coenzyme Q demand. The mechanistic model provides a framework for understanding UbiB-family kinases and highlights a key evolutionary divergence. In bacterial menaquinone biosynthesis, prenylation occurs late on a soluble quinone (38, 39), whereas in the coenzyme Q pathway, prenylation occurs at the level of the head-group precursor (Fig. 1A). This embeds early intermediates in the membrane and likely necessitates ATP-dependent extraction by COQ8 for delivery to the partially soluble, membrane-associated metabolon. Although energetically costly, this design of the pathway confines potentially toxic, redox-active intermediates to the inner-mitochondrial membrane limiting their unwanted diffusion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Detergents were purchased from Anatrace, except for Tween 20, which was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Coenzyme Q mono-prenylated precursor 4-hydroxy-3-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl) benzoic acid (intermediate 1) was purchased from BLD Pharmaceutics. The remaining mono-prenylated coenzyme Q intermediates 3,4-dihydroxy-5-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl) benzoic acid (intermediate 2), 4-hydroxy-3-methoxy-5-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl) benzoic acid (intermediate 3), 2-methoxy-6-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl) phenol (intermediate 4a), 2-methoxy-6-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl)benzene-1,4-diol (intermediate 4b), 5-methoxy-2-methyl-3-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl)cyclohexa-2,5-diene-1,4-dione (intermediate 5ox), and sorbicillin were purchased from WuXi App Tech. 1H NMR spectra of the custom synthesized intermediates 1, 2, 3, 4a, 4b, and 5ox and sorbicillin have been previously reported (7, 23). Tetra-prenylated coenzyme Q precursor 4-hydroxy-3-(3,7,11,15-tetramethylhexadeca-2,6,10,14-tetraenyl) benzoic acid and coenzyme Q4 were obtained from custom synthesis by Enamine; 1H NMR spectra are reported in fig. S28, and accurate mass and MSMS spectra are reported in fig. S29. UHPLC solvents were purchased from Romil. All other reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Reduced coenzyme Q1 was produced as previously described (40): briefly, 100 μl of NaBH4 (300 mg ml−1) in 0.1 mM NaOH was added to 800 μl of 10 mM coenzyme Q1 in ethanol. The reaction was carried out for 5 min at room temperature (RT) under continuous shaking and quenched with a fivefold molar excess of HCl. The complete reduction of coenzyme Q was measured spectrophotometrically following the shift of absorption maximum from 275 to 290 nm. The product was quantified using a NanoDrop Ultra spectrophotometer with ε290nm = 3.5 mM−1 cm−1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Construct design, cloning, and site-directed mutagenesis

Tetrapodal ancestral COQ8A and B coding sequences deprived of mitochondrial signal peptide were obtained as previously described (7). The pathological human COQ8B variants (21, 34) and the autophosphorylating mutant of human COQ8A reported by Pagliarini’s group (14) were aligned with the TetCOQ8B gene (table S2), and the corresponding variants were obtained with site-directed mutagenesis using the QuickChange protocol (41). Primers were purchased from Metabion (table S11). Briefly a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mix (25 μl) was prepared with the following: 12.5 μl of PFuUltra II Hotstart PCR Master Mix (Agilent), 1 μl of primer forward 10 μM, 1 μl of primer reverse 10 μM, 1 μl of template plasmid (100 ng μl−1), 0.4 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 9.1 μl of MQ water. Mutations were checked at each cycle by Sanger sequencing (Macrogen Europe). N-terminal truncated constructs of both TetCOQ8A and B were designed by aligning their full length-sequence with the previously reported construct of human COQ8A (fig. S2) (14). Truncated coding sequences cloned in pGEX-6P-1 vector were obtained from GenScript. Truncated constructs are referred to as TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8B ΔN89, respectively.

Protein expression and purification

All ancestral TetCOQ8 constructs were expressed in E. coli BL21-CodonPlus-RP cells transformed by heat-shock at 42°C for 25 s. Single colonies were inoculated in LB supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg ml−1) and chloramphenicol and grown at 37°C, 200 rpm, overnight. Ten milliliters of preculture was inoculated in 1 liter of Terrific Broth, grown at 37°C, 200 rpm, until an optical density at 600 nm of 0.5 to 0.7 was reached. Expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside followed by overnight incubation for 16 hours at 18°C, 200 rpm. TetCOQ8B full-length WT and point mutants were purified as insoluble proteins, whereas TetCOQ8A and B N-terminally truncated variants were purified as soluble proteins following previously published methods (7) and gel-filtered in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) prior to crystallization. Tetrapod ancestral COQ3-7 and 9 were expressed and purified as reported (7). For simplicity, unless specifically stated, we will refer to tetrapod ancestral COQ proteins simply as COQs in the following methods.

Liposome preparation

Liposomes were prepared from a mix of distearoylphosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, egg phosphatidyl choline, and dioleoylphosphatidylserine in a 65:25:5:5 ratio at a total lipid concentration of 10 mM in ethanol. Either cardiolipin (10% of total dry lipids), PPHB (2% of total dry lipids), coenzyme Q4 (2% of total dry lipids), or a combination of those were added to the lipid mix and sonicated in a water bath for 30 min. Lipid mixtures were air-dried under a stream of argon and resuspended 1:20 in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5). Lipids resuspensions were sonicated in a water bath for 30 min at 40°C. Liposomes were extruded using a mini-extruder device (Avanti) equipped with a 0.1-μm pore size polycarbonate membrane (Avanti) maintained at 60°C.

Far-Western blot detection of in vitro phosphorylated protein

Protein phosphorylation was detected using a streptavidin-HRP conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a γ-PEG8-ATP-biotin probe (Jena Bioscience). Reactions were prepared in 100 μl of buffer A [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, and 10% (w/v) glycerol] supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) n-Dodecyl-β-D-Maltopyranoside (DDM) with 5 μM TetCOQ8B, 150 μM γ-PEG8-ATP-biotin, and 5 μM of individual COQ proteins. A sample without any target protein was prepared to assess any autophosphorylation. The autophosphorylating mutant A172G, K109R was used a positive control. Incubations were carried out overnight at 25°C in a bench-top shaking block. After the reaction, 50 μl of each reaction was sampled and supplemented with 100 U of unspecific phosphatase from phage λ and 150 μM MnCl2 and further incubated overnight at 25°C. Fifteen microliters from both samples was loaded on SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) in duplicate and either stained (Coomassie), or transferred on a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Bio-Rad) using a Trans-Blot turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad). The membrane was blocked in 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) and blotted with streptavidin-HRP conjugate diluted 1:10000 in phosphate-buffered saline, both provided in the Pierce Far-Western blot kit for biotinylated proteins (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Development was carried out with solutions provided in the kit. The experiment was additionally carried out in a titration of TetCOQ8B ranging from 20 to 0.08 μM using COQ3 with and without COQ6 as targets. Integration of band intensities was performed with Image Lab (Bio-Rad). Normalization and nonlinear regression were carried out with GraphPad Prism 9.

Protein PEGylation and acetylation

PEGylated TetCOQ8B was obtained as previously described (23). Briefly, O-methyl-O′-succinyl PEG5000 N-succinimidyl ester (methoxyPEG-NHS) (2 mg ml−1) was mixed with protein (1 mg ml−1) in 10 mM sodium bicarbonate. Reaction was carried out for 1 hour at RT. Reaction was quenched with 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at 4°C. The excess of reactant was removed by desalting using a HiTrap column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) containing 0.05% (w/v) DDM and an Äkta Pure system (Cytiva). Similarly, TetCOQ8B was acetylated using the same concentration of acetyl-NHS and submitted to bottom-up proteomics to map NHS-reactive lysine residues.

Analytical size exclusion chromatography

Analytical size exclusion chromatography was performed to assess the PEGylation of TetCOQ8B. Fifty microliters of the quenched reaction or the same concentration of unmodified protein was injected into a Superdex 200 5/150 Increase column (Cytiva) previously equilibrated in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) containing 0.05% (w/v) analytical grade DDM using an Äkta Pure system (Cytiva).

Enzyme kinetics

ATPase activity was tested as previously described (7) using the malachite-green assay for free inorganic phosphate detection. Experiments were conducted with 150 μM ATP and 1 μM TetCOQ8 in buffer A supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) DDM in a 200-μl volume at 25°C, 200 rpm, in a bench-top heating block for 10 min. Fifty microliters was incubated for 10 min with 100 μl of the dye in the dark, and absorbance was recorded at 620 nm using a ClarioStar plate reader (BMG Labtech) using 96-well transparent flat-bottom plates (Greiner). A negative control in the absence of ATP was used as blank. Phosphate concentration was determined by building a calibration line with standards provided in the kit. Linear regression was performed with GraphPad Prism 9.

TetCOQ8B full-length activity was tested in a 50 μM to 48 nM serial dilution of the COQ metabolon (COQ3-7 and 9). Activity of TetCOQ8B full-length point mutants was tested under basal conditions, in the presence of 5 μM COQ metabolon and in combination with 10 μM intermediate 1. Activity of TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8B ΔN89 was tested in detergent-free buffer A in the presence of 1:3 dilutions of the above-described liposomes at a final protein concentration of 500 nM.

Small-scale reactions

Overnight small-scale reactions were performed as previously described (23). The TetCOQ proteins forming the metabolon were individually expressed and purified. They were then pooled together to reconstruct the intact metabolon. Briefly, reactions initiated from all intermediates and contained 1 μM of each TetCOQ protein (TetCOQ3-7, TetCOQ9, and TetCOQ8B), 2 μM FDXR and FDX2, 250 μM FAD, 1 mM S-adenosylmethionine, 150 μM MgCl2, 25 μM ZnCl2, and 1 mM of the substrate, with NAD(P)H regeneration (300 μM NADP+ and NAD+, 1.2 U of glucose dehydrogenase, and 1.2 mM glucose) and ATP regeneration (1 mM ADP, 4 U of pyruvate kinase, 5 mM inorganic phosphate, and 5 mM phosphoenolpyruvate). Reactions of the subpathways were prepared similarly with a subset of TetCOQs and TetCOQ8B. Feedback inhibition of coenzyme Q was tested similarly in the presence of 20 μM coenzyme Q10 and using 5 μM intermediate 1 as starting substrate. Activity of the metabolon was also tested using PEGylated TetCOQ8B and TetCOQ8B mutants using intermediate 1 as starting substrate. In addition, to discriminate the effect of protein phosphorylation and ATPase activity, the metabolon was reconstructed in the absence of ATP, and prephosphorylation was achieved by 6-hour incubation with 100 μM ATP without any regeneration. Subsequently, controls were made by adding pyruvate kinase and phosphoenolpyruvate to the reaction, while 100 U of unspecific phosphatase from phage λ and 150 μM MnCl2 were incubated overnight. Intermediate 1 was added to all samples simultaneously and were incubated overnight. All reactions using mono-prenylated intermediates were quenched by adding 1:3 final volume acetonitrile, oxidized by adding 10 mM benzoquinone, and diluted 1:10 in water:acetonitrile 2:1 containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 1 μM sorbicillin as an internal standard for downstream UHPLC-HRMS analyses.

In addition, small-scale reactions were carried out using a 1:3 dilution of the above-described liposomes containing tetra-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate as substrate. Experiments with liposomes were performed with the soluble TetCOQ8AΔN99 and TetCOQ8BΔN89 to avoid the need of detergents, which may disrupt the liposome integrity. The TetCOQ metabolon (TetCOQ3-7 and TetCOQ9) was prepared as a 50 μM stock, from which detergent was removed using HiPPR Pierce spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific) previously equilibrated in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) following the manufacturer’s instructions. At the end of the overnight reaction, liposomes were extracted with chloroform 1:1 for downstream UHPLC-HRMS analyses.

Ligand copurification assay

TetCOQ8B (50 μM) was incubated with 1 mM of either intermediate 1 or coenzyme Q1 in a final volume of 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) DDM for 30 min in ice. The unbound excess of ligand was removed by gel filtration with a Superdex 200 Increase 5/150 column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in the same buffer on an Äkta Pure system (Cytiva). The protein peak fractions were pooled and concentrated back to the starting volume using an Amicon centrifugal concentrator. Excess of detergent was removed using HiPPR resin spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific) pre-equilibrated in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5). The protein was unfolded with 1:3 acetonitrile and precipitated by centrifugation at 20,000g for 10 min. The supernatant was diluted in water:acetonitrile 2:1 containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 1 μM sorbicillin as an internal standard for downstream UHPLC-HRMS analyses. The experiments with intermediate 1 were repeated also in the presence of 2 mM ADP or AMP-PNP and 4 mM MnCl2 in the buffer.

Extraction from liposomes assay

TetCOQ8AΔN99 or TetCOQ8BΔN89 (20 μM) were incubated with a 1:3 dilution of the above-described liposomes in 200 μl of 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) with 1 mM MgCl2 and 150 μM ATP for 10 min at 37°C, 200 rpm. Protein was separated from liposomes by passing through an Amicon Ultra 0.5 with a 100-kDa cutoff (Merck) at 5000g for 15 min. Retentate and filtrate were analyzed with a Prometheus Panta (Nanotemper) using standard capillaries to monitor the presence of protein in the filtrate following its intrinsic fluorescence and the absence of liposomes using DLS. Lipids were extracted from the filtrate in two volumes of chloroform and submitted to UHPLC-HRMS.

UHPLC-HRMS

Mono-prenylated compounds

Mono-prenylated pathway intermediates and coenzyme Q1 were detected and quantitated as previously described (23). Briefly, samples were analyzed on a X500B quadrupole time-of-flight High-Resolution Mass-Spectrometry (qTOF HRMS) coupled to an ExionLC (Sciex) equipped with a Kinetex EVO C18 column (100 mm by 2.1 mm, 2.6-μm particle size; Phenomenex) at a flow rate of 0.2 ml min−1. Mobile phases consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The gradient was 2% B (0 to 0.1 min), 2–66% B (0.1 to 32.0 min), and 66–2% B (32.0 to 35.0 min). MS parameters were as follows: curtain gas 30 psi, ion source gas 1 at 45 psi, ion source gas 2 at 55 psi, temperature 450°C, spray voltage +5500 V, declustering potential +50 V, collision energy +10 V, and full-scan range m/z 50 to 1000 in positive mode. Mass calibration was performed prior to analysis using ESI positive calibration solution (SCIEX). Pathway intermediates, including intermediates 1, 2, 3, 4a, 4b, and 5ox and coenzyme Q1, were quantified using extracted ion chromatograms of the [M + H]+ ions, with sorbicillin as an internal standard. Standard solutions were prepared in the range 3 to 100 μM in purification buffer supplemented with sorbicillin, diluted 1:3 in acetonitrile containing 1 μM sorbicillin, and injected in duplicate. A mass error threshold of ±5 parts per million was applied for intermediate identification.

Tetra-prenylated compounds

Tetra-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate and coenzyme Q4 were identified and quantitated in liposome-based experiments after extraction in twice the reaction volume with chloroform using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Ultimate3000 LC (Dionex) equipped with an Ascentis Express F5 column (150 mm by 3.0 mm; 2.7-μm particle size; Supelco). Mobile phases consisted of 60% acetonitrile, 40% water (A) and 90% isopropanol, 10% acetone (B) both supplemented with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The gradient was 30–65% B (0 to 10 min), 65–85% B (10 to 13 min), 85–100% B (13 to 14 min), 100% B (14 to 16.5 min), 100–30% B (16.5 to 0.16.6 min), and 30% B (16.6 to 22 min). MS parameters were as follows: positive ion voltage 4000 V, negative ion voltage 3500 V, sheath gas 30 arbitrary units, aux gas 10 arbitrary units, sweep gas 3 arbitrary units, and ion transfer tube temperature 350°C. A full-scan negative polarity MS acquisition was carried out in the 1 to 3.5 min range for detection of tetra-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate (RT, 2.8 min). Resolution was 120,000, with scan range 50 to 500 Da, RF lenses 70%, AGC target standards, and microscans 1. An associated targeted MSMS experiment was carried out at resolution 15,000 with an HCD collision energy of 30% by selecting the [M - H]− parental ion 409.2727 Da. A full-scan positive polarity MS acquisition was carried out in the 3 to 18 min range for detection of coenzyme Q4 (RT, 4.5 min), with the same parameters above described. An associated targeted MSMS experiment was carried out with the same parameters above described by selecting the [M + H]+ parental ion 455.3191 Da. Quantitation was carried out using an external calibration line prepared by serial diluting in the 50 to 0.1 μM range a multistandard solution prepared in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) and extracted in chloroform as above described. Quantitation was performed with QuanBrowser within the Excalibur framework (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the 409.27 to 365.28 Da transition for tetra-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate and the 455.31 to 197.08 Da transition for coenzyme Q4 (fig. S30).

Nucleotides

Nucleotides were analyzed using a X500B qTOF (SCIEX) coupled to an ExionLC (SCIEX) equipped with an Adamas HILIC (100 mm by 2.1 mm; 2.7-μm particle size; SepaChrom) at a flow rate of 0.3 ml min−1. Mobile phases consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both containing 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0). The gradient was 80–0% B (0 to 17 min), 0% B (17 to 25 min), 0–80% B (25 to 25.1 min), and 80% B (25 to 30 min). MS parameters were as follows: curtain gas 30 psi, ion source gas 1 at 45 psi, ion source gas 2 at 55 psi, temperature 500°C, spray voltage −4500 V, declustering potential −80 V, collision energy −10 V, and full-scan range m/z 50 to 1000 in negative mode. MRM mode was used following the [M - H]− precursor ion 505.953 Da for ATP and 425.980 for ADP, and the fragment ion 79 Da for both nucleotides. For fragmentation, declustering potential was −150 V for ATP and −100 V for ADP, and collision energy was −96 V for ATP and −80 V for ADP. Mass calibration was performed prior to analysis using ESI negative calibration solution (SCIEX). ATP was quantitated using an external calibration ranging from 80 to 1.25 μM prepared in buffer and diluted 1:3 in acetonitrile (fig. S31). All experiments were performed in duplicate.

Nanodifferential scanning fluorimetry

Nanodifferential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) was performed following the intrinsic protein fluorescence (emission 330 nm and 350 nm) using Tycho NT.6 (Nanotemper). TetCOQ8AΔN99 or TetCOQ8BΔN89 were used. Samples were measured at a final protein concentration of 1 mg ml−1 in a temperature gradient ranging from 35° to 95°C. ADP and AMP-PNP were titrated to determine their dissociation constant in the presence of 4 mM MgCl2 or 4 mM MnCl2. Data were plotted as F350/F330 ratio and its first derivative to retrieve inflection temperatures. ΔTm values were obtained as difference with the apo protein Tm. Dose response curves were fitted by nonlinear regression of ΔTm values versus nucleotide concentrations on a logarithmic scale using GraphPad Prism 9.

NanoDSF was performed using a Prometheus Panta system (Nanotemper) and standard capillaries (Nanotemper) to evaluate the stability of unmodified and PEGylated full-length TetCOQ8B. Samples were measured at a final protein concentration of 1 mg ml−1 in a temperature range from 25° to 95°C in a 0.5°C min−1 ramp. Samples were measured as apo protein and in complex with 1 mM ADP and 2 mM MnCl2.

Dynamic light scattering

Liposome homogeneity was checked with DLS using Prometheus Panta (Nanotemper) using standard capillaries (Nanotemper). Liposome preparations were diluted 1:20 in a volume of 10 μl of 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5). DLS was also used to compare unmodified and PEGylated TetCOQ8B. Protein concentration was adjusted to 1 mg ml−1 for measurements. Experiments were performed in two independent replicates, each consisting of 10 averaged measurements of 5 s.

Blue-Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

NativePAGE was performed to assess TetCOQ metabolon formation. TetCOQ3-7 and 9 were mixed each at a final concentration of 5 μM in the presence or absence of equimolar unmodified or PEGylated TetCOQ8B in a volume of 20 μl of 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5). Samples were loaded onto a Novex 4–16% bis-tris precasted gel (Invitrogen) using the NativePAGE sample buffer 4X (Invitrogen). The NativeMark unstained protein standard (Invitrogen) was used as a ladder. Anode and Dark Blue Cathode Buffers (Invitrogen) have been used for the run. Electrophoresis was run at 4°C at 150 V for 60 min and then at 250 V for 90 min. The gel was fixed and stained using Coomassie R-250. The higher–molecular weight bands were excised and submitted to bottom-up proteomics analysis.

Bottom-up proteomics

Acetylated TetCOQ8B was digested in solution, whereas excised bands from NativePAGE were digested in gel. Acetylated TetCOQ8B (100 μg) was prepared in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and treated with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 30 min at 60°C. The sample was digested in solution with 2 μg of UHPLC-grade trypsin gold (Promega) overnight at 37°C whereas excised NativePAGE bands were destained in 50% (v/v) acetonitrile in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, reduced with 10 mM DTT, dried with pure acetonitrile, and rehydrated with 50 μl of UHPLC-grade trypsin gold (2 μg/ml; Promega) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Digestion was carried out overnight at 37°C. After in gel digestion, the digest was collected and pooled with subsequent extraction from the gel bands with 50% (v/v) acetonitrile and 1 % (v/v) formic acid. Digested samples were dried using Concentrator Plus (Eppendorf) equipped with a vacuum pump and maintained at 30°C. Samples were resuspended in 50 μl of UHPLC-grade water with 5% (v/v) formic acid.

Samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Ultimate3000 LC (Dionex) equipped with an Accucore aQ column (100 by 2.1 mm, 2.6-μm particle size; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Injection volume was 10 μl. Mobile phases were UHPLC-grade water (A) and acetonitrile (B) both supplemented with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The gradient was carried out at 0.3 ml min−1 as follows: 2–40% B (0 to 16 min), 40–90% B (16 to 17 min), hold 90% B (17 to 19 min), 90–2% B (19 to 19.5 min), and hold 2% B (19.5 to 25 min). ESI source parameters were as follows: positive spray voltage 3600 V, sheath gas 20 arbitrary units, aux gas 10 arbitrary units, sweep gas 0 arbitrary units, ion transfer tube temperature 320°C, and vaporizer temperature 100°C. MS1 parameters were as follows: resolution 120,000, scan range 350 to 2000, RF lenses 80%, AGC target standards (1 × 106), maximum injection time mode auto, and microscans 1. MS2 were collected with collision energy set on 25 V and resolution on 15,000. Precursor ions were selected in data-dependent acquisition mode by applying a dynamic exclusion filter.

Results were processed using MaxQuant 2.7.5 (42) using an ad hoc database including common contaminants, E. coli proteome, and TetCOQ sequences. False discovery rate (FDR) was set at 1%.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

1H NMR was used to monitor protein-ligand interactions by observing quenching and shifting of the ligand signals. TetCOQ8AΔN99 or TetCOQ8BΔN89 were used to avoid the requirement of detergents whose signals would have interfered with those of the ligands. Proteins were buffer exchanged in 20 mM tris-d11 (pH 8.0) at 4°C in D2O using a PD Spin-Trap G-25 (Cytiva) column. Intermediate 1 and coenzyme Q1 stocks were prepared in CDCl3. Samples were prepared in a 400-μl volume of 20 mM tris-d11 (pH 8.0) at 4°C in D2O with 10 μM protein and 200 μM ligand. 3-(Trimethylsilyl)-2,2,3,3-tetradeuteropropionic acid (TSP; 20 μM) was supplemented to each sample as an internal reference. A competition experiment was prepared by mixing both ligands in the same sample. As a control, the spectrum of intermediate 1 was collected also in the presence of 10 μM lysozyme, BSA, or TetCOQ8BΔN89 V396M mutant. Spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz Avance III instrument (Bruker) with a zg30 pulse program and the following acquisition parameters: TD 65536, DS 0, NS 60, and TD0 1. Spectra were analyzed with TopSpin (Bruker) by aligning the control spectrum recorded in the absence of any protein with the sample ones. The TSP peak was used as an internal reference control. 1H spectra of tetra-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate and coenzyme Q4 were collected in DMSO-d6 at a final concentration of 1 mM with 256 scans.

Binding kinetics

Binding kinetics was measured in a competition coupled assay by following DCPIP reduction by coenzyme Q1H2. An end-point assay was performed using 50 μM TetCOQ8B (either WT or V396M), 10 μM coenzyme Q1H2, and 100 μM DCPIP in a final volume of 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) and 0.05% (w/v) DDM using a ClarioStar plate reader (BMG Labtech) with 96-well transparent flat-bottom plates (Greiner) to validate the approach. Controls were made using single reaction component and oxidized coenzyme Q1. Reduction of DCPIP was measured following the decrease of absorbance at 600 nm (ε600nm = 19.1 mM−1 cm−1). Subsequently, a competition assay was performed by titrating intermediates 1 and 4a, performing a jump-dilution of equimolar preincubated TetCOQ8B with coenzyme Q1H2 (20 μM final) in a final volume of 100 μl. Reduced coenzyme Q release was monitored as a reporter of binding of the coenzyme Q intermediate following reduction of DCPIP using a Cary 100 ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (Agilent) equipped with 10-mm quartz cuvettes (Hellma). The determined concentration of reduced DCPIP was plotted as a binding curve by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 9.

X-ray crystallography

TetCOQ8AΔN99 was crystallized at 10 mg ml−1 in cocrystallization with 2 mM AMP-PNP and 4 mM MnCl2 using the Morpheus HT-96 crystallization screening (Molecular Dimensions) in 0.2-μl drops in a sitting-drop vapor diffusion MRC plate (Molecular Dimensions) and an Oryx8 robot (Douglas Instruments). Diffracting crystals were obtained from well F2 containing 0.12 M monosaccharides mix (0.2 M d-glucose, 0.2 M d-mannose, 0.2 M d-galactose, 0.2 M l-fucose, 0.2 M d-xylose, and 0.2 M N-acetyl-d-glucosamine), 0.1 M buffer system 1 (pH 6.5) (0.5 M imidazole and 0.5 M MES monohydrate acid), and 50% (v/v) precipitant mix 2 [40% (v/v) ethylene glycol and 20% (w/v) PEG8000]. TetCOQ8BΔN89 was crystallized at 15 mg ml−1 in cocrystallization with 2 mM ADP and 4 mM MnCl2 using the same commercial screening and setup. Diffracting crystals were obtained from well B8 containing 0.09 M halogen mix (0.3 M sodium fluoride, 0.3 M sodium bromide, and 0.3 M sodium iodide), 0.1 M buffer system 2 (pH 7.5) (0.5 M sodium Hepes and 0.5 M bicine), 50% (v/v) precipitant mix 4 [25% (v/v) MPD, 25% (w/v) PEG1000, and 25% (w/v) PEG3350]. Crystals were fished and cryo-cooled without the need of a cryo-protectant solution. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the ID30A-1 and ID30A-3 beamlines (ESRF, Grenoble, France) at 100 K. Data processing was carried out with the CCP4I suite (43). XDS and Aimless were used for data integration and scaling (44, 45). Phase problem was solved by molecular replacement using AlphaFold3 inferred models as searching model using PhaserMR (46, 47). Refinement was performed using Refmac5 (48).

Bioinformatics analyses

bindEmbed21

For the analysis of functional sites, the bindEmbed21 model (https://github.com/Rostlab/bindPredict) was applied to the human COQ8B sequence (UniProt ID: Q96D53). A threshold of 0.5 on a scale ranging from 0 to 1 was defined to consider predicted functional residues (31).

DiffDock

Docking poses of the mono-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate were generated using DiffDock-L using the TetCOQ8A-AMP-PNP complex and the TetCOQ8B-ADP complex experimental structures using NeuroSnap server (32). Fifty docking poses have been generated in each experiment. DiffDock confidence value distributions were analyzed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test using GraphPad Prism9.

Boltz-2

TetCOQ8B and deca-prenylated p-hydroxybenzoate complex was inferred using Bolt-2 using the NeuroSnap server (49).

PETIMOT

To generate models based on protein flexibility, we used PETIMOT (https://github.com/PhyloSofS-Team/PETIMOT) with the following command

python -m petimot infer \

--model-path weights/default_2025-02-07_21-54-02_epoch_33.pt \

Models displaying the most divergent conformations were superimposed and subsequently visualized in PyMOL using arrows to indicate the predicted displacement of the Cα atoms in the structure (30).
