 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Identify most promising drop-in or novel products from anhydrous sugars based on predicted
 performance, synthesis pathway, and market.

 1

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies

 Summary of Research Results:

 Purpose: The goal of this work is to identify promising drop-in or novel polymers that can be
 produced from levoglucosenone (LGO), CyreneTM, or other anhydrous sugars. LGO is a
 potentially important feedstock for polymers produced from renewable sources and can be
 produced in high yields by the company Circa from the pyrolysis of cellulose and biomass. NLR
 has a long history with anhydrosugars, pyrolysis and biomass-based polymers and has recently
 developed machine learning (ML) tools for predicting polymer properties. The DOE has recently
 supported efforts to identify coproducts from pyrolysis of biomass and their use in the production
 of performance advantaged polymers. DOE is also supporting the development of ML and
 molecular modeling for predicting and understanding polymer properties. NLR will leverage this
 expertise and experience to map out the pathways to polymers from LGO, use ML to identify the
 properties of novel polymers and estimate the market application space. From this effort NLR
 will deliver a summary report that outlines the most promising pathways based on quantitative
 and qualitative metrics for producing polymers from LGO.

 Task 1 Objective (Month 1): NLR will develop a set of synthesis pathways to polymers
 accessible through LGO, Cyrene, or other anhydrous sugars. NLR’s assessment will consider the
 synthesis pathways, the material performance and applications congruent with the predicted
 performance of the material, pricing and scale of materials within the same application space,
 and the commercial readiness. To assess the synthesis pathways, NLR will limit the number of
 synthetic steps, generally to 1 or 2 reactions to monomers, so the overall process yield, and
 production volume can potentially remain high. When available, yields, conversion, side
 products, and the scale at which the chemistry has been performed for each step will be used in
 the assessment of any pathway. Additionally, chemical intermediates that could result in multiple
 products will also be considered to determine if the intermediate could serve as a platform
 chemical. To assess commercial readiness, NLR will consider the technical readiness level of the
 production pathway and any supporting technologies that are needed (e.g., advanced separations,
 catalysts) for commercialization. Industrial precedence for proposed chemistries for both
 polymer precursors and the polymer will be considered to understand the challenges that might
 arise during scaleup. The results from this survey will guide the work in Task 2.

 Results:

 In this task, we identified 53 monomers, 5 pathways with 8 monomer classes. The polymer
 chemistries includes polyesters, polycarbonates, polyamides, polyacrylates/olefins, non-
 isocyanate polyurethanes, polyethers.

 Task 2 Objective (Month 2): NLR will use a NLR’s machine learning polymer property
 prediction tool, PolyML, to determine material properties (i.e., glass transition, melt temperature,
 density, modulus, and the gas permeability of O2, N2, CO2, & H2O). Domain of validity testing
 will be used to determine if new materials fall within the domain of prediction, and the training
 set may be augmented, when data is available, to improve domain overlap. From the predicted
 performance, application spaces will be identified for novel polymers. Advantages and
 disadvantaged relative to incumbent materials will be considered.

 Results:

 The prediction database of polymers that use anhydrous sugar-based monomers contained 32,336
 unique polymers. This was arrived at by creating the combinatorial set of all polymers from the
 list of identified anhydrous based-sugar monomers with the Sigma-KEGG monomer database.
 The python package monomers-to-polymers (m2p) was used for the structure building. Cross
 validation mean absolute errors (MAE) were used to benchmark the performance of the model.

 In light weighting applications, such as automotive, a wide range of materials were used such as
 polyurethanes, polypropylene, polyolefins, and polyesters, among others. Figure 2 shows all
 material classes from which anhydrous sugar-based polymers were predicted. The selected
 screening criteria was a density ≤ 1.05 and a tensile modulus of ≥ 1,000 MPa, which is highlighted
 by the shaded gray region in Figure 2. Polyolefins, especially poly(propylene), have very low
 density, while engineering polymers, such as acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS) have high
 modulus. The selected performance constraints were wide enough to account for error in the
 prediction while providing a reasonable down selection screen to identify candidates.

 Figure 2. Scatter plots showing tensile modulus as a function of density predictions.

 Task 3 Objective (Month 3): Based on likely applications, drop-in or novel polymers will be
 compared to existing materials market scale and pricing. Market scale will be based on the
 volume of product that could be accessed from biomass within the United States and will be
 compared to the market volume necessary for that application space. Pricing of materials utilized
 within the application will be considered to determine a general price that could be expected
 from an LGO derived replacement. Market and material performance data will be combined, and
 the most promising pathways will be determined. Down selection will be recommended to the
 most promising pathways based on performance and potential market (scale), preliminary
 process design (mass/energy balances), and benchmark against existing technology.

 Results:

 The automotive industry has moved towards light-weight materials and largely leveraged
 polymers and composites to reduce weight while maintaining structural integrity. Typically, cars
 are composed of 50% plastic by volume and 10% by weight.35 Polypropylene and polyurethanes
 compose the largest percent of polymers used in automotive applications at 32% and 17%,
 respectively.36 Polypropylene is a very lightweight polyolefin with moderate mechanical
 performance and will likely remain in high demand in the automotive space. The adoption of
 electric vehicles is also expected to impact the utilization of polymers in automotive applications.
 As internal combustion engines are replaced, the thermal performance of polymers in vehicles
 can be reduced which is reducing the need for engineering type materials, such as ABS. Overall,
 trends towards light-weight materials are expected to continue in this application.

 From this initial screening 38 materials were identified, which were all within the poly(olefin)
 subgroup. In reviewing these candidates, two structural trends were observed. First, 5 anhydrous
 sugar-based monomers were found in the polymers, 3 of which were methacrylate derivatives
 and 2 of which had acrylate functionality. The second structural trend of note is all candidates
 were copolymers where anhydrous sugar-based monomers were combined with linear or
 branches poly(olefins).

 These structural trends were further explored to investigate how performance of the materials
 could be improved and to understand structure-function relationships underpinning the predicted
 performance. The 5 anhydrous sugar-based materials were combined with a specified set of linear
 and branched olefins monomers and predictions were made on these materials, Figure 3A. Figure
 3B-D highlights three aspects of using monomer selection to alter material properties. Figure 3B
 plots tensile modulus as function of density and highlights the predicted polymers as a function of
 the anhydrous sugar-based monomer. From this plot, it was observed that the ma-Cyrene monomer
 trends towards having the lowest density and highest tensile modulus. Figure 3C shows the same
 data but highlights the materials based on olefin chain length. It was observed that there is a general
 tradeoff between density and tensile modulus as a function of chain length. As the chain length
 decreased, both tensile modulus and density increased. Figure 3D shows the same data but with
 highlights the materials based on branching. Increasing branching decreases density while
 modulus remains relatively constant.

 4

 The dataset in Figure 3 provides a data driven hypothesis for designing lightweight materials using
 anhydrous sugar-based monomers. Key design findings are:

 • The ma-Cyrene monomer is the best candidate for lightweight, high modulus materials
 • increasing olefin chain length will increase module and density
 • branched olefins can be used to reduce density
