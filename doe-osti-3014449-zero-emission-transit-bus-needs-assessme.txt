 Zero-Emissions Transit Bus Needs Assessment
 1. NEEDS ASSESSMENT
 1.1. INTRODUCTION
 The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) and Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) have made significant
investments in an electrified transportation system that is convenient, affordable, accessible, reliable, and
safe. While much attention has been paid to the investments in personal, light duty vehicle electrification
efforts, these initiatives also focus on transforming the nation’s transit and school bus fleets to zero-
emissions vehicles (ZEVs), helping ensure a cleaner multimodal transportation system for all users.
 With this funding newly available to transit agencies, the Joint Office of Energy and Transportation
seeks to support these agencies by providing technical assistance to help transit service providers
transition their fleets and operations to zero-emissions systems.
 The Transit Bus Electrification Needs Assessment project serves the Joint Office in their effort to aid
transit agencies by exploring the existing bus electrification processes, barriers to implementation, and
complementary funding sources. This project is a qualitative inquiry that investigates the state of practice
in transit bus electrification while also shedding light on the barriers to electrification as identified by
transit providers. This information will enable the Joint Office to facilitate their vision of a future
transportation system where everyone can ride and drive electric by providing meaningful and targeted
technical assistance to transit service providers.

 1.2. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

  1.2.1. Introduction
To provide effective technical assistance to transit service providers, the Joint Office commissioned this
study to examine the needs of agencies as they strive to deploy zero-emissions vehicles in their fleets. As
part of this effort, researchers from the Idaho National Laboratory conducted interviews with
representatives from transit service providers to answer key questions in understanding the existing
processes in implementing zero-emissions vehicles as well as identifying barriers so that the Joint Office
may provide tailored support. The insights from this report should be interpreted as representative of the
sample of agencies interviewed, but not necessarily as representative of the industry. While we have made
efforts to ensure that our sample is reasonably representative of the transit industry, the scope of our
efforts is not sufficient to say with certainty that sentiments shared by our respondents would be
ubiquitously shared by the entire industry.

  1.2.2. Key Findings
1.2.2.1. Bus Facilities, Operations, and Planning
 Bus facilities and operations was a discussion category that included a great deal of content covering
nine distinct topic areas, six of which we reported here because they included sentiments shared by
multiple agencies. From these discussions, we know that planning for BEBs requires additional
considerations, specifically with respect to the space needed within facilities for charging and
maintenance. While there are many challenges associated with providing adequate facilities and planning
the operations of this novel technology, this discussion category contained some degree of optimism
expressed by our key informants that they will eventually meet the challenges presented.
1.2.2.2. Funding and Finance
 In discussing funding and finance, our informants indicated that funding availability influences transit
agencies’ ability to work toward zero-emissions transition goals. In general, many of the transit agencies
that we spoke to were relieved by the increased funding that is currently available (most notable and
commonly mentioned was the increased funding to expand the Lo-No grant program), but many
expressed concerns about long-term funding needs and the uncertainty around the longevity of the
increased funding. Additional concerns were highlighted regarding funding availability for the required
infrastructure and operational costs associated with ZEV fleets. Finally, most of the agencies mentioned
the same primary funding sources, which could suggest that a comprehensive funding inventory specific
to transit agencies transitioning their fleets to ZEVs could be a useful resource to ensure all transit
agencies are aware of all potential funding resources.
1.2.2.3. Accessibility
 When asked about how they consider accessibility in the context of transitioning to zero-emissions
fleets, agencies responded with a great deal of variety, both in the way they considered the construct, and
the degree to which the agency has considered or planned for it thus far. Some agencies responded that
they had not given accessibility much thought, and others simply implied that it is inherent with a
transition to zero-emissions fleets as they produce fewer emissions and typically serve transit-dependent
residents. On the other end of the spectrum, some agencies had carefully considered policies in place to
ensure that investments in zero-emissions buses would benefit community members in ways that are fair.
Some informants pointed out that there is a tension in achieving both accessibility goals and transition
goals, as transit providers work with constrained resources, and funds put toward new technologies might
take from funds available to ensure adequate service region-wide. Others, however, were careful to point

 2

out that their priority was to provide quality and reliable service, and they would not sacrifice that priority
to achieve zero-emissions transition goals.
1.2.2.4. Vehicles and Technological Constraints
 When asked about the challenges agencies face as they transition to zero-emissions vehicles, the most
common response was related to the range provided by BEBs. Almost half of all those that we
interviewed cited concerns stemming from the lower range available from BEBs compared to their diesel
counterparts. Range figures were quite variable, however, with more agencies reporting that the
capabilities of BEBs were getting closer to achieving the daily range performance of the diesel buses they
replaced. Aside from challenges related directly to range capabilities of BEBs, some agencies reported
interoperability issues between specific bus models and specific chargers, which we covered in detail in
the Bus Facilities, Operations, and Planning section of this report.
1.2.2.5. Reliability, Maintenance, and Safety
 The transition to electrified transportation hinges on the reliability, maintenance, and safety of BEBs
and HFCBs. Significant concerns around warranties, long downtimes, maintenance complexities, and the
slow availability of parts are hindering the success of this transition. These issues complicate scheduling,
fleet expansion, and add to the operational costs, despite the theoretical simplicity of electric systems.
Addressing these challenges requires improved OEM support, better planning for tools and equipment,
and clear maintenance protocols. This will enhance reliability and cost-effectiveness, supporting
sustainability and efficiency in public transit. Lastly, safety is a critical aspect of the transition to zero-
emissions fleets, and a significant gap was revealed regarding the lack of codes and standards.
1.2.2.6. Workforce Development and Training
 With workforce development and training being pivotal to the success of electrification initiatives, the
existing knowledge in hybrid technologies provides a solid starting point for electric powertrain training.
However, reliance on manufacturers for training and the varied effectiveness and availability of such
training is posing some significant hurdles. Addressing these issues through comprehensive training
programs, improved support systems, and strategic workforce development plans will be crucial for the
successful deployment of zero-emissions fleets as well as for career development of technicians interested
in acquiring skills essential for this new technology.
1.2.2.7. Policy, Programs, and Regulations
 Our informants indicated that decision-makers like boards of directors and other regulatory agencies
are essential for the successful implementation of electrification initiatives. Having a zero-emissions
transition plan proved to be important for developing internal policy and directing implementation efforts.
Additionally, public opinion was a notable factor influencing agencies’ ability to work toward zero-
emissions transition goals. Finally, agencies reported that federal standards and policies were an
important factor impacting their implementation of zero-emissions fleets.
1.2.2.8. Procurement and Supply Chain
 The cost of BEBs and other issues related to procurement and supply chain associated with zero-
emissions buses were a major concern among agencies that we interviewed. They cited high upfront costs
as a potential roadblock to their efforts to transition their fleets. Agencies reported issues with slow
delivery of vehicles, requisite infrastructure, and replacement parts. They attributed these supply chain
issues to a small number of manufacturers serving a market with high demand, an issue which has been
made even more acute by a recent influx of federal grants.
1.2.2.9. Collaboration, Capacity, and Resources
 Working with external entities is essential to transit agencies’ ability to transition to zero-
emissions fleets. Our interviews highlighted the important partnerships that agencies utilize to expand

 3

their capacity and gain technical knowledge that is otherwise not available. Agencies have turned to local
entities like MPOs, universities, and clean cities coalitions to partner and learn valuable information
critical to the success of their transition goals. Additionally, many agencies that we spoke with have
utilized highly technical expertise from consultants to lower the barriers to entering an otherwise entirely
novel and sometime intimidating realm of zero-emissions vehicles. Another partnership that was
evidently critical to transit agencies’ efforts to move to BEB fleets were local utilities. Many agencies,
particularly those who had begun their relationship early, reported successful partnerships with their local
utilities that were leading to the ability to acquire needed power in a timely fashion. Other agencies
reported, however, many difficulties in working with their utilities that were leading to delays in securing
the power they needed to charge BEB fleets. Finally, we explored what help would be beneficial to our
key informants from the Joint Office as they consider transitions or work to deploy more zero-emissions
vehicles in their fleets. Agencies shared a variety of technical assistance needs, with the most agreement
focusing on a product providing general guidance and best practices related to transitions to zero-
emissions fleets.

 1.3. METHODS
  1.3.1. Data Sources
1.3.1.1. Selection Criteria
 With the intention of collecting data from a representative sample of transit service providers, the
research team sought to interview key informants from agencies that represent a spectrum of the
following three factors:
 1) Agency Capacity
 2) Program Maturity
 3) Geographic Distribution (Regional representation)
 Agency capacity is an important differentiator between transit agencies, as their capacity will impact
their ability to provide resources and staff time to zero-emissions fleet transitions. We hypothesize that
smaller transit agencies with fewer staff and less specialization of roles will have less time and technical
expertise to dedicate to applying for funds, planning fleet transitions, and implementing zero-emissions
fleets. On the other side of the spectrum, we expect that agencies with larger capacities will have enough
staff and resources to dedicate full-time positions to this area. Because of the potential impacts of these
differences in agency capacity, we have sought to include key informants from agencies representing the
entire spectrum of capacity, from small agencies with just a few administrative staff to some of the largest
in the country.
 Another important factor for which we are trying to provide adequate representation in our study
sample is program maturity. With a major motivation of this research being to identify common
challenges that transit service providers encounter in their transition to zero-emissions fleets, we expect
that agencies at different stages in the process will likely identify different challenges as they progress.
For this reason, we have identified key informants that represent a reasonable spectrum of program
maturity that exists within the transit industry. Please note that we use the above language of “reasonable
spectrum” because we are not aware of any agencies that have yet made complete transitions to zero-
emissions vehicles.
 Finally, we aimed to achieve a sample of key informants from agencies that represent the vast
geography of US regions. Specifically, we intended to include agencies with different climactic
conditions as climate and weather conditions are known to greatly impact the performance and range of
electric powertrains. Below, figure one identifies the agencies and regions covered in our sample.

buses cannot travel as far in a day as their diesel counterparts.
  1.3.2. Data Collection and Management
 Interviews were conducted with implied consent due to minimal risk of harm to subjects and followed
the required protocols of human subject research as prescribed by the Department of Energy as well as the
Idaho National Laboratory. Interviews were led by a single INL researcher, although additional team
members attended and took notes. Interviews were scheduled for an hour in length and usually concluded
close to the scheduled time. The interviews were conducted using a semi-structured format, including pre-
vetted questions with space allowed for interview-specific follow-up questions. Interviews were not
recorded to avoid the need for informed consent, as well as to encourage candid responses. At the
conclusion of a topic, the interviewer reflected what they had heard from the key informant to validate the
data that was recorded. We have achieved additional validation of the qualitative data by sharing a
transcription of the interview with the key informant after the research team transcribed responses and
edited the transcription. No personal identifiable information has been held or retained by the researchers.
Any attributions of important quotes that might be included in any reports or public-facing products of
this research were checked with key informants prior to their publication.
 Outside of the interview process, we also collected relevant documents related to the zero-emissions
transition of bus fleets for the respective transit service providers that we interviewed. The most common
document was the Zero-Emissions Transition Plan, which is a required document to be submitted as part
of a request for Low or No Emission Program funds from FTA. Most agencies that we spoke to referred
to their transition plans, with the plan usually acting as the primary policy document guiding decision
making for fleet transitions. Additional documents included other planning and policy documents
provided by agencies or found in independent searches of public information by the research team.
  1.3.3. Data Analysis
1.3.3.1. Thematic Text Analysis
 To identify practices and conditions within the transit industry as it relates to the zero-emissions
transition, common challenges faced by transit service providers, and unifying themes around satisfying
accessibility considerations, we applied a form of thematic text analysis known as template analysis to the
qualitative data that we collected. Template analysis uses an inductive approach to qualitative data
analysis and coding that involves the searching of themes within a text as they become apparent as critical
to description of a phenomenon.

 7

 The first step of the process was the independent review of all interview transcriptions by the research
team. While the researchers reviewed the interview transcriptions, they created categories of discussion
topics that seem to emerge from the data. As opposed to organizing and coding the data into categories
that simply represent the original interview questions, we have found that organizing and categorizing the
data in alignment with higher-order discussion categories lends to a better hierarchical structure for
further coding of the data. Each researcher independently defined higher-order discussion categories, then
these categories were reviewed collectively to determine overlap between what the researchers had
identified.
 The researchers then discussed and coalesced around a set of higher-order discussion categories
against which a sample of a few interview transcriptions were be applied. Again, the researchers
independently identified themes within the text which were called topic areas. The researchers then
convened again to compare identified topic areas and discussed their respective findings until they arrived
upon a common set. Finally, within the specified topic areas, codes that represent unique sentiments
within those themes were established. This final process in the analysis was done by a single researcher to
avoid any issues related to inter-rater reliability.
 1.4. FACILITIES, OPERATIONS, AND PLANNING
  1.4.1. Introduction
 In our analysis of interview data related to the discussion category of “Bus Facilities and Operations,”
we identified six topic areas that contained sentiments noted by multiple agencies. The topic areas that we
will cover in this chapter include transition planning, charging, facilities, scheduling and routing,
operating costs, and resiliency. The transit service providers that we interviewed considered bus facilities
and operations as an integral component of their efforts to transition fleets to zero-emissions which offers
some opportunities and challenges, especially with respect to the timing of investments.
  1.4.2. Topic Area 1: Transition Planning
 Although the question was not asked directly, many of our key informants reported on their agencies’
goals with respect to transitioning their fleets to zero-emissions. Among those that reported this
information, we found target dates between 2029 and 2045. In discussions around these dates, many
agencies explained the impetus for such goals, often describing the mechanisms of policy-making that
affect programmatic direction, which we will discuss in further detail in the “POLICY, PROGRAMS,
AND REGULATIONS” chapter later in this report. The relatively wide range of target transition dates is
also reflected in a high variability of attitudes related to the agencies’ optimism about achieving those
goals. Many agencies that we interviewed felt that the goals that were set by their boards and other
external decision-makers were too ambitious and would not reasonably be achieved. These sentiments,
however, were not entirely uniform, with some informants reporting a degree of optimism, or at least
inevitability, about their capacity to completely transition their fleets by the mandated dates.
  1.4.3. Topic Area 2: Charging
 Charging was a topic area that came up in a variety of contexts, indicating that this is a significant
consideration as transit agencies work to implement zero-emissions fleets. One way that transit agencies
often talked about charging related to power and delivery type. Agencies reported charge rates, both
observed and targeted, between 6 and 200 kW. There was also a variety of power delivery modes
including alternating current (AC) 240 volt, or Level 2, wireless inductive charging, overhead conductive
charging, and direct current fast chargers (DCFC) plug-in charging. Transit service providers also
discussed different models for charging like opportunity charging en-route, layover charging required for
drivers during short breaks, and depot charging—including overnight and mid-day. En-route charging
solutions were reported to be challenging, as the logistics of installing this infrastructure required
coordination and synchronization with multiple actors. Other challenges related to charging were
mentioned by many agencies, with multiple informants discussing the constraints of charging as it relates

 8

to reliability, scheduling and blocking of BEBs. Interoperability between specific chargers and specific
vehicle models also presented itself as a challenge to some agencies. Finally, multiple agencies pointed
out that the timing of installing charging infrastructure is important. Some indicated that they were not
able to install charging equipment prior to acquiring BEBs, which created obvious challenges up to and
including not being able to put new buses into service.
  1.4.4. Topic Area 3: Facilities
 Facilities were a common topic area that came up in discussions with our key informants. The
requirements for fueling and maintaining BEBs are quite different than their internal combustion powered
counterparts, and as such, the space provided in traditional facilities are often inadequate for BEBs. Many
agencies that we spoke to reported recently completed, ongoing, or planned construction of new facilities
designed to accommodate BEBs. Some considerations for new facilities included properly placing
charging equipment, providing adequate electrical infrastructure, and coordinating space in ways that
properly accommodate BEB charging and maintenance and financial constraints. As agencies continue to
plan for future facilities, a consistently reported issue related to the challenge of acquiring real estate in
sufficient quantities within strategic locations.
  1.4.5. Topic Area 4: Scheduling and Routing
 Given the constraints inherent in battery electric powertrains, there are challenges that accompany the
scheduling and routing of BEBs. Some agencies that we interviewed indicated that they needed to rework
route blocking schemes to accommodate the shorter range of BEBs. Despite this sentiment, however, the
agencies that reported this need also expressed a strong commitment to prioritizing reliable, high-quality
service over accommodating the constraints of any novel bus technology. This means that given a choice
between providing reduced service with BEBs or maintaining a high level of service with conventionally
fueled buses, these agencies would choose the latter. Additional considerations for routing and scheduling
included applying the constrained range of BEBs to unique geographies like sprawling or expansive
service areas or extreme topography.
  1.4.6. Topic Area 5: Operating Costs
 Operating costs were a topic area that frequently came up in relation to the facilities and operations
discussion category. Interestingly, while most agencies reporting on operating costs indicated that they
were lower for BEB fleets than for diesel fleets, largely because of the cost of fuel, a few agencies
provided a contradicting sentiment. The main source for this contradiction seemed to be the way in which
agencies calculated operating costs, as those that reported higher operating costs included maintenance
costs in their comparisons. Another chapter of this report will address RELIABILITY AND
MAINTENANCE considerations of BEBs. When evaluating just fuel costs, three agencies reported
significant cost savings with BEBs compared to diesel buses. One agency reported a helpful comparison,
claiming that a diesel bus cost about $150/day to fuel, while a BEB cost only $10/day. The degree of
savings varied from one agency to another, however, with electricity rates being the key factor
contributing to that variability. Other agencies reported that the degree of savings on fuel varies with the
cost of diesel; as diesel prices rise, savings from BEBs also rise. Finally, another factor influencing the
savings associated with operating BEB fleets was time-of-use rates, with higher electric fueling costs
stemming from high-demand times of day, usually associated with mid-day charging requirements.
  1.4.7. Topic Area 6: Resiliency
 In discussions around zero-emissions bus fleets, many of our key informants indicated a particular
focus on resiliency. One concern that was raised by multiple agencies related to the potential future
vulnerability of a fueling system that is entirely reliant on a well-functioning grid. In a disaster scenario,
these agencies reported, such reliance on the grid could lead to an inability to fuel BEBs, which would
exacerbate a disaster by not allowing the agency to provide relief via public evacuation assistance.

 9

  1.4.8. Conclusion
 Bus facilities and operations was a discussion category that included a great deal of content covering
nine distinct topic areas, six of which we reported here because they included sentiments shared by
multiple agencies. From these discussions, it is clear that planning for BEBs requires additional
considerations, specifically with respect to the space needed within facilities for charging and
maintenance, scheduling and routing for vehicles that have shorter range than traditionally fueled vehicles
and charging BEBs. While there are many challenges associated with providing adequate facilities and
planning the operations of this novel technology, this discussion category contained some degree of
optimism expressed by our key informants- that they will eventually meet the challenges presented.

 1.5. FUNDING AND FINANCE
  1.5.1. Introduction
 This chapter discusses the landscape of funding, finance, and planning which encompasses two topic
areas. First, we assess the perception of funding availability and how that funding availability impacts if
and how transit agencies convert the vehicles in their fleets to zero-emissions vehicles (ZEVs). Then we
analyze the funding sources that transit agencies specifically named during the interviews.
  1.5.2. Topic Area 1: Funding Availability
 Funding availability is an important factor for transit agencies when deciding if or when they should
transition their fleet to include ZEVs. A couple of agencies reported that funding availability was driving
their decision to transition to ZEVs. To elaborate, multiple agencies reported that the current funding
situation puts their ZEV transition goals at risk or are at least delaying their plans. For example, one
agency mentioned that they are waiting for more funding to enable fueling production expansion plans,
another expressed that more federal assistance is needed to achieve their transition goals, a third agency
mentioned that they plan to transition their fleet more quickly to ZEVs as more funding becomes
available, and a fourth agency stated that funding availability hasn’t had an impact on their decision to
transition to ZEVs. A few agencies noted general difficulty in acquiring adequate funding for their ZEV
transition. One of the reasons mentioned why funding is seen as insufficient is that current funding
assumes the same spare ratio/replacement rates of conventional buses with ZEVs. As a result, this affects
the number of buses an agency can acquire due to the much higher, current cost of ZEV buses. A final
sentiment expressed by an agency was that they spend a lot of time looking for and applying for grants
which can be a challenge especially for smaller transit agencies. However, not all agencies expressed
concerns about funding limitations to support ZEV transition for transit fleets. For example, one agency
mentioned that the Lo or No Emission grant funding program operated by the Federal Transit Authority
(FTA) had an abundance of funding for ZEV transition.
 In addition to funding availability, a couple of agencies mentioned that it can be challenging to come
up with the funding needed for the financial match requirements. A couple of agencies mentioned that
funding match requirements can be challenging as some agencies are operating at a budget deficit or due
to internal budget constraints.
 For long-term ZEV transition and sustaining their fleet transition to ZEVs, a couple of agencies
expressed anxiety about potential cuts to Lo-No funding in the future, and a couple other agencies
expressed general concerns about dwindling local funding for public transit and how that would impact
ZEV transitions for transit agencies. One agency also mentioned that they will need to consider how they
will transition to different funding streams in the future if the Lo or No Emission grant funding program
funds are reduced or no longer available.
 Finally, a couple of agencies suggested that while it might be easy to acquire funding for new ZEVs,
funding the necessary supporting infrastructure and upgrades for bus facilities and covering the
operational cost of ZEVs is much more of a challenge. One agency, mentioned that some charger
infrastructure funding requires investments in charging equipment be accessible to the public, thus
limiting their ability to rely on these types of infrastructure funding sources for charging BEBs at a transit
agency.
  1.5.3. Topic Area 2: Named Funding Sources
 In addition to describing funding availability, transit agencies named specific funding sources they
have applied for or received funds from. The Lo or No Emission grant funding program operated by FTA
was the most mentioned funding source with most agencies we spoke to mentioned it as a potential
funding source and one agency mentioned that this program is the lifeblood for ZEV transition. State and
local funding sources were the second most mentioned funding source with each being mentioned by half

 11

of the agencies we spoke to. The next most mentioned funding source was the Bus and Bus Facilities
program operated by FTA which was also mentioned by half of the agencies. The Volkswagen Settlement
fund and the Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality (CMAQ) Improvement Program operated by DOT
FHWA were both mentioned by handful of agencies. A few agencies named the Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) as a funding source, but no specific funding program was mentioned. Formula
Funding provided by FTA was also mentioned by a couple of agencies. Finally, three different agencies
mentioned three different funding sources (California Energy Commission; Rebuilding American
Infrastructure with Sustainability and Equity (RAISE) operated by DOT OST; and Metropolitan Planning
Organization operated by FTA) that none of the other agencies mentioned.
  1.5.4. Conclusion
 Within this discussion category of Funding, Finance, and Planning, our informants indicated that
funding availability influences transit agencies’ ability to work toward zero-emissions transition goals. In
general, many of the transit agencies that we spoke to were relieved by the increased funding that is
currently available (most notable and commonly mentioned was the increased funding from the Joint
Office of Energy and Transportation to expand the Lo-No grant program), but many expressed concerns
about long-term funding needs and the uncertainty around the longevity of the increased funding.
Additional concerns were highlighted regarding funding availability for the required infrastructure and
operational costs associated with ZEV fleets. Finally, most of the agencies mentioned the same primary
funding sources, which could suggest that a comprehensive funding inventory specific to transit agencies
transitioning their fleets to ZEVs could be a useful resource to ensure all transit agencies are aware of all
potential funding resources.

 1.6. ACCESSIBILITY
  1.6.1. Introduction
 Accessibility is an important element in the push to transition transit fleets to zero-emissions. This is
evidenced through the breadth and nuance of the discussion category that emerged as we analyzed the
qualitative data. One clear indication of the nuance contained within the discussions around accessibility
is the fact that eight distinct topic areas were identified through our analysis. We will cover six of these
topic areas in this chapter, as those six contained sentiments that were articulated by multiple agencies.
  1.6.2. Topic Area 1: Prioritizing Investments for Accessibility
 A general way that transit agencies reported considering accessibility concerns in the context of zero-
emissions transitions was through the prioritization of investments. Some informants claimed that their
agencies had a program-wide focus on ensuring that resources were fairly allocated to certain
communities and that this focus extended to investments in zero-emissions fleets. Another way that
multiple agencies demonstrated prioritization of investments to communities was through the location of
electrified bus garages within those neighborhoods. This was done with an expressed intent to extend the
air quality benefits of BEBs to certain communities. Finally, one agency mentioned that they had focused
workforce development resources in a certain community, hoping to help extend the economic benefits of
the transition to these residents.
  1.6.3. Topic Area 2: Route Prioritization
 One clear way to ensure that the benefits of zero-emissions buses are enjoyed by certain communities
is through the allocation of these vehicles to routes that serve and traverse specific neighborhoods. Six
different transit service providers that we interviewed indicated that they had used this strategy. In some
cases, new routes were created with the expressed intention of serving a particular need of certain
residents. In other cases, existing routes were chosen based on a knowledge of the socio-economic
characteristics of customers served by that route.
  1.6.4. Topic Area 3: Community Engagement
 An identified best practice in ensuring that the benefits of transportation electrification are extended
to certain communities is through first conducting meaningful community engagement. Multiple agencies
reported that in conducting this engagement, they discovered that certain communities were interested in
electrified buses for the purpose of avoiding diesel emissions. This expressed interest by these
communities in enjoying cleaner air from BEBs should direct agencies to seriously consider this benefit
as they plan electrified routes in their service areas.
  1.6.5. Topic Area 4: Prudence with Public Resources
 One way that informants described their consideration of accessibility as they work to transition to
zero-emissions fleets is through the fair and prudent stewardship of public resources. In this context, two
agencies reported that because of the high upfront costs of BEBs, they were concerned about the impacts
on their ability to cover those costs while still maintaining quality service. One agency pointed out,
though, that despite of those upfront costs, with lower projected maintenance and fueling costs, BEBs
would be a good long-term investment. Contradicting those concerned with the upfront costs of BEBs,
two agencies suggested that with the lower fuel costs of these vehicles, that investment in BEBs was
actually a more prudent use of funds on behalf of the public.
  1.6.6. Topic Area 6: Tension Between Accessibility and Zero-emissions
 Goals
 With perennial concerns about funding and correspondingly constrained resources, there is an
inherent tension between deploying capital projects and ensuring robust and fair provision of service (i.e.

 13

route coverage and frequency routes are operated). This tension has been confirmed by three of our key
informants and through discussions with federal partners. Despite this tension, however, some agencies
reiterated that their priority lies with providing fair and reliable service. One agency, in fact, shared an
explicit policy pertaining to their zero-emissions transition that articulated this priority for service over
favor for accommodating technological constraints of BEBs. Additionally, on the topic of competing
interests between capital investment and service provision, another agency claimed that they do not
observe competition between these interests as their zero-emissions investments have been entirely
covered by novel funding sources that were intended for just that purpose and could not otherwise be
diverted to service.
  1.6.7. Conclusion
 When asked about how they consider accessibility in the context of transitioning to zero-emissions
fleets, agencies responded with a great deal of variety, both in the way they considered the construct, and
the degree to which the agency has considered or planned for accessibility thus far. Some agencies
responded that they had not given it much thought, and others simply implied that accessibility is inherent
with a transition to zero-emissions fleets as they produce fewer emissions and typically serve lower-
income residents. On the other end of the spectrum, some agencies had carefully considered policies in
place to ensure that investments in zero-emissions buses would benefit certain community members in
ways that are fair. Some informants pointed out that there is a tension in achieving both accessibility goals
and transition goals, as transit providers work with constrained resources, and funds put toward new
technologies might take from funds available to ensure adequate service region-wide. Others, however,
were careful to point out that their priority was to provide quality and reliable service, and they would not
sacrifice that priority to achieve zero-emissions transition goals.

 1.7. VEHICLES AND TECHNOLOGICAL CONSTRAINTS
  1.7.1. Introduction
 The discussion category that contained the most consistent references to challenges that are particular
to BEBs was the category of vehicles and technological constraints. This discussion category contained
four distinct topic areas, three of which were referenced by multiple agencies and will be discussed in this
chapter. The three topic areas that we will cover include fuel technologies used or considered, BEB
technological considerations, and non-bus vehicles.
  1.7.2. Topic Area 1: Fuel Technologies Used or Considered
 While the framing of our questions remained neutral with respect to specific zero-emissions fuel
types, most agencies organically offered which technologies that they had deployed or were considering
deploying. Of the 20 agencies that we interviewed, all but one claimed that they had deployed or had
considered future deployments of BEBs. The one agency not included in that list had deployed hydrogen
fuel cell buses (HFCBs) and expressed no interest in BEBs Other agencies mentioned that they were
considering HCFBs, with many expressing trepidations based on the lack of information around fueling
and requisite infrastructure. Many agencies reported successful deployments of low emissions bus
technologies like compressed natural gas (CNG), renewable and biodiesel, and hybrid diesel buses.
  1.7.3. Topic Area 2: Battery Electric Bus Technological Considerations
 As could be expected with the understanding that BEBs were the predominant technology deployed
by agencies transitioning to zero-emissions fleets, most of the technological challenges expressed by our
key informants related to buses of this fuel type. Chief among technological concerns of BEBs related to
the range provided by BEBs. Range concerns, in fact, was the most frequently cited response to the
question of what challenges agencies faced as they transitioned to zero-emissions vehicles.
Agencies associated limited range from BEBs with issues around scheduling and blocking of bus routes,
fueling constraints, reliability of service, and overall costs associated with transitioning to zero-emissions
fleets. Limited BEB range led agencies to need to consider opportunity and daytime charging strategies,
which, as one agency described, leads to a “distributed fueling model” as opposed to the conventional
central fueling model.
The advantages of the latter include not just a spatial consolidation, but also temporal, wherein a bus need
only be fueled once a day. Additional cost factors are related to charging during the daytime, which can
be subject to time of use rates that can be higher than the costs associated with charging only overnight.
Other cost impacts of range limitations are related to the replacement factor of BEBs in relation to diesel
buses. Agencies reported that because of limited range of BEBs, in some cases, it takes two BEBs to
perform the same daily duties of a single diesel bus. Although this figure seems quite damning for the
prospects of BEBs, more agencies claimed that newer model buses are getting closer to a one-to-one
replacement factor based on range.
In line with this sentiment, many informants expressed optimism that the technology is improving and
getting closer to satisfying their needs. Finally, the limited range of BEBs, in the cases of some agencies,
requires that buses fuel in multiple locations at multiple times, also creates challenges around scheduling.
The concerns that informants shared with us around service reliability were associated with an observed
variability in range performance of BEBs. Factors like operator habits and weather reportedly affected the
observed range of BEBs, impacting their ability to provide reliable service on some routes or schedule
blocks. Most agencies that expressed concerns with range pointed out that those concerns were most
acute in times of cold or inclement weather, with heating and cooling having very noticeable impacts on
the range performance of BEBs.

 15

  1.7.4. Topic Area 3: Non-Revenue Fleet Vehicles
 The transition to zero-emissions fleets encompasses more than just buses for many agencies with
whom we spoke. Many agencies reported deploying zero-emissions vehicles that aren’t buses in
applications such as service vehicles, staff pool vehicles, and paratransit vehicles. The most cited non-
revenue fleet vehicles were staff pool light-duty vehicles. One agency shared that finding available
models for larger non-revenue vehicles with more specific use cases was more challenging than acquiring
zero-emissions light-duty vehicles.
  1.7.5. Conclusion
 When asked about the challenges agencies face as they transition to zero-emissions vehicles, the most
common response was related to the range provided by BEBs. Almost half of all those that we
interviewed cited concerns stemming from the lower range available from BEBs compared to their diesel
counterparts. Range figures were quite variable, however, with more agencies reporting that the
capabilities of BEBs were getting closer to achieving the daily range performance of the diesel buses they
replaced than those reporting less favorable replacement figures like two-to-one. Aside from challenges
related directly to range capabilities of BEBs, some agencies reported interoperability issues between
specific bus models and specific chargers, which we covered in more detail in the Bus Facilities and
Operations section of this report.

 1.8. RELIABILITY AND MAINTENANCE
  1.8.1. Introduction
 In our interviews with key informants, reliability, maintenance, and safety considerations surfaced as
important distinct topics around the sentiments of reliability of BEBs in general, downtime and uptime
issues of both BEBs and HFCBs, the need for specialty tools and equipment, warranty limitations, the
overall maintenance landscape, fire, codes and standards, and safety for maintenance workers. By
expressing these issues, agencies hope to ensure more reliable and cost-effective operations, ultimately
supporting the broader goals of sustainability and efficiency in public transit.
  1.8.2. Topic Area 1: Reliability
 Reliability of BEBs is a major concern for agencies, affecting the adoption and operational efficiency
for acquiring and future support for BEBs and HFCBs. Five agency informants reported reliability issues
with BEBs, and the lack of reliability makes it difficult to justify further bus purchases and fleet
expansion, including the challenges and impacts that reliability can and has had on route scheduling.
Interestingly, it was noted that BEBs are more reliable in comparison to diesel buses, demonstrating
variability in experiences among agencies.
  1.8.3. Topic Area 2: Downtime Issues
 The downtime of BEBs when maintenance issues arise is a critical operational challenge. Long
downtimes can severely disrupt transit schedules and reduce overall fleet efficiency. Three agencies
shared that when BEBs breakdown, the expected turnaround is quite lengthy and impactful. Additionally,
there were sentiments shared regarding concern for the role of OEMs and parts supply. It was expressed
that part availability was not expedited efficiently to meet the demand and that if OEM were to address
this, the impacts from parts delay would be less severe for the overall operational viability of BEBs. One
agency sentiment included the concern for OEM wanting to protect intellectual property and how this too
has potential to impact BEB growth.
HFCBs are another alternative in the move towards sustainable transportation, however this area is also
facing challenges, particularly with maintaining consistent uptime. Due to continual maintenance, uptime
issues are directly affecting the reliability and attractiveness of HFCBs as a viable transit solution.
  1.8.4. Topic Area 3: Specialty Tools and Equipment
 Unlike traditional diesel buses, BEBs and HFCBs require specialized tools and equipment to ensure
safe and effective maintenance. Two agencies shared this specifically and that it could impact agencies in
various ways, financially as they shift to procuring in and investing in new tools and equipment and in
specialized training and maintenance for using them.
  1.8.5. Topic Area 4: Warranty
 Warranty considerations are a crucial aspect of the overall lifecycle of electric buses and a concern
for multiple agencies regarding unknown maintenance costs and their impediment to the electrification
transition. The coverage and terms of warranties for electric buses differ from those of traditional diesel
buses, impacting maintenance costs and procurement decisions. The implications of shorter warranties for
electric buses and how these factors influence the overall maintenance strategy and costs for transit
agencies as they consider post per mile are also of concern.
  1.8.6. Topic Area 5: Maintenance
 Maintenance is a significant concern for the operation and cost-effectiveness of electric buses.
Currently costs, frequency, and nature of maintenance tasks for BEBs are unknown and differ from that of
diesel buses. Other topic sentiments revealed that the comparative costs per mile was higher for BEBs at
$1.90 vs. $.79 per mile for diesel fueled. The frequency of major failures, multifaceted maintenance

 17

challenges faced by agencies transitioning to electrification, and overall maintenance issues are the largest
impediment to the electric transition.
  1.8.7. Topic Area 6: Fire
 Fire safety was identified as a significant concern for agencies and was mentioned under multiple
topics within this chapter. Sentiments were shared over concerns with BEB fires and the need to
understand the true risk and management of BEB fires. Understanding this risk suggests a gap in
comprehensive risk assessment and data-driven understanding of these incidents. High visibility of BEB
fires can lead to a higher perception of risk compared to the actual risk and could be indictive that public
perception may be disproportionately influenced by isolated incidents. One agency shared their
experience with a BEB fire, demonstrating that while incidents have been rare, they can and will occur
and warrant serious consideration for safety planning and mitigation.
  1.8.8. Topic Area 7: Codes and Standards
 A key sentiment shared by two agencies emphasized the importance of developing and implementing
codes and standards to ensure the safe storage, charging, and operation of BEBs. This sentiment stresses a
significant gap in the current framework, sharing the urgent need for comprehensive fire safety facility
design codes. The absence of such standards can lead to increased risk and operation inefficiencies.
Establishing specific infrastructure guidelines will not only address fire safety concerns but also
contribute to the overall reliability and public trust in BEB technology.
  1.8.9. Topic Area 8: Safety for Maintenance Workers
 Sentiments in this topic area shared the concern with the lack of necessary protocols for working
conditions related to work being conducted on the roof of buses, the need for safe maintenance practices
with the use of scaffolding, and the implications for transit agencies as they adapt to these new
requirements. Ensuring safety of maintenance workers who maintain BEBs is essential as reflected in the
sentiments for the need for fall protection due to the working location and the necessity for special
personal protective equipment, reflecting that traditional PPE may not be adequate for repairs associated
with BEBs. The need to prepare staff for safely working on BEBs will require specialized training and
protocols to address the unique hazards associated with electric buses and a change in previous
technology.
  1.8.10.     Conclusion
 The transition to electrified transportation hinges on the reliability, maintenance, and safety of BEBs
and HFCBs. Significant concerns around warranties, long downtime and uptimes, maintenance
complexities, and the slow availability of parts are hindering the success of this transition. These issues
complicate scheduling, fleet expansion, and add to the operational costs, despite the simplicity of electric
systems. Addressing these challenges requires improved OEM support, better planning for tools and
equipment, and clear maintenance protocols. This will enhance reliability and cost-effectiveness,
supporting sustainability and efficiency in public transit.
Lastly, safety is a critical aspect of the electrification transition, and a significant gap was revealed
regarding the non-existence of codes and standards. This gap should address the specific requirements for
electric buses and their maintenance, ensuring both perceived and actual safety concerns are mitigated
through comprehensive risk assessments and the development of fire safety and facility design codes.
Ensuring the safety of maintenance staff involves implementing basic protocols and equipment and
offering training to handle the unique challenges posed by BEBs and HFCBs. Addressing these safety
concerns through robust standards, informed risk assessments and comprehensive training programs will
be crucial to the successful implementation of electrification initiatives.

 1.9. WORKFORCE DEVELOPMENT AND TRAINING
  1.9.1. Introduction
 The shift towards electrification within the transportation sector requires a substantial change in
workforce development and training practices, implementation, and support. As the industry adapts to
new technologies there will be a need for a workforce that is proficient in electric powertrains, increasing
training efforts, and how to address current and future workforce challenges. By communicating
sentiments and agency experiences, this chapter identifies a multitude of sentiments toward addressing
workforce and training gaps and working with hybrid technology.
  1.9.2. Topic Area 1: Knowledge of Electric Powertrains
 The transition to electrification heavily depends on the workforce’s knowledge and experience
with electric powertrains. Four agencies shared that staff that held prior experience in hybrid technologies
are better prepared for this shift. These agencies were able to leverage their existing expertise in hybrid
technologies, positioning them well to adopt and manage the new electric powertrain technologies
effectively.
  1.9.3. Topic Area 2: Training
 Training was identified as a critical component in workforce development, particularly the sentiments
shared by three agencies regarding their heavy reliance on training by manufacturers. This is an indication
that many of them depend on OEMs to provide the necessary training, however the OEM training can fall
short of the full needs of transit agencies.
To address the lack in training, two agencies shared that they provide in-house training for their
technicians, reflecting the agency’s need towards addressing self-sufficiency in training. Other significant
sentiments that were shared at least once included the lack of space needed for training facilities. From an
operations standpoint- training for HFCBs and diesel buses are the same, however there is limited
availability of training for new technologies like HFCB due partially to having very little specifics on
HFCB.
Additionally mentioned was the three-day on-site training offered by fuel cell manufactures and other
organizations like the Transit Resource Center which offers courses to certify technicians to work on
BEBs. Considering that local technical colleges also do not offer applicable programs to provide
technicians with the ample training required a need for peer agency training and an increase in
manufacturer training is still heavily sought.
  1.9.4. Topic Area 3: Workforce
 Workforce challenges and opportunities are evident through the sentiments around the high vacancy
rate, approximately 20%, which was shared by three agencies. Vacancy rates could be attributed to the
difficulty in hiring, which was identified by at least two agencies, along with the concern that the
workforce was insufficient to sustain the growth in BEBs therefore leading to staff constraints, workforce
displacement, and concerns for skill transfer from fossil fuels to electrification, not to mention the
difficulty in acquiring properly trained staff and the interest for technicians to work on high-powered
systems.
  1.9.5. Conclusion
 With workforce development and training being pivotal to the success of electrification initiatives, the
existing knowledge in hybrid technologies provides a solid starting point for electric powertrain training.
However, reliance on manufacturers for training and the varied effectiveness of such training is posing
some challenges and significant hurdles. Addressing these issues through comprehensive training
programs, improved support systems, and strategic workforce development plans will be crucial for the

 19

sustainable growth of electrification initiatives and for the growth in technicians and their skill
development and overall training and interest for working with BEBs.

 1.10. POLICY, PROGRAMS, AND REGULATIONS
  1.10.1. Introduction
 This chapter discusses the landscape of policy, programs, and regulations which encompasses four
topic areas. First, we explore the influential decision-makers shaping programmatic initiatives that direct
implementation of zero-emissions fleets. Next, we examine the policy documents referenced by our key
informants that guide and inform these efforts. Additionally, we consider the spectrum of agency and
outside pressures affecting the transition, which describes the diverse perspectives and sentiments
surrounding this process. Lastly, we analyze the regulatory frameworks that support and direct these
developments to provide a comprehensive overview of the regulatory environment in which agencies find
themselves.
  1.10.2. Topic Area 1: Decision Makers Affecting Programmatic Direction
 Decision-makers both within and outside of transit agencies play a crucial role in shaping the
direction and implementation of electrification programs. Transit boards of directors were mentioned as
key decision makers affecting electrification policies by four of the agencies that we interviewed. Other
agencies also mentioned additional important decision-making bodies such as city councils, regulatory
agencies, state departments of transportation, and metropolitan planning organizations. These decision-
makers are pivotal in setting directives for the electric transition, particularly in local and state contexts,
emphasizing their role in operationalizing policy at the local level.
  1.10.3. Topic Area 2: Referenced Policy Documents
 Specific policy documents appeared to be an important point of reference for articulating the ways
that transit agencies were implementing zero-emissions fleet transition goals. Eight agencies referenced
Zero-emissions Transition Plans in their descriptions of how their implementation efforts have been
influenced by internal agency policy. Zero-emissions transition plans are a requirement of applications for
both Bus and Bus Facilities and Low or No Emissions Program grants from the Federal Transit
Administration. The plans are required to contain information regarding fleet management, resource
availability, existing and future facilities to accommodate zero-emissions fleets, and other critical topics.
The fact that many agencies referenced these plans in their explanation of internal policy and
implementation efforts speaks to the fact that this requirement has helped to formalize many agencies’
transition plans in a way that has created internal policy frameworks that extend beyond the requirements
of the grant application. Other important policy documents that were referenced by multiple agencies
included Strategic Transformation Plans and Climate Action Plans adopted by the agencies’ local
municipalities.
  1.10.4. Topic Area 3: Public Opinion and Political Support
 A topic that surfaced in discussions with our key informants was the impact of public opinion on the
policy and implementation of zero-emissions fleets among transit agencies. Three agencies reported
notable political will and support for electrification, driven specifically by local air quality issues making
electrification favorable and the perception of electrification as a societal inevitably.
However, challenges such as a general lack of local political support for transit and difficulties complying
with “Buy America” requirements pose significant hurdles to some participating agencies. Additionally,
some agencies felt the need for more federal guidance on battery electric bus safety and mitigating fire
risks.

 20

  1.10.5.     Conclusion
 Within this discussion category of policy, programs, and regulations, our informants indicated that
governmental decision-makers like board of directors and other regulatory agencies are essential for the
successful implementation of electrification initiatives. Having a zero-emissions transition plan proved to
be important for developing internal policy and directing implementation efforts. Additionally, public
opinion was a notable factor influencing agencies’ ability to work toward zero-emissions transition goals.
Finally, agencies reported that federal standards and policies were an important factor impacting their
implementation of zero-emissions fleets.

 1.11. PROCUREMENT AND SUPPLY CHAIN
  1.11.1. Introduction
 A discussion area that emerged organically, meaning that it was not directly related to any pre-
determined questions within our semi-structured interview, was that of procurement and supply chain.
Many agencies reported issues related to delays in receiving buses as well as the necessary equipment
infrastructure necessary for maintaining and operating those buses. Two topic areas were identified to
organize the sentiments within this category, procurement and supply chain as well as price trends.
  1.11.2. Topic Area 1: Procurement and Supply Chain
 The time that it takes to procure a BEB was identified by our key informants as a major impediment
in their efforts to transition to zero-emissions fleets. Many agencies said that when compared to diesel
buses, BEBs took much longer to acquire. Some agencies even reported that wait times for BEBs were as
high as two or three years. Multiple agencies suggested that there was a limited supply of buses as a result
of very few manufacturers of BEBs compared to the high demand, and this contributed to the challenges
in procurement.
A few informants referred to the recent bankruptcy of a major BEB manufacturer leading to concern
about whether they would receive buses already ordered or be able to service those that had already been
deployed. Other components essential to the transition were also reported to be challenging to procure,
such as charging and fueling infrastructure, hydrogen fuel, and electrical equipment like transformers and
switches.
Another way in which a constrained supply chain was affecting agencies working to transition their fleets
was through delays in acquiring replacement parts. Many agencies claimed that downtimes for BEBs
were greatly extended by the need to wait for specific replacement parts that were often not readily
available and required long lead times after ordering. While these sentiments were rarer, it warrants
mentioning that some agencies said that procurement was not an issue for them and that the lead times for
diesel buses and BEBs were the same.
  1.11.3. Topic Area 2: Price Trends
 In discussing procurement and supply chain issues, the sentiment that was shared most often was the
high capital cost of purchasing BEBs when compared to conventionally fueled buses. This sentiment was
shared by nearly half of all our key informants. Some went even further to highlight the discrepancies in
price with precise figures, mentioning that BEBs cost 50% more per bus than a diesel alternative of the
same type. Many agencies reported that the prices for BEBs and associated equipment have only
increased since their initial forays into transitioning their fleets. Others even suggested that the cost of
BEBs was a considerable roadblock in their efforts to implement a zero-emissions fleet. However, this
trend might be specific to BEBs, as one key informant observed that prices had come down for HFCBs.
  1.11.4.     Conclusion
 The cost of BEBs and other issues related to procurement and supply chain associated with zero-
emissions buses were a major concern among agencies that we interviewed. They cited high upfront costs
as a potential roadblock to their efforts to transition their fleets. Agencies reported issues with slow
delivery of vehicles, requisite infrastructure, and replacement parts. They attributed these supply chain
issues to a small number of manufacturers serving a market with high demand, made even more hungry
by a recent influx of federal grants.

 1.12. COLLABORATION, CAPACITY, AND RESOURCES
  1.12.1. Introduction
 To achieve zero-emissions fleet goals, transit agencies have needed to work closely with partners that
can provide expertise and capacity beyond the existing resources of the agency alone. Agencies have
collaborated with local utilities, governmental partners, consultants, and manufacturers. Informants have
reported varying levels of success in their efforts to collaborate, with particular issues with certain entities
that we will describe in detail below.
  1.12.2. Topic Area 1: Collaboration with Local Entities
 Many agencies described relationships with local entities that helped to expand their knowledge and
capacity. The most common of these were local universities. Many informants reported working with
these local institutions in formal ways through funded joint projects as well as in less formal ways
through direct sharing of knowledge with the transit agency. Another common local partner mentioned by
our informants were Metropolitan Planning Organizations (MPOs). MPOs also provided additional
expertise, capacity, and in some cases, funding for agencies transition efforts. Technical assistance was
also offered to agencies from local Clean Cities Coalitions, state departments of transportation, and EV
working groups.
  1.12.3. Topic Area 2: Working with Consultants
 Another critical partner for transit agencies venturing into the space of zero-emissions fleets are
consulting firms. Many informants described reports, products, and general advice that they received from
consultants as critical contributions in their early efforts to understand what would be required to deploy
zero-emissions fleets. The technical knowledge and expertise of these consultants helped agencies
navigate a new space in ways that made transitioning more accessible. Some of the specific areas that our
informants referenced where help from consultants was particularly useful included charging solutions,
routing and scheduling for BEBs, market analyses of hydrogen fuel availability, and infrastructure siting.
  1.12.4. Topic Area 3: Working with Utilities
 Utilities play an important role in in the transition to electrified transit vehicles. Utilities were the
partners that came up most frequently when transit agencies discussed the need to work with external
partners. There was a great deal of variability in the sentiments that our informants shared about their
experiences working with utilities, however.
Four informants described good relationships that they had established with their utilities that facilitated
effective partnerships. Three agencies, on the other hand, described significant challenges in working with
their utilities. These agencies cited frustration with the administrative burden required to advance projects
with their utility, lack of clarity about the availability of power, long delays in acquiring needed power,
the need to work with multiple utilities, and pushback from their local utility as they pursue on-site solar
generation.
The most common sentiment that informants relayed in relation to working with their utilities was the
desire to negotiate better electricity rates, particularly in response to time of use rates that make daytime
charging more expensive. One lesson that seemed clear from our conversations around partnering with
utilities was that agencies that engaged utilities early in their process of moving toward BEB fleets
seemed to enjoy better partnerships than those who had not thought about the need to engage until later in
the process.

their capacity and gain technical knowledge that is otherwise not available. Agencies have turned to local
entities like MPOs, universities, and clean cities coalitions to partner and learn valuable information
critical to the success of their transition goals.

Additionally, many agencies that we spoke with have utilized highly technical expertise from consultants
to lower the barriers to entering an otherwise entirely novel and sometime intimidating realm of zero-
emissions vehicles.

Another partnership that was evidently critical to transit agencies’ efforts to move to BEB fleets were
local utilities. Many agencies, particularly those who had begun their relationship early, reported
successful partnerships with their local utilities that were leading to the ability to acquire needed power in
a timely fashion. Other agencies reported, however, many difficulties in working with their utilities that
was leading to delays in securing the power they needed to charge BEB fleets.

Finally, we explored what help would be beneficial to our key informants from the Joint Office as they
consider transitions or work to deploy more zero-emissions vehicles in their fleets. Agencies shared a
variety of technical assistance needs, with the most agreement focusing on a product providing general
guidance and best practices related to transitions to zero-emissions fleets.

 1.13. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE JOINT
 OFFICE

  1.13.1. Conclusions
 Our interviews with transit agencies around the country demonstrated that there are a variety of
common challenges they face as they work to transition to zero-emissions bus fleets. Conversations
coalesced around nine discussion categories, including bus facilities and operations; funding, finance, and
planning; accessibility; vehicles and technological constraints; reliability and maintenance; workforce
development and training; policy, programs, and regulations; procurement and supply chain; and
collaboration, capacity, and resources. These discussion categories were partially demonstrative of the
questions that we asked key informants, but they also represent the ways in which agencies consider the
challenges and opportunities they face as they work to decarbonize their fleets. Among these discussion
categories, we identified 55 unique topic areas that contained 388 unique sentiments, some of which were
articulated by multiple informants.
 The concern that was cited most often by agencies we interviewed was the short range of BEBs
relative to the diesel buses they replaced. The relatively short range of BEBs contributed to many follow-
on challenges related to charging, scheduling, and costs associated with transitioning bus fleets. Although
many agencies reported concerns around range, others indicated that they were observing better range
figures with newer models and replacement factors for BEBs were getting closer to one-to-one.
 Another concern of equal importance to transit agencies as they transition to zero-emission fleets
is the high relative cost of BEBs compared to their diesel counterparts. Nearly half of our informants
expressed frustration around BEB prices, citing costs premiums of up to 50% over conventionally fueled
buses. Exacerbating this issue was an upward trend in prices observed by many agencies. Agencies
suggested that a contributing factor to the high prices is the fact that there are so few manufacturers
participating in the market at a time when federal grants are creating very high demand. On top of
concerns around price, many informants shared that procurement of BEBs was taking a long time, leading
to delays in their ability to meet transition goals.
 Related to the high cost of BEBs, funding availability emerged as a major issue facing agencies
working toward zero-emission fleet goals. While many programs that assist in the transition to zero-
emission fleets, such as FTA’s Low and No Emissions Grant Program and Bus and Bus Facilities Grant
Program, have seen major expansions of their funding because of BIL and IRA, the large scale of funding
required to transition entire bus fleets still causes anxiety for transit officials when they consider their
future financial positions. Agencies also expressed funding concerns around operations, maintenance, and
facilities. They reported that finding funding for capital investments, particularly the procurement of new
zero-emission vehicles, was easier than it was for these other essential components of the transition. The
long-term sustainability or longevity of existing federal programs also emerged as a point of uncertainty
for many of our informants. They claimed that major federal investments felt cyclical in their experience,
and they did not have much confidence that the current funding landscape would continue. They shared
that this could leave them in a precarious position if they were to lose needed or expected funding at a
critical point in their transition.
 Problems with BEBs extended beyond just concerns around range, with many informants
relaying frustration stemming from reliability issues with BEBs. Many agencies reported that BEBs were
not comparing favorably to diesel buses in terms of uptime and reliability. Some figures shared by our
key informants that stood out were maintenance costs per mile of $1.90 for BEBs compared to $0.79 for
diesel buses, 65-80% uptime for BEBs compared to 95% uptime for diesel buses, and 3,750 miles mean
distance between major failures for BEBs compared to 7,000 miles for diesel buses. Reliability problems
were also accompanied, in many instances, by delays in procuring replacement parts from manufacturers.
These delays led to longer downtimes for BEBs when they experienced mechanical failures.

 26

The final key issue facing transit agencies working to transition to zero-emission fleets was the challenge
of working with local utilities. The union of the transportation and energy fields is new, and many
informants expressed frustrations and a general lack of clarity around what is required to successfully
partner with their local utilities to ensure that they can receive an adequate power supply to fuel BEBs.
While this issue was not ubiquitous, with some agencies describing excellent working relationships with
their local utilities, the difficulties observed by some seemed acute and, in some cases, created major
impediments to achieving transition goals.
  1.13.2. Recommendations
 We have developed recommendations that are responsive to the major challenges described
by our key informants, as well as to the suggestions they provided regarding potentially useful
assistance from the Joint Office.
 1) Make offerings clearer and more obvious—engage in more outreach to transit agencies.
 Many of the needs expressed by our key informants could be satisfied by existing
 resources and efforts conducted directly by or on behalf of the Joint Office. Not all agencies
 that we spoke to had heard of the Joint Office, and they were thus unaware of the technical
 assistance available to them. This lack of awareness is evidenced by the fact that none of our
 key informants mentioned the existing technical assistance and concierge service currently
 provided by NREL. Additional outreach and marketing of existing capabilities of the Joint
 Office and their contractors would likely improve awareness among transit agencies.
 A reconsideration of the role of driveelectric.gov in the dissemination of technical
 assistance could help to improve the visibility of the wide variety of existing resource
 provided by partner agencies and organizations. If the webpage were to act as clearinghouse
 for all available technical assistance materials that would be useful for transit agencies
 transitioning to zero-emission fleets, including those provided or sponsored by TCRP, FTA,
 APTA, and other key stakeholders in the space of transit electrification, this would help to
 elevate the visibility of those resources and highlight the role that the Joint Office can play in
 providing technical assistance.
 2) Develop technical assistance that highlights successful partnerships with local entities,
 with a focus on working with utilities.
 One of the most common suggestions for needed contributions from the JO regarded help
 in working with utilities. This is a space that is entirely new to transit agencies, and they are
 clearly looking for guidance and support in their efforts to engage successfully with this
 critical partner to electrification of bus fleets. One factor that we identified that seemed to
 distinguish successful working relationships with utilities from unsuccessful ones was the
 point at which transit agencies engaged this critical partner. Agencies that reported good
 relationships with utilities typically had engaged them early in their process to transitioning
 to BEBs, providing the utility with ample time to respond to their incoming increased
 electrical need. This lesson and others should be shared with transit agencies so that they can
 more effectively navigate the new landscape of merging energy and transportation sectors.
 3) Facilitating Scalable Training Programs for Servicing Electric Powertrains
 A major challenge facing transit agencies deploying BEB fleets was an observed lack of
 reliability of these vehicles. As some informants pointed out, the nascent nature of the
 technology created “first gen” problems, with quirks and bugs yet to be entirely ironed out.

 27

Another challenge related to reliability comes from the fact that these technologies have not
been deployed for very long, leading to a relative lack of knowledge around servicing and
maintaining electrically powered buses. As agencies have sought out ways to train their
technicians on these technologies, they have found few resources beyond the bus
manufacturers and peer agencies.
 The Joint Office should invest time and resources into identifying existing training
programs for electric powertrains (both from ZEV OEMs and other entities), investigate the
scalability of such programs, and evaluate any challenges associated with proprietary
constraints impacting the ability to train transit agency technicians. If the Joint Office could
facilitate a higher degree of visibility of existing training programs, or support the expansion
of small but scalable programs, this could have a significant impact on agencies’ ability to
maintain and repair BEBs, thus contributing to higher levels of reliability and potentially
higher confidence in the technology.
4) Address ZEV technical concerns with data.
 Although there is little that the Joint Office can do to solve the problems stemming from
the range, cost, and reliability of current ZEV offerings, there is much that can be done about
the perception and understanding of that limitation of the technology.
 A way that the Joint Office or federal partners like FTA could contribute to both a push
of the industry as well as a sense of agency and control among stakeholders is by creating
and maintaining a means of tracking and communicating performance related to the range,
costs, and reliability of BEBs and HFCBs. While the Joint Office cannot have a direct impact
on these technical and market-based constraints affecting agencies deploying zero-emission
fleets, they can help transit agencies understand these important issues by measuring and
publishing trends over time. Measuring and communicating total cost of ownership (i.e.,
capital and operational costs), range, and reliability experienced in real-world operating
conditions could motivate improved performance on these metrics by industry by bringing
higher scrutiny and visibility to the situation.
 We recommend the Joint Office or a federal partner like FTA establish a zero-emission
bus evaluation program, modeled after the DOE-funded Advanced Vehicle Testing Activity,
led by Idaho National Laboratory (see avt.inl.gov). This program should be led by an
independent organization, such as a national laboratory, that will work with transit agencies
across the United States and zero-emission bus manufacturers to benchmark the cost, range,
and reliability of zero-emission buses in real-world operation.
 Costs: Through surveys of agencies or, even better, data submitted to the FTA as part of
LoNo grant requirements, this organization should document and share the current state of
costs of BEBs, charging equipment, maintenance, and other critical inputs to implementing
zero-emission bus fleets. Tracking and making these figures transparent will help agencies
better plan for the financial aspects of transitioning to a zero-emissions fleet, and it might
also help to increase competitiveness in the market.
 Range: the organization leading the zero-emission bus evaluation program should
collaborate with transit agencies and bus manufacturers to collect data to quantify the real-
world range of BEBs, correlated to numerous factors, including payload (i.e., ridership),
weather conditions, and vehicle design. Analysis of range performance should be specific to

 28

bus makes, models, and years to identify how this factor might be evolving over time.
Region-specific characteristics like climate and topography should also be considered to help
prospective agencies better understand how specific models might perform in their
environment.
 Reliability: The evaluation program should also track maintenance issues reported by
participating transit agencies. These data should be used to identify if there are specific issues
that seem to be common across makes and models and also identify issue that might be
specific to a certain make/model. The program should assess maintenance costs, downtimes,
and delays in securing needed parts, as these were all major concerns expressed in our
interviews. Additionally, these measures should be tracked over time to establish any trends,
determining whether reliability issues are abating as manufacturers acquire more experience
with the technology.
5) Highlight TCRP Report 219 or make it more accessible with a more succinct product.
 The product that transit agencies requested the most from the Joint Office when asked
what resources would be most useful to them as they consider deploying zero-emission fleets
was a general guidance document that could walk them through the step-by-step process of
transitioning to zero-emission vehicles. Somewhat surprisingly, however, this resource exists
as a Transit Cooperative Research Program (TCRP) report entitled “Guidebook for
Deploying Zero-Emissions Transit Buses” and is already linked on driveelectric.gov under
the transit technical assistance page.
 The first step to alleviating the problem that there is insufficient awareness of this product
would be to promote it through webinars or other means of outreach to transit agencies.
Another way that the Joint Office could make this resource more accessible would be to
condense its findings into something that might be easier for transit officials to consume. The
TCRP Research Report 219 is quite comprehensive, but at 185 pages, it also might come off
as daunting to an agency with limited capacity or existing knowledge on the topic. An
introduction to the report with a more accessible summary of the steps and findings might
alleviate the barrier that such a tome presents to a resource constrained transit agency.

 2.2. ZEV FLEET TRANSITION COSTS
 In addition to identifying funding sources, we conducted interviews with 20 transit service providers.
As part of those interviews, we asked them about the cost of transitioning to zero-emissions vehicles and
what capital and operational challenges they are facing tied to this process. In general, many of the
service providers that we spoke to were relieved by the increased funding that is currently available (most
notable and commonly mentioned was the increased funding from the Joint Office of Energy and
Transportation to expand the Lo-No program). However, many have expressed concerns about long-term
funding needs, the uncertainty around the longevity of the increased funding in the Lo-No program, and
the future costs associated with replacing buses in their fleets. In particular, they expressed concerns that
the increased capital and/or operational costs associated with zero-emissions bus technology will prevent
them from meeting their capital and operational costs in 5 to 10 years as their fleets age and more of their
existing fleet and infrastructure will need to be replaced.
 Most agencies have stated that many of their concerns are directly tied to the additional capital costs
of both fuel cell and battery electric buses (zero-emissions vehicles). Based on GSA pricing p, 40-foot fuel
cell buses are listed at ~$1.3M with an incremental cost of ~ $780k, 40-foot battery electric buses are
listed at ~$1M with an incremental cost of ~$480k, and 40-foot compressed natural gas buses (low-
emission vehicles) are ~$560k which is an incremental cost of ~$55k. Where incremental cost is the
difference between the low or zero-emissions vehicle cost and a similarly sized low-bid conventionally
fueled vehicle. q This pricing shows the zero-emissions bus options tend to be at least twice as expensive
as conventional diesel buses while compressed natural gas buses are about the same cost as conventional
diesel buses. Another concern for battery electric buses has been with their operating range matching
advertised ratings especially when accounting for heating and cooling needs for operating the buses.
Therefore, historically, battery electric buses haven’t provided a one-to-one replacement of conventional
buses. Most agencies say until recently you need two battery electric buses to replace every conventional
bus. However, agencies have said that this replacement ratio is improving and currently is probably closer
to 1.25 or 1.5 battery electric buses for every conventional bus. Regardless, these constraints are
increasing the near-term capital costs of transit agencies wanting to transition to zero-emission fleets.

 Additional concerns were raised about the cost associated with electric charging infrastructure
installation, operation, and maintenance costs. Installation costs can be quite expensive, especially if
electrical service upgrades are needed to accommodate the increased electricity demand required to
charge the buses.
 In addition to further delay battery electric buses from being put into service. For operational costs,
many of the agencies have expressed concerns around the demand charges they end up paying. As a
result, some transit operators are looking into smart charge management solutions to help reduce their
demand charges.
 Many agencies have expressed that they would like to see utilities provide transit agencies special
electricity rates or at least caps on the demand charges. An additional operational cost that many agencies
will also need to consider is either redundancy in their electrical service to maintain resiliency in the event
of natural disasters and unexpected power outages.
 For maintenance costs, some of the larger agencies we have spoken with expressed concerns about
the amount of labor that will be required to maintain the large number of charging stations needed to keep
their fleet charged. One of the reasons for needing so many charging stations is tied to the slow charging
speeds of battery electric buses compared to fueling times of conventional buses.
 Also, the reliability of charging stations continues to be a challenge and thus many transit providers
are installing redundant charging stations to ensure they can charge their fleet, but this redundancy
requires yet additional infrastructure costs.
 For fuel cell buses, the current bus technology appears to be providing nearly a one-to-one
replacement rate when assessed by available range before refueling, but these buses due tend to be about
twice as expensive as conventional buses and slightly more expensive than battery electric buses.
Additionally, the fueling infrastructure is even more challenging than battery charging and fuel cell
fueling infrastructure tends to be very expensive to install and maintain according to the one transit
service operator that we spoke with.
 2.3. LOGISTICAL CHALLENGES AND CONSTRAINTS
 Many of the transit agencies we have spoken to describe many logistical challenges such as bus
availability and delivery time. These logistical challenges are limiting them from using the funds they
currently have and begin transitioning their fleets. Most stated that it currently takes 18-36 months to
receive a bus after an order has been placed, and this has been exacerbated by the recent bankruptcy of
Proterra.
 Another major limitation has been the time required to work with utilities to upgrade their facilities as
needed (where applicable). An additional constraint facing transit service operators is whether their
current facilities can be grown enough to accommodate both the increased number of buses and the
required charging or hydrogen infrastructure which tends to have a much higher footprint than
conventional fueling infrastructure.
 Finally, some transit service providers have expressed that the current state of battery electric and fuel
cell buses aren’t mature enough to meet their needs for meeting carbon emission goals. As a result, some
are considering transitioning to low-emission alternatives such as compressed natural gas buses or hybrid
buses that operate on biofuels such as renewable diesel. In some cases, agencies have said they are
planning or at least considering replacing or at least ceasing to acquire zero-emission technology and
transitioning low-emission compressed natural gas vehicles due to the capital and operational constraints
mentioned above.
