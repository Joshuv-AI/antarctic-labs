Overcoming Electrochemiluminescence Immunoassay (ECLIA) Limitations: Conformation-Independent Quantitation of a Therapeutic Antibody by LC–MS/MS
Authors: Claudia Gaither, Robert Popp, Pradyumn Maheshwari, Mauricio Lemus, Julie Laurin, Mario Filion, Christoph H. Borchers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monoclonal antibody therapies have transformed cancer treatment by enabling targeted intervention even in advanced disease stages. However, reliable monitoring of circulating therapeutic antibody levels is critical to ensuring efficacy and patient safety. This study investigates the limitations of an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) in quantifying a therapeutic immunoglobulin G2 (IgG2) antibody, known to undergo conformational changes in vivo. We observed that the ECLIA failed to reflect increasing antibody concentrations in patient plasma, instead producing a plateau effect. This discrepancy was attributed to the ECLIA’s reliance on the intact structural conformation of the antibody, which may be altered during circulation. In contrast, a mass spectrometry (MS)-based approach successfully quantified the accumulating antibody levels over time, independent of conformational state. These findings highlight the need for alternative quantitation strategies in clinical settings, particularly when structural heterogeneity may compromise the ECLIA performance. Mass spectrometry provides a robust and reliable option for monitoring therapeutic antibodies under these conditions.

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with “TBL cancers” (i.e., tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancers) being the leading cause of cancer death. , There were nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths in 2020, and demographics-based predictions indicate that by 2050, the number of new cases of cancer will reach 35 million. Conventional cancer treatments involve surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, selected or combined depending on the cancer type and stage of the disease at the time of diagnosis. , Surgery remains the preferred treatment for solid tumors during early stages, when the cancer remains localized and complete surgical removal is possible. −

Unfortunately, the early diagnosis of cancers is hindered by multiple factors includingbut not limited tohealthcare access challenges, delays in seeking medical care, and limited awareness and knowledge of early symptoms. Thus, a high percentage of cancers are not diagnosed until they have reached advanced stages. For these cases, novel techniques offer renewed hope to patients whose survival would have been unlikely in previous years. Immunotherapy is one such technique. Monoclonal antibodies for cancer treatment are growing in popularity due to their higher specificity and potential to target tumors more readily while slowing, preventing, and even killing tumors altogether. In modern cancer therapy, monoclonal antibody-based immunotherapy can be used on its own, or in addition to surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy. By using antibody-based therapies, tumor cells can be directly targeted while potentially promoting long-lasting antitumor immune responses. − In addition, antibodies allow a more targeted delivery and enhanced precision, which can lead to fewer side effects.

The growing success and popularity of antibody-based cancer therapies necessitate careful monitoring of dosages and circulating antibody concentrations, particularly during clinical trials, to ensure efficacy and avoid potentially harmful levels in patients. − The traditional techniques for measuring and quantifying circulating antibodies are either the Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) or Electrochemiluminescence Immunoassays (ECLIA). These techniques are popular for their reproducibility, sensitivity, and relative ease of use. − The success of ELISA and ECLIA, however, is dependent on the structural conformation of the target antibody, and any conformation deviations can lead to skewed quantitation results. In this study, we show an example where an ECLIA indeed failed to determine the accurate concentration of a circulating therapeutic Immunoglobulin G2 (IgG2) in the blood of patients participating in a clinical trial.

Naturally occurring IgG2 undergoes conformational changes in vivo and, in some cases, in vitro, depending on the redox environment. , The canonical conformation, IgG2-A, is predominant when the antibody is produced, but, in circulation, reshuffling of the disulfide bridges leads to conformation IgG2-A/B and finally IgG2-B (Figure ). It is not fully understood how these conformational changes occur, but they are well documented in the literature. It is therefore plausible that, once administered to the patient and exposed to the circulatory environment, therapeutic IgG2 would undergo the same conformational changes observed in natural IgG2. During the clinical trial, patients received cyclical doses of therapeutic IgG2, which was expected to progressively accumulate in the bloodstream as a function of the therapeutic antibody’s half-life and the continuous dosing of the patients. However, ECLIA-based measurements of longitudinal samples obtained from a patient revealed that circulating antibody concentrations were markedly lower than anticipated. We therefore hypothesized that structural changes were affecting the quantitation of the therapeutic antibody, thus leading to the underestimation of the circulating IgG2 concentration in patient plasma samples. We developed a set of liquid chromatography parallel reaction monitoring mass spectrometry (LC-PRM/MS) assays to precisely quantify the therapeutic antibody from human plasma using an Evosep One Thermo Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap setup.

(i) The IgG2-A conformation, considered the classical or canonical structural conformation, containing four intact disulfide bonds in the hinge region. (ii) The IgG2-A/B conformation, containing one Fab region disulfide-linked to the hinge. (iii) The IgG2-B conformation, containing both Fab regions disulfide-linked to the hinge. Shuffling of disulfide linkages at the CH1 and hinge regions that could possibly lead to binding-specific changes in the context of immunoassays.

While others have used LC–MS/MS to overcome hurdles of immunoassays targeting a single proteotypic peptide for protein quantitation, an important novelty of the study presented here was the selection of four peptides to ensure reliable protein quantitation.

We divided our study into five steps, as shown in Figure : (1) in silico selection of surrogate peptides, (2) assessment of the detectability of the surrogate peptides in plasma spiked with therapeutic antibody, (3) optimization of the enzymatic digestion protocol, (4) determination of the linear range for each of the surrogate peptides, and (5) successful quantitation of the therapeutic antibody in patient plasma samples collected at different time points during the clinical trial.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The Phase 2 study was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the ICH Guideline E6 for good clinical practice.

Patient Samples and Therapeutic Antibody

AB-16B5 is a humanized IgG2 monoclonal antibody that targets tumor-associated secreted Clusterin (TA-sCLU), a protein that is stimulated during the Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) and contributes to invasion of tumor cells. Blood samples were collected from a single patient to define the pharmacokinetic properties of AB-16B5 when administered intravenously once weekly. During the study, blood was drawn at several time points, as outlined in Table S1, and subsequently processed to yield plasma, which was maintained at −70 °C until processed for analysis either by the ECLIA or MS.

Moreover, an AB-16B5 reference standard was available for the optimization and validation experiments at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. The protein concentration was provided by the manufacturer and determined by the SoloVPE test.

ECLIA

The immunoassay used in our study to quantify the AB-16B5 therapeutic antibody was an ECLIA specifically designed to detect the target in human plasma. The ECLIA was developed internally and validated by a contract research organization in a GLP environment following the Food and Drug Administration’s Bioanalytical Method Validation Guidance for Industry published in May 2018, evaluating parameters such as linearity, precision, accuracy, and stability. The ECLIA antibody was an affinity-purified recombinant monoclonal antibody against AB-16B5 produced by AbD Serotec GmbH (Bio-Rad). The successfully validated assay was then performed in-house using standards and quality control samples (QCs) for verifying batch performance as described below.

ECLIA Standards and QCs

ECLIA standards and QCs were prepared by adding the AB-16B5 reference standard to blank human plasma. Ten standards ranged from 200 to 20,000 ng/mL. Low, medium, and high QCs were prepared at 600, 3,000, and 15,000 ng/mL, respectively.

ECLIA Protocol

In this ECLIA, the standards, QCs, and the patient samples were pretreated with a 327 mM acetic acid solution to dissociate the therapeutic antibody from its target and remove interferences. After a 2 h incubation period at Room Temperature (RT) at 600 rpm, the study samples were neutralized by adding 300 mM Tris solution. The neutralized standards, QCs, and patient samples were then transferred to another assay plate and incubated with the working antibody solution containing the antitherapeutic antibody conjugated to biotin and to SULFO-TAG (MSD, Meso Scale Discovery by Meso Scale Diagnostics, LLC, Rockville, Maryland, USA) for 1 h at RT at 600 rpm. At the end of the incubation period, the bound complex was transferred to an MSD streptavidin-coated plate that had been blocked by incubating for a minimum of 1 h with Blocker Casein in PBS at RT. The antibody complex was thus bound to the streptavidin immobilized to the MSD plate via the biotinylated antibody with a 1 h incubation period at RT at 300 rpm. Any unbound compounds were removed via a wash step (4 wash cycles, 300 μL/well) (wash buffer: 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline, 0.138 M NaCl, 0.0027 M KCl, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 7.4). The immobilized complex was then stabilized by adding a diluted solution of formaldehyde (2% formaldehyde solution in 1× PBS, pH 7.4) and incubating for 30 min at RT at 300 rpm. After the incubation period, the formaldehyde was removed via another wash step. A 2× MSD Read Buffer solution was then added to the plate to generate an electrochemiluminescence signal proportional to the therapeutic antibody concentration. The plate was then loaded into the MSD SECTOR Imager and read within 5 min at RT. The assay concentration range was from 200 ng/mL to 20,000 ng/mL, and sample concentrations were determined from a standard curve, which was fitted using a four-parameter logistic regression model with a 1/y 2 weighting.

Each ECLIA processing plate included ten standards and two sets of low, medium, and high QCs. One replicate per QC was run before and after the plasma samples with unknown AB-16B5 concentrations. If a sample tested above the linear range, the sample was diluted with blank human plasma to fall within the linear range and reanalyzed.

For an analysis batch to pass, the following QC acceptance criteria had to be met for at least 67% (4 out of 6 QC replicates) of the QCs with at least 50% (1 out of 2 QC replicates) per concentration:the % CV of the duplicate QC concentrations must be ≤20%the % nominal concentration of each QC replicate must be within 80 to 120% of the theoretical concentration.

Mass Spectrometry Workflow

Bioinformatic Analysis and In Silico Selection of Surrogate Tryptic Peptides

We developed a set of LC-PRM/MS assays targeting two proteotypic peptides from the therapeutic IgG2’s light chain and two peptides from the heavy chain. Peptides were selected based on previously established selection rules for multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) assay development. The light and heavy chain sequences were digested in silico using trypsin as the enzyme. The resulting list of peptides was filtered for various criteria, including a suitable length (7–20 amino acids), no missed cleavages, lack of oxidizable amino acids such as methionine and tryptophan whenever possible, and importantly, absence of these sequences in the human proteome. The final four peptide sequences selected were considered to be the most promising for successful LC-PRM/MS assay development and spanned the antibody’s complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) either partially or entirely.

Peptide Synthesis and AAA/CZE-Based Characterization

The in-silico-selected peptides were synthesized as both light (NAT, natural abundance) and stable isotope-labeled standard (SIS) peptides. They were then characterized by amino acid analysis (AAA) and capillary zone electrophoresis (CZE) in-house to ensure both accurate peptide concentrations and high purity (≥85%).

For the AAA, diluted peptide solutions were spiked with certified isotopically labeled (13C, 15N) amino acids as internal standards. Liquid-phase hydrolysis was used with 6 M HCl and heating for 24 h. The released amino acids were then analyzed by reversed-phase (RP)-UHPLC-MRM-MS and quantified using the analyte/internal standard peak area ratios. Peptide quantitation was based on the sum of the individual amino acids from the known peptide sequences. The analysis was performed using a Shimadzu Nexera XR UHPLC system coupled to a Sciex QTrap 6500+ mass spectrometer (+ESI). Chromatographic separation was performed using a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm) and a 6 min 0–50% B gradient (A: 0.1% formic acid in water; B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile).

For CZE analysis, peptides were analyzed on an Agilent 7100 capillary electrophoresis system equipped with a 40 cm base fused silica capillary (50 μm ID; Agilent) using sodium phosphate buffer at pH 3.3. Samples were injected for 12 s at 70 mbar and separated over a 16 min run time. High voltage and current settings were 30 kV, 300 μA, and 6 W. The diode array detector (DAD) acquired signal at a wavelength of 200 nm. Purities were determined as the percentage of the target peak compared to the overall area of the electropherogram.

Peptide Precursor Optimization

To identify the most intense precursor-ion charge states and fragment ions, a peptide mixture consisting of 100 fmol of each NAT and SIS peptide was loaded onto Evotips in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The peptides were separated on an Evosep One HPLC using a reversed-phase column (EV1109, 8 cm × 150 μm, 1.5 μm) and a 21 min gradient (60 samples per day, SPD). The Evosep One was interfaced to a Thermo Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap operated in the positive PRM mode at an MS1 resolution of 70,000 and an MS2 resolution of 17,500. HPLC-PRM/MS was set to scan each peptide for the 2+ and 3+ precursor-ion charge states using a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 22.

Assessing Detectability of Target Peptides in Plasma Samples Spiked with Therapeutic Antibody

We analyzed five plasma samples spiked with 5,000 ng/mL therapeutic IgG2 using an in-solution digestion protocol, as follows. All aliquots were denatured and reduced by adding the denaturant (urea) and reducing agent (dithiothreitol, DTT) to produce a final concentration of 6 M urea, 210 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0), and 14 mM DTT and incubating for 30 min at 37 °C while shaking at 800 rpm on a Thermomixer (Eppendorf). Next, the samples were incubated in the dark at RT with an alkylating reagent (iodoacetamide, IAA) at a final concentration of 40 mM. The urea concentration was then reduced to <1 M by adding 100 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0). Trypsin was added to give a total protein/enzyme ratio of 20:1 (w/w), and the solution was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. Samples were quenched by adding formic acid to give a final concentration of 1%. The sample digests were spiked with the SIS peptide mixture (50 fmol/μg total digested protein, assuming an average 70 μg/μL total protein in human plasma), and 1 μg of the digest was loaded onto Evotips in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Optimization of Digestion Protocol Variables

To optimize the in-solution digestion protocol for maximum sensitivity and robustness, we investigated the following three variables: (1) denaturant: urea, sodium deoxycholate (DOC), and trifluoroethanol (TFE), (2) SIS peptide spiked in before or after digestion, and (3) digestion duration: 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 18 h. For each of the 42 conditions tested, human plasma spiked with 20,000 ng/mL of therapeutic IgG2 was used. Each condition was prepared in triplicate.

Urea-Based Digestion

Aliquots of 10 μL of human plasma were spiked with 20,000 ng/mL of the therapeutic IgG2. Then, half of the aliquots were spiked with the SIS peptide mixture at 10 fmol/μg total protein before proceeding with the digestion protocol; the other half were spiked after digestion. To digest, samples were denatured and reduced at a final concentration of 6 M urea, 210 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0), and 14 mM DTT while incubating for 30 min at 37 °C and shaking at 800 rpm on a Thermomixer (Eppendorf). Next, the samples were incubated in the dark at RT with 40 mM IAA. The urea concentration was reduced to <1 M by adding 100 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0). Trypsin was added to give a total protein/enzyme ratio of 20:1 (w/w), and the solution was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. At each of the various digestion time points, the samples were quenched with 2% formic acid and rapidly frozen at −80 °C. Once all the time points had been collected, all the samples were thawed simultaneously. For the samples that received the SIS peptide mixture after digestion, the SIS peptide mixture was spiked in at 10 fmol/μg total protein.

DOC-Based Digestion

The second denaturant tested was DOC, and, as above, half of the samples were spiked with the SIS peptide mixture at 10 fmol/μg total protein before digestion, and the other half were not spiked until after digestion. Thus, the first half was spiked with SIS peptide mix at 10 fmol/μg total protein and then subjected to reduction and denaturation. To do this, the 10 μL aliquots of human plasma spiked with 20,000 ng/mL therapeutic IgG2 and SIS mix were incubated with a final concentration of 0.9% DOC/170 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0)/1.8 mM TCEP for 30 min at 60 °C and were shaken at 800 rpm on a Thermomixer (Eppendorf). Next, the samples were incubated in the dark at RT in a solution containing a final concentration of 23 mM IAA. Finally, trypsin was added at a total protein/enzyme ratio of 20:1 (w/w) and incubated as above, in a time-course fashion (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 18 h), at 37 °C. To quench the digest, formic acid was added to give a final concentration of 1% at each time point. Samples were rapidly frozen at −80 °C until all time points had been collected. All samples were thawed simultaneously, centrifuged at 4,000 × g at RT, and the samples that had not received the SIS spike were, at this point, spiked with the SIS mix at a concentration of 10 fmol/μg total protein digest.

TFE-Based Digestion

The final denaturant tested was TFE. Here, 10 μL aliquots of human plasma containing 20,000 ng/mL of therapeutic IgG2 were either spiked or not spiked with SIS mix. Samples were denatured and reduced at final concentrations of 80 mM ammonium bicarbonate (AmBic), 10% TFE, and 10 mM TCEP, and incubated at RT for 30 min while shaking at 250 rpm on a Thermomixer (Eppendorf). The samples were diluted to produce a final concentration of 95 mM AmBic, followed by addition of trypsin at a total protein/enzyme ratio of 20:1 (w/w), and incubated at 37 °C for varying time periods (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 18 h), as above. The digests were quenched with formic acid to a final concentration of 1%, and immediately frozen at −80 °C. All samples were thawed simultaneously, and the samples without SIS were spiked at this point with 10 fmol/μg total protein digest.

LC-PRM/MS Method and Instrument Settings

All of the prepared samples were loaded onto Evotips (20 μL) and analyzed using an Evosep One LC system interfaced to a Q Exactive Plus (Thermo) using either the C18 EV1064 (8 cm × 100 μm, 3 μm ID) column or the C18 EV1109 (8 cm × 150 μm, 1.5 μm ID), and the 60 SPD method for a 21 min gradient as defined by the manufacturer.

Linear Range and LLOQ Determination

To determine the linear range of the four target surrogate peptides, plasma samples were spiked with 500,000 ng/mL of the therapeutic antibody, then serially diluted to achieve ten concentration levels ranging from 200 to 500,000 ng/mL. Three replicates were prepared for each concentration level. In addition, a blank sample was prepared consisting of plasma without the therapeutic-antibody spike. Enzymatic digestion was performed with urea as the denaturant, with the SIS mix being spiked in before digestion, and a digestion time of 2 h at 37 °C, since this was the method that showed the highest reproducibility. In addition to the calibrators and the blank, five clinical-trial plasma samples were prepared. The digested samples were loaded onto Evotips (1 μg total protein digest, 0.5 fmol SIS) and analyzed by LC-PRM/MS, as described above. The lower and upper limits of quantitation (LLOQ, ULOQ) were defined as the lowest and highest curve points where at least two of three replicates were within 75% and 125% accuracy.

Quantitation of Therapeutic Antibody in Ten Clinical Trial Patient Samples

A calibration curve was generated for each surrogate peptide by serially diluting human plasma spiked with therapeutic antibody at known concentrations, using nonspiked plasma as a diluent to maintain comparable background matrix consistency between calibrators, ranging from 200 to 500,000 ng/mL. The calibrators and the plasma samples obtained from a patient at ten different time points during a phase II clinical trial were prepared using the optimized digestion protocol, with urea as the denaturant, SIS peptides spiked in before digestion, and a digestion time of 2 h. As before, for each digested plasma sample, 1 μg total protein digest containing 0.5 fmol of the SIS mixture was loaded onto Evotips and analyzed by LC-PRM/MS. The concentration values determined by LC-PRM/MS and ECLIA were compared.

Data Analysis and Visualization

Data visualization was carried out using the open-access Skyline Quantitative Analysis software (version 22.2.0.255, University of Washington). The chromatographic peaks for each surrogate peptide targeted were individually and manually inspected for shape, interferences, retention time shifts, transition patterns, and proper integration. The calibration curves generated were weighted using a 1/x 2 linear regression and were used to calculate the original protein concentrations in the samples as nanograms per milliliter of plasma. Further data analysis and calculations were performed using Excel (Microsoft).

Results and Discussion

Peptide Selection and Precursor Optimization

We selected peptide sequences covering the CDRs of the therapeutic antibody, comprising two peptides from the heavy chain (VSGFNIK and IDPAYGNTK) and two from the light chain (SSQSLLNSR and QSYNLWTFGQGTK), as illustrated in Figure A.

After precursor optimization, the detectability of the antibody tryptic surrogate peptides was assessed. As shown in Figure , both the light peptides (endogenous, “END”) derived from digesting the therapeutic IgG2 spiked into plasma at 5,000 ng/mL and the corresponding SIS peptides (spiked into the plasma digest) were observed, confirming detectability of the therapeutic antibody surrogate peptides at the given concentration.

This control was used to ensure that the light-peptide signals detected in the antibody-spiked plasma samples were not due to carryover from a sample containing heavy labeled peptide (i.e., if labeling efficiency was below 100%) or due to the unexpected presence of the endogenous peptide sequences in plasma.

Optimization of Digestion Variables (Denaturant, Digestion Duration, SIS Spike)

To ensure reproducible and sensitive LC-PRM/MS assays, we evaluated the impact of three digestion variables on the concentration results: type of denaturant, spiking in the SIS peptides before or after digestion, and digestion duration (Figure ).

Workflow for the evaluation of three digestion variables: (1) denaturant, (2) SIS spike before or after digestion, and (3) digestion duration.

As shown in Figure , the choice of the denaturant had a significant impact on the concentration results obtained. For peptide VSGFNIK, only with urea as the denaturant were we able to obtain concentration results above 20,000 ng/mL (Figure A). For peptides IDPAYGNTK, SSQSLLNSR, and QSYNLWTFGQGTK, both urea and DOC resulted in concentration values of comparable magnitude (Figure B–D). In contrast, the use of TFE as the denaturant resulted in no significant light peptide signal. The SIS peptide signal intensities were comparable between denaturants and whether SIS was spiked in before or after digestion, suggesting that the TFE protocol led to poor release of the target peptides from target therapeutic IgG2.

Time-course digestion study results for human plasma spiked with 20,000 ng/mL therapeutic IgG2 using urea, DOC, and TFE as denaturants, and spiking-in SIS peptides before or after digestion. Panels A, B, C, and D show peptides VSGFNIK, IDPAYGNTK, SSQSLLNSR, and QSYNLWTFGQGTK, respectively. Error bars indicate standard deviations calculated for triplicate concentration values at each time point.

Spiking SIS before or after digestion also had a significant impact on both the magnitude and precision of the concentration measurements, as shown in Figures and S2, the latter showing the % CVs obtained with all the conditions tested. Urea with SIS peptides spiked in after digestion shows significantly tighter CVs, as highlighted by the red arrow in Figure S2. Thus, the most stable and precise measurements between replicates and across the digestion time points for all four peptides were achieved using urea as the denaturant, with SIS spiked in before digestion. For this condition, the median CV across time points ranged from 2.6% for SSQSLLNSR to 4.8% for QSYNLWTFGQGTK. While urea coupled with spiking in the SIS peptides after digestion resulted in somewhat elevated concentration measurements, we observed significant variability between replicate measurements of individual time points and between time points. DOC as a denaturant also showed significant variability for most time points across peptides. For these reasons, both conditions were eliminated as the best conditions for achieving the most reproducible and robust assays. Ultimately, the combination of urea as the denaturant, spiking in the SIS peptides before digestion, and a digestion time of 2 h was determined to be optimal and was used for the subsequent experiments.

Linear Range Determination

Using the most robust and reproducible digestion conditions identified in the previous experiments, we characterized the linear ranges for the four target surrogate peptides by spiking varying concentrations of the therapeutic antibody and a constant level of the SIS peptides into the human plasma. In addition, five plasma samples of a single clinical trial patient, denoted as U1–U5, were analyzed. Sample U1 represents a predose time point, and samples U2–U5 represent different time points along the dosing schedule after receiving the drug.

The resulting linear range regression lines showed excellent correlation (Figure ), with coefficients of determination (R 2) ranging from 0.9689 to 0.9906. All CVs were below 20%, and most were below 10% (Figure S3). The linear ranges for peptides VSGFNIK, IDPAYGNTK, and SSQSLLNSR spanned 200 ng/mL to 500,000 ng/mL (a 2,500-fold range), while QSYNLWTFGQGTK exhibited a linear range of 200 ng/mL to 50,000 ng/mL (a 250-fold range). Importantly, the concentration values for the clinical trial samples, U1–U5, followed the same concentration pattern across all four surrogate peptides. For instance, sample U2 showed the highest determined antibody concentration, while sample U1 showed the lowest concentration across all surrogate peptide concentrations measured. Concentration levels for samples U2–U5 were well within their respective linear ranges. Sample U1, however, was a plasma specimen obtained from the patient before receiving the therapeutic IgG2, and therefore, the determined concentrations for that particular sample were below the LLOQ for all target peptides, except for SSQSLLNSR. The detected signal for SSQSLLNSR, however, was attributed to interference, as shown in Figure C. This interference was identified based on differing transition patterns, specifically variations in the peak intensity order of fragment ions between light and heavy signals, which resulted in an apparent but false concentration within the linear range. The ability to detect and differentiate interferences highlights the superior specificity of mass spectrometry compared with ECLIA and potentially ELISA, although we did not test the latter.

Linear ranges established for the four surrogate peptides: (A) VSGFNIK, (B) IDPAYGNTK, (C) SSQSLLNSR, and (D) QSYNLWTFGQGTK, for the quantitation of the target therapeutic IgG2. The LLOQ and ULOQ indicate the lower and upper limits of the regression lines, respectively. Labels U1–U5 indicate the clinical trial samples with “unknown” (U) antibody concentrations.

For QSYNLWTFGQGTK, the U2 sample was above the ULOQ since the ULOQ for QSYNLWTFGQGTK was 10-fold lower than that of the other three peptides.

The peptide concentrations determined for the clinical trial samples showed excellent correlation with R2 values of ≥0.986, as shown in Figure S4, suggesting that any of the peptides can be used to track antibody accumulation in clinical trial samples. The slopes for the peptide comparison ranged from 0.78 to 1.75, indicating that each peptide leads to different absolute concentrations, which is not unexpected in LC–MS/MS experiments involving internal standard peptides. Because the SIS peptides were spiked into the plasma samples prior to digestion, the different slopes can most likely be explained by peptide-specific differences in digestion efficiency. A possible way to achieve accurate concentration values, not just precise concentration values, would be to obtain and spike-in a stable isotope-labeled standard version of the target antibody (rather than stable isotope-labeled standard versions of the surrogate peptides) into the plasma samples prior to digestion.

Quantitation of Therapeutic Antibody in Ten Longitudinal Plasma Samples from a Clinical-Trial Patient

In the final stage of our study, therapeutic antibody concentrations in ten plasma samples from a patient treated with AB-16B5 during a phase II clinical trial were quantified by LC-PRM/MS and compared to the results obtained by the ECLIA (Figure ). Samples analyzed are in order of drug administration cycles (i.e., days 0, 8, 15, 22, 29, 36, 64, 148), where day 0 represents cycle 1 day 1, 0 h, and day 148 represents cycle 8 day 1, 0 h. The patient dosing schedule is shown in Table S1 and ranged from day 0 to day 148. The 0 h time points represent sample collection immediately before drug administration, and the 1 h time points represent sample collection 1 h after drug administration.

(A) Eight out of ten samples collected immediately before drug administration (0 h time points: P1, P3–7, P9–10), and (B) two out of ten samples collected 1 h after drug administration (1 h time points: P2, P8). *Note that the concentrations for QSYNLWTFGQGTK in samples P2 and P8 were above the ULOQ.

The expectation was that as the treatment progressed, the therapeutic antibody would accumulate in the patient. The ECLIA results, however, showed no evidence of accumulation at the 0 h time points (Figure A). In contrast, all four antibody-specific surrogate peptides validated in our LC-PRM/MS study showed the same trend of expected therapeutic antibody accumulation. Interestingly, the two 1 h time points (Figure B), representing plasma samples collected 1 h after drug administration on days 1 and 36, showed concentration values in the same order of magnitude for both LC-PRM/MS and ECLIA, suggesting that shortly after the drug is administered into the patient, the ECLIA method is still able to quantify the therapeutic antibody at comparable concentrations as LC-PRM/MS. Note that the concentrations determined for QSYNLWTFGQGTK by LC-PRM/MS are above the ULOQ of 50,000 ng/mL. However, as the drug spends time circulating in the patient for a week, the ECLIA method loses its ability to accurately quantify the antibody in the corresponding plasma samples, strengthening the hypothesis that the antibody undergoes structural changes in vivo that negatively affect the binding affinity of the ECLIA antibody toward the target epitope.

This demonstrates that the LC-PRM/MS approach has an advantage over the ECLIA since it does not rely on the three-dimensional structure of the target molecule. As suggested by recent literature, the in vivo transitioning of the IgG2-A form to IgG2-A/B and IgG2-B might hinder not only the ECLIA in our study but also those ECLIA or ELISA developed by other researchers aiming to reliably quantify therapeutic antibodies. Here, we show that LC-PRM/MS can solve this problem.

The samples provided for this study were from a single patient, which prevented us from being able to confirm that the drug accumulation trend holds true for other patients. However, the longitudinal nature of the samples did allow us to confirm that LC-PRM/MS enabled the detection of the accumulation of the therapeutic antibody target in circulation, while the ECLIA failed to show it. We believe that subsequent quantitative analyses using LC-PRM/MS on other patient samples would confirm our observations.

Conclusion

In this study, we have shown that LC-PRM/MS can experimentally quantify the expected accumulation of an anticancer therapeutic IgG2 in phase II clinical trial samples, while the ECLIA failed to do so. The use of LC-PRM/MS was not affected by the conversion of IgG2-A into IgG2-A/B and IgG2-B and the associated structural changes that likely negatively affected the performance of the ECLIA.

The LC-PRM/MS assays developed here showed sufficient sensitivity and linear range to cover the relevant concentration ranges expected in clinical trial samples, as demonstrated by the successful quantitation of the therapeutic antibody administered to a patient across a 148 day dosing schedule.

The successful quantitation of the therapeutic antibody using LC-PRM/MS has important implications for clinical trials and drug development as reliable quantitation is necessary to ensure safety and efficacy.
