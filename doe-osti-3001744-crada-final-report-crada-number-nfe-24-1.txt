 ABSTRACT
ThermaMatrix, Inc provides novel vision inspection solutions for a wide range of manufacturers
and industries, providing and implementing the leading technologies for nondestructive
inspection (NDI) and material characterization. Many other inspection solutions are either not
adequate or are not approachable due to implementation barriers needing expert level operators,
excessive inspection time, and high cost. ThermaMatrix’s advanced vision inspection technology
addresses all of these limitations. The Lab Embedded Entrepreneurial Program (LEEP)
opportunity by the Department of Energy (DOE) allows small-business start-ups to leverage
national laboratory capabilities and skilled scientists to rapidly develop their technology that
aligns with DOE goals. ThermaMatrix, Inc. was positioned in the Innovation Crossroads program
at Oak Ridge National Laboratory to further develop the novel Watson Vision Inspection System
to support manufacturing quality control efforts. The research goals were (1) explore fundamental
parameters that would improve preexisting capabilities, (2) full-scale industrial setup for
demonstration, and (3) capability testing and verification. Manufacturing is demanding more NDI
implementation to support their quality control needs, which this technology development would
support.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

There is an ever-growing need for industries seeking inspection and material characterization
capabilities. This includes different stages including R&D, in-line, and post-manufactured testing.
ThermaMatrix looks to bring solutions for researchers, scientists, engineers, quality control, and
line managers within academics, composites, aerospace, semiconductor, railway, metal
manufacturing sectors, and many more. Currently the metrology market is at $14.9B and expected
to grow to $18.5B by 2030. ThermaMatrix’s technology can also be segmented within the NDI
market, which is valued at $14.3B and expected to grow to $33.7B by 2030. This rapid increase is
attributed to the increasing demand for NDI and quality control methods within the
manufacturing sector, which is widely adopting more NDI methods to assure quality and
standards. Through the LEEP - Innovation Crossroads program, fundamental studies would be
performed to improve preexisting capabilities, develop a full-scale system to perform industrial
demonstrations, and test out capabilities at scale and validate results.

1.1 Task 1: Thermal and Optical Systems Optimization
Participant’s optical metrology technique utilizes a heat source to thermally excite the inspected
material in order to characterize it. Participant and Contractor will explore different modes of
heating (convection, radiation, etc.) against a portfolio of materials to best understand the
thermomechnical response of that heating method. An optimized optical system will capture the
thermomechanical response for material characterization. A testing apparatus will be constructed
by the Contractor which explores the different heating methods and will measure the thermal
strain response with optical cameras (two dimensional/three dimensional-2D/3D) using
Participant’s Local-Digital-Image-Correlation (LDIC) technique.

1.2 Task 2: Prototype System Development
The objective of this task is to combine all sub-components (optical cameras, heat source, and IR
camera) which have been optimized in Task 1 into a streamlined end-effector system. The
prototype development, by the Participant and Contractor, will provide understanding on how
each sub-component interacts with each other and how to optimize the inspection process. A
software graphical user interface (GUI) will be developed by the Contractor to automate the
inspection process and allow all sub-components to work coherently together.

1.3 Task 3: System Validation and Demonstration
Participant and Contractor will validate the prototype from Task 2 against traditional methods.
The system’s capabilities will be benchmarked compared to other methods to allow the
development of a system’s specification data sheet to display the competitive edge of the LDIC
method. A demonstration of a practical application will be performed to understand commercial
viability and establish the technology readiness level.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE’S MISSION

ThermaMatrix advances energy-efficient manufacturing with nondestructive inspection (NDI)
solutions, aligning with the Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy’s mission to drive
manufacturing innovation. ThermaMatrix’s vision-based systems provide real-time material
insights, optimizing production while minimizing waste and energy use. By enabling precision-
driven, automated quality control, ThermaMatrix helps industries achieve manufacturing
efficiency and cost improvement goals.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

ThermaMatrix is developing an optical metrology noninvasive inspection tool that utilizes a local
heating method and strain-mapping technique to measure and characterize material properties.
The developed technique is being labeled Local-Digital-Image-Correlation (LDIC). This device
measures structural directionality, thermomechanical responses, detect defects such as micro-
cracking and delaminations, and monitoring residual stress. Also, the first prototype vision
inspection system was developed, called Watson, shown in Figure 2. This system has local laser
heating, micro-scale vision system, and rapid gantry motion for quick inspection. This was
developed as a cutting-edge characterization tool design specifically for research and
development experimentation.

Figure 2: The Watson material characterization tool for R&D is able to perform four main
capabilities including structural directionality measurements, thermomechanical response
 monitoring, defect detection, and residual stress monitoring.

The first capability, structural directionality, is an important characteristic within the composites
industries. Fiber reinforced polymer (FRP) composites utilize micro-fibers embedded in a
polymer matrix system to improve structural properties. The direction of the fibers is highly
critical due to all properties (mechanical, thermal, electrical, etc.) align with the fiber direction of
the composite component. The current industry standard uses x-ray computed tomography (Xray-
CT), a radiography technology. Although, current limitations of this technology are slow
inspection and analysis time, expert level to operate and interpret, and high cost equipment cost.

ThermaMatrix’s Watson system provides precise fiber direction measurement with an accuracy of
2.5° ± 1.5°, at 10x the inspection speed and 1/10th the cost of Xray-CT, enabling reliable insights
into composite integrity and performance. A discontinuous glass fiber reinforced polypropylene
(GF-PP) composite sample was inspected by the Watson system. The system as able to capture
100 data points within an hour of the composite and provide fiber direction map with distribution
results, shown in Figure 3. This helps manufacturers monitor their production process while
preserving optimal fiber direction for specific applications.

 Figure 3: The Watson system was able to measure the discontinuous fiber direction for a
 glass fiber reinforced polypropylene composite sample. A 10x10 data capture array was
 collected to provide a fiber direction map and distribution results.

Another capability expanded on was the defect detection by capturing delamination within a composite
structure shown in Figure 4. Past research was able to showcase the detection of microcracking, but
delaminations are another common problem within the composite industry. Subsurface delaminations cause
a separation of layers within the composite, decreasing structural integrity and providing crack nucleation
sites which can lead to failures. An aerospace grade unidirectional carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP)
system with embedded Teflon tape inserts with varying sizes to simulate subsurface delaminations was
manufactured. The Teflon tapes with varying sizes were embedded 5 mm depth of a 10 mm thick sample. A
conductive heating source was explored to provide large heating coverage and induce thermal expansion
strain mismatch to capture the delamination defects. All simulated delamination defects were captured.
Although, there is a non-uniformity with the strain signal response. This is due to a non-uniform thermal
load applied the material. Improving the heating process would improve signal-to-noise responses.

 Figure 4: The ThermaMatrix technology ability to capture delamination defects within
 composites structures.

The ability to monitor residual stress utilizing our vision inspection system was explored. The current
industrial method is Xray diffraction (XRD) which monitors the diffraction patterns of x-ray radiation
interacting with the crystal structure of the material. The main limitation is XRD can only measure
crystalline materials, mostly metals. Also, it is a relatively small inspection area. The ThermaMatrix
technology utilizes a change in thermomechanical response, due to internal residual stress, and monitors this
change with full-field results.

At first a simulation was performed to confirm there is a change in thermomechanical strain response based
on an internal stress applied, simulating residual stress. The simulation consisted of a 25x25x2 mm3
aluminum 6061-T6 plate with a ramping heat flux load of 1-1000 kW/m2 applied at a 1 mm spot diameter
heated zone. A mesh scheme of 11,968 hex linear elements, 4-layers through thickness was setup. A varying
thermal strain response with different applied stress was captured, both at the local heated zone and the
global sample area, shown in Figure 5. The results are promising, although this is a macro-scale model
which has it’s limitations for thermomechanical applications. Future work will explore a micro-scale and
atomic-scale simulations for further confirmation of trends.

 Figure 5: The macro-scale simulation relating thermomechanical response to internal
 residual stress.

Experimentation was performed for model validation. Utilizing a universal frame, an aluminum 6061-T6
sample was mechanically loaded at 0 MPa, 123 MPa, and 248 MPa. A conductive heat source was placed on
the backside of the sample, while a thermography camera and optical LDIC camera monitored the front of
the sample. The analysis was furthered by taking the ratio between the thermal expansion strain response
between the x-direction and y-direction, where the mechanical load was applied in the y-direction. The
experimental results show an increasing trend in the thermal expansion ratio with increasing applied stress
trends, and align with the simulation, shown in Figure 6. Although, there is a magnitude difference which
could be caused by boundary conditions. Further investigation is necessary to confirm experimental trends
where more data needs to be captured at smaller applied stress increments.

Figure 6: Experimental results relating thermomechanical strain response to applied residual
 stress. Comparing experimental and simulation trends.

The next prototype iteration was designed and developed to satisfy a scaled-up inspection system fit for
industrial applications. A vision inspection module (VIM) was designed with all components within an
enclosure. This includes laser source, optical camera and thermal camera, along with electrical components
all within one system that can be attached to any gantry system or robotic arm. The VIM sub-components
were 3D printed and tested for fit and assembly. Next, a full-scale robotic cell enclosure was designed to
house an industrial robotic arm with the VIM attached. Bertelkamp Automation, an industrial automation
specialist company, provided a design for the robotic cell enclosure. There is space to scan industrial sized
components. Both VIM and robotic cell enclosure can be seen in Figure 7. This will allow full-scale
industrial scale demonstrations to be performed.

 5. COMMERCIALIZAION POSSIBILITIES

The greatest takeaway was a deep understanding of business operations and strategy. Innovation
Crossroads and Spark Cleantech Accelerator accelerated this learning through customer discovery,
market research, and value-proposition strategies. Through the fellowship, Participant engaged with
over 150 industry professionals to refine ThermaMatrix’s market position. Participant found that
Structural Directionality and ThermoMechanical Response technology had limited demand, Defect
Detection capability had a viable but highly competitive market, and Residual Stress capability
offered high demand with low competition but required significant technical development.

 6. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

This experience allowed Participant to explore the possibility of developing a small business. Although, it
was concluded by Participant and with help from multiple advisors there were multiple unfavorable market
situations and high risk moving forward. It was concluded to close the business and end the fellowship.
Although, this opportunity allowed Participant to accept a staff scientist position at ORNL focusing on NDI
for manufacturing. Participant is very grateful for the fellowship opportunity and will always have an
interest in the entrepreneurial pathway.

 7. CONCLUSION

This incredible experience covered all aspects of operating and scaling a small business. Participant
conducted experimental testing while collaborating with ORNL interns, post-docs, and scientists on
technical development. Participant’s business strategy focused on customer discovery, marketing,
proposal writing, and key performance tracking. Participant also leveraged Knoxville’s
entrepreneurial ecosystem, including Innovation Crossroads, UT Spark Cleantech Accelerator, UT
Research Foundation, IACMI, and Knoxville Entrepreneur Center.
