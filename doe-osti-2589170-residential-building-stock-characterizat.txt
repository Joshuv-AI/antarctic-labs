Community Context and Scope of Work
Palm Beach County (PBC), Florida, is home to nearly 1.5 million residents living in approximately
706,000 housing units. 1 Although PBC is relatively affluent, more than 11% of its population is
classified as low-income. 2 Several census tracts across PBC are classified as disadvantaged due to
high home and transportation energy burden, 3 and having environmental justice considerations
including exposure to legacy pollutants. 4 All residents are exposed to extreme heat and many
coastal and low-lying areas are at risk of flooding and sea level rise.

A 2021 study by the Florida International University’s Metropolitan Center found that 56.6% of
renters in Palm Beach County are cost-burdened, meaning they pay more than 30% of their income
on housing. 5 In addition, PBC has 107 identified disadvantaged communities under the Justice40
Initiative, including 23 along the Atlantic Ocean and Intracoastal Waterway and eight (8) along
Lake Okeechobee. These communities include some of PBC’s most vulnerable populations to
climate change and sea level rise, making it increasingly important to invest in resources for future
resiliency.

Renters in PBC face high housing costs and rely on landlords and building owners to make energy
and water conservation investment decisions. Utility costs add to the lack of affordability in
housing. The PBC Office of Resilience (OOR) and municipalities composing the Municipal
Resilience Partnership (MRP) would like to develop a program to work with rental property owners
and managers to make energy efficiency investments in their properties, passing utility cost savings
to their tenants. MRP, through PBC OOR, applied to E2C Expert Match for analysis to justify and
prioritize investment of potential weatherization and energy efficiency programs and policies to
reduce energy costs for renters. However, they lack a building inventory that characterizes the
makeup of housing units, their occupants, and their energy consumption to inform those decisions.

To address these issues, representatives from PBC OOR and NREL’s E2C Expert Match team
agreed on a scope of work to characterize the existing residential building stock through four tasks.
The first task was to use publicly available data from the NREL’s ResStockTM model to characterize
the following attributes of PBC’s residential building stock: building type, renter/owner status, size
(square footage), age of buildings (vintage), HVAC system types, and, for multi-family buildings,
number of units. The second task was to compare ResStock data to existing local open-source data
provided by the client to validate the ResStock data and add any additional perspective to the
residential building stock characterization in the first task. The third task was to complete an
analysis of ResStock data to quantify the potential changes in energy demand, utility costs, and
greenhouse gas emissions from the baseline residential building stock in PBC compared to 16
upgrade packages. The final task was to summarize the major takeaways from the previous tasks

Task 1: Residential building stock characterization
using ResStock
What is ResStock?
ResStock is a physics-based, bottom-up, white box, residential building stock energy model
developed by NREL. 6 Figure 1 gives an overview of the three-part workflow for the ResStock
model. First, a residential building stock is generated using the data from several national-level
surveys and other data sources. 7 Next, the sub-hourly energy use of each housing unit in the
building stock is calculated using its building characteristics, local weather data, and occupant
behavior. Additionally, upgrade measures can be applied to any housing unit and the same
calculation of sub-hourly energy use will be performed with these upgrades applied. In this way, it
is possible to attribute the change in energy consumption in each simulated housing unit to the
specific upgrade measures applied to that housing unit. Last, high-performance computing
resources summarize this sub-hourly data into annual energy consumption, utility bill, and
emissions data for the entire building stock modeled. This data can be used to inform decisions
about building retrofitting measures that can be made to the building stock to achieve the varying
levels of energy, utility bill, or emissions impact.

An important consideration for the retrofits is the type of HVAC system present in a particular
household. Figure 9 shows that most single-family and mobile homes have ducted cooling systems
with either traditional AC systems or heat pumps. Additionally, most multi-family dwelling units
have similar configuration, with less than 25% of these having any type of shared HVAC system
(for either heating or cooling). Most importantly, more than 90% of HVAC systems in PBC are
individual systems. It is possible that repetition factors and economies of scale could allow for
faster and less expensive retrofits for these individual systems, even in multi-family buildings.
Furthermore, central AC systems can be upgraded to the highest efficiency HVAC systems much
more directly as compared to households without ducts. There was not a meaningful difference

Task 2: Local open-source data comparison
In a review of the local open-source building stock data provided by PBC representatives, there was
alignment between several building stock data points from local building stock data and the
ResStock data. These include the number of residential building units, the total population, the
number of occupants in different sized homes (i.e., number of units), a comparison of income to
renter/owner status, a comparison of building type and number of units, and a comparison of renter,
owner, and vacant unit counts. This was unsurprising given the fact that this local data was
primarily based on results from the American Community Survey, which is also one of the national
surveys leveraged by ResStock. However, there were some valuable data points provided by PBC
that focused on cost burdened households and households of color, neither of which are reported on
by ResStock. While these data points did not inform the remainder of the work, they were included
in Appendix B given their importance to programmatic decisions.

Through a comparison of the baseline PBC low-income residential building stock with the 16
different upgrade packages, it was found that the upgrade packages that included the ultra-high
efficiency heat pump systems with electric back up produced the greatest amount of modeled
energy savings (Figure 11). See Figure C-1 in the Appendix C to see a comparison of total
electricity consumption of all upgrade packages compared to the baseline. It is important to note
that given the high penetration of fully electrified households in Palm Beach County, the
comparison of percent of baseline electricity to percent of baseline total energy is very similar. For
this analysis, we focused solely on the savings in electricity consumption compared to the baseline.

Based on Figure 11, there are three main takeaways:

 • ENERGY STAR (Upgrade 1) and Ultra-high efficiency heat pumps (Upgrade 3) account for
 a modeled savings in electricity consumption of ~6% and ~20%, respectively, both when
 applied alone and when applied with envelope and full appliance electrification with
 efficiency upgrades.

 • The addition of full appliance electrification with efficiency upgrade package increases
 modeled savings by 9% when comparing Upgrades 6 and 11 and 8.4% when comparing
 Upgrades 8 and 13. While all four of these upgrade packages include other HVAC and
 envelope upgrade measures, there is little interaction between these HVAC and envelope
 upgrade measures with the upgrades involved in the full appliance electrification with
 efficiency upgrade package. For example, installing a new induction stove will have a
 relatively minor impact on internal heat gain, and thus a minor impact on envelope and
 equipment efficiency measures. In PBC, ‘Light Touch Envelope’ improvements result in

 2.2% decrease in modeled electricity consumption when compared to the Baseline.
 Additionally, when this upgrade package was added to others, we see a 2.1% increase in
 savings when comparing Upgrades 1 and 6, and a 1.1% increase in savings when comparing
 Upgrades 3 and 8.

Of the building characteristics that PBC representatives were most interested in exploring in Task 1,
we found that building age (vintage) had the greatest impact on the potential electricity savings of
the various upgrades as compared to the baseline. Figure 12 shows that upgrades that include either
ENERGY STAR Heat Pumps (Upgrades 1, 6, and 11) or Ultra-high Efficiency Heat Pumps
(Upgrades 3, 8, and 13) generate more annual electricity savings for older households. Specifically,
households built before 1960 have markedly higher modeled energy savings even compared to
households built in the 1960s or 1970s. Additionally, we found that the difference between homes
with different vintages was more pronounced for the upgrades that included Ultra-high Efficiency
Heat Pumps (Upgrades 3, 8, and 13). While the energy savings potential of these older homes is
larger, there may be other factors that may make these homes more costly to retrofit. These include
lead paint, asbestos, and other legacy issues that may increase both the time and cost to retrofit a
household.

While not as significant, building type had an impact on annual electricity savings (Figure 13). In
general, single-family detached and attached households experienced greater modeled annual
electricity savings with heat pump upgrades as compared to mobile homes and multi-family
households. However, this could be a function of the physical size of these buildings (i.e., single-
family households have on average more conditioned space, both in terms of area and volume, and
have more exterior facing walls). These larger households with larger building enclosure surface
areas and more interior space to heat and cool will generally see greater amount of relative energy
savings with the installation of increased efficiency HVAC systems.

Modeled utility bill impact and emissions reduction potential
Using the electricity rates from the electric utility in PBC, Florida Power and Light (FPL), and the
data presented in Figure 11, it is possible to estimate the annual utility bill impact for each upgrade
package. According to the FPL’s Residential Service Electricity Rate, residents who use less than
1,000 kWh of electricity a month are charged $0.22637/kWh. 11 Based on Figure 14, we can
ascertain that upgrading low-income households in PBC to an ENERGY STAR (Upgrade 1) or
Ultra-high Efficiency (Upgrade 3) Heat Pump would decrease median annual utility bills of most of
these households by ~$150 to ~$490, respectively. In a similar analysis to that done for Figure 11,
we can infer that full electrification using high-efficiency appliances (Upgrades 11 and 13) can save
households ~$200 each year on their utility bills. Finally, as discussed previously, the addition of
the Light Touch Envelope upgrade package does little to lower utility bills for low-income PBC
residents.

Another analysis that can be done using the ResStock data for PBC is the emissions (kg-CO2e)
reduction potential for these upgrades. Using the Mid-Case 15-year Long-run Marginal Emission
Rates for electricity from the Cambium dataset, it is possible to calculate the median change in
annual household emissions for each upgrade 12 (Figure 15). The modeled reduction potential of
these upgrades is generally proportional to the utility bill savings, with the upgrades that include the
Ultra-high Efficiency Heat Pump measure (Upgrades 3, 8, and 13) creating the largest emissions
reduction potential. To put these values into context, the modeled annual emissions reduction
potential of Upgrade 3 (511 kg-CO2e) is equivalent to the emissions generated by driving a standard
gasoline powered passenger vehicle over 1,250 miles. 13

Summary
Limitations
This analysis was based in large part on publicly available data produced by the ResStock model. It
is important to note that this model has the following limitations:

 • Analysis is based on ResStock modeled energy consumption, and all models have
 uncertainties. For example, discrepancies in assumptions of HVAC setpoints and occupant
 behaviors could introduce gaps between modeled and realized energy savings for a home
 upgrade project.
 • ResStock models do not account for energy-conserving behaviors (e.g., not using an
 appliance due to bill concerns), broken equipment, or other housing conditions requiring
 remediation (e.g., mold and moisture, broken windows), which may be prevalent in some
 housing.
 • Upgrade costs do not account for additional services that may be needed to perform home
 upgrades—namely, electrical service upgrades.
 • Description of the building stock is based on national surveys and datasets, which may hide
 local unique characteristics that are limited in scale.
 • Although this analysis provides data about energy changes for low-income households with
 different upgrades, it did not provide the spatial distribution of these impacts by
 neighborhood due to a lack of smaller geographic resolutions (e.g., census tract or block).
 • This analysis does not consider the initial costs to purchase and install the equipment that
 make up each of the measure packages described above.

Major Takeaways
In this final section, we will present major takeaways from the residential building stock
characterization of PBC, the major takeaways from the electricity, utility bill, and emissions savings
as a result of upgrade packages presented for low-income households in PBC and next steps that
PBC representatives could consider utilizing this information and gather more pertinent data to
improve the energy performance of the existing residential buildings in the county.

For the residential building stock characterization, we identified three major takeaways. The first
major takeaway is that 60% of housing units in PBC are single-family dwellings and approximately
70% of these dwellings are occupied by the owner. Second, a majority of multi-family housing
units are in buildings with less than 30 units and approximately 60% of these dwellings are
occupied by renters. Third, over 90% of HVAC systems in PBC are individual systems. The high
prevalence of these systems may be beneficial to program design based on the fact that repetition
factors and economies of scale can be achieved.

Similarly for the modeled electricity, utility bill, and emissions impact for low-income households
in PBC based with the various upgrade packages, we identified three major takeaways. The first
major takeaway is that ENERGY STAR and Ultra-high Efficiency Heat Pump decrease annual
electricity consumption by 6% and 20%, respectively. Based on FPL electricity rates, this equates
to a modeled decrease of annual utility bills by ~$150 and nearly $500, respectively. Second, the
full electrification with high-efficiency appliances decreases modeled annual electricity
consumption by an additional 9% or approximately $200 in annual utility bill savings. Third, older

households, who have highest probability of older HVAC equipment and appliances, see 5–10%
more electricity savings as compared to newer households with any given upgrade.
