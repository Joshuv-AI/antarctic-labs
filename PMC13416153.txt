Alu-mediated SPAST deletion impairs golgi zinc transport and reveals a druggable vulnerability
Authors: Youngsun Lee, Onju Ham, Hana Lee, Dae-Soo Kim, Jung Hwa Lim, Cho-Rok Jung, Jae-Won Huh, Mi-Young Son, Mi-Ok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alu elements are primate-specific retrotransposons known to mediate structural genomic alterations associated with neurological disorders, yet their underlying disease mechanism remains incompletely understood. Here, we leveraged human pluripotent stem cell-derived cortical organoids and xenografts to model an Alu-mediated exon 17 deletion in the SPAST gene, a frequent cause of hereditary spastic paraplegia (SPG4) with dementia. This deletion generates fusion transcripts between SPAST and the downstream gene SLC30A6, resulting in reduced expression of ZnT6, the Golgi-resident zinc transporter encoded by SLC30A6. In SPASTΔe17 organoids, we detected pronounced cytosolic zinc accumulation, Golgi fragmentation, lipid dysregulation, and increased aggregation of Aβ and ubiquitin. These phenotypes were further exacerbated in xenograft models, concomitant with ubiquitin-positive inclusions resembling those found in patients’ brains. Knockdown of SLC30A6 in control organoids recapitulated the neurodegenerative features, supporting a critical role of ZnT6 in maintaining neuronal homeostasis. Intervention with TPEN, a zinc-specific chelator, or a GRASP55-blocking antibody to prevent Golgi fragmentation significantly restored Golgi integrity and reduced Aβ levels. Notably, immunohistochemistry revealed heterogeneous ZnT6 expression in AD brains; however, both ZnT6-low and ZnT6-high cases consistently exhibited elevated p-GM130, a marker of Golgi fragmentation. Finally, we detected a SPAST–SLC30A6 chimeric transcript in brain tissue from a sporadic AD patient. Together, our findings identify a primate-specific cascade linking Alu-mediated genomic rearrangements to zinc dyshomeostasis and Golgi pathology and suggest that dysregulation of the ZnT6–Golgi axis may represent a vulnerability and therapeutic target in structural variant–driven neurodegeneration and possibly in a subset of sporadic cases.

Introduction

Alu elements, the most abundant class of short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), are primate-specific retrotransposons that comprise approximately 10–11% of the human genome.1 While their mobility has shaped genomic diversity and regulatory evolution through retroposition and recombination events, it also predisposes the genome to instability. As a consequence, Alu–Alu recombination can introduce pathogenic structural variants into the genome (such as deletions, duplications, and gene fusions) that are implicated in a range of human diseases, including cancer, genetic syndromes, and neurodegenerative disorders.2–5 Despite growing interest in Alu-driven genome instability, its contribution to neurological disease pathogenesis remains particularly underexplored. A major obstacle is the lack of appropriate models. Rodent genomes lack Alu elements, making them unsuitable for studying primate-specific structural variants (SVs) and their consequences. Thus, whether and how many Alu-generated SVs contribute to disease pathology—particularly in late-onset neurodegenerative diseases—remains poorly defined.

A notable case is SPAST, the most frequently mutated gene in hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) type 4 (SPG4).6 A rare disease, the onset of SPG4, ranges from infancy to late adulthood. While life expectancy is normal, symptoms may worsen with age, and there are no treatment options. The SPAST gene encodes spastin, a microtubule-severing AAA ATPase essential for axonal maintenance and intracellular trafficking.3 While most individuals diagnosed with SPG4 present with lower limb spasticity, a subset of patients with large deletions exhibits cognitive decline and dementia.7 The SPAST locus is highly enriched in Alu elements (~36%), particularly around intron 16 and exon 17, a configuration that renders the locus susceptible to recombination mediated by Alu element microhomology. Nearly half of the copy number variants affecting exon 17 arise through such Alu-mediated rearrangements.8 Intriguingly, a postmortem analysis of an SPG4-HSP patient harboring a large genomic deletion encompassing exon 17 of the SPAST gene identified Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-like neuropathological features, including ubiquitin-positive grains and tau-positive neurofibrillary tangles in the neocortex.7

Although the mechanistic link between exon 17 deletion and cognitive decline remains unknown, aberrant splicing or transcriptional dysregulation was hypothesized to lead to the formation of a fusion transcript between SPAST and the downstream gene SLC30A6, which encodes zinc transporter 6 (ZnT6).8 Because ZnT6 regulates zinc import into the Golgi apparatus and zinc imbalance is known to contribute to AD pathogenesis, this mechanism could promote amyloid-beta aggregation and oxidative stress.8

Recent advances in human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived brain organoids offer a unique platform to model primate-specific genomic structures and SVs in a physiologically relevant context. These three-dimensional cultures retain human-specific repetitive elements and epigenetic features, enabling mechanistic dissection of Alu-driven genomic instability and its effects on neural cells. Here, we established a human-specific neurodegeneration model by introducing a patient-relevant Alu-mediated SPAST exon 17 deletion into hPSC-derived cortical organoids (COs). This design enables us to interrogate the shared downstream consequences of SPAST exon 17 loss, independent of breakpoint-specific variability across patients. By integrating analysis of organoids, xenografts, and postmortem AD brain tissue, we identified that disruption of the zinc–Golgi axis is a conserved driver of neurodegeneration. Our findings link primate-specific genomic instability to neurodegenerative pathology, including AD, and highlight the ZnT6–Golgi pathway as a tractable therapeutic target in dementia caused by structural variants and sporadic AD.

SPASTΔe17-hPSC clones were validated for pluripotency using alkaline phosphatase staining, immunocytochemistry, and real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 2a–c) and for their ability to differentiate into all three germ layers through embryoid body formation and teratoma assays (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), indicating intact pluripotency of SPASTΔe17-hPSCs. G-banding analysis confirmed a normal karyotype, ensuring genomic stability in the edited lines (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Using next-generation sequencing (NGS) and reverse transcription PCR (RT‒PCR), we confirmed the absence of exon 17–containing transcripts in SPASTΔe17-hPSCs (Fig. 1d, e). To assess the impact of exon 17 deletion on SPAST protein expression, we performed immunoblot analysis using antibodies recognizing distinct regions of spastin. While full-length SPAST protein was not detected in SPASTΔe17 hPSCs using a C-terminal–specific antibody, a truncated spastin protein was readily detected with an N-terminal–reactive antibody (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 1g), indicating selective loss of the C-terminal domain without complete destabilization of the truncated protein.

Next, we differentiated WT or SPASTΔe17-hPSCs into COs. We found that both wild-type (WT) and SPASTΔe17-hPSCs differentiated successfully into COs, with early-stage development appearing comparable between groups (Fig. 1g). By day 100, SPASTΔe17-COs exhibited a significant reduction in organoid size compared to WT controls, indicating the emergence of distinct late-stage phenotypic differences (Fig. 1h). Immunostaining on day 62 confirmed proper spatial organization of neuroepithelial markers (SOX2 and Tuj1), and by day 100, the presence of mature neurons (MAP2-positive) and astrocytes (GFAP-positive) in both groups (Fig. 1i). Transcriptomic analysis revealed increased expression of neuronal genes and decreased expression of pluripotency-associated genes in both organoid types, confirming successful neural differentiation without gross defects in lineage specification (supplementary Fig 4a). Consistent with the observations in hPSCs, deletion of SPAST exon 17 was similarly confirmed in SPASTΔe17-COs at both the transcript and protein levels (Fig. 1j, k).

To assess whether SPASTΔe17-COs recapitulate neuropathological hallmarks observed in patients with SPAST exon 17 deletion, we examined ubiquitin accumulation. Immunostaining revealed that the ubiquitin-positive area in SPASTΔe17 COs was approximately twofold greater than that in WT controls (Fig. 1l, m). To complement the immunostaining data, we performed Western blot analysis for ubiquitin in day‑100 COs. SPASTΔe17-COs showed an overall increase in ubiquitin signal compared with WT, with a particularly prominent enhancement of a low–molecular‑weight ubiquitin‑reactive band at approximately 10 kDa, rather than a broad increase in high–molecular‑weight poly-ubiquitinated smears (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). This pattern indicates an enrichment of specific low–molecular‑weight ubiquitin species in SPASTΔe17-COs and, together with the increased ubiquitin‑positive puncta and aggregated protein content, is consistent with emerging proteostasis stress at late stages of organoid maturation. Additionally, quantification of aggregated proteins showed an approximately 2.5-fold increase in SPASTΔe17-COs compared to WT controls (Fig. 1n, o), indicating enhanced proteostasis stress and proteinopathy, key features of neurodegenerative pathology.10 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SPASTΔe17-COs robustly mimic the clinical and molecular phenotypes observed in SPAST exon 17-deleted HSP patients with dementia, supporting the validity of this organoid model for studying disease mechanisms and therapeutic interventions.

In further analysis, we noted a significant increase in the expression of apolipoprotein E (APOE), a key regulator of lipid metabolism and a major genetic risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases, in SPASTΔe17-COs (Fig. 2b, c). In functional assays using C11-BODIPY581/591 and BODIPY493/503, we revealed that both lipid peroxidation and the size of lipid droplets were significantly elevated in SPASTΔe17-COs compared to WT controls (Fig. 2d, e), supporting a link between SPASTΔe17 and lipid-mediated neuronal injury, a common feature of neurodegenerative pathophysiology.11,12

Aberrant lipid metabolism is also implicated in amyloid-beta (Aβ) aggregation.13 In SPASTΔe17-COs, the levels of secreted Aβ1-42 and intracellular triglycerides were both markedly increased, as measured by ELISA and biochemical assays (Fig. 2f, g). Correspondingly, histological staining with thioflavin-S, AmyloGlo, and Aβ1-42 immunolabeling demonstrated an ~10-fold increase in Aβ aggregation relative to WT-COs (Fig. 2h, i), consistent with altered proteostatic and lipid metabolic states in SPASTΔe17-COs. To further validate Aβ accumulation at the biochemical level, we performed western blot analysis for Aβ1-42. SPASTΔe17-COs exhibited a pronounced increase in Aβ1-42–reactive bands migrating at ~10 kDa, consistent with oligomeric Aβ species, compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig 3e, f). Finally, immunostaining for cleaved caspase-3 (cCas3) revealed a 4-fold increase in apoptotic neurons in SPASTΔe17-COs compared to WT (Fig. 2j). Collectively, these data support that SPASTΔe17 is associated with broad transcriptomic and metabolic disturbances in COs, accompanied by heightened lipid peroxidation, abnormal protein aggregation, and increased apoptotic signaling.

UMAP-based clustering identified major neural cell populations in both WT and SPASTΔe17-COs. Although overall lineage identities were maintained, SPASTΔe17-COs showed a relative enrichment of excitatory neurons and reduced expression of the progenitor marker NES compared to WT controls (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 5c).

Differential gene expression analysis revealed a significant upregulation of genes associated with protein aggregation and proteostasis stress in SPASTΔe17-COs. Notably, several AD-related genes, including APP, MAPT, UBB, and UBC, were increased, consistent with the induction of neurodegeneration-associated transcriptional signatures (Fig. 3c). In contrast, genes involved in synapse organization, such as DGKB, IL1RAPL1, ASAP1, and KIRREL3, were downregulated, suggesting alterations in synaptic architecture (Fig. 3c).

Cell type-specific pathway enrichment analysis further identified distinct transcriptional changes within inhibitory and excitatory neuronal populations. In inhibitory neurons, genes upregulated in SPASTΔe17-COs were enriched for pathways related to protein refolding, integrated stress response signaling, unfolded protein response, reactive oxygen species metabolism, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. Conversely, pathways associated with GABAergic interneuron fate commitment, postsynaptic membrane assembly, axon guidance, and brain development were relatively downregulated, indicating altered inhibitory neuronal maturation, connectivity, and stress response (Fig. 3d).

In excitatory neurons, upregulated genes were enriched for pathways related to chemical synaptic transmission, glutamate receptor signaling, and synapse maintenance, potentially reflecting compensatory transcriptional responses. In contrast, downregulated pathways included ERBB4 signaling, synapse assembly, brain development, cell population proliferation, and calcium ion transmembrane transport, suggesting functional alterations in neuronal connectivity and calcium homeostasis (Fig. 3e).

To validate these transcriptomic changes at the protein and functional levels, we examined synaptic markers and calcium dynamics. Immunostaining revealed a significant reduction in the postsynaptic marker PSD95 in SPASTΔe17-COs, whereas the presynaptic marker synaptophysin showed no significant change, indicating preferential postsynaptic alterations rather than global synapse loss (Fig. 3f, g).

Consistently, live-cell calcium imaging using Fluo-4 AM demonstrated attenuated intracellular calcium dynamics in SPASTΔe17-COs, with reduced calcium fluctuations and significantly decreased ΔF/F₀ values compared to WT controls (Fig. 3h–j). Together, these findings indicate that deletion of SPAST exon 17 is associated with functional decline in neurons.

Xenotransplantation of COs in the mouse brain accelerates the accumulation of abnormal SPASTΔe17-CO proteins

Despite the strengths of organoid systems, in vitro models typically lack vasculature and immune system components, limiting their ability to fully recapitulate more complex neuropathological processes.14,15 For example, while we detected increased accumulation of ubiquitin and Aβ in SPASTΔe17-COs, these aggregates displayed a diffuse distribution and did not fully resemble the distinct dot- or grain-like inclusions typically observed in patient brain tissue.7 To overcome these limitations, we transplanted both WT and SPASTΔe17-COs into the mouse brain (Fig. 4a) and confirmed successful engraftment by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunostaining for human-specific nuclear antigen (hNUMA), neuronal (MAP2) and progenitor (SOX2) markers, indicating the integration of human cells into the host brain (Fig. 4b, c; Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). We noted that SPASTΔe17 xenografts developed discrete dot- and grain-like ubiquitin inclusions, closely resembling those seen in postmortem brain tissue from SPAST exon 17 deletion patients with dementia (Fig. 4d). In the analysis of postmortem human cortical brain sections from clinically and neuropathologically diagnosed AD patients, we observed an increase in ubiquitin-positive granules compared to neurologically normal control brains, although the extent was less pronounced than in SPASTΔe17 xenografts. Similarly, the levels of Aβ plaque-like aggregates were significantly elevated in SPASTΔe17 xenografts and AD patient brains relative to controls (Fig. 4e). Consistent with these proteinopathy features, immunostaining for cleaved caspase‑3 revealed a marked increase in apoptotic cells within SPASTΔe17 xenografts compared to WT xenografts (Fig. 4f).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SPASTΔe17-CO pathology is enhanced in xenografts, leading to the formation of human-like protein aggregates not observed in vitro. Comparative analysis revealed that while both AD and SPASTΔe17 xenografts exhibit increased Aβ and ubiquitin inclusions, AD pathology is characterized by more prominent Aβ aggregation, whereas SPASTΔe17 pathology is more strongly associated with ubiquitin accumulation. These data suggest that large SPAST deletions may drive a distinct neurodegenerative profile with partial overlap with AD.

SPASTΔe17 and Slc30A6 silencing induce abnormal regulation of zinc ion levels in COs, resulting in Golgi fragmentation

Unlike pure HSP, the mechanism by which large deletions of SPAST (particularly those involving exon 17) lead to dementia remains unclear. Since exon 17 is the terminal exon of SPAST, we hypothesized that Alu-mediated large deletions induce splicing abnormalities, generating fusion transcripts with the downstream gene SLC30A6 and thereby contributing to the unique neuropathology observed in these cases. To test this hypothesis, we performed PCR using primer pairs spanning SPAST exon 12 to SLC30A6 exon 7 and SPAST exon 16 to SLC30A6 exon 2 (Fig. 5a). These analyses revealed the presence of aberrant fusion transcripts specifically in SPASTΔe17-COs (Fig. 5b and supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Context-dependent regulation of Golgi integrity by SPAST and ZnT6

To determine whether the Golgi fragmentation observed in SPASTΔe17-COs was attributable to loss of SPAST itself, we first examined the effects of SPAST knockdown in WT-COs (supplementary Fig 8a). siRNA-mediated suppression of SPAST efficiently reduced spastin protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c) without affecting SLC30A6 expression or neuronal markers (MAP2, TUBB3) (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Importantly, SPAST knockdown alone did not recapitulate the Golgi fragmentation phenotype observed in SPASTΔe17-COs. Cytosolic Zn²⁺ levels, ZnT6 expression, and immunoreactivity of the Golgi fragmentation marker phosphorylated GM130 (p-GM130) remained unchanged following SPAST knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e, g), indicating preserved Golgi integrity. Notably, despite the absence of overt Golgi disruption, SPAST knockdown led to a modest but reproducible increase in lipid droplet size (Supplementary Fig. 8g, i), suggesting that SPAST can influence lipid metabolism through mechanisms that are at least partially independent of the ZnT6–Golgi axis.

We next investigated whether modulation of ZnT6 levels could account for the Golgi and lipid phenotypes observed in SPASTΔe17-COs. Overexpression of SLC30A6 in WT-COs resulted in an increase in p-GM130 immunoreactivity and lipid droplet accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 9c–e), phenocopying key aspects of the ZnT6-deficient state. These findings indicate that excessive ZnT6 activity is sufficient to perturb Golgi integrity and lipid homeostasis in an otherwise normal background.

In contrast, restoration of SLC30A6 expression in SPASTΔe17-COs significantly attenuated Golgi fragmentation and lipid accumulation, as evidenced by reduced p-GM130 signaling and decreased lipid droplet size (Supplementary Fig. 9g–i). This phenotypic rescue supports the conclusion that ZnT6 downregulation is a primary driver of Golgi lipid dysregulation in the context of SPAST exon 17 deletion.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Golgi fragmentation phenotype observed in SPASTΔe17-COs cannot be explained by loss of SPAST alone but instead arises from dysregulation of the ZnT6–Golgi axis. Moreover, our data indicate that precise tuning of ZnT6 levels is critical for maintaining Golgi integrity and lipid homeostasis, as both reduction and excessive expression of ZnT6 disrupt these processes in a context-dependent manner.

Chelation of zinc ions and inhibition of Golgi fragmentation ameliorate SPASTΔe17 in COs

To determine whether correcting zinc imbalance or preventing Golgi fragmentation could ameliorate the neurodegenerative phenotypes induced by SPASTΔe17, we treated SPASTΔe17-COs with TPEN, a zinc-specific chelator, or GRASP55-blocking antibody, which inhibits Golgi fragmentation.

TPEN treatment significantly reduced cytosolic Zn²⁺ levels in SPASTΔe17-COs (Fig. 6i) and restored Golgi morphology, as indicated by increased cisternal length and reduced p-GM130 levels (Fig. 6e, f, h). Similarly, GRASP55 antibody treatment also decreased p-GM130 expression and improved Golgi structure (Fig. 6e, f, h), supporting a mechanistic link between Zn²⁺ dysregulation and Golgi fragmentation.

Both interventions alleviated downstream pathological features, including lipid droplet enlargement, triglyceride accumulation, and elevated secreted Aβ1–42 levels. TPEN and GRASP55 treatment each significantly reduced LD size and intracellular triglyceride levels (Fig. 6g, h, j). Moreover, the secreted Aβ1–42 levels decreased from ~200 pg/mL in untreated SPASTΔe17-COs to ~135 pg/mL and ~140 pg/mL in TPEN- and GRASP55-treated COs, respectively (Fig. 6k).

Together, these findings demonstrate that both zinc chelation and Golgi stabilization can attenuate key pathological features of SPASTΔe17-induced neurodegeneration, including abnormal zinc accumulation, Golgi fragmentation, lipid dysregulation, and protein aggregation. These functional changes indicate the therapeutic potential of targeting the ZnT6–Golgi axis to restore subcellular zinc balance and preserve organelle integrity in SPASTΔe17-related neurodegeneration.

To examine whether a similar ZnT6–Golgi axis is relevant to sporadic neurodegeneration, we next analyzed postmortem cortical brain sections from AD patients (n = 13) and neurologically normal controls (n = 10) (Supplementary Table 3). Immunofluorescence costaining confirmed the Golgi localization of ZnT6 through strong colocalization with the Golgi marker GRASP65 in human cortical neurons (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed heterogeneous ZnT6 expression across AD brains, with individual cases exhibiting either reduced or increased ZnT6 signal relative to the control range, consistent with substantial interindividual variability. Importantly, despite this bidirectional ZnT6 dysregulation, both ZnT6‑low and ZnT6‑high AD samples displayed elevated p‑GM130 immunoreactivity and disrupted Golgi morphology compared to controls (Fig. 7b–d; Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating that deviations from ZnT6 homeostasis—rather than a uniform decrease—are associated with Golgi fragmentation in the AD cortex.

Finally, we investigated whether aberrant SPAST–SLC30A6 fusion transcripts, originally identified in SPASTΔe17-COs, could also arise in sporadic AD brains. Due to the limited availability of high-quality brain RNA, mRNA samples from two AD patients were analyzed. PCR using the SPAST exon 12–SLC30A6 exon 7 primer set detected two PCR products in one AD #2 sample, with sizes comparable to those observed in SPASTΔe17-COs (Fig. 7e). Subsequent Sanger sequencing revealed that the #6 PCR band represented a nonspecific amplicon, whereas the #5 PCR band corresponded to a bona fide SPAST–SLC30A6 chimeric transcript (Fig. 7f).

Together, these findings indicate that disruption of the ZnT6–Golgi axis observed in SPAST exon 17 deletion can be recapitulated in vivo. The detection of a SPAST–SLC30A6 chimeric transcript in one sporadic AD sample further raises the possibility that related transcriptional or genomic events may occur outside the context of familial HSP, although their prevalence and pathological significance remain to be determined.

Discussion

Alu elements are primate-specific repetitive sequences that represent a prominent source of genomic instability in the human genome, yet their pathological consequences in the brain have remained largely unexplored.18,19 In particular, although Alu-mediated structural variants at the SPAST locus have been linked to cognitive decline in SPG4 patients, the molecular mechanisms connecting these genomic rearrangements to neurodegeneration have remained unclear. In this study, we established a primate-specific model of neurodegeneration by recapitulating an Alu-mediated deletion of SPAST exon 17, frequently observed in patients with SPG4 exhibiting cognitive decline.8 Using human COs and xenografts, we demonstrate that this structural variant gives rise to SPAST–SLC30A6 fusion transcripts and perturbs zinc homeostasis through downregulation of ZnT6, ultimately compromising Golgi integrity and neuronal viability.

Our work provides mechanistic insight into how large SPAST deletions contribute to dementia, distinguishing them from point mutations that cause pure motor phenotypes.7 Exon 17 lies within an Alu-rich region prone to recombination, and its deletion enables aberrant fusion with the downstream SLC30A6 gene.9 While previous studies have implicated zinc dyshomeostasis20,21 and Golgi dysfunction22,23 as downstream features of neurodegeneration, these processes have largely been described independently, with limited understanding of a shared upstream genomic mechanism. In this context, our findings link a specific structural variant to coordinated disruption of ZnT6-mediated zinc transport and Golgi integrity. We demonstrate that this genomic rearrangement leads to reduced ZnT6 expression, which in turn alters intracellular zinc distribution and disrupts Golgi morphology. Importantly, these events converge on key neurodegenerative features, including GM130 hyperphosphorylation, Aβ aggregation, lipid dysregulation, and neuronal loss.24,25 Consistent with this view, single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed reduced progenitor fractions, altered neuronal composition, and decreased expression of synaptic maturation genes in SPASTΔe17 COs, indicating progressive disruption of neuronal network maturation.

Our model system capturing these pathological events has distinct advantages. Organoids enable detailed molecular dissection of early disease mechanisms, while xenografting facilitates the development of more mature neuro-pathological phenotypes, including the appearance of ubiquitin-positive dot/grain aggregates that closely resemble those observed in human brain tissues.7 The combined approach highlights the added value of incorporating in vivo environmental cues to fully manifest late-stage disease features. At the mechanistic level, our data support a role for ZnT6 deficiency in driving key aspects of the observed pathology. Knockdown of SLC30A6 in WT-COs recapitulated Golgi fragmentation, Aβ accumulation, and lipid droplet enlargement, indicating that disruption of the ZnT6–Golgi axis is sufficient to induce these phenotypes under experimental conditions. Conversely, restoration of zinc balance using TPEN or inhibition of Golgi fragmentation with GRASP55-blocking antibodies attenuated these changes, further supporting a functional contribution of this pathway. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although SLC30A6 manipulation phenocopies key aspects of the SPASTΔe17 condition, it may not fully account for all downstream effects of the structural variant, and additional pathways could contribute to the observed neurodegenerative features. In addition, it remains possible that ZnT6 dysregulation represents a downstream consequence rather than the sole initiating driver of pathology in this context. Second, the therapeutic interventions were evaluated only in COs and not in xenograft or systemic in vivo models, limiting conclusions regarding their translational potential. Finally, while the observed rescue effects support the therapeutic tractability of the ZnT6–Golgi axis, these results should be interpreted as mechanistic proof-of-concept rather than definitive evidence of clinical applicability.

Our findings provide a framework for considering how structural variation–driven pathways may inform therapeutic strategies and disease stratification. While we detected a SPAST–SLC30A6 chimeric transcript in a sporadic AD brain, the limited number of samples analyzed precludes any general conclusion regarding its prevalence or role in AD. Nevertheless, this observation raises the possibility that similar transcriptional or genomic events may contribute to neurodegeneration in a subset of cases, suggesting a potential role for genomic screening in identifying patient subgroups. Consistent with this notion, analysis of AD patient brain tissue revealed heterogeneous ZnT6 expression, with subsets of cases showing either reduced or increased ZnT6 levels relative to controls, yet a convergent increase in Golgi fragmentation. These observations indicate that deviations from ZnT6 homeostasis, rather than a uniform directional change, are associated with destabilized Golgi integrity, highlighting the importance of tightly regulated ZnT6 activity for neuronal homeostasis. Supporting this interpretation, previous studies have reported neuroprotective effects of zinc chelation in AD-related models,26,27 suggesting that modulation of zinc homeostasis may have broader therapeutic relevance. Future studies will be required to assess the frequency of such events and to validate ZnT6–Golgi axis-targeting strategies in diverse in vivo models.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which Alu-driven structural variation may represent an underappreciated source of molecular heterogeneity in a subset of neurodegenerative conditions. Beyond classical amyloid-centric and tau-centric mechanisms, stochastic genomic rearrangements leading to dysregulation of the ZnT6–Golgi axis may contribute to Golgi fragmentation, zinc dyshomeostasis, and increased neurodegenerative vulnerability. Importantly, this establishes the ZnT6–Golgi pathway as a mechanistically defined and potentially targetable axis in structural variant–driven neurodegeneration, with possible relevance to a subset of sporadic cases. Nevertheless, whether ZnT6 dysregulation constitutes a universal driver or a context-dependent contributor across heterogeneous neurodegenerative conditions remains an open question, warranting further investigation.

Cell culture

hPSCs, specifically the H9 cell line, were obtained from the WiCell Research Institute (Madison, WI, USA) and maintained under feeder-free conditions in mTeSR™ medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Undifferentiated hPSC colonies were enzymatically passaged once per week. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Generation of SPASTΔe17-hPSCs

To model the large SPAST deletions observed in patients, two guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the Alu-transposon-rich regions flanking exon 17 were designed by ToolGene (Seoul, Republic of Korea) and cloned and inserted into the pRGEN-sgRNA-U6 vector. Plasmid DNA prep was performed using a NucleoBond Xtra Midi kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For genome editing, two SPAST sgRNA-GFP plasmids and one EF1α-Cas9-RFP plasmid (2.5 μg each) were coelectroporated into 1 × 10⁶ hPSCs using the Neon Electroporation System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) under the following conditions: 1350 V, 30 width, 1 pulse. Transfected cells were plated onto Matrigel-coated dishes in mTeSR™ medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 (R&D Systems). The following day, GFP and RFP double-positive cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), seeded at low density onto mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) feeder layers for clonal selection, and maintained under standard culture conditions.

To identify hPSC clones harboring deletions of SPAST exon 17, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from isolated clones and subjected to PCR amplification. Each 25 μL PCR contained 75 ng of gDNA, 5× PCR buffer, 10 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM dNTPs, 5% DMSO, GoTaq® Hot Start Polymerase (Promega), and SPAST-specific primers for genotyping. The primer sequences were as follows:

F- TCTTTTTGGCTAAAATTTTTCAGATT, R1-TTCACAGAGTCCAGGCAATAGA, R2- TGACCCATTTTATCTTCTCTAATGG.

PCR was performed under the following conditions: 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 5 min for extension. The PCR products were electrophoresed in 2% agarose gels. To further validate the genetic integrity of edited clones, PCR products spanning the SPAST exon 17 locus were excised from agarose gels and subjected to Sanger sequencing. This analysis was performed for all WT and SPASTΔe17 hPSC clones used in downstream experiments to confirm deletion boundaries and exclude unintended mutations. To assess potential plasmid integration, genomic DNA from each clone was analyzed by PCR using primers specific for Cas9 and plasmid backbone sequences. No plasmid-derived sequences were detected in any of the clones examined.

Generation of embryonic bodies (EBs) and differentiation into COs

To generate EB, hPSCs were initially dissociated by Accutase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 1 × 104 cells per well were plated in ultralow attachment (ULA) 96 wells (Corning Incorporated, Kennebunk, ME, USA) containing low-bFGF hPSC culture media supplied with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO), 4 ng ml-1bFGF2 and 50 μM Y27632 (ROCK inhibitor, R&D system, Minneapolis, MN). The day after plating the cells, low-bFGF hPSC culture medium with 50 μM Y27632 was added to the plates every other day for 4 days of EB induction. EBs were further cultured in low-bFGF hPSCs culture media from day 4 to 6. The EBs were attached to 4-well or 60-mm dishes and further cultured for 2 weeks and harvested for experiments.

COs were differentiated as previously described.28 Briefly, hPSCs were dissociated by Accuate, and 1 × 104 cells per well were plated in ULA 96 wells with 50 μM Y27632 (R&D system) for 24 h. Then, dual SMAD inhibition was performed from day 1 to 6 under E5/E6 mTeSR (STEMCELL) containing 2.5 μM dorsomorphine (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) and 10 μM SB431542 (Sigma‒Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The media was changed every day for day 6. Next, neural induction was performed. The organoids were transferred to ULA 24-well plates containing neural induction media containing Neurobasal-A (GIBCO) 2% B27 (without vitamin A, GIBCO), 20 ng/ml EGF (R&D Systems) and 20 ng/ml bFGF (R&D Systems) from day 6 to 25. The neural induction media were changed every day for 10 days and then every other day for 9 days. The COs were then matured for 18 days (from day 26 to 43) with neural maturation media: Neurobasal-A containing 2% B27 (without vitamin A), 20 ng/ml BDNF (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ) and 20 ng/ml NT3 (PeproTech). The neural maturation media were changed every other day. For longer cultures of the COs, Neurobasal-A medium supplemented with 2% B27 (without vitamin A) was supplied every 4 days until harvested for experiments. For inhibitor experiments, 1 μM TPEN (Sigma‒Aldrich) or 1 μg.ml−1 GRASP55 protein (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was added to SPASTΔe17 COs on day 100 for 50 days, and the cells were harvested for experiments.

Alkaline phosphate (AP) staining

To verify the pluripotency of hPSCs, AP staining was performed using an ALP detection kit (Cat. No. 86R-1kt, Sigma‒Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, hPSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at RT and then stained with AP staining mixture for 30 min at RT in the dark. After discarding the mixture, the cells were washed with PBS. The stained cells were observed by BX53 microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Karyotype analysis

For karyotype analysis, hPSCs were dissociated into single cells by Accutase and seeded in a Matrigel-coated T25 flask. hPSCs were maintained until 70–80% confluence in a 37 °C incubator. The G-band karyotype is shown in the Gendix. Ind (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

H&E staining

H&E staining was carried out by the following process. Briefly, tissue slides were rehydrated in graded ethanol (70% and 50%) for 2 min and washed with PBS. Tissue slides were stained using the H&E staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Slides were incubated in hematoxylin for 5 min, washed with running tap water for 10 min and stained with eosin for 5 min. After dehydration in ethanol (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%) and xylene, slides were mounted in Cytoseal 60 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tissue morphology was examined using a BX53 microscope (Olympus).

Immunoblot analysis

Immunoblotting was performed as previously described.29 A total of 10–20 μg of lysates was separated on a 4–20% SDS‒PAGE gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for half an hour and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody. The membrane was washed with 0.05% tween 20 containing TBS, and then, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000) was incubated at RT for an hour. The immunoblots were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, AmershamTM, Amersham, UK) reagents by RAS Imager A600 (AmershamTM). The intensity of each protein band was quantified using ImageJ software and analyzed statistically. Uncropped Western blot images are provided in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13.

RNA isolation, detection of fusion transcripts, and PCR

Samples were harvested and lysed by EasyBlue (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Republic of Korea). All RNAs were isolated manually. Briefly, chloroform (Sigma‒Aldrich) was added, and the aqua phase was collected, washed with isopropanol and then centrifuged. Then, the RNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried and diluted with 0.1% DEPC-containing nuclease-free water. For analyses involving human AD brain samples, total RNA isolated from brain tissues was obtained from BioChain Institute, Inc. (Newark, CA, USA). The following commercial RNA samples were used: R1236035Alz-50 (designated AD #1) and R1236045Alz-10 (designated AD #2). Cyclic DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1500 ng of RNA by Superscript IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For detection of fusion transcripts, 2000 ng of RNA was used to synthesize cDNA, and then PCR was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 60 °C, 30 s at 72 °C, and 5 min extension at 72 °C. The PCR products were detected by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) and a 7500 Fast Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Real-time PCR was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 57 °C or 60 °C, 30 s at 72 °C, and 5 min extension at 72 °C. GAPDH was used as the internal control, and the CT value for each target gene was calculated using the software provided by the manufacturer. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Purification of PCR bands and TA cloning for Sanger sequencing

For structural characterization of PCR products, individual DNA bands were excised from agarose gels under UV illumination. Gel-purified DNA fragments were extracted using the Gel & PCR Purification System (SolGent, Daejeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, gel slices containing the target PCR products were solubilized in binding buffer, followed by column-based purification and elution in nuclease-free water. Purified PCR products were subsequently cloned using the T-Blunt™ PCR Cloning Kit (SolGent, Daejeon, Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The ligation reaction was performed using the T-Blunt vector and 6× T-Blunt buffer at room temperature, and the ligation mixture was transformed into DH5α competent cells supplied with the kit. Transformed cells were recovered in S.O.C. medium and plated on selective agar plates containing appropriate antibiotics. Individual colonies were screened by colony PCR to confirm the presence of inserts. Plasmid DNA from positive clones was isolated and submitted for Sanger sequencing (Bionics Co., Seoul, Korea) using vector-specific primers (M13 forward and reverse). Sequencing results were analyzed to determine the precise junctions and structures of the PCR-derived inserts.

Immunofluorescence staining, LD staining, and oxidative lipid staining

The slides were washed with PBS three times for 5 min to remove OCT compound. The slides were then permeabilized and blocked with 4% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton-X100 (Sigma‒Aldrich) containing 1x TBS for an hour at RT. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Information on the antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Alexa 488-anti-mouse donkey IgG antibody (1:200), Alexa 488-anti-goat-donkey IgG antibody (1:200), Cy3-anti-rabbit donkey IgG antibody (1:200), Alexa 647-anti-chicken donkey IgG antibody (1:200) and Hoechst 33342 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were incubated for an hour at RT. Secondary antibodies were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA). Then, the sections were washed three times for 5 min with 0.05% Tween 20 containing 1x TBS and mounted mounting solution (DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark).

For LD staining, we followed the process to incubate primary antibody as described above. The tissue section was added to 0.5 μg/ml BODIPY 493/503 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with or without secondary antibodies for an hour at RT and washed with 0.5% Tween 20 containing 1x TBS three times for 5 min and mounted with mounting solution (DAKO). Images were visualized by a ZEISS LSM800 (Carl Zeiss, Stuttgart, Germany), and the data were transferred. Images were analyzed by ZIESS blue software or ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

For oxidative lipid staining, we attached each CO in 4-well cell culture plates for one week. After checking that neurites were fully stretched under microscopy, 1 μM C11-BODIPY 581/591 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was incubated for 30 min, and the COs were washed with warm PBS and filled with culture media. Images were visualized with a Zeiss LSM800 (Carl Zeiss) and transferred. Images were analyzed by ZIESS blue software or ImageJ software (NIH).

Thioflavin S staining

Thioflavin S staining was carried out by the following process. Briefly, slides were rehydrated in graded ethanol (70%, 50%, and 30%) for 2 min and washed with PBS. Tissue slides were incubated with a 0.4% solution of thioflavin S (Sigma‒Aldrich) for 5 min. Slides were washed with distilled water and dehydrated in ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%) for 5 min and then cleared with xylene. The slides were covered with mounting solutions. Images were visualized by ZEISS LSM800 and transferred. Images were analyzed by ZIESS blue software or ImageJ software (NIH). At least 6 organoids were quantified in each experiment, and the thioflavin S staining results represent three independent experiments.

Amylo-Glo staining

We followed a previously described process.30 In brief, the slides were washed with PBS to remove OCT compound three times for 5 min. The slides were then incubated for 10 min in Amylo-Glo staining solution (Biosencsis, Thebarton, Australia. cat.no.TR-300). The slides were then rinsed in saline for 5 min. The slides were permeabilized and blocked with 4% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton-X 100 containing PBS for 1 h at RT. The sections were then incubated with Aβ1-42 antibody (Abcam) overnight in a humidity chamber at 4 °C. The sections were then washed with 0.5% Triton-X containing PBS three times for 5 min. The slides were then incubated with Alexa488-anti-rabbit donkey IgG antibody (1:200) as a secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and Hoechst 33342 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. The slides were washed three times for 5 min with 0.5% Triton-X containing PBS. The slides were covered with mounting solutions. Images were visualized by ZEISS LSM800 and transferred. Images were analyzed by ZIESS blue software or ImageJ software (NIH). At least 6 organoids were quantified in each experiment, and the Amylo-Glo staining results represent three independent experiments.

Aggregate protein staining

We followed the manufacturers’ protocol (Enzo, Farmingdale, NY, cat.no. ENZ-51035). Briefly, the slides were washed with PBS three times for 5 min. The slides were permeabilized and blocked with 4% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton-X 100 containing PBS for 1 h at RT. The sections were then incubated with PROTEOSTAT Aggresome detection reagent and Hoechst 33342 (1:500) for 30 min. The slides were washed three times for 5 min with 0.5% Triton-X containing PBS. The slides were covered with mounting solutions. Images were visualized by ZEISS LSM800 and transferred. Images were analyzed by ZIESS blue software or ImageJ software (NIH). At least 6 organoids were quantified in each experiment, and the aggregated protein staining results represent three independent experiments.

DAB staining

The paraffin slides were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated with ethanol (100%, 95% 70% 50%) and washed with D.W. The cryosection slides were washed with PBS three times for 5 min. The slides were treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min and washed with 1x PBS 3 times. The slides were then permeabilized and blocked with 4% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton-X100 containing TBS for an hour at RT. The sections were incubated with an anti-Aβ1-42 antibody and a ubiquitin antibody overnight at 4 °C. Information on the antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2. The slides were incubated with HRP-anti-rabbit donkey IgG antibody (1:100) for an hour. Then, the sections were washed three times for 5 min with 0.05% Tween 20 containing 1x TBS, and DAB was incubated for 10 min at RT. The nucleus was stained with hematoxylin (Sigma‒Aldrich) for 10 min. The slides were mounted with mounting solution. Images were visualized by BX53 microscopy and analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH). At least 6 organoids were quantified in each experiment, and the DAB results represent three independent experiments.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM, COs were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 1 h. The COs were washed with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate and 1.5% cyanoferrate in water for 1 h at RT in the hood. Next, COs were stained in 2% uranyl acetate in maleate buffer for 1 h in the dark. The samples were further dehydrated with ethanol and embedded with Epon (Electron Microscopy Sciences). After the polymerization of Epon, the samples were ultrasectioned to 60 nm, stained with 2% uranyl, and contrasted with lead citrate. Golgi images were captured at ×11,000 magnification. At least 6 organoids were quantified in each experiment, and the EM results represent two independent experiments.

Calcium imaging analysis

Intracellular calcium dynamics were assessed using the fluorescent calcium indicator Fluo-4 AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). COs were incubated with Fluo-4 AM in culture medium at 37 °C for 30 min. Following dye loading, organoids were washed with prewarmed culture medium and immediately subjected to live-cell imaging using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Time-lapse images were acquired under identical imaging conditions for all experimental groups. Changes in intracellular Ca²⁺ levels were quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity using ImageJ software (NIH) and expressed as ΔF/F₀, where F₀ represents the baseline fluorescence intensity prior to stimulation.

SLC30A6 knockdown and overexpression

The COs at 50–60 days of differentiation were gently dissociated by repeated pipetting (10–20 times) to generate small organoid clumps. The dissociated clumps were seeded onto Matrigel-coated culture dishes and allowed to attach and spread. For gene knockdown, cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA targeting SLC30A6 or an equal volume of negative control (NC) siRNA using Lipofectamine reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfections were carried out for 48 h, after which cells were harvested for downstream analyses. siRNAs targeting SLC30A6 were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (cat. no. #439240, #AM16708) and Bioneer (Daejeon, South Korea).

For gene overexpression of ZNT6, attached organoid-derived neuronal cells were transduced with AAV-ORF cDNA encoding human SLC30A6 at a dose of 5 × 10¹⁰ genome copies (GC) and cultured for 5–7 days prior to analysis. The AAV-ORF cDNA-SLC30A6 construct was obtained from GeneCopoeia (cat. no. #AA7m8-E3350-AV43-400).

RNA-seq bioinformatic analysis

RNA-seq experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using Agilent’s low RNA input linear amplification kit (Agilent Technology, Santa Clara, CA), cRNA cleanup module (Agilent Technology) and one-color (Cy3) Whole Human Genome Microarray 4 × 44 K (Agilent Technology). In brief, the gene expression data were processed using GeneSpring GX (Agilent Technology) and normalized by global scale normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected based on over 2-fold changes. A heatmap with hierarchical clustering was created by MeV software (v4.9.0). Chromosome clustering was performed using David Bioinformatics Resources 6.7 (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov).

Single-cell-seq analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on COs by e-biogen (Seoul, Korea). Briefly, organoids were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Accutase (Merck, New Jersey, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following dissociation, cell viability and concentration were assessed using the Countess™ II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and only samples with high viability were selected for downstream library preparation. Single-cell libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Individual cells were encapsulated with barcoded gel beads in emulsion (GEMs), enabling cell- and transcript-specific barcoding. After reverse transcription and cDNA amplification, sequencing libraries were generated and subjected to quality control prior to sequencing. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end sequencing. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger software (10x Genomics). This included demultiplexing of raw base call files, conversion to FASTQ format, and trimming of cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to 26 bp, with cDNA reads trimmed to 98 bp, in accordance with standard 10x Genomics pipelines. Trimmed reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome, and Cell Ranger was used to generate gene–barcode matrices, gene annotation tables, and comprehensive quality control metrics. Downstream quality control, exploratory analysis, and visualization were performed using Loupe Cell Browser v9.0 (10x Genomics), integrated with Cell Ranger outputs. The resulting dataset was used for dimensionality reduction, clustering, and identification of transcriptionally distinct cell populations within the COs.

For downstream normalization and comparative analyses, gene expression values were transformed using (average expression + 1) to stabilize variance and reduce the impact of low expression values. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a fold change >1.5 or <0.65 between experimental groups. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources v6.8 https://david.ncifcrf.gov), enabling the identification of significantly enriched biological processes and molecular pathways associated with transcriptional changes.

Animal experiments

Pathogen-free male BALB/c-nude mice (8–12 weeks old, approximately 25 g) were obtained from Deahan Biolink (Okcheon, Republic of Korea). All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment (20–22 °C) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water.

Teratoma formation assay

To evaluate the in vivo differentiation potential of hPSCs, a teratoma formation assay was performed. hPSCs were suspended in Matrigel and subcutaneously injected into BALB/c-nude mice. After 10–12 weeks, when visible teratomas had developed, the mice were sacrificed, and the teratomas were excised. Tissues were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C and then washed with PBS every 2–3 h for at least three cycles. Following fixation, samples were incubated in 30% sucrose at 4 °C until fully equilibrated, embedded in OCT compound, and cryosectioned using a cryostat microtome (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Cryosections were stained with H&E and examined under a light microscope to confirm the presence of differentiated derivatives of all three germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm.

Transplantation of COs into the mouse brain

Organoid transplantation into the mouse brain was performed following established protocols.31,32 Briefly, mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 6 per group): WT CO and SPASTΔe17 CO. All surgical procedures were conducted under anesthesia with 3% isoflurane inhalation. Buprenorphine hydrochloride (0.1 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously prior to surgery and every 8–12 h post-operatively for analgesia.

A small craniotomy was made over the right cerebral cortex (coordinates: AP: 0 mm; ML: ~1 mm; DV: –1.5 to –2 mm), corresponding to the frontal cortical region, and COs were transplanted into the cortical parenchyma. Hemostasis was achieved by gently removing blood using sterile gauze. COs at 60–65 days of differentiation were carefully transplanted into the cortical cavity. To promote wound healing, a 2.5 mm plastic sheet was placed over the transplantation site, and the scalp was closed with 5-0 silk sutures. Mice were closely monitored during recovery, and all animals survived the procedure.

At 90 days post-transplantation, the mice were sacrificed. Brains were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 2 days. After fixation, tissues were washed with PBS at room temperature every 2 h (three times) and then stored in PBS containing 0.02% sodium azide at 4 °C until further processing. Serial coronal brain sections (10 μm thickness) were prepared using a cryostat and stored at –80 °C for subsequent analysis. A schematic illustration of the xenograft generation process was created with BioRender.com.

Human brain tissue samples

Human brain tissues were purchased from the SNUH Brain Bank and the Institution for Corpse-Derived Substance Supply at Seoul National University Hospital (Seoul, Republic of Korea). We used 10 brain tissue slides from neurologically normal donors and 13 slides from AD patients. Detailed clinical information on the human brain tissues is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The brain slides were used for immunofluorescence staining or DAB staining.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. In particular, three to six COs were harvested in a group for all experiments, repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed by PRISM (GraphPad, version 10.0). Statistical significance was tested with ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction or independent unpaired t test. All data are presented as the means ± SDs.
