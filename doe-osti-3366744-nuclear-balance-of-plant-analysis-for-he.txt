1. Introduction
 In addition to producing electricity, advanced nuclear reactors can also serve as a source of process heat for a
wide range of industrial or residential applications. In cases where the heat application requires low-temperature
heat, waste heat from the balance of plant (BOP) can be utilized with minimal impact on electricity generation.
For applications requiring higher-temperature heat, heat can be extracted from the BOP, though this will reduce
the amount of electricity generated. In either case, the optimal BOP design will be case-specific and depend on
multiple factors, including the reactor type and the thermal requirements of the heat application.
 To determine the optimal thermal extraction design for a given heat application, the relationship between heat
extraction and electricity generation must be known. This work presents the results of thermodynamic analyses to
quantify this relationship for multiple reactor types. It should be noted that the extraction conditions presented in
this work represent the heat available at the point of extraction from the BOP; quantifying the amount of heat
ultimately delivered to the industrial process will require heat losses during transport to be accounted for.
 The reactor types studied in this work are as follows:
 • Sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) coupled to a superheated steam Rankine cycle with nominal steam
 generator outlet temperature of ~500°C;
 • Advanced pressurized water reactor (PWR) with a conventional saturated Rankine steam cycle with
 nominal steam generator outlet temperature ~273°C;
 • High temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) with superheated Rankine cycle with nominal steam
 generator outlet temperature of ~565°C;
 • Very high temperature reactor (VHTR) cooled by helium with direct helium Brayton cycle energy
 conversion with nominal reactor outlet coolant temperature ~850°C.
 For each of these reactor types, energy balance software was used to analyze the impact of heat extraction on
electricity generation for varying levels of extraction at multiple extraction locations. This report presents the
results of these analyses, which can be used to evaluate the performance and economic viability of nuclear-
coupled process heat applications. For example, if the amount of heat and electricity required for a given
industrial process is known, the results presented here can be used to find the minimum reactor power required to
meet both demands. Alternatively, if the reactor power and the specific requirements of the industrial process are
known, these results can be used to determine the maximum process capacity achievable with the available power.
Overall, the modeling results presented in this work support both the technical and economic evaluation of
nuclear reactors as a source of industrial process heat. Examples of such applications include nuclear energy
coupled with absorption chillers [1], direct air capture technologies [2], and clean hydrogen production [3].
 It is important to note that in the analysis presented here, the BOP cycle configuration (e.g., number of turbine
stages or number of feedwater heaters) was fixed based on designs originally optimized for electricity-only
production. No attempt to re-optimize the cycle configuration for combined heat and electricity production was
made, and only available extraction points were studied.

2. Reactor with Rankine Cycle
 For Rankine cycle reactors, steam can be extracted from many different locations within the BOP, such as at
the exit of the steam generator, between the high- and low-pressure turbines, from one of the turbine stages, or at
the condenser inlet. The extraction location will determine the temperature and pressure of the extracted steam
and therefore the conditions of the heat provided to the heat application.
 Extracting steam from most locations in the BOP results in a decrease in electrical generation. The higher the
extraction mass flow rate, the greater the reduction in electricity generation. Generally, extraction from a higher-
temperature/pressure stream will have a larger impact on electricity generation than extraction from a lower-
temperature/pressure stream. Therefore, it is typically desirable to use lower-temperature/pressure streams to
supply heat to heat applications when possible.
 Heat extraction from the condenser inlet is somewhat different; the only impact this extraction point has on
electricity generation is by slightly increasing the backpressure of the low-pressure turbine which will slightly
reduce its output. Otherwise, the heat at this location can be used without impacting generation, but this heat is of
a low quality and is not useful for many industrial applications. The temperature available from the condenser is
dependent on the condenser pressure, but it is generally around 45°C. The condenser saturation temperature may
vary by a few degrees over the course of the year as ambient conditions change seasonally. Note that the three
Rankine cycles shown in this work have slightly different condenser temperatures and pressures — this reflects
differences in modeling assumptions, not inherent differences between reactor types.
 In this work, extraction from multiple locations was considered for each reactor type. For turbine extraction,
only existing extraction lines were considered. However, in practice, if the BOP is designed with a specific
thermal application in mind, then the turbine can be built with additional extraction lines which are designed for
optimal matching. Additionally, it is assumed that any heat in the extraction flow is lost for electricity production
purposes: it is either used in the process HX or removed from the cycle in the condenser. In other words, no
attempts were made to re-use any remaining heat after the working fluid returns from the process HX to the cycle.
Such optimization would depend on the exact conditions of the process heat and therefore could not be
generalized in this study.
 The impact of extraction on electricity generation can be shown in different ways. The sections below show
the relationship between extraction mass flowrate and electricity generation and the relationship between
extraction mass flowrate and cycle efficiency. Additionally, an estimate was made of how much heat is available
from extraction lines in each case for the process heat. The actual amount of heat used in the process will depend
on the process itself. Consequently, only the maximum available heat (𝑄𝑚𝑎𝑥 ) is calculated in the work described
in this section. That maximum available heat is calculated using the difference in enthalpy between the extracted
steam and the flow exiting the condenser. Thus, the maximum available heat from each steam extraction line is
calculated with Equation (1):
 𝑄𝑚𝑎𝑥 = 𝑚𝑒𝑥𝑡 (ℎ𝑒𝑥𝑡 − ℎ𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑑 ) (1)

 where
 • 𝑚𝑒𝑥𝑡 = extraction flow rate,
 • ℎ𝑒𝑥𝑡 = extraction steam enthalpy at the corresponding temperature and pressure, and
 • ℎ𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑑 = enthalpy at the condenser outlet.

 2.1 Sodium-Cooled Fast Reactor
 The analysis for the SFR is based on the AFR-100 reactor design [4]. A concept of the AFR-100 steam
cycle has been developed by Argonne in previous work [5]. The steam cycle model development and the analysis
presented in the present work was carried out using GE GateCycle software [6].
 2

 The reference design conditions for the AFR-100 steam cycle are shown in Figure 1. Steam is provided in
the nuclear steam supply system (NSSS) steam generator (SG). The SG heat duty is 250 MW th, which is
converted to about 105 MWe (net) with a cycle efficiency of 41.8%. This net power output accounts for the
electricity consumed by the components shown in Figure 1, which are mainly the secondary side pumps. The
AFR-100 steam cycle employs two turbine stages, a high pressure turbine (HPT) and a low pressure turbine
(LPT), with moisture separation (MSEP) between turbine stages. After the LPT, the flow goes to the condenser
(COND), then to a series of feedwater heaters (FWHs), deaerator (DA), and condensate pumps (PUMP), before
returning to the SG. The heat in the FWHs is provided by the steam extraction lines from the turbines. The main
steam (the steam at the outlet of the SG) is at 500°C and 16 MPa and a mass flowrate of 110.8 kg/s. Figure 1 also
highlights the available steam extraction conditions at intermediate temperatures and pressures.
 As indicated in Figure 1, waste heat is normally rejected from the cycle in the condenser at 46°C and
0.01 MPa. The condenser heat rejection rate under nominal conditions is 142.7 MW (57% of reactor power). This
heat is assumed in the model as lost to the environment and thus using it, or any part of it, for the process heat
would not affect the steam cycle efficiency and plant electrical output in any way.
 For comparison with other reactor concepts, which have different power levels, the results in this section
are presented in relative terms, normalized to the total reactor (or SG) power. For example, all SFR flow rates are
provided in percent-nominal (%nom) units. From Figure 1, the relative nominal flow rate is 110.8 kg/s/250 MWth
= 0.443 kg/s/MWth.

 Figure 1. AFR-100 SFR reference design conditions and steam extraction options.

 To investigate the effect of heat extraction on the plant electrical output, a series of GateCycle
calculations were carried out with gradual increase in flow taken from the steam lines in Figure 1. These steam
lines include the steam supply to the HPT and LPT, at 500°C and 150°C, respectively, as well as steam extraction
lines to FWHs from the HPT (at 290°C and 227°C) and LPT (at 101°C and 80°C). For all these calculations, the
steam cycle layout in Figure 1 was modified to allow steam extraction for external purposes at various locations.
For example, to use the main steam at 500°C for the process heat, a flow split node was added upstream of the
 3

HPT. The primary port of that flow split still provided flow to the turbine while the secondary port of the flow
split was connected directly to the condenser to simulate heat removed from the system and thus lost to electricity
production. The mass flow rate in the secondary port for the external steam extraction was gradually increased
from 0 (no extraction) for as long as a convergent solution for the entire cycle could still be obtained by
GateCycle. These calculations were then repeated for all available steam options.
 For the steam extraction calculations, the reactor heat production (and heat addition in the SG) is still
fixed at 250 MWth, as are the boundary conditions of 500°C main steam temperature, 46°C condenser
temperatures, and 0.01 MPa/16 MPa of minimum/maximum cycle pressures. The calculations were done in the
design mode: for example, the turbine efficiency was held constant at 90%, and the effects of changing conditions
on the component design and performance were ignored. Likewise, the FWH calculations were done in the design
mode, where the desired outlet temperature (or terminal temperature difference) was specified and was held
constant, with the code calculating the required hot-side steam flow rate. As a result of these assumptions, the SG
inlet temperature also remains fixed at 240°C for all steam extraction flow rates. This means, together with the
fixed SG heat duty and the outlet temperature, that the steam flow rate in the SG remains fixed at 110.8 kg/s.
 The results of the steam extraction calculations for the AFR-100 SFR are shown in Figure 2. The net plant
electrical output (in %nom) and the steam cycle net efficiency (in %) are shown as a function of the steam
extraction flow rate (in %nom) for all considered steam extraction options. The steam at the turbine inlets is
shown by the solid lines, while the steam from the turbine extraction pipes is shown by the dashed lines. As
expected, diverting more and more steam from the turbine cycle to the process heat results in a decrease in the
plant electrical output and the cycle efficiency. Also, a higher steam grade (higher steam temperature) in the
process heat leads to a larger decrease in the plant output.

Because higher-temperature steam has higher heat capacity, extraction at higher temperature leads to more heat

 2.2 Pressurized Water Reactor
 The PWR is modeled using the AP1000 design [7]. This model is meant to represent the class of LWRs
with lower steam temperatures compared to SFR. For this work, the AP1000 steam plant was modeled in
GateCycle software [6] using the information provided in NRC licensing reports in [8].

 6

 The PWR steam cycle plant is shown in Figure 4 along with the conditions in the available steam extraction
points. The GateCycle model in Figure 4 was verified by reaching close agreement between the net cycle output
(1200.9 MW) and that provided in [8] (1199.5 MW). The net cycle output accounts for the pumping power on the
secondary side, as shown in Figure 4. The PWR BOP configuration is similar to that of the SFR, with a SG, HPT,
LPT, condenser, a number of FWHs, and condensate pumps. At the same time, there are a few noticeable
differences from the SFR analysis presented in the previous section:
 • The main steam temperature (273°C) and pressure (5.7 MPa) are significantly lower than those for the
 SFR;
 • The PWR steam cycle uses steam reheaters (RHs) before the LPT; and
 • The number of FWHs, and corresponding steam extraction lines from the turbine, is larger for the PWR.
 That difference results in a larger number of available steam extraction points (9 total: at 273, 241, 231,
         207, 258, 167, 128, 96, and 76°C), as shown in Figure 4.
 The heat addition rate in the SG is 3,415 MW, and the main steam flow rate is 1,880 kg/s, translating to
0.55 kg/s/MWth relative flow rate for comparison with other designs. The steam conditions in the condenser (43°C
and 0.009 MPa) are not that different from those for the AFR-100 SFR. The condenser heat rejection rate under
the nominal conditions is 2,178 MW (63.8% of reactor power).

 2.3 High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor
 HTGRs are advanced reactors that can provide high-temperature heat to industrial applications. In this
work, the HTGR analysis is based on X-Energy’s Xe-100 design [9]. While integration with the primary-side
helium can provide higher temperature heat, this work only considers the heat available from steam extraction
from the BOP. A Rankine cycle model representative of the Xe-100 steam cycle, which was previously developed
by INL, was leveraged in this work [10]. This model was developed in Cycle-Tempo, which is a software tool for
the analysis of energy conversion systems [11].

 9

 The baseline (no extraction) model is shown in Figure 7. The Xe-100 steam system is a Rankine cycle
with two turbine stages and several FWHs. The SG provides 203 MWth, which is converted into 81.3 MWe (net),
leading to a conversion efficiency of 40%. This net cycle output accounts for the power consumed by the primary-
side compressor and the secondary-side pumps. The main steam (outlet of the SG) conditions are 565°C and 164
bar. The main steam flow rate is 77.9 kg/s, which can also be expressed as a relative nominal flow rate of
0.384 kg/s/MWth.

 Figure 7. Xe-100 HTGR reference design conditions and steam extraction options.

 Five steam extraction locations were considered in this work. The first option for steam extraction is at
the outlet of the SG (main steam), which is also referred to as turbine bypass. The four remaining extraction
scenarios utilize the existing lines that route steam from the HPT and LPT to the FWHs. The Cycle-Tempo model
was used to analyze the impact that steam extraction has on plant electrical output. In each case, the baseline
model was modified to include a flow splitter at the steam extraction location that routes a portion of the extracted
steam to the condenser. This is a way of representing heat supply to an external process, since the steam routed to
the condenser is not available for electricity generation. To represent varying amounts of heat supply to an
industrial process, the amount of steam extracted and sent to the condenser was varied, starting at zero and
increased until the feedwater heating conditions could no longer be met. The main steam conditions, final
feedwater conditions, and main steam mass flow rate are fixed values. It is assumed that the turbine efficiency is
not impacted by the extraction and remains constant in all cases.
 Figure 8 shows the impact of steam extraction on net plant output and net cycle efficiency. As expected,
plant electrical output and cycle efficiency decrease with increasing extraction. Additionally, extracting higher-
temperature steam has a large impact on these parameters.

calculated using Equation (1); however, for the HTGR, the enthalpy at the condenser outlet is updated (36°C and

3. Reactor with Brayton Cycle
 Reactors utilizing a Brayton cycle differ significantly from the Rankine cycle reactors discussed previously.
Here a VHTR is used as an example of a Brayton cycle reactor. The analysis for the VHTR is based on the
HolosGen concept of a helium-cooled microreactor [12]. This concept was selected because Argonne has
participated in the design of HolosGen through an ARPA-E project [13]. As a part of that design work, a model of
the direct helium Brayton cycle for HolosGen was developed and described in the open literature [14, 15], and it
is therefore available for the present analysis. The HolosGen Brayton cycle is simulated using Argonne’s Plant
Dynamics Code (PDC) [16] for design, control, and transient analysis of Brayton cycles.
 The reference design conditions for the HolosGen Brayton cycle are shown in Figure 10. The reactor power is
22 MWth, which is converted to about 10 MWe with a cycle efficiency of 44.6%. The Brayton cycle layout is
typical for an intercooled direct Brayton cycle, where helium coolant (at 7 MPa pressure) leaves the reactor (at
850°C temperature) before expanding in the turbine (to 3.5 MPa), driving the generator to produce the electrical
output. After the turbine, helium goes through the recuperator, to recover some of the useful thermal energy,
before being sent to the cooler (at 125°C) for the heat rejection to the ultimate heat sink. The helium leaves the
cooler at the design temperature of 40°C and enters two stages of compression, passing through a low-pressure
compressor (LPC) (to 4.9 MPa pressure) and a high-pressure compressor (HPC) (back to 7 MPa), with
intermediate cooling between these two stages in the intercooler. The compressed helium goes to the recuperator,
then back to the reactor.

 It is important to note that the cycle calculations described here can only provide the inlet condition of the
reactor fluid (helium) entering the process-heat HX. How much heat can be extracted (or used by the process)
depends on the process itself and, in particular, the minimum temperature requirement for the process. For
helium, the extraction heat can be calculated with Equation (2):
 𝑄𝑒𝑥𝑡 = 𝑐𝑝 𝑚𝑒𝑥𝑡 (𝑇𝑒𝑥𝑡 − 𝑇𝑚𝑖𝑛 ) (2)

where

 16

 • 𝑐𝑝 = helium specific heat (5193 J/kg-K),
 • 𝑚𝑒𝑥𝑡 = extraction (bypass) flow rate,
 • 𝑇𝑒𝑥𝑡 = extraction temperature, either 850°C or 620°C, and
 • 𝑇𝑚𝑖𝑛 = minimum temperature at which the process can accept heat.
 Since that minimum temperature, in general, depends on the process to be used, the amount of heat available
to the process is also process-dependent. For the absolute limit, where a process can use heat all the way to the
minimum cycle temperature of 40°C, the amount of heat available to the process is equal to the total heat rejection
from the cycle in the cooler and intercooler. That upper limit can be easily calculated as (1 – cycle efficiency) *
reactor power, with cycle efficiency defined in Figure 11.
 In addition to the heat extraction options described above, another operating regime is also considered in this
study, where the reactor is utilized only to provide heat and not to generate any electricity. In this option, most of
the BOP components are eliminated. Instead, the coolant from the reactor, at 850°C, is sent directly to a process
HX. Then, the coolant is returned to the reactor by a circulator (or pump). To minimize the effect on the reactor
design, the return temperature (or temperature leaving the process HX) is assumed to be the same as the reactor-
inlet temperature of 590°C. The coolant will be at approximately the same pressure (around 7 MPa) everywhere
in this loop, except for the pressure drops which are compensated for by the circulator. The heat rate available in
the process HX is equal to 100% of the reactor thermal power (22 MWth).

4. Summary
 This work presents the results of thermodynamic analyses quantifying the relationship between heat
extraction and electricity generation for four advanced reactor types: a PWR, SFR, HTGR, and VHTR. For each
reactor type, multiple extraction locations were analyzed, spanning a range of temperatures and pressures relevant
to industrial heat applications. To facilitate the use of these results for future modeling efforts, curve fits giving
the relationship between extraction and electricity generation are provided in Table 1 in the Appendix.
 Several important assumptions and limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting these results:
 - All analyses were performed under on-design conditions, meaning that it was assumed that turbines and
 other components were sized for each extraction rate. In practice, a turbine can only be designed for a
 single nominal extraction rate; operating at a different extraction rate would cause components to operate
 off-design, resulting in larger efficiency penalties than those indicated by the curves presented here.
 - For the Rankine cycle reactors, only existing turbine extraction lines were considered. If the BOP were
 designed with a specific heat application in mind, additional extraction points could be considered,
 potentially enabling better matching between the extraction conditions and the process requirements.
 - All analyses assume that extraction flow is returned to the condenser or ultimate cooler and that no
 attempt is made to re-use any remaining heat after the working fluid returns from the process heat
 exchanger. Such optimization would depend on the specific conditions of the heat application and could
 not be generalized in this study.
 - Only secondary-side extraction was considered for the Rankine cycle reactors, and it is assumed that heat
 extraction from the secondary side has no impact on the primary side.
 - It should be noted that the reported results account only for the power produced and consumed by the
 components included in the BOP models. However, there may be other house loads that should be
 accounted for. For example, in the PWR and SFR models, the primary-side pumping is not accounted for.
 Finally, the extraction results presented in this work quantify the heat removed from the BOP at the point of
extraction; however, this is not equal to the heat ultimately delivered to the industrial process. The extracted heat
must be transferred to the process through a heat exchanger, which reduces the temperature of the heat delivered
to the process due to the temperature differences required to drive heat transfer across the heat exchanger. There
are also thermal losses from the transport of the heat from the NPP to the process facility — depending on the
application, transport distances could range from 100 m to several kilometers, resulting in additional pressure
drop and heat loss. As a result, the extraction conditions should be treated as an upper bound on the heat available
to the process.
 The results presented in this work are well-suited for initial evaluations of nuclear-coupled industrial heat
applications, where detailed site- or project-specific modeling may not yet be warranted. These results can be used
to quickly assess the technical and economic feasibility of a proposed heat application and narrow the design
space before investing in more detailed analysis. Together, the results for the four reactor types studied here span
a broad range of operating temperatures and cycle configurations, providing a useful reference for evaluating the
viability of nuclear energy as a flexible source of both heat and electricity.
