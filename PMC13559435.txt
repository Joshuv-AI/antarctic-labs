Pathogens and clinical characteristics of type 2 diabetes mellitus in patients with community-acquired pneumonia via bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) metagenomic next-generation sequencing: a comparative observational study
Authors: Yaling Fang, Mei Wang, Hanchao Wang, Shunzhou Xiao, Zhixiang Jie, Wei Xiong, Xiaochuan Wang, Qin Wang, Li Xie, Ya Li, Tao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) offers high sensitivity and specificity for pathogen detection. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a key risk factor for opportunistic infections, including community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). Targeted treatment of the pathogen is beneficial to the prognosis of patients with CAP. This study aimed to explore the pathogen distribution and clinical presentation of CAP in patients with T2DM via bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) mNGS.MethodsIn this comparative observational study, a total of 285 patients with CAP were enrolled. Ultimately, 117 CAP patients without T2DM and 96 CAP patients with T2DM were included. Demographics, clinical data, laboratory data, and BALF mNGS results were collected. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was performed to determine potential variables, which were subsequently included in a binary logistic regression model to identify clinical and microbiological features associated with pre-existing T2DM in CAP patients.ResultsThirteen potential T2DM-associated variables were selected by LASSO regression. Furthermore, binary logistic model identified that higher body mass index (BMI) [odds ratio (OR) =1.107, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.004–1.221], lower rate of connective tissue disease (CTD) (OR =0.046, 95% CI: 0.002–0.935), elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (OR =1.091, 95% CI: 1.003–1.187), the presence of COVID-19 (OR =6.582, 95% CI: 1.244–34.829) and Aspergillus (OR =2.909, 95% CI: 1.018–8.311) were independent variables for T2DM in CAP patients.ConclusionsCollectively, our analysis revealed that CAP patients with T2DM had a distinct clinical profile and pathogen spectrum, characterized by a higher prevalence of COVID-19 and Aspergillus infection, a greater tendency for renal dysfunction, and a higher BMI but a lower rate of CTD. These findings underscore T2DM as a significant risk factor for COVID-19 and Aspergillus pneumonia. In clinical practice, stricter infection screening strategies should be implemented for this population, along with targeted anti-infective therapy and enhanced dynamic assessment of renal function, weight management, and blood glucose control, will help improve outcomes.Keywords: Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), pathogen, risk factor

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our analysis revealed distinct clinical features and pathogen profiles between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and non-T2DM patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). Specifically, we found that CAP patients with T2DM had higher rates of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and Aspergillus infection, along with elevated body mass index and blood urea nitrogen levels, and a lower rate of connective tissue disease compared to those without T2DM.

What is known and what is new?

• Metagenomic next-generation sequencing sequences the nucleic acids of all microorganisms and the host present in a specimen, comparing them with reference gene databases for pathogen identification. This approach enables broad-spectrum pathogen detection and provides several advantages over conventional microbiological techniques, such as faster identification, broader coverage, and higher sensitivity.

• Our data showed that bacterium (84.03%) was the most common pathogen type, followed by virus (69.48%), fungi (48.37%), Mycoplasma/Chlamydia (19.71%), and tuberculosis/nontuberculous mycobacteria (6.57%), in CAP patients. Compared to CAP patients without T2DM, virus, COVID-19, other viruses, fungi, Aspergillus, Candida, and Pneumocystis jiroveci were more common in CAP patients with T2DM.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings indicate that T2DM is a key risk factor for COVID-19 and Aspergillus infections in the lungs. Therefore, these pathogens should be considered and specifically tested in CAP patients with pre-existing T2DM. Additionally, our data suggests that T2DM patients are more prone to renal dysfunction in the context of CAP. Hence, clinicians should exercise heightened vigilance when managing CAP patients with T2DM.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an infectious inflammation of the lung parenchyma occurring outside of hospital settings, caused by various pathogens, and remains a significant global health concern with a high incidence and a mortality ranging from approximately 5% to 15% (1,2). CAP frequently coexists with multiple chronic diseases, among which type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the most prevalent comorbidities (3-5). T2DM comprises a group of metabolic disorders characterized by elevated blood glucose levels, and according to a national analysis in China, the number of patients now exceeded 117 million (6,7). Epidemiologically, approximately 16% of CAP patients have concurrent T2DM (3), and patients with diabetes carry a 1.5- to 2-fold higher risk of developing CAP compared to those without diabetes (8).

Accumulating evidence indicates that CAP in patients with T2DM is a distinct clinical entity with more severe outcomes. Pitrowsky et al. found that clinical outcomes, including length of hospital stay and in-hospital mortality, were worse in CAP patients with T2DM compared to those without T2DM (9). A multicenter prospective cohort study involving 574 coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) patients with T2DM demonstrated that glycemic control was significantly associated with a decreased risk of death, intensive care unit admission, invasive mechanical ventilation, disease progression, and a composite outcome (10). A large-scale CAPNETZ cohort study involving 13,611 patients further confirmed that diabetic patients with CAP had significantly higher 180-day mortality and higher levels of inflammatory markers compared to non-diabetic patients (11).

However, the well-recognized association between T2DM and increased susceptibility to infections, the pathogen profiles of CAP in diabetic patients remain incompletely characterized. Previous studies investigating the link between T2DM and CAP primarily relied on conventional microbiological techniques (CMTs), such as microbial culture, smear microscopy, or specific antigen detection assays. These methods suffered from substantial limitations, including low sensitivity, long turnaround times, and an inability to detect fastidious, anaerobic, or unculturable organisms (12). Consequently, 30% to 60% of lung infection cases failed to achieve a definitive etiological diagnosis using conventional approaches, leading to delayed treatment or inappropriate antibiotic use (13,14). In the CAPNETZ cohort, pathogens were identified in only 23.4% of diabetic CAP patients and 21.7% of non-diabetic CAP patients using conventional methods, highlighting the substantial proportion of cases with undetermined etiology (11). These low detection rates severely hampered the comprehensive and unbiased comparison of pathogen distributions between diabetic and non-diabetic CAP patients, representing a critical gap in the current literature.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) offers a transformative approach to overcoming these limitations. By sequencing the nucleic acids of all microorganisms and the host present in a specimen and comparing them with reference gene databases, mNGS enables unbiased, broad-spectrum pathogen detection with faster identification, broader coverage, and higher sensitivity than conventional techniques (15,16). It was found that mNGS significantly outperformed conventional methods in detecting mixed infections and rare pathogens. Importantly, in patients with diabetes complicated by CAP, mNGS achieved a pathogen detection positive rate of 78% compared to only 21% with conventional microbiological testing (17). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), as a lower respiratory tract specimen, provides more representative sampling of the pulmonary infectious milieu and, when combined with mNGS, offers a powerful tool for comprehensive pathogen profiling in CAP patients (18).

Given the distinct immunological and metabolic milieu of diabetic patients and the limitations of prior studies relying on insensitive conventional methods, a comprehensive comparison of the pathogen spectrum and clinical features between CAP patients with and without T2DM using the unbiased approach of BALF mNGS is urgently needed. We hypothesized that, compared to CAP patients without T2DM, those with T2DM would exhibit: (I) a distinct pathogen profile characterized by a higher prevalence of opportunistic pathogens, including viruses, fungi, and multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacilli, etc.; (II) a higher rate of mixed infections; and (III) distinct clinical features reflecting greater inflammatory burden and disease severity. Therefore, this study sought to delineate and compare the pathogen spectrum and clinical characteristics of CAP patients with T2DM using BALF mNGS, provide evidence to guide empirical antimicrobial therapy, and improve clinical management in this high-risk population. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0909/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

This comparative observational study was performed at the Respiratory Medicine and Critical Care Medicine Department of Suining Central Hospital from December 2022 to July 2024. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Suining Central Hospital (No. KYLLKS20250080). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. According to the current clinical guideline (19), medical care and treatments were provided in our study.

Sample size determinations

According to previous studies (20-22), the sample size was determined. Briefly, a minimum of 200 participants (100 CAP without T2DM and 100 CAP with T2DM) were required with effect size =0.4, power =0.8, α=0.05, and allocation ratio =1:1. Furthermore, 10% more patients were recruited.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion was CAP with CURB-65 score ≥3 and age ≥18 years. Exclusion criteria were as follows: type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), hematological disease, antibiotic use within 2 weeks prior to admission, immunosuppressive status [immunosuppressive drugs administered in the previous 2 weeks, organ transplant recipient, and/or present human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection], systemic steroid use within the last 2 weeks, dysphagia and aspiration, dementia, history of malignant diseases, renal failure, and liver failure. A total of 269 patients with CAP were enrolled. Ultimately, 117 CAP patients without T2DM and 96 CAP patients with T2DM were included (Figure 1).

Definitions

The diagnostic criteria of CAP were (I) community-based outbreak; (II) clinical manifestations related to pneumonia, which included: (i) newly developed cough, sputum production, or worsening of pre-existing respiratory symptoms, accompanied or not by purulent sputum, chest pain, dyspnea, and hemoptysis; (ii) fever; (iii) signs of lung consolidation and/or presence of wet rales; (iv) peripheral white blood cell count (WBC) >10×109/L or <4×109/L; (III) chest imaging reveals new patchy infiltrates, lobar or segmental consolidation, ground-glass opacities, or interstitial changes, with or without pleural effusion. A clinical diagnosis was established if criteria 1 and 3 were met along with any one of the conditions in criterion 2, excluding pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), lung tumors, and other similar conditions (19,23). The diagnosis of T2DM was according to the American Diabetes Association guideline (6,24). Participants were defined as ex-smokers if they had abstained from smoking for ≥6 months (22,25).

Data collection

Demographics, underlying diseases, comorbidities, symptoms, arterial blood gas (ABG), serum electrolytes, liver function, renal function, and inflammatory parameters were recorded and collected. The blood samples for laboratory tests were all collected and performed within 24 h after admission. All patients underwent chest high-resolution computed tomography scans within 48 h of admission, and the images were reviewed by a radiologist and a pulmonologist independently.

BALF sample collection

According to the previous studies (26-28), BALF sample was collected within 48 h after admission. Briefly, lesion segments were selected for patients with limited lesions. The right middle lobe or the lingual segment of the left upper lobe was selected for patients with diffuse lesions. Then, saline with a total volume of 20–30 mL was injected into the selected lobe. Meanwhile, at least 40% of the fluid was recovered. After BALF collection, the sample was kept at −80 ℃ for further analysis.

BALF mNGS sequencing and analysis

According to the previous studies (29-32), BALF mNGS was performed within 12 h of sample collection. Briefly, human cells and DNA were removed using Tween 20 (Sigma, Mannheim, Germany) and Benzonase (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Based on the manufacturer’s instructions, the QIAamp® UCP Pathogen DNA Kit (Qiagen) was used to extract microbial DNA. Then, the library was constructed using a NextEra XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). For RNA sequencing, RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Then, purified RNA was obtained for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. Subsequently, the library was constructed using Ovation Ultralow System V2 (NuGEN, CA, USA). Next, the libraries were sequenced using Illumina Nextseq 550 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with a 75-bp, single-end sequencing kit. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) samples with 105 cells/mL from healthy donors were served as negative controls. DNA-free water also went through DNA extraction and mNGS analysis as a blank control group to assess background contamination.

For mNGS analysis, initially, fastp was used to remove adaptor contamination, low-quality reads, duplicate reads, low complexity reads, and short reads (length <40 bp). Human sequence data were identified and excluded by matching them to a human reference genome (hg38) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner software. Kraken 2 and Bracken were utilized to generate microbiome taxonomic profiles. The database used for quantifying taxonomic profiles was established using a combined database, which encompassed human, bacterial, fungal, parasite, and viral genomes [the NCBI Assembly and Genome databases (https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2)]. The multi-parameters of Species in Microbial Genome Databases were calculated and exported. Then, the results were interpreted by medical microbiologists and clinicians. Specifically, we first compared sequencing reads from clinical samples to those from negative controls; reads not significantly above background levels were considered negative and excluded. Subsequently, we primarily distinguished between infection and colonization based on the number of sequence reads and the relative abundance of pathogens obtained through sequence analysis. For colonization assessment, we referred to established baseline ranges of common respiratory commensal organisms in BALF samples, microbial counts within the expected range for colonization were classified as colonization. We also performed association analyses between mNGS findings and patients’ clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, radiological findings, and responses to antimicrobial therapy (improvement following targeted antimicrobial treatment was regarded as supportive evidence). Furthermore, all conflicting or ambiguous cases were discussed by a multidisciplinary panel comprising infectious disease specialists, pulmonologists, and clinical microbiologists to ensure the accuracy of mNGS result interpretations.

Statistical analysis

According to our previous studies (33-35), statistical analysis was performed. Data was analyzed using R (4.1.2) software. The distribution of continuous variables was analyzed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Continuous variables with abnormal distribution were expressed as the median and interquartile range [mean (Q1, Q3)]. Categorical data were expressed as the frequencies. Continuous variables with normal distribution were analyzed by the Student’s t-test. Continuous variables with abnormal distribution and ordinal variables were measured by Mann-Whitney U-test. Chi square test was used to analyze categorical variables. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to determine variable multicollinearity. VIF ≥10 indicated high multicollinearity, which was excluded in subsequent analysis. The potential variables associated with T2DM were first selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Then, based on these LASSO regression-selected variables, a binary logistic regression model was established to identify clinical and microbiological features associated with pre-existing T2DM in patients with CAP. P<0.05 indicated a significant difference.

Results

Demographics, clinical presentations, and laboratory results

This comparative observational study enrolled 285 patients with CAP. Finally, 96 (45.1%) CAP patients with T2DM and 117 (54.9%) CAP patients without T2DM were included (Figure 1). Compared to CAP patients without T2DM, those with T2DM had a higher proportion of males (74.0% vs. 59.0%), older age (69.5 vs. 65 years), higher body mass index (BMI) (23.14±3.34 vs. 22.25±3.08 kg/m2), more smoking, lower rate of pneumothorax (0.0% vs. 4.3%), higher levels of WBC (9.26×109/L vs. 8.31×109/L), neutrophil count (NEUT) (7.09×109/L vs. 6.53×109/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (6.68 vs. 5.54 mmol/L), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (7.85% vs. 5.8%), and random blood glucose (RBG) (10.6 vs. 6.8 mmol/L) (all P<0.05) (Tables 1,2).

BALF mNGS results

Our data showed that bacterium (84.03%) was the most common pathogen type, followed by virus (69.48%), fungi (48.37%), Mycoplasma/Chlamydia (19.71%), and TB/nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) (6.57%), in CAP patients (Figure 2 and Table 3). Compared to CAP patients without T2DM, the detection rates of viruses (79.2% vs. 61.5%), fungi (62.5% vs. 36.8%), and COVID-19 (9.4% vs. 1.7%), other viruses (58.3% vs. 40.2%), Aspergillus (17.7% vs. 5.1%), Candida (42.7% vs. 29.1%), and Pneumocystis jirovecii (19.8% vs. 10.3%) were significantly higher in CAP patients with T2DM (all P<0.05) (Table 3).

LASSO analysis results

LASSO regression was utilized to select T2DM-associated potential variables in patients with CAP. Then, 13 variables with nonzero coefficients, encompassing sex, age, BMI, smoking, pneumothorax, connective tissue disease (CTD), eosinophils count (EOS), BUN, other viruses, COVID-19, Pneumocystis jiroveci, Aspergillus, and Candida, were identified (Figure 3).

Binary logistic regression analysis

Subsequently, binary logistic regression model was established using 13 LASSO regression-selected potential variables. Then, binary logistic regression model revealed that BMI [odds ratio (OR) =1.107, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.004–1.221], BUN (OR =1.091, 95% CI: 1.003–1.187), COVID-19 (OR =6.582, 95% CI: 1.244–34.829), Aspergillus (OR =2.909, 95% CI: 1.018–8.311), and CTD (OR =0.046, 95% CI: 0.002–0.935) were independent variables for pre-existing T2DM in patients with CAP (Table 4).

Discussion

In this comparative observational study, 285 CAP patients were enrolled. Ultimately, 117 CAP patients without T2DM and 96 CAP patients with T2DM were included. Comprehensive data, encompassing demographics, comorbidities, BALF mNGS, and laboratory test results, were collected. Initially, LASSO regression identified 13 T2DM-associated potential variables. Subsequently, binary logistic regression revealed that BMI, CTD, BUN, COVID-19, and Aspergillus were independent variables for pre-existing T2DM in CAP patients.

China has one of the largest populations of individuals with diabetes, among whom about 90% were T2DM (36,37). Concurrently, T2DM and CAP share intertwined pathophysiological pathways. Several studies found that hyperglycemia disrupted coagulation, fibrinolysis, lipid metabolism, and endothelial function, while also impairing neutrophil and monocyte activity by inhibiting chemotaxis, adhesion, and phagocytosis (38,39). Immune dysfunction driven by these mechanisms increased the risk of respiratory tract infections in patients with T2DM by 1.5 to 4 times compared to non-diabetic individuals (40). However, the complex interplay between T2DM and CAP, along with its clinical implications, remains incompletely understood. Therefore, this study aimed to delineate the pathogen distribution and clinical characteristics for T2DM in CAP patients using BALF mNGS.

In our study, LASSO regression analysis was employed to reduce multicollinearity and prevent overfitting. Based on this approach, 13 potential variables were selected for binary logistic regression analysis. Our model revealed that BMI, CTD, BUN, COVID-19, and Aspergillus were the independent clinical features and pathogens for pre-existing T2DM in patients with CAP.

BMI emerges as a key indicator linked to both the incidence and mortality of CAP in patients with T2DM (41). Obesity, exerting pro-inflammatory and endocrine effects, promotes the development of T2DM and increases susceptibility to pulmonary infections. Marie et al. reported a nonlinear increase in diabetes prevalence with rising BMI (42). Chronically elevated free fatty acids in obese individuals contribute to reduced insulin sensitivity, impair insulin secretion and hyperglycemia (43). Consistent with these findings, our results found that elevated BMI was an independent risk factor for pre-existing T2DM among CAP patients. This result underscores the importance of effective weight management and intervention strategies in the prevention and mitigation of both T2DM and CAP.

BUN, a nitrogenous waste product in plasma, serves as a crucial biomarker for renal function. Accumulating evidence revealed that elevated BUN promoted oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction through enhanced protein carbonylation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (44). In T2DM patients, increased BUN is caused by high-protein dietary intake and excessive production of nitrogenous waste (45). Our analysis identified that elevated BUN was an independently clinical feature for T2DM in CAP patients, implying that T2DM patients are more vulnerable to renal impairment when afflicted with CAP. Pathophysiology likely involves a combination of pre-existing diabetic nephropathy, which can be exacerbated by the inflammatory and hemodynamic changes associated with severe infection, leading to a further decline in renal function. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in renal tissues, triggering oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, and ultimately glomerular and tubulointerstitial injury (46). Additionally, diabetes-associated microvascular disease compromises renal perfusion, further impairing urea clearance (47). Then, our finding indicates that clinicians should closely monitor BUN and renal function in CAP patients with concurrent T2DM.

mNGS has revolutionized the diagnosis of pulmonary infections. By providing unbiased, high-throughput nucleic acid sequencing, mNGS offers a hypothesis-free approach that is particularly valuable for detecting fastidious or rare pathogens that are difficult to culture (48). Furthermore, mNGS typically delivers etiological results within 24 to 48 hours, a rapid turnaround that can critically inform therapeutic decisions and promote rational antimicrobial use (49). When applied to BALF from patients with lower respiratory tract infections, mNGS enables rapid and accurate pathogen identification, especially in complex and severe cases such as immunocompromised hosts, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and suspected mixed infections or empirical treatment failure (50). Leveraging the unbiased nature of BALF mNGS, our study uncovered a distinct pathogen profile in CAP patients with T2DM, notably a higher prevalence of COVID-19 and Aspergillus, which may be underestimated by conventional diagnostic methods.

It was found that T2DM was associated with higher prevalence and severity of COVID-19, including increased rates of hospitalization, intensive care unit admission, and mortality, especially under conditions of poor glycemic control (51,52). A systematic review reported a pooled diabetes prevalence of 11.5% among COVID-19 patients and highlighted its association with severe outcomes (53). Singh et al. further emphasized considerable global variation in diabetes prevalence among COVID-19 cases (52). Consistent with these reports, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States listed diabetes as the most common comorbidity among COVID-19 patients. Through a survey of 7,162 patients with complete underlying disease information, the most common diseases were diabetes (10.9%) (54). Our results revealed that CAP patients with T2DM had a significantly higher rate of COVID-19 infection (9.4%) compared to non-T2DM patients (5.1%). Mechanistically, hyperglycemia upregulates the expression of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) in lung epithelial cells. ACE2 serves as the receptor through which COVID-19 enters human cells, the spike protein on the virus’s surface specifically recognizes and binds to ACE2 receptors on cell surfaces, initiating the infection process (55). Some studies showed that patients with T2DM exhibited overexpression of ACE2, and that prolonged hyperglycemic conditions altered the structure of the ACE2 protein, enhancing its binding affinity to the viral spike protein and thereby providing more opportunities for viral entry (56). On the other hand, ACE2 receptors are not only expressed in the lungs but also widely present in metabolic organs such as the pancreas (57). COVID-19 can directly target the pancreas via ACE2, potentially triggering new-onset diabetes or worsening pre-existing conditions (58). Furthermore, the chronic pro-inflammatory state and impaired interferon response commonly observed in T2DM patients can disrupt host antiviral defense mechanisms, leading to more severe clinical outcomes (59).

Pulmonary aspergillosis was caused by fungi of the genus Aspergillus. In immunocompromised hosts, particularly those with prolonged neutropenia, it can progress to invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA), a life-threatening condition characterized by angioinvasion and tissue necrosis. T2DM is increasingly recognized as a critical risk factor for IPA. Chronic hyperglycemia can impair innate immunity by damaging endothelial cells and simultaneously disrupting the function, chemotaxis, and phagocytosis of neutrophils (60). Additionally, microvascular complications compromises lung tissue integrity, facilitating fungal invasion (61). A large retrospective study by Koehler et al. demonstrated that diabetes was an independent risk factor for invasive mold diseases and was markedly associated with increased mortality. Furthermore, Shahzad A et al. elucidated a mechanistic link. They found that the key antifungal defense mechanism against Aspergillus was the neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which was composed of DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins that physically capture and kill fungal hyphae (62). Because Aspergillus hyphae are too large for efficient phagocytosis, extracellular trapping and killing via NETs is critical. However, in the hyperglycemic state of T2DM, the defense is severely impaired, because NADPH oxidase dysfunction in neutrophils reduces extracellular ROS production. Since ROS are essential for NETs formation, their disruption compromises antifungal NETs function and exacerbates neutrophil and lung tissue damage (63,64). Our findings revealed a significantly higher detection rate of Aspergillus by mNGS in CAP patients with T2DM (17.7%) compared to non-T2DM patients (5.1%). Furthermore, our binary logistic regression model also identified that Aspergillus was an independent pathogen for T2DM in CAP patients. Then, our result indicates that hyperglycemia impairs endothelial cell function, prevents the formation of NETs, and suppresses immune cell chemotaxis and adhesion, thereby compromising pathogen containment. Additionally, T2DM-related neuropathy can further compromise natural barriers, including skin, and mucosal integrity (65). These pathophysiological mechanisms collectively contribute to the increased incidence of both COVID-19 and Aspergillus infection in T2DM patients.

In this study, we found that COVID-19 and Aspergillus were independent pathogens for pre-existing T2DM in CAP patients, indicating that T2DM patients are more susceptible to these infections. Conversely, other common opportunistic pathogens, such as Pneumocystis jirovecii, Mucor, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and NTM, showed no independent association with T2DM in our CAP cohort, which probably was attributable to the limited sample size.

Interestingly, our data showed that a lower prevalence of CTD was independently associated with T2DM in CAP patients. This might be due to the relatively small sample size of CTD patients (a total of 8 cases). Alternatively, since immunosuppressive therapy is one of our exclusion criteria, patients with active or severe CTD may have been excluded, inadvertently selecting a population of CTD patients who are less prone to severe infections. However, the relationship between CTD and T2DM in the setting of CAP remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

The present study is characterized by several major strengths. Firstly, comprehensive clinical data was collected to enable a detailed analysis of the clinical characteristics for T2DM in CAP patients, which was one of major strengths of this study. Secondly, to mitigate multicollinearity and improve the reliability of variable selection, LASSO regression was employed. A binary logistic regression model was subsequently developed. However, several limitations also should be acknowledged. Given the cross-sectional design, this study did not explore the relationship between T2DM and patient prognosis or outcomes. Meanwhile, although BALF mNGS detected a wide range of pathogens, the specific pathogens corresponding to each individual case were not fully elucidated. Therefore, investigating the clinical profile associated with specific pathogens in CAP remains a promising and valuable tropic for future research. Additionally, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Thus, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these results.

Conclusions

Collectively, BALF mNGS represents a valuable and powerful tool for diagnosing lung infections. Our analysis revealed distinct clinical features and pathogen profiles between T2DM and non-T2DM patients with CAP. Specifically, we found that CAP patients with T2DM had higher rates of COVID-19 and Aspergillus infection, along with elevated BMI and BUN levels, and a lower rate of CTD compared to those without T2DM. These findings indicate that T2DM is a key risk factor for COVID-19 and Aspergillus infections in the lungs. Therefore, these pathogens should be considered and specifically tested in CAP patients with pre-existing T2DM. Additionally, our data suggests that T2DM patients are more prone to renal dysfunction in the context of CAP. Hence, clinicians should exercise heightened vigilance when managing CAP patients with T2DM. The role of CTD in the interplay between T2DM and CAP remains unclear, making it an interesting and valuable topic worthy of further investigation.
