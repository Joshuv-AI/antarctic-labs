 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project investigated how microphysical–dynamical feedbacks influence deep convective
clouds across a range of aerosol conditions, storm lifecycles, and meteorological regimes, and
how these sensitivities depend on the modeling framework used to represent convection and
microphysics. Using the Aerosol, Cloud, Precipitation, and Climate (ACPC) Working Group model
intercomparison simulations of isolated deep convection in the Houston region, we found a
robust warm-phase aerosol response in most cloud-resolving models. Increased aerosol loading
tended to suppress warm-rain production, increase cloud water, and reduce surface rainfall and
near-surface evaporation. These impacts were typically strongest early in the convective
lifecycle, with the largest aerosol-driven differences occurring during the first half of storm
evolution. The ice-phase response, on the other hand, varied widely across modeling
frameworks, and differed in sign, timing, and vertical structure. Ice microphysical pathways and
parameterizations are therefore leading sources of uncertainty in aerosol–deep convection
interactions. Theoretical analyses further suggested that aerosol-driven invigoration through
cold-phase processes was much weaker than previously hypothesized for cold-based storms,
and in warm-based storms could even reduce updraft strength. These results imply that any
invigoration signal is more likely linked to warm-phase processes and peaks early in storm
development.

To observationally constrain these modeling sensitivities, the project leveraged measurements
from the DOE Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) and related field
campaigns targeting isolated deep convection in a coastal urban environment impacted by sea-
breeze forcing, heterogeneous land-surface influences, and high aerosol variability. TRACER
observations indicate that aerosol effects on bulk convective properties are generally small
once meteorological variability is controlled, though stronger responses are evident under
specific aerosol regimes (e.g., ultrafine-rich conditions) and environmental settings. Observing
System Simulation Experiments (OSSEs) showed that resolving aerosol- and lifecycle-dependent
storm differences require rapid, cell-following sampling, as early-life peak updrafts can differ by
~5–7 m s⁻¹ between clean and polluted cases. These findings motivated optimized observing
strategies combining operational volume scans with rapid (1–2 min) targeted RHI cross
sections. This was achieved through the use of Multisensor Agile Adaptive Sampling (MAAS) to
generate a large, high-frequency convective lifecycle dataset.

Overall, the combined modeling, theoretical, and observational approaches provided an
integrated framework for assessing aerosol impacts on deep convection across storm lifecycles
and environments. Such a framework facilitates a more rigorous “apples-to-apples” comparison
of aerosol-induced deep convective regimes across numerical models.

 PROJECT ACTIVITIES AND FINDINGS

1. Overarching Goal

The overarching goal of the proposed research is to improve our understanding of the role of
microphysical and dynamical processes in the precipitation, updrafts and downdrafts, and
anvils of deep convective clouds and how these processes vary as a function of model physics,
aerosols, convective lifecycle and the environment, in order to enhance their representation
within CRMs and GCMs.

2. Science Questions

The following three science questions were addressed:

Science Question 1 (SQ1): What are the primary microphysical and dynamical processes and
feedbacks responsible for the variability in the (a) precipitation, (b) updraft and downdraft
velocities, and (c) convective anvils of isolated DCCs within current state-of-the art CRMs?

Science Question 2 (SQ2): How do the primary microphysical and dynamical processes
responsible for the variability in DCC (a) precipitation, (b) updraft and downdraft velocities, and
(c) anvils within current state-of-the art CRMs vary as a function of aerosol loading?

Science Question 3 (SQ3): How do the primary microphysical, dynamical, and aerosol processes
responsible for the variability in DCC (a) precipitation, (b) updraft and downdraft velocities, and
(c) anvils within current state-of-the art CRMs vary as a function of convective lifecycle and
environment?

3. Science Objectives

To answer our three science questions, we focused on the following 4 objectives:

Science Objective 1 (SO1): Determine the predominant microphysical and dynamical processes
impacting the precipitation, updraft and downdraft velocities, and anvil characteristics of
isolated DCCs as a function of the model physics, aerosol loading, and lifecycle for the seven
different modeling frameworks represented in the ACPC MIP simulations of convective
development around Houston.

Science Objective 2 (SO2): Provide optimal radar and lidar sampling strategies for TRACER
through the use of observing system simulation experiments (OSSEs) performed using
appropriate instrument simulators, tracking algorithms and the ACPC MIP model data, and the
knowledge gained from SO1.

Science Objective 3 (SO3): Determine the predominant microphysical and dynamical processes
impacting the precipitation, updraft and downdraft velocities, and anvil characteristics of

isolated DCCs as a function of the model physics, aerosol loading and lifecycle for several
selected TRACER case study simulations conducted using a subset of the ACPC MIP modeling
frameworks.

Science Objective 4 (SO4): Evaluate the model representations of the predominant
microphysical and dynamical processes impacting precipitation, updraft and downdraft
velocities, and anvil characteristics of the simulated TRACER DDC case studies through “apples-
to-apples” comparisons of the TRACER observations and the model output. For well-performing
modeling frameworks, draw conclusions about the role of aerosol loading, lifecycle and the
environment drawing on both the observations and model processes, while for poorly-
performing model frameworks, provide insights into future parameterization enhancements.

4. Project Findings

Science Question 1: What are the primary microphysical and dynamical processes and
feedbacks responsible for the variability in (a) precipitation, (b) updraft and downdraft
velocities, and (c) convective anvils of convective clouds within current state-of-the art
CRMs?

a. Modeling diagnosis across ACPC MIP frameworks (SO1; SO3)

 • CRM process variability is dominated by robust warm-phase behavior but uncertain
 ice-phase pathways. Using cell-tracked simulations from the ACPC Deep Convective
 Working Group intercomparison for isolated deep convection near Houston (19 June
 2013), we found consistent aerosol-induced warm-phase behavior across CRMs
 (autoconversion / accretion, cloud water, rainfall efficiency, and near-surface
 evaporation), while the ice-phase response exhibited large inter-model spread in sign,
 timing, and vertical structure. This identifies ice microphysics as a leading contributor to
 CRM divergence in precipitation and vertical structure in convective systems relevant to
 TRACER and related campaigns (Saleeby et al. 2025).
 • Lifecycle dependence is critical to interpreting process variability. Across tracked cells,
 the magnitude of process sensitivity (particularly warm-phase responses) is largest early
 in cell evolution, reinforcing the need for lifecycle information in both modeling and
 observing strategies (Saleeby et al. 2025).

b. Process constraints on ice microphysics (SO1 and SO3 interpretation and parameterization
 improvement)

 • Ice growth depends strongly on supersaturation, turbulence, and SCL. Long-term Ka-
 band radar observations show that ice growth signatures track ice supersaturation and
 temperature, with enhanced growth when turbulence and supercooled liquid water
 (SCL) co-occur, providing key constraints for improving modeled ice processes (Yan et al.
 2025).

 • Secondary ice production (SIP) can enhance mixed-phase ice early in cloud
 development. Airborne observations indicate SIP can elevate ice crystal numbers
 beyond INP levels in young congestus, with fragmentation of freezing droplets likely
 important during early stages. Model–observation discrepancies highlight needed
 improvements in SIP parameterizations (Patnaude et al. 2025).

c. Observational context for convective lifecycle and environment (TRACER as a testbed;
 SO4 supporting evidence)

 • Sea-breeze environment strongly organizes convective initiation and evolution. Long-
 term Houston radar tracking (2018–2021) quantified diurnal cycles and lifecycle
 relationships for shallow through vigorous deep convective cells, providing robust
 constraints on environmental forcing and lifecycle evolution relevant to TRACER
 convection (Tuftedal et al. 2024).
 • Shallow convection modes constrain the background state for deep convection. Joint
 radar–satellite datasets during TRACER / ESCAPE (June–September 2022) identified four
 distinct diurnal modes of shallow convection and a coastal precipitation-enhancement
 region, supporting interpretation of convective environment and organization (Mages et
 al. 2025).

Science Question 2: How do the primary microphysical and dynamical processes responsible
for variability in convective precipitation, vertical velocities, and anvils vary as a function of
aerosol loading?

d. Modeling diagnosis of aerosol sensitivity (SO1; SO3)

 • Aerosol impacts on warm-phase processes are consistent across models; aerosol
 impacts on ice are not. Increased aerosol loading consistently reduced warm-rain
 production, increased cloud water, reduced rainfall, and reduced near-surface
 evaporation across CRMs, while aerosol-driven ice responses varied widely across
 models, indicating strong dependence on aerosol–ice coupling and microphysical
 parameterizations (Saleeby et al. 2025).
 • Cold-phase aerosol invigoration is not a robust mechanism. Theory shows aerosols do
 not strongly invigorate convective updrafts via cold-phase processes. Invigoration is
 substantially reduced for cold-based storms and can become negative for warm-based
 storms. Any invigoration appears to be more consistent with warm-phase processes and
 earlier lifecycle stages (Igel and van den Heever 2021).

e. Observational constraints on aerosol impacts (SO4)

 • Aerosol impacts on bulk convective properties are modest after controlling
 confounders. TRACER observations show limited impacts of aerosol number

 concentrations on echo-top height, intensity, and area when confounding meteorology
 is addressed. Under ultrafine-particle-rich conditions, echo-top height increases of ~1
 km are identified, though uncertainties remain due to sampling limitations. Sea-breeze-
 cases may require additional confounder controls (Wang et al. 2025).
 • Aerosol proxy shows limited links to initiation location in long-term statistics. GOES-16
 AOD as an aerosol proxy shows no strong relationship between initiation location and
 aerosol concentration, aside from minor pre-initiation differences for vigorous deep
 convection (Tuftedal et al. 2024).

f. Microphysical and Radiative Pathways

 • Radiative vs microphysical pathways remain central to scale-bridging interpretation.
 We conducted a review separating aerosol effects into radiative and microphysical
 pathways. It shows strong consensus that radiative effects can alter global-mean
 precipitation and shift large-scale patterns, while microphysical effects at storm scales
 remain uncertain despite robust droplet-number/droplet-size responses (Stier et al.
 2024).

Science Question 3: How do the primary microphysical, dynamical, and aerosol processes
responsible for variability in convective precipitation, vertical velocities, and anvils vary as a
function of convective lifecycle and environment?

g. Lifecycle and environmental dependence in CRMs (SO1; SO3)

 • Aerosol sensitivity peaks early in convective evolution. Cell-tracked CRMs show
 aerosol-driven warm-phase impacts are largest in the first half of the lifecycle,
 supporting the need for high-temporal-resolution process diagnostics during early
 convective development (Saleeby et al. 2025).
 • Theory supports an early-lifecycle warm-phase emphasis. Theoretical constraints show
 any aerosol-driven invigoration is likely linked to warm-phase processes and early storm
 stages (Igel and van den Heever 2021).

h. Enhanced observing strategies and OSSE guidance (SO2; SO4)

 • OSSEs quantify required sampling to resolve lifecycle-dependent aerosol impacts. 1-
 min CRM simulations coupled with radar simulators show clean vs polluted early-life
 maximum updraft differences of ~5–7 m s⁻¹, requiring rapid scanning and accurate
 retrievals. Rapid (1–2 min) multi-radar RHIs with cell tracking outperform 5-min volume
 scans for microphysical and dynamical characterization. Sampling >10 deep cells reduces
 population uncertainty to < 3 m s⁻¹ (Oue et al. 2022).
 • Adaptive sampling provides lifecycle observations at scale. Mulmsensor Agile Adapmve
 Sampling (MAAS) enabled automated NEXRAD-driven targeting of isolated convective

 cells and coordinated dual-radar sampling at ~2-min cadence, producing >315,000
 vertical cross sections across ~1,300 isolated cells, most tracked for >15 min (Lamer et
 al. 2023).
 • Objective tracking tools enable consistent lifecycle analysis across models and
 observations. tobac v1.5 provides improved 3D tracking, split/merge handling, spectral
 filtering, and computational enhancements supporting consistent analysis workflows
 across large datasets (Sokolowsky et al. 2024).

i. Environmental controls on initiation and convective organization (TRACER/Houston and
 beyond)

 • Sea-breeze forcing produces strong diurnal organization and lifecycle signatures.
 Houston radar and satellite analyses constrain diurnal initiation and lifecycle
 relationships that provide environmental controls relevant to interpreting aerosol
 impacts (Tuftedal et al. 2024; Mages et al. 2025).
 • Land-surface heterogeneity can substantially modulate initiation pathways. Idealized
 simulations show forest-breeze circulations can dominate initiation location under dry-
 soil TRACER/ESCAPE conditions, with cold pools influencing propagation/collision timing
 and sensitivity to heterogeneity scale (Ascher et al. 2025a).

j. Observational campaign foundation (TRACER and other campaign utilization; SO4)

 • Coordinated field campaigns established integrated observational capability in
 aerosol–convection regimes. TRACER deployments in SE Texas (July 2021–Oct 2022)
 provided measurements spanning aerosol properties, thermodynamics, convective
 structure, lightning, and urban surface fluxes, supporting ongoing analysis and model
 evaluation activities including a TRACER model intercomparison (Jensen et al. 2026).
 • ESCAPE provided intensive targeted lifecycle observations in the TRACER domain.
 Experiment of Sea Breeze Convecmon, Aerosols, Precipitamon and Environment (ESCAPE)
 integrated aircraft sampling and mobile ground-based observations, culminating in late-
 season dual-pol C-band radar operations with agile sampling to track isolated DCC
 lifecycles under varying aerosol and sea-breeze conditions (Kollias et al. 2025).
 • INCUS concept testing validates reflectivity time-lapse diagnostics relevant to
 convective mass flux. Agile-sampling surface radar observations show coherent ΔZ
 structures (>5 dB) can develop in <30 s and track convective motions, supporting ΔZ-
 based dynamical constraints relevant to future observing systems and model evaluation
       (Dolan et al. 2023).

k. Supporting observing technology and process-diversity context

 • Lightning mapping supports high-temporal-resolution situational awareness in
 convective regimes. Evaluation of aircraft-mounted lightning mapping showed
 moderate detection efficiencies and improved bearing with longer integration,

 supporting practical storm circumnavigation guidance in convective environments
       (Milani et al. 2025).
 • Environmental forcing can alter precipitation organization in morphology-dependent
 ways. A side modeling study shows upstream boundary-layer modification can enhance
 or suppress precipitation depending on storm morphology and secondary circulations,
 emphasizing the broader importance of environmental controls on convective
 organization (Ascher et al. 2025b).
