Abstract
This paper presents a summary of my research and technical contributions during a summer
internship at Los Alamos National Laboratory, supporting the development of the active
veto system for the UCNProBe experiment. UCNProBe aims to perform a state-of-the-art
measurement of the free neutron lifetime, requiring low-background conditions facilitated by
an array of sodium iodide (NaI) scintillators.
 My work focused on characterizing the performance of the available NaI detectors, which
varied in optical and electronic configuration, with the goal of ensuring stable long-term
operation.
 My main efforts included measuring energy resolution as a function of source position,
determining voltage plateaus for photomultiplier tube (PMT) bases, testing the muon detec-
tion efficiency of each crystal, and quantifying peak drift behavior. Crystals equipped with
3-inch PMTs generally required higher bias voltages and exhibited better light collection,
while one 2-inch crystal (SN 01675) showed anomalous stability behavior. Muon detection
efficiency was approximately (99 ± 1)%, which is good enough for active veto detection.
 To guide detector testing, I developed a Geant4 simulation that successfully reproduced
experimental spectra and will be extended to include cosmic-ray background modeling and
coincidence veto logic.
 In addition, I contributed to the design and testing of mechanical supports for long-term
detector mounting and worked to establish interfacing for system readout, thermocouple,
and power supply systems. These combined efforts are applicable to both UCNProBe and
Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) UCN operations.

1. Introduction
1.1. UCNProBe
Free neutrons are unstable, and decay after approximately 15 minutes. There are a few meth-
ods of measuring this decay time, generally falling into two categories; “beam”-type experi-
ments, which measure the continuous rate of neutron beta decay products, and “bottle”-type
experiments, which trap neutrons and measure the quantity remaining after some time. In
recent years, a 4 σ discrepancy has emerged between these two methods, and although there
have been many experimental and theoretical efforts towards this problem, still no resolution
has been made. UCNProBe is a new experiment set to take place at Los Alamos National
Laboratory starting in 2026, aiming to resolve this discrepancy by combining techniques from
both the beam and bottle methods [1]. Ultra Cold Neutrons (UCN) from LANSCE will
be trapped in a scintillating holding box, where decay products will be measured for several
minutes until an absorbing “dagger” is inserted to empty the box and count the number of
remaining neutrons.
 Measurements of decay products are complicated by the presence of multiple sources
of background in the experiment hall, primarily cosmic rays and LANSCE spallation γ
background. To limit these effects, the entirety of the experiment is surrounded by several
tons of Lead shielding, and inside the shielding there are 16 Sodium Iodide (NaI) veto
detectors which aim to exclude any high energy background that manages to pass through
the shielding.

1.2. Summary
My work involved characterizing energy resolution and voltage response of available NaI veto
detector crystals, studying environmental effects such as peak drift, testing muon detection
efficiency, and simulating the detector response using Geant4 [3]. Additional tasks included
mechanical prototyping for the physical assembly of the detector array and interfacing of
control systems.
 This report details my approach and findings for each task and outlines the expected
contributions to be completed in the remainder of the internship. While only 10 out of 16
crystals are currently operational, further work is anticipated in coupling additional crystals
to PMTs and expanding simulations to account for cosmic-ray-induced backgrounds.

2. Detector Inventory and Preparation
Of the 16 NaI crystals intended for the UCNProBe veto system, only 10 are functional: two
have broken PMTs and four lack PMTs entirely. I expect to assist in completing the PMT
coupling and testing for the remaining crystals during the remainder of my internship.
 8 of the crystals use a 2-inch diameter PMT, while the remaining 8 use 3-inch PMTs.
The larger PMTs generally demonstrate superior light collection and require higher operating
voltage.

3. Experimental Characterization
3.1. Crystal Position-Dependent Energy Resolution
Each operational NaI crystal was fitted with a PMT and exposed to a 137 Cs source at various
positions. The energy resolution (FWHM/ peak center) varied with source position; resolu-
tion was slightly worse when the source was on the end farther from the PMT. Resolution
from spectra summed among all positions was approximately 8% for all crystals, consistent
with known NaI(Tl) scintillator performance [2].

3.2. Voltage Plateau Measurements
To determine the stable operating voltage range for each PMT, I measured counting rates
over a range of PMT voltages (1000–1500 V) for each crystal (Fig. 1). Most crystals
showed plateau behavior near 1200–1400 V, with 3-inch PMTs preferring the higher end.
The “plateau voltage” is where the detector is least sensitive to changing environmental
conditions such as voltage variation, but the actual voltage may be adjusted within this
range to fine tune the energy spectra calibration.

4. Geant4 Simulation of UCNProBe Veto Detectors
I developed a Geant4 simulation of a Reflectix-lined NaI crystal with a 3-inch PMT (Fig.
5), irradiated by 137 Cs and 60 Co sources. The simulation reproduces experimental energy
spectra by histogramming the number of detected optical photons per event, an analog to
the real trapezoid-filtered pulse processing.
 Planned extensions include simulation of cosmic-ray muon backgrounds and coincidence
veto logic. See Appendix for simulation parameters.

5. Muon Coincidence Detection
To determine the muon detection efficiency of each crystal and ultimately their effectiveness
as cosmic ray veto detectors in the actual experiment, I built an 80/20 stand to hold 3
detectors in a vertical line as shown in Fig. 6, and then I set up coincidence logic with NIM
modules to count the number of coincidences observed during a timed period.

Figure 6: Three NaI detectors are stacked vertically using the 80/20 stand support, and their
outputs are wired into NIM modules for discrimination, coincidence, and timed counting. In
the setup that measured 99% efficiency, two plastic scintillators (black taped “paddles”) were
used as the coincidence units, using the same NIM logic as before. Due to the smaller area
of the plastic scintillators and the increased signal duration, the tested NaI detectors were
now less subject to geometric and signal processing effects than the coincidence detectors.

 After aligning the spectra by varying the input voltage within the plateau range, and
adjusting the discriminator settings to capture the scintillation waveforms from each crystal
(Fig. 7), counting logic was set up to count the number of coincidences between the top and
bottom crystals, and the number of coincidences among all three crystals. If a simultaneous
event was observed to in both the top and bottom crystals, this was likely a muon due
to the low probability of such high energy events within the coincidence window ( 400ns).
Thus the middle detector’s efficiency could be measured by comparing the ratio of the top,
bottom coincidences rate with the top, bottom, middle coincidence rate. With this method,
the average muon detection efficiency ratio was (94.5 ± 2)%, with statistical error 1.4% on
each measurement. Background coincidences were determined to be negligible by moving
the detectors out of plane, and observing coincidence counts of 3 (triple coincidence) and
11 (top bottom coincidence) after a 10 hour run. This detection efficiency is much lower
than expected for high energy muon events, so to determine the issue we attempted lowering
thresholds, varying geometry, and changing the discriminator signal duration. In the end,
by using two much smaller plastic scintillators as the coincidence detectors, decreasing the
NaI threshold from 750 mV to 250 mV, and increasing the discriminator signal duration,
the muon detection efficiency of crystal 01664 was confirmed to be 99%.

6. Miscellaneous Efforts
6.1. Mechanical Support Testing
I resin 3D-printed the NaI support brackets at the LANL IHUB makerspace, and built a
stand to test their load bearing ability. Under prolonged load, the resin sagged, prompting
evaluation of stronger materials like aluminum or higher-strength resin. A final decision has
yet to be made on the material to be used.

6.2. Data Input/ Output Interfacing
I interfaced the CAEN SY4527LC power supply with C code for automatic voltage adjust-
ment, which will be informed by measurements of peak drift. I also configured the MC TC-32
thermocouple board via the uldaq software [4], setting up udev rules for device access. In
addition to aiding my crystal stability investigation, this thermocouple board is now set up
in LANSCE area B recording temperature fluctuations in startup operations for UCNTau,
and will later be used to help validate proton beam alignment.
 Additional efforts included setting up a script to monitor helium Dewar temperature
and output to an internal website, assisting with setting up the FOIL and BARE UCN flux
monitors at the test beam port, and supporting the installation of a chopper spectrometer
in the LANSCE UCN hall.

7. Summary and Outlook
This work contributed to the readiness and understanding of the UCNProBe veto system
through testing, simulation, and infrastructure integration. Further work will involve final-
izing tests of remaining detectors, expanding the simulation to include cosmic-ray rejection,
and helping integrate the detectors into the full experimental assembly once the remaining
parts arrive.
