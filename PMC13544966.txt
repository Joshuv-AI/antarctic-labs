Stem cell-based therapeutics in epilepsy: current advances and future prospects
Authors: Napasiri Putthanbut, Jea-Young Lee, Cesario V Borlongan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy represents as one of the most prevalent and serious neurological disorders, with approximately one-third of patients resistant to conventional treatments. Current pharmacologic therapies primarily suppress seizures without targeting the underlying epileptogenesis, resulting in unchanged long-term prognosis. At the same time, epilepsy surgery remains underutilized due to the requirement for extensive evaluations and specialized expertise. Stem cell-based therapy has emerged as a promising avenue, offering the potential to address both the neurological and neuropsychiatric aspects. This review summarizes recent preclinical and clinical studies on stem cell therapy in epilepsy, with a focus on their mechanisms of action, including direct engraftment, restoration of normal neurogenesis, and immunomodulation via bystander effect. Advances in genetic engineering, extracellular vesicle-based therapy, and patient-derived brain organoids offer more personalized treatment strategies. Although stem cell therapy holds significant promise as a treatment for epilepsy, further extensive research is required to establish its clinical safety and efficacy.

Epilepsy is a prevalent and disabling neurological disorder, with one-third of patients failing to achieve seizure control with existing treatments. Antiseizure medications do not alter long-term prognosis and epilepsy surgery remains underutilized owing to its demanding criteria. Stem cell therapy provides a promising adjunctive treatment that improves both neurological and neuropsychiatric outcomes. This review highlights recent preclinical and clinical studies, emphasizing key mechanisms and personalized medicine, including extracellular vesicles, genetic engineering, and patient-derived organoids, altogether enabling the safety and efficacy of stem cell-based therapy in epilepsy.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder with a global prevalence of approximately 600 per 100 000, affecting more than 50 million individuals worldwide.1,2 Seizures occur from abnormal synchronous neuronal firing involving focal or diffuse cortical networks, triggered by structural, infectious, or metabolic perturbations. Epilepsy manifests as a complex disorder with environmental effects acting on inherited susceptibility, mediated by common variation in particular genes, rather than a single mutation or cause.1 Diverse causes of epilepsy result in a bimodal distribution, with genetic causes in childhood and accumulated injury to the brain (eg, trauma, tumors) in the elderly.2 As epilepsy displays a wide range of possible pathophysiological features, from well-recognized convulsions to psychological symptoms, a detailed history serves as the cornerstone of the diagnosis.1 Additional tests such as electroencephalogram (EEG) and neuroimaging, preferably magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), help identify the structural and functional causes, facilitating diagnosis and classification of epilepsy.3,4

The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) defines epilepsy by any of the following conditions: (1) At least two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart; (2) one unprovoked seizure and a probability of further seizures at least 60% (ie, comparable to recurrent risk after two unprovoked seizures) over the next 10 years; (3) diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.5 Epilepsy is classified into four main classes: Focal, Generalized, Combined, and Unknown.6 Patients with generalized epilepsy exhibit generalized spike-wave activity on EEG, whereas focal epilepsy shows epileptiform activity that is confined to a single hemisphere. The combined epilepsy, such as Dravet syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, is defined by both generalized spike-wave and focal epileptiform discharges during seizure. Lastly, the unknown group refers to cases where epilepsy is confirmed but the type cannot be determined due to insufficient information, such as an unavailable or uninformative EEG. Noteworthy, epilepsy may involve multiple seizure types in the same patient. Progress in genomic technology is exposing the complex genetic architecture of the common types of epilepsy, facilitating more precise classification and treatment design.1,7

Epilepsy greatly reduces quality of life and poses a risk of a sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP).7 Current antiseizure medications (ASMs) depend on inhibition of sodium channel or calcium channel, enhancement of gamma-aminobutyric acid-related (GABAergic) inhibition, and glutamate-mediated excitation blockage.7 However, ASMs are effective in 60%-70% of individuals and only suppress seizures but not the underlying epileptogenesis, resulting in an unchanged long-term prognosis.7 Apart from frequent adverse effects that limit tolerance, and thus, the efficacy of ASMs, approximately 30% of epileptic patients are resistant to treatment despite accurate diagnosis and carefully monitored drug levels.7 Epilepsy surgery appears as the most effective way to achieve long-term seizure remission, up to 30%-60%, for patients with focal epilepsy refractory to medications.1 Nonetheless, epilepsy surgery remains significantly underutilized due to the requirement for extensive evaluations and specialized expertise.8 Consequently, many novel treatments have emerged in the past decades, aiming to suppress underlying epileptogenesis and restore damaged neural circuits.

Stem cell therapy in epilepsy

Stem cells, defined as primal undifferentiated cells that retain the ability to differentiate into other cell types, have been attracting attention from the research society for over four decades.9 The first stem cell therapy in humans was reported in 1939 in a patient with aplastic anemia,10 then much later, stem cell therapy for epilepsy was first registered in 2008.11 In general, the therapeutic effects of stem cells are mediated through two mechanisms: (1) cell recruitment and differentiation into damaged tissue cell types, and (2) secretion of bioactive molecules such as growth factors and anti-inflammatory agents within extracellular vesicles (EVs), also known as a bystander effect.12 There are multiple types of stem cells studied, including embryonic stem cells (ESCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs), neural stem cells (NSCs), and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs).12 Among all, MSCs are frequently used as they are conveniently harvested from the umbilical cord, bone marrow, or adipose tissue, have a multipotent nature, and can effectively modulate immune responses.12

Preclinical studies reveal that stem cell transplantation can reduce spontaneous recurrent seizures, attenuate cognitive and behavioral deficits, reduce neuroinflammation, and restore endogenous neurogenesis (Tables 1 and 2). Three key mechanisms underlie seizure reduction by stem cells: inhibitory interneuron engraftment, restoration of normal neurogenesis, and immunomodulation through bystander effects. First, engraftment of stem cell-derived inhibitory GABAergic interneurons restores excitatory-inhibitory balance and suppresses seizures. Multiple types of stem cells can differentiate into GABAergic interneurons, including ESCs, MSCs, NSCs, and iPSCs.13–20 This hypothesis is elegantly demonstrated by engineering iPSCs to express a designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) by CRISPR/Cas9, enabling precise control of the grafts.20 Graft silencing by DREADD activation increases spontaneous recurrent seizures and impairs memory, while DREADD deactivation restores seizure control and memory, showing the direct regulatory role of grafted interneurons. The inhibitory synapse formed by the grafts makes endogenous neurons less prone to hyperexcitation. Secondly, stem cells restore endogenous neurogenesis, spatial organization, and metabolism of the epileptic brain. Chronic epilepsy leads to the disorganization of neural progenitor cells (NCPs), subsequently causing ectopic migration and maturation into hyperexcitable granule cells at the hilus of the dentate gyrus, contributing to spontaneous recurrent seizures.21 Transplanted stem cells form inhibitory synapses with these adult-born granule cells and also attenuate NPC disorganization and ectopic migration.21 In addition, transplantation of umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) increases bilateral hippocampal glucose metabolism although no significant difference in seizure frequency is observed at 8 weeks post-treatment, suggesting a potential role in repairing epileptic networks.22 Preservation of hippocampal neuron density, morphology, and function is also observed.16,17,23–26 Lastly, stem cells modulate neuroinflammation and promote neuroregeneration through bystander effect. Many preclinical studies report therapeutic benefits albeit no differentiation or minimal engraftment of stem cells.22,23 Transplanted stem cells secrete neuroprotective cytokines such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), neurotrophins (NTs), amphiregulin, and osteoprotegerin.17,23 Decreased oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines are observed along with increased antioxidant enzymes and anti-inflammatory cytokines.17 The bystander effect is best demonstrated by in vitro studies co-culturing with stem cells in separate chambers and not directly contacting the neurons, still preserving therapeutic benefit.23,24 In vivo studies using condition media, stem cell lysates and stem cell-derived EVs also show similar results.27–29

Stem cell therapy has shown promising outcomes, comparable to or exceeding that of ASMs while maintaining a favorable safety profile.30 However, many factors must be considered to optimize treatment efficacy including but not limited to: sources and types of stem cells, donor species, transplantation route, and dose regimen. ESCs harvested from medial ganglionic eminence (MGE), caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE), and ventral mesencephalon (VM) show markedly different outcomes.13,31 Similarly, NPCs from pigs and humans are more effective than NPCs from rats, even when transplanted into epileptic rats.31 Some transplantation sites, such as the hippocampus and subthalamic nucleus, result in better outcomes than others.31,32 While the effects are dose-dependent with a broad therapeutic window, the long-term safety profile must be carefully evaluated.30 Notably, transplantation of human ESC-derived interneuron progenitors in pilocarpine-induced status epilepticus (SE) mouse model leads to tumor-like NSC clusters in 18%-29% of animals as early as 12 weeks post-treatment.15 Additional investigations are required to enhance the safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy in epilepsy.

Despite the significant strides in preclinical research, translating stem cell therapy for epilepsy in the clinic remains in its infancy, with only a few clinical applications reported to date (Table 3). Early clinical reports have documented the transplantation of human ESCs and MSCs in pediatric patients with epilepsy secondary to cerebral palsy. Remarkably, the treatment leads to improvement in gross motor function and language expression, with up to 30% complete seizure remission.33 Human MSC transplantation, by intravenous and intrathecal routes, in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy results in 50%-76.5% becoming ASMs responders and 30%-40% having complete remission.34–37 The patients also show improvement in psychological and cognitive functions with only mild transient side effects. In a preliminary report, transplantation of human iPSCs-derived GABAergic interneurons in patients with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy leads to 90% seizure reduction and a 50%-100% decrease in awareness-impaired seizures.38 However, this clinical trial employs interneurons differentiated from stem cells but not naive stem cells themselves, thereby requiring a tedious differentiation process prior to transplantation. Currently, other clinical trials are underway, including intranasal administration of iPSC-derived exosomes in refractory focal epilepsy (NCT05886205), human UC-MSC-derived secretome in drug-resistant epilepsy (NCT06638970), and ASC transplantation as an adjunct to deep brain stimulation (NCT06280092).39–41 Further clinical trials are required to validate safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy for epilepsy.

New developments in stem cell-based approaches

Recent advances in stem cell-based therapy have opened promising avenues for the treatment of epilepsy. Genetic engineering techniques, such as CRISPR/Cas9, allow for the modification of stem cells to secrete therapeutic agents, such as GABA and neurotrophic factors, or differentiation into favorable cells.42–45 For example, MSCs engineered to silence Hes1 expression significantly enhance GABAergic differentiation and improve functional outcomes.16 Adenosine kinase knockdown increases activity-dependent adenosine secretion in stem cells, making it a promising target for regulated treatment.44,46 Genetic engineering also offers a promising solution to limited passages of primarily isolated MSCs, by differentiating iPSCs into MSCs.47

Cell-free therapy has recently emerged as a particularly promising and rapidly evolving area of research. EVs serve as natural mediators of intercellular communication, positioning them as a promising platform for targeted delivery of neuroprotective agents and small-molecule drugs.48 Stem cell-derived EV therapy can be as effective as stem cell transplantation, highlighting the bystander effect as a primary mechanism of action.27–29 For instance, EVs derived from ASCs contain miR-23b-3p, which suppresses neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis.49 ESC-derived EVs loaded with GABA have been shown to reduce seizures and improve depressive behavior in epilepsy mice.29 In addition, EVs can be delivered via intranasal route, offering a convenient and noninvasive route for brain-targeted treatment.29 Although EVs are safer and easier to manufacture compared to stem cells, challenges remain in their molecular characterization, standardization, and scalability.50–52

Stem cells can also be used to model epilepsy for precise drug screening. Over 10 monogenic epilepsies have been modeled using human iPSCs.53–57 The iPSCs can be conveniently derived from patient skin or blood, retaining disease-specific mutations, and differentiated into 2D cultures of neurons and glial cells. In addition, genetic engineering can introduce mutations into healthy iPSCs or correct them in patient-derived iPSCs to create isogenic controls.53,57,58 However, 2D cultures lack the spatial and cellular complexity of the human brain.59 Development of 3D brain organoids from iPSCs better mimics human brain structure and function more accurately, offering improved models for studying genetic/developmental epilepsy.53,59 Notably, brain organoids can model not only anatomical but also functional brain disorders, such as replication of epileptiform-like activity in post-stroke seizure and Rett syndrome, enabling high-throughput drug screening and precision testing for individual patients.60,61

Future perspectives

Although significant advances have been made in the field of stem cell therapy, its application in epilepsy remains underexplored. At the molecular level, a critical priority is to elucidate the primary mechanisms by which stem cells exert their therapeutic effects and to identify strategies for modulating and optimizing these mechanisms. For example, if the predominant therapeutic mechanism is stem cell engraftment and inhibitory synapse formation, strategies such as intracerebral transplantation and higher dosages may offer greater efficacy. Conversely, if the therapeutic benefit is primarily mediated through bystander effects, then noninvasive delivery routes or cell-free approaches may be more viable. Preclinical research must address key questions regarding the optimal type and source of stem cells, delivery routes, and dosing regimens. While higher cell doses may enhance therapeutic efficacy, caution is warranted as excessive dosing has been associated with potential adverse effects on the graft-surrounding tissue without increased engraftment.75,76 Therefore, identifying the optimal dose represents a critical objective for future research. Additionally, the long-term safety profile, including the risks of tumorigenicity and immune rejection, requires rigorous evaluation.62 Clinically, there is a need for large-scale trials to validate the efficacy and safety of stem cell therapy in patients with epilepsy. Moreover, complimentary instruments such as imaging or biomarkers for noninvasive monitoring of stem cell function become crucial.63 Another important consideration is the interaction between ASMs and transplanted stem cells, as emerging evidence indicates that ASMs may influence stem cell viability, differentiation, and function. Moving forward, future studies should aim to integrate stem cell therapy with current gold-standard treatments in a complementary, rather than competing, manner to optimize safety and efficacy for epileptic patients.

Conclusion

Emerging evidence indicates that stem cell-based therapy holds promise for epilepsy treatment, adjunct to conventional ASMs and surgery. Advances in genetic engineering, extracellular vesicle therapy, and patient-derived organoids enhance therapeutic efficacy and enable more personalized interventions. Despite these progresses, further research is necessary to determine the optimal treatment regimen as well as long-term safety in clinical application.
