ACCOMPLISHMENTS

Major goals of the project:
 Current and future high-power accelerators put severe requirements on materials used for
target and beam windows and target facilities have been recognized as a critical challenge in
development of future particle accelerators. In accelerators, window and target materials are
exposed to extreme conditions, which include bombardment with very high energy protons (1- 100
GeV) and thermomechanical shock waves. Radiation can cause direct damage in the material, and
it leads to production of transmutation products (especially helium), both phenomena having a
potential adverse effect on the stability and durability of the target/window material. At high
enough temperatures, He can aggregate to form gas bubbles, which in turn cause significant
dimensional changes (swelling), enable easy crack propagation, and eventually cause failure by
fracture. On the other hand, if the temperature is too low, radiation damage accumulates in the
form of internal defects (e.g., dislocations), leading to hardening and a decreased ductility of the
material.
 In this project, we will focus on beryllium since it is considered to be one of the candidate
materials for beam windows and targets in the next-generation proton accelerators, e.g., the Long
Baseline Neutrino Facility (LBNF). Radiation effects in Be have been studied in the context of
nuclear fusion reactor applications. However, key differences exist between reactor and accelerator
conditions, including neutron vs. proton irradiation, continuous vs. pulsed beam flux, much higher
energies of bombarding particles in accelerators, and higher operating temperatures for typical
reactors. For example, the impact of beam pulsing on the radiation damage and the He bubble
kinetics is largely unknown. While results obtained on Be from fusion research might not be
directly transferrable to understanding target materials, there is an opportunity to bring state-of-
the-art tools from materials research in nuclear reactors to aid design of target and beam window
materials in high-power accelerators.
 To this end, the overarching goal of this project are to develop an experimentally-validated
computational framework capable of predicting radiation damage evolution in beryllium relevant
to beam window and target conditions, focusing on He bubble formation and growth as a function
of irradiation temperature. Our model will be based on the cluster dynamics formalism, where size
distribution of defects and He bubbles is simulated as a function of time, temperature, and radiation
dose. Parameters for the model will be taken from published experiments and from high-fidelity
atomistic simulations proposed in this project. In addition, we will carry out a series of targeted
ex-situ and in-situ dual-beam experiments using low-energy protons to provide critical data for
validation of the model on the effects of radiation on He clustering, He bubble distribution, and
dislocation loop density/size in proton irradiated Be.

What was accomplished under these goals:

Overview:

Simulations:
 We have developed a multiscale model for evolution of He bubbles in irradiated Be. This
model is based on cluster dynamics (CD) and it predicts size distribution of He bubbles as a
function of temperature and radiation fluence. Parameters for the CD model were determined
based on atomistic simulations using quantum mechanical approaches and classical molecular
dynamics. The results of the model show a good agreement with available experiments. However,
literature on experimental studies that report bubble size distribution is still limited as the
experiments are challenging. One important prediction of the model is that there is a significant
density of small He bubbles that are not observable in traditional transmission electron microscopy
techniques, but which will contribute to radiation-induced phenomena, such as swelling or
embrittlement.
 We have developed an atomistic model to predict stability of He bubbles in Be and bubble
mobility. We found that He bubbles become unstable with respect to plastic deformation when the
He to vacancy ratio reaches 1.25, causing the bubbles to have irregular shape. At the temperature
increases, the dominant diffusion mechanism of He bubbles changes from surface to volume
diffusion, with a crossover at approximately 900 K.
 Toward the end of the project, we have focused on new materials for future beam windows
and target applications. Specifically, we have explored high-entropy alloys, which have been
identified as potential replacement materials for next-generation multi-MW accelerator facilities.

Experiments:
 This project focused on experimentally characterizing radiation damage in beryllium under
accelerator-relevant conditions, with particular emphasis on helium bubble formation as a function
of irradiation temperature. To this end, thin Be foils were irradiated with 2 MeV protons, and
extensive efforts were made to prepare electron-transparent specimens for microstructural
analysis. However, major challenges were encountered throughout: the delivered Be samples
deviated from specified dimensions and were nanocrystalline with significant texturing,
complicating both irradiation and characterization. Electropolishing of beryllium proved
unreliable despite extensive parameter optimization and consultation with experts, and alternative
sample preparation techniques were constrained by the toxicity of beryllium. Irradiation at elevated
temperatures was further hindered by the sample dimensions, which made it difficult to control
sample temperature, thermal gradients, and oxidation. Those, in some cases, led to contamination
of the irradiation chamber. Although these issues limited the extent of successful TEM analysis,
the work yielded critical insights into the practical limitations of beryllium preparation, highlighted
the variability in commercially available Be foils, and informed future experimental strategies for
validating radiation damage models in this material system.

A. Continuum model for He bubble distribution and radiation-induced swelling in beryllium

 Helium (He), as a transmutation product in beryllium (Be) via (n, α) reaction, can interact
with radiation-induced defects and form He bubbles. Evolution of these bubbles can result in
swelling and potentially embrittlement of Be. Here, we developed a cluster dynamics (CD) model
informed by atomistic simulations to explore He bubble evolution in Be. Our specific focus was
on the bubble size and density distribution as a function of radiation dose and temperature. We
also discussed the influence of irradiation flux and He production rate on the He bubble evolution,
and found these two factors to have opposite effects on the number density and the size of He
bubbles. Predictions of the model are compared to available experiments. Our models provide a
framework for predicting He evolution and the lifetime of Be components in compact reactors.
 CD model is a computational technique for predicting microstructural evolution and it is
frequently used to study defect clustering in materials under irradiation. In CD model, the system
is described as a mean-field population of clusters defined by their size distribution. The general
form of the CD model is:
 𝜕𝐶! (𝑡)
                                             = 𝑃({𝐶! }) + 𝑅({𝐶! })           (1)
 𝜕𝑡
where 𝐶! (𝑡) is the concentration of clusters of type x at time t, 𝑃({𝐶! }) is the production rate of
cluster of type x, which can be regarded as the source term due to irradiation. The production rate
of cluster x is highly dependent on the radiation conditions. In this work, the production rate of
defect clusters are extracted from the experimental conditions.
 In order to be able to simulate larger bubbles, we have implemented in CD a particle-size-
grouping technique [Xu et al. Metals 10, 1685 (2020)]. Specifically, the clusters are sorted into
dozens or hundreds of size groups, and each group covers a characteristic cluster size range.
Instead of tracking every cluster size x with its unique individual number of monomers, we track
the number of clusters inside each group.
 We used the developed CD model to investigate the average volume, the number density,
and the volume fraction of He bubbles, as a function of irradiation time and temperature. The
results are shown in Fig. 1. The experimental results for the same irradiation conditions are marked
with a black star, and they show a good agreement with results of simulations performed at the
same temperature (blue triangles). Fig. 1 also shows trends of the underlying defect kinetics. For
example, as irradiation temperature increases, the time needed to form observable bubbles become
shorter. This is reasonable when considering the higher He interstitial diffusivity at elevated
temperatures. In addition, from Fig. 1(a) one can see that the average volume of He bubbles will
saturate with the time of irradiation. This can be explained by the fact that the diffusion of Be
interstitials becomes significant above ~400 K, which can induce a competition between the
recombination reaction and the He bubble growth reaction (by adsorbing additional Be vacancies).

 Using our newly developed CD model, we have explored the impact of ion flux and He
production on the He bubble evolution in Be. Simulations were performed at 425K. As an example,
we illustrate the effect of ion flux in Fig. 2.

 In summary, we have developed a CD model to explore the evolution of He bubbles in Be
as a function of irradiation dose, irradiation temperature, ion flux, and He production. The
predictions of the CD model have been compared to experimental observations. Predictions of the
evolution of He bubbles that are large enough to be observed in experiments (size >2 nm) are in
relatively good agreement with the experimental results once possible irradiation heating effects
are included. The nominally room-temperature experimental observations agree with our
simulations at 425 K, and we expect that local heating under ion irradiation has increased the
nominal temperature of the experimental sample. We found that there is a threshold ion flux value
(5.56 × 1015 ion·m-2s-1 in our case), below which the defect density, especially the Be vacancy
density, is not high enough to create observable He bubbles. And once the flux is higher than the
threshold value, the final He bubble size is expected to become independent of the ion flux as the
He bubble size is dependent on temperatures. Regarding the He production rate, when it is high
enough, the formation of He bubbles is predicted to be inhibited due to the formation of penta-He
clusters, which will hinder the interaction between He interstitials and Be vacancies to form He
bubbles. In that case, He atoms produced due to transmutation would prefer to accumulate as He
interstitial clusters, instead of forming He bubbles. When the He production rate was reduced, the
average volume of He bubbles was also independent of the He production rate. Our work provides
a pathway for predicting He evolution under a wide range of conditions, facilitating the design of
reliable compact reactors. The results have been submitted for publication (Xi et al. He bubble
evolution in irradiated Be: atomistically informed cluster dynamics model).

B. Atomistic simulations of He bubbles in Be
 We have investigated fundamental properties of He bubbles in irradiated Be, including bubble
shape, stability, and diffusivity, using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In MD simulations,
positions and velocities of all atoms are tracked as a function of time and these variables evolve
according to the Newton’s equation of motion. Forces are determined based on an energy function,
called an interatomic potential. To study He and Be, we have developed a new machine learning
potentials based on the SNAP formalism. The potential accurately captures such properties as
elastic constants, surface energies of Be, formation energies of point defects, and formation
energies of He-vacancy clusters. The details have been described in [Xi et al. J. Nucl. Mater. 576,
154249, 2023].
 Calculated surface energies combined with the Wulff construction were used to determine the
equilibrium shape of He bubbles in Be (see Fig. 3). The ratio of semi-axes of the ellipsoid is
determined by the ratio of surface energies between {0001} and {10120}, which is around 0.676.

Figure 3. Equilibrium Wulff shape created using surface energies from the result of MD
simulation in Table 2 illustrated in the VESTA software. The ratio of semi-axes of the ellipsoid is
around 0.676.

 We have also calculated pressure that builds up in bubbles due to accumulation of He gas.
He is formed through transmutation reactions and because it is insoluable in Be, it binds with Be
vacancies, forming He bubbles. A large concentration of He atoms to Be vacancies in a bubble
will lead to a buildup of pressure. As shown in Fig. 4, at the He to vacancy ratio of ~1.25, the
pressure is large enough to cause puncturing of the bubble, leading to plastic deformation in the
surrounding Be through emission of dislocations.

Figure 4. (a) The pressure of systems that include a He bubble made up of 3452 Be vacancies. The
insert in (a) shows atomic structures of He bubbles with ratio of 0.2, 1.0, and 1.5 after relaxation
for 200 ps at 700 K, respectively. In the inserts, red points are the Be atoms and the blue ones are
He atoms. The punch-out dislocations are shown as lines and are determined using OVITO.

 The thermodynamic driving force for coalescence and growth of He bubbles in Be can be
captured by calculating the formation energy of bubbles (Fig. 5). The formation energy of He
bubbles was found to be always positive, which means that a bubble is always more stable than
isolated vacancies. At the same time, since the bubble formation energy per vacancy decreases
with an increasing bubble size, that means that while He bubbles prefers to attract vacancies in
order to grow, this attraction becomes weaker as the bubble grows. This trend is expected due to
surface energy effects (i.e., the Gibbs-Thompson effect) and due to the decreasing ability of the
vacancies to reduce the He pressure. Furthermore, all the curves appear to be leveling off,
suggesting that for large enough number of vacancies the energy per vacancy (and the energy to
add a vacancy) becomes nearly constant, as would be expected in the limit where surface energies
and He pressure effects are negligible. We also note that the slope of the curves is more negative
for higher He/V ratios, consistent with a larger contribution from the He pressure reduction for
larger He/V ratios. The ability of He (or any gas) to make adding vacancies to a bubble more
attractive is the fundamental energetics underlying the ability of gasses to stabilize cavities in many
metallic systems.

C. Experimental studies of radiation effects and He bubbles in beryllium
 The experimental objectives of this work were to irradiate beryllium to doses up to 1 dpa
using low energy protons (2 MeV) and characterize the formation of helium bubbles. To
accomplish this, thin, 3 mm discs of beryllium were to be irradiated at elevated temperatures (200
and 400 °C) and then thinned to electron transparency with a Struers TenuPol-5 twinjet
electropolisher. The polishing setup in the beryllium fume hood is shown in Fig. 7.

  Figure 7: Fume hood for beryllium electropolishing work with Struers electropolishing unit.

 Classic Focused Ion Beam (FIB) milling was avoided to prevent contamination of the FIB
chamber with Be particles, so the irradiated samples needed to be sized for TEM specimen holders.
The discs of S-65 grade beryllium were ordered from Materion, a typical supplier of beryllium
beam windows for Fermilab. Twenty 300 µm thick samples were ordered. After irradiation the
samples would have the first 15 µm electrochemically removed from the beam facing side before
being thinned from behind to electron transparency in order to have the transparent region in the
damage plateau region, as shown in Fig. 8. However, Materion only fulfilled 14 of the 20
purchased samples, and these were extremely think, 30 µm thick, not 300 µm. Materion refused
to fulfill the remainder of the order or supply the correct thickness of samples. While this meant
that samples would not need to be polished from the back only, it also caused delays as the samples
were too thin for the electropolishing sample holders. The samples also posed much greater
challenges in ensuring the temperature was well-maintained during irradiation.

 Figure 8: Bragg peak and damage profile of 2 MeV protons in beryllium from SRIM.

 Figure 9: a,c) optical and b,d) low magnification TEM images of electropolishing hole in
 surrogate aluminum.
 Due to the toxicity and the limited number of available Be samples, initial testing of the
electropolisher and electrolyte was performed on aluminum as a surrogate. Figure 9 shows optical
and low magnification TEM images of the surrogate aluminum near the electropolished
perforation. Although electropolishing was successful on aluminum, translating these results to
beryllium proved significantly more challenging. Using the same polishing conditions as a starting
point, tests at different voltages, flow rates, and temperatures were conducted, but no consistent
polish could be obtained. Several different electrolytes were attempted, and experts who had
published on electropolished beryllium samples, Dr. David Senor at PNNL and Dr. Slava

Kuksenko at UKAEA, were consulted, as well as the Struers applications team. They indicated
that even in their work, consistent electropolishing conditions could not be reliably established,
and the success rate for producing usable samples remained low. Figure 10 shows a sampling of
results from attempts on electropolishing beryllium for three different electrolytes over a range of
conditions, showing severe pitting and frequent “fissuring” of one side of the sample. As is visually
apparent, with these conditions it was not possible to achieve a smooth, thin region at a centrally
located hole. Despite extensive literature review and testing, the origin of the fissures, which were
absent in aluminum and other materials under similar conditions, remained unexplained.

 d,e,f) sulfuric acid in methanol, g,h,i) perchloric acid with 2-botoxy ethanol in ethanol.
 On a few occasions, electron transparent areas were achieved, and some example
micrographs are shown in Fig. 11. Here the uneven morphology of the transparent region is quite
apparent. Additionally, this demonstrated that, in contrast to the material used for beam windows,
the supplied beryllium was nanocrystalline with highly textured, columnar grains. This was
confirmed by X-ray diffraction as shown in Fig. 12. Figure 12 shows the comparison of bulk
beryllium window material sent by Fermilab collaborators and the thin samples, both of which

 Significant challenges were also encountered when attempting to irradiate the thin samples
at elevated temperature. Sample heating is provided by the 2MeV proton beam, so the sample stage
needs to be cooled to reach the target temperature. However, due to the small size of the samples,
thermocouples could only be placed in the sample holder about 1 mm away from the samples.
Therefore, adequate thermal contact needed to be maintained to ensure the film was at the same
temperature as the sample holder and thermocouples. Multiple sample holder designs were tested
to improve heat removal while maintaining sufficient beam current to achieve meaningful
irradiation doses. For example, a liquid indium metal pool was used but we were unable to separate
the beryllium from the solidified indium following irradiation. Indium leakage also occurred
during irradiation. A molybdenum-copper sandwich holder was most effective at heat removal due
to copper’s conductivity and the good clamping pressure. However, at elevated temperatures, the
clamping pressure caused the beryllium to adhere to the softened copper, making it impossible to
remove the thin samples without breaking them. A molybdenum-molybdenum sandwich was
another attempted design, with a small amount of partially cured silver paste to improve contact
without adhering the samples, which is depicted in Fig. 13. However, this thermal contact was
insufficient, such that the thermal gradient between the sample and thermocouples was higher than

anticipated during the irradiation. Because beryllium oxidizes rapidly above 600 °C, the
unexpected high sample temperature led to oxide dust sputtering from the samples, causing severe
contamination of the irradiation chamber. Most of the samples fully oxidized, as shown in Fig. 13.
Procedures for handling the beryllium before and after irradiation when contamination may have
taken place had been put in place with UW Environmental Health Services, as well as for the
electropolishing containment and protection. The chamber decontamination effort was a
significant challenge, as all surfaces had to be manually wet cleaned several times until no
beryllium could be detected, in accordance with these procedures. Due to the limited success in
achieving consistent electropolishing and the complications from beryllium contamination during
irradiation, progress was significantly hindered within the available timeframe.

D. Predictions of stability in high-entropy alloys
 Given the current limitations of beryllium windows, it will most likely be unsuitable for
next generation target environments. In addition, with the significant challenges encountered in
our project as detailed above, we investigated potential other beam window alloy systems. High
entropy alloys (HEAs) have demonstrated high strength and improved radiation tolerance, with a
nearly infinite compositional space to explore. Using computational methods such as CALPHAD,
coupled with basic metallurgy principles, a novel HEA series was designed for beam window
applications.
 One of the challenging aspects of HEAs is the vast compositional space available.
Therefore, three principal design criteria were used to narrow the scope of available elements.
First, the alloy should exhibit a large single-phase region. Many HEAs can form embrittling
intermetallic compounds, and it is important to avoid these to obtain good mechanical properties.
Practically, it is also beneficial to have a single-phase alloy to be able to isolate the effects of the
HEA matrix on irradiation damage resistance. Second, the density and atomic number of the
material were to be minimized. Thermal shock in the beam window is driven by the localized heat
deposition of the proton pulse, and this heat deposition, caused by electronic stopping of the

protons, is directly proportional to the number density and atomic average atomic number of the
material. This is the primary reason beryllium windows have been employed thus far and is an
important consideration. Finally, minimal activation under high energy protons is important for
general health and safety during maintenance and replacing windows.
 These criteria led to the selection of equimolar CrMnV as a starting point, which had
previously been confirmed to be a single-phase alloy over a large temperature range. Following
earlier studies, 7 at. % Ti was added in an effort to remove impurities (C, O, and N) from solid
solution, as they are known to embrittle BCC matrices. Al was then added to reduce the alloy
density and, with the final addition of Co, form a coherent ordered B2 phase. BCC-B2 alloys are
currently of substantial interest in an effort to replicate the success of two-phase nickel superalloys,
where the ordered phase strengthens the disordered matrix without ruining its ductility. The
concentration of Co was limited to 5 at. % in design due to the short-term activation of Co under
high energy protons, though in long-term Co is not activated more than any of the other constituent
elements. Figure 14 shows the phase fractions vs temperature for the senary alloy, showing the
large range of stable BCC-B2 phase field without formation of intermetallics. Each alloy
composition from the ternary through senary have been procured and will undergo characterization
and irradiation testing.

 Figure 14: Phase fractions of Al15Co4Cr34Mn24Ti8V33 alloy with BCC-B2 region.
