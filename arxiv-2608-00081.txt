Value distributions for read-once polynomials on finite fields
A. Yashunsky, D. Tabalin

Abstract
Consider read-once polynomial functions over a finite field of order k k , i.e., functions defined by expressions built from field addition, multiplication, and constants, in which every variable occurs at most once. Let p = ( p 1 , … , p k ) p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k}) be the distribution of the values of a read-once function on independent uniform inputs. We prove that for every k ≥ 4 k\geq 4 all such distributions belong to a body ℬ k \mathcal{B}_{k} , defined by the following relation on the sorted atoms p 1 ↓ ≥ ⋯ ≥ p k ↓ p_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq p_{k}^{\downarrow} of the distribution: ℬ k = { p : p k ↓ ≥ 1 − p 2 ↓ − ( 1 − p 2 ↓ ) k k − 1 } , \mathcal{B}_{k}=\left\{p:p_{k}^{\downarrow}\geq\frac{1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}-(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{k}}{k-1}\right\}, or equivalently, ℬ k = { p : 1 − p 2 ↓ − ( k − 1 ) ​ p k ↓ ≤ ( 1 − p 2 ↓ ) k } \mathcal{B}_{k}=\{p\colon 1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}-(k-1)p_{k}^{\downarrow}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{k}\} . Our main theorem is that this body is stable under the convolutions corresponding to both field operations. More generally, convolution for any quasigroup operation on k k points preserves ℬ k \mathcal{B}_{k} ; the multiplicative conclusion needs only an absorbing zero and a quasigroup operation on the nonzero elements. The body is full-dimensional and contains the uniform law and every point mass; its normalized volume is given by an exact one-dimensional integral, and the k k th root of that volume tends to 0.2183305369 ​ … 0.2183305369\ldots . The complete development, including the two stability theorems, the exact volume formula, and its sharp exponential rate, has been formalized in Lean 4.

Introduction

The initial motivation for this work was a simple question: how do
the laws of independent random elements of a finite field transform
under the field operations? If
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
are
independent, the laws of
X+Y
and
XY
are the additive and
multiplicative convolutions
\mu\oplus\nu
and
\mu\otimes\nu
, and a
basic program, going back to work of Yashunsky on operation-stable
polyhedra of distributions
[
1
,
2
]
, asks for regions of
the probability simplex that both convolutions preserve. Such stable
regions are the natural invariants of arithmetic computation with
independent inputs: whatever a computation built from
+
and
\times

does, its output law cannot leave the region.

The independence required at every operation arises naturally in a

read-once
arithmetic formula: a binary
\{+,\times\}
tree, with
variables and constants at the leaves, in which no input variable occurs
twice. Distinct subtrees then involve
disjoint variables, hence are independent when the inputs are sampled
independently, and the law at every gate is the additive or
multiplicative convolution of the laws below it. Any
convolution-stable region containing the laws of the inputs therefore
absorbs the output law of every read-once formula. Statements about
stable bodies thus become statistical statements about read-once
computations, provided that the uniform distribution — the law of a
single variable — belongs to the body. Traditionally, the central
question on read-once functions is whether a specific function, either Boolean
or arithmetic, has a read-once representation (see
[
3
,
4
]
,
for instance). Yet we believe our approach is of value as well,
contributing to a general understanding of how read-once functions work.

This paper constructs such a body for every order
k\geq 4
. It contains
all point masses and the uniform law (Proposition
2.1
),
so its stability bounds the value distributions of read-once polynomials
on independent uniform inputs, constants allowed
(Corollary
1.2
). For
k\geq 2
let

\psi_{k}(t):=\frac{1-t-(1-t)^{k}}{k-1},

(1)

sort the atoms of a law
p
on
k
points as

p_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq p_{k}^{\downarrow}
, and define

p\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{k}^{\downarrow}\geq\psi_{k}(p_{2}^{\downarrow}).

(2)

Our main result is the following stability theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(stability of the body)
.

Let
k\geq 4
. Additive convolution preserves
\mathcal{B}_{k}
on every finite
abelian group of order
k
. Multiplicative convolution preserves

\mathcal{B}_{k}
on every finite field of order
k
.

The headline claim of the abstract is its immediate corollary.

Corollary 1.2

(read-once value distributions)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field of order
k\geq 4
. The value distribution of
every read-once polynomial function over
\mathbb{F}
on independent uniform
inputs belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

Proof.

Induct on the formula tree. The law of a leaf is the uniform law (a
variable) or a point mass (a constant), and both lie in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
by
Proposition
2.1
. At every gate the two subtrees
involve disjoint variables, so their laws are independent and the output
law is their additive or multiplicative convolution, which remains in

\mathcal{B}_{k}
by Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Call a binary operation
\star
on a finite set
Q
a

quasigroup operation
if every left and right translation

x\longmapsto a\star x,\qquad x\longmapsto x\star a

is a permutation of
Q
. If
Q
has a distinguished element
0
, call
an operation
\diamond
an
absorbing-zero quasigroup operation
if

0\diamond x=x\diamond 0=0
and the restriction of
\diamond
to

Q^{\times}:=Q\setminus\{0\}
is a quasigroup operation. Equivalently, under
absorption, left and right translation by every nonzero element is injective
on
Q
.

Theorem 1.3

(quasigroup extension)
.

Let
Q
have order
k\geq 4
.

1.

Convolution with respect to any quasigroup operation on
Q

preserves
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

2.

Convolution with respect to any absorbing-zero quasigroup operation
on
Q
preserves
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

Consequently, an ordinary quasigroup addition and an absorbing-zero
quasigroup multiplication both preserve
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, with no compatibility
law between the two operations.

Theorem
1.1
extends to every order
k\geq 4
the main theorem
of
[
5
]
, which settles the 3-element case of this
program: the value distribution of a read-once polynomial over a
3-element field — the law of its output on independent uniform
inputs — satisfies

\max p_{i}-\min p_{i}\leq(\max p_{i}+\min p_{i})^{3}
, and the proof
exhibits a region, stable under both operations, whose membership
condition is exactly this inequality. The body recovers that region at

k=3
: unfolding (
1
) in (
2
) writes
membership in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
as

1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}-(k-1)p_{k}^{\downarrow}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{k}
, and for
k=3
,
eliminating the middle atom, this becomes

p_{1}^{\downarrow}-p_{3}^{\downarrow}\leq(p_{1}^{\downarrow}+p_{3}^{\downarrow})^{3}
. The extension
was never intended to be the naive one: the region defined by

p_{1}^{\downarrow}-p_{k}^{\downarrow}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{k}
itself is not preserved at
larger orders — already over
\mathbb{F}_{5}
it fails to be closed under
multiplicative convolution (Remark
7.2
) — so the
task was to find a definition of the body that works for every

k\geq 4
and matches the known region at
k=3
.

Although the proofs in
[
5
]
take clear advantage of the
very specific structure of a 3-element field, Theorem
1.3

makes precise that the present criterion is indifferent to the finer
algebraic properties of the carrier. The proof uses only the permutation
structure of the left and right translations, together with absorption at
zero in the multiplicative case. In particular, the group laws that
motivate the field formulation of Theorem
1.1
are not needed for
the stability argument itself.

The AI-generated proof is clearly distinct from the one
in
[
5
]
, and the body itself is not an obvious
generalization of the 3-element case. Yet some traits seem to be
inherited. The initial, non-AI attempts at a generalization were
rooted in the following observation. On the part of the simplex where
the zero element of the field carries the middle probability, so that

p_{2}^{\downarrow}
is its atom, the proved inequality reads exactly

p_{1}^{\downarrow}-p_{3}^{\downarrow}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{3}
, which may be rewritten as

\frac{p_{1}^{\downarrow}}{1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}}-\frac{p_{3}^{\downarrow}}{1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{2}.

(3)

The left-hand side is the spread of the conditional probabilities on
the multiplicative group of the field — a measure of non-uniformity
of the conditional law, approaching
0
as that law approaches the
uniform one. The closure of the 3-element body under field
multiplication of random variables on this part of the simplex is due
to the fact that the spread is submultiplicative with respect to
convolution of distributions on the multiplicative group. Hence the
attempts at generalizing the 3-element result to bigger fields focused
on finding a non-uniformity measure for the multiplicative group that
would be submultiplicative with respect to convolution, because the
spread itself no longer is once
k\geq 5
. The
paper
[
6
]
finds such a measure for arbitrary
k
, but
further progress stalled there.

To our surprise, a much simpler non-uniformity measure, introduced
in
[
7
]
, surfaces in the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and
lets one trace the parallels between the 3-element and the
k
-element
case. Write
n:=k-1
; over a field
\mathbb{F}
of order
k
this is the order
of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
. For a distribution

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
on
n
points, define
d_{0}(x)=1-n\min_{i}x_{i}
.
By
[
7
, Theorem 5]
, if
*
is the convolution of
distributions with respect to a quasigroup operation — precisely the
generality used in Theorem
1.3
— then

d_{0}(x*y)\leq d_{0}(x)\,d_{0}(y).

Through this inequality, a zero-anchored companion of the body is
closed under multiplicative convolution outright. For a law
x

on
\mathbb{F}
, write
x(0)
for its zero atom and, when
x(0)<1
, let
x^{\prime}
be
the conditional law on
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
; consider

\mathcal{Z}_{k}:=\Bigl\{x:\ \min_{g\neq 0}x(g)\ \geq\ \psi_{k}\bigl(x(0)\bigr)\Bigr\},

which for
x(0)<1
is the condition

d_{0}(x^{\prime})\leq(1-x(0))^{n}
. If
x
and
y
are the laws of independent
random variables, their product is zero with probability

z=1-(1-x(0))(1-y(0))
, and conditionally on being nonzero its law is

x^{\prime}*y^{\prime}
, the group convolution on
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
. Submultiplicativity
then closes the region in one line:

d_{0}(x^{\prime}*y^{\prime})\leq d_{0}(x^{\prime})\,d_{0}(y^{\prime})\leq(1-x(0))^{n}(1-y(0))^{n}=(1-z)^{n},

so multiplicative convolution preserves
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, with no
assumption on where the zero atom ranks among the others; the same
mechanism drives the multiplication-preserved families
of
[
5
]
. Dividing the membership condition

1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}-(k-1)p_{k}^{\downarrow}\leq(1-p_{2}^{\downarrow})^{k}
by
1-p_{2}^{\downarrow}
shows
that
\mathcal{B}_{k}
coincides with
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
on the part of the simplex
where the zero atom is the second largest. Additive convolution,
however, rules
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
out as the invariant: convolving with a
point mass permutes the atoms and moves the zero anchor (the uniform
law on
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
lies in
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
; its translate by a nonzero
element does not), so a region stable under both operations cannot be
anchored at the zero element. The body is exactly the sorted
transplant of the zero-anchored shape: anchored at the second and
smallest atoms, it agrees with
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
where the zero atom is
second largest and extends the same shape to the rest of the simplex
— closely matching how the preserved region of the 3-element case is
assembled from a zero-anchored family by the symmetries of the simplex.
Even the proof difficulties appear in the same places: under
multiplication the zero atom can only grow, so products drift toward
the part of the simplex where the zero atom is largest; there the
zero-anchored bound genuinely fails, and that is precisely the K-type
regime of the dichotomy of Section
4
.

The body is a full-dimensional region of the simplex containing the
uniform law and all point masses (Propositions
2.1

and
2.2
), and its boundary carries the one-parameter
sharp family
D_{k}(u)
of Section
2.4
, on which the
multiplicative estimates are exact. Writing
\mathfrak{v}_{k}
for its
normalized relative volume, one has an explicit one-dimensional integral
for
\mathfrak{v}_{k}
and the sharp exponential rate

\mathfrak{v}_{k}^{1/k}\to 0.2183305369\ldots

(Appendix
E
).

2.3
Distinguished laws and nonvacuity

Proposition 2.1

(base laws)
.

The body
\mathcal{B}_{k}
contains the uniform law and every point mass.

Proof.

A point mass has
p_{2}^{\downarrow}=p_{k}^{\downarrow}=0
. The uniform law satisfies

p_{k}^{\downarrow}=1/k\geq\psi_{k}(1/k)
because
\psi_{k}(t)\leq t
.
∎

The invariant is not merely nonempty: it occupies a full-dimensional region
of the probability simplex. The following quantitative certificate is
uniform in
k
and has a short machine-checkable proof.
The exact fraction and its sharp exponential scale are summarized immediately
afterward and proved in Appendix
E
.

Proposition 2.2

(geometric nonvacuity)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(1/k,\ldots,1/k)
be the uniform law and put

m_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}-\psi_{k}(1/k)=\frac{(1-1/k)^{k}}{k-1}=\frac{(k-1)^{k-1}}{k^{k}}>0.

Every probability vector
p
satisfying

\lVert p-\mathbf{u}\rVert_{\infty}<m_{k}/2
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
Consequently
\mathbf{u}
is a relative-interior point of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
in the probability simplex. More
quantitatively, if volume is normalized so that the whole
(k-1)
-simplex
has volume one, then

\operatorname{vol}_{k-1}(\mathcal{B}_{k})\geq\left(\frac{(k-1)^{k-1}}{2k^{k}}\right)^{k-1}>0.

(10)

Proof.

Formula (
4
) gives

|\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)|\leq 1
on
[0,1]
. If

\varepsilon=\lVert p-\mathbf{u}\rVert_{\infty}
, each sorted atom differs from
1/k

by at most
\varepsilon
. Hence

p_{k}^{\downarrow}\geq\frac{1}{k}-\varepsilon,\qquad\psi_{k}(p_{2}^{\downarrow})\leq\psi_{k}(1/k)+\varepsilon.

The first quantity is larger than the second when
2\varepsilon<m_{k}
.

For the volume claim, set
\lambda=m_{k}/2
and consider the homothetic simplex

(1-\lambda)\mathbf{u}+\lambda\Delta_{k-1}
. Every one of its points is
at sup-norm distance strictly less than
\lambda
from
\mathbf{u}
, so
this simplex lies in

\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Homothety by
\lambda
scales
(k-1)
-dimensional volume by

\lambda^{k-1}
, giving (
10
).
∎

The normalized-volume assertion in (
10
), including its
strict positivity, is machine-checked independently of the gate theorems; see
Appendix
A
. In particular, the formal result certifies the
relative volume fraction itself, not only the preceding set inclusion.

Writing

2.4
The sharp family

The boundary family will appear repeatedly. For
0\leq u\leq 1
, let

D_{k}(u):=\left(1-u,\frac{u+(k-2)u^{k}}{k-1},\underbrace{\frac{u-u^{k}}{k-1},\ldots,\frac{u-u^{k}}{k-1}}_{k-2\text{ entries}}\right).

(11)

Its low atoms are
\psi_{k}(1-u)
, so after sorting it lies on the boundary of

\mathcal{B}_{k}
whenever its displayed middle coordinate is the largest atom: the
second atom is then
1-u
, and the smallest atom equals
\psi_{k}(1-u)

exactly.

Define

\mathcal{H}_{k}(t):=1-t-(k-2)\psi_{k}(t)=\psi_{k}(t)+(1-t)^{k}.

(12)

This is the
complementary atom
of the sharp profile: it completes

and
k-2
copies of
\psi_{k}(t)
to a law, and it is the largest atom of
that profile precisely when
\mathcal{H}_{k}(t)\geq t
. In the coordinates of
(
11
), the middle entry of

is exactly

\mathcal{H}_{k}(1-u)
.

Lemma 2.4

(sharp-family inequality)
.

For
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathcal{H}_{k}(t)\leq t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\psi_{k}(\mathcal{H}_{k}(t))\leq\psi_{k}(t).

(13)

Proof.

Put
u=1-t
,
H=\mathcal{H}_{k}(t)
,
v=1-H
, and

\chi(x)=x-x^{n+1}
. The desired inequality is
\chi(v)\leq\chi(u)
,
while
H\leq t
is
v\geq u
. Let
\alpha_{0}=(n+1)^{-1/n}=1-t_{k}^{*}
. If

u\geq\alpha_{0}
, then
\chi
is decreasing on
[u,1]
, and the result is
immediate.

Suppose
0<u\leq\alpha_{0}
; the case
u=0
is trivial. Put

W(x)=(1-x)^{n+1}-1+(n+1)x,\qquad G(u)=\frac{W(H(u))}{u^{n+1}}.

The target is equivalent to
G(u)\geq n
, and it holds at

by
maximality of
\chi(\alpha_{0})
. With

\theta=(n-1)u^{n}
, direct differentiation yields

G^{\prime}(u)=-(n+1)u^{-n-2}\bigl(v^{n}(1+u\theta)-(1-u)\bigr).

(14)

It remains to prove the bracket nonnegative. Note

\theta\leq\theta_{0}:=(n-1)/(n+1)
and

. Taking logarithms reduces the sign to

L(u,\theta):=n\log\left(1-\frac{u(1+\theta)}{n}\right)+\log(1+u\theta)-\log(1-u)\geq 0.

For fixed

,
\partial_{\theta}L\leq 0
, hence

L(u,\theta)\geq L(u,\theta_{0})
. If

L_{0}(u)=L(u,\theta_{0})
, then
L_{0}(0)=0
and

L_{0}^{\prime}(u)=\frac{2n(n-1)u^{2}}{(1-u)(n+1-2u)(n+1+(n-1)u)}\geq 0.

Thus the bracket in (
14
) is nonnegative,

is
nonincreasing, and

.
∎

3
Quasigroup and addition closure for all

Let

have order
k\geq 4
and carry a quasigroup operation
\star
.
The field-addition case is obtained by taking

to be the additive group.
If the atom vectors of two laws are sorted as

define the anti-aligned and runner-up pairings

A(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}y_{k+1-i},\qquad B(x,y):=x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}+\sum_{i=3}^{k}x_{i}y_{i}.

(15)

Lemma 3.1

(quasigroup rearrangement)
.

If
p
is their convolution with respect to
\star
, then

p_{k}^{\downarrow}\geq A(x,y),\qquad p_{2}^{\downarrow}\leq B(x,y).

Proof.

Enumerate
Q
so that the first law has atoms
x_{i}
and the second has atoms

y_{j}
. For a fixed output and each left input, the relevant right input is
the unique solution of the corresponding quasigroup equation. As the left
input varies, these solutions form a permutation, so every convolution atom
is
\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{\pi(i)}
for a permutation
\pi
. The rearrangement
inequality
[
8
, Theorem 368]
gives the first bound.

For the second, suppose
\pi(1)\neq 1
. If
\pi(1)\neq 2
, exchange the
partners of position
1
and
\pi^{-1}(2)

(x_{1}-x_{\pi^{-1}(2)})(y_{2}-y_{\pi(1)})\geq 0
. Then exchange the partner
of position
2

if necessary. The remaining

. Only one output element can pair the carrier elements supporting
a selected

and

. Hence all but at most one convolution atom are
at most

, which proves the second-largest bound.
∎

The same cap that controls a member also controls the runner-up pairing.

Lemma 3.2

(pairing cap)
.

For
x,y\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, one has

.

Proof.

For a member
y
, sorting and membership give

The right side is convex in
y_{2}
on

\bar{t}_{k}

\bar{t}_{k}

throughout.
∎

We now prove the scalar comparison
A\geq\psi_{k}(B)
. For a sorted member

is replaced by its

its lower

\displaystyle=(w_{k-1}-w_{k})-\psi_{k}^{\prime}(w_{2})(w_{1}-w_{2}),

(16)

\displaystyle C_{i}(w)

\displaystyle=(w_{k+1-i}-w_{k})+\psi_{k}^{\prime}(w_{2})(w_{2}-w_{i}),\qquad 3\leq i\leq k,

Every

Lemma 3.3

(tangent bounds)
.

For members
x,y
,

\displaystyle A(x,y)-\psi_{k}(B(x,y))

\displaystyle\geq U_{1}:=x_{2}T_{y}+\sum_{i=3}^{k}x_{i}C_{i}(y),

\displaystyle A(x,y)-\psi_{k}(B(x,y))

\displaystyle\geq U_{2}:=y_{2}T_{x}+\sum_{i=3}^{k}y_{i}C_{i}(x).

Proof.

Concavity gives

\psi_{k}(B)\leq\psi_{k}(y_{2})+\psi_{k}^{\prime}(y_{2})(B-y_{2})\leq y_{k}+\psi_{k}^{\prime}(y_{2})(B-y_{2})
.
Expanding
A-y_{k}-\psi_{k}^{\prime}(y_{2})(B-y_{2})
by coordinates of

exactly
U_{1}
; the coefficient of
x_{1}
vanishes.
Interchanging

.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(single-member inequality)
.

Every sorted member
w
satisfies

F(w):=w_{2}T_{w}+\psi_{k}(w_{2})\Sigma C(w)\geq 0.

(19)

Proof.

Fix
t=w_{2}
, put
\psi=\psi_{k}(t)
and

, and make the
dependence on the remaining displayed coordinates explicit:

\Phi_{t}(u,v,z):=t\bigl[(v-z)-d(u-t)\bigr]+\psi\bigl[1-v-(k-1)z+d((k-1)t+u-1)\bigr].

Thus
F(w)=\Phi_{t}(w_{1},w_{k-1},w_{k})
. First hold

fixed. Lower each of

w_{3},\ldots,w_{k-2}
to

and transfer the removed mass to

. The middle coordinates do not otherwise occur in

\frac{\partial\Phi_{t}}{\partial u}=d(\psi-t)\leq 0,

Let

. After this first move the vector has

Now hold

and

fixed, lower every component of the block

together, and again transfer the removed mass to

to the mass-preserving one-variable path

Its derivative is

Hence lowering

to

, and

(20)

At the two possible upper endpoints, (

(1-kt)\bigl(\psi-t\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)\bigr)\geq 0\quad(kt\leq 1),\qquad-\psi(1-kt)\left(\frac{2}{k-2}+\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)\right)\geq 0\quad(kt\geq 1).

At the lower endpoint

Lemma 3.5

(crossing)

For all members

Lemma 3.6

(polynomial-band inequality)
.

Let
k\geq 4
be real and let

satisfy

2t+(k-2)\psi_{k}(t)\leq 1
. With
\psi=\psi_{k}(t)

\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)\bigl[t(2t-1)+(k-2)\psi(2t-\psi)\bigr]+\psi(1-k\psi)\geq 0.

Proof.

Write

and
\omega=s^{k-1}
. After multiplying the left
side of (

) by
(k-1)^{3}
, direct expansion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\omega)={}

\displaystyle(k-1)^{2}(k\omega-1)t(2t-1)

\displaystyle+(k-2)(k\omega-1)s(1-\omega)\bigl(2(k-1)t-s(1-\omega)\bigr)

\displaystyle+(k-1)s(1-\omega)\bigl((k-1)-ks(1-\omega)\bigr).

(22)

Bernoulli’s inequality and the cap hypothesis place

in the
polynomial band

\max(\omega_{L},\omega_{B})\leq\omega\leq\omega_{U},

(23)

where

\omega_{L}=1-(k-1)t,\quad\omega_{U}=\frac{s}{s+(k-1)t},\quad\omega_{B}=1-\frac{(k-1)(2s-1)}{(k-2)s}

(the subscripts stand for the lower and upper edges and for the edge

. Put

\gamma=(k-2)t^{2}-kt+1
. Since

\omega_{L}-\omega_{B}=\frac{(k-1)\gamma}{(k-2)s},

the lower edge is
\omega_{L}
when
\gamma\geq 0
and
\omega_{B}
when

\gamma\leq 0
.

The polynomial
\mathcal{P}
is cubic in
\omega
with negative leading

\mathcal{P}_{\omega\omega}
decreases in
\omega
. Thus
it is enough to establish concavity at the lower edge and nonnegativity
at the two edges. The needed signs follow from the following five exact
identities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\omega_{B})((k-2)s)^{3}

\displaystyle=-k(k-2)(k-1)^{3}t(t-1)^{2}(2t-1)(kt-1),

(24)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{P}_{\omega\omega}(t,\omega_{B})(k-2)s

\displaystyle=2(k-2)(k-1)(t-1)^{2}(2k^{2}t-k+2t-2),

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\omega_{U})(s+(k-1)t)^{3}

\displaystyle=-t^{2}(k-1)^{3}G_{U},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\omega_{L})

\displaystyle=t^{2}(k-1)^{3}G_{L},

(27)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{P}_{\omega\omega}(t,\omega_{L})

\displaystyle=2(k-1)(1-t)G_{C},

(28)

where

\displaystyle-G_{U}={}

\displaystyle kt\bigl(k(1-t-t^{2})-2t(1-2t)\bigr)+(1-2t)((1-t)^{2}+t^{2}),

\displaystyle(1-t)G_{L}={}

\displaystyle\gamma\bigl(k(1-t^{2})-1\bigr)+t(1-2t^{2}),

\displaystyle G_{C}={}

\displaystyle 3k\gamma+2(k^{2}+1)t-(k+2).

On the bulk region
\gamma\leq 0
, one has

, so
(
24
) and (
25
) have the required signs. The
upper identity is nonnegative throughout
[0,1/2]
, since

1-t-t^{2}\geq 1/4
and
2t(1-2t)\leq 1/4
.

On the corner region
\gamma\geq 0
, (
27
) is nonnegative by
direct sign inspection. For
G_{C}
, put

t_{0}=(k+2)/(2(k^{2}+1))
. If
t\geq t_{0}
, both terms in its displayed
decomposition are nonnegative. If
t\leq t_{0}\leq 1/k
, then

G_{C}\geq 2(k-1)-(k^{2}-2)t\geq 0;

the last inequality is equivalent to

3k(k^{2}-2k+2)\geq 0
. Hence
\mathcal{P}
is concave across the band and
nonnegative at both endpoints in either region. Therefore

\mathcal{P}(t,\omega)\geq 0
, proving (
21
).

The cap of Lemma

Lemma 4.2

Lemma 5.1

(multiplicative
