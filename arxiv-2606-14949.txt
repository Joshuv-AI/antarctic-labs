Initiation of Superradiance from Different Collective-Spin States Preprint: APS/123-QED
Adnan N. Alabbar, Zhenghao Zhang, Girish S. Agarwal

Abstract
Superradiance is an extensive cooperative spontaneous emission phenomenon. Some atomic collective-spin states exhibit it. However, distinct initial states differ in their decay dynamics. Dicke states | j , m ⟩ |j,m\rangle with different numbers of excitations n = m + j n=m+j have their peak emission intensity shifted in time depending on the number of excitations. Emission intensity in atomic coherent states depends on their polarization. Some specific states undergo a squeezing-controlled crossover, making the emission character dependent on the amount of squeezing in the state. We present detailed results on the superradiant dynamics of a representative selection of Dicke states. For large N, we are able to predict fairly accurately the pulse profile in each case using the mean-field approximation, an approach based on the Fokker–Planck Equation. We also present results on the intensity correlation function of the emission.

II
Method

We consider a system of identical
N
two-level atoms in a cavity with a leakage rate
2\kappa
. The atoms are on resonance with the cavity mode. The coupling,
g
, of the atom to the cavity is assumed to be much smaller than
\kappa
. In such a case, the dynamical behavior of the atoms is described in terms of the superradiance master equation (
44
) for the atomic density matrix,
\partial_{\tau}\rho=2\hat{J}_{-}\rho\hat{J}_{+}-\{\rho,\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\}
, where
\tau=\Gamma t
is the normalized time, and the dissipation rate is
\Gamma=g^{2}/\kappa
. Here,
\hat{J}
is the collective angular momentum operator for
N
two-level atoms with
j=N/2
being the collective spin of the system
8
;
30
;
31
. We thus work in a totally symmetric space. We will use the basis states
|j,m\rangle
, with
\hat{J}^{2}|j,m\rangle=j(j+1)|j,m\rangle
, and
\hat{J}_{z}|j,m\rangle=m|j,m\rangle
with
-j\leq m\leq+j
. Or, in the excitation number basis,
n=j+m
is the number of excited atoms.

Though the intensity gives us the mean emission rate, it does not determine the statistical characteristics of the emitted radiation. For this reason, we also calculate the normalized second-order correlation function (henceforth called the Glauber function)
22
;
21
. This quantity compares the probability of two-photon emission with the square of the one-photon emission probability. Therefore, it distinguishes collective states that may have similar total intensities but different fluctuation statistics. Values of the Glauber function
g^{(2)}<1
indicate antibunching and nonclassicality;
g^{(2)}=1
give us the Poissonian (or coherent state) reference; and
g^{(2)}>1
indicate bunching
43
;
4
;
35
. We have chosen to focus on
g^{(2)}(\tau)
because it separates states whose intensities look similar, such as the Central Dicke state (CDS) and the
x
-polarized ACS, and it reveals the enhanced pair-emission fluctuations of the RDS and SDS.

Dicke states are symmetric collective spin
j=\frac{N}{2}

N

|e\rangle_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\

|g\rangle_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\

Of these three, only the third cannot be written as a product state, and is therefore entangled:

\displaystyle|j,j\rangle

\displaystyle=|e,\dots,e\rangle=

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{2j}|e\rangle_{\ell},

(1a)

\displaystyle|j,-j\rangle

\displaystyle=|g,\dots,g\rangle=

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{2j}|g\rangle_{\ell},

(1b)

\displaystyle|j,0\rangle

\displaystyle=\binom{2j}{j}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{P}}\mathfrak{P}\left\{|e_{1},\dots,e_{j},g_{j+1},\dots,g_{2j}\rangle\right\}.

(1c)

where the permutation sum
\mathfrak{P}

Among the three, only

The ground-state (

The singly-excited state in figure

Higher excitations build up their intensity as they decay until they reach their peak, as seen in figure

The central Dicke state (CDS), (

Indeed, we notice that for the total intensity,
\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}|j,m\rangle=\Big[j(j+1)+\frac{1}{4}-\big(m-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{2}\Big]|j,m\rangle
. This tells us that the greatest emission intensity happens for
m=1/2
for odd number of atoms
\Big(I(0)=\frac{N(N+2)+1}{4}\Big)
; or
m=0,1
for even
N

\Big(I(0)=\frac{N(N+2)}{4}\Big)
.

Let us write our Dicke states in the excitation number
n=j+m

|j,m\rangle\equiv|j,n-j\rangle
.
Since we have
j
-excitations initially for
|j,0\rangle
, the upper limit to the populations will be
P_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,j}=1
, so
n=j=\frac{N}{2}
is the only term that survives.
The populations then become

;
7
:

\displaystyle P_{n}(\tau)=C_{n,j}(\tau)=\left[\displaystyle\prod_{r=n+1}^{j}\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2\Gamma}\right]\sum_{m=n}^{j}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{m}\tau/\Gamma}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=n\\
\ell\neq m\end{subarray}}^{j}\frac{\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{m}}{2\Gamma}},

(2)

we note that the terms
\frac{1}{2\Gamma}
in the products cancel exactly, and may be dropped.
We also see that even though we have the degeneracy
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{N-n+1}
, states above
n=j
are not occupied, and therefore, there are no higher order poles.
Consequently, we have no polynomial-exponential terms.
These terms appear in the general transfer coefficients
C_{n,k}(\tau)
when the initial excitation number satisfies
k>j

\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{N-n+1}
.

We finally notice that
P_{n}(\tau)
is simply the transfer coefficients

which is the probability that a system starting in state

n

\tau

The dipole moment vanishes, so we conclude with the CDS intensity:

I(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{j}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2\Gamma}\left[\prod_{r=n+1}^{j}\lambda_{r}\right]\sum_{m=n}^{j}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{m}\tau/\Gamma}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=n\\
\ell\neq m\end{subarray}}^{j}\!(\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{m})}.

(3)

III.1
Mean-Field Solution

For a strong fit at the center (where
x_{0}\equiv n_{0}/N\approx 1/2
), we can use the density function
W(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
. Then, the drift velocity will be
d(x)=\frac{dx}{d\tau}=-2Nx(a-x)
. We separate the variables and integrate both sides to get:

x(\tau)=\frac{ax_{0}e^{-2(N+1)\tau}}{a-x_{0}(1-e^{-2(N+1)\tau})},

(4)

where
a=1+1/N
, just as in (
65
). Finally, with the help of:

\frac{A\cosh{x}+B\sinh{x}}{\sqrt{A^{2}-B^{2}}}=\cosh{\left(x+\text{Arctanh}\Bigg\{\frac{B}{A}\Bigg\}\right)},

(5)

and
W(x,\tau)=\delta(x-x(\tau))
, the mean field intensity is found to be:

\displaystyle I(\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2\Gamma}\int_{0}^{1}dx\lambda(x)W(x,\tau)=\frac{\lambda(x(\tau))}{2\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+1)^{2}x_{0}(a-x_{0})}{\left(ae^{(N+1)\tau}-2x_{0}\sinh((N+1)\tau)\right)^{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+1)^{2}}{4}\text{Sech}^{2}\{(N+1)(\tau-\tau_{D})\},

(7)

where
\tau_{D}=\frac{1}{2(N+1)}\ln\left(\frac{2\Gamma n_{0}^{2}}{\lambda_{n_{0}}}\right)
is the pulse-delay, and
n_{0}
is the initial number of excitations.
Although this approximation is better near the center
x_{0}\approx 1/2
, the numerical results show that the result has an increased, but reasonable, error for low and high excitations. The mean field intensity vs the analytical solution is shown in Figure

IV.1
Analytical Solution

For the purposes of this article, we will call the eigenstates of the
\hat{J}_{x}
operator with
\hat{J}_{x}|j,n-j\rangle_{x}={n-j}|j,n-j\rangle_{x}
, the Rotated Dicke States (RDS).
Since
\hat{J}_{x}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{J}_{y}}\hat{J}_{z}e^{+i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{J}_{y}}
, we can write
|j,n-j\rangle_{x}\equiv\hat{R}_{y}(\pi/2)|j,n-j\rangle=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{J}_{y}}|j,n-j\rangle
.
We expand the RDS in terms of Dicke states using the Wigner small-d matrix elements
(
41
, eq. (3.203))
:

|j,0\rangle_{x}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}d_{n-j,0}^{(j)}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)|j,n-j\rangle,

(9)

with
r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
r\in[\textbf{max}\{0,n-n^{\prime}\},\textbf{min}\{n,n^{\prime}\}]
. The notation can be simplified through:

\displaystyle d^{(j)}_{n-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{j!}{2^{j}}\sum_{r}(-1)^{n-j+r}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\sqrt{(n)!(N-n)!}}{(j-r)!(N-n-r)!\,r!\,(n-j+r)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n/2}}{2^{j}}\sqrt{\binom{n}{n/2}\binom{N-n}{(N-n)/2}}

(10)

Note that if
n
is odd, then
d_{n-j,0}^{(j)}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=0
. Clearly, then, the surviving initial population of the even
n^{\text{th}}
level is:

P_{n}(0)=\left|d^{(j)}_{n-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{n!(N-n)!}{2^{N}\left[\Big(\frac{n}{2}\Big)!\Big(\frac{N-n}{2}\Big)!\right]^{2}}.

(11)

However, since the central RDS now also occupies levels
k>j
, its decay paths can contain both members of the degenerate decay rates. The transfer coefficients therefore contain repeated poles. This is why the simple-pole expression used for the CDS is insufficient, and we use the repeated-pole formula in Eq. (
51
).

The intensity of the pulse peaks at
\tau=0
for the central RDS, but the pulse becomes broader and its peak is halved:

\displaystyle\tau^{\text{RDS}}_{1/2}=\frac{2\ln(1+\sqrt{2})}{N}=2\tau^{\text{Dicke}}_{1/2};

(12a)

\displaystyle I^{\text{RDS}}(0)=\frac{N(N+2)}{8}=\frac{1}{2}I^{\text{Dicke}}(0).

(12b)

As for the rest of the RDS, the general formula is
I_{n}(0)=\frac{N(N+1)-2n(N-n)}{4}
, and the intensity plots are shown in figure
4
.

The time-dependent intensity for the central RDS can be found from the transfer coefficients
C_{n,k}(\tau)
as defined in the appendix. For a decay path from
k
to
n
, with
0\leq n\leq k\leq N
, we define the rate multiplicity:

\nu_{n,k}(p)=\sum_{q=n}^{k}\delta_{\lambda_{q},\lambda_{p}},\qquad n\leq p\leq k,

(13)

where the Kronecker delta
\delta_{\lambda_{q},\lambda_{p}}=1
if
q=p
or
q=N-p+1
, and vanishes otherwise. We also define the reduced pole denominator:

D_{n,k}(p)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=n\\
\lambda_{q}\neq\lambda_{p}\end{subarray}}^{k}(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{p}),

(14)

and the reduced reciprocal pole sum:

S_{n,k}(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=n\\
\lambda_{q}\neq\lambda_{p}\end{subarray}}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{p}},

(15)

in the convention that empty products are taken to be one, and empty sums are taken to be zero.

Since
\lambda_{p}=\lambda_{N-p+1}
, the transfer coefficients may contain second-order poles. For the states considered here, no pole is higher than second order, and therefore the inverse-Laplace transform can be written as:

\displaystyle C_{n,k}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{r=n+1}^{k}\lambda_{r}\right)\Bigg[\sum_{p=n}^{k}\bigl(2-\nu_{n,k}(p)\bigr)\frac{e^{-\lambda_{p}\tau/\Gamma}}{D_{n,k}(p)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=n}^{k}\bigl(\nu_{n,k}(p)-1\bigr)\frac{e^{-\lambda_{p}\tau/\Gamma}}{D_{n,k}(p)}\left(\frac{\tau}{\Gamma}-S_{n,k}(p)\right)\Bigg].

(16)

And then, the total intensity (as seen in figure
4
) is found from
I(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2\Gamma}\sum^{N}_{k=n}P_{k}(0)C_{n,k}(\tau)
.

I_{n}(0)=\frac{N(N+1)-2n(N-n)}{4}
, as indicated in the figure.

IV.2
Mean-Field Solution

The decay shape of the continuum can be determined approximately from the mean-field approach. We see that
\lambda(x)=2\Gamma N^{2}x(a-x)
, and from (
11
) and Stirling’s approximation
\Big(n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\Big)
, we can simplify the population for large
N
:

P_{n}(0)=\frac{4^{\frac{n}{2}}4^{\frac{N-n}{2}}}{2^{N}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}n}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}(N-n)}}=\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{n(N-n)}},

(17)

and
W(x,0)=jP_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}}
. Then, we find that
x(\tau;x_{0})=\frac{ax_{0}e^{-2(N+1)\tau}}{a-x_{0}(1-e^{-2(N+1)\tau})}
by solving the drift equation. This gives us the intensity:

\displaystyle I(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Gamma}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{0}\lambda(x(\tau;x_{0}))W(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+1)^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx_{0}}{\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}}\,\frac{e^{-2(N+1)\tau}x_{0}(a-x_{0})}{\left(a-x_{0}+x_{0}e^{-2(N+1)\tau}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+1)^{2}e^{-2(N+1)\tau}}{2(e^{-2(N+1)\tau}+a-1)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\sqrt{a}-\frac{\sqrt{a}+2\sqrt{e^{-2(N+1)\tau}+a-1}}{(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{e^{-2(N+1)\tau}+a-1})^{2}}\right],

(18)

where the Stieltjes transform was used:

\displaystyle\mu(dx)=\frac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}};

(19a)

\displaystyle S_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mu(dx)}{\alpha-\beta x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}};

(19b)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\mu(dx)}{\alpha-\beta x}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}S_{0}(\alpha,\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}.

(19c)

Note that as
a\rightarrow 1
, we regain the Hyperbolic Secant Squared pulse
I(\tau)=\frac{N^{2}}{8}\text{Sech}^{2}(j\tau)
.
This mean-field approximation overshoots the exact results by
\frac{2}{N+2}
.
Figure

The fit improves with increasing system size, with the NRMSE decreasing from
0.13
at

to
0.05
at
N=200
, consistent with the expected large-

validity of the mean-field approximation.
Then, the pulse-width for the central RDS is
\tau_{1/2}^{\text{RDS}}=2\tau_{1/2}^{\text{Dicke}}=2\ln(1+\sqrt{2})/N
.

V
States from the Squeezed Bath:

Squeezed Dicke States

V.1
The Steady State Solution

Next, we consider a class of states which we will call Squeezed Dicke states (SDS). These are obtained by tilting the RDS, and are defined for even values of
N
by:

|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{e^{j\delta}}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})}}e^{\delta\hat{J}_{z}}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{J}_{y}}|j,0\rangle\equiv|j,0\rangle_{x}^{\delta},

(20)

with angle
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\ln(\tanh r)
, where
r
is the squeezing parameter, and the moment function is defined from:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}_{q}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(z\partial_{z}\right)^{q}\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\ell^{q}z^{\ell}\Big|d_{2\ell-j,0}^{j}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\ell^{q}z^{\ell}\frac{(2\ell)!(N-2\ell)!}{2^{N}(\ell!)^{2}((j-\ell)!)^{2}}.

(21)

These steady states of the squeezed bath master equation
3
;
1
;
49
are the vacuum states of the operator:

\Big(\hat{J}_{-}\cosh r+\hat{J}_{+}\sinh r\Big)|\Psi_{0}\rangle=0.

(22)

Agarwal and Puri
3
;
1
have shown that the SDS can be produced from the interaction of qubits with a broadband squeezed bath.

We may expand the state
|j,n-j\rangle_{x}^{\delta}
in terms of Dicke states
|j,n-j\rangle
with amplitudes
c_{n}
to find that they vanish for odd
n
, but for even
n

1
:

c_{n}=\frac{e^{n\delta}d^{(j)}_{n-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})}},

(23)

and
P_{n}(0;\delta)=|c_{n}|^{2}
. The superradiant pulse is highly dependent on the initial squeezing parameter. Using
r
as a knob, we realize that as it increases, the atomic system comes to resemble the RDS. In fact, we can define a crossover between the shape of the superradiant intensity being that of the weakly squeezed bath, to a fully saturated system
r\rightarrow\infty
that becomes the RDS (when
\tanh(r)\rightarrow 1
). The relation between the populations is found to be:

P_{n}(0;\delta)=\frac{e^{2n\delta}}{\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})}P_{n}^{\text{RDS}}(0),

(24)

where
P_{n}^{\text{RDS}}(0)=\left|d^{(j)}_{n-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right|^{2}
. Finally, Figure
9
, (solid curves) show the intensity, normalized by the initial height:

I(0;\delta)=\frac{2(N+1)\mathfrak{Q}_{1}(e^{4\delta})-4\mathfrak{Q}_{2}(e^{4\delta})}{\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})}.

(25)

V.2
Squeezing-Controlled Crossover

Now, it can be seen that the SDS are
\delta
-tilted RDS states. Then, to any precision, we can see how saturated the states are, and how close we are to the RDS through the crossover
r_{c}=\text{arctanh}\{e^{2\delta_{c}}\}
.

First, let us define the intensity ratio:

R(N,r)=\frac{I_{\text{SDS}}(0;\frac{1}{2}\ln(\tanh r))}{I_{\text{RDS}}(0)}.

(26)

If we take the large
N
-limit, the Mehler–Heine limit gives us
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}P_{N}(\cos(z/N))=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}P_{N}(1-\frac{z^{2}}{2N^{2}})=\mathfrak{I}_{0}(z)
, where
\mathfrak{I}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{z\cos\phi}\cos(n\phi)d\phi
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of integer-order
n
. Then, we see directly that
\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})\rightarrow e^{N\delta}\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)
. Finally, since
\mathfrak{Q}_{q}(e^{4\delta})=(e^{4\delta}\partial_{e^{4\delta}})^{q}\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}_{1}(e^{4\delta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{N}{4}e^{N\delta}\left[\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)+\mathfrak{I}_{1}(N\delta)\right];

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}_{2}(e^{4\delta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{N^{2}}{16}e^{N\delta}\left[\frac{3}{2}\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)+2\mathfrak{I}_{1}(N\delta)+\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{I}_{2}(N\delta)\right].

Substitute these values into (
25
) with
I_{\text{RDS}}(0)=\frac{N(N+2)}{8}
into (
26
) to get the asymptotic limit:

R_{\infty}(\delta)=1-\frac{\mathfrak{I}_{2}(N\delta)}{\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)}.

(27)

Now, we define a proximity parameter
\zeta\in[0,1)
such that
R_{\infty}(\delta_{c})=\zeta
. We can precisely say how close the SDS have come to the RDS limit. Since
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\ln(\tanh(r))
, we have
\delta<0
for finite
r
,
\delta\rightarrow 0^{-}
as
r\rightarrow\infty
, such that
N\delta_{c}
remains finite. Now, let us define the ratio to be found numerically
\delta_{c}\sim-\frac{a_{\infty}}{4N}
. Then
a_{\infty}=4R_{\infty}^{-1}(\zeta)
, and from the definition of
\delta
, and using
e^{-a_{\infty}/N}\approx 1-\frac{a_{\infty}}{N}
, we get a formula for the crossover:

r_{c}(N;\zeta)=\text{arctanh}\sqrt{1-\frac{a_{\infty}}{N}}.

(28)

For example, if we were to choose
\zeta=90\%
then
a_{\infty}=3.843
, and choosing
\zeta=98\%
yields
a_{\infty}=1.603
. The phase diagram is displayed in Figure

V.3
Mean Field Approximation

Starting from (
24
), and that for this state as well, we also have
\Delta n=2
, and therefore
N\Delta x=2
. The initial continuum density will be
W(x,0)=jP_{n}(0;\delta)
:

W(x,0)=\frac{Ne^{2N\delta x}\left|d^{(j)}_{Nx-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right|^{2}}{2\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})}.

(29)

Let us also take the central-binomial Stirling approximation we used for RDS with

\left|d^{(j)}_{Nx-j,0}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right|^{2}\approx\frac{2}{\pi N\sqrt{x(1-x)}}
and from the large-
N
limit we used before, we write
\mathfrak{Q}_{0}(e^{4\delta})\approx e^{N\delta}\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)
. Then:

W(x,0)=\frac{e^{N\delta(2x-1)}}{\pi\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)\sqrt{x(1-x)}}.

(30)

From the probability conserving equation, we have
W(x,\tau)=W(x_{0},0)|\frac{dx_{0}}{dx}|
. The mean field density will be given by:

W(x,\tau)=\frac{\exp\left\{N\delta\left(\frac{2xe^{N\tau}}{e^{-N\tau}+2x\sinh(N\tau)}-1\right)\right\}}{\pi\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)[e^{2N\tau}+2x\sinh(N\tau)]\sqrt{x(1-x)}}.

(31)

Then, the mean field intensity will be:

\displaystyle I(\tau;\delta)

\displaystyle=N^{2}\int_{0}^{1}dxx(a-x)W(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=N^{2}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{0}x(\tau)[a-x(\tau)]W(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(N+1)^{2}e^{-2(N+1)\tau}}{\pi\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx_{0}}{\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}}\frac{e^{N\delta(2x_{0}-1)}x_{0}(a-x_{0})}{[a-x_{0}(1-e^{-2(N+1)\tau})]^{2}}

(32)

Let us separate the solutions into the three regimes:

\displaystyle I_{\text{Strong}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{8}\text{Sech}^{2}(j\tau).

(33a)

\displaystyle I_{\text{Inter}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+1)^{2}}{4\mathfrak{I}_{0}\sinh^{2}((N+1)\tau)}

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[\frac{2\sqrt{N+1}\cosh{(N+1)\tau}}{\sqrt{N+e^{2(N+1)\tau}}}\left[\mathfrak{I}_{0}+2\mathfrak{P}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{N+1}}{\left[N+e^{2(N+1)\tau}\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times[N\cosh((N+1)\tau)+e^{(N+1)\tau}]\left[\mathfrak{I}_{0}+2\mathfrak{P}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2(N+1)}{\left(N+e^{2(N+1)\tau}\right)}\mathfrak{P}_{1}-\mathfrak{I}_{0}\Bigg].

(33b)

\displaystyle I_{\text{Weak}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{N+1}{4\delta}e^{-2(N+1)\tau},

(33c)

where
\mathfrak{I}_{0}(N\delta)
has been defined, and the
\mathfrak{P}_{p}(a,N,\tau,\delta)
function for
p=0,1,2,\ldots
is defined as follows:

\mathfrak{P}_{p}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{p}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{a-1+e^{-2Na\tau}}}{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{a-1+e^{-2Na\tau}}}\right)^{m}\mathfrak{I}_{m}(N\delta).

(34)

VI
Atomic Coherent States

VI.1
Introduction

Atomic Coherent States
10
;
36
are product states of identical single-atom spinors. When written in the Dicke basis, they become binomially-weighted superpositions of Dicke states:

|\theta,\phi\rangle=\prod_{\ell}|\theta_{\ell},\phi_{\ell}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}C^{N}_{n}(\theta,\phi)|j,n-j\rangle,

(35)

where
C_{n}^{N}(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}\cos^{N-n}(\theta/2)\sin^{n}(\theta/2)e^{-in\phi}
. The six axial states defined on the Bloch sphere are specific polarizations which are denoted by
|\pm x,y,z\rangle
. We adopt the convention for one atom:

\displaystyle|\theta,\phi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|+z\rangle

\displaystyle+e^{-i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|-z\rangle

(36a)

\displaystyle|+z\rangle=|0,0\rangle=|g\rangle,

\displaystyle|-z\rangle=|\pi,0\rangle=|e\rangle;

(36b)

\displaystyle|+x\rangle=|\pi/2,0\rangle,

\displaystyle|-x\rangle=|\pi/2,\pi\rangle;

(36c)

\displaystyle|+y\rangle=|\pi/2,\pi/2\rangle,

\displaystyle|-y\rangle=|\pi/2,3\pi/2\rangle,

(36d)

and for
N
atoms:

\displaystyle|\pm z\rangle=|j,\mp j\rangle;

(37a)

\displaystyle|\pm x\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{j}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\pm 1)^{n}\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}|j,n-j\rangle;

(37b)

\displaystyle|\pm y\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{j}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\mp i)^{n}\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}|j,n-j\rangle.

(37c)

Choosing the
|x\rangle
polarization, it can be immediately seen that
P_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\binom{N}{n}
. Since the ACS have initial population over the full Dicke ladder including levels above
n=j
, the degeneracy in the decay rates produces repeated poles in the transfer coefficients. Therefore, we use (
51
) rather than the simple-pole formula used for the CDS.

A system initially prepared in this state has the maximal macroscopic dipole moment
\langle\hat{J}_{x}\rangle=j=D(0)
. Unlike the previous states, the incoherent intensity

\mathcal{I}(0)=I(0)-D^{2}(0)=\frac{N(N+1)}{4}-\frac{N^{2}}{4}=\frac{N}{4}.

(38)

Therefore, the coherent dipole contribution dominates the emission, opposite to Dicke states. This is perhaps the main distinction between the ACS and Dicke family: They have comparable intensities; nevertheless, the ACS radiates mostly through a macroscopic first-order dipole, while the CDS radiate with none.

Finally, a comparative intensity and correlation plot are given in Figures
10
and
11
, respectively. One notices that for large
N
,

g^{(2)}(0)=\frac{(N-1)(N^{2}+3N-2)}{N(N+1)^{2}}\rightarrow 1,

(39)

which is why the Poissonian limit is also called the Coherent State Limit.

For the mean field approximation of the
|x\rangle
polarization, the initial population was found to be
P_{n}(0)=2^{-N}\binom{N}{n}.
Writing
n=Nx
, with
0\leq x\leq 1
, and using Stirling’s approximation, we have:

P_{n}(0)\approx\frac{2^{-N}}{\sqrt{2\pi Nx(1-x)}}\left[x^{x}(1-x)^{1-x}\right]^{-N}.

(40)

Since the distribution is centered at
x_{0}=1/2
, the slowly varying square-root prefactor may be evaluated at that point. Expanding
f(x)=x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)
about
x_{0}
, we find
f(x)=-\ln 2+\frac{1}{2}(2x-1)^{2}
. Therefore, we use the de Moivre–Laplace theorem to approximate the density function:

W(n_{0},0)=\frac{N}{2^{N}}\binom{N}{n_{0}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2N}{\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{2(n_{0}-j)^{2}}{N}\right\}.

(41)

Then, the intensity is calculated from:

\displaystyle I(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2N(N+1)^{4}}{\pi}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x_{0}(a-x_{0})e^{-\frac{N}{2}(2x_{0}-1)^{2}}dx_{0}}{\left[a\cosh(Na\tau)+(a-2x_{0})\sinh(Na\tau)\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{N(N+1)^{4}}{32\pi}}\text{Csch}^{2}(Na\tau)\Big[2a\coth{(Na\tau)}\mathcal{F}_{N}(z).

\displaystyle+\left.a^{2}\text{Csch}^{2}(Na\tau)\mathcal{F^{\prime}}_{N}(z)-\sqrt{2\pi/N}\text{erf}\left\{\sqrt{N/2}\right\}\right],

(42)

where
z=a\coth(Na\tau)+a-1
, and the error function, the truncated Faddeeva function, and its derivative are given below:

\displaystyle\text{erf}\{\sqrt{N/2}\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi}}\int_{-1}^{1}dxe^{-\frac{N}{2}x^{2}}.;

(43a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{e^{-\frac{N}{2}x^{2}}}{z-x}.;

(43b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{e^{-\frac{N}{2}x^{2}}}{(z-x)^{2}}.

(43c)

Appendix A
Solving the Master Equation

Here, we provide the general method by which we have solved our master equations. The superradiance master equation
2
is given by:

\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}\{\rho\}=-\Gamma\left(\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\rho-2\hat{J}_{-}\rho\hat{J}_{+}+\rho\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\right),

(44)

where
\mathcal{L}\{\bm{\cdot}\}
is the Liouvillian operator. It takes as its argument the density matrix and evolves it in time. We take the initial state,
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, and construct from it the density matrix
\rho(0)=|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|
. Since Dicke states constitute a complete basis for symmetric spin states, we expand our density matrices in terms of coefficients
\rho_{nn^{\prime}}
in the Dicke basis such that:

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=0}^{N}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)|j,n-j\rangle\langle j,n^{\prime}-j|

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho_{nn}(t)|j,n-j\rangle\langle j,n-j|

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\neq n^{\prime}}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)|j,n-j\rangle\langle j,n^{\prime}-j|.

(45)

Note that we have used the excitation number representation
n=m+j
. The first series on the RHS of (
45
) is the population matrix, with
\rho_{nn}(t)=P_{n}(t)
and
\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}(t)=1
, whereas the off-diagonal elements,
\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)
, are the coherences, representing the quantum superpositions between states.

Then, we can plug (
45
) into (
44
),with
|n\rangle\equiv|j,n-j\rangle
, and sum from
n=0\rightarrow N
. The population equation satisfies:

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|

\displaystyle=2\Gamma P_{n}(t)\left(\hat{J}_{-}|n\rangle\langle n|\hat{J}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-},|n\rangle\langle n|\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}P_{n}(t)\left(|n-1\rangle\langle n-1|-|n\rangle\langle n|\right),

(46)

with
\lambda_{n}=2\Gamma n(N-n+1)
being the decay rates. If we shift the series on the RHS by 1, and then trace over these states, we get the indicial rate equation:

\dot{P}_{n}(t)=\lambda_{n+1}P_{n+1}(t)-\lambda_{n}P_{n}(t),

(47)

which may be solved via the Laplace transform method, where
\tilde{P}_{n}(s)=\mathfrak{L}\{P_{n}(t)\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}P_{n}(t)dt
,
\mathfrak{L}\{\dot{P}_{n}(t)\}=s\tilde{P}_{n}(s)-P_{n}(0)
, and where as usual,
P_{n}(0)
is the initial probability that the system starts in level
n
. Transforming (
47
), we get after rearranging:

\tilde{P}_{n}(s)=\frac{P_{n}(0)+\lambda_{n+1}\tilde{P}_{n+1}(s)}{s+\lambda_{n}}.

(48)

This is our fundamental recursion relation. If we carry out this recursion until we arrive at
n=0
, we get:

\tilde{P}_{n}(s)=\sum_{k=n}^{N}P_{k}(0)\overbrace{\left[\frac{\prod_{r=n+1}^{k}\lambda_{r}}{\prod_{\ell=n}^{k}(s+\lambda_{\ell})}\right]}^{=\tilde{C}_{n,k}(s)},

(49)

where
\tilde{C}_{n,k}(s)
are the transfer coefficients, whose inverse-Laplace transform is the probability that a system starting in state
k
has decayed into state
n
by time
t
. Now, a subtle point: Due to the degeneracy of the decay rates
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{N-n+1}
, we may get a pole of order 1 or 2. For poles of order 1, as we find in our Dicke states, we find that the transfer coefficients are:

C_{n,k}(t)=\left(\prod_{r=n+1}^{k}\lambda_{r}\right)\sum_{\ell=n}^{k}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{\ell}t}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=n\\
q\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{k}(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{\ell})}.

(50)

To address states with pole of order q, we note first that:

\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s+\lambda_{\ell})^{q}}\right\}(t)=\frac{t^{q-1}}{(q-1)!}e^{-\lambda_{\ell}t}.

We define the set
\mathcal{M}_{n,k}
of distinct rates and the multiplicity
m_{n,k}
of the distinct rates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n,k}=\{\lambda_{\ell}:\quad\ell=n,n+1,\dots,k-1,k\};

\displaystyle m_{n,k}(\lambda_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k})=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=n}^{k}\delta_{\lambda_{\ell^{\prime}},\lambda_{\ell}};

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(q)}=\{\lambda_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}:m_{n,k}(\lambda_{\ell})=q\}.

Since no state considered in this article has a pole higher than the second order, we may write:

\displaystyle C_{n,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(1)}}A^{(1)}_{n,k}(\mu)e^{-\mu t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(2)}}\bigl(A^{(2)}_{n,k}(\mu)t+A^{(3)}_{n,k}(\mu)\bigr)e^{-\mu t},

(51)

where the weights
A^{(i)}_{n,k}(\mu)
are given by:

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{n,k}(\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\left[(s+\mu)\,\tilde{C}_{n,k}(s)\right]_{s=-\mu};

(52a)

\displaystyle A^{(2)}_{n,k}(\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\left[(s+\mu)^{2}\,\tilde{C}_{n,k}(s)\right]_{s=-\mu};

(52b)

\displaystyle A^{(3)}_{n,k}(\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{d}{ds}\left((s+\mu)^{2}\,\tilde{C}_{n,k}(s)\right)\right]_{s=-\mu}.

(52c)

Then, the populations can be read from (
49
) to be:

P_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=n}^{N}P_{k}(0)C_{n,k}(t).

(53)

Now, for the coherences. We may write for
\Delta\in[0,N]
:

\rho^{(\Delta)}_{n}(t)\equiv\rho_{n,n+\Delta}(t),

(54)

with the allowed transitions being between
0\leq n\leq N-\Delta
. We notice immediately that
\Delta=0
was the population band just addressed. In general,
\Delta=\ell
gives us the moments
\langle\hat{J}_{+}^{\ell}\rangle
. Then, the coherences separately solve:

\dot{\rho}^{(\Delta)}_{n}(t)=\alpha_{n}^{(\Delta)}\rho_{n+1}^{(\Delta)}(t)-\beta_{n}^{(\Delta)}\rho_{n}^{(\Delta)}(t),

(55)

with
\alpha_{n}^{(\Delta)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{n+1}\lambda_{n+\Delta+1}}
and
\beta_{n}^{(\Delta)}=\frac{\lambda_{n}+\lambda_{n+\Delta}}{2}
. Following the same method, we take the Laplace transform and rearrange to get:

\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{(\Delta)}(s)=\sum_{k=n}^{N-\Delta}\rho_{k}^{(\Delta)}(0)\,\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{r=n}^{k-1}\alpha_{r}^{(\Delta)}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=n}^{k}\bigl(s+\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\bigr)},

(56)

and then take the inverse Laplace transforms:

\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\overbrace{\frac{1}{\bigl(s+\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\bigr)^{q}}}^{\tilde{C}_{n,k}^{(\Delta)}(s)}\right\}(t)=\frac{t^{q-1}}{(q-1)!}\,e^{-\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}t},

(57)

to find that the transfer coefficients are:

\displaystyle C_{n,k}^{(\Delta)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(1,\Delta)}}A_{n,k}^{(1,\Delta)}\!\bigl(\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\bigr)e^{-\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,k}^{(2,\Delta)}}\left[A_{n,k}^{(2,\Delta)}\!\bigl(\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\bigr)t\right.

\displaystyle+\left.A_{n,k}^{(3,\Delta)}\!\bigl(\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}\bigr)\right]e^{-\beta_{\ell}^{(\Delta)}t}.

(58)

Finally, the coherences will be:

\rho_{n}^{(\Delta)}(t)=\sum_{k=n}^{N-\Delta}\rho_{k}^{(\Delta)}(0)\,C_{n,k}^{(\Delta)}(t).

(59)

Note that for
\Delta=0
, we retrieve the populations. It is more transparent, however, to keep these methods separate.

Then, since:

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{+}|j,m\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(j-m)(j+m+1)}|j,m+1\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{2\Gamma}}|j,n-j+1\rangle;

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}|j,m\rangle

\displaystyle=(j+m)(j-m+1)|j,m\rangle=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2\Gamma}|j,n-j\rangle;

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{+}^{2}J_{-}^{2}|j,m\rangle

\displaystyle=(j+m)(j^{2}-(m-1)^{2})(j-m+2)|j,m\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{n}\lambda_{n-1}}{(2\Gamma)^{2}}|j,n-j\rangle.

we can find the expressions of the dipole moment
D(\tau)=|\langle\hat{J}_{+}\rangle|(\tau)
, the total emission intensity
I(\tau)=\langle\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\rangle(\tau)
, and the Glauber function
g^{(2)}(\tau)=\frac{\langle\hat{J}_{+}^{2}\hat{J}_{-}^{2}\rangle(\tau)}{\langle\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\rangle^{2}(\tau)}
from:

\displaystyle D(\tau)=\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{2\Gamma}}\,\rho_{n,n+1}(\tau)\right|;

(60)

\displaystyle I(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2\Gamma}\,P_{n}(\tau);

(61)

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\tau)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{N}\lambda_{n}\lambda_{n-1}P_{n}(\tau)}{\left(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\lambda_{n}P_{n}(\tau)\right)^{2}}.

(62)

Finally, the phase uncertainty is calculated from:

\Delta\phi\sim\frac{1}{2\Delta J_{z}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\langle\hat{J}_{z}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{J}_{z}\rangle^{2}}},

(63)

where:

\langle\hat{J}_{z}^{\ell}\rangle(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(n-j)^{\ell}P_{n}(\tau).

(64)

Appendix B
Solving the Fokker-Planck Equation

We may give approximate solutions that are accurate for large values of
N
by reducing our Rates equation to a Fokker-Planck equation
39
. These solutions are often called Mean-Field Approximations, when we neglect the diffusion and take the large
N
-limit.

Let
x=\frac{n}{N}
be the excitation fraction. For large
N
,
x
may be treated as a continuous variable on
[0/N,N/N]=[0,1]
. Then, we can see that

\Delta x=\frac{n+1}{N}-\frac{n}{N}=\frac{1}{N}
. If we define
a=1+1/N
, then the decay rates are found to be:

\lambda_{n}=2\Gamma N^{2}\frac{n}{N}\big(1-\frac{n}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\big)=2\Gamma N^{2}x(a-x)\equiv\lambda(x).

(65)

Let us now define a continuum density function
W(x,\tau)
such that
\int_{0}^{1}dxW(x,\tau)=1
. To associate this density with our discrete
n
population rates, we have to write the density as a piecewise discrete function as a delta-comb measure to get the pair:

\displaystyle W(x,\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}(\tau)\delta\left(x-\frac{n}{N}\right);

(66a)

\displaystyle P_{n}(\tau)=\int_{x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}}^{x+\frac{\Delta x}{2}}dxW(x,\tau).

(66b)

We can now see that:

P_{n}(\tau)\approx W(x,\tau)\Delta x=\frac{W(x,\tau)}{N},

(67)

or
W(x,\tau)=NP_{n}(\tau)
. Now, a standard result from Operator Calculus gives us for a constant
\alpha
, the shift operator
e^{\alpha\partial_{x}}f(x)
, or the generator of infinitesimal translations:

\displaystyle e^{\alpha\partial_{x}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\alpha\partial_{x}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2!}\partial^{2}_{x}+\dots\right)f(x)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{1!}(x+\alpha-x)^{1}\partial^{1}_{x}+\dots\right)f(x+\alpha-\alpha)

\displaystyle=f(x+\alpha),

(68)

where we notice from the last step that this is the Taylor expansion of
f(x+\alpha)
around
x
. Now, let us take the time derivative of the continuum distribution function:

\partial_{\tau}W(x,\tau)=N\dot{P}_{n}(\tau)=N[\lambda_{n+1}P_{n+1}(\tau)-\lambda_{n}P_{n}(\tau)],

(69)

where we have used the rate equation. Then, we check that:

\lambda_{n+1}=2\Gamma N^{2}[(x+\Delta x)(a-(x+\Delta x))]=\lambda(x+\Delta x).

(70)

Whereas
P_{n+1}(\tau)=\frac{W(x+\frac{1}{N},\tau)}{N}=\Delta xW(x+\Delta x,\tau)
. Putting it all together, we have:

\partial_{\tau}W(x,\tau)=N\Delta x\left[\lambda(x+\Delta x)W(x+\Delta x,\tau)-\lambda(x)W(x,\tau)\right].

(71)

If we let
F(x,\tau)=\lambda(x)W(x,\tau)
, then we see that

F(x+\Delta x,\tau)=e^{\Delta x\partial_{x}}F(x,\tau).

(72)

Then:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=F(x+\Delta x,\tau)-F(x,\tau)=\left(e^{\Delta x\partial_{x}}-1\right)F(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta x}{1!}\partial_{x}F(x,\tau)+\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{2!}\partial^{2}_{x}F(x,\tau)+\dots

(73)

This reduces to what is known as the Kramers–Moyal Series, with Kramers–Moyal coefficients, respectively written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\partial_{x})^{k}\left[D^{(k)}(x,\tau)W(x,\tau)\right];

(74a)

\displaystyle D^{(k)}(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\frac{(\Delta x)^{k}}{k!}\lambda(x).

(74b)

The Fokker-Planck equation ignores all terms
k
higher than 2. Then, the drift
d(x,\tau)
and diffusion
D(x,\tau)
coefficients are given by:

\displaystyle d(x,\tau)=D^{(1)}(x,\tau)=-\frac{\lambda(x)}{N}=-2\Gamma Nx(a-x);

(75a)

\displaystyle D(x,\tau)=D^{(2)}(x,\tau)=\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{2}\lambda(x)=\Gamma x(a-x).

(75b)

The Fokker-Planck equation can be written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}W(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}[d(x,\tau)W(x,\tau)]+\partial_{x}^{2}[D(x,\tau)W(x,\tau)]

\displaystyle=2\Gamma N\left(\partial_{x}[x(a-x)W]+\frac{1}{2N}\partial_{x}^{2}[x(a-x)W]\right)

(76)

Now, we may set the boundary conditions. Whereas
W(x,0)
is state-dependent, the boundary conditions depend on processes within the context of the Fokker-Planck equation. First, let us consider the former:

W(x,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{Nx^{\prime}}(0)\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(77)

where
Nx^{\prime}=n^{\prime}
. Now, since
I(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2\Gamma}P_{n}(\tau)
, we can extend this to:

I(\tau)\approx\frac{1}{2\Gamma}\int_{0}^{1}dx\lambda(x)W(x,\tau).

(78)

To decide what
W(x,\tau)
is, let us define the probability-current:

J(x,\tau)=d(x,\tau)W(x,\tau)-\partial_{x}[D(x,\tau)W(x,\tau)],

(79)

where we set as our boundary condition the conservation of probability current
J(0,\tau)=J(1,\tau)=0
, then solve
\partial_{\tau}W(x,\tau)=-\partial_{x}J(x,\tau)
. This preserves
\int_{0}^{1}dxW(x,\tau)=1
. This allows us to shift the integration to the initial time through
W(x_{0},0)=W(x(\tau),\tau)\Big|\frac{dx}{dx_{0}}\Big|
.
