Mosunetuzumab: A bispecific CD20 × CD3 antibody for follicular lymphoma with promising evidence in other B-NHL subtypes
Authors: Geoffrey Shouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMosunetuzumab is a bispecific antibody targeting CD20 x CD3 re-directing T cells to target and destroy CD20-positive B cells. T-cell-based therapies including bispecific antibodies have revolutionized B-cell NHL treatment. Mosunetuzumab is approved for follicular lymphoma with at least 2 prior lines of therapy. There are also NCCN recommendations for its use in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and in combination with polatuzumab vedotin in relapsed/refractory mantle cell lymphoma with prior BTK inhibitor therapy. Published trials show low rates of severe CRS and support outpatient administration in appropriate settings. Utilizing a search of PubMed, conference abstracts, and clinicaltrials.gov between 2020 and 2026, prospective trial data as well as retrospective real-world evidence and ongoing studies were included for a more complete picture of available data. This review summarizes the molecular design, mechanism of action, clinical efficacy, and safety of mosunetuzumab, an agent representative of immunotherapeutic advancement in lymphoma.

Introduction

T-cell redirecting bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) have revolutionized the treatment of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL). There are currently 7 different bispecific antibodies approved for the treatment of 4 different hematologic malignancies, among which mosunetuzumab has shown promising safety and efficacy.1,2 Mosunetuzumab targets CD20 on malignant B cells and CD3 on native T cells thereby redirecting T cells to target and destroy B-NHL.3 It is a prototypic example of this drug class with a well‑characterized mechanism of action, several studies demonstrating its safety and efficacy across B-NHL indications, as well as numerous ongoing studies evaluating the immunotherapeutic both as a single agent or in combination with other drugs.

Harnessing T-cell immunity as an anti-cancer treatment strategy has led to significant improvements in outcomes in hematologic malignancies including B-NHL. The therapeutic landscape has burgeoned with numerous CAR-T and BsAb agents competing across varied indications. As the field of BsAbs continues to expand, mosunetuzumab has demonstrated a very low rate of high‑grade cytokine release syndrome or neurologic toxicities4 and comes in a formulation that can be administered as a subcutaneous injection,5 allowing for ease of use without compromising efficacy. With multiple BsAbs with similar mechanisms of action available for B-NHL, understanding what makes mosunetuzumab unique will inform clinical decisions and future studies that further harness the potential of this agent.

Methods

The purpose of this study is to review the evolving role of mosunetuzumab in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The review aimed for a broad scope attempting to encompass multiple facets of mosunetuzumab use including practical data. The search strategy aimed to be comprehensive and included a search of PubMed, clinicaltrials.gov, and available conference proceedings. Each of these sources was searched from 2020–2026. The term “mosunetuzumab” was used as the primary search term, and all records were reviewed. The search was conducted on June 11, 2026. In PubMed, this produced 148 results which were individually reviewed. Abstracts and meeting proceedings were replaced with subsequent publications where available. Studies were included if they were published in English and ranked based on the quality of evidence, impact on practice, and ability to provide evidence toward otherwise unanswered questions. For example, the clinical trials leading to mosunetuzumab approval in FL treatment were included first as this is the strongest clinical data for the use of this drug. In each section, however, prospective clinical trial data was supplemented with other forms of evidence. Real‑world retrospective data were used in the case where specific endpoints may not be available, such as overall survival data which were lacking from the pivotal trial in FL. Comparative analyses such as matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) studies were discussed in the cases where randomized controlled trial data are lacking and likely will never be completed (i.e. direct comparison to other bispecific antibodies or CAR T). Finally, cost-effectiveness studies were included and were intended to shine light on potential practical implementation of mosunetuzumab use in clinical practice. It should be noted that conclusions from non-trial sources are considered suggestive at best but are able to provide some evidence for decision-making in cases where prospective clinical trial data are lacking. Articles were excluded if they were review articles, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses (n = 53), case reports or reports of outcomes from a single institution (n = 6), articles focused solely on drug resistance (n = 6), articles focused solely on toxicity (n = 3), pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic analysis (n = 7), or basic science research not involving data from human subjects (n = 6).

Mechanism of action and preclinical data

Monoclonal antibodies have been used as cancer therapeutics since the advent of rituximab, which binds to CD20 on malignant B cells leading to cell death by multiple means, including antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, and complement-dependent cytotoxicity.6 Importantly, monoclonal antibodies with a single target, such as rituximab, are not able to harness cytotoxic T cells, which are one of the most potent anti-tumor cells in the human anti-cancer armamentarium. Chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR T) are able to utilize the power of cytotoxic T cells but require time-consuming manufacturing processes and specialized treatment centers which have limited inclusive use. Bispecific antibodies, on the other hand, represent an off-the-shelf option that harnesses cytotoxic T cells through binding of CD3 redirecting them to target cells generating a major histocompatibility/T-cell receptor-independent immune synapse that results in target cell destruction.7

Bispecific antibody structure is quite variable.8 Structures include relatively low‑molecular‑weight peptides containing only single‑chain variable fragments targeting T cell and target cell, such as blinatumomab, full‑sized monoclonal antibody structures, and also hybrid molecules with varying numbers and affinities of binding sites. Mosunetuzumab is a fully humanized, full-length IgG1 monoclonal antibody with binding sites for CD20 and CD3 (Figure 1). In preclinical studies, mosunetuzumab was able to more effectively recruit and harness gamma/delta T cells to destroy lymphoma cell lines and patient samples compared with the CD20 monoclonal antibody obinutuzumab, confirming its effectiveness at T-cell redirection.9 Figure 1.Diagram of a CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibody bringing a T cell in close proximity to a B cell.Diagram of a bispecific antibody linking a B cell and a T cell via CD20 and CD3.

The initial dosing of mosunetuzumab for first‑in‑human trials was based on the minimum anticipated biologic effect level based on in vitro studies of T-cell activation and receptor occupancy.10 Initial dosing was 50 µg based on this method with single patient accelerated dose escalation used in the initial dose‑finding study. This led to the current dosing schedule for mosunetuzumab, which is given as step-up dosing intravenously on D1 (1 mg), D8 (2 mg), D15 (60 mg), with 60 mg given on C2D1, then 30 mg given thereafter once every 21 d.4,11 A maximum of 8 cycles are given for those patients achieving CR or 17 cycles for those with a partial response. Clinical data – FDA approved indication – follicular lymphoma

Currently, mosunetuzumab is approved by the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma (FL) after 2 or more prior lines of therapy. FL is the most common indolent B-NHL with about 3–5 new cases per 100,000 people per year.15,16 Although indolent, FL is considered incurable, and disease behavior can vary widely. For example, some patients may go years without needing treatment, while others have early relapse and refractory disease to multiple lines of therapy.17–19 There are many approved treatment options for FL including CD20 monoclonal antibodies, chemoimmunotherapy, lenalidomide-based regimens, among others. In the third line setting, in addition to mosunetuzumab,3,4 targeted therapies against EZH220 and Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase (BTK),21 as well the CAR-T products, axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel),22 tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel)23 and lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel),24 and the BsAb epcoritamab,25,26 are all approved.

The approval for mosunetuzumab was based on a phase 1/2 study (NCT02500407/GO29781) demonstrating an objective response rate of 80% with a complete response (CR) rate of 60% and a median duration of response (DOR) among responders of 22.8 months (Table 2).4 Long‑term follow-up demonstrated that these response rates were maintained and that the median duration of response was updated to 46.4 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 18.7 – Not Estimable [NE]), while median PFS was 24.0 months (95% CI: 12–53.2). Among 54 patients who achieved CR, 49 remained in CR at the end of treatment, with a median duration of CR not reached (NR; 95% CI: 44.1 – NE). Median OS was also not reached (95% CI: NE), while 5 y OS rate was 78% (95% CI: 69.6–87.4).27,28 Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred in 44.4% of patients with only 2.2% experiencing grade 3 or higher. The subcutaneous formulation of mosunetuzumab has also been approved by the US FDA in third line and beyond FL. This formulation was tested in a separate arm of the same study (NCT02500407/GO29781) with results demonstrating that the co-primary pharmacokinetic endpoints of trough concentration and area under the curve were both met.12 These results demonstrated a non-inferior exposure of mosunetuzumab with subcutaneous dosing compared to intravenous (IV). Importantly, the ORR (76.6%, 95% CI: 66.7–84.7) and CR rate (61.7%, 95% CI: 51.1–71.5) were not different from response rates seen with the IV formulation, while safety outcomes demonstrated a numerically lower rate of CRS overall (29.8% vs. 44.4%) and lower rate of severe CRS (grade ≥ 2, 9.6% vs. 18.9%)

Real-world evidence studies have emerged evaluating several aspects of mosunetuzumab use in FL, including an estimate of OS, two reports of real-world efficacy and safety, and one comparison against a real-world standard‑of‑care dataset (Table 2). These studies do contain inherent biases but are included as they attempt to address questions not addressed by available clinical trial data.

The estimate of median OS study utilized a Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis of 6 real‑world datasets along with the expansion arm of the GO29781 (NCT02500407) trial.4 Utilizing the Bayesian extrapolations for mosunetuzumab, the estimates for median OS ranged from 11.6 (6.7–20.7) y to 17.0 (6.4–22.7) y depending on which of the distributions were used.29 Within the limitations of a largely retrospective dataset, the study is able to provide additional supportive evidence of mosunetuzumab use in FL.

Real-world studies suggest that in clinical practice, mosunetuzumab has similar efficacy and safety as the pivotal trial. Two studies evaluated outcomes of patients treated with mosunetuzumab in the real-world setting. The first attempted to compare real‑world results directly with data from the pivotal phase I/II trial (GO29781) by performing a matching‑adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) strategy.30 Patients were included in the comparison if they met eligibility requirements for the pivotal study and had all data available for the matching portion, which included age, line of therapy, POD24 status, double-refractory status, and presence of elevated LDH. After adjustment, the ORR (73%, 95% CI: 65.3–79.5 vs. 80.0%, 95% CI: 70.3–87.7) and CR (52.9%, 95% CI: 44.8–60.7 vs. 60.0%, 95% CI: 49.1–70.2) rate were numerically lower for the real-world patient cohort; however, it did not reach statistical significance, while 12-month PFS (59.5%, 95% CI: 51.0–69.3 vs. 57.7%, 95% CI: 46.9–68.4) was similar.30 Another study investigated the outcomes of patients with FL treated with mosunetuzumab in a large multicenter real-world evidence study. The study reported the outcomes of 75 patients with FL and demonstrated an ORR of 88% and CR of 71%.31 The median follow‑up was 13.2 months with a median PFS of 17.7 months (95% CI: 13.5–21.9) and median OS not reached, similar to the pivotal study. These indirect comparison studies provide additional supportive evidence suggesting the utility of mosunetuzumab for the FDA‑approved indication.

Since the phase I/II study leading to the approval of mosunetuzumab was non-randomized (NCT02500407), one study utilized real‑world evidence from the US nationwide Flatiron Health electronic health record de-identified database to collect data on patients that served as an artificial control arm.32 Patients were included in the analysis if they met the inclusion criteria for the expansion cohort of the pivotal study but were treated with standard therapy. Propensity scoring adjustment was also made in an attempt to balance the real-world cohort against the patients in the prospective study using age, POD24, double refractory status, refractory to last line of therapy, and number of prior lines of therapy. In total, 158 patients had available data for time‑to‑event analysis, while 125 of those patients also had response data. Mosunetuzumab was associated with a significant benefit in terms of CR (60% vs. 33%, OR 3.18; 95% CI: 1.41–7.17), a similar ORR (80% vs. 75%, OR 1.23; 95% CI: 0.52–2.93). OS at 1 y was improved for mosunetuzumab 93% (95% CI: 88–98%) vs. only 76% (95% CI: 64–88%) on the real-world cohort (HR of 0.43; 95% CI: 0.19–0.94). This retrospective propensity‑adjusted analysis provided support for the notion of improvement in response and survival outcomes with mosunetuzumab over the prior standard therapy. Similarly, a matching plus regression adjustment estimation of mosunetuzumab against bendamustine combined with rituximab utilizing data from NCT02500407, NCT02187861, and NCT02257567 demonstrated a PFS and OS advantage in favor of mosunetuzumab (HR 0.43 [95% CI: 0.13, 0.91] and HR 0.30 [95% CI: 0.05, 5.28], respectively).33 These results provide additional support to the use of mosunetuzumab in the FDA‑approved indication.

Safety and toxicity profile

One of the most significant factors that sets mosunetuzumab apart from other therapeutic options is its favorable toxicity profile. Adverse events (AEs) of interest with T‑cell redirecting therapies that target B-cells include CRS, immune effector cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) or neurotoxicity, and infections. For mosunetuzumab in general, rates of CRS are low with overall events occurring in 44% of patients on the pivotal study and only 2.2% of patients developing grade 3 or higher CRS.4,34 In terms of neurologic toxicity, ICANS was virtually non-existent with only a single patient developing a grade 1 neurologic‑related AE of tremor. In a large retrospective study of AEs reported to the FDA for bispecific antibodies, there was a signal for cerebral edema associated with mosunetuzumab;31 however, without more detailed understanding, the frequency and severity of this finding are not well characterized. Given the overall safety profile, there is no mandatory inpatient monitoring associated with the product label.32

Real-world data also support the favorable safety profile of mosunetuzumab in practice. For example, one study showed that rates of mosunetuzumab toxicity were similar to those reported in the pivotal study.4,31 In the real-world evidence study of 75 patients with r/r FL treated with mosunetuzumab, CRS occurred in 36% of patients with a maximum of grade 3 CRS seen in only one patient. ICANS occurred in only 1 patient and was grade 1. Infections were seen in 26% of patients.31 Given the excellent safety profile, mosunetuzumab is often considered an excellent choice for older adults. In support of this notion, a subgroup analysis of patients aged 65 y and older treated with mosunetuzumab on the phase I/II study GO29781 (NCT02500407) showed that safety was preserved.35 The study included patients treated with mosunetuzumab for FL as well as other B-NHL subtypes. Severe AEs were similar compared to younger patients (45.1% vs. 46.6%). CRS was the most common AE and was numerically less frequent in older patients (33.3% vs. 44.8%).

Although several studies have demonstrated a tolerable safety profile, mosunetuzumab is not without toxicity. One major issue is infection risk, with severe infections documented in up to 17% of patients, grade 3 or higher infections in 14%, and fatal infections in 0.9% of patients according to the FDA label.32 This is thought to be mediated by destruction of innocent bystander CD20 B cells leading to B‑cell aplasia as well as transient neutropenia seen during mosunetuzumab therapy. Toxicity data in the real-world are also emerging. For example, severe and life-threatening opportunistic infections have been reported, including a fatal case of HHV-6-associated encephalitis36 and a persistent parvovirus B19 infection;37 however, in both cases, patients were heavily pre-treated.

Clinical data – NCCN recommendation – DLBCL

In addition to the indication for r/r FL, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines list mosunetuzumab with or without polatuzumab vedotin (pola), the CD79B antibody drug conjugate, for the treatment of r/r DLBCL. Data for this recommendation come from several studies. For example, single-agent mosunetuzumab was evaluated in an expansion cohort from an early-phase trial in r/r DLBCL where the ORR was reported as 42.0% (95% CI: 31.6–53.1) and the CR rate was 23.9% (95% CI: 15.4–34.1) among 88 response-evaluable patients.38 Although promising, the CR rate did not reach a statistically significant improvement over historical controls.

The evidence for the combination of mosunetuzumab with pola (mosun/pola) was initially from a phase 1/2 study, followed by a confirmatory phase 3 randomized study.39–41 In the expansion arm of the phase 1/2 study in 98 patients with r/r DLBCL, the combination of mosunetuzumab and polatuzumab vedotin led to an ORR of 59% (95% CI: 48.8–69.0) and CR rate of 46% (95% CI: 35.8–56.3).39,40 The phase 3 study (SUNMO) randomized patients with r/r DLBCL deemed ineligible for autoSCT to mosun/pola vs. R-GemOx with primary endpoint of progression‑free survival. A total of 208 patients were enrolled and randomized 2:1, and at a median follow-up of 23.2 months, the median PFS was significantly longer with mosun/pola than with R-GemOx (11.5 months [95% CI: 5.6–18] vs. 3.8 months [95% CI: 2.9–4.1].41 Additionally, OS, ORR and CR all favored the experimental arm (HR for OS 0.41 [95% CI: 0.3–0.6]; P < .0001; ORR: 70% vs. 40%; P < .0001; CR: 51% vs. 24%). The toxicity profile was also favorable with low rates of severe CRS (<5% grade 2 or higher) and an improvement in patient-reported outcomes on the mosun/pola arm.

In addition, mosun/pola was also evaluated in treatment naïve DLBCL in older adults deemed unfit for intensive chemoimmunotherapy. Among 108 patients treated on that study, an ORR of 80% was demonstrated with a CR rate of 61%.42 The treatment was generally well tolerated with grade 3 or higher AEs occurring in 41% of patients, CRS occurring in 30% with max grade 3 seen in 2 patients, while infections occurred in 21%.

Despite promising outcomes for mosun/pola in older adults with DLBCL and the r/r setting, in a randomized phase 2 study of mosun/pola combined with CHP vs. pola R CHP in previously untreated DLBCL, there was no improvement seen in ORR, CR, or PFS, despite the combination being well tolerated.43 In addition, a phase 2 study of mosunetuzumab combined with CHOP chemotherapy in previously untreated DLBCL was performed.44 Among 40 patients enrolled and treated, ORR was 95% (95% CI: 83.1–99.4) with CR rate of 90.0% (95% CI: 76.3–97.2). Median DOR, PFS, and EFS were not reached. CRS occurred in 60% of patients, but all events were grade 1–2. Neutropenia was the most common grade 3 or higher AE, occurring in 65% of patients. Taken together, there is promising evidence for the combination of mosun/pola in DLBCL.

Clinical data – mantle cell lymphoma

In addition to the indication for r/r FL and NCCN guideline recommendations for DLBCL, there is also an NCCN guideline recommendation for mosunetuzumab in combination with polatuzumab vedotin for r/r mantle cell lymphoma after Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase inhibitor (BTKi) therapy. The recommendation is based on results from a phase 2 study,45 where patients received fixed duration mosunetuzumab subcutaneously for 17 cycles combined with polatuzumab vedotin for 6 cycles. The primary endpoint of the study was best ORR. A total of 42 patients were enrolled with a median of 3 prior therapies including 26% with prior CAR-T exposure. In terms of high-risk features, 67% had high Ki-67, 38% had blastoid/pleomorphic morphology, and 48% had TP53 aberration. The ORR was 88.1% among evaluable patients (95% CI: 74.4–96.0) with CR in 78.6% (95% CI: 62.3–89.7%). With a median follow-up of 18.6 months, the PFS was 18.6 months (95% CI 13.9 – not estimable) with consistent efficacy across high-risk subgroups. CRS occurred in 42.9% of patients but was limited to grade 1 and 2 events.

Clinical data – additional indications

In addition to r/r FL, MCL, and DLBCL, mosunetuzumab has been evaluated in several other B-NHL indications. Data with the subcutaneous formulation of mosunetuzumab in front-line FL with both low‑tumor‑burden FL46 and high‑tumor‑burden FL47 have been recently reported, for example. In the low‑tumor‑burden study, among 101 patients enrolled, 52 had completed treatment at the time of data cutoff with a median duration of follow‑up of 13.8 months (95% CI: 11.5–16.9).46 The ORR was 98% (95% CI: 93.0–99.8), and the CR rate was 89.1% (95% CI: 81.3–94.4). The DOR and DOCR at 12 months were 91.8% (95% CI: 81.5–96.5) and 99.0% (95% CI: 93.2–99.9), respectively. Grade 3/4 AEs were reported in 51.5% of patients with cytopenias being most common at 36.6%. CRS occurred in 48.5% of patients, all grade 1 or 2. Infections occurred in 63.4% of patients with 1% of patients dying from infection; however, B‑cell recovery was noted 6 months after completing treatment. In the high‑tumor‑burden study, 103 patients were enrolled, and an ORR of 87.4% (95% CI: 79.4–93.1) was demonstrated after a median follow-up of 22.3 months (95% CI: 20.9–24.1). The 12-month PFS was 85.5% (95% CI: 76.2–91.3), and the 12-month OS was 91.9% (95% CI: 84.5–95.9). CRS occurred in 34%, infections occurred in 77.7%, and 5 patients died from AEs.

In marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), there is an ongoing phase 3 study comparing the combination of mosunetuzumab with Revlimid and rituximab (R2) against investigator’s choice treatment in patients with r/r disease, but data from this trial have not yet been reported.48 Additionally, a front-line investigator-initiated trial using subcutaneous mosunetuzumab in indolent lymphoma including MZL demonstrated promising preliminary efficacy. The responses among the first 26 patients included an ORR of 100% and CR rate of 83%; however, only 3 patients with MZL were represented in this portion of the analysis.49

For other indolent lymphomas such as CLL and WM, there is less available data, and the current studies are ongoing without preliminary reports available. For example, in CLL, an open-label multicenter phase 1b study is evaluating safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy of mosunetuzumab as monotherapy or combined with BTKi or venetoclax (NCT05091424), while another pilot study is evaluating MRD clearance in patients with MRD‑positive CLL after a fixed duration of venetoclax (NCT07052695).

Some retrospective data are also available for Richter-transformed DLBCL from CLL treated with mosunetuzumab.50 Among the cohort of 20 patients, ORR was 40% with CR rate 20%. Median PFS was 3.4 months with median OS 10.2 months. CRS occurred in 65% of patients with maximum grade 3 seen in only one patient. The small number and retrospective nature of the study provide support for future studies investigating mosunetuzumab and/or other bispecific antibodies or combinations in this rare DLBCL subtype with a poor prognosis.

Mosunetuzumab within the treatment landscape of B-NHL

With the advent of cellular therapies including CAR T and BsAbs, the treatment landscape has changed dramatically for B-NHL. Treatment options for FL have broadened significantly as a result, and in the third-line setting, CART, BsAbs, and molecularly targeted therapies are all available. In the absence of head-to-head comparative clinical trial data, decisions about treatment selection can be based on physician experience, accessibility/availability of specific treatments, cost, patient factors, and perceived differences in various products. It is unlikely that randomized controlled trial data will become available to answer these important questions. In the absence of that data, comparative analyses such as MAICs and cost-effectiveness studies can provide some suggestions of potential relationships to help guide decision-making in the absence of prospective randomized trial data.

Several studies have attempted to compare mosunetuzumab to CAR-T products and/or other BsAbs. For example, a MAIC strategy was used to compare epcoritamab, another CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibody approved for FL to chemoimmunotherapy, mosunetuzumab, or odronextamab in FL.51 Data for the comparison came from EPCORE NHL-1, SCHOLAR-5, GO29781, and ELM-2 for each agent/modality, respectively. Key findings from the study include numerical improvements in ORR 90.9% vs. 80.0% (P = .067) and CR 72.8% vs. 60.0% (P = .159) in favor of epcoritamab, although these narrowly missed the cutoff for statistical significance. In terms of safety, no grade ≥3 CRS/ICANS were noted with epcoritamab, while grade ≥3 CRS (2.2%) and ICANS (4.4%) were observed with mosunetuzumab (2.2%) (P < .001).

Another MAIC compared liso-cel CAR T against mosunetuzumab for r/r FL in the third line and beyond.52 In that study, liso-cel was associated with an apparent improvement of efficacy with noted higher CR rate (OR 6.46, 95% CI 2.85–14.65), longer DOR (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.26–0.77) and improved PFS (HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.16–0.49). However, toxicity was also higher with liso-cel, with higher rates of any-grade CRS (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.01–3.43) and neurological events (OR 2.16, 95% CI 0.72–6.44). Use of tocilizumab and corticosteroids was also more frequent with liso-cel.

Two additional MAIC comparisons were performed evaluating axi-cel against mosunetuzumab in r/r FL in patients receiving 2 or more prior lines of therapy. The first used patient data from NCT02500407 for mosunetuzumab and data from ZUMA-5 for axi-cel.53 In this study, axi-cel was associated with improved PFS (HR: 0.38; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.23–0.61) and DOR (HR: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.26–0.77) as well as higher rates of all grade CRS, neurologic events, while rates of high-grade CRS and treatment‑related AEs were not significantly different. The second MAIC compared numerous treatments from multiple studies in third line and later r/r FL including ZUMA-5, ELARA, DELTA, DYNAMO, UNITY-NHL, AUGMENT, and NCT01897571 as well as patient level data from GADOLIN, CONTRALTO, and NCT02257567.54 Results were suggestive of favorable outcomes for mosunetuzumab compared to tazemetostat in EZH2 wild‑type patients, and overall CAR-T therapies appeared favored in terms of PFS and CR rate, although there were significant limitations to the study. Specifically, the MAIC methodology, retrospective nature of the study, and inherent biases preclude definitive conclusions regarding superiority of one product over another and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

In addition to safety and efficacy, indirect comparative studies have been used to evaluate cost-effectiveness of mosunetuzumab in third line and beyond r/r FL. One study utilized data from the ZUMA-5 and GO29781 trials for PFS and OS, and an MAIC was used to account for differences in the populations from both trials.55 The study demonstrated an estimated increase of 1.82 life years (LY) and 1.89 quality‑adjusted life years (QALY) for axi-cel vs. mosunetuzumab with PFS increase of 6.42 LY for axi-cel vs. 1.60 LY for mosunetuzumab. There was an estimated increase in cost of ~$250k for axi-cel leading to an incremental cost‑effectiveness ratio (ICER) of ~$108k per QALY gained. The conclusion of the study was that axi-cel was considered cost-effective in this setting with a 64% probability of being cost-effective across 5,000 iterations within the probabilistic sensitivity analysis. In contrast, another study used a partitioned survival model with simulated lifetime costs of mosunetuzumab against axi-cel, tisa-cel, tazemetostat, R2, BR, or BO utilizing Flatiron Health electronic health records and data from the GO29781 phase 2 study of mosunetuzumab.56 The conclusions from the authors’ analyses were that mosunetuzumab dominated over tazemetostat and axi-cel with greater QALYs and lower costs with projected cost-effectiveness against BR and BO. Compared to R2, mosunetuzumab incurred lower costs but had lower QALYs. Another cost-effectiveness analysis compared data from patients treated on ZUMA-5, ELARA, and GO29781 including ORR, CR, days of inpatient hospitalization, rates of CRS, neurotoxicity, neutropenia as well as estimates of PFS. Results suggested that although axi-cel showed equivalent QALY at 5 y and slightly better QALY at 10 y, mosunetuzumab remained cost‑effective at all time points with a net monetary benefit over axi-cel of ~ $72k and over tisa-cel of ~$119k.57 Another study confirmed the findings of mosunetuzumab vs. tisa-cel costs in Italy with an estimated cost difference of ~161k pounds in favor of mosunetuzumab.58 One final study evaluated estimated costs and utilization of novel therapies in FL and found that in the third line and beyond, costs ranged from ~$450k to ~$700k per patient for CAR T but despite costs, CAR T, mosunetuzumab, and tazemetostat would likely remain cost‑effective.59 The ultimate conclusions from multiple cost‑effectiveness studies suggest that these types of studies rely heavily on the underlying statistical models utilized. In addition, although mosunetuzumab may be cost‑effective compared to other treatment options, the results of available studies do not provide clear guidance for the choice of therapy in this complex setting.

Discussion

The treatment landscape for B-NHL has seen a dramatic shift toward novel therapies including BsAbs and CART. In r/r FL in the third-line setting, mosunetuzumab is an FDA‑approved CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibody with proven safety and efficacy. Mosunetuzumab combined with polatuzumab vedotin was also recently approved by the FDA for r/r MCL after prior BTKi therapy. FL is heterogeneous, and third-line treatment options are diverse. Factors that influence the choice of therapy include disease-specific factors as well as patient-specific factors, socioeconomic differences, costs, and challenges to access to care; as such, decision-making in r/r FL remains complicated. Several studies have performed indirect comparisons between mosunetuzumab and other therapies in an attempt to parse out strategies to prioritize one treatment over another. However, these studies remain with significant bias and limitations making firm conclusions challenging. In addition to a role in FL and MCL, there is also strong evidence for the use of mosunetuzumab in r/r DLBCL in combination with polatuzumab vedotin. Despite toxicities including CRS and neurologic toxicities, mosunetuzumab remains overall a well-tolerated treatment that can be managed in the outpatient setting and remains an excellent therapeutic option for many patients.

The future of B-NHL therapy will likely include BsAbs in novel combinations and in earlier lines of therapy. The combination of epcoritamab with revlimid and rituximab, for example, was just FDA approved in the second-line setting for r/r FL based on favorable survival data in a large phase III study.60 Promising data for the combination of mosunetuzumab combined with lenalidomide (mosun/len) in r/r FL have also been recently reported.61 In a non-randomized expansion arm of the phase III CELESTIMO trial (NCT04712097), 54 patients with r/r FL were enrolled and treated with mosun/len. With a median duration of follow-up of 12.7 months, the ORR was 96.3% with CR 87.0%. Grade 3–4 AEs were seen in 57.4% of patients, CRS occurred in 27.8 %, and the maximum grade 3 seen in 1.9%, with infections seen in 57.4%.

There is also promising evidence for the use of mosunetuzumab alone or in various combinations in other B-NHL subtypes as well. There are currently 30 active trials listed on Clinicaltrials.gov (as of a June 2026 search) in B-NHL utilizing mosunetuzumab in various combinations across numerous indications, indicating the broadening potential impact of this agent in the coming years. Two recent trials in progress in FL include combinations with pirtobrutinib62 and zanubrutinib,63 with the latter combination demonstrating a promising safety profile in a report of the first 10 patients treated. In summary, with its manageable safety profile and option for subcutaneous administration, mosunetuzumab is poised to help reshape B-NHL treatment in favor of novel approaches replacing less effective and more toxic chemotherapeutics.
