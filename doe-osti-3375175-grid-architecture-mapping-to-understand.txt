 Abstract
 The electric power system is undergoing significant transformation driven by changes in the
 generation mix, increasing reliance on information and communication technologies, evolving
 customer expectations, growing demand, and a dynamic cyber-physical environment. To
 address these challenges, substantial investments will be made over the next decade to create
 a flexible, affordable, secure, reliable, and resilient electric system. In response, the U.S.
 Department of Energy's (DOE) Office of Electricity (OE) is developing knowledge management
 tools aimed at systematically documenting technology pilots and demonstration projects. This
 initiative seeks to improve understanding of operating contexts, planning steps, and integration
 requirements for innovative grid technologies.

 OE’s project leverages Grid Architecture principles to collect and organize insights into the
 interdependencies, requirements, and capabilities of various grid solutions. By employing a
 systematic approach, the project aims to perform a feasibility study for the development of a
 software tool that will support stakeholders, including regulators, transmission and distribution
 system operators, aggregators, and technology providers. By offering a systematic, software-
 based framework to document technologies, understand their benefits and impacts, and inform
 deployment strategies, the proposed Grid Architecture Mapping to Understand Transformation
 (GAMUT) Tool is intended to reduce costs, enhance decision-making, and facilitate scaling and
 effective deployment of new technologies. The feasibility study will evaluate the technical and
 financial viability of the GAMUT Tool, identifying stakeholder needs and assessing risks and
 mitigation strategies. Ultimately, the GAMUT team aims to develop an initial minimal viable
 product, followed by staged improvements, to aid in the modernization and transformation of the
 U.S. electric grid.

 1.0 Introduction
 The electric power system is undergoing a significant transformation, characterized by changes
 in the generation mix, increased reliance on information and communication technologies, new
 customer expectations, and a changing cyber-physical environment. Government entities, the
 electricity industry, and other stakeholders are projected to invest billions, if not trillions, of
 dollars over the next decade to meet rising electricity demand with a flexible and affordable
 electricity delivery system that is secure, reliable, and resilient.

 To support decision-making and the cost-effective adoption of innovative grid technologies, the
 U.S. Department of Energy's (DOE) Office of Electricity (OE) has called for the development of
 knowledge management tools. These tools are intended to assist in systematically documenting
 technology pilots and demonstration projects to improve understanding of the operating context,
 planning steps, and integration requirements to adopt innovative grid technologies. A systematic
 and standardized approach to analysis, using a software-based evaluation tool, can help inform
 grid stakeholders of potential benefits of advanced technologies and facilitate their scaling,
 extension, and replication.

 By applying Grid Architecture principles and conducting structural assessments of pilot and
 demonstration projects, information can be systematically collected and organized. This
 approach allows insights into requirements, dependencies, and capabilities to be queried and
 reviewed by stakeholders. Consequently, it increases the awareness and understanding of
 various components, software, and processes vital to technology integration and adoption,
 thereby informing better decision-making and reducing costs and barriers associated with the
 transformation of the electricity delivery system.

 1.1 Grid Architecture and Industry Structures
 System architecture provides the conceptual framework that outlines the structure, behavior,
 and key constraints of a system. It aims to facilitate the understanding of the system's overall
 configuration, attributes, and the interactions among its components. System architectures are
 typically documented and specified in a formal manner. The discipline of systems engineering
 includes core principles and methodologies for the Grid Architecture development process.

 Grid Architecture1, developed under prior DOE projects, is the application of system architecture
 principles, network theory, and control theory to the electric power grid. It represents a high-
 level description of the entire power system, spanning multiple interdependent networks of
 structures serving as a crucial tool to comprehend and define the complex interactions. Grid
 Architecture provides methods to document and specify the highest level of the grid's
 architecture, allowing for systematic analysis of the complex interactions across the current and
 evolving grid. These interactions include the electrical infrastructure itself, supporting
 infrastructures, and other financial, jurisdictional, and regulatory dependencies. Figure 1
 provides examples of what is included in the Grid Architecture structures.

 management frameworks that can be applied to software-based solutions.

 1.2 Data and Knowledge Management
 Knowledge Management is a well-established discipline to facilitate the collection, organization,
 sharing, and analysis of knowledge. The Grid Architecture Mapping to Understand
 Transformation (GAMUT) Tool will leverage the existing developments in software tools as well
 as the developed knowledge/information that’s available through Grid Architecture, technology
 standards, and other materials. The choice of knowledge management approach and tools will
 dictate the information organization within the GAMUT Tool. The actual approach will be
 determined as part of the feasibility study and will include an assessment of multiple use cases
 to evaluate various engineering tradeoffs associated with these approaches.

 One of the examples is graph-based knowledge management approaches that offer beneficial
 outcomes to managing information and making it accessible to GAMUT’s users. Knowledge

 2.0 Project Overview
 The GAMUT project aims to establish feasibility and, with approval, deliver a minimum-viable-
 product for an approach for systematically documenting and understanding the operating
 context, specifications, processes, benefits, and impacts of various technologies and solutions
 to support grid modernization. Grid Architecture fundamental concepts will be applied in a
 systematic way to analyze and understand the various components, interdependent systems,
 and interactions of new technology, including hardware, software, and processes, being
 deployed in the electric system. This methodology also will enable stakeholders to measure the
 effectiveness of different technology deployment scenarios in relation to a future grid vision and
 how these solutions can be more easily scaled and implemented across jurisdictions. A
 framework for systematic application of Grid Architecture techniques and structural
 assessments will support collecting and organizing information, providing insights into
 requirements, dependencies, and capabilities.

 2.1 Project Process Structure
 The project structure was developed with the objective of a feasibility study for the development
 of a software tool (the GAMUT Tool) to enhance understanding of technologies that will aid in
 cost-effective transformation of the electric system. As is typical with software and systems
 design endeavors, the project team must first understand the objective and constraints to
 develop a list of requirements. The stakeholders at DOE Office of Electricity worked with the
 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory team to draft the initial set of requirements included in
 this document. These requirements will inform the initial information collection process.

 As Grid Architecture is a complex process spanning the seven structures shown in Figure 1,
 context will be needed to define the intent for each topic’s information request from stakeholder
 groups. The information collection process draft will provide the project team an opportunity to
 work with technology developers and users to refine the questions as well as the context and
 other guidance that the GAMUT Tool will eventually use. As an iterative process, there will be
 multiple rounds of review with various technologies to broadly cover all the Grid Architecture
 topics. The various iterative process components are shown in Figure 2.

 Once a general information collection process is agreed upon, the information collected through
 the validation process will be used to understand how it should be organized. The knowledge
 organization database will need to be able to associate topics and enable a variety of queries,
 as well as provide an overview summary of the technology. This will be organized according to
 the Grid Architecture-based structural attributes and other details related to advanced
 technology pilots and demonstrations. Once again, this process will be iterative and require
 validation with stakeholders, including the technology developers, project sponsors, partners,
 and utility partners who would provide inputs and benefit from outputs.

 The feasibility study will include a discussion of technical and financial feasibility aspects, with
 details related to risks and their mitigations. Tool development recommendations will include
 advantages and disadvantages of available knowledge management methodologies, including a
 discussion of input ingestion, output association, and database management alternatives. It is
 expected that both the technical assessment and stakeholder review recommendations will lead
 to a go/no-go decision and a report, including selection of the recommended potential solutions.

 2.2 GAMUT Stakeholder Perspectives
 The GAMUT Tool is designed to support a variety of stakeholders at different interaction points.
 Those providing input will include a diverse set of stakeholders such as technology developers,
 technology pilot project managers, contractors, utilities, and other jurisdictional entities. These
 users will not only contribute information but will also benefit from understanding gaps in their
 knowledge about dependencies and implications.

 As this scope is limited to a feasibility assessment, the subset of users, both input and output
 stakeholders, will be constrained to a manageable number within a few key technology
 domains. For the feasibility study, a set of projects will be engaged to validate the interrogation
 process. The projects cover topics such as distribution control schemes, dynamic line ratings,
 and other innovative technologies.

 The GAMUT Tool is expected to facilitate advanced technology development and deployment
 and identify associated gaps or challenges. One of the key benefits of Grid Architecture is its
 ability to highlight interdependencies or systemic issues. For example, technology developers
 may not be fully aware of regulatory constraints or market-related benefits.

 While Grid Architecture offers a comprehensive approach to designing, operating, and
 optimizing electrical grids, it is not expected that all users will be experts on the subject.
 Therefore, stakeholder expectation of the GAMUT Tool is to interact with standardized inputs,
 where appropriate. The structures from Grid Architecture can help to streamline and facilitate
 the data collection process, without requiring specialized in-depth guidance to understand the
 context of the input.

 Due to intellectual property and electric power system security constraints, user roles and
 associated access privileges will also need to be defined to manage access by sub-populations
 within the stakeholder community.

Project Overview 5

 2.2.1 Information Provider Perspective

 It is assumed that the information provider will need guidance related to Grid Architecture and
 its structures. Relevant context will be provided to them to help inform the information they
 provide. This will include a general description of the type of information required and its
 intended use.

 This guidance benefits the input provider by helping them identify topics for further exploration,
 such as markets, regulatory environment, or hidden coupling among industry structures. This
 allows for a more holistic understanding of the technology without requiring the input provider to
 become a Grid Architecture expert.

 Similar to other software “wizard”-type guidance, a user may need to return to the tool at a later
 date, or another information provider may be asked to provide input on a specific topic. For
 example, a project performer might provide details about a technology, while a partner utility
 might provide system or jurisdictional details.

 The user interface should cater to this variability in input providers through lookup mechanisms.
 Examples include a protected link for adding certain information or a feature for inviting or
 requesting access. To encourage maximum participation, every effort should be made to
 identify and protect sensitive information and intellectual property.

 2.2.2 Output User Perspective

 Like the information provider, the output user will also need guidance on the various topics
 available for query. In addition to queries, the output user will also benefit from a technology
 summary or a technology deployment playbook (i.e., CliffsNotes). This summary should be able
 to give an overview of all Grid Architecture network structure topics as they are requested.

 For the feasibility study, the initial output user will be considered as DOE technology experts
 looking to better understand pilot projects or demonstrations that are nearing readiness for
 large-scale deployment. Use cases may include their impact, best practices, lessons learned,
 challenges, and any jurisdictional or systemic issues that were discovered during a pilot or
 similar demonstration. While this is the initial intent, all considerations should be given to making
 the output user base and objectives flexible and extensible. Additional examples of users
 include planners, engineers, regulators, and others that provide critical feedback during usability
 studies, procurements, and other approvals.

 There are a variety of opportunities to benefit from knowledge of advanced grid technologies,
 ranging from simple time-saving reviews and comparisons to queries that are yet to be
 developed. In this way, queries should be categorized and dynamic, perhaps using keyword
 searches and topical searches for the feasibility study, but with consideration of how this may be
 extended beyond to use machine learning and artificial intelligence based searches.

 2.3 Develop Software Tool Suite Requirements
 The requirements specification will define the functionalities and capabilities required for the
 software tools, ensuring that the tools can effectively support the documentation, analysis, and
 comparative assessment of grid solutions. Additionally, a feasibility evaluation will assess the
 practicality and feasibility of developing the proposed software tool suite, evaluating technical
 challenges, resource requirements, and potential benefits.

Project Overview 6

 Software tools that manage databases and resources already exist, including artificial
 intelligence tools that can provide interpretations and responses. As part of the feasibility
 assessment, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory will provide recommendations relating to the
 level of complexity, steps that should be taken, and timeline for the program project to provide
 the most useful resource that can be reasonably provided. An example of a basic reference tool
 is the SEPA Catalog of Standards 1, while a complex large language model tool such as
 ChatGPT can provide reasonable summaries of complicated documents or topics. Risk
 management associated with improper application of large language models and the false
 information (hallucination) issue also needs to be considered.

 2.4 Information Collection and Management Structure
 The information collection process will be set up to be information carrier type agnostic. Initial
 collection efforts will be carried out with the guidance and contribution from DOE and technology
 development project teams (including case studies). At the initial stage of the project, the
 collection process is envisioned to be explorative and qualitative rather than quantitative and
 rigorous. Comprehensive information collection and processing approaches are planned to be
 part of the overall feasibility assessment.

 The main sources of information are documentation and written artifacts (including Grid
 Architecture materials, standards, reports, diagrams, etc.), including the documents provided by
 technology project participant teams (technology descriptions, lessons learned, reports, etc.).
 Another significant part of the information source will be case studies and interactions with
 providers of the specific grid solutions. This means capturing narratives and information that are
 not well documented or even defined, for example, lessons learned, anecdotal/systemic issues,
 as well as subject matter expert interviews. For the collection process, we envision using the
 structured Grid Architecture framework, as well as developing a methodology that’s a good fit
 for any chosen knowledge management solution.

 The information inputs will have to be decomposed into their relevant elements, according to
 Grid Architecture structures and consistent with the knowledge management framework. The
 decomposition process will be qualitative at first to understand the feasibility of potential use of
 machine learning tools to reliably perform this task. Information that is decomposed can later be
 retrieved through queries that might not come from the initial context, enabling the overall
 solution to be a lot more future-proof and manageable for GAMUT users.

 All information contained in GAMUT will need to be managed to ensure their security, privacy,
 and availability. Information management is a cross-cutting process that will be applied
 throughout the collection, processing, analysis, presentation, and other steps in the information
 lifecycle throughout the GAMUT project. Access to information needs to be controlled,
 especially when information is obtained through a non-disclosure agreement or other process
 that limits proliferation. The project team acknowledges that there will be many potentially
 complex information management decisions that are not obvious at the moment. Feasibility
 assessment will include assessment of the main decisions (e.g., information use under non-
 disclosure agreements, business sensitive, intellectual property, etc.) that will have an impact on
 the overall feasibility.

 2.5 Case Studies
 To validate and demonstrate the proposed methodology, the project will conduct a series of
 case studies. Specific grid solutions will be selected, and the documentation and assessment
 methodology will be applied to analyze the design, implementation, and integration of these
 solutions within the Grid Architecture. These case studies will be performed in partnership with
 technology solution development teams and those who were involved in the deployment. It is
 likely that the discussions will be staged, with introductory meetings, working sessions, and
 unsupervised use by stakeholders. All stages would collect feedback and likely require iterative
 refinement. The case studies will demonstrate the practical application and effectiveness of the
 methodology, highlighting the benefits and insights gained from this structured approach.

 2.6 Feasibility Study
 Ultimately, the project will determine if knowledge management software tools, information
 sources and approaches are practical to be developed to support this application of the Grid
 Architecture concepts in the GAMUT Tool. If feasible, the tool(s) would be beneficial for
 developing insights and supporting decision-making for various stakeholders, including funding
 sources, technology developers, grid operators, engineers, and policymakers. These tools
 would be expected to aid in the identification of interdependencies in the structural relationships
 (shown in Figure 1); dependencies between different grid components (e.g., generation,
 transmission, distribution); scalability to ensure that the technology can support and be
 integrated with current and future capacity, loads, and technologies; security, to improve any
 solution default security posture at any project stage; and resilience to withstand and recover
 from adverse conditions.

 The feasibility study should include:
 • technical feasibility for the specific application of Grid Architecture
 • results of stakeholder workshops and discussions
 • database techniques
 • a financial assessment that includes a cost analysis and budget planning for certain solutions
 • risk identification and mitigation recommendations
 • case study-based assessment of the knowledge management strategy.

 Development recommendations should include:
 • methods, structure, and database management requirements
 • a detailed assessment of compared software options
 • actionable recommendations based on the feasibility study
 • the preferred technology stack, estimated budget, project timelines, and suggested features
 to be prioritized.

 The feasibility study will provide an assessment of multiple solution domain architecture
 tradeoffs, case study experience, and requirements verification/validation. By taking a holistic
 approach to the software tooling feasibility study, proposed structures and capabilities for a
 software tool development that complies with identified and validated requirements can be
 offered. This feasibility study will include a minimum-viable-product and additional capabilities

 3.1 Systematic Approach to Knowledge Management
 During project planning and execution, the team will adopt an informal systems engineering
 lifecycle process to ensure a systematic approach and mitigate project risks. Initially, information
 on requirements, needs, and expectations will be gathered to fully comprehend the problem
 domain before finalizing the solution architecture for GAMUT.

 Figure 3. General approach to “GAMUT System”

 The individual components of inputs and outputs will be described in more detail in the next
 document chapters. The “Inputs” section covers any information that needs to be processed by
 the GAMUT Tool. We also include any potential tool use or applied external
 methodologies/approaches to enable “input” as a functionality. Defining and capturing
 information for the “Outputs” section ensures that the GAMUT Tool produces the necessary
 information/artifacts to satisfy stakeholder expectations/needs. “Enabler” and “Constraint”
 sections will be part of the solution space documentation to capture relevant information from
 adjacent systems that can either enable additional functionality/opportunities or limit GAMUT
 Tool operations (e.g., legal or regulatory requirements). This approach helps capture the entire
 system considerations for GAMUT and avoid silos. Refinements are expected to be continuous
 throughout the project lifecycle, as more information becomes available.

 The information related to innovative grid technologies and their deployment will be captured
 with guidance related to Grid Architecture structures and principles and preferred formats for
 documentation. Additionally, the prompts to provide input will aid in identifying gaps or missing
 information. This iterative process is depicted in Figure 4.

 3.3 Concept of Operation
 Figure 5 depicts a potential interaction format for the scope of a specific technology pilot
 demonstration deployment assessment. This description provides an insight into how the
 GAMUT Tool could be used by a user (not how the GAMUT Tool itself works). The GAMUT
 Tool output user in Figure 5 is providing initial questions about a specific or generic topic. In the
 most minimalistic approach, the input provider may use the GAMUT Tool to improve project
 technology documentation without oversight from the GAMUT Tool output user (e.g., DOE) and
 can provide the outcomes directly to stakeholders. In this case, an information provider user
 interacts with the GAMUT Tool to prepare the information and improve documentation by
 providing questions, clarifications, and addressing gaps. An output user may also be part of the
 process as a reviewer of the documentation.

 In a more involved use case, an output user may utilize the GAMUT Tool to interact with an
 information provider user (e.g., a project performer) and request to provide information
 considered input for the GAMUT Tool. The project performer is interacting with the GAMUT Tool
 directly and is subject to identified input requirements that are defined by the GAMUT Tool
 interface. This means that the user needs to submit the necessary information according to the
 GAMUT Tool information structure (influenced by the implemented knowledge management
 approach). Then the GAMUT Tool ingests the information and provides the output user with
 available notes, feedback, information gaps, or suggestions for further actions. Since the
 information circulation permits question/answer structure on all user interfaces, it is possible that
 the output user can generate requests for additional details to an information provider through
 the GAMUT Tool. The main benefits from utilizing GAMUT Tool versus a direct communication
 would be the structured and systemic approach to addressing the missing information and
 providing the necessary context to avoid potential misunderstanding. The process is iterative,
 and in each step the GAMUT Tool may be used to provide relevant context or missing

 4.0 Conclusion and Next Steps
 The electric power system is experiencing significant changes, driven by a changing generation
 mix, increased reliance on information and communication technologies, new customer
 demands, growing load, and an ever-changing threat landscape. To meet these challenges,
 stakeholders are poised to make substantial investments in innovative grid technologies to
 ensure a flexible, reliable, and resilient electricity delivery system.

 DOE aims to support this transformation through the development of knowledge management
 tools, such as the GAMUT Tool, based on Grid Architecture principles. These tools will facilitate
 the systematic documentation, understanding, and deployment of innovative grid technologies.
 By improving our grasp of dependencies, interactions, and systemic issues, these tools will aid
 stakeholders in making informed decisions and optimizing technology integration.

 Initial phases of this project focus on a feasibility study to evaluate the development of a
 software tool suite that organizes and manages information related to various grid technologies.
 By involving a diverse group of stakeholders—ranging from technology developers to utility
 operators—the project aims to create a comprehensive framework for the effective
 documentation and analysis of technology pilots, demonstrations, and deployments.

 Once validated, the GAMUT Tool will provide significant benefits, including identifying gaps and
 challenges, offering insights into best practices, and facilitating knowledge sharing among
 stakeholders. Additionally, it will cater to multiple stakeholder perspectives, ensuring broad utility
 and engagement.

 4.1 Next Steps
 The project team will finalize the requirements gathering process, bounded by the limits of a
 feasibility study. Any future requirements identified will appear in an appendix to the feasibility
 study, retained for future development. This process includes engaging with stakeholders to
 refine and confirm the initial set of tool requirements through workshops and interviews to
 collect their input. This input will also help the project team to establish guidance and
 educational materials to help stakeholders understand and effectively use the tool.

 We will then draft the GAMUT Tool structure, which will require iterative development of
 information collection processes. This includes developing and testing the initial information
 collection process with a select group of technology developers and users. The project team will
 refine the process based on feedback and expand to cover more Grid Architecture topics.

 The feasibility study will then assess technical and financial feasibility, potential risks, and
 mitigation strategies. The project team will utilize the case studies to validate the proposed
 methodology and demonstrate practical applications. This will lead to tool development
 recommendations, focusing on key features, technology stacks, and timelines. It will also
 include a summary of an evaluation of different knowledge management methodologies and
 recommend the most suitable ones for implementation.

 4.2 Future Work
 Stakeholder engagement will continue to be an important component of the GAMUT Tool
 development. This will aid in the identification and definition of additional roles, capabilities, and

 requirements. Defining user roles and access privileges will ensure the protection of sensitive
 information and intellectual property. Implementation and testing will also be important when

 pilot tests to gather user feedback and make necessary adjustments.
