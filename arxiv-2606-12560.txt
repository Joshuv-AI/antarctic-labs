The state/defect correspondence


Abstract
We formulate a one-to-one correspondence between states and defects for higher-form gauge theories in arbitrary dimensions. The correspondence is not predicated on conformal invariance, as these theories are in general not conformal. Instead, it relies on the existence of infinitely many conserved charges associated with the mixed anomaly of electric and magnetic higher-form symmetries. In p p -form Maxwell theory, these charges generate an extended Kac–Moody algebra that acts simultaneously on states and on extended operators. We show that this algebra organizes the Hilbert space on S p × S d − p − 1 S^{p}\times S^{d-p-1} into highest-weight representations allowing for a direct identification between states and p p -dimensional defects. In particular, Wilson–’t Hooft defects dressed with local gauge-invariant operators are mapped to squeezed energy eigenstates. We further relate these novel symmetries to higher-spin currents and demonstrate that their construction persists in a class of interacting non-linear electrodynamics theories.

2.2
Including interactions

At first sight, the infinite family of dressed charges constructed above may look like an artifact of the free theory. This is a reasonable suspicion. We have just seen that the conserved currents

2.7

,
3
,
4

The story extends most directly to non-linear (higher-form) electrodynamics

(2.42)

is a smooth function of

This class of theories has two standard origins. In the allowed dimensions, namely

\displaystyle S=\int_{X}*\mathrm{P}\quantity(\u,w)~,\qquad w=*(f\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}f)~.

(2.43)

This applies, for instance, to a photon (

\displaystyle\differential*\quantity(\kappa f)=0~,

\displaystyle\differential\widetilde{\eta}+2\pi i\,\kappa\quantity(\u)\,*\differential\eta=0~.

(2.48)

For

(2.51)

In the interacting version of the dressed charges, the electric field is replaced by the non-linear displacement field

Finally, let us reinstate the dependence on

\displaystyle\theta\quantity(\u,w)=2\,\partial_{w}\mathrm{P}\quantity(\u,w)~,

(2.54)

\displaystyle\differential{\tilde{\eta}}+2\pi i\quantity( \kappa*\dd{\eta}+ \theta\dd{\eta})=0~,

(2.56)

More generally, any interaction that changes only the local constitutive relation between currents and field strengths, while preserving current conservation, preserves the infinite family of charges in a deformed form. The examples above do not exhaust such interactions, but they cover a large class. By contrast, dynamical charged matter sources the electric current, while dynamical magnetic defects source the Bianchi identity. Such couplings typically disrupt the construction, and the infinite family of charges ceases to exist.

The dressed-charge algebra

Returning to the dressed charges constructed in

The commutator of

\commutator{\cQ_\eta}{\cQ_{\zeta}}=-i\,\Gamma(\eta,\zeta)~,

\Gamma(\eta,\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\quantity(\eta\w\dd\tilde{\zeta}- \zeta\w\dd\tilde{\eta})~.

We end this section with a preliminary comment which we will expand upon in

Quantization and the Hilbert space

The current algebra of the previous section plays an essential role in organizing the spectrum of the theory. We now put these ingredients to work to build up the first half of the story: the spectrum of states living on
\Sigma
. To this end, we now switch to Lorentzian signature (with mostly-plus convention), considering

, where
\Sigma
is a closed, compact Cauchy slice upon which we quantize the theory.

For

30

\displaystyle H_{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}\quantity(\norm{\f{E}{p}}^2_\Sigma+\norm{\f{B}{d-p-2}}^2_\Sigma),

and
\widetilde{J}_{r}

equation

2.22

\begin{split}H_{\Sigma}&=2\pi\sum_{n\sigma}\frac{1}{2}(\mathtt{J}_{n\sigma})^{2}\norm{\Phi_{\inda\sigma}}_{\Sigma}^{2}\\
&=2\pi\quantity[ \sum_{i=1}^{b_{\pp}} j_{0,i}^2 +\sum_{k=1}^{b_{p+1}} \tilde{\jmath}_{0,k}^{\kern 2pt 2} +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\substack{\inda\\ \mu_\inda\neq 0}} \qty\Big(\qty(\J_{\inda+})^2 + \qty(\J_{\inda-})^2)]~.\\
\end{split}

(4.3)

We have written

and

The above expression of the Hamiltonian motivates defining ladder operators as combinations of the current modes:

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity\big(\J_{\inda+}-i(-1)^p \J_{\inda-})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity\big(\J_{\inda+}+i(-1)^p \J_{\inda-})~,\end{split}

(4.5)

These satisfy the expected commutation relations for ladder operators:

(4.6)

which follows directly from the commutator of the modes in

3.5

\mathrm{U}(1)_{m}

4.1
Interlude: spontaneous symmetry breaking

The by-now-standard understanding of a free
p
-form gauge field is as a Goldstone mode for the spontaneous breaking of a
p
-form symmetry
[
75
,
107
,
91
,
116
]
. In other words, the higher-form Maxwell theory we have been considering is best seen as the Goldstone effective field theory for the corresponding higher-form symmetry. This effective theory is valid in the low-energy regime, below some UV cutoff
\Lambda
, here encoded in the coupling constant,
\mathrm{g}^{2}\sim\Lambda^{2p+2-d}
. However, a generalization of the Coleman–Mermin–Wagner (CMW) theorem
[
75
,
107
]
(see also
[
30
]
for a recent entropic proof) forbids the symmetry breaking of continuous
p
-form symmetries in dimensions
d\leqslant p+2
. The quantization on
S^{p}\times S^{d-p-1}
presented above already hints at the higher-CMW theorem. Concretely, let us compare the energy of the primary states to those of the first excited states. Let
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
denote the radii of
S^{p}
and
S^{d-p-1}
, respectively, and take
R_{2}\gg R_{1}
. We then have:

\begin{split}{\text{energy of primary}}\,\,\Delta_{e}&\sim\frac{1}{R_{2}}\frac{\quantity(\Lambda R_1)^{p}}{(\Lambda R_{2})^{d-p-2}}\,e^{2}\\
{\text{energy of 1\textsuperscript{st} excited state}}&\sim\frac{1}{R_{2}}\frac{\quantity(\Lambda R_1)^{p}}{(\Lambda R_{2})^{d-p-2}}\,e^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{d-p-1}}{R_{2}}~,\end{split}

(4.19)

where we also used the fact that the first excited state is obtained by exciting a single oscillator mode with unit angular momentum on
S^{d-p-1}
, for which
\mu_{n}=\sqrt{d-p-1/R_{2}^{2}}
(cf.
appendix

B
). In the thermodynamic limit, where
\Lambda R_{2}\gg 1
with
\Lambda R_{1}
kept fixed, we see that the primary states are parametrically lighter than the first excited states if and only if
d>d_{\text{crit}}=p+2
, establishing the correct lower critical dimension. See also
[
111
,
30
]
for similar arguments.

At finite volume, the symmetry is restored (when
d>d_{\text{crit}}
, and trivially preserved otherwise) and there is a single vacuum
\ket{e=0}
. In the strict infinite volume limit, and in
d>d_{\text{crit}}
, while the primary states become gapless, they do not constitute vacua. Instead, the primaries can be rearranged into the symmetry-breaking vacua,

\ket{\theta}=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{ie\theta}\ket{e}~,

(4.20)

where

U_{\alpha}\ket{\theta}=\ket{\theta+\alpha}~.

(4.21)

In the case
p=0
, one can explicitly check that the states
\ket{\theta}
satisfy cluster decomposition. The analogous statement for
p>0
is that large Wilson defects, serving as order parameters for the breaking of the
p
-form symmetry, exhibit perimeter-law behavior on these states.

5.3

5.5
Squeeze ’em

The operators
\mathcal{B}_{n},\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{n}
are a Bogoliubov transformation of the state operators
\mathcal{A}_{n},\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{n}
. Equivalently, the relation

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{n}&=\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})\mathcal{A}_{n}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}_{n}(r_{0})\\
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{n}&=\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}_{n}(r_{0})~.\end{split}

(5.63)

The operator
\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})
is known as a

[
49
,
34
]
,
25
25

25

In principle, one could include an extra factor in

\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})=\exp[\frac{\pi}{\mu_{n}(r_{0})}\quantity(\zeta_\inda\qty\big(\cA_\inda^\dagger)^2 -\zeta_\inda^* \qty\big(\cA_\inda)^2)]~,

(5.64)

where

\zeta_{n}=\rho_{n}\mathrm{e}^{i\psi_{n}}~.

(5.65)

Matching the Bogoliubov transformation with the radial solution gives

\psi_{n}=0~,\quad\zeta_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\log(\frac{\mu_{\inda}(r_0)\cI_{\inda}(r_0)}{\cI'_{\inda}(r_0)})}~.

(5.66)

Thus,

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})&=\exp[\frac{\pi\,\zeta_{n}}{\mu_{n}(r_{0})}\quantity( \qty\big(\cA_\inda^\dagger)^2 -\qty(\cA_\inda)^2)]\\
&=\exp[\frac{i(-1)^{p}\,\pi\,\zeta_{n}}{\mu_{n}(r_{0})}\quantity(\J_{\inda+}\J_{\inda-}+\J_{\inda-}\J_{\inda+})]~.\end{split}

(5.67)

Despite being defined on a codimension-1 manifold, this operator is equivalent to an operator inserted on
S^{p}
at

S^{p}
, where it will define a
p
-dimensional operator due to its singularities at

The extended operators of the theory are organized by creation and annihilation operators
\mathcal{B}_{n}
,
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\dagger}
, into squeezed versions of the modes that organize the states.

As a sanity check, the above construction reduces to the usual 2d CFT state-operator correspondence. In 2d CFTs, necessarily with
p=0
, the operators acting on local insertions in the Euclidean path integral are simply
\mathcal{A}_{n},\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{n}
. They can be radially evolved inward without any squeezing. Indeed, when
p=0

\gamma_{n}=0
(recall,
\gamma_{n}
is the eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
S^{p}

equation

B.12
). In this case

\rho_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{d-p-2-2h}{2\sqrt{\nu_{n}}}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{d-p-2-2h}{2\sqrt{\nu_{n}}}\right)^{2}+1}\right)~,

(5.68)

which is independent of
r_{0}
. Imposing further

\left.\mathcal{B}_{n}\vphantom{\Big|}\right\rvert_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=2\\

p=0\end{subarray}}=\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{n}~.

(5.69)

In all other cases, including the conformal

The squeezing operator built above has the effect of squeezing a single mode, labeled by
n

, as

\mathcal{S}(r_{0})=\prod_{n\neq 0}\mathcal{S}_{n}(r_{0})~.

(5.70)

As ladder operators with different quantum numbers commute,

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\mathcal{S}(r_{0})\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\,\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(r_{0}),\qquad{\text{for all}}\ n~,

(5.71)

and similarly for their adjoints.

6
The state-defect correspondence

Everything so far has been building up the pieces to define a state-defect correspondence for
p
-form Maxwell theories in general dimensions. Now, we can collect these pieces and fit them together to get the full picture, explicitly mapping out the relation between operators and states.

6.1
Preparing the ground state

Let us start off by showing the relation between the ground state
\ket{0}
and the state prepared by the empty path integral. At finite volume, the ground state is unique. With respect to the local Hamiltonian
equation

4.1
, this is the state,
\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}
, satisfying

\mathcal{A}_{n}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=0~\quad\forall n~,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad j_{0}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=0~.

(6.1)

We have added a subscript to keep track of the set of oscillators that annihilate the state.

Interestingly, the above ground state does not exactly correspond to the empty path integral. Indeed, taking
e=0
in the Wilson-operator state
equation

5.8
described above corresponds to the identity operator, and hence the empty path integral. As shown there, this state is annihilated by the
\mathcal{B}
-set of oscillators. With the intuition about squeezing operators built above, this state corresponds to an
all-mode squeezed vacuum

[
92
,
153
]
,

\ket{0}_{\mathcal{B}}=\ket{\1}=\int\mathinner{\mathcal{D}a}\,\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\mathcal{\,}=\,\mathcal{S}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}~.

(6.2)

One can immediately see that this state has exactly the properties of
\ket{W_{e=0}}=\ket{\1}
. In particular:

\mathcal{B}_{n}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{B}_{n}\,\mathcal{S}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}_{n}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=0~,\qquad{\text{for all}}\ n~,

(6.3)

as well as,

j_{0}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{B}}=0~,

(6.4)

since
j_{0}
commutes with all the oscillators.

Dually, the lowest energy state is prepared by inserting the squeezing operator itself into the path integral, and shrinking it onto
S^{p}
. Inverting the above relation, one gets,

\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=\int\mathinner{\mathcal{D}a}\,\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}~.

(6.5)

Recall that the squeezing operator (and its adjoint) is made out of the modes
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, or equivalently, out of the modes of the dressed charges. As such, it too can be used both as a codimension-1 operator acting on the Hilbert space, as in eq.
equation

6.2
, and as a
p
-dimensional defect, upon collapsing it to its locus of singularities. The latter viewpoint is the one used in
equation

6.5
. Explicitly,
\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}
takes the form

\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}=\exp(-\sum_{\inda\neq 0} \frac{\ii(-1)^p\pi\zeta_\inda}{\mu_\inda(r_0)} \int_{\Sigma_r(x)}\int_{\Sigma_r(y)} \qty\big[f_{\inda+}(r,x)f_{\inda-}(r,y)+f_{\inda-}(r,x)f_{\inda+}(r,y)])

(6.6)

with

f_{n\pm}(r,x)=\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\phi_{n\pm}(r,x)\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}*_{\Sigma_{r}}J_{r}(r,x)+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\mathrm{g}}\widetilde{\phi}_{n\pm}(r,x)\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}*_{\Sigma_{r}}\widetilde{J}_{r}(r,x)~.

(6.7)

In the limit
r\rightarrow 0
, it defines a genuine
p
-dimensional defect.

With this, we find that indeed,

\mathcal{A}_{n}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=0~,\qquad\forall\ n~,

(6.8)

while, of course, we still have
j_{0}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}=0
. Hence, the state prepared by
equation

6.5
is the ground state of the Hamiltonian
equation

4.1
.
We conclude that for the ground state, the correspondence is

\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}\leftrightsquigarrow\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}~.

(6.9)

This result, while seemingly exotic, agrees with the existing literature. Indeed, in
[
26
]
the authors attempt to define a state-operator correspondence for a 3d CFT with the spatial slice
T^{2}
, and find that the empty path integral does not correspond to the vacuum state for
\Sigma=S^{1}\times S^{1}

6.2
Building the Hilbert space

Having understood the preparation of the ground state, building the rest of the Hilbert space is straightforward. To summarize, in
section

4

d\neq 2p+2
, states are graded by their electric charges. Primary states
\ket{e}
are those that are annihilated by all lowering operators,

\mathcal{A}_{n}\ket{e}=0\qquad\forall n\neq 0~,

(6.10)

and acting on them with zero modes returns their electric charge,

j_{0}\ket{e}=\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{2}\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\opbraces{\volume}(S^{p})}{\opbraces{\volume}(S^{d-p-1}_{r_{0}})}}\,e\ket{e}~.

(6.11)

On the other hand, the states prepared by insertions of Wilson surfaces in the path integral are killed by the annihilation operator
\mathcal{B}_{n}

\mathcal{B}_{n}\ket{W_e}=0\qquad\forall n\neq 0~,

(6.12)

while the action of the zero mode

j_{0}\ket{W_e}=\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\opbraces{\volume}(S^{p})}{\opbraces{\volume}(S^{d-p-1}_{r_{0}})}}\,e\ket{W_e}~.

(6.13)

This fact lends itself to the interpretation of Wilson surfaces as primary operators with respect to (a different representation of) the same Kac–Moody algebra.

The squeezing relation between the two sets of oscillators implies that

\ket{W_e}=\mathcal{S}\ket{e}~.

(6.14)

In other words, primary
p
-dimensional operators prepare all-mode squeezed primary states, or dually, primary states correspond to Wilson surfaces dressed with squeezing operators:

\ket{e}\leftrightsquigarrow\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\times W_{e}~.

(6.15)

It is time to bring back the excluded case

\ket{e,m}

\ket{N_{\inda}, e,m}=\prod_{n}\quantity(\cA^{\dagger}_{\inda})^{N_{n}}\ket{e,m}~.

7
Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we have studied
p
-form gauge theories with continuous symmetries and a mixed anomaly, and we have established that it is precisely this symmetry structure that allows for the state-defect correspondence in this (in general) non-conformal case. In fact, we saw that the existence of such a mixed anomaly implies that there exists an infinite family of conserved charges,
\mathcal{Q}_{\eta}
, with an extended Abelian Kac–Moody-like algebra,

[\mathcal{Q}_{\eta},\mathcal{Q}_{\zeta}]=\frac{-i}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\quantity(\eta\w\dd\tilde{\zeta}- \zeta\w\dd\tilde{\eta})~.

(7.1)

This set of charges is found to act non-trivially on both the states and the extended operators of the theory, allowing us to organize the spectrum into representations of this algebra and facilitating the identification between defects and states. One technical detail that allowed us not only to formulate a state-defect correspondence, but also to match individual states and operators, was that we were able to explicitly solve for the radial evolution of the aforementioned charges (or a combination thereof).

In this paper, we focused mainly on the free case, while also commenting on the construction of dressed charges in a certain class of interacting theories that do not disrupt the overall symmetry structure of the theory. The success of this construction in extending the state-operator correspondence past the confines of conformal invariance (alongside
[
153
,
51
]
) forces us to re-evaluate what underpins this correspondence. It seems probable that, given any QFT, a (typically infinite-dimensional) spectrum-generating algebra of charges would be sufficient.
27
27

27

Of course, it is by no means necessary.

Such a statement is reminiscent of one of the defining features of classical two-dimensional integrability, namely the existence of infinitely many charges in involution. Here, in the free case, we saw that the spectrum-generating algebra implies the infinite tower of higher-spin charges. It would be interesting to see if the above observations have a deeper connection. Given this, it seems imperative to test the idea of a state-defect correspondence in other classes of theories with spectrum-generating algebras. In particular, there are two directions that we find particularly interesting for further study.

First, it would be interesting to find the explicit map between states and (extended) operators in gapped theories—all of the examples provided here were gapless. A paradigmatic example would be to study three-dimensional Maxwell–Chern–Simons (MCS) theory, which has gained interest as a topologically massive gauge theory containing one massive propagating vector field with mass proportional to (the absolute value of) the Chern–Simons (CS) level,
k
,

S_{\text{MCS}}=\int_{X}\quantity(\frac{1}{2\g^2} f\w* f + \frac{ik}{4\pi} a\w f)~.

(7.2)

The addition of a Chern–Simons term serves to break the U(1) electric symmetry to a discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
symmetry. This makes the construction of dressed charges more complicated due to now only having one continuous, conserved current, but it is still possible. The algebra in this case becomes:

\commutator{\cQ_{\eta}}{\cQ_{\zeta}}=\frac{-i}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\left(\eta\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\differential_{\Sigma}\widetilde{\zeta}-\zeta\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\differential_{\Sigma}\widetilde{\eta}\right)+\frac{ik}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\eta\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\zeta~.

(7.3)

The first term is the same as in the pure Maxwell theory, while the second is new arises from the Chern–Simons coupling. Upon mode decomposition, this algebra implies that the zero modes of
Q_{\eta}
will not commute among themselves. Relatedly, the Hilbert space of the theory has a more interesting spectrum of low-lying states. The corresponding map between states and operators remains a bit more subtle for Maxwell–Chern–Simons and other topologically massive theories. Apart from exemplifying the state-defect correspondence in gapped theories, this example is important for another reason. It lives in three dimensions and has
p=1
, meaning it is one of the cases that our construction was unable to reach (
d=2p+1
). The Chern--Simons coupling seems to be mandatory to obtain the correspondence. Presumably, the reason is that, viewed as an EFT of the magnetic conserved current, the CS term is more relevant than the usual Maxwell term, and so it must be included.
28
28

28

Of course, in the deep IR the CS term dominates, the theory is topological, and the state-defect correspondence is straightforward. The interesting observation in this case, is that it persists when the Maxwell term is also present.

This case, along with other instances of gapped systems, will be discussed in forthcoming work
[
13
]
.

Additionally, the construction of the dressed charges
\mathcal{Q}_{\eta}
here was carried out explicitly only for continuous
invertible
symmetries. Non-invertible constructions of the dressed charges are certainly possible (for a construction in the conformal case see
[
92
]
), starting from a variant of generalized Maxwell theory with gauged charge-conjugation. It would be interesting to develop the representation theory of such non-invertible Kac–Moody algebras and study its effects in detail on the spectrum of the theory—both on states and on operators. Relatedly, it would be interesting to extend the question of state-operator correspondence to generic symmetry breaking phases of
more general symmetries, including non-Abelian, and 2-group symmetries.

Apart from possible extensions of our construction, one may ask what it is good for. A first concrete application is entanglement. In CFTs, the state-operator correspondence aids entanglement computations, by replacing the density matrix with a path integral on a manifold with a cut (and a choice of boundary conditions to factorize the Hilbert space
[
123
]
) and, in the case of excited states, local operator insertions. The computation of Rényi entropies is then reduced to the computation of a correlation function of local operators. In gauge theories the situation is much more restrictive. Besides known issues with defining entanglement entropies unambiguously in the presence of edge modes
[
48
,
67
,
134
,
65
]
, there are comparatively few available computations
[
38
,
2
,
66
,
135
,
146
,
30
]
, and even fewer away from the ground state
[
46
,

[

[

Another direction suggested by our construction points to the fusion of defects and the operator product expansion (OPE). One of the standard arguments in CFT establishes the convergence of the OPE, as a consequence of the state-operator correspondence. See
[
131

126

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\cO_1(x)\cO_2(y)\prod_{i}\cO_i(x_i)}=\sum_{k}C_{12}^{k}P(x-y,\partial_{y})\expectationvalue{\cO_k(y)\prod_{i}\cO_i(x_i)}~.

(7.4)

Here

\mathcal{O}_{1}(x)\mathcal{O}_{2}(y)\sim\sum_{k}C_{12}^{k}P(x-y,\partial_{y})\mathcal{O}_{k}(y)~,

(7.5)

Appendix A
The transversal Laplacian

In this appendix, we examine the spectrum of the transversal Laplacian on

, with the product metric

\displaystyle\differential{s_\Sigma^2}=\differential{\Omega_p^2}+r^{2}\differential{\Omega_{\pp}^2}.

(A.1)

We take the radius of

to be unity and that of

to be

, in accordance with the main text. (In reality,

on

, the set of transversal (i.e., co-closed)
k
-forms on
\Sigma

and
equation

2.4

and

Before we jump in, we note a property of the Hodge star operator that will come in handy at several points later on. That is, its action splits when acting on product forms. Namely, if

\displaystyle*_{\Sigma}\quantity(\f{\omega}{\ell} \w\f{\eta}{k})=(-1)^{k(p-\ell)}*_{p}{\omega}_{\quantity[\ell]}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}*_{d-p-1}\,{\eta}_{\quantity[k]}~.

(A.2)

A.1

-form spherical harmonics

We begin by describing the spectrum of eigenvalues and eigenforms of

on a single

\displaystyle\differential^{\dagger}\differential\,\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{n}}=\Lambda^{[k],N}_{\mathsf{n}}\,\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{n}},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\differential^{\dagger}\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{n}}=0.

In the above,

The associated eigenvalues,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{[k],N}_{\mathsf{n}}=(\ell_{1}+k)(\ell_{1}+N-k-1),

\displaystyle\operatorname{deg}\!\quantity(\Lambda^{[k],N}_\sfn)=\frac{(2\ell_{1}+N-1){(\ell_{1}+N-1)}!}{{k}!{(N-k-1)}!{(\ell_{1}-1)}!(\ell_{1}+k)(\ell_{1}+N-k-1)}~.

(A.5)

The eigenforms

that solve the eigenvalue problem in

A.3

-form spherical harmonics
on

. They have been constructed in terms of harmonic polynomial forms in

[

]

First, we write the metric of

\displaystyle\differential{s_{\S^N}^2}=\differential{\theta^2}+\sin^{2}\!{\theta}\,\differential{s_{\S^{N-1}}^2},

(A.6)

recursively, in terms of the line element of

\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{n}}
come in two families. The first possibility is

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\cdots}=(\sin\theta)^{k-2}\quantity(f_{\ell_1\ell_2}(\theta) \dd{\theta}\w\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_2\cdots} + \sin^2\theta\; g_{\ell_1\ell_2}(\theta) \dd{\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_2\cdots}}),

(A.7)

where
\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_{2}\cdots}
are also eigenforms of the transversal Laplacian on

\displaystyle\differential^{\dagger}\differential\,\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_{2}\cdots}=(\ell_{2}+k-1)(\ell_{2}+N-k-1)\,\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_{2}\cdots}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\differential^{\dagger}\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k-1],\ell_{2}\cdots}=0~.

(A.8)

The functions

and

are given as:

\displaystyle f_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\theta)=(\sin\theta)^{\ell_{2}}\;{}_{2}F_{1}\quantity(\ell_1+N+\ell_2-1,\ell_2-\ell_1;\ell_2+\frac{1}{2}N;\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})^2)~,

(A.9)

in terms of the

\displaystyle g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\ell_{2}+k-1)(\ell_{2}+N-k-1)}\quantity{\dv{\theta} + (N-k-1)\cot\theta}f_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\theta)~.

(A.10)

The second family are the eigenforms constructed recursively as

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\cdots}=(\sin\theta)^{k}f_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\theta)\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k],\ell_{2}\cdots}~,

(A.11)

where

\displaystyle\differential^{\dagger}\differential\,\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k],\ell_{2}\cdots}=(\ell_{2}+k)(\ell_{2}+N-k-2)\,\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k],\ell_{2}\cdots},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\differential^{\dagger}\Upsilon^{N-1}_{[k],\ell_{2}\cdots}=0~.

(A.12)

The

-form spherical harmonics can be made orthonormal with the normalization factor:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{N}_{[k],\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}=\quantity(\frac{(\ell_2+k-1)(\ell_2+N-k-1)}{k(\ell_1+k)(\ell_1+N-k-1)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\quantity(\frac{2\ell_1+N-1}{2}\frac{\fact{(\ell_1+\ell_2+N-2)}}{\fact{(\ell_1-\ell_2)}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2^{\ell_{2}+(N-2)/2}\Gamma\quantity(\ell_2+\frac{1}{2}N)}~.

(A.13)

Explicitly, the harmonics

In what follows, we assume that

already includes this prefactor (dropping the hat), such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{n}},\Upsilon^{N}_{[k],\mathsf{m}}\right\rangle=\delta_{\mathsf{n}\mathsf{m}}~.

(A.14)

Since it will be important for the rest of the discussion, we point out that when

again with

Appendix B
The generalized curl operator

Here, we describe in more detail the generalized curl operator
\mathcal{D}_{p}
, introduced in the main text in
equation

2.20
. To start, we present its general definition and the generalized curl eigenvalue equation. Then, we go on to explicitly construct the eigenforms of this operator—of which there are two families for the non-zero modes. Lastly, we calculate certain inner products of these eigenforms which will be useful for our computations of the radial evolution.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed
d-1
-dimensional manifold, and consider the following space of co-closed
k
-forms:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\perp}^{k}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\quantity{\omega\in\Omega^k(\Sigma)\suchthat\dd*\omega=0}.

(B.1)

in line with the previous section, such forms are referred to as transversal. Acting on transversal forms, the Hodge Laplacian,
\Delta_{k}=\differential^{\dagger}\differential+\differential\differential^{\dagger}
, reduces to
\differential^{\dagger}\differential=(-1)^{k(d+1)+1}*\differential*\differential
, and is a self-adjoint operator.

For our purposes, it is useful to introduce a packaging of forms of different degree into one vector-like object. We notate this as,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\perp}^{k,\ell}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\Omega_{\perp}^{k}(\Sigma)\oplus\Omega_{\perp}^{\ell}(\Sigma)~,

(B.2)

where elements in
\Omega_{\perp}^{k,\ell}
are
transversal
(k,\ell)
-forms
(often we omit
“transversal”
for brevity).
It is valid to consider
\Omega_{\perp}^{k,\ell}(\Sigma)
as a Hilbert space, endowed with an inner product which is given by the inner products of
\Omega_{\perp}^{k}
and
\Omega_{\perp}^{\ell}
component-wise.

For such transversal
k,\ell
-forms, a useful operator is the
“square-root”
of the transversal Laplacian, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}\coloneqq\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & (-1)^{k(d-1)+1}*_\Sigma{\dd}_\Sigma\\ *_\Sigma{\dd}_\Sigma& 0\endlx@physics@matrix):\quad\Omega_{\perp}^{k,d-k-2}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\Omega_{\perp}^{k,d-k-2}(\Sigma).

(B.3)

This operator can be thought of as a generalization of the familiar curl operator of three-dimensional calculus,
\curl\sim*_{\Sigma}{\differential}_{\Sigma}
. In three dimensions, the operator
*_{\Sigma}{\differential}_{\Sigma}
is sometimes called the Beltrami operator
[
71
]
. In general dimensions, we will refer to
\mathcal{D}_{k}
as the
generalized curl operator
.

Indeed, by performing the generalized curl twice, we reproduce the Laplacians for the respective form components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{D}_{k}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat[0]{\lapl_k,\lapl_{d-k-2}}\endlx@physics@matrix)~.

(B.4)

Moreover, it is easy to show that
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is self-adjoint, with respect to the inner product on
\Sigma
. Self-adjointness is guaranteed by the choice of sign difference between the two non-zero components of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
. This property implies that any element of
\Omega_{\perp}^{k,d-k-2}
can be validly expanded in eigen-
(k,d-k-2)
-forms of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
.

Another observation is that the spectrum of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is symmetric around zero, due to the fact that it anticommutes with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}
, the Pauli-
z
matrix,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathcal{D}_{k}=-\mathcal{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}~.

(B.5)

We can view the Pauli-
z
matrix as a “chirality” operator, which grades eigenforms of the generalized curl operator—we shall henceforth call these eigenforms
“curly eigenforms”
—by their chirality. In particular, if
\Phi_{n}
is a curly eigenform, with non-zero eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
, then it satisfies the eigenvalue equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}\Phi_{n}=\mu_{n}\Phi_{n},.

(B.6)

The combination
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\Phi_{n}
is a linearly independent eigenform of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, but instead has the eigenvalue
-\mu_{n}
. With this in mind, we will denote the curly eigenforms as
\Phi_{n\sigma}
, where
\sigma=+,-
labels the chirality and therefore the sign of the eigenvalue
\mu_{n\sigma}=\sigma\mu_{n}
. Both
\Phi_{n\pm}
are eigenforms of the Laplacians
\Delta_{k}\oplus\Delta_{D-k-1}
with the same eigenvalue,
\mu^{2}_{n}
.

A Hodge-star identity

A

Appendix C
Radial evolution of the currents

In this appendix, we provide the details on the differential equation governing the radial evolution of the radial component of the currents

This should be viewed as an alternate, complementary approach to the radial evolution of the

5.30

\mathcal{B}_{n}
. There, the
\mathcal{B}_{n}
operators are defined at

(C.1)

To radially evolve the charges
\mathcal{B}
inward to act on states, one way is to give

and
\widetilde{v}_{n}
a radial profile, viewed as functions on

equation

C.1
. This is what was done in
section

5

\mathtt{J}_{n\sigma}
is composed out

and

by taking specific inner products

2.22
, we can solve for the radial profile of the currents instead. These two approaches are complementary. Here we detail the second approach. We note that for the operator
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to be topological, the radial evolution matrix of the currents must be the inverse of that of the coefficients
\quantity(v_\inda,\tilde{v}_\inda)

C.1
The radial evolution equation

We begin by decomposing the conserved currents into their radial and spatial parts:

{J}_{\quantity[p+1]}=\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}J_{r}+J_{\Sigma}\,\quad,\quad{\widetilde{J}}_{\quantity[\pp]}=\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\widetilde{J}_{r}+\widetilde{J}_{\Sigma}~,

(C.2)

where
\widetilde{J}_{\Sigma}
and
J_{\Sigma}
do not have non-zero components along
\differential{r}
.
The two currents are related to one-another by

*J=\frac{i2\pi}{\mathrm{g}^{2}}\widetilde{J}~.

(C.3)

With the above decomposition, this implies,

\begin{split}*_{\Sigma}J_{r}&=\frac{2\pi i}{g^{2}}\widetilde{J}_{\Sigma}\\
\differential{r}*_{\Sigma}J_{\Sigma}&=(-1)^{p+1}\frac{2\pi i}{g^{2}}\differential{r}\widetilde{J}_{r}~.\end{split}

(C.4)

Using these relations, the conservation law of the currents can now be re-expressed as,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\differential*J=0\\[5.0pt]
\differential*\widetilde{J}=0\end{cases}\Longrightarrow\begin{cases}\differential_{\Sigma}*_{\Sigma}J_{r}-\dfrac{2\pi i}{\mathrm{g}^{2}}\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\differential_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}+\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\partial_{r}*_{\Sigma}J_{r}=0\\[5.0pt]
\differential_{\Sigma}*_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}+\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\partial_{r}*_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}+(-1)^{(d-1)(p-1)}\dfrac{i\mathrm{g}^{2}}{2\pi}\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\differential_{\Sigma}J_{r}=0~,\end{cases}

(C.5)

where we have decomposed the exterior derivative as
\differential=\differential{r}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\partial_{r}+\differential_{\Sigma}
. Separating out the radial and
\Sigma
components, the above relation gives the two Gauss’ laws:

\begin{split}\differential_{\Sigma}*_{\Sigma}J_{r}=0~,\\
\differential_{\Sigma}*_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}=0~,\end{split}

(C.6)

and two equations governing the radial evolution of the current components:

\begin{split}\partial_{r}*_{\Sigma}J_{r}-\frac{2\pi i}{\mathrm{g}^{2}}\differential_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}=0\\
\partial_{r}*_{\Sigma}\widetilde{J}_{r}+(-1)^{(d-1)(p-1)}\frac{ig^{2}}{2\pi}\differential_{\Sigma}J_{r}=0~.\\
\end{split}

(C.7)

Enforcing the two Gauss’ laws, i.e. co-closedness of the radial components of the currents, allows us to decompose
J_{r}
and
\widetilde{J}_{r}
in terms of eigenmodes of the generalized curl operator (cf.
appendix

B

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}J_{r}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\mathrm{g}}\widetilde{J}_{r}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{n\sigma}\mathtt{J}_{n,\sigma}\Phi_{n\sigma}~,

(C.8)

where again the factors of
\mathrm{g}
are determined from dimensional analysis. Plugging

C.8

C.7
implies a radial evolution equation for the current modes:

*_{\Sigma}^{-1}\partial_{r}\quantity\big( \J_{\inda,\sigma}*_{\Sigma}\Phi_{\inda,\sigma})+(-1)^{p}\,i\mathtt{J}_{n,\sigma}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathcal{D}_{p}\,\Phi_{n,\sigma}=0~.

(C.9)

Taking the inner product of this with
\Phi_{n+}
and
\Phi_{n-}

\begin{split}\partial_{r}\mathtt{J}_{n+}-\left\langle\Phi_{n+},\partial_{r}\Phi_{n,+}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}\mathtt{J}_{n+}-\left\langle\Phi_{n+},\partial_{r}\Phi_{n,-}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}\mathtt{J}_{n-}-(-1)^{p}i\mu_{n}\mathtt{J}_{n,-}&=0\\
\partial_{r}\mathtt{J}_{n-}-\left\langle\Phi_{n-},\partial_{r}\Phi_{n,+}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}\mathtt{J}_{n+}-\left\langle\Phi_{n-},\partial_{r}\Phi_{n,-}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}\mathtt{J}_{n-}+(-1)^{p}i\mu_{n}\mathtt{J}_{n,+}&=0~,\end{split}

(C.10)

where we have also used
equation

B.21
. Now we can assemble
\mathtt{J}_{n+}
and
\mathtt{J}_{n-}
into a vector,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{n}
and with the aid of the radial Berry connection,

as introduced in

B.33

\quantity(\pd_r - \bbA^\inda)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{n}(r)+(-1)^{p}\mu_{n}(r)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{n}(r)=0~.

(C.11)

C.3
The general solution

In order to solve the differential equation for the radial evolution for non-zero modes, we will go through various redefinitions and changes of basis, to massage

B.33

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{-1}\mathbb{A}^{n}\,\mathsf{H}=\mathbb{A}^{n}_{\text{diag}}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat{\dfrac{d-p-3-2\wdeg}{2r},\dfrac{\pd_r \mu_\inda}{\mu_\inda}-\dfrac{d-p-3-2\wdeg}{2r}}\endlx@physics@matrix)~,\quad\,\quad\mathsf{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\endlx@physics@matrix)~.

Observe, further that the diagonalized Berry connection is, in fact, pure gauge. In particular,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}_{n}+(-1)^{p}\,\mu_{n}(r)\,\quantity[\sfD_\inda(r)^{-1} \, \sfH\vec{\sigma}_y \sfH\sfD_\inda(r)]\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}_{n}(r)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}_{n}+(-1)^{p}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\admat{\ii\, r^{-(d-p-3-2\wdeg)} \mu_\inda^2(r),-\ii\, r^{d-p-3-2\wdeg}}\endlx@physics@matrix)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}_{n}
