 Abstract

  Characterization of Inorganic Solids for Cherenkov-Based
 Radiation Detectors

 Cherenkov radiation is of interest to a variety of radiation detection applica-
tions including treaty monitoring, medicine, high energy physics, pulsed power, and
material science. Proposed is a study to identify new Cherenkov media that can
improve upon the performance of fused silica, which is the most common choice of
solid Cherenkov radiator. Optical transmission, radioluminescence (RL), photolumi-
nescence (PL), photoluminescence excitation (PLE), and thermoluminescence (TL)
measurements would be performed and used to develop detector response functions
for these materials. Then the materials would be ranked in order of their suitability
for use in Cherenkov detectors. Finally, the top material would be used to design
a detector module with optimized light collection for a chosen application and its
performance simulated using MCNP6.

 Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Objective
 The goal of this study is to identify and characterize solid materials that can
be used to generate Cherenkov radiation for fast, high-time-resolution detection of
gamma ray fluxes.

1.2 What is Cherenkov Radiation?
 Cherenkov radiation is luminescence resulting from the movement of a charged
particle through a medium faster than the phase velocity of light in that medium,
cm . Cherenkov1 radiation is also known as “Čerenkov” 2 , “Cerenkov” 3 , or “Vavilov-
Čerenkov” 4 radiation.

 The emission of Cherenkov radiation is shown in Figure 1. A charged particle
(purple) moves to the right with velocity υ > cm . The charged particle’s electric
field causes polarization of nearby material, a disturbance that propagates outward
in all directions (a spherical wavelet, grey dotted). Because the charge particle is
moving faster than the disturbances can propagate, successive wavelets ”pile up” in
the direction of motion. They constructively interfere to form a conical wavefront
(solid grey). The result is a coherent flash of light emitted with angle θ relative to
the original charged particle trajectory.

 Cherenkov radiation is to optics as the sonic boom is to acoustics. A super-
sonic aircraft generates pressure waves in air faster than they can emanate, causing

 As previously stated, the purpose of this study is to identify materials that can
serve as Cherenkov radiators in gamma ray detectors. Because Cherenkov radiation
is produced by charged particles, it is an indirect form of gamma detection. Incident
gamma rays must have interactions in the Cherenkov medium that produce charged
particles (such as photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, pair production, etc.)
to be detected, as it is the latter that create Cherenkov light.

1.3 Cherenkov Detector Applications
 There is a long history of using the Cherenkov effect as a method of radiation
detection, which spans many fields. In most cases, a Cherenkov radiator is used to
convert ionizing radiation into an optical signal and a photomultiplier tube (PMT) is
used to record it.

1.3.1 Treaty Monitoring

 This author and collaborators have previously developed a fused silica Cherenkov
detector system for potential use in treaty monitoring [3]. The system is portable and
is intended for deployment on nuclear weapon physics experiments to prove their com-
pliance with the Comprehensive-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT). The detector measures the
intensity of fission gamma rays coming from an object under test over time, using the
slope trend to infer whether the test was subcritical or supercritical.

1.3.2 Cancer Treatment

 Cherenkov detectors can support efforts to both find and treat cancer.

 Positron emission tomography (PET) is a medical imaging technique where
a patient is injected with a radioactive tracer that accumulates in areas with high
metabolic activity, like tumors. When the radioisotope decays, it emits a positron,
and the PET scanner detects the opposing annihilation gammas. The points where
opposing gamma rays are detected can be connected by a “line of response”. Lines of
response from many radioactive decays intersect at the radioactive source location(s).
The result is a 3D image showing where tumors are located in the body. There is a
related technique called time of flight (TOF) PET, where higher resolution images
can be constructed because the time offset between detection of the two gamma rays
is indicative of the specific point along the line of response where the annihilation
occurred. This method demands picosecond-order time resolution, prompting interest
in Cherenkov-based detectors [4] [5].

 12

 Proton therapy is an advanced, noninvasive cancer treatment that uses high-
energy proton beams instead of X-rays to destroy cancer cells by damaging their DNA.
Bragg peaks allow these particles to deliver maximum energy directly into the tumor
and stop, limiting damage to surrounding healthy tissue and reducing side effects.
However, uncertainties associated with computing the range as well as variations in
clinical implementation motivate the use of range verification methods. Cherenkov
detectors have been studied as a potential prompt-gamma-type verification method
[6].

1.3.3 High Energy Physics

 Cherenkov detectors have been used extensively in the field of high energy
physics, for example, as a means of detecting neutrinos and/or cosmic rays. Low
interaction probabilities require very large detection volumes, making liquid water and
ice practical choices of detector media [7]. Examples include the High Altitude Water
Cherenkov Experiment (HAWC), IceCube, Hyper-Kamiokande and its predecessors,
and the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory.

1.3.4 Pulsed Power Machine Diagnostics

 Cherenkov detectors of various forms have also been used to diagnose oper-
ating characteristics of pulsed power machines. Aerogel Cherenkov detectors have
been deployed as spectral diagnostics on the Cygnus [8] and Mercury [9] pulsed x-ray
sources. Both gaseous [10] and solid [11] Cherenkov detectors have been deployed as
inertial confinement fusion diagnostics at the National Ignition Facility (NIF). Simi-
larly, solid Cherenkov detectors have been deployed on tokamak magnetic confinement
fusion reactors [12] [13].

 13

1.3.5 Material Science

 Positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) is a non-destructive spec-
troscopy technique that allows material properties, such as void fraction, to be stud-
ied on an atomic scale. A radioactive source emits characteristic gamma rays and
positrons, which eventually annihilate with electrons in the sample under test. The
longer the offset between when characteristic gammas and annihilation gammas are
observed (the positron lifetime), the more voids are present. Solid Cherenkov detec-
tors have been used for gamma detection in this application due to their nanosecond-
order time response [14].

2.1 Theory of Cherenkov Radiation
2.1.1 Energy Threshold

 The speed of light in a medium is cm = c/n, where c is the speed of light
in vacuum and n(λ) is the wavelength-dependent index of refraction (dimensionless).
Cherenkov radiation of a particular wavelength, λ, is only emitted when the velocity
of a charged particle, υ, exceeds cm for that wavelength. Therefore, in this context,
cm can also be referred to as the threshold velocity υth .

2.2 Identifying and Evaluating Cherenkov Materials
 The following subsections outline general criteria for selecting Cherenkov me-
dia and explain some methods that can be used to evaluate their suitability for
Cherenkov detector systems.

2.2.1 Desirable Material Properties

 Several important properties of materials used for Cherenkov detectors are
listed below; this likely non-comprehensive.

 • High density– The more atoms that are present per unit volume, the more
 material can be polarized along a given charged particle track, increasing the
 number of Cherenkov photons produced.

 • High index of refraction– The higher the refractive index, the lower the
 Cherenkov energy threshold. The lower the threshold, the more particles stand
 to produce Cherenkov light, increasing overall light yield. However, an applica-
 tion where appreciable low-energy backgrounds (for example, 511 keV photons
 from pair production) are present may motivate the selection of a material with
 a lower refractive index to improve the signal-to-background ratio.

 • Low UV-VIS absorption– Cherenkov photons cannot reach a detector if
 they are absorbed in the medium that generated them. Because the Cherenkov
 intensity is highest at low wavelengths, transmission also needs to be high there.
 High transmission increases detection efficiency.

 • Lack of competing luminescence mechanisms– The de-excitation that re-
 sults in Cherenkov emission occurs on the picosecond time scale [20]. If another
 type of luminescence is in play, such as scintillation, the time response can be

 21

 orders of magnitude slower. A material’s luminescence behavior is driven by
 its electronic structure. A material can have intrinsic luminescence due to lat-
 tice defects such as interstitials and vacancies. Additionally, luminescence can
 be caused by the presence of foreign matter, either as intentional dopants or
 impurities.)

2.2.2 Material Evaluation Methods

 The following is a description of various measurements whose results can be
used to understand whether a candidate material is suitable for use as a Cherenkov
radiation detector. They all probe different aspects of a material’s electronic struc-
ture.

 • Radioluminescence – Radioluminescence (RL) is the emission of visible or UV
 light from a material caused by excitation from ionizing radiation. Cherenkov
 radiation is one form of RL. Knowing the energy spectrum of the Cherenkov
 emission from a particular radiator material is important to the design of the
 greater detector system. However, it is likely that most radiator materials will
 also exhibit other types of RL, such as scintillation, that could spectrally overlap
 with the Cherenkov emission. Time-resolved spectroscopy can help differentiate
 between the two because Cherenkov emission occurs on shorter timescales. For
 example, a fused silica prism might be irradiated with a pulsed x-ray source,
 the emitted light passed through a diffraction grating, and a streak camera
 used to record the spectrum as a function of time, as shown in Figure 9. The
 resulting record is a 3D ”streak spectrogram” with axes of emitted wavelength
 (or photon energy), time, and intensity, as shown in Figure 10. Picosecond-order
 time resolution and nanometer-order wavelength resolution may be required.

  • Optical Transmission– Optical transmission (or conversely absorption) in
 a material can be measured as a function of input wavelength by attaching
 a scanning monochromator to a broadband light source, placing a sample in

 the monochromatic beam, and measuring the transmitted light downstream

• Photoluminescence & Photoluminescence Excitation– Photolumines-
 cence is a process where a material absorbs photons, exciting electrons to a
 higher energy state, and subsequently emits lower energy photons as the elec-
 trons relax. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements and Photoluminescence-
 Excitation (PLE) measurements are complementary spectroscopic techniques.
 PL measures emitted intensity versus emission wavelength at a fixed excitation
 wavelength. Ideally none of the emission peaks overlap with the Cherenkov
 spectrum of the given material, which would be nuisance signals. Figure 11
 shows a basic laboratory setup for measuring PL. The continuous-wave (CW)
 laser emits light at a fixed wavelength, and the filter removes that wavelength
 from the emission spectrum so that it does not dominate the signal. The spec-
 trometer analyzes the spectrum of light coming from the sample by using a
 diffraction grating to record the intensity of each constituent wavelength one at
 a time.

 Figure 11: A basic PL measurement setup

 PLE measures emitted intensity versus excitation wavelength at a fixed emission
 wavelength. In this case, it helps identify what specific excitation wavelengths

  are causing nuisance signals so that potential workarounds (such as filtering)
  could be identified. Figure 12 shows a basic laboratory setup for measuring
  PLE. The scanning monochromator makes a beam using one wavelength from

 Figure 12: A basic PLE measurement setup

  In practice, both PL and PLE measurements are sometimes performed using
  the same measurement setup. This is because both require similar equipment
  and because comprehensive characterization requires iterating between the two
  methods. The resulting record would be a 3D specrogram with axes of exci-
  tation wavelength, emission wavelength, and intensity. The overall goal is to
  understand which excitation wavelengths will cause luminescence in a sample
  and what the spectrum of that luminescence will be in each case.

• Thermoluminescence– Thermoluminescence (TL), also known as Thermally-
  Stimulated Luminescence (TSL), is the emission of visible or UV light from a
  material upon heating due to having previously absorbed energy from ionizing
  radiation. Rather than characterizing the instantaneous emission behavior as
  in PL and RL, TL characterizes a material’s trap structure. Radiation creates
  trapped carriers, heating releases those carriers, and then radiative recombi-
  nation can occur. Observing the radiative recombination can help understand
  how deep the traps are (based on the temperature of charge release) and how

 25

many are present (based on light intensity upon release). Traps are of interest
because (a) they can reduce the yield of prompt luminescence mechanisms and
(b) de-trapping can create a time-varying background signal in a current-mode
Cherenkov measurement. For this study, samples could be irradiated using the
same source as the RL measurements. Then glow (intensity vs. temperature)
curves could be constructed by slowly heating the irradiated sample and col-
lecting the emitted light with a photodetector at different intervals.

3.1 Material Down-Selection
 The selection of candidate Cherenkov materials for purchase for this project
took into account the ”desirable material properties” described in 2.2.1 as well as
some application-specific considerations described below.

 Cherenkov radiation is universal in the sense that all transparent bodies in
the liquid, gaseous, and solid states glow under the action of charged particles with
sufficient energy [20]. However, the scope of the current effort will be restricted
to solid Cherenkov media for several reasons. Solids offer relatively high detection
efficiency [19] and/or long detector shelf life compared to liquids and gases. Gases
also bring unique hazard considerations (pressure safety and chemical safety) and
the inefficiency of using a conversion target. Conversion targets are used to convert
photons into electrons in order to generate Cherenkov radiation, but high Z materials
that have high photon interaction cross sections have high self-attenuation of the
resulting electron signal. In contrast, a single homogenous solid can both convert
an incident photon signal to an electron signal and allow 100% ”collection” of those
electrons for subsequent Cherenkov generation.

 For scheduling reasons, the candidate materials must also be readily commer-
cially available.

3.2.1 Fused Silica

 Cherenkov production in fused silica is well known both as a nuisance signal
in optical fibers [27, 28] and as an intentional form of radiation detection used by
both by my team [3] and others [11] in the past.

 Some meta-analyses [29, 30] have been conducted to provide a comprehensive
picture of optically-active defect centers in amorphous silicon oxide. These detail the
types of defects present and how they affect optical transmission, PL, and PLE of
the material. More focused and contemporary papers such as [31–33] offer additional
insight into the structure of the material, common color centers, and the defects that
they are associated with.

 The body of work concerning the optical properties of fused silica is very
large. However, values reported ”vary significantly due to numerous sample features
and experimental conditions” [34]. Therefore, in order to ensure the relevance of
results, the search was narrowed to focus specifically on Corning 7980.

 The manufacturer provides two key data sheets for 7980 [15, 35] which include
manufacturing information as well as optical, chemical, thermal, and mechanical prop-
erties. There are several different grades of this material: standard grade, KrF grade,
ArF grade, mirror grade, and industrial grade. The ArF grade was selected for this
study because it has the lowest impurity content (metallic impurities <100 ppb and
OH content 800-1000 ppb). It contains hydrogen to minimize UV absorption and is
certified to have internal transmittance ≥ 99.5%/cm at 193 nm. Index of refraction
vs. wavelength data are reported in [35]. The manufacturer states in [15] that the
ArF grade exhibits ”no visible fluorescence when exposed to a deuterium source from
215 nm - 400 nm”.

 31

 The most thorough measurement of optical transmission vs. wavelength for
Corning 7980 ArF is provided in Figure 1 of [36]. This dataset goes down to a
wavelength of 150 nm and the x-intercept appears to be around 162 nm.

 Photoluminescence data for virgin (undamaged) Corning 7980 is available in
[37–39]. None of these references state which grade of material was used; it is assumed
that the lack of specificity implies standard grade. While additional PL data provided
in [40] are not as relevant because they were measured using radiation-damaged rather
than virgin material, experimental setup information may be helpful. This paper also
states that the spectroscopic characteristics of non-bridging oxygen hole centers (a
common type of point defect in fused silica) were the same regardless of whether they
were induced by a laser or ionizing radiation.

 Studies involving the irradiation of fused silica tend to focus on the impacts of
radiation-induced damage [36, 40, 41] rather than the immediate luminescent response
of the material to ionizing radiation. However, some RL data are provided in [28] and
[27]. In [28], fused silica fibers were irradiated with an electron beam and the angle
of the emitted light was indicative of Cherenkov radiation. In [27], fused silica fibers
were irradiated both at gamma irradiation facility and at a fission reactor. Though
spectrally-resolved RL data are reported, because detailed information about the
samples is not given, any comparisons to RL from Corning 7980 ArF should be made
with caution.

 A Cherenkov spectrum for fused silica subjected to a pulsed electron beam
is reported in [19]. A streak camera and a set of optical filters allowed for both
time-resolved and spectrally-resolved measurements. The measured Cherenkov pulse
duration was 27.0 ± 4.0 ps. A theoretical prediction of the Cherenkov spectrum
which appears to be different than that predicted in the theory section of this report
warrants a closer look. The measured Cherenkov spectrum aligned with prediction
between 350 nm and the upper measurement limit of 500 nm. Several possible reasons
were given for poor agreement below 350 nm. The peak of the Cherenkov spectrum

 32

could not be observed. Fused silica sample characteristics were not reported.

 Thermoluminescence data for fused silica are available in [42]. However, all of
the samples were doped with rare-earth elements and data on the intrinsic material
were not reported as a baseline. However, the experimental method used here sounds
applicable to the present study, so it is helpful to have details on the instruments
used and how results might be analyzed and presented.

 The only relevant past doctoral research that surfaced in the fused silica lit-
erature search was [43]. For the most part, this study’s data will not overlap with
that proposed here due to the use of doped fused silica samples. (An exception is
Figure 2.4, which provides an RL spectrum for an undoped sample). However, the
background information on the scintillation process, the Cherenkov radiation pro-
cess, and optical properties in Chapter 1, the experimental setups and analyses used
in Chapters 3, 4, and 5, and the general level of detail with which material electronic
structure is discussed make this a valuable resource to guide the current effort.

3.2.2 Sapphire

 The sapphire selected for this study is a C-cut (also known as Z-cut) single
crystal grown by the heat exchanger method (HEM). The manufacturer, Crystal
Systems, provides basic material properties in [44]. The manufacturing process is
designed to yield ”high-purity optical sapphire that eliminates almost all intrinsic
photon absorption”. The quoted transmission range is 150 nm to 4.6 µm and a plot
of transmission vs. wavelength in the UV region shows transmission of nearly 80%
or greater from 190 nm to 298 nm. This data sheet also includes basic physical,
mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties. Impurity concentrations and indices
of refraction are notably absent from this data sheet.

 General information concerning sapphire structure, defects, and color centers
is available in [45–49]. However, color centers are strongly influenced by the crystal
growth procedure [50, 51], so it is unclear how well sapphire data from the literature

 33

will represent the behavior of the specific material chosen for this study.

 In the absence of index of refraction data available from the supplier of this
material, the indices reported in [52] will be used. They are for C-cut sapphire at
ambient temperature and cover a wavelength range of 200 nm to 5.5 µm. It is unclear
whether these are calculated or measured values.

 PL/PLE data are available from several sources [48, 53, 54], though none would
be directly comparable with that from this study due to lack of information about
and/or differences in the method of sample manufacture, morphology, and purity.
However, these studies do offer helpful information about experimental setups that
could yield the desired data. [48] describes setups for time-resolved spectroscopy
measurements on millisecond and nanosecond time scales; the latter is fast enough
to differentiate Cherenkov from other luminescence. Also, the combination PL/PLE
setup used by [53] yielded data of the exact format that I hope to generate, where
emission wavelength is plotted against excitation wavelength with intensity as a color
map.

 There have been multiple studies on the radioluminescence of sapphire irra-
diated with ion beams. [45] and [55] both used C-cut, monocrystalline samples of
unknown manufacturing method and purity. While this makes the data of limited
use for comparison, they describe how the intensity of RL in sapphire changes based
on the species of ions used, their energy, and fluence, which could help with experi-
ment design. [45] also contains useful insight about why so many irradiation studies
use ions rather than neutral particles: the latter causes damage that is more uniformly
distributed, but sometimes with so much induced absorption that RL is difficult to
observe. Finally, [47] reports RL data for polycrystalline samples with a final purity
of 3N that were irradiated with an ion beam. Their technique for time-resolved, in-
situ spectroscopy allowed measurement of the luminescence produced only in the first
10 ns after irradiation (see Figure 5), which peaks at a wavelength of roughly 325
nm. It’s likely that most of this signal is Cherenkov light; it would be an excellent

 34

measurement to replicate with the HEM LAOS sapphire.

 The thermoluminescence of sapphire has been studied in [50, 56, 57]. Of these,
the results from [50] are the most relevant. Sapphire crystals grown by ”thermal
gradient techniques” (which sounds similar to the HEM) were irradiated with a Co-60
source at room temperature and then heated to 700 K and the resulting TL recorded.
Figure 2 contains a TL glow curve which features a prominent peak at 450 K. I
may choose to perform low temperature TL measurements to get more information
about shallow traps instead of or in addition to deep ones, depending on what is most
relevant to my final application.

 There are some studies which give rather comprehensive optical characteriza-
tions of sapphire in adddition to the more focused papers previously discussed.

 The first is [51]. Scintillation and dosimetric properties of ”pure” (no quan-
titative purity figure is given) Al2 O3 samples synthesized by the Czochralski and
the Bridgman growth techniques were studied. The Czochralski sample exhibited an
intense scintillation emission peak at 300 nm due to the F+ center. The scintilla-
tion decay times of the Czochralski and the Bridgman sample were 7.6 ns and 2.8 ns,
respectively. Then samples were irradiated with x-rays so that the immediate radiolu-
minesence (which showed emission peaks at 240 nm and 300 nm) and post-irradiation
TL could be studied. Both samples showed good linearity between the dose and TL
intensity. The Bridgman technique is quite similar to the heat exchanger method that
my sapphire samples were grown with, so it is encouraging to see that these samples
had fewer defects and exhibited less intense luminescence as a result. Transmission
vs. wavelength (Figure 2), x-ray-induced luminescence spectra (Figure 3), scintilla-
tion lifetimes (Figure 4), TL glow curves (Figure 5), and TL spectra (Figure 6) are
all directly relevant.

 The second is [49], which studied the luminescence and the thermal stability of
defects formed in Al2 O3 single crystals (C-cut; Ti and Cr impurities <0.5 ppm) after
pulsed treatment with a beam of C+ /H+ ions with an energy of 300 keV and a pulse

 35

duration of ∼80 ns. Measurements included optical absorption, photoluminescence,
and pulsed cathodoluminescence. They found that exposure to a pulsed electron beam
leads to intense generation of both single F and F+ centers and more complex defects
(F2 aggregate centers or vacancy–impurity complexes) in Al2 O3 . The thermal stability
of F-type defects formed in Al2 O3 upon exposure to a pulsed ion beam is comparable
to the stability of radiation-induced defects in neutron-irradiated samples. Table 1 has
a concise summary of absorption and luminescence centers and the defects responsible
for them. The pulsed ion beam treatment intentionally melts the surface of the
crystal, but the reason for liquid versus solid state measurement is not clear. This
is a radiation damage study, but most datasets include an undamaged baselines and
most of them seem applicable for comparison with my measurements except maybe
Figure 1. Whether luminescent intensity increases or decreases with temperature
seems to depend on the specific color center in play, as annealing can help heal
certain radiation damage.

 The work in [58] is probably the best analog to the study proposed here.
Its specific goal was to perform optical characterization of candidate materials for
Cherenkov radiators. It includes data on cathodoluminescence, photoluminescence,
and Cherenkov radiation from 200 nm – 800 nm in crystals excited by an electron
beam and an excilamp with a peak wavelength of 222 nm. Spectral and amplitude-
time characteristics are reported for many different materials. They conclude ”that
cathodoluminescence and photoluminescence should be accounted for in Cherenkov-
type detectors of runaway electrons” for tokamaks. This paper came up during the
search for sapphire data, as sapphire was one of the materials studied. However, none
of the sapphire results are shown. The reason for this is unclear. There is still a
lot of helpful background and theory on Cherenkov radiation (section II) and how
it can be differentiated from other luminescence that usually destroys the signal-to-
background ratio. For instance, this can be done either by analyzing luminescence
lifetimes or by focusing only on the Cherenkov light generated in the wavelength band
between the UV absorption edge and the lowest wavelength cathodoluminescence
 36

peak. Lastly, there is an interesting comment that “increasing the refractive index not
only decreases the [electron kinetic energy] threshold . . . but also provides a several-
fold increase in its spectral energy density at the same electron energy.”

 Finally, [20] is a very long, detailed review paper which combines meta-analysis
with original measurements. “The survey presents results of investigations of spec-
tral, amplitude–time, and spatial VCR characteristics obtained mainly in recent years
upon excitation of transparent substances by a flux of electrons with energies of tens
or hundreds of keV. The VCR spectra in diamond (natural and synthetic), quartz
glass, sapphire, and leucosapphire are presented. It is reported about VCR detection
in MgF2 , Ga2 O3 , and other transparent samples. The spectra and amplitude–time
characteristics of VCR and pulsed CL are compared at different electron energies.” It
provides a thorough introduction to Cherenkov radiation, including history, theory,
and general properties. It will be helpful for better understanding the relationship
between a Cherenkov spectrum and the electron spectrum used to generate it. The in-
troduction states that somewhere it also offers “conditions under which the Cherenkov
intensity can exceed the pulsed cathodoluminescence intensity”. It gives optical char-
acteristics (transmission band, refractive index, electron energy threshold) of many
potential Cherenkov materials, where IFG and ZFG are notably absent. Almost ev-
ery figure is likely to teach something, though Figure 15.c (emission and transmission
spectra of (1,3) leucosapphire) and Figures 16-17 (emission spectra from sapphire
with various filters) may be directly comparable to my datasets.

3.2.3 Heavy Metal Fluoride Glasses

 In 2024, one of the original discoverers of heavy metal fluoride glasses (HM-
FGs) wrote an excellent primer on them. [59] explains that, since the discovery of
fluorozirconate glasses by the Poulain brothers in the 1970s, three families of heavy
metal fluoride glasses have come into popular use: fluorozirconates, fluoroindates,
and fluoroaluminates. These are designated ”heavy metal” fluorides to contrast them
with with fluoroberylate glasses, which were discovered earlier and have a structure
 37

resembling fused silica. Except for BeF2 , no pure fluoride exists in the vitreous state.
In general, additional fluorides must be introduced to create a melt with low enough
crystallization that a glass can be formed by fast cooling. The ”confusion principle”
expresses that glass stability increases as the number of glass components increases
[60]. However, having too many components can lead to variations in local stoichiom-
etry, which introduce scattering [61].

 One of the best characteristics of HMFGs is their unusually large optical trans-
mission range. This has helped them achieve widespread use for infrared fiber ap-
plications, though they also transmit reasonably deep into the UV. One concern is
handling requirements, as some sensitivity to moisture has been observed even at
ambient temperature [62, 63]. In general, fluoride glasses are chemically aggressive
(for instance, with metal crucibles used for melts) and hygroscopic [61]. However,
bulk HMFGs can tolerate a humid atmosphere so long as the temperature does not
exceed 100 degrees C and condensation is prevented [59]. Storing the samples with
desiccant and exercising care with what they are cleaned with or otherwise exposed
to should be sufficient. The lower the pH, the higher the rate of corrosion. Bulk
HMFGs are stable in buffered solutions that are slightly alkaline (pH around 8) [59].

3.2.3.1 Fluoroindate Glass

 ”Fluoroindate” is a broad term encompassing many possible glass compositions
where InF3 is the primary glass former. The samples selected for this study are of
the IZSB family; their composition is 40 InF3 – 20 ZnF2 – 20 SrF2 – 20 BaF2 .

 The manufacturer has a technical data sheet [64] on this composition which
quotes a transparency range of 255 nm to 8 µm and gives the glass transition tem-
perature, density, refractive index, non-linear refractive index, and phonon energy.

 There have been many studies on the luminescence of fluoroindate glasses
doped with rare earth metals [65–70]. Dopants are included for several reasons,
including scintillation, up-conversion, and stabilization. Most of these studies are

 38

irrelevant to the current endeavor because they do not report results on the intrinsic
material as a baseline, the baseline fluoroindate composition is different than my
samples, or both. One exception is [71], where the baseline material is the same
composition as mine. Photoluminescence data recorded with an excitation wavelength
of 1550 nm for samples with the lowest dopant concentrations show almost no emission
between 400 nm and 1100 nm. This is encouraging to see. It is difficult to know
whether emission data at lower wavelengths were excluded because there was no
observable signal in that regime or because the author simply wasn’t interested in it,
as the instrument recording emissions was sensitive down to 200 nm.

3.2.3.2 Fluorozirconate Glass

 Much like the case with ”fluoroindate”, ”fluorozirconate” is a broad term en-
compassing many possible glass compositions where ZrF4 is the primary glass former.
The samples selected for this study are of the ZBLAN family; their composition is
53 ZrF4 – 20 BaF2 – 4 LaF3 – 3 AlF3 – 20 NaF.

 The manufacturer’s technical data sheet [64] on this composition quotes a
transparency range of 220 nm to 7 µm and gives the glass transition temperature,
density, refractive index, non-linear refractive index, and phonon energy.

 There are not many published studies on the luminescence of ZBLAN. How-
ever, there is one very thorough study conducted by Yang et al in 1997 [61] that is
practically a one-stop shop.

 The goal of the study was to see if the luminescence behavior of a ZBLAN glass
with composition 54 ZrF4 – 23 BaF2 – 4 LaF3 – 3 AlF3 – 16 NaF (similar to, but not the
same as, mine) could be used as a probe of material quality. The author concluded
that it can be. Cathodoluminescence (Fig. 1), x-ray radioluminescence (Fig. 2-5),
and thermoluminescence (Fig. 7-8) were all measured. A de-convolution of the RL
spectra revealed six visible-range emission bands that were common to all samples
(Fig. 6) and are assumed to arise from intrinsic electron-hole recombination processes

 39

because all samples were highly pure. “. . . The form of the spectra and the number
of constituent bands did not vary greatly between the samples studied, despite a
variety of doping and production changes”. However, the signal intensities increase
when more defects (impurities, lattice defects, etc.) are present, and there was still
some emission observed during low-temperature TL that they believe is coming from
rare-earth impurities. All of these datasets are relevant for comparison with my own.
In addition, there are many bits of information that can shape my measurement plan:
Low-purity samples are likely to have so many color centers that the spectra can’t
easily be analyzed. Impurities cannot only add broadband emission signals but also
interact with the glass network to modify the shape and intensity of peaks from intrin-
sic processes. Samples that were irradiated and then heated above room temperature
for TL swelled and started to decompose. CL and x-ray RL results differed in that
the RL spectral intensities varied a lot more with temperature. RL was more intense
at low temperature and decreased on the approach to room temperature; the relative
intensities of different peaks within a spectrum also change with temperature. At-
tempts to attribute specific color centers to specific structural features in the material
quickly become highly speculative. The author reinforces that CL, RL, and TL all
offer unique insights: “The competitive nature of the charge distribution between
alternative luminescence sites means that CL, RL, and TL will all emphasize the
presence of different components of the possible luminescence decay routes”. Finally,
RL, TL, and CL spectra should be continuous distributions; if discontinuities appear,
it may be due to the presence of bulk phase transitions.

A.1 Fused Silica
[19 ] Shohei Wakita, Masakazu Washio, Yoshiro Suzuki, and Masamitsu Hirai. Pi-
 cosecond pulse sources by čerenkov light generated from fused silica and water.
      Japanese Journal of Applied Physics, 24(4):502–505, 1985. doi: 10.1143/JJAP
 .24.502

 A 10-ps electron beam was used to irradiate water and fused silica.
 The resulting Cherenkov light pulses were recorded as functions of
 time and wavelength. A streak camera was used to resolve time and
 a series of filters were used to resolve wavelength. The measured pulse
 durations were in the vicinity of 30 ps for both materials, though the
 author believes the true duration to be shorter and cites several pos-
 sible reasons why this experiment may overestimate it. The measured
 Cherenkov spectra agree with theoretical prediction between 350 nm
 and 500 nm but the intensity is far lower than predicted toward the
 UV.
 Relevance: Key reference. Experimentally validates the notion that
 Cherenkov emission occurs on picosecond time scales, makes a some-
 what novel suggestion to use conical Cherenkov radiators for opti-
 mized light collection, and describes an experimental method for mea-
 suring Cherenkov spectra using a streak camera.

[29 ] David L. Griscom. Optical properties and structure of defects in silica glass.
      Journal of the Ceramic Society of Japan, 99(1154):923–942, 1991. doi: 10.210
      9/jcersj.99.923

 This is a review paper that aggregates electron spin resonance and
 optical correlations data for fused silica from the literature to trace
 the origin of many optical absorption bands to specific point defects.
 Relevance: Since this is a review paper, there is probably a lot of
 variation in the characteristics of fused silica samples involved. It
 may be useful for interpreting spectral features in my data so that I
 55

 can identify what could be changed about the samples to eliminate
 them.

[28 ] A. S. Beddar, T. R. Mackie, and F. H. Attix. Cerenkov light generated in optical
 fibres and other light pipes irradiated by electron beams. Physics in Medicine
     and Biology, 37(4):925–935, 1992

 Pure fused silica optical fibers were irradiated with an electron beam
 and the resulting luminescence studied. The relationship between
 the intensity of emitted light and the emission angle is indicative of
 Cherenkov radiation. The author discusses a method for measuring
 this signal independently so that it may be subtracted as a back-
 ground in scintillation dosimetry measurements.
 Relevance: Cherenkov emission in fused silica is a documented nui-
 sance signal in optical fibers.

[30 ] Linards Skuja. Optically active oxygen-deficiency-related centers in amorphous
 silicon dioxide. Journal of Non-Crystalline Solids, 239(1–3):16–48, 1998. doi:
     10.1016/S0022-3093(98)00720-0

 This is reminiscent of Griscom’s 1991 paper and will probably be
 useful for the same reasons.
 Relevance: See entry for Griscom 1991

[27 ] K. Toh, T. Shikama, T. Kakuta, S. Nagata, B. Tsuchiya, C. Mori, J. Hori, and
 T. Nishitani. Studies of radioluminescence in fused silica core fibers. In Scin-
 tillating and Fiber Sensors and Their Applications, volume 4786 of Proceedings
     of SPIE, pages 215–220, 2002. doi: 10.1117/12.451745

 Fused silica optical fibers of varying sizes and compositions (doped,
 undoped) were irradiated at both a gamma irradiation facility and

 the author attributes to Cherenkov radiation as well as some peaks
 and absorption features.

[42 ] A. Vedda, N. Chiodini, D. Di Martino, M. Fasoli, L. Griguta, F. Moretti, and

 Rare-earth(RE)-doped sol-gel glasses were irradiated with X-rays and

[34 ] Rei Kitamura, Laurent Pilon, and Miroslaw Jonasz. Optical constants of silica
 glass from extreme ultraviolet to far infrared at near room temperature. Applied
     Optics, 46(33):8118–8133, 2007. doi: 10.1364/AO.46.008118

 This is a review paper that aggregates real (refractive index) and
 imaginary (absorption index) parts of the complex refractive index
 of silica glass from 30 nm to 1000 micron. There is generally good
 agreement on the general features of optical constants as functions
 of wavelength. However, the actual values can vary significantly due
 to variation in sample manufacturing method, sample quality, and
 measurement method.
 Relevance: This is a cautionary tale. Make sure samples are charac-
 terized adequately before reporting measurements made using them,
 and don’t pay too much heed to studies where this characterization
 is absent because the relevance is unclear.

 This material would be an excellent candidate for my study were it
