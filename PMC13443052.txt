Screening for Psychiatric Disorders in Dermatological Settings: A Review of Clinically Useful Assessment Tools
Authors: Kyung Ho Lee, Chi-Un Pae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe possibility of coexistence of dermatological diseases and psychiatric disorders has been proposed through a number of previous studies since considerable commonality in underlying pathophysiology including alteration in immune function, hormonal dysfunction, vulnerability to stress, patient-specific personality traits, genetic factors, and environmental factors, while the two clinical conditions share few similarities in phenomenology. From a diagnostic aspect, many dermatological disorders are frequently questioned to have comorbid psychiatric disorders, and dermatological delusions/preoccupations are even officially recognized among formal psychiatric disorders diagnosis criteria. Interestingly, academic terms such as “psychodermatology” and “psychocutaneous disease” are also commonly utilized in clinical practice. When comorbid psychiatric disorders are present but not recognized, patients may not receive timely and proper psychiatric intervention or psychosocial support, inadequate treatment and poor compliance to therapy may lead to failure of optimal treatment outcomes. Hence, proactive screening for comorbid or underlying psychiatric conditions can substantially enhance both treatment benefit and prognosis of dermatological diseases in routine clinical practice. However, it is very challenging for dermatologists without psychiatric training backgrounds to efficiently screen and diagnose psychiatric disorders in busy clinical settings. Thus, this review tries to provide dermatologists with simple, valid, and useful psychiatric rating scales for their routine practice.Keywords: Comorbidity, Diagnosis, Psychiatric rating scale, Psychodermatology, Screening

INTRODUCTION

Psychiatric symptoms and disorders are prevalent and also constitute important clinical considerations affecting diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis across various medical fields1. In this context, psychiatric disorders have been found to be frequently associated with dermatological disorders. Indeed, dermatological disorders often encompass important psychiatric symptoms and signs beyond physical symptoms. The connection between mental illness and dermatological conditions has been consistently proposed as early as the era of psychoanalysis, well before the growing body of scientific evidence presented in recent researches2.

There is increasing evidence that dermatological disorders frequently coexist with psychiatric disorders. According to numerous studies3,4,5,6,7,8,9, psychiatric disorders may be comorbid with at least 30% and up to 90% of chronic inflammatory skin diseases including eczema, urticaria, atopic dermatitis, and psoriasis. Interestingly, academic terms such as “psychodermatology” and “psychocutaneous disease” are also recognized and dermatological delusions/preoccupations are even officially recognized as one part of diagnostic criteria for certain psychiatric disorder in recent standard psychiatric diagnosis system2,10.

In fact, these two clinical conditions may influence each other through complex biopsychosocial interactions through immune dysfunction, genetic predisposition, neuroendocrine changes, individual psychological vulnerability, and stress response mechanisms11.

Intriguingly, the potential connection of causality between some dermatological conditions and psychiatric disorders was recently investigated in the European population, using Mendelian randomization analysis based on a database of genome-wide association study which provides substantial evidence for causal inference between the two clinical conditions12. According to the results, bipolar disorder (BD) was associated with increase of the incidences of psoriasis, and major depressive disorder (MDD) increased the risk of eczema, while psoriasis was not identified as a risk factor for BD and eczema was not associated with an increased risk for MDD. In addition, no causalities were found between genetic predisposition to BD, MDD, schizophrenia, or anxiety disorder and urticaria, which is different from the previous studies6,7. These studies suggest that early and proper diagnosis and management of psychiatric disorders can positively influence the clinical outcomes of certain dermatological diseases.

Given these findings, timely screening and recognition of psychiatric symptoms and disorders may substantially influence patient management and the prognosis of diseases, emphasizing the importance of an integrated and multidisciplinary approach to achieve optimal treatment outcomes in dermatology13.

Therefore, this paper tries to provide dermatologists, especially for busy practitioners, with practical guidance for efficient and pragmatic pathways for screening of psychiatric symptoms (disorders) and psychological factors, which are revealed to significantly impact on the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of dermatological conditions/diseases encountered in routine clinical practice.

BARRIERS IN EARLY DETECTION OF PSYCHIATRIC SYMPTOMS IN ROUTINE PRACTICE

Numerous studies have proposed substantial difficulties in the early detection of psychiatric symptoms and comorbidity upon diagnosis and treatment of dermatological diseases. This difficulty may originate from many issues that psychiatric symptoms tend to be concealed by prominent skin symptoms and that psychological factors are often not adequately identified by both patients and clinicians. Non-psychiatrists including dermatologists often lack systematic education or experience in recognition of diverse psychiatric diseases (symptoms and/or behaviors), resulting in significant limitations in the utilization of tools for mental health assessment as well as pertinent interview skills. Non-psychiatrists may also underestimate the influence of psychiatric symptoms on the treatment effect and clinical course of dermatological diseases. Moreover, physicians may not be aware of psychiatric screening tools, or they may feel that usage of such screening tools is cumbersome or inefficient in complicated clinical situations, although there are numerous fast, simple, and validated psychiatric rating scales. The busy clinical environment should be another consideration, because practitioners are usually pressured to focus on more streamlined and task-oriented management rather than hearing detailed and cued patients’ story. Finally, patients’ ignorance, unawareness, misunderstanding, or unwillingness can act as major barriers to screening for psychiatric disorders. In fact, the stigmatization on mental illness may make patients reluctant to do spontaneous report of psychiatric symptoms, and thereby interfere with timely diagnosis and intervention8. Consequently, poor treatment response, decreased treatment compliance, and increased risk for chronicity may occur in routine practice in dermatology.

WHAT IS MEASUREMENT-BASED CARE?

Differently from other medical fields, the screening, assessment, and follow-up evaluation of psychiatric symptoms are rarely assessed with the help of biological markers or laboratory works due to their limited availability and applicability. Therefore, mental illness outcomes are usually assessed by utilizing various rating scales by both clinicians or patients themselves, respectively. In this context, measurement-based care can be considered as an alternative approach in routine practice. Clinicians can utilize standardized and validated rating scales to collect patient data, evaluate and assess various symptoms, utilize for further decision-making, and inform patients of proper next stages of management14. In this aspect, timely appropriate screening and follow-up of patients using rapid, valid, and standardized rating scales for psychiatric symptoms and quality of life (QoL) may facilitate dermatologists to make optimal planning for treatment and further management of their patients.

WHO SHOULD BE CONSIDERED FOR PSYCHIATRIC EVALUATION?

The assessment and evaluation of psychiatric symptom and psychological factors should be timely performed even in non-psychiatric settings to ensure timely recognition and appropriate management of underlying psychiatric conditions, when clinicians realize any peculiar and odd behavior, language, attitude, or contextual factors suspicious of concern on a patient's mental health.

In fact, a number of lifetime prevalence studies15,16,17,18 and projected lifetime risk researches19 have suggested the high occurrence of mental health issues in general population. According to such studies, the estimated lifetime prevalence of one or more mental illnesses varies significantly across countries, 47.4% in the United States, 18.1% in Japan, 27.8% in Korea, 31.7% in Netherlands and 30.3% in South Africa, potentially showing potential differences by cultural, socioeconomic, and diagnostic factors19. Previous prevalence studies have also demonstrated the high burden of mental health issues in the general population, emphasizing a need for routine mental health screening across diverse medical settings. Furthermore, the cross-national lifetime prevalence rates for suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts have been found to be 9.2%, 3.1%, and 2.7%, respectively. Notably, across all countries participated in the survey, 60% of transition from suicidal ideation to planning or attempt was found within the first year after the onset of ideation20. This raises the meaningful questions of how and on whom psychiatric or psychosocial assessments should be conducted.

In general, it is important to categorize patients as either first-time visitors or those returning for follow-up or maintenance care. The term General Appearance, Attitude, and Behavior (GAAB) is used to evaluate a patient’s physical appearance, attitude, and observable behaviors in a routine mental status examination. This approach usually helps psychiatrists intuitively and rapidly understand a patient’s clinical background on underlying mental issues as well as assist for accurate diagnosis and treatment plan. This is one of the most fundamental methods in psychiatric screenings through identification of potential issues by observances of verbal and behavioral cues. These may include assessment of whether the patient appears clean and appropriately dressed for their age, displays a cooperative and open attitude during the interview, exhibits proper and expressive affect, and shows natural behaviors. Conversely, signs and symptoms such as anxiety, sweating, tremulousness, passivity and evasiveness to questions with defensive attitude, frequent avoidance of eye contact, paranoid symptoms, repetitive and obsessive questions, and restlessness may indicate underlying psychological concerns. Hence, GAAB should be considered one of the most essential and basic practices commonly useful across various medical specialties beyond psychiatry, clearly it should be the primary approach to understand the patients’ overall mental condition.

During the initial interview, it is particularly imperative to evaluate potential psychiatric conditions and psychosocial factors when patients carry following features: repetitive complaints of vague or non-specific dermatological symptoms, evidence of self-inflicted dermatological lesions, excessive use of alcohol or other substances, the presence of chronic medical illnesses, sleep problem, abnormal appetite, emotional instability, excessive preoccupation on symptom, exaggerated symptoms, suspiciousness of primary psychiatric disorders, persistent same questioning, health anxiety, or a clear temporal association between psychosocial stressors and the onset of symptoms.

In the context of follow-up or maintenance care, psychiatric and psychosocial considerations remain equally critical, particularly when certain clinical patterns emerge. These include suboptimal or paradoxical treatment responses, ongoing interpersonal conflicts with healthcare providers, cyclic relapses or symptom exacerbations, persistent complaints without objective dermatological findings, inappropriate requests for psychoactive medications (e.g., sedatives), exposure to significant life stressors such as unemployment or divorce, functional impairment disproportionate to the clinical presentation, or strong psychological stress possibly contributing to disease chronicity or exacerbation.

STRUCTURED DIAGNOSTIC INTERVIEWS IN GENERAL PRACTITIONERS IN BUSY ROUTINE CLINICAL PRACTICE

Numerous convenient and validated structured clinical interviews have been available for diagnosis of various psychiatric disorders based on the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) published by the American Psychiatric Association, which is considered as the gold standard for the formal diagnosis of psychiatric conditions worldwide21. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM disorders (SCID) is the standard structured diagnostic interview in line with criteria of DSM-5. However, it also has substantial pitfalls including considerable length, complexity, and the need for formal education for its usage, which yield significant challenges for general practitioners operating in time-constrained clinical settings22. Other structured interviews may include the Composite International Diagnostic Interview23 and the Schedule for Clinical Assessment in Neuropsychiatry24, having similar limitations with the SCID in general practice settings though. On the other hand, the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI)25 is also a brief, stepwise, and structured diagnostic tool for identifying major psychiatric disorders, based on DSM-4 or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) criteria. It can be utilized in both clinical and research settings in the psychiatry with efficiency, reliability, and user-friendly format25,26. However, MINI needs education and training before its use and lack of easy manual, which should be significant shortcomings to busy practitioners. The MINI adopts stepwise “yes/no” format for all items, with each question based on diagnostic criteria of individual psychiatric disorders. Therefore, if a non-psychiatrist has an inadequate understanding of the underlying meaning and clinical context of each item, it can be difficult to follow and apply the interview accurately, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis or failure to reach a definitive conclusion. In fact, non-psychiatrists may feel uncomfortable and difficult to closely explore or contextualize a patient’s psychological expressions. In the absence of effective rapport during the MINI interview, patients should also be reluctant to answer truthfully to sensitive questions (e.g., particularly those related to suicidal ideation or auditory hallucinations). The MINI is limited to offering diagnostic suggestions, while it does not provide a comprehensive clinical formulation, specific cut-off scores for diagnoses, or treatment recommendations. Hence, the results must be interpreted within the broader framework of clinical judgment. In addition, completion of a full interview usually needs at least 30 minutes with training background, which may limit its utility in busy clinical settings. The short version of MINI available requiring 10 minutes and it should be potentially useful for busy practitioners, however, its basic structure is similar to the original version (no diagnostic confirmation, no cut-off score, yes/no structure, and fluctuation of reliability depending on empathy and rapport between clinician and patient)26.

HOW SHOULD PSYCHIATRIC ASSESSMENTS BE IMPLEMENTED, AND BY WHOM, IN BUSY CLINICAL SETTINGS?

Given the time pressure and lack of specialized psychiatric training background in general practitioners, which alternative assessment tool can be easily applicable to routine clinical practice to screen for psychiatric symptoms and relevant psychological issues, ultimately for final diagnosis? Specifically, how can general practitioners efficiently and effectively identify mental health problems without in-depth knowledge of psychiatric symptomatology/psychopathology, formal diagnostic categories, or structured diagnostic systems? Fortunately, a number of psychiatric symptom rating scales are available that can be easily usable by general practitioners in busy clinical settings. These include both self-administered psychiatric rating scales (SPRSs) completed by patients themselves and clinician-administered rating scales (CPRSs) usually applied by mental health professionals such as psychiatrists or clinical psychologists. Reasonable concerns about the reliability and validity, particularly in regard with the accuracy and consistency of SPRSs compared to CPRSs conducted by trained mental health professional should come up. In this context, numerous studies have consistently investigated the reliability and validity of SPRSs in comparison with CPRSs. These studies have demonstrated that SPRSs have also robust valid psychometric properties comparable to CPRSs, supporting their usefulness for screening psychiatric diagnosis (symptoms), monitoring progression of clinical course and treatment response27,28,29,30. In addition, the utilization of the both assessments has been also proved to be ideal approach31,32, especially in clinical trials as well.

The primary considerations for general practitioners when screening for psychiatric symptoms or potential diagnoses in busy clinical settings should be considered for time-efficiency, cost, easy application, no necessity for additional training, and diagnostic accuracy. The majority of currently available SPRSs meet the aforementioned criteria. For instance, one of the most reliable and commonly used SPRSs across the world, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)28 needs only 2–5 minutes for accomplishment of both application and interpretation in routine practice, it can be done in the waiting room or at the meeting time with physician-assistance or registering nurse in the clinic, or it should already be completed by electronic submission through internet appointment process before clinic visit.

PSYCHIATRIC ASSESSMENT SCALES AVAILABLE IN THE DERMATOLOGY FIELD

In this section, the most widely used SPRSs providing both simplicity and validity will be briefly introduced to facilitate prudent and efficient employment in routine practice settings. However, these scales are intended to complementary way to identify preliminary diagnostic impression or detect early signals that may warrant further psychiatric evaluation. A definitive diagnosis should be confirmed through a structured interview performed by trained mental health professionals25.

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6)

The K616 is a brief, 6-item SPRS developed as part of the World Health Organization World Mental Health Survey, intended to rapidly screen for nonspecific psychological distress in the general population and to identify individuals at pending risk for serious mental disorders (SMD). Patients are asked to assess the frequency of 6 key symptoms associated with psychological distress over 2 recall periods: the “past month” (e.g., 30 days immediately preceding the survey) and the “worst month” (defined as the 30-day period within the past 12 months during which they experienced the most severe psychological distress). These symptoms include nervousness, hopelessness, restlessness or fidgety, so sad that nothing could cheer you up, everything was an effort, and worthlessness. Patients rate a frequency of each of symptoms within the specified timeframes, along with a standardized measure of distress severity. The K6 adopts a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (none of the time=0, a little of the time=1, some of the time=2, most of the time=3, all of the time=4). A K6 score of 13 or higher is commonly adopted as a cutoff point for screening of SMDs, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 6% in U.S. adults. Scores of 0 to 4 indicate a less likelihood of significant psychological distress, while scores of 5 to 12 reflect moderate psychological distress33,34. It takes less than 2 minutes for completion of the screening process. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for the entire sample (as well as stratified by race and ethnicity) also yielded an area under the curve of 0.82 at a cut-point of K6 ≥5, indicating high diagnostic accuracy34.

PHQ-2

The PHQ-2 is a brief SPRS that rapidly, conveniently, and efficiently screens for depressive symptoms in primary care and general medical settings. It is retrieved from the PHQ-9 scale, only including the first 2 items of it (anhedonia and depressed mood), which are fundamental criteria for the diagnosis of DSM-535. The scoring method is a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3 (not at all=0, several days=1, more than half the days=2, nearly every day=3), leading to a total score ranging from 0 to 6. A PHQ-2 score of 3 or more has been shown to achieve a sensitivity of 83% and a specificity of 92% for identifying MDD. Analyses using likelihood ratios and ROC curves identified a score of 3 as the optimal threshold for screening purposes35. The screening process can usually be completed in less than one minute. It should be proper to perform more comprehensive SPRSs (e.g., PHQ-9) to facilitate a refined diagnostic impression when the patient exceeds the established cutoff score indicating clinically significant depressive symptoms.

PHQ-9

The PHQ-9 evaluates both the frequency and severity of depressive symptoms and is widely utilized (in more than 45 different languages) not only for initial screening but also as a quantitative measure for monitoring treatment response in patients with MDD28. It is derived from the MDD criteria from the DSM-4/5 (Table 1). Each 9 item is scored from 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day), for a total score of 0 to 27. Total scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 on the PHQ-9 correspond to cutoff points for mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe MDD, respectively28. Importantly, the item 9 serves as a single-item screener for suicide risk and thus a positive response warrants further evaluation by a qualified psychiatrists or associated mental health professional to assess the level and impending risk of suicide. Active intervention by mental health professionals may be recommended for patients with 10 or more points on PHQ-9. The PHQ-9 also includes a functional assessment that evaluates the impact of emotional difficulties on the patient’s occupational, domestic, and interpersonal functioning. The responses with “very difficult” or “extremely difficult” may suggest a significant functional impairment (other responses: “not difficult at all” and “somewhat difficult”). Based on the recent meta-analysis36, combined sensitivity and specificity showed the highest 88% and 85%, respectively, at a cut-off score of 10 or more. It takes less than 5 minutes to complete the screening process.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7)

A criterion-standard study was conducted in 15 primary care settings in the United States from November 2004 to June 2005, after which a brief SPRS, the GAD-7, was finally developed to screen GAD37. Each item was rated on a 4-point (0 to 3) Likert scale anchoring options of “not at all (0),” “several days (1),” “more than half the days (2),” and “nearly every day (3)”. A cutoff point of ≥10 is at least moderate anxiety with a sensitivity of 89% and specificity of 82%, along with a positive likelihood ratio of 537,38. Meanwhile, according to a recent meta-analysis39, pooled sensitivity and specificity values appeared acceptable at a cutoff point of 8 (sensitivity, 83%, and specificity, 84%), although cutoff scores 7 to 10 also had similar pooled estimates of sensitivity/specificity, indicating that the GAD-7 also demonstrates acceptable properties for identifying GAD at a cutoff score of 7 to 1039. Despite developed initially as a screening scale for GAD, it has also demonstrated reasonable diagnostic utility in identifying other common anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, and posttraumatic stress disorder38. Completion of the screening process generally takes approximately 2 minutes.

PHQ-15

The PHQ-15 is a brief SPRS for the assessment of the severity of somatic symptoms and detection of possible somatoform disorders in primary care settings40. It can be particularly useful for screening of patients with somatization, somatic symptom disorder, or significant health-related anxiety, indeed it contains 14 of the 15 most commonly reported somatic symptoms associated with the criteria of somatization disorder based on DSM-4. For instance, the PHQ-15 assesses a wide range of somatic symptoms including stomach pain, back pain, pain in arms, legs and joints, headaches, chest pain, menstrual cramps, dizziness/lightheadedness, palpitations, short breath, pain during sexual intercourse, common gastrointestinal issues (e.g., constipation, nausea, and indigestion, etc.), fatigue/weakness, and sleep disturbance. Interestingly, fatigue/weakness and sleep disturbance are commonly associated with MDD and also included in the PHQ-9. Each item of PHQ-15 is scored on a 3-point Likert scale (0=“not bothered at all,” 1=“bothered a little,” and 2=“bothered a lot”), leading to a total score ranging from 0 to 30. The PHQ-15 scores of 5, 10, and 15 serve as the threshold values indicating low, moderate, and high levels of somatic symptom severity, respectively. Using a threshold of 3 or more severe somatic symptoms within the past 4 weeks, the PHQ-15 proved a sensitivity of 78% and a specificity of 71%41. Interestingly, Kroenke et al.40 also found that somatic symptom severity is strongly associated with general health perceptions, body pain, and impairments in physical and role functioning, whereas depressive symptom severity is primarily related to mental health and social functioning. In aspects of healthcare resource utilization, somatic symptoms show stronger association with the frequency of clinic visits, while the number of self-reported disability days is significantly influenced by depressive symptoms. It takes less than 5 minutes to complete the screening process.

Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)

The ISI42 is a 7-item SPRS for the assessment of insomnia, with each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (0=“not at all,” “none,” or “very satisfied,” and 4 indicating “very severe,” “very dissatisfied,” or “very much interfering” depending on the item). The 7 items evaluate the following domains: difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, problems with early morning awakening, satisfaction with current sleep patterns, interference with daily functioning, perceived noticeability of impairment, and distress caused by sleep difficulties. The ISI total score ranges from 0 to 28, with higher scores representing greater insomnia severity. Several cutoff points have been proposed: total scores of 0 to 7 are considered within the normal range, while scores of 8, 15, and 22 represent thresholds for subthreshold, moderate, and severe insomnia, respectively42. In a community sample, a cutoff score of 10 proved optimal performance, showing a sensitivity of 86.1% and a specificity of 87.7% for detecting insomnia, and thereby a cutoff of 10 point may be the practical threshold reflecting both sensitivity and specificity in population-based samples42. Moreover, the ISI maintains superior diagnostic performance across a range of different psychiatric diagnoses in the subsequent study43. At the respective optimized cut-off points, the ISI detects insomnia based on ICD-10 criteria with a cut-off score of 14 points, achieving a sensitivity of 81.3% and a specificity of 80.9%43. For DSM-5 insomnia, a cut-off score of 11 points demonstrates a higher sensitivity of 88.2% but a lower specificity of 65.6%, indicating that a higher clinical cut-off point may be necessary for the diagnosis of clinical insomnia in psychiatric populations compared to the general population43. Furthermore, a decrease of 8.4 points on an ISI total score was associated with moderate improvement, as assessed by an independent evaluator following treatment42. Hence, the ISI may be useful for clinicians both in the initial assessment of patients and in determining the need for treatment, as well as for monitoring treatment response after commencement of treatment. For researchers, the ISI provides a reliable and valid patient-reported outcome measure that complements objective sleep parameters (e.g., sleep latency and wake after sleep onset) usually adopted as primary endpoint for treatment efficacy42. Numerous studies also showed the high correlation of the ISI with other measures of sleep quality scales44. The administration time is less than 5 minutes.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

The PSQI45 is one of the most commonly utilized SPRSs in psychiatric clinics for the assessment of sleep quality, as well as in researches. The PSQI has 7 items including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty) and a total score ranges from 0 to 21. A total score of >5 may indicate the probability of poor sleep quality, with a sensitivity of 89.6% and a specificity of 86.5%. However, the PSQI has substantial limitations such as vulnerability to recall bias, particularly in older adults and individuals with cognitive impairment, since all items force subjects to recall their sleep patterns over the past month. The complex scoring process may also be a practical challenge for the utilization in busy routine practice. The PSQI usually needs 5 to 10 minutes to complete, it may vary depending on the respondent’s cognitive status, literacy level, and familiarity with sleep-related concepts. In addition, manual scoring the PSQI usually takes additional 5 minutes for trained personnel, computerized scoring methods can further expedite the process in research or clinical settings though44.

Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI)

The DLQI is a brief 10-item SPRS to assess the impact of dermatological conditions on the QoL of patients46. The DLQI evaluates a wide range of areas including various senses of skin (e.g., burning), interference with household functions, clothing selection, social/leisure activities, sports and work/study, troubles with family and friends, sexual problems, and problems caused by treatment. The each is scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3 (0=not at all [not relevant], 1=a little, 2=a lot, and 3=very much), providing a total score ranging from 0 to 30. The cutoff points are suggested as 2 for small effect, 6 for moderate effect, 11 for very large effect, and 21 for extremely large effect, indicating that 10 should be the proper cutoff point of DLQI for significant impairment of QoL47. DLQI is also widely used in clinical trials, research and clinical settings. It is also utilized to evaluate the changes of QoL over time. It takes less than 10 minutes to complete.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

The PSS is a 10-item brief SPRS to evaluate the level of stress subjectively experienced in daily life over the past month48. Overall, it asks about feelings and thoughts regarding various types of stress with distinct questions. Respondents are instructed to answer quickly without hesitation. The items ask about the frequency on feelings regarding “upset by unexpected events,” “unable to control important things in life,” “nervous and stressful,” “difficulty in handling personal problems,” “things were going their way,” “difficulty in coping with responsibility,” “control of irritation,” “on top of things,” “anger by events beyond their control,” and “overwhelmed by piling difficulty.” The each of the PSS is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0=never, 1=almost never, 2=sometimes, 3=fairly often, and 4=very often), with a total score ranging from 0 to 40. Importantly, the items of 4, 5, 7, and 8 should be reversely scored, requiring careful attention during assessment. With respect of interpretation, a total score of 0 to 13 is generally considered as low perceived stress, 14–26 as moderate stress, and scores of 27 or higher as high perceived stress49. Higher total scores on the PSS were significantly associated with increased levels of depression, anxiety, and negative affect, as well as decreased levels of positive affect49. The PSS has also been found to significantly correlate with somatic symptoms as well as mediation of mindfulness49,50. In addition, a number of studies have shown a positive association between PSS total score and cortisol functions and the firm biological markers of stress49,51,52. In fact, the PSS is widely used in the field of dermatology to evaluate the correlation between various skin diseases and stress. In particular, it is used as an important tool to quantify the stress level of patients with chronic inflammatory skin diseases and comprehensively understand the severity of the disease, QoL, prediction of treatment, and treatment response53,54,55.

Body Dysmorphic Disorder Questionnaire (BDDQ)

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is a mental disorder in which a person with normal appearance is obsessed with the idea that he or she has an imagined deformity or defect in his or her appearance, or that he or she views a minor defect as an exaggerated deformity21. The DSM-5 refers to a case in which there is an imagined preoccupation with one’s appearance, and even if there is a slight physical deformity, the patient’s obsession is excessive, and leads to significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other function. According to a recent meta-analysis using strictly focused on dermatology clinic population, the weighted mean prevalence of BDD was 12.5% among general dermatology patients and 25.0% among cosmetic dermatology patients, indicating a high popularity of BDD in people visiting a dermatology clinic in routine practice56. Therefore, early screening of patients suspected of having BDD has the advantage of increasing treatment efficiency for practitioners and providing optimal treatment to patients.

The BDDQ57 should be one of the most common screening tools for BDD, other useful screening instruments also exist though. The BDDQ includes 4 sets of “Yes/No” questions focused on concerns about physical appearance based on DSM. These questions include “Are you worried about how you look?,” “Are you concerned that you’re not thin enough or might become too fat?,” “How has this problem with your appearance affected your life?” (with 4 specific follow-up items), and “How much time do you spend thinking about your appearance?” (options: less than 1 hour, 1–3 hours, and more than 3 hours). Interestingly, no specific criteria or cutoff score has been established yet.

In the meantime, the BDDQ-dermatology version (DV) has been redeveloped and simplified to prove its specific and valid psychometric characteristics for dermatology settings but not for general psychiatric population. The BDDQ-DV was found to show high sensitivity (100%) and specificity (93%), indicating that it should be a very effective screening tool for BDD, especially in cosmetic dermatology58. In this BDDQ-DV, they replaced the format of Yes/No questions with a Likert scale (ranges from 1 for no distress/limitation to 5 for extreme distress/functioning) to indicate a range of distress and functioning caused by the defect. If the patients say “No” on the following first question: “Are you very concerned about the appearance of some part of your body, which you consider especially unattractive?,” the screening process is simultaneously terminated. A patient must report preoccupations and at least moderate (3 or more) levels of distress or dysfunction to be positively screened as BDD58. It takes 1–2 minutes to complete.

6-Item Adult Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) Self-Report Scale (ASRS-6)

The ASRS-659 is a brief SPRS for primarily screening ADHD over the past 6 months. Main symptoms included in the ASRS-6 are troubles in finishing tasks, difficulty in organization of tasks or activities, problems in remembering appointments or obligations, avoidance of tasks requiring focus or sustained effort, feelings of fidgety or restlessness when sitting for long, and feeling overly active or driven by a motor. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (0=never, 1=rarely, 2=sometimes, 3=often, 4=very often). Scoring is based on the presence of positive responses rather than the total sum of scores. Specifically, items 1, 2, 3, and 5 are considered positive if rated as “often” or “very often,” while items 4 and 6 are deemed positive if rated “sometimes” or higher. Positive result is considered when 4 or more items meet these thresholds, in which case further clinical evaluation for ADHD is recommended. Indeed, anxiety symptoms may inflate the total score of ASRS-6 since it would aggravate restlessness, cognitive function and psychomotor function, which are associated with symptoms of ADHD60. The ASRS-6 demonstrated superior performance compared to the 18-item original version of ASRS, showing higher sensitivity (68.7% vs. 56.3%) and greater specificity (99.5% vs. 98.3%), respectively. The ASRS-6 is widely utilized for adult ADHD screening, involving both inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity domains, and usually needs 2 to 3 minutes for completion of it.

Evaluation of patients with psychotic symptoms

The prevalence of classical psychotic symptoms shown in schizophrenia is not high in patients visiting dermatological clinics5, however, dermatological problems are common in patients with schizophrenia though61. Overall, routine screening tests for psychotic disorders are not prudent but also not recommended directly being performed by dermatologists and/or clinic staffs in routine treatment settings since such assessment are highly sensitive matters to patients, possibly causing uncomfortable feelings of humiliation, shamefulness, sense of being stigmatized, or frustration62.

Indeed, the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale63 and Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale64 have been widely used scales for assessing psychotic symptoms in routine treatment or research-based facility in psychiatric field. However, these scales require specialized background knowledge and training to conduct interviews, and they must also be administered in a structured settings. Furthermore, PANSS and BPRS need long time to complete (half an hour to 1 hour), indicating practical limitations as screening tools in busy, routine clinical settings. Furthermore, currently there are no simple, clear, and validated alternative scales for assessing psychotic symptoms that can be used directly by primary health professionals including dermatologists.

However, clinicians should remain alert for red flag symptoms, including persistent delusional beliefs (e.g., delusional parasitosis [DP]) or severe body image distortion since such patients are not uncommon to be seen in dermatological clinics than we expect. The diagnosis of DP or delusional infestation should be defined as delusions that persist for at least one month and are classified as primary or secondary, depending on the presence of an underlying psychiatric disorder, medical condition, or substance use65, which is classified as delusional disorder in DSM-5 criteria66. According to one survey study with 172 dermatologists (response rate, 68.6%)67, DP was found to be common, where most dermatologists (84.7%) had seen at least one patient with DP during their practice, with one to 5 cases seen in the past 5 years being the most common. Approximately 20% of dermatologists reported currently treating DP patients. Forty point seven percent (40.7%) requested psychiatric consultations always, while 28.8% requested them frequently, with more experienced dermatologists requesting more consultations.

For such cases, dermatologists should prioritize empathy for the patient's distress and prompt referral to a psychiatrist, rather than directly conducting a targeted psychiatric evaluation. The first step for dermatologist should be “show empathy to patient” to make a therapeutic alliance (doctor-patient relationship) by presenting following communications: “How difficult have you been with these symptoms?” and/or “You must have been very painful for a long time!.” However, one important point to remember during this process is that clinicians should not attempt to replace the role of psychiatrists or clinical psychologists, as psychological counseling involves highly complex processes, including the management of patients’ thoughts, emotions, cognitive errors, and therapeutic dynamics such as transference and counter-transference. When clinicians confront their patients by directly denying those symptoms or blaming them for bringing such issues to dermatology, it may potentially cause uncooperativeness, loss of follow up visiting, troublesome happenings or unpredictable behaviors in such patients. Next steps should include specification of symptoms, evaluation of dermatological symptoms including proper laboratory tests, and proposal for psychiatric evaluation as a way of collaboration treatments68.

For elaboration of patients’ symptoms, clinicians may ask them for specified issues as followings: onset of the symptoms, relationship with stress, family history, or influence on their routine life activities such as social and occupational functions. The assessment of dermatological symptoms should be simultaneously performed to meet patients’ expectation on dermatological conditions to be resolved, which is their main reason for visiting for dermatologist. This process should also include exclusion of underlying medical diseases or medications. Finally, clinicians may gently introduce patients to see psychiatrists or psychologists, as a part of multidisciplinary assessments, after thoughtful discussion with patients by persuading them that these symptoms could be caused by stress-related issues. PRACTICE POINT

Effective screening for psychiatric symptoms in dermatology patients may require a collaboration with mental health professionals who have proper understanding on the relationship between dermatological conditions and mental illnesses, alongside the use of structured assessment instruments and specialized interviewing techniques. Nevertheless, integration of such practices remains challenging to busy and demanding routine clinical practice settings. A large tertiary and/or university-affiliated training institutions may be feasible to implement such systematic approach, however, primary care settings, often operating with limited resources, have to find more efficient and cost-effective strategies. In this context, the active use of SPRSs which can be easily administered by patients themselves to evaluate their own mental health, stress levels, and related factors, may serve as a practical and appropriate alternative.

There are many different SPRSs available for clinical use, but primary care physicians do not need to know or use all of them. Therefore, they should use SPRSs that are already widely used, have a small number of questions, are easy to understand, include essential psychiatric evaluation items but also have a diagnostic tendency for specific mental disorders, can be completed in a short time, have a simple and clear scoring system, and have guaranteed validity and reliability.

Then, as a primary care physician, you may ask what and how many SPRSs to use when practicing dermatology. It is reasonable to think first about psychiatric disorders or symptoms that are commonly associated with dermatological diseases. In general, depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, stress, ADHD, and body image concerns are among the most common and comorbid psychological issues in dermatological patients. Given their prevalence and interconnected nature—often interacting to exacerbate or precipitate other psychiatric conditions—it is both reasonable and efficient to utilize SPRSs that assess these domains from the outset.

A flexible and patient-sensitive approach is recommended, allowing each clinic to select appropriate SPRSs based on available resources, patient volume, regional demographics, clinical progression, and practice system of the clinic. These SPRSs can be organized into practical screening packets, implemented systematically, and regularly refined through feedback from both patients and involved personnel.

Furthermore, it is crucial to develop a streamlined yet structured evaluation protocol or module which reflects a tailored and pragmatic system for assessing psychosocial stress, along with a strategic response framework. When healthcare providers lack awareness or understanding of the significance of the screening for psychosocial factors in dermatology, and do not acknowledge its necessity or not confident, patients are also less likely to engage or cooperate with the evaluation process.

In addition, the strategy and tactic of approach to patients should also be tailored and may be differed between first-time and long-term follow-up (or returning) patients to ensure appropriate support and continuity of care. For instance, during a patient’s initial visit—when medical history and basic environmental factors are comprehensively assessed—it is advantageous to administer a bundled packet of SPRSs such as the PHQ-9 (for depression), GAD-7 (for anxiety), ISI (for insomnia), and PSS (for psychosocial stress). This integrated approach allows for a thorough assessment of multiple aspects of psychological distress in a single session, enabling improvement of treatment planning, prediction of treatment response and prognosis assessment. In time-sensitive clinical settings where the use of multiple self-report rating scales may be impractical, the PHQ-2 and GAD-7 also offer efficient and effective alternatives. Administration of these 2 brief instruments requires less than 5 minutes, yet they yield clinically meaningful results. Both scales are extensively validated and widely implemented in psychiatric practice across the globe, demonstrating sensitivity rates exceeding 80%.

On the other hand, for long-term follow-up patients, when temporary stressors are suspected to influence the clinical course or when treatment responses deviate from expectations, conducting targeted evaluations—such as the PSS or ISI—at regular and timely intervals can provide valuable insights. This streamlined approach allows clinicians to monitor psychosocial factors efficiently and adjust treatment strategies accordingly, without overburdening the patient or the clinical workflow.

Should we uniformly perform SPRSs on all first-time patients? A more targeted approach may be efficient depending on the clinic’s situation, available resources, and patient volume. In such case, SPRSs can be selectively used toward individuals who exhibit psychological cues or behaviors that differ significantly from the general patient population during the initial visit. This allows for efficient use of resources while still identifying patients who may benefit most from psychosocial support and tailored interventions. Meanwhile, since there are some differences in the mental disorders and symptoms accompanying specific dermatological diseases, the composition of SPRSs packets according to dermatological diseases is also reasonable and enables a more patient-oriented approach. In the case of psoriasis, since depression, stress, and body image distortion among psychiatric comorbidities are the main issues, it is appropriate to use the PHQ-9 to first evaluate depressive symptoms, followed by the PSS to measure stress related to disease exacerbation, the BDDQ-DV to evaluate body image distortion, the GAD-7 to identify anxiety symptoms, and the ISI to check for sleep problems when necessary. On the other hand, since atopic dermatitis tends to be more marked by sleep disturbance due to itching, high anxiety level, and stress response, it is appropriate to apply SPRSs following priority: ISI to evaluate sleep disturbance first, GAD-7 to evaluate anxiety level, PSS to measure stress, PHQ-9 to check depressive symptoms, ADHD Self-Report Scale especially for children and adolescents, and BDDQ-DV to check body image problems in chronic medical history.

Patients who have suicidal ideation should be promptly handled by collaboration with psychiatric care and a multidisciplinary approach since it should be clinically prioritized due to potentiality of transition to direct suicidal behaviors. The PHQ-9 is a validated SPRS that effectively identifies individuals at pending risk for suicide attempts in primary care clinics as well as in specialized psychiatric settings. Importantly, answers to item 9 of the PHQ-9 (thoughts of death or self-harm behaviors) is strongly associated with future risk. Indeed, patients who reported frequent suicidal thoughts were found to be 6 times more likely to attempt suicide and 5 times more likely to die by suicide within the subsequent year compared to those without such ideation69. In fact, the suicide rate among dermatology patients is clinically significant and meaningful. A recent meta-analysis70 of 18 studies (n=1,767,583 patients, psoriasis n=330,207) found that subjects with psoriasis had a significantly elevated risk of suicide. Based on the results, the pooled odds ratio (OR) for suicidal ideation was 2.05, indicating that patients with psoriasis were more than twice as likely to experience suicidal thoughts compared to individuals without the condition. Additionally, the pooled OR for suicidal behaviors, including both attempted and completed suicides, was 1.26. In a subgroup analyses, increased risks for both suicide attempts (OR, 1.32) and completed suicides (OR, 1.20) among psoriasis patients were also found. Notably, higher levels of suicidality were found to be associated with greater disease severity and younger age.

For practical application in busy dermatology outpatient settings, the PHQ-2, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and DLQI may be considered as core assessment tools for routine screening, while the K6, PHQ-15, ISI, PSQI, PSS, BDDQ, and ASRS-6 can be optionally used depending on specific clinical circumstances. The primary barriers to effective intervention were time constraints and insufficient knowledge. Many dermatologists expressed interest in collaborating with mental health specialists and participating in suicide prevention training71. Research has demonstrated a widespread lack of understanding regarding multidisciplinary approaches to diagnosing and managing dermatological conditions, in relation to appropriate psychiatric referrals and consideration of the psychological components of dermatological disorders72. Moreover, there is a notable deficiency in awareness of resources available to support patients and families dealing with the psychiatric symptoms associated with dermatological conditions72. Hence, the establishment of assessment modules or protocols for patients likely to experience difficulties due to psychiatric disorders or psychosocial factors would facilitate early identification and screening of those with underlying psychiatric concerns. Finally, given the expanding use of artificial intelligence and other advanced digital technologies, including wearable devices, in psychiatry for symptom assessment, diagnosis, and the early detection of potential relapse, integration of these technologies into dermatological practice may also contribute to more efficient and accurate symptom monitoring and diagnostic evaluation73,74,75.

CONCLUSION

Early screening and detection of psychiatric disorders and psychosocial factors are essential and also imperative in dermatology practice, as these commonly influences on the onset, exacerbation, recurrence, and treatment responsiveness in numerous dermatological conditions. Common psychiatric comorbidities including MDD, anxiety disorders, and body image distortion may significantly aggravate skin symptoms. Early identification through timely and appropriate screening enables more effective, integrated care, improves patient adherence, and enhances clinical outcomes. Incorporation of mental health evaluations tailored to the specific clinic settings and available resources can substantially improve both treatment results and patient satisfaction. Finally, successful establishment of specialized clinic service such as dermatology-psychiatry liaison would also further support the management of these patients in clinical settings.
