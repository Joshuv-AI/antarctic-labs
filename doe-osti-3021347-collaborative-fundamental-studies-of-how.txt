TITLE: Collaborative: Fundamental studies of how water influences the synthesis and behavior of
zeolite catalysts for reactions in the circular carbon economy.

TECHNICAL REPORTING PERIOD: 9/1/2024 to 12/31/2024

DOE OFFICE OF SCIENCE PROGRAM: OFFICE OF BASIC ENERGY SCIENCES (BES)

TOPIC AREA: Catalysis Science

PROGRAM MANAGER: Viviane Schwartz, Chris Bradley

The goals of this project are:

a) To develop predictive, first-principles models that describe how organic and inorganic structure-
directing agents (SDAs) influence the siting of framework Al and other trivalent heteroatoms in MFI
zeolites.

b) To determine how active site location and confinement within MFI influence reaction energetics,
mechanisms, and selectivity in arene methylation chemistry, particularly in the presence of oxygenates
and water.
c) To establish quantitative structure–reactivity relationships that connect zeolite synthesis conditions,
atomic-scale active site distributions, and catalytic function, using density functional theory (DFT) as the
primary tool.

Brief Summary:
This project established a computational framework to predict and interpret how synthesis conditions
control active site location in MFI zeolites and how those site distributions influence catalytic reactivity.
The Hibbitts Group led the theoretical effort, applying periodic DFT calculations to quantify (i) SDA–
framework–heteroatom interactions during crystallization and (ii) the energetics and mechanisms of arene
methylation reactions within distinct MFI environments.

First, DFT was used to evaluate the relative stability of Al, Ga, Fe, and B substitution at
crystallographically distinct T-sites in MFI in the presence of representative SDAs. These calculations
quantified how electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and confinement effects bias heteroatom
siting between channel and intersection voids. The results establish a predictive link between SDA
identity and framework heteroatom distribution, enabling rational design of synthesis conditions to
control active site location.

Second, DFT was used to determine reaction pathways and free energy barriers for toluene methylation
mediated by dimethyl ether and methanol. Calculations compared mechanisms involving surface methyl

species, co-adsorbed oxygenates, and cluster-mediated transition states, and evaluated how these
pathways vary between channel and intersection sites. The results demonstrate that confinement alters
both transition-state structure and regioselectivity, providing a molecular-level explanation for
experimentally observed selectivity trends.

Collectively, this work advances fundamental understanding of how zeolite synthesis controls atomic-
scale structure and how that structure governs catalytic function. The computational models developed
here provide transferable tools for predicting active site distributions and reaction energetics in other
zeolite frameworks and oxygenate conversion chemistries relevant to the circular carbon economy.
The project trained graduate students in advanced periodic DFT methods, microkinetic interpretation of
first-principles data, and mechanistic analysis of confined catalytic systems, strengthening U.S. expertise
in computational catalysis and materials design.

This project has been continued, under a new award number, at Purdue University, as PI Hibbitts has
relocated there.
