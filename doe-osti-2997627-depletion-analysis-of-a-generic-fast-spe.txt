EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Advanced reactors are of interest for a variety of use scenarios, and there are numerous advanced reactor
designs being considered, which includes molten salt reactors (MSRs). Depending on the design,
advanced reactors may have more extensive material control and accounting (MC&A) processes than
current Light Water Reactor (LWR) designs.

This project evaluated the use of Monte Carlo simulations for generating information that could guide the
development of MC&A approaches for MSRs. In particular, the current capabilities of MCNP version 6.3
internally coupled with CINDER’90 were assessed for a fast spectrum liquid fueled MSR with online
fission product removal and refueling. The fission product removal and refueling was performed in
batches, and a Python wrapper was developed to control the simulations and update the fuel composition.

Obtaining experimental data to develop MC&A methods for MSRs will be costly, time intensive, and
limited by available facilities. While modeling and simulation techniques could augment experimental
campaigns for evaluating and optimizing MC&A methods, it is essential to understand the limitations of
the simulations to adequately assess the results. In this effort several simulation parameters were varied,
and the impact on the predicted uranium and plutonium mass were studied.

The parameters that were considered as part of this project were the convergence of the depletion
simulation and the fission product removal frequency. In this study, the calculated isotope inventory was
sensitive to the size of the initial timestep, and this simulation parameter needs to be considered when
evaluating results. For example, without proper convergence studies, the differences in keff and special
nuclear material (SNM) mass could be misinterpreted as being due to the reprocessing frequency, when in
reality were caused by step size. It was found that when reprocessing frequency was varied to better
account for gaseous bubbling removal of some fission products, there was a noticeable change in keff and
SNM mass. However, it was unclear whether the difference was attributed to a simulation parameter or a
physical effect. These types of behaviors require careful consideration and understanding when using
modeling and simulation (M&S) predictions to generate MC&A information.

The process for producing a gamma spectrum with this simulation methodology was also demonstrated.
Comparison spectra were generated for different reactor operating times. While there are some gamma
source lines which change as a function of reactor operating time, the complexity of the spectra illustrated
the challenges associated with performing this type of analysis for MC&A purposes. Model validation
will be an essential aspect of leveraging simulations for guiding MC&A approaches.

This study was a M&S exercise and did not represent any particular reactor design. Additionally, the
purpose of this exercise was not to provide any recommendations to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission
(NRC), but rather to provide a generic example with representative features that could inform NRC
considerations.

While this study was looking at a generic reactor design, the method could be leveraged by advanced
reactor designers and developers to support the development of their MC&A approaches. Additionally,
examples of analyses needed to ensure underlying physics are well represented were also included.

 Introduction
Advanced reactors are of interest for a variety of use scenarios. There are numerous designs being
considered, including molten salt reactors (MSRs). Some MSR designs are based on solid fuel, while
others utilize a liquid fuel. MSR designs typically operate at low pressure and have passive safety features
(e.g. freeze plugs, etc.). Their higher operating temperatures compared to traditional designs allow for
increased thermal efficiency, and the potential to utilize waste heat for industrial applications. More
importantly, the ability to reprocess fuel continuously (or semi-continuously in batches) allows for
actinides and fission products to stay in the core longer. Online fuel reprocessing also eliminates the need
to shut the reactor down, which increases economic feasibility.

Depending on the design, advanced reactors may have more extensive material control and accounting
(MC&A) processes than current Light Water Reactor (LWR) designs. Intact LWR fuel assemblies can be
treated as single items for MC&A, while fuel systems for advanced reactors could have more discrete
items or require accounting for bulk material. MC&A for advanced reactors may benefit from leveraging
modeling and simulation (M&S) for material predictions and the use of measurements. M&S can help to
identify the best MC&A practices for a nuclear facility in a cost-effective, risk-informed way. To
effectively leverage M&S for assessment of MC&A methods, it is imperative that the approach is well
understood, and any limitations are identified. Unique challenges are present when simulating the
evolution of the reactor inventory as fuel moves around the core and is reprocessed.

A previous study simulated three broad categories of advanced reactors and examined the data that could
be generated by Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP®) depletion calculations (Hoffman et al., 2025). Based
on the results of that effort, a more detailed analysis of a generic MSR is presented here.

This report describes what is currently possible using the MCNP code’s internal depletion solver
combined with a Python wrapper to adjust fuel inventory and emulate reprocessing. Areas needing
improvement are highlighted, and MC&A considerations are provided along with M&S results. Inventory
calculations were examined for a simplified sodium chloride fast reactor using high-assay low-enriched
uranium (HALEU) fuel with reprocessing (two group fission product removal, fuel addition, and no
actinide removal). The relevance of the results for assessing MC&A approaches and next steps were
discussed.

This study was an M&S exercise and did not represent any particular reactor design. Additionally, the
purpose of this exercise was not to provide any recommendations to the NRC, but rather to provide a
generic example with representative features that could inform NRC considerations.

 Background
There are many advantages to MSR designs, which are either solid or liquid fueled. In solid-fueled
designs, a coated particle fuel is typically used, similar to gas-cooled reactor concepts. Liquid fuel is often
associated with the U-233 – Th-232 fuel cycle because more neutron-capture events are required to
transmute thorium into transuranic elements compared to the U-235 - U-238 fuel cycle. However, the
thorium cycle requires the aid of fissile fuel from the uranium cycle to start up (Serp et al., 2014). Liquid
fuel can also be used with the more traditional uranium fuel cycle, and there have been numerous studies
exploring different designs, spectra, salts, and other reactor metrics.

Molten salt reactor development was historically performed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL),
starting with the aircraft reactor experiment (ARE), which first operated in 1954. Early designs used
fluoride salt because F-19 is the only stable fluorine isotope, which simplified design and analysis. Other
material challenges motivated a simpler design, which led to the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment
(MSRE) which achieved criticality in 1965. The MSRE was a thermal spectrum fluoride salt reactor.
Metallic components in contact with salt were made from a nickel-based alloy, later called Hastelloy-N
(Serp et al., 2014).

The MSRE project was eventually dropped in the early 1970s in favor of the more mature Liquid Metal
Fast Breeder Reactor (LMFBR). Since 2005, much of MSR-specific research has shifted toward fast
spectrum designs with the intent to improve nuclear fuel utilization and minimize nuclear waste (Serp et
al., 2014).

One of the primary advantages of fluid-fueled reactor designs is the potential for convenient refueling and
reprocessing compared to traditional reactors. This increases the residency time of actinides inside the
core, improving the probability of long-lived radioactive isotopes transmutation into stable nuclides.
These systems could theoretically be designed to only produce low-level waste, such that no high-level
waste repository would be needed for the spent fuel. In an online reprocessing scheme, the separations
occur inside the reactor loop and happen continuously. In an offline scheme, reprocessing occurs in
batches with discrete removal events, and the reprocessing would likely be performed in a separate
facility.

Liquid fuel is challenging to model with traditional neutron transport and depletion codes, which are
typically designed to simulate solid-fueled reactors. However, in the past decade significant work has
been done either coupling transport codes to fuel cycle codes or using wrappers to modify material
inventories. Recently, some codes dedicated to MSR startup and equilibrium routines have even been
developed (Hombourger et al., 2020).

The removal of fission products is modeled according to their “cycle time.” Cycle time is defined as the
time needed to process the entire inventory given a constant volumetric rate. There are two main
modeling approaches for isotope removal: the first is to introduce an additional term in the Bateman
equations such that the removal of the fission products is done during the depletion calculation. The
second method is to remove the fission products in batches, at discrete points in time between the
transport and depletion calculations (Hombourger et al., 2020).

There have been more studies focused on fluoride salt designs than chloride (Bettis et al., n.d.;
MacPherson, 1985). However, fast spectrum reactors are generally considered more effective at
transmuting actinides, due to higher neutron flux and the tendency for the ratio of the fission to capture
cross sections to be larger at high energies (i.e. above 1 MeV). This trend in cross section is shown for
four isotopes of plutonium in Figure 1. Sodium chloride (NaCl) is suggested for fast systems because it

The various configurations and system parameters lead to a variety of MSR designs. As was noted in the
introduction, these different parameters include the energy spectra, fuel forms, salt compositions, and
materials. To provide context for the results presented in this report, the model used for the study is
described here.

 MSR Model Description

The geometry used in this work was derived from a fast spectrum thorium-fueled breeder reactor which
was modified to use the uranium fuel cycle (Brovchenko et al., 2019). The design is highly simplified and
underwent limited optimization to reduce reactor size or improve neutron economy. During model
development, priority was given to parameters with a strong influence on depletion calculations, and this
section is ordered accordingly.

The geometry of the model was simplified as much as possible so that more computational resources
could be allocated to depletion parameters such as tracked isotope inventory and time steps. Figure 2
shows a cross-sectional view of the MCNP geometry. The left image is a horizontal slice taken at the
center of the reactor (the XY plane), while the right image is a vertical slice, also taken at the center (the
XZ plane).

 Fuel Composition

The fuel salt was modeled as a mixture of NaCl and UCl3. The only eutectic point for this mixture is at 66%
NaCl and 34% UCl3, which allows for a liquid fuel at the relatively low temperature of 796 K. The change
in melting temperature as a function of fission product accumulation has not been thoroughly investigated
(Mausolff et al., 2021). HALEU was used as the fuel, enriched to 19.75%. The density of the fuel was 3.112
g/cm3 at 900 K, using a correlation found by Mausolff et al., 2021 based on data collected by Desyatnik et
al., 1975.

The chlorine in the NaCl and UCl3 was enriched to 99% Cl-37, from the natural ratio of 76% Cl-35 and
24% Cl-37 (Degueldre & Merk, 2024). Chlorine-37 has a smaller neutron absorption cross section than Cl-
35, shown in Figure 3. Additionally, Cl-36 (produced from neutron activation of Cl-35) has a 300,000-year
half-life, while Cl-37 decays quickly into a stable isotope with minimal impact on reactor performance.
Enriching chlorine is physically easier than enriching uranium, and the lower cross section allows for
reactor designs with less fissile material.

 Fuel Volume and Reactor Power

The size of the reactor model was parametrically adjusted to achieve five dollars of excess reactivity with
fresh fuel. The excess reactivity can be accounted for by conventional control mechanisms, and the
margin helps maintain an average keff closer to one over the lifetime of the reactor. Although keff is not
used directly in MCNP burnup calculations, the amount of fuel present in the reactor strongly influences
the depletion results.

Approximately 32 m3 of fuel salt was simulated. The reactor model was a cylinder approximately 5.2
meters in diameter and 4.8 meters in height. The power was set to 3,000 MWth, which was in line with
existing literature. However, reported designs had a lack of information on consistent material data, core
geometry details, and flow path specifics (Mausolff et al., 2021). The reactor power has a strong influence
on depletion results, as it is used to normalize reaction rate tallies in the simulation. The parameters used
resulted in a power density of 93.75 kW/L, which was on the low end of the distribution of MSR
published values, shown in Table 1.

 Other Materials

A NiCrW Hastelloy was used as a neutron reflector and was the primary structural material in contact
with the fuel salt (Brovchenko et al., 2019). As Brovchenko et al. suggested, the fertile blanket in the
original design has been replaced with more nickel alloy. Boron carbide is modeled as a neutron shield,
which would protect pumps and other equipment (which are not modeled). The B4C is not depleted,
although this may be important in a more detailed analysis.

 Fuel Addition and Removal

Circulating molten fuel can be filtered, processed, removed, or added during operation. Two processing
methods are represented in the simulation: helium gas-based extraction and offline reprocessing. In a
helium gas-based extraction, helium bubbles are used to remove noble metals and gaseous fission
products. This is an efficient process, where the removal half-life could be just 30 seconds for the
products the bubbles can effectively extract (Brovchenko et al., 2019). For the offline reprocessing
removal method, a portion of the fuel is removed from the reactor during operation, cleaned of fission
products, and returned to the system. For this study a volume equivalent to the total inventory was
processed each simulated year. This means that if batch reprocessing is simulated every month, then
1/12th of the relevant fission products will be removed each time. In a physical plant, a batch reprocessing
scheme might look like removing a portion of the fuel and replacing it with fresh material. The removed
fuel would then be reprocessed in a separate facility while the reactor continues to operate.

The flow rate of the molten salt is fast, approximately four seconds for the heat exchanger loop and 30
seconds for a complete circuit (Brovchenko et al., 2019). The simulations in this work treat the liquid fuel
as homogenously mixed. This allows the entire core to be treated as a single depletion volume and greatly
simplifies the spatial dimension of depletion calculations. In reality, this assumption does not hold for
isotopes with half-lives near or below the recirculation time (Abou-Jaoude et al., 2021), however it should
be appropriate for the time scales and global parameters analyzed in this report.

 Removal Scheme

Fission product removal was simulated in batches. A Python wrapper was used to run small MCNP
burnup calculations and update material composition between simulations to account for removal and
replacement. The results were stitched together, which is illustrated in Figure 4 below. Each color
represents a single MCNP depletion calculation with several timesteps. The rise in keff between
calculations is due to the removal of fission products, which occurred instantaneously at discrete intervals.
As the reprocessing interval decreased, the frequency more closely resembled continuous removal (rather
than batches), and the gaseous helium removal mechanism was better represented. Since the design goal
of this reactor concept was actinide transmutation, actinides were not removed by reprocessing.

In this report the phrase “depletion time steps” was used to describe the traditional predictor-corrector
time steps taken by MCNP/CINDER in the coupled depletion calculation. “Reprocessing steps” were
performed by the Python wrapper in between MCNP depletion calculations. A single reprocessing step is
outlined below:

 1. Python generates an MCNP input file containing the burn card, using either the initial fuel
 inventory, or a calculated one.

 2. Python executes MCNP; a standard burnup calculation is performed using one or several steps.

 3. Python parses the MCNP output file for the resulting isotope inventory.

 4. Python performs fission product removal and fuel addition as described in Section 2.2. The new
 inventory is calculated and the process repeats.

When updating material composition (i.e. reprocessing), all non-soluble fission products were removed
(noble metals and gases). This is an approximation for these isotopes, as their removal half-life is closer
to 30 seconds. The elements removed by helium gas bubbling were taken from Brovchenko et al., 2019.

Cycle time is used to determine what fraction of an isotope was removed. One year was an average cycle
time used to simplify the analysis for offline reprocessing isotopes. In reality, different fission products
have shorter or longer removal rates (Hombourger et al., 2020; Rykhlevskii et al., 2019). The elements in
this category (also from Brovchenko et al., 2019) are also listed in Table 2.

 Addition Scheme

After fission product removal, eutectic NaCl-UCl3 was added to the fuel to maintain constant mass. Since
the volume did not change during the simulation, this also enforced constant density. As the simulation
progressed less fuel was added due to the accumulation of fission products and actinides, which were not
removed.

 Summary of Model Assumptions

The geometry of the MSR was highly simplified to allow for a greater fraction of computational resources
to be spent on depletion time steps (Section 2.1). The model consisted of fuel, surrounded by a neutron
reflector, and a neutron shield. There were cylindric volumes representing fuel flow paths on the top,
bottom, and sides of the reactor. The entire core was surrounded by a structural material (Figure 2). Heat
exchangers and pumps were not modeled, neither was any kind of reactivity control (Section 2.1.3).

The entire fuel volume was treated as one material in depletion simulations, a result of assuming the fuel
was homogeneously mixed (Section 2.2). Only the fuel material was depleted, and reprocessing occurred
instantaneously. Isotopes were split into two removal groups (Table 2): “bubbling” isotopes (completely
removed at each reprocessing event), and “reprocessing” isotopes (fractionally removed according to the
frequency of reprocessing). All isotopes in the reprocessing category were given an average cycle time of
one year (Section 2.2.1), although in reality different isotopes have different removal rates.

During reprocessing (i.e. between MCNP calculations) the ratio of sodium to uranium and plutonium
atoms was calculated to be 66 to 34 (Section 2.2.1). To calculate the number of chlorine atoms, the
simplification was made that all chlorine existed as either NaCl, UCl3, or PuCl3. The eutectic ratio for
uranium chloride was also used for plutonium chloride. This was done to calculate the removal of excess
chlorine (and sodium, to a much lesser extent).

MCNP only outputs the masses of isotopes which can be included in the transport calculation, not the
entire inventory produced by the depletion solver. This means that not all isotopes produced by the
depletion solver were able to be reinserted into the next calculation. Isotopes with transport cross sections
make up a vast majority of the total mass, but not the total activity.

 Methodology
To generate the data needed for this study, 3D reactor core models were created with MCNP to provide
nuclear material quantities. The models were also used to demonstrate simulation capabilities for other
relevant MC&A signatures. The MCNP code is a general-purpose radiation transport code developed at
LANL over the past forty-six-plus years (Kulesza et al., 2022). MCNP is capable of simulating the
fundamental physics of particles as they move through a model and interact with materials. MCNP has
been the international gold standard for particle transport for over three decades. Depleted fuel material
compositions can be estimated at various points in time using the internally coupled transmutation code
CINDER’90 (Wilson et al., 2007). Neutron and gamma source terms can be generated from the inventory
of unstable nuclides, which can in turn be input back into MCNP to simulate potential radiation signatures
present at various points in time.

Transport solutions were obtained with MCNP version 6.3 in conjunction with data from the ENDF/B-
VIII.0 Evaluated Nuclear Data Library (D. A. Brown et al., 2018). Python was used for scripting and
implementing isotope removal and addition. Fuel salt neutron cross sections at 900 K were used in all
simulations, while other material cross sections were at 600 K. For individual criticality calculations, the
Shannon entropy reported by MCNP was used to assess the spatial convergence of the fission source. The
rest of this section outlines studies performed, and any changes made to the model for certain analyses.

 Long Term Behavior and Simulation Convergence

A three-month batch reprocessing scheme was initially chosen to explore long term reactor behavior. This
decision was made to balance reprocessing frequency with computational efficiency. Using this model,
every three months all “bubbling” fission products were removed along with one-fourth of all
“reprocessing” isotopes (Table 2). Fresh fuel was then added to maintain constant mass. The neutron
multiplication factor over time was plotted to explore large trends in neutron economy, the neutron
spectrum was briefly considered to build familiarity with the system, and isotope mass trends were
assessed and related back to the neutron multiplication factor and spectrum.

Since Monte Carlo uncertainty is not propagated to isotope mass, there are two ways to assess depletion
simulation convergence. The first is to examine the uncertainty of the MCNP Collision Rates table,
however it can be difficult to determine which isotope collisions are important to the simulation. The
second method is to run a higher resolution simulation and ensure the results do not change with the
number of histories. This is very similar to the mesh convergence study typically done with deterministic
codes. The results of several simulations executed with increasing amounts of histories were compared,
and several example plots were provided in this report for illustration.

Initially four refueling events per year were simulated. At each reprocessing step, all “bubbling” isotopes
were removed, along with one fourth of “reprocessing” isotopes (Table 2). The one fourth removal
fraction corresponded to a cycle time of one year (Section 2.2.1). Some preliminary results are presented
first, followed by an assessment of simulation convergence, and finally a more detailed analysis.

An example of the results obtained using a single 91.25-day depletion step between each reprocessing
event is shown in Figure 8. The error bars represent the uncertainty in the Monte Carlo result,
corresponding to a 95% confidence interval (Kulesza et al., 2022). Ten thousand histories per cycle and
200 active cycles were used, which produced a standard deviation for keff of around 40 pcm. The time step
scheme, reprocessing frequency, and number of histories were initially chosen to accommodate runtime
constraints and are evaluated in the next section.

 Figure 8: MSR neutron multiplication factor over time with four reprocessing events per year.

The neutron multiplication factor dropped during the first 10 years of operation, corresponding to the
consumption of U-235, the primary power producing isotope during this time (Figure 8). Common
thermal neutron poisons (e.g. Xe-135, Gd-159, Sm-149) are not as effective in fast systems, because their
absorption cross sections drop dramatically at higher energies. The rise in neutron multiplication factor
between years 15 and 60 corresponded to the accumulation of plutonium in the fuel, after which
equilibrium was reached. In this report, ‘equilibrium’ refers to any period for which an apparent steady
state is achieved.

The neutron spectrum for this design was also simulated after 5, 15, 30, and 70 years of reactor operation
to provide insight into expected differences as the uranium and plutonium content changed. The results
show some initial changes (i.e. from 5 to 15 years) due to fission product accumulation and the transition
from U-235 to Pu-239 as the main power producing isotope (Figure 9). The spectrum does not change as
much over the remainder of the simulated lifetime. The significant drop in the neutron population around
3 keV was due to the sodium cross section (Figure 9), which scattered neutrons out of that energy.

 Discussion
 M&S Considerations

Depletion results were converged with a small number of active histories due to the geometric simplicity
of the model used. However, the size and spectrum of the reactor did require a considerable number of
skipped cycles to converge individual KCODE calculations. This occurred because neutrons in fast
systems typically have more variable path lengths and fewer absorptions than in thermal systems. This
means that the fission source takes more iterations to become spatially stable. This will have a more
significant impact on a more spatially complex system. Although an ENDF VIII.0 to ENDF VIII.1
comparison was not done in this report, the nuclear data update has been shown to have minimal impact
on results, but noticeable impact on the uncertainty (Valentin et al., 2025). This could reduce the number
of skipped cycles needed to spatially converge the fission source.

Despite the geometric simplicity, the simulations in this work involved significant computational
expense. On a High-Performance Computing (HPC) cluster with 72 MPI tasks (ran across a single node)
with 2.4 GHz CPUs, results took between 10 and 50 days to complete, depending on the study. To run
simulations in a reasonable amount of time, assumptions, simplifications, and approximations must be
made, and it is important to understand the potential impacts on the results.

It should be noted that extra simulations are needed to determine if the highest resolution simulation in
the convergence study was actually converged (Section 4.1). The largest differences in isotope inventory
in these studies were from the size of the initial timestep. As the fuel started to accumulate fission
products and plutonium, the shape of the flux changed significantly (Figure 9), and smaller time steps
were needed to resolve the non-linear behavior.

If the three-month reprocessing frequency were included in Figure 20, it could be misleading without the
context of the convergence study to illustrate that the resulting discrepancy in keff and SNM mass were
due to step size and not reprocessing frequency. It is also not clear why the green line in Figure 20
showed a slightly different trend as plutonium reached equilibrium. It was most likely due to the change

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 23

in the neutron spectrum over time, however it is unknown if this change was physical, or an artifact of the
simulation.

A simulation parameter that could have caused the difference in Figure 20 is the AFMIN argument on the
burn card. AFMIN is used to stop isotopes from entering the simulation unless they represent a significant
atom fraction. As time steps were made shorter, less isotopes would have reached the AFMIN threshold
during a single reprocessing interval. One way to tell would be to run a sensitivity study on the AFMIN
parameter to see if it influenced the results. These types of behaviors require careful consideration when
using M&S predictions to generate MC&A information.

When reprocessing frequency was varied to better account for gaseous bubbling removal (such as Xe-
135), there was not a significant change in keff or SNM mass. The accumulation of fission products has
very little neutronic impact on fast spectrum reactors. This makes them better candidates for load
following than thermal systems. Reactor shutdown periods or frequent changes in power levels may also
need to be accounted for when generating signatures.

The highest intensity components of the gamma source changed significantly over time as the reactor
transitioned from U-235 to plutonium. When the gamma source was transported through a simple
geometry to a detector, lines above 6 MeV had poor stochastic error (Figure 23). Although these gammas
are unlikely to be emitted, variance reduction could be used to sample these lines more frequently if they
were desired for an MC&A analysis.

 Limitations of MCNP

The MCNP internal depletion solver has two options for adjusting fuel composition mid-calculation.
SWAPB is typically used in fuel shuffling calculations to change the position of fuel elements in the core.
MATMOD can adjust the fractions of individual isotopes, but only by setting them to discrete values
(e.g., it could not remove a fraction of existing fission products). Therefore, some kind of wrapper must
be used to perform the reprocessing material adjustments, and neither MATMOD nor SWAPB were used
in this study.

MCNP only outputs inventory information (atom density, mass, activity, etc.) for isotopes in the transport
calculation. The isotopes in the CINDER library, which have 63-group transmutation probabilities but not
neutron cross sections, are not reported by MCNP at all. Typically, around 300 isotopes are available with
transport cross sections, and over 3000 are transmuted by CINDER. The isotopes in the CINDER library
without transport cross sections make up a very small portion of the mass (around 0.01%) but represent
one to two thirds of the total activity during operation (many have very short half-lives). When the
wrapper code restarts the MCNP calculation, it is not able to carry these isotopes over, so their masses are
lost each time. This is not expected to be a significant source of error for the global results in this report,
but will affect detailed activity or spectrum calculations.

MCNP prints depletion results in scientific notation, with three significant figures. For isotopes with large
masses, the third digit can represent a large amount of mass. When calculating the concentration of
impurities, or performing activation analysis, rounding error may be significant. This is especially true
when a wrapper propagates the error throughout a larger calculation. The Monte Carlo uncertainty is also
not transmitted to the nuclide inventory, which may be important, depending on simulation parameters.

The number of depletion steps needed to get close to the cycle time of “bubbling” isotopes is
prohibitively computationally expensive. One solution not explored in this paper was to use the OMIT

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 24

feature of the MCNP burn card to omit the bubbling isotopes from the transport calculation. They would
still be produced by CINDER, but they would not have any impact on the transport solution (keff, neutron
spectrum, etc.). This may be a better approximation for these isotopes, and allow for larger reprocessing
steps to be taken, while still converging to the same steady state.

 MC&A Considerations

As was discussed in Section 4, simulations can be used to obtain information to guide the development of
MC&A approaches. However, it is important to understand the context of model limitations (described
above), to fully assess the meaning of any MC&A information that is generated. As can be seen in the
results presented in Section 4, SNM quantities change with time (particularly at BOL), these changes will
need to be understood so that an appropriate MC&A approach can be developed. It should be noted that
this is also true for LWRs, however their fuel form is different than what will be used for MSRs, and the
codes used to predict SNM inventories as a function of burnup for LWRs have been well validated.

M&S of MSRs can be used to predict SNM inventories and expected changes under different operating
conditions. This may be useful to meet the requirement of annual inventory reporting through the Nuclear
Materials Management and Safeguards System (NMMSS). M&S techniques can also be leveraged to
provide data for comparisons while operating knowledge is being obtained. However, it is essential that
the simulation methodologies are validated, and adequate bench-marking data is available. Further, as was
shown in Section 4, there are numerous parameters which will influence the results and must be
considered when assessing information for use in developing an MC&A program (e.g. time-step size).
Several other MC&A considerations related to the simulations presented in this report are discussed
below.

 Simulation Convergence Impacts

Monte Carlo codes do an excellent job of transporting neutrons across complex and novel geometries,
however there is less experience and “rules of thumb” to rely upon when simulating advanced reactors.
Like most simulations, calculations which predict MC&A relevant parameters (i.e. SNM quantities) need
to be sufficiently converged such that the average of the simulation represents the average of the physical
quantity being estimated. Thus, it is important that any use of models to support an MC&A approach is
based on converged simulation results.

As was noted previously, depletion codes for LWRs are well-established and have been thoroughly
validated. For the advanced reactor designs it will be important to document any parameters that can
cause variations, and make sure that information is presented with simulation results. The results shown
in Section 4.1 (e.g. Figure 14) illustrate that the predicted plutonium mass is affected by the size of the
depletion time steps. If simulations are used to generate predictions for SNM quantities throughout the
lifetime of the reactor, a large depletion time step could lead to an under-prediction of plutonium and an
over-prediction of uranium. The appropriate time step for different reactor designs would need to be
identified; initially, small time steps would likely be required and the results validated as operating
experience is gained. Based on validated simulation data, the acceptable time steps could be updated.

 Fission Product Removal

The results presented in Section 4.3 (e.g. Figure 20) illustrate that the reprocessing frequency might have
an impact on SNM inventory prediction. This demonstrates the importance of using simulation
parameters that are aligned with the operating scheme for a particular reactor, and that the predictions are
sound. For example in the simple MSR modeled in this study, the reactor transitioned from being
predominantly powered by U-235 to being mostly powered by Pu-239 after about 15 years. This change

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 25

may happen more gradually, or at a different time depending on fuel composition, operating history, and
cycle times of isotopes. This will have an impact on what signatures can be measured at what time, and
how the MC&A program is developed. The uranium and plutonium enrichment were also both highest at
the start of the fuel cycle (Figure 18 and Figure 19). However, the Pu-239 mass was lowest during this
time (Figure 19). This means that plutonium diversion became less attractive over time, but some period
must pass before there was enough to divert. These results also highlight the need for code comparisons
and experimental data to verify and validate against.

 Gamma Ray Spectra

The simulated gamma ray spectrum shown in Figure 23 illustrates that this will be a complex signature,
and the method in which it is simulated requires careful evaluation. There are many gamma ray emissions
associated with the fuel salt sample. When these gamma rays are detected, the complexity of the spectrum
increases due to the Compton continuum, escape peaks, and detector resolution. To establish if a signature
has potential to be used in an MC&A program, a high-fidelity simulation of the measuring environment
needs to be performed, including a realistic detector geometry. The MCNP results would then need to be
processed to identify peaks. Additionally, the Compton continuum and interference from other lines could
result in the highest intensity peaks being ineffective as MC&A signatures. Similar studies have been
performed recently for generic cases, but as more M&S results are published, the need for verification and
validation will increase (Kovacevic et al., 2023).

The intersection of experimental data and M&S predictions must also be considered. For example, in a
physical measurement, there will be other effects which will need to be evaluated. It is possible that the
fuel sample will be too hot after 15 minutes of decay for a reliable measurement to be obtained (due to
detector deadtime). This will influence the signatures that are available; neptunium-239 has a 2.4 day
half-life, and may have decayed significantly by the time the sample is cool enough to measure.
Additionally, short-lived isotopes are highly sensitive to changes in neutron flux over time during
depletion calculations (Figure 22). It was shown in Section 4.1 that small changes in the simulation
methodology can lead to unconverged results, which can be difficult and computationally expensive to
identify.

The specific information being extracted will impact the necessary analysis approach. For example, there
are peak ratios that will change as a function of reactor operating time and these peaks may be correlated
to predicted SNM quantities for specific operating times. For a signature to be useful for MC&A purposes
it needs be correlated to a parameter of interest (e.g. SNM). Therefore, just because there is a strong
signature that does not necessarily guarantee that it will be useful for MC&A. Some work has been
performed examining signatures relevant to diversion scenarios in a thermal spectrum MSR with fluoride
fuel and partial refueling (Kovacevic et al., 2023). Additionally, new analysis methods for MSR gamma
spectra are being evaluated, such as leveraging machine learning techniques (Mishra et al., 2024).

 Future Work

The impact of different M&S parameters for several different predicted values in a generic MSR were
examined in this work. The results illustrate that these parameters will need to be considered when
assessing M&S results for any MSR design. Future work should further examine signatures that could be
present with representative samples, including quantifying effects associated with non-homogeneous
samples and variations expected with different sample sizes. Further, M&S could be used to compare

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 26

different potential measurement locations. For example, looking at a representative fuel salt composition
in various pipes, or sample apparatuses. Any samples pulled will cool down, thus the impact of phase and
temperature on measurements should also be examined. The impact of fission product accumulation on
salt properties is also mostly unknown. Future work should also consider the sensitivity of any signatures
selected for MC&A purposes to variations in reactor operating parameters. Understanding changes
expected during normal operations will provide insight into what variations could indicate an off-normal
scenario.

A more comprehensive proliferation analysis could be done using the ratio of actinides present to
determine when the reactor is most susceptible to fuel diversion. This could change depending on the
starting fuel composition, the equilibrium concentrations of plutonium, and the timeframe in which the
fuel composition evolves. It was mentioned in Section 5.2.2 that the Pu-239 purity is highest at the
beginning of operation, but the mass is lowest. A study could be done to assess plutonium mass verses
purity to determine when the reactor is at highest risk. This could inform what times are used to look for
relevant signatures in an MC&A analysis.

A more detailed study could be performed on how the gamma spectrum changes during and after a
diversion scenario. To determine if specific signatures are measurable, a high-resolution model of a
mature design will need to be used. The specific detector of interest will also need to be modeled. Even
then, code comparisons (such as between MCNP/CINDER and Serpent/SCALE) and experimental data
are needed to verify and validate results.

 Conclusions
This study demonstrated that simulations could be used to generate special nuclear material information
and associated gamma ray signatures. This type of approach and information could potentially be used by
vendors in the development of their own unique MC&A approach.

M&S methods provide a way to generate results faster than what can be achieved experimentally.
Simulations also provide a powerful method of guiding experimental designs and assisting with the
development of measurement approaches. This work illustrated several parameters that are important to
consider when performing MSR simulations. These parameters include the convergence of the depletion
simulation and fission product removal frequency. It is also important to note that MCNP only outputs
inventory information for isotopes in the transport calculation. If transport cross sections are not available,
then those isotopes are not reported by MCNP. It is necessary to understand these limitations when
interpreting any simulation results.

M&S can be used to determine fuel inventory as a function of time and simulate a detector response to the
resulting gamma radiation. Peak heights could be compared over time to identify when the primary power
producing isotope changes from U-235 to Pu-239, and potentially the age of the nuclear fuel. Diversion
scenarios could also be simulated to determine whether a significant change in the fuel composition
would be evident in the gamma spectrum. However, these simulations require a high degree of model
fidelity and are computationally expensive to converge. Code comparisons and experimental data are
needed to verify and validate results, which will provide confidence in the predictions.
