Cholecystectomy before diagnosis of primary sclerosing cholangitis is associated with markedly worsened transplant-free survival
Authors: Martti A Färkkilä, Johanna T Arola, Andrea Tenca, Kalle Jokelainen, Sonja Boyd, Hannu Kautiainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsGallbladder abnormalities are common in primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and commonly lead to cholecystectomy. As the gallbladder plays a role in regulating bile composition and biliary pressure, it has been proposed to exert a protective effect in PSC. We aimed to evaluate the impact of cholecystectomy and its timing on biochemistry, bile composition, and outcomes in PSC.MethodsA total of 977 patients with PSC enrolled in the prospective Helsinki PSC registry who underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERCP). Of these, 163 patients (16.7%) underwent cholecystectomy, either before (n = 100) or after (n = 63) the diagnosis of PSC. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the composite endpoints of liver-related death, hepatobiliary malignancy, or liver transplantation.ResultsPatients who had undergone cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis had more advanced disease at baseline, as reflected by higher ERCP scores, (p = 0.012), Mayo risk scores, Fibrosis-4 indices (p <0.001), and more advanced fibrosis on liver histology (p = 0.020). The composite endpoint was reached by 37 patients (37.0%) with prior cholecystectomy, compared with 53 patients (6.5%) with an intact gallbladder and 10 patients (5.9%) who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. In multivariable regression analysis adjusting for timing of cholecystectomy, sex, age, and disease severity, cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis was associated with a more than fivefold increased risk of reaching the composite endpoint (HR 5.16, 95% CI 3.00–8.88, p <0.001); using patients with PSC with an intact gallbladder as the reference. The restricted mean survival time was 5.0 years shorter in patients with prior cholecystectomy, p <0.001.ConclusionsCholecystectomy performed before the diagnosis of PSC is associated with more advanced disease and markedly impaired prognosis, including significantly reduced liver transplantation-free survival and substantially increased risk of liver transplantation.Impact and implicationsThis large prospective registry study demonstrates that cholecystectomy performed before the diagnosis of PSC is strongly associated with more advanced biliary disease and profoundly worse prognosis. The markedly increased risk of liver transplantation and reduced transplantation-free survival suggest that gallbladder loss may accelerate disease progression or identify a high-risk PSC phenotype. By contrast, cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis was not clearly associated with adverse outcomes, highlighting the importance of timing. The impact of prior cholecystectomy on prognostic models of PSC should be evaluated. These findings challenge the perceived neutrality of gallbladder removal in PSC and support a more cautious, individualized approach to cholecystectomy, while reinforcing the gallbladder’s potential protective role in PSC.Keywords: Gallbladder, ERCP, Outcome, Bile composition

Introduction

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the bile ducts that leads to strictures of the bile duct and eventually to liver cirrhosis.1 Chronic inflammation is associated with increased proliferation of biliary epithelial cells and a markedly increased risk of biliary dysplasia and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) development. The cumulative risk of CCA after 10 years of PSC is 6%.2 In addition, gallbladder abnormalities are common in patients with PSC,3 such as cholecystitis (25%), gallstones (25%), polyps, and gallbladder carcinoma. The prevalence of gall bladder polyps is 6–17%.3,4 Gallbladder polyps increase the risk of gallbladder carcinoma through a dysplasia-carcinoma sequence.5 The cumulative 10-year risk for developing gallbladder carcinoma is ∼2%.6 Because of the increased risk of gallbladder carcinoma,3 cholecystectomy is recommended in patients with PSC with gallbladder polyps ≥8 mm and smaller polyps growing.7 However, cholecystectomy has been associated with impaired PSC outcomes. A study consisting of 11 patients with PSC with cholecystectomy suggested a protective role of the gallbladder based on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) changes and elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in patients with cholecystectomy. No significant difference in serum bile acid levels was observed in patients with or without cholecystectomy.8 Another small study including five patients with PSC who underwent cholecystectomy showed that cholecystectomy was associated with worse Mayo scores and elevated Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) scores.9 More than 50% of patients with PSC have enlarged gallbladders, which are suspected to be caused by hyperpressure in the biliary tract.5 Cholecystectomy may thus eliminate this reserve function and lead to the progression of bile duct strictures. Increased fasting gallbladder volume has been reported with normal gallbladder emptying.10 In mice, gallbladder dilatation, mediated by the Takeda G protein-coupled receptor (TGR5), controls bile acid composition, reduces intrahepatic biliary pressure, and thereby protects the liver against bile acid-induced injury.11 TGR5 is enriched in the biliary tract epithelium and gall bladder,11 where it has been proposed to control chloride (Cl−) secretion,12 cholangiocyte proliferation,13 biliary epithelial permeability,14 and gallbladder filling.15 Cholecystectomy has shown to increase the enterohepatic recirculation rates of bile acids, leading to increased secretion rates of bile acids and cholesterol, but no long-term effects on the size or composition of the serum bile acid pool after cholecystectomy have been found.16 In patients who underwent cholecystectomy for cholesterol gallstones, cholecystectomy had no significant effect on biliary lipid composition, and the bile remained lithogenic after gallbladder removal.17,18 In patients with PSC, no studies have evaluated the impact of cholecystectomy on bile composition, so far.

The aim of the study was to evaluate the impact of cholecystectomy, performed before or after the diagnosis of PSC, on bile duct disease progression, liver function tests, biliary bile composition, and the overall outcome of patients with PSC.

Patients and methods

We included 1,129 patients from the prospective Helsinki PSC registry who had undergone endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERCP) with brush cytology by the end of 2024. We excluded 152 (13.5%) patients: those diagnosed with gall bladder carcinoma (n = 11), patients younger than 18 years at the time of diagnosis (n = 66), patients who had undergone liver transplantation, and patients who were diagnosed with CCA or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), or died of liver-related causes at the time of diagnosis or within 12 months (n = 14), and patients with PSC with follow-up ≤1 year (n = 61), Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study population.

GBC, gallbladder cancer; LTx, liver transplantation; PSC, primary sclerosing cholangitis.

The inclusion date was the time of PSC diagnosis. Of these, 163 patients (11.3%) underwent cholecystectomy — 100 before and 63 after the PSC diagnosis. Patients with gallbladder cancer were excluded. The diagnosis of PSC was made according to the EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines.7 Plasma and serum clinical chemistry parameters were obtained from the Helsinki University Laboratory (HUS Diagnostic Center). Bile samples were aspirated during ERCP using a balloon occlusion catheter and stored at -80 °C. Calprotectin and IL-8 were quantified as previously described.19

ERCP examinations and bile sample collection

ERCP was performed at diagnosis time for all the patients. The indications for ERCP were as follows: (1) documentation of the diagnosis after MRCP and individual risk stratification, (2) follow-up of bile duct disease progression and assessment of the need for dilatation, and (3) biliary dysplasia surveillance. ERCP was performed by three experienced gastroenterologists (MAF, KJ, and AT) using the balloon occlusion technique to ensure adequate and standardized filling of the intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts during successive ERCPs. Images were evaluated using the ERCP-score (modified Amsterdam score).20 Dominant stricture (DS) seen at ERCP was defined as stenosis with a diameter of <1.5 mm of the common duct or <1.0 mm of a hepatic duct within 2 cm of the bifurcation.21 Dilatation was performed when DS was diagnosed or when the cytology brush could not pass through the stenosis.

Brush cytology

To exclude biliary dysplasia or CCA, brush cytology was systematically collected from both the extra- and intrahepatic bile ducts, with the aim of reaching as many intrahepatic branches as possible, regardless of the absence or presence of DSs. Cytology was collected using a brush with a guide wire (RX Cytology Brush, Boston Scientific, MA, USA) to obtain an adequate number of cells for evaluation, as described in detail elsewhere.20

Liver histology

A histological core needle liver biopsy was performed in patients with PSC to exclude concomitant autoimmune hepatitis or to further confirm the PSC diagnosis in cases where only mild intrahepatic disease was detected in the ERCP at time of diagnosis. K7 staining of PSC liver biopsy specimens was routinely performed to evaluate chronic cholestasis and bile duct loss. Additionally, Herovici staining (which highlights tissue collagen) was performed to evaluate the stage of fibrosis. K7 and Herovici staining were performed as previously described.22

Biliary bile samples

Bile composition was assessed in a subgroup of 180 patients receiving ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) therapy for whom samples were available, including 32 individuals who had undergone cholecystectomy. The mean time between sample collection and time of PSC diagnosis was 1 year (IQR 0–6.5) in those cholecystectomized after the diagnosis and 7 years (IQR 6–10) in patients cholecystectomized before PSC diagnosis. The median dose of UDCA was 17.3 mg/kg, (IQR: 14.8, 19.2). After cannulation of the common bile duct with a papillotomy knife guided by a wire, bile was aspirated using a balloon catheter and immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Bile acids were silylated to trimethylsilylethers and quantified using gas–liquid chromatography on an SE-30 capillary column; the contents of individual bile acids, deoxycholic acid (DCA), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), cholic acid (CA), lithocholic acid (LCA), and UDCA, were analyzed.23 The amount of bile acids was expressed as content (mM) and as molar percentages (mM%). 5-α-Cholestane was used as an internal standard. Phospholipids were analyzed enzymatically using commercial kits (Enzabil-kit; Nycomed, Zurich, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means with SDs, median with IQR, or as counts with percentages. Differences in clinical and laboratory characteristics between groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance, Kruskal–Wallis test, or logistic models, as appropriate. Sidak’s multiple comparison procedure was used to correct significance levels for post hoc testing. We used a Cox proportional hazards model to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and a Fine and Gray competing risks regression model to calculate subhazard ratios (sHRs) for different causes. Models, age, sex, ERCP-score FIB-4 were used as covariates when appropriate. Time-to-event analysis was based on the product limit estimate (Kaplan–Meier) of the cumulative failure function with the log-rank test. Adjusted Kaplan–Meier cumulative rates were estimated using inverse probability weighting (IPW); 95% CI were obtained by bias corrected bootstrapping (5,000 replications). The restricted mean survival time (RMST) was estimated at 25 years. The RMST is the mean time for the event to occur; its clinical interpretation depends on the event and follow-up time studied.24,25 It is determined by calculating the area under the survival curve.24,25 This area can be estimated as a non-parametric curve (Kaplan–Meier survival curve) using numerical integration.24,25 In the case of violation of the assumptions (e.g. non-normality or small number) for continuous variables, a bootstrap-type method or Monte Carlo p values variables were used. The normality of variables was evaluated graphically and using the Shapiro–Wilk W test. Stata 18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) was used for the statistical analyses.

Ethics

All patients included in the PSC registry provided written informed consent. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the GCP and the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (6th revision, 2008). The study protocol was approved by the Helsinki University Hospital Ethical Committee IV, HUS/1566/2020.

Results

Clinical and laboratory data at time of PSC diagnosis

The demographics, ERCP-findings, and liver function tests at baseline, stratified by the time of cholecystectomy, are presented in Table 1. Cholecystectomy was more common in women, both before and after PSC diagnosis. Patients who underwent cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis were older and less frequently had concomitant inflammatory bowel disease compared with patients with an intact gallbladder. Among patients who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis, the main indications were gallbladder polyps and cholecystolithiasis, whereas the indication for cholecystectomy performed before PSC diagnosis was unknown in up to 62% of cases. BMI did not differ between the groups. Patients who underwent cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis more often exhibited combined intra- and extrahepatic disease and had more advanced disease, as reflected by higher ERCP scores, Amsterdam-Oxford Model (AOM) scores, Mayo risk scores, and FIB-4 values, compared with patients without cholecystectomy. By contrast, no differences were observed in serum transaminases, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), or liver function tests, and biliary levels of IL-8 and calprotectin were comparable across the three groups at the time of PSC diagnosis. The median interval between cholecystectomy and PSC diagnosis was 5 years before diagnosis (IQR 2–9) among patients undergoing cholecystectomy prior to PSC diagnosis, and 4 years after diagnosis (IQR 1–10) among those undergoing cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis.

Clinical and laboratory data during follow-up

The median follow-up time (IQR) was 10.6 (5.6, 16.6) years in patients with intact gallbladders, 11.6 (5.3, 17.7) years in those with a prior history of cholecystectomy, and 16.6 (9.6, 21.6) years in patients who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. The patient years of follow-up were 9,332, 1,271, and 1,019 years, respectively, p <0.001.

During the follow-up period, the time-weighted AUC for the ERCP-score was elevated in patients with a history of cholecystectomy. However, the need for dilation did not vary among the three patient groups (Table 2). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in serum transaminase levels, ALP levels, liver function tests, and biliary IL-8 and calprotectin levels (-AUC). The incidence of biliary dysplasia on brush cytology, whether at any point or on three or more occasions, was found to be highest in patients who underwent cholecystectomy before the diagnosis of PSC and lowest in those with an intact gallbladder (Table 2).

Liver histology

In total, liver biopsy was available in 336 (34%) patients at time of diagnosis, 281 (35%) in patients with intact gall bladder, 29 (29%) in those with preceding cholecystectomy, and 26 (41%) in patients undergone cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. Stage 2–4 fibrosis was detected in 19.2%, 37.9%, and 26.8% across the groups, p = 0.020 adjusted for age and sex. Stage 4 was diagnosed with 1.1%, 6.9%, and 0% respectively (Table S1).

Impact on cholecystectomy on composite end point

We evaluated the impact of cholecystectomy and its timing on the cumulative incidence of the composite endpoint, defined as liver transplantation, liver-related mortality, HCC, or CCA, as well as on each individual component across the three study groups (Table 3). The composite endpoint occurred in 53 patients (6.5%) with an intact gallbladder, compared with 37 patients (37.0%) who had undergone cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis and 10 patients (5.9%) who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. Among the individual components of the composite endpoint, liver transplantation was significantly more frequent in patients with prior cholecystectomy than in those without (p <0.001). The RMST for composite endpoint was 5.0 years shorter in patients with cholecystectomy before PSC than in those with an intact gallbladder (p <0.001). Consistently, transplant-free RMST was reduced by 4.7 years, respectively. In multivariable regression analysis adjusting for timing of cholecystectomy, sex, age, and disease severity at PSC diagnosis, cholecystectomy performed before PSC diagnosis was associated with a more than fivefold increased risk of reaching the composite endpoint compared with no cholecystectomy (HR 5.16, 95% CI 3.00–8.88; Table 4). By contrast, cholecystectomy performed after PSC diagnosis was not associated with an increased risk of the composite endpoint. Also, male sex, ERCP-score at baseline and liver fibrosis at time of diagnosis were associated with increased risk. The cumulative incidence of the composite endpoint in patients without cholecystectomy and those who underwent cholecystectomy before or after the diagnosis of PSC is shown in Fig. 2, adjusted for age, sex, and intra- and extrahepatic disease. The adjusted hazard ratio for the composite endpoint was 4.58 (2.92–7.16) for patients with prior cholecystectomy and 1.48 (0.75–2.94) for those with cholecystectomy after PSC, using 1.00 as a reference for the group with an intact gallbladder. The hazard ratios for the individual components of the composite endpoint are shown in Fig. S1, adjusted for age, sex, and disease extent, demonstrating a 20-fold increased risk for liver transplantation in patients with prior cholecystectomy compared with those with no cholecystectomy. In the study population, 38 (3.9%) patients underwent liver transplantation, nine (11.1%) in the group with intact gall bladder, 24 (24.0%) in those with cholecystectomy before, and five (7.9%) in those after the diagnosis of PSC, p <0.001. The indications for liver transplantation are presented in Table 5. In total, 83% of the transplantations for patients cholecystectomized before PSC diagnosis were performed as a result of either end-stage liver disease or suspicion of malignancy.

Bile composition

To evaluate the possible role of cholecystectomy in bile content, we analyzed bile acid, phospholipid, and cholesterol content (Table S2). We found no differences in bile content across the groups, except the higher content of UDCA in patients with cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis compared with those with intact gallbladder. The concentrations of primary and secondary bile acids did not differ between the groups. All patients were on UDCA therapy.

Discussion

In this large cohort study examining the association between cholecystectomy and subsequent outcomes in PSC, including hepatobiliary malignancy, liver transplantation, and PSC-related mortality, we observed a strong association between the timing of cholecystectomy and adverse clinical outcomes. Patients who underwent cholecystectomy before the diagnosis of PSC had a substantially increased risk (HR 5.16) of reaching the composite endpoint compared to those with intact gallbladder or those who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. In multiple regression analysis including sex, disease severity and age at time of diagnosis, cholecystectomy before diagnosis was the most significant risk factor for reaching the composite endpoint In addition, the RMST for the composite endpoint was 5.0 years shorter in patients who underwent cholecystectomy before PSC compared with those with an intact gallbladder (p <0.001). Similarly, RMST for liver transplantation-free survival was reduced by 4.7 years in patients with cholecystectomy preceding PSC diagnosis relative to those with an intact gallbladder. Among the individual components of the composite endpoint, cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis was particularly associated with a markedly increased risk of liver transplantation, with a subdistribution HR of ∼20 (Fig. S1). Even though the incidence of biliary dysplasia on brush cytology was highest among patients who underwent cholecystectomy before the diagnosis of PSC risk for hepato-biliary malignancies did not differ across the three groups. We also considered whether cholecystectomy performed before PSC diagnosis might represent a marker of pre-existing but unrecognized disease, implying a longer disease duration compared with patients with an intact gallbladder at diagnosis. However, the median follow-up time did not differ in patients with intact gallbladders compared with those with prior cholecystectomy.

To date, evidence supporting a protective role of the gallbladder in PSC has been limited and derived from small studies. Two previously published reports, together with only 16 patients with prior cholecystectomy, suggested a potential association between gallbladder removal and more severe disease. In the study by Cazzagon et al.,8 64 patients with PSC were included, of whom only 11 had undergone cholecystectomy. However, the timing of cholecystectomy in relation to PSC diagnosis was not specified, and the interval between surgery and study inclusion was not reported. Patients with prior cholecystectomy were described as having more advanced biliary disease on MRCP, characterized by increased strictures and ductal dilatation, compared with those with an intact gallbladder. Importantly, no data was provided on clinical outcomes, duration of PSC, follow-up time, or event rates, precluding any meaningful inference regarding the impact of cholecystectomy on disease prognosis.

The second study included only five patients who had undergone cholecystectomy before PSC diagnosis, of whom three subsequently underwent liver transplantation or died.9 However, data on disease duration, follow-up, or the interval between cholecystectomy and clinical endpoints were not reported. Consequently, the limited sample size and lack of temporal and outcome data in these studies prevent firm conclusions regarding the role of cholecystectomy in the clinical course of PSC.

Biliary tract hyperpressure has been suggested as a cause of enlarged gallbladders commonly seen in patients with PSC, and cholecystectomy may further aggravate the pressure, leading to the progression of bile duct strictures. When we analyzed the possible factors associated with impaired outcomes in patients with PSC who underwent cholecystectomy, we found that these patients had more advanced bile duct disease based on a higher ERCP score; however, no differences were found in the need for dilatation. This finding was in line with the study of Cazzagon et al.8 Interestingly, the markers of biliary tract inflammation, IL-8 and calprotectin in bile, previously shown to be the most sensitive surrogate markers for bile duct disease progression,19 did not differ between the groups. In addition, the Mayo score and FIB-4 were elevated in this group, as was the proportion of patients with increased liver fibrosis. Cholecystectomy performed before the diagnosis of PSC was associated with more rapid progression of biliary ductal changes and, subsequently, with increased hepatic fibrosis. In addition, the risk of biliary dysplasia was higher, and in our cohort, a significantly greater proportion of patients with prior cholecystectomy were referred to liver transplantation because of suspected CCA (Table 5) compared with patients with an intact gallbladder or those who underwent cholecystectomy after PSC diagnosis. Notably, the incidence of CCA did not differ among the three groups, most likely because patients with recurrent biliary dysplasia were referred to liver transplantation before progression to overt CCA. Finally, we evaluated the impact of cholecystectomy and its timing on biliary bile composition; however, no statistically significant differences were observed across the three groups, suggesting that cholecystectomy does not substantially alter biliary lipid composition in PSC or explain the accelerated progression of bile duct pathology and hepatic fibrosis.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of the present study is its large, well-characterized patient cohort with long-term follow-up and comprehensive clinical and laboratory data. In contrast to the two previous reports, which were limited by small sample sizes, we assessed long-term outcomes and explored the potential mechanisms underlying the impaired prognosis observed after cholecystectomy. The main limitations of this study were its single-center, retrospective study design, which is shared with previous studies in this field. Our findings should therefore be validated in independent cohorts. Additionally, we lack data on the timing of the first documented abnormal liver function tests and the diagnosis of PSC, which might be interesting regarding the duration of PSC. Because the indication for cholecystectomy performed before PSC diagnosis was unknown in a subset of patients, we cannot exclude the possibility that cholecystectomy represented an early manifestation of PSC in some cases. Liver stiffness measurement as a prognostic marker recommended to stratify PSC severity was available only in small cohort of patients, making further analysis impossible.

Conclusions

In conclusion, cholecystectomy performed before the diagnosis of PSC was associated with a significantly poorer overall prognosis, reflected by five times elevated risk for reaching the composite endpoint in multivariate analysis and a 4.7-year reduction in restricted mean liver transplantation-free survival compared with patients with an intact gallbladder. Moreover, patients with a prior history of cholecystectomy had a 20-fold increased risk of liver transplantation, most commonly attributable to end-stage liver disease or suspected CCA. We found no evidence that the accelerated disease progression in patients with a prior history of cholecystectomy was attributable to alterations in biliary bile composition or increased biliary pressure necessitating more frequent endoscopic interventions. Cholecystectomy in the pre-diagnostic phase of PSC may alter the disease trajectory. Our data suggest that careful consideration of preoperative imaging and non-invasive markers is warranted in patients presenting with atypical biliary symptoms to avoid this deleterious surgical risk and to exclude PSC. The possibility of PSC should be considered if liver enzyme levels remain elevated after cholecystectomy. Patients with PSC who underwent prior cholecystectomy probably require more intensified follow-up than those with an intact gallbladder.
