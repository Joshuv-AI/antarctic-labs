3. Executive Summary:
 This project addresses a key barrier to commercialization of perovskite photovoltaics:
achieving scalable and stable fabrication of large-area perovskite solar modules.
Although small-area perovskite cells now exceed 27% power conversion efficiency
(PCE), scaling to manufacturable modules remains limited by coating non-uniformity,
patterning-induced damage, and accelerated degradation. The objective of this project
was to improve module scalability and operational stability by co-optimizing perovskite
ink formulations, printing processes, and module patterning using data-driven methods.
 The core technical approach was flexographic printing of self-leveling perovskite inks
designed to exploit Marangoni flows to heal coating defects and produce uniform
absorber layers over large areas. This strategy was coupled with Bayesian optimization
of ink composition and printing conditions, using large-area photoluminescence (PL)
mapping, profilometry, and photovoltaic measurements as quantitative metrics of film
uniformity and device performance. Module patterning, interconnection, and
encapsulation processes were developed to evaluate performance and module stability.
 This research advanced fundamental understanding of scalable perovskite
processing by quantifying how ink rheology, solvent volatility, and interfacial properties
govern self-leveling behavior and film uniformity, directly linking fluid-mechanical
dynamics to spatial PL variations. PL uniformity was established as a strong predictor of
device efficiency and reproducibility, and remains an important diagnostic at the module
scale when interpreted in the context of patterned features and drying gradients. The
project further developed and validated a Bayesian optimization framework that explicitly
incorporates uniformity metrics, providing a transferable methodology for data-driven
development of printed optoelectronic devices.
 The project demonstrated strong technical effectiveness. Iterative optimization
produced open-air-processed perovskite inks yielding small-area solar cells with
champion PCE exceeding 21.7% and batch-level median PCE above 20% across
high-statistics device sets, using fast-drying 2-methoxyethanol-based formulations.
Quantitative correlations between PL uniformity, thickness uniformity, and photovoltaic
performance were established. At the module level, printed perovskite modules achieved
active-area efficiencies up to ~17.3 % on 25 cm² substrates. Comparative studies showed
that additively patterned (printed) interconnections provide a consistent performance
advantage over laser-scribed controls, and preliminary accelerated aging tests indicated
improved operational stability for modules incorporating printed interconnections.
 The approach is economically and technologically scalable. Flexographic and other
roll-compatible printing methods enable high-throughput, large-area manufacturing with
reduced material waste and lower capital intensity than vacuum-based or batch
processes. Open-air processing combined with data-driven optimization shortens
development cycles and reduces process complexity, and the optimization framework
developed here is transferable to other printing platforms and materials systems. The
project also contributed to workforce development by training graduate students,
postdoctoral researchers, and undergraduates in photovoltaics, materials science, and
data-driven manufacturing. Overall, this work establishes a data-driven framework for
scalable, high-speed printing of perovskite solar modules and defines the key materials
and process parameters governing uniformity, interconnection quality, and module-level
performance.

5. Background:
 Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) continue to demonstrate rapid gains in
power conversion efficiency, with small-area devices now exceeding 27%[1]. However,
commercialization depends critically on the development of scalable, low-cost
manufacturing methods that preserve performance and stability at the module level.
Printing-based fabrication techniques such as screen printing, inkjet printing, gravure, and
flexography are increasingly recognized as enabling pathways toward low capital
expenditure (CapEx), high-throughput manufacturing under ambient conditions. Despite
recent progress, printed perovskite technologies continue to face challenges related to
film non-uniformity, crystallization control, patterning-induced defects, and long-term
operational stability, particularly as device area increases.
 A growing body of very recent work (2024–2025) demonstrates that multiple leading
research groups are actively pursuing printing and patterning strategies to advance
perovskite scalability. In screen-printed architectures, Duan et al. [2] reported flexible
perovskite solar cells (f-PSCs) with efficiencies of 18.6% and excellent mechanical
durability by introducing selective siloxane coupling agents at the perovskite/SnO₂
interface. This study highlights how interfacial chemistry can simultaneously improve
crystallization, charge extraction, and mechanical robustness in printed devices.
Complementary progress was reported by Zhang et al. [3], who introduced a synergistic
Ostwald ripening strategy using ionic liquids and ambient moisture to regulate
crystallization in high-viscosity screen-printed films, achieving fully screen-printed devices
with efficiencies approaching 19.5% and operational stability beyond 1,000 hours.
Together, these studies establish screen printing as a viable low-cost manufacturing route
while underscoring the acute sensitivity of printed perovskite performance to ink
formulation and drying dynamics.
 Additional recent studies emphasize the central role of ink design, rheology, and post-
deposition evolution in printed perovskites. Investigations of green-solvent screen-printed
carbon-based PSCs[4] revealed that residual solvent removal and natural aging can
significantly improve crystallinity and device performance, indicating that time-dependent
film evolution must be considered in scalable manufacturing workflows. High-speed
imaging and rheological studies of screen-printed mesoporous TiO₂ layers[5] further
demonstrated that defect formation during printing can be mitigated through careful
control of ink properties, resulting in measurable performance gains in both cells and
modules. These findings are consistent with recent reviews of printing-based PSC
manufacturing[6], which identify crystallization control, defect suppression, and stability
as dominant barriers to commercialization.
 Parallel efforts in inkjet and roll-to-roll printing further highlight both the promise and
limitations of scalable perovskite fabrication. Inkjet-printed PSCs processed under
ambient conditions have achieved efficiencies in the 13–18% range through solvent
engineering and additives, though reproducibility and large-area uniformity remain
challenging. Notably, the first entirely roll-to-roll fabricated perovskite modules under
ambient conditions[7] demonstrated module efficiencies of ~11% with projected
manufacturing costs near $0.7/W. While these demonstrations underscore the economic
potential of printed perovskites, they also reveal a persistent performance gap at scale
and the need for improved control over coating uniformity and crystallization.

 In parallel with advances in materials and printing hardware, data-driven optimization
approaches are emerging as powerful tools to accelerate process development. Machine-
learning-assisted experimentation has been applied to printed and mesoscopic
perovskite architectures to rapidly identify promising additive combinations and
processing windows, achieving large performance gains with significantly reduced
experimental effort[8]. However, many prior approaches rely primarily on device efficiency
as the optimization metric, which can obscure underlying process–structure relationships
and slow translation to larger areas.
 In contrast to these approaches, flexographic printing offers a unique combination of
patterning resolution and industrially relevant throughput, with printing speeds that can
be 10–100X higher than screen printing or inkjet methods[9], while remaining compatible
with roll-to-roll manufacturing. However, this speed advantage comes at the cost of a
highly coupled and multidimensional design space involving ink rheology, solvent
evaporation, ink transfer, and leveling dynamics. Recent machine-learning-assisted
approaches have begun to address this complexity. For example, ML-guided
experimentation has enabled rapid optimization of printed and mesoscopic perovskite
architectures, identifying synergistic additives and processing windows with dramatically
reduced experimental effort. Nevertheless, many prior approaches rely primarily on
device efficiency as the optimization metric, which can obscure the underlying process–
structure relationships required for scalable manufacturing.
 The present SIPS project builds directly on this rapidly evolving literature while
addressing key limitations. Rather than optimizing efficiency alone, this project
emphasizes spatial uniformity as a unifying physical metric linking ink chemistry, printing
physics, device performance, and stability. Our recent work introduced a spatial-
uniformity–driven Bayesian optimization framework for flexographically printed perovskite
films, explicitly incorporating large-area photoluminescence (PL) variance into the
optimization objective. This approach enables efficient navigation of the large ink and
process design space inherent to high-speed printing and has yielded state-of-the-art
printed perovskite device efficiencies exceeding 21%, surpassing reported efficiencies for
other printed absorber architectures to date.
 By leveraging insights from recent screen printing, inkjet printing, and roll-to-roll
demonstrations, and combining them with Bayesian optimization informed by large-area
characterization, the present project accelerates the development of scalable perovskite
manufacturing processes. The integration of fluid-mechanical understanding, ultra-high-
throughput flexographic printing, and data-driven optimization places this work in direct
continuity with the current state of the art while advancing beyond it, providing both
improved device performance and deeper insight into the physics governing printed
perovskite uniformity and stability.

6. Project Objectives:
6.1 Overall Objectives
Perovskites’ technological impact depends on resolving critical challenges in their
scalable large-area fabrication and inherent chemical instabilities. The main goal of this
project is to advance the uniformity and stability of perovskite modules by addressing
weak points arising from scribing and coating defects that seed reactions accelerating
module degradation and lead to performance drop-off at large areas. The approach of
this SIPS project is to develop flexographically printed self-leveling perovskite inks that
compensate intrinsic coating defects and particles via redistributive Marangoni flows.
Flexographic printing with inline drying is an ultrafast method for directly patterning
modules and enables elimination of subtractive scribing steps that can induce thermal
and chemical damage. The expected outcomes of this work included:
 1) Producing design rules for self-leveling perovskite inks suitable for high-speed
 printing.
 2) Identifying the mechanisms linking the spatial distribution of micro-scale coating
 defects with module-level photovoltaic performance and stability.
 3) Demonstrating large-area, high-efficiency perovskite modules incorporating these
 fluid-mechanical ink design strategies.
Through these objectives, this project enables scalable, low-capital-expenditure
manufacturing pathways for high-performance perovskite photovoltaics by establishing
quantitative design rules for uniform film formation and damage-minimized module
integration.
6.2 Technical Scope Summary
The proposed approach applied the team’s experience in printing perovskite solar cells
by flexography (Fig. 1), a versatile roll-based printing method offering high-resolution
(<10 µm) patterning and exceptional thickness uniformity through soft-contact ink
transfer. Compared with other printing approaches such as screen printing or inkjet
printing, flexography provided substantially higher throughput and is well suited for
industrial roll-to-roll manufacturing. This project advanced flexographic technology for
perovskite module fabrication through the integration of Bayesian optimization (BO) with
high-throughput, large-area characterization, specifically targeting the combined goals of

high uniformity and high operational stability. Iterative BO incorporated stability
measurements and large-area photoluminescence (PL) mapping to guide ink and process

6.3 Tasks, Milestones, and Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO)
Task 1.0 (M1–M6): Bayesian Optimization of Self-Leveling Inks for Ultra-Uniform
Printing
The team applied Bayesian optimization to ink and process design to improve leveling of
perovskite inks. Preliminary studies provided a benchmark range of concentration and
viscosity suitable for flexography. The major factors included in the optimization were
precursor concentration, cosolvent fraction, printing speed, drying delay, annealing
temperature, N₂ air-knife flow rate, and substrate temperature during printing. Ink
development required measurement of Marangoni coefficients and characterization of
drying velocity as a function of ink concentration and solvent composition. The inks
consisted of mixtures of volatile non-coordinating solvents (VNCSs) such as
2-methoxyethanol (2-ME), γ-valerolactone (GVL), and acetonitrile (ACN), combined with
non-volatile coordinating solvents (NVCSs) such as DMSO in low volume fractions (e.g.,
2–3%) to control crystallization. ACN and 2-ME provided low viscosity and high vapor
pressure favorable for high-speed printing, while NVCS components regulated
crystallization dynamics.
 As shown in Fig. 1, the iterative BO process began with experimental planning,
incorporating information from large-area PL mapping and photovoltaic measurements,
and proceeded through sequential learning approaches similar to those demonstrated by
Liu et al. (Joule, 2022). Initial BO training was based on at least 40 experimental
conditions sampling a seven-variable parameter space describing ink composition,
printing process parameters, and perovskite composition (e.g., MA0.6FA0.4PbI3 versus
CsFAPbI3). Subsequent BO rounds sequentially tested sets of ~10–20 recommended
conditions, including selected replicates and controls.

Using Task 1 data, the team characterized the stability of printed cells and analyze film

Development
The final task applied conditions identified in Tasks 1–2 to perform fine local optimization

Comparative stability testing was performed between modules with flexographically
patterned P2 gaps and modules using laser-scribed P2 features to isolate the impact of
additive versus subtractive patterning.

Perovskite Device and Module Architecture
Perovskite devices and modules in this project were fabricated using flexographic printing
as the primary deposition and patterning method. The overall architecture was designed
to enable direct patterning of subcells and interconnections, minimizing or eliminating the
need for laser scribing and enabling scalable module fabrication. The perovskite absorber
layers consisted of lead halide perovskite compositions including CsFAPbI3 and
MA0.6FA0.4PbI3, deposited from solution-based inks specifically engineered for
flexographic printing. Ink formulations incorporated mixtures of volatile non-coordinating
solvents (e.g., 2-methoxyethanol, γ-valerolactone, and acetonitrile) and low-volume
fractions of non-volatile coordinating solvents (e.g., DMSO) to control drying behavior,
crystallization kinetics, and film leveling. These inks were printed using a roll-based
flexography platform with in-line drying, allowing direct patterning of perovskite films and
subcell gaps.
 Module architectures consisted of series-connected subcells with dimensions of
approximately 5–8 mm × 40 mm, targeting printed P2 interconnection gap widths in the
range of 50–100 μm. Flexographic printing was used to define patterned regions,
including perovskite absorber edges and interconnection features, with the objective of
achieving high geometric fill factors and minimizing coating-induced defects. For
comparison and benchmarking, selected samples incorporated laser-scribed P2 features
produced using a 532 nm green laser. Charge transport layers and electrodes were
deposited using established processes compatible with the printed architectures. In some
module configurations, hole transport layers were patterned to align with printed P2 gaps

                                          Page 11 of 45

to ensure coverage of perovskite edges. The top metal electrode was deposited by
thermal evaporation and patterned by conventional laser scribing using a shadow mask.
This architecture enabled systematic investigation of the relationship between ink
formulation, printing conditions, coating uniformity, interconnection quality, and
operational stability at both the cell and module levels.
Sampling, Performance, Uniformity, and Stability Measurement Protocols
Device and film characterization followed standardized sampling and reporting protocols
to ensure statistical relevance and reproducibility across optimization rounds and project
milestones. For device-level performance evaluation, multiple devices were fabricated
per condition, with batch-level metrics used to assess median performance and variability.
Photovoltaic performance was evaluated using current–voltage measurements to
determine power conversion efficiency (PCE), with batch medians reported for
comparisons across Bayesian optimization (BO) iterations. Operational stability was
assessed using maximum power point tracking (MPPT), enabling time-resolved
evaluation of efficiency under continuous illumination. Stability studies were conducted
for both individual cells and modules, including accelerated aging tests under combined
light and heat stress (85 °C, 1 sun) for durations of up to 300 hours, allowing extraction
of normalized efficiency loss rates and T80 metrics.
 Film uniformity was quantified primarily through large-area scanning
photoluminescence (PL) mapping. PL variability was assessed over areas ranging from
4 cm² to 50 cm², depending on the milestone, using statistical metrics such as coefficient
of variation or root-mean-square (RMS) variation. Thickness uniformity was evaluated
using stylus profilometry with measurements sampled across each film. Coating defects,
including pinholes and flow-induced artifacts, were characterized using
transmission-mode large-area scanning microscopy in combination with image
processing to extract defect areal density and size distributions. For patterned module
regions, line width roughness (LWR) and line edge roughness (LER) of interconnection
gaps were quantified using commercial lithography analysis software. These
characterization data were incorporated into iterative Bayesian optimization workflows to
refine ink formulations and printing parameters, construct process windows for
self-leveling behavior, and establish correlations between film uniformity, defect density,
photovoltaic performance, and operational stability.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
7.1 Establishing the Baseline and Optimization Framework
This section establishes the technical baseline and experimental framework used
throughout the project to evaluate progress against the Statement of Project Objectives
(SOPO). The goal of this section is to define the printing, characterization, and
optimization methodologies that underpin the task-level results presented in Sections
7.2–7.4, and to provide the reference point against which quantitative improvements in
uniformity, efficiency, and stability are assessed. The project is structured around the
development of flexographically printed, self-leveling perovskite inks designed to mitigate
coating non-uniformities and defects that limit large-area module performance. A central
hypothesis of this work is that redistributive Marangoni flows during drying can
                                       Page 12 of 45

compensate for intrinsic coating defects, enabling improved film uniformity and,
ultimately, enhanced device and module performance. To test this hypothesis in a
scalable and statistically rigorous manner, we implemented an iterative optimization
workflow that integrates ink formulation, printing process parameters, large-area
characterization, and Bayesian optimization (BO).
 At the outset of the project, baseline printing conditions and ink formulations were
established based on prior flexographic printing experience. These baseline conditions
provided reference distributions for photoluminescence (PL) variability, thickness
non-uniformity, and device performance, which were used to initialize the BO framework
and define quantitative targets for improvement. The optimization framework was
designed to operate in a seven-dimensional parameter space encompassing both ink
variables (e.g., solvent composition, concentration, and perovskite composition) and
printing process variables (e.g., printing speed, printing pressure, anilox roller
parameters, drying delay, and air-knife conditions). This combined ink–process
parameterization reflects the coupled nature of fluid dynamics and drying kinetics in
flexographic printing.
 A standardized sampling and characterization protocol was adopted across all tasks
to ensure reproducibility and comparability of results. Device performance was evaluated
using batch-level statistics rather than single champion values, with median power
conversion efficiency (PCE) and variability used as primary metrics. Film uniformity was
quantified using large-area scanning PL mapping and stylus profilometry, enabling direct
comparison of optical and thickness variability across multiple length scales. These
metrics were incorporated directly into the BO objective functions to guide subsequent
experimental planning. The workflow established in this section enabled systematic,
task-level comparisons between anticipated and realized outcomes throughout the
project. Subsequent sections build on this foundation to report quantitative progress
against specific milestones, including reductions in PL variability, increases in device
efficiency, expansion of process windows for self-leveling behavior, and the translation of
optimized conditions to module-level fabrication and stability testing.
7.2 Task 1 Results: Bayesian Optimization of Self-Leveling Inks for Ultra-Uniform
Printing

engineer self-leveling perovskite inks and flexographic printing conditions that

Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), the primary objectives of this task were to (i)
establish a BO model spanning ink and process variables, (ii) reduce photoluminescence
(PL) variability through iterative optimization, and (iii) demonstrate high-efficiency printed
perovskite solar cells with strong batch-level reproducibility. Progress under this task is
reported below in direct comparison to the associated milestones.

7.3 Task 2 Results: Film Uniformity, Defect Characterization, and Stability Modeling
Task 2 focused on establishing quantitative relationships between film-level uniformity,
coating defects, and operational stability for flexographically printed perovskite devices.
As defined in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), the objectives of this task were
to (i) evaluate whether improvements in photoluminescence (PL) uniformity translate to
enhanced operational stability, (ii) characterize coating defect densities and sizes and
incorporate these metrics into predictive models, and (iii) define process windows for
self-leveling behavior and patterned feature quality relevant to module integration.
Milestone 2.1 (M6): Stability as a Function of Uniformity and Defect Density
Comparison of operational cell stability via maximum power point tracking (MPPT) as a
function of PL uniformity, PL abs. magnitude, and coating defect areal density (# cm-2).

• Quantify defect density and size distributions using large-area scanning microscopy
• Correlate MPPT stability with PL uniformity and defect density

Transmission-mode large-area scanning microscopy was used to evaluate coating quality
across a broad set of printed perovskite films, with representative images shown in Figure
11, where PL coefficients of variation (COVs) and corresponding device PCEs are

annotated. To improve defect visibility and classification, dark-field imaging was
employed, enabling identification and tagging of individual defects for quantitative
analysis (Figure 12). Defect areal densities were extracted via image processing, and the
corresponding distributions of defect diameters were compiled across multiple ink
formulations and printing conditions. The extracted defect size distributions are
summarized in Figure 13. These data provided a quantitative basis for comparing film
defect populations across optimized printing conditions. Operational stability was
assessed using maximum power point tracking (MPPT) measurements. Representative
MPPT curves for devices incorporating additives such as MACl and MAH2PO2 are shown

Across the optimized printing conditions, the spread in defect density was found to be

observed between defect density and MPPT stability, nor between PL COV and
operational stability, within the subset of high-performing conditions examined (Figure

densities may be constrained by background contamination and handling rather than ink
formulation alone, limiting their discriminating power for stability within the optimized
regime.
Assessment: Milestone achieved. Although strong correlations were not observed, the
measurements fulfilled the milestone requirements and provided important constraints for
subsequent modeling and interpretation.

Milestone 2.2 (M7): Process Window Mapping and BO Model Retraining
Retraining of BO model to test predictive accuracy of models including PL uniformity and
coating defect density for PCE and documentation of process window for ink leveling and
coating artifacts.

• Document process windows for ink leveling behavior
• Demonstrate ≥50% increase in pass (leveled) conditions relative to baseline
• Evaluate the impact of including defect density and PL uniformity in BO retraining

A systematic process window mapping study was conducted by printing 60 combinations
of ink formulations and printing conditions, as summarized in Figure 16. Each condition
was evaluated using a binary pass/fail criterion based on the presence of Saffman–Taylor
and related flow-induced artifacts. Process windows were constructed as a function of
drying velocity and characteristic wavelength of non-uniformities for multiple optimized
inks and a baseline formulation. Representative process window diagrams are shown in
Figure 17, where “green” regions correspond to complete ink leveling and “red” regions
indicate leveling failures. Compared to the baseline ink, Bayesian-optimized inks
exhibited significantly broadened process windows, with approximately a 50% increase
in pass conditions for the most optimal formulations.
Model retraining outcomes:
The BO model was retrained to include defect density as an additional input variable. This
retraining resulted in lower root-mean-square error (RMSE) and tighter fits, as shown in
Figure 18, although defect density did not emerge as a dominant independent predictor
of PCE. Additional retraining incorporating both PL COV and defect density further
improved predictive accuracy for PCE, yielding modest reductions in RMSE relative to
the original composite objective function (Figure 19).
Assessment: Milestone achieved. The process window analysis provided actionable
design rules for self-leveling inks, and model retraining clarified the relative importance of
uniformity and defect metrics.

Summary of Task 2 Outcomes
Task 2 established a quantitative framework for understanding how film uniformity, defect
populations, and process windows influence device performance and stability. A key
outcome of this task was the recognition that, within optimized printing regimes, PL
uniformity and defect density exhibit diminishing returns as predictors of operational

7.4 Task 3 Results: Module Integration, Interconnections, Performance, and
Stability
Task 3 focused on translating the ink formulations and printing conditions optimized in
Tasks 1 and 2 to module-level fabrication, with particular emphasis on patterned
perovskite uniformity, interconnection resistance, module efficiency, and operational
stability. As defined in the SOPO, this task addresses the critical challenges associated
with scaling from small-area cells to larger-area, series-connected perovskite modules
and evaluates whether improvements in film uniformity and printed interconnections can
improve module performance and stability.

Final-period update:
During the final period of the project, additional small-area device batches were fabricated
to improve statistical confidence and respond to feedback from the November quarterly
review meeting. A pixel-based device architecture was employed to enable
higher-throughput statistics, and further optimization of SnO2 processing and PTAA

7.5 Cross-Task Insights and Lessons Learned
This section synthesizes key technical insights that emerged across Tasks 1–3, with
emphasis on how materials design, printing physics, data-driven optimization, and
module integration interact when scaling printed perovskite photovoltaics from small-area
cells to functional modules. We offer these notes that they might be relevant for translation
to future manufacturing-oriented perovskite research efforts.
7.5.1 Predictive Power and Limits of PL Uniformity Metrics
A central outcome of this project was the identification of spatial photoluminescence (PL)
uniformity as a strong predictor of device performance at the small-cell scale. In Tasks 1
and 3, Bayesian optimization (BO) explicitly incorporating the PL coefficient of variation
(COV) consistently guided the ink design and printing process toward conditions yielding
both higher efficiency and improved reproducibility. Final small-cell batches as well as the
full set of data demonstrated that PL COV values near or below ~2% were associated
with higher device efficiencies exceeding 20%, reinforcing a physical link between film
uniformity, thickness control, and the optoelectronic quality of the printed perovskite
absorber. While we evaluated other dimensions of the photoluminescence data, we found
variations in the normalized intensity to be the most predictive, with the PL emission
wavelength varying little and being only weakly correlated with the device performance.

                                           Page 36 of 45

 However, Task 3 also revealed that the predictive power of PL uniformity becomes
more nuanced at the module scale. While PL COV measured within the central regions
of subcells remained correlated with performance, local drying gradients near patterned
features introduced non-uniformities that were not fully captured by global PL metrics but
were also less significant given their proximity in the scribing regions. This does highlight
one particular insight for scaling, which is that patterned perovskite devices are likely
going to exhibit some degree of edge effects. In large module dimensions at the cm-scale
this may be a manageable risk. In devices with smaller dimensions, or in module
geometries looking to maximize the geometric fill factor, this consideration is potentially
a limitation. This also highlights an important scaling insight: metrics that are predictive at
the cell scale must be spatially contextualized when applied to patterned,
series-connected modules.
7.5.2 Bayesian Optimization Effectiveness and Practical Constraints
Bayesian optimization proved effective in this project for navigating a high-dimensional
ink and printing parameter space in Task 1, enabling rapid identification of wide process
windows for uniform, high-performance films using relatively small experimental datasets.
The integration of performance and uniformity into a single objective function was
particularly impactful for manufacturability-oriented optimization. At later stages (Tasks 2
and 3), the marginal returns from continued BO-driven refinement diminished as extrinsic
factors—including particle contamination, yield loss, and interfacial defects—became
dominant. These observations underscore a key lesson: data-driven optimization is most
powerful when intrinsic process variability dominates, but must be complemented by
additional process controls and hardware/process maturity as performance ceilings are
approached. We do anticipate, however, that the uniformity driven BO approaches would
apply well to printing other higher performance and higher stability perovskite device
architectures. We anticipate that the charge transport layers for many state of the art
architectures could be formulated into printable inks and that the BO approach would
accelerate translation of spin coated (or blade coated) perovskite recipes for small cells
into scalable processes for modules.
7.5.3 Drying Physics Near Patterned Features as a Scaling Bottleneck
Across Tasks 1 and 3, the project identified drying-rate gradients near patterned gaps and
interconnection features as one potential source of non-uniformity when scaling to
modules. PL mapping showed that higher-concentration inks exacerbated edge-affected
regions adjacent to printed gaps, whereas lower-concentration, self-leveling formulations
reduced these effects but introduced tradeoffs with throughput and film thickness. This
establishes drying physics—not just ink chemistry—as a primary design constraint for
patterned perovskite modules, emphasizing the need for coordinated optimization of ink
formulation, drying dynamics, and subcell geometry in scalable printing processes.
Narrowing the ink composition space or printing parameter space would be key for
enabling BO of additional parameters including the exact inline drying conditions for a
potential roll-to-roll implementation.
7.5.4 Role of Additive Patterning vs. Laser Processing
A consistent cross-task finding was that additive patterning provides clear advantages for
module integration, particularly by eliminating laser-induced damage at P2
interconnections. Task 3 demonstrated that printed P2 features enabled higher module

                                         Page 37 of 45

efficiencies and improved operational stability relative to laser-scribed controls fabricated
with otherwise identical materials and processes. At the same time, the work highlighted
that laser steps remain valuable for some patterning operations, particularly where
precision removal or isolation is required, for example, for the P1 isolation step. The
broader lesson is that hybrid manufacturing approaches—strategically combining additive
printing with selective laser processing—offer a pragmatic pathway toward scalable,
high-performance perovskite modules that can reduce or eliminate prominent
degradation pathways.
7.5.5 From Device Optimization to Manufacturable Systems
Taken together, Tasks 1–3 illustrate that achieving high efficiency in printed perovskite
devices is not solely a materials problem, but a systems-level challenge involving ink
rheology, drying kinetics, patterning strategy, interconnection design, and process
control. The project demonstrates that material–device–process co-design, supported by
data-driven optimization and informed by physical diagnostics, is essential for translating
laboratory-scale advances into manufacturing-relevant module technologies.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
This project has made substantial progress toward addressing key challenges in the
scalable fabrication of uniform and high-performance perovskite solar modules. A central
accomplishment of the work is the development of printed perovskite ink formulations and
flexographic printing process conditions that enable high device efficiencies while
operating under manufacturing-relevant constraints. Through iterative process refinement
and finer local optimization, the project demonstrated champion printed perovskite solar
cells exceeding 21.6% power conversion efficiency, with batch-level median efficiencies
above 20% across 68 devices. These results demonstrate that high photovoltaic
performance can be maintained when transitioning from laboratory-scale deposition
methods to roll-compatible printing approaches, a critical requirement for commercial
viability.
 Another significant outcome of this project is the establishment of quantitative
correlations between photoluminescence (PL) uniformity, thickness uniformity, and
photovoltaic performance. By systematically analyzing large-area PL maps in conjunction
with profilometry and device measurements, the team demonstrated that improvements
in film uniformity directly translate to enhanced device efficiency and reproducibility.
These insights formed the basis for the rational design of self-leveling ink formulations
and optimized printing conditions, enabling a data-driven pathway for process
development rather than reliance on empirical trial-and-error approaches. The project
also advanced understanding of how module-level patterning impacts perovskite
absorber uniformity and performance. Detailed characterization of printed gap features
revealed how flexographic patterning introduces local drying gradients that influence PL
variability and defect formation near interconnection regions. These findings provided
critical feedback for refining printing resolution, ink concentration, and subcell geometry,
informing strategies for achieving high geometric fill factors while minimizing uniformity
losses associated with module integration.
 At the module level, the project established a functional integration framework for
additively patterned perovskite modules, culminating in the demonstration of printed
modules with active-area efficiencies up to 17.2% on ~17 cm² devices and approximately
17.3% on 25 cm² substrates. Statistical comparisons between additively patterned
(printed-P2) modules and laser-scribed control modules showed a consistent
performance advantage for printed interconnections, highlighting the benefit of
eliminating laser-induced damage during module fabrication. Preliminary accelerated
aging experiments further indicated a 2–3X improvement in operational stability for
modules incorporating printed P2 interconnections relative to laser-scribed controls,
suggesting a promising pathway toward improved module durability.
 Several challenges were encountered during the course of the project. In particular,
translating ink and process conditions optimized for uniform small-area films into fully
integrated modules introduced additional sources of performance loss, including
non-ideal interconnection regions and increased sensitivity to drying gradients near
patterned features. These challenges underscored the importance of incorporating
module-relevant diagnostics such as large-area PL mapping and patterned-feature
analysis earlier in the optimization workflow. In response, the project refined its scope to
place greater emphasis on linking film-level metrics directly to module-level outcomes,
representing a strategic realignment rather than a reduction in ambition. Beyond technical

                                       Page 40 of 45

outcomes, the project had significant impacts in workforce development and training. The
research directly supported three female scientists, including two PhD students and one
postdoctoral researcher, contributing to the growth of the domestic STEM workforce.
Additionally, multiple undergraduate researchers were trained through this program, with
several serving as coauthors on DOE-funded publications. These training outcomes
strengthen the domestic talent pipeline for the U.S. solar and advanced manufacturing
sectors.
 In summary, this project demonstrates that integrating fluid-mechanical ink design,
large-area uniformity characterization, and Bayesian optimization provides a powerful
framework for advancing printed perovskite photovoltaics. The lessons learned regarding
the interplay between ink formulation, drying physics, patterning strategy, and module
integration offer valuable guidance for future efforts aimed at translating high-efficiency
perovskite materials into scalable, stable photovoltaic technologies.

9. Path Forward:
 The results of this project identify several clear opportunities and priorities for future
research and development aimed at advancing printed perovskite photovoltaics toward
commercial relevance. A primary next step is continued optimization of module-level
integration, with particular focus on mitigating drying-rate gradients and defect formation
near patterned features. This includes coordinated optimization of ink rheology, solvent
systems, drying conditions, and subcell geometry to improve uniformity across wider
subcells while maintaining high geometric fill factors. Improved in-line drying control and
environmental management are expected to play a critical role in this effort, particularly
for high-speed printing processes where solvent removal dynamics strongly influence film
formation.
 Second, the promising performance and preliminary stability advantages observed for
additively patterned P2 interconnections motivate continued development of laser-free or
fully additive module architectures. While this project employed a hybrid approach
combining printed and laser-patterned features, the full benefits of flexographic printing—
namely low capital expenditure, high throughput, and compatibility with roll-to-roll
manufacturing—would be most fully realized if the entire module architecture could be
printed. The results of this work suggest a viable pathway toward such fully printed
architectures, which would eliminate the need for laser scribing altogether and further
reduce process complexity, cost, and potential damage introduced during patterning.
 Related to this goal, the project explored the printability of charge transport layers,
including the hole transport layer PTAA. While functional devices were ultimately
fabricated using non-printed PTAA layers, preliminary efforts demonstrated that printing
of these layers is feasible in principle. Future work will require focused development of
appropriate solvent systems and ink formulations that balance printability, film quality, and
compatibility with underlying perovskite layers. Successful printing of charge transport
layers would represent a critical step toward fully printed, monolithic perovskite module
stacks. Importantly, this project also demonstrated the ability to accurately align and
register printed and laser-patterned features across multiple process steps, even within
complex module architectures. This capability provides a strong foundation for future work

                                        Page 41 of 45

exploring both hybrid and fully additive patterning strategies, and reduces risk associated
with multi-step registration in scaled manufacturing environments.
 Third, the preliminary stability improvements demonstrated in this project highlight the
importance of encapsulation and interface engineering for printed perovskite modules.
Future studies should focus on optimizing encapsulant selection, application methods,
and electrode–encapsulant interfaces to reduce early-stage degradation and to more
clearly isolate intrinsic device stability from packaging-related failure modes.
Extended-duration testing under relevant thermal, illumination, and bias conditions will be
particularly valuable when paired with next-generation perovskite device architectures
that offer improved intrinsic long-term stability relative to those explored here. From a
data-driven perspective, this project demonstrates that Bayesian optimization is highly
effective for navigating high-dimensional process spaces during early- and mid-stage
development. Future efforts may integrate Bayesian optimization with real-time
diagnostics, yield metrics, and contamination monitoring to extend its utility into
later-stage manufacturing optimization, where extrinsic factors increasingly dominate
performance and reproducibility.
 Finally, the technologies developed in this project are well suited for technology
transfer and scale-up partnerships with industrial stakeholders interested in printed
electronics and photovoltaics. The demonstrated compatibility of the ink formulations and
processes with high-speed, roll-compatible flexographic printing platforms provides a
strong foundation for pilot-scale demonstrations. Remaining technical risks include
long-term operational stability, environmental robustness, and yield control at larger
areas; addressing these barriers will be critical for enabling commercial adoption. Overall,
this project establishes a clear roadmap for advancing printed perovskite photovoltaics
from high-performance laboratory demonstrations toward fully printed, scalable, and
manufacturable solar energy technologies, while identifying the key scientific and
engineering challenges that must be addressed to realize this vision.
