 SUMMARY
 Burnable absorbers (BAs) are used in UO2 fuels to tune the reactivity in a light
 water reactor (LWR) particularly at the beginning of life of the fuel. BAs are
 elements that have high neutron absorption cross sections and because of this
 property can prevent power peaking. The addition of BAs reduces the reactivity
 at the beginning of life, and this in turn allows for the increase of enrichments from
 > 5 % 235U up to ~ 10 %. This also enables the increase of the burnup limit to 75
 GWd/MTU and the cycle length to 24 months. The BA are eventually consumed
 under irradiation, and their concentration is diminished at roughly 10-20
 GWd/MTU. The objective of this report is to fabricate UO2 fuels that have been
 doped with BAs (Gd2O3 in this case) and subsequently irradiate them in the high
 flux isotope reactor (HFIR) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The test specimen
 that are fabricated include 4 and 8 weight % Gd2O3 doped UO2 to investigate the
 influence of the BAs on the thermophysical properties and microstructure of the
 fresh and irradiated fuel.

 FABRICATION AND CHARACTERIZATION OF HFIR
 MINIFUEL SPECIMEN

1. INTRODUCTION
 Achieving high burnup in light water reactors (LWRs) has several advantages that enable a
higher efficiency reactor. These advantages include increasing the cycle time, increasing the
fraction of uranium atoms that are used to reduce the amount of spent fuel that is generated over
the lifetime of the reactor, and it decreases the reactor outage duration due to fewer refueling
outages. In order to achieve high burn up, the UO2 fuels need to be enriched from the current
standard of ~3-5 % 235U up to 10%. The high enrichment causes the reactivity of the reactor at
the beginning of life (BOL) to be very high, which can cause power peaking in the core. This can
lead to overheating, which can degrade core components, such as the cladding, causing concerns
with the safety and reliability.
 In order to control the reactivity in the core in BOL, burnable absorbers (BAs) can be added
to the fuel.1-3 These BAs absorb neutrons when new fuel rods are inserted, lowering the
reactivity at the BOL. As the BAs absorb neutrons, they are converted to other elements via the
fission process, and their concentrations decreases as a function of time and are typically
depleted after 10-20 GWd/MTU. Due to these advantages, industrial fuel vendors are interested
in incorporating BAs, with particular interest in gadolinium and boron. These BAs have thermal
neutron absorption cross sections of 49,700 and 767 barn, respectively. For reference, other core
materials such as Zr and Fe have thermal neutron absorption cross sections of 0.185 and 2.56,
respectively. Due to the very high thermal neutron absorption cross sections of Gd, incorporating
small quantities significantly impacts the reactivity and Gd doped fuels are being explored.
Although work has been ongoing in recent years, there is a data gap for irradiated samples with
Gd dopants to understand the evolution of the microstructure and thermal physical properties as a
function of burnup.
 To fill this data gap, current efforts in the Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) are utilizing
existing expertise in ceramic fuel fabrication, test reactor application, and post irradiation
examination. Recent work carried out at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) in FY24 have
shown the successful fabrication of BA doped UO2 specimen. In these studies, Gd, Er, and Eu
samples were studied with concentrations that varied from 6 to 15 wt.% of the oxide dopant and
had theoretical density > 92%. The thermal conductivity of these samples were collected, which
showed a decrease in thermal conductivity relative to UO2 (Fig.1). The information gained here,
along with other published work on sintering of Gd-doped UO2 have been leverage to fabricate
samples with specific geometries as specified in the previous assessment report submitted in
early FY25.4
 The High Flux Isotope Reactor at Oak Ridge National Laboratory enables test irradiations of
small samples (MiniFuels), which enable isothermal irradiations at specific burn up.5, 6 This
differs from conventional irradiations which uses prototypic fuel specimen that exhibit large
thermal gradients in the UO2 pellets due to the low thermal diffusivity.7 In MiniFuel specimen,
samples are kept at a sufficiently small size so that the entire specimen is exposed to the same
temp same. The information that is gained from this is beneficial for engineering scale and
performance modeling, where both temperature and burnup play a significant role in the
microstructure at high burnup.8, 9 The high neutron flux in HFIR additionally allows for high

burnup conditions to be achieved in a fraction of the time relative to conventional irradiation
tests. By utilizing HFIR for these accelerated irradiations and inputting the resulting date into
BISON models, these tools can be instrumental in using and qualifying accelerated fuel
qualification (AFQ) as a tool to expedite the time needed to put new fuels into use in commercial
reactors.

2. POWDER AND PELLET FABRICATION
2.1 Dopant Concentration and Pellet Specifications
The assessment report that was submitted in February 2025 indicated that the Gd dopant
concentration would varying between 4 to 10 wt.% Gd2O3. Additionally, two of the targets
would contain pellets fabricated with 10 wt. % Gd2O3, one of which would go to high burnup
(60-70 GWd/MTU) relative to the other four targets (5-30 GWd/MTU). This was revised as
requested by industrial partners so that the data can be compared to conventional irradiations that
have been carried out previously. The pellets were then requested to be fabricated with two
targets containing pellets with concentrations of 4 wt.% Gd2O3 doped UO2 and two additional
targets with 8 wt.% Gd2O3 doped UO2 plus a fifth target with samples without any dopants that
will be used as a baseline measurement to compare to established irradiation data. The original

 The requested geometries of the MiniFuel specimen were originally specified to have a
diameter of 3.0 ± 0.1mm and a height of 1.0 ± 0.1mm. Further neutronics calculations were
carried out by Jacob Gorton, which indicated that a height of 1.0 mm would lead to a large

handling samples with thickness of 0.3 mm during PIE, which were the target thickness in
previous MiniFuel specimen.

2.2 Powder and Pellet Preparation
 Gd2O3 powder (Alfa Asear) was dried in a box furnace to remove any absorbed water and to
ensure the correct Gd content in the final pellets. The powder was placed in an alumina crucible
and heated to 500 °C in air. An XRD pattern was collected after drying to ensure that the
powder consisted of pure Gd2O3. The dried Gd2O3 powder was mixed Areva UO2 powder
feedstock (235U = 0.35 wt.%) to make powder mixtures that contained 4 and 8 wt. % Gd2O3. The
isotopic and trace impurity analysis of the Areva feedstock is provide in Appendix 5.1.
Approximately 8 g lots were made of each concentration. The UO2 and Gd2O3 were milled
using a SPEX high energy ball mill for three 15-minute intervals. The powders were hand
stirred between each subsequent interval. 1 wt.% of Ethylene bis-sterateamide (EBS) was added
to the mixture as a binder, then milled for an additional 15 minutes. The undoped UO2 powder
was used without any further processing of the Areva UO2 feedstock.
 The Gd2O3 powder mixture was then added to a 3.4 mm dual action punch and die, using ~
0.3 g aliquots, and pressed to a load of 250 MPa. The undoped UO2 pellets were pressed to a
load of 300 MPa in a 3.8 mm dual action punch and die punch and die assembly. Due to the
difference in shrinkage of the doped and undoped UO2, different sizes of punch and dies were
used to achieve the targeted 3.0 ± 0.1mm diameter. The die walls and the punches were
lubricated by applying a thin layer of zinc sterate. The green pellets were pressed in a Carver
uniaxial hand press. The pellets were then added to an alumina crucible, which contained a UO2
powder bed to prevent any reaction that could form a Al-Gd-O solid solution, as indicated by the
psuedobinary phase diagram. The undoped UO2 pellets were placed directly in an alumina
crucible without the use of a powder bed.

2.3 Pellet Sintering
 The pellets were sintered in a horizontal Carbolite tube furnace with an alumina tube.
Sintering tests were carried out to determine the optimal sintering parameters to obtain high
density and near standard grain sizes (~ 11 µm) for the undoped UO2 and Gd2O3 doped UO2
pellets. Parameters that were investigated included the partial pressure O2 (pO2), temperature,
and isothermal hold. These results of these tests provided information to develop the sintering
profile that is shown in Figure 1. Figure 1(a) was used for sintering of UO2, whereas the profile
was modified for the Gd2O3 doped UO2 material, as is shown in Figure 1(b). A sintering
temperature of 1750 °C with a dwell time of 24 hours was found to provide pellets with
appropriate densities. The purpose of the higher pO2 levels at and below 1150 °C was used to
aid in solubility of the Gd2O3 into the UO2 lattice and to reduce the porosity.

2.4.1 Pellet Dimensions and Geometric Density Measurement
 Density measurements consisted of measuring the density by weight and geometric
dimensions to determine the density. This provides a geometric density and helps to determine
whether the pellets can be used for further testing. Starett calipers were used for measuring
diameter and a Mitutoyo Absolute micrometer was used height of the pellets (to ± 0.001 mm)
and weight was measured using a Mettler Toledo Balance (to ± 0.1 mg). The density was then
calculated by assuming a perfect right cylinder geometry, and the percent theoretical density
(TD) was then calculated using the TD, which is 10.96 g/cm3 for pure UO2, and is 10.73 and
10.53 g/cm3 for 4 and 8 wt.% Gd2O3 doped UO2 material, respectively. It should be noted that
the 8% Gd2O3 doped UO2 material had a slight slope of the top face, and the height varied along
the pellet by 24 µm. The average of five height measurements were used to calculate the
density. The height variation for both the undoped UO2 and 4% Gd2O3 doped UO2 exhibited a
height variation less than 7 µm, and the maximum height measurements was used to calculate
the densities. A summary of the averages of the pellets for each type is shown in Table 3, which

2.4.3 Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry
 The isotopic analysis of the Areva UO2 powder feedstock was carried out by Inductively
Couple Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) in the Chemistry Division at LANL. The UO2
material was digested in a concentrated nitric acid and hydrofluoric acid solution, then loaded
into a pre-conditioned UTEVA column as per Y12’s procedure (Y-50-AC-65-6550). Each
sample was measured in ten replicates. A uranium isotopic Certified Reference Material (New
Brunswick Laboratory, CRM U200) was also prepared for validation of the isotopic
measurement. The isotopic distribution of 234U, 235U, 236U, and 237U of the reference sample
(both certified and measured values) and the UO2 feedstock were determined. The final report
documenting the methods used, sample preparation, and data analysis is summarized in
Appendix 5.1.

2.4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy
 To prepare the pellet for imaging by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), thinned and
polished pellets were subsequently polished with a 0.25 µm diamond suspension followed by a
colloidal silica final polishing step for clear imaging of the grain structure for grain size analysis.
The samples were imaged using a Phenom ProX Desktop SEM (ThermoFisher Scientific,
Waltham, MA), using backscatter SEM images, which are shown in Figure 2(a)-(d). The images
collected of the polished samples still contained fine scratching on the surface, as is seen in
Figure 2(a). In order to remove these scratches, a thermal etching step was carried out by
heating the polished pellets to 1300 °C. The images of the thermally etched samples show
clearly defined grain boundaries. In the case of the undoped UO2 material, the thermal etching
produced some artifacts on the surface that were not present prior to etching. This effect has also
been seen in other UO2 samples treated with the thermal etch step.
 The SEM images were then analyzed using FIJI ImageJ freeware to evaluate the grain sizes.
To determine the average grain size, the linear intercept method was used, as described in the
ASTM standard E112-13. The grain size analysis is shown in Table 5, and shows that the
addition of the Gd2O3 decreases the grain sizes relative to undoped UO2.
 Energy dispersive spectroscopy was collected on the polished Gd2O3 doped UO2 pellets, and
the results are shown in Figure 3. The EDS maps show a homogeneous distribution of uranium
and Gd, which indicates that the Gd was dissolved into the UO2 lattice to form a Gd-U-O solid
solution. There is no evidence of G2O3 existing as a secondary phase.

2.4.5 Thermogravimetric Analysis and X-ray Diffraction
 The phase analysis of the sintered pellets was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and
the oxygen to metal (O/M) ratio was determined by using both XRD and thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA). The TGA data was collected on a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA) system
(STA 449 F3, Netzsch Instruments, Selb, Germany). The mass change of the sample was
measured as a function of temperature and time. The furnace was equipped with Al2O3 fixturing
(furnace protective tube and thermocouple sheath) and Al2O3 crucibles were employed to carry
the sample. Prior to carrying out the analysis, the furnace and sample chamber were evacuated
and refilled with ultra-high purity (UHP) Ar gas three times to minimize residual oxygen in the
sample chamber during ramp up. The furnace was ramped up to 900 °C at 10 °C/min under Ar
and allowed to equilibrate at 900 °C for 20 minutes. Air was then introduced during the
isothermal hold and was flowed for 3 h, then cooled down under Ar at 10 °C/min. The oxidized
powder was then prepared for analysis by XRD by crushing the powders with a mortar and pestle
and were then applied to a silicon crystal zero background sample holder then sealed in a
polymer dome equip with a scatter shield. A Bruker XRD (D2 phaser, Bruker, Madison, WI,
USA) was used to collect the XRD patterns that scanned a range of 10 to 90 ° 2-Theta, with a
0.02° step size and 2 second dwell time. As sintered samples were also prepared for XRD using
the same method as stated above.
 The O/M of the sintered UO2 pellet was determined by oxidizing the sample in the STA and
collected the mass gain. The UO2 structure can incorporate additional oxygen within its lattice
without undergoing a phase change, and therefore the O/M ratios can change slightly. Typically,
the O/M ratio can vary from 2.00 to 2.19 while retaining its cubic structure. Beyond this O/M,
U3O8 forms, and this phase only forms as stoichiometric U3O8 and the O/M ration does not vary.
By oxidizing the sintered material to U3O8 and carefully following the weight using TGA, it is
possible to determine the O/M of the sintered material. The furnace profile and thermogram is
provided in Figure 4. The weight was collected before and after oxidation to that the uranium
and oxygen content was determined. After sintering the pellet consists of only UO2.00, as is seen
in the red diffraction pattern in Figure 4(b), After oxidation in the STA, the XRD pattern (blue)
shows that the material was fully converted to U3O8. The O/M was calculated and found to be
2.00. The O/M ratio of the Gd2O3 material was not collected since this is more complicated with
the formation of the Gd-U-O solid solution during sintering.

3. Summary
 Standard UO2 and Gd2O3 doped UO2 MiniFuel pellets were fabricated at LANL for the
 purpose of collecting HFIR irradiation data to compare with data collected by conventional
 irradiations. The pellets were fabricated within the specified diameter, density, and height.
 The average grain sizes of the standard UO2 pellets were shown to be similar to commercial
 UO2 fuels, but the grain sizes for the Gd2O3 doped pellets were less than the undoped UO2.
 Analysis by EDS and XRD proved that the Gd was dissolved into the UO2 lattice, as no
 secondary phases were detected. Currently, the pellets that are tabulated in Appendix 5.2 are
 ready to ship and will be shipped out in early FY26.
