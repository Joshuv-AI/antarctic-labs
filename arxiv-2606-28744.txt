Geodesic Lévy flights on Zoll surfaces
Yann Chaubet, Emanuel József Godfried, Leo Tzou

Abstract
. We study the mean first capture time of isotropic Lévy flights on Zoll surfaces, namely the expected time for a geodesic Lévy process to reach a shrinking geodesic ball. While the leading-order asymptotics are universal, we prove that the first correction term encodes subtle geometric information. More precisely, it is completely determined by the local singularity type of the conjugate locus, quantified by the degree of the conjugate point. This yields a hierarchy of asymptotic regimes governed by the Lévy exponent.



1.
Introduction

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be a smooth closed Riemannian surface and
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be an isotropic Lévy process on
\Sigma
in the sense of Applebaum–Estrade
[
1
]
.
We assume that
(X_{t})
is a pure jump process with associated radial Lévy measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}\mu(s)=\frac{c_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}s}{s^{1+2\alpha}}\quad\text{ for some }\alpha\in\left]0,1\right[,

where
c_{\alpha}=4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)/\pi\Gamma(-\alpha)
is a normalization constant. Informally, the process
(X_{t})
can be obtained as a limit of jump processes whose jumps follow geodesic trajectories, with uniformly distributed directions and jump lengths governed by the Lévy measure (
1.1
).

In this article we are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the expected time required for the process
(X_{t})
to reach a small ball. More precisely, fix a point
p_{\star}\in\Sigma
. For any
p\in\Sigma
and
\varepsilon>0
, let

u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)=\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\inf\Bigl\{t\geqslant 0\penalty\ :\penalty\ X_{t}\in B(p_{\star},\varepsilon)\Bigr\}\,\Bigl|\,X_{0}=p\Bigr]

be the expected time for the process to reach the ball
B(p_{\star},\varepsilon)\subset\Sigma
, when starting from
p
. Then
u_{\star,\varepsilon}:\Sigma\to[0,\infty]
is measurable and we denote by

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\int_{\Sigma}u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)\mathrm{d}\vol_{g}(p)

the mean expected stopping time.

In
[
3
]
, it was shown that if
\Sigma
is either the round sphere, the flat torus or is Anosov (in the sense that its geodesic flow has the Anosov property), then we have

(1.2)

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}\sim\overline{c}_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{2(\alpha-1)}\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

for some
\overline{c}_{\alpha}>0.
Moreover, for any
p\neq p_{\star}
it holds

(1.3)

u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)=\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}+\mathcal{O}(1)

except if
\Sigma
is the round sphere,
4\alpha<1
, and
p
is the antipodal point of
p_{\star}
, in which case

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}-u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)\sim\tilde{c}_{\alpha}\,\varepsilon^{-1+4\alpha}\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0,

for some other constant
\tilde{c}_{\alpha}>0.

The round sphere therefore provides the first indication that conjugate points may influence the asymptotic behavior of hitting times. The main purpose of this article is to show that, on Zoll surfaces
[
42
]
, this phenomenon is in fact much more subtle than suggested by the antipodal singularity of the sphere. Zoll surfaces are Riemannian surfaces all of whose geodesics are simple closed curves of common length. While this property may suggest a highly rigid geometry, Zoll metrics form an infinite-dimensional class
[
2
,
14
]
whose conjugate loci can exhibit remarkably intricate singular behaviors. Our main result shows that the asymptotic behavior of
u_{\star,\varepsilon}
near a conjugate point is entirely determined by the local singularity type of the conjugate locus.

To quantify this phenomenon, we use the
cuspidal degree
of a
conjugate point
p
of
p_{\star}
. It is defined as the infimum in

\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}\cup\{\infty\}
of all integers
k
such that the
following property holds. In a neighborhood of
p
, the conjugate locus of

p_{\star}
is canonically parametrized by a finite union of plane curves, and
for each such curve there exists an integer
\ell\leqslant k
such that, in
suitable local coordinates, its
\ell
-jet is given by

u\mapsto(u^{\ell-1},u^{\ell})
. A more detailed discussion of this notion is given in §
2.3
. In particular for the round sphere, the degree of the antipodal point is infinite. The degree should not be confused with the
order
of conjugacy, which always equals 1 on surfaces for dimensional reasons. Rather, the degree measures the singularity type of the exponential map near conjugate vectors, see Proposition
2.7
below.

In what follows we write
f(\varepsilon)\sim g(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
if
f(\varepsilon)/g(\varepsilon)\to 1
. Our main result goes as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is an orientable Zoll surface. Then if
\overline{c}_{\alpha}
is the constant from (
1.2
) one has

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}\sim\overline{c}_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{2(\alpha-1)}\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Suppose
\alpha<1/2
. Then
u_{\star,\varepsilon}
is continuous and for any
p\in\Sigma
, the following holds.

(i)

If
p
is not a conjugate point of
p_{\star}
, then
u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)=\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}+\mathcal{O}(1)
.

(ii)

If
p
is a conjugate point of
p_{\star}
of degree
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
and
4\alpha<k/(k+1)
then there is
c>0
such that

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}-u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)\sim c\,\varepsilon^{-1+4\alpha+(k+1)^{-1}}.

If
4\alpha\geqslant k/(k+1)
then
u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)-\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(1).

(iii)

If
p
is conjugate to
p_{\star}
of degree
k=\infty
and
4\alpha<1
then for small
\varepsilon
we have

D^{-1}\varepsilon^{-1+4\alpha}f(\varepsilon)\leqslant\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}-u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)\leqslant D\varepsilon^{-1+4\alpha}f(\varepsilon)

for some
D>0
and some function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\left]0,2\right]
which is a slowly varying correction term in the sense that

\varepsilon^{-\delta}f(\varepsilon)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\infty\quad\text{for every }\delta>0.

(iv)

If
p_{\star}
has only one conjugate point
p
then
p
is of infinite degree and if
4\alpha<1
then there is
c>0
such that

\overline{u}_{\star,\varepsilon}-u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)\sim c\,\varepsilon^{-1+4\alpha}.

This result shows that different local geometries of the conjugate locus produce different asymptotic signatures in the mean first capture time. The above hierarchy is non-vacuous. Indeed, using Guillemin’s local deformation theory for Zoll metrics
[
14
]
and a transversality argument, we show that every finite degree can be realized by a conjugate pair on a Zoll surface. More precisely, we prove that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist a Zoll surface
(\Sigma,g)
and points
p,q\in\Sigma
such that
q
is conjugate to
p
with degree
k
, see Theorem
2.11
. We also show that there exist Zoll surfaces admitting conjugate points of infinite degree but whose conjugate locus are not reduced to a point, see Lemma
2.14
. The round sphere provides a distinguished example of infinite degree, where the entire conjugate locus collapses to a single point.

Zoll surfaces originate from the classical construction of non-round metrics on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
all of whose geodesics are closed by Zoll
[
42
]
. The first infinitesimal obstruction to deforming the round metric within this class was identified by Funk
[
9
]
, and the converse problem was later solved by Guillemin through the Radon transform and a Nash-Moser argument
[
14
]
. More generally, the global geometry of Zoll surfaces has been the subject of extensive study in Riemannian geometry
[
37
,
2
,
19
,
23
]
.

Beyond these geometric aspects, Zoll manifolds have provided a fundamental
testing ground for spectral and microlocal analysis. The periodicity of the
geodesic flow leads to strong constraints on the spectrum of geometric
operators, while still allowing subtle spectral phenomena
[
6
,
38
,
5
,
33
,
40
,
41
,
22
]
.

In this paper, we exploit a different aspect of the same geometry: despite the
global rigidity imposed by the periodicity of all geodesics, Zoll surfaces can
exhibit a rich hierarchy of singular conjugate loci, and these singularities are
detected by the asymptotic behavior of Lévy hitting times.

Strategy of proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the analysis of the generator

(1.4)

\mathrm{A}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}c_{\alpha}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\pi_{*}(\varphi_{t}^{*}-\mathrm{I})\pi^{*}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t^{1+2\alpha}}:\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\Sigma)\to\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma),

of the process
(X_{t})
introduced in
[
1
]
. Here
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma)
is the space of distributions on
\Sigma
while
\pi:S\Sigma\to\Sigma
denotes the unit tangent bundle projection and
(\varphi_{t})
is the geodesic flow. A first step consists in establishing the connection between
\mathrm{A}
and the expected hitting time. More precisely, adapting the arguments from
[
3
]
, we prove that
u_{\star,\varepsilon}
is characterized as the solution of a non-local boundary value problem involving
\mathrm{A}
. This reduces the study of hitting times to the analysis of an almost inverse of the generator.

A key step is therefore to obtain a microlocal description of
\mathrm{A}
. We show that it decomposes

as

\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{A}_{\alpha}+\mathrm{B}

where
\mathrm{A}_{\alpha}
is an elliptic pseudo-differential operator of order
2\alpha
and
\mathrm{B}
is a Fourier integral operator of order
-1
whose canonical relation is the graph of an exact, involutive symplectomorphism

G:T^{*}\Sigma\setminus\underline{0}\to T^{*}\Sigma\setminus\underline{0}

which is canonically determined by the geometry of conjugate points, see Propositions
4.1
and
4.3
.
Since
G
is involutive, compositions involving the Fourier integral part generate no new canonical relations. In particular, parametrices of
\mathrm{A}
remain within the same microlocal class. This yields a precise description of the Green kernel of
\mathrm{A}
and allows us to relate its singularities to the local geometry of the conjugate locus. The local analysis underlying this geometry relies on normal forms for the exponential map corresponding to the different degrees of conjugate points. Such normal forms are in the spirit of the classical work of Warner
[
34
]
, which, in arbitrary dimension, treats the cases corresponding to degrees
1
and
\infty
in our terminology. In turn, this provides the key tool for deriving sharp asymptotics for
u_{\star,\varepsilon}
.

The non-orientable case

If
(\Sigma,g)
is a non-orientable Zoll surface, then (
1.3
) holds as
\varepsilon\to 0
, for every
p\neq p_{\star}
. Indeed, by Theorem
B.1
,
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
and
p_{\star}
is conjugate only to itself along geodesics through
p_{\star}
(in fact a result of Green
[
11
]
implies that
(\Sigma,g)
must be isometric to the standard projective plane; see Remark
B.2
). As we shall see in §
5.5
, the absence of non-trivial conjugate points implies that the generator
\mathrm{A}
is an elliptic pseudodifferential operator, exactly as in the torus and Anosov cases and the analysis from
[
3
]
yields (
1.3
).

Related works

Geodesic Lévy flights on general Riemannian manifolds were first introduced in
[
1
]
where the formula for the generator
\mathrm{A}
in (
1.4
) was derived. The work of
[
32
]
proved that, on
general
Riemannian manifolds,
\mathrm{A}
as defined in (
1.4
) is a Fourier integral operator when time integral is over a
finite
interval. However, the only detailed analysis of
\mathrm{A}
when the time integral is
infinite
is in
[
3
]
where the authors showed that it is an elliptic pseudodifferential operator on negatively curved manifolds and the flat torus. As for the first arrival time function
u_{\star,\varepsilon}(p)
, the leading asymptotic was computed in
[
3
]
for Anosov manifolds, the round sphere, and the flat torus—on the sphere, it was also observed a special case of Theorem
1.1
(iv).

Organization of the article

The article is organized as follows. In §
2
, we study the geometry of conjugate loci on Zoll surfaces and derive normal forms for the exponential map near singular conjugate points. In §
3
we recall standard facts about pseudo-differential and Fourier integral operators. In §
4
, we analyze the generator of the Lévy process and describe the microlocal structure of a parametrix for its inverse. In §
5
, we express the expected stopping time as the solution of an integral equation involving the generator and prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in Appendix
A
we give normal forms for singular maps in dimension
2
and in Appendix
B
we recall classical facts on Zoll surfaces.

Geometry of the unit tangent bundle

*§10.4.1.1 . We have the Liouville one-form

\langle\alpha(z),w\rangle=\langle\mathrm{d}\pi(z)\cdot w,v\rangle,\quad{z=}(p,v)\in M,\quad w\in T_{(x,v)}M.

\pi^{*}\vol_{g}=\alpha\wedge\beta.

We denote by

\iota_{V}\,\psi=1,\quad\mathrm{d}\alpha=\psi\wedge\beta,\quad\mathrm{d}\beta=\alpha\wedge\psi,\quad\mathrm{d}\psi=-(\kappa\circ\pi)\alpha\wedge\beta,

(2.3)

[V,X]=H,\quad[V,H]=-X,\quad[X,H]=(\kappa\circ\pi)V.

The orientation of

\mathbf{V}(z)=\mathbb{R}V(z)=T_{z}\Lambda_{\pi(z)}\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda_{\pi(z)}=S_{\pi(z)}\Sigma\subset M

For any

\iota_{\pm}:T^{*}\Sigma\setminus\underline{0}\longrightarrow\mathbf{H}^{\star}\setminus\underline{0},\quad(q,\eta)\longmapsto(z_{\eta}^{\pm},\mathrm{d}\pi(z_{\eta}^{\pm})^{\top}\eta).

Denote by
\lambda_{T^{*}M}
and
\lambda_{T^{*}\Sigma}

\widetilde{R}_{\pi}\circ\iota_{\pm}=\iota_{\mp}.

2.2.
First conjugacy time

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a vector
z\in M
is conjugate at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
if the intersection

(2.7)

\Bigl(\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}(z)\cdot\mathbf{V}(z)\Bigr)\cap\mathbf{V}(\varphi_{t}(z))

is not reduced to
\{0\}
.

Since
\mathbf{V}(z)=\mathbb{R}V(z)
is of dimension
1
, the condition (
2.7
) is equivalent to the fact that there is
a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
such that

(2.8)

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}(z)V(z)=aV(\varphi_{t}(z)).

Moreover, note that
\ker\alpha=\mathbb{R}H\oplus\mathbb{R}V
is preserved by the geodesic flow. In particular
z
is conjugate at time
t
if and only if

\langle\beta(\varphi_{t}(z)),\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}(z)V(z)\rangle=0,

which reads

\langle\varphi_{t}^{*}\beta(z),V(z)\rangle=0.

Another equivalent formulation is

(2.9)

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}(z)^{-\top}\mathbf{H}^{\star}(z)=\mathbf{H}^{\star}(\varphi_{t}(z)).

From now on we assume that
\Sigma
is a Zoll surface of period
2\pi
, which means that the geodesic flow is periodic of period
2\pi
and for any
z\in S\Sigma
, the map

(2.10)

\gamma_{z}:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma,\quad t\mapsto\pi\circ\varphi_{t}(z)

is injective. In particular this implies that for every
z\in M
, one has
\mathrm{d}\varphi_{2\pi}(z)=\mathrm{I}:T_{z}M\to T_{z}M
, hence according to (
2.7
) there exists

so that
z
is conjugate at time
t
. This allows us to make the following

Definition 2.2
.

For any
z\in M
, we denote by

\tau(z)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\penalty\ :\penalty\ z\text{ is conjugate at time }t\}

the first conjugacy time when starting from
z
.

(Properties of the conjugacy time)
.

The map
\tau:M\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

. Moreover we have
\tau\circ\mathrm{R}_{\pi}=2\pi-\tau
and a point

2.3.
Degree of a conjugate vector and normal form of the exponential map

As mentioned in the introduction, we define the degree of a conjugate point, which describes the singularity type of the exponential map near a conjugate vector. It is worth mentioning that this notion already appears implicitly in the work of Waters
[
35
,
36
]
on bifurcations of singularities of conjugate loci on surfaces.

Definition 2.5
.

The
degree
of
z\in M
is defined by

\deg(z)=\inf\bigl\{k\in\mathbb{N}\penalty\ :\penalty\ V^{k}\tau(z)\neq 0\bigr\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

Note that the degree is different from the

of the conjugacy

\mathrm{ord}(z)=\dim\Bigl(\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\tau(z)}(z)\cdot\mathbf{V}(z)\cap\mathbf{V}\bigl(\varphi_{\tau(z)}(z)\bigr)\Bigr)

which, in our case, is always equal to

\dim\Sigma=2.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
z\in M

In particular one may compute

Since

Hence

2.4.

For any

F_{k}(\mathbf{a},\theta)\neq 0\quad\text{ for every}\quad(\mathbf{a},\theta)\in(C\setminus 0)\times I_{\star,\delta}.

Next, we consider the restriction

Lemma 2.14

3.
Background material on microlocal analysis

In this section we recall some basic facts about microlocal analysis.

3.1.
Pseudodifferential operators

We refer to
[
12
,
16
,
17
,
20
]
for a general treatment. Let
\Sigma
be a closed
n
-dimensional manifold. For
k\in\mathbb{R}
we define
S^{k}(T^{*}\Sigma)\subset C^{\infty}(T^{*}\Sigma)
the space of symbols of order
k
as the set of smooth functions
a
satisfying the following bounds, in any coordinate chart
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
: for all
\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, there exists
C=C(U,\alpha,\beta)>0
such that

(3.1)

\forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}U\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad|\partial^{\gamma}_{\xi}\partial^{\beta}_{x}a(x,\xi)|\leqslant C\langle\xi\rangle^{k-|\gamma|}.

It can be checked that (
3.1
) is invariant by diffeomorphism, which implies that
S^{k}(T^{*}\Sigma)
is intrinsically defined on
\Sigma
.

We define
\Psi^{-\infty}(\Sigma)
, the set of
smoothing operators
, as the space of linear operators on
\Sigma
with smooth Schwartz kernel (with respect to some, hence any, volume form on
\Sigma
). Denote by
\mathrm{Op}
any quantization procedure on
\Sigma
, given in a local coordinate patch
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\mathrm{Op}(a)f(x)=\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{y}}e^{i\xi\cdot(x-y)}a(x,\xi)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\xi,

where
a\in S^{k}(T^{*}U)
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U)
. The set of
pseudodifferential operators
of order
k\in\mathbb{R}
is then defined as

\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)=\left\{\mathrm{Op}(a)+R\penalty\ |\penalty\ a\in S^{k}(T^{*}\Sigma),R\in\Psi^{-\infty}(\Sigma)\right\}.

It can be checked that
\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)
is intrinsically defined and independent on the choice of quantization
\mathrm{Op}
. We will denote by
\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{k}(\Sigma)
the set of
classical
pseudo-differential operators, that are operators
A=\Op(a)\in\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)
such that
a\in S^{k}(T^{*}M)
admits an asymptotic expansion of the form

a\sim\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}a_{\ell}

where
a_{\ell}\in S^{k-r_{\ell}}(T^{*}M)
is homogeneous of degree
k-r_{\ell}
far from the zero section and
(r_{\ell})
is an increasing sequence such that
r_{0}=0
and
r_{\ell}\to\infty
.

There exists a well-defined
principal symbol map

\sigma:\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)\to S^{k}(T^{*}\Sigma)/S^{k-1}(T^{*}\Sigma)

such that we have the following exact sequence:

0\longrightarrow\Psi^{k-1}(\Sigma)\longrightarrow\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)\longrightarrow S^{k}(T^{*}\Sigma)/S^{k-1}(T^{*}\Sigma)\longrightarrow 0.

An pseudo-differential operator
A\in\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)
is said to be
elliptic
if there exists
C>0
such that

\sigma(A)\geqslant C|\xi|^{k}\quad\text{ whenever}\quad|\xi|\geqslant C.

The important property of elliptic operators on
\Sigma
is that they are invertible modulo smoothing remainders, that is, one can find
B\in\Psi^{-k}(T^{*}\Sigma)
and
R\in\Psi^{-\infty}(\Sigma)
such that

BA=\mathbf{1}+R.

Such an operator
B
is called a
parametrix
for
A
.

3.2.
Fourier integral operators

We refer to
[
7
,
17
]
for a general treatment. If
P
is a given smooth manifold we denote by
T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}
the cotangent bundle of
P
with the zero section removed and by
\omega_{P}\in\Omega^{2}(T^{*}P)
the canonical symplectic form. Let
P,Q
be smooth manifolds. A
canonical relation

\mathscr{C}\subset T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}

is a conical submanifold which is Lagrangian with respect to the symplectic form
\pi_{P}^{*}\,\omega_{P}-\pi_{Q}^{*}\omega_{Q}
on the product
T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}
. Here
\pi_{P}
and
\pi_{Q}
are the projections from
T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}
towards
T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}
and
T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}
, respectively. For
m\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\mathrm{I}^{m}(P,Q,\mathscr{C})
to be the set of Fourier integral operators of order
m
whose canonical relation is
\mathscr{C}
. Concretely, these are operators
\mathscr{C}_{c}^{\infty}(Q)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(P)
whose Schwartz kernel can be written as a locally finite sum of oscillatory integrals of the form

K(p,q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{i\varphi(p,q,\theta)}a(p,q,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta,

where
\varphi
is a non-degenerate phase function parametrizing
\mathscr{C}
locally, that is
\mathrm{d}\varphi\neq 0
on the support of
a
and we have locally

(3.2)

\left\{(p,\partial_{p}\varphi,q,-\partial_{q}\varphi):\partial_{\theta}\varphi=0\right\}=\mathscr{C}\subset T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}.

Here
a\in S^{\mu}(P\times Q\times\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\theta})
is a classical symbol with

(3.3)

m=\mu+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{\dim P+\dim Q}{4}.

In particular, for any
\mathrm{A}\in\mathrm{I}^{m}(P,Q,\mathscr{C})
the kernel
K_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(P\times Q)
of
\mathrm{A}
(with respect to any volume forms on
P
and
Q
) satisfies

(3.4)

\WF(K_{\mathrm{A}})\subset\mathscr{C}^{\prime}=\{(p,\xi,q,-\eta)\penalty\ :\penalty\ (p,\xi,q,\eta)\in\mathscr{C}\}.

Pseudo-differential operators on
P
correspond to the special case where
\mathscr{C}
is the diagonal

\Delta(T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0})=\left\{(p,\xi,p,\xi)\penalty\ :\penalty\ (p,\xi)\in T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\right\}.

Next let
R
be a third manifold and let
\mathscr{C}\subset T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}
and
\mathscr{D}\subset T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}R\setminus\underline{0}
be two canonical relations. We say that their composition is
clean
if the intersection

(\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{D})\cap(T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times\Delta(T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0})\times T^{*}R\setminus\underline{0})

is a clean intersection, that is, it is a smooth submanifold and its tangent space is equal to the intersection of the tangent spaces. The integer
e
is the
excess
of the composition, namely the dimension by which this intersection fails to be transverse.
If the composition of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
is clean with excess
e
, then the composition of Fourier integral operators satisfies

(3.5)

\mathrm{I}^{m}(P,Q,\mathscr{C})\circ\mathrm{I}^{\ell}(Q,R,\mathscr{D})\subset\mathrm{I}^{m+\ell+\frac{e}{2}}(P,R,\mathscr{C}\circ\mathscr{D})

where the composition
\mathscr{C}\circ\mathscr{D}\subset T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\times T^{*}Q\setminus\underline{0}
is given by

\mathscr{C}\circ\mathscr{D}=\{(p,\xi,r,\rho)\penalty\ :\penalty\ \exists(q,\eta),\ (p,\xi,q,\eta)\in\mathscr{C}\text{ and }(q,\eta,r,\rho)\in\mathscr{D}\}.

In particular, if

\mathscr{C}=\mathrm{graph}(G)=\{(G(q,\eta),(q,\eta))\penalty\ :\penalty\ (q,\eta)\in T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\}

is the graph of a homogeneous symplectomorphism

G:T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0}\to T^{*}P\setminus\underline{0},

then the composition is transverse and has zero excess. Hence, if we are given

\mathrm{A}\in\mathrm{I}^{m_{1}}(P,P,\mathrm{graph}(G))\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{B}\in\mathrm{I}^{m_{2}}(P,P,\mathrm{graph}(H)),

then

\mathrm{A}\mathrm{B}\in\mathrm{I}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}(P,P,\mathrm{graph}(G\circ H))
.

3.3.
Functional spaces

We now introduce the functional spaces we will be working with. We denote by
\Delta_{g}\leqslant 0
the negative Hodge Laplacian acting on functions. For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
(\mathbf{1}-\Delta)^{s}
defined using the spectral theorem (applied to the self-adjoint operator
\Delta_{g}
on
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathrm{vol}_{g})
) is an invertible pseudodifferential operator of order
2s
.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\in(1,\infty)
and
u\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, we set

(3.6)

\|u\|_{W^{s,m}}=\|(\mathbf{1}-\Delta)^{s/2}u\|_{L^{m}},

and define
W^{s,m}(\Sigma)
to be the completion of
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
with respect to the norm (
3.6
). Taking
m=2
, we retrieve the usual Sobolev spaces which we will rather denote by
H^{s}(\Sigma)=W^{s,2}(\Sigma)
. Note that the spaces
W^{s,m}(\Sigma)
intrinsically defined, that is, they are independent of the choice of metric
g
, and changing the metric only replaces the norm (
3.6
) by an equivalent norm.

The following boundedness result for pseudodifferential operators holds: for all
k\in\mathbb{R},A\in\Psi^{k}(\Sigma)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\in(1,\infty)
,

(3.7)

A:W^{s+k,m}(\Sigma)\to W^{s,m}(\Sigma)

is bounded.
More generally, let
\mathscr{C}\subset T^{*}\Sigma\setminus 0\times T^{*}\Sigma\setminus 0
be the graph of a homogeneous symplectomorphism and let

. Then, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\in(1,\infty)
,

(3.8)

A:W^{s+k+\mu_{m},m}(\Sigma)\to W^{s,m}(\Sigma)\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{m}=(n-1)\left|\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{2}\right|.

is bounded, see
[
28
]
*Corollary 2.4.

Eventually, given
\Omega\subset\Sigma
be an open subset with non-empty smooth boundary, we define, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\in[1,\infty)
, the spaces

\dot{W}^{s,m}(\overline{\Omega})=\{u\in W^{s,m}(\Sigma)\mid\supp(u)\subset\overline{\Omega}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{W}^{s,m}(\Omega)=\{u|_{\Omega}\penalty\ |\penalty\ u\in W^{s,m}(\Sigma)\}.

4.

In this section we analyse the Lévy flight generator, construct its parametrix, and analyse their behaviour.

4.1.

From now assume that

The main result of this paragraph is the following

Corollary 4.7
.

There exists a bounded operator
\mathrm{A}^{+}:\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\Sigma)\to\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma)

\mathrm{A}^{+}\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{A}\mathrm{A}^{+}=\mathrm{Id}-\Pi

is an essentially self-adjoint and bounded map from

Let

Definition A.1

(
1
-singular maps)
.

Let

and
\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)\neq 0

The following result is immediate.

Lemma A.2

Let

be a smooth map which is
1
-singular at the origin and set
\lambda=\det\mathrm{d}f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}.
Then the singular set
C=\{\lambda=0\}

\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\operatorname{rank}\mathrm{d}f(x)=1
for every

close enough to
0

\eta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a non-vanishing map such that
\eta(x)\in\ker\mathrm{d}f(x)
for all
x\in C
close enough to zero. Let
\tilde{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a local defining function for
C
, that is, we have
C=\{\tilde{\lambda}=0\}
and
\mathrm{d}\tilde{\lambda}|_{C}\neq 0

k(f)=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \eta^{k}\tilde{\lambda}(0)\neq 0\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

Then the following holds

(i)

The number
k(f)
is independent of the choice of the vector field
\eta
and the local defining function
\tilde{\lambda}
.

(ii)

This number
k(f)
is invariant under changes of coordinates: if
\tilde{\kappa},\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

\tilde{\kappa}(0)=\kappa(0)=0
that are local diffeomorphisms near the origin then
\tilde{f}=\tilde{\kappa}\circ f\circ\kappa
is
1
-singular at the origin and

.

Proof.

By assumption
\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)\neq 0
for
x
near the origin so by the implicit function theorem the set
C
is a
1
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
near the origin. By definition,
\mathrm{d}f(x)
is not invertible for any

and thus exactly
1
by upper semi-continuity of the rank.

To verify (i), let
\tilde{\lambda}
be a local boundary defining function of

. Then
\tilde{\lambda}(x)=c(x)\lambda(x)
for some smooth positive function

defined near the origin. Similarly, suppose
\tilde{\eta}
is another non-vanishing vector field such that

\tilde{\eta}(x)\in{\rm ker}(\mathrm{d}f(x))={\rm span}\{\eta(x)\}\quad\text{ for every }x\in C.

Then we must have
\tilde{\eta}(x)=a(x)\eta(x)
for some positive function
a
defined near the origin. Suppose

\eta^{k}\lambda(0)\neq 0\quad\text{ but }\quad\eta^{j}\lambda(0)=0\quad\text{ for all }j\leqslant k-1.

Then we have that
\tilde{\eta}^{j}\tilde{\lambda}(0)=0
for all

while
\tilde{\eta}^{k}\tilde{\lambda}=a(0)c(0)\eta^{k}\lambda(0)\neq 0
. The converse holds by swapping the role of
\tilde{\eta}
with
\eta
and
\tilde{\lambda}
with
\lambda
. So we have that
k(f)
is independent of choice of local defining function and the vector field generating
{\rm ker}(\mathrm{d}f(x))
at every

.

To verify (ii), let
\tilde{f}=\tilde{\kappa}\circ f\circ\kappa
and set
\lambda_{\tilde{\kappa}}=\det\mathrm{d}\kappa
and define
\lambda_{\kappa}
similarly. Then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}={\rm det}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{f}=(\lambda_{\tilde{\kappa}}\circ f\circ\kappa)\cdot(\lambda\circ f)\cdot\lambda_{\kappa}

vanishes at the origin. Furthermore
\mathrm{d}\tilde{\lambda}(0)=\det\mathrm{d}\tilde{\kappa}\det\mathrm{d}\kappa\mathrm{d}\lambda(0)\neq 0
so
\tilde{f}
is also
1
-singular. To verify that

we suppose

\tilde{\eta}\in{\rm ker}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{f})
so that

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}^{k}\tilde{\lambda}(0)\neq 0,\quad{\rm but}\quad\tilde{\eta}^{j}\tilde{\lambda}(0)=0\quad{\rm for}\quad j\leqslant k-1.

Then
\eta=\mathrm{d}\kappa(\tilde{\eta})\in\ker\mathrm{d}f
and by (

\eta^{j}\lambda=\tilde{\eta}^{j}(\lambda\circ\kappa)=\tilde{\eta}^{j}\left(\tilde{\lambda}\left((\det\mathrm{d}\tilde{\kappa})\circ f\circ\kappa\right)^{-1}(\det\mathrm{d}\kappa)^{-1}\right).

The function
\tilde{\lambda}\left((\det\mathrm{d}\tilde{\kappa})\circ f\circ\kappa\right)^{-1}(\det\mathrm{d}\kappa)^{-1}

t\mapsto g(s,t)
. Now setting
(s,v)=(s,t-t(s))=\kappa(s,t)
we have

g\circ\kappa^{-1}(s,v)=g(s,t(s))+v^{2}\mu(s,v)\quad\text{where}\quad\mu(s,v)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}^{2}g(s,t(s)+cv)(1-c)\mathrm{d}c.

After possibly changing the sign of the second target coordinate,
we may assume that
\mu(0,0)>0
. Now set
b(s)=g(s,t(s))
. Then up to composing
f
by the local diffeomorphisms

(s,t)\mapsto(s,[t-t(s)]\mu(s,t)^{-1/2})\quad\text{and}\quad(x,y)\mapsto(x,y-b(x))

on the right and on the left, respectively, we may assume
g(s,t)=t^{2}
. This is (i).

Next, assume that
k>1
is finite. Then by (
A.4
) we can find two smooth maps
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\beta(0)\neq 0
as well as
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(A.5)

g(s,t)=a(s)+st\alpha(s,t)+t^{k+1}\beta(s,t)

for
s,t
close enough to zero. Up to composing on the left by
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y-a(x))
one can assume
a=0.
Notice that
\det\mathrm{d}f(s,t)=\partial_{t}g(s,t)
. Then, since
f
is
1
-singular, we have
\mathrm{d}\partial_{t}g(0)\neq 0
. Therefore by (
A.4
) and (
A.5
) we must have

\alpha(0)=\partial_{s}\partial_{t}g(0)\neq 0.

Hence
(s,u)=(s,t\alpha(s,t))=\kappa(s,t)
are coordinates and setting
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha\circ\kappa^{-1}
and
\tilde{\beta}=\beta\circ\kappa^{-1}
we have

g\circ\kappa^{-1}(s,u)=su+\rho(s,u)u^{k+1}\quad\text{where}\quad\rho(s,u)=\tilde{\beta}(s,u)\tilde{\alpha}(s,u)^{-(k+1)}.

Now up to making a linear change of variables at the target and source level we may assume
\rho(0)=1
, that is, (ii) holds.

Finally, assume
k=\infty
. In that case (
A.4
) yields

g(s,t)=a(s)+st\alpha(s,t)+\beta(s,t)\quad\text{with}\quad\partial_{t}^{\ell}\beta(0)=0\text{ for all }\ell=0,1,\dots

Again, up to composing on the left by
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y-a(x))
we may assume
a=0
and in the preceding case we have
\alpha(0)\neq 0
. Then as above the change of variable
(s,u)=(s,t\alpha(s,t))=\kappa(s,t)
allows us to obtain (iii).
∎

Appendix B
Topology of Zoll surfaces

The purpose of this appendix is to recall well known facts about the topology of Zoll surfaces. The arguments presented below essentially follow those of LeBrun–Mason
[
19
]
which are valid for general projective Zoll structures; for the convenience of the reader, we give a condensed and self-contained exposition in the Riemannian setting relevant to the present work.

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be a Riemannian surface and denote by

\pi:S\Sigma\to\Sigma

its unit tangent bundle. We will say that
(\Sigma,g)
is
Zoll
if all its geodesics are closed, simple, and of length
2\pi
.

Theorem B.1

(
[
2
,
19
]
)
.

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be a Zoll surface.

(i)

If
\Sigma
is orientable, then
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to the
2
-sphere. Moreover for each point
p
on the image
C
of a closed geodesic , there is exactly one other point
q\in C
which is conjugate to
p
.

(ii)

If
\Sigma
is not orientable, then
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
. Moreover the only conjugate point of some point along a closed geodesic is itself.

Remark B.2
.

If
\Sigma
is a Zoll surface which is not orientable it follows from a result of Green
[
11
]
that
\Sigma
must be
isometric
to
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
, endowed with its canonical Riemannian metric. In fact, this is also true if we only assume that all the geodesics of
\Sigma
are closed (and not necessarily simple with same length) by a result of Pries
[
26
]
.

We start with the following fact.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be a Riemannian surface all of whose geodesics are closed. Then
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to
S^{2}
or to
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}.
Let
c:S^{1}\to\Sigma
be a smooth curve. Assume that
c
is not homotopic to a point. Then there exists a closed geodesic
\gamma:S^{1}\to\Sigma
which is homotopic to
c
, as can be seen by taking a minimising curve in the homotopy class of
c
or by using the shortening curve flow of Grayson
[
10
]
. Now let
(p,v)=(\gamma(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))\in S\Sigma
. Let
w:S^{1}\to S_{p}\Sigma
be a parametrization. For any
\theta\in S^{1}
we consider
\gamma_{\theta}
the closed geodesic starting at
p
with velocity
w(\theta)
. Then
[\gamma_{\theta}]=[\gamma]=[c]\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
for each
\theta\in S^{1}
. Therefore
[c]=[\gamma_{\pi}]=[\gamma]^{-1}=[c]^{-1}
. This shows that every element in
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
is of order
2
hence
\Sigma=S^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
by classification of surfaces.
∎

Let us introduce some notation. We consider the reversing action of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on
S\Sigma
given by
a\cdot(p,v)=(p,v)
if
a=1
and
a\cdot(p,-v)
if
a=-1
. Then we will denote by
\mathbf{P}T\Sigma=S\Sigma/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
the projective tangent space of
\Sigma
. In what follows, we consider
\mathcal{G}
the manifold of (unoriented) closed geodesics in
\Sigma
and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
be the manifold of oriented closed geodesics in
\Sigma
. More precisely, the manifold
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
is simply
S\Sigma/S^{1}
where the
S^{1}
action is free and properly discontinuous and given by
(z,t)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(z)
. Then
\mathcal{G}=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
where the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on
\mathcal{G}
is given by
(a,[z])\mapsto[az]
.

Lemma B.4
.

The manifolds
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
are diffeomorphic to
S^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

The projection map
\mathbf{P}T\Sigma\to{\mathcal{G}}
is smooth and surjective. Moreover its fibers are path connected so the induced map
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)\to\pi_{1}({\mathcal{G}})
is surjective. Therefore
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{G}})
is finite, hence
\mathcal{G}=S^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
by classification of surfaces. However
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}
is a double cover of
\mathcal{G}
, so
\mathcal{G}
cannot be
S^{2}
. We get the sought result.
∎

Let
\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}=T\mathcal{G}/\mathbb{R}^{\times}
be the projective tangent bundle of
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma B.5
.

The fundamental groups of
\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
and
\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}
are finite.

Proof.

If
\Sigma
is orientable we have
S\Sigma\simeq SS^{2}\simeq SO(3)
. In particular
\pi_{1}(S\Sigma)
is finite and so is
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)
since there is a double covering
S\Sigma\to\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
. If
\Sigma
is not orientable consider the double covering
\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
. It induces a double covering
\mathbf{P}T\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
. However
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\widetilde{\Sigma})
is finite by the orientable case hence the result follows.
∎

We now introduce a map

\Phi:\mathbf{P}T\Sigma\to\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G},

as follows. Let
[z]=[(p,v)]\in\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
and
w:S^{1}\to S_{p}\Sigma
be one of the two natural parametrization such that
w(0)=v
. For
\theta\in S^{1}
we let
C_{\theta}\in\mathcal{G}
be determined by
[(p,w(\theta))]
and set
C=C_{0}
for convenience. Then we define

\Phi([z])=\bigl[\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}C_{\theta}\bigr]\in\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}.

Note that this is independent of our choices since changing the parametrization would only change the sign of
\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}C_{\theta}\in T_{C}\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma B.6
.

Consider the restriction

\Phi_{C}=\Phi|_{\mathbf{P}TC}:\mathbf{P}TC\to\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}

where
\mathbf{P}TC\subset\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
is the projective tangent bundle of
C\subset\Sigma
. Note that
\mathbf{P}TC
is naturally identified with
C
itself. Then
\Phi_{C}
is an immersion and for any
p,q\in C
we have

\Phi_{C}(p)=\Phi_{C}(q)\in\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}\quad\text{iff}\quad p\text{ and }q\text{ are conjugate along }C.

Proof.

Indeed, let
q=\pi(\varphi_{t}(p,v))
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
z=(p,v)\in S\Sigma
with
v\in TC
. Then
q
is conjugate to
p
along the curve
s\mapsto\pi(\varphi_{s}(z))
if and only if
\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}\pi(\varphi_{s}(p,w(\theta)))=0
. The latter condition is equivalent to the fact that for any parametrization
u:S^{1}\to S_{q}\Sigma
the vector
\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}\left[\varphi_{s}(p,w(\theta))-(q,u(\theta))\right]
lies in the kernel of the linearized action of
S^{1}
on
S\Sigma
, which implies
\Phi_{C}(p)=\Phi_{C}(q)
. The reverse implication follows from the fact that two Jacobi fields along
C
vanishing at a same point are multiple of each other. Note also that since Jacobi fields are non degenerate,
\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}\pi(\varphi_{s}(p,w(\theta)))=0
implies that
\partial_{s}\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=0}\pi(\varphi_{s}(p,w(\theta)))
is not zero and orthogonal to
TC
. This shows that the map
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{C}(p):T_{[z]}\mathbf{P}TC\simeq C\to\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}
is injective. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

Note that the composition
\widetilde{\pi}\circ\Phi
is a submersion where
\widetilde{\pi}
is the natural projection
\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
, simply because
\widetilde{\pi}\circ\Phi=\pi
. Let
[z]\in\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
and write
\Phi([z])=(C,\rho)
for
C\in\mathcal{G}
and
\rho\in\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}
. Let
A=\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{d}\Phi([z]))\subset T_{(C,\rho)}\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}
. Since
\widetilde{\pi}\circ\Phi
is a submersion we have that
A
is transversal to
\ker\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\pi}(C,\rho)
. However the latter space coincides with
\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}
which is contained in
A
since by Lemma
B.6

\mathrm{d}\Phi_{C}([z])
is an isomorphism

T_{[z]}\mathbf{P}TC\to T_{(C,\rho)}\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}.

Hence
A=T_{(C,\rho)}\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}
. It follows that
\Phi
is covering map. By Lemma
B.6
, for each
C\in\mathcal{G}
we have that
\Phi_{C}
is a covering map
C\to\mathbf{P}T_{C}\mathcal{G}\simeq\mathbb{R}P^{1}
whose degree coincides with the number of conjugate points along
C
of any point of
C
(including itself). Since
\Phi
maps the fibers of
\mathbf{P}T\Sigma\to\mathcal{G}
to the fibers of
\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
, we conclude that for any
C\in\mathcal{G}
and
p\in C
, the number of conjugate points along
C
of any point of
C
(including itself) coincides with the degree of the covering map
\Phi
. However by Lemma
B.5
, the fundamental groups of
\mathbf{P}T\Sigma
and
\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}
are finite. Since
\Phi
is a covering map,
\Phi_{*}:\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)\to\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G})
is injective and we obtain

\deg(\Phi)=[\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G}):\Phi_{*}\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)]=\frac{|\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G})|}{|\Phi_{*}\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)|}=\frac{|\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\mathcal{G})|}{|\pi_{1}(\mathbf{P}T\Sigma)|}

In particular, this number is independent of the Riemannian metric
g
. If
\Sigma
is orientable, then
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to the sphere. If
g
is the round metric, then we know that for every great circle
C\subset\Sigma
and
p\in C
, the conjugate points of
p
along
C
are
p
and its antipodal point
q
. Hence
\deg(\Phi)=2
. If
\Sigma
is not orientable, we get that
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
. For the canonical Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
we have that for any closed geodesic
C
and any conjugate points
p,q\in C
it holds
p=q
. Hence
\deg(\Phi)=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this appendix by stating the following theorem, due to Gromoll–Grove
[
13
]
.

Theorem B.7

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be an orientable Riemannian surface such that all whose geodesics are closed. Then all its primitive closed geodesic are simple and have the same length.

Since we will not make use of this result we only outline below the proof of Gromoll–Grove. First, a result of Epstein
[
8
]
implies that any smooth flow on a compact three manifold whose orbits are circles is periodic up to reparametrization of the flow. Combining this with the fact that closed geodesics are critical points for the length function implies that the geodesic flow itself is periodic, say of period
2\pi
. Hence the length of any closed geodesics must be of the form
2\pi/k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}
. Now the orientation preserving diffeomorphism
\psi_{k}=\varphi_{2\pi/k}
has finite order and from basic differential topology this implies that its fixed point set is smooth and of dimension
1
. Therefore it must coincides with the union of the closed orbits of
(\varphi_{t})
whose minimal period is
2\pi/k
. Lifting everything to
\widetilde{S\Sigma}\simeq S^{3}
one obtains a Seifert fibration of
S^{3}
by circles (indeed by Lemma
B.3
we have that
\Sigma
diffeomorphic to
S^{2}
). By a classical result of Seifert
[
29
]
there are at most two degenerate circles, which correspond to closed orbits of period
2\pi/k
for some
k>1
. However by the Lusternik-Schnirelmann theorem
[
21
,
10
]
there are at least three simple closed geodesics on
\Sigma
. Hence at least one of their lifts to
\widetilde{S\Sigma}
is not singular for the Seifert fibration. This means that there exists one simple closed geodesic of length
2\pi
. Next, notice that the set of vectors in
S\Sigma
whose geodesic trajectory is closed and simple in
\Sigma
is open. But it is also closed, since it is the complementary of the set of vectors of
S\Sigma
whose associated closed geodesic has a transversal self-intersection. By connectedness this set coincides with
S\Sigma
.
