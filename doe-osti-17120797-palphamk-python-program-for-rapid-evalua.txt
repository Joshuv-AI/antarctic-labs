Introduction
This report presents the theory and use of Ted Carney’s PAlphaMK python program. The program
provides a standalone capability for rapid evaluation of porous material Hugoniots without resorting
to hydrocode analysis. Evaluation of the response of porous materials such as foams, powders,
sintered metals, and geologic materials is difficult and time consuming. Often times, researchers
must resort to multiple hydrocode calculations with variable constitutive inputs in order to obtain
an approximate description of the material under study. Hydrocodes will never be replaced with
such a capability. The objective is to rapidly match the Hugoniot response so that attention can be
focused on additional important phenomena requiring hydrocode analysis.

Menikoff-Kober P-Alpha Model
A porous material consists of a matrix and void components. The are denoted by the subscripts
m and v respectively. Variables without a subscript are either total quantities such as volume and
mass or mean values of thermodynamic quantities. There are various measures of the void volume
partitioning. In this report we use porosity, ϕ, which is defined as the void volume fraction. The
following illustrates volume partitioning

                                      Vv /V + Vm /V = 1
 (1)
                                      fm = Vm /V       = 1 − ϕ.

Here, fm is the matrix volume fraction and V is the total volume within a local region of the porous
material. The α in P-Alpha refers to an alternative void volume measure called the distension where

                                  α = V/Vm = 1/fm = 1/(1 − ϕ).                                    (2)

This equation conveniently relates distension, porosity, and matrix volume fraction.
The density of a porous material is

                                       ρ = fm ρm = (1 − ϕ)ρm                                      (3)

where ρm is the density of matrix. It follows that the specific volume is of the material is given by

                                         V = 1/ρ = Vm /fm                                         (4)

and Vm is the specific volume of the matrix.
The simplest models of compaction exclude strength effects and the presence of fluid in the pore vol-
ume. With these simplifications, porous materials densify under pressure through void compaction
and compression of the matrix component. For materials with disconnected voids, compaction is re-
sisted by plastic flow and viscous effects which originate at the void/matrix interfaces and evolve to
encompass the entire matrix volume under continued loading. For powders, compaction is resisted
by grain-grain interaction and friction. P-Alpha models provide a phenomenological description of
these micro-mechanical processes that cannot be directly represented in continuum descriptions.

 compacted state.

Rankine-Hugoniot Equations
The Rankine-Hugoniot equations provide the velocity and thermodynamic state jumps across a
single shock. They are derived by Davis [4] by manipulation of integral forms of the conservation
equations across a shock. Although derived for shocks of infinitesimal thickness, they are commonly
applied to structured waves where the finite rise time is small compared to the time and spatial
scales of interest. Hugoniot solutions are independent of time, the final state is unaffected by the
wave structure.
The conservation equations for mass, momentum, and energy across a single shock propagating into
a material at rest are, respectively:

Rankine-Hugoniot Solutions
The presence of porosity can greatly influence the shape of the Hugoniot. The Hugoniot for full a
density material without a phase change in the range of interest will be continuously concave with a
slope increasing with pressure in the P V plane. Hugoniots for porous materials may be anomalous as
discussed by Zeldovich and Raizer (ZR-2002) in that they may curve back on themselves and become
multi-valued with respect to volume. This behavior is illustrated in Figure 1 which compares the
shape of the P V Hugoniots for full density and initially 1 g/cm3 for CeO2 . The porous Hugoniots
are somewhat anomalous because of the value chosen for Pc .
The potential for multi-valued solutions is accommodated by choosing a target pressure on a
Rayleigh line and then iterating to find the volume that is compatible with both the equation-
of-state and P-α model. For each volume guess a full P-α iteration is required if the porosity is
greater than the user defined porosity at full compaction.

Equation-of-State
The above described Hugoniot solution require an equation-of-state (EOS) for the material of inter-
est. PAlphaMK provides a choice between Sesame tabular and a two analytics: the ideal gas and
the Grüneisen EOS (linear Us -Up ). These analytic EOS are Python implementations of the EOS
as described in Weseloh, et. al. [12]. The Sesame EOS utilizes the pyeospac interface to EOSPAC6
that is available at https://github.com/luli/pyeospac.

Material Strength
During shock loading materials are subject to large deformations at high strain rates and ele-
vated temperatures. Accurate prediction of these deformations and temperatures requires strength
modeling in addition to the calculation of pressure from equation of state. The propagation of
discontinuities, shock waves, has a directional component in the change in volume

                                            ∆V = V − V0 .                                         (15)

in the direction of volumetric change is given by

where the σi are the principle values of Cauchy stress and p is the pressure.
The current strength modeling capability of PAlphaMK is implemented within the Strength class. It
assumes the distortional response of the material is isotropic and hypoelastic-plastic. These models
contain the following components:
 1 Melt Model
 2 Shear Modulus Model
 3 Flowstress model
 4 Plastic flow model
The melt temperature Tmelt is a phase boundary between solid and liquid phases. The Sesame data
base provides tabular melt temperatures for many materials. PAlphaMK has the capability to read
tabular melt temperatures as a function of density ρ (form = sesame). In the absence of tabular
data, the analytic Lindemann melt model is available (form = Lindemann). The Lindemann melt
temperature is given by

                            Tmelt (η) = Tm0 exp{2a(1 − η −1 )}η 2(γ0 −a−1/3)                      (18)

where the compression is η = ρ/ρ0 and Tm0 , a and γ0 are constants. Steinberg [11] provides these
parameters for many materials. In the absence of a tabular data or model parameters a constant
melt temperature (form = constant, tmeltc = value K) may be specified assuming the variations
due to density changes can be neglected.
Solid materials resist distortional deformations by developing shear stresses. In an isotropic material
these stresses depend upon the magnitude of the shear modulus G and the distortional elastic strains.

Cold shear modulus tables are available in the Sesame data base. Again, a constant cold shear

The Steinberg strength model has an analytic density and temperature dependent shear modulus
of the form
                        G(ρ, T ) = G0 [1 + Ap(ρ, T )η −1/3 − B(T − Tref )]              (20)
where η = ρ/ρ0 is the compression, G0 is the initial shear modulus and A and B are material
constants. The default value of reference temperature is Tref = 300.0K. The Steinberg shear
modulus softens linearly with increasing temperature.
As a material goes through phase transition from solid to liquid, the shear modulus softens pre-
cipitously. The Ko variation of the Steinberg model introduces the melt shaper ϕ(T, Tmelt ) =
exp{−fg T / max(Tmelt − T, 0)} where T < Tmelt to simulate this effect which occurs within a mixed
phase region.
A constant shear modulus can be specified (form = constant, smc = value Mbar. In all cases when
the temperature exceeds the melt temperature, the shear modulus is set to zero.

The flow solver in PAlphaMK is an iterative implementation of the radial return algorithm.

Initial Verification
The previous section describes the solution procedures used for solving for the Hugoniot of porous
materials and provides results for CeO2 powders. This section provides an initial verification of
our solution approach that is achieved by comparison with results obtained with a Lagrangian
hydrocode. Hydrocode and analytic Hugoniot solutions should yield the same results because both
methods solve the same conservation equations expressed in different forms. This is a convenient
but non-traditional approach to verification that is justified because strictly analytic solutions are
unavailable for the non-linear compaction of porous materials.
The X −t response of a piston generated wave passing X0 at t0 − is fully defined by the Hugoniot. At
time t, the shock position is located at Xshock = X0 + Us (t − t0 ) and the initial material coordinate
has moved to X = X0 + Up (t − t0 ). These simple transformations allow direct comparison with
results obtained by a Lagrangian hydrocode. Material ahead of Xshock is at the initial state and
the material between X and Xshock is at the shocked state.
Figure 7 shows waveform solutions for CeO2 powder at initially 1 g/cm3 driven by a 15 Kbar piston
generated shock. The three curves in each plot, pressure, porosity, density, and specific energy show

how the results vary with the characteristic compaction pressure Pc . The trends are clear, for the
given initial density and shock pressure, the shock velocity is directly proportional to Pc while the

Appendix: Material Validation
A set of calculations were initially setup to provide validation and verification of the strength models
implemented in the PAlaphaMK program. The three strength options are currently available in
PAlphaMK: a variation of the Steinberg-Guinan strength model (YKos), the Johnson-Cook strength
model (jc) and a pressure dependent flow-stress model (YPFunc). However, the strength models
have been implemented as interchangeable components. In all cases the strength models assume
decoupling between pressure and the deviatoric stress and that elastic-plastic behavior assuming
associated flow in the deviatoric stress space.
The PAlphaMK program requires that the pressure and temperature are computed with an equa-
tion of state (EOS) that can be either be analytic functions (Mie-Grüneisen) or tabular functions
(Sesame). The melt temperature can be a constant, an analytic function (lindemnn) or a tabular
equation (Sesame). The shear modulus can be a constant, the Steinberg-Guinan analytic function
(YKos) or in a Preston-Wallace form (pw) that depends on a tabular (Sesame) cold shear modulus
and the melt temperature.
The flow stress model for the kospall implementation in PAlhphaMK depends upon the shear mod-
ulus and the plastic strain. The default behavior is to calculate the shear modulus based analytic
form in kospall model. This form of the shear modulus has no explicit dependence on the melt tem-
perature. Hence, model will work with a constant, the analytic (lindemann) or tabular (Sesame)
melt temperature. With the proper setup the kospall model will work with the Preston-Wallace
shear modulus (pw).
The Johnson-Cook strength model does not specify how the shear modulus and melt temperature
are computed. The flow stress depends on a non-dimensional temperature that is modulated by
the melt and reference (room) temperatures as well as the plastic strain and the plastic strain rate.
The EPIC code was used to derive the parameter sets in LA-11463-MS where the melt temperature
and shear modulus to were assumed to be constants. In PAlphaMK the Johnson-Cook can be run
with constant as well as functional forms of the shear modulus and melt temperatures.
The pressure dependent flow stress model (YPFunc) does not explicitly a particular form for the
shear modulus and melt temperatures. So, these models can be used interchangeably with this
model.
A set of calculations were run to assess the application of the three strength models available for
modeling metallic Al-1100 (99% pure Al) in PAlphaMK . Material parameters for this material are
available for the Steinberg-Guinan and Johnson-Cook strength models. In addition, there was initial
setup of for Al-1100 using the density dependent flow stress model (YPFunc) in the PAlphaMK
repository using a tabular EOS (SESAME id = 3720). This same EOS was used in all calculations.

The Hugoniot pressure as a function of density remains the same in the PAlaphaMK calculation
regardless of strength. This is because the Hugoniot calculated by PAlphaMK is computed on
pressure rather than normal stress.
