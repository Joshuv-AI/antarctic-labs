A Systematic Investigation of RL-Jailbreaking in LLMs
Montaser Mohammedalamen, Kevin Roice, Reginald McLean, Alyssa Lefaivre Škopac

Abstract
The evolution of generative models from next-token predictors to autonomous engines of complex systems necessitates rigorous safety hardening. Adversarial jailbreaking, the strategic manipulation of models to elicit harmful output, remains a primary threat to safe deployment. While Reinforcement Learning (RL) frames jailbreaking as a multi-step attack through sequential optimization, a mechanistic understanding of why the framework succeeds remains incomplete. To fill this gap, we present the first systematic decomposition of RL jailbreaking. We deconstruct the framework into problem formalization (reward function, action space, episode length), and algorithmic measures (RL algorithm, training data, reward-shaping) to identify the structural determinants of adversarial success. Our results reveal that the RL-jailbreaker successfully compromised all targeted models and safeguards. Through this first-of-its-kind analysis, we demonstrate that environment formalization, specifically dense rewards and extended episode lengths, is the primary driver of jailbreaking success. This work provides a tool for improving RL-jailbreaker efficiency and, ultimately, harden generative models resistant to RL-based attacks.

We quantify the jailbreaking success using two complementary metrics for the Attack Success Rate (ASR):

4.2
Structural decomposition

In our second contribution
2
, we perform a structural decomposition of RL jailbreaking, starting with environment formalization, specifically, investigating the impact of the
reward function
.
We evaluate two reward settings: i) dense, as the average cosine similarity between the model output and the ground truth response embeddings
r_{t}\in[0,1]
, providing a continuous feedback signal to guide the agent closer to the target; however, it could potentially trap the agent in local minima where semantic proximity is high, and ii) sparse,
r_{t}\in\{0,1\}
, a binary signal providing
r_{t}=+1
for a cosine similarity
>0.7
, and
r_{t}=0
otherwise, could present a credit-assignment challenge due to the sparsity of the objective if the agent keeps receiving zero rewards.

Our decomposition reveals a distinct divergence in performance based on the target model: On Llama (1B/3B) models, the jailbreaking agent outperforms under dense reward.
However, using a sparse reward outperforms in the Qwen and Tiny-aya models, as shown in Table
1
and Figure
2

We hypothesize that sparse rewards underperforms in stronger target models (higher refusal rate) due to infrequent feedback, which prevents PPO from establishing an effective learning signal.
Although dense rewards address this through frequent shaping signals, they can hinder performance on simpler models if the dense proxy is poorly aligned with true success.

Episode length:
Given the inherent strengths of RL in sequential decision-making, we investigate how the episode length influences adversarial success.
Within the RL-jailbreaker framework, an episode terminates after a fixed number of steps, at which point the context windows for the helper agent, target model, and safeguards are reset.
This experiment aims to determine whether a longer episode provides the RL-jailbreaker with the necessary runway to execute complex, multi-turn attack sequences or if it primarily complicates the task through the credit assignment challenge.
Although the original RL-jailbreaker framework utilizes a fixed episode length of
5

5,10,25

As illustrated in Figure

In Llama-3.2 models (1B & 3B), longer episodes (

In contrast, the

In contrast, the Qwen model, with shorter episodes (

This suggests that for this specific model, RL jailbreaker finds more immediate failure modes, and longer temporal windows may exacerbate credit assignment issues.
Similarly, in the Tiny-aya model, the performance trajectories for nearly all episode lengths overlap.
This indicates that the model’s vulnerabilities are accessible regardless of the episode duration, with no significant marginal utility gained from increasing the sequence length beyond

Reward shaping:
This section investigates a targeted form of reward shaping by introducing a discrete bonus added to the dense reward signal.
This bonus is triggered once per episode if the agent achieves a pre-defined average cosine similarity threshold, with the intent of facilitating the agent’s search for responses that align closely with the “ground truth” response and guiding the optimization toward the semantic region required for a successful jailbreak.

We evaluated three settings, no bonus (
bonus=0
), a moderate bonus (
bonus=10
), and a high bonus (
bonus=20
).
As illustrated in Figure
6
, we observed no measurable performance gains from the addition of these success bonuses across any of the target models, including Llama and Tiny-aya.
The near-overlapping trajectories suggest that the primary dense reward function is already sufficient to guide the agent effectively.
Interestingly, for the Qwen model, the configuration without a bonus demonstrated slightly superior performance in both ASR and average similarity compared to the settings with reward shaping.
These findings imply that the standard dense reward can provide a stable learning signal, and that high-magnitude discrete bonuses do not offer additional marginal utility for adversarial convergence in these contexts.

Number of Training Questions:
In this experiment, we explore the relationship between the training data volume and the agent’s ability to develop a successful jailbreaking policy, varying the number of training questions across
5,20,50
, and the full dataset of
520

We hypothesize that training on a highly restricted sample size likely leads to overfitting, where the agent memorizes prompt-specific features rather than capturing the cross-prompt patterns necessary for generalization.
Conversely, while the full dataset provides broader coverage, it may introduce excessive complexity, potentially confusing the agent and hindering its ability to identify stable adversarial patterns across questions.
Therefore, our objective is not to pinpoint a precise threshold for optimal convergence, but rather to investigate the relationship between the training data size and the RL-jailbreaker performance.

Figure

5

Conversely,

Intriguingly, expanding the training set to the full
520

20

It is important to note that the current sampled prompts are drawn from diverse domains; consequently, future work could explore this relationship by training on domain-specific subsets to evaluate the agent’s capacity for cross-domain transfer and generalization.

5
Limitations and Future Work

This work provides a structural decomposition of RL-jailbreaking, and represents an initial step toward a more comprehensive understanding of adversarial mechanics.
Our evaluation was restricted to small-scale, open-weight LLMs.
Scaling these experiments to closed-weight and multi-modal models (specifically vision and audio) remains a critical next step.
Furthermore, while we investigated individual components in isolation, future work could explore the interactions between these components.

Complementing this expansion in scale, we aim to move from identifying key optimization variables to actively enhancing the RL-jailbreaker’s performance.
This involves refining the reward function to integrate multiple objectives, including adding: i) computational budget constraints that prioritize the discovery of resource-efficient exploits, and ii) user intent, e.g., privacy breach, misuse, dual-use, and manipulation.
By isolating the successes and failures of different reward structures, we can better understand the specific optimization strategies that facilitate or hinder an agent’s ability to navigate safety boundaries.

To ensure these strategic optimizations generalize to real-world deployment, the experimental scope could be broadened to encompass real-world settings and testing.
This includes validating findings across multilingual datasets and specialized high-risk domains, e.g., healthcare, and comparing how threat actors from different sectors differ in their adversarial approaches.
Furthermore, we intend to conduct extensive benchmarking against established jailbreaking approaches on benchmarks like StrongREJECT
(
50
)
and HarmBench
(
29
)
.

Ultimately, this research trajectory facilitates a transition from vulnerability discovery toward proactive safety hardening, as more foundation models become capable of autonomous vulnerability identification.
We envision proactive safety through the development of a co-evolutionary self-play framework: in this two-player zero-sum game, an adversarial jailbreaker and a dedicated mitigation agent would engage in iterative training, providing a principled pathway for autonomous and continual model defense.

6
Conclusion

This paper provides an RL-centric evaluation of jailbreaking effectiveness against many open-weight models and safeguards.
Through a structural decomposition of the RL jailbreaking framework, we isolate the drivers of success by decoupling environment formalization from algorithmic design.
Our findings reveal that the RL-jailbreaker successfully compromised all target models and safeguards.
Moving beyond surface-level performance metrics, our study offers the first decomposition of the RL jailbreaking framework to isolate the drivers of adversarial success.
While models with strong refusal mechanisms posed a challenge, we show that environment formalization is the primary determinant of success.
Specifically, the integration of dense rewards and extended episode lengths allows agents to uncover vulnerabilities.

Future research could focus on scaling evaluations to closed-weight and multimodal models while refining reward functions to prioritize resource-efficient exploits across multilingual and high-risk domains.
Ultimately, we envision transitioning from jailbreaking toward proactive safety hardening through a co-evolutionary self-play framework.

Impact Statement

This paper studies automated red-teaming of large language models through a systematic analysis of reinforcement learning-based jailbreaking.
Our goal is to advance the scientific understanding of adversarial vulnerabilities in safety-aligned systems, with the belief that rigorous, open study of attack mechanisms is a prerequisite for building more robust defences.
We acknowledge that the methods studied here could, in principle, be misused to
elicit harmful content from deployed models.
To mitigate this risk, we restrict our experiments to open-weight models in a
controlled research setting, report findings at an aggregate level, and omit
successful jailbreak prompts from the paper.
We encourage the community to treat this work as a diagnostic tool and hope it contributes to the development of stronger safeguards rather than weaker ones.

Relying on an RL agent for decision-making at the prompt mutation-level makes the LLMs strictly a part of the environment. This paradigm differs from the use of RL in the majority of prior works, where fine-tuning at the token-generation level makes the LLM a part of the agent. Our paradigm comes with intricacies, which we detail in this section.

Observation.

The observation at timestep
t
,
O_{t}
, is the natural language response from the target LLM. The agent then constructs an approximation of a state
(
51
)
as

\displaystyle\bigl[\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(O_{t})\circ t\circ f_{t}\circ A_{t-1}\,\bigr]^{\top}\in\,\mathbb{R}^{d+3},

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}(O_{t})
is the
d

\ell_{2}
-normalized encoder embedding of the current natural language observation
O_{t}
,
t
is the current step index,
f_{t}\in\{0,1\}
is a per-process termination flag,
A_{t-1}
is the index of the previously selected action, and
\circ
denotes concatenation.
The encoder network is never updated throughout training.

Reward functions.

Let
\sigma_{i}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}(y_{i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(O_{i})}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(y_{i})\|_{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(O_{i})\|_{2}}
denote the
cosine similarity between the target response
O_{i}
and the Vicuna reference
y_{i}
for question
q_{i}
, and let
N
be the number of questions
evaluated in parallel.
The two reward functions are:

\displaystyle r_{t}^{\text{dense}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\max_{i}\,\sigma_{i}}{T},

\displaystyle r_{t}^{\text{sparse}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\!\bigl[\sigma_{i}>\delta\,\land\,\mathcal{K}_{t}=\emptyset],

where
T
is the maximum episode length,
\delta=0.7
is the cosine similarity threshold for a successful attack, and
\mathcal{K}
is the set of refusal keywords {“I’m sorry”, “Sorry”, “I am sorry”, “I apologize”,
\dots
}.

Safeguard pipeline.

When a safeguard model is active, each environment step executes the following five-stage pipeline: (i) the helper LLM mutates template
\tau_{t}
to produce
\tau_{t+1}
; (ii) each question
q_{i}
is substituted into the placeholder of
\tau_{t+1}
to form a jailbreak prompt; (iii) the safeguard classifies each prompt at the
input
stage, replacing flagged prompts with a fixed refusal string; (iv) the target LLM generates a response for each unblocked prompt; (v) the safeguard classifies each response at the
output
stage, again replacing flagged responses with a refusal string.

Parallel processes.

At each step,
N_{\text{proc}}
questions are attacked simultaneously, each
with its own template and episode state but sharing the same policy.
This provides
N_{\text{proc}}
independent transitions per environment step,
increasing gradient signal without additional wall-clock cost beyond the
batched inference overhead.
The value of

used in all experiments is listed in
