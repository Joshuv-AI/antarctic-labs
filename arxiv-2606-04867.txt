AICompanionBench: Benchmarking LLMs-as-Judges for AI Companion Safety
Yanjing Ren, Reza Ebrahimi, Tengteng Ma

Abstract
As AI companion platforms such as Replika and Character.AI rapidly grow, concerns about unsafe human–AI interactions have intensified. This study introduces AICompanionBench, the first, to our knowledge, publicly available benchmark dataset of human–AI companion conversations annotated with fine-grained safety risk categories. The dataset contains 2,123 real‑world Replika conversations collected from Reddit, annotated through human–AI collaboration across nine categories: sexual behavior, antisocial behavior, physical aggression, verbal aggression, substance abuse, self-harm & suicide, control, manipulation, and no-harm. Using this benchmark, we evaluate 20 state-of-the-art open- and closed-source LLMs under an LLM-as-judge framework for the detection of unsafe interactions. Results show substantial variation in model performance, with stronger models achieving high overall accuracy but still struggling with nuanced categories such as manipulation, as well as benign conversations identified as false positives. Our findings suggest that while using a set of current LLMs could be effective at detecting explicit harmful content, they remain limited in identifying implicit unsafe interactions. Overall, our work contributes a new benchmark dataset for AI companionship safety research and offers insights for monitoring AI companion systems using LLMs. We made the dataset publicly accessible at https://github.com/anonymousresearcher2026/AICompanionBench/blob/main/AICompanionBench.xlsx

We make two primary contributions. We release this dataset publicly to facilitate future research in the community. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first publicly accessible labeled dataset focused specifically on human–AI companion interactions. Second, leveraging this dataset, we then systematically benchmark a suite of state‑of‑the‑art LLMs in detecting unsafe interactions in this context, revealing their strengths and limitations in safety‑critical scenarios. Our results show that the GPT family generally outperforms other models. We also find that performance improves with scale, whereas reasoning ability does not yield corresponding gains. II-B

LLMs-as-Judges in Safety Evaluation

The purpose of this step is to examine how LLMs are used to identify safety risks. In recent years, the use of LLMs-as-judges has become increasingly prevalent across a wide range of academic tasks (see

As shown in Table

Moreover, prior work typically employ only one LLM, primarily to demonstrate the effectiveness of a proposed method for enhancing model performance rather than to benchmark the reliability of LLM-based judges themselves. As a result, the robustness and consistency of LLMs-as-Judges remain largely underexamined. To address this gap, we systematically evaluate a broad range of LLMs on multi‑class unsafe‑interaction classification using real human–AI companion conversations, enabling a comprehensive assessment of their reliability in this safety‑critical context.

Yet as AI companionship becomes increasingly prevalent, safety evaluation in this domain requires dedicated benchmarks that reflect its unique interaction patterns and risks. Furthermore, most existing benchmarks rely on binary safety labels, which are insufficient for capturing the diverse and fine-grained safety concerns that arise in companion-style conversations. This gap underscores the need for a more comprehensive, multi-category benchmark tailored to LLMs-as-judges in AI companionship contexts.

In summary, our review of related work reveals two critical gaps in the current literature. First, despite the abundance of human–AI companion interactions available on public forums, there is no publicly released dataset with safety‑relevant annotations. Second, while LLMs‑as‑Judges approaches have been widely adopted to assess safety risks across various task‑oriented domains—typically relying on one or a small number of models—there remains no comprehensive benchmark that systematically evaluates both open‑ and closed‑source models. This gap is especially pronounced in the context of human–AI companion interactions, where safety concerns and evaluation criteria differ substantially from those in task-driven or general-purpose settings.

Accordingly, this paper introduces AICompanionBench, the first benchmark tailored to evaluating LLMs-as-judges in AI companionship safety. It provides a curated set of human–AI companion conversations with fine‑grained safety‑risk annotations, together with comprehensive evaluations of state‑of‑the‑art LLMs against these ground-truth labels. Our goal is to advance the development of reliable, automated methods for identifying safety risks in human–AI companion interactions.

III-A

Conversation Compilation

We collected Replika conversations from the Reddit subreddit r/replika during the period from January 2021 to January 2026. All conversations were shared in the form of images (i.e., user-posted screenshots). For each post, we gathered the title, textual content, and associated images. The textual content of the conversations was extracted using Pytesseract, an optical character recognition (OCR) tool. We also distinguished the speakers between user and AI companion (Replika) based on the horizontal position of the message bubbles: bubbles on the left side of the center line were labeled as Replika, and those on the right as the user. If an image contained only a single message bubble, it was assigned to Replika. In total, we collected 43,851 conversation snippets from 7,074 unique Reddit users.

III-B

Conversation Annotation

Given the dataset’s scale and the eight categories of unsafe interactions under analysis, we adopted a strategy combining LLM‑based data screening with human-AI collaboration in annotation.

•

LLM‑based Data Screening. The purpose of this step is to identify those potentially unsafe conversations. To efficiently process the large volume dataset, we employed a funnel‑style filtering process. Following role‑playing techniques and one‑shot learning
[
9
]
, we used six LLMs from the Llama and Qwen families to classify each conversation into nine categories: eight unsafe categories and one safe category (no-harm).

We first applied three models—Llama3‑8B, Llama3‑70B, and Qwen2.5‑14B—to all conversations. Conversations flagged as unsafe by at least two of these models (8,666 of 43,851) were then evaluated by three additional models: Qwen3‑4B‑thinking, Qwen3‑8B, and Qwen3‑14B. In total, 8,666 conversations were assessed by all six models. We selected the 1,595 conversations labeled unsafe by four models for manual review, as these exhibited higher uncertainty. To further expand coverage, we additionally sampled 700 conversations flagged unsafe by at least five models, resulting in 2,295 conversations for human inspection.

•

Human-AI Collaboration in Annotation. The first author manually reviewed all 2,295 conversations to remove noise and ensure dataset quality. After cleaning, a total of 2,123 conversations were retained and annotated by both a human annotator and additional LLMs, constituting the final AICompanionBench dataset.

For human annotation, a trained annotator (a native English speaker over age 21) independently reviewed each conversation and assigned the most probable category among the nine, producing the initial human labels. For machine annotation, building on the earlier LLM‑based filtering stage, all 2,123 conversations have been evaluated by six open‑source models from the Llama and Qwen families. We then expanded the evaluation to include 12 additional state‑of‑the‑art models from four closed‑source families—GPT, Mistral, DeepSeek, and Claude—along with two additional 30B models from Qwen3. Using the same prompt in previous step, we varied only the underlying model to examine differences in safety judgments. Each conversation was therefore evaluated by 20 LLMs. For every conversation, we extracted the top‑one predicted category (or categories) as the machine‑generated prediction, as shown in (

III-C

Benchmark Evaluation

AICompanionBench serves as a standardized benchmark for evaluating LLMs’ safety‑judging capabilities. The finalized ground-truth labels enable systematic and comparable evaluation across different models. We compare each LLM’s prediction against the ground truth, and the results are reported in the following section.

IV

In this section, we first provide an overview of AICompanionBench’s composition and the distribution across its nine categories. We then report the performance of LLMs‑as‑Judges on AICompanionBench, assessing their ability to reliably distinguish unsafe interactions from benign ones.

AICompanionBench consists of 2,123 human–AI companion interactions, encompassing both single‑turn AI responses and multi‑turn dialogues. Table

Such a composition is particularly suitable for evaluating LLMs‑as‑Judges for two reasons. IV-B

Evaluating LLMs-as-Judges on AICompanionBench

The main purpose of this work is to assess the ability of state‑of‑the‑art LLMs to detect unsafe interactions, especially within the context of AI companionship. Till now, we’ve already applied 6 LLM families, which are 20 models in total, against the AICompanionBench. Fig.
4

GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy (86%), followed closely by GPT-5.4 and Claude-opus-4.6 (both 83%). In contrast, Llama-3-8B obtained the lowest score (26%), indicating that smaller models struggle considerably on the benchmark tasks.

The GPT family ranks as the top performer on AICompanionBench, with both GPT models and Claude-opus-4.6 achieving accuracy above 83%.

Within model families, performance generally improved with model scale. For example, the Qwen3 series increased from 53% (4B) to 76% (30B). However, the Mistral series showed marginal gains from 78% to 79% as model size increased. The Claude family also demonstrated consistently strong performance, ranging from 78% to 83%. Similarly, DeepSeek-v4flash variants achieved competitive results (82%), approaching the performance of leading proprietary models. However, the thinking-enhanced variants (e.g., Qwen3-30B-thinking, DeepSeek-v4por-thinking, and DeepSeek-v4flash-thinking) did not consistently outperform their corresponding base models, suggesting that additional reasoning enhancement may not benefit AICompanionBench tasks.

Key Finding 2: Scaling Helps, Reasoning Doesn’t.

Model performance improves with model scale, while additional reasoning enhancement may not benefit AICompanionBench tasks.

The evaluation involves nine categories of unsafe interactions. Table

For instance, Claude‑sonnet-4.6 demonstrates the strongest capability in detecting sexual behavior (98% in precision), DeepSeek‑v4pro-NTK (i.e., DeepSeek‑v4pro model with its reasoning module disabled) achieves the highest precision in substance abuse (99%), while Mistral‑medium-3 performs perfectly in the no‑harm category (100%). Meanwhile, GPT-5.4 shows consistent performance across multiple categories, ranking highest in both antisocial behavior and verbal aggression (92%). We also observe that although Qwen3‑8B is the best model for manipulation, its precision is only 75%.

The results also show that aggressive behaviors posed moderate challenges. Specifically, verbal aggression and physical aggression had 15 and 14 underperforming models, respectively, while control and antisocial behavior had 11 and 9 models below the threshold. Overall, the findings suggest that categories involving subtle intent, psychological influence, or contextual ambiguity—such as manipulation and aggressive behaviors—remain more difficult for current models to identify reliably compared to more explicit harmful behaviors.

Key Finding 3: Weak Performance on Implicit Cases.

Models struggle most with categories involving implicit intent or contextual ambiguity—such as manipulation and physical or verbal aggression—resulting in consistently low precision across many models.

Interestingly, the no-harm category also exhibited substantial classification difficulty, with 16 out of 20 models achieving precision scores below 80%. To further investigate this issue, Table

This finding indicates that some models adopt overly conservative detection strategies, prioritizing risk avoidance at the expense of classification accuracy for non-harmful content.

Many models exhibit strong over‑identification of potential risks, particularly in the no‑harm category, leading to extremely high false positive rates and unreliable detection of benign conversations.

In summary, the results reveal a major limitation of using LLM‑based judges to identify unsafe interactions in human–AI companion conversations. While modern LLMs are sensitive to harmful cues, they still struggle to reliably identify benign, neutral, or context‑dependent interactions. Their difficulties with implicit categories, along with over‑identification of harmless content, can undermine overall classification reliability and negatively affect real‑world deployment scenarios where balanced and accurate judgment is essential.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a novel evaluation framework for objectively assessing LLM-based judges in the context of AI companionship. Building on this framework, we present AICompanionBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate how effectively LLMs-as-Judges can automatically detect unsafe interactions in human–AI companion conversations. Our study addresses the growing need to rigorously measure LLM-as-Judge performance as the AI companion market rapidly expands. We hope that this framework and benchmark will offer meaningful insights for improving the detection of safety‑related risks in future LLM applications and contribute to mitigating emerging risks in AI companionship.
