 ABSTRACT
 Triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS)-based structures show marked potential in novel nuclear
reactor fuel designs, as their high surface area-to-volume ratio increases the efficiency of heat transfer out
of the fuel, enabling safer, more innovative reactor designs. This milestone report addresses the role of
dUO2 powder processing route on the fill behavior of TPMS-based cladding shells to understand and
advance the feasibility of manufacturing TPMS-based nuclear fuel forms.
 dUO2 powder was processed through either a dry granulation route, varying consolidation pressure, or
through milling, varying milling time, milling method and milled size distribution. The lowest tapped bulk
densities (TBD), but best powder flowabilities, were obtained when testing unprocessed dUO 2 powder
which was prone to self-agglomeration and formed low-density spheroids. The highest TBD and lowest
flowabilities were obtained when using powder produced by hammer-milling dUO2 powder to pass through
a 200-mesh sieve, which led to particles with angular morphologies. Powder produced by dry granulation
exhibited TBD that varied according to the consolidation pressure used to form the initial pellets and
exhibited improved flowabilities when compared to hammer-milled material. Because of the large span of
granule sizes formed as well as the irregular shape associated with the granules, a packing fraction of 0.69
was achieved, exceeding the analytical solution for random close packing of mono-sized spheres.
 TPMS polymer shells were loaded with unprocessed, granulated, and hammer-milled dUO2 powders,
and their qualitative packing behaviors were analyzed using x-ray computed tomography (xCT). TBDs
calculated after loading TPMS polymer shells were 10-20% lower when compared to tapped bulk density
measurements taken in a glass graduated cylinder, indicating a non-trivial impact on the tapped bulk density
of either the TPMS channel size, TPMS channel surface material, powder cohesiveness, or a combination
of the two parameters. A metallic zircaloy-4 TPMS shell will be loaded with hammer-milled dUO 2 powder
upon receipt of the shell from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and shipped to Idaho National Labs
(INL) for subsequent hot isostatic pressing (HIP) densification experiments.

 1. Introduction

 1.1 Manufacturing of Triply Periodic Minimal Surfaces for Nuclear
 Energy Applications
 The advent of advanced additive manufacturing techniques has led to rapid potential for innovation in
a variety of disciplines by enabling the manufacturing of parts whose geometries are not conducive to
typical subtractive manufacturing processes. An example of this geometry is known as a triply periodic
minimal surface (TPMS) lattice. In this design, sinusoidal-type surfaces that repeat in three dimensions are
intertwined, creating an interconnected bi-phasic network. An example TPMS structure, the diamond
TPMS, was discovered in 1970 by Alan Schoen and is described by the level-set equation below:

 C = sin(𝑥) sin(𝑦) sin(𝑧) + sin(𝑥) cos(𝑦) cos(𝑧) + cos(𝑥) sin(𝑦) cos(𝑧) + cos(𝑥) cos(𝑦) sin(𝑧) (1)

where x, y and z are respective cartesian coordinates and c is a constant value that defines the volumes of
the phases created by the TPMS surface. By tuning c, different phase volumes can be attained; in the case
of c = 0 for a diamond lattice, two equivalent volumes are generated. 1, 2
 TPMS-type structures show marked potential in modernized light water reactor (LWR) designs, as their
high surface area and interconnectivity confer unique improvements in properties driven by surface area-
to-volume (S/V) ratio, such as heat transfer coefficients, when compared to standard cylindrical
geometries.3, 4 Idaho National Labs (INL) has investigated the performance of TPMS-based structures for
nuclear reactor applications previously.4-7 Thermohydraulic analyses of heat transfer in these systems
showed improvements in fuel S/V’s by two orders of magnitude in some cases when compared to
cylindrical geometries.4 This has the benefit of reducing centerline fuel temperatures, extending fuel
lifetime, and potentially improving reactor safety in loss-of-cooling accident (LOCA) scenarios.
Interestingly, nuclear fuel power densities can also potentially be improved substantially from rod-type
assembly geometries, as there is a large improvement in fuel temperature control. 4, 5
 An outstanding issue surrounding this innovative fuel form is its manufacturing/assembly, as well as
applicability to different types of reactors. Implementation of a TPMS geometry for a LWR can be
envisioned with two manufacturing routes. One manufacturing route would use different fuel types
(uranium dioxide, TRISO, metallic fuels) loaded into an additively manufactured cladding shell that is
subsequently densified, while the other route would involves additively manufacturing the fuel into a dense
TPMS geometry and coating the fuel form with cladding. In this assembly style for LWRs using uranium

 1

dioxide, additively manufacturing the cladding and loading the fuel in the form of a powder that is co-
consolidated via hot isostatic pressing (HIP) or pressureless/conventional sintering is a less complex process
compared to coating the fuel form, although significant knowledge gaps exist in the role of powder
processing on the fill dynamics of uranium oxide powders in TPMS cladding shells.
 The goal of this milestone report is to evaluate the role that powder processing routes play on the
maximum fill density, flowability and flow rate of dUO2 powder, and additionally to report on the status of
deliverables associated with this project, including preparation of powder with maximized tapped bulk
densities (TBD) and subsequent loading of the powder into metallic TPMS shells for use in future HIP
experiments at INL.

 1.2 Influence of Powders on Manufacturing Processes
 Control of the fill dynamics of the uranium oxide powders within the cladding must be understood in
order to demonstrate process feasibility of a hot isostatically pressed cladding loaded with powder, as any
non-uniformities present in the initially filled cladding will lead to non-uniform shrinkage, resulting in
warped TPMS cooling channels that hinder heat transfer and possibly destroying the cladding shell if non-
uniform shrinkage stresses are too large.8
 Die fill uniformity is paramount in industrial ceramic manufacturing. Because ceramic powders that
are pressurelessly sinterable are typically submicron and possess high surface area, interparticle frictional
forces are significant and result in poor flowability into dies and channels. This leads to non-uniform
distributions of powder within the die, which when consolidated, can be a contributing factor to gross
defects in both green and sintered ceramic articles, such as gross porosity and cracking caused by
nonuniform shrinkage within the part. A common industrial technique to produce powders with improved
flowability is called granulation.8, 9
 Granulation is a method of converting poorly flowable, cohesive powders to easily flowable, non-
cohesive powders, and is particularly common in ceramic manufacturing to produce ready-to-press
powders.10 Industrial granulation is often performed as a wet process, where a solvent vehicle (either
organic or aqueous) is used to disperse the ceramic powder alongside any sintering aids and organic
additives required to achieve the desired compaction and sintering responses. Dispersed slurries are then
granulated, typically using a spray dryer, which atomizes the slurry into a fine mist of droplets that rapidly
dry in a hot gas stream and are separated cyclonically to produce nominally spherical granules that contain
a uniform distribution of ceramic particles, sintering aids and organic additives.11 The reduced surface area
of granules combined with the improved spherical morphology reduces interparticle frictional forces,

 2

resulting in improved die fill uniformity and significant reductions in green density gradients. 12 This in turn
minimizes dimensional tolerance issues and distortions in the final sintered piece.13
 Alternative to wet granulation routes, dry granulation routes exist and are common in technological
applications where the introduction of either solvent or moisture can lead to degradation of materials in the
formulation.14 Typically, dry granulation methods are common in the pharmaceutical industry, since active
pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) can exhibit both moisture and heat sensitivity. One method of dry
granulation is through a “slugging” process, where the API (in this case, ceramic) and excipients (sintering
aids and organic additives) are homogeneously blended, compacted into a crushable tablet or pellet of
material that is milled and classified to a specific size distribution.15 The size-classified granules exhibit
improved flowability and better uniformity of die fill when compared to non-granulated powders. Because
uranium dioxide exhibits moisture and oxygen sensitivity and radioactive liquid waste streams are relatively
complex to manage, dry granulation methods are suitable to produce uranium oxide powders with improved
flowability.

2.1.1 High Energy Ball Milling

 High-energy ball milling (HEBM) was performed in a SPEX mill using a zirconia jar and one ½”
zirconia milling media. 10 g powder charges were loaded into the jar alongside media, sealed with electrical
tape and milled for the allotted milling time in 15 minute increments, with 5 minutes of downtime between
each increment to minimize heat generation during milling. After milling, powder was removed from the
jar without screening for subsequent studies.

2.1.2 Dry Granulation

 Dry granulation of UO2 powders was accomplished by loading 1.8 g charges of powder into an 11 mm
punch and die set and consolidating (“slugging”) for 1 minute at the pressures listed in Table 1. Each dry
granulation batch consisted of 6 pellets. Pellets were removed from the die and green measurements were
taken to determine green densities. After measurement, pellets were placed into a 30 mL Nalgene bottle,

 4

shaken for 20 seconds and passed through a US Series 45-mesh sieve screen. Sieve stack distributions were
measured by passing the powders through the following US Series sieve stack: 12, 45, 100, 170, 200, 325.
For one powder set, both high energy ball milling and dry granulation were performed by milling 10 g of
powder for 30 minutes following the procedure in Section 2.1.1, followed by dry granulation of the milled
powder at 250 MPa and subsequent screening through a US Series 45-mesh sieve screen.

2.1.3 Hammer-Milling

 To produce high-density powder through hammer-milling, 10 g of UO2 was batched with a 0.5wt%
super-addition of ethylene bis-stearamide (EBS). The batched powder was co-milled following the
procedure outlined in Section 2.1.1. After milling, the powder was passed through a US Series 200-mesh
sieve screen using a mortar and pestle. Pellets ranging from 2-5 g were consolidated at 200 MPa in an 11
mm punch and die set for 3 minutes and loaded on an Al2O3 tray into a Carbolite Al2O3 tube furnace with
Kanthal Superelements rated for continuous operation at 1800°C. Pellets were sintered according to the
thermal profile shown in Figure 1 under 500 sccm gas flow of Ar-6%H2 without water vapor additions.
Furnace loadings of 60g of UO2 were used in each batch firing. Geometric densities were calculated from
dimensional measurements taken using a Mitutoyo drop micrometer. After sintering, pellets were placed in
a Plattner’s mortar and pestle (hammer-mill) and comminuted to pass through a US Series 200-mesh sieve
screen. Some of the powder produced by hammer-milling and screening through a 200-mesh sieve was dry-
blended with Raw UO2 in ratios of 67 wt% Unprocessed UO2/33 wt% hammer-milled UO2, and 33 wt%
Unprocessed UO2/67 wt% hammer-milled UO2. The hammer-milled powder used for loading into the final
TPMS shell was produced by hammer-milling sintered dUO2 pellets, passing them through a 45-mesh sieve,
separating the coarser particles with a 200-mesh sieve, then HEBMing the coarser particles in 10g charges
in two five-minute milling segments. After each 5-minute milling step, powder was screened through a
200-mesh sieve to remove fines, and the +200-mesh particles were reloaded and milled until <0.5g of
material remained.
 For pellets sintered to 71% TD for investigation of packing fractions, the sintering profile in Figure 1
was modified to perform an isothermal hold at 1450°C rather than 1760°C under the same conditions of
gas flow, ramp rate and hold duration.

 2.2 Materials Testing and Characterization
2.2.1 Powder Characterization

 Powder size distributions were collected by passing powders through US Series sieve stacks (12, 45,
100, 140, 200, 325) and for select powders, an Anton Parr PSA 1190 laser diffraction unit was used. Laser
diffraction measurements were performed on unprocessed dUO2 powder, powder that had been HEBM’ed
for 30 minutes, powder granulated at 250 MPa and screened through a 45-mesh sieve, and powder produced
by hammer-milling sintered pellets with >93% theoretical density (TD) with respect to UO 2. Scans were
performed on powders suspended in either Isoton II, or in water using Triton X-100 surfactant. All
suspensions were ultrasonicated for 1 minute before testing, except for the granulated powders, as
preliminary process development resulted in destruction of the granule structure through the ultrasonication
process.
 Powder bulk density (PBD) and tapped bulk density (TBD) for each powder were measured using an
AutoTap tap density tester following a procedure modified from ASTM C1770-21. Masses of powder
ranging from 4-6 g were slowly poured into a 5 mL graduated cylinder. Initial volume measurements were
taken and used to calculate PBDs. The graduated cylinder was carefully loaded on the AutoTap and tapped
at a frequency of 260 taps/min, with a tap distance of 1/8”. Interim volume measurements were taken by
pausing the instrument after 10, 100, 1000, 2500 and 5000 taps, with the final TBD being calculated after
10,000 taps. Both Hausner Ratio (HR) and Carr Index (CI) were calculated from PBD and TBD results
using the equations shown below:

2.2.2 TPMS Shell Manufacturing and Powder Loading

 TPMS polymer shells were 3D printed using Loctite 3843 resin and an Origin One Digital Light
Processing resin printer. The resin bath was de-aired with a roughing pump before printing to minimize the
inclusion of bubbles in the print. Prints were leak-checked by filling with water, and any observed pinholes
were infilled with a UV-curable resin. To load and tap the powder into the polymer TPMS shell, a 7/8”-14
TPI threaded adapter for a glass funnel was designed and 3D printed, as well as a custom holder to secure
the shell to the AutoTap. Powder was measured and poured into the assembly while simultaneously tapping
to encourage powder flow through the funnel (Figure 2). Powder was added until the central baffle started
to fill. When the central baffle had cleared, more powder was added until there was no more visible
consolidation of powder during tapping.

 3. Results and Discussion
 The following sections describe efforts to characterize the produced powders, experiments to analyze
the TBDs and packing fractions of the powders, as well as efforts taken to fill and analyze polymer TPMS
shells loaded with powders.

 3.1 Powder Characterization
 Sieve stack distributions (SSDs) of powders produced by granulation show little impact of slugging
pressure. For Gran50, Gran150, and Gran250, approximately 50% of the powder falls between a 45-mesh
and a 100-mesh sieve (355 and 150 µm), with less than 10% of the powder passing through a 325-mesh
sieve (<45 µm). The SPEX30Gran250 powder showed a slight deviation, with slightly more -200 mesh
powder compared to the other granulated materials. The similarity of the sieve distributions regardless of
slugging pressure for the granulated material indicates that the formed granules have some amount of green
strength, despite the lack of binder emphasizing that the consolidation pressure provides sufficient green
strength for handling and transfer.

 Particle size distributions (PSDs) measured via laser diffraction are shown in Figure 5 below.
Unprocessed powder, powder HEBM’ed for 30 minutes (SPEX30) and powder that was granulated at 250
MPa (Gran250) show similar PSDs. The Unprocessed dUO2 powder (Raw) exhibits two primary peaks,
one centered at 0.3 µm and one centered at 1 µm. The SPEX30 powder shows a slight increase in intensity

of particle size reduction during the 30-minute milling cycle as evidenced by the smaller d 50 (0.433 vs.

SPEX30 and Gran250 powders were produced using the Unprocessed powder as an initial feedstock, so

there will only be slight variations in the PSDs between the three samples as a result of the destruction of

 The HMdUO2 -200 mesh powder exhibits a very broad PSD ranging from ~0.3 – 100 µm. Two primary

exhibited significant size segregation within the graduated cylinder, leaving large void spaces within the
graduated cylinder. The density of the agglomerates is unknown, but using the linear regression calculated
in Figure 8, the RD of the primary agglomerates that formed can be roughly estimated as ~36%, or 3.949
g/cc. For the unprocessed powder, the formation of these agglomerates occurred while tap testing
unprocessed powder that was de-agglomerated within the high-energy ball mill for 30 seconds. The
presence and formation of these coarse agglomerates is responsible for the notably low flowability
indicators, as the PBD is primarily controlled by interparticle frictional forces, coarser particles with
lower specific surface area tend to exhibit higher PBDs.12 A comparison of Carney flow rates and CI’s is
shown in Figure 6. The authors exercise caution in the quantitative nature of the Carney flow rates, as no
powders exhibited free flow behavior; all powders required external agitation to pass through the funnel,
as core-flow behavior and funnel blockages were observed in all powders.
 HEBM powders showed no significant difference in either PBD or TBD with time, with TBD values
reaching a maximum of ~4.4 g/cc, indicating no significant shift in the span of the PSD with respect to
milling times longer than 30 minutes. Significant improvements in TBD with respect to the unprocessed
powder are apparent and are likely the result of the increased PSD span for milled materials indicated in
Figure 5, alongside a possible reduction in the propensity for agglomeration. Slight differences in calculated
TBD’s exist as a function of milling times, although the precision of the tapped powder volume
measurement is insufficient for determination of TBD differences at this small of a magnitude. High CIs

 12

and HRs are observed for HEBM powders, likely due to the lack of self-agglomeration present, and the
intrinsically high surface area for particles in the sub-micron size range.
 Powders granulated at varying slugging pressures and passed through a 45-mesh sieve had PBDs
ranging from 1.743 to 2.659 g/cc and TBDs ranging from 2.864 to 4.439 g/cc. The largest TBD was
exhibited by powders that were high-energy ball-milled for 30 minutes and subsequently granulated at 250
MPa. No statistically significant difference is observed in the TBD measurements between this powder and
powder that was only high-energy ball milled for 30 minutes; however, differences arise in the PBD (2.659
g/cc for SPEX30Gran250 compared to 2.462 g/cc SPEX material). This, combined with Carney flow
measurements shown in Figure 7, indicate that the formation of granules of increased size (i.e., reduced
specific surface area) and sphericity for powder particles of a given size and distribution improves the bulk
flow properties of the powder; however, CIs and HRs for the granulated materials do not directly reflect
the same trends. Interestingly, the powders granulated without milling all exhibited lower TBDs,
highlighting a difference in compaction response between powders slugged at the same pressure but milled
for differing times (wider PSD span), as well as the role of slugging pressure on TBDs for granulated
materials.
 Powders prepared by sintering dUO2 to >93% TD and hammer-milling through sieves showed the
largest magnitude of both PBD and TBD. For hammer-milled powders passed through a 45-mesh sieve, the
PBD was the largest at 4.610 g/cc, whereas the hammer-milled powder passed through a 200-mesh sieve
showed a PBD of only 4.150 g/cc. TBD of -45-mesh HMdUO2 and -200-mesh HMdUO2 showed no
significant differences (6.670 vs. 6.687 g/cc); although there are no laser diffraction particle measurements
for the -45-mesh HMdUO2 powder, it is very likely that they exhibit similarly wide PSD spans, as the span
of the -200-mesh HMdUO2 powder is also a contributing factor to the high TBDs. CIs and HRs for
HMdUO2 varied dramatically with sieve stack distribution; for the coarse powder (-45 mesh), excellent
flowability was observed (HR =30.885), while for the fine powder (-200 mesh), very poor flowability was
observed (HR = 56.229).
 Blends of unprocessed powders and HM materials show predictable trends in both TBD and Carney
flow rates, with Carney flow rates decreasing with increasing -200-mesh HMdUO 2 content, and TBD
increasing with increasing -200-mesh HMdUO2 content. Powders prepared by sintering dUO2 to 71% TD
showed significantly lower TBD’s than expected, possibly related to a difference in particle size distribution
for this material, as the fracture behavior of partially sintered pellets with a significant fraction of open
porosity will be different compared to pellets sintered to have no open porosity (>90% TD).
 For powders prepared by either granulation or sintering and hammer-milling, an estimation of the
packing fraction can be determined by plotting the TBD relative to dUO 2 versus the RD of the pellets before
screening (slug density for granulated materials, or sintered pellet density for hammer-milled materials).

 13

This is shown in Figure 8, where the slope of the black dashed line indicates the estimated packing fraction
for a distribution of particles all exhibiting the same RD value. A statistically significant fit is obtained (F
= 4435). When forcing the y-intercept of the trendline to pass through the origin, slopes (packing fractions)
of 0.66 ± 0.01 or 0.67 ± 0.01 are obtained, depending on whether the 71% TD datapoint is included in the
fit. This curve likely is not truly representative of the packing behavior of the overall system, as there are
clear regions where the data is underfit and overfit, indicating a difference in absolute packing fraction
depending on powder processing route, possibly originating from differences in PSDs and SSDs.
 Isolating the granulated material TBD versus RD data (Figure 8, blue trendline) results in calculated
packing fractions of 0.69 ± 0.01, exceeding the analytical solution for random close packing of a monosized
distribution of spheres (~0.63), but falling short of the analytical solution for FCC close-packed monosized
spheres (~0.74).16, 17 The granulated particles produced in this study are generally non-spherical (Figure 4)
and exhibit a wide distribution of sizes, both having a positive impact on the TBD by virtue of improving
the maximum possible packing fraction of the system when compared to random packing of spheres. Figure
8 highlights the role that primary particle density plays in tailoring the powder fill behavior to fit desired
applications; it is possible to tailor powder fill behaviors by controlling the primary particle density for
powders with low specific surface area in wide channels where there is minimal impact of die wall and
interparticle frictional forces.

 3.2 TPMS Powder Loading and xCT
 X-ray computed tomography (xCT) radiographs of powder-loaded TPMS structures are shown in
Figure 9. For unprocessed dUO2 loaded in a thin-fuel region polymer TPMS shell, substantial void fractions
can be observed. Interestingly, these low coordination packing regions/void spaces appear to exist in the
diamond layers of the TPMS lattice near saddle points where the layer shifts from concave-up to concave-
down, resulting in a height-dependent size segregation within the lattice. Size segregation of particles within
the powder bed is extremely prominent for the unprocessed dUO2 since the powder readily self-
agglomerates, resulting in 1-2 mm beads of material that easily rise to the top of the TPMS structure through
granular convection (i.e., Brazil Nut Effect).9, 18 In cavity regions where the curvature changes (saddle
points), the coarse particles appear to be locked in place as the fine particles elutriate beneath the coarse
agglomerates and consolidate into a packed bed, confining the coarse agglomerates and preventing them
from moving further upward within the TPMS shell. xCT radiographs of the SPEX30Gran250 powder
shown in Figure 9D do not indicate any self-agglomeration or gross variations in tapped density within the
compact, unlike the Unprocessed dUO2. Interestingly, radiographs of the thin fuel TPMS loaded with
HMdUO2-200 mesh powder shown in Figure 9F and Figure 10 reveal that there are internal regions of
locally poor packing near the top corners of the TPMS fuel region. Void spaces created by particle bridging
are also observed, and in some instances, there are agglomerates present within the void region.
Additionally, there are zones near the cladding shell that exhibit low coordination packing. Some
agglomeration and size separation can also be observed at the top of the powder compact, although it is
much less extreme than for the Unprocessed dUO2 powder. These observations support conclusions that in
the absence of severe self-agglomeration, bulk powder flowability has a marked impact on the local packing
fraction and coordination of particles in polymer TPMS geometries.
 Based on the total mass of powder added to the known TPMS volume, a ~20% reduction in TBD is
calculated for the Unprocessed dUO2 powder, a 12% reduction is calculated for the SPEX30Gran250
powder and a 9% reduction in TBD is calculated for the HMdUO2-200 mesh powder, although this is likely
a lower estimate for both the SPEX30Gran250 and HMdUO2-200 mesh powders, as appreciable amounts
of powder had built up in the central baffle. These TBD reductions are indicative of the non-negligible
impact of surface roughness/friction and channel size on the packing behavior of powders in TPMS shells
at this length scale.
 xCT midplane reconstructions of powder-loaded TPMS structures shown in Figure 11 highlight the
dramatic influence of powder processing route on the fill behavior of TPMS structures. The Unprocessed
dUO2 powder shows large void spaces throughout the structure, with noticeable variation in green density
as a function of height, whereas the SPEX30Gran250 powder shows a mostly uniform distribution within

 18

the structure, regardless of height in the compact. Some size segregation of coarse particles is observed in
regions of the structure that are sufficiently thin for x-rays to penetrate; however, because the tapped density
of the system is sufficiently high, x-rays could not penetrate completely through the structure where the
cross-sectional thickness of UO2 is appreciably large. Some minor low coordination packing regions are
observed in thinner regions throughout the midplane reconstruction for the SPEX30Gran250 powder.
Buildup of powder in the central baffle was also observed for this powder, indicating that some
modifications to the powder distribution baffle on top of the structure are required for more efficient
distribution of powders to the diamond layers of the structure, and a different experimental method such as
neutron diffraction will be required for proper analysis of the internal packing structure of these systems.

 3.3 Impact of Powder Processing Route on Densification
 To understand the role of powder processing route on UO2 densification, pellets of both HMdUO2-200
mesh and HMdUO2-45 mesh were hand-mixed with 5 wt% EBS, pressed for 3 minutes at 200 MPa and
subsequently sintered at 1760°C for 8 hrs under 500 sccm flowing Ar-6%H2. Results of this densification
trial are shown in Figure 12. The Unprocessed dUO2 control pellet maintained shape uniformity and sintered
to 94% RD, with a radial shrinkage of 20%, while the HMdUO2-200 mesh pellet partially disintegrated and
the HMdUO2-45 mesh pellet completely disintegrated, indicating that particles were too coarse to
appreciably sinter (no global driving force for sintering, since particles start with a very low specific surface
area).8, 9, 19
 There are clear engineering trade-offs to consider when choosing the optimal powder for producing
TPMS-based fuel forms. Generally, coarser powders with improved flowability are desired from the
viewpoint of maximizing powder packing and tapped densities; however, particles cannot be so coarse such
that they will not sinter, nor can they be so fine that they do not flow uniformly into the TPMS shell. In
addition, tailoring both the local and global shrinkage of the powder during sintering must be considered to
minimize both density gradients within the fuel and shrinkage stresses associated with non-uniform
shrinkage of the fuel-TPMS cladding assembly, although this may be accomplished by carefully classifying
granules to specific SSDs. From the viewpoint of enhancing flowability/die-fill uniformity of the powders
while maintaining sinterability, dry granulated powders are recommended for use TPMS fuel form
applications, although the influence of pressure application during HIPing of the fuel assembly on
densification of powders processed through different means has not been investigated yet. Additionally,
questions remain around granule deformation during HIP testing, and its impact on densification.
 Unprocessed dUO2 powder has been sent to INL for HIP process development. Currently, LANL is
waiting on delivery of a zircaloy-4 metallic TPMS cladding shell printed by ORNL collaborators. This shell
will be loaded with HMdUO2-200 mesh powder, which exhibited the highest tapped densities observed in
this study. After loading, the zircaloy-4 shell will be shipped to INL for HIP testing, followed by
characterization by LANL personnel to understand the role of HIPing on densification of UO 2 in these fuel
forms, alongside any fuel-cladding chemical interactions that may arise during processing of this system,
as previous investigations across a wide range of temperatures have indicated substantial interaction
between zircaloy-4 and UO2 at elevated temperatures, including embrittlement of the cladding shell.20, 21

 4. Deliverables Status and Future Work
 LANL personnel have submitted a proposal to LANSCE titled “Elucidating the role of dUO 2 powder
processing routes on the fill dynamics of TPMS based structures” to investigate the internal structure of the
dUO2-filled TPMS shells via neutron imaging since xCT investigations have proven to be unsuccessful in
resolving internal particle packing in these systems due to the large attenuation and sample thickness
associated with the uranium phase. Discrete element modelling of the granular flow behavior of powders
using LAMMPS GRANULAR module could provide an excellent supplement to these investigations by
enabling an understanding of the local packing coordination and flow behavior for model particles in
various regions of complex TPMS-based geometries. These studies, when performed on different
combinations of TPMS fuel region sizes and shell materials with analogous particle packing experiments,
could enable a quantitative understanding of the role of interparticle and die wall frictional forces on the
random packing of UO2 in TPMS geometries and its role in the densification behavior of TPMS UO2 fuel
forms. Additional investigations into the self-agglomeration behavior of dUO2 powders could provide
valuable insights into bulk handling of dUO2, as previous literature assessing the bulk powder properties
for UO2 and PuO2 has determined that poor powder flowability intrinsic to the raw materials is only
worsened through typical powder handling processes, such as vibratory feeding.22 Finally, future work
assessing the porosity distribution in densified UO2 TPMS fuel forms would provide key insights into
process parameters required to tune densities and pore sizes for accommodation of fission gases and
swelling produced during fuel irradiation, since these fuel forms do not have any plenum present to
accommodate those products.
 Future powder processing experiments are planned to maximize packing fractions of dUO2 loaded into
TPMS shells by tailoring the SSDs of the granulated materials to optimize the infill of interstices between
packed particles. Significant work has been performed in the literature to understand the packing behaviors
of both discrete and continuous particle size distributions, examples of which include the Furnas model and
the Andreasen-Funk-Dinger-Zheng (AFDZ) equations.23, 24 For quaternary mixtures of dense, carefully
sized spheres of stainless steel, the application of the Furnas model has enabled packing fractions of 0.95
theoretical density, while certain applications of AFDZ-type continuous particle size distributions have
resulted in packing fractions exceeding 0.8. In the case of extremely high packing fraction systems,
shrinkage during sintering/HIPing can be significantly minimized, which may be required to demonstrate
the feasibility of this manufacturing route.
 LANL personnel are additionally investigating the feasibility of two alternative manufacturing
methods:

 24

 1. Additively manufacturing the UO2 fuel and applying a coating through a two-step process involving
chemical vapor deposition of a conductive strike layer, followed by molten salt electrodeposition of a
thicker metallic cladding;
 2. DC magnetron sputtering a metallic barrier coating to individual UO2 particles to prevent reaction
between the fuel and cladding, loading into a metallic cladding shell and HIPing to densify.

 Surrogate Al2O3 TPMS lattice forms manufactured by Admatech have been received from INL (Figure
13); future experiments involving chemical vapor deposition of a Zr strike layer followed by subsequent
molten salt electrodeposition to coat a thick Zr layer are being planned utilizing these surrogates.

 5. Conclusion
 This work sought to address the role of dUO2 powder processing route on the fill behavior of TPMS
shells so that a metallic TPMS shell could be loaded with dUO2 packed to the highest achievable density
for future HIP testing by INL collaborators in support of the INFLUX project. Key conclusions are listed
below:
  Unprocessed UO2 powders exhibited self-agglomeration to form 1-2 mm spheroids that hindered
 the maximum achievable TBD.
  Powder flow was primarily driven by size distribution, with the coarsest powders exhibiting the
 best flow, although all powders exhibited non-free flowing behavior.
  Tunable TBD responses were achieved by controlling the density of the primary particles used to
 fill the volume; higher slugging pressures for the granulated material resulted in improved
 flowability and TBD compared to unprocessed materials, while the highest TBDs were achieved
 with particles prepared by pressurelessly/conventionally sintering UO2 pellets to >93% TD and
 hammer-milling to the desired distribution.
  Packing fractions of 0.69 can be achieved using granulated powders, owing to the improved width
 of the SSD and improved flowability.
  Powder flowability appeared key to controlling defects in powder-loaded TPMS shells investigated
 by xCT: the poorly-flowing HM powders bridged and formed internal voids, whereas the
 granulated powders showed minor size segregation and excellent uniformity.
  An impact of the TPMS channel surface roughness and channel size on the TBD was observed,
 although detailed experimental investigations are still required to quantify trends.
 From these experiments, profound control over the packing behavior of dUO2 powders can be
accomplished, expanding the application space and processing routes that are feasible for TPMS-based UO 2
fuel systems. In addition, dry granulation routes are suitable methods for improving the packing fraction of
dUO2 in these systems and may provide a convenient lever to control material responses in the HIP, such
as linear and volumetric shrinkage, although many questions remain regarding the deformation that may
occur while HIP testing these materials. Loading of HMdUO2-200 powder into a metallic TPMS shell will
be completed upon receipt of the shell from ORNL and subsequently sent to INL for HIP testing, as well
as preliminary experiments evaluating the feasibility of applying cladding shells as a coating onto UO 2 that
is additively manufactured to a TPMS geometry. Future powder processing experiments optimizing
packing fractions through tailored particle/granule size distributions are planned.
