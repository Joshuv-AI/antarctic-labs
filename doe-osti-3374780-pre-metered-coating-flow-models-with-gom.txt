ABSTRACT
Tutorials for modeling of slot-die and slide-die coating flows with Goma 7, an open source finite
element code, are presented. The tutorials cover the workflow to attaining steady state solutions
for these flows, and continuation strategies for navigating the operating windows. Advanced topics
of coating window prediction, automated multiparameter continuation, non-Newtonian rheology,
dynamic contact line modeling, and some more solution strategies are also covered.

1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

This workflow document captures procedures and knowledge base developed under the Hydrogen
Fuel Cell Technology Office consortium on roll-to-roll coating. As such, its examples are aimed at
inks and materials that are common in the fuel cell and related energy materials and device (e.g.
batteries) industries. This document also serves as a general guide to the development of models
for all pre-metered coating flow processes.
Goma tutorials have been used for decades, with the originals dating back to the late 1990s. As Goma
was originally developed with coating flow process modeling as a primary application, some of the
early tutorials addressed workflows for coating-head deposition models of classical pre-metered
coating flows, like slot-die coating (GT-002) and slide-die coating (GT-011). Additionally, tutorials
that addressed advanced capabilities, using pre-metered coating flow models as examples, were
also put forth: GT-006 addressed remeshing and remapping and GT-010 addressed optimization for
slot-die coating die lip geometry. While these tutorials for the most part are still useful in the year
2025, significant improvements in Goma solvers, free-surface boundary tracking techniques, and
other sundry boundary condition and solver capabilities have greatly enhanced Goma’s usefulness
in modeling such flows. We are recommending to users to use these early tutorials as high-level
workflow guidance to running such simulations, but not as exact step-by-step documents because
the input script syntax has changed and backward compatibility is not guaranteed.
From 2020-2025, significant improvement in Goma solvers and advanced capabilities has made
modeling of coating flows much more robust and expedient. This workflow document will emphasize
many of these new capabilities.
This tutorial specifically addresses slot-die and slide-die coating flow configurations, as they are
the most common pre-metered coating flow modalities. Pre-metered coating deposition processes
share the common mass-balance characteristic that the coat weight h (or film thickness) is precisely
determined by a specified volumetric flow rate of liquid per unit coating width, 𝑄, and the relative
speed of the substrate and coating die-head, 𝑈𝑤 , during steady state operations, Viz. ℎ = 𝑄/𝑈𝑤 .
Often, 𝑈𝑤 refers to a web-conveyance speed in common roll-to-roll operations. So-called extrusion
coating and curtain coating are related pre-metered coating flow modalities because they are both
logical physical extensions of slide-die and slot-die coating that can be achieved with the same die
design. However, we do not explicitly address these configurations in this document. Spray coating
is technically a form of pre-metered coating but clearly represents a different coating flow family.
Spray coating is not addressed in this tutorial.
Slot-die and slide-die coating processes, depicted in Figure 1-1, are often put in a class of incom-
pressible flow known as capillary hydrodynamics. Specifically, the underpinning fluid mechanics
are dominated by a balance of viscous, inertial and capillary forces, with capillarity manifesting
through liquid-gas surfaces, three-phase contact lines (solid/liquid/gas), and other extraordinary

forces that manifest as long-range molecular forces in thin films and interfaces. Even for simple
Newtonian liquids, the governing differential equations are nonlinear, mainly due to presence of
capillary forces. These forces are critical to the location and behavior of the liquid/solid contact
lines and the overall menisci shapes. Basically, that means that the domain geometry over which
the flow is being computed is itself unknown a priori and highly coupled to the flow. Pre-metered
coating flows are successful when they are steady in time, producing a continuous film that is
uniform in thickness down and across web. That is, successful continuous liquid film coating is
two-dimensional in nature, except near the edges and at the leading and trailing portions of the film
after startup and shutdown of the operation.

The underpinning physics of continuous liquid-film coating are central to the suitability of Goma’s
highly implicit, Newton Raphson-based solver strategy which was designed for modeling such
processes with efficiency and accuracy. The processes are governed by the Navier-Stokes equations
coupled with free-and-moving boundary geometry equations and additional required boundary
conditions. We chose to solve the equations as a fully coupled set (including those which govern
the geometry and mesh) direct to steady state solutions. Such steady-state solutions usually exist
only within a narrow set of parameters and conditions, or they may not exist at all as the parameters
are outside of the so-called coating “window”, as we cover in the next section. Hence, the user
must supply initial guesses to the solution that are near-enough to enable convergence and within
that operating window. The strategy for doing this involves the solution to a sequence conditions,
starting with a nearby case that assumes a geometry and solves for a steady internal flow. This
tutorial addresses additional complications to this strategy that include non-Newtonian rheology
and constraint conditions, deploying Goma’s advanced capabilities.

It’s fair to mention that alternative strategies exist for problems in capillary hydrodynamics that
are considerably easier to set up and run. For example, Goma 7 has explicit boundary tracking
techniques that allow for the free surfaces to travel through a fixed mesh. While we will not cover this
approach in this document, the user is more than welcome to explore these techniques as a perhaps
more suitable approach (cf. GT-20 and GT-26). Commercial codes like FLOW3D and COMSOL
deploy explicit free-volume boundary tracking in a similar fashion. While these software packages
have been demonstrated to provide a turn-key approach to this class of problems, the compute

power required and patience in time, especially for low Capillary numbers (when surface tension is
very dominant), is sometimes prohibitive. We feel the modeling and simulation approach described
in this workflow document is superior for coating process design and scale-up.

Beyond the central topic of providing solution strategies and workflows to slot-die and slide-die
coating, this tutorial addresses advanced concepts and capabilities, including coating window
prediction, non-Newtonian rheology (Carreau and thixotropic fluids), remeshing and remapping,
automated continuation, augmenting conditions, multi-layer flow, and more. This tutorial assumes
the user has gone through the beginner’s training tutorial (GT-001) and is familiar with meshing
tools (CUBIT) and the basic topic flow of incompressible fluids.

This document is organized as follows. First we discuss the underpinnings of the coating “operability
window” and describe the specific attributes as they pertain to slide-die and slot-die coating. We
will then discuss the details of the geometry models that are used to generate initial finite element
meshes. We then tackle in succession slot coating and slide coating case studies. Specifically we
will address the workflow to attaining steady state solutions, and subsequent continuation strategies
for navigating the operating space (within the coating “operability” window). Finally we will
cover advanced topics of coating window prediction, automated multiparameter continuation, non-
Newtonian rheology, dynamic contact line modeling, and some more solution strategies. These
sections will continually refer to Goma 7 input template files that can be provided on request.

1.1. Coating operability windows

Any set of process parameters that yield a successful film coating, free of defects, lie within what is
known as the operating window, or coating window. Parameters that fall outside the “boundaries”
of this window will lead to failed coatings. For pre-metered coating, such parameters include the
material properties, parameters that define the die geometry and coating die/substrate configuration,
and the process operating conditions. Conventionally, most of these remain fixed when heading
into a set of coating trials, viz. coating experimental studies aimed at finding stable, successful
operating states. For pre-metered coating, the parameters at an operator’s disposal for adjustment
are typically web speed (or line speed), inlet flow rate of coating liquid, back pressure (if vacuum is
applied to the upstream meniscus) and coating gap. These parameters define regions of successful
coating, i.e. one free of discontinuities in film thickness.

Even though we focus on the parameters that are available to the operator in this workflow document,
the user is free to examine the effects of geometric parameters like die-lip shape, die-substrate
configuration (orientation), slot width, and broader thermophysical parameters of the coating liquid
(rheology, surface tension, etc.) on coating operability. As is common in engineering practice,
however, we often deploy dimensionless parameters that combine operating conditions and material
properties in a way that parameterizes the process in terms of a competition of forces (like viscous
and capillary) that are highly relevant. The Capillary Number is one such example, as it embodies
both viscosity and surface tension, but it is really a convenient way to examine the effects of
substrate speed.

 15

Coating window boundaries in pre-metered operations are often demarcations that define the so-
called low-flow limit, minimum film thickness achievable, and vacuum limits. These are important
operational limits that address often the economics and feasibility of a process. As discussed
by Carvalho and Kesghi [1], the low-flow limit is highly relevant to low-viscosity liquids as it
defines the minimum film thickness achievable at a given coating speed or the maximum speed
that can be achieved at a given coating thickness. So-called “air entrainment” defects are typically
a manifestation of a low-vacuum limit at higher speeds, at which point the dynamic contact line
becomes unable to advance at the expense of invading air entrained by the substrate. The “weeping”
limit is also known as the high vacuum limit, as it leads to leakage of coating liquid out of the
upstream mensiscus. These and other instabilities are depicted in the following subsections.

1.2. Slot coating flow

For slot-die coating, commonly-used parameterizations were suggested by Sartor (1990) [8], one
of which is illustrated in Figure 1-2. This operability window is expressed in terms of capillary,
𝐶𝑎 = 𝜇𝑈𝑤 /𝜎( 𝜇 is the viscosity, 𝑈𝑤 the web speed, and 𝜎 the surface tension) and the dimensionless
gap 𝐻 = 𝑔/ℎ with all other parameters including vacuum pressure being constant (𝑔 is the distance
between the coating substrate and die lips and ℎ is the target final film thickness downstream). The
operating window boundaries are set by the onset of hydrodynamic instabilities that lead to film
defects such as rivulets that are cross-web nonuniformities of alternating wet and dry regions.

The lower bound of the operating window is termed the low-flow limit: that is, the minimum film
(defect free) thickness that can be deposited at a given web speed (Carvalho and Kesghi, 2000 [1]).
Below this limit, instabilities that lead to coating defects dominate (e.g., air-entrainment, rivulets,
etc.). Currently, the low-flow limit has two known regions, as shown in Figure 1-2.

What became clear in earlier analysis of the fluid mechanics of slot-die coating was that the pressure-
drop across the bead (from the upstream to the downstream meniscus) is critical in determining bead
stability, and hence an important factor on determining the limits of operability. A “stable” bead fills
the gap between the slot-die lips and substrate and remains steady in time. The parameterization
shown in Figure 1-1a is typically applied to cases without applied vacuum pressure at the upstream
meniscus. This operating mode is common for sheet-to-sheet modalities of slot coating, coating
over non-planar substrates, and in cases of very low capillary number (i.e. dominant surface tension).
However, as viscosity and speed rise, a common tactic is to stabilize the bead using applied vacuum
at the upstream meniscus. Together with the vacuum, the pressure drop is governed by the capillary
pressure of the upstream and downstream menisci and the pressure drop of rectilinear flow across
the bead. Viscocapillary models have made clear how the pressure differential can be used to
construct analytical representations of this operability window (see Higgins and Scriven 1980 [3]).
It has also becomes clear that the applied pressure (vacuum) at the upstream meniscus becomes
an important operating parameter and usurps dimensionless film thickness (shown in figure 2) as
the most important parameter beyond the capillary number. While the dimensionless thickness is
still important and will affect operability, it is typically not depicted in a 2-dimensional operating
window in vacuum cases (see figure 3). Instead the effect of t (or h in our case) on operability

1.3. Slide Coating Flow

Slide coating flow, single or multilayer, is highly sensitive to the coating bead stability. Like slot
coating flow, the bead is the liquid bridge that spans the slide die and the moving substrate. In
slot coating, that liquid bridge’s ability to remain in the gap between the die lips and substrate is
critical. Likewise in slide coating, but the breakdown modes can be different. The breakdown
of the slide-coating bead, viz. outright breakage with coating liquid invading the space into the
vacuum box, or ribbing/rivulet flow bridging the gap, leads to discontinuous coating of a variety of
forms, including patch-wise bands of dry/wet, rivulets, ribs, etc. The bead stability depends most
heavily on the applied vacuum, applied flow-rate per unit width, and webspeed, much in the same
way as slot-die coating.

Tjiptowidjojo and Carvalho (2011) [9] propose a theoretical coating window as a function of
coating thickness, web speed, and applied vacuum. Simplifications can be made to the window
parameterizations at lower 𝐶𝑎 = 𝜇𝑈𝑤 /𝜎 (typically much less than 1). In this range, Tjiptowidjojo
et al. (2022) [10] show one of the more common coating window parameterizations is expressed
in terms of applied vacuum vs coating speed. The limits of the window delineate maximum and
minimum vacuum pressure needed underneath the bottom meniscus to maintain defect-free coating.
Too high of vacuum results in leakage to the vacuum box. Too little of vacuum results in coating

 17

Figure 1-3. Coating window predictions (solid lines) and experimental observations (magenta symbols) at
 three different concentrations (or three measured shear-thinning rheologies) taken from Creel et al. [2]

bead invasion of the bottom meniscus to the bead which leads to formation of alternating dry and
wet lanes of coating, commonly referred as rivulets. Other studies by these authors show that those
limits can be correlated to location of the static contact line on the die face, much like in slot-die
coating. At high vacuum limit, the line is located on the bottom edge, and at low vacuum limits, at
the upper edge. These high and low vacuum limits values can be solved simultaneously with the
flow field by adding the applied vacuum pressure on bottom meniscus, Pvac, as an unknown and
an equation or constraint that forces the static contact line to be located in either bottom edge of
die lip, which corresponds to high vacuum limit or top edge for low vacuum limit. One example of
vacuum limit window of the three-layer configuration shown in Figure 1-4.

2. DESIGN-TO-ANALYSIS – SLOT COATING GEOMETRY AND
 PARAMETERIZATION

In this section we cover the chosen parameterizations for slot-die coating as the workflow that
generates the finite element meshing of the initial geometry. The parameters include both geometric
and thermophysical liquid properties, like density and viscosity. While the initial geometry for
slot-die coating is largely dependent on the initial liquid properties, this is not the case for slide
coating. Specifically, slide coating flows involve gravity-driven flow down an inclined surface. The
thickness of the liquid film flowing down that surface is highly dependent on the viscosity of the
liquid, and hence so is the initial geometry required to obtain a converged solution.

2.1. Slot Coating

The initial free surface shapes and the transition between two flat die faces across which a contact
line can locate are approximated in the initial domain/mesh. The final shapes are determined by
mesh distinguishing boundary conditions during the start-up sequence. We will discuss this more
in the following section. While there is no such thing as an infinitely sharp corner between two flat
edges, precision machining can achieve vanishingly small curvatures that appear as sharp corners
to the naked eye. As we mentioned earlier, moving contact lines between the die face and die
lands, or along the die lands can only be accommodated by mesh motion. Meshes do not move
around sharp corners without numerical difficulty. As one significant mitigation to this problem,
we “round” the relevant corners significantly, with curvatures of the order of 5 to 10% of the coating
gap. This can be accomplished by solving for a new solid-surface shape (corner fillet shapes) and
moving the mesh to conform to that shape, or by generating it through Cubit’s geometry generation
capability. In this work we start with “sharp” corners generated in CUBIT and we round corners
using the fillet capability in Goma. We discuss these steps later when we discuss the use of the
DOUBLE_FILLET boundary condition.

2.1.3. Slot coating problem setup

As in all modeling and simulation endeavors, the goal is to “simulate” or “predict” the relevant
physical aspects of the target process. In this case we aim to predict the capillary hydrodynamics
in a slot-die coating bead in the film deposition zone by solving the relevant differential equations.
Many different sources can be consulted for a more detailed description of those equations. We
recommend the works of Tjipdowidjojo, Carvalho and others and their associated publications
found in the reference section of this document. In words, we are solving the Navier Stokes
system of equations for momentum transport of incompressible flow coupled with a mesh-motion
equations that are actually the equations of nonlinear elasticity with special boundary-conforming
conditions. Both of these systems are solved in two dimensions for this case study, over a geometry
or domain pictured in Figure 2-1. We are also solving the steady version of these equations (sans
time dependent/mass terms) to achieve steady-state solutions. We pursue some transient solutions
later in this document when we study time-dependent rheology. We chose to simulate the flow states
that define the stable operating states for solid-state battery electrolyte coatings described in a paper
by Wiegandt et al. [12]. The system configuration for the experiments in that paper started with a
nTact nRad slot-coating tool that is in sheet-to-sheet configuration (https://ntact.com/products/nrad-
systems/). That is, the slot die itself travels on a gantry arm across a solid substrate. One aspect
of these sheet-to-sheet systems is that they are designed for slower speeds (less than 10 cm/s)
and precision gaps (less than 100 microns) over areas as large as a square meter. They are also
operated without a vacuum box on the upstream die face, which means that backpressure shown in
Figure 2-1 is always atmospheric. While the bead deposition regime may be significantly different
for these simulations than that for an industrial-scale system on moving web, with a vacuum box,
the procedure for startup in this workflow is still applicable.
The remainder of this section discusses the problem setup through Goma’s main input file that
specifies the equations and boundary conditions to be solved and the mode in which they are solved,
together with other numerical capabilities. As we progress with the simple steps to achieve the first
steady solution and then continue around parameter space with Goma’s advanced capabilities, we
will discuss other features of the model that are enabling and convenient.
At this point we will discuss the important features of the main input files. The entries (lines or
“cards”) not discussed can be understood by consulting the Goma 7 user’s manual. Every line that
is not a comment in the following files are detailed in this user’s manual.

Choose initial parameters that are known to be in a stable part of the operating windows. For this

2.1.3.3. Step 3 - Release downstream meniscus

Again, assuming that the reader is familiar with Goma’s beginner’s tutorial (GT-001), we only

meniscus (the film draw-down region) in this step. While we are at it, we also change the
upstream slot-die lip shape from a sharp corner to a rounded fillet to facilitate more robust solution
space exploration (see discussion above where we mention DOUBLE_FILLET). While this step
is typically easily executed due to the “nearby” solution from a fixed mesh (but slippery would-be

fixed mesh solution fixedmesh.exoII. Next, we change over from a fixed Newton correction
strategy to a relaxed one that is staged based on the level of the L2 error. This card is as follows in
release_DSMo.input:
Newton correction factor = 0.4 1.0e-6 0.2 1.e-6 0.05 1.e-5
This capability and associated parameters are chosen with robustness in mind. Of course, one
could probably converge with a few relaxed steps at a Newton Correction Factor of 0.1, followed by
1.0 (or full Newton-Raphson steps), but this automates and assures convergence over 10s of steps.
The relaxation levels and thresholds we chose through experimentation. We encourage the reader
to look at this card description in the Goma 7 manual.
Notice in release_DSMo.input a second major changeover in BCs is adding the DOUBLE_FILLET
condition on the upstream slot die lip which sets the geometry for the mesh to follow from the up-
stream die face to the inside of feed slot. Two “corners” are included that are now to be “rounded”,
hence the term DOUBLE_FILLET. Basically, the result is that the sharp corner in the initial mesh
is rounded and the mesh will now slide along this surface. We take off nodeset 23 DX and DY
conditions to achieve a “sliding mesh”.

2.1.3.5. Step 5 - Release the upstream contact lines

Release the contact lines that terminate the upstream meniscus at the web and die lip, viz. run

2.1.3.7. Step 7 - Set target contact angles

On this step we set the target contact angles on the die lips/faces. These are static contact angles.
The Wiegandt et al. [12] paper states in the supplementary information that the measured contact

to presume they are “wetting”, or somewhat hydrophilic and set the angles at about 45 deg.

We apply a CA to the downstream separation line at nodeset 700, viz. we uncomment the boundary
condition on CA at this nodeset. The contact angle is set to idsca aprepro variable. We also change
the upstream CA from 100 to 45 deg, making it more wetting (sca to sca2). This will help reduce
the backpressure required to maintain the upstream meniscus at its current location when we take

the meniscus at 0.15*up_die is -4.4e-3 dyne/cmˆ2. This value is output at the end of the run on

of 45 deg, the backpressure required to maintain the current position actually increases and goes
positive, viz. absent the pressure the meniscus would likely want to fully fill the upstream die land.

2.2.2. A note on solution methodology

Typically for ALE problems we follow a general process:

 1. Find a solution on a fixed mesh using a Newtonian fluid, free surfaces constrained to be
 VELO_NORMAL or sometimes no slip.
 2. For each free surface release them one at a time.
 a. We might also release these piecewise if they are a large free surface, such as release
 the portion on the web then release another section further upstream and so on.
 b. The upstream meniscus is often under vacuum pressure for coating flows if we are
 searching for no vacuum flows we usually start with a vacuum pressure and slowly
 reduce it.
 3. Add aditional physics, this would be things like Non-Newtonian fluids, species dependence,
 and temperature dependence.
 4. Use continuation to search parameter space: such as webspeed, vacuum limits, nearby
 rheology, etc.

Most of the time step 2 is the most difficult to reach convergence of solutions without mesh
deformation causing issues.
The most common way we alleviate this is by damping (see Newton correction factor).
Typically we will run a good amount of steps with some low value for damping like 0.1 so the
mesh can move into postion, then we will gradual increase this damping factor to 1.0 to reach a
converged solution.
This could be semi-automated using the optional floats to specify it based on the residual value
with good sucess but often some manual intervention was required.
Recently we have introduced a line search type which combines a backtracking approach with the
semi-automated approach for the mesh portion of the solution, see (Newton line search type). This
approach has proven quite a bit more robust as the backtracking automates the search for other
variables of interest while allowing us to take small mesh movement steps at the same time.
For the slide coating tutorial all input files will use this approach:

Newton line search type = BACKTRACK_MESH

 1. Wall boundaries are fixed in two ways, for the mesh we use either a DX,DY Dirichlet
 boundary condition to fix them in place or when mesh movement is more substantial we
 switch to a DOUBLE_FILLET_GEOM case which allows the mesh to move along the die face
 using a distance residual to replace the mesh equations on the boundary. An alternative
 would be to use the DOUBLE_FILLET condition like seen in the slot coater but we found that
 the large size of the die proved difficult to achieve convergence near the extremes of the die
 edges. DOUBLE_FILLET_GEOM uses a different approach to set the residual equation. For the
 velocity we employ no slip through a Dirichlet condition U,V are set to zero, an alternative
 might be to use VELO_NORMAL and VELO_TANGENT or VELO_NORMAL and VELO_SLIP if you
 wish to include some wall slip.

 2. For Free surface BCs we use the kinematic condition n · (u − x¤ ) = 0 and include surface
 tension through the CAPILLARY card. Because we have an outflow our CAPILLARY boundary
 condition needs a CAP_ENDFORCE card applied at the outflow point of the free surface.

 3. For Static Contact Angles we use the CA card to set a fixed value, ideally this is known for the
 die material and fluid interaction.

 4. For the Dynamic Contact Angle we can either use a CA card to set a fixed contact angle or use
 one of the more advanced models such as VELO_THETA_HOFFMAN, lately some preference has
 been shown to use the VELO_THETA_HOFFMAN model especially with Non-Newtonian fluids.
 A note of WARNING the VELO_THETA_HOFFMAN model requires the contact point to be in a
 rotated cordinate system using VELO_NORMAL for no penetration and either VELO_TANGENT
 or VELO_SLIP for the web speed and slip.

 5. For the Moving Web we have a few choices we combine a no penetration condition like a
 Dirichlet condition if we are correctly aligned (𝑢 𝑥 = 0 this case), a VELO_NORMAL for non
 aligned boundaries or if using VELO_THETA_HOFFMAN. At the dynamic contact line we often
 need to apply some slip so in our case we use VELO_SLIP to apply both the web speed of the
 moving web and allow slip near the contact point. An alternative is to use VELO_TANGENT
 with its slip parameter.

 6. No BC conditions for outflows. As we integrate our equations by parts we end up with an
 n · T = 0 natural boundary condition on all boundaries for the momentum conservation
 equation. Typically this can behave well for fully developed flows and especially Newtonian
 flows, however that is not always the case when we are searching for solutions or are using
 Non-Newtonian flows, especially if we have conditions like gravity enabled. So we put this
 boundary term back into the equation using FLOW_STRESSNOBC at the outflow. If you have
 difficulties near the outflow you can do some temporary constrictions such as FLOW_GRADV_T
 or set VELO_TANGENT to 0 but ideally you should move to a NO BC for the final result.

2.2.4.1.    Step 1 - Fixed Mesh

The first step in single layer slide coating solution is to prepare an initial guess for the free surface
flow. As with most of our ALE problems we do this by solving a Newtonian flow problem in a
fixed domain.
The domain is a close approximation to what we expect the slide coating to look like. We have a
fixed height for the wet thickness which is set by the substrate speed and inlet flow rate. There is
a curve to the free surface and the upstream meniscus is also approximated to have vacuum so is
further down the die lip and substrate to accomodate this.
We solve this fixed domain flow in the usual manner and as it is Newtonian we can use a Full-Newton
approach.

mpirun -np 4 goma -a -i step01_fixed_mesh.inp

The free surfaces are constrained by VELO_NORMAL boundary conditions so that they do not
move during the solve but slip is allowed tangentially. Results seen in Figure 2-10.

2.2.4.2. Step 2 - Free bead and substrate free surface

The next steps are to release the free surface incrementally to allow the domain to deform to the
correct shape. We do this by changing the VELO_NORMAL boundary conditions to the traditional
KINEMATIC boundary conditions which allow the free surface to move normally with the fluid
velocity. Surface tension is also added to the free surface through the cards CAPILLARY and
CAP_ENDFORCE.
For the second step we take the initial guess from our step 1 solution and release the free surface of
the bead and along the substrate while still keeping the free surface fixed along the slide length.
This incremental approach helps allow the domain to deform slowly without finding an unstable
configuration.
Instead of Full-Newton we use a Modified-Newton approach here to help with convergence the
relaxation during each step is controlled by a backtracking search and fixed parameters for mesh
variables using the Newton line search type = BACKTRACK_MESH option.

2.3. Thixotropy in coating flows

Liquids often used in the coating industry commonly consist of multicomponent mixtures of poly-
mer solutions, alcohols, metallic solids, binders, and colloidal suspensions, leaving a structurally
complex network of particles and entangled polymer chains at the microscopic scale, which gives
rise to a multitude of non-Newtonian behaviors such as shear-thinning, extension-thickening, vis-
coelastic, viscoplastic, or thixotropic. The focus of this section is modeling slot-die coating of
thixotropic properties in fluids in GOMA. Effectively, thixotropy is a time-dependent, reversible
phenomenon that affects viscosity in two ways: 1) viscosity is reduced under flow stress due to the
breakdown of particulate/polymer network structures and a reversible recovery of viscosity once
the stress is removed, and 2) viscosity is reduced by shearing deformation, i.e., the liquid is shear-
thinning (deformation rate dependent). Under steady shear, thixotropic fluids reach an equilibrium
viscosity that depends on the applied shear rate, at which the rates of structural breakdown and
reformation are balanced.
Building on the prior sections and files, we describe the thixotropic model used in GOMA and how
to model the thixotropic fluid below.

2.3.1. Thixotropic Model

In GOMA there are two types of thixotropic models, a micro-structural bond evolution model and
a fluidity based model. In this tutorial, the micro-structural bond evolution model is of focus. In
this model, the thixotropic nature of the fluid by employing a version of the indirect microstructural
structure model derived by Mujumdar et al. (2002) [7], where the evolution of the fluid structure
can be expressed as,
 𝐷𝜉
                                    = 𝑘 𝑓 (1 − 𝜉) + 𝑘 𝑔 (1 − 𝜉) 2 − 𝑘 𝑏 𝛾¤ 𝑚 𝜉                   (2.1)
 𝐷𝑡
where 𝜉 is the extent of reaction, 𝑘 𝑓 is the formation rate constant, 𝑘 𝑔 is the quadratic formation
rate constant, 𝑘 𝑏 is the breakup rate constant, 𝑚 is the exponent for the rate of structure breakup,
and the left hand side is the substantial derivative of the extent of reaction. Originating from the
work of Moore [6], in the indirect structure microstructural model, a scalar parameter 𝜉 is used to
measure the extent of structure breakdown of the microstructure and couple the structural kinetics
with a measurable fluid mechanic property, where shear viscosity is typically the measurable
fluid mechanic property of choice due to its ease of measurement. This model found success in
modeling various thixotropic suspensions such as slurry fuels, particle-filled epoxy suspensions
(Grillet et al. (2009)), concentrated suspensions of silicon particles and silicon carbide whiskers in

 55

polyethylene, common food products, and bauxite mud residue and is able to capture the expected
separation of time scales of the build-up and break-down of the structure in the suspension. The
first-order model is commonly used where the quadratic term is neglected, leaving,
 𝐷𝜉
                                         = 𝑘 𝑓 (1 − 𝜉) − 𝑘 𝑏 𝛾¤ 𝑚 𝜉.                                (2.2)
 𝐷𝑡
The viscosity of the fluid is linked to the extent of reaction by,

                                        𝜇 = 𝜇∞ + (𝜇0 − 𝜇∞ )𝜉 𝑝                                      (2.3)

where 𝜇∞ is the infinite shear rate viscosity, 𝜇0 is the zero shear rate viscosity, and 𝑝 is the extent of
reaction exponent. The infinite and zero shear rate viscosity of the fluid are steady-state equilibrium
properties of the coating material, found from the shear-viscosity to steady shear-rate curve.

2.3.2.3. Difficulties in Modeling Thixotropic Fluids

Prior to presenting an example problem, we briefly discuss several key challenges and considerations
that arise when simulating thixotropic fluids, as opposed to simpler generalized Newtonian models.
Unlike the Carreau–Yasuda model, in which the viscosity depends solely on the local shear rate,
thixotropic fluids exhibit a viscosity that depends on both shear rate and time. This time dependence
arises from the evolution of the material microstructure, which is commonly modeled through a
transient reaction–diffusion equation for a structure variable.
As a consequence, thixotropic simulations can exhibit large spatial and temporal variations in
viscosity, particularly during the early stages of the computation when the structure field evolves
from its initial condition. Based on trial-and-error experience, we have found that such simulations
are best performed in a fully transient manner rather than solving directly for a steady-state solution,
as this approach improves numerical stability and convergence.
Furthermore, because the viscosity is directly coupled to the structure factor, careful selection of
the initial condition is essential. A common and robust choice is to initialize the domain with a
spatially homogeneous viscosity field for which the corresponding structure factor is known. For
example, initializing the viscosity at the zero-shear value, 𝜇0 , corresponds to a fully structured
material with structure factor equal to 1, whereas initializing at the infinite-shear viscosity, 𝜇∞ ,
corresponds to a fully broken structure with structure factor equal to 0.
Another important distinction between generalized Newtonian and thixotropic models lies in the
treatment of the shear rate. In generalized Newtonian formulations, the shear rate is computed

 59

directly from the local velocity gradients. In contrast, thixotropic models typically introduce an
additional scalar shear-rate invariant that is governed by its own transport equation and solved
concurrently with the momentum and structure evolution equations.

To promote numerical stability in the evolution of the structure variable, 𝜉, two numerical dif-
fusivities are commonly introduced: one associated with the structure evolution equation and a
second applied to the scalar shear-rate invariant equation. These diffusivities play a critical role in
stabilizing the coupled system. However, we have found that their values can significantly influence
the predicted structure, shear-rate, and resulting viscosity fields.

As a result, careful tuning of these numerical diffusivities is often required, and a limited degree of
trial and error is recommended to achieve a stable and converged solution. An exercise exploring
the sensitivity of the solution to these parameters is provided in the exercises section.
