Leveraging tails for adaptation
Sergios Agapiou, Ismaël Castillo, Paul Egels

Abstract
We consider contraction of Bayesian posterior distributions in nonparametric settings where coefficients of a function over a basis or dictionary are given priors with p p –exponential tails, including Laplace tails ( p = 1 ) (p=1) and heavier tails ( p < 1 ) (p<1) . It is shown that contraction rates improve as p p decreases and that full adaptation to smoothness, up to logarithmic factors, is obtained in an appropriate p → 0 p\to 0 regime. As applications, we consider both series priors in white noise regression and shallow ReLU neural networks in random design regression. In particular, we show that overparametrised shallow ReLU networks can adapt to any regularity 0 ≤ β ≤ 2 0\leq\beta\leq 2 . Through a simulation study, we show strong empirical agreement with the behavior predicted by our theory.



keywords

Frequentist analysis of Bayesian procedures, adaptation, nonparametric regression, Bayesian neural networks, overparametrization.

Introduction

A central goal in nonparametric statistics is
adaptation
: the ability of an estimator to perform simultaneously and optimally across a wide variety of settings with little to no tuning. When inference is carried out over a class of functional spaces, it is desirable that the estimator automatically adapts to unknown features of these spaces, such as smoothness, geometry, sparsity or other finer structural properties. A large body of literature has focused on adaptation: Lepski’s method
Lepskiĭ 1990
,
Lepskiĭ 1991
, thresholding
Donoho et al. 1995
and model selection
Barron et al. 1999
are amongst the most well-known non-Bayesian approaches. Bayesian methods, on the other hand, have a natural ability to achieve adaptation, as we discuss in more detail below, by choosing prior distributions that are flexible enough to achieve this task (one possibility is for instance to draw certain prior parameters at random in a hierarchical Bayes fashion).

Recently, motivated by the remarkable empirical success of deep learning methods, there has been a growing interest in understanding how neural networks can automatically learn structural parameters, such as smoothness of functions or ‘effective’ dimensions, for instance in regression settings exhibiting a compositional structure as in
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
,
Kohler and Langer 2021
or for
data lying on geometric structures (e.g.
Nakada and Imaizumi 2020
).
While the above works prove that optimal convergence rates can indeed be achieved by
appropriately
choosing the network’s architecture, this choice still typically depends on parameters unknown to the statistician. In principle a solution to this
adaptation
problem could be to compare empirically risks for different architectures (by a method such as model selection or cross-validation); this would be particularly demanding computationally, and in practice overparameterized networks are used. These have a width or a depth larger than what traditional bias–variance trade–offs would suggest as optimal ones, but still empirically perform remarkably well. It is therefore particularly desirable to understand statistical properties of overparameterized networks, in particular, whether they can achieve adaptation to smoothness or to other structural parameters.

In this work, we take a step in this direction by showing, in a simple regression setting, that overparameterized prior distributions, with similar tails to those arising as outputs of typical neural networks with random Gaussian weights, have natural adaptation properties. Our results are relevant both for adaptation properties of classical projection estimators in regression as well as for neural network-type estimates –here for simplicity we consider shallow networks–. We now briefly review adaptation methods from a Bayesian perspective.

In the Bayesian setting, Gaussian processes (henceforth GPs) are among the most celebrated and widely used priors in machine learning and statistical applications (see e.g.
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
,
Liu et al. 2020
). Thanks to pioneering work by
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2008
building on the general contraction rate theory of
Ghosal et al. 2000
, contraction rates of posterior distributions for GPs are now fairly well understood from a theoretical perspective. While
per se
Gaussian processes are not adaptive to smoothness (see
Castillo 2008
), a large body of literature has shown that GPs can be made adaptive to (homogeneous) smoothness
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2009
,
Szabó et al. 2013
,
Castillo et al. 2014
,
Knapik et al. 2016
,
Rousseau and Szabo 2017
as well as to geometry in manifold settings
Berenfeld et al. 2024
,
Yang and Dunson 2016
,
Tang et al. 2026
, provided they are properly rescaled (depending on the observed data) or their parameters are suitably estimated or drawn from a hyperprior.

However, several drawbacks are associated with Gaussian process approaches. First, achieving adaptation requires an additional computational layer in order to adjust scale or regularity parameters, which can render GP regression computationally demanding, especially in high-dimensional and data-rich regimes, see for example
Agapiou et al. 2014
. A number of works aim to mitigate this complexity by developing scalable approximations of GPs, for instance through sparse variational inference
Nieman and Szabó 2025
or local approximations (see e.g.
Szabó and Zhu 2026
for further references). Second, and more fundamentally, recent negative results show that even properly rescaled GPs may fail to adapt when finer notions of adaptivity are required, such as adaptation to compositional structures
Giordano et al. 2022
(see also
Abraham and Deo 2023
in the context of inverse problems) or inhomogeneous smoothness
Agapiou and Wang 2024
. In order to obtain stronger forms of adaptation, a number of works advocate and study deep Bayesian methods, such as Bayesian deep GPs
Damianou and Lawrence 2013
,
Finocchio and Schmidt-Hieber 2023
,
Castillo and Randrianarisoa 2025
and Bayesian deep (Gaussian) neural networks
Kong and Kim 2025
. These approaches exhibit stronger adaptive properties compared to standard GPs, notably with respect to compositional structures. Nevertheless, with the currently available theory, they still require hyperparameter (or hyperprior) estimation. Contributions on posterior rates for Bayesian deep networks include
Polson and Ročková 2018
for spike–and–slab priors on weights (see also
Chérief-Abdellatif 2020
,
Bai et al. 2020
,
Ohn and Lin 2024
for variational Bayes counterparts) and
Lee and Lee 2022
,
Kong et al. 2023
,
Kong and Kim 2025
; a review on Bayesian deep neural networks can be found in
Arbel et al. 2026
.

It turns out that replacing the Gaussian distribution with heavier-tailed distributions can lead to improved contraction rates. One example is the Laplace–Besov prior, which replaces Gaussian coefficients by double–exponential ones and has recently been studied in a variety of nonparametric settings, including regression, density estimation, and inverse problems
Agapiou and Wang 2024
,
Giordano 2023
,
Dolera et al. 2024
. These works build on a more general theory for
p
–exponential priors, corresponding to densities proportional to
\exp({-|x|^{p}/p})
and whose tails interpolate between Laplace (
p=1
) and Gaussian (
p=2
), which was initiated in
Agapiou et al. 2021
and later complemented by
Agapiou and Savva 2024
. Interestingly, although the contraction rates obtained in
Agapiou et al. 2021
for such
p
–exponential priors (
1\leq p<2
) are not minimax optimal, for
1\leq p<2
they are still polynomially faster than the corresponding Gaussian rates (
p=2
). Moreover, when scaling and/or regularity hyperparameters are drawn at random, these works establish adaptation properties of
p
–exponential priors (
1\leq p\leq 2
) with respect to inhomogeneous smoothness in an
L_{2}
-sense (see also
Agapiou et al. 2026
for further discussion on adaptation to inhomogeneous smoothness). However, such
p
-exponential priors still require some form of hyperparameter estimation in order to achieve adaptation, and proof techniques crucially rely on log-concavity of the prior density, which only holds for
p\geq 1
.

Very recently,
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
initiated the theoretical study of even heavier–tailed priors, for instance with polynomially decaying tails such as the Student–
t
distribution. One main advantage of such priors is that they only require a fixed universal deterministic (in particular, data–independent) rescaling, so that no hyperprior or hyperparameter estimation is needed to achieve adaptation. In
Agapiou et al. 2026
, these heavier–tailed priors were shown to possess strong adaptive properties with respect to non–homogeneously smooth Besov functions, this time in any
L_{r}
–norm (
r\geq 1
).
When applied to overparameterized deep Bayesian neural networks,
Castillo and Egels 2025
showed that they yield posteriors that adapt to compositional structures and manifold geometry, again without requiring hyperparameter estimation.

The primary goal of the present paper is to understand adaptation properties of
p
–exponential type priors over the full range of tail indices
0<p\leq 2
, and in particular in the (formally defined below) limiting regime
p\to 0
. There are two main motivations for investigating the “small
p
regime" for
p
–exponential priors. The first is that the existing results on contraction rates for
p
–exponential priors indicate that the rate may (further) improve as
p
decreases below
1
, suggesting that adaptation may be obtained for such priors by formally letting
p\to 0

without
the need to sample hyperparameters. The second motivation comes from the study of deep learning methods, in particular Bayesian neural networks with Gaussian priors on network weights. Indeed, several recent works show that, conditionally on the input, the output of deep neural networks with i.i.d. Gaussian weights exhibits heavy–tailed behavior,
Vladimirova et al. 2019
,
Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan 2021
,
Noci et al. 2021
. More precisely, such outputs are of (generalized) sub–Weibull type with index
L/2
(see e.g.
Vladimirova et al. 2020
), which is closely related to the
p
–exponential distribution with
p=2/L
, where
L
denotes the depth of the network. These results suggest that, for deep neural networks with i.i.d. Gaussian weights, increasing depth naturally induces marginally
p
-exponential outputs, in the regime where
p
approaches
0
inversely proportionally to the network depth.

Our study, on the one hand, complements the work of
Agapiou et al. 2021
,
and builds a bridge between the very heavy–tailed (polynomial) case of
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
, where posteriors exhibit powerful adaptive properties, and the lighter heavy–tailed priors such as the Laplace–Besov case, which are more commonly used in practice. On the other hand, this will enable us to analyse
overparameterized
shallow ReLU neural networks with
p
–exponential weights, and show that the latter satisfy a remarkable adaptation to smoothness property.

We now summarize our main contributions:

1.

In a white-noise regression setting, we study series priors with independent
p
–exponentially distributed coefficients (
p>0
) and derive an upper bound on the contraction rate of
\rho
–posteriors (
0<\rho<1
) toward the unknown regression function. This bound considerably broadens previously known results of
Agapiou et al. 2021
for
1\leq p\leq 2
. Notably, the obtained rate becomes near-optimal as the tail parameter
p
approaches
0
, that is, as the prior becomes heavier-tailed. Also, previous works for
p\in[1,2]
left open the question whether the obtained rates could be improved; here we provide a matching lower bound, valid also for standard posteriors (
\rho=1
).

2.

In random design nonparametric regression, we study a class of
overparameterized
ReLU shallow neural network priors, where the number of neurons is taken explicitly much larger than the oracle number that achieves the optimal minimax rate. An independent
p
–exponential prior (
0<p\leq 1
) is placed on the weights of the hidden layer, and an upper bound on the contraction rate of the corresponding
\rho
–tempered posterior is obtained. Although different in nature from the series prior setting, these neural network rates share a similar type of improvement as
p
approaches
0
, with a rate that is near-minimax for small
p
;

3.

In both of the above settings, we formally show that taking the limit
p\to 0
as the sample size
n
goes to infinity,
leads to fully adaptive posteriors over smoothness classes. These results can be linked to earlier findings of
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
for series priors and
Castillo and Egels 2025
for neural networks. In particular, in the latter case, our results provide further insight into the adaptivity of deep Gaussian Bayesian neural networks (here small
p
may be interpreted as mimicking the effect of increasing depth in a network; see also the discussion in Section
5
).

4.

We provide numerical experiments confirming that in both frameworks adaptation automatically occurs for the resulting estimators as the tail–index
p
approaches
0
. In particular, ReLU shallow networks with overparametrised width of order
n
and weights with
p
–exponential distributions and small deterministic scalings indeed achieve adaptation to smoothness in practical experiments, closely matching the predicted behaviour from our theory.

\sigma_{k}=k^{-1/2-\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall k\geq 3,\quad p_{k}=(\log k)^{-1}\log\log k.

(18)

2.

Faster than polynomial decay. For
\gamma>0
,
p_{1}=p_{2}=1
and
c>2.1>\log 8
,

\sigma_{k}=\exp(-\log^{1+\gamma}k)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall k\geq 3,\quad p_{k}=c(\log k)^{-(1+\gamma)}.

(19)

The first natural choice matches the oversmoothing polynomial choice of sequence
\sigma_{k}
in Theorem
1
. Compared to Theorem
1
(fixed
p
) for which the rate was polynomially slower than minimax, now choosing
p_{k}
going to
0
leads to contraction at quasi–minimax rate (up to log factors). With the choice (
18
) one obtains one–sided adaptation on the range
\alpha>\beta
. In order to obtain full minimax adaptation, we use the second choice of sequence
\sigma_{k}=\exp(-\log^{1+\gamma}k)
which ensures oversmoothing thanks to its faster than polynomial decay. This automatic oversmoothing choice matches the ones introduced in
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
,
Agapiou et al. 2026
and similarly shows full adaptation without being restricted by hyperparameters (the choice of
\gamma
is free and need not depend on
\beta
).

3
Overparameterized shallow neural network priors

In this section we focus on random–design nonparametric Gaussian regression. Given an integer
n\geq 1
, we observe
n
i.i.d. pairs of random variables
(X_{i},Y_{i})\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
, with

Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\xi_{i},

(20)

where
(X_{i})
are i.i.d. from a distribution
P_{X}
on
[0,1]
and independently
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
variables. For simplicity, we work in dimension
1
, but all our results extend to an input space
[0,1]^{d},d\geq 2,
in a straigthforward way. The sample
((X_{i},Y_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n}
has distribution denoted
P_{f}^{n}
, where
P_{f}
is the law of a single pair
(X_{i},Y_{i})
; the corresponding log-likelihood is

\ell_{n}(f,(X,Y)):=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-f(X_{i}))^{2}.

Given a prior
\Pi
on
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
to be defined below, the
\rho
–posterior
\Pi_{\rho}[\,\cdot\,|\,X,Y]
is formed using (
1
). We study it
assuming
((X_{i},Y_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n}=:(X,Y)
is distributed from
P_{f_{0}}^{n}
, where the true
f_{0}
belongs to a Hölder ball: denoting by
D^{j}f
the
j
–th derivative of
f
, define

\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L):=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\;:\;\max_{0\leq i\leq\underaccent{\bar}{\beta}}||D^{i}f||_{\infty}+\underset{x\neq y}{\sup}\frac{|D^{\underaccent{\bar}{\beta}}f(x)-D^{\underaccent{\bar}{\beta}}f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\beta-\underaccent{\bar}{\beta}}}\leq L\right\},

(21)

for
L\geq 0
,
\beta\in(0,2]
and where
\underaccent{\bar}{\beta}\in\{0,1\}
is the largest integer strictly smaller than
\beta
. We aim to characterize the contraction rate of
\Pi_{\rho}[\,\cdot\,\mid X,Y]
around
f_{0}
using the population loss

||f-f_{0}||_{2,P_{X}}^{2}:=\int(f-f_{0})^{2}\,dP_{X}.

(22)

3.1
Definition of the prior

Shallow neural networks.
Functions in
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
can naturally be approximated by shallow neural networks (henceforth SNN). The realization of a shallow ReLU neural network with
M\geq 1
neurons is a function

f_{M}:x\in[0,1]\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}w_{k}(v_{k}\times x+a_{k})_{+}+b,

(23)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
w_{k},v_{k},a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,M-1\}
. The ReLU activation function
x\mapsto(x)_{+}:=\max(0,x)
is piecewise linear, so that the function
f_{M}
is piecewise affine.

To construct an approximation of a smooth function
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
with an SNN, one can consider a uniform subdivision of
[0,1]
in
M
intervals
I_{k}:=[k/M,(k+1)/M)
, and approximate
f
by the piecewise affine function
f^{\star}_{M}
changing slope and interpolating
f
precisely at the boundary points of
I_{k}
. Lemma
10
(see Section
C.3
) formalizes this intuition and controls the approximation error.
For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, let us denote
N_{s}:=2^{m_{s}}
, where
m_{s}
is the closest integer solution to
2^{m_{s}}=n^{1/(2s+1)}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Ideal ‘oracle’ approximator.
Suppose first, to fix ideas, that the smoothness parameter
\beta
of
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
is known. In this case one could set
M=N_{\beta}
and

f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}(x)=f_{0}(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\beta}-1}w_{0;k}\left(x-\frac{k}{N_{\beta}}\right)_{+}.

(24)

Lemma
10
implies
||f_{0}-f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}||_{\infty}\leq 2LN_{\beta}^{-\beta}
, for
\beta\in(0,2]
, which is the optimal approximation error for
\beta
–Hölder functions.

Priors on overparameterized shallow neural networks.
We now define an
overparameterized
prior on shallow networks (that is, with possibly much larger than oracle number of neurons). Let us choose a number of neurons
N_{\alpha}\geq N_{\beta}
(equivalently
\alpha\leq\beta
; typically one can think of the choice
N_{\alpha}=n
, or
N_{\alpha}=n^{1-\delta}
for some small
\delta>0
) and set

f=\sum_{k=0}^{{N_{\alpha}-1}}w_{k}(x-a_{k})_{+}+b,\qquad w_{k}\overset{iid}{=}\sigma_{n}\zeta_{k},\qquad a_{k}=k/N_{\alpha},\qquad b\sim\pi_{b},

(25)

for some deterministic
\sigma_{n}>0
to be chosen,
\zeta_{k}
independent and identically distributed random variables with density
h
satisfying conditions (
4
)–(
6
), and where the prior
\pi_{b}
on the bias is any distribution with symmetric, continuous and strictly positive density on
\mathbb{R}
, independent of the prior on the weights
(w_{k})
(note also that, for simplicity, we have taken the shifts
a_{k}
to be deterministic).

3.2
Contraction results for overparameterized SNN priors

For any
\rho\in(0,1)
and
P,Q
probability measures, the
\rho
–Rényi divergence is defined as

D_{\rho}(P,Q):=\frac{1}{\rho-1}\log\int\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\rho}\,dQ.

(26)

The next result examins contraction rates of
\rho
–posterior distributions under SNN priors.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\beta\in(0,2]
and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
. Consider data
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
generated from
P_{f_{0}}^{n}
in model (
20
). Let
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and
\Pi
be the overparameterized SNN prior defined in (
25
), with
0<p\leq 1
,
q>0
and some
\sigma_{n}
to be specified below. For any
\rho\in(0,1)
and
D_{\rho}
as in (
26
), there exists a large enough constant
M>0
, such that, as
n\to\infty
,

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}\left[\left\{f\,:\,\frac{1}{n}D_{\rho}(P_{f}^{n},P_{f_{0}}^{n})\geq M\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right\}\mid X,Y\right]\to 0,

where, letting
\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}=n^{-\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta}}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{+}=n^{-2/5}
(equal to
\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}
for
\beta=2
),
\varepsilon_{n}
is given by

i)

(Oracle
\sigma_{n}
) for
\sigma_{n}=N_{\alpha}^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}N_{\beta}^{\frac{2}{2+p}-\beta}\log^{-\frac{2}{q(2+p)}}(n)
,

\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}(N_{\alpha}N_{\beta})^{\frac{p}{2+p}}\log^{-\frac{p}{q(2+p)}}(n);

ii)

(Non-oracle
\sigma_{n}
) for
\sigma_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{+}/N_{\alpha}

\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\>n^{\,p\>\left(\frac{1-\beta}{2+4\beta}+\frac{1}{5}+\frac{1}{2+4\alpha}\right)}.

Corollary 2
.

In the setting of Theorem
4
, assume further
||f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq F
, for some
F>0
. Define
T_{F}:f\mapsto-F\vee(f\wedge F)
and consider the “clipped” posterior
\Pi_{\rho}^{F}[\,\cdot\,\,|\,X,Y]=\Pi_{\rho}[\,\cdot\,\,|\,X,Y]\circ T_{F}^{-1}
. As
n\to\infty,

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}^{F}\left[\left\{f\,:\,||f-f_{0}||_{2,P_{X}}\geq M^{\prime}\varepsilon_{n}\right\}\mid X,Y\right]\to 0,

where
M^{\prime}>0
is a large enough constant (depending on
F
) and
\varepsilon_{n}
is defined as in Theorem
4
.

The proofs of Theorem
4
and Corollary
2
can be found in Section
A.5
. These results give conditions on the deterministic prior scaling
\sigma_{n}
to obtain posterior contraction rates in
L_{2}(P_{X})
–loss (or in Rényi divergence if clipping is not applied) with overparameterized SNN
p
-tailed priors. The first choice of
\sigma_{n}
is said to be oracle as it depends on the unknown ‘true’ smoothness
\beta
, while the second choice does not. Although both lead to polynomially slower than minimax rates (here the minimax rate is
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
), similarly to the series priors in Section
2
, this extra polynomial factor decreases as
p
goes to
0
.

Remark 2
.

The case of lighter tails is also interesting (since it includes the case of Gaussian priors on the weights and bias) and is examined in Theorem
9
in the supplement. As expected, the rate becomes worse as
p
increases.

To obtain a minimax adaptive contraction rate, we now devise an overparameterized SNN prior with suitably chosen deterministic prior scalings
\sigma_{n}
and decaying tail parameter
p_{n}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\beta\in(0,2],
and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
. Consider data
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
generated from
P_{f_{0}}^{n}
in model (
20
). Let
\Pi
be the overparameterized SNN prior defined as in (
25
), for
\alpha=0
(hence the width is
N_{\alpha}\asymp n
),
\sigma_{n}=n^{-t}
with
t>2.5
, and
\zeta_{k}
independent and identically distributed according to
p_{n}
–exponential distributions (defined in (
3
)) with
p_{n}=2/\log{n},\;n\geq 8
. For any
\rho\in(0,1)
, there exists a large enough constant
M>0
, such that, as
n\to\infty
,

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}\left[\left\{f\,:\,\frac{1}{n}D_{\rho}(P_{f}^{n},P_{f_{0}}^{n})\geq M\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right\}\mid X,Y\right]\to 0,

for any
\varepsilon_{n}\geq\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\sqrt{\log{n}},
where
\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}=n^{-\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta}}
and
D_{\rho}
is Rényi divergence. Assuming further
||f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq F
and considering the clipped posterior
\Pi_{\rho}^{F}[\,\cdot\,|X,Y]
as in Corollary
2
, for a large enough constant
M^{\prime}>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}^{F}\left[\left\{f\,:\,||f-f_{0}||_{2,P_{X}}\geq M^{\prime}\varepsilon_{n}\right\}\mid X,Y\right]\to 0.

The proof can be found in Section
A.6
. Theorem
5
shows that, for an overparameterized (with
\alpha=0
) SNN prior with
p_{n}
–exponential weights rescaled by a sufficiently small polynomial factor
\sigma_{n}
and sufficiently heavy tails

, we obtain a minimax fully
adaptive
contraction rate (up to a
\sqrt{\log n}
factor). This choice of tail parameter

on an SNN

–exponential prior can be (informally) thought of as having a deep neural network prior with i.i.d. Gaussian weights and depth
L=\log n
(see Section
5
for more discussion).

While the last result is theoretically appealing since it establishes adaptation over the full range
\beta\in(0,2]
up to logarithmic factors, neural network priors of width
n
can become computationally demanding for large sample sizes. To alleviate this, one can instead use networks of slightly smaller width
N_{\alpha},\;0\leq\alpha<2
(and small
\alpha
e.g.
\alpha=.5
), still yielding adaptation over the range
\beta\in[\alpha,2]
. Also, the condition

on the scalings
\sigma_{n}
, can be relaxed, facilitating posterior sampling. See Remarks
4
and
5
in the supplement for details.

Moreover, the proof of Theorem
5
carries over, with minor modifications, to the choice

\sigma_{n}=\exp\!\bigl(-a(\log n)^{1+\gamma}\bigr),\qquad p_{n}=\frac{b}{(\log n)^{1+\gamma}},

(27)

for
\gamma>0
and
a,b>0
such that
ab>\log 8
, corresponding to the second regime of Corollary
1
. See Remark
3
in the supplement for details.

Simulations

In this section we provide a numerical simulation study. Even though results with series priors in Section
2
were formulated in the white noise model, we compare the performance of the various priors considered in the following random design regression setting:

y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\xi_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(28)

where
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
unknown, the
x_{i}\in[0,1]
are i.i.d. with uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and
\xi_{i}
are independent
N(0,\sigma^{2})
for
\sigma~=~1/4
(the latter value has no special meaning and is chosen for easier visualization in the pictures below).

We define a true function through the series expansion

f_{0}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{0,k}\varphi_{k}(x),

for
\varphi_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos((k-1/2)\pi x))
and
f_{0,k}=(3/2)k^{-3/2}\sin{k}
. This function has regularity
\beta=1
in the sense of (
11
). We generate noisy observations
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
according to the above random design regression model, for
n=400
and
n=4000
. We consider the following priors:

-

series priors as in (
9
), (
10
) defined over the same basis as the truth,
\{\varphi_{k}\}
, with
p
–exponential tails for
p=2,1,1/2,1/4
and
\alpha=2
;

-

series priors defined over
\{\varphi_{k}\}
, with
p_{k}
–exponential tails varying with frequency
k
(and
n
), either as (
18
) for
\alpha=2
or as (
19
) for
\gamma=1/2
;

-

Cauchy HT
(\alpha)
for
\alpha=2
and OT for

series priors defined over
\{\varphi_{k}\}
. These are series priors as in (

) but with Cauchy-distributed
\zeta_{k}
, with
\sigma_{k}=k^{-1/2-\alpha}
or
\sigma_{k}=\exp(-\log^{1+\gamma}{k})
, respectively. We use them as a benchmark as they have been shown to be partially adaptive for
\alpha\geq\beta
and fully adaptive for any

, respectively,
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
,
Agapiou et al. 2026
;

-

shallow neural network priors defined as in (

) with

, for

and oracle choice of

, see Theorem

(i);

-

shallow neural network priors defined as in (

) with

, for

and

as in Theorem

(ii);

-

shallow neural network priors defined as in (

) with

and

,

, and with

(width–

) for

as in (

), with
\gamma=1/2
,

and

.

Note that compared to the shallow neural network priors, the series priors are somewhat
favored
by the fact that the basis
\{\varphi_{j}\}
on which they are defined coincides with the one on which the true function is defined, which is not the case for the ReLU prior. In particular, both the true function and draws from the studied series priors are restricted to take the value zero at the right edge (
x=1
). Similarly, shallow neural networks with the
oracle
choice of
\sigma_{n}
use knowledge of the regularity
\beta
of the truth
f_{0}
, which is unrealistic in practice. For the series priors, we chose
\alpha=2
so that we are in the oversmoothing prior regime
\alpha>\beta
, in which the differences in performance depending on
p
arise. For shallow neural network priors, we chose
\alpha=1/2
so that we are in the overparameterized regime studied in Section
3
. Note that the first varying–
p
shallow network prior used (corresponding to
\alpha=1/2,p_{n}=1/\log{n},\sigma_{n}=n^{-7.5-0.01}
) is not strictly covered by the results of Section
3
, however our theory suggests that it should perform well, at least asymptotically for large
n
(see Remarks
4
and
5
in the supplement). For the second such prior,
a
and
b
were chosen so that
p_{n}
did not become too small for the sample sizes
n
considered, while still satisfying the conditions under which our theory holds. Smaller values of
p_{n}
necessitated a smaller step size in the posterior sampling algorithm used and hence more iterations (see the next paragraph for details). As this prior already incurred a higher computational cost due to its width
n
, compared with the width
\sqrt{n}
of the other neural network priors considered, the resulting increase in the number of iterations would have made posterior sampling computationally expensive. Furthermore, in Section
3
, for convenience we assumed
p
-exponential priors on the weights as in (
3

p
-exponential tailed distributions in the broader sense of (
4

p
-exponential tail series and neural network priors are expected to be optimal, while for the rest of the priors, smaller
p
is better.

Regarding the implementation, we discretized the unit interval using 200 uniformly spaced points, while we truncated the spectral series (for the truth or the series priors) up to
k=200
.
For sampling the posterior we employed the (whitened) preconditioned Crank-Nicholson (wpCN) algorithm,
Chen et al. 2018
,
Cotter et al. 2013
, which is a derivative-free Metropolis-Hastings algorithm robust with respect to dimension (truncation level). The vanilla version of pCN is suitable for Gaussian priors, while the whitened version is suitable for non-Gaussian priors admitting a transformation
f=T(\xi)
, where
\xi
is a sequence of i.i.d. standard normal variables and
f
is a random draw from the non-Gaussian prior of interest, see Algorithm 2 in
Chen et al. 2018
. For example, to get series priors with
p
-exponential tails for
p>0
, we employed the transformation
T(\xi)=|\xi|^{2/p-1}\xi
which transforms
\xi\sim N(0,1)
to a random variable with
p
-exponential tails, while for the shallow neural network priors this transformation is composed with the map taking the sequence of weights and biases to function realizations as in (
25
). For all considered priors, we initialized the Markov chains using draws from the prior. In all runs, we tuned the proposal step size (i.e., the scaling of the proposals) to achieve an acceptance rate of approximately 30%. For all priors, the wpCN algorithm is less efficient compared to the Gaussian series prior case, in the sense that smaller step sizes are required to maintain this target acceptance rate. To keep the total distance explored by the sampler roughly constant across experiments, we scaled the total number of iterations inversely proportional to the step size. In all runs, we retained 20,000 samples. Depending on the prior used, a thinning factor proportional to the scaling applied to the total number of iterations was used.

To compare the performance of the considered priors quantitatively, we averaged errors over 100 realizations of the data, for each of
n=400
and
n=4000

for

f_{0}
.

5
Discussion

This work shows that equipping the coefficients of a function (on a basis, or on a ReLU dictionary) with
p
-exponential priors leads to near (small
p
) or full (regime
p\to 0
) adaptation to smoothness. The presented results are obtained for simplicity in white noise (for series priors) and random design regression (for neural network priors). However, they hold much more generally for
\rho
–posteriors in other statistical models, see Section
B.1
of the supplement.

We now comment on the overparameterized
p
–exponential shallow neural network prior. The prior architecture we have chosen originates from the piecewise affine approximation result Lemma
10
. To recover
f_{0}
, the prior randomly draws weights in the hidden layer and the weights of the input layer are fixed on a certain grid. This specific architecture allows us to define a “simplest possible" overparameterized SNN, having much more active neurons in the hidden layer compared to the oracle network in Lemma
10
. This choice also allows us to easily compare performances of different choices of
p
–exponential prior distributions on the weights. In particular, we show (e.g. in Theorem
4
) that on this architecture heavier tailed priors perform better (than Gaussian for example). We now informally link this
p
–exponential shallow prior to deeper Gaussian priors. The recent works
Vladimirova et al. 2019
,
Zavatone-Veth and Pehlevan 2021
,
Noci et al. 2021
have shown that in a deep NN with i.i.d. Gaussian weights, the distribution of the output of a layer at depth
L\geq 1
is a random vector whose coordinates (conditionally on the input
\mathbf{x}
of the NN) are
marginally

2/L
–exponential, in the sense of equation (
5
) with
q=2/L
(see for instance Theorem 3.1 in
Vladimirova et al. 2019
). The
p
–SNN prior in (
25
) can then be viewed as a ‘summary’ of an
i.i.d.
deep Gaussian prior with depth
L=2/p
. Indeed, we take for prior on the single hidden layer a distribution whose marginals are the same as the output of an
L
–layered Gaussian DNN and removing dependencies.
With this analogy, the improved performances of heavier priors (e.g. in Theorem
4
) can be related to improved performances of DNN priors as the depth increases. Particularly looking at Theorem
5
, we show that taking
p=2/\log n
provides a smoothness-adaptive procedure. Such result can be related to those of
Castillo and Egels 2025
who showed that overparameterized heavy-tailed (with heavier polynomial tails rather than
p
–exponential as in the present work) DNN priors of depth
L\gtrsim\log n=2/p
are fully adaptive. Our results provide a first link between the theoretical results for overparameterized heavy-tailed priors and the more practically employed overparameterized Gaussian DNN prior (we are not aware of any existing posterior contraction results for such Gaussian priors). The case of purely Gaussian weights for deeper networks will be addressed in future work.

Finally, for simplicity, we restricted to results to fractional posteriors with parameter
\rho<1
. The main technical reason is that one does not need to build sieve sets and control their entropy, as is usually the case for classical posteriors (
\rho=1
) when using the generic posterior contraction theory
Ghosal et al. 2000
. Still, we believe all our results go through also for
\rho=1
: this can be formally proved in the white noise model (see Section
B.4
in the Supplement for an example of such a result), but for more general models such as random design regression or classification, it is currently an open question; we refer to the discussion in
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
for more details on this point.

\displaystyle=n(X_{k}-\theta)-\frac{\theta^{p-1}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}},

(38)

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-n+(1-p)\frac{\theta^{p-2}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}}.

(39)

Hence, as
\theta>0
grows,
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}
strictly decreases from
+\infty
to
-n
and vanishes at a single inflection point
\theta_{2}^{*}
, such that
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{2}^{*})=0
, where

\theta_{2}^{*}:=\left(\frac{n\sigma_{k}^{p}}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}.

(40)

Denote
\psi(u):=4u^{1/(1+u)}+4^{-u}u^{-u/(1+u)}
, for
u\in(0,1)
. Using
u\log\frac{1}{u}\leq 1+u
, available for all
u\in(0,1)
we obtain
\psi(u)\leq 4+e<7
. We can then compute

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\prime}(\theta_{2}^{*})\geq h_{k}^{\prime}(4\theta_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle=n\left(X_{k}-4\left(\frac{n\sigma_{k}^{p}}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}-\frac{4^{p-1}}{n\sigma_{k}^{p}}\left(\frac{n\sigma_{k}^{p}}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p-2}}\right)

\displaystyle=n\left(X_{k}-(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-2}}\psi(1-p)\right)

\displaystyle\geq n(X_{k}-7(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-2}}).

Since
M>7^{2-p}
in (
35
) and thanks to our choice of
d>0
, by (
36
) we have
nX_{k}^{2}>7^{2-p}(X_{k}/\sigma_{k})^{p}
and thus
h_{k}^{\prime}(\theta_{2}^{*})\geq h_{k}^{\prime}(4\theta_{2}^{*})>0
.

Thus,
h_{k}^{\prime}
starts from
-\infty
at
0
, ends at
-\infty
at
+\infty
and takes a positive value at
\theta_{2}^{*}
, therefore
h_{k}^{\prime}
vanishes at exactly two critical points
\theta_{m}^{*}
and
\theta_{M}^{*}
, such that
\theta_{m}^{*}<\theta_{2}^{*}<\theta_{M}^{*}
(these critical points obviously depend on
k
; we do not make this explicit to avoid overloading the notation). In particular, at
\theta_{M}^{*}
there is a local maximum of
h_{k}
(because
\theta_{M}^{*}>\theta_{2}^{*}
implies
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{M}^{*})<h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{2}^{*})=0
).

First notice that
\lim_{\theta\to\infty}h_{k}(\theta)=-\infty
. Second, recalling (
40
) the definition of
\theta_{2}^{*}
, we have
h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})>h_{k}(0)
, this shows that the global maximum of
h_{k}
is attained at
\theta_{M}^{*}
and not at the boundary. Indeed

\displaystyle h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})-h_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=2nX_{k}\theta_{2}^{*}-2n(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}-\frac{2^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}(\theta_{2}^{*})^{p}

\displaystyle=2n\theta_{2}^{*}\left(X_{k}-(1+\frac{2^{p}}{2p(1-p)})\theta_{2}^{*}\right)>0,

where the last inequality comes from choice of
M
large enough in (
35
).

We finally set, for any
k\leq n_{\gamma}
,
\mu_{k}
as the global maximizer of
h_{k}
,

\mu_{k}:=\theta_{M}^{*}.

We apply now a Laplace’s method–type argument, to upper bound, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
,

\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{+}(\theta)\,d\theta:=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}.

Taylor’s formula at
\theta_{M}^{*}=\mu_{k}
, provides
\xi_{\theta}\in[\theta\wedge\theta_{M}^{*},\theta\vee\theta_{M}^{*}]
, such that

h_{k}(\theta)=h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta_{M}^{*})^{2}h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\theta}).

(41)

Recall (
39
) the expression of
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}
. Plugging the simple bound
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}\geq-n
in (
41
), allows us to bound the denominator

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta\geq e^{h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\theta-\theta_{M}^{*})^{2}}\,d\theta\geq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}e^{h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}.

For the numerator, recall (
40
) the definition of
\theta_{2}^{*}
and recall that we showed
h_{k}^{\prime}(4\theta_{2}^{*})>0
, thus
2\theta_{2}^{*}\leq 4\theta_{2}^{*}\leq\theta_{M}^{*}
. We cut the integral at
2\theta_{2}^{*}
and obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{2\theta_{2}^{*}}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}_{I_{1}}+\underbrace{\int_{2\theta_{2}^{*}}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}_{I_{2}}.

Bounding
I_{2}
first, for
\theta\in[2\theta_{2}^{*},\infty)
, we have
\xi_{\theta}\geq 2\theta_{2}^{*}
, hence
h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\theta})\leq h_{k}^{\prime\prime}(2\theta_{2}^{*})=-n(1-2^{p-2})\leq-n/2
and thus

I_{2}\leq e^{h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}\int_{2\theta_{2}^{*}}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{4}(\theta-\theta_{M}^{*})^{2}}\,d\theta\leq{e^{h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}\frac{4\sqrt{\pi}}{n\sqrt{n}}}.

Now for
I_{1}
, we know
h_{k}
decreases from
0
to
\theta_{m}^{*}
and then increases up until
\theta_{M}^{*}\geq 2\theta_{2}^{*}
, thus

I_{1}\leq\big(e^{h_{k}(0)}\vee e^{h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})}\big)\int_{0}^{2\theta_{2}^{*}}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}\,d\theta\leq 4\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}](e^{h_{k}(0)}\vee e^{h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})}).

Considering the maximum on the right hand side, we showed in the study of
h_{k}
above, that, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, we have
h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})>h_{k}(0)
. Along with
{\theta_{M}^{*}}\leq X_{k}
(from the critical equation
h_{k}^{\prime}(\theta_{M}^{*})=0
in (
38
)), this provides,

I_{1}\leq 4\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+X_{k}^{2}]e^{h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})}.

Along with the denominator bound, we obtain

\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}\lesssim\sqrt{n}\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+X_{k}^{2}]e^{h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})-h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}+{\frac{1}{n}}.

We showed above that
4\theta_{2}^{*}\leq\theta_{M}^{*}
, so that
2\theta_{2}^{*}-\theta_{M}^{*}\leq-\theta_{M}^{*}/2
. Combined with an application of the mean-value theorem, and noticing that
h_{k}^{\prime}
is decreasing in
[2\theta_{2}^{*},\theta_{M}^{*}]
(since it vanishes at
\theta_{m}^{*}
and
\theta_{M}^{*}
, while
h_{k}^{\prime}(\theta_{2}^{*})>0
, where
\theta_{m}^{*}<2\theta_{2}^{*}<\theta_{M}^{*}
), we have

h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})-h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})\leq h_{k}^{\prime}(2\theta_{2}^{*})[2\theta_{2}^{*}-\theta_{M}^{*}]\leq-\frac{1}{2}\theta_{M}^{*}h_{k}^{\prime}(2\theta_{2}^{*}).

Doing similar computations as for the bound of
h_{k}^{\prime}(4\theta_{2}^{*})
above and using
nX_{k}^{2}\geq 10^{2-p}(X_{k}/\sigma_{k})^{p}
(again
M\geq 10^{2-p}
in (
35
)), we have (from
2+e\leq 5
)

h_{k}^{\prime}(2\theta_{2}^{*})\geq n(X_{k}-5(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-2}})\geq nX_{k}/2.

These inequalities combined provide
e^{h_{k}(2\theta_{2}^{*})-h_{k}(\theta_{M}^{*})}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}nX_{k}\theta_{M}^{*}}
and thus

\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}\lesssim\sqrt{n}\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+X_{k}^{2}]e^{-\frac{1}{4}nX_{k}\theta_{M}^{*}}+\frac{1}{n}.

Recall
\theta_{2}^{*}\leq\theta_{M}^{*}
. Using the definition of
\theta_{2}^{*}
in (
40
), the fact that
h_{k}^{\prime}(\theta_{M}^{*})=0
where
h_{k}^{\prime}
is given in (
38
), as well as
(1-p)^{-\frac{1-p}{2-p}}\leq 2
, we obtain

\theta_{M}^{*}=X_{k}-\frac{(\theta_{M}^{*})^{p-1}}{n\sigma_{k}^{p}}\geq X_{k}-(1-p)^{-\frac{1-p}{2-p}}(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{p-1}{p-2}-1}\geq X_{k}-2(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-2}}.

(42)

Since
M\geq 4^{2-p}
in (
35
), we have
nX_{k}^{2}\geq 4^{2-p}(X_{k}/\sigma_{k})^{p}
and the last display is further lower bounded from below by
X_{k}/2
, so that

\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta-{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{h_{k}(\theta)}\,d\theta}\lesssim\sqrt{n}\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+X_{k}^{2}]e^{-\frac{1}{8}nX_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}.

Summing up over
k\leq n_{\gamma}
gives two terms on the right hand side. The first term is smaller than any polynomial power of
n
. Indeed, thanks to Lemma
8
, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, we have
f_{0,k}/2\leq X_{k}\leq 3f_{0,k}/2
. There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, such that, recalling
\alpha>\beta
,

\sqrt{n}\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}\theta_{2}^{*}[(\theta_{2}^{*})^{2}+X_{k}^{2}]e^{-\frac{1}{8}nX_{k}^{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{n}e^{-c_{2}nn_{\gamma}^{-2\beta-1}}\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-2}}[(n\sigma_{k}^{p})^{\frac{2}{p-2}}+f_{0,k}^{2}]\lesssim n^{c_{1}}e^{-c_{2}n^{\frac{p(\alpha-\beta)}{1+2\beta+p(\alpha-\beta)}}}.

The last displayed bound is
o(n_{\gamma}^{-2\beta})
as
n\to\infty
. For the third term, simply notice that
n_{\gamma}/n=o(n_{\gamma}^{-2\beta})
, so that it holds

E_{f_{0}}\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}w_{k}^{+}\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{+}(\theta)\,d\theta\mathbf{1_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}}=o(n_{\gamma}^{-2\beta}).

(43)

Control on the weight of the negative part.
We are only left to control

(1-w_{k}^{+})\int(f_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(f_{k})df_{k},

on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
We start by bounding the weight
1-w_{k}^{+}=(1+G_{k}^{+}/G_{k}^{-})^{-1}\leq G_{k}^{-}/G_{k}^{+}
. For
\theta<0
and
X_{k}\geq 0
, we have
(X_{k}-\theta)^{2}\geq X_{k}^{2}
, so that

G_{k}^{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-\frac{n}{2}{(X_{k}-\theta)^{2}-\frac{|\theta|^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}}\,d\theta\leq e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta.

The change of variable
\theta=\sigma_{k}(pu)^{1/p}
yields

G_{k}^{-}\leq p^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\Gamma\left(1/p\right)\sigma_{k}e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{2}}.

For
G_{k}^{+}
, restricting the integral on
[X_{k}/2,3X_{k}/2]
, so that
(X_{k}-\theta)^{2}\leq X_{k}^{2}/4
, provides

G_{k}^{+}\geq\int_{X_{k}/2}^{3X_{k}/2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}{(X_{k}-\theta)^{2}-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}}\,d\theta\geq e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{8}}\int_{X_{k}/2}^{3X_{k}/2}e^{-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta\geq{X_{k}}e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{8}}e^{-\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{X_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{p}}.

Combining, we obtain

1-w_{k}^{+}\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{k}}{X_{k}}\exp\left\{-\frac{3}{8}nX_{k}^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{X_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{p}\right\},

where the constant depends only on
p
. We now employ Lemma
8
, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and thanks to our choice of
M
in (
35
), we have for all
k\leq n_{\gamma}
and large enough
n
,

\frac{L}{2}\sigma_{k}\leq\frac{f_{0,k}}{2}\leq X_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad nX_{k}^{2}\geq\frac{8}{p}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{p}\frac{X_{k}^{p}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}}.

This leads to the bound, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and under
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
,

1-w_{k}^{+}\lesssim\exp\{-\frac{1}{16}nf_{0,k}^{2}\}.

(44)

We are left to control

\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta+{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+\theta)^{2}-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+\theta)^{2}-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta}.

First use
(\theta+{\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}\leq 2\theta^{2}+2({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}
and upper bound similarly as before

\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+\theta)^{2}-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta\leq e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta^{2}e^{-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta\lesssim e^{-\frac{nX_{k}^{2}}{2}}p^{\frac{3}{p}-1}\Gamma\left(3/p\right)\sigma_{k}^{3}.

For the denominator, restricting the integral up to
c\sigma_{k}
, where
c>0
is a small enough constant to be chosen below, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+\theta)^{2}-\frac{\theta^{p}}{p\sigma_{k}^{p}}}\,d\theta\geq e^{-\frac{c^{p}}{p}}\int_{0}^{c\sigma_{k}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+\theta)^{2}}\,d\theta\geq c\sigma_{k}e^{-\frac{c^{p}}{p}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+c\sigma_{k})^{2}}.

Finally, combining the two previous bounds yields

\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta\lesssim({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}X_{k}^{2}+\frac{n}{2}(X_{k}+c\sigma_{k})^{2}}.

By Lemma
8
, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, we have
L\sigma_{k}\leq 2X_{k}
and
2X_{k}\leq 3f_{0,k}
, thus

\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta\lesssim({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}e^{2\frac{c}{L}(1+\frac{c}{L})nX_{k}^{2}}\lesssim({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}e^{\frac{9}{2}\frac{c}{L}(1+\frac{c}{L})nf_{0,k}^{2}}.

Using the bound (
44
) above, we finally have

\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}(1-w_{k}^{+})\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta\lesssim\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}(({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}e^{\frac{9}{2}\frac{c}{L}(1+\frac{c}{L})nf_{0,k}^{2}})e^{-\frac{1}{16}nf_{0,k}^{2}}.

Using
{\theta_{M}^{*}}=X_{k}-({\theta_{M}^{*}})^{p-1}/n\sigma_{k}^{p}\leq X_{k}\leq f_{0,k}/2
and
L\sigma_{k}\leq 2X_{k}
, this bound becomes

\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}(1-w_{k}^{+})\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta\lesssim\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}f_{0,k}^{2}(1+e^{\frac{9}{2}\frac{c}{L}(1+\frac{c}{L})nf_{0,k}^{2}})e^{-\frac{1}{16}nf_{0,k}^{2}}.

We choose
c>0
so that
\frac{9}{2}\frac{c}{L}(1+\frac{c}{L})=\frac{1}{32}
and we obtain, on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, recalling
\alpha>\beta
(hence
\gamma>\beta
)

\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}(1-w_{k}^{+})\int(\theta-\mu_{k})^{2}g_{k}^{-}(\theta)\,d\theta\lesssim\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}f_{0,k}^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{32}nf_{0,k}^{2}}=o(n^{-1})=o(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

(45)

Conclusion of the Proof
. We gather the bounds (
43
) and (
45
) into equation (
34
) and finally obtain

E_{f_{0}}\Pi[\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}>m\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X]\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}=o(1).

We are only left to show that the indicator
1\!{\rm l}\{\|f_{0}-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 2m\varepsilon_{n}\}
in (
33
) goes to
1
under
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
. Taking the expectation and using
\|\mu-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}-\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}
by the triangle inequality,

P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}[\|\mu-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 2m\varepsilon_{n}]\geq P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}[\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq m\varepsilon_{n}\,,\,\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 3m\varepsilon_{n}].

Recall the definition of the event
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, which satisfies
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}(\mathcal{B}_{n})\to 1
(Lemma
8
),

\mathcal{B}_{n}:=\left\{|\xi_{k}|\leq\sqrt{2\log n},\quad\text{for all }k=1,\dots,n_{\gamma}\right\}.

On this event and under
P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
, using
\alpha>\beta
, we have for
n
large enough

\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}=\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}\frac{1}{n}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{n_{\gamma}}{n}\times 2\log n=o(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

Second, recall that

, defined by

, satisfying

so that, since

,

\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}=\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{-2p}}{n^{2}}|\theta_{M}^{*}|^{2(p-1)}\geq\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{-2p}}{n^{2}}|X_{k}|^{2(p-1)}.

On the event

\displaystyle\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}\gtrsim\sum_{k\leq n_{\gamma}}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{-2p}}{n^{2}}\left|f_{0,k}\right|^{2(p-1)}\gtrsim\frac{n_{\gamma}^{2(1+\beta+p(\alpha-\beta))}}{n^{2}}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{2}.

Combining the two previous facts shows that for a small enough constant

where

. We have thus shown that the indicator in (

-expectation, hence also in

-probability, which concludes the proof.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3

Recall
N_{\beta}=\lfloor n^{1/(2\beta+1)}\rfloor
is the usual frequency cutoff. Using independence of the
f_{k}
drawn from the prior, it is sufficient to show

\prod_{k\geq 1}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\geq\exp(-n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}),

where

\delta_{k}:=\begin{cases}\,1/\sqrt{n},&\quad 1\leq k\leq N_{\beta},\\
\,2Lk^{-1/2-\beta},&\quad k>N_{\beta}\end{cases}.

(46)

Indeed, recalling
\varepsilon_{n}=n^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log^{\eta^{\prime}}n=N_{\beta}^{-\beta}\log^{\eta^{\prime}}n=\sqrt{N_{\beta}/n}\log^{\eta^{\prime}}n
, we have, for
n
large enough,

\sum_{k}\delta_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k\leq N_{\beta}}\frac{1}{n}+4L^{2}\sum_{k\geq N_{\beta}}k^{-1-2\beta}\leq\frac{N_{\beta}}{n}+4L^{2}N_{\beta}^{-2\beta}\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{2}.

The following inclusions hold,

\left\{f\,:\,\forall k\geq 1,\quad|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}\right\}\subset\left\{f\,:\,\sum_{k\geq 1}|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|^{2}\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\delta_{k}^{2}\right\}\subset\{f\,:\,||f-f_{0}||_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\},

establishing the claimed sufficiency of the condition above.
Let us start with the indices
k\leq N_{\beta}
. We bound from below

\displaystyle\Pi\left[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}\right]=P\left[|\sigma_{k}\zeta_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right]=\int_{(f_{0,k}-1/{\sqrt{n}})/\sigma_{k}}^{(f_{0,k}+1/{\sqrt{n}})/\sigma_{k}}h_{p_{k}}(x)dx,

for all
k\geq 1
. Since
h_{p_{k}}
the density of
\zeta_{k}
is symmetric (see (
3
)), we can assume without loss of generality that
f_{0,k}\geq 0
in the bounds to follow. We can then bound the previous integral using the smallest value of
h_{p_{k}}
, which leaves us with

\Pi\left[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}\right]\geq\frac{2}{\sigma_{k}\sqrt{n}}h_{p_{k}}\left(\frac{f_{0,k}+1/\sqrt{n}}{\sigma_{k}}\right).

Using the lower bound on
h_{p}
given in Lemma
9
, we obtain

\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\gtrsim\frac{U_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\frac{f_{0,k}+1/\sqrt{n}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\right\},

(47)

where

U_{k}:=\sqrt{p_{k}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{p_{k}}-\frac{p_{k}}{12}\right)\sigma_{k}^{-1}.

Using first the smoothness condition on
f_{0}
, we have
f_{0,k}\leq Lk^{-1/2-\beta}
and then using
k\leq N_{\beta}
we obtain
k^{-1/2-\beta}\geq n^{-\frac{1/2+\beta}{2\beta+1}}=1/\sqrt{n}
. Since
p_{k}\in(0,1]
, we obtain

(f_{0,k}+1/\sqrt{n})^{p_{k}}\leq(L+1)^{p_{k}}k^{-p_{k}(1/2+\beta)}\leq(L+1)k^{-p_{k}(1/2+\beta)}.

Using
\sigma_{k}\leq 1
and
p_{k}\leq 1
, we have
\sqrt{p_{k}}e^{\frac{1}{p_{k}}-\frac{p_{k}}{12}}\geq 1\geq\sigma_{k}
and thus
U_{k}\geq 1
.
Finally plugging these two inequalities in (
47
), we obtain,

\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{L+1}{p_{k}}\left(\frac{k^{-\beta-1/2}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\{-(L+1)z_{k}\}.

From this bound, it follows

\prod_{k\leq N_{\beta}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\gtrsim\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\leq N_{\beta}}(\log\sqrt{n}+(L+1)z_{k})\right\}.

Employing the summability condition (
17
) on
z_{k}
, we obtain
C>0
and
\eta>1
, such that

\prod_{k\leq N_{\beta}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\geq e^{-CN_{\beta}\log^{\eta}n}\geq e^{-n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}.

For the other part of the product, indexed by
k>N_{\beta}
, we have
\delta_{k}=2Lk^{-1/2-\beta}
, such that

[f_{0,k}-\delta_{k},f_{0,k}+\delta_{k}]\supset[-Lk^{-1/2-\beta},Lk^{-1/2-\beta}].

Recalling
r_{k}=\sigma_{k}^{-1}k^{-1/2-\beta}
, we can then bound the probability of interest as

\Pi\left[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}\right]\geq\Pi\left[|f_{k}|\leq Lk^{-1/2-\beta}\right]=P\left[|\zeta_{k}|\leq L\sigma_{k}^{-1}k^{-1/2-\beta}\right]=P\left[|\zeta_{k}|\leq Lr_{k}\right].

Recalling that
\zeta_{k}
has symmetric density
h_{p_{k}}
and survival function
\overline{H}_{p_{k}}
, we get

\Pi\left[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}\right]\geq 1-2\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(Lr_{k}).

Taking now the product over
k>N_{\beta}
, using
L\geq 1
and the monotonicity of
\overline{H}_{p_{k}}
, we obtain

\prod_{k>N_{\beta}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\geq\exp\left\{\sum_{k>N_{\beta}}\log\left(1-2\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(r_{k})\right)\right\}.

Thanks to the condition (
16
), we have
r_{k}\geq 1
. Using Lemma
9
, we get

\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(r_{k})\lesssim\frac{e^{\frac{1}{p_{k}}}}{\sqrt{p_{k}}}r_{k}e^{-r_{k}^{p_{k}}/p_{k}}\lesssim\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{p_{k}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}+\log r_{k}-\frac{r_{k}^{p_{k}}}{p_{k}}\right\}.

Using first
\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{p_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{k}}
and then
\log r_{k}=\frac{1}{p_{k}}\log r_{k}^{p_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{2p_{k}}r_{k}^{p_{k}}
, we get

\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(r_{k})\lesssim\exp\left\{\frac{2}{p_{k}}-\frac{r_{k}^{p_{k}}}{2p_{k}}\right\}\lesssim\exp\left\{-\frac{r_{k}^{p_{k}}}{4p_{k}}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\frac{z_{k}}{4}\right\},

where the assumption
r_{k}^{p_{k}}\geq 8
was used in the last inequality. As the general term of a convergent series (thanks to condition (
17
)) the previous bound goes to zero. Thus, for
n
large enough and
k>N_{\beta}
, we have
\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(r_{k})\leq 1/4
. We then use the inequality
\log(1-2x)\geq-4x
, available for
x\leq 1/4
, to obtain

\prod_{k>N_{\beta}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\geq\exp\left\{-4\sum_{k>N_{\beta}}\overline{H}_{p_{k}}(r_{k})\right\}\geq\exp\left\{-C\sum_{k>N_{\beta}}\exp\left\{-\frac{z_{k}}{4}\right\}\right\}.

This last sum goes to
0
when
n\to\infty
, as the remainder of a converging series, such that

\prod_{k>N_{\beta}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq\delta_{k}]\geq e^{-n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}},

for any
n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\to\infty
.
∎

A.5
Proof of Theorem
4
and Corollary

Corollary
2
is directly obtained by applying (the second part of) Lemma

to lower bound

in Theorem
4
. We now prove Theorem
4
. In the random design regression setting of (
20
), Lemma
4
and
5
show that it is sufficient to get, for some constant
C>0
,

\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n}]\geq e^{-Cn\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}.

(48)

Recall
f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}
is the approximating network in (
24
) obtained by Lemma
10
and satisfies
||f-f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}||_{\infty}\leq 2LN_{\beta}^{-\beta}
. For the considered
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim N_{\beta}^{-\beta}=\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
, the triangle inequality implies, for some constant
d>0
small enough, that
\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n}]\geq\Pi[||f-f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}||_{\infty}\leq d\varepsilon_{n}].

We now relabel the approximating network
f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}
, so that it has larger width
N_{\alpha}
(corresponding to the prior network). To this end, we define the following partition of the index set
\{0,\dots,N_{\alpha}-1\}
into

S_{n}=\Big\{k=l\frac{N_{\alpha}}{N_{\beta}},\;l=0,1,\dots,N_{\beta}-1\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}=\{0,\dots,N_{\alpha}-1\}\setminus S_{n}

(49)

We then let

f^{\ast}(x)=b^{\ast}+\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}w_{k}^{\ast}(x-a_{k}^{\ast})_{+},

where
b^{\ast}=f_{0}(0)
,

w_{k}^{\ast}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if }k\in T_{n},\\
w_{0;l},\ \text{for}\ l=\dfrac{kN_{\beta}}{N_{\alpha}},&\text{if }k\in S_{n}.\end{array}\right.

and

a_{k}^{\ast}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if }k\in T_{n},\\[2.0pt]
l/N_{\beta},\ \text{for }l=\dfrac{kN_{\beta}}{N_{\alpha}},&\text{if }k\in S_{n}.\end{array}\right.

It is straightforward to check that
f^{\ast}=f^{\star}_{N_{\beta}}
(notice the different ‘star notation’), so that this is indeed a relabeling of the approximating shallow network, where only the
N_{\beta}
weights indexed by
k\in S_{n}
are nonzero; for these indices, note that
a_{k}^{\ast}=k/N_{\alpha}
. According to Lemma
10
and after the relabeling, the nonzero weights still satisfy
|w_{0}^{\ast}|\leq LN_{\beta}^{(1-\beta)_{+}}
and
|w_{k}^{\ast}|\leq 2LN_{\beta}^{1-\beta}
for
k=1,\dots,N_{\alpha}-1
and
|b^{\ast}|\leq L
. With this relabeling, we now have

\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n}]\geq\Pi[||f-f^{*}||_{\infty}\leq d\varepsilon_{n}].

Recalling
a_{k}=k/N_{\alpha}
in the definition of the prior (
25
), notice that whenever
w_{k}^{\ast}\neq 0
, it holds
a_{k}^{\ast}=a_{k}
, so that

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\alpha}-1}w_{k}^{\ast}\big((x-a_{k})_{+}-(x-a_{k}^{\ast})_{+}\big)=0

and thus

\displaystyle f-f^{*}=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}w_{k}(x-a_{k})_{+}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\alpha}-1}w_{k}^{\ast}(x-a_{k}^{\ast})_{+}+(b-b^{\ast})=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}(w_{k}-w_{k}^{\ast})(x-a_{k})_{+}+(b-b^{\ast}).

From the triangle inequality, follows

\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n}]\geq\Pi\left(\big\|\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}(w_{k}-w_{k}^{\ast})(x-a_{k})_{+}\big\|_{\infty}\leq d\varepsilon_{n}/2\,,\,|b-b^{\ast}|\leq d\varepsilon_{n}/2\right).

Since
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}(x-a_{k})_{+}\leq 1
, the probability displayed above is further lower bounded by

\Pi\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}|w_{k}-w_{k}^{\ast}|\leq d\varepsilon_{n}/2\,,\,|b-b^{\ast}|\leq d\varepsilon_{n}/2\right).

Using independence, this probability can be split in the following product (recall
w_{k}^{*}=0
for
k\in T_{n}
), where we set
c=d/2

\underbrace{\prod_{k\in S_{n}}\Pi(|w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha})}_{I}\times\underbrace{\prod_{k\in T_{n}}\Pi(|w_{k}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha})}_{II}\times\underbrace{\Pi(|b-b^{\ast}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n})}_{III}.

(50)

We first study term III, which (denoting the density of
\pi_{b}
also by
\pi_{b}
), by symmetry, positivity and continuity, is lower bounded by
2rc\varepsilon_{n}
for
r=\min_{x\in[-2L,2L]}\pi_{b}(x)>0
. The latter lower bound vanishes much slower than
\exp(-n\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
, as long as
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim n^{-s}
for some
s<1/2
, as is the case for the considered
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}
.

We then study term II. Assuming
\varepsilon_{n}/(\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha})\to\infty
, the symmetry and tail assumptions (
4
) and (
6
) on
h
, allow us to lower bound as follows

\Pi(|w_{k}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha})=1-2\overline{H}(\frac{c\varepsilon_{n}}{\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha}})\geq 1-2d_{0}\exp\left\{-d_{1}(\frac{c\varepsilon_{n}}{\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha}})^{q}\right\}.

We can then lower bound the product over indices in
T_{n}\subset\{0,\dots,N_{\alpha}-1\}
as

II\geq\left(1-2d_{0}\exp\left\{-d_{1}^{\prime}(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha}})^{q}\right\}\right)^{N_{\alpha}},

which, provided
\varepsilon_{n}/(\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha})\gtrsim\log^{1/q}n
, can be shown to remain bounded away from zero. Indeed, it is equivalent to show that the negative logarithm remains bounded, which can be verified under the previously mentioned condition, using the inequality
\log(1-x)\geq-x/\sqrt{1-x}
, for
0\leq x<1
.

Finally for the term
I
, assuming without loss of generality (due to the symmetry of
h
) that
w_{k}^{\ast}>0
, using (
5
) we have

\Pi(|w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha}){\geq}\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}\int_{w_{k}^{\ast}}^{w_{k}^{\ast}+\frac{c\varepsilon_{n}}{N_{\alpha}}}h(x/\sigma_{n})dx\geq cc_{0}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha}}\exp(-c_{1}|w_{k}^{\ast}+c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha}|^{p}\sigma_{n}^{-p}),

so that, since the cardinality of
S_{n}
is
N_{\beta}
,

I=\prod_{k\in S_{n}}\Pi(|w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}|\leq c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha})\geq\left(c_{0}^{\prime}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{N_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}\right)^{N_{\beta}}\exp\left\{-c_{1}\sigma_{n}^{-p}\sum_{k\in S_{n}}|w_{k}^{*}+c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha}|^{p}\right\}.

We study the sum in the exponent. Using the available bounds for
w_{k}^{\ast}
,
combined with
|a+b|^{p}\leq|a|^{p}+|b|^{p}
valid for any
p\in(0,1]
, we can bound the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S_{n}}|w_{k}^{*}+c\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha}|^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim|w_{0}|^{p}+\sum_{k\in S_{n}\setminus\{0\}}|w_{k}^{\ast}|^{p}+N_{\beta}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{p}}{N_{\alpha}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N_{\beta}^{p(1-\beta)_{+}}+N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p}+N_{\beta}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{p}}{N_{\alpha}^{p}}.

(51)

We next note that the third term is dominated by the second if and only if
\varepsilon_{n}/N_{\alpha}\ll N_{\beta}^{1-\beta}.
Since
N_{\alpha}>N_{\beta}
(recall
\alpha>\beta
), for this condition to hold it suffices that
\varepsilon_{n}\ll N_{\beta}^{2-\beta}
, which is always the case since
\beta\leq 2
. Hence the second term always dominates the third. Recalling
p\in(0,1]
, we compare the first and second terms to find that:

1.

for
\beta\in(1,2]
, we have
1+p-\beta p\geq(2-\beta)p\geq 0
, hence the second term dominates the first and overall in the right hand side of the bound;

2.

for
\beta\in(0,1]
, we have
1+p-\beta p>p(1-\beta)_{+}
, hence again the second term dominates the first and overall in the right hand side of the bound.

For any
\beta\in(0,2]
we thus get that

I\geq\exp(-N_{\beta}(c_{2}+\log(\frac{N_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}}))-c_{3}\frac{N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p}}{\sigma_{n}^{p}})

and combining with the bounds for the previous terms, we obtain that under the assumption

\varepsilon_{n}/(\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha})\gtrsim\log^{1/q}n,

(52)

\Pi(||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n})\geq\exp\left(-N_{\beta}\big(c_{2}+\log(\frac{N_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}})\big)-c_{3}\frac{N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p}}{\sigma_{n}^{p}}-{c_{4}}-n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right).

The latter lower bound, is in turn lower bounded by
\exp(-c_{5}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
for a large enough constant

, provided the following hold

1.

N_{\beta}\lesssim n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
, or equivalently
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}
which always holds;

2.

N_{\beta}\log(N_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}/\varepsilon_{n})\lesssim n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
or equivalently

\log(N_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}/\varepsilon_{n})\lesssim(\varepsilon_{n}/\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast})^{2},

which for
\sigma_{n}\leq 1
,
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim n^{-1/2}
and
N_{\alpha}
as defined above, holds if, for some
\delta>0
,

\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\log^{1/2+\delta}(n),

(53)

3.

N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p}\lesssim\sigma_{n}^{p}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
, or equivalently

\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\Big(\frac{N_{\beta}^{1-\beta}}{\sigma_{n}}\Big)^{p/2}.

(54)

Hence, it suffices that
\varepsilon_{n}
satisfies (
52
) and (
54
) to obtain the prior mass bound (
48
).
For the oracle choice of
\sigma_{n}
: we optimize the choice of
\sigma_{n}
based on (
52
),(
54
), and then check that (
53
) also holds. Since (
52
),(
54
) imply that

\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\{\sigma_{n}N_{\alpha}\log^{1/q}n\}\vee\{\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\Big(\frac{N_{\beta}^{1-\beta}}{\sigma_{n}}\Big)^{p/2}\},

(55)

where the first term in the maximum improves with a faster decay of
\sigma_{n}
while the second deteriorates, we choose
\sigma_{n}
to balance the two terms, resulting in

\sigma_{n}\asymp N_{\alpha}^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}N_{\beta}^{\frac{p}{2+p}-\beta}\log^{-\frac{2}{q(2+p)}}n.

This results in

\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim N_{\alpha}^{\frac{p}{2+p}}N_{\beta}^{\frac{p}{2+p}-\beta}\log^{\frac{p}{q(2+p)}}n=\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}(N_{\alpha}N_{\beta})^{\frac{p}{2+p}}\log^{\frac{p}{q(2+p)}}n.

This
\varepsilon_{n}
also satisfies (
53
), as required.

For the non-oracle choice of
\sigma_{n}
, choosing
\sigma_{n}=\varepsilon^{+}_{n}/N_{\alpha}
, where
\varepsilon^{+}_{n}=n^{-2/5}
is the minimax rate for
\beta=2
(that is, for the highest considered smoothness), we have that the inequality arising from the first term in the maximum (

) becomes trivial and the admissible choices of
\varepsilon_{n}
are determined solely by the second term in the maximum. The resulting constraint is

\varepsilon_{n}\geq\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}n^{\frac{p}{2}(\frac{1-\beta}{1+2\beta}+\frac{2}{5}+\frac{1}{1+2\alpha})},

which as
p
becomes smaller, approaches
\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}
.
∎

:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S_{n}}\left|w_{k}^{\ast}+\frac{c\varepsilon_{n}}{\sqrt{2}n}\right|^{p_{n}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|w_{0}|^{p_{n}}+\sum_{k\in S_{n}\setminus\{0\}}|w_{k}^{\ast}|^{p_{n}}+N_{\beta}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{p_{n}}}{n^{p_{n}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{p_{n}(1-\beta)_{+}}+(2L)^{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{p_{n}(1-\beta)+1}+\frac{N_{\beta}\varepsilon_{n}^{p_{n}}}{n^{p_{n}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N_{\beta}^{p_{n}(1-\beta)_{+}}+N_{\beta}^{p_{n}(1-\beta)+1}+\frac{N_{\beta}\varepsilon_{n}^{p_{n}}}{n^{p_{n}}}.

As in the proof of Theorem
4
, it is straightforward to check that the second term dominates in the right hand side, using
p_{n}\leq 1
and
\beta\leq 2
. Hence, for some
c^{\prime\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Bigg(-c^{\prime\prime}N_{\beta}\Big(1+\log\big(\frac{e^{-c_{0}/p_{n}}}{\sqrt{p_{n}}}\big)+\log\big(\sigma_{n}n/\varepsilon_{n}\big)+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{-p_{n}}}{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{(1-\beta)p_{n}}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Bigg(-c^{\prime\prime}(N_{\beta}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{-p_{n}}}{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p_{n}})\Bigg),

where for the last bound we used that, under our assumptions on
p_{n},\sigma_{n},\varepsilon_{n}
, the two logarithms in the exponent in the top line are negative.

Combining the bounds on terms I, II and III, we get, for some constant
c^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
and for sufficiently large
n
,

\Pi(||f-f_{0}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{n})\geq\exp\Bigg(-c^{\prime\prime\prime}(N_{\beta}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{-p_{n}}}{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p_{n}})\Bigg),

which in turn is lower bounded by
\exp(-Cn\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
for large enough
C>0
, provided

1.

N_{\beta}\lesssim n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
or equivalently
\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}
;

2.

it holds

\frac{\sigma_{n}^{-p_{n}}}{p_{n}}N_{\beta}^{1+(1-\beta)p_{n}}\lesssim n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}

or equivalently

\varepsilon_{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\frac{n^{(\frac{1-\beta}{1+2\beta}+t)p_{n}/2}}{\sqrt{p_{n}}}.

The rate
\varepsilon_{n}\geq\varepsilon_{n}^{\ast}\sqrt{\log{n}}
in the statement trivially satisfies the first condition, while for the second one, under our assumptions
n^{(\frac{1-\beta}{1+2\beta}+t)p_{n}/2}
is bounded, so it is again satisfied.
∎

B
Additional results

B.1
Handling other statistical models

We briefly explain how to derive, from the prior mass results obtained in the present paper, contraction rates in other statistical models (above we restricted for simplicity to regression models).
Suppose for instance to fix ideas that one wishes to derive a posterior contraction rate for the neural network (log)–priors of Section
3
in density estimation. This is done in a similar way as for the models investigated in
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
. For instance, results for nonparametric binary classification can be derived similarly as well, or in any model where one can appropriately link Kullback–Leibler neighborhoods to
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
–neighborhoods.

In the case of density estimation, one observes iid data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of unknown density
f
on
[0,1]
. Given one of the neural network priors of Section
3
, one can use the exponential transform
g\to e^{g}/\int_{0}^{1}e^{g}
to induce a prior
\Pi
on density functions.

Using the lower bound on the prior mass in the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
–norm investigated in the proofs of Theorems
4
–
5
, one can use a generic result on
\rho
–posterior contraction such as Theorem 3 in
L’Huillier et al. 2023
. The latter requires to bound from below the prior mass of a Kullback–Leibler type neighborhood: in density estimation for log-density priors this mass can be bounded from below by the prior mass of a
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
–neighborhood (see e.g. Lemma C.2 in
Agapiou and Castillo 2024
). Theorem 4.1 in
L’Huillier et al. 2023
then gives posterior contraction in terms of the
\rho
–Rényi divergence: in density estimation the latter is bounded from below by a constant times the
L^{1}
–norm: this gives posterior contraction in the
L^{1}
–norm in the density estimation model as desired.

B.2
Series prior: Upper bound for Sobolev truths

Define the
L_{2}
–Sobolev–type ball, for any
\beta,L>0
, as

\mathcal{S}^{\beta}(L):=\left\{f=(f_{k})\,:\,\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{2\beta}|f_{k}|^{2}\leq L^{2}\right\}.

Theorem 6
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
\alpha>\beta>0
. Suppose
f_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{\beta}(L)
for some
L>0
and assume
X^{(n)}\sim P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}
from the model (
7
). Then, for any
\rho\in(0,1)
, starting from the prior
\Pi=\Pi(p,\alpha)
defined in (
9
)–(
10
), as
n\to\infty
, we have

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}\left[||f-f_{0}||_{2}\leq M\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X\right]\to 1,

where
\varepsilon_{n}
is given in (
13
) and
M>0
is a large enough constant.

As in the proof of Theorem
1
, we recall that it suffices to show, for some
C,D>0

\Pi\left[\|f-f_{0}\|_{2}\leq D\varepsilon_{n}\right]\geq\exp(-Cn\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

Recall
N_{\gamma}
is the closest integer to
n^{1/(1+2\beta+p(\alpha-\beta))}
. From
f_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(L)
, there exists
D>0
, such that, as
n
gets large enough

\{f\,:\,1\leq k\leq N_{\gamma},\,|f_{0,k}-f_{k}|\leq 1/\sqrt{n}\}\cap\{f\,:\,\forall k>N_{\gamma},\,|f_{k}|\leq k^{-\beta-1/2}\}\subset\{f\,:\,||f-f_{0}||_{2}^{2}\leq D\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\}.

Indeed, suppose
f
belongs to the left hand side set displayed just above. Using first Parseval’s equality and
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
, we have a constant
D>0
, such that, for
n
large enough

\displaystyle||f-f_{0}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|^{2}+2\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}|f_{k}|^{2}+2\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}|f_{0,k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N_{\gamma}}{n}+2\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}k^{-2\beta-1}+2\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}k^{-2\beta}k^{2\beta}|f_{0,k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N_{\gamma}}{n}+\beta^{-1}N_{\gamma}^{-2\beta}+2L^{2}N_{\gamma}^{-2\beta}\leq D\varepsilon_{n}^{2}.

Using independence of
(f_{k})
under the prior, leads to

\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{2}^{2}\leq D\varepsilon_{n}^{2}]\geq\prod_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq 1/\sqrt{n}]\times\prod_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|f_{k}|\leq k^{-1/2-\beta}].

For the first product, we lower bound the integrand with its minimal value

\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq 1/\sqrt{n}]\geq c_{0}\int_{\sigma_{k}^{-1}(f_{0,k}-1/\sqrt{n})}^{\sigma_{k}^{-1}(f_{0,k}+1/\sqrt{n})}\exp(-c_{1}|x|^{p})\,dx\geq\frac{2c_{0}}{\sigma_{k}\sqrt{n}}\exp\left(-c_{1}\frac{|f_{0,k}+1/\sqrt{n}|^{p}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}}\right).

Using
|a+b|^{p}\leq|a|^{p}+|b|^{p}
, available for
p\in(0,1)
, we get

\prod_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq 1/\sqrt{n}]\geq\prod_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\frac{2c_{0}}{\sigma_{k}\sqrt{n}}\times\exp\left(-c_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\frac{|f_{0,k}|^{p}+n^{-p/2}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}}\right).

Since
p<1
, one notes that
\gamma\leq\alpha
by definition, so that
\sigma_{k}^{-1}\geq k^{1/2+\gamma}
. Lemma
6
(applied with
\alpha
therein replaced by
\gamma
) then implies

\prod_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\frac{2c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{k}}\geq e^{-(1/2+\gamma-\log(2c_{0}))N_{\gamma}}\geq e^{-C_{0}N_{\gamma}}.

Furthermore, noting that
n^{-p/2}\lesssim k^{-1/2-\beta}
, for
k\leq N_{\gamma}
and
\alpha>\beta
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\frac{|f_{0,k}|^{p}+n^{-p/2}}{\sigma_{k}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}k^{p(\alpha+1/2)}|f_{0,k}|^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}k^{p(\alpha-\beta)}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}k^{p(\alpha+1/2)}|f_{0,k}|^{p}+N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta)+1}.

To take care of the last sum we use Holder’s inequality with exponents
a=2/p>1
and
b=2/(2-p)
(such that
1/a+1/b=1
), as well as
f_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(L)
, to obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}k^{p(\alpha+1/2)}|f_{0,k}|^{p}\leq N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta+1/2)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}k^{p\beta}|f_{0,k}|^{p}\leq N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta+1/2)}N_{\gamma}^{1-p/2}L^{p}\lesssim N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta)+1}.

Finally, noting that for
n
large enough,
N_{\gamma}\leq N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta)+1}\leq n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
, we obtain large enough constants
C_{1},\tilde{C}_{1}>0
, such that

\prod_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|f_{k}-f_{0,k}|\leq 1/\sqrt{n}]\geq\exp(-C_{0}N_{\gamma}-C_{1}N_{\gamma}^{p(\alpha-\beta)+1})\geq\exp(\tilde{C}_{1}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

For the second product, since
\zeta_{k}
’s are symmetric, have density
h
and survival function
\overline{H}
,

\prod_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|f_{k}|\leq k^{-1/2-\beta}]=\prod_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}\Pi[|\zeta_{k}|\leq k^{\alpha-\beta}]=\prod_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}(1-2\overline{H}(k^{\alpha-\beta})).

Using Condition (
6
), and the inequality
\log(1-2x)\geq-4x
, valid for
x\in[0,1/4]
, we obtain

\prod_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}(1-2\overline{H}(k^{\alpha-\beta}))\geq\exp\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}\log\left(1-2d_{0}e^{-d_{1}k^{q(\alpha-\beta)}}\right)\geq\exp\{-4d_{0}\sum_{k>N_{\gamma}}e^{-d_{1}k^{q(\alpha-\beta)}}\}.

Since the series
\sum_{k}e^{-ck^{\delta}}
converges for any given constants
c,\delta>0
, one deduces that the last display converges to
1
as
n\to\infty
and in particular is bounded from below by
1/2
for
n
large enough. Gathering the previous bounds for both products provides a constant
C>0
, such that for
n
large enough, one obtains

\Pi[||f-f_{0}||_{2}^{2}\leq D\varepsilon_{n}^{2}]\geq\exp(-Cn\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).\qed

B.3
Series prior: Lower bound (case
p=1
)

The following is the special case of Theorem
2
where
p=1
(Laplace priors on coefficients).

Theorem 7
.

Let
\alpha>\beta>0
. Suppose the data
X
follows the white noise model for some true function
f_{0}
, and let the prior on
f
as in (
9
) be defined by taking
\zeta_{k}
s to be drawn iid from a standard Laplace distribution. There exists a function
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\beta,L)
such that, if

\zeta_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}(1,\alpha,\beta)=n^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta+1}},

then for any
\rho\in(0,1]
, for
m>0
small enough, as

,

E_{f_{0}}\Pi_{\rho}[\|f-f_{0}\|_{2}<m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]\to 0.

To simplify the notation, we give the proof first for the standard posterior
\rho=1
.
Let us choose
f_{0}
as the function in
\mathcal{F}(\beta,L)
defined through its basis coefficients by
f_{0,k}=Lk^{-1/2-\beta}
for
\beta>0
.
Let us recall the definitions of
\gamma
in (
12
) and
N_{r}
in (
15
) and let us set
p=1
. With this notation we have
\zeta_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}(1,\alpha,\beta)=N_{\gamma}^{-\beta}
by definition. Denoting, for any square-integrable function
g
, by
\|g\|_{N_{\gamma}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\gamma}}g_{k}^{2}
, it is enough to prove, for small
m>0
to be chosen and
n_{\gamma}=dN_{\gamma}
for some small enough constant
d
to be chosen below, that

E_{f_{0}}\Pi[\|f-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]\to 1.

Under the prior distribution, coefficients
f_{k}
have distribution
\sigma_{k}\text{Lap}(1)
.
By writing the Laplace distribution as a mixture of two exponential distributions (one for the positive part, one for the negative part), and using Bayes’ formula, one can write the posterior distribution
\mathcal{L}(f_{k}\,|\,X)
of the
k
th coefficient
f_{k}
as a mixture

\mathcal{L}(f_{k}\,|\,X)=w^{+}_{k}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},1/n)_{+}+(1-w^{+}_{k})\mathcal{N}(\nu_{k},1/n)_{-},

(58)

where
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})_{+}
denotes the distribution of
Z\vee 0
if
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})_{-}

Z\wedge 0
, and where we have set

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=X_{k}-\frac{1}{n\sigma_{k}},\qquad\nu_{k}=X_{k}+\frac{1}{n\sigma_{k}}

(59)

\displaystyle w_{k}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{n\mu_{k}^{2}/2}\Phi(\sqrt{n}\mu_{k})}{e^{n\nu_{k}^{2}/2}\bar{\Phi}(\sqrt{n}\nu_{k})+e^{n\mu_{k}^{2}/2}\Phi(\sqrt{n}\mu_{k})},

(60)

where
\Phi
is the distribution function of the standard normal distribution and
\bar{\Phi}=1-\Phi
.

The triangle inequality gives, denoting
\mu=(\mu_{k})
for
\mu_{k}
as in (
59

\|f_{0}-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq\|f_{0}-f\|_{n_{\gamma}}+\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}
. This implies

\Pi[\|f-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]\geq\Pi[\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]\cdot 1\!{\rm l}\{\|f_{0}-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 2m\zeta_{n}\}.

It now suffices to show that each term of the product of the right hand side of the last display goes to
1
in probability under
P_{f_{0}}
.

Starting with the indicator, its expectation under
P_{f_{0}}
equals, denoting
X=(X_{k})
, and using
\|\mu-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}-\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}
by the triangle inequality,

P[\|\mu-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 2m\zeta_{n}]\geq P[\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq m\zeta_{n}]\cdot 1\!{\rm l}\{\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}\geq 3m\zeta_{n}\}.

By definitions of
\mu
and
(\sigma_{k})
, we have, for a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
,

\|\mu-X\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{-2}}{n^{2}}\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{n_{\gamma}^{2+2\alpha}}{n^{2}}=C^{\prime}\zeta_{n}^{2}.

for

and

the constant such that

.
Hence for
m

3m<C^{\prime}
, the indicator in the last but one display equals

.
Also,
\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}/n

since
\alpha>\beta

P[\|X-f_{0}\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq m\zeta_{n}]=1+o(1)
.

To conclude the proof, it suffices to check that
\Pi[\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}\leq m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]
goes to

in probability under
P_{f_{0}}
. By Markov’s inequality, for
\mathcal{A}_{n}
the event as in (

\displaystyle\Pi[

\displaystyle\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}>m\zeta_{n}\,|\,X]1\!{\rm l}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{(m\zeta_{n})^{2}}\int\|f-\mu\|_{n_{\gamma}}^{2}d\Pi(f\,|\,X)1\!{\rm l}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1\!{\rm l}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}}{(m\zeta_{n})^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\left[w_{k}^{+}\int(f_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},1/n)_{+}(f_{k})+(1-w_{k}^{+})\int(f_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\nu_{k},1/n)_{-}(f_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1\!{\rm l}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}}{(m\zeta_{n})^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\left[\int(f_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},1/n)_{+}(f_{k})+c_{1}e^{-c_{2}n^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2\gamma+1}}}\int(f_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\nu_{k},1/n)_{-}(f_{k})\right],

where for the last inequality we use
w_{k}^{+}\leq 1
and the uniform bound on

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{n^{\prime}\mu_{k}^{2}/2}\Phi(\sqrt{n^{\prime}}\mu_{k})}{e^{n^{\prime}\nu_{k}^{2}/2}\bar{\Phi}(\sqrt{n^{\prime}}\nu_{k})+e^{n^{\prime}\mu_{k}^{2}/2}\Phi(\sqrt{n^{\prime}}\mu_{k})}.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\alpha>\beta>0
. Suppose the data

follows the white noise model for some true function
f_{0}
, and let the prior on
f
be as in (
9
) and taking
\zeta_{k}
’s to be drawn iid from a standard Laplace distribution. For any function

, if

\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}(1,\alpha,\beta)=n^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta+1}},

then for

E_{f_{0}}\Pi[\|f-f_{0}\|_{2}>M\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X]\to 0.

Let

, where

\|f-f_{0}\|^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}(f_{k}-f_{0,k})^{2}=(\sum_{k\leq K_{n}}+\sum_{k>K_{n}})(f_{k}-f_{0,k})^{2}
and distinguish two cases:

We deal first with the indices

By definition of

and for

\sum_{k>K_{n}}f_{0,k}^{2}\leq L^{2}K_{n}^{-2\beta}=L^{2}N_{\gamma}^{-2\beta}\lesssim\varepsilon_{n}^{2}.

As
\|f^{[K_{n}^{c}]}-f_{0}^{[K_{n}^{c}]}\|_{2}\leq\|f^{[K_{n}^{c}]}\|_{2}+\|f_{0}^{[K_{n}^{c}]}\|_{2}

\sum_{k>K_{n}}\int f_{k}^{2}d\Pi(f_{k}\,|\,X_{k})=o(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

(62)

Let us consider the event

\mathcal{A}:=\bigcup_{j\geq 0}\ \left\{\max_{jn<k\leq(j+1)n}|\varepsilon_{k}|\leq\sqrt{2\log{\{(j+1)^{2}n}\}}\right\}.

A union bound argument shows that

. By definition of
K_{n},\sigma_{k}
and the fact that
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\beta,L)
, we have, for any

that
|f_{0,k}|\leq(1/2)(n\sigma_{k})^{-1}
, provided the constant

is chosen large enough.
This implies, on the event

\mu_{k}=X_{k}-\frac{1}{n\sigma_{k}}\leq-\frac{1}{2n\sigma_{k}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\log{n}}{n}}.

Since

, so that for
K_{n}<k\leq n
, on
\mathcal{A}

\mu_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{4n\sigma_{k}}.

Since the growth of
\sigma_{k}^{-1}
is polynomial in
k
, this also implies that the inequality in the last display also holds for large enough
n
and any index

, so that the inequality holds for all
k>K_{n}
. Similarly, we have, on the event
\mathcal{A}
and for any

\nu_{k}\geq\frac{1}{4n\sigma_{k}}.

Now bounding

and

by

, one can bound from above, on the event

\displaystyle\int f_{k}^{2}d\Pi(f_{k}\,|\,X_{k})\leq\int f_{k}^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},1/n)_{+}(f_{k})+\int f_{k}^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\nu_{k},1/n)_{-}(f_{k})\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{2}}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{\nu_{k}^{2}},

2
with
\mu=\mu_{k},\nu=\nu_{k}
and
\sigma^{2}=1/n
. One deduces that on
\mathcal{A}
, the last display is bounded from above by
C\sigma_{k}^{2}
. Since
\sum_{k>K_{n}}\sigma_{k}^{2}\lesssim K_{n}^{-2\alpha}=o(K_{n}^{-2\beta})

, and next using that
K_{n}^{-2\beta}=O(\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
, one concludes that
\sum_{k>K_{n}}\int f_{k}^{2}d\Pi(f_{k}\,|\,X_{k})=o_{P}(\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
as desired.

It now remains to deal with the indices

. Here one can follow the final bounds in the lower bound argument in Theorem
7
and extend these to any function

. Denoting by

from its basis coefficients

as follows:

for

and, for

h_{k}=\begin{cases}\,\mu_{k}:=X_{k}-1/(n\sigma_{k})&\text{if }\mu_{k}>(\log{n})/\sqrt{n},\\
\,\nu_{k}:=X_{k}+1/(n\sigma_{k})&\text{if }\nu_{k}<-(\log{n})/\sqrt{n},\\
\,0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(63)

Note that the first two cases in the above definition are mutually exclusive, as

by definition. Now one can further write, for

\displaystyle\Pi[

\displaystyle\|f-f_{0}\|_{K_{n}}\geq M\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X]

\displaystyle=\Pi[\|f-f_{0}\|_{K_{n}}\geq M\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X]1\!{\rm l}\{\|f_{0}-h\|_{K_{n}}\leq M\varepsilon_{n}/2\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Pi[\|f-f_{0}\|_{K_{n}}\geq M\varepsilon_{n}\,|\,X]1\!{\rm l}\{\|f_{0}-h\|_{K_{n}}>M\varepsilon_{n}/2\}

where one uses the triangle inequality and that indicators and probabilities are bounded from above by
1
. It now suffices to show that the expectation under

of the last display goes to
0
.

Starting first with the indicator, and denoting by

the function with coefficients

for

and
0

P_{f_{0}}^{(n)}[\|f_{0}-h\|_{K_{n}}>M\varepsilon_{n}/2]\leq P[\|e\|_{K_{n}}/\sqrt{n}+\|(1/(n\sigma_{k}))\|_{K_{n}}>M\varepsilon_{n}/2].

, we have

. Hence for

. It now suffices to bound

. By Markov’s inequality, this is bounded by a multiple of
(n\varepsilon_{n}^{2})^{-1}E[\|e\|_{K_{n}}^{2}]=(n\varepsilon_{n}^{2})^{-1}K_{n}^{2}=o(1)

.

.

Lemma 2

For any
\mu<0

, the following bound holds

\int x^{2}d\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})_{+}(x)\leq 2\frac{\sigma^{4}}{\mu^{2}}.

The integral in the display of the lemma equals, with

\mathcal{I}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\exp\{-(x+z)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})\}dx/\mathcal{D},

where

Lemma 3

d\in\left[\frac{1}{2M},\frac{1}{M}\right]
, such that

|w_{0}|=M\left|f\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)-f\left(\frac{1}{2M}\right)+f\left(\frac{1}{2M}\right)-f(0)\right|=M\left|\frac{f^{\prime}(c)}{2M}+\frac{f^{\prime}(d)}{2M}\right|\leq\frac{L}{2}\leq L,

where we have used the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
to upper bound the derivative. For
k=1,\dots,M-1
, again using the mean value theorem, there exist
c_{k}\in I_{k},d_{k}\in I_{k-1}
, such that

\displaystyle|w_{k}|

\displaystyle=M\left|f\left(\frac{k+1}{M}\right)-f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)-\left[f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)-f\left(\frac{k-1}{M}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=M\left|\frac{f^{\prime}(c_{k})}{M}-\frac{f^{\prime}(d_{k})}{M}\right|\leq L\left(\frac{2}{M}\right)^{\beta-1}\leq 2LM^{1-\beta},

where in the first upper bound we have used the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(L)
, while in the second the fact that
\beta\leq 2
.

The assumption
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(L)
also implies
|f(0)|\leq L
, while
|k/M|\leq 1
for all
k\in 0,\dots,M-1
, hence, combining with the bounds on
|w_{k}|
we get
f_{M}\in N\!N_{1}(M,2LM^{(1-\beta)_{+}}\vee 1)
, as claimed.

For the last uniform approximation bound, fix
x\in[0,1]
and let
k
be the unique index among
\{0,\dots,M-1\}
such that
x\in I_{k}
. Using that
f_{M}=f^{\star}_{M}
, we have

|f(x)-f_{M}(x)|=\left|f(x)-f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)-M\left[f\left(\frac{k+1}{M}\right)-f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)\right]\left(x-\frac{k}{M}\right)\right|.

(72)

If
\beta\in(0,1]
, using the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(L)
, we have

|f(x)-f_{M}(x)|\leq\left|f(x)-f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)\right|+M\left|f\left(\frac{k+1}{M}\right)-f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)\right|\left|x-\frac{k}{M}\right|\leq 2LM^{-\beta},

since
|x-k/M|\leq M^{-1}
. For
\beta\in(1,2]
, using the mean value theorem, there exist
c_{k},d_{k}\in I_{k}

such that

f(x)=f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)+f^{\prime}(c_{k})\left(x-\frac{k}{M}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f\left(\frac{k+1}{M}\right)=f\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)+\frac{1}{M}f^{\prime}(d_{k}).

Plugging the last two identities into (
72
), using again the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(L)
and the fact that
|x-k/M|\leq M^{-1}
, we again get the bound

|f(x)-f_{M}(x)|=|f^{\prime}(c_{k})-f^{\prime}(d_{k})|\left|x-\frac{k}{M}\right|\leq LM^{1-\beta}\left|x-\frac{k}{M}\right|\leq LM^{-\beta}\leq 2LM^{-\beta}.

∎
