Knowledge, attitude and practices on antimicrobial resistance among medical students in Africa: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Happy Asiimwe, Justine Kulu, Allan Bosis, Christine Nannungi, Musa Kagimu, Hillary Ngolobe, Ivaan Pitua, Felix Bongomin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains a global public health threat contributing to over 1.27 million deaths in 2019. The knowledge, attitude and practices (KAP) on AMR among medical students have a direct impact on the future of combating this global problem. Despite the presence of country-specific data on KAP among medical students, there still lacks a consolidated study synthesising this information in Africa. This study aims to assess the KAP on AMR among medical students in Africa.MethodsThis will be a systematic review of studies assessing the KAP on AMR among medical students in Africa. Relevant studies will be identified using PubMed, SCOPUS, CINAHL, Google Scholar and African Journals Online (AJOL) accessed via HINARI Access to Research for Development and Innovation programme or direct Open Access for PubMed, AJOL and Google Scholar. All searches will be conducted for studies published from 2015 to 2025. Both quantitative and qualitative studies will be included. A narrative synthesis will be conducted as well as a meta-analysis if data permitting the analysis will be obtained. Risk of bias will be assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist for cross-sectional studies.Ethics and disseminationThis review involves synthesising data from existing studies; therefore, no ethical approval will be required. Our study findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at local and international conferences.PROSPERO registration numberThis study has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD420251207301.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a critical global public health and development challenge, as highlighted by the WHO.1 Estimates from 2022 indicate that bacterial AMR was associated with approximately 4.95 million deaths globally in 2019, with 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to resistant bacterial infections. Notably, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) exhibited the highest mortality rate, with 23.5 deaths per 100 000 population linked to AMR.2 Recent data indicate that AMR rates across SSA continue to rise, driven by inadequate healthcare infrastructure, weak surveillance systems and high rates of inappropriate antibiotic use, with some subregions recording resistance rates to first-line agents exceeding 50%.3 Furthermore, projections suggest that without effective interventions, AMR could incur an additional US$1 trillion in healthcare costs by 2050.4

The primary drivers of AMR include the inappropriate and excessive use of antimicrobials across human, animal and agricultural sectors. Global antibiotic consumption has been directly linked to regional patterns of AMR, with countries recording higher antibiotic sales between 2010 and 2021 consistently demonstrating higher proportions of resistant isolates.5 Globally, it is estimated that a substantial and persistent proportion of antibiotic prescriptions are unnecessary or inappropriate, a pattern that has shown little improvement over two decades across both high- and low-income settings.6 7 This irrational use of antimicrobials in the clinical setting can be due to patient factors, such as demand for antibiotics even when not indicated by the healthcare worker8 9 or healthcare provider factors, such as diagnostic uncertainty10 11 and insufficient knowledge and awareness of AMR.12 13 Unfortunately, such misuse exacerbates the emergence of infections that are increasingly difficult to treat due to the diminishing efficacy of existing antibiotics.14

Critically, the WHO AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) classification system which categorises antibiotics by their importance for human medicine and their propensity to drive resistance provides a practical global stewardship framework and surveillance purposes.15 The 2024 United Nations Political Declaration on AMR set a global target that at least 70% of antibiotic use in human health should consist of Access antibiotics by 2030,15 16 building on the earlier WHO benchmark of 60%.17 However, there are ongoing concerns about the alignment of national antibiotic guidelines in many African low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with AWaRe recommendations, and the WHO AWaRe antibiotic book has been proposed as a critical resource to enhance the rational use of Access category agents in these settings.18 19 Understanding whether medical students in Africa are aware of and aligned with AWaRe guidance is therefore an important and previously underexamined dimension of knowledge, attitudes and practice (KAP) assessment that this review will address.

While medical students represent the future prescribing workforce, it is essential to contextualise their role within the broader AMR landscape in Africa. In primary care settings across LMICs, nurses and clinical officers are also key antibiotic prescribers, with primary care collectively accounting for 85%–95% of total human antibiotic consumption in these settings.20 21 KAP deficiencies are therefore not unique to medical students; addressing AMR effectively requires coordinated interventions spanning all prescriber cadres, and there is evidence that prescribing patterns in primary care in LMICs have not shown improvement in recent years.22

Furthermore, OTC dispensing of antibiotics without a prescription is a pervasive and persistent problem across Africa, representing a major community level driver of AMR.23 Studies show that Watch and Reserve category antibiotics those most critical to preserve are regularly dispensed without prescription in African community pharmacies and drug shops, and rates of this practice have not significantly declined in recent years.24 These patterns underscore that improving medical students’ KAP on AMR, while necessary, represents only one component of a comprehensive multisectoral response needed to reduce AMR burden on the continent.

Enhancing the knowledge base of health professionals is a critical strategy for mitigating AMR. Practising physicians have emphasised the need for targeted educational programmes on AMR, particularly for junior clinicians.25 Several studies have described the KAP of medical students in Africa. A study involving final-year medical students in Egypt revealed misconceptions and inappropriate antibiotic use, underscoring the need for robust educational interventions and training to promote rational antibiotic prescribing and curb resistance.26 A study in Malawi, among final year medical students, found that the majority of them had adequate knowledge on AMR; however, it still highlighted the need for incorporation of AMR into the curriculum.27 Similarly, two studies conducted in Uganda among medical students found that the students had good knowledge of AMR; however, they still agreed that a separate course unit on AMR is necessary.28 29 All these findings highlight a gap in AMR knowledge acquisition among medical students. However, there remains no study that has consolidated these findings across Africa.

A systematic review of KAP on AMR in Africa has been reported; however, it involved a diverse study population, underscoring the need to synthesise population-specific data that can guide targeted intervention development.30 Furthermore, despite the availability of country-specific studies on the KAP regarding AMR among medical students in various African nations, there is a lack of comprehensive, continent-wide research synthesising these findings. Such consolidated data are essential for informing targeted interventions to enhance medical students’ understanding of AMR and improve clinical practices across Africa. This study aims to address this gap by systematically investigating the KAP of medical students in Africa concerning AMR. This will contribute towards the achievement of the Global Action Plan on AMR objectives 1 and 2, which highlight the need to increase awareness and improve knowledge on AMR through research.31 Furthermore, it will provide a foundation for evidence-based reforms in the medical curriculum.

Review question

What is the KAP on AMR among medical students in Africa?

Methods

Study design

This study will assess the KAP on AMR among medical students in Africa. The study has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD420251207301. Our study protocol will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocols (PRISMA-P) (online supplemental file 1)32 and will be conducted in line with the 2020 updated PRISMA guidelines (online supplemental file 2).33 The selection process of eligible studies will be conducted using the PRISMA flow diagram (online supplemental file 3).

Population

This review will target studies involving medical students, pharmacy students, nursing students, dental students and allied health students (undergraduate and postgraduate) enrolled in medical schools or universities within African countries.

Comparator(s) or control(s)

Comparison will be done across different groups of medical students, that is, pre-clinical versus clinical year students, students with versus students without prior AMR training and based on differences in institutions or countries within Africa. No external comparator (eg, non-medical students) will be included unless used as a subgroup analysis for context.

Outcome(s)

Primary outcomes will be the knowledge scores on AMR (proportion of correct answers or mean scores), attitude scores on AMR (Likert scale means or proportions of favourable attitudes) and practice scores on antimicrobial use and stewardship (proportion of appropriate practices) and secondary outcomes will be the determinants of KAP (sociodemographic data).

Study design

Both quantitative and qualitative studies will be included in the protocol.

Time

Studies published from 2015 to 2025. This timeframe was selected because it aligns with the period following the publication of the WHO Global Action Plan on AMR (2015) which marked a turning point in global AMR awareness and research activity and to capture contemporary, policy relevant evidence.

Information sources

We will develop our literature search strategies using the medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words on AMR. We will search PubMed, African journals online, SCOPUS, CINAHL and Embase accessed via HINARI Access to Research for Development and Innovation programme or direct Open Access for PubMed, AJOL and Google Scholar to obtain studies matching our review. We will also review the reference lists of the searched studies to obtain any other relevant studies. Grey literature will be searched from Google Scholar using the search terms ;(AMR or antibiotic resistance or drug resistance) AND (medical students OR health profession students OR nursing students OR pharmacy students) AND (Knowledge OR Attitude OR practices OR perceptions OR awareness) and the first 150 search outputs will be screened for inclusion.

Search strategy

Our study will involve both quantitative and qualitative studies. Only studies published in English from 2015 to 2025 will be included. We will use the PubMed search strategy for the initial search. After exploring the PubMed strategy, it will be adapted to fit the syntax and subject headings of the other databases.

A pilot search of PubMed was conducted to confirm the availability of primary data; using the search strategy below, 415 records were retrieved as of June 2026, confirming that sufficient primary literature exists to justify this review.

Medical students

The complete full search strategies for all databases and platforms including adapted syntax, subject headings, filters and date limits for each are provided as search strategies (online supplemental file 4).

Mendeley desktop software will be used as our referencing software to store the obtained search articles.

The deduplication process will be performed in two stages. First, we will use Covidence to detect duplicate files using the DOI (Digital Object Identifier), PMID (PubMed Identifier), Titles/Abstracts, First Author and year of publication. Studies with exact matches will be deleted automatically. Two independent reviewers (HN and NC) will screen the remaining potential duplicates manually, and a third reviewer (AB) will investigate any discrepancies. The counts of studies before and after each stage will be reported in the33 flow diagram.

Data collection process

Studies will be selected by title and abstract screening, full text screening and inclusion and exclusion criteria. Two reviewers (HN and NC) will independently screen titles, abstracts and full texts and extraction. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or through third reviewer (AB) involvement.33 The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram will be used to describe the selection process, including studies obtained and excluded at every stage, including reasons for exclusion. Screening of the studies will be performed using Covidence. We will develop a standardised data extraction form in Microsoft Excel to collect the data on each study, including: first Author, year of publication, study design, country, study population and sample size. Risk of bias assessment

We will use the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist tool for cross-sectional studies to assess for risk of bias of studies to include in the analysis.34 The tool is made up of 11 items comprising: focus of the study, methods, recruitment, data collection, number of participants, results, among others.

Two independent reviewers (HN and NC) will perform the risk assessment, and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or the involvement of a third reviewer (AB).

Studies will be classified as either low risk or high risk. If the majority of the studies have a high risk of bias, the analysis will be conducted with caution.

Data analysis plan

We will conduct a narrative synthesis, meta-analysis and thematic analysis for our review based on the relatedness between our findings.

For heterogeneous data, a narrative synthesis will be performed, and it will describe the KAP on AMR based on the differences by regions and study populations.

Data will be synthesised quantitatively if at least three studies report comparable numerical outcomes on medical students’ KAP regarding AMR. Quantitative data will be summarised as means and proportions depending on the comparability and availability of the data. For outcomes eligible for pooling, a random effects model will be used to obtain a pool for the KAP on AMR among medical students. Heterogeneity will be evaluated using the I² statistic and χ2 (Q) test, with I² values of 25%, 50% and 75% representing low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. Sub-group meta-analysis will be performed for the different groups according to: region of the study, year of study of the participants (pre-clinical or clinical years), type of institution (private or public institution) and prior AMR training or stewardship training. STATA V.15.0 will be used to perform the meta-analysis.

Publication bias is a significant concern in KAP systematic reviews, as studies finding high levels of knowledge or appropriate practices are more likely to be published, potentially inflating aggregate estimates. Publication bias will be assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s test and Begg’s test (p<0.05 will indicate potential bias)35 and results will be reported transparently.

For qualitative data, we will use thematic synthesis to identify and synthesise key themes related to attitudes and practices on AMR. NVivo V.14 software will be used to analyse qualitative data.

Results will be presented as statements, tables, charts and forest plots.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

We will assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome using the Grades for Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation.

Discussion

This systematic review protocol outlines the methodology for synthesising continent-wide evidence on KAP regarding AMR among medical students in Africa. The rationale for this review is compelling despite the existence of individual country-level studies and a systematic review and meta-analysis having been conducted;30 no consolidated Africa-wide synthesis exists for medical students. Given that medical students and other health professionals are the prescribers of the future, understanding the current state of their KAP is critical to designing effective interventions at the medical education level.

We predict that the findings will reveal considerable heterogeneity in KAP levels across African countries, with variability linked to medical curriculum differences, AMR training and healthcare infrastructure. Consistent with existing evidence,28 29 we expect that knowledge levels may be generally acceptable but that significant gaps remain in practice, especially regarding AWaRe-aligned prescribing, stewardship engagement and awareness of non-prescription antibiotic dispensing as an AMR driver. These anticipated findings will have direct implications for curriculum reform and will provide evidence to guide the integration of AMR and antibiotic stewardship as formal components of undergraduate medical education in Africa.

The strengths of this review include its comprehensive multi-database search strategy, dual reviewer processes and inclusion of both quantitative and qualitative designs. However, several limitations must be acknowledged; the English language restriction may introduce language bias, potentially excluding studies from Francophone or Lusophone African countries. Heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measurement tools across settings may complicate data pooling, and the 2015 publication date, while methodologically justified, may cause the exclusion of relevant earlier studies.

Publication bias will be carefully assessed, given the well-recognised tendency for studies with favourable or statistically significant findings to be disproportionately represented in the published literature.35 36 Furthermore, it is important to contextualise the contribution of this review within the broader AMR landscape in Africa: improving medical student KAP is a necessary but insufficient intervention. Addressing AMR effectively also requires targeting other prescriber groups such as nurses and clinical officers, regulating OTC antibiotic dispensing and strengthening national antibiotic stewardship frameworks aligned with WHO AWaRe guidance.

The findings of this review are expected to provide actionable evidence for medical school’s curriculum committees and AMR stewardship programme21 37 designers across Africa. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this systematic review, as it synthesises previously published data.

Ethics and dissemination

Our study will not require any ethical approval since it involves a review of published literature. Although ethical approval is not required, inquiries regarding research integrity may be directed to the Research Integrity Office at Gulu University, Uganda, mkiduma@yahoo.com. The review writing will be in line with the protocol, and any amendments will be registered. The review results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at local and international conferences.
