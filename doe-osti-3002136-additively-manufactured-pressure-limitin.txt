 ABSTRACT

The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program previously demonstrated an
additively manufactured (AM) irradiation capsule (commonly referred to as a rabbit) from 316H stainless
steel (SS) to be inserted into the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL).1 This report details efforts to design and fabricate an AM pressure limiting structure (PLS) into
one of the end caps of a rabbit capsule and qualify it for insertion into HFIR. The PLS includes a thin,
cylindrical rupture wall; a shield; and internal supports to facilitate printing and ensure mechanical
integrity. Its overall dimensions are 9 mm tall and 10 mm in diameter—equivalent to approximately one-
fourth the size of a AAA battery. The PLS maintains safe internal operating pressures for a rabbit capsule
while in the reactor. Although this application is specific to HFIR, the approach lends itself to further
applications in industrial, aeronautical, advanced space, and power generation environments. Several PLS
rabbits capsules have been successfully designed, fabricated, pressure tested, and qualified for future
insertion into HFIR for irradiation and postirradiation evaluation.

 1. BACKGROUND

A key thrust of the AMMT program is manufacturing and demonstrating AM components for use in the
deployment of nuclear energy. Additive manufacturing can offer a unique opportunity to tailor the
designs of components to perform better in the harsh irradiation environments (i.e., high temperatures,
pressures, and/or corrosivity) found in nuclear reactors. This report describes an effort to design and print
316L SS AM rabbit capsules with integrated PLSs for containment of test specimens in the ORNL HFIR.
A previous report1 described the same effort for a standard version of a 316 SS AM rabbit capsule.1

Annually, as many as approximately 600 rabbits may be used in HFIR for specimen containment. Most of
these rabbits are fabricated of aluminum using conventional machining and welding. Figure 1 shows a
schematic view of a standard HFIR rabbit.2 The outside of the rabbit consists of a cylindrical, thin-walled
housing welded to two end caps. Its external geometry must conform to the physical and environmental
requirements of the reactor. The inside of the rabbit depends on the dimensions and requirements of the
test specimens. Although it is typically cylindrical, a desirable characteristic is the ability to tailor the
inside of the housing cost effectively to accommodate specimen-specific requirements.

  Figure 1. Schematic view of a standard HFIR rabbit capsule with solid end caps filled with specimens for
 irradiating.

All housings and end caps for the irradiation capsules described within this report were printed using a
General Electric (GE) Concept M2 LaserCUSING laser powder bed fusion printer with 15–45 m
diameter, gas atomized, 316L SS powder. The powder (TRUFORM 316-3) was procured from
Linde/Praxair. Except for the rupture walls of the PLS end caps, the capsule housings and end caps were

 2. RESULTS†

For a hermetically sealed irradiation capsule in an experimental nuclear reactor, overpressurization is a
safety concern. Overpressurization may arise from thermally induced chemical or nuclear reactions within
the capsule or from anomalous external mechanical events. Overpressurization may lead to capsule
swelling, which, in turn, may locally restrict the flow of cooling fluid and negatively affect the contents of
the capsule and adjacent capsules. In the extreme case, flow restriction can lead to the reactor coolant
boiling and the reactor fuel melting.

Smith et al.4 examined the mode of failure and extent of deformation for pressurized aluminum rabbit
capsules with standard end caps. They found a wide range of rupture pressure for the housings of these
capsules (4,300–5,600 psi) and several modes of failure. To reduce these variations and risks of

2.1 DESIGN AND TESTING OF THE PLS

The design of the PLS was based on the classic forward-acting rupture disc5 wherein overpressurization
initiates irreversible failure of the disc, which relieves pressure. This PLS is simple, compact, and
generally compatible with the harsh environments found in a nuclear reactor. Because of the cylindrical
symmetry of the irradiation capsules and the challenges of printing unsupported horizontal features, the
rupture walls of the PLS were chosen to be generally cylindrical with the cylinder axis parallel to the
build direction. Figure 3a shows some of the creative rupture wall geometries examined and tested for the
PLS. Except for the cylinder and cone geometries, all other freestanding geometries with approximately
160 m thick walls (Figure 3b) leaked ethanol and were eliminated from further consideration.

  Figure 3. (a) Schematic view of a PLS HFIR rabbit capsule and solid models of freestanding wall external
 geometry considered for the PLS and (b) image of the printed PLS geometries with walls approximately
 160 μm thick.

 3

A successful PLS for this application is one with a burst (or rupture) pressure (Pb) that is less than the
pressure at which the irradiation capsule housing plastically yields and irreversibly swells in the reactor
(Py or yield pressure). The yield pressure for the irradiation capsule was experimentally determined using
40 printed irradiation capsules with monolithic end caps and nuts for attaching pressure fixturing. Figure
4 shows the yield pressure to be approximately 6,250–6,500 psi. This yield pressure places an upper
bound on the burst pressure (i.e., Pb < 6,250–6,500 psi.)

 pressure testing and (right) one after approximately 12,000 psi.

To identify designs for a PLS with suitable burst pressure, 48 full-length irradiation capsules with

Pressure testing of two of the irradiation capsules with monolithic end caps, one with tall cylindrical
rupture walls and the other with tall conical rupture walls, revealed a significant difference in the mode of
wall deformation. Figure 6 shows high-resolution XCT results for a pressure sequence for the tall
cylindrical PLS wall for a vertical plane that contains the rupture site and the long axis of the capsule.
Symmetrical barreling of the cylindrical PLS wall is apparent at 4,000 psi and develops further until
rupture occurs at 5,372 psi. The rupture site is near the midpoint of the wall, where the deflection of the
wall is largest.

 Figure 6. High-resolution XCT (side view planar section) shows the progression of wall deformation and
 failure for a monolithic, tall cylindrical PLS as pressure is incrementally increased. The red circle indicates
 the point of failure (or rupture site).

A similar pressure sequence to that in Figure 6 is shown in Figure 7 for the monolithic, tall conical PLS
wall. The swelling of the conical wall appears to begin at 3,000 psi near the base of the cone, where the
wall diameter and hoop stress are largest, and continues upward as the pressure increases. The onset of
swelling for the conical wall (approximately 3,000 psi) is less than that for the cylindrical wall
(approximately 4,000 psi), and the ratio of these pressures (3,000/4,000) is nearly equal to the inverse of
the ratio of their maximum diameters (6.30/8.72). This result is consistent with hoop stress driving the
onset of plastic yielding, as shown in Eq. (1).

Because the shield surrounding the conical PLS wall provides support for the swollen PLS wall, this
progression of deformation tends to reduce the height and average diameter of the PLS wall that is
susceptible to swelling, which, in turn, tends to increase the burst pressure. For this reason, conical walls
do not seem to be a solution for reducing the burst pressure of these PLSs.

Figure 8 shows the outcome of pressure testing for more full-length monolithic irradiation capsules with
both tall and medium cylindrical PLS walls. The burst pressures for all the tall cylindrical walls are well
below the yield pressure for the capsule (6,250–6,500 psi), and the distribution of the burst pressure is
surprisingly narrow (Pb = 5,270 ± 120 psi). For the PLSs with medium cylindrical walls, the average burst
pressure is greater than the pressure at which the rabbit housing begins to swell; therefore, the medium
cylindrical geometry is not suitable for this application.

Twenty sets of components were printed on the build plate, shown in Figure 9, in 17 h (print time <1 h
per set). After removing all components from the build plate with wire electrical discharge machining, the
ends of the capsule housings were counterbored to accommodate the outside diameters of the end caps.
End caps with tall cylindrical PLSs were first electron beam welded to one end of the housings (see
Figure 10). Test specimens were then loaded into the housing, which was followed by electron beam
welding of the perforated, short, solid caps. Before loading the irradiation capsule into the reactor, the
capsule was evacuated, backfilled with argon, and hermetically sealed with a tungsten arc weld over the
perforation.

Pressure testing of several welded PLS caps has been completed. The average burst pressure for the
welded caps (4,764 ± 36 psi) is about 500 psi less than that for the monolithic caps (5,270 ± 120 psi), yet
the distribution of pressure is narrow. Figure 11 shows a comparison of the behavior of the welded and
monolithic caps. Unlike the monolithic cap that undergoes a simple symmetrical barreling of the
cylindrical PLS wall before rupturing (Figure 11b), the welded cap exhibits a symmetrical barreling and
an elongation of approximately 0.8 mm before rupturing (Figure 11a). The performance of either cap,
monolithic or welded, is suitable for use with an irradiation capsule, and two sealed irradiation capsules
with welded cylindrical PLS caps have been qualified and accepted for insertion into HFIR for testing
using methods and procedures described previously 1.

2.2 PREPARATION FOR IRRADIATION

An irradiation campaign for AM capsules that incorporate the PLS caps is part of this work scope. As
described in previous work,1 various critical assembly and inspection practices must be demonstrated and
accepted by the reactor user facility (in this case, HFIR) before reactor qualification and insertion. This
critical activity ensures readiness for irradiation and maintains alignment with HFIR safety and
performance requirements. The qualification effort encompassed

 approval of updated engineering drawings and
 revision of the Experiment Authorization Bases Document (EABD) (see Appendix A).

The qualification documents are used to verify that the irradiation experiment meets reactor insertion
specifications and that all critical characteristics for safety and design are properly documented in
accordance with Nuclear Quality Assurance (NQA)-1 quality assurance protocols. For this specific
irradiation campaign, six assemblies were received and inspected, each including

 one AM pressure-relief cap,
 one AM capsule body, and
 one solid AM cap with a backfill hole (virtually identical to the part used in the initial AM
 experiments described earlier1).

Prototypic components representative of the batch intended for irradiation in this second campaign are
shown in Figure 12.

 11

 Figure 12. Prototypic capsule components intended/qualified for HFIR irradiation.

This campaign mirrors previous AM capsule irradiations conducted during HFIR cycle 510. The objective
is to generate data for the new pressure relief design that is directly comparable to that of the original
capsule design, thus enabling a clear evaluation of performance across capsule styles. Although parity in
irradiation performance is expected, minor design differences required updates to the qualification
documentation.

The most significant change, aside from the modified end cap geometry, is the transition from 316H SS
(original capsules) to standard 316L SS (current capsules). This material change affects welding and
reactor operation specifications. Although welding procedures for the two alloys are identical, their
mechanical properties differ: 316H SS exhibits higher tensile strength than 316L. To address this change,
the revised EABD established unified safety limits based on the lower strength of 316L SS. As a result,
both alloys are now formally qualified, with minimum strength values of 316L SS credited for all cases.

All required components, specifications, and qualification documents are in place to support HFIR
insertion. At least three capsules are planned for inclusion in the next available irradiation cycle.

 3. CONCLUSIONS

For the AM rabbits with PLS end caps, a design and method for printing a compact, integrated PLS have
been demonstrated. Critical design characteristics, both geometrical and dimensional, have been identified
for a 316L SS PLS device. The methodology is based on simple mechanical modeling and experimental
iteration and is directly applicable to other material systems. The AM approach facilitates rapid
customization and tuning of the burst pressure to accommodate the requirements of the application. The
thickness, height, method of fabrication, and geometry of the burst wall have all been shown to affect the
burst pressure. Yet for fixed dimensions and geometry, the distribution of the burst pressure appears to be
narrow (approximately ±2%). Other complex geometries for the burst wall have been investigated, but the
simple cylindrical geometry was proven to be most reliable for this application. Although this approach in
its current form is not likely amenable to lower-pressure applications (approximately <1,000 psi), it
clearly represents a viable, low-cost, rapid, and adaptive alternative for a wide range of scientific and
industrial applications. The focus of future efforts may include understanding the cause of and eliminating
pores found in the burst wall, demonstrating the design for other materials (e.g., aluminum), and
developing a reverse-buckling version of the present design.
