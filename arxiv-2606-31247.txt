FlexiSLM: A Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rate Spoken Language Model
Jiaqi Li, Chaoren Wang, Xiaohai Tian, Mingjie Chen, Xinyu Liang, Xu Li, Yufan Lin, Junwen Qiu, Jun Zhang, Lu Lu, Haizhou Li, Zhizheng Wu

Abstract
Spoken language models (SLMs) extend LLMs to speech input and output. Existing SLMs represent speech at fixed frame rates (e.g., 25 25 or 12.5 12.5 Hz), ignoring the time-varying information density of speech and offering no flexibility to trade off quality for speed at inference time. Recent audio tokenizer research has proposed dynamic frame rate speech coding, which exploits this non-uniformity and enables two new capabilities: very low average frame rates and frame rate controllability. However, this technique has not yet been applied to SLMs. We introduce Flexi ble S poken L anguage M odel (FlexiSLM), the first SLM that supports dynamic and controllable frame rates on both speech input and output. Using dynamic frame rate representations, FlexiSLM outperforms fixed-frame-rate 7B models including Qwen2.5-Omni and Kimi-Audio at its high-quality operating points. We further verify that FlexiSLM can be accurately steered down to 4.0 4.0 Hz; at 6.25 6.25 Hz, it roughly halves inference time relative to 12.5 12.5 Hz while retaining strong speech-to-speech quality. Audio samples are available at https://flexislm.github.io .

Ding et al. 2025

These models jointly model text and speech with a large language model (LLM) backbone, but typically represent speech at a fixed frame rate

Related Work

Speech Tokenization.

Speech tokenization converts continuous audio into discrete tokens suitable for speech language modeling.
Early neural audio codecs such as SoundStream
(
Zeghidour et al. 2021
)
and EnCodec
(
Défossez et al. 2022
)
use residual vector quantization (RVQ) to produce acoustic tokens at fixed frame rates (e.g., 50 Hz or 75 Hz), prioritizing reconstruction fidelity.
Semantic tokens derived from self-supervised models like HuBERT
(
Hsu et al. 2021
)
capture linguistic content, and are increasingly used in speech language modeling
(
Borsos et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2024a
;
Ding et al. 2025
)
.
Recent work has pushed toward more efficient representations while maintaining high audio quality: single-codebook approaches (50–75 Hz)
(
Wu et al. 2024
;
Ji et al. 2024
)
and semantic-enhanced codecs (12.5–50 Hz)
(
Li et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.

Recent work has explored dynamic frame rates, leveraging the temporal sparsity of speech so that lower average frame rates reduce the computational cost of speech language models.
FlexiCodec
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
, the tokenizer used in this work, merges 12.5 Hz semantic features based on similarity to achieve dynamic-rate tokenization at an average of 6.25 Hz. The authors of FlexiCodec also demonstrate controllable frame rate tokenization and TTS.
Other dynamic frame rate works, including CodecSlime
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, TFC
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, and VARSTok
(
Zheng et al. 2025
)
, explore higher average frame rates from 18.75 Hz to 40 Hz.
However, dynamic-rate codecs have not been applied within a spoken language model framework, where low frame rates and frame rate controllability offer larger practical benefits.

Overall architecture of FlexiSLM.

Spoken Language Models.

Spoken language models (SLMs) are general-purpose speech processing systems
(
Arora et al. 2025
)
. Analogous to text LLMs, they can follow natural-language instructions across diverse speech tasks.
Recent end-to-end SLMs extend text-based LLMs to directly comprehend and generate speech.
A dominant design follows a three-stage paradigm: a pretrained speech encoder extracts acoustic features, which condition a decoder-only LLM, followed by an additional transformer module or prediction head that predicts speech tokens
(
Wang et al. 2026
;
Xu et al. 2025a
;
Ding et al. 2025
)
.
Kimi-Audio
(
Ding et al. 2025
)
models parallel speech-text at 12.5 Hz using a separate LM head for speech tokens.
Qwen2.5-Omni
(
Xu et al. 2025a
)
adopts a thinker–talker architecture operating at 25 Hz, whose talker module predicts speech tokens.
Active research areas include full-duplex capability (e.g., Interaction Models
(
Défossez et al. 2024
)
), interleaved speech-text sequences (e.g., GLM-4-Voice
(
Zeng et al. 2024
)
),
low-frame-rate audio tokenization for SLMs (e.g., Moshi
(
Défossez et al. 2024
)
), and dual-resolution speech representations (e.g., Fun-Audio-Chat/DrVoice
(
Tan et al. 2025b
;
Tan et al. 2025a
)
and Mimo-Audio
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
group speech into 5 Hz or 6.25 Hz sequences).

3
Method

3.1
Architecture Overview

Figure
2
illustrates the overall architecture of FlexiSLM, a parallel speech-text model with the following components:

Figure 3:
Talker Transformer input-output structure.

Audio Encoder.

This module encodes the user’s speech into a semantic-rich continuous representation suitable for LLM understanding.
We adopt the pretrained Qwen2.5-Omni audio encoder
(
Xu et al. 2025a
)
, which extracts 25 Hz continuous speech features from waveforms.

Frame Merging Module.

This module compresses the number of frames in a sequence. It appears twice in our model: (1) on the input side, it reduces the 25 Hz continuous features from the Audio Encoder to a dynamic-rate sequence
\leq
12.5 Hz; (2) inside the pretrained FlexiCodec audio tokenizer, it merges 12.5 Hz ASR features before quantization.
Both instances share the same merging mechanism.
We describe this module in Section
3.2
.

LLM Backbone (Thinker).

We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
as initialization.
This LLM has also been used in Qwen2.5-Omni and Kimi-Audio to initialize their backbones.

FlexiCodec Audio Tokenizer.

We use the open-source pretrained FlexiCodec to obtain discrete speech tokens as the prediction target of FlexiSLM’s Talker module.
As illustrated in Figure
2
(a), the codec discretizes each frame with Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ
(
Mentzer et al. 2023
)
); each token is paired with a frame length attribute for audio reconstruction.
We use FlexiCodec’s semantic tokens and omit its RVQ acoustic tokens.
Appendix
H
provides more details.

Talker Transformer.

The Talker decodes the Thinker LLM’s hidden states and outputs into FlexiCodec’s dynamic-frame-rate speech tokens.

•

Input:

As shown in Figure
3
, its input sequence runs over the entire user + assistant context.
At each position, the Talker input embedding is projected from the concatenation of (1) the backbone LLM’s last-layer hidden state, (2) a sinusoidal embedding of the target frame rate (Section
3.3
), and (3) the embeddings of the previously emitted speech and frame length tokens.

•

Output:
The Talker produces two parallel output streams: FlexiCodec FSQ codes and their associated frame lengths, enabling dynamic-rate output.
The Talker uses two output LM heads to predict the streams in parallel.

•

Token delay:
As shown in Figure
3
, the Talker’s FSQ token stream is delayed by 5 tokens from the text stream.
This provides a small lookahead that prevents speech from preceding its corresponding text
(
Du et al. 2024b
)
.
The frame length tokens are delayed by an additional one position, allowing the model to predict a frame’s duration after knowing its corresponding speech token
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
.

Audio Decoder.

The audio decoder is a frozen non-autoregressive (NAR) flow-matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
Transformer that decodes mel spectrograms from the speech tokens.
A Vocos
(
Siuzdak 2023
)
neural vocoder then converts the mel spectrogram into 24 kHz speech.
We use the pretrained flow-matching model and vocoder from the open-source FlexiCodec repository.
We provide additional details in Appendix
G
.

Talker-to-Thinker Connection.

In addition to the standard cascaded Thinker-to-Talker information flow
(
Xu et al. 2025a
)
, FlexiSLM contains an optional
Talker-to-Thinker
connection
(
Tan et al. 2025a
)
(the red dashed arrow in Figure
2
) that feeds the Talker’s previously emitted speech-token embeddings back into the Thinker LLM Backbone at the next step.
This gives the Thinker LLM explicit access to what has already been spoken.
The connection projects the concatenation of the Talker’s embeddings (speech code + frame length) and the text embedding into the Thinker LLM’s hidden state.
We can disable this connection by zeroing out the contribution of the Talker’s embeddings in the concatenation.

3.2
Frame Merging Module

As shown in Figure
2
(b),
the Frame Merging Module adaptively compresses a fixed-rate semantic feature sequence by merging adjacent frames that carry redundant information.
Given a sequence of feature vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}
at a base frame rate,
we compute the cosine similarity between consecutive frames:
s_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{t+1}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\|},\quad t=1,\ldots,T{-}1.

If
s_{t}
exceeds a merging threshold
\tau
, frames
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
are grouped and their average is computed.
This process runs greedily from left to right, with contiguous high-similarity frames merged into a single averaged representation.

After merging, each group produces an averaged feature
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and a frame length attribute
l_{k}
denoting the number of original frames in the group.
We interleave the original and averaged features to form an augmented sequence, which is processed by a lightweight Transformer with local attention.
Finally, we retrieve the representations at positions corresponding to the averaged features, yielding the merged sequence with associated frame lengths.

3.3
Controllable Frame Rate

A key feature of FlexiSLM is its ability to control the output frame rate at inference time, enabling a single deployed model to operate across a range of compute budgets without retraining.
For a dynamic frame rate sequence, the average frame rate is defined as
\frac{\text{Total number of frames after merging}}{\text{Audio duration in seconds}}
.
We first describe a baseline threshold-based strategy and its limitations, then introduce our proposed direct frame rate control.
We focus on output frame rate control; input frame rate is controlled by computing the target number of merged frames from the desired rate and selecting a merging threshold per utterance to match it.

Merging Threshold Control (Baseline).

A straightforward approach is to control the merging threshold
\tau
: a higher
\tau
merges fewer frames (higher frame rate), while a lower
\tau
merges more (lower frame rate).
This approach is used in FlexiCodec-TTS
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
.
However, it provides only
indirect
control, with several limitations: (1) the resulting frame rate varies significantly across utterances and datasets, making the speedup difficult to predict (Table
4
); (2) it is a one-to-many mapping: a single threshold maps to a wide distribution of average frame rates, increasing modeling complexity; and (3) it is unintuitive for users unfamiliar with the model architecture.

Direct Frame-Rate Control.

To overcome these limitations, we directly condition the Talker Transformer and the Frame Merging Module on the target average frame rate.
During training, we randomly sample merging thresholds, compute the resulting average frame rate for each utterance, and feed this empirical rate as a conditioning signal.
At inference, the user simply specifies the desired frame rate.

To enable continuous control over a range of frame rates, we encode the scalar frame rate
r
using sinusoidal positional encoding
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
:

\text{PE}(r)=[\sin(r\omega_{1}),\cos(r\omega_{1}),\ldots,\sin(r\omega_{d}),\cos(r\omega_{d})]
,
where
\omega_{i}=10{,}000
are frequency bases.
This encoding serves as one of the inputs for Talker. Each position in the talker input sequence receives the same frame rate condition.

3.4
Training Procedure

Training proceeds in three stages:

Stage 1: Talker Pre-training.

In this stage, we freeze the LLM backbone and train only the randomly initialized Talker.
We train FlexiSLM end to end with TTS data.
This stage uses English samples from Emilia
(
He et al. 2024
)
and Multilingual LibriSpeech (MLS)
(
Pratap et al. 2020
)
, totaling about 100K training hours.
The Talker-to-Thinker connection is disabled in Stages 1 and 2.

Stage 2: Multi-Task LoRA Fine-Tuning.

In this stage, we activate the speech input-side Frame Merging Module, Thinker, and Talker. LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
is applied to the Thinker.
The Frame Merging Module is randomly initialized.
We train on a mixture of speech processing tasks.
Table
2
shows the distribution of the data sources in Stage 2.
A central component is
FlexiSLM-Data
,
a speech-to-speech dialogue corpus constructed for this work.
FlexiSLM-Data is distilled from a 30B Qwen3-Omni
(
Xu et al. 2025b
)
SLM, with data sourced from a wide range of datasets.
We detail our data construction process in Appendix
A
.

Stage 3: Full Fine-Tuning.

This stage further unlocks the backbone’s capacity and improves speech perception. It continues from the Stage 2 checkpoint and uses the same data, with two differences.
First, we perform full-parameter training after merging the LoRA updates back into the target LLM weight matrices.
Second, we enable and train the Talker-to-Thinker connection.

3.5
Training Loss and Configuration

Our model is trained end to end with a weighted cross-entropy loss over multiple token streams.
The total loss is:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\text{text}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}+\lambda_{\text{speech}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}+\lambda_{\text{speech\_len}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}
,
where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}
are the cross-entropy losses for text tokens, FlexiCodec speech codes, and per-token speech frame lengths, respectively.
We set
\lambda_{\text{text}}=2
and
\lambda_{\text{speech}}=\lambda_{\text{speech\_len}}=1
. For non-speech sequences,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}
are set to 0.

We train FlexiSLM on 24 A100 80G GPUs.
During training, we randomly sample an input frame rate target from
\mathcal{U}(4,12.5)
Hz and a FlexiCodec merging threshold
\tau\sim\mathcal{U}(0.85,1.0)
to support multiple frame rates.
The Talker has 630M parameters; the Audio Encoder has 640M parameters; each Merging Transformer has 20M parameters.
We discuss more training details in Appendix
C
.

4.3
Frame Rate Controllability

4.3.1
Frame Rate Control Accuracy

We evaluate how accurately FlexiSLM can control the average output frame rate.
We compare two variants that differ only in their control mechanism: merging threshold control and direct frame rate control.
Table
4
reports the resulting average output frame rates across benchmark datasets.
We focus on the output side because the input frame rate is controlled deterministically and therefore does not require a separate analysis.

\bullet

Direct frame rate control is more accurate than threshold.

Merging threshold control exhibits high per-utterance variance: for example, targeting roughly 8 Hz via
\tau{=}0.90
yields a wide range of frame rates (
3.91
~
10.74
Hz,
\sigma{\approx}0.70
) and varying averages across datasets (
7.91
~
8.34
Hz).
In contrast, direct frame-rate control, used in our final FlexiSLM, closely matches the requested average rates, with errors below
0.1
Hz across all settings.

\bullet

When might merging threshold control still be preferable?

One could argue that a dynamic frame-rate system should adapt to utterance-level redundancy, and that the higher variance of threshold control reflects this adaptivity.
We view the two strategies as complementary.
Threshold control fixes the
compression intensity
applied to local frame pairs, allowing the resulting frame rate to vary with the redundancy of each utterance.
Direct frame-rate control instead fixes the
average frame rate
of each utterance and adjusts the effective compression intensity accordingly.
Thus, threshold control may be preferable when the goal is to apply a consistent compression criterion across utterances.

For FlexiSLM, we adopt direct frame-rate control for two reasons.
First, it reduces the modeling ambiguity faced by the Talker.
As discussed in Section
3.3
, a single merging threshold can correspond to a wide distribution of output frame rates, making the threshold condition an indirect and ambiguous generation target.
Second, predictable compute budgets are important for deployment.
In practical SLM serving, committing to a target frame rate, and therefore an approximate real-time factor, is useful.

6
Conclusion

We presented FlexiSLM, to our knowledge the first spoken language model with dynamic and controllable frame rates.
FlexiSLM applies dynamic frame rates to both speech input and output, and introduces direct frame rate conditioning so that a single model operates from
12.5
down to
4.0
Hz without retraining.
Experiments show that FlexiSLM achieves the best speech-to-speech QA score among 7B SLMs at
12.5
Hz and remains stronger in s2s than fixed-rate 7B baselines at
6.25
Hz.
More aggressive
5.0
and
4.0
Hz settings provide additional low-token operating points with larger but smooth quality degradation.
Halving the output frame rate halves inference time with only
1.0
-point s2s loss.
FlexiSLM demonstrates a practical paradigm for efficient spoken language models.

Limitations

FlexiSLM has several limitations.
First, we have not explored post-training techniques such as RLHF
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
or DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
, which could further improve response quality and alignment.
Second, the current model is not a streaming model; adapting the audio decoder for causal operation is an important next step for FlexiSLM to support real-time conversation.
Third, our training data does not cover reasoning-intensive tasks, multi-turn dialogues, or many multiple-choice questions, limiting generalization to these scenarios.

This section details the pipeline for building a large-scale speech-to-speech dialogue dataset, which we call
FlexiSLM-Data
.
The current dataset is limited to single-turn interactions.
The construction pipeline has the following steps:

•

Prompt collection.
We collect text prompts from public QA, instruction-following, and dialogue datasets (Table
7
). For multi-turn datasets, we use the user’s first-turn utterance as the prompt, skipping samples whose first turn is a generic greeting (e.g., “Hello”).

•

Response generation.
We use Qwen3-Omni
(
Xu et al. 2025b
)
to generate text responses for these prompts. We choose a spoken language model rather than a text-only LLM because Qwen3-Omni produces speech-friendly responses that are short, conversational, and free of formatting that does not transfer to speech (e.g., bullet points, code blocks, or long enumerations). We feed the
text
version of the prompt rather than its synthesized speech, since text-input responses are typically more accurate.

•

Speech synthesis.
The generated responses are synthesized into speech by Qwen3-TTS
(
Hu et al. 2026
)
with a fixed speaker ‘‘Ryan’’.
The speech prompts are synthesized using Fish-Audio TTS
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/fishaudio/s1-mini

(
Liao et al. 2024
)
, with speaker prompts randomly sampled from English Emilia utterances longer than 5 seconds
(
He et al. 2024
)
. This produces 4M samples with a total audio duration of 30K hours.

•

Quality filtering.
We apply format-based filtering to remove samples containing code, formulas, excessive punctuation, or non-target languages; correctness filtering using the DeepSeek-V4-Flash API; and ASR-based filtering with Whisper-medium to discard cases with WER greater than 20%.

•

Final scale.
After filtering, we obtain
1.4

9.9

7.2

Appendix C
Additional Model and Training Details

Talker Transformer configuration.

The Talker module of FlexiSLM uses the same Transformer implementation as the Thinker LLM.
It has hidden size 1280, 20 Transformer layers, 8 attention heads, and intermediate size 5,120.
FlexiCodec and its SenseVoice
(
An et al. 2024
)
semantic encoder have 450M parameters in total.

Training configuration.

We use AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
for training.
The Talker Transformer, Talker-to-Thinker connection, and Frame Merging Module use learning rate
2\times 10^{-4}
in each stage.
The LLM backbone uses learning rate
2\times 10^{-5}
in Stage 2 and
1\times 10^{-6}
in Stage 3.
We apply LoRA to the LLM backbone with rank
r=32
and LoRA scaling parameter
\alpha=64
.
For each stage, we use a 5% linear learning-rate warmup followed by cosine decay to 1/10 of the maximum learning rate.
All stages use a dynamic batch size of 3K tokens per GPU. Stages 1 and 2 use Distributed Data Parallel; Stage 3 uses DeepSpeed ZeRO-2
(
Rasley et al. 2020
)
.
Stage 1 spans
300\text{K}
steps, Stage 2 spans
240\text{K}
steps (3 epochs), and Stage 3 spans
160\text{K}
steps.
Gradient accumulation is set to
1
in Stages 1 and 2, and to
2
in Stage 3.

Appendix G
Flow-Matching Audio Decoder Details

FlexiSLM uses the audio decoder provided in the open-source FlexiCodec repository
4
4

4

https://github.com/amphionteam/flexicodec
.
The decoder is a two-stage pipeline: a VoiceBox
(
Le et al. 2023
)
-style NAR flow matching model followed by a vocoder. The VoiceBox model has 363M parameters, and the vocoder has

The NAR flow matching model takes FlexiCodec’s FSQ semantic tokens and associated frame lengths as input, and generates mel spectrograms conditioned on a short reference audio prompt that defines the target speaker identity.
The model input is a 12.5 Hz fixed-frame-rate sequence interpolated to 50 Hz to match the mel-spectrogram rate.
To construct the 12.5 Hz sequence, each FlexiCodec FSQ semantic token is repeated according to its frame length.
The flow matching model learns a velocity field
v_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t)
that transports samples from a Gaussian prior
\mathbf{z}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
to the mel-spectrogram distribution
\mathbf{z}_{1}
under the conditioning inputs.
The mel spectrograms are then converted to 24 kHz waveforms by a pretrained Vocos
(
Siuzdak 2023
)
vocoder.

Appendix H
Audio Tokenizer Details

We use pretrained FlexiCodec
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
to encode dynamic frame rate speech tokens as FlexiSLM’s training target.
Our Talker Transformer predicts FlexiCodec’s FSQ tokens and the associated per-token frame lengths, and the flow-matching decoder (Appendix
G
) synthesizes the final waveform.
FlexiCodec follows an encoder-quantizer-decoder architecture and has the following two features:

ASR-Feature-Assisted Dual-Stream Encoding.

FlexiCodec encodes FSQ semantic tokens through a semantic-encoding branch.
The semantic-encoding branch encodes SenseVoice
(
An et al. 2024
)
ASR features at 12.5 Hz, while a parallel acoustic-encoding branch encodes RVQ tokens from raw waveforms.
In FlexiSLM, we use only the semantic branch; acoustic reconstruction is handled by the flow-matching audio decoder.

Dynamic Frame Rate via Frame Merging.

The Frame Merging Module is applied to the 12.5 Hz ASR features.
Adjacent frames whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold
\tau
are merged, producing a variable-frame length sequence.
The merged features are then quantized via FSQ (in the semantic-encoding branch) or RVQ (in the acoustic-encoding branch).
By varying
\tau
from 0.8 to 1.0, FlexiCodec supports average frame rates from 3 Hz to 12.5 Hz.
At 12.5 Hz no merging occurs and FlexiCodec behaves as a standard fixed-rate codec.

Appendix I
Future Work

While FlexiSLM validates dynamic and controllable frame rates inside an SLM, several directions remain open and motivate our future work:

\bullet

Full-duplex and interactive SLMs.

The Talker-to-Thinker connection introduced in Section
3.1
routes the actually-rendered speech tokens back to the Thinker LLM, which gives the Thinker a coherent acoustic context across turns.
This is a natural foundation for full-duplex SLMs that can listen, think, and speak concurrently.
We plan to extend our FlexiSLM framework to full duplex in future research.

\bullet

Streaming and on-device deployment.

The current FlexiSLM is non-streaming on both input and output. Adapting the Frame Merging Module and audio decoder to causal or chunk-based streaming, combined with FlexiSLM’s frame rate controllability, would enable real-time conversation and let a single model match heterogeneous compute budgets from cloud to edge devices.

\bullet

Post-training for spoken dialogue.

We have not yet explored RLHF
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
or DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
on top of FlexiSLM. Spoken-dialogue-specific reward models, including prosody, naturalness, and turn-taking signals, are likely to improve response quality beyond what supervised distillation alone can provide.

\bullet

Reasoning, multi-turn, and tool-use coverage.

FlexiSLM-Data is dominated by single-turn factual and instruction-following responses. Extending the data construction pipeline to multi-turn dialogues, chain-of-thought reasoning, and tool-augmented interactions would broaden the model’s capability surface while keeping the same dynamic-rate backbone.

\bullet

Unified low-rate audio modeling.

The frame-merging and frame rate-conditioning machinery is not specific to speech. Applying the same controllable dynamic-rate recipe to general audio (music, environmental sound, mixed audio) is a promising direction toward unified, efficient audio language models.
