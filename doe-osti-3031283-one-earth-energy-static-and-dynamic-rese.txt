EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The CarbonSAFE Phase III - Illinois Storage Corridor project, funded by the US Department of
Energy (DOE), partnered with One Earth Energy (OEE) to develop a carbon dioxide (CO2) storage
project at the OEE site near Gibson city, north-central Illinois, that will store commercial quantities
(over 50 million tonnes) of industrially-sourced CO2 in the subsurface. The site was evaluated
through drilling of OEE #1, a stratigraphic test well, conducting 2-D seismic surveys, and carrying
out subsurface analyses and simulation based on data acquired. Major components of this project
involve (1) the development of static geocellular models that integrated much of the acquired data
to represent the geologic architecture and properties of the Mt. Simon storage complex and (2)
dynamic simulation to evaluate injection characteristics and behavior of injected CO2.
One of the measures for commercial-scale CO2 storage as defined by the US DOE in their
CarbonSAFE program is the storage of 50 million tonnes (Mt) injected over 30 years. This target
is the basis for many of the dynamic simulations performed during this project. Additional
simulation scenarios considered the feasibility of storing 20-50 million tonnes over shorter periods
(12-20 years) to address OEE’s interests associated with current 45Q laws (26 U.S.C. § 45Q, 2022;
IRA, 2022).
Geocellular models were developed in Petrel®, a Schlumberger software, by interpreting and
integrating data (obtained from core test and analysis, geophysical and seismic surveys, and in situ
well tests) from the newly drilled OEE #1 well in McLean County, Illinois. Additionally, data
from nearby wells (Furrow #1 and Hinton #7) were used to support the development of the models.
The developed Mt. Simon storage complex geocellular model domain included 7 stratigraphic
zones;
 • the Eau Claire shale, the topmost zone that will serve as the sealing formation,
 • the upper Mt. Simon Sandstone,
 • the middle Mt. Simon Sandstone,
 • the lower Mt. Simon Sandstone,
 • the arkosic zone, lower part of the lower Mt. Simon Sandstone and primary injection zone,
 • the Argenta, and
 • the upper 100 ft of the Precambrian basement.
The models were updated periodically as new data became available during and after drilling of
the OEE well, and as a result four different versions of geocellular models were used at different
times as inputs for dynamic reservoir modeling.
The dynamic reservoir modeling/simulation studies were carried out in different phases:
preliminary study phase, sensitivity study phase, and UIC Class VI permit study phase. Each study
phase was carried out at the time when a new geocellular model version was finalized by ISGS
geologists.
Preliminary study phase- For the preliminary study phase, version 1 of the static geocellular
models were used as inputs for dynamic reservoir modeling. The phase involved simulation of
four CO2 injection scenarios in which vertical wells were used to inject CO2 at targeted rates, based
on local emission rate (0.46 Mt/yr) and field-scale rate (1.7 Mt/yr), over 12 years and 20 years.

 ii

The objective of the study at this phase was to preliminarily assess CO2 plume distribution and
Area-of-Review (AoR) that will result from the following scenarios:
 • CO2 injection rate of 0.46 Mt/yr over 12 years
 • CO2 injection rate of 0.46 Mt/yr over 20 years
 • CO2 injection rate of 1.7 Mt/yr over 12 years
 • CO2 injection rate of 1.7 Mt/yr over 20 years
In all four scenarios, the pressure front area at the end of injection, based on calculated threshold
differential pressure of 55 psi, is larger than that of the CO2 plume. Pressure front range between
3.8–141 mi2. As a result, the pressure front defines the Area-of-Review (AoR) for these scenarios.
Sensitivity study phase- For the sensitivity study phase, version 2.0 of the static geocellular models
were used for dynamic reservoir modeling. The objective of this phase was to address the
uncertainty in geology (specifically the permeability prediction), and injection operations,
including their impacts on CO2 injection rate, CO2 plume size, and pressure front size. In this
phase, 10 scenarios were ran using three different permeability models (KISGS, KTIM, and
KSDR) that were generated based on different permeability prediction methods. These scenarios
were simulated to predict injection rates that could be achieved, based on the parameters
(permeability model and well orientation) that could vary. The scenarios include the following:
 • CO2 injection via a vertical well, where the entire arkose zone (200 ft) is perforated.
 • CO2 injection via a horizontal well, where horizontal section (1,000 ft) is perforated in the
 arkose zone.
 • CO2 injection via a horizontal well, where horizontal section (2,000 ft) is perforated in the
 arkose zone.
 • CO2 injection via a horizontal well, where horizontal section (5,000 ft) is perforated in the
 arkose zone.
 • CO2 injection via a slant well with a true vertical angle of 45°, perforated in the arkose
       zone (280 ft).
 • CO2 injection via a slant well with a true vertical angle of 60°, perforated in the arkose
       zone (400 ft).
 • CO2 injection via a slant well with a true vertical angle of 78°, perforated in the arkose
       zone (1,000 ft).
 • CO2 injection via a vertical well, where 200 ft of the upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone is
 perforated.
 • CO2 injection via a horizontal well, where horizontal section (1,000 ft) is perforated in the
 upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone.
 • CO2 injection via a horizontal well, where horizontal section (1 mi) is perforated in the
 upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone.
The simulation results demonstrate that a vertical well achieves an injection rate of 1.2–2.5 Mt/yr.
A slant well, with a 40% increase in perforated length compared to a vertical well injecting into

the arkosic zone, can enhance the injection rate by 4–34%. A 1,000 ft horizontal well allows a rate
increase of 33–75% compared to the vertical well scenario. In all scenarios, a vertical well can
meet a target injection rate of 1 Mt/yr. Perforating the thicker upper Mt. Simon Sandstone unit
generally leads to a higher injection rate, except in the KISGS model, where the permeability in
the arkosic zone surpasses that of the upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone.
UIC Class VI permit study phase- During the permit study phase, given that there were newly
acquired data, the static geocellular and dynamic models were updated. Also, there was a project
scope change which required the use of three vertical wells as injectors, as demanded by One Earth
Sequestration LLC (OES), the site operator. OES plans to use OEE #1 as a monitoring well. By
considering and planning for the plume size that will develop from CO2 injection and arrival time
of the CO2 at the monitoring well, the objective for the dynamic reservoir simulations in this phase
was to plan optimal locations for the three injection wells. As a result, scenarios involving
sensitivity on well distances were simulated. These scenarios include the following:
 • Locating injector 1 (OES #1) 0.6 mi from OEE #1, which is injecting CO2 at 0.5 Mt/yr
 • Locating OES #1 0.8 mi from OEE #1, which is injecting CO2 at 0.5 Mt/yr
 • Locating OES #1 0.6 mi from OEE #1, injector 2 (OES #2) 1 mi from OEE #1, and injector
 3 (OES #3) 1 mi from OES #2. All injectors inject CO2 at 1.5 Mt/yr over 20 years.
 • Locating OES #1 0.6 mi from OEE #1, OES #2 1.5 mi from OEE #1, and OES #3 1.5 mi
 from OES #2. All injectors inject CO2 at 1.5 Mt/yr over 20 years.
 • Locating OES #1 0.6 mi from OEE #1, OES #2 2 mi from OEE #1, and OES #3 2 mi from
 OES #2. All injectors inject CO2 at 1.5 Mt/yr over 20 years.
Sensitivity on well distance from the monitoring well (scenarios 27 and 28) determined that the
first injector needs to be 0.6 mi away from OEE #1 so the CO2 plume can be detected at OEE #1
after 3– 4 years of injection. The other two injectors need to be approximately 1 mile apart to yield
a small plume size (when the 3 plumes from the injectors are combined), ensuring that the injection
rate would not be compromised by pressure interference. Maximum vertical extension of the
plume (707 ft) was observed at the first injector. The pressure front area is similar among the
different scenarios: 178 mi2 based on a threshold pressure of 86 psi.
This study indicates that commercial-scale CO2 storage at the OEE site is feasible, with a potential
to store 90 Mt CO2 (prospective storage resource) over 20 years using three injection wells,
resulting in a 32 mi2 plume and a 178 mi2 pressure front areas.
Pressure front area predetermines the AoR of the OEE site. Pressure front area is sensitive to
threshold differential pressure, which is influenced by the initial pressure of the injection zone.
Future work to progress OEE site from CarbonSAFE Phase III to the next phase includes;
 • calibrate the reservoir model to well test data, correcting initial pressure and fluid salinity
 change with depth based on measured or observed data,
 • update the geocellular model as injectors are drilled and seismic surveys are conducted,
 • Establish project baseline, and calibrating the reservoir model to measured CO2 saturation
 and pressure honoring actual injection rates, and evaluating AoR as the model is updated.

INTRODUCTION
The objective of the Illinois Storage Corridor (ISC) project’s static and dynamic reservoir
modeling task was to assess the feasibility of storing over 50 million tonnes (at least 4.167 million
tonnes annually; MMTA) of industrially-sourced carbon dioxide (CO2) in the Mt. Simon storage
complex at One Earth Energy (OEE) Site, McLean County, north-central Illinois, over a period of
20 years. The targeted geologic reservoir is Mt. Simon Sandstone.
Data acquired from the recently drilled OEE #1 stratigraphic test well (which will later be
converted to a monitoring well) and adjacent wells were integrated and used in developing static
geocellular models for the Mt. Simon storage complex, which were further used as inputs for
dynamic reservoir modeling. This report describes the process of developing the static geocellular
models, including updates that were made to the models as newly acquired data became available.
The updates made to models led to realization of different versions of geocellular models. The
report also describes the construction of dynamic reservoir models and present modeling results
obtained to assess the feasibility of injecting CO2 into the Mt. Simon storage complex.

STATIC GEOCELLULAR MODEL
The goal of the static modeling effort was to build representative three-dimensional (3-D) property
models (porosity and horizontal and vertical permeability) of the Mt. Simon Sandstone storage
complex for the One Earth Energy (OEE) well site located in eastern McLean County, Illinois.
The models were used as inputs for dynamic reservoir modeling effort that will predict the effect,
fate, and transport of CO2 when injected into the storage complex at this site. To build the property
models, a structural model consisting of structural surfaces of the subdivisions of the storage
complex was required. These surfaces were imported into Petrel v2021.1 and used in developing
a structural model of the storage complex. The structural model of the storage complex was further
used in creating static geocellular models. The static geocellular models were developed to have
representative properties of the Mt. Simon storage complex, including the Mt. Simon Sandstone
(storage unit) and the Eau Claire shale (caprock or sealing unit). To evaluate the downward
propagation of pressure into subjacent formation zones/intervals due to CO2 injection into the
storage unit, pre-Mt. Simon Sandstone (informally referred to as Argenta formation) and
Precambrian basement were also represented in the models.
The process of developing the static geocellular models involved the following steps:
 1) compiling and importing created structural surfaces of the defined study area;
 2) compiling and importing different versions of petrophysical logs (porosity and
 permeability) from wells within the defined study area;
 3) creating a 3-D structural framework and geocellular grid for the Mt. Simon storage
 complex and confining units;
 4) delineating and layering defined stratigraphic zones;
 5) vertical upscaling of 0.5-ft rate petrophysical logs into layered grid cells along well path;
 6) analyzing log data and constraining the variogram parameters based on performed analysis;
 7) determining property trend that should be honored in the models; and

 1

 8) populating grid cells in each stratigraphic zone with porosity and permeability values using
 a geostatistical algorithm and constrained variogram parameters.

2.1 Model area
Locations of wells that penetrated the entire Mt. Simon Sandstone and are less than 30 miles from
the newly drilled OEE #1 well in McLean County, Illinois, were used to define the limits of the
geocellular model area. The defined area encompassed the locations of the Furrow (API-
1211322942), OEE #1 (API-1211325373), and the Hinton brothers #7 (API-1201923996) wells
(Figure 1). (Furrow and Hinton brothers #7 wells are ~27 miles and ~15 miles from OEE #1).
Other wells that penetrate part of the Mt. Simon Sandstone were also within the defined area.
Petrophysical well log data collected from these wells were valuable in refining the structural
surface of the Mt. Simon Sandstone. However, because the wells did not penetrate the zone of
interest (i.e., the arkosic zone) within the Mt. Simon Sandstone and well logs from these wells did
not cover the arkosic zone, they were excluded from the property modeling/distribution process.
Based on the criteria of well penetration through the entire Mt. Simon Sandstone, the defined static
model area (Figure 1; purple outline) was 50 by 50 sq. miles (~80.5 x 80.5 km2). However,
considering the total number of grid cells within the model area and the simulation time, a smaller
area, 20 mi by 20 mi (~32.2 x 32.2 km2) was defined for dynamic model area (Figure 1; red
outline).

2.2 Data input
2.2.1 Input petrophysical log data
Continuous petrophysical well log data (0.5-ft rate) available from the 3 wells (i.e., Furrow, OEE
#1, and Hinton Brothers #7) and discrete core analysis data from OEE #1 and Hinton brothers #7
wells were used to characterize the storage complex and subjacent geologic zones. Output from
the characterization effort was used to inform the construction of the static geocellular models.
The continuous petrophysical well log data includes traditional triple combo logs, i.e., gamma-ray,
neutron porosity, density, and resistivity logs, and they were available from the 3 wells. Also
available within the OEE #1 well were advanced petrophysical well logs, i.e., nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR), dipole sonic, elemental spectroscopy, and resistivity and sonic image logs.
All the logs were imported into PetrelTM, Schlumberger’s reservoir modeling software and they
were used to subdivide Mt. Simon Sandstone, evaluate the different formation units, and refine the
different geologic surfaces. Based on petrophysical attributes/characteristics on geophysical well
logs and petrographic studies, Mt. Simon Sandstone was subdivided into four zones: the upper
zone, middle zone, lower zone, and the arkosic zone.
The formation evaluation involved calculating porosity and permeability logs. To calculate the
porosity log, the neutron and density porosity logs were averaged. To calculate the horizontal
permeability log, three different methods, namely the Schlumberger Doll Research, Timur Coates,
and ISGS proprietary method, were used in deriving KSDR, KTIM, and KISGS permeability logs,
respectively. The process of computing the permeability logs was an iterative process, which
resulted in creating multiple versions of the permeability logs.
The KSDR and KTIM logs were derived for the OEE #1 well using inputs from the NMR log.
Note that NMR log was not available from the other two wells (Hinton brothers #7 and Furrow),
hence these logs were not derived for these wells. To make the KSDR and KTIM log consistent
with measured data, they were calibrated to the discrete core analysis data and injection well testing
data from the OEE #1 well.
Additionally, KISGS logs were derived for the three wells using inputs from the traditional triple
combo logs. The derived KISGS logs for OEE #1 and Hinton brothers #7 wells were also calibrated
to the discrete core analysis data. The derived permeability logs for the OEE #1 well are shown in
Figure 2. These different permeability logs resulted in the generation of different versions of 3-D
permeability models.

2.2.3 Input structural surfaces
The gridded structural surfaces used for static modeling were obtained from previously prepared
regional static model, which covered nearly the entirety of the Illinois Basin. Before the surfaces
were used, they were trimmed to the size of the model area (Figure 1) and refined to honor and
integrate newer information/interpretations on well log data and 2-D and 3-D seismic reflection
data. The refined surfaces include the Eau Claire shale (model top), subunits of the Mt. Simon
Sandstone (upper, middle, lower, and arkosic zone), a pre-Mt. Simon Sandstone called the Argenta
Formation, Precambrian basement, and a model base surface (which conforms to the Precambrian
basement surface but 100 ft deeper). ISGS geoscientists reviewed all surfaces to ensure the
surfaces intersect defined formation tops at well locations and were smoothened (particularly in

2.3 Structural framework (gridding and layering)
The static model's structural framework (Figure 4) was built by using trimmed structural surfaces
for structural modeling. The process of stacking the surfaces created seven stratigraphic zones.
Within the static model area, the surfaces were laterally gridded into cell sizes that were ~1000 ft

 5

by 1000 ft in x- (easting) and y- (northing) directions. To capture each stratigraphic zone's vertical
heterogeneity and estimate vertical permeability (kvert), specifically within the upper Mt. Simon
Sandstone (UMS), the lower Mt. Simon Sandstone (LMS), and the arkosic zones, the zones were
proportionally layered at approximately every 0.5 ft which created a finely layered structural
framework (structural framework 1). The detail of structural framework 1 is provided in Table 1.
This resulted in a structural framework with ~182 million grid cells and was used to develop a
finely layered static model.
Subsequently, to cut down the total number of grid cells within the model domain for
computational reasons, a coarsely layered structural framework (Structural Framework 2) was
developed, which was made up of ~15.6 million grid cells. The detail of structural framework 2 is
provided in Table 2. After the property models were developed in Structural Framework 1, the
models were averaged into Structural Framework 2 to create a coarsely layered static model. The
averaging process is discussed further in section 2.4. Note that the only difference between the
structural frameworks is the grid cell count. Because of the lower number of grid cells in Structural
Framework 2, it was used for dynamic reservoir modeling.

2.5 Property distribution/modeling
The development of the property models involved multiple iterations and interrogations of the
realized models. To develop the 3-D porosity and Khorz models, the process involved populating
the model domain of the constructed structural framework 1 (i.e., the finely layered model) zone
by zone with upscaled porosity and Khorz log data, using the geostatistical modeling algorithm in
Petrel. The exponential variogram parameters that were required as input for the modeling process
were determined for each zone from the well data analysis. The Mt. Simon and pre-Mt. Simon
Sandstone zones were populated using the Sequential Gaussian Simulation (SGS) algorithm.
However, for the Eau Claire Formation and Precambrian basement zones, single porosity and
permeability values were assigned due to limited data and difficulties in estimating effective
porosity and permeability from logs for the Eau Claire shale and Precambrian igneous rocks.

 10

Given the stochastic modeling approach (i.e., SGS) used for the Mt. Simon and pre-Mt. Simon
Sandstone zones and the distance between the wells in the area to be modeled, the variogram
parameters were varied to create a series of realizations representing a range of possibilities. The
generation of multiple realizations aimed to provide ISGS geologists with various model options
that they can interrogate, from which they can identify the option that best corresponds to their
conceptual model of the depositional environment of the delineated stratigraphic zones. The
selected realization's modeling approach is discussed in this document.
In terms of the interpreted depositional environments for the different stratigraphic zones, the
Argenta zone is interpreted to have an environment that transitions from an alluvial plain to a
fluvial plain from west to east. The arkosic zone and the lower and middle Mt. Simon Sandstone
are interpreted to have a fluvial/eolian plain environment in the west, which transitions to a delta
front in the east. The upper Mt. Simon Sandstone is interpreted to have a deltaic plain environment
transitioning to a tidally influenced marine coastal system to the east (F. Delpomdor and J.
Freiburg, personal communication 2021).
To populate the upscaled porosity log in the arkosic zone, primary and secondary inputs were used.
The secondary input was an average porosity map, which was used to guide the lateral and vertical
distribution of porosity in the area. For other zones, only the variogram parameters were used as
inputs to populate the upscaled porosity log. The upscaled porosity log for each stratigraphic zone
within the model is presented in histograms in Figure 8.
To populate the Khorz (excluding the arkosic zone), the upscaled Khorz log was co-kriged with the
3-D porosity model (as a secondary input), using the same variogram parameters applied for
porosity modeling. However, for the arkosic zone, the upscaled permeability log was populated
using only the variogram parameters defined for the porosity modeling. This was done because of
the geologists' uncertainty regarding the lateral permeability distribution within this zone.
Additionally, the lateral distribution of permeability in fluvial rocks is variable and depends on
various factors such as grain size, sorting, sediment composition, channel morphology, and
diagenesis.
The process of developing the 3-D Kvert model involved incorporating the empirical relationship
between Kvert and Khorz observed from core data of the Mt. Simon Sandstone retrieved from the
OEE well and wells drilled ~50 mi from the OEE site, i.e., wells for the Illinois Basin Decatur
(IBDP) and Illinois Industrial Carbon Capture and Storage (I-CCS) projects. The process involved
multiplying each zone of the 3-D Khorz permeability model (developed using structural framework
1) by a Kvert to Khorz permeability ratio that was estimated from core data. From the OEE core data,
it was observed that for every ~10ft, a median Kvert to Khorz ratio of ~0.4 is observed in the arkosic
zone. From core data from the IBDP and the I-CCS projects, it had been observed that for every
0.5 ft of core, Kvert is approximately 0.85 times the Khorz value (S. Frailey, personal
communication).
Based on these observations, the arkosic zone within the developed 3-D Khorz model was multiplied
by 0.4, while the other Mt. Simon Sandstone zones were multiplied by 0.85. The difference in ratio
between the arkosic zone and the other Mt. Simon Sandstone zones is believed to be related to the
differences in depositional environments of the different zones, which can be proven by comparing
the gamma-ray signal between zones. The gamma-ray signal appears to be higher and more
variable within the arkosic zone, likely due to the presence of feldspar mineral. Additionally, the

 11

occurrence of low permeability streaks was found to reduce the Kvert based on geologic
interpretation and core test data.

OEE DYNAMIC RESERVOIR MODELING
A reservoir simulation study was performed to assess the feasibility of commercial-scale CO2
storage at the One Earth Energy (OEE) site near Gibson City, Illinois. The simulation work has
gone through three phases (Table 5). The phases include 1) a preliminary study to obtain a general
understanding of the Area-of-Review (AoR), 2) a sensitivity study to determine the maximum CO2

 15

injection rate and assess AoR under various geologic and operational conditions, and 3) update
reservoir model with newly acquired field data and perform reservoir simulations to inform UIC
Class VI permit preparation. For the preliminary, sensitivity, and permit studies, model versions
1.0, 2.0, and 2.1, respectively, were used (described in Table 3).
Landmark’s Nexus (version 5000.4.12), a commercial software package, was used to conduct
numerical simulations. Nexus allows multiphase flow simulations with multiple compositions for
multiple reservoirs and models the reservoir, wells, and surface and subsurface facilities as a single
system for a comprehensive representation of reservoir behavior. Two major CO2 trapping
mechanisms were modeled: structural or stratigraphic trapping and residual gas trapping.
Solubility trapping was not considered due to a software malfunction and because it is negligible
compared to stratigraphic trapping. Mineral trapping was considered negligible because the
storage unit is primarily quartz.

3.1.2 Simulation inputs
The geocellular (static reservoir) model was used as input for dynamic reservoir models, which
was used to simulate CO2 migration and pressure response. Table 7 lists the major simulation
inputs. The model was constructed using 254,718 grid cells (i.e., 53×54×89 grid cells in the x-, y-
, and z-directions, respectively). Each grid cell was 1,000 ft long, 1,000 ft wide, and 34 ft thick.
The well cell and nearby cells (10 cells from the well cell in both x and y directions) were refined
into 500 ft by 500 ft cells laterally. The rock fluid properties and initial conditions are based on
information from the Decatur site. Three sets of relative permeability were created to represent
high- (permeability greater than 100 mD), mid- (permeability between 1 mD and 100 mD), and
low-quality rock (permeability less than 1 mD). They were generated using Corey’s function
embedded in Nexus (Table 8 and Figure 16), which closely matched the relative permeability data
Schlumberger used for the Illinois Basin–Decatur Project (IBDP) (Senel et al., 2014). The relative
permeability data of high-quality rock was based on lab measurements of lower Mt. Simon
Sandstone rock samples by Schlumberger Reservoir Laboratories, Houston, Texas, in 2015
(unpublished report). The relative permeability data of mid- and low-quality rocks were generated
based on general knowledge. It followed the trend that irreducible water saturation increases as
permeability decreases, and the movable saturation range decreases as permeability decreases.
The initial reservoir pressure was 2,911 psi at 6,430 ft (5,740 ft subsea), and the reservoir
temperature was 122.6 °F based on IBDP well data. An open boundary condition was assumed for
the Mt. Simon Sandstone and Argenta units because they are interpreted to be present across the
region. Eau Claire Shale has closed boundary because it is tight as a confining unit. The open
boundary condition was achieved by attaching infinite-acting Carter-Tracy analytical aquifers
(related equations and parameters are defined in Nexus manual, average properties of each unit
was used in the calculation) to all four sides of the Mt. Simon Sandstone storage complex. The
infinite-acting behavior was validated using the derivative of reciprocal rate (1/rate) vs. time plot
from water injection constrained by bottomhole pressure (Figure 17).

3.1.3 Simulation scenarios
Scenarios involving varying injection rate (0.46 Mt/yr, 1.7 Mt/yr) and injection time (12 years and
20 years) were simulated to understand the CO2 plume and pressure front distribution within the
Mt Simon Sandstone complex (i.e., Eau Claire Shale, Mt Simon Sandstone, and Argenta) at OEE
site. An injector was placed one mile north of the OEE #1 well location. The CO2 was injected at
two rates: 0.46 Mt/yr based on local emission and 1.7 Mt/yr based on a target of 50 Mt over 30
years. The maximum well bottomhole pressure was constrained to be 4109 psi at a datum of 6,430
ft, following a pressure gradient of 0.64 psi/ft (90% of the estimated fracture pressure gradient of
0.71 psi/ft at McMillen #2 well). The well was perforated 500 ft in the arkosic zone, which is
located at the bottom of lower Mt. Simon Sandstone. Two injection periods were considered: 12
years and 20 years. The post-injection period was 50 years for all cases.
3.1.4 Simulation results
The CO2 plume and pressure front areas were evaluated for all simulated scenarios. The plume
radius was an equivalent radius based on the area calculated from the plume length and width. The
pressure front radius was determined based on a calculated threshold differential pressure of 55
psi, which is the minimum pressure required for fluid traveling from the top of Mt. Simon
Sandstone to the bottom of the St. Peter Sandstone freshwater zone. The AoR is determined as the
greater of the free-phase CO2 plume and the threshold pressure front areas. Figure 18 to Figure 21
show the side and top view of CO2 plume distribution for all cases. Figure 22 shows the AoR of
all four cases. Table 9 summarizes the CO2 plume and pressure front distribution of all four cases.
In summary, the CO2 plume radius is about 1 mi for the low-rate injection cases and 1.5–2 mi for
the high-rate injection cases, respectively, at the end of injection. The plume radius extended about
3% at 50 years post-injection. Vertically, the CO2 plume extended into lower Mt. Simon Sandstone
and Argenta units and stayed within lower Mt. Simon Sandstone in all cases (Figure 18 to Figure
20) except for the long injection with the high-rate case (Case 4 in Table 9). For the long injection

 20

with the high-rate injection case, the CO2 plume reached middle Mt. Simon Sandstone 50 years
into post-injection (Figure 21).
The pressure front radius is about 1 mi, 20–25% larger than the CO2 plume radius, for the low-rate
injection case and about 5 to 7 mi, 3.7 times the CO2 plume radius, for the high-rate injection case.
As a result, the pressure front is the AoR at the end of injection because its radius (i.e. area) is
larger than that of the CO2 plume for all four simulated cases.

4.2.4 Simulation results
The simulation results of sensitivity on perforation and well orientation scenarios (Table 14) show
that a vertical well allowed an injection rate of 1.2–2.5 Mt/yr. A slant well can increase injection
rate by 4–34% when the perforated length is increased by 40% (5 times) compared to that of the
vertical well case that injects into the arkosic zone. A 1,000 ft horizontal well allowed a 33–75%
rate increase. Overall, in all cases, a vertical well could meet a target injection rate of 1 Mt/yr.
Perforating the thicker upper Mt. Simon Sandstone unit generally resulted in a higher injection

 28

rate, except in the case of the KISGS model, where the permeability in the arkosic zone is higher
when compared to the upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone.
When CO2 was injected at a rate of 1 Mt/yr via a vertical well, the resulting CO2 plume and AoR
defined by pressure front is shown in Figure 24 to Figure 28, taking the KISGS model results as
examples.
Table 15 lists the CO2 plume radius and pressure front radius evaluated for all cases. The plume
radius was an equivalent radius based on the area calculated from the plume length and width. The
pressure front radius was determined based on a calculated threshold differential pressure of 55
psi, which is the minimum pressure required for fluid traveling from the top of Mt. Simon
Sandstone to the bottom of St. Peter Sandstone freshwater zone via an open wellbore.
For CO2 injection into the Arkose zone, the CO2 plume radius was around 1.4–1.5 mi at 12-year
injection and 1.6–1.8 mi at 20-year injection. The 50-year post-injection plume extension was 2–
4% (Figure 26 and Figure 27). Vertically, CO2 extended to both the lower Mt. Simon Sandstone
and Argenta units for the KISGS model case, while in the KTIM and KSDR model cases, CO2
reached middle Mt. Simon Sandstone zone. The pressure front radius was about 3 mi at 12-year
injection and 4 mi at 20-year injection, which was 2.2–2.5 times the CO2 plume radius.
For CO2 injection into the upper Mt. Simon Sandstone zone, the CO2 plume radius was around
0.8–1 mi at 12-year injection, 1–1.2 mi at 20-year injection, and 1–1.3 mi at 50-year post injection.
Vertically, CO2 extended to both Eau Claire Shale and middle Mt. Simon Sandstone zones for all
models. The pressure front radius was 3.3–3.6 mi for 12-year injection and 4.1–4.4 mi for 20-year
injection, which was 3.4–4.1 times the plume radius.

4.3.3 Simulation scenarios
Three injectors rather than one injector were considered as required by OEE company to possibly
store more CO2. The characterization well, OEE #1, will be converted to a monitoring well.

run to determine the optimal location and perforation interval of the three injectors. First, the
distance between the first injector, OES #1, and OEE #1, was varied from 0.6 mi to 0.8 mi to
determine the distance the CO2 plume from OES #1 can be detected at OEE #1 within 3–4 years
of injection at a rate of 0.5 Mt/yr (local emission rate). The distance among the other two injectors
were then varied from 1 mi to 2 mi to determine the distance required to minimize plume size and
pressure interference i.e., without affecting injection rate (Figure 31). These well distance cases
considered perforation in the arkosic zone, and each well was injecting 1.5 Mt/yr for 20 years.
Once the well distance is determined, two perforation intervals were considered: the 500 ft arkosic
zone and the 530 ft high permeability section in UMS zone. Cases with mixed perforations (all
injectors perforated in UMS, two injectors perforated in UMS, and one injector perforated in
arkosic zone) were run to determine the plume and pressure front sizes after injecting 1.5
Mt/yr/well CO2 for 20 years.

A more restrictive constraint compared to BHP, the tubing fluid velocity, was considered with the
well location and perforation determined. The procedure to determine the maximum CO2 injection
rates for the three injectors was first to inject CO2 at a specified rate for one year, calculate CO2
velocity in pipe and compare it with allowable fluid velocity, and then iterate on CO2 injection rate
until CO2 velocity equals allowable velocity.
The final reservoir model for permit application, TriINJ, was then used to assess the CO2 plume
and pressure front sizes after injecting CO2 into the arkosic zone at the OEE site via three injectors
at 1.5 Mt/yr/well for 20 years, followed by a post-injection period of 50 years. Sensitivity cases
were conducted on permeability and porosity to determine their impacts on plume and pressure. A
±20% change in permeability and porosity was applied to all grids using a multiplier.
4.3.4 Simulation results
4.3.4.1 Well location and perforation
Sensitivity on well distance indicated that the first injector needs to be 0.6 mi away from OEE #1
so that the CO2 plume can be detected at OEE #1 after 3– 4 years of injection (Figure 32 and Figure

small, combined plume size; however, the injection rate of OES #2 was greatly reduced due to

with an equivalent radius of 3.3 mi at 30 Mt/well injection, and the maximum vertical extension

however, was very large due to a low threshold differential pressure resulting from the shallower
depth of the UMS unit, compared to the arkosic unit. The arkosic perforation was selected for all

4.3.4.3 CO2 plume, pressure front, and AoR

plume edge was defined by a CO2 saturation cut-off of 1%, which accounts for 99% of injected
CO2. The plume radius was an equivalent radius based on the area calculated from the plume
length and width. The pressure front area is where the pressure change is greater than or equal to
the threshold differential pressure. The AoR is the greater of the CO2 plume size and pressure front
area.

2009). The approach for estimating the minimum pressure in this study assumes fluid density in
the wellbore to be uniform and equal to the fluid density in the injection zone (Birkholzer et al.,
2011).

5.0 Conclusion and Discussion
Commercial-scale CO2 storage at the OEE site is feasible based on the simulation study carried
out. With good permeability and thickness in Mt. Simon Sandstone formation, CO2 could be
injected via a vertical well at a rate up to 2.6 Mt/yr based on tubing fluid velocity constraint, and
the rate was even higher with a maximum BHP that was constrained to fracture pressure gradient
and a 90% safety factor. Perforating the high permeability arkosic zone resulted in a smaller
pressure front than perforating upper Mt. Simon Sandstone.
A total of 90 Mt CO2 could be injected at the OEE site via three injectors about 1 mi apart over 20
years. The resulting plume was 3.2 mi and extended slightly to 3.3 mi (3% increase) at 50-year
post injection. Pressure front radius was 2–2.4 times that of the CO2 plume during injection. It
reached its maximum at 20-year injection, 7.5 mi in radius, and diminished after 7-year post
injection. The AoR followed pressure front during injection, was highest at the end of injection,
178 mi2, quickly dropped to 43 mi2 at 6-yr post injection, then followed plume size, and was 34
mi2 at 50-year post injection. A 20% change in porosity and permeability resulted in a 7% change
in AoR radius. Smaller plumes could be attributed to higher porosity, and pressure front was more
affected by permeability than porosity.
This study is ongoing, and the models will be updated as more data becomes available. Some
important aspects and future work are as follows:
First, AoR is sensitive to threshold differential pressure, which is directly related to the initial
pressure of injection zone. In this study, a normally pressured reservoir was assumed, the pressure
front was larger than plume size during injection. However, if a reservoir is abnormally pressured,
low initial reservoir pressure can lead to high threshold differential pressure, the resulting pressure
front can be smaller than plume size, thus AoR will be small. Future work should verify the initial
pressure of reservoir and freshwater zone to calculate the threshold differential pressure accurately.
Second, CO2 solubility trapping and residual gas trapping due to hysteresis were not considered in
the TriINJ model due to software issue. Future work can include these two trapping mechanisms
in the models and assess their impact on plume, pressure front, and AoR.
Third, other important future work includes 1) calibrating reservoir model to well test data, 2)
correcting initial pressure and fluid salinity change with depth based on measured or observed
data, 3) updating geocellular model as the injectors are drilled and seismic surveys are conducted,
4) calibrating reservoir model to measured CO2 saturation and pressure honoring the actual
injection rates, and 5) evaluating AoR as the model is updated.
