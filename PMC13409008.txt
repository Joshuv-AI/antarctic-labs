Radical Resection of the Third Portion of the Duodenum for Secondary Aortoduodenal Fistula
Authors: Koji Kubota, Akira Shimizu, Tsuyoshi Notake, Tatsuichiro Seto, Yuji Soejima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSecondary aortoduodenal fistula (sADF) is a rare but life‐threatening complication following abdominal aortic aneurysm repair. Its management requires multidisciplinary collaboration; however, even gastrointestinal surgeons—who play a pivotal role in duodenal resection and reconstruction—often lack sufficient experience with this condition. Therefore, a clear understanding of sADF is essential for gastrointestinal surgeons. We retrospectively analyzed 16 sADF patients who underwent combined vascular and gastrointestinal reconstruction at Shinshu University Hospital between September 2018 and December 2025. We describe our step‐by‐step approach to radical resection of the third portion of the duodenum performed in collaboration with cardiovascular surgeons. The procedure includes complete division of the Treitz ligament, mobilization of the fourth portion from the uncinate process, preservation of the papilla of Vater, en bloc resection of the involved segment, and duodenojejunostomy. The greater omentum is mobilized through a retrocolonic route to facilitate secure graft coverage following vascular reconstruction. Postoperative course, complications, and survival rates were evaluated. The median age was 75 years (range: 67–83 years). The median operation time was 651 min (range 481–1477 min), and the median blood loss was 3050 mL (range 850–15 000 mL). Severe postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo classification grade IIIa or higher) occurred in 10 patients (63%), and the 90‐day mortality rate was 21%. The 1‐year overall survival rate was 57%. Careful multidisciplinary coordination and precise gastrointestinal surgical technique are essential for achieving safe and durable management of this complex condition.

Introduction

Secondary aortoduodenal fistula (sADF) is a rare but life‐threatening communication between a previously reconstructed abdominal aorta and the duodenum after abdominal aortic aneurysm repair [1]. It is generally caused by chronic mechanical erosion and/or graft infection [2] and typically presents with gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal pain, or sepsis [3, 4]. This rare complication has been reported in both open abdominal aortic graft replacement and endovascular aortic repair (EVAR), with respective incidence rates of 0.36%–1.6% [5, 6, 7] and 0.32%–0.46% [8, 9, 10]. Treatment for sADF requires resection of the infected graft, vascular reconstruction, and repair or resection of the duodenal fistula. At our facility, cardiovascular surgeons perform removal of infected grafts and vascular reconstruction, while gastrointestinal surgeons conduct resection of the third portion of the duodenum and gastrointestinal reconstruction. However, because of its rarity, optimal treatment remains unclear, and gastroenterological surgeons often lack sufficient familiarity with this condition and its operative strategy. Emergency endovascular repair may serve as a bridge in cases of uncontrolled hemorrhage or hemodynamic instability [10, 11]. However, durable management requires removal of the infected graft and resection of the involved duodenum [10]. In this report, we describe our operative approach with emphasis on the gastrointestinal surgical technique within a structured multidisciplinary framework.

Indications for Surgery

Indications for surgery were determined based on hemodynamic status and findings of infection or fistula formation in the graft and intestine. For patients who responded to fluid resuscitation or blood transfusion, radical open surgery was indicated even if they were in shock. In contrast, for patients who did not respond, emergency endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) should be performed as a life‐saving bridging therapy, followed by radical open surgery if possible. In patients who were not in a life‐threatening condition or were asymptomatic, radical resection was indicated when CT or endoscopy suggested a perigraft abscess, persistent infection, ectopic gas, or communication between the graft and the duodenum.

Radical resection of the involved duodenum was the preferred strategy for definitive treatment because primary repair alone was considered insufficient to prevent recurrent fistulization and graft infection.

Surgical Technique (Gastrointestinal Focus)

The operation is performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. The procedure proceeds in a staged manner with clearly defined role allocation. The gastrointestinal surgery team initiates open surgery, examining the abdominal cavity to assess contamination and abscess formation. After exposing the aorta and fistula area, duodenal resection is performed. If hemodynamics are stable, gastrointestinal reconstruction proceeds; if unstable, the cardiovascular surgeon establishes vascular control and performs graft harvesting and reconstruction. Following vascular reconstruction, the gastrointestinal surgeon resumes the procedure to complete gastrointestinal reconstruction and abdominal closure.

One closed suction drain was placed near the duodenojejunostomy. Oral intake was started after contrast examination confirmed the absence of anastomotic leakage, and the drain was removed as early as possible when there was no evidence of leakage, infection, or abnormal drainage. In all patients, the nasogastric tube was exchanged intraoperatively for a double‐lumen nasoenteric tube (Kangaroo W‐ED tube; Cardinal Health, Dublin, OH, USA). One lumen was used for gastric and duodenal decompression and the other for enteral nutrition, thereby avoiding the need for routine gastrostomy or enterostomy.

Results

During the study period, 16 cases with sADF received treatment at our hospital. The median age was 75 years (range 67–83), with 13 males (81%) and 3 female (19%). Herald bleeding was observed in 10 cases (63%). Major bleeding was observed in 5 cases (31%). The most common diagnostic method was abdominal CT scan in 15 cases (94%), followed by esophagogastroduodenoscopy in 6 cases (38%), and colonoscopy in 1 case (6%) (overlap possible). Colonoscopy was performed as part of the evaluation of gastrointestinal bleeding; however, no patients had colonic involvement, and all fistulas involved the duodenum. Past abdominal aortic aneurysm surgeries included 12 cases (75%) of open surgical graft replacement and 4 cases (25%) of EVAR. Detailed patient background information is shown in Table 1.

Vascular reconstruction consisted of in situ reconstruction only in 13 patients (81%), in situ reconstruction with adjunctive femoro‐femoral bypass in 2 patients (13%), and true extra‐anatomical reconstruction with axillo‐femoral bypass in 1 patient (6%). The median operation time was 651 min (range: 481–1477), and the median blood loss was 3050 mL (range: 850–15 000). Transfusion was performed in all cases. The median volume of transfused red blood cells were 2520 mL (range 1120‐9800), fresh frozen plasma 2160 mL (range 480–9360), and platelet concentrate 200 mL (range 0–800). Pathogenic organisms were identified from intraoperative abscess or tissue cultures in 15 of the 16 patients and included Gram‐positive bacteria, Gram‐negative bacteria, fungi, and anaerobes (Table S1). Antibiotics were selected according to susceptibility testing and administered for a median of 56 days (range, 23–110 days). Histopathological examination confirmed fistula formation in all patients, and no malignant lesions were identified.

No deaths occurred within 30 days postoperatively. Three patients (19%) died within 90 days postoperatively. Two patients died of recurrent sepsis despite initial postoperative improvement, and one patient died due to exacerbation of gastrointestinal amyloidosis. For overall survival (OS), the median survival time was not reached, and the one‐year survival rate was 57%.

Discussion

This study reports the treatment outcomes for sADF, a disease in which gastroenterological surgeons are likely to be involved in treatment.

The primary complaint in sADF is often gastrointestinal bleeding [10, 12, 13]. Typically, massive bleeding may occur following a bleeding episode known as “Herald bleeding” [3]. In a large‐scale review by Koliakos et al. [1], gastrointestinal bleeding was the most common initial symptom, observed in 80% of cases. In this study, herald bleeding was observed in 10 cases (63%). Katsinelos et al. [4] reported herald bleeding in 83% of cases, emphasizing the importance of not overlooking this early sign. Abdominal pain was reported in about 30%–40% [7, 14] of cases. In this study, abdominal pain was observed in 5 cases (31%). These findings support the high frequency with which gastrointestinal diseases are initially suspected, suggesting that both gastroenterologists and gastroenterological surgeons should be aware of this condition.

The sADF is reported to occur in the third and fourth portions of the duodenum in 92.9% of cases [1], with nearly all arising in the duodenum. In this study, the rate was 100%. This is thought to be due to the mechanism of sADF formation, with the persistent stimulation of the intestinal wall by aortic pulsations playing a decisive role [2, 15]. Other mechanisms are based on the presence of localized inflammation due to aortic graft infection during the initial surgery and subsequent intestinal wall erosion [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, the majority of fistulas occur adjacent to the anastomotic site of the third portion of the duodenum. This location is situated between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery. Treatment requires surgical intervention and must address both vascular and digestive system issues [7]. Recurrence of sADF worsens prognosis [11, 18]; we consider it dangerous for the duodenum repaired by fistula excision alone to come into contact again with a new graft. Therefore, we perform resection of the third portion of the duodenum. Primary repair alone was not routinely performed because persistent infection and recurrent fistulization remained concerns. We experienced one patient who underwent EVAR and fistula closure without radical duodenal resection and subsequently died of recurrent disease, supporting our preference for radical resection whenever definitive surgery was feasible. The mesenteric approach has been reported as a useful method for pancreas‐preserving partial duodenectomy, especially when the inflamed duodenum is widely fixed and conventional mobilization is difficult [19]. Our approach combines mobilization from the Treitz ligament and right‐sided Kocher maneuver, allowing exposure of the third and fourth portions while preserving the papilla of Vater and maintaining fistulous attachment until vascular control is secured.

EVAR is sometimes an effective treatment option for sADF. A systematic review by Kakkos et al. [20]. Reported that while the incidence of postoperative complications after EVAR is lower compared to open aortic reconstruction, long‐term survival rates are worse. To address this weakness, they propose a stepwise approach to sADF. Following emergency EVAR and bypass surgery as a life‐saving measure, graft replacement should be performed as a second‐stage procedure [10, 21, 22]. Therefore, treatment options involve either performing the third portion of duodenectomy with duodenojejunostomy and vascular reconstruction as an emergency procedure or performing these procedures in a two‐stage approach after stabilizing the patient's general condition with EVAR. When treating sADF, the following are essential for gastroenterological surgeons: 1. Gastroenterological surgeons must consider the possibility of sADF, ensure it is not overlooked, and facilitate initial treatment by cardiovascular surgeons. 2. When encountering sADF, they must possess the ability to understand and perform resection of the third portion of the duodenum with duodenojejunostomy.

The major causes of postoperative mortality were uncontrolled infection and deterioration of severe pre‐existing comorbidities. Although radical resection and vascular reconstruction achieved initial source control, recurrent sepsis remained a major cause of death in two patients. These findings highlight the importance of prolonged infection surveillance, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, nutritional support, and multidisciplinary postoperative management. Because patients with sADF often present with severe infection and multiple comorbidities, postoperative care remains as important as the surgical procedure itself. No consistent intraoperative technical factors or clinical characteristics clearly distinguished the non‐survivors from the survivors in our series. Rather, fatal outcomes were mainly associated with failure of postoperative infection control and deterioration of severe pre‐existing comorbidities.

This study has several limitations. It was a single‐center retrospective study with a small sample size, which may limit generalizability. In addition, because this report focused on the gastrointestinal surgical perspective, vascular reconstruction techniques and graft‐related outcomes were not fully evaluated. Finally, the rarity and emergency nature of sADF make prospective randomized studies difficult, although multicenter studies may help clarify optimal treatment strategies.

In conclusion, radical resection of the third portion of the duodenum with duodenojejunostomy and vascular reconstruction may provide a definitive strategy for sADF when performed within a coordinated multidisciplinary framework. A clear understanding of this technique may help gastrointestinal surgeons contribute safely to the management of this rare but life‐threatening condition.

This video demonstrates radical resection of the third portion of the duodenum for secondary aortoduodenal fistula. The jejunum is divided approximately 20 cm distal to the Treitz ligament. The Treitz ligament is completely divided to allow full mobilization of the duodenojejunal flexure. The fourth portion of the duodenum is mobilized from the uncinate process using both sharp and blunt dissection. Branches of the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery are ligated and divided. Importantly, the fistulous attachment is preserved at this stage to prevent uncontrolled hemorrhage. An extended Kocher maneuver is performed to obtain circumferential exposure of the aortoduodenal interface. Preoperative imaging is reviewed to confirm the location of the papilla of Vater. The duodenum is transected distal to the papilla to preserve biliary and pancreatic drainage. Reconstruction is performed by duodenojejunostomy. Depending on anatomical conditions, either an end‐to‐end or side‐to‐side anastomosis is created. Omental reinforcement is performed. The greater omentum is mobilized through a retrocolonic route. The gastrointestinal surgeons ensure sufficient mobility to allow complete coverage of the reconstructed graft. After vascular reconstruction, the cardiovascular surgeons wrap the omentum around the graft and anastomotic site to enhance infection control.
