The Loss of Tension in an Infinite Membrane with Holes of Decaying Spatial Density
Lukas Early, Stanislav Volkov

Abstract
What is the effect of randomly removing material from an infinite stretched membrane? Under what conditions can the membrane still sustain tension? This problem was introduced by Robert Connelly in connection with applications of rigidity theory (see e.g. [ 4 ] ) in the natural sciences, and was later studied in [ 7 ] ; a discrete version was also considered in [ 3 ] . We study a mathematical framework based on a non-homogeneous Poisson point process whose intensity λ \lambda tends to zero at infinity. The hole shapes are i.i.d. and independent of their locations. We show that if the intensity does not decay too quickly, then tension is still lost throughout the whole plane, as in the homogeneous model studied in [ 7 ] . Conversely, we give sufficient conditions under which complete loss of tension does not occur. Thus both destruction and non-destruction regimes are possible even when the intensity tends to zero, indicating a phase transition in the model. The processes studied here are closely related to bootstrap percolation.



Keywords:
bootstrap percolation, Poisson point process, tension, rigidity.

1
Introduction

Let
{\cal M}
be a stretched two-dimensional membrane clamped along its boundary.

Definition 1.1
.

A hole
H
is a bounded closed convex two-dimensional subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with non-empty interior, with a special point in its interior, called its centre. The diameter of a hole is the quantity
\mathrm{diam}(H)=\sup_{x,y\in H}\lVert x-y\rVert
,
where here and later throughout the paper
\lVert z\rVert
denotes the Euclidean distance between
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the origin
{\bf 0}=(0,0)
.

A number of holes are punched in this membrane. Removing such a hole from the membrane leads to a redistribution of tension in the remaining region. With multiple holes, the behaviour becomes more complex: If two holes overlap, tension is lost at least on the convex hull of their union, not just their union. What happens next is that whenever this convex hull of the union intersects some other hole, the whole convex hull of the original hull and the new hole also loses tension (see Figure
1
), and the process continues ad infinitum. Note that the convex hull is a closure operator: it is extensive, monotone, and idempotent, and as a result, the order in which the convex hulls are taken does not matter. Consequently, we can define the “limiting” convex hull (formally defined as the infinite union of the convex hulls for each stage).

Figure 1:
Sequential growth of convex hulls

Formally, for
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\hull(A)
denote the convex hull of
A
, the smallest convex set containing
A
, i.e.,

\hull(A)=\bigcap_{C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ is convex, }C\supseteq A}C.

Definition 1.2
.

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\mathcal{C}(A)
be the collection of connected components of
A
. The propagation of loss of tension is the operator

\phi(A)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}(A)}\hull(C).

The process of propagation of loss of tension can be iterated. Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}=A
and for
k\geq 1
define
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\phi(\mathcal{L}_{k-1})
. Since
\{\mathcal{L}_{k}\}_{k}
is an increasing sequence of sets, we can define the limit

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{k}.

If
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we say that
the whole membrane is destroyed
by the loss of tension propagation.

It was shown in
[
7
]
that if the initial configuration
\mathcal{L}_{0}
consists of a collection of holes whose shapes are chosen randomly from the same distribution, and their centres are distributed according to a
homogeneous
spatial Poisson Point Process (later abbreviated as PPP) with constant intensity
\lambda>0
, then the loss of tension process above will eventually cover the whole
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, leading to loss of tension everywhere, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Notably, this fact holds for any value of
\lambda
, however small it is, implying that there is no phase transition in this model. This result is similar to those in bootstrap percolation models, which exhibit a similar behaviour, see e.g.
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
; for a three-dimensional version see
[
5
]
.

The proof of the above fact heavily depends on the fact that the placement of holes by the PPP is homogeneous, so by e.g. the Borel-Cantelli lemma, there must be a region
somewhere
in the plane with a high concentration of holes, from which, using the sequential process of loss of tension, the plane gets covered. This method can be easily extended to the case when the intensity
\lambda=\lambda(\mathbf{z})
depends on
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
but
\liminf_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert\to\infty}\lambda(\mathbf{z})>0
. An interesting question therefore, is whether the complete loss of tension can occur when the intensity of the Poisson process decreases to zero. In this paper, we show that there are two functions
\bar{\lambda},\underline{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, both converging to zero, such that the whole plane will be covered by the process if
\lambda(\mathbf{z})\geq\bar{\lambda}(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
, and on the contrary, that this will not happen a.s. when
\lambda(\mathbf{z})\leq\underline{\lambda}(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
, thus suggesting a phase transition in the model.

The model described in this paper is also related to entanglement percolation
[
2
]
.

Main results

Assume that centres of holes are placed at some countable non-empty subset

Suppose that all the holes are unit discs, with their natural centres being the holes’ centres. Let

(1)

for some
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(X)=\mathbb{R}^{2})=1
.

Let

B_{r}(\mathbf{z}_{0})=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\rVert\leq r\}

Definition 2.2
.

Let
n_{0}
be a sufficiently large positive integer. For each
n=n_{0}+1,n_{0}+2,\dots
define the
n
th seed as
B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})

\mathbf{w}_{n}=(\ell_{n},0)

\ell_{n}=n\log^{2}n

Lemma 2.3
.

Assuming

n

Proof.

The distance between the centres of the consecutive seeds (which are the closest to each other) satisfies

\displaystyle(n+1)\log^{2}(n+1)-n\log^{2}n\geq\log^{2}(n+1)\gg 2\log^{2-2\delta}(n+1)=2R_{n+1}^{2}

for sufficiently large

Hence
B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\cap B_{R_{n+1}}(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})=\emptyset

\inf\left\{\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime\prime}\rVert:\ \mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n}),\ \mathbf{z}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{R_{n+1}}(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})\right\}\to\infty\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Since
\log^{2}n/R_{n}^{2}=\log^{2\delta}n\to\infty
, the distance
between consecutive seed centres is much larger than
R_{n}^{2}
, and in particular much larger than
R_{n}+R_{n+1}
. Hence the seeds are disjoint for all sufficiently large
n
.
∎

Definition 2.4

We call the

B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1}

2.1

\mathcal{L}_{0}
be a collection of holes centred at the points of
X

\mathcal{L}_{0}=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rVert\leq 1\text{ for some }\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in X\}.

Lemma 2.5
.

With probability
1
, there are infinitely many good seeds.

Proof.

Let
A_{n}
be the event “the
n
th seed is good”.
Since the seeds are disjoint and
A_{n}
depends only on the configuration of
X
inside
B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
, the events
A_{n}
are independent by the independence of Poisson processes on disjoint regions.
We will show that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\geq 1/n
, and
then the result will follow from the second Borel-Cantelli lemma and the fact that the harmonic series diverges.

Indeed, split the ring

B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\setminus B_{R_{n}-1/2}(\mathbf{w}_{n})=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ R_{n}-1/2\leq\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}_{n}\rVert\leq R_{n}\}

into
K_{n}:=\left\lfloor 13R_{n}\right\rfloor

\alpha_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{K_{n}}
. Then for large enough
n
we have the following:

(a)

the maximum distance between two points of the same annular sector is smaller than

\alpha_{n}\,R_{n}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2\pi R_{n}}{\left\lfloor 13R_{n}\right\rfloor}+\frac{1}{2}<1;

(b)

the maximum distance between two points of adjacent annular sectors is smaller than

2\alpha_{n}\,R_{n}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{4\pi R_{n}}{\left\lfloor 13R_{n}\right\rfloor}+\frac{1}{2}<2;

(c)

the area of each annular sector is

\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{n}^{2}-\left(R_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\alpha_{n}=\frac{\pi(R_{n}-1/4)}{\left\lfloor 13R_{n}\right\rfloor}>\frac{1}{5}.

Now note that a sufficient condition for the
n
th seed to be good is that each annular sector contains at least one point of
X
, as then we will have a sequence of
K_{n}
overlapping discs, whose convex hull covers
B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
entirely.
Indeed, because of (a), each annular sector will be completely covered by the hole centred in it, implying that the piece of the circumference corresponding to this sector is covered. Because of (b), every two holes in the neighbouring sectors overlap.
See Figure
2
.

Since the area of each annular sector is at least
\frac{1}{5}
by (c), and
\lambda(\mathbf{z})\geq g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})
for all
\mathbf{z}\in B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
as
(\ell_{n}+R_{n},0)
is the furthest point from the origin belonging to the
n
th seed, the number of points of
X
in each of the annular sectors has the Poisson distribution with the rate at least
\frac{1}{5}g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})
, yielding

\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\geq\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})}{5}\right)\right]^{K_{n}}\geq\left(\frac{g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})}{10}\right)^{K_{n}}=e^{K_{n}\log(g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})/10)}

where we used the fact that
1-e^{-x}>x/2

0\leq x\leq 1
, which is applicable since
g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})\to 0
.
Now

\displaystyle-K_{n}\log(g(\ell_{n}+R_{n})/10)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor 13R_{n}\right\rfloor\log\left(10\,\log^{1/2-\delta}(\ell_{n}+R_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=(6.5-13\delta+o(1))\left(\log^{1-\delta}n\right)\left(\log\log n\right)<\log n,

hence
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})>e^{-\log n}=\frac{1}{n}
and thus the lemma is proven.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix
\nu\in(0,1)
. Suppose that

Fix

Since, after removing the forbidden cone, each such sector has area at least

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
\nu\in(0,\delta)
. Then for
n
large enough, for each
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
the
k
th seed lies entirely inside of the forbidden zone
F_{\nu}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
. Indeed, for
\mathbf{z}=(x,y)\in B_{R_{k}}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
we have

|y|\leq R_{k}\text{ and }|x-\ell_{n}|\geq\ell_{n}-\ell_{k}-R_{k}.

For
k<n
, the right-hand side of the second inequality is minimized, up to lower-order terms, when
k=n-1
. Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
,

|x-\ell_{n}|^{(1-\nu)/2}\geq\left(\ell_{n}-\ell_{n-1}-R_{n-1}\right)^{(1-\nu)/2}\gg\log^{1-\delta}n\geq R_{k}\geq|y|,

(see the proof of Lemma
2.3
) because
\nu<\delta
. Therefore
B_{R_{k}}(\mathbf{w}_{k})\subseteq F_{\nu}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
.

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
. Let
A_{n}
be the event that the
n
th seed is good; note that
A_{n}
depends solely on
X\bigcap B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
.
Let
C_{n}
be the event that, starting with the whole disc

B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})
already destroyed, and using only holes with centres in

X\setminus\left(B_{R_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\cup F_{\nu}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\right),

the propagation
does not
cover all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
By Lemma
2.8
there is
n_{1}
such that for all
n\geq n_{1}

\mathbb{P}(C_{n})\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\tau=\inf\{n\geq n_{1}:\ A_{n}\text{ occurs}\}
; by Lemma
2.5

\tau<\infty
a.s. On the event
\{\tau=n\}\cap C_{n}^{c}
,
the
n
th seed is good and the outside propagation succeeds; hence

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(X)=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(X)\neq\mathbb{R}^{2})\leq\mathbb{P}(C_{\tau})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\tau=n\text{ and }C_{n}).

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\tau=n)\mathbb{P}(C_{n})\leq\varepsilon\sum_{n\geq n_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\tau=n)\leq\varepsilon

because
C_{n}
is independent of
\{\tau=n\}
.
Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Suppose that the holes are chosen i.i.d. from some collection of holes
\mathcal{H}
, and the intensity of the PPP satisfies
\lambda(\mathbf{z})\geq g(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
where
g
is defined by (
1
), then the membrane will eventually be destroyed a.s.

Proof.

For each hole
H\in\mathcal{H}
let
r_{H}
be the maximum radius of the discs centred at the centre of the hole, completely contained inside of
H
. Then
r_{H}
is a positive random variable, and hence there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(r_{H}\geq\epsilon)\geq 1/2
. Now, after placing the holes on the membrane, throw away all those with
r_{H}<\epsilon
; by this, we make the task of destroying the membrane only harder, because of the monotonicity of
\phi
. As a result, each of the remaining holes contains the disc of radius
\epsilon
, centred at the points of the PPP. Now we have reduced the model to the one in Theorem
2.1
, except that the radius of all discs is
\epsilon
and the intensity has been reduced by at most a factor
2
.

The model can now be rescaled with the new intensity of the PPP
\tilde{\lambda}(\mathbf{z}):=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\lambda(\epsilon\,\mathbf{z})
, so that all discs have unit radius.
The multiplicative constant
\epsilon^{2}/2
can be absorbed by replacing
\delta
with a smaller positive value, and the conditions of Theorem
2.1
are fulfilled.
∎

3
Non-propagation

Throughout this section, we will assume that almost surely any bounded domain of
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
has finitely many holes centred in it, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Lambda(A):=\iint_{A}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}<\infty,

(5)

as the number of such centres has a Poisson distribution with parameter
\Lambda(A)
.

Obviously, the membrane will not be destroyed if there are only finitely many holes on the plane, regardless of the set
\mathcal{H}
, which leads to the following straightforward statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose

\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}<\infty.

(6)

Then the membrane will not be destroyed a.s., that is,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)=0
.

Proof.

The total number of holes on the whole plane has a Poisson distribution with parameter
\Lambda<\infty
. Therefore, the number of holes is a.s. finite, and the union of all of them lies inside of a bounded set a.s. (see Definition
1.1
). This implies the statement.
∎

Note that if
\lambda(\mathbf{z})=f(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
then (
6
) is equivalent to

\int_{0}^{\infty}rf(r)\,\mathrm{d}r<\infty

which will be true e.g. if
f(r)\leq\frac{1}{r^{2+\varepsilon}}
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

We can strengthen the above proposition as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that for some constant
D>0
all holes are bounded by
D
in size, i.e.
\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{diam}(H)<D
, and the Poisson process is such that for large
\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert
its intensity satisfies
\lambda(\mathbf{z})\leq f(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
where

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\,\,\mathrm{d}r<\infty

and the function
f(r)
is decreasing for large
r
.
Then the membrane will not be destroyed a.s.

For example, any
\lambda
with the corresponding
f(r)=\frac{1}{r\log^{\gamma}r}
for some
\gamma>1
and sufficiently large
r
will satisfy the condition of the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

It is sufficient to show that we can find an integer
h
such that the horizontal strip

S_{h}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ 2Dh<y\leq 2D(h+1)\}

is completely free from the points of the Poisson process; call this event
A_{h}
(see Figure
5
.)

An impenetrable strip
S_{h}
separating two parts of the plane

Then the convex hull of all the holes below this strip and the convex hull of those above the strip will not have common points, and hence no point on the line
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ y=2Dh+D\}
will be covered by
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
.

Assume that
h
is so large that
f(r)
is decreasing for
r\geq 2Dh
. Now, the probability of
A_{h}
, i.e., that no point of the Poisson process lands in
S_{h}
equals
\mathbb{P}(A_{h})=e^{-\Lambda_{h}}
where

\Lambda_{h}=\iint_{S_{h}}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\int_{2Dh}^{2D(h+1)}\lambda(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 4D\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+4D^{2}h^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

for large enough
h
. Set
c(h)=\frac{\log h}{5Df(2Dh)}
. The integral in the RHS is bounded by

4D\int_{0}^{c(h)}f(2Dh)\,\mathrm{d}x+4D\int_{c(h)}^{\infty}f\left(x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{4}{5}\log h+o(1)<\log h\quad\text{ for large }h

where
o(1)\to 0
as
h\to+\infty
since
c(h)\to\infty
.
Hence
\Lambda_{h}<\log h
for large positive

yielding that
\mathbb{P}(A_{h})>\frac{1}{h}
. Since the events
\{A_{h}\}
are independent and
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h}=\infty
, it implies that a.s. there exists an
h
for which
A_{h}
occurs and hence
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\neq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

In the setup of Theorem

Proof.

By the proof of Theorem

and the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, almost surely there are infinitely many empty horizontal strips

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that for some constant
D>0

\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{diam}(H)<D

Remark 3.4
.

While Theorem
3.1
covers the case with
f(r)=\frac{1}{r\log^{\gamma}r}
for some
\gamma>1
, Theorem
3.3
is applicable even if

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Since the property (
7

D=1
. Fix a pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}
where

\mathcal{I}=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}

is a set of four elements, and split the plane into
4\times 4
disjoint boxes

R_{a,b}^{i,j}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ 4a-2<x-2i\leq 4a+2,\ 4b-2<y-2j\leq 4b+2\},\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{Z}.

The number of hole centres in such a box is Poisson(
\mu_{a,b}

\displaystyle\mu_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\Lambda(R_{a,b}^{0,0})=\iint_{R_{a,b}^{0,0}}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=\int_{4a-2}^{4a+2}\int_{4b-2}^{4b+2}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{a,b}^{0,0}\text{ contains more than one hole centre})=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{a,b}^{k}e^{-\mu_{a,b}}}{k!}\leq\mu_{a,b}^{2}.

(8)

At the same time,

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu_{a,b}^{2}<\infty.

(9)

Indeed, by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

\displaystyle\mu_{a,b}^{2}=\left[\iint_{R_{a,b}^{0,0}}\lambda(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\right]^{2}\leq 16\iint_{R_{a,b}^{0,0}}\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

and now (
9
) follows from (
7
)

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, (
8
) and (
9
) imply that almost surely there are only finitely many boxes
R_{a,b}^{0,0}
with more than one centre of hole. The same argument applies to the other three shifted decompositions corresponding to
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}
. Hence, almost surely, only finitely many boxes among all four decompositions contain more than one hole centre. Since two overlapping holes have centres at distance
<2
, and any two points at distance

lie in a common box

for at least one

(see Figure

such that no two holes with centres outside

overlap.

Let
k>\nu/2

is contained in

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\neq\mathbb{R}^{2}

We have

\mathbb{P}(C_{2k}\text{ has no hole centres})=e^{-\Lambda(C_{2k})}

Consequently, after some time the process of taking convex hulls will stop with some finite component completely inside of

and only the original holes outside of it.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that the fact that outside of a certain box
[-L,L]^{2}
no two holes overlap, does not guarantee that the whole plane is not destroyed. Indeed, the construction in the proofs of Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
shows just the opposite: once you have a “good seed” (e.g. inside the box
[-L,L]^{2}
) it is possible, even with quite sparsely located holes, to destroy the whole plane.

We finish the article with an open problem.

Problem 3.6
.

Is there a positive function
g_{\gamma}(r)
decreasing to zero as
r\to\infty
, continuously depending on a parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, such that if the intensity of the PPP is given by
\lambda(\mathbf{z})=g_{\gamma}(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert)
, then
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2})=1
if
\gamma>\gamma_{\mathrm{cr}}
and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2})<1
if
\gamma<\gamma_{\mathrm{cr}}
for some
\gamma_{\mathrm{cr}}
?

A positive answer to this question would establish the sharp phase transition in our model.
