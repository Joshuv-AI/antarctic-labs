Eight Ways Agrivoltaics Installations Can Impact
Communities: Insights from the Energy to
Communities Peer-Learning Cohort
From July to December of 2024, the National Laboratory of the Rockies partnered with World
Resources Institute to run a peer-learning cohort for local governments and community-based
organizations on “Implementing an Agrivoltaics Project.” This cohort effort, funded by the U.S.
Department of Energy’s Energy to Communities program, provided 15 organizations with expert
presentations, peer-learning opportunities, and technical assistance related to agrivoltaics project
development. Pulling from cohort discussions, this document shares a summary of the benefits
that agrivoltaics projects can provide to surrounding communities.

Agrivoltaics is the practice of colocating solar and agriculture, with agricultural production
occurring underneath or between rows of solar panels. This practice, which can include either
crop production or animal grazing, offers many benefits to farmers and surrounding
communities. For farmers, agrivoltaics installations can provide additional income in that this
practice allows them to produce both energy and food in the same area. In addition, dependent on
climate zones and weather conditions, agrivoltaics can reduce irrigation requirements, improve
crop yield, and improve crop resistance in droughts.

However, many communities are not familiar with the concept of colocating agriculture and
solar energy production, which can create barriers to planning and implementing these projects.
Additionally, farmers and communities may be unfamiliar with the many benefits that
agrivoltaics can offer. This guide describes several ways in which agrivoltaics installations can
benefit the communities where they are located.

 1. Diversifying local economies: Rural communities that are reliant on specific types of
 agriculture can be subject to rapid stressors and changes in agricultural markets.
 According to a 2023 U.S. Department of Agriculture study, 84% of farmers hold at least
 one or more jobs off the farm to supplement their farm income. Agrivoltaics installations
 provide diversity to farm incomes and local economies and, because the agriculture
 practices and solar photovoltaics are colocated, can keep farmland in agricultural
 production.
 2. Boosting local energy independence and reducing utility bills: Dependent on the
 regulatory structure of the state, agrivoltaics installations can choose to use the energy
 produced on-site, or export energy to the grid for bill credits. By generating solar energy,
 farmers can significantly reduce utility bills. If the system is configured to allow for on-
 site energy usage, agrivoltaics can also boost the energy independence of the farm and
 surrounding community. This means that energy production is localized to the
 community, which may reduce the need for import of outside fuels. Agrivoltaics
 installations can also provide an opportunity for further electrification of farm equipment,
 with the potential of reducing local air pollution.

  3. Supporting local access to healthy food: Many urban and rural communities experience
 food deserts, or lack of access to healthy, fresh foods. Agrivoltaics installations can serve
 as an important source of fresh food for local communities. Producing fresh, nutritious
 food and keeping that food in the local economy can provide healthy alternatives in areas
 where processed foods are typically more accessible.
  4. Rebuilding soil health: Monocultural agricultural practices can deplete the soil of
 necessary nutrients, bacteria, and microorganisms, which can damage the active health
 and structural integrity of the soil. To understand optimal crop growing conditions,
 today’s agrivoltaics installations that involve crop production often rotate crops
 seasonally to test and analyze how they grow in the shadier conditions under or between
 the solar panels. These small- or medium-sized installations can pair crop rotation with
 other conservation agriculture strategies, which typically leads to increased soil health.
  5. Providing educational programming and capacity building: Educational
 programming about dual-use agrivoltaics projects, such as the seminars and tours held at
 Jack’s Solar Garden in Colorado, can build knowledge and interest in the surrounding
 region for the development of similar projects. Through tours, internships, and various
 learning experiences, implementers of agrivoltaics projects can educate current and future
 farmers about colocation of solar energy installations and about other agricultural
 practices that complement the use of agrivoltaics.
  6. Supporting workforce development programs: In the cohort, multiple nonprofits
 considering agrivoltaics projects planned to support their communities through workforce
 development programs. Examples of these activities include using the agrivoltaics project
 to train workers on solar installations more broadly, engaging farmers on agricultural
 practices, and teaching culinary workers about the usage and benefits of incorporating
 local produce and meats in their work.
  7. Making procurement decisions: Agrivoltaics installations require specific knowledge
 on the part of the installer, the farmer, and the project owner or developer. By working
 with local nonprofits and businesses to develop these agrivoltaics projects, farmers and
 project owners can build a specific local knowledge base and skill set that allows for
 replicability of agrivoltaics projects and provides additional economic opportunity.
  8. Contributing to the literature on the interactions between solar arrays and
 agricultural processes: Since agrivoltaics is an emerging field, researchers and experts
 are early in the process of learning how the installation, shading, and positioning of solar
 arrays affect specific crop types in various climates. By collaborating with schools and
 universities, farmers with agrivoltaics installations that test multiple types of crops can
 contribute to this growing body of literature. This type of research is crucial to scaling
 agrivoltaics across various climates.
