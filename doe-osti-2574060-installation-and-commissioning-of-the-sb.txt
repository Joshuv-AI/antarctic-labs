 Abstract
 This report describes the progress towards a scalable detector capable of
 quasi-background-free detection of sub-keV nuclear recoils under DOE award DE-
 SC0024254. Work supported by this award follows two distinct thrusts: (1) the
 first calibration of sensitivity to sub-keV nuclear recoils in a liquid-noble bubble
 chamber, based on data taken in a prototype liquid xenon bubble chamber run pre-
 viously at Northwestern University, and (2) the successful assembly and installation
 of the 10-kg liquid argon bubble chamber SBC-LAr10 in the MINOS underground
 area at Fermilab. These accomplishments have paved the way for the precision cal-
 ibration of SBC-LAr10 in the MINOS underground area at Fermilab in the coming
 year, which will use novel calibration techniques to measure the potential of the
 liquid noble bubble chamber technology for the detection of GeV-scale dark mat-
 ter particles and the measurement of coherent elastic nuclear scattering by reactor
 anti-neutrinos (CEνNS).

1 Introduction
Liquid noble bubbles chambers have the potential to achieve ton-year quasi-background-
free exposures measuring the O(100) eV nuclear recoils produced by elastic nuclear scat-
tering of particles with O(1) MeV/c momentum, such as dark matter particles with masses
down to 1 GeV/c2 and anti-neutrinos produced at nuclear reactors. Such interactions will
both generate faint (few-photon) scintillation light and nucleate a single bubble in the
superheated target of the bubble chamber. Backgrounds from ambient radioactivity, on
the other hand, typically create either no bubbles (e.g., electronic recoils from beta-decays
and interactions with gamma-rays), create multiple bubbles (nuclear recoils from neutrons
with few-cm mean free paths), or create one bubble with intense scintillation light (alpha-
decays). In the context of a dark matter search in a shielded, underground environment,
the combination of scintillation and bubble nucleation signals enables the suppression of
all backgrounds except for neutrino coherent nuclear scattering (CEνNS) to levels below
one event per ton-year, while at a nuclear reactor it allows measurements of up to O(100)
CEνNS events per day while suppressing the gamma and neutron backgrounds prevalent
in the reactor environment. Reference [1] describes the reach of a liquid noble bubble

chamber dark matter search, and Reference [2] describes these detectors’ potential to
constrain BSM physics through measurements of CEνNS rates at a nuclear reactor.
 The background discrimination capability described above was initially shown at an
8-keV bubble nucleation threshold in a 30-gram xenon bubble chamber operated at North-
western in [3], with hints that ER-discrimination might extend to much lower thresholds
in noble liquids than in freon-based bubble chambers, where electronic recoils begin to
nucleate bubbles when the nuclear recoil detection threshold is at or below ∼3 keV [4, 5].
The onset of bubble nucleation by ERs is in part due to the presence of molecular de-
grees of freedom, which allow electrons to locally heat the fluid (by exciting vibrational
modes in the molecules present) and thereby nucleate bubbles in the superheated target.
These degrees of freedom are absent in a noble liquid, allowing much greater degrees of
superheat (lower nuclear recoil detection threshold) while remaining blind to ERs in the
bubble nucleation channel.
 This technical report describes progress towards evaluating liquid noble bubble cham-
ber sensitivity and background (ER) discrimination at the sub-keV thresholds required for
GeV-scale dark matter searches and reactor CEνNS measurements. Progress is reported
on two fronts: (1) development of the analysis techniques needed to validate sensitivity
to sub-keV nuclear recoils and the application of those techniques to low-threshold data
taken in the 30-gram xenon prototype; and (2) assembly, installation and commissioning
of SBC-LAr10, a 10-kg liquid argon bubble chamber that will operate in the MINOS
underground area at Fermilab, exploring detector performance at the highest degrees of
superheat allowed by statistical mechanics.
2 Sub-keV Calibration of the Northwestern Prototype
The most common method for calibrating nuclear recoil response in dark matter and
CEνNS detectors is through tagged scattering of mono-energetic neutrons, as in [6]. In
this technique, mono-energetic neutrons (often produced via (p,n) interactions in a thin
target) scatter in the detector under test and in an array of backing detectors, and the
reconstructed scattering angle of each neutron gives the corresponding nuclear recoil en-
ergy deposited in the detector under test. Unfortunately this technique is impractical for
bubble chamber calibration at low energies due to the bubble chamber’s rate limitations.
A single-cell bubble chamber can record at most ∼1,000 bubble-nucleating events per day,
and in a typical tagged scattering experiment less than 1% of the beam-induced bubble
nucleation events are tagged recoils below 150 eV. To calibrate a bubble chamber at low
energies then, two things are needed: an analysis technique that does not rely on tagged
scattering and a nuclear recoil source where most recoils are near the energy of interest.
 Constraining nuclear recoil sensitivity without tagging recoils can be done by exposing
the detector to a variety of sources producing different nuclear recoil spectra and com-
paring the rate of observed recoils from each source to Monte Carlo simulation. This
approach was used successfully by PICO for freon-based bubble chambers [5], and was
applied with the support of this grant to past data taken with the Northwestern 30-gram
xenon bubble chamber. The application to the xenon bubble chamber modified PICO’s
algorithm to better handle uncertainty on background bubble nucleation rates and also
incorporated threshold interpolation for data taken at varying degrees of superheat. This
 2

analysis showed the nuclear recoil bubble nucleation threshold to match that calculated
by the Seitz “Hot-spike” model to within 20% at a calculated threshold of 0.9 keV, while
also showing better than 1 in 106 ER discrimination at calculated thresholds down to
at least 0.5 keV [7]. This constitutes the first demonstration of sub-keV nuclear recoil
detection with background discrimination in a detector with a liquid target.
 The analysis in [7] relied on mono-energetic neutrons produced by the 9 Be(γ,n) reac-
tion [8], well suited to nuclear recoil calibrations for thresholds around 1 keV. To reach
thresholds of 100 eV, new sources capable of generating predominantly lower energy nu-
clear recoils are required. Two such sources have been identified, both taking advantage
of the bubble chamber’s insensitivity (in the bubble nucleation channel) to electron re-
coils: thermal neutron capture and Thomson scattering of high energy gamma rays. In
thermal neutron capture, an 41 Ar nucleus recoils against the gamma cascade produced in
the 40 Ar(n,γ) reaction, giving recoils with a typical energy of ∼300 eV. Thomson scatter-
ing, which can be thought of as the photonic equivalent to the CEνNS interaction, can
generate nuclear recoil spectra with endpoints ranging from 87 eV (with a 22 Na gamma
source) to 363 eV (with a 228 Th/208 Tl gamma source). These novel calibration sources
and the specific calibration plan for SBC-LAr10 were presented at the 2025 APS Global
Physics Summit [9] and are described in detail in an upcoming publication, now under
final review within the SBC Collaboration.
3   SBC-LAr10
The Scintillating Bubble Chamber (SBC) Collaboration was formed following success of
the prototype xenon bubble chamber at Northwestern, and collaboration’s first experimen-
tal effort, SBC-LAr10 (Fig. 1), is a 10-kg argon bubble chamber constructed with support
from Fermilab Lab-Directed R&D funds (LDRD-2018-003), the Canada Foundation for
Innovation, and the McDonald Canadian Astroparticle Physics Research Institute. The
SBC-LAr10 detector will operate in the MINOS underground area at Fermilab where it
will (1) demonstrate the operation of a physics-scale liquid noble bubble chamber; (2)
measure the greatest superheat (lowest threshold) at which the detector remains blind
(in the bubble nucleation channel) to electron recoil backgrounds; and (3) calibrate the
nuclear recoil bubble nucleation threshold at that superheat. A full description of the
SBC-LAr10 detector is given in [10].
3.1 Installation and Commissioning of SBC-LAr10
At the beginning of the performance period of this grant, the SBC-LAr10 cryostat had
just completed its engineering run, demonstrating pressure- and temperature-control of
a liquid-argon-filled cryogenic pressure vessel, and SBC Collaborators at Queen’s Univer-
sity had completed a test assembly (at Queen’s) of the bubble chamber assembly to be
deployed inside the SBC-LAr10 cryostat. The following is a summary timeline showing
the progression of SBC-LAr10 since the start of this grant’s performance period, culmi-
nating in a device that is now ready for first operation as a bubble chamber, set to begin
immediately following the annual summer shutdown planned power outage in the MINOS
underground area.

3.2 Development of the SBC-LAr10 Data Acquisition System
The SBC-LAr10 Data Acquisition (DAQ) System is the specific responsibility of the post-
doc (Baisakhi Mitra) and graduate student (Zhiheng Sheng) supported by this grant. This
system must coordinate the disparate data streams coming from the detector including
pressure and temperature histories, bubble images, scintillation pulses, and ultrasonic
acoustic recordings of bubble nucleation. Mitra and Sheng have described this system
at the CPAD (Coordinating Panel for Advanced Detectors) 2024 Workshop [11] and the
APS 2025 Global Physics Summit [12, 13]. Figure 9 shows the data structure and DAQ
architecture adopted for SBC-LAr10, and Figures 10, 11 and 12 show elements of the
bubble imaging, scintillation, and acoustic modules of the DAQ, respectively. All figures
in this sub-section are adapted from Sheng’s 2025 APS presentation [12].

Figure 9: Above: Data structure for SBC-LAr10. All data is stored in “Bubble Events,” be-
ginning with expansion of the chamber to the superheated state and ending with a Bubble
Trigger. Scintillation pulses are self-triggered and saved throughout the event, while other data
streams are either continuous (e.g., pressure and temperature histories) or saved in a window
around the Bubble Trigger (e.g., bubble images and acoustic records). Below: Architecture of
the SBC-LAr10 Data Acquisition System. Each data stream has a corresponding module, with
a central event loop managing synchronization between modules. The DAQ is designed so that
any combination of modules may be active in a given run.

4 Future Work
The SBC Collaboration anticipates condensing liquid in SBC-LAr10 in August 2025 and
operating it as a bubble chamber for the first time in September 2025, following planned
power-outages associated with Fermilab’s annual summer shutdown. This will be followed
by a one-year calibration period, identifying the onset of bubble nucleation by background
electron recoils and measuring the nuclear recoil detection threshold to better than 20%
precision, which together determine the reach of the liquid noble bubble chamber tech-
nology as a dark matter detector. If thresholds below 400 eV are demonstrated, this will
be followed by a campaign to calibrate nuclear recoil sensitivity with the 5% precision
necessary to support physics analyses from a future reactor CEνNS measurement [2].
Dedicated papers will also be written on the source development of thermal neutron cap-
ture and Thomson scattering as new calibration tools, and their applicability to other
dark matter and neutrino detection efforts.
