 ABSTRACT

Re-Du Company (Re-Du) is developing an organocatalytic platform for converting difficult-to-
recycle plastic waste into valuable chemical building blocks. The technology is based on
selective, temperature-controlled depolymerization of condensation polymers and is intended to
address waste streams that are poorly served by conventional mechanical recycling, including
mixed, contaminated, multilayer, and industrial plastic wastes. Through the DOE Lab-Embedded
Entrepreneurship Program and Innovation Crossroads at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Re-Du
collaborated with ORNL to advance the technology from small laboratory-scale experiments
toward pre-pilot-scale demonstration.

The CRADA focused on (1) feedstock and process development, including catalyst synthesis,
depolymerization, monomer separation and purification, and evaluation of polymer products; (2)
evaluation of the scale-up path from laboratory-scale experiments (0.5–5 g) toward continuous
processing, with early scale-up to 1.0 kg/day completed and a staged pathway identified toward
pre-pilot (10 kg/day), pilot (1 ton/day), and commercial (30 tons/day) operation; and (3) techno-
economic and life-cycle assessment to understand the cost, energy, and greenhouse-gas
implications of the process.

Across representative demonstrations, the platform converted mixed and multilayer waste —
including metallized packaging films and multi-color post-consumer/post-industrial plastics —
into recovered valuable monomers without requiring feedstock pre-sorting, operating below
200°C with greater than 90% conversion. Comparative analytical characterization showed
recovered BHET monomer reaching approximately 99% purity, matching commercial reference
purity of BHET, with comparable FTIR and NMR spectra. PET repolymerized from Re-Du's
recovered monomer reached a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of approximately 60,000
g/mol, exceeding the commercial PET reference (Mn ≈ 31,000 g/mol), with comparable melting
point and moisture content. The collaboration established a technical and commercial foundation
for continued scale-up of a flexible recycling platform capable of recovering value from diverse
plastic waste streams.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

Plastics are essential to modern society, but the imbalance between production and end-of-life
management results in substantial material loss, energy consumption, and carbon emissions. The
objective of this CRADA was to develop and scale a disruptive recycling approach that converts
plastic waste into valuable chemicals and monomers. The collaboration sought to advance an
ORNL-developed organocatalytic depolymerization technology capable of selectively
deconstructing condensation polymers, including PET, PC, PU, and PA, across diverse waste
forms such as bottles, packaging, foams, lenses, textiles, carpets, and blended polymer materials.

1.1 Task 1: Feedstock and Process Development

The first objective was to establish representative sources of post-consumer and post-industrial
plastic waste and evaluate their suitability as recycling feedstocks. The technical work included
process optimization across catalyst synthesis, polymer deconstruction, monomer separation and
purification, and evaluation of recovered monomers for conversion into valuable condensation
polymers. The broader goal was to maximize monomer yield and purity while developing an
understanding of process efficiency, energy requirements, and scalability.

1.2 Task 2: Evaluation of Scale-Up Path

The second objective was to advance the process from small laboratory-scale experiments,
approximately 0.5–5 g, toward pre-pilot-scale operation. The work was intended to demonstrate
recycling of multiple industrial and post-consumer feedstocks while identifying practical
engineering requirements for larger reactors, solids handling, heat and mass transfer, product
recovery, and safe operation. Consistent with the Statement of Work target of 500 g–1 kg
demonstration scale, the team completed early scale-up to continuous processing at 1.0 kg/day
and identified a staged pathway toward pre-pilot (10 kg/day), pilot (1 ton/day), and commercial
(30 tons/day) operation.

1.3 Task 3: Techno-Economic and Life Cycle Assessment

The third objective was to evaluate the potential cost, energy consumption, and greenhouse-gas
emissions of the Re-Du process. The planned analysis included comparison of monomers
produced from plastic waste with conventional fossil-derived monomers and assessment of
mixed-plastic processing relative to processing individual polymer streams.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE’S MISSION

This project supports DOE objectives in advanced manufacturing, resource efficiency, industrial
decarbonization, and circular use of materials. By recovering chemical building blocks from
plastic waste, the technology has the potential to reduce reliance on virgin fossil feedstocks,
retain the embodied value of manufactured materials, and expand recycling options for waste
streams that are difficult to process mechanically. Demonstrated operation without feedstock pre-
sorting, at moderate temperature (<200°C) and high conversion (>90%), is directly relevant to
DOE's interest in energy-efficient, scalable approaches to plastics circularity and securing supply
chains. The work also supports the Lab-Embedded Entrepreneurship Program mission by

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

The CRADA work centered on development of a selective organocatalytic depolymerization
platform for condensation polymers. Re-Du led much of the experimental and
commercialization-focused activity, while ORNL personnel provided technical guidance,
laboratory capabilities, characterization support, process-development expertise, and assistance
with scale-up planning.

3.1 Feedstock Development and Industrial Relevance

A key element of the project was evaluation of realistic waste streams rather than reliance solely
on model polymers. Feedstock-development activities were directed toward identifying and
obtaining post-consumer and post-industrial materials representative of commercial recycling
challenges, sourced from real industry and consumer-product supply chains spanning beverage
packaging, industrial film, carpet, and foam. These materials included colored or contaminated
PET, multilayer structures, adhesive-containing materials, foams, textiles, carpets, and other
mixed-plastic formats (see Figure 8, Section 3.5).

3.2 Process Optimization and Selective Depolymerization

Process-development work investigated the operating conditions needed to selectively
deconstruct target condensation polymers while enabling recovery of valuable monomer
products. Development activities included catalyst preparation, reaction-condition optimization,
feedstock preparation, product isolation, purification, and analytical characterization. Because
the platform is intended for diverse waste streams, emphasis was placed on understanding how
polymer composition and non-target materials affect reaction performance and product recovery.

Two representative demonstrations illustrate this work. Figure 3 shows depolymerization of
Flexcon's multilayer, metallized packaging film — an industrially relevant, difficult-to-recycle
format — into a recovered monomer product and residual non-target-polymer fractions. Figure 4
shows depolymerization of mixed, multi-color post-consumer/post-industrial plastic waste,
similarly separated into a recovered monomer fraction and distinct residual fractions.

3.3 Monomer Recovery, Purification, and Characterization

Recovered products were separated and characterized to assess whether the process could
generate monomer streams suitable for subsequent reuse. Analytical methods, including FTIR
and NMR spectroscopy, melting point, and moisture content, were used to confirm product
identity and evaluate purity against commercial reference materials. This work informed the
design of downstream separation and purification steps and helped identify the process variables
most important for producing commercially relevant outputs.

3.4 Scale-Up to Pre-Pilot Quantities

The CRADA sought to bridge the gap between small laboratory experiments and pre-pilot
operation. Scale-up activities focused on transferring the chemistry to larger batch and
continuous operation, identifying equipment and operating constraints, and evaluating
reproducibility with realistic waste feedstocks. As shown in Figure 7, the team completed scale-
up from laboratory-scale synthesis (0.5–5 g) through early continuous scale-up at 1.0 kg/day, and
identified a staged pathway toward pre-pilot (10 kg/day), pilot (1 ton/day), and commercial (30
tons/day) operation. The scale-up effort to date has provided practical information relevant to
reactor configuration, mixing, heating, solids handling, filtration, solvent or glycol recovery, and
product isolation; the pre-pilot-through-commercial stages remain ongoing beyond the period of
this CRADA.

3.5 Demonstration with Post-Consumer and Industrial Waste

The process was evaluated with representative real-world waste to assess performance beyond
clean model substrates. As shown in Figure 8, evaluated feedstocks were sourced from various
kinds of industry and consumer-product waste streams, including PET beverage bottles (Coca-
Cola), multilayer packaging film (Flexcon), carpet (Southeastern Recycling), and PU foam and
backing materials (FieldTurf, Carpenter). These demonstrations establish the breadth of the
platform across post-consumer and post-industrial waste and identify feedstock-specific
challenges that must be addressed during further scale-up and commercialization.

3.7 Technology and Commercial Development During Innovation Crossroads

In parallel with technical development, Re-Du used the Innovation Crossroads environment to
refine the technology-to-market strategy, conduct customer discovery, evaluate priority waste
streams, and build relationships with potential feedstock suppliers, customers, and scale-up
partners. Re-Du's commercialization activity during this period included participation in the
Spark accelerator program, NSF Regional I-Corps (Vanderbilt University), and the SeaAhead
BlueSwell accelerator program, as well as execution of a signed pilot evaluation agreement with
Flexcon covering real industrial waste. These activities helped focusing development on
applications where selective chemical recycling can create value from materials that are difficult
to recycle through existing pathways.

 5. COMMERCIALIZATION POSSIBILITIES

The CRADA advanced a platform with potential applications across multiple difficult-to-recycle
condensation-polymer waste streams. Potential commercialization pathways include deployment
of recycling systems for concentrated industrial waste streams, partnerships with manufacturers
and recyclers, production of recovered monomers for polymer manufacturing, and development
of modular or regional processing capacity. Re-Du's signed pilot evaluation agreement with
Flexcon — covering real adhesive-backed, multilayer PET waste — is an early example of this
pathway. The most attractive early applications are expected to be those in which conventional
recycling is limited by contamination, mixed material composition, adhesives, coatings, color, or
other product-design features.

Continued commercialization will require demonstration at larger scale, validation of consistent
product quality, development of robust separation and recovery operations, completion of
techno-economic and environmental analyses, and engagement with industrial partners capable
of supporting feedstock supply, product qualification, and deployment.

 6. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

Future collaboration may focus on continued process scale-up, reactor and separation
engineering, catalyst and glycol recovery, long-duration or repeated-use studies, processing of
additional industrial feedstocks, monomer qualification, polymerization of recovered monomers,
and validation at pilot-relevant scale. Collaboration with ORNL and other technical partners
could also support process modeling, life-cycle assessment, techno-economic analysis, and
development of the engineering basis required for commercial deployment.

 7. CONCLUSION

CRADA NFE-24-10024 provided a framework for Re-Du and ORNL to advance a selective
organocatalytic approach for converting plastic waste into valuable chemical building blocks.
The work addressed feedstock development, process optimization, monomer recovery, scale-up,
and commercial and environmental assessment. Across representative demonstrations, the
platform converted mixed, multilayer, and industrially sourced waste into recovered monomer
without feedstock pre-sorting, at moderate temperature and high conversion, and comparative
analytical characterization showed recovered BHET and repolymerized PET properties
comparable to, and in molecular weight exceeding, commercial reference materials. The project
moved the technology from laboratory-scale experiments through early continuous scale-up (1.0
kg/day) and defined a staged pathway toward pre-pilot, pilot, and commercial operation,
generating technical knowledge needed for continued pilot development. The collaboration also

helped connect laboratory research with market requirements and established a foundation for
future efforts to scale and deploy a circular-plastics technology in the United States.
