Experiences of home-based caregivers of patients with chronic mental illness in Ga-Dikgale Limpopo Province
Authors: M I Novela, M B Setwaba, K M Rantho, T Sodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCaring for persons with mental illness can be burdensome and could lead to home-based caregivers experiencing various physical, emotional, and psychological challenges, such as anxiety, depression, and stress. Despite these challenges, studies prioritise the experiences of the mentally ill persons and their immediate families, with less focus on the experiences of the home-based caregivers, especially those in rural areas. Thus, this study sought to explore the experiences of home-based caregivers of people living with chronic mental illness in Ga-Dikgale, Limpopo Province, South Africa.MethodsThe study used a qualitative research approach and an exploratory case study design. The population included home-based caregivers from Dikgale. A combination of purposive and snowball sampling techniques was used to sample eight (six female and two male) home-based caregivers of family members with chronic mental illness from Ga-Dikgale, Limpopo Province. Data was collected using face-to-face semi-structured interviews and analysed using inductive thematic analysis.Results and conclusionFindings revealed that some caregivers are not adequately prepared to care for patients due to a lack of training and knowledge of handling mental health patients. Caregivers were also emotionally burdened, with minimal support from patients’ families, the government, and mental health professionals. These caregivers used coping mechanisms such as group discussions, prayer, and support from their friends and families to deal with these challenges. This study recommends a collaborative approach among the Department of Health, NGOs, and community healthcare services to facilitate support for home-based caregivers.Keywords: Coping, Chronic mental illness, Experiences, Ga-Dikgale, Home-based caregiving

Introduction

Mental illness is a widespread phenomenon that places a heavy burden on populations, patients, and their families [1]. Mental health has a major impact on health at both the individual and community levels [2]. According to the World Health Organization, the suffering, hardship, and costs associated with caring for the mentally ill are tremendous for families and societies [3]. Acharya, Maru [4, 5] state that mental illness is the largest contributor to disabilities in mostly low- and middle-income countries globally. Statistics show that one in three people in South Africa is likely to suffer from mental illness in their lifetime [6]. There is no denying that mental illness places a heavy burden on South African society on both a social and economic level. Anxiety disorders, drug addiction disorders, mood disorders, and depression are the four most common mental illnesses in South Africa [7]. Despite the huge number of people suffering from mental illness, there is a lack of mental health experts; hence, the implementation of home-based caregiver services remains significant [8].

Unlike in the 19th and early 20th centuries, today care for mentally ill patients is more decentralised, with greater emphasis on community-based and integrated care over long-stay psychiatric hospitals [9]. This is because centralized and institutionalized mental health care relies mainly on scarce specialist manpower and creates a major treatment gap for patients and places an unnecessary burden on the individual, their family, and society [10]. Therefore, the introduction of home-based care for patients with mental illnesses was meant to provide treatment and rehabilitation of the severely mentally ill within the community, and the promotion of mental health generally [11]. Moreover, there is no strong evidence that a comprehensive system of mental healthcare can be provided by either hospital-based care or community-based services; rather, the tendency is to include both components [12, 13]. Home-based care is an important part of community mental health services.

Caring for people with chronic mental illness not only harms the patient, but it also imposes a significant burden on home-based care givers (HBCGs) and is associated with certain challenges. Verity, Turiho [14] state that such challenges include high burden of care, social exclusion, health impact, not meeting the needs of caregivers, burnout (emotional distress), high social stigma, lack of support networks (low social support for caregivers), and low quality of life of caregivers. For instance, a study by Shamsaei, Cheraghi [15] found that family HBCGs of persons with severe mental illness suffer from significant stress and face different challenges, and one-third to one-half of caregivers suffer from serious psychological distress and mental illness more than the overall population. Another study conducted in a rural area in Limpopo Province Ntiseni [16] found that voluntary home-based caregivers experienced challenges because of the shortfall of home-based care kits, such as facemasks and gloves. Mbele [17] argued that caregivers in Zambia reported not receiving adequate psychosocial counselling to help them cope with the stress of caregiving. As a result, this contributed to the poor quality of care they delivered. Home-based caregivers who provide nourishment to people with severe mental disorders are impacted in different ways. The interplay of caregiving consequences, poverty, discrimination, stigma, diminished social relationships, emotional trauma, and depression is associated with the caregiving burden [18]. Valjee and Van Dyk [19] emphasised how family home-based caregivers have a significant role to play in the management of patients with chronic mental illness. The well-being of family home-based caregivers is therefore crucial because the impairment of their physical or mental health could negatively impact the management of their chronically mentally ill family member. Mokwena and Ngoveni [20] mentioned that home care for patients with serious mental illness becomes a daunting task when the patient is violent and presents safety concerns, and the lack of skills required to manage patients that would make caregivers wish patients were kept in the mental health institutions.

The obstacles and health risks associated with their profession as home-based caregivers for older people with chronic diseases have an impact on their health, well-being, and quality of life. There is, however, little information about home-based caregivers and the caregiving profession. Even though research and documentation of family members’ experiences caring for relatives with chronic mental illness in Africa have advanced, there is limited information about the support that these caregivers receive. Also, the literature that is currently available has not been integrated. Therefore, the integration of the current study findings is required to understand the complexities of caregiving, considering contextual relevance.

According to a study conducted by [21] among Muslims, Whites, Indians, and Coloreds, home-based caregivers (HBCGs) were unable to cope with the stress and emotional problems of caring for elderly patients with chronic illnesses. Black home-based caregivers were not included in the study because the study was conducted in the Western Cape province of South Africa [21]. However, the respondents thought that caring for the elderly was exhausting and too much work for them since they had to handle different tasks, such as cooking and working [21]. Furthermore, the caregivers also view caring for patients as a task that emotionally drains them to the extent that they are so angry and upset that they end up drinking alcohol. Thus, the importance of this study is to examine the experiences of the caregivers in caring for patients with long-term mental illness. Based on all the aspects that have been highlighted, it is clear that mental illness impacts the caregivers in various ways, especially those who work from home.

Generally, home-based carers help patients with activities of daily living such as dressing, eating, cooking, taking medication, bathing, and accompanying them to follow-up appointments at the hospital. According to [21], the longer a patient is cared for, especially one with schizophrenia, the higher the chances of the caregiver becoming overwhelmed. The burden of home-based caregivers is a multifaceted notion that considers the physical, psychological, emotional, social, and economic effects of looking after a disabled family member [22]. The concept of home-based caregiver burden is referred to as a burden arising because of being close to a severely ill psychiatric family member [23, 24]. These studies suggest that family members generally have little prior knowledge of mental illness, and as a result, the impact of mental illness on home-based caregivers is multifaceted. It is these responses and other psychological problems that encouraged the investigator of this study to seek and explore more about home-based caregivers’ experiences.

Methods

Research approach and design

This study used a qualitative research approach to gain a deep understanding of home-based caregivers’ experiences in their real work environment. A qualitative approach allowed researchers to remain flexible and adapt to new insights as they arose during the research process [25, 26]. This study was underpinned by the exploratory case study design methodological orientation [25, 27]. An exploratory case study is mainly used when a phenomenon is not well defined [27]. It allowed the researchers to explore individuals’ perceptions and social dynamics to help fill the gap in the literature regarding the experiences of black home-based caregivers of patients with chronic mental illness.

Study setting

The study included home-based caregivers of patients with chronic mental illness from the Dikgopheng community at Ga-Dikgale, Limpopo Province. Dikhopeng is a Sepedi name that means a place of aloe [28]. Dikgopeng community is a relatively small village, located in Ga-Dikgale, which is 45 km northwest of Polokwane [28]. Polokwane is the capital city of Limpopo Province in South Africa. The community has only two main primary health-care clinics and home-based care centres that assist with health services [29].

Study population

The participants for the study were members of the Ga-Dikgale community who cared for patients with long-term mental illnesses and were able to communicate in both Sepedi and English.

Sampling technique

Purposive and snowball sampling techniques were used to sample eight participants from the population. The purposive sampling technique was used based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, which were in line with the study’s purpose [25]. Etikan and Bala [30] underscored that researchers deliberately select participants who fit the inclusion criteria of the study because they can help to achieve the research purpose. In this study, participants were individuals who had patients with chronic mental illness, residing in the Ga-Dikgale community, and were able to comprehend English and Sepedi. To get more participants, a snowball sampling technique was also employed. Snowball sampling technique was used by the female lead researcher after interviewing the first participant and requesting him or her to link with other home-based caregivers. The lead researcher collected data until the level of saturation, when participants were providing similar information. The lead researcher holds a master’s degree in Psychology and is trained in interviews data collection method. At the time of the study, the lead researcher was unemployed. However, she had an honours degree in psychology, wherein she conducted qualitative research. Before conducting the study, the researcher had no prior established relationship with the participants of the study. The participants who participated in the study were informed about the study’s purpose and the reasons for researching caregiver experiences. Due to a prior professional interest that the lead researcher had in the topic, she maintained reflexivity by attending supervision meetings with (TS) and (MS) and journaling her personal assumptions to eliminate bias. In a review conducted by Braun and Clarke [31] it was indicated that some studies reach saturation with 6 participants, and in this study, saturation was reached with 8 participants. To be able to see the saturation, the data was analysed as it was collected, and repeating themes were quickly noted in that regard. All participants were given pseudonyms to protect their identities and to maintain confidentiality.

Data collection and recruitment

Before collecting data, the researcher applied for ethical clearance from the Turfloop Research Ethics Committee (TREC). After getting the ethical clearance, the lead researcher asked for goodwill permission from the religious leader who owns a home-based care centre. This goodwill permission was limited to gaining access to the research site and did not influence the participant’s decision to participate in the study. The lead researcher approached a research assistant (fourth author), KM, to serve as an independent person. The role of the independent person was to provide the participants with informed consent and to ensure that the participants did not feel pressured by the lead author to participate. The independent person first explained the content in the informed consent and shared it with them to decide after at least 72 h. Those who had questions were told to note them down and raise them before signing. After 72 h, the independent person collected the signed informed consent and shared it with the lead author to organise interviews. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were used to collect the data from participants. The data was collected in an office at the home-based care centre. The lead researcher placed a do not disturb sign outside the office door to eliminate disruption between sessions. These sessions were held after 16:00 because people were limited at the home-based care centre. The interviewer used an interview guide, followed by probing questions to gain more information on the participants’ experiences. The interview guide was reviewed by both supervisors, who are experts in the qualitative research method. The interviews were conducted in Sepedi and English, lasting 35–60 min. The sessions included short breaks to accommodate participants’ comfort. The participants were allowed to respond in their home language (Sepedi), audio recorded, and the transcripts were later translated into English. While conducting the study, the researcher used a book and a pen to write down things such as the behavioural responses of the participants which could not be recorded on audio. After each interview, participants were asked if they knew other caregivers who might be interested in participating. To maintain confidentiality, the participants were asked to share the contact details of the independent person with the prospective participants instead of giving the contacts of the caregivers to the researcher. This approach reduced recruitment bias while allowing snowball sampling.

Data analysis

The researchers used inductive thematic analysis to analyse the collected data, following the six steps of thematic analysis [31]. The first step was to familiarize oneself with the data. The lead researcher took the recorded data and wrote it in the transcript. All the participants’ views were translated into English in the transcript. The lead author (MI) took about 2 days to familiarise themselves with the data to ensure that the content is understood and shared it with the third author, who served as the co-coder. After familiarizing themselves with the data, two authors (MI and MB) moved to the step of generating the codes. The researchers used the in vivo coding method to independently generate the codes. After generating the codes, the two authors (lead author - MI and third author - MB) met to compare the codes, and when there was conflict, the second author (TS) was requested to assist in resolving the conflict. Generated codes were then used to generate the themes (step 3), with the lead author (MI) taking over the role. The themes were developed guided by the research purpose to ensure that they are specific and relevant in the study. The third author (MB) was requested to review the themes (step 4) and did not make any changes to the initially developed themes. Following the generation of themes, the researchers could not conduct a member checking process because the participants were not available. Although that is the case, credibility was improved by prolonging the engagement with the data. After reviewing the themes, the researchers described the theme (step 5) in the writing process of the paper (step 6).

Ethical consideration

In this study, informed consent, privacy, respect, anonymity, and withdrawal were all taken into account as ethical considerations. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Limpopo ethics committee. Written consent was also sought and obtained from participants before data collection.

Results

The study focused on exploring the experiences of home-based caregivers of patients with chronic mental illness. The results were categorised under the following six main themes: the role of home-based caregivers caring for patients with chronic mental illness, lack of knowledge and training, physical and psychological exhaustion, support systems for home-based caregivers, the psychological well-being of home-based caregivers, and coping strategies of home-based caregivers (Tables 2, 3).

Main themes identified

Theme 1: the role of home-based caregivers caring for patients with chronic mental illness

Several participants (n = 4) described the role of caregiving as taking care of people who are unable to care for themselves because of chronic illnesses, providing support and care in their homes, and referring them to appropriate service providers. However, while some participants were clear about their roles, not all participants understood the expectations bestowed on them. Some seemed to work because it was demanded of them; they said no guidelines or policies were guiding the different roles they play as caregivers.

“When you take a mentally ill person, the first thing that you notice is the physical appearance, the dirty clothes, and the smell. So you have to make sure that you bathe them, give them clothes to wear, feed them, and make sure they have a proper place to sleep” participant 1.35. Male.

In support, another participant echoed that:

“We work because we are required to, and often we have no idea what we are doing. There are no protocols or policies to help us understand what our roles are. This job is incredibly hectic.”’ Participant 8. 47 Female.

Another participant shared the nature of the service that she provides as a caregiver in the following manner:

“It’s my duty to make sure doctor’s appointments are not missed for medication collection, so I make it a point to go along with” participant7. 42 Female.

Another shared that:

“I help him with daily needs such as ensuring he eats, and he looks presentable always, and I ensure he takes medication.” Participant 6. 38 Female.

Theme 2: lack of training and knowledge

Participants highlighted how performing their daily jobs without proper training poses a challenge for them to work effectively. They argued that they were not equipped with the necessary skills or enough training to be able to provide care for mental illnesses in a homely environment. They mentioned being faced with the frustration and feelings of inadequacy when they are not able to respond to queries from the patients, especially since they are portrayed as the immediate experts on caregiving. Participants also shared how patients sometimes can be physically aggressive towards them (physical aggression or destruction of property), how uneasy and frightening the experience can be. They also mentioned that it is often difficult to stabilise them after an episode of aggression because of a lack of proper training in handling and regulations around restraining such patients. They posited that the caregiving role is a challenge sometimes, as it comes with an emotional burden. Some participants shared how the stigmatisation of staying with a person with a mental illness and the negative perceptions regarding mental illness in the community have affected them personally and socially.

“It would be wonderful to be given training and more information so that I may know what to do when we visit the patients and how to respond to their queries. When a patient has a question, it is upsetting when you are unsure of how to respond because they believe we are experts, since we are their regular caregivers. We need training,” Participant 5. 45 Male.

Another caregiver echoed:

“It’s not an easy thing to do, I must be honest with you. They beat me, they literally beat me, they kick me, and they break windows. There are some things that they do that you cannot share with people, and you can’t even chain them because it’s not within the laws of government.” Participant 1. 35 Male.

On the other hand, another participant shared:

“My social life is affected as I’m always viewed as the person who stays with a mad person.” Participants6. 38 Female.

Another shared:

“I mostly suffer from fatigue because I work long hours sometimes, the daily chores of cooking and cleaning, together with caring for a sick person, can be extremely exhausting,” participant 7. 42 Female.

Theme 3: physical and psychological exhaustion

Caregivers complained about extreme fatigue and body aches sometimes. They shared how the caregiving role can be exhausting both physically and emotionally, especially for the much older caregivers. They mentioned how very challenging it is physically and emotionally to deal with sick patients who do not always listen and cooperate. They mentioned that some patients do not make their job easy, and they are sometimes physically aggressive, uncooperative, and verbally abusive. They also shared how some patients make their caregiving role extremely difficult by refusing to take medication, eat, or follow simple instructions. Some of the participants’ narratives are below.

“Sometimes when you try to calm them down and assist, they get physically aggressive. It’s physically tiring,” Participant 4. 50 Female.

In support, another participant shared:

“I once found him on the street dirty and looking disoriented, I tried walk back with him, so that I can assist him with bathing and eating, but he wanted to physically attack me and started swearing at me. This job is very challenging” Participant 2. 40 Female.

Theme 4: support systems available for home-based caregivers

Participants argued that they do not have sufficient support systems from the government and family members, and that there is no adequate professional health system. Others argued that they do receive support from family members. They indicated how family members are absent physically and emotionally from patients’ lives and shared how this is also a contributing factor in making the caregiving role very challenging. One participant shared how he does not need any support from family members. He always rejects their financial assistance because he voluntarily took this role as a church leader to help those who cannot help themselves.

“I volunteer to help and take in mentally challenged patients in my home. So, I don’t receive anything from the family members. At some point one family wanted to buy food and give me the SASSA grant money, but I stopped them because I volunteer to assist the people and I don’t want people’s money, it has got nothing to do with whether the family is getting SASSA grant at home for that particular individual and this particular individual I’m talking about has been living in the street for over 20 years and now the family thinks we are helping him because we want his SASSA grant money but the truth is we don’t. I use my own money, my own personal budget to maintain these guys.” Participant 1. 35Male.

Others said:

“There’s no support system at all, it’s actually very sad the things we see in other families, because of the SASSA grant money the patient receives. Members of the family will actually completely forget about their sick family members and their needs, just to satisfy their own greed. People are greedy and only care about the SASSA grant. What happens to the sick individual, and how they survive, is actually none of their concern? In most cases, you find that the patient can’t even speak for themselves, and who are you to say anything on their behalf? Next thing, you’ll be accused of the only reason you are assisting this person is because you are also after the SASSA grant, while that’s not true. Some of us actually have a good heart and empathy for others.” Participant 6.38 Female.

Another supported and shared that:

“There’s not much support. One thing I haveXXX generalized in my years of caregiving is that most families are afraid of the caregiving role. They are afraid to take on any family member that’s not well and can’t look after themselves because they are afraid of the burden that comes with being a home-based caregiver and I don’t blame them, because it’s a full-time challenging job that requires most of your time and energy” Participant 7 42. Female.

Theme 5: the psychological well-being of home-based caregivers

Participants provided feedback that certainly demonstrated there were negative psychological consequences stemming from the work that they do with patients. Five participants out of 8 pointed out the need for psychological intervention for caregivers, and pointed out that they are feeling psychologically exhausted, frustrated, and have feelings of giving up as a result of this role. However, some participants did not express any need for psychological interventions.

Caregivers (N = 3) opined that even though their work is emotionally draining and psychologically challenging, they have become accustomed to learning different coping mechanisms for coping with stress. Some of the participants argued that they struggle hard, as patients do not make work easier for them, something that leaves them helpless and powerless at times. They agreed that group support between caregivers helps them cope as a safe environment for expressing mutual challenges. Others reported resorting to prayer as a source of strength, while others approached family and friends for help. This highlights the dual reality: though many caregivers experience severe psychological distress and recognize the need for formal psychological services, they also employ informal coping strategies to manage the emotional load of caregiving.

“The emotional experience that I once experienced was after l had tried to help the patient by bathing him, feeding him, and hours later he died. I was emotionally disturbed, even though death is part of us and it’s inevitable, but the truth is no one can truly prepare for it.” Participant 2. Female 40.

Another one shared that:

“There’s really not much you can do, especially when dealing with a difficult patient, after all, we are not professionally trained to deal with this kind of situation. We counsel ourselves in groups”. Participant 3.40 Female.

In support, one of the home-based caregivers shared that:

“I pray and tell myself all will be well.” Participant 2. 40 Female.

Another shared that:

“We do not see the medical professionals who are supposed to be encouraging us. We don’t often receive counselling. Given how difficult the work we do is, debriefing meetings where we can discuss our issues would be tremendously helpful.” Participant 8. Female 47.

Another caregiver also echoed that:

“No, I don’t really need psychological help, I am normal. I don’t have psychological issues. Yes, everybody needs a psychological assistant at some point, even psychologists are not normal, we all need help at some point, but not concerning this for me.” Participant 1. 35 Male.

Despite the emergence of similar theses across the different participants, the differences in experiences were also noted. These variations were included within each main theme to show diversity of experiences that were shared.

Discussion

The findings provided insight into the caregiving experiences of home-based caregivers of people living with mental illness. The important theme was that the home-based caregivers experienced challenges with caregiving and changed perspectives regarding caregiving. Within the theme of caregiver responsibility, the home-based caregivers felt overwhelmed and frustrated. They did not know how to seek help. This finding is supported by previous research, which underscored that caregiver responsibility may lead to a physical and emotional burden for the caregivers [32, 33].

The study found that home-based caring has grown in importance as a component of the community healthcare system as South Africa continues to struggle with chronic non-communicable diseases, which are exacerbated by a lack of resources. The provision of home-based care is not without difficulties, though. Some of the challenges revealed in the study included a lack of training, dealing with difficult patients, and the issue of their services not being recognised by community members and health professionals in the clinic. A few participants shared how the issue of stigmatisation affects them and how society views them as “people who live with a mad individual”. Participants shared how this negatively impacts them. Based on these findings, it is evident that participants demonstrated emotion-focused coping in the appraisal of challenges as suggested by Lazarus and Folkman [34]. The participants have made suggestions that reveal that they have tried to gain control over their emotions by altering the meaning of a stressful situation. The findings also support Park and Seo [35] that family caregivers of chronic mental illness suffer a lot of stigma as a result of a mentally ill family member. Also, the findings revealed that the issue of insufficient funding and poor remuneration poses a challenge to their work and demotivates them.

Participants revealed that the psychological and physical exhaustion mostly stems from aggressive and difficult patients. The study also revealed that being a home-based caregiver for a mentally ill patient can render an individual at high risk as a result of patients who are physically aggressive towards them. According to Hanzawa, Bae [36], violence and aggressive behaviour are common problems among chronically mentally ill patients. He further revealed that these patients are not only physically aggressive but can also be verbally aggressive and self-aggressive (Hanzawa, 2013). Participants also revealed that this job is not easy and has a lot of emotional and psychological effects as a result of the burden that comes with the role. This supports Ae-Ngibise, Doku [18] and Mthembu, Brown [37]’s contention that home-based caregivers experience psychological problems that are a result of the burden of caring for people with chronic conditions. The findings of this study are consistent with previous studies. For example, a study conducted by Mthembu, Brown [37] in the Western Cape discovered that home-based caregivers were unable to deal with the emotional challenges and stress that came with caring for older persons living with chronic illnesses. Similar studies were conducted by Shamsaei, Cheraghi [15], who discovered that the effects of caregiving reveal that one-third to one-half of HBC suffer an extreme amount of psychological distress.

Coping with the role, participants argued that they mostly need to cope with the burden that comes with caregiving, which includes dealing with difficult patients and the issue of not being well-trained for the job. What stood out the most was that some participants shared how they mostly rely on prayer to cope when the job gets too overwhelming for them. They also have their support groups where they share their experiences, and this somehow helps them cope as they motivate each other. This is supported by a study by Moahi [38] where caregiver participants shared how they rely on God for coping with their role. However, this did not apply to all participants. This is supported by Thapa, Sung [39], indicating that various cultural practices, such as coping mechanisms, familial bonds, and religious convictions, affect people’s readiness to seek assistance and capacity to respond to mental health services. The researcher noted that participants did not share any specific coping methods. They just indicated that there is not much that they can do to deal with challenges because this is the only way they can make a little income for themselves and be able to feed their families. A female participant shared that creating a comfortable relationship with the patient makes them feel secure and comfortable, and it seemed to work for them when coping with difficult patients.

This is supported by the theory of stress and coping Folkman and Lazarus [40], which states that coping is determined in terms of personality styles or defence mechanisms derived from ego psychology. How a person perceives a difficult situation will determine how they will decide to deal with it. Folkman and Lazarus [40] identified two main categories of coping strategies: (1) emotion-focused coping, which refers to the regulation of emotions generated by the appraisal process, such as anger or sadness in response to the appraisal of loss, anxiety or fear in the case of the appraisal of threat, and eagerness and excitement, mixed with some worry, in the case of the appraisal of challenge. Based on the two coping mechanisms noted by Lazarus and Folkman, it is evident that participants relied on both problem-focused coping mechanisms and emotion-focused coping mechanisms. Participants’ resort to prayers is an emotional response to a stressful situation that helps them cope. Furthermore, participants building a safe and secure relationship with the patient to help ease the tension and manage the challenge is regarded as a problem-focused mechanism by attempting to modify the circumstances as suggested by Folkman and Lazarus [40]. The theory’s relevance was proven in this study because it was revealed that a person’s daily environment can determine their psychological well-being, and cognitive appraisal plays a huge role in how an individual decides to cope with complex stressful situations.

Existing studies found that taking care of others may put a strain on the caregiver’s mental and physical health [32, 33]. Participants revealed that there are consequential psychological effects stemming from the work that they do as caregivers. Five participants out of 8 pointed out the need for psychological intervention, stating that they were psychologically exhausted, frustrated, and had feelings of giving up. These findings support Lazarus and Folkman’s theory, which stipulates that cognitive appraisal plays a huge role in how an individual chooses to cope with emotionally threatening situations [40]. Caregivers experiencing feelings of giving up suggest that at times they are unable to adopt the two coping mechanisms suggested by the theory, and as a result, they experience feelings of frustration. Findings revealed that most participants need psychological intervention to express their feelings and receive therapy for their frustrations because of this role. They felt that they needed to receive counselling because of traumatic experiences they encountered previously, such as the death of a patient in their care. However, some participants did not express any need for psychological interventions and felt that they were coping well.

Recommendations

Despite the shortcomings mentioned above, this study has advanced our knowledge of South African caregivers for patients with chronic mental illness. The study also deepens our comprehension of the requirements and difficulties faced by carers who provide care at home. It looked into the experiences of home-based caring and the psychological effects of their labour. It also looked at coping mechanisms used by home-based caregivers in the workplace. As a result, the study helps identify optimal practices for home-based caregivers and elements that have a detrimental effect on home-based care service delivery. Many difficulties were revealed by the home-based caregivers’ experiences, and suggestions were provided. There is a request for policymakers to address the needs articulated through regulation to control the training of HBCs that will guarantee their distinct roles and job descriptions. It is suggested that the state take action by increasing the payment of HBCGs who deliver a crucial service to the healthcare system. The report also suggests that to facilitate support for HBCs, the Department of Health, NGOs, and community healthcare services work together. To maintain the HBCGs, policies and support systems must be improved or enhanced to deliver comprehensive and integrated care.

Limitations of the study

Firstly, the Sepedi to English translation of the interview data may have resulted in the omission or incorrect replacement of the rich data originally provided by participants. Secondly, the study was limited to Ga-Dikgale in Limpopo Province; therefore, findings reveal only significant and limited experiences of home-based caregivers in this area. However, based on literature and observation, HBC’s actual experiences are comparable in other provinces and regions. Thirdly, this study relied only on home-based caregivers’ personal experiences, and other people, Patients, for instance, were not interviewed. As a result, the current study’s interpretation of the phenomenon was biased. Lastly, as the objective was to understand home-based caregivers’ experiences in depth, the number of participants was small, thereby affecting the generalizability of the study.

Conclusion

The study has established that home-based caregivers caring for patients with chronic mental illness face various challenges. Their work is draining, and they are also susceptible to suffering from mental health issues if their challenges are not taken into cognizance. However, instead of pitying themselves, the home-based caregivers had solutions to their challenges and believed that those challenges required multiple stakeholders joining hands to work towards assisting them. These answers include regular, encouraging supervision, mentoring, efficient coordination amongst healthcare workers, and community participation in patient and family care. Any home-based palliative care service should include a well-designed caregiver program to train caregivers because the tasks and responsibilities assumed by HBCGs are not recognized and are undervalued in South Africa. To manage the stress of caring for patients in households, HBCGs will benefit from psychological support.
