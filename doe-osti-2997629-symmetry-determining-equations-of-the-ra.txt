1 Introduction
The “constant velocity piston” problem [1] (Fig. 1), also known as the “piston problem,” is a
standard model for a one dimensional, in our case linear, symmetric shock wave moving through an
inviscid, perfect gas. The model can be divided into two regions - a perturbed section on the left
and an unperturbed section on the right - by a moving shock wave moving left to right. Both the
perturbed and unperturbed sections, i.e. the shocked and unshocked regions, respectively, obey the
Eulerian conservation equations; however, at the exact location of the shock, there is a mathematical
discontinuity not satisfied by the Euler equations. To ensure continuity and conservation of certain
quantities when crossing between the unshocked and shocked regions, we evoke a series of equations
derived from the Eulerian conservation equations, called the Rankine-Hugoniot equations, or “jump”
equations as it is often referred to in the literature on the topic [2].
 The classical constant-velocity piston problem assumes the piston features a constant driving
velocity (among many other willing suspensions of belief required in the pursuit of a first principles
equation model); consequent to this assumption is a constant-velocity shock and a constant-velocity
shocked flow state. However, using Lie Group Theory (LGT), also known as symmetry analysis, we
can attempt to reinterpret the model with a shock wave of variable velocity in time and space. An
extension of the model in this way opens up the possibility for obtaining new analytical solutions
to the piston problem for certain shock velocity models. In this report, we use LGT to derive the
symmetry determining equations (SDEs), whose solutions are Lie groups, which permit analytical
solutions. In the future, we can then use the SDEs to define constraint equations on the shock
velocity model and what the successive solutions to the Euler equations might be based off such
constraints.
 This report is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a brief derivation of the Rankine-Hugoniot
(“jump”) equations; Section 3 gives an overview of Lie group theory; Section 4 derives the SDEs
of the jump equations; Section 5 derives the Euler conservation equations for fluids; and Section 6
presents concluding remarks and opportunities for future studies.

2 Deriving the Jump Equations
The instantaneous moment in crossing the shock wave between the unperturbed and perturbed
regions requires the Rankine-Hugoniot jump equations to enforce conservation of mass, momentum,
and energy. The jump equations address the discontinuity created by the shock wave in the system
which are not reliably modeled by the Euler equations [3]. These equations are equivalent derivations
of the Euler equations, albeit for a near-instantaneous moment in time, and therefore closely resemble
the Euler equations from which they are derived. We have included ṙs to denote the shock wave
velocity, given as a derivative of the shock location with respect to time.
 Considering Fig. 1, the initial condition of the gas is given by an initial pressure P1 , initial
particle velocity u1 , and initial density ρ1 , and an initial energy e1 . Once the shock wave passes
through the material, it has a shocked-state set of variables for pressure, particle velocity, density,
and energy given by P2 , u2 , ρ2 , and e2 , respectively. The location of the shock wave is given by a
spatial coordinate r and therefore the shock velocity can be written the dr/ dt = ṙs , as mentioned
above. The jump equations and their respective conservation laws are defined as follows:

3 Lie Group Theory - A Primer
It may be possible to understand the behavior of certain functions if we first understand their
symmetries. Think of it as mathematical origami - if we can see the folds in the paper, we might
better understand how the shape itself is formed. This idea of “mathematical origami” has its
roots in a Norwegian mathematician during the late-19th century: Sophus Lie. Lie discovered
that continuous transformation groups could be linearized into corresponding vector fields called
infinitesimal generators. These infinitesimal generators (IG) can serve as a sort of compass used to
develop these vector fields and thus shed some light on the mysteries of certain functions. The IG
is defined by a Lie group, which is a solution to the aforementioned SDEs. Therefore, if we can
derive the SDEs and then solve them, we have a map to a solution. In this section, we provide an
algorithm for doing so.

the jump equations will do likewise. That being said, a deeper look into the Euler equations would
behoove us.

6 Palante: Conclusion and Opportunities for Future Studies
Unfortunately, here is where the report ends, at least for now. As mentioned, further analysis can be
done to derive solutions for the jump equations, possibly using the pattern provided by the solutions
to the Euler equations. Possible strategies could include deforming these solutions or approaching
the solutions to the jump equations using a gradual approximation approach. It appears highly likely
that said solutions do exist and are just waiting to be uncovered. Further study may be warranted
in the works of Sedov-Taylor shock waves or Zeldovich-Raizer problems as well, which may offer
additional insight into uncovering solutions for the Rankine-Hugoniot discontinuity.
 Further study may also include applying this approach when unraveling the corollary viscid
equation, the Navier-Stokes equation. Interestingly, the shock waves predicted by the Navier-Stokes
equations are continuous with respect to their adjoining flow fields. In this case, the viscosity and
heat conduction mechanisms included in Navier-Stokes induce the finite-width albeit narrow shocks.
Given the Euler equations are the zero-viscosity, zero-conduction limit of Navier-Stokes, one can see
that as those processes assume zero limits the predicted shock waves also assume their discontinuous
Euler forms. One would expect that similarly, a Lie group solution would be interrelated as seen in
the solutions of the Euler equations.
