Executive Summary
The objective of this project was to develop a novel, low-cost process intensified modular process
to directly convert flare gas or stranded gas to carbon nanomaterials and co-product hydrogen
(H2) with high conversion, selectivity, and stability. The proposed project was based on our
exciting exploratory research on a patent pending technology for one-step conversion of natural
gas to carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and carbon fibers (CNFs) without emitting CO2: CH4  H2 + C
(CNT, Carbon Fibers)
The proposed technology was based on microwave-enhanced, multifunctional catalytic system
to directly convert the light components of stranded natural gas. Specifically, the approach
integrated microwave reaction chemistry into the modular reactor design with the goal to achieve
energy and capital efficiency comparable or better than large commercial unit operation. The
major focus was on the application of process intensification at modular component scales with
the objective of deployment at flare gas location. Particularly, the project has demonstrated the
modular component having a large turndown ratio which can operate under varying feed rate and
composition.
Commercial catalysts for methane decomposition are not optimized for microwave catalytic
reactors. This is largely because the electromagnetic energy delivered to the catalyst sites (metals)
are different from conventional heat transfer mechanisms. The catalytic materials themselves have
dielectric properties that allow microwave adsorption, especially at higher temperatures. In
addition to dielectric heating, the catalyst can be designed to interact with the microwave through
other mechanisms. For example, metal dopants, like iron, can be added to catalytic sites for
magnetic coupling. These ferromagnetic species can couple with the magnetic component of the
microwave field, putting additional energy into the reaction. An additional manner of putting
energy into this reaction to improve conversion and selectivity is through coupling of the
microwave field with polar intermediate species on the surface of the catalyst. These species are
known to be strong microwave absorbers.
Essentially, microwave energy can be selectively delivered to the interface between active sites
and reaction intermediates without losing energy to the surrounding environment, therefore,
significantly improving energy efficiency.
The Recipient has evaluated the effectiveness of catalyst-CNTs/CNFs separation using acid
treatment and catalyst regeneration. Metal nitrates formed upon acid treatment were dried and
calcined thus reforming the active catalyst precursor, either directly used to make unsupported
bimetallic or introduced onto CNTs support to make supported catalyst. At least five cycles have
been achieved for the pyrolysis-separation processing. The catalyst was remade using the salts
obtained from acid treatment (e.g., NiNO3) and then dried/calcined thus re-preparing the catalyst
for subsequent methane pyrolysis. The effectiveness of the acid concentrations for remaking the
catalyst was investigated and a determination was made for how much catalyst make-up is required.
Characterizations of the spent catalyst materials have been performed including TEM, Raman IR,

 5

XRD, and TPO to understand changes of the catalyst upon cycling, as well as understand the
properties of the produced carbon product.
The Recipient has studied the effect of the promoters on the surface morphology of bimetallic
catalysts such as Ni-Pd and Fe-Co based catalysts by using advanced microscopy (e.g. HRTEM,
SEM). Alkali and alkaline earth metal compounds served as textural or electronic promoters for
catalysts in various catalytic processes. This has caused a difference in surface morphology of the
catalyst with different promoters. The Recipient has determined the particle structure and metal
dispersion on the catalysts using microscopy analysis. The internal structure and porous texture of
the fresh catalysts and spent catalysts, and the carbonaceous products on the catalysts have been
examined by TEM. Further research has been performed to confirm multi-walled carbon nanotubes
(MWCNT) were generated. To learn more about the structure and electron promotion effects of
the promoters, the Recipient used XPS to measure the surface composition, oxidation states, and
carbonaceous species present under the reaction atmosphere. The XPS studies revealed the forms
of carbonaceous products. In addition, the Recipient used special instrumentation such as BET,
TPD, and TPR to characterize these catalysts to understand the origin of the promotion effects.
The Recipient evaluated carbon fiber formation via methane pyrolysis using in situ Environmental
Transmission Electron Microscopy (ETEM). The Recipient compared growth over a bimetallic
NiPd catalyst with a monometallic Ni catalyst counterpart. Both unsupported and CNT-supported
catalysts be evaluated. For these four catalysts, the Recipient took pictures and/or videos of these
four catalysts as a function of time.
The Recipient carried out kinetic studies at a microwave frequency of 2.45 GHz and 5.86 GHz-
6.65 GHz in the dedicated microwave systems. The reactors system was set up so that gas could
flow over a catalyst that was positioned in the center of the microwave applicator. The exiting gas
was measured by a totalizing mass flow meter. At all times, both the surface temperature and the
internal temperature of the catalyst were monitored with a pyrometer focused on the surface
through a germanium window and in an internal fiber optic thermometer. The composition of the
gas and amount of product that formed over time was determined using an in-line gas
chromatography, and all of the products formed were characterized as they leave the system by an
in-line mass spectrometry. CNTs and CNFs remained in the reactor and characterized after each
test.
The Recipient has established a detailed rate law for the methane pyrolysis reaction in the
microwave reactor. The Recipient has determined which primary rate constants are most affected
by the microwave, and this aided in selecting which types of catalyst surfaces exhibited efficient
interaction with methane. In these studies, the Recipient first established the power threshold at
which products begin to appear. This threshold depended on the course of microwave power and
hence on the temperature of the carbon surface. This allowed the Recipient to optimize reaction
for the lowest power. Once the threshold was established, the Recipient systematically changed
the temperature of the catalyst by changing the applied power and carrying out kinetic analysis of
products generated over a range of fixed temperatures.

 6

During the project, microwave plasma catalytic reaction was carried out using proven microwave
generator units which operate at a relatively small wavelength (12.8 inches for 915 MHz), enabling
a high energy density within a small footprint. The Recipient has designed, procured and fabricated
a fluidized bed microwave plasma reactor without dielectric containment, capable of sustaining
direct contact between microwaves/plasma and the catalyst particles. A method of catalyst delivery
and removal was designed to allow batch-style removal and replenishing of spent catalyst load
without halting the process. The Recipient integrated the fluidized bed reactor with its modular
engineering scale microwave system equipped with 6 mass flow controllers, a 6 kW microwave
generator, optical spectrometry hardware, high speed camera, and other diagnostic equipment.
Shakedown and parameterization tests utilized a cost-effective catalyst stand-in (e.g. accordingly
sized activated carbon) were performed to evaluate particle fluidization regimes and catalyst
interactions with microwaves and the plasma environment, aided by optical diagnostics. The
reactor design was iterated as needed, with reliance on computer-aided design and simulation and
modeling tools. The Recipient conducted a parameterization study to characterize reactor
performance and catalyst particle interaction with plasma and effect on process conditions.
Endurance testing at optimal conditions was performed. Collected spectral data were recorded and
analyzed online and post-factum for in-situ gas and electron temperature measurements. Spent
catalyst/nanotube samples were characterized.
The Recipient developed process performance models using commercial flow-sheeting software
(e.g., Aspen Plus) to supply the heat and material balances needed to estimate production costs.
The economic analysis was derived from iterative interactions between modelers and the
experimental researchers. A reactor system design was developed by comparing fixed-bed,
moving-bed, and Fluid Catalytic Cracking (FCC) reactor. The system was also comparatively
assessed against benchmark modular steam methane reforming technology. The performance and
cost of the projected system were assessed early on to assess economic viability and to inform the
research. A comprehensive TEA sensitivity study has been performed.
Potential markets and corresponding tech-to-market strategy were developed by the Recipient
through a collaborative effort. The Recipient has identified potential markets for the produced
carbon product and provided guidance on development activities and providing industrial
outreach.

Background and Technical Approach
There is a high demand in the market to develop a novel, low-cost process intensified modular
process to directly convert flare gas or stranded gas to carbon nanomaterials and co-product
hydrogen (H2) with high conversion, selectivity, and stability. The proposed technology is based
on our exciting exploratory research on a patent pending technology for one-step conversion of
natural gas to carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and carbon fibers (CNFs) without emitting CO2:
CH4  H2 + C (CNT, Carbon Fibers)
Over 200 billion cft/year of natural gas at remote production sites is rejected via flaring in the
U.S [1] due primarily to the limitations in pipeline transportation capacities or fluctuation in the
well production rate. This is a significant waste of valuable resources and unrealized profit.
Process intensified modular unit appears to be promising in converting flared gas to value-added
solid carbon and hydrogen. The modular equipment can be deployed and transported between
remote locations. However, optimizing processes to produce both hydrogen and valuable carbon
co-products has been a challenge to shale gas industry.
The major challenges in the methane thermal catalytic decomposition process for solid crystalline
carbon and H2 production are the development of highly efficient catalysts and the regeneration
of spent catalyst, which is also critical in the overall economics of the process. Catalyst efficiency
includes not only its specific activity but also its resistance to coking given the large amount of
carbon accumulation at a practical conversion, which is closely related to its lifetime. Optimum
operating conditions need to be realized. Although, there are different methodologies with respect
to the exact atomic scale physicochemical processes underlying catalytic activation and
nucleation of CNTs/CNFs, the process generally proceeds by the arrangement of carbon atoms
on the surface of the catalyst nanoparticles, leading to CNTs/CNFs formation with either tip or
base growth. A major challenge is to identify which phase is the catalytically active phase and
which pathways lead to the most active carbon crystallization.
The challenge in direct conversion of methane to hydrogen without using oxidants (O2 and H2O)
is energy efficiency, whereas new energy-efficient methods for hydrogen production and
extraction must be realized on both centralized and distributed geographic scales [2]. From an
economic standpoint of view, the challenge of direct methane conversion to hydrogen is to
produce high value byproducts that can offset the capital and feedstock costs.
This project has identified some of the key aspects of utilizing microwave energy to provide a
very high degree of molecular dissociation in order to isolate hydrogen from methane molecule.
The most challenging hurdles are engineering scalability from laboratory fixed bed reactors to
pilot fluidized bed reactor where catalyst stability and product quality need to be maintained.
Typically, dielectric containment is necessary to contain microwave plasmas and to prevent it
from arc-discharging against the metal waveguide structures or propagating along the waveguide
toward the microwave energy source. When applied to carbonaceous gases such as methane,
plasma deposits carbon on the exposed dielectric surfaces, which then acts as a microwave

 8

absorber and alters or disrupts thermodynamics or kinetics of the process and reduces structural
integrity of the containment vessel. Reducing concentration of carbon in the feed stream by
dilution with inert gases or reduction of pressure can avoid carbon contamination, but drastically
reduces throughputs, precluding scale-up and economic viability of the process.
Additionally, separating solid carbon can be difficult, and most processes have high energy
requirements (e.g., either high temperature operation or large electrical voltage and/or current)
[3-9]. This work addressed the development of technology that can simultaneously generate high
volumes of both hydrogen and a valuable, crystalline carbon product that is easily separated from
the process stream.
Our transformative approach relies on the use of microwaves to enable conversion of methane
into hydrogen and value-added forms of carbon. As illustrated in Figure 1, a microwave catalytic
reactor system has been designed and built for methane decomposition reaction. Microwave
irradiation decouples the methane activation step from the conventional catalytic surface reaction:
CH₄ CHx* + (4-X)H+ + (4-x)e⁻
This has reduced the energy required to break C-H bond. In conventional way of methane
decomposition reaction, overheating metal sites cannot be avoided. Metal sintering becomes a
major issue preventing catalytic decomposition of methane from commercialization. In contrast,
due to the decoupling of the methane activation step from catalyst surface reaction, the
microwave activated species react onto a catalyst surface to form CNT and hydrogen under lower
severity.

 Figure 1. Microwave induced methane decomposition to CNTs and H2

The reactor is a variable frequency microwave reactor system. One of the mechanisms by which
the catalyst and reacting species can interact with the microwave field and provide energy to the
reaction is through relaxation processes. These relaxations include dipolar or Debye processes,
which involve the coupling of the radiation with dipoles in the solid catalyst. As shown in Figure
1, these dipoles can be defecting sites (i.e. atomic vacancies) in the catalysts or dangling bonds
on the surface of the catalyst. From the standpoint of catalysis, dipoles on the surface can be
reactant or products that would be susceptible to selective bond activation effects, which in turn
can affect reaction rates.

 9

The work plan was broken down into eight interconnected tasks, each with its own specific
objective that allowed the overall objective of achieving efficient conversion of natural gas to
carbon nanomaterials. The initial effort was focused on catalyst development and optimization.
The project developed two different electromagnetic sensitive catalyst systems, bimetallic
supported (using a fraction of products CNTs/CNFs) and unsupported. In parallel, detailed kinetic
studies were carried out using two dedicated microwave systems, one operating at 2.45 GHz and
another operating with variable frequency of 5.85-6.65 GHz. From these kinetic studies, a detailed
reaction pathway for methane pyrolysis was able to be developed. The goal was to optimize the
frequency of the microwave radiation to produce the highest yield with the lowest expenditure of
energy. The reaction was carried out at the frequencies associated with the loss tangent maxima
using a variable frequency microwave system.
The catalysts and the properties of the produced CNTs/CNFs were characterized using a number
of in-situ and ex-situ analytical instrumentations, and the results were correlated with microwave
reactor performance. The objectives are to optimize catalysts formulation and process conditions.

After catalyst and process optimization, a pilot test was conducted in 6 kW microwave reactor.
The pilot unit has been used for a demonstration in many industrial processes. Energy and mass
balance were measured to demonstrate process intensification in terms of improvement in energy
efficiency and reduction in capital expenses (CAPEX) and operating expenses (OPEX). An
engineering scale-up feasibility analysis and a technoeconomic analysis (TEA) were conducted to
elucidate commercial viability.

Task 2. Catalyst Design, Synthesis and Characterization

2.1. Ni-M-CNT Catalysts
Ni-Pd bimetallic catalyst was prepared by incipient wetness impregnation using nickel and
palladium nitrites salts. Carbon nanotube was used as substrate. Upon impregnation, the process
was modified by adding an extra step which was being heated to 110 °C in autoclave. This step
can improve the impregnation of the nickel and palladium onto carbon nanotube substrate. The
dried catalyst precursor was reduced in 10% hydrogen flow balanced with nitrogen. The reduction
was conducted at 400 °C and lasts for 4 hours. Nickel (Ni) and Palladium (Pd) bimetallic catalyst
supported by carbon nanotube was used for the carbon nanotube and COx-free hydrogen from the
following pyrolysis of methane.
We have focused on the development of catalyst for the proposed reaction under microwave
irradiation. We have developed two types of catalysts. The first one is Ni-Pd bimetallic catalyst.
This specific catalyst has been mentioned in the project report to be very promising for pyrolysis
of methane using convective heating to producing carbon nanomaterials. The microwave heating
characteristics of this very catalyst were vigorously investigated under current work schedule. The
other one was Ni atomic layer deposition catalyst, which was recently discovered to be a promising
catalyst for the proposed reaction as well. The new nickel catalyst was prepared by atomic layer
deposition which could pave a single layer of nickel atom on the carbon nanotube. We have
preliminarily tested it, to date.
Initially, the catalyst used in this project was 10Ni-1Pd@CNT. The mass ratio of the metallic part
is 11% which included 10% total mass of Ni, 1% total mass of Pd. The remaining 89% of the total
mass was substrate CNT. The scheme of the catalyst design has been displayed in Figure 2. The
fundamental idea of this series of catalysts is to disperse the metals on CNT, which later serves as
active cites during the reaction. Upon reaction, the final products can be acidified to retrieve the
metallic materials while leaving the carbon nanotube intact due to the inertness of the carbon
nanotube in acid. The acidified metal elements again become salts which can further be adopted
as precursor for catalyst preparation.

 Figure 2-1-1. Heating curves of carbon nanotube support and our catalyst.

The heating curves have been obtained by logging the time profile of the temperature, which is
displayed in Figure 2-1-1. 10Ni-1Pd@CNT has been compared with pure CNT support, showing
the same heating time (30 minutes) required for the temperature being ramped from 250 to 550 °C
with microwave generator. Note that the catalyst bed temperature IR detector starts from 250 °C.
The goal of this test is to figure out that if adding metal compositions to the substrate affects the
heating curve of the support. The result shows that adding 11% of metal to the support does not
have a noticeable change to the heating curve of the support. It is also observed that the heating
curve under microwave is relatively stable. The programming of the microwave controls the
temperature well without major fluctuation. The attenuation property of this very catalyst is
considered well to the microwave wavelength under this specific frequency (5.85GHz).

 Figure 2-1-2. Methane conversion by time on stream at different reaction temperature using
 catalyst 10Ni-1Pd@CNT.
Upon confirming that our catalyst is very responsive to the microwave heating effect. The further
work has been accomplished with different heating temperatures. The temperatures of the catalyst
bed have been ramped to 400, 450, 500, 550, 600 °C for the proposed reaction. The methane
conversion and stable performance resulted from this catalyst have been taken into consideration
when it comes to optimizing the reaction temperature (shown in Figure 2-1-2). The reaction being
conducted at 550 °C is the best condition after careful selection. It provides an initial conversion
at around 65%, which later decreases to a conversion at 35% to 40% as the reaction time reaches
up to 5 hours, given flow rates all at 30 standard cubic centimeter per minute (sccm) and 1 standard
atmosphere pressure. The methane conversions at 400 and 450 oC are too low, while they are
relatively stable. The temperature at 500 and 600 °C does have a high conversion but failed to
deliver a stable performance.

 Figure 2-1-3. The reproductive performance of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT for methane conversion
To further confirm the reproductivity of our catalyst performance at 550 °C, we have conducted
four individual tests of our 10Ni-1Pd@CNT catalyst. Conversion profiles of the four tests have
been displayed in Figure 2-1-3. Four conversion curves are highly consistent with each other,
indicating that the performance of our catalyst is stable and consistent given the optimized reaction
temperature.

 Figure 2-1-4. Effect of initial methane concentration on the conversion of methane
Upon confirming the optimal temperature for our proposed reaction using microwave irradiation,
we further conducted testing for the effect of different concentrations of methane in the feed
(shown in Figure 2-1-4). The feed gas consists of methane and nitrogen. The feed with 30%, 50%
and 100% methane has been tested with microwave heating at 550 °C. The average conversion

 17

from 0 to 75 minutes shows the highest value in the attempt with 30% methane feed. When it
comes to the conversion beyond 75 minutes, the conversion does not have a noticeable difference.
The effects of various operating conditions/parameters on the catalytic performances of 10Ni-
1Pd@CNT, such as temperature, methane partial pressure and feedstock flowrate, were
systematically studied for both microwave and convention heating conditions. Data of
conventional heating is considered as a reference. We discuss the characteristics of microwave
heating by comparing it with conventional heating.

Figure 2-1-5. Catalytic performances of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT under varied reaction temperatures via
(a) microwave heating and (b) conventional heating. (Reaction conditions: Total flow rate= 30
sccm; Gas composition=30%CH4/N2; Amount of catalyst = 0.2g; MW frequency = 5850 MHz)

Figure 2-1-5 demonstrates how the catalytic capability of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT is affected by
temperatures under microwave heating and conventional heating. In agreement with publicly
reported results, the catalytic behavior of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT under conditional heating increases
with the increased temperatures until a critical temperature is reached. In our case, the critical
temperature is between 600 oC and 650 oC (shown in Figure 2-1-5b) Similarly, the conversion of
methane over 10Ni-1Pd@CNT under microwave heating increases from 400 oC to 600 oC. It’s
worth noting that at as low as 400oC, 10Ni-1Pd@CNT still exhibits some certain conversion,
suggesting the advantageous interactions between 10Ni-1Pd@CNT and microwave. Further, the
temperature dependence of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT under microwave heating is much lower than that of
thermal heating. According to kinetics study, this indicates that methane decomposition over 10Ni-
1Pd@CNT at microwave heating exhibits much lower activation energy than that of conventional
heating. Despite the advantages of microwave, the data curve is not very stable for microwave
heating tests, which is possibly due to local temperature fluctuations (the existence of hotspot) or
related to the recently proposed non-thermal effects.

Figure 2-1-6 shows the comparison of the catalytic performances of 10Ni-1Pd@CNT under
microwave heating and thermal heating with the variations of methane content in feed stock. 10Ni-
1Pd@CNT with microwave heating demonstrates a similar trend with thermal heating in
catalyzing methane decomposition: conversion dropping from a very high initial value to a stable
but still efficient value. Although 10Ni-1Pd@CNT exhibits the same capability for 30% methane
for both heating modes, 10Ni-1Pd@CNT with microwave heating obviously outperforms that with
thermal heating in higher methane partial pressures. This is an exciting result in terms of economics
because the trade-off of increasing methane partial pressure which normally leads to lower
conversion seems not a big issue. One possible understanding is microwave’s non-thermal effect
on catalysis which hypothesizes that microwave could affect the electron’s activity and promote
some involved elementary steps, in addition to the thermal effect. Based on that, we might think
that microwaves decrease the barrier energies of reaction leading to a higher k value (reaction
constant) and thus a higher capability of catalyzing methane decomposition.

2.2. SrTiNiO Catalyst
Cr-based ABO3 perovskite materials have been synthesized and characterized as support for
microwave-assisted methane cracking. These materials are designed to work as stable support to
resist coarsening and structure change during operation in MW. They are also designed to provide
an appropriate level of electrical conductivity so that the support can provide local electron density
oscillation to assist the catalytic reaction. According to the literature and our previous experience,
Cr at B site of the perovskites provided the lattice structure with great stability toward reducing
atmosphere as in the methane MW reaction chamber, working as robust support for catalyst loaded
to them. These compounds are doped at A and/or B site to tune the electrical conductivity. Three
Cr-based perovskites were synthesized, YCrO3, Y0.8Ca0.2Cr0.8Co0.2O3(YCCC), and
Y0.8Ca0.2Cr0.9Ni0.1O3 (YCCN).

EDTA-citric Sol-Gel method was used to synthesize these materials, as illustrated in Figure 2-2-1
Standard nitrates (from Alfa Aesar or Acros Organics) in stoichiometric percentage together with
citric acid (Alfa Aesar) were dissolved into distilled water. EDTA powders (Fisher Scientific) along
with ammonia water (Alfa Aesar) was dissolved into the other set of distilled water. The above
solutions were blended followed by adjusting pH to 8 through ammonia water, then held at ~80oC
and stirred until gelation. The gel was heated to 400oC to decompose nitrates and organic residual.
Resultant powders were calcined at different temperatures for several cycles with or without
intermediate ground to crystallize.

STNO-based microwave active catalysts successfully yielded CNT formation within a period of
150-200 min. A degradation issue is still associated with the STNO catalysts. We believe the

 65

degradation can be mitigated by an appropriate morphology control of Ni nanoparticles. When
exsolving from the STNO lattice, Ni nanoparticles have a wide distribution in particle size, from
1 nm to 20 nm. The particle size could be controlled by partial substitution of La for Sr in the
STNO system. La at the A-site can compensate for the low valence of Ni in perovskite to get a
pure phase before exsolution. When A-site deficient composition is used, the Ni cation diffusion
rate could be improved considering the enlarged bottleneck threshold in the diffusion path. It has
been shown in reference that (LaSr)0.8Ni0.06Ti0.94O3 displayed even and temperature-dependent Ni
nanoparticle exsolution.
The potential concern regarding the La-containing STNO is twofold:

(1) It could be electrical conductivity, making it non-responsive to microwave
(2) Although we get pure phase and Ni nanoparticles out of it, these Ni nanoparticles might not be
efficient in forming CNT

But based on our previous experience, the conductivity of La-containing STNO highly depends on
the valance of Ti. Through A-site deficiency design and low-temperature reduction like 800 °C,
we could limit its conductivity to a 0.1 S/cm level, which stands a strong chance of heating up in
microwave. And whether CNT forms from these Ni (socketed, 10~20nm) was affected by the
morphology of the La-containing STNO. Fast gas transport through the catalyst and smaller STNO
particles (0.2-0.5 microns) could support fast CH4 chemisorption, therefore, helping fast base
growth of CNT.

2.5. La0.63Ca00.37Ni0.1Ti0.9O3 Catalysts
Previously, we tried to use perovskite materials La0.5Sr0.5Ti0.94Ni0.06O3 (LSTNO) for surface Ni
exsolution to obtain base growth CNT. But there were still many foreign phases even after several
rounds of intermediate treatment. Therefore, we developed a new A-site deficient recipe for
crystalline phase formation, La0.8Ce0.1Ti0.6Ni0.4O3 (LCTNO). In this formula, the content of Ni is
substantially increased to 40% at B site to facilitate the formation of Ni particles, and to reduce the
strong repelling effect of titanate. Note, for a Ti B site dominate formula, the maximum solubility
of Ni to the B site is only 6%. The stoichiometric amount of A site is calculated to meet the charge
balance with oxygen anions. The resultant occupancy of A site is 90%. This charge balance-based
formula can facilitate a controlled surface metal particle exsolution.

LCTNO was synthesized by solid state reaction method. We used La2O3, CeO2, TiO2, NiO as.
Precursors. Powders were dried at different temperatures (TiO2 and at 300 °C and CeO2, La2O3 at
800 °C for 1h, no need for NiO). Powders were weighed out while hot and added to the ball miller
jars. PVB solution (serve as binder) was added to the jar as well at a weight percent 3% of the
precursor powders. Ethanol was added to form slurry during ball-milling. Afterwards, we dried the
slurry thoroughly at temperature up to 150 °C, then pressed the powders to 30 mm pellet, 5 grams
each. Pellets were put in crucibles and sintered in a box furnace at 1400 °C for 12 hours. 2 grams
from the sintered pellet was crushed to powders to run XRD. For reduction to generate surface
exsolution particles, the pellets were placed in a tube furnace, and heated to 900 °C at a 5 °C/min
ramp. 5% H2 balanced by N2 was fed to the tube furnace to reduce the pellet samples for 12 h at
900 °C. The resultant pellets were crushed to powders for XRD and MW operation.

A new material, Ni0.4Mg0.6O2, (NMO) was developed for the purpose of MW-excited CNT growth
from ceramic catalysts. NiO and MgO have the same symmetry and the lattice parameters are very

nitrate for 0.2 mol target material Ni0.4Mg0.6O2 were dissolved in a 1L large plastic beaker with

(Ni0.4Mg0.6)(OH)2 flake using a lab vacuum filtration apparatus. The precipitant was washed and
filtered twice using DI water to remove residual soluble species. The final flake was collected to
an alumina crucible and dried at 100 °C for 12 h and subsequently calcined to 1000 °C for 5 h.

Task 4: Separation of Catalyst-CNTs & CNFs and Catalyst Regeneration
The feasibility of cyclic reaction-regeneration protocol for self-sustained methane thermochemical
decomposition has been demonstrated experimentally in the study on a bimetallic 10Ni-1Cu-CNT
catalyst. After each catalytic TCD cycle, a portion of the used catalyst was refluxed with nitric acid
to leach out the metal content from the catalyst. And a portion regenerated CNT/CNF was used as
support to synthesize the bimetallic catalyst for next cycle using fresh Ni-Cu nitrates salts. After 4
cycles of reaction-regeneration protocol, no abrupt changes in methane conversion and
morphology/crystallinity of the regenerated CNT/CNF were observed as demonstrated by TEM,
TGA and Raman analysis. A scheme of proposed self-sustained cyclic methane TCD is shown in
Figure 4-1.

 Figure 4-1. A scheme of proposed self-sustained cyclic methane TCD.
Catalyst Synthesis
Bimetallic catalyst containing 11 wt.% metals (10 wt.% Ni and 1wt.% Cu) were dispersed on
different CNT support and synthesized via solvothermal method. Metal precursors nickel (II)
nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2.6H2O) (Acros Organics, 99%, USA) and copper (II) nitrate hemi
(pentahydrate) (Cu(NO3)2.2.5H2O) (Alfa Aesar, USA) were dispersed in 30 ml acetone under
sonication for 20 minutes, then solution was continuously stirred for 20 minutes. Support (CNT
support (Cheap tubes Inc>95%, USA) was added in the metal precursor solution under stirring.
After 20 minutes, the resultant solution was transferred to Teflon lined S.S autoclave and acetone
was added to make 50 ml solution, sealed, and heated to 120 °C for 12 hrs. in a hot air oven. The
autoclave was cooled down to room temperature and Teflon liner was left to evaporate acetone at
room temperature for overnight. The resultant sample was dried in oven at 80 °C for 12 hrs.
Catalyst pre-treatment (reduction) was carried out in pure H2 environment over the dried catalyst
samples placed in quartz tube which was heated with electrical furnace. Temperature was increased
 118

from the room temperature to 400 ºC with a ramping rate of 10 ºC/min. Afterwards, the temperature
was kept at 400 ºC for 5 hours.
Catalyst Preparation for Cyclic Reaction-Regeneration Test
After first methane decomposition cycle was carried out over the fresh reduced catalyst, a portion
of spent catalyst was refluxed with 3 M and 15 M nitric acid (HNO3) (LabChem, USA) for 4 hrs.
and 12 hrs. respectively under constant stirring (200 rpm) at 100 °C. After each cycle, the residue
carbon was filtered and washed with deionised water till the filtrate equals pH 7 and dried at 80 C
for 12 hrs. in oven. A portion of the dried carbon residue (0.25-0.3 gm) was used to synthesize the
catalyst for next CH4 decomposition cycle and catalyst synthesis protocol was the same as that
followed during the fresh catalyst preparation using fresh Ni and Cu precursors. Metal dissolved
in acid can be used for further processed to recover metal precursors by crystallization process. In
the reaction-regeneration study, the focus was on the recovery of carbon products.
Catalyst Activity
Variable frequency microwave reactor (Lambda Technology, USA) with working frequency 5.85
to 6.65 GHz and a solid-state microwave generator source was used to heat the reduced catalyst
bed in a quartz tube. Temperatures of the catalyst bed and quartz tube wall were monitored by two
infrared pyrometers. For each test, 0.2 g catalyst was loaded into the reactor quartz tube reactor
with 10 mm internal diameter. The composition of the outlet gas was analyzed by a two-column
Micro Gas Chromatography (Micro-GC, Agilent Technologies). The flow rate of inlet gas was set
initially at 30 mL/min with 30 mol % CH4 and 70 mol % N2. Nitrogen, as an inert and internal
marker, was adopted for the conversion calculation.
Under reaction conditions, the CH4 conversion (%) was described as follows in eq. (1).
CH4 conversion (%) = (1 – (CH4out N2in/CH4in N2out)) × 100%
Where, CH4out and CH4in were the mole concentrations of the CH4 at outlet and inlet, respectively.
and N2out and N2in were the mole concentration of the N2 outlet and inlet, respectively.

Task 7. Pilot Demonstration with a 6 kW Microwave Plasma Reactor
H Quest initiated design and fabrication of a pilot scale reactor capable of operation in the fluidized
bed mode, and the associated material procurement and system integration activities. HQV
demonstrated production of chemicals (acetylene, ethylene, aromatics), hydrogen, and solid
carbon directly from methane and other hydrocarbon gases at high throughputs (>50 slpm) and
microwave power (> 6kW). In a key technical development HQV addressed the principal challenge
of hydrocarbon conversion with microwave plasma: fouling of the plasma entrainment vessel.
Previously, using an older generation of microwave plasma reactor design H Quest demonstrated
viability of particle entrainment and fluidization within the plasma environment.
The key challenges to address in the present work have been identified as follows:
 1) control over fluidization or entrainment and particle residence time
 2) prevention of catalyst sintering.

Figure 7-1. Distinct regions of the microwave plasma as it propagates away from the excitation
zone.
Microwave plasma is a dynamic, non-homogeneous environment, as shown in Figure 7-1. Similar
to flames, the plasma can be comprised of distinct, dynamically changing regions differing in
temperature, gas velocity, and concentration of reactants and active species. Changes in applied
microwave power and gas composition, velocity and injection patterns can affect configuration of
the plasma regions. Injection of the gas with entrained catalyst would need to be optimized with
respect to desired residence time and catalytic conditions. For example, injections into the region
with excessive temperature may lead to catalyst sintering, while injection too far downstream of
the dense plasma may not promote carbon crystal growth.
To enable rapid iteration and testing in the Test Campaigns A-C, H Quest has developed quick,
low-cost reactor fabrication method. Reactor configurations can be readily fabricated in-house

 196

from commercial/off-the-shelf ISO 2852 stainless steel clamp pipe (‘tri-clamp’) couplings,
stainless steel tubes, and Swagelok fittings. Reactor cooling, if necessary, can be affected through
external copper coils with circulating coolant, as shown in Figure 7-2.

 Figure 7-2. Tri-clamp and related equipment
Tri-clamp part geometry, placement of the orifice, tube diameter and its orientation relative to the
gas flow axis, and gas flow and composition are then parameters controlling the entrainment and
fluidization patterns. The orifices may also serve as probe and sight ports to enable real-time
plasma and fluidization diagnostics.
A solution for catalyst dosing, entrainment, and injection has been developed and demonstrated
using a programmable powder pump for free-flowing solid substances. Custom fittings enable gas-
tight operation and precise, controllable dosing of solid powders into an entrainment gas flow
within a Swagelok-fitted tube.
The next steps include a study of entrainment and fluidization patterns of stand-in catalysts (sized
activated carbons, carbon black, silica, etc.) in cold tests. A high-speed camera and a laser-based
particle velocity measurement system was assembled and integrated with the reactor system to
enable these tests.
H Quest has integrated the programmatically controllable catalyst dosing powder pump with
several robust (no lab glassware) vessels to enable supplying and replenishing of the catalyst into
the plasma reactor. The resulting feeders are gas-tight to 15+ psi pressures. (Figure 7-3).
The powder dosers have been evaluated and calibrated with solid (carbon) powders of several size
distributions used as catalyst stand-ins (Fig 7-4). All except -120 mesh show good, stable feeding
performance. Clumping and arrested feeding were observed with -120 mesh powders.
Entrainment of powders in gas streams has been captured with the high-speed video (Figure 7-5).

H Quest has also started evaluation of design directions for enabling catalyst interaction with the
plasma stream. It's a complex problem, which intertwines parameters and properties of the
microwave plasma, of the underlying chemical process, and of the catalyst particles themselves.
Process parameters to be controlled include:

 • residence time
 • gas flows and velocities
These are affected by catalyst properties, including:

 • density
 • mass
 • particle size
 • flowability
 • fluidization properties
 • sintering / melting temperatures
The residence time of the process determines the duration for which the catalyst particles must be
retained in the active zone. Although microwave plasmas typically do not achieve the temperatures
of the DC plasma torches (up to 10,000 K), the temperature can easily exceed 1200 K (Figure 7-
6), which is sufficient to sinter or melt many metals.
 199

 Figure 7-6. Gas stream temperature and response to microwave power input

H Quest initiated evaluation of the following approaches:
1. Direct catalyst entrainment in the plasma stream.
This approach is attractive in its simplicity. It decouples the microwave plasma generation from
catalyst entrainment and injection, allowing selection of the active zone. It also limits duration of
contact of the catalyst with the high temperature plasma stream, which helps when catalyst is
temperature-sensitive, and enables high throughput of the catalyst. However, this approach is not
acceptable if the required residence time of the catalyst exceeds several seconds.
2. Fluidized bed entrainment
This approach maximizes exposure of the catalyst to the microwave plasma and is compatible with
long residence times. However, it imposes significant design and process control challenges, since
the plasma generation must be closely coupled with the fluidization regime of the catalyst and may
require batch rather than continuous replenishment. Additionally, the entrained catalyst would be
exposed to high temperatures for the duration of the operation.
3. Spouted bed entrainment
Similarly to the direct entrainment, this approach decouples microwave generation from
entrainment and fluidization. However, it’s similar to fluidization in that this approach enables
extended or repeated exposure of the catalyst to the microwave plasma and is compatible with long
residence times. Continuous replacement of the catalyst is also possible in this configuration.

The horizontal plasma launcher was designed, unit-tested and assembled. Multiple diagnostic ports

A spouting bed assembly was designed to enable rapid entrainment and recirculation of the catalyst

a central inner injection tube. The particles are spouted upward and fall back down into the bed as

Microwave plasmas are non-homogeneous in spatial and temporal dimensions. In the excitation
region, the microwave electromagnetic field couples and transfers its energy to the free electrons
in the gas. The accelerated electrons excite, activate, and ionize heavy species through particle
collisions. In that region, the distribution of energies is non-equilibrium: the electron temperature
is much higher than that of the heavy species.

As the ionized gas travels away from the excitation region, the temperatures begin to equilibrate,
as electrons lose their higher kinetic energy and are recaptured by the ionized species. The neutral
species and radicals gain temperature but also remain excited, as is indicated by characteristic
emissions visible on the captured spectra. This region corresponds to the dense plasma shown on
Figure 7-1.

Finally, the gas thermalizes, and black body emissions begin to dominate. This corresponds to the
afterglow regime in Figure 7-1.
Certainly, chemical activity and reaction kinetics would vary with the distance from the excitation
zone. The developed spouting bed design allows varying the distance of the catalyst injection point
from the excitation region, as well as other process parameters, and capture of the corresponding
spectral signature for post-test analysis (Figure 7-9).

System implementation
The catalyst protocol developed by PNNL utilizes a tube furnace as the energy input into the
catalyst bed and requires a heat-pretreatment/reduction step performed at 350~400 °C, while the
temperatures in the CNT growth step should not exceed 600 °C. HQV spectral measurements of
micro-plasma and bulk microwave plasma indicated temperatures well more than 1000 °C. To
address the temperature requirements, H Quest developed an alternative approach to direct
exposure to plasma. Instead, the plasma reaction chamber has been segregated from the catalyst
bed.
Another addressed problem, which has been identified in the previous reporting period, is
contamination of the catalyst samples with amorphous carbon black formed in the microwave
plasma reactor. The ingress the carbon material into the catalyst bed (a) thwarts CNT growth by
coating the catalyst surface resulting in decreased interaction between the catalyst and the reactor’s
effluent gas, and (b) complicates material characterization since amorphous carbon cannot be
readily separated from the catalyst and synthesized CNTs.
Two avenues were taken to reduce ingress of amorphous carbon into the catalyst bed. Firstly, the
processing conditions (gas flow rate, concentration, injection location, microwave power input)
were adjusted to minimize carbon formation in the plasma reactor (< 10% selectivity). A quenching
gas was injected to suppress the acetylene polymerization process, which is responsible for carbon
formation. Secondly, a 3-stage, solid-gas separation system (Figure 7-27) was developed and
employed before the catalyst bed to ensure complete removal of carbon from the gas stream before
entering the bed. The separation system consists of 3x filters with decreasing mesh size and surface
area -- #60 mesh filter for collecting the bulk of the carbon, #120 mesh for collecting finer particles,
and a #325 mesh for collecting the finest particles.
A “bypass” line was also installed to characterize the composition of the gas stream prior to the
catalyst bed. The block flow diagram and a schematic of the system developed by HQV is shown
below (Figure 7-28).

To support experimental work in this reporting period, HQV designed and fabricated two different
catalyst bed configurations (fluidized and fixed bed). Both were designed built to operate at
temperatures > 750 °C, offer multiple temperature measurement points, and could be insulated to
prevent heat loss.

The catalyst bed design was updated (Figure 7-38) to address the following difficulties
encountered during the previous reporting period:
 1) Expansion of the catalyst bed housing dislodged the press-fit fritted quartz support disc
 during operation and allowed the catalyst to fall out of the bed. In this reporting period, a
 perforated metal support structure was added below the fritted disk to contain the catalyst.
 The perforated support allows reactant gas to flow into bed while securing the quartz disc
 in place.
 2) The cyclone separators employed for cleaning the input gas stream for carbon black formed
 in the plasma reactor had less than 60% separation efficiency -- likely due to the sensitivity
 of cyclone separator efficiency to carbon black agglomerate sizes. Unfiltered carbon black
 clogged the fritted discs within 5 mins of operation and led to unsustainable pressure drop
 (< 5 psig) across the bed. In this reporting period,this problem was resolved by adding
 baghouse filters ( 20 micron) as a second stage filter to remove >99.9% of carbon from the
 gas stream before it enters the catalyst bed.
 3) Finally, the effluent exiting the catalyst bed was too hot (>200 °C) for downstream
 components, many of which were made of plastic materials (rated to 60-100 °C). In this
 reporting period, this problem was resolved by integrating a water-cooled heat exchanger
 at the outlet of the catalyst bed. This heat exchanger cooled the gas from >200 °C to <30
 °C -- well within the range of all downstream system components.

Based on analysis of process data and benchtop experiments performed by WVU researchers, two
main causes were identified for not seeing any significant CNT yield in the previous reporting
period:
High concentration of hydrogen (35-50 vol%) used in H Quest’s system as a quench gas to suppress
carbon black formation and shift selectivity towards gaseous products. Benchtop experimentation
showed that CNT growth can be achieved at hydrogen concentrations up to 8.3 vol%.

The temperature of the catalyst was not high enough to promote dehydrogenation and CNT growth.
The temperature controller was set to heat the gas to 400 °C. But benchtop experimentation
revealed that the catalyst material itself must be heated to 600 °C for activation.
Using results from the benchtop experimental conditions which resulted to successful CNT
growth, the following requirements were derived for H Quest’s catalyst bed assembly:
(1) Temperature
a. The catalyst temperature must be maintained at 600 ± 10 °C.
b. Temperature of the bed shall not exceed 650 C at any point.
(2) Effluent composition
a. The catalyst was reduced with a 100% hydrogen atmosphere for 90 mins prior to CNT growth
mode.
b. The catalyst shall not be exposed to any oxygen after the material has been reduced.
c. The max hydrogen conc. in the input stream to H Quest MPP system shall not exceed 7 vol%
when running in CNT growth mode.
(3) Catalyst bed parameters
a. The catalyst bed shall be loaded with 50 g of catalyst material per test.
b. The catalyst material shall be packed by tapping the catalyst bed wall to densify the catalyst.
c. The flow rate of effluent through the bed shall be maintained at 5 slpm in both reduction and
CNT growth modes.

temperature of the gas at the outlet was also monitored using a thermocouple (T_out). Gas supply
to the bed (5 slpm) was precisely controlled using Alicat mass flow controllers and the composition

8.1. Process Description
The process flow diagram of the core process, A100 methane pyrolysis, is given in Figure 8-1.
Figure 8-2 provides the detailed process configuration of A300 acid wash and carbon recovery. As
shown in Figure 8-1, NG is preheated and first sent to fluidized bed catalytic reactor (R-102) to be
converted into H2 and CNT. The gas product from R-102 consists of unconverted NG and H2,
which is compressed to 20 bar and sent to a pressure swing adsorption (PSA) unit for H2 recovery.
A portion of the off-gas from PSA unit is sent to A200, an on-site combined heat and power (CHP)
unit, to produce heat and electricity, while the remaining is sent to the H-Quest Microwave Plasma
Pyrolysis (MPP) reactor (R-101), which gives higher NG conversion due to higher operating
temperature. The split ratio of PSA off-gas was set such that all electricity and heat consumed in
the process is produced on-site from purged gas, instead of from grid. The solid product from R-
102 is a mixture of CNT product and catalyst, which is further separated in A300 acid wash and
carbon recovery unit. As MPP reactor (R-101) does not have catalyst, all solid in the outlet is CB,
which is separated from gas produce in the carbon handling unit. The gas product from R101
mainly contains unconverted NG, H2 and a small amount of C2H2, which is combined with the gas
product from R-102, and then sent to the PSA unit.

Figure 8-1. Process flow diagram of A100 methane pyrolysis.
In A300, the hot solid-phase product from the main reactor is first cooled by water quench before
sent to the acid digester (R-301) along with the make-up 3M HNO3. In R-301, metal catalyst is

 248

dissolved into liquid phase, while producing NO2 as a by-product, while CNT stays in the solid
phase. The product from R-301 is then sent to a two-stage filter for CNT recovery, while the metal
nitrate-rich solution is sent to A310 for catalyst regeneration. The NO2 by-product is sent to A320
along with water and air to regenerate HNO3 via the commercial Ostwald process (Ecofys, 2012).

For both SOT and goal cases, three plant scales are investigated. Both small-scale and medium-
scale plants represent modular and distributed H2 production with daily production rates of 250 kg
and 1000 kg, respectively. The large-scale plant represents centralized H2 production with a scale
of 50,000 kg H2 per day, which is comparable to commercial scale natural gas steam reforming

in the current design, a portion of NG is used for on-site electricity generation with a typical
efficiency around 50% to supply all heat and electricity required in the pyrolysis reactor. If grid
electricity is used, the thermal efficiency of the proposed methane pyrolysis technology can be
roughly 5-10% higher than the current estimates (roughly 71% for SOT, and 76% for the goal
case). The plant scales of all cases are summarized in Table 8-4, while the estimated MHSP and

Conclusion
This project has discovered Ni-M-CNT bimetallic catalyst and SrTiNiO catalysts that can
effectively absorb microwave energy and drive the methane pyrolysis reaction while facilitating
the reaction with catalytic effect. The results showed a methane conversion up to ~50% methane
conversion. The selectivity to hydrogen is close to 100%. The carbon product is mainly multi-
walled CNT. The separations of metal and CNT products were completed and showed the
feasibility of catalyst regeneration. The different frequencies of microwave were tested and
showed slight differences have taken place. 5.85 GHz was found performing well for the
microwave catalytic pyrolysis. The intrinsic nature of all catalysts developed were revealed by
SEM-EDX, TEM-Mapping, XRD, and BET. The kinetic studies for selected catalysts have been
conducted. It was found that under microwave irradiation, the activation energy was lower due to
selective heating.
A microwave plasma pilot plant was designed, built and successfully demonstrated in natural gas
field. The results show that microwave or microwave plasma catalytic pyrolysis is scalable. This
project has ended with TRL 6. TEA analysis was completed for the process and results show that
the carbon or hydrogen selling prices are lower than commercial processes.
