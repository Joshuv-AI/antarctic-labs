 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Tereform, Inc. (Tereform) is developing molecular deconstruction processes to transform waste
 materials into chemical building blocks. During the CRADA, Tereform deconstructed real-world
 post-consumer substrates into chemical monomers, validated their performance on laboratory
 scales, demonstrated feasibility of the degradation products in downstream transformations, and
 successfully scaled the reaction to kilogram-scale. These results were used to develop and refine
 technoeconomic analysis and lifecycle assessments to evaluate the economic feasibility and
 environmental impacts of the process.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Economic and Lifecycle Development

 This task will leverage NLR-developed polyethylene terephthalate (PET) to terephthalic acid
 (TPA) technoeconomic analyses (TEA) and life cycle assessments (LCA) and will update the
 cost projections with Tereform-relevant parameters and inputs.

 Task 1 Results:

 NLR has provided this report of the TEA and LCA of the Tereform depolymerization process.
 Throughout the CRADA effort, Tereform and NLR worked together to update these models
 based on experimental results obtained by Tereform, as well as to model several different
 process scenarios to compare multiple technological pathways. A process flow diagram (PFD)
 was generated modeling the entire process from textile feedstock inputs, through pre-processing
 steps such as shredding, washing, and dewatering, followed by the depolymerization process,
 purification of the TPA and ethylene glycol (EG) monomers, and eventually polymerization to
 produce new PET polymer.

 This process was originally developed for the oxidative depolymerization technology that
 employed oxygen, cobalt, and manganese catalysts, and a radical initiator to depolymerize PET.
 In this process, the TEA was found to be cost-competitive with virgin prices. However, due to
 combustion of a portion of the acetic acid solvent during oxidative depolymerization, the LCA
 revealed resource utilization for the process to be at a similar scale to existing industrial methods.
 Alongside the development of this initial TEA/LCA, Tereform and NLR researchers developed
 an additional method for effective integrated separation and depolymerization of blended
 synthetic textiles (containing blends of nylon 6, nylon 6,6, polyurethanes, and polyesters), that
 would avoid the degradation of acetic acid solvent. NLR conducted TEA and LCA analysis on
 this updated process, revealing that it remains economically viable with lower loss of process
 inputs relative to virgin production, alongside the ability to accommodate more challenging

 substrate streams. NLR also performed a sensitivity analysis of key parameters relevant to the
 scale-up of the process.

 In summary, NLR modeling of the TEA and LCA was instrumental in guiding the technology
 direction to ensure the process with the most favorable economic outlook was selected for further
 scale-up. The results from this Task were continuously updated by the information obtained in
 subsequent Tasks outlined below in this Report.

 Task 2: Substrate Characterization

 Tereform has received various post-consumer textile materials and will be characterizing them
 and the degradation products as a user of NLR facilities by: nuclear magnetic resonance
 spectroscopy (NMR), infrared spectroscopy (IR), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-
 MS), and/or high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC).

 NLR will also perform complimentary analyses on behalf of Tereform to evaluate the samples’
 elemental composition, chemical and physical properties using: ultra-high pressure liquid
 chromatography (UHPLC), LC-MS/MS, HPLC, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), gel
 permeation chromatography (GPC), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), ultraviolet-visible
 spectroscopy (UV-Vis), NMR, GC-MS, IR. NLR will ship samples off for inductively coupled
 plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and optional polymerization of TPA, with optional in-
 house inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). Optional analysis
 may include using a high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and/or colorimeter.

 Task 2 Results:

 This is a key task due to the high complexity of post-consumer textile material composition.
 Textiles often contain blends of materials, coatings, finishes, dyes, and a variety of other
 additives, making it challenging to quantitatively obtain product yields from real materials due to
 an unknown weight percentage of polymer in the starting materials. For an accurate
 determination of product yields, it is critical to quantify the amount of polymer present in a given
 mass of material. This is challenging to conduct without specialized techniques such as NMR.
 Accordingly, NMR studies were conducted on a variety of materials used as inputs for the
 Tereform depolymerization process, including garments, carpets, footwear, and technical
 textiles, among others. An example of material quantification by NMR is included in Figure 1,
 where a jacket composed of a mixed PET/polyurethane (PU) material was subjected to analysis,
 determining that 61.9 weight percent (wt%) of the fabric weight is PET.

 3

 Fig. 1. Example NMR quantification of a jacket. Fabric samples are dissolved in a trifluoroacetic
 acid (TFA)/deuterated chloroform (CDCl₃) solution and analyzed via proton nuclear magnetic
 resonance (1H NMR). Integrations are quantified by comparing to 1,3,5-tribromobenzene (TBB)
 internal standard to determine the weight percentage of the fabric that is composed of PET.

 NLR analytical services and equipment were also used in the determination of product yields
 throughout the course of the CRADA research project. High-performance liquid chromatography
 (HPLC) was used extensively to quantify the TPA and EG yields after deconstruction of the
 PET, while GPC, DSC, and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were used to determine the
 molecular weight of polymers both as feedstocks and as outputs after repolymerization of
 isolated TPA and EG monomers. Analysis using these techniques was instrumental in
 completing Tereform’s project goals and enabled effective quantification of product yields.

 Task 3: Validating real-world substrate for process yield

 NLR employees will set up reaction processes (which may include weighing out reactants,
 reactor operation, reaction workup, clean up, sample submission for analytics, data processing)
 and train Tereform representatives to operate 75 mL reactor systems relevant to the
 depolymerization to validate the reaction fidelity on real-world textile substrates based on
 analyses obtained in Task 2. Upon satisfactory training, Tereform may be permitted to run
 reactors independently and perform process optimization for all relevant post-consumer
 substrates on the parent process. Jointly, NLR and Tereform will analyze products using the
 UPLC analytical methods, with optional LC-MS/MS and HRMS outlined in Task 2.

 Task 3 Results:

 For this Task, a variety of substrates were employed in deconstruction demonstrations to
 evaluate the scope of the feedstocks that could be used as inputs for the process. Tereform’s
 technology is designed to achieve high product yields even in the presence of additives,
 contaminants, blended materials, and other potential disruptors, which other recycling
 technologies cannot accommodate. Using 75 milliliter (mL) Parr reactors, NLR and Tereform
 conducted a series of depolymerization reactions on substrates from several industries, focusing
 on particularly challenging materials, as shown in Figure 2, where Tereform was asked to
 evaluate the process’s ability to deconstruct several particularly hard-to-recycle materials
 obtained from an apparel brand partner. By using these small-scale reactions, NLR and Tereform
 were able to deconstruct these polymers and obtain TPA yields, demonstrating the ability to
 achieve significant yields even from highly challenging substrates.

 Fig. 2. Left: 75-mL Parr systems used for high-throughput evaluation of deconstruction on a
 variety of materials. Right: materials obtained from brand partners used for deconstruction
 evaluation. TPA product yields are taken as wt% of the total material, not including the weight% of
 PET in each material.

 5

 Similar analyses were conducted on a variety of post-consumer and post-industrial textiles
 obtained from brand and manufacturing partners, demonstrating that the process can effectively
 deconstruct a wide range of synthetic condensation polymers. Work primarily focused on the
 deconstruction of PET products, but results revealed an ability to achieve high yields of
 monomers from nylon 6, nylon 6,6, and polyurethane materials as well. Process conditions were
 optimized using these 75 mL systems, which informed the conditions to be used for scale-up
 efforts in the subsequent Task.

 Task 4: Scale-up of process

 NLR employees will set-up reaction processes (which may include weighing out reactants,
 reactor operation, reaction workup, clean up, sample submission for analytics, data processing)
 and train Tereform representatives to operate 8 L (Jabba) reactor systems relevant to the
 depolymerization to validate the reaction fidelity on real-world textile substrates based on
 analyses obtained in Task 2 and Task 3. Upon satisfactory training, Tereform may be permitted
 to run reactors independently and perform process optimization for all relevant post-consumer
 substrates on the parent process. Jointly, NLR and Tereform will analyze products using the
 UPLC analytical methods, with optional LC-MS/MS and HRMS outlined in Task 2.

 Furthermore, upon final commissioning and installation of the 27 L reactor, safety and
 operational assessments will be made, and depending upon review, NLR will oversee and
 communicate options to Tereform accordingly. If independent operation is prohibitive in terms
 of personnel or safety requirements, NLR will lead operation of the 27 L system.

 Task 4 Results:

 Reactions in the 8 liter (L) “Jabba” reactor were completed using post-consumer PET carpet
 substrates. These carpets had been densified prior to Tereform obtaining the materials. These
 carpet pellets had been previously characterized in Task 2, and small-scale trials had been
 conducted in Task 3 to determine optimal processing parameters. Depolymerization trials were
 conducted at the 1 kilogram (kg) scale, generating 90% yields of TPA monomers (Figure 3).
 Results of analytical characterization were delivered to Tereform to confirm yield and purity
 measurements. These experiments demonstrated the ability for this process to obtain high
 monomer yield and purity even from challenging streams such as post-consumer carpets.

 6

 Fig. 3. Process for deconstruction of postconsumer carpets using the 8 L “Jabba” reactor. 1kg of
 postconsumer carpet pellets (92% PET by mass) were added to the reactor and deconstructed.
 TPA was isolated and purified, achieving 90% monomer yield.

 The process was then further scaled up using the larger 22-L reactor at NLR, demonstrating
 deconstruction of postconsumer garments. The most significant cost in any recycling process is
 the cost of feedstock acquisition and pre-processing. As such, Tereform aimed to demonstrate
 the ability to deconstruct shredded garments without requiring pelletization to increase density.
 The larger reactor volume enabled this processing, which is a key step in reducing the
 preprocessing that feedstocks must undergo prior to deconstruction. Shredded postconsumer
 garments (1.2 kg of polyester garments) were loaded into the reactor and were deconstructed into
 terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol monomers (Figure 4). Product yields of greater than 80%
 from the reaction demonstrated that this depolymerization process is feasible even from complex
 postconsumer inputs.

 Fig. 4. Process for deconstruction of postconsumer carpets using the 22 L “Greivous” reactor. 1.2
 kg of postconsumer polyester garments were added to the reactor and deconstructed. TPA and
 EG were purified to obtain polymerization-grade monomers.
 Performance of these key tasks then enabled Tereform to bring the process in-house to their
 facility using a similar reactor to the 22 L NLR reactor, using the data and optimizations from
 this Task to inform their continued development and scaling efforts.

 Task 5: Chemical Derivatization – Exploratory (If budget should remain)

 Tereform will evaluate derivatization of the TPA to produce specialty products. Examples could
 include terephthaloyl chloride and its derivatives, dioctyl terephthalate, Kevlar, PET, etc.

 Task 5 Results:

 No work was completed on Task 5, as both NLR and Tereform prioritized continuing the
 successful depolymerization and scale-up efforts achieved in the preceding tasks. Given the
 progress demonstrated in Task 4, the project team agreed that resources would be most
 effectively applied toward scaling and advancing those proven processes, utilizing available
 remaining funds to maximize CRADA outcomes.
