Lifespan & Performance
Considerations for Large-
Scale BESS

 Expected Performance Lifetime

Large-scale BESS using current lithium-ion battery (LiB)
technology have an expected performance lifetime of 15–20
years, depending on duty cycle, operating conditions, and
performance thresholds (NREL 2024; Botswana Power 2024;
Wisconsin Power and Light 2023).

- Expected performance lifetimes are increasing with
  technology innovation. For example, in 2020 lithium iron
  phosphate (LFP) and lithium nickel manganese cobalt
  (NMC) BESS were commonly expected to operate for
  approximately 10 years; by 2022, expected lifetimes had
 (Photo by Werner Slocum / NLR)
  increased to 16 years (Viswanathan et al., 2022).

- Project lifetimes beyond 20 years are achievable through
  planned augmentation, replacement, or repowering, which
  are increasingly anticipated in procurement and regulatory
  frameworks.                                                                                   National Laboratory of the Rockies | 3

 Performance Contracts and Term
 Lengths
BESS performance contract term lengths are typically based on
the performance lifetime (approximately 15-20 years) and the
manufacturer warranty for the main asset––the battery.
- Manufacturing warranties typically range from 10-15 years,
  or 5,000 to 10,000 full cycles, depending on chemistry and
  operating profile.
Typical performance contract lengths for large-scale BESS are
approximately 20 years, with provisions for mid-term
performance management.
 (Photo by Dennis Schroeder / NLR)​
BESS performance-related contracts include Energy
Performance Contracts (EPCs), Power Purchase Agreements
(PPAs), Energy Storage Tolling Agreements, Build-Transfer
Agreements (BTAs), contractor performance guarantees.

Mid-Term and End-of-
Performance Considerations
Extending energy performance contracts,
repowering or augmentation, and
decommissioning

Mid-Term Considerations: Augmentation
 and Repowering

Mid-term evaluations, typically around year 10, may include augmentation and repowering
considerations.

- Limited operational experience to date, as most large-scale BESS have been installed within
  the past 5-7 years.

Augmentation is a process to extend the operational life of a project by adding battery capacity
to an existing system, typically to address performance degradation or changing operational
requirements.

Repowering is a process to extend the operational life of a project by updating or replacing
equipment, which may include redesigning elements of the system to improve power
performance, safety, security, and/or overall system capabilities.

                                                                                   National Laboratory of the Rockies | 6

 End-of-Term Considerations: Extending
 Performance Contract and Decommissioning
Near- and end-of-term evaluations, around year 15, may include
repowering, extending a performance contract, or
decommissioning options.

Extending a Performance Contract, if pursued, is often considered
in combination with augmentation or repowering. This typically
requires renewing or amending the performance contract and
may also involve modifying land access or land-use leases and
amending operating permits, depending on jurisdiction or scope.
 (Photo by Dennis Schroeder / NLR)

Decommissioning is a process to remove the equipment and
infrastructure associated with the project, and restore the land,
consistent with applicable permit conditions and land-use
requirements.

Mid-Term Performance: Procurement
  and Regulatory Considerations
When a BESS is anticipated to be augmented, an applicant for a site approval is typically required to
submit an augmentation plan as part of the approval or permitting process.

- Example language:
 An "Augmentation Plan must include a detailed description of the proposed
 augmentation, an annotated site plan, and anticipated augmentation phases,
 including which structures or components are expected to be installed and associated
 timeframes " (University of Michigan 2024)

If augmenting or repowering would change the approved project footprint or involve a significant
change in battery chemistry, regulators may treat the activity as:

• A new or revised application for land use, siting, and/or zoning approval; and/or
• A modification to an existing operating permit, requiring an amendment.

 Mid-Term and End-of-Performance:
 Procurement and Regulatory Considerations
– Internal Technical and Regulatory Guidance: national, regional, subnational, provincial or
  local government guidance addressing BESS expected performance lifetimes; mid-term and
  end-of-performance considerations; performance contracts; and regulatory considerations
  including model frameworks and language.

– External Regulatory Guidance: public-facing, non-binding best practice guidance addressing
  mid-term and end-of-performance activities for BESS, including permitting and application
  considerations (e.g., when amendments and modifications to siting or operational permits
  may be required).

– Regulation or Request for Proposals (RFPs): adoption of binding requirements or
  restrictions through RFPs or existing or new regulations governing mid-term and end-of-
  performance BESS activities (e.g., requiring submission of an augmentation plan during
  initial siting or permitting).

System Decommissioning

Decommissioning plans, cost estimates, financial
assurance, and performance requirements

 Decommissioning Regulatory
 Considerations
Applicability of Regulatory Requirements typically extends to BESS that are permitted, installed, modified, or
retired, but may vary based on technology type and capacity thresholds (e.g., requirements applying to lead-
acid BESS above 70 kWh; lithium-ion BESS above 30 kWh) (see Hardwick Massachusetts 2026).
Decommissioning Plan is often required as a pre-requisite or condition to a permit required for initial
development. It typically must detail how the project equipment will be removed and how the site will be
restored.
Decommissioning Cost Estimate is often required as a pre-requisite or condition to a permit required for initial
development and may require periodic updates and resubmission. It is often included as part of the
decommissioning plan, but may be a separate requirement, and it typically informs the amount of financial
assurance required. Some regulations specify allowable or prohibited cost components and may require the
estimate to be prepared by a qualified third party.
Financial Assurance may be required as a capital cost and/or an operating cost to the project. Periodic
resubmission and re-evaluation–often every 5 year—is a common regulatory requirement.
Specific Performance Requirements typically define when a project is considered decommissioned (e.g., after
a specified period of non-operation – 6 months), and outline equipment removal and site restoration
standards.

 Decommissioning Plans
 Illustrative examples drawn from existing regulations and model regulatory language.

Source Regulatory or Model Language

NYSERDA “The applicant shall submit a decommissioning plan, developed in accordance with the Uniform Code, to be implemented
Model upon abandonment and/or in conjunction with removal from the facility. The decommissioning plan shall include:
Language • Narrative description of the activities to be accomplished, including who will perform that activity and at what point in
 time, for complete removal of all BESS components, structures, equipment, security barriers, and transmission lines
 from site;
 • Disposal of all solid and hazardous waste in accordance with local, state, and federal waste disposal regulations;
 • The anticipated life of the BESS;
 • The estimated decommissioning costs and how said estimate was determined;
 • The method of ensuring that funds will be available for decommissioning and restoration;
 • The method by which the decommissioning cost will be kept current;
 • The manner in which the site will be restored … ; and
 • A listing of any contingencies for removing an intact operational BESS from service, and for removing a BESS from
 service that has been damaged by a fire or other event” (NYSERDA 2025).
Washington State law requires that decommissioning plans must:
State •Identify, evaluate, and resolve all major environmental, public health, and public safety issues relevant to the site
 • Describe the facility owner’s methodology in choosing measures to be used to restore or preserve the site
 • Compare economic factors of costs and benefits for different restoration options against any risks to the public (Wash.
             Admin.Code § 463-72-20).

 Decommissioning Cost Estimate
 Illustrative examples drawn from existing regulations.

Source Regulatory Language
New York “The cost estimate must include a gross and net decommissioning and site restoration estimate, with the net cost estimate
 including project salvage value (including reference to the salvage value data source) with line items (and associated dollar amounts)
 for decommissioning of all facility components.

 The gross cost estimates shall include a 15% contingency cost based on the overall decommissioning and site restoration estimate.

 The net amount shall be allocated between cities, towns, or villages where the facility is located, based on the estimated cost
 associated with the removal and restoration of the facility located in each.

 The cost estimate must include a net decommissioning and site restoration estimate including project salvage value (including
 reference to the salvage value data source) with line items (and associated dollar amounts) for decommissioning of all facility
 components.

 The cost estimate must be submitted as part of the decommissioning plan, prior to construction (N.Y. Comp. Codes R. & Regs. tit. 19
           § 900-2.24)
Texas The cost estimate must be prepared by an independent third-party professional licensed engineer and provide the estimated cost
 of removing the facilities and restoring the property to as near as reasonably possible to its pre-construction condition.

 The cost estimate must include the estimated salvage value of facilities and be submitted by the landowner prior to construction.

 The cost estimate must be updated on or before the 10th year of facility operations and at least once every 5 years thereafter for
 the remainder of the agreement (Tex. Util. Code Ann. §§ 302.0005, 301.0004(b)).

 Decommissioning Financial
 Assurance
 Illustrative examples drawn from existing regulations and model regulatory language.

Source Regulatory or Model Language

Montana Owner must submit financial assurance (surety bond or collateral bond) to the Montana Department of Environmental
 Quality (DEQ) prior to the 15th year of operation. Amount is determined by DEQ based on:
 • The decommissioning cost estimate submitted by the owner
 • Character and nature of the site
 • Costs of managing, maintaining, and/or decommissioning the facility in the event of abandonment
 • Current salvage value of the facility infrastructure (e.g., modules, supports, inverters, and other ancillary equipment)

 Financial assurance must be resubmitted every 5 years (Mont. Admin. R. 17.86.102, 106, 115).

New York Owner must submit financial assurance to the New York Office of Renewable Energy Siting after 1 year of facility
 operation, in the form of a letter of credit or another approved instrument.

 Financial assurance must be updated and resubmitted every 5 year, including inflation or cost adjustments (N.Y. Comp.
           Codes R. & Regs. tit. 19 §900-10.2).

NYSERDA “The owner and/or operator shall maintain a fund or bond payable to the [Village/Town/City], in a form approved by the
Model [Village/Town/City], for removal of the battery energy storage system, in an amount determined by the [Village/Town/City],
Language for the life of the facility” (NYSERDA 2025).

 Decommissioning Performance
 Requirements
 Illustrative examples drawn from existing regulations and regulatory agency guidance.

Source Regulatory Language and Guidance
Texas The facility owner must return property to as near or as reasonably possible to the same condition as before the
 grantee dug holes or cavities, including reseeding with native grasses, and clear, clean, and remove:

 • Each BESS, including ancillary equipment,
 • BESS foundations to a depth of at least 3 feet below surface grade, with all holes or cavities filled with soil;
 • Underground cables to a depth of at least 3 feet below surface grade, with all holes or cavities filled with soil
 • Roads constructed on the property, unless otherwise agreed (Tex. Util. Code Ann. §§ 302.0004, § 301.0003).

Oregon A facility owner must retire and decommission the facility if construction or operation of the facility permanently
 ceases.

 The owner must retire the facility according to a final decommissioning plan approved by the Council, as described
           in OAR 345-027-0410.

 The owner must pay the actual cost to restore the site to a useful, non-hazardous condition at the time of retirement,
 regardless of any previously approved estimated restoration amount (Or. Rev. Stat. § 345-025-0006(9)).

 The owner must decommission a retired or abandoned facility and restore the site to a useful, non-hazardous
 condition (Energy Facility Siting Guidance).

  Decommissioning: Considerations for
 Regulators
  – External Regulatory Guidance: public-facing, non-binding best practices addressing planning, financing, and performance activities for
 BESS decommissioning.
  – Regulation or RFP: adoption of binding requirements or restrictions through RFPs or existing or new regulations, including:
 • Decommissioning triggers: when a BESS must be decommissioned (e.g., after 12 months of ceased operations)
 • Decommissioning plans: requirement for a detailed plan specifying scope, timing, responsibilities, and required information
 • Decommissioning cost estimates: requirements addressing
 ▪ who prepares the estimate,
 ▪ how the estimate is calculated,
 ▪ update frequency, and
 ▪ submission and resubmission timing (e.g., every 7 years)
 • Financial assurance: requirement specifying
 ▪ the instrument (e.g., bond, letter of credit),
 ▪ timing of posting (e.g., 50% prior to project construction, remaining 50% at year 10 of operation), and
 ▪ cost components included or excluded (e.g., treatment of salvage value)
 • Performance standards: specific removal and restoration requirements (e.g., removal of all equipment and
 underground/aboveground wiring, restoration to greenfield or other defined condition).

(Sources: Hardwick Massachussett 2026; NYSERDA 2025; Wash. Admin. Code §463-72-20; N.Y. Comp. Codes R. & Regs. Tit. 19; Tex. Util. Code Ann. §§
302.0003-0004; Mont. Admin. R. 17.86.102, 106, 115; Or. Rev. Stat. § 345-025-0006(9); Or. Rev. Stat. § 345-025-0006; OR Energy Facility Siting Guidance; Port
of Oakland 2024; The Waste Batteries and Accumulators Regulations 2009)

Management pathways and regulatory
considerations for BESS components

 Equipment Decommissioning and
 Management Options
 Illustrative examples drawn from existing regulations and RFP language.

Source Regulatory or RFP Language
Port of “The Seller shall make provision for decommissioning, removal, disposal, and recycling of the
Oakland battery and other BESS components at end-of-life. At a minimum, the contract requires that
RFP any BESS waste products, including packaging from new parts, be recycled when feasible”
 (Port of Oakland 2024)
United “A producer of industrial or automotive batteries must ensure that all identifiable
Kingdom waste batteries taken back or collected by that producer under this Part are delivered to
 and accepted by— (a)an approved battery treatment operator for treatment and recycling;
 or (b)an approved battery exporter for export for treatment and recycling outside the
 United Kingdom” (The Waste Batteries and Accumulators Regulations 2009).

 Consideration: End-of-life equipment management (e.g., recycling or disposal) is often incorporated into contracts
 and financial models to ensure total lifecycle costs are considered for BESS.

 Handling, Transport, and Storage
 Prior to Reuse, Recycling, or Disposal
Regulations and contractual frameworks commonly shape how BESS are managed prior to reuse, recycling and disposal.

 Management Activity Regulations and Contracts Commonly Require

 Handling, Storage Compliance with applicable requirements governing handling, storage, labeling, and reporting prior to reuse,
 recycling, or disposal.

 Transport and Export Compliance with applicable requirements governing transport, shipping, and export prior to reuse, recycling,
 or disposal.

 Regulatory and Contractual Considerations
 In Malawi, the Environment Management Act 2017 and the Environment Management (Waste Management and
 Sanitation) Regulations, 2008 may provide a starting point for assessing how existing waste management requirements
 apply to BESS prior to reuse, recycling, or disposal. Part VI addresses hazardous waste management, including
 provisions relevant to handling, disposal, and transfer. Engagement with the Malawi Environmental Protection
 Authority may help clarify applicability and identify whether relevant requirements are most appropriately reflected in
 permits, requests for proposals (RFPs), or performance contracts.

Sources: Curtis et al. 2021; Malawi Environment Management Act 2017; Malawi Environment Management Regulations 2008; Malawi Environmental Protection Authority 2025
