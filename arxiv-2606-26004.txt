Non-invertible symmetries in the axiverse, and the imaginary wormholes


Abstract
We study the symmetry structure of four-dimensional axiverse effective field theories with multiple axions coupled to abelian gauge sectors, including their extensions to broad classes of 𝒩 = 1 {\cal N}=1 models. We identify the invertible and non-invertible generalized symmetries, and discuss the associated symmetry-breaking mechanisms together with the resulting hierarchies of energy scales. In particular, we discuss the quantum-gravitational breaking of non-invertible axion shift symmetries predicted by the existence of wormholes and by the corresponding recently proposed Imaginary Distance Bound. In 𝒩 = 1 {\cal N}=1 axiverses, these wormhole-based arguments imply that towers of BPS EFT instantons play a distinguished role and generate infinitely many superpotential terms.

Introduction

From a bottom-up viewpoint, light axion-like fields are motivated by several important questions in particle phenomenology. In particular, they furnish an appealing mechanism for solving the strong CP problem
[
107
,
124
,
126
]
, have long been considered viable dark matter candidates
[
74
]
, and may also participate in inflationary dynamics
[
48
]
.
It has also been understood since the early 1980s that such light degrees of freedom arise naturally in string theory constructions; see
[
119
]
for a review. Indeed, the number
n_{\rm A}
of axions present in the low-energy theory can easily be of order hundreds, or even thousands. Models with
n_{\rm A}\gg 1
axions therefore exhibit a very rich phenomenology and are commonly referred to as the axiverse
[
8
]
; see
[
96
]
for a recent review.

Before incorporating effects associated with a UV quantum gravity completion, it is useful to analyze axiverse models as low-energy effective field theories in their own right. A natural first step is to identify their structural properties and, in this respect, global symmetries provide particularly valuable information, as they strongly constrain the dynamics. Of course, global symmetries are expected to be broken, and hence can at most be regarded as approximate, once the theory is embedded into quantum gravity
[
100
,
60
,
10
,
59
]
; see also
[

,

. Within the Swampland program

,

, this expectation is usually formulated as the “No Global Symmetry Conjecture”. Nevertheless, the emergence of global symmetries in suitable limits can provide a useful organizing principle for understanding the dynamics in those regimes, as well as the possible symmetry-breaking mechanisms.

A particularly powerful framework for this analysis is the generalized notion of symmetry introduced in
[
53

Among the most important examples are non-invertible, or categorical, symmetries, generated by topological defects that do not possess inverses under fusion. These symmetries have been intensely studied in recent years; for reviews and further references, see

,

The goal of this paper is two-fold. First, we determine the maximal global symmetry structure of general four-dimensional axiverse models with no supersymmetry or minimal supersymmetry, initially leaving aside quantum gravity constraints. We focus in particular on continuous invertible symmetries and on quasi-continuous non-invertible ones, whose parameters can take arbitrarily dense rational values, since these impose the strongest constraints. In this context, quasi-continuous axion shift symmetries are especially important: even when ordinary invertible axion shift symmetries are absent, they can forbid the generation of a non-trivial axion potential and account for its natural smallness, as in
[
34
,
25
]
.

Our second goal is to begin a systematic analysis of quantum-gravitational symmetry breaking mechanisms. In particular, we study the consequences for axiverse models of the recent perspective on axion wormholes proposed in
[
39
,
90
]
and of the corresponding
Imaginary Distance Bound
.

We address the first problem by considering general axiverse theories containing an arbitrary number of axions together with an arbitrary number of abelian U(1) gauge fields. We also include Gauss-Bonnet and Pontryagin curvature-squared couplings, and comment on the possible effects of non-abelian gauge sectors and additional matter sectors. Our results build on and extend earlier work on the axion-Maxwell theory
[
24
,
34
,
25
,
130
]
, making use of the framework developed for more general Gaillard–Zumino models in
[
5
]
; see also
[
72
]
for related work. We will provide a general construction of the relevant non-invertible topological operators. As in
[
25
]
, see also
[
19
,
117
]
, this construction makes manifest their interplay with invertible higher-form symmetries and leads to hierarchies among the corresponding symmetry breaking scales.

As mentioned above, our discussion of symmetry-breaking effects induced by quantum gravity will significantly rely on the novel viewpoint on wormholes proposed in
[
39
,
90
]
. In the axiverse theories considered here, both invertible and non-invertible axion shift symmetries are tied to the presence of axion wormholes connecting two asymptotically flat regions, of the kind first constructed in
[
55
]
. Such configurations may be reinterpreted as wormhole saddles with imaginary axion profiles, interpolating between imaginary Dirichlet boundary conditions imposed at the two asymptotic ends. The same interpretation extends to more general wormhole solutions
[
7
]
; in what follows we will refer to this type of configurations as
imaginary wormholes
. The papers
[
39
,
90
]
, using complementary arguments, propose that the appearance of these saddles signals a breakdown of the original effective description and therefore points to the need for its modification, which in particular breaks the axion shift symmetries. More precisely, the total imaginary distance between the asymptotic wormhole boundary conditions sets the Imaginary Distance Bound (IDB), below which the analytic continuation of the symmetry-breaking corrections must become relevant.

In this paper we assume the validity of the viewpoint proposed in
[
39
,
90
]
and explore its consequences for our axiverse models. As we will see, the strongest results can be obtained within the
{\cal N}=1
axiverse framework introduced in
[

85
,
86
]
, which captures broad classes of string compactifications. In particular, a distinguished role will be played by a special class of BPS fundamental instantons, the
EFT instantons
as defined in
[

,
94

, in realizing the IDB.

Axiverse models

In this section we will describe the axiverse effective field theories (EFTs) on which we will base our discussion. We will first consider non-supersymmetric models, and later describe the relevant features of their minimal supersymmetric extensions. In this section, we will not include any effective potential, or superpotential, for the axion sector. As we will see in Section
3
, this requirement can be rephrased in terms of the existence of generically non-invertible axion shift symmetries.

2.1
Basic models

Our models include
n_{\rm A}
axions
a^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n_{\rm A}
, with integral peridicity

a^{i}\simeq a^{i}+1\,.

(2.1)

The “angular” variables

often used to denote axions are related to our fields via
\vartheta^{i}=2\pi a^{i}
. Furthermore, we also include
n_{\rm V}

F^{I}={\rm d}A^{I}

\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint F^{I}\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.2)

Our ‘minimal’ axiverse EFT takes the form

-\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\int{\cal G}_{ij}\,{\rm d}a^{i}\wedge*{\rm d}a^{j}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int f_{IJ}F^{I}\wedge*F^{J}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\int a^{i}\,F^{I}\wedge F^{J}\,.

(2.3)

Here
M
is a reference mass scale, so that the axion kinetic matrix
{\cal G}_{ij}
is dimensionless. In a gravitational context, we will identify it with the Planck mass:

M=M_{\text{\tiny P}}
.
The eigenvalues of
M^{2}{\cal G}_{ij}
represent the squared decay constants of the canonically normalized axions. Similarly, the dimensionless kinetic matrix
f_{IJ}
determines the U(1) gauge couplings. In the non supersymmetric models discussed in this subsection,
f_{IJ}
and
{\cal G}_{ij}
are constant, while in the supersymmetric models of Section
2.2

2.3

2.1

\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}=\mathsf{K}_{iJI}\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.4)

Non-abelian gauge sectors, such as QCD or GUT sectors, could be added to (
2.3

a^{i}
through theta-like terms, they generically have important effects. These effects can be straightforwardly taken into account at a later stage. Hence, to avoid overloading the presentation, we will mostly assume the absence of non-abelian sectors, only occasionally commenting on their effects, and relegating more details to the Appendix.

We also implicitly allow for the inclusion of other sectors, as long as they only introduce derivative axion interactions. In particular, one can add matter charged under the abelian (and non-abelian) gauge groups. This will only be relevant for the discussions of Section
3.3

2.2
Supersymmetric extension

We will also consider minimally supersymmetric extensions of the axiverse models introduced in Section
2.1
, which we now describe. These extensions will not play any role until Section
4
. Thus, some readers may prefer to first read Section
3

In supersymmetric models the axions
a^{i}
combine with corresponding
saxions

s^{i}
into complex fields

t^{i}\equiv a^{i}+{\rm i}s^{i}\,,

(2.15)

which are the lowest components of chiral multiplets. The spectrum also contains the supersymmetric fermionic partners, which, however, will be irrelevant for most of our purposes and will be explicitly taken into account only in Section
6.4
.

The kinetic terms of the (s)axionic sector is determined by a Kähler potential
K
, which will be assumed to be invariant under axion shift symmetries, namely to depend on

and

{\cal G}_{ij}(s)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial s^{i}\partial s^{j}}\ .

(2.16)

The same matrix also determines the saxionic kinetic terms

The gauge field kinetic terms in (

saxionic domain

K=-\log P({\bm{s}})\,,

(2.20)

with
P({\bm{s}})

P(\lambda{\bm{s}})=\lambda^{k}P({\bm{s}})\,.

(2.21)

The integrality of

is just an assumption motivated by experience from string theory, with no clear bottom-up motivation. We emphasize that the Kähler potential (
2.20

s^{i}\rightarrow\lambda s^{i}
with
\lambda\rightarrow\infty

Note that this structure naturally leads one to consider conical saxionic domains, namely domains that are preserved under arbitrary rescalings
s^{i}\rightarrow\lambda s^{i}
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\Delta
and
refer to it as the

85

86

is convex and rational polyhedral. A prototypical example of

2.3
for more explicit examples and to Figure
1
for a visual illustration.
In order to define more explicitly a saxionic cone satisfying these conditions, it is convenient to adopt a basis independent notation, in which we introduce an
n_{\rm A}
-dimensional lattice
V_{\mathbb{Z}}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n_{\rm A}}
, which can be considered as the integral subset of a corresponding real vector space
V_{\mathbb{R}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm A}}
. For instance, the saxions
s^{i}
can be identified with the components of the vector
{\bm{s}}=\{s^{i}\}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}
, while the axions
a^{i}\simeq a^{i}+1
are the components of an element
{\bm{a}}=\{a^{i}\}
of
V_{\mathbb{R}}/V_{\mathbb{Z}}

\Delta\subset V_{\mathbb{R}}
is convex if
{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\in\Delta
implies that
{\bm{s}}+{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\in\Delta
, and is rational polyhedral if it is generated by a set of integral vectors
{\bf e}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}

Alternatively, it satisfies these properties if

\Delta=\{{\bm{s}}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}\ |\ \langle{\bf q},{\bm{s}}\rangle>0,\forall{\bf q}\in{\cal C}_{\rm I}\}

(2.22)

where

\langle{\bf q},{\bm{s}}\rangle\equiv q_{i}s^{i}\,.

(2.23)

Notice that the positivity of the gauge couplings (

\mathsf{K}_{IJi}s^{i}
is positive definite for any

It is clear that
{\cal C}_{\rm I}

{\cal C}_{\rm I}=\Delta^{\vee}\cap V^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

s^{i}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial{\cal F}}{\partial\ell_{i}}\,,

and the inverse saxionic metric,

{\cal G}^{ij}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal F}}{\partial\ell_{i}\partial\ell_{j}}\,.

(2.32)

The general relation (
2.30
), that holds for general Kähler potential that are invariant under axion shift symmetries, simplifies by assuming the homogeneity condition (
2.21
). Indeed, (
2.29
) implies that, omitting an irrelevant additional constant, the dual saxion kinetic potential (
2.30
) takes the form

{\cal F}({\bm{\ell}})=\log\tilde{P}({\bm{\ell}})\,,

(2.33)

where
\tilde{P}({\bm{\ell}})\equiv 1/P({\bm{s}}({\bm{\ell}}))
.
Notice that
\tilde{P}({\bm{\ell}})
is homogeneous too:

\tilde{P}(\lambda{{\bm{\ell}}})=\lambda^{k}\tilde{P}({{\bm{\ell}}})\,.

(2.34)

We emphazise that, given such a theory with homogeneity
k
, one may consider (s)axionic subsectors with homogeneity
k^{\prime}<k
. One can identify these subsectors by considering limits in which some subset of saxions become much larger than the other, or some subset of dual saxions become much smaller than the others. If this limit gives a consistent decoupling of the former set from the latter, we can use it to describe a theory with reduced
k
. In the following Subsection we will provide some examples of how this can work.

2.3
Simple examples

It is useful to discuss possible concrete realizations of the general class of models introduced in Section
2.2
, as well as their possible string theory interpretation. We will be brief here; more details and other examples within the same framework can be found in
[
85
,
95
,
94
]
.

The simplest example contains just one (s)axion
s^{0}
, with saxionic cone
\Delta=\{s^{0}>0\}
. In this case we necessarily have
K=-k\log s^{0}
, that is
P(s)=(s^{0})^{k}
. The dual saxion is then given by
\ell_{0}=\frac{k}{2s^{0}}
,
{\cal P}=\{\ell_{0}>0\}
and the dual kinetic potential is
{\cal F}(\ell_{0})=k\log\ell_{0}
, that is
\tilde{P}(\ell_{0})=(\ell_{0})^{k}
. For instance, the Kähler potential of heterotic compactifications on Calabi-Yau three-folds at weak string coupling contains such a contribution with
k=1
, where
\ell_{0}
parametrizes the four-dimensional dilaton.

In the large volume regime, one can add
h^{1,1}
saxions
s^{a}
, which are the kähler modoli that parameterize the (string frame) Kähler cone of the Calabi-Yau. These combine with the saxion
s^{0}
introduced above to parametrize an
n_{\rm A}=h^{1,1}+1
dimensional saxionic cone
\Delta
. This can be represented as a fibration where the fiber is a one-dimensional cone parametrized by
s^{0}

K=-\log(s^{0}-\frac{1}{2}p_{a}s^{a})-\log\left(\kappa_{abc}s^{a}s^{b}s^{c}\right)\,,

(2.35)

where

Hence, we have enlarged the homogeneity from

to

s^{0}

s^{0}>p_{a}s^{a}
generically obstructs taking a large (string-frame) volume limit in which all the saxions
s^{a}
become much larger than
s^{0}
. Therefore, one cannot simply focus on the

s^{0}
fixed.

3
Non-invertible symmetries in the axiverse

In this section, we discuss the invertible and non-invertible global symmetries, together with the corresponding topological defects and charged operators, of the axiverse models described in Section
2
. We follow a ‘descending’ order, starting with the two-form symmetries and then turning to the one- and zero-form symmetries. This organization allows us to begin with the invertible symmetries and helps clarify the relations among the various defects. Supersymmetry plays no role in the present discussion. The results presented here can be regarded as an axiverse generalization of previous analyses of the axion-Maxwell model
[
24
,
34
,
25
,
130
,
37
]
, see also
[
69
,
70
,
71
]
, and
[
72
]
for previous work considering axiverse settings. For the invertible symmetries discussed in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, the generalization is essentially straightforward. By contrast, the axiverse generalization of the non-invertible symmetries discussed in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
is less obvious, and builds on and extends the techniques introduced in
[
94
]
to describe the non-invertible symmetries of more general Gaillard-Zumino models
[

.

3.1
Winding two-form symmetries and vortex operators

We start our discussion of the global axiverse symmetries by considering the
winding
two-form symmetries.
They are associated with the one-form conserved currents

{\cal J}_{(\rm w)}^{i}=2\pi{\rm d}a^{i}\,.

(3.1)

In our notation the Noether current of a continuous
p
-form symmetry is given by a closed
(3-p)
-form
{\cal J}
– such that
{\rm d}{\cal J}=0
– rather than by its Hodge dual
(p+1)
-form. The winding two-form symmetry is realized by codimension-three topological operator

{\cal D}{}^{(\rm w)}_{\bm{\beta}}(\gamma)=\exp\left({\rm i}\beta_{i}\oint_{\gamma}{\cal J}_{(\rm w)}^{i}\right)\,

(3.2)

which is supported on one-dimensional curves
\gamma
. The periodic parameters
\beta_{i}\simeq\beta_{i}+1
label the corresponding U(1)
{}^{n_{\rm A}}
element.

The winding topological operator (
3.2
) acts on codimension-two extended operators, supported on two-dimensional surfaces
\Sigma
. These operators can be defined by excising a small tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
and imposing that the axions undergo an integral shift

a^{i}\rightarrow a^{i}+e^{i}\quad,\quad e^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(3.3)

around them. We will denote these operators as
{\cal V}_{\bf e}(\Sigma)
, where the charge vector
{\bf e}=\{e^{i}\}
takes values in the lattice
V_{\mathbb{Z}}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n_{\rm A}}
, and we will refer to them as
vortex
operator. The axion winding (
3.3
) condition implies that the insertion of
{\cal V}_{\bf e}(\Sigma)
modifies the conservation equation
{\rm d}{\cal J}^{i}_{\rm(w)}=0
into

{\rm d}{\cal J}^{i}_{\rm(w)}=2\pi e^{i}\delta_{2}(\Sigma)\,,

(3.4)

which encodes the contact term contribution appearing in Ward identities for the winding two-form symmetry.
Hence, if
\gamma\simeq S^{1}
is a circle that links
\Sigma
, we get

{\cal D}{}^{(\rm w)}_{\bm{\beta}}(\gamma){\cal V}_{\bf e}(\Sigma)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle}{\cal V}_{\bf e}(\Sigma)\,,

(3.5)

where we have introduced the index-free notation for the pairing
\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle\equiv\beta_{i}e^{i}
between
{\bf e}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset V_{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}=\{\beta_{i}\}\in V^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}/V^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
V^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}
denoting the dual of
V_{\mathbb{R}}
, and
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
V^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are their integral sublattice.

Importantly, as emphasized e.g. in
[
25
]
, the surface operator
{\cal V}_{\bf e}(\Sigma)
must support a non-trivial world-sheet sector. This is because of the anomaly inflow mechanism
[
21
,
102
]
– see also
[
49
,
46
,
66
,
95
]
. The sum of the last term
in (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) can be rewritten as

-\int{\cal J}^{i}_{(\rm w)}\wedge I^{(0)}_{3,i}

(3.6)

where
I^{(0)}_{3,i}
are the Chern-Simons three-form defined in (
2.14
).
These are not invariant under infinitesimal gauge and local Lorentz transformation:

\delta I^{(0)}_{3,i}={\rm d}I^{(1)}_{2,i}\,.

(3.7)

Hence, (
3.4
) produces the following anomalous contribution localized on
\Sigma
:

\delta S_{\rm bulk}=-2\pi e^{i}\oint_{\Sigma}I^{(1)}_{2,i}\,,

(3.8)

This can alternatively be understood in the dual formulation introduced at the end of Section
2.1
, in which the axion vortex supports the coupling
\exp({\rm i}e^{i}\int_{\Sigma}{\cal B}_{2,i})
. Then (
3.8
) follows from (
2.13
) and the gauge invariance of
{\cal H}_{3,i}
.

The anomaly (
3.8
) must be canceled by a world-sheet anomaly.
7
7

7

In a QFT setup in which gravity is not dynamical, we could allow for a non-vanishing ’t Hooft gravitational anomaly, captured by the last term in (
2.14
). However, having in mind the eventual coupling to a dynamical gravity, for later convenience we keep this condition as one of the defining properties of
{\cal V}_{\bf e}
.
Hence,
{\cal V}_{\bf e}
must support a non-trivial world-sheet sector including chiral fermions or bosons, whose anomaly polynomial under bulk gauge and local Lorentz transformations is precisely given by
e^{i}I_{4,i}
. See
[
95
, Section 3.2]
for more details in a very similar setting – though considering dynamical axion strings rather than codimension-two operators – including also the contribution of non-abelian gauge sectors as in Footnote
3
. Note that, for any anomaly polynomial
e^{i}I_{4,i}
, the choice of the worldsheet degrees of freedom is not unique. However, we will not need to specify them and we will use
{\cal V}_{\bf e}
to denote any such operator.

3.2
Magnetic one-form symmetries and ’t Hooft lines

The magnetic one-form symmetries of our axiverse model are generated by the two-form currents
F^{I}
. The corresponding U(1)
{}^{n_{\rm V}}
one-form symmetries are realized by the invertible topological operator

{\cal D}^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}(\Sigma)=\exp\left({\rm i}\beta_{I}\oint_{\Sigma}F^{I}\right)\,,

(3.9)

and labeled by the periodic parameters
\beta_{I}\simeq\beta_{I}+1
. Associated charged operators are provided by ‘t Hooft lines
H_{\bf m}(\gamma)
carrying magnetic charges
m^{I}
, whose insertion modifies the Bianchi identity
{\rm d}F^{I}=0
into

{\rm d}F^{I}=2\pi m^{I}\delta_{3}(\gamma)\,.

(3.10)

The ’t Hooft lines can be defined by excising a small tubular neighborhood of
\gamma
and imposing that

\oint_{S^{2}}F^{I}=2\pi m^{I}\,,

(3.11)

along it.
So, if we pick a two-sphere
\Sigma\simeq S^{2}
linking
\gamma
we have

{\cal D}^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}(\Sigma)H_{\bf m}(\gamma)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf m}\rangle}H_{\bf m}(\gamma)\,.

(3.12)

Here we are again using and index free notation for the canonical pairing
\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf m}\rangle\equiv\beta_{I}m^{I}
between
{\bf m}=\{m^{I}\}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}=\{\beta_{I}\}\in W^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}/W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
W_{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the lattice of magnetic charges,
W_{\mathbb{R}}
the corresponding real vector space, and
W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
W^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}
the associated duals.

Analogously to what happens for the vortex operators in Section
3.1
, and what discussed in
[
49
,
46
,
25
]
for the axion-Maxwell model, ’t Hooft lines must support a non-trivial world-line sector. This basically comes from the Witten effect
[
127
]
, namely the fact that monopoles acquire electric charge under a
2\pi
shift of the theta-angle, and can also be understood as an anomaly inflow
[
30
,
31
,
49
]
.
In our framework, this inflow produces an anomalous behavior under integral axion shifts
a^{i}\rightarrow a^{i}+k^{i}
,
k^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. One way to see it is to compute from (
2.3
) the dual field strengths (
2.3
)

G_{I}=-f_{IJ}*F^{J}-\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}a^{i}F^{J}\,.

(3.13)

An ’t Hooft line
H_{\bf m}(\gamma)
should contain the factor
\exp({\rm i}m^{I}\oint_{\gamma}\tilde{A}_{I})
, where
\tilde{A}_{I}
are the dual magnetic potentials, such that
{\rm d}\tilde{A}_{I}=G_{I}
. However, from (
3.13
) it is clear that the field strengths
G_{I}
, and hence the magnetic potentials
\tilde{A}_{I}
, are not invariant under integral axion shifts.
More precisely,

\tilde{A}_{I}\quad\rightarrow\quad\tilde{A}_{I}-k^{i}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}A^{J}

(3.14)

under
a^{i}\rightarrow a^{i}+k^{i}
, and hence

\exp\left({\rm i}m^{I}\tilde{A}_{I}\right)
acquires an anomalous phase

\exp\left(-{\rm i}m^{I}k^{i}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\oint_{\gamma}A^{J}\right)\,,

(3.15)

which must be canceled by a non-trivial world-line sector. The choice of this sector is not unique, but a simple realization
[
75
]
is obtained by introducing a set of periodic world-line axions
\sigma_{i}\simeq\sigma_{i}+2\pi
, providing a many-body generalization of the particle-on-a-circle quantum mechanics
[
30
,
52
]
. These world-line axions
couple to the bulk fields through the world-line partition function

\int[D\sigma]_{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{{\rm i}}{2}h^{ij}\int_{\gamma}D_{A}\sigma_{i}\wedge*_{\gamma}D_{A}\sigma_{j}-{\rm i}\oint_{\gamma}a^{i}D_{A}\sigma_{i}\right)\ ,

(3.16)

where
h^{ij}
is the world-line kinetic matrix and

D_{A}\sigma_{i}\equiv{\rm d}\sigma_{i}-m^{I}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}A^{J}\,.

(3.17)

Gauge invariance requires that
\sigma_{i}\rightarrow\sigma_{i}+m^{I}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\lambda^{J}
under the gauge transformation
A^{I}\rightarrow A^{I}+{\rm d}\lambda^{I}
. It is clear that, under the bulk axion shift
a^{i}\rightarrow a^{i}+k^{i}
, the second term in (
3.16
) produces an anomalous phase that precisely cancels (
3.15
). We emphasise that the choice (
3.16
) is not the only possible one. Furthermore, it is not even uniquely defined by itself, since
h^{ij}
is arbitrary.

3.3
Non-invertible electric one-form symmetries

We now turn to the non-invertible symmetries present in our class of models. The results of the previous two subsections remain valid in the presence of fields charged under the U(1) gauge fields. Throughout this subsection, we assume that electrically charged particles are absent, since their presence would partially or completely break the electric one-form symmetries; see Section
4.3
.

Let us forget for a moment that the axions
a^{i}
are periodic. In this case, we can say that two-form electric currents can be identified with the dual field strengths
G_{I}
defined in (
3.13
). However, as already emphasized in Section
3.2
, the
G_{I}
are not invariant under the axion periodicity and therefore cannot be used as gauge-invariant two-form currents.
As we are going to discuss, one can generalize the construction presented in
[
25
]
for axion–Maxwell theory to show that most of the electric one-form symmetries are not lost: rather, a dense subset of them survives, albeit in a non-invertible form.

More precisely, one can construct a topological defect
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
for each
{\bm{\alpha}}=\{\alpha^{I}\}\in W_{\mathbb{Q}}/W_{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
W_{\mathbb{Z}}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n_{\rm V}}
is the lattice of magnetic monopole charges, and
W_{\mathbb{Q}}
is its
\mathbb{Q}
-span. In other words,
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
is labeled by
\alpha^{I}\in\mathbb{Q}
, with
\alpha^{I}\simeq\alpha^{I}+1
. We will first define
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
for any
{\bm{\alpha}}\in W_{\mathbb{Q}}/W_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and then argue that it is topological.

In order to define
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
, let us first introduce the
n_{\rm A}\times n_{\rm V}
matrix
\mathsf{Q}({\bm{\alpha}})
of components

\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\equiv\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\alpha^{J}\,.

(3.18)

Since
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\in\mathbb{Z}
(see (
2.4
)), it is clear that
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Q}
for any
{\bm{\alpha}}\in W_{\mathbb{Q}}
(and
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
for any
{\bm{\alpha}}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}
). As explained in Appendix
A
, one can then always pick a
right-coprime
factorization

\mathsf{Q}({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathsf{M}\mathsf{L}^{-1}\,,

(3.19)

in terms of integral matrices

\mathsf{M}=\{\mathsf{M}_{iI}\}\in{\rm Mat}(n_{\rm A},n_{\rm V},\mathbb{Z})\quad,\quad\mathsf{L}=\{\mathsf{L}^{I}{}_{J}\}\in{\rm Mat}(n_{\rm V},\mathbb{Z})\,.

(3.20)

In the same appendix, there is also an algoritm to construct such factorization. In components, (
3.19
) reads
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathsf{M}_{iJ}(\mathsf{L}^{-1})^{J}{}_{I}
, and we keep implicit the dependence of
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{L}
on
{\bm{\alpha}}
. As discussed in Appendix
A
, being right-coprime,
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{L}
are unique, up to an unimodular transformation

\mathsf{M}\rightarrow\mathsf{M}\mathsf{T}\quad,\quad\mathsf{L}\rightarrow\mathsf{L}\mathsf{T}\,,\quad\text{with $\mathsf{T}\in{\rm GL}(n_{\rm V},\mathbb{Z})$}\,.

(3.21)

Notice that, in particular, if
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
, then we can just pick
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{Q}({\bm{\alpha}})
and
\mathsf{L}=\mathds{1}
.

The defect
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
can then be defined as follows,

{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)\equiv e^{{\rm i}\alpha^{I}\oint_{\Sigma}{\cal J}_{I}^{\rm(e)}}\int[{\cal D}\phi{\cal D}c]_{\Sigma}\exp\left[{\rm i}\oint_{\Sigma}\left(\mathsf{L}^{I}{}_{J}\phi_{I}{\rm d}c^{J}+\mathsf{M}_{iI}a^{i}{\rm d}c^{I}+\phi_{I}F^{I}\right)\right]\,,

(3.22)

where

{\cal J}_{I}^{\rm(e)}\equiv-f_{IJ}*F^{J}=G_{I}+\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}a^{i}F^{J}\,,

(3.23)

and
we have introduced
n_{\rm V}
compact scalar fields
\phi_{I}\simeq\phi_{I}+1
and
n_{\rm V}
U(1) one-form vector fields
c^{I}
, all living on
\Sigma
. Notice that the two-form current
{\cal J}_{I}^{\rm(e)}
is gauge invariant but not conserved:

{\rm d}{\cal J}_{I}^{\rm(e)}=\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}{\rm d}a^{i}\wedge F^{J}\,.

(3.24)

The analogous defect constructed in
[
25
]
for the axion-Maxwell model can be regarded as the
n_{\rm V}=n_{\rm A}=1
subcase of (
3.22
).

It is clear that (
3.22
) is well defined, since all the coefficients appearing in the path-integrated exponential are properly quantized.
As in
[
25
]
, a simple heuristic way to check that (
3.22
) is topological and provides a realization of the electric one-form symmetry is to try to integrate out the world-sheet fields
\phi_{I}
and
c^{I}
. The corresponding equations of motion
\mathsf{L}^{I}{}_{J}{\rm d}c^{J}+F^{I}|_{\Sigma}=0
and
\mathsf{L}^{J}{}_{I}{\rm d}\phi_{J}+\mathsf{M}_{iI}{\rm d}a^{i}|_{\Sigma}=0
can be ‘naively’ solved by setting
{\rm d}c^{I}=-(\mathsf{L}^{-1})^{I}{}_{J}F^{J}|_{\Sigma}
and
\phi_{I}=-\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})a^{i}|_{\Sigma}
. This solution is naive because it does not respect the appropriate quantization conditions of the world-sheet fields. But by plugging it into (
3.22
) and taking (
3.23
) into account, it produces precisely the naive topological operator
\exp\left({\rm i}\alpha^{I}\oint_{\Sigma}G_{I}\right)
, supporting the idea that (
3.22
) does exactly what we need for our purposes. This conclusion can be confirmed by a more conceptual derivation of (
3.22
) from a half higher gauging
[
36
,
78
,
25
]
, carried out in Appendix
B
, along the lines of what was discussed in
[
25
]
for the axion-Maxwell model.

For our purposes, it is sufficient to mention that one must perform a half higher gauging with respect to a discrete subgroup of U(1)
{}^{n_{\rm V}}\times
U(1)
{}^{n_{\rm A}}
. Insight into the structure of this subgroup is provided by studying the fusion
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}\times\overline{{\cal D}}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, which also highlights the non-invertible nature of this symmetry operator. This fusion is computed in Appendix
B.1
and here we just quote the result

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}\times\overline{{\cal D}}^{\rm(e)}_{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{[{\bm{\eta}}]\in H^{1}(\Sigma,\Gamma_{\mathsf{L}})}\exp\left\{{\rm i}\mathsf{Q}_{iI}\oint_{\Sigma}\eta^{I}\cup{\cal J}_{(\rm w)}^{i}\right\}\sum_{[\tilde{\bm{\eta}}]\in H^{0}(\Sigma,\Gamma_{\mathsf{L}}^{*})}\,\exp\left\{{\rm i}\,(\mathsf{L}^{-1})^{I}{}_{J}\oint_{\Sigma}\tilde{\eta}_{I}F^{J}\right\}\,,

(3.25)

where for simplicity we have omitted the overall normalization factor.
In (
3.25
) we have introduced the finite subgroups
\Gamma_{\mathsf{L}}\equiv W_{\mathbb{Z}}/\mathsf{L}W_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\Gamma^{*}_{\mathsf{L}}\equiv W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}/\mathsf{L}^{\rm t}W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. So, for instance, an element
[\tilde{\bm{\eta}}]\in\Gamma^{*}_{\mathsf{L}}
corresponds to an integral vector
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}
, of components
\tilde{\eta}_{I}
, modulo the identification
\tilde{\eta}_{I}\simeq\tilde{\eta}_{I}+k_{J}\mathsf{L}^{J}{}_{I}
for any
k_{I}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

It is clear that (
3.25
) can be regarded as a condensation defect
[
113
]
involving a finite number of winding two-form and magnetic one-form symmetries.
The fact that the fusion (
3.25
) does not yield the identity reflects the non-invertible nature of (
3.22
). We also remark that the operator (
3.23
) does not depend on the specific coprime factorization we choose. Indeed, as already emphasized, different coprime factorizations are related by unimodular transformations (
3.21
), which can be reabsorbed by the field redefinition
c^{I}\rightarrow(\mathsf{T}^{-1})^{I}_{\;J}c^{J}
. Furthermore, one can explicitly check that (
3.22
) is invariant under
{\bm{\alpha}}\rightarrow{{\bm{\alpha}}+{\bf m}}={{\bm{\alpha}}}
for any
{\bf m}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}
, consistently with the fact that it should depend only on
\alpha^{i}
mod
\mathbb{Z}
; see Appendix
C
for details. Once again, it is crucial that (
3.19
) is right-coprime.

We also observe that, if more general
\alpha^{I}\in\mathbb{Q}
are such that
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
, one can again set
\mathsf{M}_{iJ}=\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\alpha^{J}
and
\mathsf{L}^{I}{}_{J}=\delta^{I}{}_{J}
, and exactly integrate out
\phi_{I}
as in the previous paragraph. This means that in these cases (
3.22
) reduces to the invertible operator

\exp\left({\rm i}\oint_{\Sigma}\left[\alpha^{I}{\cal J}_{I}^{\rm(e)}-\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})a^{i}F^{I}\right]\right)=\exp\left({\rm i}\alpha^{I}\oint_{\Sigma}G_{I}\right)

(3.26)

which is well defined precisely because
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
, and non-trivial if
{\bm{\alpha}}\notin W_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
8
8

8

In fact, the operator (
3.26
) is well defined for any set of real parameters
\alpha^{I}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathsf{Q}_{iI}({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\alpha^{J}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This condition selects the group of invertible electric one-form symmetries, which of course strongly depends on
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}
. As a trivial example, if
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\equiv 0
, then we have a U
(1)^{n_{\rm V}}
group of invertible one-form symmetries, and no non-invertible ones. As another simple case, suppose that
n_{\rm A}=1
and
\mathsf{K}_{IJ}\equiv\mathsf{K}_{1IJ}
is non-degenerate. Then the invertible electric one-form symmetries form the finite subgroup
\mathsf{K}^{-1}W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}/W_{\mathbb{Z}}
. More general cases can be analyzed similarly.

Finally, let us discuss the action of the topological defect (
3.22
) on various operators. First of all, it acts in an invertible way on Wilson lines

W_{\bf n}(\gamma)\equiv\exp\left({\rm i}\,n_{I}\oint_{\gamma}A^{I}\right)\,,

(3.27)

by measuring their electric charge. Namely, if we pick a two-sphere
\Sigma\simeq S^{2}
linking
\gamma
, we have

{\cal D}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)W_{\bf n}(\gamma)=e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf n},{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle}W_{\bf n}(\gamma)\,.

(3.28)

This comes just from the exponential in (
3.22
) containing
\oint_{\Sigma}{\cal J}^{\rm(e)}_{I}
, which acts on Wilson lines like
\oint_{\Sigma}G_{I}
. The action on local operators is clearly trivial, since a local operator cannot be linked with a codimension-two defect
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\Sigma)
. By contrast, the action on ’t Hooft lines and vortex operators is more intricate, as already happens in the simpler axion-Maxwell case
[
25
]
. We will not discuss the details of this non-trivial action in the present work.

3.4
Non-invertible axion shift symmetries

We finally turn to the non-invertible zero-form symmetries associated with arbitrary rational shifts of the axions. This kind of symmetries were previously studied in the context of the axion-Maxwell model
[
24
,
34
,
25
,
130
]
, see also
[
110
,
28
,
37
]
, and for the more general Gaillard-Zumino models in
[
5
]
. As in the previous paragraph of this section, we will focus only on the defect operator and its properties, postponing the discussion of physical implications to Sections
4
-
6
.

In the absence of the axion-gauge couplings in (
2.3
) and axion-Pontryagin couplings (
2.7
), the action is invariant under a real shift of the axion,
{\bm{a}}\rightarrow{\bm{a}}\,+{\bm{\alpha}}
for
{\bm{\alpha}}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}
. The associated conserved and gauge invariant currents are

{\cal J}^{\rm(a)}_{i}=-M^{2}{\cal G}_{ij}*{\rm d}a^{j}={\cal H}_{3,i}\,.

(3.29)

where in the second equation we use the dual formulation, cf. (
2.9
). However, non-vanishing
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}
in (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) break the closure relation (
3.29
) and hence, at first glance, appear to break the continuous axion shift symmetries. As we will now explain, a dense subgroup of
rational
axion shift symmetries nevertheless survives, albeit in a non-invertible form.

For the time being, let us focus on the coupling between the axions and the abelian gauge sector by setting
\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}=0
. We will return to the axion-Pontryagin coupling later. Then the exterior derivative of (
3.29
)

{\rm d}{\cal J}^{\rm(a)}_{i}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\,F^{I}\wedge F^{J}\,.

(3.30)

Our aim is to identify a codimension-one topological operator
{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)
, that implements the axion shift
{\bm{a}}\rightarrow{\bm{a}}\,+{\bm{\alpha}}
for any
{\bm{\alpha}}=\{\alpha^{i}\}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}/V_{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is for any
\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
\alpha^{i}\simeq\alpha^{i}+1
.
In order to define this operator, let us first introduce the
n_{\rm V}\times n_{\rm V}
matrix
\mathsf{C}({\bm{\alpha}})=\{\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\}
of components

\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\equiv-\alpha^{i}\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\,.

(3.31)

Since
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the matrix
\mathsf{C}({\bm{\alpha}})
has rational entries, i.e.
\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Q}
, for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We can therefore directly apply the construction of the
\mathsf{C}
-defect developed in
[
5
]
, which in turn generalizes the strategy originally proposed in
[
24
,
34
]
.

First of all, we have to pick a
right-coprime
factorization (see Appendix
A
for definition and properties of these factorizations as well as an algoritm to construct them):

\mathsf{C}({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}

(3.32)

in terms of integral matrices
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{N}
, as was done for the electric one-form symmetries discussed above. In this case,
\mathsf{P}=\{\mathsf{P}_{IJ}\}
is also a square matrix. Obviously,
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{N}
depend on
{\bm{\alpha}}
, but we keep this dependence implicit.
As explained in
[
5
]
, given the right-coprime factorization
(
3.32
), on any oriented three-dimensional submanifold
\Sigma
one can
define a corresponding
minimal

[
73
]
topological QFT (TQFT)
with
\Gamma^{(1)}_{\mathsf{N}}
one-form symmetry, where

\Gamma_{\mathsf{N}}\equiv W_{\mathbb{Z}}/\mathsf{N}W_{\mathbb{Z}}
(cf. comments below
(
3.25
)), and with corresponding anomaly fixed by
\mathsf{P}
.
One can couple this theory to

(\mathsf{N}^{-1}F)^{I}\equiv(\mathsf{N}^{-1})^{I}{}_{J}F^{J}
, regarded as a background flat
connection for the
\Gamma^{(1)}_{\mathsf{N}}
one-form symmetry.
We will denote the resulting partition function by
{\cal A}^{(\mathsf{N},\mathsf{P})}_{\Sigma}[\mathsf{N}^{-1}F]
.

If
{\bm{\alpha}}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}
were such that
\mathsf{P}=\mathds{1}
, then
{\cal A}^{(\mathsf{N},\mathds{1})}_{\Sigma}[\mathsf{N}^{-1}F]
would simply be the partition function of the three-dimensional Chern–Simons theory

\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathsf{N}^{IJ}\oint_{\Sigma}a_{I}\wedge f_{J}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\Sigma}a_{I}\wedge F^{I}\,,

(3.33)

where
\mathsf{N}^{IJ}\equiv\mathsf{N}^{I}{}_{K}\delta^{KJ}
and
a_{I}
are world-volume U(1) gauge fields. However, it is not a priori obvious that vectors
{\bm{\alpha}}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}
with this property even exist, and one should generically use more complicated TQFTs, which nevertheless always admit a description in terms of abelian Chern–Simons theories
[
14
]
. Alternatively,
{\cal A}^{(\mathsf{N},\mathsf{P})}_{\Sigma}[\mathsf{N}^{-1}F]
can be identified more directly with the partition function of the four-dimensional TQFT defined on an auxiliary four-dimensional manifold
Y
with boundary
\partial Y=\Sigma
. This theory is described by the action

\frac{1}{4\pi}(\mathsf{N}^{\rm t}\,\mathsf{P})_{IJ}\int_{Y}B^{I}\wedge B^{J}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathsf{N}^{I}_{\;J}\int_{Y}B^{J}\wedge\tilde{F}_{I}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\Sigma}\tilde{A}_{I}\wedge F^{I}\,,

(3.34)

where
\tilde{A}_{I}
and
B^{I}
are dynamical one- and two-form potentials living on Y.

The topological defect realizing a generic rational axion shift symmetry
{\bm{a}}\rightarrow{\bm{a}}\,+{\bm{\alpha}}
for any
{\bm{\alpha}}=\{\alpha^{i}\}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}
can then be obtained by stacking this TQFT on the codimension-one operator
\exp\left({{\rm i}\alpha^{i}\oint_{\Sigma}{\cal J}_{i}^{\rm(a)}}\right)
:

{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)\equiv e^{{\rm i}\alpha^{i}\oint_{\Sigma}{\cal J}_{i}^{\rm(a)}}{\cal A}^{(\mathsf{N},\mathsf{P})}_{\Sigma}[\mathsf{N}^{-1}F]\,.

(3.35)

In the following, we will analyze some simple properties of this defect. For the explicit construction, which shows that the defect is topological, we refer to
[
5
]
.

One first simple but rather important property is the independence of the defect on the specific right-coprime factorization. This can for instance be checked by using (
3.34
). Any two coprime factorizations are related by a unimodular transformation
\mathsf{T}
, as discussed in Appendix
A
, which can always be reabsorbed by the field redefinition
B^{I}\rightarrow(\mathsf{T}^{-1})^{I}_{\;J}B^{J}
. Furthermore, one can check that (
3.35
) is invariant under
{\bm{\alpha}}\rightarrow{\bm{\alpha}}+{\bf m}
for any
{\bf m}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
, consistently with the fact that (
3.35
) depends on
{\bm{\alpha}}
only through its equivalence class in
V_{\mathbb{Q}}/V_{\mathbb{Z}}
– see Appendix
C
.

One can also easily show that if we take
{\bm{\alpha}}
such that
\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
then the defect assumes a simpler form. In particular, we can choose the factorization
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{N}=\mathds{1}
. In this way, we can exactly integrate out the fields in (
3.34
) and find that (
3.35
) reduces to

exp\left({\rm i}\oint_{\Sigma}\left[\alpha^{i}{\cal J}^{\rm(a)}_{i}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\,A^{I}\wedge F^{J}\right]\right)\,.

(3.36)

Notice that the condition
\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})\in\mathbb{Z}
can be satisfied not only for
\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
but also, depending on the form of
\mathsf{K}_{iIJ}
, for more general values
\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
. In the former case the operator is trivial, whereas in the latter the corresponding defect is non-trivial, showing that a non-trivial invertible subgroup of U
(1)^{n_{\rm V}}
survives.

The topological defect realizes the expected axion shift. Since the axions are periodic, they can enter any local operator only through factors of the form
\exp\left[{2\pi{\rm i}\,\langle{\bf q},{\bm{a}}(x)\rangle}\right]
, with
\langle{\bf q},{\bm{a}}\rangle\equiv q_{i}a^{i}
and
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Picking a three-sphere
\Sigma\simeq S^{3}
that surrounds the point
x
, we get

{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\Sigma)e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},{\bm{a}}(x)\rangle}=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{{\bf q},{\bm{\alpha}}}\rangle}e^{2\pi iq_{i}a^{i}(x)}\,.

(3.37)

From this equation it is clear that (
3.35
) acts in an invertible way on the charged local operators
\exp\left[{2\pi{\rm i}\,\langle{\bf q},{\bm{a}}(x)\rangle}\right]
. On the other hands, when one analyzes the action on extended operators, its non-invertible nature shows up. In particular on ’t Hooft lines are mapped to non-genuine line operators, because under the axionic shift the dual field strengths
G_{I}
(see (
3.13
)) are mapped to
G_{I}+\mathsf{C}_{IJ}({\bm{\alpha}})F^{J}
, which are generally not properly quantized to be identified with U(1) field strengths. See
[
5
]
for more details, as well as
[
24
,
34
,
25
,
130
,
37
]
for the analogous phenomenon in the simpler axion-Maxwell model.

The non-invertible nature of (
3.35
) also shows up in the fusion
{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}\times\overline{{\cal D}}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}
.
In the particular case
\mathsf{C}({\bm{\alpha}})
admits the simple factorization with
\mathsf{P}=\mathds{1}
, this fusion gives the condensation defect
[
5
]

\begin{split}{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}\times\overline{{\cal D}}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{{\bm{\eta}}\in H^{0}(\Sigma,\Gamma^{*}_{\mathsf{N}})}&\exp\left[{\rm i}(\mathsf{N}^{-1})^{I}{}_{J}\oint_{\Sigma}\eta_{I}\cup F^{J}+\pi{\rm i}\delta^{IJ}\oint_{\Sigma}\eta_{I}\cup\beta_{J}(\bm{\eta})\right]\,,\end{split}

(3.38)

where
\bm{\beta}
is the Bockstein image of
\bm{\eta}
, and for simplicity we have again omitted the overall normalization. For our purposes, it is important to notice that this condensation defect is non-trivial and involves the magnetic one-form symmetries with parameters in
\mathsf{N}^{-1{\rm t}}\Gamma^{*}_{\mathsf{N}}\equiv W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}/\mathsf{N}^{\rm t}W^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. This shows that the existence of
{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}({\bm{\alpha}})
requires the presence of this subgroup of magnetic one-form symmetries. The same conclusion extends to more general
\mathsf{C}({\bm{\alpha}})
, as can also be seen from the half-space gauging construction of
{\cal D}^{\rm(a)}_{\bm{\alpha}}

[
5
]
, which explicitly makes use of the subgroup
\Gamma^{*}_{\mathsf{N}}
of magnetic one-form symmetries.

Let us now reconsider the possibility of a non-vanishing axion-Pontryagin coupling (
2.7
). (According to
[
95
]
, such a coupling is actually expected to be necessarily present in
{\cal N}=1
quantum gravity theories with a non-trivial gauge sector.) This term modifies the non-conservation equation (
3.30
) by adding the term

-\frac{1}{192\pi}\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\mathop{\rm tr}(R\wedge R)\,,

(3.39)

to its right-hand side. In the presence of a non-flat metric, this appears to render the defect
(
3.35
) non-topological. However, as discussed in
[
110
]
, this type of violation can be canceled by an appropriate modification of (
3.35
), which restores its topological nature. Briefly, one can add to the defect the counterterm

e^{\frac{{\rm i}}{48}\alpha^{i}\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm GCS}(\omega)}\,,

(3.40)

where
{\rm GCS}(\omega)
is the gravitational Chern-Simons three-form, satisfying
{\rm d}{\rm GCS}(\omega)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathop{\rm tr}(R\wedge R)
. This term, together with the minimal TQFT supported on the defect, introduces a framing anomaly
[
128
]
with an overall rational coefficient. This anomaly can then be canceled by stacking the defect with a decoupled TQFT that has an appropriate rational chiral central charge
[
110
]
.

Finally, as discussed more explicitly in Appendix
D
, we note that the inclusion of non-abelian sectors generically removes part of the possible non-invertible axion shift symmetries. In what follows, we will continue to assume that these sectors are either absent, or that we have restricted attention to axions that do not couple to them.

4
Symmetry breaking of higher-form symmetries

The models described in Section
2
should generally be regarded as low-energy effective field theories. Hence, the global symmetries discussed in Section
2
may either uplift to global symmetries of the corresponding UV-complete theory or be approximate symmetries emerging in the infrared. Both possibilities can arise when gravity is decoupled. In a quantum gravity context, however, it is widely believed that the former possibility can never occur
[
100
,
10
,
59
]
.

The purpose of this section is to discuss the possible mechanisms for breaking higher-form symmetries. Since higher-form symmetries act on extended operators, their breaking cannot occur without introducing new degrees of freedom into the EFT. The breaking of each higher-form symmetry can therefore be associated with the appearance of a corresponding charged sector, characterized by a symmetry-breaking energy scale below which the charged objects cannot be excited.

For each case, we first consider the simpler non-supersymmetric setting of Section
2.1
– see
[
19
,
34
,
25
,
32
,
117
]
for related discussions of the simpler axion-Maxwell model – and then discuss what additional insights can be gained in the supersymmetric extensions of Section
2.2
. Throughout, we will also strive to distinguish clearly between conclusions that rely on conjectural quantum gravity constraints and those that hold more generally.

4.1
Winding symmetry breaking and axion strings

Let us proceed in the same order as in Section
3
, and first consider the invertible winding two-form symmetries. Their breaking requires the existence of
dynamical
axion strings, namely finite-tension counterparts of the axion vortex operators
{\cal V}_{\bf e}
discussed in Section
3.1
. We denote by
{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}\subset V_{\mathbb{Z}}
the set of axion charges populated by physical strings.

From (
3.5
), a dynamical string with charge
{\bf e}\in{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}
breaks the winding symmetries with parameters
\beta_{i}\simeq\beta_{i}+1
whenever
\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle\notin\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, for fixed
{\bm{\beta}}
, we can identify the following scale at which the corresponding winding two-form symmetry is certainly broken:

M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}\equiv\min\left\{\sqrt{2\pi{\cal T}_{\bf e}}\ \big|\ {\bf e}\in{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}\,,\ \langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle\notin\mathbb{Z}\right\}\,,

(4.1)

where
{\cal T}_{\bf e}
denotes the tension of the lightest string of charge
{\bf e}
. A rough motivation for (
4.1
) is the following. At the EFT level, one can consider the nucleation of string loops. For a string not to be resolved below the EFT cutoff
\Lambda
, one should have
2\pi{\cal T}_{\bf e}>\Lambda^{2}
, and the loop radius should satisfy
R>\Lambda^{-1}>1/\sqrt{2\pi{\cal T}_{\bf e}}
. The loop then has mass
2\pi{\cal T}_{\bf e}R>\sqrt{2\pi{\cal T}_{\bf e}}
. This suggests that the winding symmetry in the direction
{\bm{\beta}}
can only emerge at energies below
M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}
. Denoting by
E^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}
the corresponding symmetry-breaking scale, we get the upper bound

E^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}\lesssim M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}\,.

(4.2)

In particular, the winding symmetry may be broken at energies much smaller than
M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}
if the string is not fundamental, namely if it admits a solitonic description within an intermediate four-dimensional EFT; see for instance
[
19
]
. On the other hand, in the absence of supersymmetry, little can be said about
{\cal T}_{\bf e}
, and hence about
M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}
, without specifying the UV completion.

In the presence of supersymmetry, more can be said. The tension of BPS strings is fixed by supersymmetry to be
[
83
]

{\cal T}_{\bf e}=M^{2}e^{i}\ell_{i}\\
,,

(4.3)

where
\ell_{i}
are the dual saxions introduced in (
2.26
), and
M=M_{\text{\tiny P}}
in the presence of gravity.
Anti-BPS strings instead have tension
{\cal T}_{\bf e}=-M^{2}e^{i}\ell_{i}
. For a given string charge vector
{\bf e}\in{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}
, we expect BPS strings to be the lightest and most stable ones. Therefore, using (
4.3
) in (
4.1
) gives a more explicit expression for the upper bound (
4.2
).
9
9

9

In the presence of massless saxions, the interpretation of (
4.3
) is affected by the saxionic backreaction, which should be appropriately understood as an RG flow along the string
[
84
,
85
]
.

In practice, applying (
4.1
) requires knowing the spectrum of axion strings, not only their tensions. In quantum gravity models, further information follows from standard quantum gravity expectations. First, the absence of exact global symmetries
[
100
,
10
,
59
]
implies that the set of string charges
{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}
must generate the entire lattice
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Furthermore, the Weak Gravity Conjecture (WGC)
[
6
]
for multiple gauge fields
[
23
]
– see
[
58
]
for a review and more references – implies that the convex hull of the vectors

{\bf w}_{\bf e}\equiv\frac{M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}{\bf e}}{{\cal T}_{\bf e}}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}

(4.4)

with
{\bf e}\in{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}
should contain a ball of radius
\gamma\sim{\cal O}(1)
, as measured by the metric
{\cal G}_{ij}
appearing in (
2.3
). This follows from (
2.10
), with
M=M_{\text{\tiny P}}
, since
{\cal G}_{ij}
defines the couplings of the dual two-form gauge fields. In particular, the lightest strings are expected to satisfy the WGC bound

\frac{M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}{\cal Q}_{\bf e}}{{\cal T}_{\bf e}}=\|{\bf w}_{\bf e}\|\geq\gamma\,,\qquad{\cal Q}^{2}_{\bf e}\equiv{\cal G}_{ij}e^{i}e^{j}=\|{\bf e}\|^{2}\,.

(4.5)

Hence, for such strings,
{\cal T}_{\bf e}\leq M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}{\cal Q}_{\bf e}/\gamma
, which gives the bound

M^{\rm(w)}_{\bm{\beta}}\leq M_{\text{\tiny P}}\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\|{\bf e}\|}{\gamma}}\ \big|\ {\bf e}\in{\cal I}^{\rm(w)}\,,\ \langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle\notin\mathbb{Z}\right\}\,.

(4.6)

The above WGC considerations should hold independently of supersymmetry, but they can be sharpened in the supersymmetric models described in Section
2.2
. Using (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) in (
4.3
), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

{\cal T}_{\bf e}=M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}{\cal G}_{ij}e^{i}s^{j}\leq M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}\|{\bf e}\|\|{\bm{s}}\|=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}\,M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}{\cal Q}_{\bf e}\,.

(4.7)

Thus, in models with Kähler potential (
2.20
), the BPS string tension (
4.3
) satisfies a WGC bound (
4.5
) with
\gamma_{\text{\tiny BPS}}=\sqrt{2/k}
. A more detailed discussion on this bound, including the subtleties in the definition of string tension, can be found
[
84
,
85
]
; see also the recent discussion in
[
45
]
.

As argued in
[
84
,
85
]
, there is a distinguished subclass of BPS axion strings, the
EFT strings
, which are genuinely gravitational in nature and have special properties. In particular, they can saturate the WGC bound with
\gamma_{\text{\tiny BPS}}=\sqrt{2/k}
. More precisely, the set
{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}
of EFT string charges can be identified with the discretization of the closure of the saxionic cone
\Delta
introduced in Section
2.2
:

{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}=\overline{\Delta}\cap V_{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

(4.8)

See Figure
2
for an illustration. Since
{\bm{s}}\in\Delta
, the bound (
4.7
) can be saturated by taking
{\bm{s}}
aligned, or arbitrarily close, to the direction identified by
{\bf e}
.

Figure 2
:
The violet and orange bullets represent the set
{\cal C}_{\rm S}
of BPS string charges of the
\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
F-theory model, with twist parameter

, discussed in Section

. The violet bullets represent the subset
{\bf e}\in{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}
of EFT string charges, while the orange bullets represent BPS but non-EFT string charges.

There may exist other BPS strings, filling out a larger set of charges
{\cal C}_{\rm S}\supset{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}
. In concrete string theory compactifications, BPS strings typically arise from higher-dimensional branes or bundles localized on internal supersymmetric cycles. EFT strings are distinguished by their association with “movable” internal configurations, which can recombine and explore the full configuration space
[
85
]
. In particular, string theory realizations suggest that only EFT strings generically satisfy completeness, namely, that for each
{\bf e}\in{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}
there exists a physical string carrying charge
{\bf e}
. This would also guarantee that the winding symmetries are fully broken by EFT strings alone, without the need to invoke additional axion strings, whether BPS or not. From the quantum gravity viewpoint, non-EFT strings appear accidental rather than necessary.

Since EFT strings are fundamental, their tension directly sets the corresponding winding symmetry-breaking scale. We may therefore identify the
quantum gravity
winding symmetry-breaking scale with

E^{\rm(w)}_{\text{\tiny QG},{\bm{\beta}}}({\bm{\ell}})\equiv\min\left\{M_{\text{\tiny P}}\sqrt{2\pi\langle{\bm{\ell}},{\bf e}\rangle}\ \big|\ {\bf e}\in{\cal C}^{\text{\tiny EFT}}_{\rm S}\,,\ \langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bf e}\rangle\notin\mathbb{Z}\right\}\,,

(4.9)

where we have emphasised the dependence of
E^{\rm(w)}
on the moduli space position.
An additional piece of information comes from the relation between EFT strings and the species scale

4.2
Magnetic one-form symmetry breaking

Similarly to axion strings, dynamical magnetic monopoles, which are the finite mass counterparts of the ’t Hooft lines

the set of magnetic charges populated by dynamical monopoles. From (

is expected to provide only an upper bound on the actual magnetic one-form symmetry-breaking scale
E^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}

E^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}\lesssim M^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}\,.

The scale
E^{\rm(m)}_{\bm{\beta}}

109

Unlike axion strings, monopoles in

on the lightest monopoles, and hence on (

4.14

In any case, as in the axion-string case, we expect some subclass of monopoles to stand out as genuinely gravitational, and to set the scale of the quantum-gravitational breaking of the magnetic one-form symmetries. We leave a more detailed investigation of these aspects to future work.

Recently,

In the absence of a cosmological constant, axion wormholes connect two asymptotically flat Euclidean spacetimes. One can then work with Dirichlet boundary conditions, fixing the asymptotic values of the axions, and of any other scalar fields, on each side of the wormhole.
The strategy of

Thus, in a certain sense, these symmetries are responsible for the existence of the specific types of wormholes that appear once gravity becomes dynamical. These, in turn, signal the quantum-gravitational inconsistency of the symmetries one starts with. The proposal of
[

6.3
EFT instanton corrections

As for the purely axionic wormholes discussed in Section

In a supersymmetric context, BPS instantons are expected to dominate the non-perturbative contributions. These generate terms proportional to (

(6.54)

[

6.1

In order to address this question, we evaluate (

6.23

All the wormholes discussed so far completely break supersymmetry. Consider first the cases

k=3(1+\varepsilon)\,,\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1\,,

(6.69)

as in

. The precise way in which this deformation is implemented will be irrelevant.

Consider first (
6.40

\varepsilon

\displaystyle{\bm{\ell}}(r_{\pm})

\displaystyle\simeq{\bm{\ell}}_{*}\left[\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}_{\pm}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\arccos\left(\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}_{\pm}}\right)\sqrt{1-\frac{L^{4}}{r^{4}_{\pm}}}\right]\simeq\frac{{\bf q}}{2\pi M^{2}_{{\text{\tiny P}}}r^{2}_{\pm}}+{\bm{\ell}}_{\infty}\,,

(6.70)

with

{\bm{\ell}}_{\infty}=\frac{\varepsilon{\bf q}}{8M^{2}_{{\text{\tiny P}}}L^{2}}\,.

In the second step of (

r^{2}_{\pm}\lesssim L^{2}/\varepsilon

S^{\text{\tiny WH}}_{\bf q}\simeq 4\pi\langle{\bf q},{\bm{s}}_{\infty}\rangle\left[1+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{2})\right]=\left[S^{\text{\tiny BPS}}_{\bf q}({\bm{s}}_{\infty})+\overline{S}^{\text{\tiny BPS}}_{-\bf q}({\bm{s}}_{\infty})\right]\left[1+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{2})\right]\,.

Quantitatively, this can be implemented in the bilocal effective theory by integrating out the fermionic variables
\bar{\theta}^{\dot{\alpha}}_{+},\theta^{\alpha}_{-}

\bar{\theta}^{\dot{\alpha}}_{+},\theta^{\alpha}_{-}

-\frac{1}{4}M^{2}_{\text{\tiny P}}\int({\cal R}_{i\bar{\imath}j\bar{\jmath}}-\frac{1}{2}g_{i\bar{\imath}}g_{j\bar{\jmath}})(t,\bar{t})\chi^{i}_{+}\chi^{j}_{+}\overline{\chi}^{\bar{\imath}}_{-}\overline{\chi}^{\bar{\jmath}}_{-}*1=c\,\bar{\theta}_{+}\bar{\theta}_{+}\theta_{-}\theta_{-}\,,

\overline{\chi}^{\bar{\imath}}_{\dot{\alpha}}(z_{+})\overline{\chi}^{\bar{\jmath}}_{\dot{\beta}}(z^{\prime}_{+})

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}}{\rm d}^{4}x_{+}{\rm d}^{2}\theta_{+}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}_{-}}{\rm d}^{4}x_{-}{\rm d}^{2}\bar{\theta}_{-}\sum_{{\bf q}\in{\cal C}_{\text{\tiny WH}}}c_{\bf q}\,e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},{\bm{T}}_{+}\rangle}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},\overline{\bm{T}}_{-}\rangle}\ +\ \text{c.c.}\,,

T^{i}=t^{i}+\chi^{i}\theta+\ldots
are the chiral superfield extensions of the complex scalars

t^{i}_{+}=t^{i}_{\infty}=a^{i}_{\infty}+{\rm i}s^{i}_{\infty}
and
\overline{t}^{i}_{-}=\overline{t}^{i}_{-\infty}=a^{i}_{-\infty}-{\rm i}s^{i}_{\infty}
. For instance, (

\Big(\sum_{{\bf q}\in{\cal C}_{\text{\tiny WH}}}c_{\bf q}\,q_{i}q_{j}q_{k}q_{l}\,e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},{\bm{t}}_{+}\rangle}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},\overline{\bm{t}}_{-}\rangle}\Big)\left(\chi^{i}_{+}\chi^{j}_{+}\right)(x_{+})\left(\overline{\chi}^{k}_{-}\overline{\chi}^{l}_{-}\right)(x_{-})\,.

(6.75)

For real axions, the exponentially suppressed factors guarantee the convergence of the sum. On the other hand, we are interested in the contribution of the imaginary wormhole, which has imaginary Dirichlet boundary conditions. This can be obtained by making the imaginary axion shift
\Delta{\bm{a}}_{\infty}\rightarrow\Delta{\bm{a}}_{\infty}-2{\bm{\varphi}}_{\text{IDB}}
, with
{\bm{\varphi}}_{\text{IDB}}

\sum_{{\bf q}\in{\cal C}_{\text{\tiny WH}}}c_{\bf q}\,q_{i}q_{j}q_{k}q_{l}\,e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle{\bf q},\Delta{\bm{a}}_{\infty}\rangle}\,,

(6.76)

which badly diverges. This is just another manifestation of the divergence observed directly from the vanishing on-shell action of the imaginary wormhole.

From (
6.74
), it is clear that the wormhole contribution appears as an infinite sum of bilocal superpotential terms. In combination with the observations of Section
6.3
, if we interpret these bilocal terms as coarse-grained information about the UV physics, this suggests that the effective field theory on each side of the wormhole should be modified by an infinite sum of superpotential terms generated by EFT instantons. If correct, in concrete string theory models this picture should admit a microscopic counterpart in terms of movable brane configurations. This possibility was already suggested in
[
94
]
, and here we see how the IDB provides a natural justification thereof. As already noted in
[
94
]
, such a mechanism would also give a concrete realization of the Supersymmetric Genericity Conjecture proposed in
[
104
]
. In Section
6.3
, we found that, also for
k=1,2
, EFT instantons are expected to provide the key UV ingredient realizing the IDC. In these cases, however, one cannot repeat the arguments just presented for
k=3
, because the corresponding wormholes degenerate and require an extension of the moduli space. It is therefore difficult to draw definite conclusions about the corresponding potential F-term contributions. In the next subsection, we will encounter another subtlety associated with the cases
k=1,2
.

6.5
Implications of the Gauss–Bonnet term

In Section
6.2
-
6.4
we have neglected the contribution of the Gauss–Bonnet term (
2.5
), where
\gamma
has the form (
2.19
). We also recall that, according to the bound (
4.12
), in the presence of gauge fields one
cannot
generically set
\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}=0
. Rather, one typically has

\tilde{\mathsf{K}}_{i}s^{i}>0\,;

(6.77)

see
[
95
]
for a large class of string theory examples. One may therefore wonder whether the Gauss–Bonnet term can affect our conclusions. Notice, by contrast, that the Pontryagin term (
2.7
) does not contribute, because of the SO(4) symmetry of the wormhole solution.

An important point in the approach of
[
39
,
90
]
is that, in the argument leading to the IDB, wormholes with small charge
{\bf q}
and small radius
L
are irrelevant. This is a significant advantage over the approach followed in much of the literature on the phenomenological implications of wormholes; see, for instance,
[
61
]
for a review. The latter typically requires sufficiently small wormholes in order to obtain a sizeable factor
\exp(-S^{\text{\tiny WH}}_{\bf q})
and hence a not too suppressed physical effect. By contrast, in the imaginary-wormhole approach, the divergence that leads to the IDB comes from the large-
L
sector. One may therefore expect higher-derivative corrections to be irrelevant.

We expect this conclusion to certainly hold for the wormholes corresponding to saxionic sectors with homogeneity
k\geq 4
, since the saxion vector (
6.48
) remains finite along their entire trajectory. On the other hand, for
k\leq 3
, the trajectory (
6.48
) diverges at the critical points
\tau=\pm\tau_{\rm cr}
, with
\tau_{\rm cr}\leq\tau_{\infty}
; see (
6.42
). In these cases, the harmlessness of the Gauss–Bonnet term is therefore less obvious.

One can estimate the impact of the Euclidean counterpart of (
2.5
), namely

S_{\text{\tiny GB}}\equiv-\int\gamma\,E_{\text{\tiny GB}}\,*1\,,

(6.78)

by simply evaluating it on the homogeneous wormholes, which solve the two-derivative equations of motion. A direct calculation gives

S_{\text{\tiny GB}}|_{\text{\tiny WH}}=\frac{\pi}{2}L^{8}\langle\tilde{\mathsf{K}},{\bm{s}}({\bf q})\rangle\int_{L}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{9}\tilde{\ell}(r)\sqrt{1-\frac{L^{4}}{r^{4}}}}\,,

(6.79)

where
\tilde{\ell}(r)
is the profile (
6.38
) expressed as a function of
r
through (
6.2
). By setting
L^{2}/r^{2}\equiv\xi
, we can rewrite (
6.79
) as

S_{\text{\tiny GB}}|_{\text{\tiny WH}}=\frac{\pi}{12}\langle\tilde{\mathsf{K}},{\bm{s}}_{*}\rangle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\xi^{3}{\rm d}\xi}{\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{k}}\arccos\xi\right)\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}}\,.

(6.80)

It is now clear that the cases
k\geq 3
and
k=1,2
have quite different Gauss–Bonnet contributions. Suppose first that
k\geq 3
. The integrand in (
6.80
) is positive definite and, furthermore,

\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{k}}\arccos\xi\right)\geq\xi\,.

(6.81)

Hence,

S_{\text{\tiny GB}}|_{\rm WH}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\langle\tilde{\mathsf{K}},{\bm{s}}_{*}\rangle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\xi^{2}{\rm d}\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}\langle\tilde{\mathsf{K}},{\bm{s}}_{*}\rangle\,,

(6.82)

with equality for
k=3
. Thus, for
k\geq 3
, the Gauss-Bonnet term gives only a finite additional suppression that does not affect the conclusions of the previous subsections.

On the other hand, if
k<3
, the integrand diverges at the point
\xi_{\rm cr}
corresponding to
\tau_{\rm cr}
. We can split the integral (
6.80
) into two contributions, over the intervals
(0,\xi_{\rm cr})
and
(\xi_{\rm cr},1)
. For
k=1,2
, these two contributions diverge separately. This clearly signals that the contribution of the Gauss–Bonnet term cannot be ignored a priori and should be reconsidered. Interestingly, however, the contribution from
(0,\xi_{\rm cr})
gives a positive divergence, while the contribution from
(\xi_{\rm cr},1)
gives a negative one. One may hence sum them as in a principal-value regularization, getting a finite result. It would be interesting to understand whether this may have any physical interpretation. In fact, for
k=1,2
, the two-derivative on-shell action (
6.39
) has an even stronger divergence. However, as already observed, the formula (
6.41
) may still be meaningful for
k=1,2
. We hope to return to these interesting questions in the future.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have studied various aspects of the generalized symmetry structure and of the associated symmetry breaking mechanisms of four-dimensional axiverse effective field theories, including their extensions to broad classes of
{\cal N}=1
models.

The first part of the paper, building on previous works, most notably
[
24
,
34
,
25
,
130
,
5
]
, provides a unifying framework to identify and study the invertible and non-invertible symmetries of axiverse models including an arbitrary number of periodic axions coupled to an arbitrary number of abelian gauge sectors, with arbitrary kinetic terms and axion-gauge couplings. We also included Gauss-Bonnet and Pontryagin curvature-squared terms, and commented on the effect of non-abelian gauge and other sectors.

As in
[
25
]
, the non-invertible nature of the electric one-form symmetries and axion shift zero-form symmetries, and their interplay with the invertible magnetic one-form symmetries and winding two-form symmetries, implies hierarchies between the corresponding symmetry-breaking scales. While these hierarchies – see (
4.18
) and (
5.1
) – are inevitably more intricate than their simple counterpart in the axion-Maxwell model considered in
[
25
]
, their physical interpretation in terms of anomalous sectors supported by axion strings and monopoles remains equally clear.

Among the possible symmetry-breaking mechanisms, we focused in particular on the breaking of the non-invertible axion shift symmetries generated by fundamental instantons. The interpretation of the imaginary wormholes recently proposed in
[
39
,
90
]
plays a key role in our discussion, providing a purely bottom-up motivation for the existence of fundamental instantons contributing to the realization of the Imaginary Distance Bound. By focusing on our
{\cal N}=1
supersymmetric axiverse models, we obtain our strongest results. Namely, by adopting the viewpoint proposed in
[
39
,
90
]
, the existence of a specific class of imaginary wormholes in saxionic sectors with homogeneity degree
k=3
points to a non-vanishing superpotential generated by an infinite tower of EFT instantons, a possibility already suggested in
[
94
]
. For saxionic sectors with
k\geq 4
, the same argument predicts the existence of towers of non-BPS instantons generating D-term corrections to the EFT. The cases
k=1,2
also suggest the existence of corresponding infinite towers of EFT instantons generating F-term corrections, but in these cases the saxion profiles along the wormholes pass through infinite field-distance points, which makes the interpretation of these saddles more delicate. Moreover, we argue that precisely for
k=1,2
, the Gauss–Bonnet terms, which are generically expected to be non-vanishing
[
95
]
, cannot a priori be regarded as small perturbations, and their effect on the wormhole solutions should therefore be properly taken into account.

Of course, many open questions remain. In particular, while we have established the general framework for investigating non-invertible symmetries in axiverse models, we have discussed only a limited number of possible applications. For instance, by explicitly reintroducing non-abelian sectors, it would be interesting to investigate the potential implications of our results for the strong CP problem in the presence of an axiverse sector. Furthermore, several QFT aspects, such as fusion rules and the categorical structure of the symmetry defects, together with their possible implications, remain to be developed. Finally, the proposal of
[
39
,
90
]
revives the potential role of wormholes as a purely bottom-up window into phenomenologically relevant quantum gravity effects, and the categorical structure of invertible and non-invertible symmetries may act as an organizing principle for decoding and investigating these effects.

Appendix A
Integral coprime matrix factorization

In this appendix, we collect some definitions and properties regarding the factorization of rational matrices in terms of integral matrices. More details can be found in
[
123
]
, which works with matrices taking values in a general principal ideal domain and in the corresponding field of fractions, while here we focus on
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively. See also Appendix C of
[
77
]
for another discussion of coprime matrices.

Any
n\times m
rational matrix
\mathsf{Q}\in{\rm Mat}(n,m,\mathbb{Q})
can be factorized in terms of pairs
(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{N})
and
(\tilde{\mathsf{P}},\tilde{\mathsf{N}})
of integral matrices, with invertible
\mathsf{N}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{N}}
, as follows:

\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathsf{N}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathsf{P}}\,.

(A.1)

Here
\mathsf{P}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
are
n\times m
matrices, while
\mathsf{N}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{N}}
are invertible
m\times m
and
n\times n
matrices, respectively.

The factorization defined by
(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{N})
is called
right-coprime
if there exist two integral matrices
\mathsf{X}\in{\rm Mat}(m,n,\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathsf{Y}\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\mathsf{X}\mathsf{P}-\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{N}=\mathds{1}_{m}\,,

(A.2)

while the factorization defined by
(\tilde{\mathsf{P}},\tilde{\mathsf{N}})
is called
left-coprime
if there exist two integral matrices
\tilde{\mathsf{X}}\in{\rm Mat}(m,n,\mathbb{Z})
and
\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}\in{\rm Mat}(n,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\tilde{\mathsf{P}}\tilde{\mathsf{X}}-\tilde{\mathsf{N}}\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathds{1}_{n}\,.

(A.3)

As discussed below, any rational matrix
\mathsf{Q}\in{\rm Mat}(n,m,\mathbb{Q})
admits both a right-coprime and a left-coprime factorization.

One can prove that a right-coprime factorization
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}
is also
weakly right-coprime

[
111
]
, which means that

\forall\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}\;\hbox{ such that }\;\mathsf{P}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\;\hbox{ and }\;\mathsf{N}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\,.

(A.4)

Similarly, a left-coprime factorization
\mathsf{Q}=\tilde{\mathsf{N}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
is also
weakly left-coprime
, namely

\forall\tilde{\mathsf{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}\;\hbox{ such that }\;\tilde{\mathsf{P}}^{\rm t}\tilde{\mathsf{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\;\hbox{ and }\;\tilde{\mathsf{N}}^{\rm t}\tilde{\mathsf{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\tilde{\mathsf{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,.

(A.5)

Let us prove, for instance, the first proposition. The second can be proved in a completely analogous way. Consider a right-coprime factorization
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}
and a vector
\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
such that
\mathsf{P}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\mathsf{N}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. The definition (
A.2
) then implies

\mathsf{\alpha}=(\mathsf{X}\mathsf{P}-\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{N})\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\,,

(A.6)

since
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
are integral matrices. Conversely, if
\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, then
\mathsf{P}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\mathsf{N}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, since
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{N}
are integral matrices.

It is also possible to prove that a weakly left/right-coprime factorization is left/right-coprime. To see this, let us first discuss how weakly coprime factorizations are related to general factorizations. Suppose that
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}
is a weakly right-coprime factorization and take another factorization, not necessarily weakly right-coprime,
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}^{\prime}\mathsf{N^{\prime}}^{-1}
. Then

\mathsf{P}^{\prime}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{T}\,,\qquad\mathsf{N}^{\prime}=\mathsf{N}\mathsf{T}\,,

(A.7)

where
\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{N}^{-1}\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
and
\det\mathsf{T}\neq 0
. Take now any vector
\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Since
\mathsf{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
are integral matrices, one has

\mathsf{P}^{\prime}\mathsf{\alpha}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{T}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,,\qquad\mathsf{N}^{\prime}\mathsf{\alpha}=\mathsf{N}\mathsf{T}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\,.

(A.8)

By (
A.4
), we deduce that
\mathsf{T}\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Since
\mathsf{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\mathsf{T}\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{Z})\,.

(A.9)

If
\mathsf{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
are also weakly right-coprime, then the same argument applied to the inverse transformation gives
\mathsf{T}^{-1}\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{Z})
. Hence
|\det\mathsf{T}|=1
, that is,

\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}^{\prime}\mathsf{N}^{\prime-1}\quad\text{weakly right-coprime}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathsf{T}\in{\rm GL}(m,\mathbb{Z})\,.

(A.10)

We can then notice the following two facts.

•

A unimodular transformation maps right-coprime factorizations to right-coprime factorizations. Indeed, consider a unimodular
\mathsf{T}
in (
A.7
). We can define
\mathsf{X}^{\prime}=\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}^{\prime}=\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{Y}
. Then

\mathds{1}_{m}=\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{P}\mathsf{T}-\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{X}^{\prime}\mathsf{P}^{\prime}-\mathsf{Y}^{\prime}\mathsf{N}^{\prime}\,.

(A.11)

•

We have proven that a coprime factorization is also weakly coprime.

These two facts imply that any weakly right-coprime factorization is related by a unimodular transformation to any right-coprime factorization of the same rational matrix. Since right-coprime factorizations always exist, as shown in the next subsection, and unimodular transformations preserve right-coprimeness, we conclude that weak right-coprimeness is equivalent to right-coprimeness. Hence, one can use either (
A.2
) or (
A.4
) to characterize a right-coprime factorization. The same argument applies to left-coprime factorizations. In this sense, we can say that a left/right-coprime factorization is the “minimal” possible factorization.

A.1
Construction of coprime factorizations

In the following, we provide a method for constructing left/right-coprime factorizations of a rational rectangular matrix
\mathsf{Q}\in{\rm Mat}(n,m,\mathbb{Q})
. This also provides a constructive proof of the existence of such factorizations.

The procedure is as follows:

1.

Find the smallest
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
\mathsf{F}=b\mathsf{Q}\in{\rm Mat}(n,m,\mathbb{Z})
.

2.

Find the Smith normal form of
\mathsf{F}
:

\mathsf{U}\mathsf{F}\mathsf{V}=\hat{\mathsf{F}}\,,\qquad\mathsf{U}\in{\rm GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\,,\qquad\mathsf{V}\in{\rm GL}(m,\mathbb{Z})\,.

(A.12)

If
\mathsf{Q}
, and hence
\mathsf{F}
, has rank
l
, then
\hat{\mathsf{F}}\in{\rm Mat}(n,m,\mathbb{Z})
has the form

\hat{\mathsf{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{1}&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&\hat{a}_{2}&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&\hat{a}_{l}&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{bmatrix},

(A.13)

where the
\hat{a}_{i}
are the invariant factors of
\hat{\mathsf{F}}
; see Appendix B.2 of
[
123
]
for more details.

3.

Obtain the Smith-McMillan form of
\mathsf{Q}
:

\mathsf{U}\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{V}=\hat{\mathsf{Q}}\,.

(A.14)

The matrix
\hat{\mathsf{Q}}
has a form similar to (
A.13
), but now the non-zero entries are

q_{i}\equiv\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}=\frac{\hat{a}_{i}}{b}\,,\qquad\gcd(a_{i},b_{i})=1\,.

(A.15)

In this way, we can write

\hat{\mathsf{Q}}=\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathsf{B}}^{-1}\mathsf{A}\,,

(A.16)

where
\mathsf{A}
is the
n\times m
matrix of the form (
A.13
) with entries
a_{i}
, while
\mathsf{B}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{B}}
are diagonal matrices of dimensions
m
and
n
, respectively. Their first
l
diagonal entries are
b_{i}
, while all remaining diagonal entries are equal to
1
.

4.

We can then obtain coprime factorizations by choosing

\begin{split}\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{U}^{-1}\mathsf{A}\,,\qquad\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{V}\mathsf{B}\quad\Rightarrow\quad&\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{P}\mathsf{N}^{-1}\,,\\
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}=\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{-1}\,,\qquad\tilde{\mathsf{N}}=\tilde{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{U}\quad\Rightarrow\quad&\mathsf{Q}=\tilde{\mathsf{N}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathsf{P}}\,.\end{split}

(A.17)

To see that the factorizations obtained with this procedure are actually left/right-coprime, let us choose, for any
i=1,\ldots,l
, two integers
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

x_{i}a_{i}-y_{i}b_{i}=1\,,

(A.18)

which always exist by Bézout’s identity. Let us focus on the right factorization, since the left one can be treated in a similar way. We define

\hat{\mathsf{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&x_{2}&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&x_{l}&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{bmatrix},\qquad\hat{\mathsf{Y}}={\rm diag}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l},-1,\ldots,-1)\,,

(A.19)

where
\hat{\mathsf{X}}
is an
m\times n
matrix, while
\hat{\mathsf{Y}}
is an
m\times m
matrix. Then, by taking

\mathsf{X}=\hat{\mathsf{X}}\mathsf{U}\,,\qquad\mathsf{Y}=\hat{\mathsf{Y}}\mathsf{V}^{-1}\,,

(A.20)

one immediately verifies that (
A.2
) is satisfied. Hence the factorization is right-coprime.

Notice that the above procedure identifies a particular pair of coprime factorizations, while the most general one can be obtained by composing them with unimodular matrices, as discussed above.

Appendix C
Invariance under integral shift

In this appendix, we prove that the defects (
3.22
) and (

{\bm{\alpha}}
. Let us start with the 1-form symmetry defect. Consider the integer shift
{\bm{\alpha}}\rightarrow{\bm{\alpha}}+{\bf m}

{\bf m}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}

3.19

{\bf m}\in W_{\mathbb{Z}}

\mathsf{Q}({\bm{\alpha}}+{\bf m})=\left[\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{Q}({\bf m})\mathsf{L}\right]\mathsf{L}^{-1}\,.

Then we can use this factorization to define
{\cal D}^{\rm(e)}_{{\bm{\alpha}}+{\bf m}}(\Sigma)

{\rm i}\oint_{\Sigma}\mathsf{Q}_{iJ}({\bf m})\mathsf{L}^{J}{}_{I}a^{i}{\rm d}c^{I}\,,

\alpha^{I}\in\mathbb{Q}

Appendix D
Comments on non-abelian sectors

In this appendix we discuss how the inclusion of a non-abelian sector affect the results of Section
3
. It is well known
[
53
]
that, in the absence of charged matter transforming in non-adjoint representations, these theories can admit
Z_{\rm e}^{(1)}
electric and
Z_{\rm m}^{(1)}
magnetic one-form symmetries, where
Z_{\rm e}
and
Z_{\rm m}
are finite groups corresponding to the center of
G
and the Pontryagin dual of
\pi_{1}(G)
, respectively. These extend the set of symmetries identified in the previous subsections.

More crucial for us is the impact of the non-abelian sector on the zero-form symmetries described in Section
3.4
. Let us focus on a given simple gauge group factor
G
, since the generalization to more general non-abelian gauge groups is straightforward. As in Footnote
3
, it will generically couple to the axions through a term of the form

-\frac{1}{8\pi}\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\int a^{i}\mathop{\rm tr}\left(F\wedge F\right)\,,

(D.1)

where
\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the trace is normalized so that
(4\pi)^{-2}\int\mathop{\rm tr}\left(F\wedge F\right)\in\mathbb{Z}
. With this normalization, instantons do not break the axion periodicity
a^{i}\simeq a^{i}+1
for generic
\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}
.

Suppose first that the corresponding
Z_{\rm m}^{(1)}
magnetic one-form symmetry is trivial. Then the term (
D.1
) generically breaks the zero-form symmetries identified by (
3.35
). The only surviving ones are those corresponding to
{\bm{\alpha}}\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}/V_{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\langle\widehat{\bf K},{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle\equiv\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(D.2)

Roughly speaking, this condition removes the rational axionic shift symmetries along one direction, up to a possible finite group of additional surviving symmetries. More precisely, we can introduce a sublattice
\tilde{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset V_{\mathbb{Z}}
annihilated by the vector
\widehat{\bf K}=\{\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\}\in V^{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
and generated by
n_{\rm A}-1
vectors
\tilde{\bm{v}}_{a}
. We then have

\langle\widehat{\bf K},\tilde{\bm{v}}_{a}\rangle=0\,.

(D.3)

Thus, we can identify a “quasi-continuous” set of zero-form symmetries preserved by the inclusion of the non-abelian sector by taking

{\bm{\alpha}}=\tilde{\alpha}^{a}\tilde{\bm{v}}_{a}\quad\text{mod}\ V_{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

(D.4)

for any choice of
\tilde{\alpha}^{a}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Of course, there may also be other choices of
{\bm{\alpha}}
satisfying (
D.2
), but these give only an additional finite set of possibilities. If
G
is not simply connected, this finite set of preserved axion shift symmetries can be further enlarged by following
[
34
]
. However, these additional zero-form symmetries cannot be regarded as quasi-continuous and therefore provide much weaker constraints on the generation of potential terms.

In supersymmetric models, the constants
\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}
identify the saxionic contribution to the kinetic terms:

-\frac{1}{8\pi}\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\int s^{i}\mathop{\rm tr}\left(F\wedge*F\right)\,.

(D.5)

Requiring this kinetic term to have the correct sign gives the condition
\langle\widehat{\bf K},{\bm{s}}\rangle\equiv\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{i}s^{i}>0
. Recalling (
2.22
), we conclude that
\widehat{\bf K}\in{\cal C}_{\rm I}
. This imposes further restrictions on the possible zero-form axion shift symmetries that are not broken by the non-abelian sector. In particular, we may try to restrict to saxionic directions that do not couple to the non-abelian sector. This is possible if we can formally identify the restricted saxionic cone
\tilde{\Delta}
with a codimension-one facet of
\Delta
orthogonal to
\widehat{\bf K}
.
