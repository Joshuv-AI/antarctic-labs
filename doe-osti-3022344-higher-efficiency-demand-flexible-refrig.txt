 1. Project Abstract:
Refrigerator technology has advanced significantly over the last couple of decades.
Today’s refrigerators use only about 25% of the energy that was required to power models
built in 1975. Even as they continually improve efficiency to meet standards, refrigerators
have increased in size by almost 20%, added energy-consuming features such as
through-the-door ice, and provide more benefits than ever before. However, a few
challenges and technology gaps are preventing further improvement of the demand
responsiveness and efficiency of the refrigerators. One of the major technology gaps in
existing refrigerators is their outdated de-icing process. When the evaporator generates
frost, an old-fashioned resistive heating element melts the ice. Most refrigerators have a
timed defrost cycle, rather than an active system that could monitor the state of the frost.
In these systems, not only is the precious electricity used at its least efficient form of
conversion (direct conversion of electricity to heat), but also all the latent heat associated
with the ice is wasted during the melting process. On top of that, the refrigerator needs to
work harder to bring the temperature down after defrosting, and, last but not least, food
quality is severely affected by temperature swings during the defrost cycle. According to
a study, the EU alone wastes 89 million tons of food in the supply chain every year. Any
temperature swing during defrosting (about 6F according to Emerson for low-temperature
cases) can negatively impact the shelf life of meat and other products for multiple days.
All these issues can happen during the peak demand time of the electric grid. Unlike the
conventional systems, the proposed novel advanced micro-vibrational deicing process
uses no heat for defrosting. Instead, it uses the micro vibrations generated by a
piezoelectric or vibration-generating module to mechanically break ice from the heat
exchanger almost instantaneously.

The PI, the former program manager of the HVAC, Appliances, and Water Heating
program at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, invented direct contact ultrasonic drying at
ORNL. After licensing the technology from ORNL, he launched Ultrasonic Technology
Solutions back in 2018 and took an entrepreneurial leave of absence from ORNL back in
2020. Ultrasonic Technology Solutions, an ORNL spinoff company specializing in the
wide applications of ultrasonic transducers and vibrational solutions, teamed up with
Emerson (now Copeland), a Fortune 500 company specializing in compressor
manufacturing and refrigeration in a wide range of industrial and consumer markets, to
explore the feasibility of the proposed vibrational defrosting technology.

The project titled “Higher Efficiency, Demand Flexible Refrigerator with On-Demand
Micro-Vibrational De-icing Technology, performed by Ultrasonic Technology Solutions,
LLC (UTS) of Knoxville, TN, in collaboration with Emerson (now Copeland), represents
the final phase of a multi-year effort funded under the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Building Technologies Office (BTO) BENEFIT FOA 2020. Initiated on October 1, 2021,
and completed after a nine-month no-cost extension ending September 30, 2025, this
project aimed to develop and validate a novel micro-vibrational mechanical defrosting
system, achieving more than 25% improvement in defrosting energy efficiency over
conventional baseline defrosting technologies. Over sixteen quarters, the project
advanced from fundamental ice-mechanical characterization and prototype
development to full-scale system integration and validation. Initial efforts established
project management infrastructure and characterized ice adhesion properties, followed
by the design and fabrication of early aluminum-based prototypes for resonance
frequency testing. Subsequent quarters saw rapid technical progression, including the
identification of optimal piezoelectric and motor-based vibration mechanisms, the
demonstration of effective de-icing over 6x6-inch aluminum surfaces. The team achieved
its Go/No-Go milestone by exceeding the 25% energy-efficiency improvement
target—reaching up to 3,340% under optimized conditions—and later confirmed
that magnetic-driven systems offered superior performance and energy efficiency
compared to piezoelectric alternatives. Continued refinement led to the development
of amplifier systems on printed circuit boards, improved control and instrumentation
hardware, and integration into full-scale heat exchanger (HX) prototypes at both UTS and
Copeland facilities. Multiple vibration-mounting studies and frost-growth experiments
guided mechanical optimization and noise-mitigation strategies, achieving a 17.5 dB
reduction in sound pressure level and verifying robust mechanical performance.
Advanced analyses, including modal and harmonic simulations, established a
quantitative understanding of vibrational behavior and de-icing efficiency across
>1000 cm² systems. The final project phase successfully demonstrated scalable
integration within reach-in and chest freezer prototypes, confirmed >25% efficiency
improvements in large-area systems, and completed a comprehensive business

model and scale-up strategy identifying electric defrost systems as the primary
beachhead market. The culmination of this DOE-supported effort establishes micro-
vibrational defrosting as a viable, high-efficiency, low-noise, and demand-flexible de-icing
technology, paving the way for commercial deployment and broader application in next-
generation refrigeration systems.

 3. Milestone Requirements:
We have completed the first screening experimental campaign and identified the
piezoelectric transducers (or vibrational generator mechanisms) that break frost most
effectively. The initial testing was very successful on multiple occasions; the system
successfully removed most frost crystals from the surface of the aluminum sheet within
one to two minutes. We found that vibrational defrosting is pretty effective on various
vibration mechanisms and frequency ranges, and the effective operating region is much
more flexible than we initially thought.
We have also completed the impedance and resonance frequency tracking measurement
while frost is growing on the surface.
In Q4, we investigated how far de-icing can travel across sheet metal and found it to be
successful on a sheet as big as 6x6 inches for a single vibration generator. Additionally,
we learned that the motor impedance correlates with sheet resonance frequencies and
found that many of the vibration-generating mechanisms can exceed 100% efficiency
when compared to the latent heat of fusion of ice. With these tasks complete, Q4 has
been successfully met.
In Q5, we successfully demonstrated de-icing on ≥5 piezos, achieving a projected
efficiency improvement of> 25% over the baseline (estimated efficiency of 3340%).
Additionally, the team spent time modifying the experimental setup and investigating the
feasibility of using a phone motor-type vibrating mechanism to de-ice.
In Q6, we investigated mounting methods for both motors and piezoelectric transducers
and came to valuable conclusions. We designed a breadboard amplifier to meet the
project's needs and soft-started the work of mounting and building frost on a full-scale
heat exchanger.
In Q7, we designed and fabricated a > 100 cm2 prototype, completed shakedown testing,
and conducted a suite of mounting-study experiments. We simulated the full-scale heat
exchanger and began work to test different de-icing methods on the unit. We used the
laser vibrometer to gain valuable insights about the technology.
In Q8, we completed the development of the PCB amplifier through software advances,
selected heat exchangers for testing, initiated accelerated life testing, built an analytical
model, completed various experiments, and initiated new experiments. We used a laser
vibrometer to collect valuable data and made significant improvements to our frost-
building apparatus in the freezer.
In Q9, we compiled test data to show an efficiency improvement of more than 25% over
the baseline on a prototype with an area> 100 cm2. Additionally, we analyzed laser
vibrometer and de-icing data to identify trends; demonstrated that continuous vibration
can significantly prevent frost growth; fabricated and tested different vibration methods

 14

on Mini heat exchangers; and completed multiple tests on the full-size HX mounted to the
vibration table. We achieved encouraging scaled-up results on the vibration table using
a hybrid defrosting strategy that combines thermal and mechanical defrosting methods.
In Q10, we successfully improved our system design and acquired HX for retrofitting both
UTS freezers and Copeland reach-in freezers. We initiated the integration and customer
discovery activities by laying out the detailed plan and action items associated with each
team.
In Q11, we simulated the HX's vibration to assess stresses and risks, improved the UTS
prototype heat exchanger, and fabricated the Copeland prototype heat exchanger/walk-
in freezer. We made progress on customer discovery through a customer discovery
workshop, and we projected the BOM and ROI of the technology.
In Q12, we improved the freezer prototype with an iterative experimental process, which
included several changes and improvements to both the apparatus and procedure. This
quarter's effort concluded with a successful experiment that demonstrated significant
performance improvement over the baseline. We also made progress on the reach-in
freezer prototype at Copeland's Helix Innovation Center by completing shakedown testing
and initiating experimentation.
In Q13, we continued experimentation in the freezer with iterative process improvement,
and we tested multiple variables, including motor speed and frosting length/duration. We
improved and iterated the testing procedure and the data processing methods. The
Copeland team completed customer discovery interviews, which provided valuable
insight and learning.
In Q14, we upgraded the control system and instrumentation. A new accelerometer was
implemented, which was installed and set up to produce useful data. A continuous
vibration test was completed, followed by the reinforcement of the evaporator's mounting
flanges. The team completed the first round of noise-reduction efforts, achieving a ~17.5
dB reduction in sound pressure level.
In Q15, the reach-in freezer was received after being shipped by the Copeland team, and
preparations began to run the system. A second round of noise testing was completed
by constructing a duplicate system outside the freezer. This system is lighter and can
become a testbed for changing motor size. Boxing the prototype resulted in significant
reductions in sound pressure level. Finally, another chest freezer experiment was
completed, yielding interesting findings that suggest a hybrid cycle clears the HX coil
more effectively than a traditional thermal cycle.
In Q16, we completed the preparation and set up the reach-in freezer. The team also
starts wrapping up the project by making the prototypes operational. The team also
